# Photoreceptor control of Platynereis growth and lifespan via evolutionarily conserved molecular pathways

## Abstract
SignificanceChanges to natural light are among the biggest anthropogenic alterations. To judge their impact and to weigh possible countermeasures, model systems provide insight into how light information is decoded and used. Our work on the bristleworm Platynereis reveals that light has unanticipated strong effects on growth, reproductive onset, and lifespan. Worms lacking light-receptive cryptochrome, similar to wild-types in darkness, are healthy but delay their reproductive stage. They remain in a juvenile-like stage and grow longer. Our data are consistent with the interpretation that part of the effects are mediated by evolutionarily conserved hormonal pathways, with some related to those orchestrating mammalian puberty. Overall, this study provides insights into the impacts of light on fundamental aspects of life, previously underestimated in animals.Keywords: growth, life span, photoreceptor, ecology, hormone
The duration of life is influenced by many factors. The programs that determine this, and more generally life-history traits (i.e., lifespan, adult size, growth rate, reproductive timing, fecundity), can be plastically reshaped by environmental changes (1, 2). While food availability/intake, temperature, social interactions, or circadian synchronization have been shown to influence lifespan and growth (3–9), the role of light itself does not readily come to mind as lifespan determinant in animals. Understanding the nonvisual effects of light is, however, particularly relevant considering that changes to natural illumination are among the largest anthropogenic effects with already documented influences on human health and the environment (10, 11). The latter is increasingly recognized for marine ecosystems, whose integrity and prosperity are threatened by anthropogenic light changes (10, 12).
Like many marine animals from corals to vertebrates, the physiology and behavior of the bristleworm Platynereis dumerilii is highly dependent on the timing, intensity, and spectrum of the light that reaches its habitat, corresponding in nature to sun- and moonlight (11, 13, 14). Consistently, it possesses a repertoire of at least ten light-sensitive opsins and two light-sensitive cryptochromes (11, 15–17). The light receptor L-Cryptochrome (L-Cry) functions as a light interpreter, discriminating between sun- and moonlight, and providing key information on moon phase (18). In line with this, Platynereis l-cry−/− knockouts show altered reproductive rhythms and entrainment dynamics (18, 19).
In addition to this chronobiological phenotype, we observed that l-cry−/− knockout worms exhibit an increase in lifespan of more than 40% due to an extension of their immature (“youth”) and premature (“puberty-like”) stages, during which they exhibit most characteristics of an adult worm, except reproductive traits. In reference to classical literature, both immature and premature are atoke stages, whereas the onset of reproductive traits marks the epitoke stage. While these worms also attain significantly larger sizes, no morphological abnormality was found. Our molecular analyses revealed an altered endocrine system, with the single worm ortholog of vertebrate atypical nuclear receptors Dax-1 (Nr0b1) and Shp (Nr0b2) as a major target of L-Cry signaling. By disabling this nuclear receptor (lost from arthropod and nematode lineages), we unveiled molecular signatures of conserved functions between nr0b1/2 and its vertebrate orthologs. Overall, our study reveals light-dependent effects of photoreceptor function on animal lifespan and growth, and provides insights into the hormonal pathway evolution and function downstream of animal photoreceptors connected to sexual development and reproduction.

## Results

## l-cry−/− Knockouts Extend Their Lifespan and Adult Size.
Phenotypic analyses performed in density-controlled conditions revealed that l-cry−/− knockouts prolonged the time required to achieve sexual maturity/reproduction from fertilization, compared to wild-type (wt) siblings (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Specifically, male and female mutants required on average 2.7 and 2.6 mo longer to complete their life-cycle, respectively, with an average lifespan increase of ~45% and ~40% (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The remarkable lifespan extension was accompanied by a significant increase in final size (+9% and +7% in male and female average segments number, +90% and +50% in average body wet weight) (Fig. 1 C–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). The prolonged lifespan of l-cry−/− knockouts correlated with an initial delay in body growth (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D–G), and particularly germline development, compared to age-matched l-cry+/+ worms (Fig. 1 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 H–J). The extended growth period of l-cry−/− mutants (Fig. 1B) finally resulted in the larger size and body weight, as the worms continued to grow when wild-types had long stopped such processes and entered reproduction.

We assessed two timepoints after the onset of reproductive age of wt siblings. While after 5 mo post fertilization (mpf) ~6% of the wt worms showed undifferentiated germline (immature worms: germline has not yet visibly differentiated into spermatogonia or eggs), this was the case in 40% of l-cry−/− worms (Fig. 1G). For animals that possessed differentiated spermatogonia or oocytes (premature worms, Fig. 1A), l-cry−/− worms showed less advanced stages of germline development compared to l-cry+/+ individuals, quantified by measuring oocytes size in the two genotypes (Fig. 1 G and H). After 6 mo post fertilization, ~10% of knockouts were still immature compared to 0% of wt (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). Similar to the previous timepoint, l-cry+/+ worms showed more advanced gametogenesis, with ~60% that had already reached sexual maturity and had reproduced, compared to 0% in knockouts (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 H and I). Despite significant differences in their growth rate, oocytes from both sexually mature l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− females reached comparable sizes at release (~160 to 190 μm, SI Appendix, Fig. S1J). Given that knockout worms grow significantly beyond the age and size of wt worms without initiating sexual maturation, a delayed germline development is a plausible explanation.

## Light Affects Lifespan and Growth Via l-cry.
As the phenotypes observed resulted from the lack of a functional photoreceptor, we next tested how lifespan and final size of l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− worms were affected by the exposure to naturalistic light conditions and altered light regimes (Fig. 2A).

Animals were reared under standard laboratory settings and comparable density conditions for ~2 mo and subsequently transferred to one of the following conditions: i) a light/dark cycle using naturalistic sunlight and moonlight (LDM (19, 20), ii) constant sunlight (LL), iii) constant darkness (DD, Fig. 2A). We reasoned that, if the lifespan/growth extension is the result of a delayed germline development, light-dependent effects on worm lifespan and growth should still be visible after the transfer at ~2 mo. Under both naturalistic LDM and LL conditions, l-cry−/− knockouts extended their lifespan and attained larger final size compared to wt siblings similar to the previous experiments conducted under standard artificial LDM conditions (compare Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B vs. Fig. 2 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). In contrast, under DD, l-cry+/+ animals significantly increased their lifespan and final size, reaching values comparable to those of l-cry−/− knockouts under identical conditions (Fig. 2 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). More specifically, we observed that under DD both l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− worms increased their lifespan compared to their respective counterparts reared under LDM and LL conditions (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Importantly, wt and mutant worms showed no significant difference in their final size under DD. For lifespan, the average difference is markedly reduced (3% compared to 34% and 37% for LL and LDM conditions, respectively). The remaining difference is caused by an even further increased lifespan of the wild-type. The latter is likely due to a complex interplay of additional effects of the l-cry mutation on the animals, including effects on the plastic circadian-circalunidian oscillator (19) (also see SI Appendix, Supplementary Text and Figs. S3 and S4 and discussion), additional light receptors impacting on the worms’ physiology (see section below and SI Appendix, Supplementary Text), as well as possibly changes in extrinsic factors, such as the microenvironment in the boxes. It is plausible that these additional factors account for the even extended life span in wild-type, of which- however- the phenotypic contribution already accounted for by l-cry is the largest. In turn, this evidence also suggests that the lifespan and overall body growth extension observed under illuminated conditions are indeed majorly due to L-Cry’s light receptive function.

## Multiple Photoreceptors Colocalize With l-cry, but l-cry’s Phenotype Is Unique.
In order to further understand how light impacts on the worms’ biology, which might explain the observed slightly longer life span of wild-type vs. l-cry−/− in constant darkness (Fig. 2B), and the specificity of the observed l-cry phenotype, we next explored the associations between l-cry and other photoreceptors in the single-cell Platynereis brain atlas (21). A range of opsins and also other cryptochromes/photolyases coexpress in l-cry-positive cell populations (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6). This includes c-opsin1, previously shown to have light-dependent effects on the worms’ development, physiology, and behavior (17, 21). Other coexpressed transcripts included members of the cryptochrome/photolyase family, such as pt-cry, which we had previously also shown biochemically to be capable to function as light receptors (22). We recorded spawning and life span data recorded for c-ops1 mutants (17) and pt-cry mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Unlike l-cry−/− knockouts, neither pt-cry−/− nor c-ops1−/− worms showed visible and significant lifespan differences when compared to respective wild-type controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–D), while c-ops1−/−, l-cry−/− double mutant worms displayed the lifespan increase of the l-cry−/− single knockouts (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D). While the extended life span of the wild-type vs. l-cry mutants under constant darkness argues that other photosensitive processes contribute to this, the life span analyses of these other photoreceptor mutants strongly suggest that l-cry’s phenotype is specific and not simply mimicked by other coexpressed light receptors. Obviously, we have not systematically tested all other (coexpressed) light receptors for their impact on life span, but have not observed any effects comparable to the l-cry−/− phenotype for any of the other photoreceptor mutants present in the lab so far.
The fact that l-cry mutants themselves do not extend their life span similarly to wild-type under constant darkness suggests that what causes this extension in wild-type is likely converging downstream of the l-cry molecular mechanism.

## The Endocrine System, Including nr0b1/2, are Targets of l-cry.
To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the lifespan and growth alterations of l-cry−/− knockouts, we bulk-sequenced head transcriptomes of l-cry−/− mutant and wt worms. The worm brain has been shown to orchestrate maturation/reproductive timing (18, 19, 23), and to modulate both germline development and posterior segment addition (i.e., body growth) (23, 24). L-Cry localizes prominently into the brain, specifically to the eyes and a region that exhibits functional and molecular similarities to the vertebrate hypothalamus (18, 25, 26).
We sequenced head transcriptomes from immature worms (sexes cannot be differentiated) and premature female worms (Fig. 3A). We focused on female worms, because their gonadal stages can be better differentiated than male worms. In immature worms at ~30 to 45 segments, germline proliferation and early differentiation occur (27, 28), while in premature females, more advanced processes of germline development and gametogenesis can be quantitatively assessed. In males, such temporal germline analyses are less precise as spermatogonia do not grow progressively in size till sexual maturity (29).

l-cry−/− knockouts showed altered expression levels of transcripts associated with a variety of processes. However, what stood out was a broad regulation of the worm’s endocrine system in both immature and premature stages (Fig. 3 B and C and Datasets S1 A–D and S2 A–I). Specifically, the lack of L-Cry signaling resulted in the altered expression of a bursicon (burs)-like glycoprotein hormone, Nuclear Receptor subfamily 0 group B 1/2 (nr0b1/2), and the steroidogenic enzyme Dehydrogenase/reductase SDR family member 11 (dhr11) in both developmental stages (Fig. 3 B–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Fig. 3 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). In addition to these generally differentially regulated transcripts, we identified a variety of hormonal players regulated in a stage-dependent manner, including insulin receptor substrates (irs, als), putative G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), nuclear receptors like Retinoic acid receptor (rar), Ecdysone Receptor (ecr), Nuclear Receptor subfamily 5 group A1/2 (nr5a1/2), Vitamin D3 receptor-like (vdr-like), and Ecdysone-induced protein 78C-like (e78c-like) (SI Appendix, Figs. S11 and S12), components of thyroid hormone signaling (i.e., diodinase enzyme, dio), and putative steroidogenic enzymes like Steroid 17-alpha-hydroxylase/17,20 lyase-like (cyp17a1/2) and cholesterol 24-hydroxylase (cyp46a1) (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A and B and Dataset S1 A–D). Orthologs of these factors coordinate developmental and reproductive aspects in either arthropods (i.e., als, irs, burs family, ecr, e78c, hr96) (30–37) or vertebrates (nr0b1, nr0b2, dhr11, nr5a1, dio, cyp17a1, rar, vdr) (38–47). In mammals, CYP17A1 and CYP46A1 are key enzymes for corticoid/sex steroid and cholesterol/oxysterol biosynthesis, respectively (47, 48). Moreover, we found among the most regulated transcripts, PAR domain protein 1 (pdp1) and clockwork orange (cwo) (Fig. 3C and Dataset S1 A–D) two transcriptional regulators mostly known for their function in the circadian clock of Drosophila melanogaster, and at first glance mainly consistent with l-cry’s role in circadian-circalunidian timing (see also SI Appendix, Supplementary Text and SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4) (49, 50). Yet, they also represent homologs of vertebrate’s PAR subfamily of basic leucin zipper (PAR bZip) transcription factors hepatic leukemia factor (hlf), thyrotroph embryonic factor (tef), albumin D-box binding protein (dbp), and Differentiated Embryo-Chondrocyte expressed gene 1-2 (dec1-2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B). Of note, mice deficient for HLF, TEF, and DBP display a dramatically shortened lifespan (51), whereas their common Platynereis ortholog, pdp1, is significantly upregulated in the long-lived l-cry−/− knockouts (Fig. 3C and Dataset S1 A–D). Moreover, in insects, pdp1 plays important roles in developmental progression, growth, and reproductive arrest (52–54).
One of the most robustly regulated transcripts in l-cry−/− knockouts is the worm ortholog of vertebrate Nuclear Receptor subfamily 0 group B (nr0b) members (Fig. 3 B–F): nr0b1 (also dax-1) and nr0b2 (also shp).
DAX-1 is an atypical orphan nuclear receptor crucial for the development of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, steroidogenic tissues (gonads and adrenal glands), gonads differentiation and maturation (38). SHP, besides roles in the regulation of bile acids, cholesterol, and glucose metabolism in the liver (55), is also implicated in gonadal development and steroidogenesis (39, 56). nr0b1/2 genes have long been considered a vertebrate novelty, primarily because they are absent from the ecdysozoan lineage, including fruit flies and nematodes (57, 58) (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Instead, our phylogenetic analysis revealed that dax-1 and shp are likely the result of a vertebrate-specific gene duplication of a single ancestral nr0b1/2 gene (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Gene expression analyses by qPCR and HCR-based in situ staining of nr0b1/2 mRNA confirmed the upregulation of Pdu-nr0b1/2 during full moon (FM) in l-cry−/− vs. l-cry+/+ (Fig. 3 G–I and SI Appendix, Fig. S16), while it was downregulated or less impacted at other lunar phases (Fig. 3G). Transcriptome sequencing of wt heads across different lunar phases confirmed a significant lunar regulation (Fig. 3J). It is notable that nr0b1/2 profiles appear slightly divergent in immature (Fig. 3G) and premature (Fig. 3J) l-cry+/+ worms. While these differences may be in part technical in their nature (qPCR vs. bulk RNAseq), it is also potentially in line with changes in expression dynamics associated with the progression along the maturation trajectory (59).
Given the prominent role of Dax-1 in the temporal control of ovulation in mammals, which ultimately dictates reproductive cycle length (60), it is noteworthy that L-Cry and the lunar cycle impact on the levels of its ortholog. Is it possible that pathways of reproductive timing share conserved molecular components downstream of environmental signal sensors?
Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that nr0b1/2 mRNA prominently copresent in cells expressing l-cry in the median neurosecretory brain regions and in the eyes (Fig. 3 K–N’’’). At present, we have no stain for cellular outlines available for this staining procedure. The nuclei indicated by Hoechst dye thus serve as orientation, with the mRNAs of both l-cry and nr0b1/2 surrounding the same nuclei (see arrows in Fig. 3 N–N’’ for examples). Importantly, as HCR-based in situ hybridization visualizes individual mRNA molecules, these can, but do not have to be in the same position within the cell. In all examples analyzed closely (in 5/5heads), we found l-cry and nr0b1/2 staining surrounding the same nuclei, suggesting that all l-cry+ cells possessed nr0b1/2 mRNA (Fig. 3 K–N’’’); a result confirmed for L-Cry protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A’–D’). Of note, while for the mRNA staining, we always found staining that we identified as copresence in the same cell, this was different for L-Cry protein and nr0b1/2 mRNA. Specifically, some of the median-anterior region only possessed nr0b1/2 mRNA (arrows in SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A and A’).
As we did not find any l-cry+/L-Cry+ cells without nr0b1/2, it suggests a likely tight functional interaction between the two factors, and that L-Cry can directly regulate nr0b1/2 functions in the eye, posterior oval-shaped domain and a small medial anterior domain.
A recent study in the clam Tridacna crocea indicates an involvement of nr0b1/2 in spermatogenesis (61), and this, together with the findings presented above, prompted us to investigate the possible functions nr0b1/2 might possess in Platynereis and how this might interconnect with the l-cry phenotypes. Given the complex temporal regulation of Pdu-nr0b1/2 over the lunar cycle (Fig. 3 G and J), and that depending on the phase of the lunar cycle it can be either up- or down-regulated in l-cry−/− worms (Fig. 3G), it is obvious that none of the functional genetic manipulation available in P. dumerilii will fully mimic or produce opposite phenotypes of l-cry knockouts.

## nr0b1/2 Mutants Partially Recapitulate l-cry−/− Phenotypes.
The Platynereis nr0b1/2 transcript encodes a nuclear receptor with a protein length comparable to mammalian DAX-1 (rather than SHP) and other invertebrate Nr0bs (alignment SI Appendix, Fig. S18). By comparing vertebrate and spiralian sequences, the C-terminal putative ligand binding domain (LBD) shows overall higher similarity than the putative DNA-binding domain (DBD) (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). The typical LXXLL (or its variant LXXXL, called here LXXLL-related) motifs, mediating DAX-1/SHP repressive activity on other nuclear receptors (38, 62), are present in putative DBDs and LBDs of both vertebrate and spiralian Nr0b1/2 proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S18).
We generated Pdu-nr0b1/2 mutants using established TALEN-targeted mutagenesis (63). We identified worms carrying 5-bp and 4-bp deletions, resulting in premature stop codons (Fig. 4A). We analyzed the effect of both mutations on Nr0b1/2 itself in order to determine the most plausible nature of the mutation. Analyses in cell culture are consistent with, but do not constitute proof of nr0b1/2 loss of function (details in SI Appendix, Supplementary Text and Fig. S19).

As we did not identify nr0b1/2 homozygous mutant worms reaching premature or mature stages under standard culturing conditions (consistent with a rather severe functional loss), we conducted a large-scale genotyping analysis of the progeny from nr0b1/2+/− incrosses at different stages of early development. We found Mendelian ratios of homozygous nr0b1/2Δ5/Δ5 (hereafter referred to as nr0b1/2−/−, likewise nr0b1/2Δ4/Δ5 trans-heterozygous) worms at 1- and 6-days post fertilization (dpf) (Fig. 4B), suggesting fertilization and larval development were not impacted.
Yet, from scoring genotypes of worms with ~10 to 20 segments and onward, we observed a progressive decline in the proportion of trans-heterozygous and homozygous nr0b1/2−/− animals and their subsequent disappearance after worms reached ~30-40 segments (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Consistently, under regular culturing conditions we never identified a nr0b1/2−/− worm larger than 34 segments. Of note, this phase of worm development coincides with the temporal window in which germ cell clusters should start proliferating (28). This process marks the beginning of a developmental stage requiring a more complex regulation of energy allocation (64), as a second major physiological program adds to body growth. Thus, we wondered whether lowering the competition with other genotypes would allow some homozygous nr0b1/2 mutants to survive beyond 35 to 40 segments size, and potentially until maturation. For this, we isolated and genotyped randomly selected worms during early phases of worm development (~10 to 30 segments, see SI Appendix, Fig. S20E).
Of the 45 worms, which survived the procedure, all individually isolated nr0b1/2−/− worms matured into visibly distinct males and females. We next assessed lifespan, final size at reproduction and monthly spawning distribution (the major phenotypes of l-cry−/− knockouts) in the few nr0b1/2−/− we successfully raised for the entire life-cycle. nr0b1/2−/− worms exhibited a significantly increased size and also a clear trend to a longer lifespan compared to respective nr0b1/2+/+ controls (Fig. 4 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S20 A–C) and phenotypically reminiscent of l-cry−/− animals (Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B).
Nr0b1/2 mutants showed no obvious morphological abnormalities in soma, germline/gametes, and egg area (SI Appendix, Fig. S20D) and also showed no change in the monthly spawning pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S20 G–I).
This suggests that despite lunar regulation of nr0b transcripts in wt animals, nr0b1/2 is rather involved in growth and possibly aspects of gonadal development, but not essential for its temporal monthly control. Given DAX-1/SHP roles in gametogenesis (38, 39, 56), we tested fertilization success in nr0b1/2 animals reaching sexual maturation in isolated conditions by crossing them with a wt individual of the opposite sex, and evaluating the proportion of living/swimming larvae generated in the following 1 to 2 d. We observed a reduction in nr0b1/2−/− reproductive success (both in males and females), as 50% of their matings resulted in a complete absence of living larvae, i.e. only dead eggs. Given that such complete batch failures are unusual in our hands, this potentially suggests a fertilization problem in homozygous mutants, while fertilization success of nr0b1/2+/− worms may be mildly affected (Fig. 4E). Given that nr0b1/2−/− zygotes and larvae develop successfully, at least initially, when derived from heterozygous incrosses (Fig. 4B), our observations point at developmental defects in the germline of nr0b1/2−/− animals that are not morphologically visible. The analyses of nr0b1/2 mutants provide evidence that, consistent with its expression pattern and complex regulation, L-Cry signaling is partially transduced (directly or indirectly) via changes in nr0b1/2 and its downstream network.

## nr0b1/2 and l-cry Regulation Converges on Transcripts Associated With Sexual Development/Reproduction and Circadian Rhythms.
In order to test which parts of the L-Cry signaling might be molecularly transduced via (direct or indirect) changes in nr0b1/2 expression, we performed RNAseq analyses on nr0b1/2 mutants and compared them to the corresponding l-cry results. We adopted two parallel strategies to cope with the problem of raising nr0b1/2−/− worms. First, in order to guarantee the sampling of nr0b1/2−/− worms, we collected 315 immature worms (12 to 40 segments, SI Appendix, Fig. S20F) from several nr0b1/2+/− crosses (both nr0b1/2+/Δ4 and nr0b1/2+/Δ5) over an 8 h-window. For each worm, anterior portions (including the head) were collected for RNAseq (Fig. 5A), and 6 biological replicates (BRs) were generated by pooling worms with the same genotype (Fig. 5A). Second, we also sequenced the head transcriptome of previously genotyped immature nr0b1/2+/+ and nr0b1/2+/− sampled at ZT10, a strategy which provided a higher diel temporal precision (Fig. 5D).

To facilitate the identification of commonly regulated genes in l-cry and nr0b1/2 mutants, we included l-cry−/− knockouts and wild-type counterparts sampled with the same strategies. We opted to perform our sampling during new moon (NM), as at this timepoint nr0b1/2 expression in l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− worms shows a large difference (Fig. 3G). In both transcriptomic strategies, we confirmed the prominent regulation of the endocrine system in l-cry−/− knockouts (including genes involved in sexual development and reproduction in other animals) (Datasets S3 A–H, S4 A–H, and S5 A–D). More specifically, we identified nuclear receptors in addition to nr0b1/2 [ecr (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), Nuclear hormone receptor HR3 (hr3)-like (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), Retinoic acid X receptor (rxr), Nuclear receptor coactivators (ncoa2,4,7)], peptidergic factors [Pyroglutamylated RFamide peptide receptor (qrfpr), FMRFamide receptor (fmar), Pro-neuropeptide Y (npy) (17), a bursicon (burs)-like neuropeptide (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), other GPCRs], enzymes putatively involved in steroidogenesis [cyp17a1/2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A), dhr11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), cyp1a1, cyp2a1, hydroxysteroid 11-beta-dehydrogenase 1 protein (dhi1l), hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-like protein (hsdl2), 3-oxo-5-alpha-steroid 4-dehydrogenase 1 (s5a1)-like (SI Appendix, Fig. S21)], and transcriptional regulators involved in rhythmic processes [pdp1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A), cwo (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B), period (per), CLOCK-interacting protein circadian (cipc)] and sexual development [transcriptional regulator ovo-like 1/2/3 (ovol1/2/3) (SI Appendix, Fig. S22), doublesex- and mab-3-related transcription factor 1 (dmrt1) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23)], with several prominently regulated during FM as well (dio, cyp17a1/2, dhr11, ecr, cwo, pdp1, nr0b1/2) (Figs. 3 B and C and 5 B and E). Of note, we also found nr0b1/2 significantly downregulated at NM in l-cry−/− knockouts, consistent with the qPCR data (Fig. 3G and 5B).
From the analyses of nr0b1/2−/− and nr0b1/2+/− transcriptomes, we noticed that [with one single exception (ependymin-related protein 4.9, (erp4.9)], there were no significant differences between the two genotypes at the molecular level (Fig. 5G and Dataset S4 A–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S24), consistent with their overall phenotypic similarity (SI Appendix, Fig. S20 A−I). This likely suggests compensatory effects in the surviving homozygous animals. Differential expression analyses between each transcriptome and its nr0b1/2+/+ counterpart showed a significant regulation of similar gene classes identified in l-cry−/− worms, including nuclear receptors and coactivators [nr5a1/2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), estrogen-related receptor α/β/γ (errα/β/γ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S25), hr38-like (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), ncoa2, ncoa7], putative steroidogenic enzymes [s5a1-like (SI Appendix, Fig. S21)], cytochromes P450 (i.e., cyp2j1, cyp2c8), neuropeptides [npy, an insulin-like peptide (ilp), prepro-vasotocin-neurophysin (vtn)], peptide receptors [insulin receptor (inr), other GPCRs], thyroid hormone signaling (dio), transcriptional regulators involved in sexual development [doublesex- and mab-3-related transcription factor 3/C2 (dmrt3/c2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S23)], rhythmic processes [pdp1, cwo, vertebrate-like cryptochrome (tr-cry)], and cholesterol biosynthesis/lipid homeostasis [sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (srbp1)] (Fig. 5C and Dataset S4 A–H). Several genes or pathways were represented in nr0b1/2+/− as well (nr5a1/2, ncoa2, s5a1-like, npy, ilp, GPCRs, dio, dmrt1, dmrt3/c2, cyp3ab, cyp2cn) (SI Appendix, Fig. S24C and Dataset S4 A–H).
Using the second strategy (sampling at a precise timepoint), we complemented the transcriptional landscape affected by Nr0b1/2 signaling, including a prominent regulation of putative steroidogenic enzymes [dhr11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), cyp17a1/2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13), cyp1a1, dhr12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), 17-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 13 (dhb13)], cholesterol homeostasis [cyp46a1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B)], other cytochromes P450 (cyp2j2, cyp3at), nuclear receptors (hr3-like (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), Vitamin D3 receptor-like (vdr-like) (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), Estrogen receptor (erα/β)] (SI Appendix, Fig. S25), peptidergic signaling [bursicon (burs)-like and gpcrx, a still unidentified GPCR (Dataset S5 A–D and SI Appendix, Supplementary Text], and again transcriptional regulators involved in both rhythmic processes [pdp1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A), tr-cry, timeless (tim)], and sexual development [ovol1/2/3, (SI Appendix, Fig. S22) and Fig. 5F and Dataset S3 A–H).
Among the most regulated transcripts between nr0b1/2 mutants and nr0b1/2+/+, dhr11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), cyp17a1/2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A), s5a1-like (SI Appendix, Fig. S21), nr5a1/2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), errα/β/γ (SI Appendix, Fig. S25), dmrt1, dmrt3/c2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S23), ovol1/2/3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S22) are all homologs (and in some cases single worm orthologs) of important components of the vertebrate molecular machinery orchestrating steroidogenesis, sexual development, and/or directly interacting with DAX-1 (38, 40, 42, 47, 62, 65–68). In mammals, DHR11, CYP17A1, and S5A1 are involved in the biosynthesis of steroid hormones, particularly of sex steroid hormones (40, 47, 67). Yet, it has been proposed that steroidogenesis evolved independently in the three major bilaterian lineages (Vertebrata, Spiralia, Ecdysozoa) (69). In particular, no genes orthologous to vertebrate steroidogenic enzymes were thought to be present in lophotrochozoans (part of the Spiralia clade that includes annelids) (69). While our analyses do not show that the conserved enzymes identified take part in the very same reactions in annelids, our findings challenge the notion of a completely independent evolution of steroidogenesis between deuterostomes and protostomes. It suggests rather secondary losses in specific lineages (i.e., ecdysozoans), as observed for several other genes (70).
We then intersected the nr0b1/2 datasets with the respective l-cry ones to identify commonly regulated genes (Fig. 5H). By intersecting candidates differentially expressed in l-cry−/− and nr0b1/2−/− animals (relative to their wt counterparts, first strategy, see Fig. 5A), we found peptidergic GPCRs (gpcrx, gpcry, Datasets S6 A–C and S7 A–U and SI Appendix, Supplementary Text), the nuclear receptor coactivator ncoa2, the putative steroidogenic enzyme s5a1-like, and the cytochrome P450 cyp2c8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S24 A, and F and Datasets S3 A–H and S6 A–C). On the other hand, intersecting candidates differentially expressed in nr0b1/2+/− and l-cry−/− heads (second strategy, Fig. 5D), we found putative steroidogenic enzymes (dhr11, dhr12, cyp17a1/2), other cytochromes P450 (cyp3at, cyp2j2), the nuclear receptor rxr, a bursicon (burs)-like neuropeptide, gprx, and the transcriptional regulators pdp1 and ovol1/2/3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S24 B and G and Datasets S3 A–H and S6 A–C). Gpcrx shares a robust similarity with Allatostatin A receptors (AstAR) and Gonadotropin-releasing hormone receptors (GnRHR), by reciprocal best blast against other invertebrate genomes (Dataset S6 A–C and SI Appendix, Supplementary Text). Although we could not pinpoint the exact phylogenetic relationships for this GPCR, both classes identified share important roles in development/maturation and reproduction in invertebrates and vertebrates (64, 71, 72).
All combined, our data suggest that light-dependent phenotypes of l-cry knockouts (control of lifespan and growth but not monthly reproductive timing) are partially mediated by nr0b1/2, likely through the transcriptional regulation of a specific set of endocrine players and transcription factors (Fig. 5 H and I) which orchestrate sexual development and maturation in both vertebrates and invertebrates.

## Discussion
The present study emphasizes the strong and direct impact of light on processes that are typically not immediately associated with light dependency in animals: lifespan and growth.
As the regulation of lifespan and growth are fundamental for organisms’ survival and reproductive success (fitness), our findings harbor evolutionary and ecological relevance. Specifically, we wondered what could be driving the evolution of such light-dependent phenotypic plasticity in the worms’ natural habitat.
In the ocean, light intensity changes significantly in relation to depth and time (11, 17, 73). Thus, a light-dependent control of lifespan and growth trajectories might have evolved around a trade-off between reproductive success and predator avoidance (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). In many species (including Platynereis) larger bodies can accommodate a higher number of gametes (as the latter show comparable size between l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− worms), hence likely increasing reproductive success of a given individual. Consistently, the offspring numbers of l-cry−/− animals are typically at least twice as large as wild-type. Yet, larger animals take more time and resources to grow and are more easily noticed by predators, particularly when inhabiting shallow waters. In this context, a light-dependent developmental plasticity could enable worms to maximize both reproductive success and survival, adjusting their size (and thus number of gametes) based on lighting conditions (and thus risk of predation) (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). An increased number of offspring also allows for wider progeny dispersal and by this can increase habitat diversity and reduce intraspecific competition (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). In line with this hypothesis and even though P. dumerilii lives primarily in 1 to 10 m-deep shallow waters (14), individuals of this species have been reported and collected even up to 100 m depth (74).
Light information in the worms’ habitat also varies in a seasonal-dependent manner not only in terms of photoperiod, but also relative spectral intensities (17). The adjustment of lifespan and growth in a light-dependent manner could thus also be part of a seasonal optimization program reminiscent of those described for insects and vertebrates that enable animals to time their reproduction (or specific developmental transitions) to likely favorable environmental conditions (75, 76).
The phenomena we describe are unlikely to represent Platynereis peculiarities, as light-dependent modulation of developmental time has been shown in flies (77) (with longevity covarying with this life-history trait, (78)). However, unlike in flies, our study provides evidence for extensive molecular similarities in the reproductive machinery of annelids and vertebrates, often considered to have evolved largely independently. Thus, these light-dependent effects provide further insights into the possible implications of anthropogenic impacts on natural illumination, via light pollution, but also by reductions of light intensity caused by floating solar farms, as well as the increasingly discussed blocking of sunlight to prevent further global warming. Our work suggests that such interventions pose risks to environments that were so far unknown.
Indeed, under constant darkness (DD) we observed a significant lifespan increase in both l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− worms compared to their counterparts reared under the other two light regimes. The complexity of the phenotype – extension of life span under DD beyond what is observed under constant light and light/dark conditions for both genotypes, but wild-type life span even further extended beyond that of the l-cry−/− mutants – clearly indicates the action of multifactorial processes. This phenotype under DD appears to exhibit l-cry dependent and l-cry independent components. L-cry dependent processes are responsible for the extended life span in the wild-type beyond the worms of the l-cry−/− genotype, while l-cry independent processes lead to the extension of both genotypes under DD. Given the multitude of photoreceptors, that in part even coexpress with l-cry in the same cells and are regulated by it (e.g., r-opsin1, r-opins3), it is highly likely that several of those may also control light-dependent processes that can impact on life span. While, where available, the data of other photoreceptor mutants tested under light/dark conditions show that l-cry’s life extending phenotype in its magnitude appears rather unique, it is well possible that under DD these photoreceptors may contribute to life span relevant processes. In line with this, extended developmental time under DD [compared to both LL, short- and long-day) has been observed even in Caenorhabditis elegans, (79)], an animal that does neither possess opsins nor cryptochrome orthologs. This clearly argues that light-dependent regulated processes are likely to be under additional control than just l-cry (for additional details see SI Appendix, Supplementary Text).
Mechanistically, we identified a molecular axis linking light perception/interpretation via L-Cry with a complex array of molecular effectors including, among others, putative steroidogenic enzymes, as well as transcription factors orchestrating timing and reproductive aspects (Fig. 5i). Moreover, we demonstrated that L-Cry action is partly mediated by Nr0b1/2, as 1) phenotypes observed in nr0b1/2−/− mutants partially recapitulate those identified in l-cry−/− worms, and 2) transcriptomic signatures between these mutants largely overlap for genes involved in reproductive and timing aspects. The remarkable coexpression pattern of l-cry/L-Cry and nr0b1/2 within the same cells suggests a cell-autonomous regulation. Yet, how L-Cry impacts on nr0b1/2 levels, whether directly or indirectly and on transcriptional or posttranscriptional level, remains to be investigated (for more detailed hypotheses on underlying molecular mechanisms, see SI Appendix, Supplementary Text).
The prominent role of Nr0b1 and Nr0b2 (Dax-1 and Shp) in the hormonal control of reproduction in vertebrates (38, 39, 56), recent functional evidence for the involvement of nr0b1/2 in mollusk spermatogenesis (61), and its contribution to lifespan/growth control via conserved endocrine pathways, suggest that these are likely ancestral roles for this class of nuclear receptors. In line with Nr0b1/2 pivotal role in orchestrating aspects of sexual development also in Platynereis, among the regulated genes in nr0b1/2 mutants we found homologs of dmrt1 (and dmrt3/c2, also potentially involved in sexual development), and ovol1/2/3. Proteins sharing the DM domain (i.e., Doublesex and mab-3 related transcription factors 1, Dmrt1) are transcription factor known as conserved actors in male sex differentiation and gametogenesis in bilaterians (65, 80), yet found to coordinate oogenesis as well (81). Ovol1/2/3 encodes an evolutionary conserved zinc finger transcription factor which controls germline development in both flies and mammals (68). In addition, we also identified signatures reminiscent of SHP functions not directly connected with sexual development/reproduction, like lipid/cholesterol metabolism (cyp46a1), transcriptional control of other CYP450s (i.e., cyp2j2, cyp3at), and the regulation of rhythmic processes (pdp1, tr-cry, tim, rev-erbα/β/nr1d1/2). Indeed, in mammals, SHP orchestrates nutrient availability and lipid metabolism with the liver circadian clock, by regulating BMAL1, and interacting with RORγ and Rev-Erbα (82, 83) (for further details on the possible nr0b1/2 regulatory network and its comparison to vertebrates see SI Appendix, Supplementary Text). Overall, this suggests that, after dax-1 and shp emerged from an originally single gene as a result of vertebrate whole genome duplications, they underwent processes of subfunctionalization.
Of course, we do not exclude that neofunctionalization processes also led to the evolution of novel roles in the two vertebrate genes, or that spiralian/species-specific functions were not inherited by either of the two vertebrate genes or evolved secondarily. An example of the latter might be the strong regulation of jaw proteins (JPs) in nr0b1/2 mutant animals. However, overall our data suggest a so far unknown similarity between the downstream molecular pathways of Platynereis Nr0b1/2 and vertebrate Nr0b1(Dax1) and Nr0b2 (SHP). Future investigations will provide a better understanding of the evolutionarily conserved versus deviating pathways.

## Methods

## Worm Culture.
Platynereis cultures were maintained in temperature-controlled rooms at 18 °C under a rectangular light–dark (LD) cycle (LD 16:8) according to standard protocols as described previously (64, 84). For details see SI Appendix, Supplementary Text.

## Lifespan, Maturation, Final Size, and Wet Weight.
See SI Appendix, Methods.

## Light Dependency in Life-History Traits.
In the experiment designed to assess the effects of different light conditions on lifespan and final size, the selected conditions were 1) naturalistic light/dark (LD) 16:8 (using naturalistic sunlight) with naturalistic moonlight (M) during 8 nights/month (LDM) (18, 20); 2) constant sunlight (LL); 3) constant darkness (DD). To mimic naturalistic sunlight and moonlight conditions, we took advantage of NELIS (Natural Environmental Light Intensity System) (Marine Breeding Systems GmbH, Switzerland) (20). Under LL, worms were not subjected to naturalistic moonlight, as this would have been covered by sunlight intensity during the subjective night. Light spectra were measured using an ILT950 Spectroradiometer (International Light Technologies) (SI Appendix, Fig. S27), and oscillations in light intensity and temperature (°C) monitored using HOBO® devices (Onset Brands). The light intensity used was within the intensity range experienced by this species in its natural habitat (17, 20). On average, temperature recorded in the three shelves was LDM: 19.082 °C, LL: 19.081 °C, DD: 18.312 °C (Dataset S8 A–C for detailed recordings). Worms were raised in standard LDM laboratory conditions (84) for ~2 mo since fertilization, and then transferred to one of the three conditions described above for the rest of their life-cycle (~ in proximity of their entry in premature phase, marked by germline proliferation and differentiation). For this experiment, seawater in culture boxes was exchanged every week.
Growth rate. To assess growth rate, we first measured the length of 1.5-months-old worms kept in comparable density conditions and fed ad libitum with algae suspension. Then, worms were isolated in boxes with the same final density (see above) (Fig. 1F), or in 6-well dishes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E), based on the experimental design, and fed with the same amount of food (spinach and Tetramin fish food). Every month, worms were anesthetized and photographed (Fig. 1F), or segments counted (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). Worm length was measured using Fiji v. 2.9.0 (ImageJ) (85), or assessed as number of segments (as clearly stated on graphs’ Y-axis). It has to be noted that, before the end of sexual metamorphosis, worm’s length and segment number covary linearly (SI Appendix, Fig. S28). For regeneration experiments, worm’s trunks from age-matched ~30 to 40 segments-long immature individuals were halved, and the number of regrown segments assessed every week by stereomicroscope inspection (after brief anesthesia) for 4 wk. Animals were fed twice a week with a mixture of spirulina and Tetramin. Data were analyzed using Student’s t test: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. Correlation between segment number and worm’s length was analyzed using simple linear regression.

## RNA Sequencing.
To investigate stage-specific transcriptional dynamics in l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− worms (Fig. 3A), ~2-mo-old immature worms were collected and decapitated at ZT6 during the full moon (FM) phase. For further details, see SI Appendix, Methods.

## qRT-PCR.
l-cry+/+ and l-cry−/− immature worms were used to a) assess the expression levels of nr0b1/2 in the different artificial lunar phases resulting from standard laboratory LDM (LD 16:8, Fig. 3G), as well as to b) profile clock genes expression throughout the daily light–dark (LD) cycle (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Worms were starved for ~1 wk before the sampling, quickly decapitated a) at ZT10 during the 4 main lunar phases (FM = full moon, FM+1 wk, NM = new moon, FM-1 wk), or b) every 3 to 5 h starting from ZT3 at NM, and heads snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. For each biological replicate, mRNA was extracted from 4-5 worm heads using the BioZym kit (Zymo Research) and reverse transcription carried out using the LunaScript kit (New England Biolabs). qRT-PCR was performed using the Luna Master Mix (New England Biolabs) and the QuantStudio 3 System (Applied Biosystems). mRNA levels were normalized to those of a) cdc5 and rps9 housekeeping genes, or b) cdc5 only, and relative quantification performed by using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Results were analyzed using Two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparison test. For daily expression patterns, data were analyzed also using One-way ANOVA to evaluate differences throughout timepoints within the same genotype. Area under the curve (AUC) has been calculated as in (87). *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. Statistical analysis was performed using Graph Pad Prism v. 9. Primers used were nr0b1/2 F: 5’-TCCATCCAGAACTTCATCAGG -3’, R: 5’-CGAACTCTCCATTCTCAAGTCC -3’cdc5 F: 5’-CCTATTGACATGGACGAAGAT G-3’, R: 5’-TTCCCTGTGTGTTCGCAAG-3’; rps9 F: 5’-CGCCAGAGAGTTGCTGACT-3’, R: 5’-ACTCCAATACGGACCAGACG-3’, for circadian clock gene transcripts as published (87).

## Interrogation of Single-Cell Platynereis Adult Brain Atlas.
Single-cell transcriptomic data and Platynereis head atlas were generated from a described dataset, employing the same methods and software packages used to generate the published code (21). For atlas interrogation, l-cry-expressing cell populations were determined by selecting the cells with the relative imputed logarithmic value of normalized l-cry reads equal to or higher than 2.5 (~50% of the highest value; a stringent cutoff was chosen in order to avoid background levels). Following this, the l-cry-expressing cells were categorized into (super-)clusters corresponding to the predefined cell types in the atlas. Then, all identified Platynereis putative opsins and cryptochromes/photolyases were analyzed to extrapolate a) % of cryptochromes/photolyases-positive cells within a specific (super-)cluster expressing them; b) % of cells expressing them in all the other (super-)clusters; c) significance of the difference in their expression levels in each cryptochrome/photolyase-expressing (super-)cluster when compared to those in all other (super-)clusters. The coexpression was then visualized in a dimensionally reduced space as previously published (21).

## Generation and Genotyping of nr0b1/2 and pt-cry Mutant Worms.
TALENs design and preparation were carried out as in (63). The usage of TALENs over the Cas9/Crispr technology for the generation of mutants has historical reasons. However, we prefer to still work with our TALEN mutants over newly generated Cas9/Crispr-based mutants, because the TALEN recognition sites are longer and- due to obligatory dimerization requirements of FokI endonuclease- necessitate TALEN-binding from two sites, making the sequence specificity for the target locus higher than Cas9/Crispr approaches. We targeted exon 1 (~1.3 kb) in the nr0b1/2 locus, and the TALENs recognition site was 5’- GGGAGTGTTTCATCAcccagtgatagccagGCTGCTGCTGCTGCT-3’. nr0b1/2+/− (nr0b1/2+/Δ5 or nr0b1/2+/Δ4) and nr0b1/2−/− (nr0b1/2Δ5/Δ5 or nr0b1/2Δ4/Δ5) strains were generated from single founders, carrying a 5-bp or a 4-bp deletion in the nr0b1/2 coding region (Fig. 4A). These deletions created a frameshift that introduced premature stop codons after 7 and 21 amino acids, respectively (Fig. 4A). To identify worm genotypes, the region encompassing TALENs-targeted site was amplified (primers F: 5’-AAACGTTTAAAAGGATTGTACCCGG-3’, R: 5’-CCGACGATTTGGGCGCTTCC-3’), and the restriction digestion performed using BtsIMutI (New England Biolabs). Alternatively, following PCR amplification, amplicons were sequenced via Sanger sequencing using forward primers, and sequences obtained compared bioinformatically using CLC Main Workbench (Qiagen). Notably, the generation of nr0b1/2Δ5/Δ5 homozygous and nr0b1/2Δ4/Δ5 trans-heterozygous mutants was possible only by isolating worms early in development in individual wells. Indeed, nr0b1/2−/− worms appeared to suffer from fitness disadvantage under competitive conditions (presence of the other genotypes, or simply other individuals, within the same space), and they never reached maturation in the presence of nr0b1/2+/+ or nr0b1/2+/− animals (Fig. 4B). Specifically, to obtain nr0b1/2−/− homozygous worms, 95 ~10-30 segments-long individuals were randomly selected from several age-matched heterozygous mating. Worms were genotyped by amputating their posterior half, before raising them for their entire life in isolated wells. Genotype frequency within batches was analyzed using the χ2 test: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.
For pt-cry the TALEN recognition sequence was targeted to exon 1. For further details see SI Appendix, Fig. S7.

## Phylogenetic Study and Sequence Analysis.
To reconstruct phylogenetic relationships for the major candidates identified in RNAseq experiments (nr0b1/2, dhr11, cyp17a1/2, cyp46a1, pdp1, cwo, nr5a1/2, errα/β/γ, s5a1-like, burs-like, dmrt1, dmrt3/c2, other nuclear receptors, ovol1/2/3), Platynereis transcripts [generated mapping reads on both a cDNA database with genome-predicted transcripts (89), and a previously published transcriptome (84)] were used to identify the putative coding sequences and obtain the corresponding translations using ORFfinder (NCBI). For the other species, protein sequences were retrieved by Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) from NCBI databases. Hit sequences were aligned using MUSCLE (90). Maximum likelihood phylogenies were generated using the IQ-TREE (91) web server, with default settings. Consensus trees generated were visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life tool (92), and rooted using a protein outgroup (outside the protein group of interest) identified by BLAST searches. Protein sequences used for phylogenetic reconstructions and their respective accession numbers can be found in Dataset S9 A–P. Sequences of Platynereis transcripts identified and phylogenetically validated as described above are available under the following GenBank identifiers: nr0b1/2-PX926525, nr2cd-PX926524, dhr11a (dhr11)-PX926527, dhr11b (dhr11)-PX926528, dhr12-PX926526, dhr2/4-PX928723, dhi1l-PX928724, cyp17a1/2a (cyp17a1/2)-PX928725, cyp46a1a (cyp46a1)-PX928727, cyp46a1b (cyp46a1)-PX928726, cwo-PX928728, nr4a1-4- PX943765, nr5a1/2/3 (nr5a1/2)-PX943757, hr39 (nr5b1, ftz-f1 beta)-PX943761, nr6a1/2-PX943755, erra/b/g (errα/β/γ)-PX943763, s5a1l (s5a1-like)-PX943759, bursal (burs-like)-PX943752, bursa-PX943753, bursb-PX943754, bursa2-PX943756, thra/b (thr)-PX943764, nr1h1-5 (ecr)-PX943766, nr1i1/2/3b (hr96-like)-PX943782, nr1i1/2/3a (hr96)-PX943781, nr1f1-4 (hr3-like)-PX943767, dmrt3/c2-PX943758, ovol1/2/3a (ovol1/2/3a)-PX943760, pt-cry-PX943762.
For the analysis of Nr0b1/2 domains and motifs conservation across bilaterian species, protein sequences obtained as described above were aligned in CLC Main Workbench v. 22, and boundaries of ligand- and DNA-binding domains displayed based on human DAX-1 (see141). For the identification and visualization of nuclear receptor (NR)-binding box motifs (typical of NRs coactivators and corepressors), we searched for and displayed all LXXLL and LXXXL (LXXLL variants with the leucine at position 4 substituted) (overall called LXXLL-related) consecutive residues, including those overlapping, as several are evolutionary conserved (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Protein sequences used to generate the alignment and their respective accession numbers can be found in Dataset S9 A–P.
