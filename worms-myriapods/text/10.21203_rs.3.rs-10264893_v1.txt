Abstract
Background
Chronic and non-healing wounds remain a major clinical challenge due to persistent inflammation,
impaired angiogenesis, and defective extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling. Human Mesenchymal
Stem Cells have emerged as promising regenerative therapies, with their effects mainly attributed to the
secretome. In particular, the secretome, derived from human Dental Pulp Stem Cells cultured under
hypoxic conditions (hDPSC-HS), is enriched in pro-regenerative and immunomodulatory factors. Previous
in vitro studies demonstrated that hDPSC-HS promotes endothelial cell migration and angiogenesis,
enhances fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, and modulates macrophage polarization.
However, its in vivo regenerative potential remains poorly defined. Here, we used the medicinal leech
Hirudo verbana, an evolutionarily conserved model that reproduces key cellular and molecular
mechanisms of tissue repair, to explore the biological effects of hDPSC-HS in vivo.
Methods
Standardized surgical wounds were created in adult leeches and then a locally injection was made with
either hDPSC-HS or serum-free Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (control). Tissues were collected at
48 h, 72 h, and 1 week post-treatment. Wound healing was evaluated by morphological, histological,
immunofluorescence, and gene expression analyses focusing on ECM remodelling, angiogenic
activation, and muscle regeneration.
Results
hDPSC-HS significantly accelerated wound closure and improved tissue restoration compared with
controls. Treated animals showed enhanced muscle fibre regeneration and a more organized ECM
architecture. Molecular and immunofluorescence analyses revealed modulation of key pathways
involved in ECM remodelling and tissue regeneration, indicating the establishment of a pro-regenerative
microenvironment that supports more efficient healing.
Conclusions
Although generated in an exploratory in vivo model, these findings demonstrate that hDPSC-HS
promotes coordinated tissue repair by modulating evolutionarily conserved regenerative mechanisms.
Because these biological pathways are shared with vertebrates and are critically involved in the
pathophysiology of chronic human wounds, the Hirudo verbana model provides biologically relevant
evidence supporting the translational potential of hDPSC-HS. These results provide a rationale for further
preclinical validation and clinical investigation of hDPSC-HS as a therapeutic strategy for chronic
wounds characterized by impaired healing.

Background
Page 3/30

Tissue repair is a tightly regulated and dynamic biological process that requires the coordinated
temporal and spatial interplay of inflammatory mediators, extracellular matrix (ECM) reorganization,
angiogenesis, and activation of resident and circulating progenitor cells [1, 2]. In acute wounds, these
events unfold through partially overlapping phases, including haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation,
and remodelling, that culminate in functional restoration. Conversely, chronic wounds remain stuck in a
persistent inflammatory state characterized by excessive proteolytic activity, impaired angiogenesis, and
cellular senescence, ultimately leading to defective regeneration and fibrotic outcomes [3, 4]. Such
pathological conditions represent a major clinical and socioeconomic burden worldwide, affecting
millions of patients and generating high healthcare costs. These challenges underscore the urgent need
for innovative regenerative strategies capable of restoring tissue homeostasis rather than merely
achieving wound closure [5, 6].
Within this framework, regenerative medicine has shifted from conventional symptomatic treatments
toward biologically driven strategies that actively modulate the wound microenvironment. In this context,
human Mesenchymal Stem Cells (hMSCs) have emerged as powerful therapeutic candidates due to their
immunomodulatory, pro-angiogenic, and trophic properties [7, 8]. Increasing evidence demonstrates that
the regenerative potential of hMSCs is largely attributed to their paracrine activity rather than long-term
engraftment or direct differentiation [9, 10]. In particular, these cells release of a complex repertoire of
bioactive molecules, including cytokines, growth factors, chemokines, proteases, and extracellular
vesicles (EVs), collectively known as the secretome [11–13]. These factors act in a coordinated manner
to regulate inflammation resolution, promote angiogenesis, regulate ECM remodelling, inhibit apoptosis,
and stimulate resident cell proliferation, thereby orchestrating key processes involved in tissue repair
[14].
Among the various hMSC sources, human Dental Pulp Stem Cells (hDPSCs) have attracted growing
interest due to their high proliferative capacity and potent secretory profile [9, 15]. Isolated from the
dental pulp of extracted third impacted molars, hDPSCs combine high proliferative capacity, multilineage
differentiation potential, low immunogenicity, minimal ethical concerns, and non-invasive harvesting
accessibility. Similar to other hMSCs, the therapeutic efficacy of hDPSCs is largely attributed to their
secretome (hDPSC-S), which includes angiogenic mediators such as Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor
(VEGF) and Fibroblast Growth Factor-2 (FGF-2), immunomodulatory cytokines comprising Transforming
Growth Factor-β (TGF-β) and Interleukin-10 (IL-10), as well as cytoprotective and chemoattractive factors
like Hepatocyte Growth Factor (HGF) and Stromal cell-Derived Factor-1 (SDF-1) [16–18]. In addition,
hDPSC-derived EVs, enriched in regulatory microRNAs, contribute to intercellular communication and
modulation of angiogenesis, oxidative stress responses, apoptosis, and ECM architectural
rearrangement [19, 20].
The recognition that these therapeutic effects derive from the synergistic interaction of multiple soluble
mediators has fostered growing interest in cell-free regenerative medicine [21]. Compared with direct cell
transplantation, secretome-based therapies offer several advantages, including a better safety profile,
reduced immunogenicity, and easier storage and handling. They retain the multifactorial regenerative
Page 4/30

potential of their cellular origin while facilitating scalability and regulatory standardization. Notably,
culturing hDPSCs under hypoxic conditions has been shown to further enhance the regenerative
potential of their secretome, enriching it with pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatory factors that can
significantly influence the wound healing process [9, 22, 23].
Despite promising in vitro evidence, the in vivo mechanisms through which hypoxia-conditioned hDPSC
secretome (hDPSC-HS) regulates inflammation, angiogenesis, and ECM remodelling during tissue repair
remain poorly understood. A comprehensive understanding of these processes requires experimental
systems capable of recapitulating the complexity of living tissues while allowing detailed cellular and
molecular analyses. In this context, the medicinal leech Hirudo verbana represents a valuable and
evolutionarily conserved in vivo model for studying wound healing and tissue regeneration [24–26]. Its
well-characterized innate immune system, hematopoietic tissues, and conserved molecular pathways
governing angiogenesis and ECM dynamics provide a simplified yet highly informative platform for
investigating regenerative processes while avoiding the additional complexity of adaptive immunity.
Building on these premises, the present study aims to evaluate the regenerative potential of hDPSC-HS
with particular emphasis on its ability to modulate angiogenesis, immune response, and ECM
remodelling in an in vivo injury model. By elucidating the mechanisms underlying secretome-driven
tissue repair, this work seeks to further support hDPSC-HS as a promising, clinically translatable
medical-surgical device for chronic wounds characterized by impaired regeneration.

Methods
Sample source and cell culture
hDPSCs were isolated from the dental pulp tissue of three healthy donors (Table 1) undergoing third
molar extraction at the “Ospedale di Circolo Fondazione Macchi”. All the donors were non-smokers, had
no history of metabolic disorders, and were not under medication at the time of the surgical procedure.
Informed consent to be involved in the study was obtained from the donor’s legal guardians. The study
was approved by the institutional review board ethics committee (Protocol No. 0034066, approved on
October 3, 2013), according to the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2013.
In vivo experiments were conducted on medicinal leeches (H. verbana), a species not included among
those protected under Directive 2010/63/EU or the Italian Legislative Decree No. 26 of March 4, 2014,
concerning the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. Nevertheless, their use was
authorized by the Animal Welfare Body (OPBA) of the University of Insubria.
Secretome preparation and characterization
hDPSCs were isolated from dental pulp by slightly modifying the protocol described by Gronthos et
al. [15]. Cells were cultured until the 5th passage and, once reached 70-80% confluence, the culture
medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells
Page 5/30

were then incubated in foetal bovine serum (FBS)-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) for 72
h under hypoxic conditions (2% O2, 5% CO2, 93% N2). After incubation, hDPSC-HS were collected and
centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 minutes to remove cell debris. To maximize protein yield, secretomes were
concentrated using an Amicon Ultra 15 mL Centrifugal Filter Device (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) with
a 3 kDa cut-off; following the manufacturer’s instructions, hDPSC-HS was centrifuged at 4000×g for 40
min at 4°C, and the concentrated supernatants were collected [27].
Protein quantification was performed using the NanoOrange® Protein Quantitation Kit (Promega, Milano,
Italy), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Aliquots containing 50 μg of total proteins were
stored at -80°C until use for in vivo experiments.
Secretome characterization was performed by Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), as
reported in Marcozzi et al. [28], and following the manufacturer’s instructions (FineTest®, Wuhan, Hubei,
China), to investigate the abundance of VEGF. The total amount was determined through the absorbance
recording at 450 nm using the GloMax® Discover Microplate Reader (Promega, Milano, Italy). Each
experiment was repeated 3 times, and the obtained values were expressed as pg/mL.
Experimental design
In vivo experiments were conducted in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.
Adult leeches of the species H. verbana (Annelida, Hirudinea) were kindly donated by ILFARM S.r.l.
(Varese, Italy) and maintained in oxygenated tanks, filled with lightly salted water (NaCl 1.5 g/L), at 20°C.
For the experiments, three animals were randomly assigned to each of the four groups using a
computer-generated randomization list (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, USA). Each
experiment was performed in triplicate.
Group 1: injured leeches, injected with 50 µL of FBS-free DMEM, used as control (CTRL)
Group 2: injured leeches, injected with 50 µL of donor 1 hDPSC-HS
Group 3: injured leeches, injected with 50 µL of donor 2 hDPSC-HS
Group 4: injured leeches, injected with 50 µL of donor 3 hDPSC-HS
Before injury induction, leeches were anesthetized using a 10% ethanol solution in freshwater until
complete cessation of movement and absence of response to tactile stimuli. Three longitudinal wounds,
measuring 5 mm in length, were performed on the dorsal portion of all leeches using a razor blade. Each
wound was 10 mm apart from the previous one and affecting the entire thickness of the body wall
(extending to cuticle, epithelium, and muscle layers). The media, either FBS-free DMEM or hDPSC-HS,
were then inoculated laterally to the site of injury using insulin micro-syringes. To assess the differences
in tissue regeneration among the experimental groups, animals were sacrificed 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week
after the injection. Prior to tissue dissection, euthanasia was performed by prolonged immersion in a
10% ethanol solution in freshwater to ensure complete and irreversible cessation of vital functions.

Morphological characterization by light and transmission electron microscopy
Leech tissues were dissected and immediately fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde diluted in 0.1 M cacodylate
buffer (pH 7.4), overnight at 4°C. Samples were then rinsed in the same buffer and post-fixed using 2%
osmium tetroxide (OsO4) diluted in cacodylate buffer, for 1 hour at room temperature, protected from
light. Samples were dehydrated with a serial ethanol scale (50%, 70%, 90% added with uranyl acetate,
and 100%), then soaked in propylene oxide for 1 hour, and transferred to a 1:1 mixture of epoxy resin and
propylene oxide for an additional hour at room temperature. They were subsequently immersed to a 2:1
epoxy resin/propylene oxide solution overnight at room temperature and finally embedded in EponAraldite 812 epoxy resin (Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy).
Semithin sections (750 nm) were cut using an RMC Power Tome XL (Boeckeler Instruments, Tucson,
USA), collected on glass slides, and stained with crystal violet (1 g in 100 mL of distilled water) and basic
fuchsine (0.13 g in 100 mL of distilled water). Sections were then observed under a light microscope
(Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and images were captured using a Digital Sight 10 camera (Nikon, Tokyo,
Japan).
Additionally, from the same samples, ultrathin sections (70 nm) were also obtained and deposited on
300-mesh copper grids (Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy), counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate,
and examined using a JEOL1400Plus transmission electron microscope. Data were acquired using a
MORADA digital camera system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).
Histological Analyses through Masson’s trichrome staining
Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C, then washed in PBS buffer solution (138
mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 4.3 mM Na2HPO4 – pH 7.4). Tissues were then dehydrated with increasing
ethanol concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 96%, 100%), transferred to a 1:1 ethanol/xylol solution for
30 min, and cleared in pure xylol for 30 min. After an overnight incubation in a 1:1 xylol/paraffin solution
at 65°C, leech tissues were paraffin-embedded into moulds.
7 µm sections were obtained with a rotary microtome (Jung multicut 2045, Leica, Wein, Austria) and
processed for Masson’s Trichrome staining (Bio Optica, Milan, Italy). Samples were observed under a
light microscope (Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and images were acquired as described above.
Immunofluorescence Assays
Collected leech tissues were placed into moulds, previously filled with OCT (Tissue-Tek OCT compound,
Sakura Finetek, USA), then progressively frozen in dry ice, and stored at -80°C until use.
7 µm cryosections were obtained using a cryostat (CM1850, Leica, Wein, Austria), mounted on
gelatinised slides, and stored at -20°C.

Page 7/30

Cryosections were rehydrated for 10 min in PBS buffer and pre-incubated for 30 min with BSA blocking
solution (2% Bovine Serum Albumin, 0.1% Tween in PBS), which was also used to dilute both primary and
secondary antibodies. Samples were then incubated for 90 min with the primary antibody (Table 2) and,
after washings in PBS buffer, for 60 min with the suitable secondary antibody, conjugated with Cyanine3
(CY3, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA – dilution 1:500). Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6diamidino-2-phenylinedole (DAPI, 0.1 mg/mL in PBS) for 8 min, and the slides mounted with Citifluor
(Citifluor Ltd., UK).
Experimental negative controls were performed by incubating tissues with secondary antibodies only.
All samples were analysed using a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), equipped
with the emission filters 550/580 nm for CY3 and 340/488 nm for DAPI signals. Images were acquired
using a Digital Sight 10 camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and combined with Adobe Photoshop (Adobe
System, San Jose, USA). Immunofluorescence results were analysed using the ImageJ software
package (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html).
Molecular profiling by RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR)
Leech tissues were collected, instantly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C until use.
Samples were pestled with a mortar, and the obtained powder was resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol
reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA), centrifuged at 9,500×g for 10 min at 4°C, and the
supernatant incubated at room temperature for 5 min. After the addition of 200 µL of chloroform,
samples were centrifuged at 11,800×g for 15 min at 4°C, to allow phase separation. 500 µL of
supernatant were therefore collected and the same amount of isopropanol was added. After 15 min of
incubation in ice, samples were centrifuged at 11,800×g for 15 min at 4°C. 1 mL of 75% ethanol in DEPCtreated water was used to wash the RNA pellet. After a further centrifugation at 6,600×g for 10 min, the
obtained pellets were resuspended in DEPC water.
After Nanodrop (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) quantification, the purity of the samples was
evaluated on a 1% agarose gel. 2 µg of RNA were then retro-transcribed into cDNA by using the M-MLV
reverse transcriptase enzyme (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA). qPCR was then conducted in triplicate
with the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) using a 96-well CFX Connect
Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) and specific primers (Table 3). Gene
expression was normalized using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as reference
gene and the relative gene expression was calculated with the 2-ΔΔCt method.
Statistical analysis
Experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were reported as mean values ± standard deviation
(SD). The total fluorescent area was calculated by analysing ten different slides, using random fields of
45,000 μm² each, and analysed using the ImageJ software package (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html),
Page 8/30

while statistical evaluations were performed using Graph Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla,
USA; https://www.graphpad.com/features).
Significant differences were determined by two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value
lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the graphs, substantial differences between
the control and hDPSC-HS-treated groups are highlighted by asterisks as follows: * p<0.05, ** p<0.01, ***
p<0.001.

Results
ELISA characterization of hDPSC-HS
ELISA performed on hDPSC-HS obtained from the three different donors revealed inter-individual
variability in VEGF levels (Fig. 1). Nevertheless, wound-healing outcomes remained comparable among
the experimental groups.
Morphological analyses of hDPSC-HS in wound healing process
To evaluate pseudoblastema formation, ECM remodelling, and muscle regeneration, samples were
collected at 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week post-injury. The morphological evaluation of control (CTRL) and
hDPSC-HS treated leeches was conducted by light microscopy after staining with crystal violet and
fuchsine (Fig. 2a-f).
At 48 h post-injury (Fig. 2a, b), both groups showed early pseudoblastema formation at the wound
margins, a structure that acts as a temporary cellular plug that reduces fluid loss, promotes wound edge
closure, and provides a structural scaffold for subsequent regenerative events. However, in CTRL
samples (Fig. 2a), the pseudoblastema was poorly organized and embedded in a loose ECM surrounded
by botryoidal tissue, a hematopoietic component with myeloid and erythroid features that supports
angiogenesis. Differently, in hDPSC-HS-treated animals (Fig. 2b), it appeared more compact and
structurally organized, suggesting a more coordinated onset of the regenerative process.
At 72 h (Fig. 2c, d), structural differences became more evident; hDPSC-HS-injected leeches (Fig. 2d)
exhibited early formation of organized muscle fibres within the regenerating area and a more advanced
wound closure. Contrarywise, CTRL samples (Fig. 2c) were characterised by disorganised ECM and
active botryoidal tissue, with only minimal muscle development. At 1 week post-treatment (Fig. 2e, f), the
hDPSC-HS group (Fig. 2f) exhibited a marked reduction of the pseudoblastema, near-complete wound
closure, and well-organized muscle fibres arranged in a mature pattern. In contrast, CTRL animals (Fig.
2e) retained a broader wound area, persistent pseudoblastema, and underdeveloped muscle fibres,
indicating slower and less coordinated tissue repair.
hDPSC-HS accelerates myofibroblast-like cell activation during tissue regeneration

Page 9/30

To investigate the acquisition of a contractile phenotype during wound healing, immunofluorescence
assay for α-Smooth Muscle Actin (α-SMA) was performed. Distinct temporal patterns of α-SMA
expression were observed between groups. CTRL animals displayed weak staining at 48 h (Fig. 3a) and
72 h (Fig. 3c), reaching maximal fluorescence only at 1 week post-injury (Fig. 3e). In contrast, hDPSC-HStreated leeches showed higher α-SMA signal at 48 h and 72 h (Fig. 3b, d), which declined after 1 week
(Fig. 3f). Quantitative fluorescence analyses (Fig. 3i) confirmed a significantly greater α-SMA signal at
early timepoints in hDPSC-HS-treated samples compared with CTRL animals. Ultrastructural evaluation
by TEM supported these findings. In CTRL animals (Fig. 3g), lesions were largely populated by
vasocentral cells showing limited cytoplasmic differentiation, whereas hDPSC-HS-treated leeches (Fig.
3h) exhibited elongated cells with abundant cytoplasmic contractile filaments, consistent with a
differentiated myofibroblast-like phenotype and a more advanced regenerative process.
MyoD-dependent myogenic differentiation and muscle fibre maturation promoted by hDPSC-HS
To evaluate the myogenic regeneration program, immunofluorescence analyses for Myoblast
Determination protein (MyoD) was performed. MyoD is a key myogenic regulatory transcription factor
and an early marker of muscle precursor activation and commitment. Both groups exhibited a similar
trend, characterized by the progressive increase in MyoD expression over time, peaking at 1 week postinjury (Fig. 4a-f). However, fluorescence intensity was consistently greater in hDPSC-HS-treated leeches
at all timepoints, with a markedly stronger MyoD signal near the wound margin at 1 week (Fig. 4f),
suggesting accelerated activation and differentiation of muscle precursor cells. Quantitative
fluorescence analyses confirmed these data (Fig. 4i). Ultrastructural TEM analyses further supported
these findings. Wounds in CTRL samples (Fig. 4g) were predominantly surrounded by undifferentiated
blast-like cells with high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and poorly organized cytoplasm, whereas hDPSCHS-treated leeches (Fig. 4h) displayed numerous well-organized muscle fibres with evident sarcomeric
structures, consistent with advanced myogenic differentiation. In parallel, qPCR analyses of vegf
expression (Fig. 4j) revealed a significant reduction at 72 h and 1 week post-injury in the hDPSC-HS
group, whereas its level remained elevated in CTRLs. Indeed, in treated leeches, the reduction in vegf
expression levels correlated with increased MyoD-positive cells, further supporting an accelerated
regeneration process.
hDPSC-HS promotes coordinated ECM remodelling and advanced muscle fibre organization
To better evaluate ECM remodelling and tissue restoration during wound healing, Masson’s Trichrome
staining was also performed (Fig. 5a-f). At 48 h post-injury (Fig. 5a, b), wounds in both groups appeared
deep and were bordered by migrating botryoidal tissue. By 72 h, CTRL (Fig. 5c) still displayed a loose and
poorly structured ECM, whereas hDPSC-HS-treated leeches (Fig. 5d) exhibited numerous newly formed
muscle bundles adjacent to the wound, embedded within a more compact and organized collagenous
connective tissue, as evidenced by the more intense blue staining. At 1 week post-injury, CTRL animals
(Fig. 5e) retained a broad wound area with a prominent pseudoblastema, abundant and disorganized
collagen deposition, and sparse, poorly differentiated muscle fibres. In contrast, hDPSC-HS-treated
Page 10/30

animals (Fig. 5f) showed near-complete wound closure, reduced pseudoblastema size, and wellorganized muscle groups within a dense and structured collagen matrix. Ultrastructural TEM analyses
(Fig. 5g, h) confirmed that collagen fibres in hDPSC-HS-treated samples were more densely packed and
structurally organized compared with CTRL. Additionally, qPCR analyses of col3a1 and col1a2 (Fig. 5i, j)
supported these observations. Both groups showed early col3a1 upregulation, consistent with transient
ECM deposition. However, CTRL animals showed progressive col1a2 upregulation, peaking at 1 week
post-injury, whereas hDPSC-HS-treated leeches showed significantly lower col1a2 expression,
suggesting accelerated resolution of collagen deposition.
ERK and TGF-β signalling modulation by hDPSC-HS during wound repair
Given the central role of Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinases (ERK) in regulating cell proliferation,
migration, and ECM remodelling during tissue regeneration, we investigated whether hDPSC-HS
treatment influenced ERK activation dynamics during the wound-healing process. Immunofluorescence
analyses revealed a similar spatial distribution of ERK in both groups, predominantly localized in cells
surrounding the wound at all timepoints (Fig. 6a-f). While CTRL animals showed only a few ERK-positive
cells (Fig. 6a), hDPSC-HS-treated leeches already exhibited a stronger signal surrounding the wound area
(Fig. 6b). By 72 h, ERK signal increased in both groups (Fig. 6c, d), with hDPSC-HS-injected animals
maintaining significantly greater intensity. At 1 week, ERK fluorescence reached its peak in both groups
(Fig. 6e, f), yet remained consistently higher in hDPSC-HS-treated leeches, indicating sustained protein
accumulation throughout regeneration. Quantitative fluorescence analyses (Fig. 6g) showed a
progressive increase in ERK signal over time in both CTRL and treated animals, with the latter
consistently displaying significantly higher fluorescence. qPCR analyses of the erk transcript (Fig. 6h)
revealed that in CTRL animals, mRNA levels peaked at 72 h, whereas treated leeches showed only a
modest increase followed by stabilization. Moreover, both groups displayed reduced transcript levels by
1 week, suggesting a decoupling between ERK protein abundance and erk transcript levels in hDPSC-HStreated animals. tgf-β mRNA expression (Fig. 6i), a key regulator of myofibroblast differentiation and
ECM remodelling, peaked at 72 h in both groups. However, whereas CTRL animals maintained elevated
levels at 1 week, hDPSC-HS-injected leeches showed a decline, consistent with a more temporally
regulated response. This pattern suggests that hDPSC-HS enhances ERK-dependent signalling while
coordinating downstream pathway modulation, ultimately supporting a more controlled and efficient
regenerative process.
For all the immunofluorescence assays conducted, no signals were detected in control experiments, in
which the primary antibodies were omitted, confirming the specificity of the staining (Additional file 1
Data, Fig. 1S).

Discussion
This study provides mechanistic insight into the regenerative activity of hDPSC-HS using H. verbana as a
simplified in vivo platform. By integrating molecular, cellular, and ultrastructural analyses, we show that
Page 11/30

hDPSC-HS not only accelerates wound closure but also reshapes the temporal progression of tissue
repair. Rather than targeting a single phase, the cell secretome coordinates early activation, proliferative
expansion, and subsequent tissue organization through the combined action of multiple paracrine
mediators [11]. Furthermore, hDPSC-secretome produced under hypoxic conditions is enriched with proangiogenic and immunomodulatory factors, which further enhance its regenerative potential in the
wound healing process [22, 23, 29].
These findings align with the modern paracrine hypothesis, which attributes the beneficial effects in
wound healing to the cell secretome, comprising soluble factors and EVs, rather than to direct
engraftment or differentiation [7, 17, 18]. From a translational perspective, this evidence supports the
development of cell-free approaches capable of overcoming limitations inherent to live-cell therapies,
including poor engraftment, immunological barriers, and manufacturing constraints [19, 30].
In the leech, α-SMA serves as a specific marker for myofibroblast-like cells, which acquire contractile
properties essential for wound contraction and closure. We found that a prominent effect of hDPSC-HS
was a sustained increase in α-SMA expression across all analysed timepoints, indicating enhanced
activation of myofibroblast-like cells at the lesion site. These results are consistent with a more
advanced regenerative process in which myofibroblast-like cells have largely completed their contractile
activity as the wound approaches structural restoration. Furthermore, in leeches, vasocentral cells
acquire a contractile phenotype following injury, promoting wound contraction and provisional scaffold
formation [31, 32]. This process parallels the fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition in vertebrates, which
is largely regulated by TGF-β-dependent cytoskeletal remodelling [33, 34]. In treated animals, earlier and
more intense α-SMA positivity suggests accelerated acquisition of contractile function, improving early
mechanical stabilisation. Importantly, despite sustained activation, reduced late tgf-β expression
distinguishes this response from the persistent signalling associated with fibrotic scarring in mammals
[35, 36], supporting a regenerative rather than pathological outcome.
In parallel, hDPSC-HS enhanced myogenic commitment, as reflected by consistently elevated MyoD
fluorescent signal compared with controls. As a master regulator of muscle lineage specification [37],
sustained MyoD expression up to 1 week post-injury indicates prolonged engagement of the
differentiation program and an earlier transition from proliferation to structural maturation.
Ultrastructural evidence of organised sarcomeres in treated tissues, at stages when controls retained
undifferentiated blast-like cells, further supports accelerated myogenic progression, consistent with the
conserved responsiveness of muscle precursors to secretome-derived cues [38].
Growth factor dynamics reinforced this coordinated trajectory. VEGF, a key mediator of angiogenesis and
metabolic support during the proliferative phase [39], remained elevated for longer in controls, indicating
an extended reliance on proliferative programs. In contrast, its progressive downregulation in treated
animals coincided with enhanced differentiation and ECM remodelling, reflecting a timely shift toward
tissue maturation [14, 40, 41]. This pattern aligns with previous studies showing that in leeches, VEGF
downregulation is crucial for the transition from proliferation to differentiation [14, 40]. Despite the
Page 12/30

variability in VEGF levels among the secretomes from the three donors, similar wound-healing outcomes
were observed. This finding likely reflects the multifactorial regulation of tissue regeneration, which
depends on the coordinated and potentially synergistic action of multiple growth factors, resulting in
comparable biological effects.
ECM remodelling further distinguished treated from control animals. While control animals exhibited a
disorganised collagen network and persistent pseudoblastema, indicative of delayed maturation, hDPSCHS treatment promoted earlier deposition of a dense, organised collagen scaffold supporting nascent
muscle fibres. Both groups showed transient upregulation of type III collagen, consistent with provisional
matrix formation [42, 43]; however, only controls maintained elevated type I collagen expression at later
stages, suggesting prolonged remodelling and a shift toward fibrosis [44]. Attenuated late type I collagen
expression in treated animals instead indicates progression toward resolution. Such modulation of
collagen dynamics likely provides structural stability while delivering instructive biochemical cues that
sustain differentiation and functional recovery.
At the signalling level, the MAPK/ERK pathway, a central regulator of proliferation and differentiation [45,
46], exhibited sustained protein abundance in treated animals despite stabilised transcript levels,
whereas controls displayed an early transcriptional peak consistent with canonical proliferative
activation. This dissociation implies post-transcriptional regulation, potentially involving altered
translation or protein stability [47], and highlights the importance of signalling duration in determining
cellular outcomes [48, 49]. Concurrent attenuation of tgf-β transcription further supports a shift away
from sustained pro-fibrotic signalling [36, 50]. Together, these findings indicate that hDPSC-HS refines
signalling kinetics, promoting a balanced progression from proliferation to differentiation and ECM
remodelling.
Overall, the data support a model in which hDPSC-HS regulates tissue repair through the integrated
modulation of multiple regenerative processes [51]. The compositional complexity of hMSC-S, including
growth factors (e.g., VEGF, FGF-2), immunomodulatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β family members),
chemokines, and EV-associated microRNAs, likely reinforces these coordinated effects [17, 19, 30].
Comparable multifactorial signalling in mammalian systems has been linked to reduced inflammation,
enhanced angiogenesis, improved structural organisation, and limited fibrosis [52, 53], reinforcing the
translational relevance of our findings.
The use of H. verbana provides an evolutionarily informative yet experimentally tractable model. Unlike
vertebrate wound repair, characterised by complex vascular and multi-compartmental interactions, leech
regeneration occurs in a predominantly avascular environment in which specialised botryoidal and
vasocentral systems coordinate cell recruitment and matrix deposition [25, 54]. This relative simplicity
facilitates a direct correlation between molecular signalling and cellular outcomes while preserving the
conservation of key regenerative pathways, thereby strengthening cross-phyla interpretation.
Nevertheless, several limitations should still be acknowledged. The respective contributions of the
soluble fraction and EV components of hDPSC-HS were not independently evaluated. Dissecting their
Page 13/30

individual and potentially synergistic roles will refine mechanistic understanding and optimise
therapeutic translation. Comparative analyses of isolated fractions represent a logical next step toward
defining context-dependent effects and advancing secretome-based regenerative strategies. Future
studies should aim to identify the active secretome components and validate efficacy and safety in
vertebrate models to support clinical translation.

Conclusions
Although generated in an exploratory in vivo model, these findings demonstrate that hDPSC-HS
promotes coordinated tissue repair by modulating evolutionarily conserved regenerative mechanisms.
Because these biological pathways are shared with vertebrates and are critically involved in the
pathophysiology of chronic human wounds, the Hirudo verbana model provides biologically relevant
evidence supporting the translational potential of hDPSC-HS. These results provide a rationale for further
preclinical validation and clinical investigation of hDPSC-HS as a therapeutic strategy for chronic
wounds characterized by impaired healing.
