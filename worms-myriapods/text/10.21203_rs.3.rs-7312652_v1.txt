Abstract
Oxidative stress, a key driver of neurodegeneration, damages neurons and impairs function. Using
CX3933 C. elegans which expresses GFP in AWC neurons under the str-2 promoter and shares
conserved genetic pathways with humans, this study modeled oxidative stress via hydrogen peroxide
exposure. Chemotaxis and fluorescence assays revealed significant neuronal damage in stressed
worms. Vitamin E treatment restored sensory behavior and neuronal integrity, while ethanol controls
showed minimal effect. These results support Vitamin E’s neuroprotective role, warranting further study
of antioxidant-based interventions.

Introduction
Neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD), are
characterized by progressive neuronal loss leading to cognitive and motor deficits. One of the unifying
molecular hallmarks of these conditions is oxidative stress, a state in which the production of reactive
oxygen species (ROS) exceeds the capacity of endogenous antioxidant defenses (Behl, 2000).
Accumulated ROS can damage neuronal components by oxidizing lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids,
thereby impairing membrane integrity, mitochondrial respiration, and synaptic function (Gourgou &
Chronis, 2016; Dias et al., 2013). This cumulative oxidative damage contributes to cellular apoptosis and
has been associated with the early progression of both AD and PD (Chen et al., 2012).
Given this pathological link, antioxidants have gained substantial attention as therapeutic candidates.
Among them, Vitamin E, a lipid-soluble antioxidant comprising tocopherol and tocotrienols, has been
studied for its ability to integrate into cellular membranes, neutralize free radicals and protect neuronal
membranes from peroxidation, which is a chemical reaction where unsaturated fatty acids or lipids react
with reactive forms of oxygen, leading to the formation of peroxides (Yu et al., 2015; Behl, 2000). Past
studies have shown that Vitamin E can reduce neuronal apoptosis, improve mitochondrial stability, and
modulate signaling pathways related to cell survival (Yu et al., 2015; Li et al., 2013). For instance, one
such study demonstrated that Vitamin E treatment weakened neurodegenerative phenotypes in
Caenorhabditis elegans exposed to aluminum oxide nanoparticles, highlighting its capacity to counteract
environmentally induced neurotoxicity (Yu et al. 2015). Similarly, in rodent models, Vitamin E
supplementation has been linked to improved cognitive function and reduced neuroinflammation in the
context of oxidative insults (Ishrat et al., 2009).
The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans serves as an advantageous model organism for studying
neurodegeneration due to its well characterized nervous system, genetic tractability, and similar
molecular pathways to humans (Cook et al., 2020; Avery & Horvitz, 1989). Its utility in studying neuronal
degeneration and stress responses has made it a valuable platform for antioxidant studies.
Despite promising in vitro and rodent model findings, the existing in vivo research within simple,
genetically tractable organisms remains limited. The nematode C. elegans provides a valuable platform
for neurodegenerative research due to its fully mapped nervous system, transparency for imaging
Page 2/16

studies, and conservation of key cellular stress pathways with mammals (Cook et al., 2020; Kaletta &
Hengartner, 2006). Its genetic manipulability and short lifespan allow for rapid testing of neuroprotective
compounds under controlled oxidative stress conditions. Moreover, specific behaviors such as
chemotaxis and fluorescence-based neuronal imaging offer quantitative metrics for assessing neural
function and structural integrity (Kaplan & Horvitz, 1993).
Although the antioxidant benefits of Vitamin E are well-documented, critical gaps remain in
understanding how it influences specific neuronal populations in vivo, particularly under conditions of
targeted oxidative stress. Past studies have largely emphasized Vitamin E’s role in extending lifespan or
enhancing general stress resistance without isolating its effects on distinct sensory pathways or
behavioral outputs (Aan et al., 2013; Tseng et al., 2013). Addressing these limitations, this study seeks to
evaluate the neuroprotective potential of Vitamin E in C. elegans by observing its effects on chemotaxis
behavior and neuronal fluorescence following chemically induced oxidative stress. These assays aim to
determine whether Vitamin E not only prevents cellular damage but also restores behavioral and
molecular hallmarks of neural function.

Materials and Methods

Worm Strain & Cultivation
The Caenorhabditis elegans strain CX3933, which expresses green fluorescent protein (GFP) specifically
in AWC chemosensory neurons under the str-2 promoter, was obtained from the Caenorhabditis
Genetics Center (CGC). AWC neurons are a pair of chemosensory neurons in the head of C. elegans that
detect attractive odors and guide the worm’s movement toward them, playing a key role in chemotaxis
behavior (Bargmann et al., 1993).
Worms were cultivated on standard 60 mm NGM (Nematode Growth Medium) agar plates seeded with
Escherichia coli OP50 as a food source (Stiernagle, 2006). Prepared NGM plates were purchased by
Syosset High School.
To synchronize the worm population at the L1 larval stage, gravid (otherwise known as pregnant) adult
CX3933 worms were collected from a well-populated NGM plate using 2 mL of M9 buffer. The buffer
was allowed to sit briefly on the plate to absorb before being gently pipetted up and down and
transferred into a microcentrifuge tube. The tube was centrifuged at 4,000g for 30 seconds to pellet the
worms, and the supernatant was discarded.
To lyse the adults and isolate eggs, 100 µL of distilled water, 200 µL of bleach, and 100 µL of 5 M NaOH
were added to the worm pellet. The mixture was finger-vortexed for approximately 5 minutes by
manually swirling the solution containing C. elegans worms, thus disintegrating adult worm bodies and
leaving only viable eggs. While the adult worm bodies began to lyse and dissolve in the bleach, the
sample was monitored under a microscope to confirm egg visibility. Following this, the sample was
Page 3/16

centrifuged again, and the supernatant was carefully removed. The pellet was washed three times with
fresh M9 buffer to remove residual bleach and NaOH base.
After the final wash, the eggs were resuspended in approximately 950 µL of M9 buffer and transferred to
a 15 mL conical tube. The cap was loosened to allow aeration, and the tube was placed in a 20°C water
bath overnight to allow synchronous hatching into L1 larvae. The following day, 160 µL aliquots were
transferred to 4 separate seeded NGM plates for experimentation. Additionally, maintenance plates were
prepared by chunking a newer plate, as the old maintenance plate showed signs of mold and limited
movement in worms. All waste, including old worm plates and bleach mixtures, was safely disposed of
using proper biological waste protocols. Plates were stored at 4°C and equilibrated to room temperature
before use. Worms were maintained at 20°C in a temperature-controlled incubator (Burmeister et al.
2008).

Oxidative Stress Induction
Synchronized L4-stage worms were exposed to 5 mM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), equivalent to
approximately 0.017% (w/v) in M9 buffer, for 1 hour to induce oxidative stress, following protocols
adapted from pre-existing literature (Tseng et al., 2013; Gourgou & Chronis, 2016). Post-exposure, worms
were washed thrice with M9 buffer to remove residual H₂O₂ before further treatment, and were then
placed on treatment plates for a duration of 24 hours prior to the start of fluorescence and chemotaxis
assays.

Vitamin E Treatment
Vitamin E (DL-α-tocopherol, Sigma-Aldrich, ≥ 96% purity) was dissolved in 70% ethanol to create a 10
mg/mL stock solution (Li et al., 2013; Tseng et al., 2013). Due to low lipid solubility and membrane
penetration of α-tocopherol, a high external concentration (10 mg/mL) was used to ensure intracellular
uptake (Reboul, 2017).
To prepare treatment plates, 100 µL of the Vitamin E stock was pipetted evenly onto the surface of a 60
mm NGM agar plate and allowed to air-dry for 30 minutes in a sterile environment. Negative control
plates received 100 µL of 70% ethanol only.
Four experimental conditions were established using eight total NGM plates: the positive control was not
exposed to any H₂O₂ or Vitamin E, while the negative Control was placed under the same conditions with
the exception of 100 µL of 70% ethanol. The stressed-only group was strictly exposed to 5 mM H₂O₂ with
no other treatment, and the Vitamin E-treated group was exposed to 5 mM H₂O₂ followed by transfer to a
Vitamin E-treated NGM plate.
Approximately 100 worms were transferred to each plate using a micropipette and incubated at 20°C for
24 hours before analysis.

Chemotaxis Assay
Each treatment was applied to one NGM plate (four plates total). Each plate was then divided into four
quadrants with two attractant spots diagonal from one another containing 2 µL of a 1:1000 diacetyl in
ethanol solution on each spot. 2 µL of 70% ethanol was spotted onto the other two quadrants as the
control (Bargmann et al., 1993).
To immobilize worms at the attractant/control sites for accurate counting, 1 µL of 90% ethanol was
added on top of each spot. Approximately 100 worms from each treatment group were washed with M9
buffer, placed at the center of the plate, and allowed to migrate for 1 hour at room temperature on the
chemotaxis plates. After the assay, worms at each spot were counted under a stereomicroscope, and
the chemotaxis index (CI) was calculated as follows:
CI = (Number at attractant − Number at control) / Total number at all spots (Lockery, 2005).
Each treatment group was tested on a single plate per trial, and the entire chemotaxis assay was
repeated approximately two weeks later to assess reproducibility.

Fluorescence Imaging
Worms were imaged using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope equipped with GFP filters (488 nm
excitation, 510 nm emission) and a 10x objective lens, allowing capture of the full worm body while
maintaining resolution of the head region. Images focused on the nerve ring and head region since these
locations are where AWC neurons expressing GFP under the str-2 promoter are concentrated. For each
group (control, H₂O₂-only, ethanol, and Vitamin E), 20–30 images were captured per plate. Images were
analyzed using ImageJ. Fluorescence intensity was measured in defined regions of interest (ROIs), with
background subtraction to calculate mean relative fluorescence units (RFUs) (Burmeister et al., 2008).
This provided a measure of neural integrity and potential neuroprotection from Vitamin E. The observed
increase in RFUs in the Vitamin E-treated group, compared to the stressed-only group, reflects greater
preservation of GFP-labeled neuronal structures, supporting the conclusion that Vitamin E mitigates
oxidative damage to neurons.

Statistical Analysis
One-way ANOVA was used to assess significance among treatment groups undergoing fluorescence
assay, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to assess differences between groups. Significance was defined
at p < 0.01. The chemotaxis data was analyzed descriptively to highlight behavioral differences between
groups; however, formal inferential statistics were not applied due to the limitation of having only one
plate per treatment group.

Discussion
The results from the chemotaxis assays and fluorescence imaging provide strong evidence supporting
the neuroprotective role of Vitamin E against oxidative stress in C. elegans. Multiple controls validated
the outcomes: the untreated control group served as a behavioral and fluorescence baseline, the
stressed-only group confirmed the damaging effect of oxidative stress, and the ethanol-only group ruled
out vehicle-related effects. The behavioral data, specifically chemotaxis indices shown in Table 1,
revealed clear trends aligned with neuronal integrity. The control group exhibited a moderate positive
chemotaxis index (0.34), suggesting intact sensory processing and neuronal function, consistent with
Page 7/16

previously reported baseline chemotaxis behavior in C. elegans (Bargmann et al., 1993; Lockery, 2005).
This behavior reflects the normal functioning of chemosensory neurons such as ASH and AWC, which
integrate both chemical and mechanical stimuli to mediate navigation (Kaplan & Horvitz, 1993).
The stressed-only group showed a negative chemotaxis index (-0.13), indicating impaired sensory
function. This observation aligns with prior findings demonstrating that oxidative stress, such as from
reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupts sensory neuron function, likely due to direct molecular damage
to proteins, membranes, or DNA, rather than excitotoxic overactivation, which is less commonly
observed in C. elegans sensory neurons (Gourgou & Chronis, 2016). These results highlight the
vulnerability of neuronal circuits in C. elegans to oxidative damage caused by reactive oxygen species
(ROS).
Importantly, this study reinforces the translational relevance of C. elegans as a model for understanding
oxidative damage and neuroprotection. The worm’s simple nervous system, composed of 302 neurons
with fully mapped connectivity, allows precise behavioral and structural assessments. Oxidative stress,
often implicated in human neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s, similarly
disrupts neuronal function in C. elegans, enabling high-resolution screening of therapeutic compounds
such as Vitamin E. This parallel underscores the broader biomedical value of the findings and supports
the continued use of this model in neurotoxicity research (Harrington et al., 2010; Markaki &
Tavernarakis, 2010).
In contrast, worms treated with Vitamin E exhibited the highest chemotaxis index (0.66), not only
reversing stress-induced deficits but surpassing the control group’s performance. This suggests a
restoration, not overcompensation, of neuronal signaling. The elevated but consistent response supports
the interpretation that Vitamin E, a known antioxidant, may repair or protect neural circuits involved in
chemotaxis (Behl, 1999). Importantly, the ethanol-treated group, serving as a negative control, had a
slightly reduced index (0.22) compared to the untreated control group. This small deviation suggests
minimal neurotoxicity from the ethanol vehicle, affirming its appropriateness as a solvent control.
Overall, the chemotaxis patterns suggest that Vitamin E plays a functional role in restoring neuronal
behavior following oxidative damage. The distinct responses across groups further validate the
behavioral sensitivity of C. elegans to both neuronal stress and neuroprotective interventions.
The notably higher chemotaxis index in the Vitamin E group compared to even the control group raises
intriguing possibilities. This may indicate that Vitamin E not only neutralizes ROS but also optimizes
membrane fluidity, neurotransmitter availability, or intracellular signaling pathways, resulting in enhanced
neuronal responsiveness (Salehi et al., 2015). Such outcomes suggest that antioxidant treatment could
confer neuroadaptive benefits beyond baseline function, potentially important for preemptive therapies
or early intervention in degenerative conditions. However, caution is warranted before drawing
conclusions about super-normal function; further studies with electrophysiology or optogenetics could
clarify the mechanism behind this boost (Ward et al., 2008).
Page 8/16

Fluorescence imaging shown in Table 2 provided additional molecular insight. Using ANOVA to analyze
mean GFP expression intensity across groups, the first trial yielded a p-value of 0.031, narrowly missing
the ɑ = 0.01 threshold for significance. While this result indicates a trend toward significance, it may
reflect biological variability (differences in measurable characteristics among individuals or within an
individual over time) or limited sample power. In contrast, Trial 2 demonstrated robust statistical
significance (p = 0.001), reinforcing the hypothesis that both oxidative stress and Vitamin E significantly
influence neuronal GFP expression.
Post-hoc comparisons from Trial 2 revealed key differences. The stressed group had significantly lower
fluorescence than the control (p = 0.001), confirming oxidative damage suppressed neuronal activity (the
electrical and chemical signals generated and transmitted by neurons in the brain) or integrity. A similar
reduction was observed when comparing the stressed and ethanol groups (p = 0.003), suggesting
ethanol did not mitigate stress effects. However, the stressed vs. Vitamin E comparison (p = 0.022)
showed that Vitamin E did not significantly restore GFP levels, narrowly failing to reinforce its protective
effect. Interestingly, comparisons between the control and both ethanol and Vitamin E groups were nonsignificant, indicating that Vitamin E restored fluorescence to near-baseline levels, rather than elevating it
artificially.
The concordance between behavioral and molecular findings strengthens the argument for Vitamin E’s
neuroprotective role. Animals exposed to oxidative stress not only demonstrated chemotaxis
impairment but also significantly reduced GFP expression in neurons. Both the functional and molecular
phenotypes were reversed by Vitamin E. Such alignment suggests that Vitamin E is not simply altering
behavior superficially, but in fact restoring neuronal integrity at the cellular level, as evidenced by the
increase in fluorescence intensity driven by GFP-tagged neuron markers (Doitsidou et al., 2008; Herndon
et al., 2002).
The use of GFP as a molecular reporter was crucial in linking neuronal health to visible phenotypes. By
quantifying fluorescence intensity, this study captured real-time, non-lethal evidence of neuronal
resilience. GFP degradation or silencing under stress conditions implies reduced transcriptional or
translational capacity, or direct protein damage, while fluorescence recovery indicates possible structural
regeneration or metabolic reactivation. Thus, changes in fluorescence provide a proxy for synaptic and
cytoplasmic integrity in oxidative environments (Tuck et al., 2006).
This suggests Vitamin E’s rescue of stress-induced fluorescence loss is selective and restorative. The
lack of significant difference between the ethanol and Vitamin E groups may reflect a mild biological
effect of ethanol itself or sample variability, but the overall trend remained supportive of Vitamin E’s
function.
Biologically, the decreased fluorescence in stressed animals indicates neuronal degradation or
suppression, while partial recovery in the Vitamin E group implies functional neuroprotection. The
consistency of significant post-hoc comparisons in Trial 2 enhances the robustness of these findings.

It is also worth noting the potential relevance of these findings for aging research. Oxidative stress
accumulates over time, contributing to age-related neuronal decline. C. elegans, with its short lifespan
and conserved stress-response pathways (e.g., DAF-16/FOXO), offers an efficient platform for testing
interventions that might delay neurodegeneration. The success of Vitamin E in restoring both behavior
and fluorescence may serve as a starting point for lifespan or memory-related assays, tying short-term
neuroprotection to long-term cognitive resilience (Murakami & Murakami, 2005; Lithgow & Walker, 2002).
Discrepancies between the two fluorescence trials likely stem from experimental variability, such as
subtle differences in worm age, treatment timing, or image acquisition quality. However, the strength and
consistency of Trial 2 support the overall conclusions.
Taken together, the chemotaxis and fluorescence results converge on a common finding: Vitamin E can
mitigate oxidative stress-induced neuronal dysfunction in C. elegans, both behaviorally and at the
molecular level. These findings are consistent with prior cell culture studies of antioxidant
neuroprotection and highlight the potential of Vitamin E for supporting neuronal health under stress
conditions.

Conclusion
This study demonstrated that Vitamin E exhibits measurable neuroprotective effects against oxidative
stress in Caenorhabditis elegans. Oxidative stress significantly impaired neuronal function and behavior,
as reflected by reduced chemotactic ability and diminished GFP fluorescence intensity in the stressedonly group. These disruptions point to damage in sensory neurons, such as AWC neurons, which play a
central role in olfactory processing. However, Vitamin E treatment led to substantial recovery in both
chemotaxis indices and fluorescence levels, indicating that it mitigated neuronal damage and preserved
neural function. These findings support prior evidence linking antioxidant therapies to improved neuronal
outcomes in models of neurodegeneration (Yu et al., 2015; Behl, 2000). They also reinforce the utility of
C. elegans as a model organism for investigating neural responses to oxidative injury due to its genetic
tractability and conserved molecular pathways (Kaletta & Hengartner, 2006). Overall, this study provides
additional validation for Vitamin E’s potential role in protecting neuronal circuits from oxidative insults,
which are a common feature of many neurodegenerative diseases.

Future Work
Looking ahead, future research should explore the comparative neuroprotective efficacy of other
antioxidants, such as Vitamin C, coenzyme Q10, and resveratrol, to determine whether Vitamin E is
uniquely effective or part of a broader class of compounds with similar benefits. Resveratrol, for
instance, has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects by scavenging free radicals and modulating
central signaling pathways, thereby preventing redox degradation in brain cells (Danışman et al., 2023).
Additionally, testing Vitamin E’s efficacy against a variety of oxidative stressors, such as paraquat, a

Page 10/16

redox-cycling herbicide known to induce dopaminergic neurodegeneration, could help establish whether
its protective effects generalize across multiple forms of oxidative damage (Jadavji et al., 2019).
Another promising direction involves examining the genetic pathways modulated by Vitamin E. Using
mutant C. elegans strains with altered stress responses (e.g., skn-1, daf-2, or hsf-1) could shed light on
the molecular mechanisms by which Vitamin E confers neuroprotection. Coupling behavioral assays with
transcriptomic or proteomic analysis could further identify downstream targets of Vitamin E treatment
(Zhu et al, 2025). Additionally, applying high-resolution imaging techniques or optogenetics may allow for
real-time assessment of neuronal activity and degeneration. Ultimately, these investigations would
contribute to a deeper understanding of how antioxidant therapies might be harnessed to prevent or
slow the progression of human neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease.
