# Enhancing Reproducibility in Chemotaxis Assays for Caenorhabditis elegans

## Abstract
AbstractThe ability of Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) to navigate complex environments is essential for their survival. This natural behavior is commonly used in chemotaxis assays, which are important tools for studying the function of sensory neurons and neural circuits. Chemotaxis has been essential for discovering fundamental functions in neuronal signaling during the past decades. However, a lack of thoroughly optimized and standardized procedures can lead to variable results that can be difficult to interpret. To improve reproducibility, we optimized several aspects of chemotaxis protocols by testing different odorant concentrations, numbers of worms, and assay durations, as well as the preparation of chemotaxis plates and the washing procedures of worms. The usage of a 2‐choice or a 4‐choice assay was also evaluated. Our new protocol improves the clarity of results and simplifies worm counting. The protocol optimization is condensed into a 5‐day step‐by‐step protocol that increases the reproducibility of chemotaxis in C. elegans. Compared to previously published chemotaxis protocols, the revised method reduces day‐to‐day variability using an improved and standardized assay design that ensures clear and reliable results. Several key components in the assay preparation and during the assay have been evaluated based on previous protocols, such as odor concentration, worm density, and assay length. By considering multiple factors that influence the worm's behavior, our optimized protocol enhances the reproducibility of chemotaxis assays in C. elegans, making them more reliable and accessible for studying phenotypes related to olfaction and neural circuit behavior. © 2025 The Author(s). ## INTRODUCTION
Chemotaxis is an extensively studied behavior that serves as a powerful tool for investigating olfactory receptor function across a range of species, from bacteria (Wadhams & Armitage, 2004) to mammals (Roussos et al., 2011; Teves et al., 2009). It provides an ideal framework for exploring sensory integration and signal transduction within neuronal circuits. The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) is a leading model organism for the study of chemotaxis due to its minimal and well‐characterized nervous system, as well as its robust behavioral responses to chemical stimuli (Bargmann, 2006; Bargmann & Mori, 1997; Meneely et al., 2019). C. elegans thrives in soil‐like environments rich in microbial life and decomposing organic matter, including rotting fruits and decaying plants. Within these biologically active ecosystems, C. elegans interacts with diverse microbial populations that serve multiple roles, acting as direct food sources, components of a symbiotic gut microbiota, parasites, pathogens, and potential competitors (Schulenburg & Felix, 2017). This ecological context necessitates the ability of these animals to distinguish between different water‐soluble and volatile odors, many of which are natural products of bacterial metabolism.
To achieve this sensitive and effective distinction, C. elegans relies on a set of 12 amphid chemosensory neuron pairs (Zhang et al., 2014). These neurons express a wide array of olfactory receptors that enable them to differentiate among chemical cues (Li & Liberles, 2015). Notably, these receptors are particularly effective in their detection of volatile odors, which can be sensed at nanomolar (Bargmann et al., 1993) concentrations, in contrast to water‐soluble odors that typically require detection at micromolar concentrations (Bargmann, 2006; Bargmann et al., 1993).
Out of the five pairs of neurons involved in olfaction, the AWC and AWA neurons are the two pairs of amphid sensory neurons essential for chemotaxis toward attractive volatile odors. AWC neurons detect odorants like butanone and isoamyl alcohol, while AWA recognizes the commonly studied odorant diacetyl as well as pyrazine (Bargmann, 2006; Lans et al., 2004). In contrast, the AWB neurons are sensitive to the repulsive odor of 2‐nonanone at high concentrations (Troemel et al., 1997). The G‐protein‐coupled receptor ODR‐10, expressed in AWA neurons, plays a crucial role in mediating attraction to diacetyl. Interestingly, when ODR‐10 is expressed in AWB neurons, diacetyl becomes repulsive, whereas its expression in AWC neurons makes diacetyl attractive. These findings suggest that each sensory neuron is wired to elicit a specific behavioral response; AWA and AWC are associated with attraction, while AWB neurons are linked to repulsion (Matsuura et al., 2013; Troemel et al., 1997). In addition to AWB, the ASH and ADL neurons also contribute to aversive behavior, which is characterized by rapid withdrawal rather than chemotaxis (Zhang et al., 2014).
The knowledge outlined above has been instrumental in exploring various neurobiological questions, including receptor function (Sengupta et al., 1996), neurotransmitter signaling (Hardege et al., 2022), and pathogen avoidance (Zhang et al., 2005). The method of choice has typically been chemotaxis assays, a technique first employed in the 1970s. Early studies focused on observing the movement of C. elegans in response to various substances, such as cyclic nucleotides and ions, by tracking their migration toward or away from chemical gradients. This approach provided early insights into chemotactic behavior (Bargmann et al., 1993; Queirós et al., 2021; Ward, 1973). The assays have been continuously refined, particularly by adapting the setup for volatile substances. These improvements have allowed for more precise measurement of attraction or repulsion responses (Nuttley et al., 2001; Queirós et al., 2021; Troemel et al., 1997). Typically, the assay is performed by placing an attractive or aversive odorant on one side of a chemotaxis plate and a control odorant on the other side. The worms are synchronized and washed before the assay and then placed at a distance equal to that between the test odorant and the control. The worms are then left to move freely on the plate for a designated period. If camera tracking is not used, the worms are immobilized and counted after the assay (Margie et al., 2013; Queirós et al., 2021).
Despite years of optimization, working with chemotaxis assays remains challenging. Reproducibility can be difficult to achieve due to the potential variations in experimental conditions, which might manifest as a large day‐to‐day variation. Factors such as the age and health of the worms, the number of worms used, the chemical purity of attractants or repellents, and environmental conditions like temperature and humidity can impact the outcome (Glenn et al., 2004; Margie et al., 2013; Queirós et al., 2021; Raiders et al., 2022) (Fig. 1). Queirós et al. reviewed how different factors impact the results and what to consider for choosing the most appropriate approach for your specific experiment. Several of these factors have been considered in this protocol. Another challenge arises from the wide range of odorant concentrations used in chemotaxis assays. It has been shown that at lower concentrations, typically attractive odorants induce positive chemotaxis (Pu et al., 2023). However, as the concentration of these odorants increases, the response can shift from attraction to repulsion (Yoshida et al., 2012). Moreover, different concentrations of odorants activate distinct sets of chemoreceptors, allowing the worms to detect and adapt to various environmental cues. Therefore, evaluating different odorant concentrations is crucial when using chemotaxis assays, as even slight variations can alter the behavioral outcome (Pu et al., 2023).

Herein, we present an updated and improved protocol for chemotaxis assays in C. elegans, addressing several challenges that have previously led to discrepancies in the obtained results. We started our optimization from previously published protocols (Margie et al., 2013; Queirós et al., 2021). By optimizing key aspects of these protocols, including the preparation of chemotaxis plates, the number of worms used, the concentration of odorants, and the selection between 2‐choice and 4‐choice assays, we have established a robust routine that significantly enhances reproducibility. The optimized protocol is designed to produce more consistent and reliable measurements of chemotactic behavior, thereby offering a powerful tool for studying sensory neuron function and olfactory receptor signaling in C. elegans.

## OPTIMIZED CHEMOTAXIS ASSAY FOR C. elegans
This protocol describes an optimized chemotaxis assay to evaluate the responses of C. elegans to chemical cues. The protocol features a streamlined 5‐day workflow, using a 2‐choice assay format and improved washing/handling steps to minimize variability. When performed correctly, it is possible to observe clear and consistent chemotactic behavior, providing a robust tool for studying olfactory function, sensory neurons, and neural circuits in C. elegans.
The protocol described is for performing an assay with 10 CTX plates, which is the recommended number of plates to use for each strain/day. CTX medium is a defined minimal medium optimized for C. elegans chemotaxis assays (Bargmann et al., 1993). Figure 2 shows the graphical workflow.

## Day 1

## Preparations
Prepare 10 CTX plates (9 cm) with 25 mL media per plate.
Plate humidity can have a significant impact on the outcome of the assay; therefore, it is important to maintain consistency in plate age when running replicates. In this protocol, plates were allowed to dry in a laminar flow hood prior to use on the day of the assay to ensure optimal dryness for the assay. It is not recommended to prepare large batches of plates for long‐term storage and subsequent use on different assay days, as this may introduce variability and compromise reproducibility. Each laminar flow hood will dry the plates in a unique way, and it is important to maintain consistency between experiments. In this protocol, we allowed the plates to dry for 4 days, and after this, they were not used for chemotaxis assay purposes.
Prepare 10 NGM plates (6 cm) with 10 mL media per plate.
Transfer 10 × 5 L4 worms to previously seeded NGM plates to have enough L4s to pick on Day 4.

## Day 3

## Preparations
Seed 10 NGM plates with 100 µL OP50 (OD600 = 1)/plate and store at room temperature overnight.
Seeding the plates the day before with an exact volume prevents a very thick bacterial lawn, which might make the washing of bacteria harder on Day 5. It also increases consistency between plates.

## Day 4

## Preparations
Transfer 60‐70 L4s from Day 1 plates to new NGM plates seeded on Day 3.
The worms are transferred using a standard worm pick. As this procedure is performed one day prior to the assay, the worms have sufficient time to recover from any stress or damage caused during the transfer. Although this method is time‐consuming, it ensures both an accurate number of worms and a very accurate judgment of the worm age.

## Day 5

## Preparations
Wash the worms from each plate with 2 mL M9 media into a 1.5‐mL tube (this volume fits exactly into a 1.5‐mL tube as some liquid is absorbed by the plate). Use a glass pipette to transfer the worms to reduce the risk of damaging the worms.
Let the worms settle for 5 min and aspirate the supernatant without disturbing the worm pellet. Letting the worms settle only by gravity is preferred to centrifugation as it is gentler on the worms.
Wash worms with 1 mL of M9 media and repeat the previous step.
Wash at least four times in M9 media to ensure very little to no bacteria are left.
Label the bottom of the assay plates according to Figure 2 during the waiting times, marking the areas where odorants and worms are placed.
During the second to last settling time of worm washing, prepare the odorant mixture: For the test odor, dilute diacetyl 1:2000 (0.05% v/v) in 96% ethanol, and then mix this solution with 1 M Na azide (1:1).For the control odor, mix 96% ethanol with 1 M Na azide (1:1).
For the test odor, dilute diacetyl 1:2000 (0.05% v/v) in 96% ethanol, and then mix this solution with 1 M Na azide (1:1).
For the control odor, mix 96% ethanol with 1 M Na azide (1:1).
Add 2 µL each of the test and control odors to the CTX plates during the last settling time of worm washing. Make sure to always keep the lid on the plates.
In this protocol, we wash the worms four times in total for 20 min; this reduces the risk of clumping, as variations in the number of bacteria have been noted to interfere with the assay as worms tend to remain in the center of the plate due to residual OP50. However, it is crucial that this step does not exceed 20 min to avoid starvation of the worms.

## Assay
Aspirate the M9 media up to approximately 100 µL without disturbing the worm pellet.
Add the worm pellet with a glass pipette to the center of the assay plate and remove excess liquid with filter paper.
Run the assay for 60‐90 min without turning the plates around.
Store the plates in the fridge until ready to count.

## REAGENTS AND SOLUTIONS

## CaCl2, 1 M
Prepare 7.35 g CaCl2•2H2O (Merck, cat. no. 102382) in 50 mL ddH2O and sterile filter into an autoclaved bottle.

## CTX plate media
Prepare 20 g agar (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 443570010) in 1 L ddH2O and autoclave. Let it cool on a magnet stirrer until 55°C. Add 5 mL KPO4 buffer, 1 mL 1 M CaCl2, and 1 mL 1 M MgSO4 and mix until evenly distributed. Add 25 mL per plate.

## KPO4 buffer
Prepare 108.3 g KH2PO4 (Sigma‐Aldrich, cat. no. 104871) and 35.6 g K2HPO4 (Sigma‐Aldrich, cat. no. 105101) and make up to 1 L with ddH2O, pH 6.0; sterilize by autoclaving.

## MgSO4, 1 M
Prepare 12.3 g MgSO4•7H2O (Sigma‐Aldrich, cat. no. 105886) in 50 mL ddH2O and sterile filter into an autoclaved bottle.

## M9 media
Prepare 3 g KH2PO4 (Sigma‐Aldrich, cat. no. 105104), 6 g Na2HPO4 (Merck, cat. no. 106586), 5 g NaCl (Fisher Scientific, cat. no. S/3160/65), and 1 mL 1 M MgSO₄ in 1 L ddH2O and autoclave.

## Na azide, 1 M
Prepare 650 mg NaN3 (Merck, cat. no. S2002) in 10 mL ddH2O.

## NGM plate media
Prepare 18 g agar (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 443570010), 2.5 g Bacto Peptone (BD, cat. no. 211677), and 3 g NaCl (Fisher Scientific, cat. no. S/3160/65) in 1 L ddH2O and autoclave. Let it cool on a magnet stirrer until 55°C. Add 5 mL KPO4 buffer, 1 mL 1 M CaCl2, 1 mL 1 M MgSO4, and 1 mL 5 mg/mL cholesterol (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 110191000) in 96% ethanol (Solveco, cat. no. 6097096); mix until evenly distributed. Add 25 mL per plate.

## OP50 liquid culture
Fresh OP50 cultures are routinely prepared by picking a single colony from a streaked agar plate and growing it overnight in 20 mL 2×YT Media (Sigma, cat. no. Y1003) at 37°C, 80 rpm in a 50‐mL tube. If not used the following day, the culture can be stored at 4°C for up to 1 week.

## COMMENTARY

## Critical Parameters
The following observations and optimizations were made while performing chemotaxis assays comparing adult N2 worms as well as mutant worms over several months. The starting protocol was partially adapted from earlier studies (Margie et al., 2013; Bargmann et al., 1993). During extensive chemotaxis testing, it was noted that maintaining consistency in every detail, such as plate age and humidity, as well as the choice of pipettes, odor concentration, and worm density, was crucial. The protocol from Margie et al. (Margie et al., 2013) was chosen for its simplicity and accessibility, making it particularly suitable for laboratories that do not have the specialized equipment required for newer methods, such as microfluidic chip–based assays (Fryer et al., 2024) or high‐throughput analysis methods (Yu et al., 2019). This approach ensures that the assay can be reliably performed in standard laboratory settings without the need for extensive infrastructure. These insights led to the development of a precise protocol that ensures consistent quality of the assay.
On Day 1, NGM and CTX plates are prepared and dried in a sterile hood to ensure that the humidity level is the same for each experiment. Simultaneously, on Day 1, 5 L4 larvae (per plate) are transferred onto 10 OP50 seeded plates and stored at 20°C to produce enough L4 larvae for the picking day (Day 4). On Day 3, the NGM plates prepared on Day 1 are seeded with 100 µL of OP50 with an OD600 = 1. This step is crucial to standardize the wash procedure on Day 5 before the start of the assay, as variations in bacterial amount have been noted to cause inconsistencies in the wash procedure and subsequently interfere with the assay, as worms tend to remain in the center of the plate due to residual OP50. However, starvation and extensive washing can significantly alter the behavior of C. elegans, affecting locomotion, turning frequency, and chemosensory neuron sensitivity, which are critical for chemotaxis assays (Baugh & Hu, 2020). To preserve assay accuracy, worms should be starved for the shortest possible time to limit starvation‐induced behavioral changes while still ensuring responsiveness to chemical cues. The plates seeded on Day 3 are then used on Day 4 to pick 50‐70 L4 larvae for the chemotaxis assay the following day (see Fig. 2 for experimental flow).
On Day 5, adult worms are collected by washing plates with 2 mL of M9 media into 1.5‐mL tubes. The worms are allowed to settle at the bottom of the tube for 5 min, followed by the removal of supernatant and the addition of new M9 media (1 mL). This washing step is repeated four times to remove all residual bacteria. Repeating the washing step this many times has been identified as one of the crucial factors that determine the quality of the assay. During the final settling period, 2 µL of odorant and control solutions is added to the CTX plates. It is important to keep the lids closed as much as possible to prevent the odorant from diffusing. In this optimized protocol, diacetyl was used at various concentrations, diluted 1:1 with 1 M Na azide. For the control, 96% ethanol was diluted 1:1 with 1 M Na azide. Our data indicate that lower concentrations of diacetyl result in more consistent and clear chemotaxis indices, leading us to propose using 0.05% v/v diacetyl in this protocol instead of the previously used standard concentration of 0.5% v/v.
During the optimization process, different worm numbers, from 50 to 300 worms per plate, were tested. Using fewer worms made the assays more robust, eased the counting of worms, and prevented the clumping of worms in the center of the plate. Due to the large degree of clumping at higher worm densities, chemotaxis assays could not always be performed and a robust comparison between low and high worm densities could not be carried out, as the worms transferred with plastic pipettes did not participate in the chemotaxis assay to a high enough degree.
In this protocol, worms are transferred to the center of the assay plates using a glass pipette. Glass pipettes used with a rubber bulb provide smoother control over liquid flow and are less sticky than plastic tips, even more so than specialty‐made low‐retention tips. This enables gentler handling of the worms, and fewer worms are lost in the process of transferring worms after washing. The non‐sticky nature of glass minimizes the risk of damaging the worms during transfer, as well as losing large numbers of worms, which increases the consistency in worm numbers between plates. The excess liquid around the worms is then carefully removed using a piece of filter paper.
The assay plates are kept in a fume hood for 60‐90 min to avoid interference from external odors in the laboratory environment. After completing the assay, the plates are stored overnight in a refrigerator, which means that the worms can be counted the following day, easing the scoring if using a large number of plates.
Initially, during protocol optimization, a 4‐choice assay was used as in Margie et al. (2013). The protocol has been modified from a 4‐choice assay to a 2‐choice assay (Fig. 3A) after noticing that N2 worms exhibit a significantly higher chemotaxis index toward diacetyl in a 2‐choice assay compared to a 4‐choice assay, with reduced variability within the cohort (Fig. 3B). This modification provided clearer quantification of the worms’ response. The 2‐choice assay has previously been extensively used, but a stringent comparison between the two forms has been lacking. In addition, our evaluation shows that the commonly used assay duration of 60 min should be extended to 90 min, as worms continue to move toward the test odorant beyond the 60‐min assay time (Fig. 3C). Our data also indicate that using a 90‐min assay time can reduce the dispersion between worms in the raw data. Assay times up to 180 min have been evaluated already by Bargmann et al. (1993); results showed a plateau after 60 min with only small improvements at 180 min, which has led to 60 min being the norm in the field today. In our hands, the effect is significantly larger between 60‐ and 90‐min assay times.
(A) Description of the 2‐ and 4‐choice assays with a comparison of the resulting chemotaxis indices using N2 worms and 0.05% v/v diacetyl. The difference in data variability between the 2‐ or 4‐choice assay can easily be recognized based on the spread of the data (nplates = 10 and nworms = 50‐70/plate). (B) Increased odor concentration can have negative effects on the chemotaxis index as seen in the comparison between using 0.05% v/v, 0.25% v/v, and 0.5% v/v diacetyl (nplates = 25 and nworms = 50‐70/plate). (C) Temporal distribution of N2 worms during the 2‐choice chemotaxis assay indicates that the standard time point of 60 min might not be sufficient to capture the full behavioral effect. ## Understanding Results
This optimized chemotaxis assay protocol aims to enable the measurement of behavioral responses in C. elegans to odors with higher precision and greater reproducibility. While this assay is widely used, with several protocols published (Bargmann et al., 1993; Margie et al., 2013; Troemel et al., 1997), consistent and well‐documented handling is crucial to ensure comparability of results. In order to minimize variability, the protocol outlined above has been established.
In comparison to existing protocols, our modifications are in line with the findings of previous studies while offering distinct improvements. Although the 4‐choice assay (Lee et al., 2009; Margie et al., 2013) has been widely employed in recent years, our findings indicate that using a 2‐choice assay offers more precise and consistent outcomes (Fig. 3A). The 2‐choice assay, in which an odorant and a control are positioned on opposite sides of the plate, has been used continuously since its development, but has also been modified in several cases (Nuttley et al., 2001). In studies such as that of Troemel et al. (Troemel et al., 1997), the assay was adapted to use a square plate setup. While both the 2‐choice and 4‐choice assays have been used successfully in different studies, they have never been directly compared with each other. The 2‐choice and 4‐choice assays are both valid approaches, but, in our hands, the 2‐choice setup using a circular plate allowed for more straightforward counting and yielded less spread between groups and over the different testing days, thereby enhancing the reproducibility and interpretability of data.
This work also demonstrates the critical importance of accurately calibrating odorant concentrations (Fig. 3B), as individual odors can have dual effects depending on their concentration (Pu et al., 2023). This was already noted by Bargmann et al. (1993), who showed that the balance between attraction and repulsion for benzaldehyde is concentration‐dependent. At lower concentrations, C. elegans exhibits positive chemotaxis toward attractants, while higher concentrations can lead to repulsion (Bargmann et al., 1993; Nuttley et al., 2001; Pu et al., 2023; Yoshida et al., 2012). It has been shown that C. elegans initially approaches benzaldehyde, but this response shifts to aversion at higher concentrations due to distinct neural mechanisms (Nuttley et al., 2001). GPCR signaling pathways involved in these concentration‐dependent behaviors have been identified among the suggested genes that are involved in modulating these responses, i.e., npr‐1, flp‐21, and fmi‐1, which are important for AWC‐mediated chemotaxis (Pu et al., 2023). It has also been demonstrated that different sensory neurons are activated at different odorant concentrations, with AWC neurons mediating attraction at low concentrations and ASH neurons eliciting aversion at high concentrations (Yoshida et al., 2012). These findings highlight the importance of accurately calibrating odor concentration in experiments based on the specific research question to ensure reliable and consistent results. In this study, we have limited the optimization to only using diacetyl to focus on environmental factors and the handling of worms.
The improved washing and handling procedures we have implemented, such as using a glass pipette rather than a standard plastic pipette tip, are critical in minimizing variability, particularly in reducing clumping and ensuring consistent distribution of worms across the assay plate. It also reduces the number of lost worms during transfer, thus increasing consistency in the worm number. The clumping issue was further reduced by consistent seeding of plates with uniform and lower amounts of OP50. By carefully controlling for factors such as worm age, health, and environmental conditions, as well as temperature and humidity, our protocol aims to address some of the reproducibility issues that have previously troubled chemotaxis research. Additionally, our data indicate that the run time of the assay needs to be considered, as some mutants may still be attracted to the odor but require more time to reach the area of interest. This was represented by a reduced spread in the results at a 90‐min run time as compared to 60 min (Fig. 3C). Similar experiments performed by Bargmann et al. (Bargmann et al., 1993) did not observe a significant difference beyond 60 min; however, this likely depends on the specific mutants being tested. Our data demonstrate that extending the assay duration can reveal differences in chemotactic behavior that might otherwise be missed, highlighting the importance of adjusting the run time according to the strains and conditions being studied. Other observations include the advantage of using approximately only 50‐70 worms as it reduces clumping and lingering in the center of the assay plate, thereby improving the overall quality of the assay.
Finally, while chemotaxis assays are a powerful tool for observing behavior and studying the nervous system, it should be recognized that they are highly sensitive assays that require meticulous and consistent handling to yield reliable results. By employing the method outlined in this protocol, the problems in performing chemotaxis should be reduced to a minimum and yield data that are reliable and reproducible.
