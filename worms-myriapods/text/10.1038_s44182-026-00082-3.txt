Water waves help aquatic animals locate environmental stimuli. While previous studies suggest that
leech mechanoreceptors have evolved to respond to relevant wave frequencies, it remains unclear
how information from multiple sensors is combined and processed. This work presents a simpliﬁed
computational model that mimics leech goal seeking behavior in an agent-based simulation. A
simulated leech (agent) was tasked with ﬁnding the source of an artiﬁcial water wave stimulus. The
agent’s distributed mechanoreceptor array detected wave motion, which was processed with a
computational neuroscience Winner-Take-All (WTA) framework to generate motion commands. The
computational model’s performance aligned with data from animal experiments. The model also
enables us to address questions about how different patterns of sensor ablation or placement might
affect navigation performance in the animal. In this way, our model can complement animal
experiments by enabling questions to be posed that are challenging to address with live animals.
Furthermore, through considering how biological systems may process distributed information, our
study may also provide insights into novel approaches for processing data from multiple sensors in
man-made systems.

Invertebrates and vertebrates alike use arrays of sensors to detect
sensory stimuli, a phenomenon known as distributed sensing. Distributed sensing is found across a wide variety of sensory modalities
that include mechanoreception1,2, visual sensation3, and potentially
magnetoreception4,5. As an example, Fig. 1 shows an illustration of a
leech and its more than 294 multimodal visual sensors and mechanoreceptors distributed across its body. This array of multimodal
receptors is used to locate prey within the leech’s aquatic environment.
Understanding distributed sensing is essential for understanding how
animals combine data from multiple sensors to effectively behave and
navigate in their environment. Beyond enhancing our understanding
of navigation in biological systems, the study of distributed sensing and
computational neuroscience/neuromorphic computing approaches
may provide several beneﬁts for man-made systems6, including but not
limited to: 1) enhancing system robustness to individual sensor failures, 2) using simpler and/or lower quality sensors that collectively
provide actionable data7, and 3) developing systems that use lower size,
weight, cost, and power.

Leeches are an attractive organism for studying distributed sensing as
they have more than 294 distributed sensors (known as sensilla) and a
relatively simple nervous system ( ~ 104 neurons vs. ~ 105 for the common
fruit ﬂy). The leech nervous system is comprised of segmental ganglia that,
along with a relatively simple brain, help to coordinate motion between each
segment of the animal (for review see ref. 8). While the majority of neurophysiological study has been limited to the medicinal leech, Hirudo verbana, ecological ﬁeld studies tell us that different leech species can be
identiﬁed by the layout of their sensors9. Further, these different species of
leech live in a variety of habitats and seek different prey, suggesting that an
individual species’ sensor layout may confer ecological advantages in a
speciﬁc species’ ecological niche.
Previous studies have sought to elucidate the relationship between
sensory stimulation, neural responses (i.e., spike rate frequency), motor
commands, and organism-level behavior10–13. However, it is still unclear
how sensory information is processed such that it can direct a locomotor
response. Furthermore, it is unknown how ablating (i.e., disabling) different
sensors or sensor groups via damage or other malfunctions would affect

navigation performance. Experiments of this type can be difﬁcult to perform
on live animals, owing to the large number of sensors (294+) and the small
size of individual sensors (approx. 20 micron diameter). Furthermore,
surgical manipulations can lead to impacts on behavior that are outside of
those caused by the loss of a sensor itself.
To address these questions, in the present study, we employ a computational approach that combines computational neuroscience with agentbased modeling. Speciﬁcally, we use the winner-take-all (WTA) computational neuroscience framework described by Wilson14 in conjunction with
an agent-based model (ABM) to enable a simulated leech to navigate to a
water wave source solely using mechanosensation. The WTA network (also
known as a neural ﬁeld15,16) uses populations of neurons that can inhibit or
excite the activity of their neighbors. This type of neural organization has
been shown to be capable of tasks such as path integration and categorical
decisions14. We chose this model formulation because of its demonstrated
biological relevance and ability in navigation and orientation contexts (see
Section 3 for further discussion).
We tasked a simulated agent (i.e., a ﬁctive animal) that processes
sensory information using a neural ﬁeld to perform distributed mechanoreception as a means of navigating from a starting point to a goal. Rather
than try to simulate every detail of the leech precisely, we made several useful
abstractions to preserve the nature of the problem we are investigating while
simultaneously simplifying our study (see Table 1 for details). Speciﬁcally,
our simulated leech model only has 24 mechanoreceptor “populations” to
more easily employ Wilson’s pre-existing model14 as compared to over 294
multisensory sensilla and sensory neurons in live leeches. This allowed for
easier implementation while providing the ability to add or subtract sensors
for comparison or additional studies (see Section “Sensor Quality”). Our
model also only has an effective peripheral nervous system vs. leeches that
have segmental ganglia and a “head” brain. Finally, our model moves as a
rigid body as compared to a leech that swims through water with an
undulating motion.
Despite these abstractions, this model enabled us to examine the
impact of intrinsic features (e.g., number of sensors, the level of neural
activation required for salient stimulus perception (i.e., the neural threshold)), and extrinsic features such as the impact of wave frequency on the
ability of the agent to locate the water wave stimulus. Additionally, we
ablated (i.e., disabled) different combinations of sensors to simulate both
sensory ablation and to examine the performance of our agent in response to
different sensor layouts. Our model reproduced ﬁnd rates (i.e., percentage of
leeches that successfully navigate to a target) similar to those observed in
animal experiments17. Our model also demonstrated how leech behavior

demonstrates how different sensor layouts may affect performance. The fact
that our model is able to recapitulate real-world animal behavior with so
many abstractions and with no tuning or optimization of our model’s
parameters suggests that it is a reasonable approach to modeling leech

Much of our present study is based on work developed in Nichols18,
which was later adapted for magnetoreception in Nichols et al.19. The present study expands on these works in two substantial ways. First, the present
study incorporates the impact of water wave amplitude on sensing, an
important step that we have not previously undertaken. Second, the present
study explores the effects of sensor ablation, which allows us to examine the
effects of different sensor layouts and the impact of sensors being damaged
and unusable.

Results
As leeches of different ecological niches differ in sensor position and
number, we wanted to examine potential impacts of this on our model.
Thus, we ﬁrst consider the performance of the simulated agent in locating
the source of a water wave stimulus when all of its sensors are functioning
normally. We then examine how performance is affected by ablating (i.e.,
disabling) subsets of sensors. This allowed us to examine the impact of
changing either the number of available sensors or the sensor layout.
Additionally, we explore the effects of varying sensor quality by modifying
our sensor model so that the sensors inherently provide more errant data,
and of varying the number of sensors present.
In all simulations, the agent began ~76 cm away from a water wave
source, with a random initial heading. It was given up to 180 s to reach its
goal, the water wave source. A trial was considered successful if the agent
remained within 4 cm of the goal for 5 s consecutively. For every combination of model parameters considered below, we report the performance of the agent as the proportion of 50 trials in which the goal was
successfully reached. The agent had 24 sensors, each of which is connected to a neural population. These neural populations had speciﬁc
rules underlying their interactions: Each population did not excite or
inhibit its 4 nearest neighbors, and its 7 most distant neighbors. A
population inhibited all 12 of its other neighbors (see Section “Methods”
for details). As an additional parameter, because natural sensors may
incur damage or be subject to environmental noise, Noise and uncertainty are added to the model by adding noise to the input, noise to the
direction of the input, or randomness into the direction that the
agent moves.
To examine the role of neural sensitivity in this navigation task, a
parameter called the neural threshold was varied to examine the model’s
performance under different conditions. This is a threshold level of neural
activity at which a neural population’s response is considered to be salient
and is allowed to inﬂuence the agent’s perceived direction of the water wave
source. We found this parameter to be critical to both the agent’s ability to
ﬁnd the water wave source and the conditions under which it was able
to do so.

Normal functioning
The simulated agent responded to water waves in a frequency-dependent
manner. This is illustrated by trajectory plots shown in Fig. 2A–C. Wave
frequencies that are too low (Fig. 2A) or high (Fig. 2C) relative to the neural
threshold result in under- or overstimulation, respectively, and ultimately
unsuccessful navigation behavior. Under-stimulated behavior resulted in
the agent rarely turning and nearly always missing the goal, tending instead
to travel in straight lines. Overstimulated behavior resulted in the agent
tending to turn too frequently and randomly, wandering along an undirected path, again nearly always missing its goal. If the frequency and neural
threshold were varied with each other appropriately, navigation success
could be achieved (Fig. 2B).
Because sensor stimulation increases with wave frequency, the interaction between the neural threshold and wave frequency is important for the
agent’s performance. Figure 3 plots the success rate of the simulated agent as
a function of the wave frequency for a range of neural thresholds. As the

neural threshold increased and the sensor more sensitive, the stimulus
frequencies at which the agent was successfully able to locate the target
increased. Beyond a neural threshold value of 0.2, it became harder for the
agent to differentiate frequencies near its preferred frequency, resulting in
the peak of each curve widening as the frequency intensity increased.
We found that a neural threshold between 0.02 and 0.2 resulted in a
peak success rate for the agent at stimulus wave frequencies of approximately between 8 Hz and 12 Hz (Fig. 3, shaded box). This is comparable to
the results of laboratory experiments with live adult leeches (Fig. 3, blue
line–live leech data taken from ref. 17). It is also comparable to the frequency
of 13.5 Hz, which Piephoff et al.20 noted corresponds to the phase velocity
minimum of water at 15 °C (see Section “Discussion” for a deeper discussion). These comparisons helped to validate our model against real-world
animal behavior. Interestingly, we found that across the range of neural
thresholds we investigated the best performance was found in the 8–12 Hz
range (Fig. 3).

Because a real leech’s lateral (i.e., side) sensors may be stimulated from
the tangential motion of water over them (e.g., ref. 20) we varied the angular
sensitivity of our sensors to account for increasingly sensitive lateral sensors.
We found similar performance through a variety of lateral sensitivities (see
Fig. 10 for summary data, section 4.2 for implementation description, and
Eq. (8) for mathematical detail).
Sensor ablation at the same frequency
To examine the impact of sensor number and location on the agent’s ability
to localize the stimulus, we performed additional simulations in which
subsets of the agent’s 24 sensors were ablated (i.e., disabled) throughout an
entire trial so that they could not be activated by any level of stimulation. The
control case was running the agent with all sensors available and active (i.e.,
no ablation). We ablated the following subsets of sensors:

• the 12 odd-numbered sensors;
• the 12 even-numbered sensors;
• the rightmost 7 sensors, with preferred headings within ± 45° of the
agent’s rightward vector;
• the leftmost 7 sensors, with preferred headings within ± 45° of the
agent’s leftward vector.
These ablation experiments allowed us to determine how the agent
performs with reduced numbers of sensors across the body. To further
examine the role of sensor location in localization ability, we also performed
several similar ablation experiments that effectively changed the placement
of sensors around the body. In these experiments, we ablated:
• the frontmost 7 sensors, with preferred headings within ± 45° of the
agent’s forward vector;
• the rearmost 7 sensors, with preferred headings within ± 45° of the
agent’s backward vector;
• both the frontmost 7 sensors and the rearmost 7 sensors, similar to
descriptions 5 and 6
In these experiments, the neural threshold was ﬁxed at 0.15, an
intermediate value of neural threshold that was found to generate model
results that align with behavioral data from ref. 17 (see Fig. 3). The stimulus
wave frequency was 11 Hz.
The left- and right-ablated agents suffered dramatic performance
reductions as compared to the control case (Fig. 4A). As one might expect,
left-ablated agents were biased towards right turns, and right-ablated agents
were biased towards left turns. Odd and even-ablated agents also experienced dramatic performance reductions compared to the control case
(Fig. 4A).
For our sensor placement experiments (Fig. 4B), we saw a reduction in
performance when the rear sensors were removed, since agents that started
facing away from the target had difﬁculty detecting the waves. Interestingly,
removing the front sensors alone did not impact performance compared to
the control (80% success in both cases), but it did have a noticeable effect on
the shapes of trajectories, causing them to gradually curve toward the target,
rather than head straight towards it (Fig. 4B).
Sensor ablation at different frequencies
In addition to different ablation conﬁgurations at the same frequency, we
performed sensor ablations at different water wave frequencies to determine
whether performance depended on frequency. For most sensor conﬁgurations, we found that performance could be improved by changing the frequency of the water wave stimulus. To summarize the results, we plotted
ﬁnd rate vs. water wave frequency in increments of 1 Hz with a neural
threshold of 0.15 (Fig. 5). Only the cases of the right- and left- ablated
sensors remain universally low (i.e., less than about 20%) for all frequencies.
As was shown earlier in Fig. 3, the control case had a peak ﬁnd rate at 11 Hz.
Each of the ablation cases (excluding right- and left- ablated sensors) had
peak ﬁnd rates that were shifted towards higher frequencies (12 Hz for evennpj Robotics | (2026)4:24

and front-ablations, 13 Hz for odd- and rear ablations, and 18 Hz for frontand-rear-ablation). In addition to having a higher frequency, these ablation

Sensor quality
In this paradigm, we modiﬁed our sensor model by enabling the cyan term
of Eq. (9). This allowed all sensors to provide spurious measurements,
increasing the chances for the neural ﬁeld to provide an incorrect perception
of the wave source’s direction. This simulates a response to environmental
noise, a less than ideal sensor, or inherent imperfections in sensory data or
the nervous system. In this paradigm, we tested the agent with 8, 24, and 72
sensors to examine how performance changes with the number of sensors
(Fig. 6). In general, the maximum possible ﬁnd rate increased as the number
of sensors increased. For 8 sensors, the maximum ﬁnd rate was about 55% at
a frequency of 16 Hz for a neural threshold of 0.5. With 24 sensors, neural
thresholds of 0.15 and 0.5 yielded ﬁnd rates of about 60% at 8 Hz and 12 Hz.
For 72 sensors, a neural threshold of 0.5 yielded about 70% success at 8 Hz.
For comparison, we also ran our “high quality” sensor model with 8, 24, and
72 sensors and found that performance appeared to be similar in all cases
(see Section 3 for details).

Discussion
Despite its simplicity, our model was able to recapitulate behavioral results
observed in real-world leech navigation. Speciﬁcally, we were able to
reproduce the rates at which leeches localize our stimulus at their preferred
water-wave stimulus frequency (Fig. 3).
The model’s behavior at low, high, or intermediate stimulus frequencies is explained by the dependence of sensor activation on stimulus
wave frequency (see Sec. “Methods” for full model description). Low wave
frequencies equate to low wavenumber (Eq. (1)), leading to weak activation
of sensors (Eq. (8)). This in turn leads to weak activation of neural populations that make up the neural ﬁeld responsible for processing sensory
input (Eqs. (10)–(11)) and potentially no populations exceeding the
aforementioned neural threshold. When none of the neural populations are
above the neural threshold, the agent is programmed to either remain
stationary or make random small angle turns before moving forward (See
section 4 for implementation details). A similar behavior occurs in leeches
during their navigation behavior21,12. This results in the behavior seen in
Fig. 2A, where the agent tends to move in relatively straight lines, ignoring
the goal.
Conversely, high wave frequencies lead to strong activation of many
sensors, including sensors that are not well aligned with the wave source,
ultimately resulting in many supra-threshold neural populations. When
there are multiple supra-threshold neural populations, the agent is programmed to select one at random for determining turning direction before
moving forward. When the random selection is made from many populations encoding distinct directions, this results in the behavior seen in Fig. 2C,
where the agent tends to turn too frequently and in random directions, again
not reaching the goal.
There are, therefore, two major failure modes for the agent. The
ﬁrst is under-stimulation by waves of low frequency (or low amplitude)
relative to the neural threshold, leading to too few supra-threshold
neural populations, and effectively failing to sense any water waves.
The second is over-stimulation by waves of high frequency (or high
amplitude) relative to the neural threshold, leading to too many suprathreshold neural populations, and effectively sensing that water waves
are approaching from many directions. Between these extremes, for
combinations of neural threshold and wave frequency that maximize
the success rate, there are only a small number of supra-threshold
populations with similar encoded directions, leading to directed
motion toward the wave source, as seen in Fig. 2B. In summary, for a

It is worth noting here that in many biological systems, if overstimulated, the sensory system may be able to dampen its response. For
example, when speaking or exposed to loud noises, middle ear muscles
contract, decreasing the amount of pressure put on the eardrum and thus
dampening the sound22. In the case of loud sounds, this decreases the
probability of damage to the inner ear. In the case of self-generated
sound, this same mechanism decreases the possibility of sensor fatigue
or interference with other stimuli. It is possible that the leech could
employ a similar mechanism of signal dampening when overstimulated.
Indeed, leeches are known to have a decreased sensory response when
swimming, which causes a high level of self-induced water ﬂow around
the sensors23.

Our results revealed a shift in frequency preference when we adjusted
the neural threshold, something which could be of interest in animals. If
present in animals, this would provide a rather simple means of adapting the
system to respond to different stimuli. This is something we see in leeches as
they age and is thought to be related to age-associated changes in prey
preference17,24. How might this change in sensor sensitivity happen in a
living organism? The neurons involved could change their threshold
through changing membrane dynamics. Such a mechanism would allow for
the occurrence of similar changes to our model in the animal’s frequency
preference, which could provide a fast means of adapting to new food
sources or new niches. We note here that the neural threshold in this study
was set as a threshold level of neural activity at which a neural population’s
response is considered to be salient. Also, all neurons in this study had the
same inhibitory and excitatory connectivity to their neighbors. In reality,
neurons might not have identical connectivity to their neighbors, and the

Across the range of neural thresholds we explored in this study, our
simulated leeches showed a global performance maximum for water waves
in the 8–12 Hz range, agreeing with experimentally observed live animal
data (Fig. 3). Other parameters in our model that we did not systematically
vary, such as water depth (see sec. “Water waves”), the number of neural
nodes or the time constants of neural decay or inactivation (see sec. “Neural
processing”), or the speed of the agent (see sec. “Motion model”) may affect
this global maximum, and we would like to explore these possibilities in
future work.
To examine the impact of sensor placement and number on the agent’s
localization ability, we performed ablation experiments at a neural threshold
of 0.15, a value that gives an 80% ﬁnd rate at water wave stimulus frequency
of 11 Hz (Fig. 4). For this water wave frequency, sensor ablation, especially
unilateral ablation of the left or right side, generally reduced performance
compared to no ablation (Fig. 4). Ablation of the front sensors in this case
did not reduce performance. However, it is worth noting that forward sensor
ablation changed the agent’s behavior so that it approached its goal indirectly. When we examined the effects of ablation and sensor placement across
other wave frequencies (Fig. 5), we saw a shift in the performance curves to
higher frequencies for most sensor ablation conditions, which was likely a
compensatory mechanism of responding to stronger stimuli with fewer
sensors. While it would make sense that decreasing the number of sensors
would change the ability of the model to localize a stimulus, some of what we
see is not a decrease in success, but rather a shift in preference toward a
higher frequency stimulus (Fig. 5). Interestingly, we see a shift in Hirudo
verbana frequency preferences as they develop, a difference which is related
to changes in both the sensitivity and number of their sensillae17. Younger
leeches which have fewer well-developed mechanoreceptors, prefer lower
frequencies than their adult counterparts. Our results suggest that the
number of sensors impacts the preferred stimulus frequency but not
necessarily localization ability. Our model more accurately reﬂects adult
leeches than juveniles. Speciﬁc differences between our results and live
animal experiments with juveniles are likely due to the fact that our model

contains only a single sensory modality, and does not account for less well
developed juvenile sensors or neural connectivity. Developmental sensory
changes will be explored in future studies.
When we examine speciﬁc layouts, we note that the only conﬁgurations that are truly unsuccessful occur when sensors on one entire side of the
agent are ablated, suggesting that animals would likely compare sensory
information from their two sides to be able to appropriately navigate. Perhaps of interest is that while right and left ablations had similar outcomes,
disabling front sensors or rear sensors, respectively, had quite different
outcomes. Ablating the rear sensors resulted in a minor shift from the
8–12 Hz preferred stimulus frequency range to ~10–13 Hz (Fig. 5), and
decreased ability to localize the wave source (from 80% at 11 Hz without
ablations to 50% at 13 Hz with rear ablations). In contrast, disabling the
front sensors shifted the preferred frequency more, to ~11–16 Hz, and
increased the performance within this new range (from 80% at 11 Hz
without ablations to 98% at 12 Hz with from ablations). The reason for the
sizable increase in peak performance when front sensors are ablated is not
entirely clear. However, it is possible that the rear and side sensors enable the
agent to sense and reorient towards the goal over a wide range of body
orientations. In contrast, when only using the front and side sensors, when
the agent is oriented with the direction of wave propagation, it may effectively become blind to wave stimuli because the rear sensors that are facing
into the waves cannot be stimulated.
The model makes clear that changing the number and location of
sensors impacts the frequency of peak response. This becomes more
interesting in our model organism, which often exhibits different sensor
layouts for different ecological niches, suggesting a mechanism to further
adapt animals to the prey within their niche. Future studies could further
explore this possibility.
Based on our model’s ﬁnd rate success with 24 sensors, one may ask,
why would a leech have 294 sensors when prey and other targets could be
localized with a smaller number? Leeches are capable of a wide range of
behaviors beyond simply localizing prey, such as avoiding predators, ﬁnding
mates, and moving around obstacles in their environment. A larger number
of sensors may help them to execute this diversity of behaviors. Further, a
larger number of sensors may offer a level of redundancy in the event that
subsets of sensors are injured or damaged. This would be true especially if

While neural threshold and sensor location are important and can tune
the response of the agent, additional factors not present in this model could
be used to further tune the systemic response to wave properties. In general,
water waves in natural environments are a superposition of different water
waves with different frequencies and amplitudes. As discussed in the
development of a single-sensor physics and computational neurosciencebased leech mechanoreceptor model by Piephoff et al.20, leech mechanoreceptors may be tuned to the phase velocity minimum of water, which is
13.5 Hz. Brieﬂy, the phase velocity minimum is the point at which the effects
of gravity and surface tension on a wave are balanced. It is also the point at
which the wave’s phase velocity is equal to its group velocity. Piephoff et al.20
discuss that wave components at the phase velocity minimum would travel
with the least amount of amplitude damping and waveform distortion, even
over long distances. Piephoff et al.20 also describe leeches as relatively
indiscriminate feeders, feeding on a variety of animals that generate different
wave frequencies. Thus, the signal most relevant to a leech is possibly the one
that simply indicates the presence of any potential meal in their habitat, even
from a relatively far distance. That Piephoff et al.20 found individual
mechanoreceptors might respond best to waves around 13.5 Hz, our present
study ﬁnds that a simulated leech with only mechanoreception has peak ﬁnd
rates for various sensor conﬁgurations around 13.5 Hz, and that both of
these simulated studies agree with real-world animal experiments suggests
that our model is a reasonable representation of real-world animal behavior,
and might be used to make predictions of animal behavior in scenarios that
would be difﬁcult to test in vivo. It is worth noting here that the leech has
sensor modalities not included in the model such as chemosensation which
would ﬁne-tune navigation in the short range.
At this point, it is important to consider what our model is capable of
given its simpliﬁcations and abstractions (see Table 1). The fact that our
model has so many abstractions yet is able to recapitulate real-world animal
behavior suggests that it is a reasonable approach to modeling leech behavior. Adding additional complexity and features would likely only improve
its ﬁdelity and predictive power in novel environments in which it is difﬁcult
to perform in vivo animal experiments. It is important to note that, while we
used a WTA neural architecture based on its abilities to do path-integration
and categorical decisions14, and while WTA-like mechanisms are believed to
be involved in localizing stimuli in other species (e.g., spatial localization in
barn owls25,26), to the authors’ knowledge, there is not currently direct
physiological evidence for a WTA neural architecture in the leech. Lewis and
Kristan27 explored this experimentally and with modeling for a different
sensory-response behavior: the local bending reﬂex. Like our model, theirs
used cosine-weighted tuning curves and preferred directions for sensory
and interneuronal layers of a neural network. However, unlike our model,
their model lacked WTA inhibitory connections between the interneurons.
In our model, these inhibitory connections enhance the salience of a singular
stimulus direction. Although those authors concluded that a WTA

mechanism was unlikely for the bending reﬂex they were studying, there are
important differences between the bending reﬂex and the task of navigating
to a target. Navigation to a target likely involves higher-level information
processing at the level of the whole organism, unlike the local bend reﬂex,
which is segmental and was studied in isolated segments. The bending reﬂex
is a response to local stimuli, and it is possible that distant stimuli are
processed differently. For distant stimuli, WTA frameworks are common in
other animals. Whether a WTA mechanism is involved in leech prey
localization and navigation is still undetermined. A live-animal experiment
that combines the approaches of Harley et al.17 and Lewis and Kristan27—
where multiple wave stimuli are present, and the animal could be observed
either to pick one or else to choose an intermediate heading—could be
insightful for determining whether WTA mechanisms are involved at this
level of processing. That a simple WTA-based model was able to recapitulate
real-world leech behavior demonstrates that, at the very least, it is able to
capture essential components of the leech’s behavior.
The work here offers potential lessons and inspiration for engineered
systems. As is discussed in section 4, the sensors in our model are not high
quality (i.e., noise is present), and the model itself has stochasticity built into
it (e.g., turning in random directions when under-stimulated, random
population selections when overstimulated). Also, as our ablation and
placement experiments suggest, for two of the seven different sensor conﬁgurations, the agent can locate the target ≳ 60% of the time across a range of
different stimulus frequencies (roughly 11–20 Hz). Two other sensor conﬁgurations can locate the target with similar accuracy for frequencies
between 12 Hz and 15 Hz. Furthermore, as indicated by Piephoff et al.20,
leech mechanoreceptors may be tuned to detect minimally distorted signals
from far-away disturbances. Taken together, these facts suggest that even
when different sensors are absent in different conﬁgurations, the agent can
still succeed. This means that with low quality noisy sensors that may or may
not be present due to events such as physical damage, this kind of sensory
processing paradigm can robustly locate targets. These are all traits that are
desired in manmade systems. The biologically-derived distributed sensing
and processing approach presented here can be adapted for mobile systems
that need to locate (or evade) a particular stimulus source, even if that source
is somewhat distant, or if the mobile platform suffers sensor uncertainty or
damage. Additionally, the distributed sensing and processing approach in
this work could be applied to other man-made platforms in a variety of other
use cases where data from multiple sensors could help to provide a more
complete and/or actionable picture of a system’s current health and capabilities (e.g., sensors on a prosthetic device, automated monitoring of the
loading on a structure).
In future studies, we would like to take several steps. First, we would
like to further vary the number of sensors and neural populations in our
model to determine how many, or how few sensors are needed before
performance qualitatively changes, or before the system’s performance
degrades to an unacceptable level. For example, with 24 sensors and a
neural threshold of 0.15, the agent’s peak ﬁnd rate (80%) occurs at 11 Hz.
However, when the even or odd sensors are ablated, the peak ﬁnd rate shifts
to 62% at 12–13 Hz or to 70% at 13 Hz, respectively; more exploration is
needed to determine when the peak rate starts to shift. Related to this point,
a parametric study and/or sensitivity analysis investigating the relationship
between all of the model’s underlying parameters and output performance
could be useful in further characterizing our system’s performance, especially for expanding it to a tool that can be used alongside real-world
biological hypothesis testing, or in the development of neuromorphic
engineered systems. In adding further functionality to our model, we
would like to model the multimodal nature of leech sensing17. Engineered
systems frequently assimilate data from multiple sensory modalities to
execute their tasks (e.g., using range and bearing to different features for
navigation). This model only demonstrates a single means of combining
data across sensors. In the future, we would like to compare the neural ﬁeld
approach described here to classically engineered data fusion, state estimation approaches, and control approaches, speciﬁcally computational
complexity and regions of applicability.

We modeled the leech as a 2D ellipse that is 10 cm long and 1 cm wide.
These measurements are based approximately on the size of an adult in
the leech species Hirudo verbana35. This approach was deemed acceptable for mirroring Harley et al.17, which primarily examined leech
behavior in 2D. Whereas real leeches are highly ﬂexible and use an
undulating motion while swimming, for simplicity, our ellipse is rigid

Neural processing
A neural ﬁeld is composed of neural populations (also referred to as nodes).
Each population responds to a preferred input. These populations can
interact with each other through excitatory and inhibitory connections.
Neural ﬁelds are useful for studying the aggregate behavior of populations of
neurons, vs. examining the performance of individual neurons or the collective performance of a set of individual neurons that are synaptically
connected14,36. While details of our neural ﬁeld approach can be found in
[ref. 14, section 7.2], we summarize here for completeness.
In our study, the neural ﬁeld has 24 nodes. Each node is paired with and
receives excitation from a set of sensors sensitive to speciﬁc directional
inputs. Nodes inhibit neighboring nodes at intermediate distances by convolving the activity of the network via negative weights in a weighting array
(Fig. 11). This results in the nodes having a distribution of activity, with one
or some of the nodes having activity that exceeds a threshold (i.e., a “winning” neuron or set of neurons). Because each node represents a physical
direction, as illustrated in Fig. 11, the winning neuron and its neighbors
encode a perceived direction, which the agent uses to control its motion.

To model the biological phenomena of over- and under-stimulation,
we set a constant, called the neural threshold, which is varied between
simulations in this study. Nodes with activation levels Rj that exceed this
threshold are considered salient. The preferred direction Ωj encoded by
these salient nodes can inﬂuence the perceived direction of the wave source.
Ideally, just one or a few nodes with preferred directions corresponding to
the true direction of the wave source will reach this threshold. Setting the
threshold too low can cause many nodes to produce salient signals, leading
to over-stimulation. Setting the threshold too high can cause no nodes to
reach an activity level at which they are considered salient, leading to understimulation.
At each time step, one node is selected at random from among all nodes
with activations above the neural threshold parameter (see sec. “Motion
model” for handling the case when no neural node activations exceed the
threshold). The activation level of the selected node (Rselect) with preferred
direction Ωselect, along with the activations of its two nearest neighbors
(Rselect−1, Rselect+1), are used together to estimate the perceived direction of
the stimulus wave source, Ωinterp, via parabolic interpolation (Eq. (12)),
which is more accurate than the 15° intervals encoded by the nodes’ preferred directions, and an approach that has been used in this type of neural
modeling37. The perceived direction is used to determine bearing changes
for our motion model (see sec. “Motion model”). Because this study focused
on neuromechanics underlying distributed sensation and motor control or
actuation, we elected to use the parabolic interpolation method for computing a perceived direction rather than implement additional neural
populations to accomplish this task. An additional neural population could
be implemented to select the perceived direction, which would increase the
biological relevance of our model.
The activity of all nodes (i.e., Rj’s) is updated every time step. Based
on both preliminary observations and biological considerations, the
sensors take measurements for 2 s, and are inactive for 6 s. In Eq. (11),
this corresponds to the excitatory and inhibitory input terms being
active with nonzero values for 2 s, and then both being set to a value of
zero for 6 s. This 6 s rest period allows neural node activity to decay,
preventing the winner-take-all network from becoming “stuck” with one
winning node inhibiting all others even as the agent turns and sensory
inputs change. This helped ensure that later measurements are not
corrupted by information from earlier measurements, and is also biologically inspired as a neuron’s response can decay over time after a
stimulus is presented. This also reﬂects spiking patterns commonly
observed in single celled neuron responses38,21,12.

Assuming at least one neural node’s activation exceeded the neural
threshold (i.e., deemed to be salient), the neural ﬁeld returns a perceived
direction (Eq. (12)). Using Eq. (13), the agent converts the perceived angle
Ωinterp into a change in bearing, Δβ, using the constant kp = 0.4 s−1. kp is
similar in nature to a proportional control constant. During testing of our
model, we found that larger values could cause motion oscillations, while
smaller values could result in slow turning. The value chosen was found to
provide a good balance of these phenomena, though it is not necessarily an
optimal value. Eq. (14) updates the agent’s bearing. Based on our agent’s size
and behavioral studies that suggest leeches crawl their body length about
every 6.4 s21, our simulated leech moves at s = 1.56 cm/s. Using Eq. (15), Eq.
(16), and its new bearing, the agent calculates the distances {Δx, Δy} that its
center of mass travels, and updates its center of mass. Eq. (17) updates the
positions of the agent’s sensors, ﬁrst by rotating them around the previous
center of mass, and then by translating them by {Δx, Δy}.
If no neural population exceeded the neural threshold, which can occur
especially during the 6-second windows when sensors are inactive, the agent
chooses a uniformly random integer angle on the interval [ − 45°, + 45°] for
Ωinterp, rotates via Eqs. (13) and (14), and then has a 40% chance of not
moving forward at all. This model behavior is inspired by the real behavior
of the leech when a stimulus is too weak to be detected, and the leech may
not move or may turn randomly. The 45° angle was chosen for simplicity,
but can be adjusted in future studies that aim to improve model ﬁdelity.
We note here that this model of motion is greatly simpliﬁed from a
leech’s actual crawling or swimming behavior. We plan to incorporate more
detailed and physically relevant forms of locomotion in future studies.
Simulation setup and quantiﬁcation
We compared the performance of our simulated agent to real-world leech
behavior observed in Harley et al.17. In those in vivo experiments, leeches
explored a circular arena of diameter 83 cm, with water ﬁlled to a depth of
2 cm. Our simulations take place in an unbounded plane, but our plots are
cropped to show an arena of approximately the same diameter. Thus, our
simulated leeches may leave the plotted region and return to it. Agents begin
~76 cm from their target.
We gave the simulated agent 180 seconds to explore and sense the
behavioral arena. It had to stay within 4 cm of the target location for at least
5 s to count as a successful ﬁnd. While these times were shortened from the
animal experiments to reduce simulation run time, they produce similar
trajectories.
A point source gravity wave propagated outwards from a northeastern
position in the arena (Fig. 2), while our agent always started at a southwestern position relative to the wave source.
To compare our model to Harley et al.17, we ran our model for a range
of water wave stimulus frequencies (1 Hz to 24 Hz, in 1 Hz increments) and
several neural thresholds that followed a pseudo logarithmic sequence
(0.001, 0.005, 0.02, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1). In addition to these values, we performed
a set of trials at a neural threshold of 0.15 to visualize ﬁnd rate results at an
intermediate value between 0.05 and 0.2. All trials were performed at a depth
of 2 cm to match the live-animal work done by Harley et al.17. Navigational
performance was measured by computing the ﬁnd rate across 50 trials for
each parameter combination. A ‘ﬁnd’ is deﬁned as the agent successfully
locating the source, and the ﬁnd rate is the proportion of successful ﬁnds
across all of the trials for a given combination of water wave frequency and
neural threshold. When a ﬁnd occurs during the simulation, the trial terminates and is marked as a success. Similar to Harley et al.17, we plot ﬁnd rate
against wave frequency so that we may compare our simulation to real
