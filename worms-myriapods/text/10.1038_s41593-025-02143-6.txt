# Cold and lithium delay forgetting of olfactory memories in Caenorhabditis elegans

## Abstract
The persistence of memory reflects not only how it is formed but also how forgetting is delayed. The mechanisms controlling forgetting remain obscure, and in particular it is unclear to what degree this process is actively regulated. We discovered that cold exposure delays forgetting of specific olfactory memories by more than eight-fold and that adaptation to cold abolishes this effect. To study the underlying mechanism, we performed RNA sequencing, mutant analyses and pharmacological assays. Here we show that regulation of membrane properties underlies the presence and absence of cold-induced delayed forgetting. Furthermore, lithium can also delay forgetting in cold-sensitive worms but not in cold-tolerant worms; this effect involves downregulation of the diacylglycerol pathway in AWC neurons and long-term suppression of activity in the downstream AIY interneurons. Thus, the genetic tractability of worms might be harnessed to study the mechanism of action of lithium and cold exposure and, more fundamentally, how memory is stored and lost.

## Main
The nervous system switches between different states, and the tradeoffs between conflicting needs such as sleep versus wakefulness1, fight–flight or rest–digest2,3 have been studied at both the physiological and behavioral levels4,5. Many studies over the years have demonstrated that imbalance between brain states can be pathological—for example, it could cause mood disorders6 and accelerate neurodegeneration7—but it can also lead to extraordinary abilities, such as hyperthymestic syndrome (highly superior memory)8 or savant syndrome (exceptional ability in one domain and impairments in other domains)9.
One important tradeoff is that between remembering and forgetting. Both processes are adaptive and enable organisms to flexibly respond to changing environments. Obviously, the inability to properly remember has many downsides (for example, in dementia), but also forgetting, when disrupted, can become maladaptive. For example, in posttraumatic stress disorder, memories become hyperaccessible, persistent and intrusive10,11.
The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans is an excellent model for examining tradeoffs in the nervous system and for studying mechanisms of behavioral plasticity12, as it can learn associations13 using a relatively simple and fully mapped nervous system that is composed of only 302 neurons14. The C. elegans genome encodes more than 1,000 olfactory receptors15, and the worm is innately attracted to a variety of odors (for example, butanone, benzaldehyde, pentanedione and diacetyl)16. The classical assay for studying memory and forgetting in C. elegans (conducted for over 20 years)17 entails weakening of the worm’s inborn attraction to appetitive odorants by pairing the odors with a short period of starvation. After such pairing, the worms learn to associate the otherwise attractive odor with stress; however, the association is typically forgotten quickly so that the worms again exhibit strong attraction to the odor17–19.
It remains unclear whether forgetting is an active, biologically regulated process or whether memory is lost passively (for example, refs. 10,20). It is possible that the rate of forgetting is controlled and adjusted according to an animal’s needs, which, in turn, are influenced by the environment. One property of the environment that might affect memory is temperature. Across phyla, different mechanisms have evolved that enable animals to sense and respond to alterations in temperature21,22. C. elegans remembers its cultivation temperature, and upon sensing temperature changes, modulates its temperature-seeking behavior accordingly23,24. Moreover, the worm possesses a plastic, genetically encoded mechanism to acquire a ‘cold-tolerant state’ that enables it to survive in very cold environments: adult C. elegans kept at 15 °C for more than 5 hours can survive for 48 hours in a temperature of 2 °C, whereas worms kept at 20 °C or higher are not cold adapted and do not survive in a 2 °C environment25–27. Worms in the cold-tolerant state exhibit an increase in phospholipid unsaturation and enhanced membrane fluidity (and there are probably additional protective mechanisms that are not fully understood)25,26. Elegant studies further elucidated the molecular pathways underlying this adaptation strategy and revealed that systemic cold tolerance is, in part, regulated by the nervous system27–29. However, commitment to a specific state can have terminal consequences involving tradeoffs between tissues and systems: in cold-sensitive worms, acute cold shock triggers a transfer of vitellogenic lipids from the soma to the germline, enhancing progeny fitness at the cost of parental survival30.
In this paper, we first describe an observation linking forgetting and cold sensitivity: although worms normally forget olfactory memories after 2–3 hours, we found simple conditions in which memory maintenance of olfactory memories can be extended by at least eight-fold. Specifically, we show that olfactory memories are retained for as long as worms are kept on ice. However, this phenomenon does not occur in cold-tolerant worms. We identify the diacylglycerol (DAG) pathway as integral to cold-induced delayed forgetting and show that the internal ‘forgetting switch’ responds to lithium, a mood-stabilizing drug used in the treatment of bipolar disorder. In summary, the poet W. B. Yeats wrote that ‘All that is personal soon rots; it must be packed in ice or salt’. Indeed, in C. elegans, we show that memories are preserved on ice and in lithium salt.

## Results

## Cold delays forgetting
In an attempt to examine whether forgetting can be slowed down, we tested if cold temperatures affect memory kinetics. We conditioned worms to reduce their attraction toward the AWC-sensed odorant butanone (see Supplementary Fig. 1a for concentration rationale), as previously described31 (Methods), by pairing butanone with starvation (see schemes in Fig. 1a,b). Notably, although under normal conditions, worms typically forget olfactory memories after 2–3 hours, we found that worms maintain the acquired memory for as long as they are kept on ice (0–1 °C) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1e). Similar results were obtained with two additional odorants that are also sensed by the AWC neurons: benzaldehyde and pentanedione (Supplementary Fig. 1b,c). Different lines of evidence point to the conclusion that the cool worms remain unattracted to the odor owing to the training (pairing to starvation) and not because of damage caused by the cold: (1) The naive worms (unconditioned control group) retained their normal attraction toward butanone although they were also kept on ice in the exact same way. (2) Butanone-conditioned worms that were kept on ice showed no change in preferences for benzaldehyde, a different odor that is also sensed by the AWC neurons17 (Fig. 1d). In summary, we conclude that cooling worms on ice dramatically extends the retention of memory.
Next, we performed experiments to distinguish between two alternative possibilities: (1) Forgetting is temporarily delayed on ice (in this case, the prediction is that memory will be lost quickly upon return to a normal growth temperature), or (2) cold leads to memory consolidation and long-term memory. We found that the worms forget 3 hours after they were transferred from the ice to room temperature (Fig. 1e), and therefore we conclude that cooling worms on ice delays forgetting only temporarily and that the memory is not consolidated.

## Downregulation of PAQR-2 and IGLR-2 delays forgetting
Cold temperatures increase membrane rigidity32. We therefore hypothesized that changes in membrane rigidity could lead to delayed forgetting, because the membrane’s rigidity affects neuronal activity and homeostasis (for example, vesicles release dynamics33), and would thus extend the time it takes neurons to return to their pre-learning state.
PAQR-2 (ortholog of the adiponectin receptors AdipoR1/R2) and its functional partner IGLR-2 are C. elegans plasma membrane proteins that detect and respond to increased membrane rigidity. Together, these proteins facilitate fatty acid desaturation to restore membrane fluidity34–36. Mutants defective in these genes were found to exhibit a highly rigid membrane.
To test the hypothesis that different factors that affect membrane rigidity, similar to cold, may delay forgetting, we tested if the membrane rigidity sensor mutants exhibit delayed forgetting. We found that both iglr-2 and paqr-2 mutants delay forgetting in room temperatures (Fig. 2a,b). FLD-1, a transmembrane protein in C. elegans, regulates membrane fluidity by limiting the incorporation of highly fluidizing long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids into phospholipids, limiting acute increase in membrane rigidity. Loss of FLD-1 suppresses the membrane defects of iglr-2 mutants37. We found that fld-1(et46) mutation suppresses iglr-2(et34) induction of delayed forgetting (Fig. 2d) and that fld-1(et46) mutants do not delay forgetting on ice (Fig. 2c). This contributes to our notion that membrane rigidity plays a crucial role in cold-induced memory retention. ## Cold tolerance abolishes delayed forgetting on ice
We next examined whether the worms nevertheless possess the capacity to regulate the rates of forgetting in different temperatures according to their needs. To examine this possibility, we examined forgetting in worms acclimated to low temperatures.
C. elegans assume a cold-tolerant state when acclimated to low temperatures28,29. Specifically, Ohta et al.27 showed that a drastic improvement in survival in an otherwise lethal cold temperature (typically 48 hours at 2 °C) occurs when the cultivation temperature is changed from 20 °C to 15 °C (the worms must be allowed to adapt to 15 °C for at least a couple of hours). Moreover, these authors discovered that the acquired cold tolerance is plastic and is lost 2–3 hours after the cultivation temperature is changed again from 15 °C to a higher cultivation temperature.
We tested if there is a link between the internal processes that give rise to the cold tolerance state and the mechanisms that delay forgetting. We found that worms that were kept at 15 °C overnight before conditioning ceased to delay forgetting on ice (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, when worms that were cultivated overnight at 15 °C were transferred back to 20 °C (for 2 hours, to lose the acquired cold tolerance), they once again delayed forgetting on ice (Fig. 3b). Thus, the worms can switch between two states: a cold-sensitive state, during which forgetting is delayed on ice, and a cold-tolerant state, during which the memory is lost. ## RNA sequencing implicates the DAG pathway in delayed forgetting on ice
To identify genes that are involved in delayed forgetting on ice, we leveraged the link that we identified between forgetting and the cold tolerance state of the worms. We sequenced messenger RNA from six different experimental conditions (three independent biological replications were performed): we compared the gene expression profiles of conditioned and naive worms that were (1) cultivated at 20 °C (worms in the cold-sensitive or cold-tolerant state that delay forgetting on ice), (2) cultivated overnight at 15 °C (worms in the cold-tolerant state that do not delay forgetting on ice) or (3) cultivated at 15 °C overnight and then transferred to 20 °C for 2 hours (these worms lose their cold tolerance state and become cold sensitive again and, thus, delay forgetting on ice).
We identified genes that differentiate between worms that are present in the cold-sensitive and cold-tolerant states (see the experimental design in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 2a,b, the bioinformatic pipeline in Methods and the complete list of genes in Supplementary Table 1). We hypothesized that among the 3,182 genes upregulated in worms that exhibited the cold-sensitive state compared to the cold-tolerant state, there might be genes whose action promotes delayed forgetting on ice, whereas among the 3,284 downregulated genes, there might be genes whose action interrupts delayed forgetting on ice. Although we identified genes whose expression differentiates between worms that are in the cold-sensitive state (that delay forgetting) and worms that are in the cold-tolerant state (that do not delay forgetting), no differential gene expression was observed between naive and conditioned worms (specifically in the groups that delay forgetting on ice). A Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that the lists of enriched functions, which are found for upregulated or downregulated genes in worms that delay forgetting, are almost a mirror image of one another. The list of the genes that were upregulated in worms that delay forgetting on ice exhibited enrichment of genes that perform germline functions (Discussion and Supplementary Fig. 2c) and depletion of genes involved in neuronal functions. We observed the exact opposite pattern in the list of the downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Notably, we found that multiple genes (goa-1, egl-30, egl-8, unc-13, pkc-1 and unc-64) in the DAG pathway38 (Fig. 4b) are differentially regulated in worms that delay forgetting on ice. DAG is known as an important membranal regulator of different synaptic processes that are connected to memory and learning—for example, vesicle priming, docking and release39—also in C. elegans18,40. In particular, DAG signaling was found to be essential for adaptation to the odorants butanone, benzaldehyde and isoamyl alcohol and to salts and also for butanone appetitive learning and mechanosensory habituation by affecting synaptic vesicles release18,40–43.
GOA-1 is encoded by an ortholog of the mammalian Gi/o class of Gα subunits and negatively regulates the Gαq protein EGL-30, whose activation induces an increase in the concentration of DAG44. Loss of goa-1 function impairs adaptation to AWC-sensed odorants, leading to persistently high chemotaxis responses (Chemotaxis Index (CI))40. Our sequencing results indicated that goa-1 mRNA levels are significantly upregulated in worms that delay forgetting on ice, whereas egl-30 levels are significantly downregulated (scheme in Fig. 4b). We, thus, tested forgetting in multiple mutants in this pathway, on and off ice. We found that unlike wild-type animals, goa-1 mutants do not delay forgetting on ice (Fig. 4d). However, it should be noted that goa-1 mutant animals exhibit very weak learning capabilities (as noted previously by Matsuki et al.40). Similarly, we found that transgenic animals that overexpress egl-30 are defective in their ability to delay forgetting on ice (Fig. 4e). We found that additional loss-of-function egl-30 mutants are dramatically cold sensitive and do not survive 3 hours on ice and have reduced chemosensation and learning capabilities. Nevertheless, these mutants are still capable of learning and exhibit delayed forgetting in room temperature (Fig. 4f). DAG kinase catalyzes the conversion of DAG to phosphatidic acid and, thus, mediates reduction of DAG levels in the cell membrane45. We tested a loss-of-function allele of dgk-1, which encodes DAG kinase, and found that dgk-1 mutant worms do not delay forgetting on ice (Fig. 4g).
Lastly, we treated worms with a DAG analog, phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA), to test if indeed cold delays forgetting by reducing DAG levels and if this effect could be reversed by drugs and not only genetically. We found that supplementing the worms with the DAG analog canceled cold-induced delayed forgetting (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Together, all these experiments supported the hypothesis that DAG accumulation antagonizes cold-induced delayed forgetting (see summary in Fig. 4c).

## Lithium delays forgetting in cold-sensitive worms
The establishment of systemic cold tolerance is known to be regulated by the nervous system. More specifically, it was found that the ASJ neurons are the main regulators of this non-cell-autonomous effect27. It was recently shown that lithium causes a selective neuronal dysfunction in the ASJ neurons46. To examine if ASJ affects forgetting, we treated the worms with lithium chloride salt (LiCl). We subjected young adult worms to 15 mM LiCl overnight, as previously described46, removed them from the lithium and then trained them to avoid butanone. Notably, we found that worms that were pretreated with lithium retained the memory even 5 hours after training in room temperature (Fig. 5a). Next, we tested if lithium indeed delays forgetting by interfering with the function of the ASJ neurons, as we originally hypothesized based on lithium’s known effect over ASJ46. It was previously shown that inhibition of the ASJ neurons by lithium depends on the ASJ-specific gene ssu-1 (ref.46). However, we found that lithium delays forgetting even in ssu-1 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Therefore, it is unlikely that lithium extends memory by inhibiting the ASJ neurons. In light of all these results, and also because we found that ASJ-ablated worms47 did not consistently delay forgetting (Supplementary Fig. 4b), we reasoned that lithium delays forgetting via an ASJ-independent mechanism. Lithium affects many biological processes, and it is, therefore, difficult to characterize all of its possible effects. Still, it is well established that lithium inhibits various phosphoinositol phosphates that function in inositol phosphate metabolism, such as IMPase and inositol polyphosphatase 1-phosphatase, which influence DAG turnover48,49. The ability of lithium to deplete inositol was suggested to be crucial for its therapeutic outcome50,51. As we showed that DAG is a key factor in the regulation of delayed forgetting, we tested whether lithium-delayed forgetting depends on DAG signaling pathway. We found that lithium-induced delayed forgetting is suppressed in dgk-1 mutants, in which DAG levels are elevated (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Furthermore, exposing lithium-treated worms to the DAG analog PMA during training prevented lithium-induced delayed forgetting, although it also reduced the learning capacity of the worms (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 3b). These experiments suggest that lithium-induced delayed forgetting, similar to cold shock, depends on a reduction in DAG.

## Delayed forgetting correlates with reduced DAG levels in the AWC sensory neurons, but a prolonged suppression of neuronal activity can be detected only in the downstream AIY neurons
Next, we conducted RNA sequencing and analyzed the transcriptional changes of cold-tolerant and cold-sensitive worms in response to lithium (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5). We performed gene enrichment analysis and searched for genes that are differentially expressed in response to lithium treatment specifically in cold-sensitive worms, as this is the condition in which worms delay forgetting (Supplementary Fig. 5a,b).
Interestingly, our Gene Ontology enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5b,c) revealed a compelling similarity in gene expression patterns between the worms that delay forgetting after lithium treatment and the worms that delay forgetting on ice. Specifically, in the group of genes that were downregulated, we observed an enrichment of genes associated with neuronal or somatic functions and depletion of germline-related terms (as observed also in cold-sensitive worms on ice; Supplementary Fig. 2c,d). Conversely, the group of genes that were upregulated showed an increase in germline functions (again, as did the cold-sensitive worms on ice; Supplementary Fig. 2c,d). This similarity in the transcriptional profile patterns in the worms that delay forgetting suggests a common molecular basis for these phenomena that enables memory retention.
As described above, we did not find evidence that lithium delays forgetting via its effect on the ASJ neurons (Supplementary Fig. 4a,b). To examine if delayed forgetting is caused by a reduction in DAG levels specifically in the AWC sensory neurons, we tested goa-1 mutants (that do not delay forgetting on ice) and goa-1 mutants in which the expression of goa-1 was rescued only in the AWC neurons under the control of the odr-1 promotor41. First, we found that rescuing goa-1 specifically in the AWC neurons was sufficient to enable goa-1 mutants to delay forgetting on ice (Fig. 6a). Second, whereas lithium-treated goa-1 mutants are severely impaired in learning, rescuing the expression of goa-1 in the AWC neurons significantly improved their learning ability and restored their capacity to delay forgetting in response to lithium (Fig. 6b), although not fully to wild-type levels. Although lithium likely affects multiple tissues (and different neurons), these results support the conclusion that maintaining low DAG levels in the AWC sensory neurons is required for delayed forgetting, both on ice and in response to lithium. As we have shown (Fig. 2a), iglr-2 mutants, which are associated with increased membrane rigidity, display delayed forgetting at room temperature. Inositol contributes to membrane dynamics and synaptic transmission indirectly by serving as a precursor in the phosphoinositide pathway, leading to the generation of DAG and other signaling lipids. Notably, in different studies, lithium was suggested to inhibit key enzymes involved in inositol phosphate metabolism, including IMPase and inositol polyphosphate 1-phosphatase51,52. To test whether inositol treatment could rescue the memory phenotype in iglr-2 mutants, we treated worms with inositol and found that they no longer exhibited delayed forgetting at room temperature (Fig. 6c). ## Discussion
Our work revealed that worms have an internal, thermoregulated forgetting ‘switch’ that lends itself to manipulation by cold immersion and lithium. Among the many effects of lithium, we found that in addition to delaying forgetting, lithium changes the way the worms move and explore their surroundings. We first noticed, by examining the tracks that the worms leave on agar plates, that even after the worms are removed from lithium, they continue to move differently. Using automated Worm Tracking54,55, we found that worms that were treated overnight with lithium display distinct behavior (Supplementary Fig. 6a,b) and in particular, elevation in angular velocity (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Analysis of their movement, together with our memory retention assay, may be used in the future to explore lithium’s mechanism of action. In addition, we hypothesize that switching between the two ‘states’, which affect memory retention and movement, may also affect other traits as well. As lithium controls this transition, we speculate that the power of the C. elegans model might be used to study the fluctuations between internal states that occur in bipolar disorder (characterized also by changes in energy and activity levels56). It is obvious that many aspects of the pathology are unique to humans and will never be amenable for characterization in simple model organisms, but perhaps some of the most basic effects of lithium on cellular processes could be explored.
Our study exemplifies the profound ways by which temperature affects neuronal functions (in this case, forgetting). Indeed, unsurprisingly, many studies have linked cold to changes in cognitive performance57,58. It would be interesting to continue and examine how memory loss is affected by temperature also in other animals and more specifically, whether and how lithium and the DAG pathway may influence cold-induced effects in other organisms. We note that the genes involved in the DAG pathway are not only implicated in forgetting but also play a role in the learning process itself, as was shown in the past18,40 and as can be deduced from the fact that mutants in these genes display altered learning baselines that are further affected by lithium and temperature acclimation. There is vast literature on temperature adaptation in different animals (far beyond the scope of this discussion), some of which exhibit remarkable cold tolerance abilities. Possible links across the tree of life between different strategies of cold adaptation (for example, hibernation) and memory capabilities would be fascinating to explore.
Our results support an active model for forgetting (see schematic model in Fig. 8), and the Gene Ontology enrichment analyses further suggest that delayed forgetting is supported by upregulation of germline functions and downregulation of neuronal functions (Supplementary Fig. 2c), hinting at possible system-level tradeoffs. These raise interesting ecological and evolutionary questions that will hopefully be addressed in future studies. For example, why do worms forget fast when they are perfectly capable of maintaining memories for longer periods of time? What are the tradeoffs of holding memories long term? Do the worms forget because of energy considerations or perhaps optimize some other function relevant to their ecology and life history? ## Methods

## Ethics statement
The only animals used in this study are C. elegans nematodes, which are not subject to animal welfare regulations. No ethical approval was required.

## Cultivation of worms
Worms were grown and maintained at 20 °C using standard protocol59 (unless otherwise stated) on Nematode Growth Medium (NGM) plates seeded with OP50 Escherichia coli (obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetic Center (CGC)).

## Worm strains
Wild-type worms were Bristol strain N2 hermaphrodites. QC136; iglr-2(et34), QC129; paqr-2(tm3410), MT2426; goa-1(n1134), PS2444; dpy-20(e1282) IV;syIs36; syIs36 [(pLB2) egl-30(+) + pBS + (pMH86) dpy-20(+)], KP1097, dgk-1(nu62), CX17256, kyIs722 [str-2p::GCaMP5(D380Y) + elt-2::mCherry], obtained from the CGC. CX17256, kyIs722 [str-2p::GCaMP5(D380Y)+ elt-2::mCherry], CX16890, kyEx4857(mod-1::GCaMP5; myo-3::mCherry), gifted by Cornelia Bargmann. BFF318; goa-1(n1134); qjEx30[odr-1p::goa-1+myo-3p::gfp] was created by crossing MT2426;goa-1(n1134) and QD130; goa-1(n1134); tir-1(tm3036); qjEx30[odr-1p::goa-1+myo-3p::gfp] worms, gifted by Takeshi Ishihara41 (see below for more details).

## Aversive olfactory learning
Gravid adult worms (10–20 individuals) were randomly selected and placed on NGM plates seeded with OP50 to facilitate egg laying over a period of 4–12 hours, after which they were removed. Synchronized populations of worms, upon reaching day 1 of adulthood, were harvested using S-basal buffer (0.1 M NaCl, 0.05 M KPO4) in preparation for subsequent training procedure.

## Butanone training
The protocol was adapted with modifications from Cho et al.31. In brief, day 1 adult worms were rinsed three times with S-basal buffer and transferred to 15-ml glass vials (20 × 150 mm; Glassco), each containing 3 ml of S-basal buffer. For the conditioning process, 30 µl of butanone diluted to 100 mM in ethanol was added to the buffer for the conditioned group (resulting in 1 mM butanone in training vial), whereas to the naive group vials, 30 µl of ethanol was added as control. Subsequently, the worms were maintained in the liquid for either 90 minutes or 45 minutes for the cold tolerance assays only (Fig. 3a,b and Supplementary Fig. 4c). Shortening the conditioning time to 45 minutes for the cold tolerance experiments was necessary because cold tolerance diminishes after 2 hours at room temperature. Nonetheless, this change did not cause any significant difference in memory formation or retention. After conditioning, worms were washed three times with S-basal buffer and allocated into designated groups for further experimental assays, including ice placement or room temperature maintenance.

## Ice placement
For the cohorts assigned to cold exposure, 15-ml glass vials (20 × 150 mm; Glassco) prefilled with 2 ml of S-basal buffer were precooled on ice prior to worm transfer. Worms, after undergoing three washes with S-basal buffer, were placed into the chilled vials. For short-term ice exposure, vials were kept directly on ice. For extended cold treatment (of 16 hours or 24 hours), the vials were positioned within a large cooler filled with ice, which was subsequently stored in a refrigerator set to 4 °C. Worms treated on ice take a while to fully recuperate but remain capable of roaming and moving toward the odorant at a similar rate. Moreover, the absolute number of worms observed under ice treatment conditions was similar to those counted in room temperature conditions or immediately after training. Although, after 3 hours, some residual memory remains in certain experiments, we chose the 3-hour timepoint for most experiments as it enables identification of conditions (and genetic backgrounds) that delay forgetting. In certain cases, we also tested memory retention over longer time periods (for example, at 5 hours or 16 hours), but, in most cases, this was not possible because the worms become sick when kept on ice for long (the learned aversion paradigm requires starving the worms, and this could be problematic if extended for too long).

## Room temperature maintenance
After conditioning, worms designated for room temperature conditions were transferred to 15-ml glass vials (20 × 150 mm; Glassco) containing 2 ml of S-basal buffer. These vials were maintained at ambient laboratory conditions. Control conditions for extended exposure (16 hours) were as follows: The control group for the 16-hour duration was incubated at a stable 20 °C, using a temperature-controlled incubator to ensure consistency.

## Rationale for 3-hour timepoint
The 2–3-hour post-training timepoint was chosen based on classical studies (for example, Colbert and Bargmann17) showing near-complete forgetting after approximately 2 hours. Although some residual memory can persist, this timepoint effectively reveals conditions or genetic backgrounds that delay forgetting. Longer intervals (for example, 5 hours or 16 hours) were tested in selected cases but are not always feasible due to animal poor survival, especially when prolonged starvation or cold exposure is required in combination with specific genetic backgrounds and pharmacological interventions.

## Chemotaxis to tested odors
Chemotaxis toward the tested odors was evaluated using 90-mm-diameter plates filled with 14 ml of chemotaxis agar (comprising 2% agar, 5 mM phosphate buffer at pH 6.0, 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgSO4), prepared 24 hours prior to the assay. Prior to initiating chemotaxis, worms were washed once with a chemotaxis buffer (5 mM phosphate buffer at pH 6.0, 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgSO4). For the assay, two 1-µl aliquots of the odorant (diluted 1:1,000 in ethanol) and an ethanol control were spotted on opposite sides of the plate. Additionally, 1 µl of 1 M sodium azide (NaN3) was placed adjacent to the odorant and control spots to immobilize animals upon reaching the target area, ensuring accurate counting. Worms were centrally placed on the chemotaxis plate, and excess liquid was gently removed using Kimwipes. The assay commenced with populations ranging from 50 to 400 individuals per plate. Once all the worms were immobilized, the plates were transferred to a 4 °C refrigerator for storage. Quantification was conducted no later than 2 days after the assay. Each experiment was conducted at least three times. Plates with fewer than 50 animals reaching the designated odor locations at the assay’s conclusion were omitted from the final analysis. Quantification involved counting the number of worms at the odorant versus control spots, with the CI, calculated as (number of worms in odor − number of worms in control) / (number of worms in odor + number of worms in control). The LI, indicating the differential response between naive and conditioned populations within the same technical replicate, was determined by LI = CInaive − CIconditioned. Delayed forgetting is characterized by a similarity to trained worms in T0 (immediately post training). Specifically, the difference in CI between naive and conditioned groups (also evident in the LI value) is similar to that observed in trained worms examined immediately after training (T0).

## Quantification and analysis of behavioral differences
Behavioral assay data were analyzed focusing on two primary metrics: the CI and the LI. CI quantifies the preference of the worm population toward the odor presented on each assay plate. To assess the significance of CI differences, conditioned groups were compared to their corresponding naive groups at each experimental timepoint. Statistical analysis was chosen based on data distribution and model assumptions: t-test was applied for normally distributed data and variance equality, whereas Mann–Whitney test was used for data not meeting these criteria. LI represents the behavioral change attributed to conditioning, calculated as the difference in CI between a conditioned group and a naive group at a single technical repeat. For evaluations encompassing three or more timepoints where data adhered to statistical prerequisites, one-way ANOVA with subsequent Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was conducted. In cases where data did not fit one-way ANOVA conditions, Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test, was applied. For binary timepoint analyses, the choice between t-test and Mann–Whitney test was dictated by the data’s distribution characteristics. All statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

## Lithium treatment
Young adult worms were harvested from standard NGM plates and subjected to an overnight (approximately 16-hour) treatment by placing them on NGM plates supplemented with 15 mM LiCl (Sigma-Aldrich, L9650). The lithium treatment protocol was adapted from Meisel and Kim46. Plates used were preseeded with E. coli OP50 bacteria 1–2 days prior to the initiation of lithium treatment, ensuring optimal conditions for the assay.

## Inositol treatment
Young adult worms were harvested from standard NGM plates and subjected to an overnight (approximately 16-hour) treatment by placing them on NGM plates supplemented with 200 mM myo-inositol (Sigma-Aldrich, L5125). The inositol treatment protocol was adapted from Tanizawa et al.51. Plates used were preseeded with E. coli OP50 bacteria 1–2 days prior to the initiation of inositol treatment, ensuring optimal conditions for the assay.

## PMA administration
Synchronized adult worms were exposed to PMA (0.05 mg ml−1; Sigma-Aldrich, P8139) or an ethanol solvent as a control. This exposure occurred under two distinct conditions: either for a 2-hour period prior to the conditioning step when placed on NGM plates infused with PMA or concurrently during the 90-minute conditioning phase placed inside S-basal buffer in 15-ml glass vials (20 × 150 mm; Glassco). During conditioning, worms were presented with either 1 mM butanone (for the conditioned group) or an equivalent volume of ethanol (for the naive group) in S-basal buffer, as outlined in the section detailing aversive olfactory training.

## goa-1 rescue in AWC
The following strain was created by crossing goa-1(n1134, MT2426) to QD130 (goa-1(n1134);tir-1(tm3036);qjEx30[odr-1p::goa-1+myo-3p::gfp]), kindly gifted by the Takeshi Ishihara laboratory41. To identify and isolate goa-1, the following primers were used followed by Sanger sequencing: 5′-AGCTGCACCACATACAGTGA-3′, 5′-TCGGACGTTCTATGGGACAA-3′. To identify tir-1(tm3036), the following primers were used: 5′-AAGTGGCCTCCTCTCCAGAC-3′, 5′-GCACCCAATCCTCACAGTTA-3′.

## mRNA library preparation and sequencing for exploring the transcriptional basis of delayed forgetting on ice
Worms were first trained with the odorant butanone and then placed on ice for 2 hours before RNA extraction using TRIzol (worms were placed in TRIzol immediately upon removal from ice). mRNA sequencing was performed to investigate gene expression profiles under six distinct experimental conditions as described in Fig. 4a. The worms were either trained or not, and the three different cultivation regimes were as follows: (1) worms cultivated at 20 °C, representing the group of cold-sensitive individuals that exhibit delayed forgetting when exposed to ice; (2) worms cultivated overnight at 15 °C, cold-tolerant individuals that do not delay forgetting on ice; and (3) worms initially cultivated at 15 °C overnight before being transferred to 20 °C for 2 hours, a treatment intended to reverse the cold tolerance acquired from the overnight exposure.
cDNA was prepared with either the SMART-Seq HT Kit or the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra. A total of 10 ng of RNA was used as input and processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Double-stranded cDNA was amplified by a 10-cycle polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for SMART-Seq HT samples and 12 cycles for SMART-Seq v4 Ultra samples. cDNA quantity and quality were verified using a TapeStation 2200 (Agilent). One nanogram of cDNA was used as input for preparation of sequencing libraries using the Nextera XT DNA Sequencing Kit (Illumina). Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality and concentration were verified with TapeStation 2200, and libraries were pooled according to molarity for sequencing on the NextSeq 500.

## Exploring the transcriptional basis of delayed forgetting after lithium treatment
Worms were placed overnight on plates containing either 15 mM LiCl for the experimental group or NGM as control. The worms had been cultivated overnight at one of two temperatures: 15 °C, to induce cold tolerance, or 20 °C, to keep the worms cold sensitive. Worms were then collected in TRIzol for RNA extraction. RNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II directional RNA library prep kit for Illumina according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quality and concentration were verified with TapeStation 2200, and libraries were pooled according to molarity for sequencing on the NextSeq 500.

## mRNA sequencing data analysis
The output FASTQ files were assessed for quality with FastQC60 version 0.11.5. Reads were aligned to version ce11 of the C. elegans genome using HISAT2 version 2.0.5 (ref. 61). The aligned reads were next counted and assigned to genes using the Python-based script HTSeq-count62 and the Ensembl-provided gff file (release 105), using the ‘intersection-nonempty’ mode and ignoring secondary alignments. Next, we used the HTSeq version 0.11.12 output as an input file for differential expression with R DESeq2 (ref. 63) version 1.30.1. Genes were regarded as differentially expressed if they passed the criterion of false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.1. Further analysis was performed using the Python-based package RNAlysis (Python package version 2.1.1)64.

## Calcium imaging and analysis
Microfluidic chip fabrication and calcium imaging setup were performed as described65. Worms were recorded in response to a stimulus of 1 µM butanone. Imaging was carried out using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with a ×40 magnification water objective. Imaging frequency was 6.6667 Hz, total imaging duration was 80 seconds and stimulus duration was 20 seconds. The fluorescence intensity of GCaMP5a was measured using FIJI. Regions of interest for the cell bodies or for zone 2 were manually drawn, and the mean intensity of the gray values was measured. MATLAB or Python 3.12 was used for downstream data processing. For each worm individually, the baseline fluorescence level (F0) was calculated by averaging the mean gray value of 185 frames before stimulus delivery. For each frame, ΔF was obtained by subtracting F0 from the value of that frame. This value was then divided by F0, thereby normalizing individual fluorescence baseline levels and fluctuation variability (ΔF/F0). The maximal values during stimulus delivery (AIY) or the minimal values during stimulus delivery and maximal values after stimulus delivery (AWC) were then identified and extracted to GraphPad Prism for statistical comparisons (Mann–Whitney or multiple comparisons test). For AWC imaging, CX17256 worms were employed to investigate the response of AWC neurons to butanone exposure, after lithium treatment. Young adult worms were cultivated overnight on either 15 mM lithium plates or control plates (see lithium treatment section) before assessment. For AWC imaging after training, CX17256 worms were cultivated overnight on either 15 mM lithium plates or control plates. Worms were trained for the odor butanone (see the aversive olfactory training section) and then kept at room temperature for delayed assessment after 3 hours. For AIY imaging after training, CX16890 worms were cultivated overnight on either 15 mM lithium plates or control plates. A fraction of the population was tested as baseline, and another fraction was trained for the odor butanone or control (see the aversive olfactory training section) for 90 minutes. Then, a fraction of the animals were recorded as post-training group (up to 1 hour after training) while the rest of the animals were kept at either room temperature or ice for 3 hours before recording to assess the effect of temperature on AIY response. For details on the statistical tests used, see the legend of Fig. 7.

## Experimental design
No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar and fall within the same order of magnitude as those reported in previous publications16,31, with each chemotaxis assay including large populations of animals that were segregated into treatment groups randomly. Each experiment included technical replicates and was conducted on at least three independent biological replicates using different populations of animals across separate days to ensure reproducibility in accordance with standard behavioral assay methodology16,31. Key results were also independently replicated by several individuals. Data collection and analysis were not performed blinded to the conditions of the experiments, aside from the calcium imaging experiments.

## Figure Fig. 8: Schematic model: cold and lithium affect membrane rigidity and DAG signaling, which integrate environmental cues to control forgetting in C. elegans.This model illustrates how environmental and molecular cues converge to regulate the rate of olfactory memory forgetting in C. elegans. Worms alternate between a cold-sensitive state (20 °C), in which cold or lithium delays forgetting, and a cold-tolerant state (15 °C), in which these cues are ineffective. The activators of delayed forgetting that were identified are cold exposure, lithium, rigid membrane mutants and reduced DAG signaling. The suppressors that reinstate normal forgetting rates are cold acclimation (15 °C), restored membrane fluidity or interventions that increase DAG. Convergent signaling in AWC sensory neurons, decreased DAG or increased membrane rigidity are proposed to reduce synaptic release, producing long-term suppression of AIY activity. Together, the model shows how a reversible physiological state, integrating membrane biophysics with signaling dynamics, determines whether memories are forgotten or retained.
