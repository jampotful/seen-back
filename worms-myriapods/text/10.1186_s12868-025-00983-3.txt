The vab-1/Eph receptor and ephrins efn-1 and efn-4 are required for C. elegans chemosensory and thermosensory circuit assembly and function.

# The vab-1/Eph receptor and ephrins efn-1 and efn-4 are required for C. elegans chemosensory and thermosensory circuit assembly and function

## Abstract
BackgroundThe nematode Caenorhabditis elegans displays many distinct behaviors, making it an ideal system to understand the relationship between genes, cells, and behavioral output. In C. elegans, thermal information is primarily detected by the AFD sensory neurons, with minor input from the AWC neurons. Similarly, ionic solutes such as sodium chloride are detected by the ASE chemosensory neurons. Both sensory neuron pairs synapse primarily onto the AIY interneurons.ResultsWe show that AIYL/R ventral contact is mediated via the ephrin gene efn-1 in a linear genetic pathway with vab-1. In addition, we show that this activity requires both a VAB-1 kinase-dependent and a kinase independent function. We also show that efn-4 is required for AFD neuron axon extension, and that this strongly correlates with AIY neuron morphology. ASE morphology, however, does not require efn-4 function. In addition, efn-4 is required, in part, for normal synaptic vesicle cluster count in both AFD and ASEL neurons. Finally, we demonstrate that efn-4 is required for normal thermotaxis behavior, and to a lesser degree, salt-dependent chemotaxis, while vab-1 and efn-1 also contribute to normal thermosensory function, albeit to a lesser degree than efn-4.ConclusionsThese data illustrate that left-right circuit connectivity, between the left-right thermosensory neuron pair and/or the left-right first layer interneuron pair, may be required for normal thermotactic responses. These data also highlight the importance of EphRs and ephrins in multiple aspects of sensory circuit assembly and function and suggest that efn-4 may coordinate thermosensory circuit assembly.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12868-025-00983-3.

## Background
The survival of an organism depends on its ability to detect and react to fluctuations in the external environment [1]. In multicellular organisms, environmental cues such as temperature, odor, and taste, are detected by sensory neurons, with specific classes of sensory neuron detecting a subset of inputs [2–15, and references therein]. This information is processed by interneurons to create a response that can drive a motor output, resulting in behavior such as attraction or avoidance of a specific cue [16, 17]. Sensory circuit function requires proper synaptic connectivity between the sensory, inter, and motorneurons involved in that circuit. There has been extensive analysis of genes controlling an individual neuron’s shape [18–21]. However, the factors coordinating assembly of multiple parts of a sensory circuit are less well understood.
The Eph receptor tyrosine kinase pathway has multiple roles in nervous system development, including axon outgrowth, circuit assembly, synaptogenesis, and neuronal cell sorting [22–26]. In vertebrates, there are two classes of Eph receptor, EphA and EphB [27]. EphA receptors typically bind to GPI-anchored ephrin-A ligands, while the EphB receptors bind to single-pass trans-membrane ephrin-B ligands. However, the considerable crosstalk between the EphA/ephrin-A and EphB/ephrin-B classes makes it difficult to analyze individual EphR and ephrin contributions to nervous system development and function [28]. The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans has a single EphA receptor, VAB-1, and only four ephrin ligands, EFN-1–4, making it a compelling system to investigate the role of Eph receptors and ephrins in the nervous system [29].
We previously identified a role for the C. elegans Eph receptor vab-1 and ephrin efn-1 (also known as vab-2 in Wormbase and previous publications) in shaping AIY interneuron anterior axon projection [30]. In addition, we found that the ephrin efn-4 is required, in part, for dorsal axon extension. This is supported by other work which reveals a role for efn-2 in facilitating efn-4-dependent AIYL axon extension [31]. We have built on our earlier work, and found that vab-1, efn-1, and efn-4 are required for normal AFD thermosensory neuron morphology, and thermosensory circuit function. In addition, we also find that EphR/ephrins play a modest role in normal chemosensory detection of inorganic solutes via the ASE sensory neurons but have no overt role in the detection of AWC-sensed volatile odorants.

## Result

## EphR vab-1 and ephrins efn-1 and efn-4 are required for AIY interneuron development
C. elegans has well-described sensory neurons that respond to defined stimuli [2, 4, 7, 8, 14, 15, 32–35]. The first layer of integration for thermosensory pathways is primarily through the AIY interneurons, whereas volatile odorant sensation is distributed across the AIA, AIB, and AIY interneurons (Fig. 1A) [2, 5, 8, 36, 37]. AIY interneurons are bilaterally symmetric unipolar cells, with the cell bodies lying below and to the posterior end of the pharyngeal bulb (Fig. 1B). The axons extend anteriorly, meet in the middle at a ventral plexus, then navigate dorsally to form a gap junction with their contralateral partner (Fig. 1C). We previously described a role for the EphR and ephrin pathways in ectopic AIY axon branching, in a C. elegans model of Kallmann syndrome [30]. As our original data was in a kal-1 over-expression background, we needed to reconfirm roles for vab-1, efn-1, and efn-4 in shaping AIY interneuron morphology [38]. Concurring with our previous work, vab-1(dx31) and efn-1(ju1 and e96) predicted null mutants show a high penetrance ventral plexus gap between the AIYL and AIYR neurons (arrowheads, Figs. 1D and 1E). vab-1(e2) is a kinase-dead allele and shows a slightly weaker phenotype than the vab-1(dx31) null, suggesting that there is a kinase-independent component to shaping AIY ventral morphology (Fig. 1G). Double-mutant combinations between vab-1 and efn-1(ju1) are no worse than either single mutant, suggesting that these two genes function in the same genetic pathway. Expression of efn-1 from an AIY-specific promoter partially rescues the efn-1(ju1) ventral plexus defects, although pan-neuronal expression of efn-1 was not significantly different from the efn-1 mutant alone. This lack of rescue from a pan-neuronal promoter, coupled with previously reported neomorphic phenotypes from efn-1 rescue experiments, could be caused by efn-1's complex expression pattern in the nervous system and hypodermis [39, 40]. We also examined efn-2 and efn-3 deletion mutants. efn-2(ev658) showed a weak (6.6%) ventral plexus defect that was not significantly different from wild type, while efn-3(ev696) showed no AIY morphology defects, nor did the efn-3 mutation enhance that of efn-1, suggesting that efn-3 has no overt role in shaping AIY interneuron morphology. In contrast, efn-4 mutants show highly penetrant dorsal extension defects (40%, Figs. 1F and 1H), but showed a weaker (23%) ventral plexus defect. ## efn-4 is required, in part, for AFD thermosensory neuron axon guidance
The AFD thermosensory neurons are bilaterally symmetrical, with the left and right cell bodies lying anterior and dorsal to the posterior pharyngeal bulb [41]. They are characterized by a single finger-like ciliated dendrite anteriorly in the nose tip, and a single axon that projects ventrally, turns, then migrates dorsally to meet its contralateral partner at the dorsal midline above the pharynx ([42] (Fig. 2A). Using a gcy-8p::GFP reporter gene to specifically label the AFD neurons, and a ttx-3p::RFP reporter gene to label the AIY interneurons, we asked if efn-4 was required to shape AFD thermosensory neuron morphology in parallel with that of the AIY neurons. In wild type animals, 17.2% (10/58) showed a dorsal gap between the AFDL and AFDR axons of greater than 3 µm, while 12.7% of animals (7/55) showed a dorsal gap between the AIYL/R neurons (Fig. 2A–C). In vab-1 mutants, 32.8% of animals (19/58) show a dorsal AFD gap, while 24.1% (14/58) show a dorsal AIYL/R gap (Fig. 2D–F). This was similar in efn-1 mutants; 32.4% (12/37) and 13.5% (5/37) showing AFD and AIY left/right gaps respectively (Fig. 2G–I). We occasionally saw additional axon guidance defects in AFD neurons, such as axons that failed to project ventrally, and instead turned to the dorsal side, straight from the cell body (arrows, Fig. 2), and continued to see ventral contact defects in AIY neurons (open arrowheads). In contrast, efn-4 mutants showed a striking 74% (37/50) AFDL/R gap and a 52% (26/50) AIYL/R gap (Fig. 2J–L, p < 0.001, compared to wild type for both cell types). We also asked if the AFD and AIY axon extension defects correlated. In efn-4 mutants, 48% (24/50 pairs of cells) showed correlated defects, which is almost the same as for the AIY cells alone (Fig. 2M). ## efn-4 has no role in ASE chemosensory neuron axon guidance
The AFD thermosensory neurons are similar to the AIY interneurons in that both of their axons terminate growth at the dorsal midline, making a gap junction with their contralateral partner [42, 43]. The AFD nerve ring processes are closely associated with those of the AIY neurons [43]. In contrast, ASE chemosensory neurons, which detect soluble Na+ and K+ cations, also synapse onto the AIY interneurons, but extend their axons over the top of the pharynx, terminating midway down the contralateral side of the nerve ring [42] (Fig. 3A). In this case, we used a flp-6p::GFP transgene to label the ASEL/R neuron pair, in parallel with a ttx-3p::RFP reporter gene to label AIYL/R [33]. In wild type and vab-1 animals, we saw no dorsal gap in the ASEL/R neuron pair (0/55 for wild type, 0/28 for vab-1, Fig. 3A–F). efn-1 mutants showed a low penetrance 9% (4/41) dorsal gap in ASE neurons, while efn-4 mutants showed a 7.1% (2/28) dorsal gap phenotype. This in contrast to the AIY neurons, where vab-1 (32%, 9/28), efn-1 (24.4%, 10/41), and efn-4 (46.4%, 13/28) showed significant AIYL/R gaps when compared to wild type (3.6%, 2/55, p < 0.05 for efn-1, p < 0.001, for vab-1 and efn-4 respectively). These AIY data follow a similar pattern to that of Figure 2M (note that the AIY data are not significantly different between these datasets by two-tailed Fisher exact test). Also, there was no correlation between ASE and AIY dorsal guidance defects (data summarized in Fig. 3A–M), although there was occasional low penetrance ectopic axon branching seen in efn-4 mutants (open arrow, Fig. 3J). These data suggest that efn-4 has little (if any) role in ASE dorsal axon guidance. ## efn-4 has no role in AWC olfactory neuron axon guidance
In C. elegans, attractive volatile odorants are detected via the AWC amphid sensory neurons, among several other sensory neurons (e.g., ADL, AWA, and AWB, to name a few) [32, 44, and references therein]. Unlike the AFD-dependent thermosensory and ASE-dependent chemosensory circuits, which synapse primarily onto the AIY neurons, the AWCs connect to three pairs of interneurons; AIA, AIB, and AIY [37, 43]. To assess whether the EphR/ephrin pathway played any role in volatile odorant chemosensory circuit assembly, we used an odr-1p::dsRed transgene to examine AWC chemosensory neuron axon morphology, in parallel with a ttx-3p::GFP transgene to examine AIY interneurons [34, 45]. Note that odr-1p::dsRed is expressed in both AWB and AWC neurons, but has stronger expression in the AWCs, making it easy to identify these cells. We found that EphR and ephrin mutations had no significant effect on AWC morphology when compared to wild type, although 46% (17/37, p < 0.001) of efn-4 mutants showed AIY dorsal axon extension defects, compared to 4% (1/25) of wild type animals (Figure S1). This AIY phenotype was not significantly different from that seen in our previous efn-4 mutant assays (Figs. 2M and 3M, Fisher exact test). This suggests that efn-4 has no overt role in AWC dorsal axon guidance.

## efn-4 plays a role in establishing the number of sensory-to-interneuron vesicle clustering domains
The above data demonstrates a clear role for efn-4 in guiding dorsal axon extension in both the AFD thermosensory and AIY interneurons, although it has no overt role in guiding ASE or AWC dorsal axon extension. However, it is possible that the AFD-to-AIY and ASE-to-AIY circuits may form synaptic connections independently from efn-4’s role in axon guidance. We first examined this using the AFD, ASE and AIY-specific reporter genes used above. As an example, Fig. 4A–C shows how the AFD and AIY neurons juxtapose alongside each other in discrete locations (ASE and AIY neurons show similar cell type-to-cell type contacts). The zoomed image panels (Fig. 4A’-B’’’) show individual z-planes from a confocal z-stack dataset, with clear co-localization shown in only a small number of yellow pixels (Fig. 4C, red and green channel overlay). Using these two-color datasets, we counted the number of AFD-to-AIY contacts (typically around 10 co-localized pixels) in wild type, and EphR/ephrin mutants (Fig. 4D). Wild type AFD neurons contacted AIY neurons on average 2.9 (±0.1 s.e.m.) times per neuron pair (n = 51). This number was reduced in vab-1 mutants to 2.3 ± 0.1 (n = 57, p < 0.001), in efn-1 mutants to 2.0 ± 0.1 (n = 37, p < 0.001), and in efn-4 mutants to 2.0 ± 0.1 (n = 43, p < 0.001) contacts per neuron pair. Similarly, wild type ASE neurons contacted AIY neurons on average 2.8 ± 0.1 times per neuron pair (n = 51, Fig. 4E). This is reduced in vab-1(dx31) animals to 2.3 ± 0.1 (n = 28, p < 0.05), in efn-1(ju1) animals to 1.8 ± 0.2 (n = 38, p < 0.001), and in efn-4(bx80) mutants to 2.3 ± 0.3 (n = 26, not significantly different from wild type). While cell-to-cell contact juxtaposes two cells such that they can make a synapse, this does not prove that a synapse (or gap junction) has been made. To further investigate the connectivity between AFD (and ASE) sensory and AIY interneurons, we use a gene fusion between synaptobrevin (a protein required for synaptic vesicle formation) and RFP (snb-1::RFP) and selectively drove its expression either in the AFD or ASEL sensory neurons. This labeled discrete nerve ring puncta in the presynaptic sensory neurons with RFP, and when used with a cytoplasmic, AIY-specific GFP reporter gene, allowed us to count individual vesicle clustering domains between the AFD and AIY interneurons, and the ASEL sensory and AIY interneurons (Fig. 4F-S) [46, 47]. In wild type animals carrying an AFD-specific snb-1::RFP, we saw an average of 1.7 puncta per animal in the nerve ring (n = 36), compared to 0.76 per nerve ring in efn-4 mutants (n = 50, p < 0.01). In animals carrying an ASEL-specific snb-1::RFP marker, we saw a similar pattern, with an average of 1.2 puncta in the nerve ring of wild type animals (n = 23), compared to 0.43 puncta per nerve ring in efn-4 mutants (n = 42, p < 0.01). These data suggest that either, efn-4 plays a role in presynaptic vesicle localization, and/or, efn-4-dependent axon guidance is required, in part, for correct vesicle cluster localization between these cell types.

## The Eph receptor vab-1 and ephrins efn-1 and efn-4 are required for normal thermotaxis behavior
Our data above demonstrate roles for both the Eph receptor vab-1 and ephrins efn-1 and efn-4 in shaping both thermo and chemosensory neurons, and also the morphology of AIY interneurons. In addition, we show a role for efn-4 in governing the number of presynaptic vesicle clusters between the sensory and interneuron circuit layers. However, it is possible that C. elegans thermo- and chemosensory circuits are robust to cell morphology level perturbations and can generate normal thermo- and chemosensory responses to cell-type specific stimuli, despite neuronal morphology defects. To investigate this, we examined thermotactic behavior using a plate-based assay [48]. Fig. 5A shows the thermotaxis platform, composed of an aluminum plate and pedestal bolted to two Peltier devices, one set at 28 °C (hot side, left) and the other at 12.5 °C (cold side, right). A thermal imaging camera shows that this assay environment has a roughly linear thermal gradient from hot to cold (Fig. 5B, C). When conditioned at 21.5 °C for 24 hours, then placed in the center of the plate (surface temperature = 20° C), wild type animals show a strong attractive thermotactic response towards their incubation temperature, generating an average thermotaxis index (TI) of 0.63 ± 0.05 (n = 8 assays, with > 100 animals per assay, ± s.e.m., Fig. 5D, E). In contrast, ttx-3(ot22) mutants show a striking cryophilic attraction to the cold side of the plate (TI = −0.85 ± 0.04, n = 5 assays with > 100 animals per assay, Fig. 5F, G), which parallels previously published thermotaxis data on ttx-3 mutants [34, 49]. vab-1 and efn-1 mutants show a positive but diminished thermotactic response (TI = 0.24 ± 0.1 and 0.2 ± 0.06, respectively, n = 7 assays each). We previously showed that AIY axon growth defects could be rescued by reintroducing efn-4 under its own promoter or non-cell autonomously from the hypodermis [30]. This also suggests that AIYL-to-AIYR connectivity is required for normal thermotaxis. However, we cannot rule out a role for efn-4 in helping to assemble other parts of the thermosensory circuit or facilitating its thermotactic function in some other way.

In C. elegans, the ASE chemosensory neurons are required for chemotaxis towards soluble cations such as Na+ and K+ when conditioned in the presence of those salts [8]. ## efn-4 plays no role in volatile odorant chemotaxis
AWC chemosensory neurons mediate attraction to certain volatile odorants [44]. To assess whether the EphR/ephrin pathway played any role in AWC-dependent volatile odorant chemosensation, we examined odorant chemotaxis to isoamyl alcohol, butanone, and 2,3-pentanedione in EphR and ephrin mutants (Figure S2). Wild type animals showed strong chemotaxis responses towards all three odorants, with average CIs = 0.81 ± 0.05 s.e.m. (isoamyl alcohol), 0.76 ± 0.07 (butanone), and 0.8 ± 0.11 (2,3-pentanedione) respectively (n = 5 trials each). vab-1 mutants showed a weak (but significant) odorant chemotaxis defect to isoamyl alcohol, with an average CI = 0.62 ± 0.07 (p < 0.05, n = 5). However, the remaining EphR and ephrin mutants were not significantly different from wild type in each assay. These data corroborate our AWC morphology analysis (Figure S1) and suggest that the EphR/ephrin pathway has only a minor role in guiding the assembly and function of the AWC-to-AIA/AIB/AIY volatile odorant chemosensory circuit.

## Discussion

## Sensory and interneuron morphology correlate with sensory circuit function
Our previous work, using a kal-1-overexpression line identified roles for the C. elegans Eph receptor and ephrin family in shaping the AIY interneuron [30]. We confirm and extend that data to show that members of the Eph receptor and ephrin family contribute to the normal shape of not just the AIY interneurons, but also the AFD thermosensory neurons (Figs. 1 and 2). As the architectural adage goes, form follows function. Many C. elegans sensory neuron cell types, including the AFD neurons, are bilaterally symmetrical. What is unusual is that AFD dorsal axon extension into the nerve ring terminates at the dorsal midline, where a gap junction connects the AFD left and AFD right neuron pair [43]. In efn-4 mutants, over 70% of animals observed have defects in dorsal axon extension, suggesting that efn-4 plays a major role in this dorsal guidance choice. This defect is mirrored in the AIY interneurons, where the dorsal axon extension also terminates at the dorsal midline. Intriguingly, AFD and AIY dorsal defects correlate, suggesting that either (a) loss of guidance in one cell type influences the other, or (b) that both cell types are responding to the same guidance cue (Fig. 2M).
The primary location of C. elegans neural connectivity is the nerve ring, which is a region of axon and dendritic innervation that wraps around the pharynx in a tight bundle [43]. The anatomical structure of the nerve ring was recently resolved using serial electron microscopy reconstruction [53]. These studies reveal that the nerve ring is formed of four neural process toroids arranged along the anterior-posterior axis labeled S1 through S4. The neural connectivity within each toroid is localized based on sensory function, with the most anterior toroid (S1) dedicated to mechanosensory information, whereas S3 and S4 comprise mostly sensory axons synapsing onto first layer interneurons. Developmentally, the S1 toroid forms first, when SIAD, SIBV, and SMDD axons, along with CEPsh glial cells, extend around the nascent pharynx at the “bean” stage of embryonic development [53–55]. Axons and dendrites forming the S2 through S4 toroids follow slightly later in development, during the comma to 2-fold stage. AIY and AFD neural processes are found in the S4 toroid, with AIY entering the nerve ring at the 1.5-fold stage, followed by AFD axons at the 2-fold stage (Figure S3). Based on this temporal sequence, it is possible that the AFD axons may use the AIY processes as a guidance cue.
EphR and ephrins are required for accurate neural connectivity in many neural cell types [22, 27, 56, 57]. Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals that the C. elegans EphR vab-1 is highly expressed in CEPsh glial cells, which contribute to the S1 nerve ring toroid, whereas the ephrin efn-4 is highly expressed in the AIY interneurons [40]. We speculate that vab-1/EphR and efn-4/ephrin interactions promote dorsal extension of the AIY axons, as loss-of-function in either of these genes leads to partially penetrant AIY axon extension defects (Fig. 1). As mentioned above, the correlation of AIY and AFD axon defects seen in efn-4 mutants suggest an axon guidance relationship between these two cell types. As the AIY interneurons enter the nerve ring prior to the AFD axons, the simplest explanation is that the AIY axons act as a pioneer cue for the AFDs, such that if AIY guidance goes wrong, so does the AFD. However, efn-4 is not the only dorsal guidance cue used by the AFD and AIY cells, as almost 50% of axons make the appropriate dorsal contact in the absence of efn-4, demonstrating some redundancy in dorsal extension guidance, possibly via unc-6/netrin signaling [58].

## Is it important to be connected?
Thermal stimulus of AFD neurons leads to temperature-dependent calcium transients in the AFD cells [49, 59]. This calcium signal is relayed in a phase-locked manner to the AIY neurons via a synaptic varicosity near the base of the nerve ring. Severing the ipsilateral AFD dendrite still causes AFD-to-AIY firing, suggesting that the contralateral dendrite can provide a stimulus via the AFDL-to-AFDR gap junction [49]. However, when both AFD dendrites are severed, AFD-to-AIY firing is blocked. Together, this suggests that AFD neurons normally share information from both sides of the thermosensory circuit prior to interacting with the first tier AIY interneuron partners. We find that AFD and AIY neurons still make synaptic connections (AFDL-to-AIYL, and AFDR-to-AIYR) in the absence of efn-4, although the average number of presynaptic vesicle clusters is reduced by around 50% (Fig. 4F–S). Left-right connectivity, with important behavioral consequences, has also been demonstrated for the AIY interneurons, showing that AIYL and AIYR are electrically coupled at the ventral plexus via the innexin/gap junction protein INX-1 [60]. This electrical coupling integrates AIY activity, dampening responses to sub-threshold stimuli, and allowing normal isothermal tracking. In inx-1 mutants, a step voltage increase in one cell does not propagate to its contralateral partner. At the behavioral level, this promotes inappropriate isothermal tracking. To assess overall thermotactic circuit function, we examined thermotactic behavior in EphR and ephrin mutants. vab-1 and efn-1 mutants show weak (but significant) thermotactic defects, whereas efn-4 mutants show an atactic/weakly cryophilic phenotype when compared to wild type (Fig. 5D). These data suggest that dorsal axon connectivity between the AFD left and right thermosensory neurons, and also the AIY left and right interneurons is required for normal thermosensory behavior. Why these neurons require both dorsal and ventral left-right connectivity is unclear but suggests that the AIY nerve ring projection and ventral plexus may be functionally distinct. The AIY neurons also form the primary interneuron processing layer for ASE chemosensory neurons [43]. Unlike the AFD neurons, we find that the EphR and ephrin pathway has little influence on ASE dorsal axon extension (Fig. 3). Of note, ASE axon morphology is different from that of the AFD thermosensory neurons, extending beyond the dorsal midline and terminating halfway down the contralateral side of the nerve ring [43]. In addition, the ASEL and ASER neurons do not appear to communicate via gap junctions. The lack of overt ASE dorsal extension defects seen in efn-4 mutants suggests that the ASE neurons may use a different set of guidance cues to define dorsal axon morphology distinct from the efn-4 dependent process used by AFD and AIY neurons. This is supported by the lack of correlation between ASE and AIY axon extension defects seen in efn-4 mutants (Fig. 3M). Despite the normal ASE morphology seen in efn-4 mutants, they show a significant reduction in presynaptic vesicle clusters adjacent to the AIY interneurons (Fig. 4) and show significant defects in Na+ and K+ chemotaxis (Fig. 6). This indicates that correct interneuron architecture helps support the synapse formation required for normal chemotaxis. A caveat to our approach is that compared to previously published electron microscopy data of the AFD-to-AIY and ASEL-to-AIY connectome, we are under-counting presynaptic vesicle clusters by around 10-fold [37]. This may be due to spatial coalescence of our clusters, and/or smaller clusters being below the detection limits of our assay.
Interestingly, the chemotaxis defects seen in efn-4 mutants are similar to those seen in daf-18/PTEN mutants [50]. In parallel, daf-18 mutants show high-penetrance dorsal axon guidance defects in AIY neurons, similar to that seen in efn-4 animals [61]. The similarity between these two defects suggests two possible outcomes; 1) dorsal AIYL-to-AIYR axon connectivity is required for normal interneuron function and processing of ASE-dependent chemosensory inputs, or 2) dorsal axon extension is required to reach a presynaptic region presented by the ASE sensory neurons. In support of the second hypothesis, we see significantly fewer presynaptic vesicle clusters in the ASEL-to-AIY circuit, despite ASEL axons being capable of synapsing onto both the ipsi- and contralateral neurons of the AIYL/R pair. Whether efn-4 has a direct role in positioning ASE-to-AIY vesicle clusters, or whether the synaptic defects are a consequence of truncated AIY axon extension, remains to be seen.

## Is efn-4 required for the dorsal axon guidance of other neurons?
As mentioned above, this work has examined two types of bilaterally symmetric neurons in C. elegans; ones with axon termination at the dorsal midline (for instance AFD and AIY), and one that wraps around (such as the ASEs). An additional class of sensory neuron that wraps an axon around the pharynx are the AWC olfactory neurons, which sense attractive volatile odors such as benzaldehyde [44]. We examined circuit assembly and chemotaxis to volatile odorants in EphR and ephrin mutants, and found no defects compared to wild type (Figures S1, S2). Unlike ASE and AFD neurons, AWC neurons synapse onto AIA, AIB, and AIY interneurons. We note that AIB interneurons extend processes dorsally, then ventrally down the contralateral side. This “up-and-over-the-top” morphology may provide additional opportunities to synapse onto both the left and right sides of the AIB interneuron layer, thus smoothing neural output. In addition, AWC (and indeed ASE neurons) have asymmetric expression of some transcription factors and signal transduction components, such that the left versus right cells detect distinct types of sensory input, despite their apparent left-right mirror image morphology [62, 63]. Distributing output across multiple interneurons may facilitate the functional specialization of the left and right cells respectively [64]. Intriguingly, the AIA interneurons are somewhat similar to AIYs, in that they too terminate at the dorsal midline [43]. AIA neurons, which were not examined in this study, weakly express efn-2 [40], so it is possible that dorsal AIA axon extension may be governed by efn-2 in a manner similar to AIYs dependency on efn-4 expression.

## Conclusions
Taken together, these data support key roles for EphR and ephrin expression, not only in shaping sensory and interneurons of the same circuit, but also in their underlying sensory behaviors.
The deep conservation of EphR/ephrin signaling across phyla illustrates the importance of this signaling pathway in sensory circuit assembly and suggest that disruption in EphR/ephrin signaling may contribute to neurological disorders.

## Methods

## Strain maintenance
C. elegans strains were maintained on nematode growth medium plates (NGM Lite) streaked with OP50 E. coli according to standard methods [65]. All analyses were conducted at 21.5 °C unless otherwise noted. The following previously published mutations were used in the course of this work: vab-1(dx31) II, vab-1(e2) II, daf-18(ok480) IV, efn-1(ju1) IV, efn-1(e96) IV, efn-2(ev658) IV, efn-4(bx80) IV, efn-3(ev696) X, and ttx-3(ot22) X. All alleles used are predicted to be null mutations apart from vab-1(e2), which generates a kinase dead-variant of the VAB-1 protein. The following previously published transgenes were used in the course of this work: otIs133[ttx-3p::RFP + unc-4(+)] II, mgIs18 [ttx-3p::GFP] IV, oyIs44 [odr-1p::dsRed] V, oyIs18 [gcy-8p::GFP] X, and dbEx724 [flp-6p::tax-2(cDNA)::SL2::GFP + lin-15(+)]. See Table S1 for a list of strains, and Table S2 for a list of plasmids generated during this work. Table S3 summarizes transgenic strain construction.

## Microscopy
Neuron morphology was visualized using GFP, RFP, dsRed, or mCherry fluorescent reporter genes driven by the cell-specific promoters. Z-stack images were captured using Zeiss LSM 700, LSM900, or Leica SP8 confocal laser scanning microscopes and analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ software [66, 67]. Presynaptic vesicle clusters were visualized using cell-specific transgenes containing synaptobrevin fused to RFP, and Z-stacks captured using an Olympus BX61epifluorescence microscope. Vesicle cluster Z-stack images were processed using a deconvolution algorithm (CellSens Dimension). Three-dimensional projections were generated for all datasets using Fiji to facilitate measurements, and to confirm registration of vesicle clusters adjacent to the AIY-nerve ring process. Worms were mounted on 2% agarose pads and immobilized with 10% sodium azide solution before imaging. AIY interneurons were considered to have a dorsal gap if they failed to reach their terminal target on the dorsal side of the nerve ring and showed an axon-axon gap of at least 3 μm. Interneurons were considered to have a ventral gap if the axons failed to contact each other on the ventral side of the nerve ring. Gap distance was measured using the Segmented Line function in Fiji [66].

## Transgenic line generation
Plasmids were generated using standard molecular techniques and verified by restriction digest and Sanger sequencing. Transgenic C. elegans strains were generated via germline microinjection in conjunction with a co-injection marker plasmid [34]. Table S3 shows details of transgenic strain construction. Plasmid pCZGY1839 [unc-119p::efn-1] was a kind gift from Andrew Chisholm (University of California, San Diego). Plasmid pBA109 [unc-25p::snb-1::RFP] was generously provided by Brian Ackley (University of Kansas).

## Thermal preference assay
The thermal preference assay was adapted from Hedgecock and Russell [68], Ito et al. [48], and Goodman et al. [69]. 2% assay agar (NGM lite minus bacto-tryptone) was poured into a rectangular tray plate (127 × 85 × 16 mm, SPL Life Sciences). A thermal gradient was generated using two AHP 150CP Peltier thermoelectric blocks (Teca USA). An aluminum slab with an aluminum pedestal was attached to the warm and cold blocks to establish the thermal gradient. Assay agar trays were preincubated for 30 mins to establish the thermal gradient (hot side = 23.5 °C; origin/center = 20 °C; cold side = 17.5°C). Well-fed worms were conditioned at 21.5 °C for at 24 hours with ample food, washed three times with nematode growth (NG) buffer, then dropped at the origin vertically with a few microliters of buffer. Excess buffer was removed using Kim wipes. After 30 mins, the assay tray was removed from the aluminum slab, 0.5 µl of 1 M sodium azide (Sigma Aldrich) dropped at several spots to paralyze the worms, then transferred to 4 °C to stop further movements. The thermotaxis index (TI) was calculated as the number of animals on the warm side (W) minus the number of animals on the cold side (C) divided by the total (TI = W - C/W + C). A TI > 0 = an attractive (thermophilic) response, whereas a TI < 0 = a repulsive (cryophilic) response. A value around zero is atactic (worms migrate to the hot and cold sides equally).

## Gustatory behavioral assays
Salt-sensing gustatory assays were adopted from Jansen et al. [50] and Kalichamy et al. [51]. Four quadrant lines were drawn on assay agar plates. 25 µl aliquots of 2 M sodium chloride or 2 M potassium chloride (Sigma) dissolved in distilled water were dropped on the opposite quadrants (A, C) and water control was added to the other pair of quadrants (B, D), after which the plates were allowed to dry at room temperature for 60 min. 0.5 µl of 1 M sodium azide was added to the salt or control spots to paralyze animals that approached those spots. After completing the assay, the assay agar plates were transferred to 4 °C to stop further migration of the animals. Animals at each quadrant were counted and the salt chemotaxis index (CI) calculated as the number of animals on the salt (A + C) minus number of animals on the control (B + D) quadrants, divided by the total number of animals (salt CI = (A + C) - (B + D)/A + B + C + D). A salt CI > 0 indicates an attraction response, whereas a CI < 0 = repulsion.

## Olfactory behavioral analysis
AWC-dependent volatile odorant chemotaxis assays towards butanone, isoamyl alcohol, and 2,3-pentanedione (Sigma-Aldrich) were adapted from Bargmann et al. [44]. The odorants were diluted in ethanol (v/v) as follows: butanone, 1:1,000; isoamyl alcohol, 1:100; 2,3-pentanedione, 1:10,000. Ethanol alone was used as the vehicle control. 1 µl of 1 M sodium azide was dropped in advance at both the odorant and control spots to paralyze animals that reached the target. Well-fed and washed young adult C. elegans were loaded to agar assay plates and incubated for 120 min at 20 °C, after which plates were moved to 4 °C to stop animal movement. The odorant chemotaxis index (CI) was calculated as the number of animals at the attractant spot (O) minus the number of animals at the control spot (C), divided by the total (odorant CI = O - C/O + C). An odorant CI > 0 indicated an attractive response to a volatile compound, whereas CI < 0 was a repulsive response.

## Statistical analysis
Proportion data was determined to be significantly different using a Z-test with an α-level of 0.05. Differences were considered significant if p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), or p < 0.001 (***). Averages were determined to be statistically different using the student’s t-test. Error bars on graphs show the standard error of proportion or standard error of the mean. Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons were applied as necessary.

## Figure Fig. 7: Lateral connectivity between left-right neuron pairs may be required for normal thermo and chemosensation. (A) The AFD thermosensory neurons detect temperature in their dendritic ends, then relay that information across the thermosensory left/right pair via their axon projections into the nerve ring (pink arrow). Conversely, ASE chemosensory neurons do not cross-couple, but instead, distribute their signaling output to both the AIYL and AIYR cells. The AIY interneuron pair also cross-couples between the left and right cell via a gap junction (orange arrow). These cells output sensory information to drive normal thermotactic and chemotactic behaviors (black arrows). (B) In efn-4 mutants, the left/right connectivity is frequently lost between the AFDL/R thermosensory neurons. ASE chemosensory neurons in efn-4 mutants retain broadly normal morphology, although the number of presynaptic vesicle clusters from the sensory to interneurons are reduced in both cases (grey arrows). At the interneuron layer, the AIYL/R interneurons frequently lack left-right connectivity. The defective thermotaxis (and to a lesser degree chemotaxis) seen in efn-4 mutants, suggests that AIY left-right integration of sensory information is crucial for normal thermosensation and chemosensation
