# Unexpected culprit: High output heart failure induced by severe anemia from laryngeal leech infestation: A case report

## Abstract
AbstractLeeches are uncommon blood-feeding parasites found in polluted water, posing serious health risks. They typically affect individuals who swim in contaminated streams or drink water that has been contaminated. Once ingested, they can attach in various parts of the upper aero-digestive tract, but leeches in the larynx are rarely observed. A 6-year-old male presented with a 2-week history of hemoptysis, blood-stained saliva, and a sensation of a foreign body in his throat. He reported hoarseness, shortness of breath while lying down, fatigue, blurred vision, and lightheadedness. Initially diagnosed with acute tonsillopharyngitis and treated with amoxicillin, his condition worsened, leading to bilateral lower extremity swelling. Upon examination, he showed tachycardia, tachypnea, fever, and pale conjunctivae. Cardiovascular examination highlighted an S3 gallop, and abdominal examination indicated tender hepatomegaly approximately 4 cm below the right costal margin. Musculoskeletal evaluation revealed grade 2 pitting edema in the lower extremities. Laboratory results indicated severe anemia (hemoglobin level of 3.0). Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy revealed a live leech in the larynx. Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent direct laryngoscopy for removal of the leech, followed by blood transfusions, antibiotics, and diuretics. He recovered well and was discharged on the tenth postoperative day with dietary recommendations and instructions to avoid untreated spring water. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and intervention to prevent potentially fatal complications from leech infestations, particularly in young children who experience unexplained airway or gastrointestinal symptoms after freshwater exposure. ## Introduction
Leeches are uncommon blood-feeding parasites that inhabit polluted water and can lead to serious health issues [1]. They typically affect people who swim in infested streams or consume contaminated water [2]. Once ingested, a leech can attach itself at various locations in the upper aero-digestive tract, including the nasal cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, trachea, and esophagus. However, a leech stuck in the larynx is rarely seen [3,4].

## Case presentation
A 6-year-old male presented to our surgical emergency outpatient department with a 2-week history of hemoptysis, frequent spitting of blood-stained saliva, and a sensation of a foreign body in his throat. He also reported hoarseness, worsening shortness of breath while lying down, easy fatigability, blurred vision, and lightheadedness, but notably had no throat pain, epistaxis, melena, or history of trauma. Initially diagnosed with acute tonsillopharyngitis at a local health center, he was prescribed amoxicillin but exhibited no improvement. His condition deteriorated, leading to progressive bilateral lower extremity swelling and increased shortness of breath, which prompted a referral to our facility. The patient resides in a rural area of North Gondar, Ethiopia, where he consumes unfiltered, unboiled natural spring water.
Upon presentation, the patient appeared acutely ill, exhibiting tachycardia at 140 beats per minute, tachypnea at 34 breaths per minute, a temperature of 38°C, and an oxygen saturation of 90% in ambient air. Examination of the oral cavity revealed blood-stained mucosa in the oropharynx, palate, and tongue; however, no visible foreign body was found. The patient also exhibited pale conjunctivae. Cardiovascular examination highlighted an S3 gallop, and abdominal examination indicated tender hepatomegaly approximately 4 cm below the right costal margin. Musculoskeletal evaluation revealed grade 2 pitting edema in the lower extremities. Laboratory investigations showed a leukocyte count of 13,000, a hemoglobin level of 3.0, and a platelet count of 302,000.
Based on the patient’s clinical history and physical examination, we suspected high-output heart failure due to severe anemia caused by a laryngeal leech infestation. Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy revealed a moving, dark-colored foreign body in the subglottic area of the larynx, accompanied by signs of inflammation; however, vocal cord motility remained intact. The diagnosis of laryngeal leech infestation was strongly supported.
The patient underwent direct laryngoscopy under general anesthesia. A 5 cm live leech (as shown in Fig. 1) was successfully grasped with forceps and completely removed as shown in a video [1]. A thorough inspection of the laryngeal area revealed no additional abnormalities. The patient was then transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU). Following this, the patient received 4 bags of whole blood transfusion and was started on furosemide and ceftriaxone. Recovery was smooth, and the patient was discharged on the tenth postoperative day with instructions to avoid drinking spring water and to include iron-rich foods in his diet.

## Discussion
Laryngeal leech infestation is a rare but clinically significant condition that can lead to life-threatening airway obstruction [3,4]. These parasites thrive in tropical and subtropical regions, making them more common in rural areas of Africa, Asia, and South America. The rural population, especially children, is at higher risk of infestation due to the frequent consumption of untreated water [5,6]. This association emphasizes the need for public health interventions focused on improving water sanitation in these regions to prevent such infestations [3,7].
Once inside the body, the leech attaches to the mucosal surfaces of the respiratory or gastrointestinal tracts, including the larynx. Here, it secretes an anticoagulant called hirudin, which prevents blood clotting, allowing the leech to feed unnoticed for extended periods [8]. In addition to hirudin, the leech’s saliva contains anesthetic properties, further delaying symptom onset. The patient may remain asymptomatic initially or present with vague symptoms such as a foreign body sensation in the throat, cough, or dysphagia [3,9]. However, as the leech grows and consumes more blood, the obstruction worsens, leading to more severe symptoms like hoarseness, dyspnea, hemoptysis, and eventually airway compromise [10]. In some cases, as seen in reports from Sudan and Ethiopia, delayed diagnosis can result in significant complications, including severe anemia due to prolonged blood loss [11,12]. This highlights the critical importance of early recognition and intervention.
Laryngoscopy is the gold standard for diagnosing laryngeal leech infestation, allowing for direct visualization of the parasite. However, it is worth noting that radiographic imaging is rarely useful in detecting the leech itself, although it may help rule out other causes of airway obstruction, such as foreign bodies or tumors [10]. Once diagnosed, the definitive treatment is removal of the leech, and there are various options, but induction of general anesthesia via face mask without using an endotracheal tube is a quite safe method for removing the leech from the larynx, typically done using laryngoscopy or endoscopy to ensure the entire parasite is removed intact [13]. Care must be taken during removal because incomplete extraction, particularly if the head or sucker remains attached, can result in continued bleeding or secondary infections. [10,14].
After immediate removal, post-removal care is crucial. Antibiotics are often prescribed to prevent secondary infections, and corticosteroids may be used to reduce inflammation in the affected area [10]. In severe cases where significant blood loss has occurred, as documented in reports from Ethiopia and Tanzania, blood transfusions may be necessary to address anemia. The post-removal recovery is generally uneventful if managed properly, and patients typically experience a rapid resolution of symptoms after the leech is removed [5,11].

## Conclusion
This case highlights the importance of early recognition and timely intervention in preventing potentially fatal complications, especially in young children and individuals who present with delayed symptoms. Clinicians should maintain a high level of suspicion for leech infestations when patients show unexplained airway or gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly after exposure to freshwater. Additionally, there is a need for preventative measures and increased awareness. In regions where leeches are endemic, health education focusing on avoiding untreated water consumption and promoting better hygiene practices can help reduce the risk of leech infestations.
