Abstract

12

Jumping spiders display some of the richest visually-mediated behaviors in nature. Vision is

13

indeed the most important sensory modality in these spiders where motion detection and

14

response to visual stimuli allow key behaviors such as hunting, escaping from predators, and

15

mating. These spiders have been used in various experiments demonstrating the existence of

16

good associative learning and memory abilities, whose mechanism parallels that found in

17

vertebrates. Here we focused on the habituation and dis-habituation (H/DH) paradigm,

18

indicating either a gradual decrease in responsiveness to repeated visual stimuli (H), or a

19

recovery of the habituated stimulus (DH). H is an elementary form of non-associative learning

20

and memory, which is expected to vary from individual to individual. The link between

21

personality and H/DH has been shown in many vertebrates, but rarely in invertebrates. To

22

tackle this question we tested whether personality affects H/DH in the jumping spider

23

Menemerus semilimbatus. In our protocol, habituation was assessed by presenting repeatedly

24

a visual stimulus on a screen to spiders tethered on a locomotor compensator. In the same

25

individuals, personality (namely boldness) was assessed in a walking arena equipped with a

26

shelter. We found that M. semilimbatus habituated and dishabituated to our visual stimulus

27

and that they differed in personality along a shy-bold axis. However, contrary to our

28

expectations, personality was not related to learning. We discussed the results and speculated

29

that the nature (neutral value) of the stimulus might have played a role in making learning

30

independent from personality.

Introduction

35

For animals, it is crucial to be able to detect and memorize important stimuli, and thus to shift

36

and/or improve their behavioral strategies as a result of experience and to adapt to constantly

37

changing circumstances. However, memorizing every single object or event would not only

38

be impossible but would require too many resources and time without any ultimate benefit for

39

the individual. Therefore, given the limited storage capacity and computing power of brains,

40

animals necessarily filter information, discerning relevant from irrelevant ones (Shettleworth,

41

1993).

42

The ability to learn and memorize is ubiquitous in nature and fundamental to any taxa, from

43

vertebrates to invertebrates and plants (Gagliano, 2018; Menzel & Benjamin, 2013; Purves et

44

al., 2019; Squire & Kandel, 2003). One of the most elemental forms of learning is habituation

45

(Giles & Rankin, 2009), which refers to the reduction in physiological responses to a repeated

46

homotypic (same) stimulus. Habituation is reversible: if the stimulus is withheld, the response

47

recovers over time (spontaneous recovery). As a historical example, in Aplysia, repeated

48

electric shocks or tactile stimulations that initially generated a defensive withdrawal reflex

49

induced habituation and response extinction (Rankin & Carew, 1987). After the stimulus
2

50

became habituated, the re-presentation of the same stimulus (electric shock or physical touch)

51

after some time evoked (reactivated) the same defensive behavior again, i.e., induced dis-

52

habituation.

53

Central to the present study, habituation can also be induced by the repeated presentation of

54

stimuli with no intrinsic value. In this case, habituation attests to the animal’s ability to learn

55

that a particular stimulus is indeed irrelevant (Thompson & Spencer, 1966) (i.e., when the

56

animal realizes the stimulus has no effect or value, the responses drop). After habituation has

57

occurred, dis-habituation can be induced by presenting a novel stimulus (Giles & Rankin,

58

2009). To be considered novel, a stimulus may vary from the habituation one even by a single

59

characteristic (shape, size, intensity, motion direction, speed, etc.). In this case, the occurrence

60

of dis-habituation is an indication of an animal’s ability to discriminate between the two

61

stimuli.

62

Individuals of the same species may vary consistently in the way they perceive,

63

recognize/discriminate objects or stimuli (Augustsson & Meyerson, 2004; Carere & Locurto,

64

2011; Guillette et al., 2015; Matzel et al., 2011). These consistent inter-individual differences

65

can be referred to as a personality trait – namely average-level stable, long-term repeatable

66

behavioral, emotional, and physiological differences in suites of traits across time and/or

67

contexts among individuals of the same species (Réale et al., 2007). Personality traits such as

68

boldness, aggressiveness, and activity level (Bell et al., 2009; Carter et al., 2013) have been

69

reported to have a profound effect on many aspects of animals’ behavior and cognition

70

(Dougherty & Guillette, 2018; Griffin et al., 2015; Matzel et al., 2011), including associative

71

and non-associative learning abilities (Carere & Locurto, 2011; Allan et al., 2020, Finke et al.,

72

2021, 2023). A recent meta-analysis performed on 19 species across a wide spectrum of taxa,

73

including insects, showed that bold animals are generally faster learners (Dougherty &

74

Guillette, 2018). The link between personality differences and habituation has been mainly

75

documented in vertebrate taxa (e.g., in mammals: Mazza et al., 2018; fish: Etheredge et al.,

76

2018; birds: Dissegna et al., 2022; Jha & Kumar, 2017; humans: LaRowe et al., 2006;

77

O’Gorman, 1977). However, only a very limited number of invertebrate models are

78

represented in the study of habituation itself (Baglan et al., 2017; Pietrantuono et al., 2021;

79

Engel & Wu, 2009; Haupt, 2004; Haupt & Klemt, 2005; Pinsker et al., 1970; Plowright et al.,

80

2006, Baracchi et al., 2018), and effect of individual differences (personality) in habituation

81

and dis-habituation on invertebrate models remains unexplored.

82

Spiders are one of the most behaviorally diverse and successful groups of terrestrial

83

predators (Coddington & Levi, 1991) which challenges the conventional assumption of

84

cognitive limitations in small animals (Cross et al., 2020; Jakob et al., 2011). The presence of

85

personality types and their link with various behaviors has been observed in many spider

86

species (Beydizada & Pekár, 2023; Chang et al., 2018, 2017; Foellmer & Khadka, 2013;

87

Grinsted & Bacon, 2014; Liedtke et al., 2015; Michalko et al., 2017; Royauté et al., 2015).

88

Among spiders, salticids display some of the richest behaviors and varied personality types

89

(Chang et al., 2018, 2017; Chia-Chen, 2019; Harland & Jackson, 2004; Liedtke et al., 2015).

90

As active hunters, salticids must selectively attend to a wide range of visual stimuli. Their

91

visual system is impressive (Harland et al., 2012), divided into functionally distinct eye pairs.

92

Some of these are specialized in motion detection and recognition (De Agrò et al., 2021;

93

Spano et al., 2012; Zurek et al., 2010; Zurek & Nelson, 2012a) while others are equipped with

94

long eye tubes, capable of saccadic tracking (Bruce et al., 2021; Jakob et al., 2018; Land,

95

1969a, 1969b), and are specialized in object recognition (Bednarski et al., 2012; Dolev &

96

Nelson, 2016, 2014; Rößler et al., 2022). Jumping spiders have been tested in various

97

experiments demonstrating the presence of associative learning and memory (Cross &

98

Jackson, 2017; De Agrò, 2020; De Agrò et al., 2017; Hoefler & Jakob, 2006; Jakob & Long,

99

2016; Peckmezian & Taylor, 2015; Skow & Jakob, 2006; Taylor et al., 2016). Response

100

decrement over time has been shown previously in some jumping spiders (De Agrò et al.,

101

2021; Humphrey et al., 2018, 2019; Nelson et al., 2019; Zurek et al., 2010; Zurek & Nelson,

102

2012a). Habituation instead has been directly tested only in a salticid, Trite planiceps

103

(Melrose, 2015; Melrose et al., 2018).

104

In this study, we investigated whether a personality trait (i.e., boldness) is related to

105

the habituation and dis-habituation rates to visual stimuli in the jumping spider Menemerus

106

semilimbatus, Hahn (Salticidae). We hypothesized that bolder individuals would have a

107

higher probability of responding to any stimulus (i.e., those who initially react more likely)

108

and maintain their response rate higher for a longer time compared to shy individuals.

Material and Methods
3.1

110

Study organism

111

We collected 91 jumping spiders of the species Menemerus semilimbatus (mean ± SEM of

112

prosoma length = 2.852 ± 0.639 mm) of two developmental stages (70 juveniles, 10 adult

113

males, and 11 adult females) in the autumn (September-October) 2022 around the University

114

campus. Spiders were transferred to the laboratory and measured with millimeter graph paper

115

(prosoma length and width in mm). They were then housed singly in clean boxes (75 × 65 ×

116

155 mm). Shelter and water for each spider were provided. Spiders were kept under an

117

automatized light regime (LD=12:12).
3.2

118

Experimental procedure

119

Each spider was kept in the lab in total for five days, during which it underwent a visual

120

habituation/dis-habituation (H/DH) test and two personality assays.

3.2.1 Day 1: Subject capture and preparation

122

A spider was captured and immediately prepared for the subsequent test by fixing a magnet to

123

its carapace. To do so, a spider was first gently constrained between a sponge and a latex film.

124

The latter presented a hole in correspondence with the location of the spider’s head. Here, a

125

1×1mm cylindrical neodymium magnet was glued using a UV-activated resin. .

126

3.2.2 Day 2: Habituation/Dis-habituation (H/DH) test

127

The spiders were subjected to a visual H/DH test. This was performed using a spherical

128

treadmill identical to the one described in De Agrò et al. (2021). In brief, each spider was

129

attached to the end effector of a micro-manipulator by means of the magnet glued to their

130

cephalothorax. Then, the spider was positioned on top of a styrene ball, made frictionless

131

thanks to the continuous flow of compressed air. The distance between the spider and the ball

132

was set to mimic the same distance the animal naturally maintains during locomotion. In this

133

setup, the spider is unable to move or change orientation, but its intended behavior is

134

transferred to the ball. By recording the latter with a high-speed camera and analyzing the

135

videos with special software (FicTrac, Moore et al., 2014) we were able to infer the animal

136

movement “intentions”.

137

The styrene ball was placed 20 cm away from the computer monitor (27 inches, 60hz, 1080p),

138

with the spider facing directly towards its center. Stimulus presentation was controlled via a

139

custom script written in Python 3.8 (Van Rossum & Drake, 2009) and the packages Psychopy

140

(Peirce et al., 2019), OpenCV (Bradski, 2000) and Numpy (Oliphant, 2006; van der Walt et

141

al., 2011). After 210 seconds of desensitization, where the monitor showed a blank, white

142

canvas and the spider was free to move, the first stimulus appeared. This consisted of a black

143

dot, 4° in diameter, 30° to the left (or to the right for the other half of the spiders) of the

144

screen’s center. The dot wobbled slightly left-right (or up-down for half of the spiders), at a

6

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.05.09.539210; this version posted May 16, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

145

speed of 30° per second in a span of 7°. After 0.7 seconds, the stimulus disappeared. Then,

146

after a 15 second pause (ITI, inter-trial interval), a second stimulus appeared, identical to the

147

previous one (in size, direction, speed, and position). A total of 10 stimuli were presented to

148

each spider to induce habituation. The 11th stimulus appeared in the same position, had the

149

same speed, and was of the same size, but wobbled up-down (or left-right if the first 10

150

moved up-down). This stimulus was inserted to induce dis-habituation. Stimuli 12-20 were all

151

identical to stimulus 11, and the 21st changed again, back to what stimulus 1 was. The spiders

152

underwent a total of 45 stimuli presentations with a fixed ITI of 15 seconds, alternating

153

between the two motion directions every 10 stimuli.

154

When detecting an object with their secondary eyes, jumping spiders perform peculiar pivots

155

(whole-body movements), intended to face the target with their primary eyes (Land, 1985;

156

Zurek et al., 2010). Crucially, the pivots are not produced indiscriminately but rather can be

157

selective (De Agrò et al., 2021). We hypothesize that spiders will lower their response rate

158

across repeated presentations of the same stimulus (will habituate) but will produce responses

159

again when the stimulus changes in one of its characteristics (will dis-habituate). These pivots

160

can be observed in the treadmill setup by analyzing the Z-axis rotations of the sphere.

161

3.2.3 Day 3 and 5: Personality assay

162

163

164

165

166

individual was gently released into a novel environment (circle-shaped, 140 mm in diameter,

167

platform) equipped with a shelter (i.e., a piece of cardboard covering one-third of the

168

platform, creating an 8 mm high slit allowing the spider to hide, Fig. 1). The platform was

Ethical Note

197

Each spider used in this study was kept in the laboratory for a total of five days, during which

198

it underwent a visual habituation/dishabituation (H/DH) test and two personality tests (with

199

one-day interval between these personality tests). After spiders were captured, they

200

immediately prepared for the H/DH test by attaching a magnet to their carapace using a UV-

201

activated resin to glue the magnet. Observations show that the magnets did not appear to have

202

a negative effect on the animals. At the end of the test, the magnets were gently removed from

203

the spider’s head. The next day animals were prepared for personality tests. All spiders were

204

released in the same location where they were captured at the end of the study (after the last

205

personality test was completed). The study followed all the legal requirements of the country

206

in which the work was carried out and all institutional guidelines.

208

The data outputted from FicTrac was extracted using custom software written in Python 3.8,

209

employing the packages Pandas (Reback et al., 2020) and scipy (Virtanen et al., 2020) From

211

212

camera recorded at 120fps, so 200 frames correspond to 1.66 seconds. Values of ΔZ could be

225

226

227

229

230

we included subject identity as a random effect for both intercept and slope. After having

231

232

the subsequent stimuli was discarded and the model was repeated for the first batch only. This

233

was done to exclude the effect of fatigue from the analysis. The measures of personality were

Habituation/Dis-habituation

262

There was a significant effect of the interaction between the frame number from stimulus

263

appearance on ΔZ (GAM, EDF = 8.666, RefDF = 8.968, F = 85.606, P < 0.0001), an effect of

264

266

269

324

Discussion

325

In this study, we showed that Menemerus semilimbatus display a consistent personality trait,

326

specifically boldness, measured patterns using various measures. Furthermore, our finding

327

provides evidence that although the spiders were habituated and dishabituated to visual

328

stimuli, their personality did not play a role in this process.
5.1

329

Consistency in Personality

330

Our personality test consisted of four different behaviors which, we hypothesized, could be

331

combined into one personality axis, specifically boldness. We hypothesized that these

332

measurements correlate with each other. Specifically, we predicted that bolder individuals

333

would spend less time hiding under shelter, leading to a negative correlation to the

334

presence/absence of jumps toward the water; Additionally, bolder individuals would walk

335

longer distance and exhibit a higher speed of movement.

336

In accordance with our hypothesis, we found that the latency to stay under shelter after

337

the disturbance in both trials was significantly repeatable, as well as the total walking distance

338

in the first and last 9 minutes, and the average speed during the first 9 minutes. However, we

339

failed to find evidence of significant repeatability in walking speed during the second half of

340

the trials. This was probably due to the fact that the speed calculation for the second half was

341

based on partial data, as, the spiders spent varying amounts of time in shelter after the

342

disturbance, with some individuals never emerging for the remaining 10 minutes of the test.

17

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.05.09.539210; this version posted May 16, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

343

The speed of these spiders was therefore only measured for the time they were outside of the

344

shelter. As spiders alternated between periods of movement and inactivity, calculating speed

345

over short intervals could lead to gross overestimation (if mostly motion sections are

346

observed), or underestimation (if mostly stasis sections are observed). The wide range of

347

potential errors likely prevented us from observing any significant repeatability in this

348

measure.

349

Jumping behavior can be considered as a willingness to explore based on risk-taking

350

tendencies. Using this assumption, we predicted the occurrence of jump events would

351

correlate positively with other measures. However, there are alternative explanations. For

352

instance, in our experiment, the spider may have jumped toward the water not due to a risk-

353

taking attitude, but as an attempt to escape from the stressful situation. This perspective

354

suggests that shy spiders would be more prone to demonstrate escape (jump). Nevertheless,

355

since we found a positive correlation between the occurrence of jumps and other bold

356

behavioral patterns we measured, it is much more likely that jumping is a component of

357

boldness. Additionally, we observed other spiders inspecting the area before jumping in any

358

direction, suggesting a more risk-prone exploration approach. Therefore, individuals

359

performing the jump are probably not scared but are using this behavior to explore their

360

surroundings.

361

Unfortunately, there is no universal consensus on which behaviors do and which do

362

not correlate with boldness, making it difficult to define this trait within a broader context.

363

Most behaviors that we recorded in our study could indicate boldness or the opposite,

364

depending on the context in which they are displayed (Mather & Carere, 2019). Locomotion

365

is the flagship example of this uncertainty. In our experiment, we predicted that walking

366

distance and average speed could be direct signs of boldness or related traits. However, some
18

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.05.09.539210; this version posted May 16, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

367

authors found high locomotion was not always an indication of being bolder (Chapman et al.,

368

2011; Coleman & Wilson, 1998; Dellu et al., 1993; Found & St. Clair, 2017; Mazza et al.,

369

2018; Toms et al., 2010). This issue arises from the uncertain definition of boldness as a trait.

370

Some studies suggest that boldness advances locomotor activity (Wilson & Godin, 2010),

371

while others see it as nearly equal to exploratory behavior (Herde & Eccard, 2013) or even co-

372

occurrence of both (Newar & Careau, 2018). Particularly, in our experimental design, we

373

define boldness as the combination of both approaches. This is similar to a behavioral

374

approach and protocol used in a fish model (Mazué et al., 2015). Our results indicate that

375

boldness correlates negatively with latency to stay under shelter (an expression of shyness)

376

and positively with speed and movement patterns.

377

Despite all the challenges in designing personality measure, the individual differences of these

378

animals appeared stark, especially observing their naive reaction to the trap arena (falcon)

379

during capture where some individuals performed “freezing” behavior without or being less

380

willing to enter the trap while others showed immediate or quick curiosity to explore the trap

381

arena. These inter-individual differences in reactivity level, along with the boldness

382

parameters we measured during the experiment, are most likely a result of a simulated

383

foraging context, although the spiders were not visually fixated on a prey or a predator.
5.2

384

Habituation/Dis-habituation and Personality

385

The Habituation/Dis-habituation data analysis revealed two main findings. Firstly, we

386

observed a clear decrease in response rate across subsequent stimuli, with a steep drop after

387

just 5 stimuli. Secondly, a key to the current experiment, we observed a significant increase in

388

rotational movement in response to the first dis-habituation stimulus, and a regular pattern in

389

the smooth of stimulus number, with an increase of every 10 stimuli. This demonstrates, as

390

expected, that presenting the animals with a novel stimulus induces an increase in response
19

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.05.09.539210; this version posted May 16, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

391

rate. However, as reported by others (Melrose et al., 2018; Nelson et al., 2019), we also

392

observed a general decrease in response rate, as the response to the dis-habituation stimuli

393

never reached the same magnitude as the response to the very first stimulus. This may be due

394

to the fact that our dis-habituation stimulus becomes the start of a new habituation train, and

395

the spider may maintain a memory of both stimuli after the initial experience, leading to a

396

decreased efficacy in dis-habituating.

397

We observed the effect of the time spent under shelter on overall activity in the experiment.

398

However, the lack of any other similar effect of any other of the collected measures

399

disqualifies the idea that this is a true effect of personality. Indeed, if it was an effect of

400

personality, it should have been consistent through all, or at least most, boldness measures.

401

Moreover, the effect is on the overall performance, rather than on the habituation or dis-

402

habituation stimuli, and as such linked to the general activity of the animals rather than on

403

their learning processes. In the end, given the small size of the effect and its isolation, we

404

believe this to be a false positive.

405

Overall, we did not observe any effect of personality in the learning task. We assume that this

406

may be due to the experimental setup we used, where the visual stimuli were formless,

407

leaving the spiders to assign value to them. If the spiders differ in personality, their value

408

attribution process may also vary, resulting in opposite effects for shy and bold individuals. It

409

is possible that bold individuals interpreted the stimuli as a prey and did not habituate because

410

of their boldness and motivation to hunt. On the other hand, shy individuals may have

411

interpreted the stimuli as a predator and did not habituate due to fear and heightened alertness.

412

The response of the two personality types was the same because they interpreted the stimuli

413

differently. Indeed, the link between personality and motivation is related to the context and

414

value (see conceptual papers: Sih & Del Giudice, 2012; Corr et al., 2013 for a review).
20

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.05.09.539210; this version posted May 16, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

415

Alternatively, it is also possible that the spiders did not assign any value to the presented

416

stimuli and viewed them as meaningless moving targets. In this case, the spiders' reaction to

417

the stimuli would not have been related to their motivation to hunt or escape but rather to their

418

innate tendency to pivot towards moving objects, which is hardwired into the brain and less

419

likely to be affected by personality.

420

To date, many authors have studied the whole-body saccade behavior of jumping spiders

421

(Bruce et al., 2021; De Agrò et al., 2021; Harland et al., 2012; Jakob et al., 2018; Land,

422

1972a, 1972b; Zurek et al., 2010; Zurek & Nelson, 2012a, 2012b). Most have reported how

423

the production of pivots is immediate upon stimuli detection. The spiders’ secondary eyes

424

project into dedicated brain areas that occupy most of their visual system (Steinhoff et al.,

425

2020). Given these elements, stimulus detection, recognition, and subsequent turn may be

426

likely foundational to their brain mechanisms, imperturbable by their current motivational

427

state, situation, or indeed personality type as stated before (see above). This remains true also

428

for the dis-habituation stimuli, as motion discrimination seems to be fully functional in the

429

secondary eyes alone (De Agrò et al., 2021), occurring prior to the production of any pivot.
6

430

Conclusion

431

In summary, our study shows that Menemerus semilimbatus spiders display consistent

432

personality traits as evidenced by the behaviors we measured. Bold individuals exhibited

433

longer total distance traveled, faster average speed, less time spent hiding under shelter, and

434

more frequent risk-based behavior (jumps/escape) than shy individuals. These measures were

435

positively correlated, suggesting that they may represent a single behavioral trait- boldness. In

436

addition, we described an effective procedure for visual habituation and dis-habituation in the

437

jumping spider M. semilimbatus which allowed us to observe a clear decrease in response rate

21

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.05.09.539210; this version posted May 16, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

438

to stimulus due to habituation as well as a significant increase in rotational movement in the

439

first dis-habituation stimulus.

440

Lastly, we found no significant correlation between personality and habituation/dis-

441

habituation rates in this spider. In our opinion, this could be due to the fact that bold or shy

442

individuals may have assigned opposite values to ambiguous visual stimuli, interpreting them

443

as either prey or a predator, respectively. This would have placed bold individuals in a

444

hunting context, thereby increasing their attention, while shy individuals would have been in a

445

hiding context, also increasing their attention. Alternatively, the ambiguous stimuli may have

446

had no value at all, and the spiders’ reactions might have relied solely on the hardwired pivot

447

behavior typical to all jumping spiders, making this learning independent of personality.

448

Future research focused on the specific nature of the stimulus would enhance our comparative

449

understanding of how the motivational stage and “pure” response tendency drive an animal’s

450

reaction to such stimuli in relation to the personality axis.
