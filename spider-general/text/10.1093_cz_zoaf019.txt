Are <i>Sepsis</i> flies Batesian mimics of ants?
Baranovičová L, Pekár S. (2026). Current zoology. . doi:10.1093/cz/zoaf019. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Are Sepsis flies Batesian mimics of ants?

## Abstract
AbstractMyrmecomorphy is the most prevalent type of Batesian mimicry among arthropods. Some species of the fly family Sepsidae also bear resemblance to ants. We examined whether the black scavenger fly, Sepsis thoracica, bears a phenotypic resemblance to the abundant ant Lasius niger (workers and males), and whether this resemblance protects it from predation by the syntopic wolf spider Pardosa agrestis. We compared the motion, body shape, size, and coloration traits of S. thoracica to Lasius workers and males, as well as to Drosophila hydei, a non-mimetic fly. Overall, S. thoracica resembled Lasius ant males in motion and body shape, D. hydei in size, and Lasius workers in coloration. Visual models of spider and avian predators revealed that in terms of luminance, S. thoracica was indistinguishable from ant males but different from D. hydei. In predation experiments, S. thoracica was preyed upon less frequently by spiders than D. hydei, and at a frequency similar to ant males. The low consumption frequency of S. thoracica was presumably in part due to the release of defensive chemicals. We conclude that it is an inaccurate Batesian mimic of several Lasius species. This paper brings the first empirical evidence of myrmecomorphy in flies.Keywords: Araneae, Batesian mimicry, diptera, inaccurate mimicry, myrmecomorphys
Defensive mimicry, where the mimic gains protection from predation by resembling an unprofitable organism, can be subdivided into three main types. Batesian mimics are palatable animals which degrade the protection of their model in the eyes of the predator. Müllerian mimicry occurs between unpalatable species which all benefit by splitting the burden of educating the predator. Quasi-Batesian mimics are non-palatable yet still reduce the protection of their even less palatable model (Speed 1993).
Numerous defensive mimics imitate ants, whose complex protection includes a powerful bite, noxious chemicals, high levels of aggression, and within-group coordination. An animal that has diverged from its ancestral phenotype and evolved an adaptive resemblance to ants is called a myrmecomorph.
Myrmecomorphy has been found in several arthropod orders (e.g., McIver & Stonedahl 1993; Pekár et al. 2022). So far, however, it has been mainly studied in ant-mimicking spiders (e.g., Nelson et al. 2006; Cushing 2012; Pekár 2014; Nelson and Card 2015). Among insects, myrmecomorphy has been mainly found in Mantodea, Thysanoptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, and Coleoptera (reviewed in McIver and Stonedahl 1993). For other insect orders, records of myrmecomorphy are scarce.
This is the case for flies (Diptera). These comprise an order of generally small, nutritionally rich, palatable insects. A few fly families have been reported to include ant mimics, but the evidence is primarily anecdotal. Among them, myrmecomorphy has been suspected in the family Sepsidae (Pont and Meier 2002; Dettner 2015). Members of this family exhibit ant-like morphology (shape and coloration) and appear to imitate ants behaviorally (Pont and Meier 2002). However, unlike some other myrmecomorphs, these flies possess apparent wings, and to a human observer, the resemblance is greater to ant males than to workers. Although ant males have limited potential as models due to their lesser defenses and restricted seasonal occurrence (Hölldobler and Wilson 1990), they resemble the workers in several traits, such as the qualitative cuticular hydrocarbon profile (Morrisson and Witte 2011—Lasius niger) or some morphological and behavioral traits. It remains untested whether they are accepted as prey by generalists or evaluated as unprofitable—if the latter is the case, they might participate in mimetic systems.
Flies in the family Sepsidae are probably not completely defenseless. Adults of many species possess a rectal gland with a likely defensive function. The gland produces a mixture of solvents and chemicals (Zenk 1990; in: Pont and Meier 2002) with a faint odor, which can be prominent in species that exhibit swarming behavior, especially the lesser dung fly, Sepsis fulgens Meigen. The defensive function has been proposed based on observations with ants and orb-weaving spiders, both of which display a distaste for feeding on sepsid flies (Pont and Meier 2002). From prey acceptance trials with Sepsis fulgens and various predators, Bristowe (1979) inferred that while the body fluid of sepsids is palatable, they release an odoriferous substance if given enough time. Predatory Scathophaga flies and wasps in the genus Vespula have also been observed to avoid attacks on sepsid flies (Parker 1972).
The black scavenger fly, Sepsis thoracica (Robineau-Desvoidy), presents polyphenic coloration. While the females are always black, males exhibit melanic or amber (bright orange, somewhat darker dorsally) coloration (Busso and Blanckenhorn 2018a). Melanic coloration is assumed to be cryptic and especially efficient against vertebrate predators, whereas amber morphs have been shown to be better protected against arthropod predators of several guilds, because their larger size affords them superior evasion skills, which are more efficient than crypsis against invertebrates (Busso and Blanckenhorn 2018b).
Here, we tested the hypothesis of myrmecomorphy in the melanic individuals of Sepsis thoracica (Figure 1A), because to a human observer, they resemble common sympatric ants, whereas the amber individuals, due to their coloration, do not. As myrmecomorphy is a multi-trait phenomenon (Pekár et al. 2022), we compared the body shape, size, color, and motion traits of melanic S. thoracica flies to those of two putative models—workers and males of the black garden ant, Lasius niger (Linnaeus) (Figure 1D,C), which is the dominant ant species in the microhabitat of S. thoracica; and to those of a non-mimetic fly Drosophila hydei Sturtevant (Figure 1B). Then, we modeled the visual similarity in coloration to see whether these organisms could be distinguished from each other by two potential predators, an arthropod and a vertebrate. Finally, we conducted predation experiments offering these insects to a common syntopic natural enemy, a visually oriented generalist spider predator that avoids ants, Pardosa agrestis (Westring), to find how well-protected the flies are.

## Materials and methods

## Choice of putative model and control
Lasius niger ants were chosen as putative models for Sepsis thoracica because they are found across most of its area of distribution (Janicki et al. 2016; Guénard et al. 2017), occur on and around fresh cow dung pats and in the surrounding vegetation, and we even found several nests in the ground directly under the dung pats. Furthermore, to a human observer, the ants (males and workers) seem similar in size and coloration to the sepsid fly. The males, commonly observed from July to September (e.g., Van der Have et al. 2011), match the phenology of S. thoracica, which also occurs from July to September across most of its area of distribution (Pont and Meier 2002). Winged virgin L. niger queens could not possibly serve as models to S. thoracica, as they are much larger.
As control species, we could not choose another fly in the family Sepsidae, because all but one coastal species share the ant-like morphology of the study species (Pont and Meier 2002). We therefore opted to use Drosophila hydei, a fellow member of the broader Schizophora clade (Bayless et al. 2021), which was readily available to us. These flies are close in size to S. thoracica and possess a typical “fly-like” body shape. They have a cosmopolitan distribution (Zhao and Begun 2017) and are frequently observed on dung piles (Naseri-Karimi et al. 2023), which means that they share microhabitats, as well as some natural enemies, with S. thoracica.

## Materials
We collected Sepsis thoracica flies using a sweeping net from a cow pasture in Neslovice, Czech Republic. We chose not to separate the melanic individuals into males and females, as they are morphologically similar and likely experience a common predation rate. The flies were maintained in a temperature-controlled chamber (at 12 °C, L:D = 16:8) in 386 ml flasks plugged with a piece of polyurethane foam to ensure sufficient air exchange. We supplied the flies ad libitum with either a honey solution, or both a honey solution and fresh cow dung.
We collected workers and males of the ant Lasius niger using a pooter from a single colony in Bohunice, Brno, Czech Republic. Each caste was kept separately in a 120 ml plastic container with a piece of damp tissue in a temperature-controlled chamber (at 8 °C, L:D = 16:8).
We collected Pardosa agrestis spiders (females and large juveniles) by hand from a meadow in Brno, Czech Republic. We excluded adult males, which are naturally less voracious than females. We kept the specimens individually in 6.5 cm tall vials with a layer of damp gypsum at the bottom in a temperature-controlled chamber (at 21 °C, L:D = 16:8). The vials were plugged with polyurethane foam to allow for adequate air exchange. Every three days, we offered two live Drosophila hydei flies to each spider. We removed uneaten flies the following day.
Drosophila hydei flies came from a laboratory culture.

## Phenotypic Similarity
We measured a number of mimetic traits classified into four categories: motion, size, shape, and coloration.
For motion, we placed ten individuals each of S. thoracica, L. niger workers, L. niger males, and D. hydei singly into a lidded Petri dish arena (diameter = 9 cm). We recorded the specimens from above using a Canon LEGRIA HF R56 camcorder for a period that lasted at least one minute and included at least 30 seconds of active motion. We analyzed the videos of each recorded individual using EthoVision XT (version 11.5) (Noldus Information Technology 2015) motion tracking software to obtain the mean velocity, the percentage of time occupied by movement, the mean angular velocity, and the mobility (the movement of the organism’s body with respect to its centerpoint).
For body shape and size, we killed ten individuals each of S. thoracica, L. niger workers, L. niger males, and D. hydei using ethyl acetate vapors, and mounted them onto sticky tape with their appendages spread around the body in a natural position (as in Figure 1). We captured photographs of the dorsal sides of these insects under an OLYMPUS SZX12 stereoscopic microscope with an OLYMPUS SC50 microscope camera using Olympus Stream Motion (version 1.9.4) (Olympus Soft Imaging Solutions GMBH 2014) software. We illuminated the specimens from the lateral side by means of two fluorescent bulbs (Exo Terra Natural Light Compact 13 W) which provide a spectrum similar to natural light.
We determined the actual body lengths from the photographs using the software ImageJ (version 1.54ga) (Rasband et al. 2023). We then used the MAPHIS software (version 1.0.9) (Mráz et al. 2024) to generate and manually correct masks from the photographs mapping the insects’ cephalon, mesosoma (corresponding to the thorax in flies, the alitrunk in ants), metasoma (i.e., abdomen in flies, gaster in ants), antennae, and three pairs of walking legs. We did not include wings in the masks because ant workers possess none and the wings of the other insects are translucent. Furthermore, while the wings might be visible under certain light conditions, they are usually folded above the body in both fly species as well as the ant males, or held nearly horizontally in Sepsis flies, and to a predator observing from the lateral, they would appear less prominent compared to the rest of the body, and would not obscure its shape. From every mask, we extracted the following measurements: 40 regularly-spaced measurements of the body width without the appendages as a measure of body profile; antennal flagellum and walking leg thickness (proximal, medial and distal regions); body length; and circularity index, the latter being calculated for the body without the limbs as the ratio of the area of the body to the area of its circumscribed circle (Ježek 2015). For body shape analysis, we divided the 40 body-width measurements per individual by the maximum value for that individual to standardize the body profiles.
For coloration analysis, we extracted the RGB coloration from these same photos using MAPHIS to obtain the mean R, G and B values for the proximal (femur) segment of each walking leg. We then averaged the colors between the right and left femur of each leg pair. We excluded the medial and distal parts of the legs from this analysis because they were too thin, and we also excluded the antennae as these are very short in Diptera: Brachycera. From information on the visual acuity of lycosids (Clemente et al. 2010), it is not clear whether P. agrestis spiders could even discern these fine details. Because we wanted to capture the coloration of some discrete features such as the eyes or the paler stripes on the abdomen of Drosophila hydei, we used ImageJ to manually outline patches and extract the mean RGB coloration for the following body parts: eyes; rest of head; mesosoma; lighter part of metasoma; darker part of metasoma. The abdomen of S. thoracica is uniform in color; therefore, we used a single color value twice here. The resulting data consisted of triplets of (R, G, B) intensities for eight body regions—three femora (each averaged from both sides) plus the five regions studied using ImageJ. We further used MAPHIS to calculate the relative area taken up by each of these body parts to be used as weights during the data analysis.
We investigated the degree of phenotypic resemblance in the four mimetic categories between S. thoracica, L. niger workers, L. niger males, and D. hydei using a combination of two methods: principal component analyses (PCA), and permutation-based analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) tests. Prior to all analyses, we scaled the variables pertaining to the motion, shape, and size because they differed in units and scales. For coloration analysis, we left the variables unscaled because they had the same units, and the actual differences were meaningful. Because we wanted to control for the size of the body regions in the color analysis, we multiplied the measurements related to that body part by the square root of its relative area—this scales the variability, which determines the weight in the analysis, by the relative area itself.
We used PCA by means of the function “rda” from the R package vegan (Oksanen et al. 2024) to reduce and visualize the multivariate relationships, as the measured variables were highly correlated (0.40 to 0.95 for motion characteristics, 0.0002 to 0.99 for shape features, 0.38 to 0.98 for size measures, and 0.03 to 1.00 for coloration). We calculated Euclidean distances between the centroids of the groups to show the mean dissimilarity. To statistically compare the species, we then subjected the phenotype measurements to PERMANOVA (which, unlike parametric ANOVA, does not assume the distribution of the data) using adonis, function “adonis2” from the package vegan. If the groups differed, we performed pairwise comparisons between S. thoracica and the other insect types by means of pairwise adonis tests, function “pairwise.adonis2” from the package pairwiseAdonis (Arbizu 2020), with Bonferroni-Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

## Visual models
Once we had taken the dorsal view photographs of the insects, we carefully removed all ten individuals of each species from the sticky tape and placed them dorsal side up on a piece of black paper to measure their reflectance using an Ocean Optics USB4000 spectrometer connected to an Ocean Optics PX-2 pulsed Xenon light source, which emits light in the range of 220 –950 nm. The probe was a Y-bifurcated fiber. The reflectance values were relative to a white standard (PTFE optical diffuser reflecting >98% along the entire wavelength range, Ocean Optics WS-1). We obtained a black standard by pointing the fibers at a piece of black paper. The optical fiber was 400 nm in diameter and was held 1 cm above the subject, positioned at an angle of 60º from the vertical. We made one measurement per individual, capturing the entire body.
We selected and further processed the measurements in the wavelength range of 300-700 nm (Supplementary Figure S1). We used the function “procspec” from the R package pavo (Maia et al. 2019) to smooth the reflectance data and to correct negative values, which are artifacts of light scattering, to zero.
During the preliminary analysis of the reflectance data, we found that one of the ant males was an outlier or a measurement error; therefore, we removed it from the dataset.
To determine whether a spider and an avian predator could distinguish S. thoracica from Lasius niger workers, L. niger males, and D. hydei, we used the “vismodel” function from the pavo package (Maia et al. 2019). We calculated noise-corrected chromatic and achromatic contrasts between S. thoracica and the other organisms using the pavo function “bootcoldist.” The function uses bootstrap to estimate 95% confidence intervals for the mean contrast between a pair of species (Maia and White 2018).
For the avian model, we used the average avian UV visual system, with the achromatic (luminance) receptor stimulation based on the sensitivity data of the blue tit, Cyanistes caeruleus (Linnaeus). Inhabiting woodlands (Shutt et al. 2018) which are commonly found within range of cow pastures, the blue tit is one of many passerines which could feasibly hunt S. thoracica. Most importantly, the species is considered representative of the UVS type visual system (Antonov et al. 2011) found in many passerines (Ödeen et al. 2011). We calculated the contrasts against a background spectrum simulating green foliage, as this is one of two main backgrounds found in the environment of S. thoracica. We set the relative photoreceptor density for the “bootcoldist” function at 1:2:2:4 (UV:short:medium:long wavelength receptors) according to Maier and Bowmaker (1993). For the purposes of noise correction, we assumed a Weber fraction of 0.1 for both chromatic and achromatic receptors (Maia et al. 2018).
We based the spider model on the visual system of the jumping spider Habronattus pyrrithrix (Chamberlin), as this is the only spider model available in pavo to date. We calculated the achromatic (luminance) stimulation on the basis of the summed response of all photoreceptors since no suitable specific sensitivity data were available. We set the relative receptor density at 1:2:4 (UV:green:red), in accordance with Zurek et al. (2015). We again calculated the contrasts against a green background spectrum, and assumed Weber fractions of 0.1.
If the confidence interval for the contrast between two studied organisms intercepted the threshold of 1 JND (Just noticeable difference), which is the threshold contrast that an observer can distinguish (Weber 1996), we predicted that the predator might not be able to discriminate between the pair of organisms based on coloration / luminance.

## Predation experiments
Before the experiments, we left each spider without food for 3–5 days, which is enough to induce moderate hunger. At the beginning of each experiment, we placed a Pardosa agrestis specimen (N = 37) individually into a Petri dish (diameter = 6 cm) with its bottom lined with white paper. We allowed the spider to rest without disturbance for at least two hours.
We used a constrained randomized design. Initially, we offered Lasius niger workers to the spiders to ensure that each specimen had experience with ant workers. We introduced an ant worker into the Petri dish containing the spider. Five minutes after the spider had first responded to the ant (positioning itself to face the ant, dodging the ant, attacking it, etc.), we removed the ant and allowed the spider to rest for a period of at least 1 hour, up to several days.
Then, we offered each spider either a S. thoracica fly (N = 28) or a L. niger male ant (N = 27) in a random order. If the spider did not catch and consume the prey item offered within a five-minute period, we removed the insect and allowed the spider to rest for at least 30 minutes. Subsequently, we performed a control trial by introducing a D. hydei specimen into the Petri dish for a five–minute period, measured from the moment that the spider first reacted to it. We did this to verify that the spider was willing to hunt but had refused the previously offered prey item. Some spiders were used in experiments with both S. thoracica and ant males, always with a delay of several days. We excluded spiders that had refused all prey from the analyses because a refusal of Drosophila flies, which are a readily accepted prey, indicates that the spider is not in a state to hunt (e.g., preparing to molt, or in a bad physiological condition).
We recorded all interactions between the spider and the ants or flies using a Canon LEGRIA HF R606 camcorder for a maximum of 10 minutes. From the videos, we noted the occurrence of, and the latency to, the first touch initiated by the spider (either with its legs or chelicerae), the occurrence and latency of capture (the spider grasping the prey in its chelicerae), release (the spider holding the prey in its chelicerae but releasing it instead of killing it), and consumption (the spider beginning to consume the prey, not necessarily processing it fully, as that is not relevant for the exertion of selection pressure). We also noted the occurrence and nature of spider defensive behaviors towards the offered prey.
We used generalized linear models with binomial errors (GLM-b) to test whether the proportions of prey individuals that the spider touched, captured, released, and consumed were similar among the prey types. We used proportion tests to compare the proportions of observed touches, captures, releases, and consumptions between S. thoracica and every other potential prey type. We used ANOVA to compare the latencies to touch, capture, and consumption among the prey types (following logarithmic transformation due to heteroscedasticity).
We performed all statistical analyses in R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team 2023). We depended on Rtools version 4.4 (https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/Rtools/) to be able to install the package pairwiseAdonis on a Windows machine. We constructed the plots using functions within the R packages ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), forcats (Wickham 2023) and vegan (Oksanen et al. 2024), the latter with dependencies permute (Simpson 2022) and lattice (Sarkar 2008), and edited them graphically in Inkscape version 1.2 (Inkscape Project 2022). During the preparation of this work, we used ChatGPT 3.5 to correct grammar and improve sentence structure.

## Predation experiments
The spiders displayed both defensive and aggressive behaviors towards the prey. Defensive behaviors were either mild (only dodging the prey with the limbs several times) or more intense (running away from the prey across the dish, often accompanied by dodging). In aggressive cases, the spiders approached the prey and touched it with their forelegs. This was often followed by an attack with the chelicerae, after which the spiders either released the prey, or kept hold of it and initiated consumption.
In trials with L. niger workers, Pardosa spiders showed no aggressive behaviors, but almost always behaved defensively. Thus, no touch, capture, release, or consumption was recorded for Lasius niger workers (N = 37). On the other hand, the spiders readily touched, captured, and consumed all D. hydei (N = 36). Defensive behaviors were displayed with all prey apart from Drosophila flies.
The relative frequency of touch differed significantly among the offered prey types (GLM-b: χ32=103.2, P < 0.0001, figure 4): the frequency of touch was significantly higher for S. thoracica than for L. niger workers (Proportion test: χ12 = 27.36, P < 0.0001), significantly lower for S. thoracica than for D. hydei (Proportion test: χ12 = 14.43, P = 0.0001) and not different between S. thoracica and L. niger males (Proportion test: χ12 < 0.1, P = 1).
Comparison of the proportions of spiders that touched, captured, consumed, and reacted defensively to four prey types: the mimic, the control and two putative models (L. niger males and workers). Bars are means; whiskers are 95% CI for the bar.
The relative frequency of capture also differed among the insects (GLM-b: χ32=101.61, P < 0.0001, Figure 4): the frequency of capture was significantly higher for S. thoracica than for L. niger workers (Proportion test: χ12 = 16.71, P < 0.0001), significantly lower for S. thoracica than for D. hydei (Proportion test: χ12 = 24.47, P < 0.0001) and not different between S. thoracica and L. niger males (Proportion test: χ12 < 0.1, P = 1).
The observed frequencies of attack followed by release were low (n = 3 for S. thoracica, n = 2 for L. niger males, and n = 0 for both L. niger workers and D. hydei).
The relative frequency of consumption differed among the prey types (GLM-b: χ32=104.15, P < 0.0001, Figure 4): the frequency of consumption was significantly higher for S. thoracica than for L. niger workers (Proportion test: χ12 = 11.24, P = 0.0008), significantly lower for S. thoracica than for D. hydei (Proportion test: χ12 = 31.57, P < 0.0001) and not different between S. thoracica and L. niger males (Proportion test: χ12 < 0.1, P = 0.92).
The relative frequency of defensive spider behaviors also differed among prey types (GLM-b: χ32=79.606, P < 0.0001, Figure 4): defensive behaviors were less frequent against S. thoracica compared to L. niger workers (Proportion test: χ12 = 22.61, P < 0.0001), more frequent for S. thoracica compared to D. hydei (Proportion test: χ12 = 9.29, P = 0.002), and did not differ in frequency between S. thoracica and L. niger males (Proportion test: χ12 < 0.1, P = 1).
There was no significant difference in the latencies to touch (ANOVA: F2,67 = 0.18, P = 0.836), capture (ANOVA: F2,57 = 0.047,P = 0.954), release (ANOVA: F2,4 = 1.286, P = 0.37), and consumption (ANOVA: F2, 52 = 0.132, P = 0.877) among the prey types.

## Discussion
We performed a comparison of multiple mimetic traits of a mimic, two models, and a non-mimetic species. The phenotypic analysis revealed that melanic individuals of Sepsis thoracica are similar in some traits to Lasius niger males and workers, and in others to Drosophila hydei. The choice of Drosophila hydei as control in this study was suitable from the point of view of the ecology it shares with Sepsis thoracica, but phylogenetically, these species are more distant relatives. Besides using another sepsid, which was not possible due to their ant-like phenotypes, the second best choice would have been a fellow member of the superfamily Sciomyzoidea, but none were available to us with an ecology similar to Sepsis thoracica.
In terms of motion characteristics, S. thoracica did not differ significantly from L. niger males. The primary trait shared between S. thoracica and ant males was their high angular velocity. However, the motion traits of S. thoracica also overlapped with those of the workers. The workers exhibited the greatest mean velocity and spent the greatest proportion of time in motion, followed by S. thoracica in both characteristics. Given their ability to fly, the fast walking of Sepsis flies is likely not used to escape predation. One possible explanation is behavioral ant mimicry, as seen in several ant mimics whose resemblance to their models is amplified by the tendency to walk faster and rest less compared to their non-mimetic relatives (McIver and Stonedahl 1987a, 1987b—true bugs; McAlpine 1990—flies). Furthermore, a faster walking speed can hinder the identification of inaccurate myrmecomorphs by confusing the predators (Pekár 2021).
In terms of body shape, S. thoracica was closest to L. niger males. Male ants use flight for dispersal and mating (Hakala et al. 2019), so their body morphology is adapted for flight. In contrast, ant workers possess several morphological adaptations for efficient ground locomotion and labor (Peeters et al. 2020). Sepsis thoracica is likely similar in body shape to ant males rather than workers because it employs similar means of locomotion. Achieving a more worker-like morphology might come with penalties to flight.
In terms of coloration, S. thoracica exhibited the greatest similarity to ant workers, followed by ant males. Dividing the coloration into several regions analogous between the insects allowed us to show the overall dissimilarity between the two fly species. D. hydei, in particular, differed from the others in the brightness (R, G, B intensities) of the pale part of its abdomen (Supplementary Figure S2).
The predator vision models, which are based on spectrophotometry measurements and calculated against a green foliage background, yielded mixed results. One visual model simulated the vision of the general avian predator based on the blue tit Cyanistes caeruleus. According to the results, the general avian predator should be able to distinguish S. thoracica from all other studied insects on the basis of chromatic contrast (i.e., coloration), but should not distinguish the fly from L. niger males on the basis of achromatic contrast (luminance). However, the effectiveness of fooling the predator matters only if the fly is discovered. Vertebrate predators, such as birds, exert a negative selection pressure on prey body size due to the greater conspicuousness of larger prey items (e.g., Maiorana 1981; in Troost et al. 2008; Busso and Blanckenhorn 2018b), and for the same reason (visibility), cryptic coloration is also selected for in their prey (Marples et al. 2005). Moreover, they are likely to observe S. thoracica from above against a dark background of cow dung. This implies that the most significant defense strategy of the females and melanic males of S. thoracica against vertebrate predators is crypsis, rather than mimicry. Furthermore, several studies suggest that, compared to vertebrates, invertebrate predators may exert stronger selection pressure on small insects (e.g., Fagan and Hurd 1994; Lang et al. 1999; in Busso and Blanckenhorn 2018b), meaning that evaluating mimicry from the perspective of arthropod perception might be more relevant.
The modeled invertebrate, the jumping spider Habronattus pyrrithrix, would not be able to distinguish S. thoracica from D. hydei based on chromatic contrasts. However, in achromatic contrasts, the spider could only distinguish the fly from D. hydei. The accuracy of the visual model for the spider predator simulated herein is limited by the lack of data on achromatic receptor stimulation in jumping spiders. Additionally, salticids are not very representative of natural enemies of S. thoracica, as their abundances in the microhabitat are low compared to other potential predators. Pardosa spiders, for instance, likely do not possess the same superior visual capabilities as salticids (Rovner 1989); in fact, only a single type of photoreceptors has been found in the eyes of several lycosid species, which suggests that they do not have color vision (DeVoe et al. 1969; DeVoe 1972). Chromatic contrasts based on the vision of jumping spiders, which possess multiple types of photoreceptors (DeVoe 1975), might be irrelevant. Information on the vision of other predatory arthropods is frequently scarce. As such, it remains unknown whether actual arthropod enemies of S. thoracica can discriminate the fly from the other organisms on the basis of coloration alone.
For defensive mimicry to be present, the mimic should experience a reduced predation rate. In the predation experiments, S. thoracica was captured less frequently than Drosophila hydei. In a small Petri dish arena, the lower predation cannot be attributed to crypsis or an inability of the predator to capture the fly; in fact, though there were plenty of opportunities to attack, the spiders were reluctant to do so. In several cases, the flies would seek out the spider to touch or chase it, eliciting the reaction of dodging or even running away—behaviors also displayed with both ant castes, but not with Drosophila flies. These spider individuals likely tried to put distance between themselves and S. thoracica because they perceived it as dangerous.
There was also evidence for the release of defensive chemicals by the fly. The attacks that did occur were almost always intermediate, and in three trials, the spiders released a still alive S. thoracica, having already spent some time handling it. These findings agree with Bristowe (1979), who found that another species, S. fulgens, is palatable if killed immediately, but might be rejected if given the opportunity to release a distasteful substance.
We must note here that the ant males were also only captured by only about one third of hungry spiders. It is evident that they do have some protection from Pardosa agrestis, a generalist capable of accepting a wide range of prey. Unlike L. niger workers, the males do not spray formic acid, nor do they behave aggressively. It was not clear from our research whether the males are palatable to P. agrestis, or whether they deter the enemy by their resemblance to the workers. At any rate, they possess weak defenses (potential unpalatability) at best, and almost certainly cannot serve as models to S. thoracica.
Considering that several phenotypic traits of S. thoracica were most similar to those of ant workers or ant males with which they share the means of dispersion, inaccurate mimicry of the workers is a plausible explanation for the lowered predation rates compared to D. hydei. In such a case, there might not be pressure to increase mimetic fidelity, as predators might only discriminate among prey based on a handful of traits (Sherratt and Peet-Paré 2017). Arthropod predators, such as wolf spiders, do not have much time to ponder possible mimics in nature, because they themselves face the risk of predation, as well as the opportunity cost of missing other prey. Additionally, the erroneous decision to attack prey that turns out to be an ant might have severe, even fatal consequences. Chittka et al. (2009) hypothesize that a predator under time constraints should avoid lengthy discrimination between mimics and dangerous models and make the safe decision to reject the prey.
The inaccuracy of the mimicry would provide several advantages to the fly. Firstly, it would mitigate the morphological (and also behavioral) limitations, such as wing reduction, that a more perfect resemblance might demand. Secondly, rather than closely imitating only one ant species, S. thoracica might mimic multiple Lasius species. In central Europe, there are three species of similar body size and coloration: L. niger, L. platythorax Seifert, and L. alienus (Foerster) (S. Pekár, unpublished data). Superficial mimicry of multiple species would mean a greater abundance of models, which would alleviate constraints on the population size of the mimic (Edmunds 2000).
If Sepsis thoracica is indeed a mimic of ant workers, the type of the mimicry depends on the presence and degree of its chemical defense. Proper classification would require analyzing the contents of the “Dufour” gland and testing their palatability to predators. In the lack of direct evidence, we suggest that it be considered Batesian for now. If chemical defense is nevertheless present, the mimicry when considering Pardosa agrestis as operator would not be Müllerian, because Lasius niger workers are already strictly rejected as prey and have nothing to gain from the presence of a mimic. In our experiments, Sepsis flies were consumed fairly often. We therefore expect that they should be under negative frequency-dependent selection within the mimicry system. If predators in pastures frequently encounter S. thoracica and learn that in some cases, it can be quite edible (if the attack is immediate), they might become desensitized to ant-like phenotypes and increase their predation on ants. As such, if S. thoracica is chemically defended, it could be a quasi-Batesian mimic—not completely palatable but degrading the protection of its even more defended model (Speed 1993).
In this study, we only examined the response of a single arthropod predator towards the sepsid fly. While Pardosa agrestis is a dominant predator of small arthropods on dung, it is not the only agent that could exert selection pressure on Sepsis thoracica. Other invertebrate predators we identified on the dung and in the surrounding vegetation included mainly thomisid spiders, whose sensory capabilities or prey discrimination criteria could differ from those of P. agrestis, as well as insects such as rove and ground beetles, damsel bugs, wasps, or Scatophaga stercoraria (Linnaeus) flies. Vertebrates were represented in great numbers by the barn swallow Hirundo rustica (Linnaeus), and we would also expect the presence of the sand lizard Lacerta agilis (Linnaeus) (L. Baranovičová and S. Pekár, unpublished data). Thus, the entire community of predators may exert various selection pressures (Pekár et al. 2011).
In conclusion, Sepsis thoracica faces a significantly reduced predation rate by the wolf spider Pardosa agrestis. We propose the hypothesis that S. thoracica is a Batesian inaccurate mimic of the workers of several Lasius ant species.
