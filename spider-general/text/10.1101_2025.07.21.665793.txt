Abstract

14

Spiders exhibit tremendous variations in eye numbers, but the genomic basis underlying this

15

diversity remains largely unexplored. Here, we analyzed the genomic data of 148 spider species

16

in 31 families, representing all phenotypes of eye numbers including 8, 6, 4, 2, and 0. Our

17

analyses revealed that a core set of 29 genes involved in eye development and

18

phototransduction is conserved across all spiders, irrespective of their eye number, indicating

19

that eye reduction is not caused by the loss of key developmental genes. We found that

20

evolutionary transitions leading to reduced numbers of eyes are primarily associated with

21

parallel genome-wide relaxed selection. While these independent reduction events shared

22

several genes under consistent selective pressures, they did not share any genes under

23

positive selection, indicating putatively divergent molecular mechanisms. In contrast, the

24

evolution of complete eye loss in cave-dwelling spiders is associated with parallel genome-wide

25

intensified selection. Notably, we identified shared genes under both intensified and positive

26

selection across independent origins of eyelessness, suggesting a parallel molecular

27

mechanism. Altogether, our study provides genomic insights into the parallel evolution of eye

28

reduction and complete eye loss in spiders.

Introduction

31

The evolution of spider visual systems is fascinating but complex, characterized by tremendous

32

diversity in eye number (e.g. 8, 6, 4, 2, 0 eyes), eye arrangement and eye size (1–3). For

33

example, most spider species have 8 eyes, while few species have no eyes (Fig. 1A). This

34

raises the intriguing question: why do spiders have diverse eye numbers? Past studies have

35

focused on understanding how environmental factors and ecological niches shape eye number

36

evolution (2). For instance, cave-dwelling spiders exhibit reduced or no eyes, representing a

37

trait associated with adaptation to lightless habitats (4–8). Thus, spiders in visually complex

38

environments typically possess diverse visual systems, facilitating enhanced adaptive capacity.

39
40

Parallel evolution of eye numbers is common in spiders. One exemplar trait is the consistent

41

eye number in phylogenetically close clades (i.e. family), such as 6-eyed species in families

42

Scytodidae, Sicariidae, Oonopidae, and Leptonetidae (1) (Fig. 1A). Another example is the

43

parallel complete loss of eyes in cave spiders within genus Leptonetela (4, 7, 8). These parallel

44

features underscore the role of environmental pressures in shaping the evolution of eye

45

numbers in spiders, illustrating how different species have parallelly evolved similar traits to

46

meet the challenges of their respective ecological niches, while the underlying genomic

47

landscape remains largely unexplored.

48
49

Here, we assembled a comprehensive genomic dataset of 148 species within 31 spider families,

50

representing all phenotypic traits of eye numbers including 8, 6, 4, 2 and 0. Using this dataset,

51

we aimed to uncover parallel changes associated with the evolution of eye numbers in spiders.

52

Specifically, we first tested whether presence or absence of key genes involved in eye

53

development and phototransduction are associated with the evolution of eye numbers in

54

spiders. This is because a recent study identified a nearly complete set of phototransduction

55

pathway genes in eyeless cave spiders (4), while this evolutionary scenario has not been fully

56

tested in spiders with diverse eye numbers across the phylogeny. We further tested whether

57

parallel changes in the patterns of molecular evolution are associated with multiple evolutionary

58

transitions leading to the diversity of eye numbers.

59
60

Results

61

We analyzed the publicly available high-quality genomes of 27 spider species with 8 eyes and

62

representative transcriptome assemblies of 22 species with 6, 4, 2, or 0 eyes (Dataset S1). Our

63

results showed that a core set of 29 genes associated with eye development and

phototransduction is retained across all these species, irrespective of their eye numbers

65

(Dataset S2). This comparison across species with varying degrees of eye reduction reveals

66

that the fundamental genetic toolkit for vision and eye formation has not been lost, even in

67

lineages where the number of eyes is significantly reduced. The consistent presence of these

68

29 core genes across all analyzed genomes and transcriptomes suggests their essential role is

69

maintained throughout the spider phylogeny.

70
71

We next focused on the patterns of molecular evolution associated with evolutionary transitions

72

leading to the diversity of eye numbers, including shifts from 8 eyes reduced to 2 eyes (i.e.,

73

Caponiidae), 8 eyes reduced to 4 eyes (i.e., Symphytognathidae), and 8 eye reduced to 6 eyes

74

(i.e., Scytodidae, Sicariidae, Oonopidae, Leptonetidae). In these families, the number of eyes is

75

a consistent trait, and the six-eyed spiders, in particular, are closely related phylogenetically

76

(Fig. 1A). Given that the ancestral state for spiders is with 8 eyes, these lineages represent

77

distinct evolutionary routes of eye degeneration in adaptation to different environments. Through

78

comparative genomics analysis of 9,146 orthologous genes among 148 spider species, we

79

investigated natural selection pressures associated with different routes. We found that a

80

majority of genes experienced parallel relaxed selection across all three major evolutionary

81

transitions (Fig. 1B, Dataset S3). Furthermore, we identified a set of genes, including

82

Hermansky-Pudlak syndrome 1 protein, AP-3 complex subunit delta-1, exocyst complex

83

component 4, that were subject to shared relaxed selection across different routes (Fig. 1C,

84

Dataset S4). Interestingly, however, the genes under positive selection were unique to each

85

independent evolutionary route, with no overlap observed (Fig. 1D, Dataset S5), potentially

86

indicating that different molecular mechanisms underlie parallel instances of eye reduction.

87
88

Finally, we investigated the evolution of complete eye loss (i.e. eyeless) in spiders. Unlike the

89

clade-specific patterns observed in spiders with 2, 4, or 6 eyes, the eyeless trait often

90

differentiates at within-family (Fig. 2A and 2B) or within-genus level (Fig. 2C). For example,

91

species within the cave-dwelling genus Leptonetela (family: Leptonetidae) have lost their eyes,

92

while other species in the same genus retain 6 eyes (4, 8). A similar pattern has been seen in

93

family Agelenidae (typically 8 eyes) spiders, where the cave-dwelling species Troglocoelotes

94

proximus is eyeless (9). We conducted phylotranscriptomic and molecular evolution analyses at

95

genus-level (Leptonetela) and family-level (Agelenidae) to examine these independent

96

instances of complete eye loss. Our analysis revealed that the degeneration of eyes into a

97

complete eyeless state was primarily associated with intensified selection on a majority of genes

(Fig. 2D, Dataset S6). Notably, two independent evolutionary paths to eyelessness shared

99

several genes under intensified or relaxed selection, such as fibroblast growth factor receptor 1

100

101

identified a shared set of positively selected genes, such as FGF1, titin, zig3 (Fig. 2F and 2G,

102

Dataset S8), suggesting parallel molecular evolution associated with the complete loss of eyes

103

in these distinct lineages.

105

Discussion

106

Why do spiders exhibit such a remarkable diversity in eye numbers? One possibility is that this

107

diversity reflects adaptations to various ecological niches they inhabit. The ancestral state for

108

spiders is believed to be 8 eyes, and as they colonized different habitats, some lineages

109

encountered environments with low-light conditions, such as dense bushes or leaf litter (10). In

110

these settings, the selective pressure to maintain a full complement of eyes may have

111

diminished, leading to various degrees of eye degeneration over extended evolutionary time.

112

This hypothesis is consistent with our findings from the evolutionary path from 8 to 6 eyes,

113

where we found that an eye development gene (peroxidasin) and a retinal degenerative disease

114

gene (retinitis pigmentosa 9) experienced positive selection (11). Concurrently, we found that a

115

gene associated with muscle development (Tropomyosin) (12) was under positive selection in

116

these six-eyed spiders, which may be linked to compensatory adaptations in predatory behavior

117

required in low-light environments.

118
119

Our comparative genomic analysis suggests a fundamental distinction between the evolutionary

120

mechanisms associated with eye reduction versus complete eye loss. The transition to a

121

reduced number of eyes appears to be a relatively moderate evolutionary process, primarily

122

characterized by parallel relaxed selection at the genome-wide level. This "relaxed" selective

123

pressure has allowed for diversified evolutionary outcomes, as evidenced by the fact that the

124

positively selected genes we identified were unique and independent to each eye-reduction

125

route. In contrast, the adaptation to the absolute darkness of cave environments imposes much

126

stronger selective pressures. The complete loss of eyes in cave-dwelling spiders is associated

127

with genome-wide intensified selection. This intense pressure appears to drive a more

128

constrained and parallel evolutionary trajectory, a conclusion supported by our findings of

129

shared intensification patterns and, critically, a common set of genes under positive selection

130

across independent lineages of eyeless spiders.

Study design

134

Spiders exhibit tremendous diversity in number of eyes, varying from most species with 8 eyes

135

to few species with no eyes. We sought to identify genetic basis underlying the evolution of eye

136

numbers in spiders. We aimed to analyze and compare the genomic data of spider species

137

representing all phenotypic traits of eye numbers including 8, 6, 4, 2, and 0.

Transcriptome assembly and gene model prediction

181

For Nanopore RNA sequencing reads, we performed long read filtering and trimming using

182

NanoFilt with parameter (-q 10 -l 500) (14) and de novo assembly using RNA-Bloom2 (15).

183

Next, we performed gene model (protein-coding gene) prediction in each reference

184

transcriptome using TransDecoder (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder). Finally,

185

we used BUSO pipeline (13) to assess the quality of reference transcriptome assemblies.

Phylogenetic tree construction and divergence time estimation

188

We included the genomes and reference transcriptomes with more than 90% identified

189

complete BUSCO genes. In addition, we downloaded the genome of striped bark scorpion

190

Centruroides vittatus (GCF_030686945.1) for phylogenetic tree construction as the requirement

191

of an outgroup species. BUSCO complete gene repertoire includes two categories, such as

192

“Complete-Single-Copy (S)” and “Complete-Duplicated (D)”. We extracted the single-copy

193

BUSCO genes “(S)” from 27 genomes and 121 reference transcriptomes, and identified one-to-

194

one single-copy genes across 148 species using OrthoFinder (16). Further, we used a

195

phylogenomic approach to reconstruct the phylogeny of 148 spider species and an outgroup

196

scorpion species, based on a dataset of amino acid (AA) sequences corresponding to a pooled

197

set of 1:1 single-copy genes. We performed AA sequence alignment using MAFFT (17),

198

removed gaps using trimAL (18), and assembled a concatenated dataset that included all 1:1

199

single-copy genes with a minimum length of 200 AA. Finally, we used ModelFinder (19) to

209

and further explore orthologous relationship of protein-coding genes across 148 spider species.
