Abstract

29

Previous research on attention to fear-relevant stimuli has largely focused on static

30

pictures or drawings, and thus did not consider the potential effect of natural motion. Here, we

31

aimed to investigate the effect of motion on attentional capture in spider-fearful and non-

32

fearful participants by using point-light stimuli and naturalistic videos. Point-light stimuli

33

consist of moving dots representing joints and thereby visualizing biological motion (e.g. of a

34

walking human or cat) without needing a visible body. Spider-fearful (n = 30) and non-spider-

35

fearful (n = 31) participants completed a visual search task with moving targets (point-

36

light/naturalistic videos) and static distractors (images), static targets and moving distractors,

37

or static targets and static distractors. Participants searched for a specified animal type

38

(snakes, spiders, cats, or doves) as quickly as possible. We replicated previous findings with

39

static stimuli: snakes were detected faster and increased distraction, while spiders just

40

increased distraction. However, contrary to our hypotheses, spider targets did not speed up

41

responses, neither in the group of control nor in the group of spider-fearful participants.

42

Interestingly, stimuli-specific effects were toned down, abolished, or even changed direction

43

when motion was introduced. Also, we demonstrated that point-light stimuli were of similar

44

efficiency as naturalistic videos, indicating that for testing effects of motion in visual search,

45

“pure” motion stimuli might be sufficient. As we do show a substantial modulation of visual

46

search phenomena by biological motion, we advocate for future studies to use moving stimuli,

47

equivalent to our dynamic environment, to increase ecological validity.

48
49

Keywords: attention bias, fear of spiders, visual search, biological motion

Introduction

52

Motion attracts our attention. It is perceptually very salient (Dorr et al., 2010) as well

53

as emotionally significant (Krumhuber et al., 2013; Schmidt et al., 2022; St. Peter et al.,

54

2022). For example, when asked about the most scary and disgusting features of spiders,

55

individuals with spider phobia most frequently report “movement of the spider” (Lindner et

56

al., 2019). However, research on visual attention to fear-relevant stimuli has rarely considered

57

motion, and even less so natural movement. Instead, experiments typically used static pictures

58

or drawings (Abado, Sagi, et al., 2020; Burra et al., 2019; Haberkamp et al., 2013; Rinck et

59

al., 2005).

60

Research on early attentional processes

61

Research on attentional processes in specific phobia has been vital to understand the

62

etiology and maintenance of these disorders (Okon-Singer, 2018; Van Bockstaele et al.,

63

2014). In case of acute threat, early attentional engagement enables the organism to protect

64

itself (e.g. fight, flight). However, each startle response at the sight of a green stick that we

65

mistook for a snake is wasting resources. Still, the gain of surviving an actual snake encounter

66

seems to outweigh the costs of false alarms (Martin et al., 2021). In anxiety disorders, this

67

leads to hypervigilance – a constant monitoring of the environment for threat (Eysenck, 1992,

68

2014).

69

Early attention processes for threatening and fear-relevant stimuli have been studied

70

extensively using static images in visual search paradigms. In these paradigms, groups of

71

stimuli are presented on the screen and participants have to indicate as fast as possible if and

72

where a “target” stimulus is presented among the other (“distractor”) stimuli. By comparing

73

response times for different constellations of target and distractor stimuli, attentional

74

processes for different stimuli are compared. The experimental findings indicate two early

75

attentional biases: Facilitated attentional capture and prolonged disengagement (e.g. Abado,
3

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
76

Aue, et al., 2020; Aue & Okon-Singer, 2015). In these experiments, stimuli are labelled as

77

fear-relevant (e.g. spiders in spider phobia), threatening (e.g. snakes), or neutral (e.g.

78

mushrooms). The studies show that individuals with spider-phobia attend faster to fear-

79

relevant stimuli (spiders) and take longer to disengage from them than individuals without

80

spider-phobia (Aue et al., 2013, 2019; Miltner et al., 2004; Mogg & Bradley, 2006; Rinck et

81

al., 2005; Rinck & Becker, 2006). Individuals with and without spider phobia attend faster to

82

threatening stimuli (snakes) and need longer to disengage from them compared to neutral

83

stimuli (mushrooms) (Aue et al., 2016; Gallup & Meyers, 2021; LoBue et al., 2014; LoBue &

84

Matthews, 2014; Öhman et al., 2001, 2012; Veerapa et al., 2020). However, these findings

85

have not been reported in all studies, potentially because of methodological differences in task

86

or measures of attention (Aue & Okon-Singer, 2015; Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Lipp, 2006;

87

Miltner et al., 2004; Quinlan, 2013; Rinck et al., 2005, 2010; Zsido et al., 2024).

88

The role of movement

89

All of these findings for early attentional effects were obtained with static stimuli. This

90

is potentially problematic, as many threatening and fear-relevant stimuli (e.g., animals) in our

91

environment are typically encountered when moving. This also appears to impact the

92

acquirement of specific fears: Infants spent more time looking at snake videos compared to

93

videos of other animals but they did not make a difference between pictures of different

94

animals (DeLoache & LoBue, 2009). Also, motion is well known to affect early attentional

95

effects: For example, the onset of movement as well as biological motion capture attention in

96

experimental settings (Abrams & Christ, 2006; Dorr et al., 2010; Smith & Abrams, 2018) and

97

moving targets in chasing detection paradigms are faster identified among static distractors

98

than vice versa (Hofrichter & Rutherford, 2019; Meyerhoff et al., 2014; Pratt et al., 2010).

99

Other findings point to a specific role of biological motion cues by showing that only upright,

100

animate motion but no other motion triggers cued attention (Hirai et al., 2011; Shi et al.,
4

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
101

2010). To isolate the effect of biological motion, the authors used point-light stimuli that

102

consist of moving dots, each representing a joint (Johansson, 1973). Together, these dots

103

visualize biological motion (e.g. of a walking human or cat) without a explicitly visible body

104

(Chang & Troje, 2008; Troje, 2002; Troje & Chang, 2023).To this day, the effect of

105

biological motion on attention has been studied separately from the effects of emotional

106

significance (Fernández-Folgueiras et al., 2022; Ogren et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2010), with no

107

studies showing fear-relevant biological motion stimuli to individuals with specific phobia.

108

Specifically in spider phobia, the movement of spiders has been reported most

109

frequently as anxiety-inducing factor (Lindner et al., 2019). However, only few experimental

110

studies investigated the role of motion. For example, Vrijsen et al. (2009) modified the dot

111

probe task – in which one of two simultaneously presented stimuli is followed by a target – by

112

moving images across the screen instead of presenting a static image. However, this

113

movement was no “biological” motion as the spiders did not move their legs and did not turn

114

when their motion path described a curve. As in previous studies with static images, spider-

115

fearful participants showed an attentional bias towards moving spider images compared to

116

moving neutral stimuli. Schmidt et al. (2022) showed videos and images of different animals

117

to participants with and without spider fear and obtained emotional judgements. Their results

118

show that motion enhances the emotional evaluation (positive and negative) of animals, which

119

is further pronounced for feared animals such as spiders; additionally, in line with qualitative

120

reports (Lindner et al., 2019) it seems to be the unpredictability of spider motion that

121

increases fear and disgust in individuals with spider-fear but not without spider-fear (Grill &

122

Haberkamp, 2023).

123

The current study

124
125

The present study was motivated by three research questions: First, can we replicate
earlier findings of preferential processing of threatening/fear-relevant stimuli? Second, is
5

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
126

there a specific effect of motion on attentional capture and disengagement? Third, does

127

motion further boost the preferential processing of threatening/fear-relevant stimuli? In

128

addition, we want to contrast the effects of “pure” biological motion in point-light stimuli

129

with those of videos of moving animals.

130

Therefore, we presented threatening and fear-relevant stimuli in a visual search task to

131

replicate earlier results on attentional capture and disengagement, and test the modulating

132

effects of different types of motion. Participants had to identify a target among eight

133

distractors by key presses. We chose to combine neutral and threatening/fear-relevant

134

target/distractors, since this comparison made it possible to distinguish attentional capture

135

from a difficulty in disengaging from threat.

136

Hypotheses

137

(1) Influence of valence on target search. (1a) Target valence: Threatening targets

138

should be found faster compared to neutral targets in the group of non-fearful participants

139

(RTs: cat/dove target > spider/snake target), and fear-relevant targets should be found fastest

140

in the group of spider-fearful participants (RTs: cat/dove > snake > spider). (1b) Distractor

141

valence: We expected the reversed effect of distractor valence on target search, with

142

threatening and/or fear-relevant stimuli slowing the search compared to neutral stimuli (non-

143

fearful group: spider/snake > cat/dove; spider-fearful group: spider > snake > cat/dove).

144
145
146

(2) Motion: The presence of motion was expected to affect target identification (i.e.,
faster search for moving targets, slower search when distractors move).
(3a) We hypothesized that motion and stimulus valence would interact, such that

147

moving targets would increase the attentional bias towards threatening/fear-relevant targets

148

(i.e., the search advantage of threatening targets increases when targets move). We expected

149

this difference for fear-relevant targets to be more pronounced in the spider-fearful group.

6

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
150

(3b) We expected slower disengagement from threatening/fear-relevant distractors

151

which should be even slower for moving distractors. For fear-relevant distractors this

152

difference should be more pronounced in individuals with spider-fear.

153

(4) We aimed to isolate the effect of biological motion on early attentional processes

154

by testing point-light stimuli, allowing us to compare our findings for “pure” biological

155

motion with those for videos of moving animals.

Methods
Participants
A total of 61 participants were included in the study (30 spider-fearful, 31 non-fearful

159

individuals). The sample size was determined by a power simulation using R. To detect the

160

hypothesized effects with 1 - β of .80 at α = .05, we determined a sample size of n = 30 per

161

group (for details see Supplementary A). Participants were recruited via a mailing list of the

162

local university and leaflets distributed on campus. They received 15 € or course credit for

163

payment. Participation requirements were normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no

164

known psychotic disorder diagnosis (current or prior) or previous treatment for spider phobia.

165

To assess eligibility, participants completed the following questionnaires via the online tool

166

Unipark (EFS Survey, 2021): Spider Phobia Questionnaire (SPQ; German version, Hamm,

167

2006; original version Klorman et al., 1974), Fear of spiders questionnaire (FAS German

168

version, Rinck et al., 2002; original version, Szymanski & O’Donohue, 1995), Snake Anxiety

169

Questionnaire (SNAQ; German version, Hamm, 2006; original version, Klorman et al., 1974),

170

and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II, German version, Hautziger et al., 2009, original

171

version, Beck et al., 1996). Additionally, we used the specific phobia section of the

172

Diagnostic Interview for Psychological disorders (DIPS, German version, Margraf, Cwik,

173

Suppiger, et al., 2017) to test for the clinical relevance of spider phobia.

174

185 individuals participated in the study. Participants with BDI sum scores > 13

175

(indicating “mild depression”) were excluded (18 participants). Only participants with SNAQ

176

177

average snake fear. To obtain two substantially different experimental groups, participants

178

179

192

195

217

Stimuli were natural and point-light videos, as well as pictures of spiders, snakes,

218

doves, and cats. We used doves and cats in contrast to previous studies which used plants or

219

arthropods (e.g. flowers/butterflies) since the latter do not move on the ground like spiders.

220

The natural video stimuli consisted of eight different animals per category (i.e. eight

221

different cats). The videos were selected from stock footage databases. In all videos, the

222

animal moved straight ahead at the center of the screen with no or little camera motion. The

226

227

313

Category Search Task

327

(1a) Snake but not spider targets speed up search irrespective of spider-fearfulness

328

To compare our results to previous research, we first focus on target/distractors type

329

differences for the motion condition “static target/static distractors” across groups (see Figure

330

2b). In contrast to our expectations, spider targets slowed down search (t(121) = -3.32, p = .002;

331

dark blue bar in Figure 2b), while, interestingly, snake targets elicited faster reaction times

332

than neutral targets (t(121) = -10.7, p < .001; dark red vs. green bar in Figure 2b). The group ×

333

target/distractors type interaction barely failed to reach significance (F(3.16, 186.22) = 2.352, p =

334

.056), but no main effect of group was found within any of the different target/distractors

335

types (p > .1, see Supplementary D). This implies that spider-fearfulness did not modulate the

336

reactions to different target/distractor combinations.

337

Discussion
Summary of findings
To allow for a comparison with previous research our experiment also featured static

401

stimuli. In line with our hypothesis for static stimuli, snake targets sped search up, while

402

snake and spider distractors slowed search down. However, contrary to our hypotheses,
19

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
403

spider-fearfulness had no effect on reaction times and spider targets did not speed up search

404

but slowed it down.

405

In addition to frequently used pictures of animals, we employed naturalistic as well as

406

point-light video stimuli. As expected, moving targets were identified faster than static

407

targets, and moving targets prolonged search times compared to static distractors.

408

Additionally, point-light stimuli were identified faster than natural stimuli.

409

Also, attentional capture by snake targets and distraction by spider distractors persisted

410

when stimuli were moving. But unexpectedly, motion did not enhance these effects: Moving

411

snakes sped up search to the same extent as static snakes. Moving spiders did even slow down

412

search less than static spiders. Finally, contrary to our hypotheses, moving spider targets did

413

not speed search up more than neutral targets, and moving snake distractors did not slow

414

search down more than neutral distractors – rather they sped it up.

415

Increased distraction by spiders

416

Distraction by spiders in our experiment is in line with previous research on prolonged

417

disengagement by threatening stimuli (Burra et al., 2019; Öhman et al., 2001). Surprisingly,

418

the effect was not stronger for spider-fearful participants, which is in contrast to prior research

419

demonstrating prolonged disengagement from phobic stimuli (Miltner et al., 2004; Rinck et

420

al., 2005). Also, we did not replicate earlier findings of a faster search for static spider targets

421

by spider-fearful individuals (Aue et al., 2013, 2019; Rinck et al., 2005; Rinck & Becker,

422

2006), adding to previous studies that failed to observe this effect (Abado, Sagi, et al., 2020;

423

Aue et al., 2016; Miltner et al., 2004; Weymar et al., 2013, for review see Cisler & Koster,

424

2010). Instead our participants reacted slower to pictures of spiders as targets (cf. Miltner et

425

al., 2004).

426
427

Since our stimuli did induce significantly different levels of anxiety, disgust, arousal,
and valence in spider- and non-spider-fearful participants, the absence of a group difference is
20

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
428

not explained by a lack in induction of emotion evaluation. However, our failure to replicate

429

previous findings might be explained to some extent by differences in task: The majority of

430

previous studies did use different task instructions where participants search for a picture,

431

which is different from the others (e.g. spider among butterflies), instead of being instructed

432

to search for a specific type of stimulus (e.g. spider) (Aue et al., 2013, 2019; Rinck et al.,

433

2005). As the convergent validity between visual search tasks is rather poor (Van Bockstaele

434

et al., 2020), minor task differences might explain the absence of attentional capture by spider

435

targets in spider-fearful participants in our study. Importantly, there are no reliability or

436

validity coefficients for studies using spiders or other specific phobia stimuli. Even if task

437

differences modulate the effect size, our study supports previous accounts that attentional

438

capture by spiders is not a stable effect and therefore cannot be replicated across all visual

439

search tasks (Rinck et al., 2005).

440

Faster search for snakes and distraction by snakes

441

Snakes were identified faster than neutral targets and static snake distractors slowed

442

search down, replicating earlier findings with static snake stimuli (Gallup & Meyers, 2021;

443

Öhman et al., 2001, 2012). How can we explain that the snake-in-the-grass effect is more

444

stable than the attentional interference effect, induced by spiders? In line with others, we

445

suggest that a major factor is visual complexity: Spiders are visually more complex than

446

snakes, so that the snake targets have a perceptual advantage (cf. Landová et al., 2023;

447

LoBue, 2014; LoBue & Adolph, 2019; Lobue & Deloache, 2011; Pakai-Stecina et al., 2024)

448

and pop out more than spiders. Also, snake distractors are more similar to each other, and

449

might therefore provide a more homogeneous background for visual search which is easier to

450

ignore compared to the more heterogeneous spider distractors. These explanations in terms of

451

perceptual rather than emotional differences are supported by the emotional judgements,

21

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
452

which show that snake stimuli did not induce more anxiety, arousal, disgust or negative

453

valence than spiders.

454

Point-light and natural video stimuli show similar response times

455

Our findings show that point-light stimuli are sufficient to study early attentional

456

processes: They were as effective as natural videos in inducing a motion effect as well as

457

demonstrating a distraction by spiders and an attentional capture by snakes. We suggest that

458

this shows once again the strong saliency of motion (Abrams & Christ, 2003, 2006; Smith &

459

Abrams, 2018) and effectiveness of point-light stimuli (Chang & Troje, 2008; Shi et al., 2010;

460

Troje, 2002; Troje & Chang, 2023), which compensates for the absence of features such as

461

color and texture. Notably, the emotional valence of point-light stimuli was lower compared

462

to video stimuli, so that point-light stimuli might be less effective in studies mainly focusing

463

on emotional (top-down) effects on attention. Therefore, in clinical research, point-light

464

stimuli would probably underestimate the effect of emotional valence. Still, different point-

465

light animal stimuli induced different emotional ratings, so that for experimental research,

466

point-light stimuli allow to study the influence of biological motion and its threat evaluation

467

on attentional processes across different animals without confounding visual features (color,

468

texture).

469

Motion affects reaction times

470

In general, motion had the expected effects on overall reaction times, with moving

471

targets that were found faster and moving distractors that slowed search down. This supports

472

existing evidence for a strong effect of motion on attentional processes (Hofrichter &

473

Rutherford, 2019; Smith & Abrams, 2018). Interestingly, in our study the motion effect was

474

not strong enough to boost the already faster identification of snake targets or the slower

475

identification of targets among snake distractors. Furthermore, introducing motion for targets

476

eliminated all differences between different target/distractors types, with the exception of
22

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
477

snake targets. Thus, only the snake-in-the-grass effect is strong enough to overcome the effect

478

of motion. Again, both findings might be explained in terms of accounts that preferential

479

processing of snakes is not (only) due to their evaluation as threatening or due to their motion,

480

but rather due to their visual form (LoBue & Adolph, 2019).

481

Contrary to our expectations, motion did not increase the target/distractors type

482

differences (cf. Schmidt et al., 2022). In fact, motion rather reduced (i.e., the increased

483

distraction by spider distractors and the speeded detection of snake targets), abolished (i.e.,

484

slower reaction to spider compared to neutral targets) or changed direction of

485

target/distractors type effects (i.e., static snake distractors distract, moving snake distractors

486

speed up search). Our findings suggest that previously reported effects of static threatening

487

stimuli might not translate to (biological) motion stimuli.

488

As many threatening and fear-relevant stimuli in our environment are typically

489

encountered when moving, specifically animals, static stimuli are of limited ecological

490

validity. Therefore, we advocate for future studies to use moving stimuli, equivalent to our

491

dynamic real-world environment, to obtain experimental findings that are relevant for and can

492

be translated to everyday life.

493

Limitations and strengths

494

To distinguish between effects of motion and valence, we used mixed motion

495

conditions (a moving target between static distractors and vice versa). Including an all-motion

496

condition (moving target/moving distractors) would have allowed to disentangle the effects of

497

motion in general vs. the effects of a difference in biological motion between target and

498

distractors. A further limitation of our study is the absence of measurements that would allow

499

us to map the time course of attention. For example, future studies might track eye

500

movements to study the reaction time differences in more detail.

23

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
501

A strength of our study is the natural stimulus material: To achieve higher ecological

502

validity, we chose videos and images of animals within their natural habitat. Although we did

503

not include any videos with (much) camera motion or occluded animals, which might have

504

been even more realistic, our videos featured different background textures. Future studies

505

might take our approach one step further by investigating visual search for different animals

506

within the same complex, naturalistic scenes (i.e., using the same background for all stimuli

507

instead of search arrays of separate images/videos).

508

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first use threat-relevant biological

509

motion stimuli in a visual search paradigm. This opens the opportunity to study the effects of

510

“pure” biological motion without potential confounds like background texture or color of the

511

animal.

512

Given the medium sample size (n = 61) of our study, our results should be interpreted

513

with caution. In our power analysis, we estimated the effects of motion to be small to

514

medium, so that our design was not optimal to detect potentially small interaction effects (e.g.

515

group × display × motion). Future studies might harness online data collection to obtain larger

516

sample sizes.

517

Conclusion

518

Our study investigated the influence of biological motion on attentional capture by and

519

the prolonged disengagement from threatening stimuli. Our results demonstrate a stable

520

snake-in-the-grass effect for moving and static stimuli. However, contrary to our hypotheses,

521

spider-fearfulness did not modulate attentional processes – and distraction by spiders was

522

even less pronounced when using moving compared to static stimuli. Also, we found no

523

significant difference between the visual search for moving spiders compared to neutral

524

targets. Our results demonstrate that results with static stimuli might not translate easily to

525

moving stimuli. Future research should investigate whether the snake-in-the-grass effect is
24

MOVING SPIDERS DO NOT BOOST EARLY VISUAL PROCESSING
526

indeed the only attentional capture phenomenon that is preserved with moving stimuli. To test

527

this, the effects of basic visual features (shape/motion) must be dissociated from the effects of

528

biological motion and emotional valuation. Despite our rather nuanced results, our findings

529

open a new avenue of research by using visual search to investigate the role of biological

530

motion in the evaluation of threat, which will help to increase the translation from the lab into

531

everyday life.
