A flexible spiking hair sensillum for ultralow power density noncontact perception.

# A flexible spiking hair sensillum for ultralow power density noncontact perception

## Abstract
The ability to recognize targets before physical contact is crucial for organisms’ adaptability to real-world environments. However, current noncontact sensing systems face challenges in power density and biological fidelity. Here, we report a flexible spiking hair sensillum (FISH) with an ultralow power density < 100 nanowatts per square millimeter for achieving noncontact tactile perception (NCTP). This device can transduce proximity or airflow into spike trains with a frequency range of ~500 to 1500 hertz. A spiking NCTP system is developed, achieving high accuracy (>92%) in multidimensional recognition of noncontact targets, mediated by airflow. Furthermore, we show that a spider robot equipped with a FISH matrix outperforms one relying exclusively on machine vision in tasks of predation and evasion, demonstrating high adaptability to complex environments. Our design enriches the perceptual modalities for neuromorphic systems, offering great potential for advancing future robotics and autonomous vehicles.

## INTRODUCTION
Noncontact perception plays a pivotal role in the survival strategies of many organisms, enabling them to detect environmental changes and potential threats without direct interaction. Vision perceives the real world with the highest resolution in a noncontact manner, while it has the highest power density compared to other sensory modalities (1, 2). For example, the human eye, despite occupying only a small fraction of the body’s sensory surface, consumes more than 40% of the brain’s sensory processing energy (3). Consequently, visual systems are primarily restricted to specific spatial domains, resulting in limited threat prediction and high vulnerability to exposure. To strike a balance between the detection field and energy consumption, spiders have a photoreceptor density 20 times lower than that of mammals (4–9) but maintain notable higher hair sensillum density (400/mm2) across their body (10, 11). Specifically, hair sensilla can transduce external noncontact stimuli (e.g., airflow caused by prey) into spike trains with an energy consumption of less than 100 pJ per hair per event (12, 13), resulting in an energy density hundreds of times lower than that of visual systems (1–3, 14). This strategy can achieve broad sensory coverage while minimizing energy expenditure (15, 16) and allows continuous sensing without the limitations of vision, such as blind spots or low visibility.
Noncontact sensors inspired by hair (17), feathers (18), antennas (19), and skin (20, 21) have been reported. These sensors achieve noncontact sensing through media such as air (18), electric fields (17, 21), magnetic fields (19, 20), water vapor (22), etc. For example, a neuromorphic antennal system based on an electronic antenna sensor with three-dimensional (3D) flexible structures has been reported, revealing the possibility to have magnetic navigation and touchless interaction with high biological plausibility (19). Noncontact tactile input is encoded into amplitude-modulated signals for most devices at this stage, while biological sensory receptors encode the information into frequency-modulated signals. Recent developments in artificial spiking receptors, which integrate sensors and oscillating neuron devices (encoders), make it feasible to encode sensory information directly into spike trains (23–28). For example, an artificial mechanoreceptor based on a polypyrrole-based resistive pressure sensor and an NbOx memristor achieves tactile coding by converting external mechanical stimuli into frequency-modulated electrical spikes (23). However, there remains a lack of reports on replicating hair sensilla with spike-encoding capability, and the potential of low–power-density noncontact perception has not been fully exploited.
Here, we report a flexible spiking hair sensillum (FISH) with low power density that converts airflow information into spike trains for noncontact perception. This FISH consists of a polyimide (PI)–based hairlike sensor for detecting airflow or contact and a PI-based threshold switching (TS) memristor for encoding frequency-modulated spikes. It generates spikes at a frequency of ~500 to 1500 Hz and consumes around 600 nW. The energy is ~660 pJ per event, very close to a spider’s mechanoreceptor. As a result, a power density of less than 100 nW/mm2 is achieved, which is at least 100 times lower than that of reported noncontact sensing devices. A noncontact tactile perception (NCTP) system is built on the basis of a FISH matrix and a spiking neural network (SNN) for processing the airflow information through population coding and receptive field integration, achieving a high accuracy of more than 92% in multidimensional recognition (types and directions) of noncontact targets. As a proof of concept, this NCTP system is integrated into a spider robot to verify adaptability under different environmental conditions. Our results show the continuous perceptual coverage of the spider robot, unconstrained by the low visibility of vision, revealing great potential for advancing future robotics and autonomous vehicles.

## RESULTS

## Conceptual design and structural characterizations of the FISH
Figure 1A shows the conceptual diagram of the NCTP system inspired by spiders. The airflow generated by the looming target causes the hair sensilla to produce spike trains (fig. S1). The characteristics of the airflow are influenced by the target’s size and location. This enables the spider to identify the type and direction of the target and respond accordingly, in other words, the NCTP. Inspired by this, Fig. 1B shows the integration of our FISH matrix into a spider robot to mimic such perception. This spider robot thereby has visual perception via a digital camera (at the front) and the NCTP through the FISH matrix (at the back). The inset shows an optical image of the hairlike sensor array. Figure 1C shows the processing flow of our FISH matrix for encoding airflow, and then the encoded information is processed through a neural network. As shown in Fig. 1D, each FISH consists of a laser-induced graphene (LIG)/PI-based hairlike sensor and a flexible Ag/PI/LIG/PI-based TS memristor that are connected in series. The hairlike sensor mimics the function of biological hair that can reflect the airflow information through its bending angle. The bending of the LIG/PI hair causes the LIG electrode to be stretched, thus forming a longer equivalent conductive path (29). Therefore, the bending of the LIG/PI hair would modify the resistance of the sensor, thereby altering the current flowing through the TS memristor. As a consequence, the output frequency of this circuit would change via the self-oscillating property of the TS memristor (30). In other words, the TS memristor can be used as an encoder to transduce the sensing information into spike trains. The inset (right panel) of Fig. 1D qualitatively shows the dependent relationship between airflow intensity and the spike frequency. Optical image (left) and micrograph (middle) of the hairlike sensor and the scanning electron micrograph (right) of LIG are shown in Fig. 1E, respectively. The LIG sensing element with a width of ~25 μm and the LIG intercontact area are fabricated on a PI hair, which is patterned by laser (the details can be found in fig. S2). The scanning electron micrograph of LIG on the sensor shows a foamlike porous structure, which helps to enhance the sensor’s responsiveness and flexibility (31–35). The Raman spectrum of the LIG sensing element is shown in fig. S4A, and the 2D peak indicates the composition of graphene (29, 31). Figure 1F shows the optical image (left) of the TS memristor array, the micrograph (middle) of a single memristor in a crossbar structure, and the scanning electron micrograph (right) of an oblique cross section of the memristor, respectively. The flexible TS memristor shows a specific structure as shown in the cross-sectional micrograph (the detailed fabrication process can be found in Materials and Methods), where the LIG electrode shows a dome-like structure.

## Electrical characterizations of the hairlike sensor
Figure 2A shows the relative resistance change (ΔR/R) of the hairlike sensor in response to different contact distances. Different contact distances would result in varied bending angles and change the resistance of the sensor in turn. Figure 2B shows a typical response of the hairlike sensor at a contact distance of 1.5 mm. The response and recovery times are roughly 72 and 56 ms, respectively. Furthermore, Fig. 2C shows the sensor’s response to four static contact distances, ranging from 0 to 2000 μm (with a step of 500 μm), indicating that the sensor reacts consistently to various contact distances. Figure 2D shows the results in response to different airflow speeds by our sensor. The lowest speed of airflows detected by this sensor can be as low as 0.4 m/s. At an airflow speed of 7.0 m/s, the response and recovery times are roughly 40 and 26 ms, respectively (Fig. 2E). Notably, the instability inherent in airflow would cause the LIG/PI hair to vibrate, and fluctuations can be observed in the response curve. Therefore, for airflow response, the average value over a 1-s period was taken. Moreover, when a series of airflows with speeds ranging from 0 to 9.0 m/s was applied to the sensor, distinguishable responses can be observed in Fig. 2F. In addition, the detection limit can be tuned through changing the thickness of the PI substrate, and the lower detection limit of 0.04 m/s can be achieved (details can be found in fig. S5). The reliability of the sensor was evaluated over 4500 cycles by repeatedly positioning and removing it from the airflow (airflow speed: 7.0 m/s) using a motorized displacement system, with negligible variation observed between cycles, as shown in Fig. 2G. In addition to these dynamic tests, sensor-to-sensor variation was also assessed (details can be found in fig. S4B). These statistical measurements indicate good reproducibility and consistency of the sensor across a large batch of devices.

## Electrical characterizations of the flexible TS memristor
The typical TS behavior of the memristor is shown in Fig. 3A, which also shows that the TS behavior is stable even after 1000 consecutive cycles (details can be found in fig. S6). A sweeping voltage (with a step of 0.01 V) was applied to the Ag electrode under a compliance current of 100 nA with the LIG electrode grounded. When the forward voltage exceeds the threshold voltage (VTH) of the TS memristor, Ag conductive filaments (CFs) are fully formed between the Ag and LIG electrodes, switching the device from the high resistance state (HRS) to the low resistance state (LRS) (36–42). When the sweep voltage is swept backward, the CFs can be maintained until the applied voltage is lower than the hold voltage (VHOLD). In this case, the CFs rupture spontaneously, resulting in a transition from LRS to HRS due to interfacial energy minimization of the Ag CFs (43, 44). The distributions of HRS and LRS of the TS memristor in 1000 repeated cycles are shown in Fig. 3B. The coefficient of variation of HRS and LRS that can be obtained by dividing the SD by the mean is calculated as 7.71 and 10.71%, respectively. Figure S7 shows the impact of the fabrication process on device performance. Figure 3C shows the distribution of HRS and LRS with different bending radii, indicating good mechanical stability (details can be found in fig. S8). Figure 3D shows the device performance at different temperatures (from 40° to 200°C), indicating good temperature stability (details can be found in fig. S9). Figure 3E shows the spike frequencies of the device under current biases (Iin). As Iin increases from 100 pA to 200 nA, the frequency increases from 155 to 2650 Hz. Figure 3F further shows spiking responses at Iin values of 10, 100, and 200 nA, with frequencies of 430, 1700, and 2650 Hz, respectively. The spiking behavior of this TS memristor is attributed to the parasitic capacitance (Cp) in the device (details can be found in fig. S10). While the memristor is in the HRS, Cp charges under the current bias, causing the output voltage (Vout) to rise. Once Vout reaches VTH, the device switches to the LRS, leading to a sudden drop in resistance and a rapid discharge of Cp. When Vout falls below VHOLD, Cp begins charging again, resulting in spontaneous HRS-LRS-HRS switching and the generation of voltage spikes (30). Higher currents lead to faster charging, which increases the spike frequency. Table S5 shows the comparison of our PI-based memristor with flexible TS memristors in terms of power consumption, thermal stability, mechanical flexibility, and compatibility with array-level integration (27, 45–51).

## Implementation of airflow-mediated noncontact recognition
Figure 5A shows a schematic diagram of the NCTP experimental setup for airflow-mediated multidimensional noncontact target recognition. In this setup, different target types are simulated by stencils with distinct shapes, and the directions of looming targets are simulated by airflows in different directions. The direction of the airflow is shown in Fig. 5B, where the hairlike sensor is fixed at the coordinate origin and extends obliquely within the xoz plane. The PI substrate is placed horizontally within the xoy plane, and thus the FISH’s receptive-field direction is projected onto the yoz plane. All airflow angles in this work are referenced from this coordinate system. Airflows from different directions pass through stencils before being applied to the FISH matrix to simulate a specific airflow pattern of the looming object. In this case, the specific airflow would induce spike trains from each FISH, which are recorded through a multichannel data acquisition system. The recorded raw spike count per unit time is termed “frequency,” which is then normalized to obtain normalized spike frequency (NSF). To characterize its directional selectivity, we rotated a single FISH around the x axis in increments of 90°, enabling it to sequentially face four orthogonal directions. This configuration allows the FISH to detect airflow stimuli from all directions within the 360° range of the yoz plane, with airflow directed toward the origin point. Each tested direction is represented by a distinct color covering a specific quadrant, as shown in Fig. 5C. The NSFs of the FISH are measured at four distinct rotation angles under airflow stimuli from different directions, maintaining an airflow speed of 8.0 m/s. The results exhibit neuron-like direction-tuning characteristics, demonstrating that FISH has a specific preferred sensing angle (52). As shown in Fig. 5D, a “butterfly” airflow pattern can be mapped by a 5 × 5 FISH matrix, where each pixel is represented by spikes with a certain NSF. Notably, the mapped butterfly image would be varied with changing directions of the airflow, as shown in Fig. 5E. This sensory processing draws on two biological mechanisms: population coding and receptive-field integration. When airflow interacts with the matrix, each FISH produces a response reflecting the local airflow conditions within its receptive field. Variations in airflow direction lead to distinct nonuniform patterns in the matrix outputs, while airflow patterns determine the spatial distribution of intensity within the matrix output. Consequently, although a single FISH is unable to distinguish the essential features (including profile and direction) in looming object, multiple FISH would be able to encode these complex stimuli and retain multidimensional information about airflow patterns and directions. To verify our assumption, we built a custom dataset comprising 10 labels, representing five distinct pattern types and two directional conditions (left and right), as shown in Fig. 5F (details can be found in Supplementary Text and fig. S15). The SNN architecture and training parameters are described in Supplementary Text and figs. S17 to S19. After 70 training epochs, an accuracy of 92.25% was achieved by this SNN (Fig. 5G), as further verified by the confusion matrix in Fig. 5H. In summary, our NCTP system can convert airflow information by the FISH matrix into spiking images containing both shape and direction, which facilitates the processing capability of the SNN. As shown in Fig. 5I, the FISH is featured with close power density and operating frequency to its biological counterpart (spider’s hair), and its power density is lower than existing spiking tactile receptors (10, 13, 23–27). Table S6 shows the detailed comparison of FISH with its biological counterpart, existing artificial spike tactile receptors, and noncontact sensing devices, in terms of power density, operating frequency, and information-encoding properties (18–27). Table S7 shows a performance comparison between FISH and other airflow-sensing devices (53–60). Our system integrates speed, direction, and target recognition via spike encoding on a fully flexible platform.

## Demonstration of NCTP-enhanced spider robot
Figure 6A shows a schematic diagram of this robotic system. A commercial digital camera (ESP32) is mounted at the front of the robot’s head unit, while the 2 × 3 FISH matrix is mounted at the back. Airflow stencils of either prey (flies) or predators (birds) were placed in front of the FISH matrix to simulate specific airflow patterns of these looming targets. Airflow directions were also used to simulate directions of these looming targets. The circuit diagram of the system is shown in Fig. 6B. A microcontroller unit (MCU) was used for processing sensor data and motion control of the robot. Figure 6C shows five experimental scenes and the detection results in those scenes, with varied targets and lighting conditions used to test the robot’s reactivity in response to visual (light) and noncontact tactile (airflow) stimuli. The completed reaction sequences in each scene are recorded by a video recorder on top of the robot. Four phases of the reaction sequences are shown in Fig. 6D. The four phases are, from left to right, environmental conditions, target detections, decisions, and actions, respectively. In scene 1 (line 1), the robot remains stationary because the digital camera cannot detect the target due to the dark environment. In scene 2 (line 2), upon illumination, the digital camera detects prey located at the left front, prompting the robot to attack. Scenes 3 and 4 (lines 3 and 4) demonstrate noncontact tactile detection of prey, where airflow indicating prey positioned at the robot’s rear (right and left, respectively) prompts turning and attacking behaviors regardless of lighting conditions. Scene 5 (line 5) demonstrates noncontact tactile detection of a predator at the left rear, prompting an escape response toward the right front. Detailed information about the experiments is provided in Supplementary Text, figs. S20 to S26, and movies S1 and S2.

## DISCUSSION
In summary, we developed a FISH that incorporates a LIG/PI-based flexible hairlike sensor and a PI-based flexible TS memristor, achieving ultralow power density (<100 nW/mm2) for NCTP. Leveraging population coding and receptive-field integration, an NCTP system is built with a 5 × 5 FISH matrix, which achieves recognition accuracy exceeding 92% for identifying both the type and direction of noncontact targets. A spider robot equipped with a 2 × 3 FISH matrix effectively performs noncontact tactile sensing independent of visual conditions, greatly extending environmental adaptability with ultralow power density.
Although machine vision can provide much higher precision in noncontact object recognition, it depends on good lighting conditions and operates at an extremely high power density. This makes machine vision data intensive and energy intensive. For example, ~144 Mb/s of visual information is transmitted through a digital camera (estimated by 6 Mb per frame and 24 frames/s). Continuous image capture, processing, and data transmission demand a significant energy budget, which limits the applicability of vision-based systems in energy-constrained environments. In contrast to these high energy demands, many insects rely on hair sensilla distributed across their bodies, which operate in an energy-saving manner. External stimuli are encoded into spikes by sensing spots with low density, enabling an extremely small data size. This low-cost strategy also compensates for the inefficiency of eyes under dim conditions and the limited field of view.
This biological strategy allows organisms to perform continuous long-distance and all-around environmental sensing without high energy expenditure, which has inspired the development of our NCTP system. Notably, by mimicking the sparse distribution and efficient signal encoding of biological hair receptors, our approach achieves an ultralow power density that minimizes energy consumption and extends sensor coverage within the same energy budget. This design, which relies on a FISH to resemble hair receptors, also takes advantage of a spike-encoding scheme for noncontact perception, improving signal robustness, enabling data sparsity, and ensuring compatibility with SNNs. Furthermore, while many existing noncontact sensing systems can detect the presence of an object, few have been applied to recognize multiple features of the object, such as its type, direction, or movement. This study paves the way for sensing schemes that are energy efficient, adaptable to realistic conditions, and highly biologically plausible. It offers promising applications in advanced robotics, autonomous vehicles, and intelligent human-machine interfaces, where energy efficiency and continuous perception are critical for success. Future research will focus on further miniaturizing the FISH matrix, improving spatial and temporal resolution, and exploring the integration of additional sensory modalities to ensure its applicability to more complex environments and diverse fields.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Fabrication of the hairlike sensor
Figure S3 shows the fabrication process of the hairlike sensor. A PI substrate, ~25 μm thick, was first cleaned with ethanol and then affixed to a silicon wafer using polyurethane double-sided tape. The wafer was placed in a vacuum environment at 150°C for 30 min and subsequently allowed to stand at room temperature for 12 hours. A commercial ultraviolet (UV) laser scribing machine (5 W and 355 nm; Lixia) was then used to pattern LIG according to the parameters shown in tables S1 and S2. Compared with graphene induced by a 10.6-μm CO2 infrared laser, UV-laser–induced LIG exhibits low thermal diffusion and forms high-density patterns, yielding denser, more uniformly graphitized electrodes with excellent conductivity and flexibility (33, 61). Accordingly, this work used UV laser processing to induce LIG. Then, the LIG/PI was cut into predesigned patterns (parameters are provided in table S3). Last, the PI film was peeled off using an alloy gold wire. The 3D structure was formed because of the combined effects of out-of-plane deformation induced by the laser ablation process and residual curvature.

## Fabrication of the flexible TS memristor
First, LIG bottom electrodes (~80 μm in width) were induced by a UV-laser scribing machine on a PI substrate (the parameters are shown in table S4). Then, a fully imidized thermoplastic polyimide (TPI-12, Mitsui Chemicals; 20 wt %) was diluted to 12.5 wt % using anhydrous N,N′-dimethylformamide. A 40-μl aliquot of this solution was dispensed onto the LIG/PI substrate (25 mm by 25 mm) and spin coated at 700 rpm for 40 s. To accelerate residual solvent removal, the coated sample was baked at 100°C for 12 hours in ambient air. If the curing were performed at room temperature instead, then the curing time would need to be extended by at least 36 hours. Following the baking step, the sample was stored at 45°C in a desiccator for at least 24 hours. Because the polymer was already fully imidized, additional high-temperature curing was unnecessary. Last, the Ag top electrodes (~100 μm in width) were deposited on the PI switching layer with a metal mask process by thermal evaporation.

## Characterization and measurement
Cross-sectional images of Ag/PI/LIG/PI were measured by a field emission scanning electron microscope (EVO18, Carl Zeiss AG); top views of the hairlike sensor and memristor were measured by a Research-Grade Upright Materials Microscope (Axioscope 5, Carl Zeiss AG). The Raman spectra of the LIG sensing element were carried out by an intelligent, fully automatic Raman spectrometer (HORIBA LabRAM Xplora, HORIBA Scientific). The electrical characteristics of the memristor and the spiking performance of the system were measured by the Fs-Pro PX500. The capacitance of the PI switching layer was measured by an instrumentation impedance analyzer (IM3533-01 LCR, HIOKI). The model of the hot stage is V-D100, produced by VTEC. The humidity and temperature during testing are ~300 K and ~50% relative humidity.

## Experiments on proximity and airflow perception
A silicon wafer (0.5 μm thick) was mounted on an electric displacement device (ZLZ, Zhiyuan Automation) and inverted for contact distance testing of the hairlike sensor. For the airflow tests, a hair dryer was positioned above the sensor array, and the airflow speed 5 mm above the hairlike sensors was measured using a JT1406 airflow velocity sensor (Jantytech), which served as the reference for airflow speed.
