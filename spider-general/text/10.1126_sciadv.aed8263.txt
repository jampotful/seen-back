Architecting bioinspired nanocrystalline domains for ultimate robust and transparent cellulose photonic hydrogels.
Li Q, He C, Zhang W, Zhang Y, Huang J, Xu K, Zhang F, Qing G. (2026). Science advances. . doi:10.1126/sciadv.aed8263. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Architecting bioinspired nanocrystalline domains for ultimate robust and transparent cellulose photonic hydrogels

## Abstract
AbstractBiomimetic spider silk achieves remarkable functionalities through hierarchical architectures with highly oriented crystalline domains, offering potential across multiple disciplines. However, achieving uniform alignment and spatial control of nanocrystalline domains remains a critical challenge, limiting the realization of structure-derived optical and mechanical functionalities in bioinspired systems. Here, we develop an ultrastrong, transparent photonic hydrogel composed of cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), wherein a programmable five-stage stretching-pause process enables precise alignment of CNC domains without sacrificing their intrinsic chirality—unattainable in conventional flexible polymers. This strategy facilitates uniform nanocrystal reorientation (orientation factor = 0.91) and transforms the porous network into aligned nanofibril bundles, yielding optical transparency (>90%) with anisotropic polarization responses, superior mechanical strength (61.6 MPa), toughness (251.8 MJ·m−3), and fatigue resistance (226.7 kJ·m−2). The flexible hydrogel resists creasing and serves as a sustainable scattering polarizer for programmable polarized displays and secure information encryption, providing a versatile platform for advanced optical and electronic applications.

## INTRODUCTION
Spider silk fibers have a hierarchical architecture spanning nanoscale fibrillar bundles to micrometer-scale assemblies, in which elementary fibrils contain β-sheet nanocrystalline domains embedded within an amorphous protein matrix (Fig. 1A) (1–3). Rather than serving as a direct structural blueprint, spider silk represents a paradigmatic construct-function archetype. This configuration confers exceptional integrated properties, including extraordinary toughness (4, 5), water-triggered supercontraction (6), high energy absorption (7), as well as optical transparency combined with camouflage functionality (8). Collectively, these attributes enable highly effective prey capture through visual deception.

Inspired by the functional and architectural principles underlying such hierarchical ordering, researchers have pursued mechanical training strategies to induce microconformational reorganization in soft polymer chains, such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polyethylene glycol, thereby enhancing the mechanical performance of hydrogels (9–11). Initially, these polymers display a disordered random coil conformation (Fig. 1B, left inset). Under repetitive stretching, the polymer chains align and undergo mechanochemical reconstruction, facilitating the nucleation and growth of crystalline domains (Fig. 1B, middle inset) (12, 13). This process recapitulates key architectural motifs of hierarchical reinforcement by generating strain-induced nanocrystalline regions that markedly improve mechanical strength and toughness. Moreover, the moderate orientation of these nanocrystalline domains stabilizes chain conformations during unloading, mitigating relaxation-induced degradation of the crystalline structure (9, 12, 14). However, rapid nucleation and localized alignment often compromise spatial homogeneity, resulting in substantial optical opacity (Fig. 1B, right inset) (14). These limitations impede the application of such hydrogels in emerging fields, including transparent electronics (15, 16), inconspicuous soft robots (17, 18), implantable biomaterials (19), and advanced optical systems (20, 21). Furthermore, microscale heterogeneity in domain distribution induces localized stress concentrations (22), fostering crack initiation and propagation (23), which ultimately compromises long-term operational durability. Therefore, the simultaneous attainment of high toughness and optical transparency necessitates the uniform development of nanocrystalline regions—a challenge that remains beyond the reach of current methodologies. This persistent shortfall underscores the continued relevance of spider silk as a biological construct-function archetype, in which hierarchical ordering integrates mechanical robustness with optical functionality at a level not yet synthetically replicated.
Cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), derived from renewable biomass, are highly crystalline polysaccharides capable of spontaneously self-assembling into cholesteric liquid crystalline phases with a polydomain architecture, exhibiting vivid structural colors through selective light reflection (24–26). The inherent crystallinity of CNCs promotes the formation of well-defined nanocrystalline domains, enabling the construction of mechanically stiff, stable networks in CNC-based materials. To achieve macroscopic transparency, techniques such as shear coating (27, 28), shear printing (29), and roll-to-roll deposition (30), have been used to impose unidirectional alignment of CNC domains through externally applied shear fields. Alternatively, CNCs could be organized into Bouligand-type helicoidal structures, followed by solvent swelling (e.g., with dimethyl sulfoxide) and elastomer encapsulation, which shifts the reflective wavelength beyond the visible range, yielding transparency (31). Despite these advances in improving the macroscopic transparency of CNC-based materials, the resulting photonic structures remain mechanically fragile and cannot withstand substantial external stresses, making them highly susceptible to deformation and damage under both physical loads and chemical perturbations (e.g., moisture, pH, and organic solvent). Typically, these systems exhibit either limited optical transparency (<40%) (31), insufficient tensile strength (<1 MPa) (31), or restricted elongation at break (<100%) (32). Recent advances leveraging hydration-induced dissociation to balance intercellulose and cellulose-water hydrogen bonding have enabled the fabrication of CNC-based hydrogels with remarkable stretchability (>400%) (33) and high mechanical strength (~46 MPa) (34). This hydrogen-bond reconstruction strategy enhances the mobility of CNCs within the helical matrix, accompanied by an increase in the orientation degree of nanocrystalline domains from 0.02 to 0.67. However, the inherent rigidity of CNCs and their strong intermolecular interactions impede the achievement of near-perfect alignment and spatial homogeneity under monotonic strain.
In this work, we present a photonic CNC-based hydrogel that combines exceptional mechanical robustness, high optical transparency, and dynamically tunable birefringence, achieved via a stepwise stretching-pause process that guides the hierarchical reorganization of nanocrystalline domains within the chiral nematic network. This approach effectively transforms the initial opaque hydrogel into a transparent one, with structural analyses confirming that the resultant properties stem from a highly oriented (order parameter ≈ 0.91) and spatially uniform arrangement of CNC and PVA nanocrystalline domains (Fig. 1, C and D). The incorporation of intermittent pauses during stepwise stretching promotes stress relaxation, facilitating a progressive multiscale reorganization. This stepwise stretching-pause process leads to the evolution of the initially porous network into aligned nanofibril bundles at the hundreds-of-nanometers scale, ultimately forming a robust framework. Compared with the untreated hydrogel, the treated sample exhibits enhanced toughness (251.8 MJ·m−3), tensile strength (61.6 MPa), and fracture resistance (226.7 kJ m−2) (Fig. 1, E and F), surpassing conventional cellulose hydrogels, synthetic elastomers, and other bioinspired structural materials. This system exemplifies how CNC domains can serve as rigid nanocrystalline reinforcements in forming a multiscale-oriented network that unites high strength, toughness, and optical transparency (~91.2%). As flexible and durable scattering polarizers (Fig. 1G), the hydrogel films demonstrate potential applications in image displays and information encryption. Given their sustainable origin and multifunctionality, these materials offer promising avenues for development in intelligent metamaterials, adaptive camouflage, information cryptography, and multimodal biointerfaces.

## RESULTS

## Mechanical and optical properties of hydrogels during FSSP treatment
A 1:1 mass ratio mixture of CNCs (2 wt %, pH 2.4) and PVA (alcoholysis degree > 99%) was cast and dried at room temperature, leading to coassembly into iridescent solid films (fig. S1). Immersion of these films in 0.05 M sodium citrate (SC) solution induced hydration and swelling, converting them into hydrogels, which were subsequently frozen to promote extensive hydration, crystallization, and aggregation of the PVA chains (35). The resulting photonic hydrogels contained nanocrystalline domains, as confirmed by characteristic diffraction peaks in the x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (fig. S2). This process creates spatial freedom among the densely packed rod-like CNC nanoparticles (36), enabling their reorientation under strain, and providing a tunable microstructural platform for the nanocrystalline domains. To determine the strain threshold for nanocrystalline domain engineering, we conducted loading-unloading cycles at varying strains. Polarized optical microscopy (POM) observations revealed an obvious birefringence orientation at 200% strain, indicating that 200% strain serves as the critical threshold for the directional rearrangement of CNC and PVA chains (Fig. 2A and fig. S3). Subsequently, the hydrogels were stretched to 200% strain and held at different durations (2, 4, 6, or 8 min) after each cycle (fig. S4). To link the experimentally determined pause duration to the intrinsic relaxation dynamics of the hydrogel network (37), stress-relaxation measurements were performed under controlled conditions (saturated water vapor, ≈80% relative humidity, and 20°C). When stretched to 200% strain at 5 mm·min−1 and held isothermally, the stress decayed rapidly within the first 3 min, followed by a slower relaxation phase, and reached a plateau between 6 and 8 min (Fig. 2B), indicating that beyond this timescale, additional contributions from stress relaxation become greatly diminished. Consistently, birefringence coefficient (Δn) measurements revealed a rapid decrease in Δn during the initial 3 min, followed by gradual stabilization around 8 min (fig. S5). Creep-recovery tests further showed a modest increase in plasticity from 2 to 6 min (33 to 42%), but a sharp rise to 68% at 8 min (fig. S6). This pronounced increase indicates that extending the pause duration into the late-stage relaxation regime promotes a higher degree of irreversible deformation within the hydrogel network.

To directly visualize the evolution of the hydrogel during successive stretch-pause cycles, we recorded macroscopic appearances after each cycle. As shown in Fig. 2D, photographs taken after each cycle clearly demonstrate perceptible color evolution: light green after the first cycle, light blue after the second, bluish green after the third, uniform blue after the fourth, and near-transparency after the fifth cycle, at which point the hydrogel attains a stable hydrated state (fig. S7). These macroscopic color shifts reflect the progressive compression of the cholesteric pitch within the CNC architecture during cyclic stretching (24). Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging confirmed a reduction in hydrogel thickness from ~200 to about 50 μm (fig. S8). Although thickness compression alone cannot uniquely determine the helical pitch, this morphological change provides independent evidence supporting the pitch contraction inferred from the optical response (38). Instead, optical transparency is critically dependent on structural uniformity at the microscopic scale, which is essential for suppressing light scattering and thereby preventing whitening or striping (39). To verify this, we examined the hydrogel after six stretching cycles. Notably, a fracture occurred during the sixth stretching cycle. Pronounced stress-induced whitening was observed at the fracture site and its adjacent regions, whereas the remaining areas distant from the fracture maintained high optical transparency (fig. S9). Structural analysis of these two regions further suggests that optical transparency is closely correlated with microscopic structural uniformity, rather than being solely determined by the disappearance of visible-light reflection. For further details on microstructural analysis, see the “Molecular evolution of hydrogels during FSSP treatment” section. Based on these observations, five stretching cycles were established as the upper limit for effective structural reorganization. A dynamic five-stage stretching-pause (FSSP) protocol—comprising initiation, alignment, relaxation, elongation, and fixation—was thus designed. Hydrogels underwent cyclic stretching to 200% strain, followed by an 8-min hold, unloading, and restretching in subsequent cycles (Fig. 2C). Hydrogels subjected to five FSSP cycles (each with 200% strain and 8-min hold) are denoted as Step-5 hydrogels (movie S1).
The mechanical and optical properties of FSSP-treated hydrogels were systematically evaluated. During tensile loading-unloading cycles, the Step-5 hydrogel exhibited notable mechanical hysteresis (Fig. 2E), indicating substantial energy dissipation attributable to the disruption and reformation of hydrogen-bonding networks within the matrix (40). This energy dissipation alleviates local stress concentration, enhancing the hydrogel’s tensile stress tolerance and toughness, reaching 61.6 MPa and 251.8 MJ·m−3, respectively (Fig. 2, F and G). Notably, as presented in the top panel of Fig. 1F, the tensile stress and fracture energy of our Step-5 hydrogel surpass those of all previously reported CNC-based hydrogel systems (33, 34, 36), representing a record-high mechanical performance in this material category.
FSSP-induced stretching not only improved the overall mechanical performance of the Step-5 hydrogels but also introduced notable anisotropy. Toughness values reached 79.1 MJ·m−3 perpendicular to the stretching direction and 251.8 MJ·m−3 parallel to it (Fig. 2J). Rivlin-Thomas pure shear tests were conducted on both notched and unnotched Step-5 hydrogels, evaluated along and perpendicular to the tensile axis to assess fracture energy (41). Figure 2H shows a representative notched sample mounted in acrylic clamps under specified boundary conditions (41). The corresponding stress-strain curves (Fig. 2I) show substantial transverse strain perpendicular to tension, whereas the parallel direction sustained higher stress. Fracture energy was considerably lower in the perpendicular orientation (55.3 kJ·m−2) compared to the parallel (226.7 kJ·m−2) (Fig. 2J), indicating a highly anisotropic network that efficiently resists crack propagation under tensile load. Fracture morphology further confirmed this anisotropy: Parallel-oriented samples failed cleanly, whereas perpendicular samples exhibited fibrous, string-like failure (fig. S10), consistent with FSSP-induced chain alignment. These findings demonstrated that the FSSP not only maximized the mechanical robustness of CNC-based hydrogels but also imposed pronounced structural anisotropy, influencing both mechanical and optical responses. To gain further insights into the fracture behavior of the hydrogels, digital image correlation (DIC) was used to monitor the strain (EYY) evolution during tensile testing (42, 43). DIC was particularly useful in revealing the difference in crack propagation mechanisms between the primitive and Step-5 hydrogels. As tensile deformation progressed, substantial strain concentration developed at the crack tip. As the crack further propagated, the region of strain concentration expanded, leading to local failure and triggering an avalanche-like fracture process (fig. S11). This behavior is indicative of localized damage exacerbation at the crack tip, intensifying the stress and eventually leading to catastrophic failure (movie S2). In contrast, the Step-5 hydrogel exhibits a fundamentally different strain evolution pattern (fig. S11). During crack propagation, the high-strain region at the crack tip progressively extends along the tensile (longitudinal) direction, forming a spatially distributed strain propagation zone rather than remaining confined to the crack tip (movie S3). This extended strain distribution indicates that deformation is accommodated by a larger fraction of the aligned network, effectively altering the crack propagation pathway and suppressing crack-tip strain localization. Although DIC directly probes strain rather than stress, under identical material composition and loading conditions, the observed differences in strain localization and propagation provide strong evidence that the FSSP-induced structural anisotropy modifies how the applied load is accommodated during fracture.
Beyond mechanical reinforcement, FSSP-induced anisotropy imparts distinctive optical properties on the Step-5 hydrogel. Optical characterization, conducted using a customized setup that comprises a light source, polarization optics, and a detector (Fig. 3A), confirmed these effects. Figure 3D shows prominent birefringence when two hydrogels are oriented perpendicularly, which vanishes under parallel alignment, confirming inherent optical retardation. Transmittance spectra of the hydrogels obtained during the FSSP process exhibit symmetric inversions between the cross-polarized and parallel-polarized configurations when the stretching direction is oriented at 45° relative to the polarizer (fig. S12). Compared to the primitive hydrogel, Step-5 spectra showed increased light intensity and reduced fluctuations, consistent with birefringence originating from its anisotropic architecture (Fig. 3B).

As observed, the intensity of polarized transmittance intensity depends on the Δn, optical path length, and the angle between the alignment and polarization axes (44). Progressive structural ordering during FSSP increased Δn from 0.3 × 10−3 in the primitive hydrogel to 1.2 × 10−3 in the Step-5 sample (Fig. 3C), which is a change attributable to strain-induced nanostructural realignment. Transmittance evolution across FSSP stages showed a consistent increase within the 400-to-650–nm range, culminating in a maximum transmittance of 91.2% for the Step-5 hydrogel (Fig. 3E). Figure S13 highlights the enhanced scattering properties of the Step-5 hydrogel, evidenced by progressively blurred underlying liquid crystal display images. These results affirmed the efficacy of FSSP in precisely tuning the optical properties of hydrogels for advanced photonic applications.
To determine whether the optical anisotropy stems solely from structural alignment or specifically requires CNC requires, we applied control FSSP treatments to pure PVA hydrogels. Although transmittance in the 400-to-650–nm range increased to 75% after five cycles (fig. S14), neither POM (fig. S15) nor polarized transmission spectroscopy (fig. S16) detected birefringence. In contrast, the Step-5 hydrogel exhibited pronounced birefringence (fig. S15) while maintaining high transparency. This optical anisotropy is attributed to the chiral liquid crystalline nature of CNCs: Their inherent chirality and rod-like geometry facilitate self-assembly into chiral nematic phases, which undergo strain-directed alignment. The Step-5 hydrogel preserves its left-handed cholesteric nematic structure throughout this process (Fig. 3F). This control experiment confirmed that the observed birefringence and multifunctionality were intrinsically linked to the CNC-directed nanoarchitecture, distinguishing Step-5 hydrogels from conventional transparent polymer systems.

## Comparison of mechanical and optical properties with representative hydrogels
To benchmark the performance of our Step-5 hydrogel (denoted by a red star), we conducted a systematic comparison with three representative classes of high-performance tough hydrogels from the literature (table S1): hydrogels with high-order structures (class II), cellulose-based hydrogels (class III), and biomimetic hydrogels (class IV). Evaluations focused on three key mechanical metrics: stress versus toughness, stress versus fracture energy, and fracture energy versus toughness (Fig. 4, A to C). The Step-5 hydrogel ranks among the top performers in all three comparisons, surpassing most previously reported systems. Notably, compared with CNC hydrogels aligned via external shear from suspension, Step-5 hydrogels fabricated from a chiral nematic precursor via the FSSP method exhibit mechanical toughness approximately three orders of magnitude higher. However, certain hydrogels documented in the literature exhibit superior toughness or fracture energy. This underscores the capability of densely packed, ordered nanocrystalline domains to substantially enhance mechanical properties. Such domains—often evolved from initially disordered states—frequently exhibit either large sizes or spatial heterogeneity, contributing to exceptional toughness and fracture energy. However, these features also induce pronounced light scattering, thereby compromising optical transparency (14, 35). These examples collectively highlight a recurring challenge: Strategies aimed at achieving ultrahigh mechanical performance through nanoscale structural control often entail a loss of transparency, reflecting a fundamental compromise among competing material properties.

Further comparisons across both mechanical and optical dimensions reaffirm the distinctive performance profile of the Step-5 hydrogel (Fig. 4, D and E). In contrast to most tough hydrogels, which typically sacrifice optical clarity for mechanical robustness, our system simultaneously achieved high toughness, fracture energy, and transparency. These results position the Step-5 hydrogel as a rare example that successfully integrates high mechanical strength, exceptional toughness, substantial fracture energy, and excellent optical transparency, an achievement that motivates further structural investigation.

## Morphological characterizations
To investigate the mechanism underlying performance enhancement, we systematically characterized the micro- and nanostructural evolution of the hydrogel caused by the FSSP process. SEM images revealed that the primitive hydrogel surface (Fig. 5A) displayed a disordered porous architecture spanning nanoscale to microscale dimensions, while its cross-section preserved chiral nematic structures (Fig. 5E). As the FSSP treatment progressed, surface pores gradually elongated along the tensile direction, eventually reorganizing into densely packed, aligned nanofibers (Fig. 5, B to D, and fig. S17) with diameters ranging from ~100 to 500 nm (fig. S18). This transformation results from accumulated plastic deformation during FSSP, which induces elongation and realignment of the nanofibers along the stretching direction, culminating in an aligned surface morphology (9, 14). Concurrently, the cross-sectional structure evolved from an initial chiral nematic texture into a smoother, more uniform alignment, indicating stress-driven reorientation of the CNCs (Fig. 5, F to H) (45).

Wide-angle x-ray scattering (WAXS) analysis of nanocrystalline domain distribution—parallel (//) and perpendicular (⊥) to the stretching direction—revealed two prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ = 22.7° [corresponding to the (200) plane of CNC; (31)] and 2θ = 19.7° [attributed to the semicrystalline (1011¯) plane of PVA; (46)]. With progressive FSSP cycling, the intensity of both peaks decreased (Fig. 5, I and K), indicating a reduction in structural order. The average nanocrystalline domain size, calculated using the Scherrer equation (47), decreased along the tensile direction from 8.0 to 7.2 nm for CNC and from 5.5 to 4.5 nm for PVA. Perpendicular to the strain, the nanocrystalline domain size of CNC decreased from 7.9 to 7.4 nm, while that of PVA decreased from 5.3 to 4.4 nm (Fig. 5, J and L). Notably, CNC domains shrank by only ~10%, which is less than half the reduction observed for PVA domains (~18%) under identical strain, underscoring the superior mechanical resilience and crystalline stability of the CNC framework. Small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) was used to quantify the evolution of crystalline morphology (48). After the FSSP treatment, the average interdomain spacing increased from 5.8 to 7.8 Å (fig. S19), indicating a clear increase in the distance between nanocrystalline domains. This robustness underpins the maintenance of long-range structural alignment during FSSP.
Two-dimensional (2D)–SAXS further elucidated the FSSP-induced nanostructural orientation. Diffraction patterns confirmed the gradual reorientation of CNC domains toward the stretching axis (horizontal direction; Fig. 5, M and N). The continuously increasing orientation order parameter (f), obtained from azimuthal intensity diffraction, demonstrates that the FSSP process effectively promotes the alignment of CNC rods (Fig. 5O). The Step-5 hydrogel attained an f value of 0.91, exceeding those achieved by most reported alignment methods, including strain distribution induction (49), strain-programmed crystallization (50), self-assembly–assisted salting out (14), salting out-alignment-locking (51), microcrystal dispersed growth (52), and macromolecular network homogenization (53) (Fig. 5P and table S2).
Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed on dried hydrogels (see Materials and Methods) to evaluate changes in crystallinity. Integration of the endothermic transition (150° to 240°C) yielded the enthalpy of crystalline melting (Hcrystalline) per unit mass. The integrated area decreased substantially after FSSP treatment (fig. S20), reflecting a decrease in Hcrystalline. Such structural changes suggest partial unfolding of crystalline domains and increased chain mobility, which are crucial for sustained energy dissipation. Thus, the FSSP process drives a dual structural transformation: The partial loss of crystallinity enhances flexibility, while pronounced alignment facilitates efficient load transfer. These factors synergistically endow the hydrogel with exceptional strength, toughness, stretchability, and optical anisotropy.

## Molecular evolution of hydrogels during FSSP treatment
To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which FSSP enables a high orientation order parameter without inducing pronounced domain heterogeneity, we investigated infrared attenuated total reflection (IR-ATR) spectroscopy (Fig. 6A and fig. S21). The broad absorption band observed between 3600 and 3100 cm−1, attributed to ─OH stretching vibrations of CNCs, reflects both intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonds [e.g., O(2)H─O(6), O(3)H─O(5), and O(6)H─O(3)] (54). After FSSP treatment, the pronounced decrease in its intensity indicates progressive disruption of these hydrogen bonds (Fig. 6D). Similarly, the reduced intensity in the region of 2980 to 2835 cm−1, which corresponds to CH2 and CH2OH stretching vibrations in CNCs (55), suggests a breakdown of interlayer hydrogen bonding interactions (Fig. 6F). As the stretching progresses, the originally split double peaks at 2946 and 2912 cm−1 merge into a single peak, indicating a more homogeneous local environment around the C─H groups. In addition, a peak at 1142 cm−1 is attributed to symmetric C─C stretching or C─O vibrations involved in intramolecular hydrogen bonds between adjacent hydroxyl groups of PVA, which is sensitive to the crystalline. The attenuation of this peak is consistent with a reduction in the degree of PVA crystallinity (56). The reduced crystalline constraint enhances the conformational mobility of PVA chains within the hydrogel network. In combination with the concurrent disruption and reorganization of hydrogen bonding interactions revealed by the ─OH and C─H stretching bands, these molecular-level changes are consistent with a progressive reduction in chain ordering and interchain constraints within the polymer matrix. Such a molecular environment is expected to be more permissive to chain rearrangements under deformation. While IR-ATR does not directly probe dynamic mechanical processes, the observed weakening of hydrogen bonding and reduction in crystalline order provide molecular-level indications of a less constrained and more deformable polymer network.

To decipher the sequence of hydrogen-bond disruptions during FSSP, 2D correlation spectroscopy (2D-COS) IR was applied (57). The resulting synchronous and asynchronous spectra (Fig. 6, B and C) reveal a clear sequence of molecular events: Changes in ─OH vibrations of CNC occur first, followed by alterations in C─O vibrations of PVA, which indicate weakened intermolecular hydrogen bonding between adjacent PVA chains. Last, modifications in the intersheet CH─O hydrogen bonding of CNC are observed. This progression is schematically illustrated in Fig. 6 (D to F), which provides a visual mapping of the bond-breaking sequence and associated spectral changes, thereby clarifying the molecular-scale structural reorganization during FSSP. This progressive hydrogen-bond disruption is consistent with the reduced crystallinity and the decreased nanocrystalline domain size of the Step-5 hydrogel characterized above, indicating that CNC nanodomains evolve from relatively rigid, ordered aggregates into a more dispersed state.
Different from IR spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy is sensitive to local vibrational environments and can indirectly probe hydrogen bonding–related structural changes (54) and has been widely used to monitor microstructural evolution (58). As shown in Fig. 6G and fig. S22, the peak at 1061 cm−1, related to OH─O hydrogen bonding in CNC (59), decreased markedly after FSSP, indicating disruption of intersheet interactions. Meanwhile, the peak at 1289 cm−1, associated with the CH environment (54), narrowed and attenuated, reflecting weakened CH─O hydrogen bonds in CNC. These spectral changes signify not catastrophic bond rupture but progressive hydrogen-bond unlocking. These changes indicate substantial microstructural reorganization under the FSSP process. Raman mapping was performed based on the 1289 cm−1 peak. This peak, corresponding to the CH─O vibration, was selected in consideration of the fact that ─OH vibrations are generally broad and susceptible to variations in water content and temperature (60). As a result, CH─O vibrations were considered more suitable for capturing changes in the local spatial arrangement of CNC domains. Raman mapping based on this peak visualizes the structural evolution (Fig. 6, H and I) (61). The color intensity corresponds to the local CH─O bond density. The Step-5 hydrogel exhibited a more uniform and reduced Raman intensity distribution compared to the initial state, indicating the enhanced spatial uniformity and alignment of CNCs. To evaluate the periodicity of nanoscale CNC domains, a 2D fast Fourier transform (2D-FFT) was applied to the Raman mapping images, converting spatial information into frequency space to reveal their arrangement regularity (62). The 2D-FFT image of the primitive hydrogel is diffuse and lacks a clear pattern (Fig. 6J), whereas the Step-5 hydrogel exhibits a petal-like pattern (Fig. 6K), indicating a more spatially periodic and synchronized distribution of CNC domains. Gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) analysis of Raman mapping images quantified CNC domain uniformity. As shown in Fig. 6L, the Step-5 hydrogel exhibits lower contrast, higher homogeneity, and lower energy than the primitive hydrogel, reflecting smoother intensity transitions and more consistent domain alignment.
Figure 6M schematically illustrates the structural evolution of the CNC-PVA network under FSSP treatment. In the initial state, CNCs and PVA nanocrystalline domains form a rigid network stabilized by strong hydrogen-bonding interactions. Upon FSSP treatment, hydrogen bonds between PVA molecular chains are partly disrupted, as revealed by 2D-COS IR analysis, which directly leads to a reduction in the size of PVA nanocrystalline domains, consistent with the decreased PVA crystallite size (D value) obtained from XRD analysis (Fig. 5, J and K). Then, FSSP partially breaks interlayer and intralayer hydrogen bonds within the CNC molecular chain, reducing the structural constraints on CNC nanorods and enabling their directional rearrangement, manifested as a progressive increase in orientational order and gradual alignment along a common direction. As FSSP proceeds, the overall weakening of hydrogen-bonding interactions allows the nanocrystalline domains of CNC and PVA to align cooperatively, giving rise to a more uniform and highly oriented network. This continuous evolution, corroborated by SEM observations (Fig. 5, A to H), reflects a hierarchical reorganization process spanning from the macroscopic scale to the nanoscale and is consistent with nanocrystalline domain reconstruction rather than nanocrystalline domain aggregation. Notably, despite extensive hydrogen-bond rearrangement, CD spectra confirm that the chiral ordering of CNC is preserved in the Step-5 hydrogel (Fig. 3F), indicating that FSSP primarily induces molecular-chain rearrangement and orientational reorganization without causing catastrophic disruption of the chiral architecture.

## Display and encryption for security applications
To comprehensively demonstrate the multifunctional performance of the Step-5 hydrogel—integrating high mechanical robustness, transparency, and birefringence—we developed an optical information display platform as a proof of concept (Fig. 7). The increasing demand for foldable displays and flexible electronics has exposed the limitations of conventional optical films, which often exhibit inadequate mechanical resilience. Under repeated bending, these films develop visible creases and stress concentration, resulting in nonuniform optical transmission and degraded display quality (Fig. 7A). These shortcomings represent a major barrier to their use in next-generation flexible devices. To address these challenges, we explored the Step-5 hydrogel as an advanced optical modulation material. The system uses an ATmega microcontroller as the central processing unit, which coordinates display operation through three core functions: (i) initializing the rendering state, (ii) precisely regulating timing sequences via internal timers, and (iii) ensuring seamless data transmission to a 1.44-inch thin-film transistor (TFT) display via serial peripheral interface communication (Fig. 7B). Owing to its high optical clarity, the Step-5 hydrogel remains nearly invisible under ambient conditions (fig. S23), minimizing visual interference and enabling effective optical concealment. Without optical modulation, the encoded image on the TFT display remained undetectable to the naked eye (Fig. 7C). The introduction of a linear polarizer faintly revealed the underlying image (Fig. 7D, left). Notably, when the Step-5 hydrogel was placed directly between the linear polarizer and the TFT display, the optical output was markedly enhanced, producing a vivid, high-contrast, and colorful encoded image (Fig. 7D, right). Moreover, the hydrogel enabled real-time display of the changing graphics on the screen, enhancing visual effects through its metallic hues and optical properties (Fig. 7E and movie S4). This functionality highlights its potential for visual encryption and advanced smart displays.

By refining the information encoding strategy, we further improved the optical modulation performance. Integrated with the display and polarizer, the Step-5 hydrogel clearly revealed intricate details in classical artworks such as Sun Wukong, Portrait of a Young Woman, and Girl with a Pearl Earring, with particularly enhanced facial expression fidelity (fig. S24). Interference effects from the hydrogel’s metallic hues enhanced image layering and created a 3D perception, greatly enriching the visual experience. The ability to maintain spatial coherence and high contrast in complex moving images is further demonstrated by a dynamic rendering of a running tiger (movie S5).
To rigorously evaluate the durability of the hydrogel under extreme conditions, we conducted sequential folding tests (Fig. 7F) and compression under a 1423-kg vehicle (Fig. 7G). The material showed no permanent creases or structural damage after such demanding mechanical insults. Its optical performance remained uncompromised, as confirmed by the clear decoding of images postcompression (Fig. 7H). After five cycles of folding and rolling, the hydrogel retained nearly original values in toughness, transparency, and red, green, and blue (RGB) color metrics, demonstrating its exceptional mechanical and optical durability (Fig. 7I). The successful integration of superior mechanical robustness, high transparency, and programmable birefringence in the Step-5 hydrogel establishes an advanced standard for optical materials. It overcomes the classic trade-off between deformation tolerance and optical functionality, offering reliable polarization control and visual output even under substantial stress. This combination of properties breaks previous limitations of conventional films and opens avenues toward truly flexible and durable photonic platforms. Consequently, it represents a promising candidate for future foldable displays, rollable screens, or wearable optical electronics.

## DISCUSSION
Inspired by the functional and hierarchical design principles underlying the exceptional performance of natural spider silk, we have developed a nanocrystalline domain engineering strategy to integrate high strength, toughness, and optical transparency within a single hydrogel material. Through a programmable FSSP process, nanoscale fibrillar reorganization was achieved, resulting in spatially uniform and highly aligned nanocrystalline domains with an orientation order parameter of 0.91. The material transitions seamlessly from initial structural color to high optical transparency while retaining birefringence, structural ordering, and exceptional toughness under high strain—a rare combination of mechanical and optical properties among existing functional hydrogels. This approach enables the simultaneous enhancement of both orientational order and spatial homogeneity within the nanocrystalline domains, two key structural parameters traditionally difficult to optimize concurrently. This work expands the design principles for microstructural control in biomimetic materials and establishes a versatile pathway toward transparent, multifunctional systems unifying mechanical robustness and optical performance. These advancements hold promising potential for applications in diverse fields such as protective coatings, aerospace materials, and precision optical devices.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Materials
All chemicals and solvents, such as PVA (99.0 to 99.4% hydrolysis grade) and SC, were procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. An aqueous PVA solution (2.0 wt %) was prepared by dissolving the powdered polymer at 95°C for 4 hours. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was used in all experimental procedures.

## Preparation of CNC suspension
A stable CNC suspension was prepared by sulfuric acid hydrolysis of commercially available absorbent cotton. Forty grams of cotton was dispersed in 600 ml of 64.0 wt % sulfuric acid and hydrolyzed under constant mechanical stirring (400 rpm) at 45°C for 2 hours (fig. S25, step one). The reaction was quenched by diluting the mixture with 6000 ml of ultrapure water (fig. S25, step two), followed by standing for 12 hours to allow phase separation. The resulting lower milky colloid (unpurified CNC dispersion) was collected by centrifugation at 7000 rpm (fig. S25, step three) and transferred into a regenerated cellulose dialysis membrane (molecular weight cutoff: 8000 to 14,000 Da) (fig. S25, step four). Dialysis was performed for 6 days with continuous replacement of deionized water until the dispersion became optically clear and the pH stabilized. The dialyzed suspension was subsequently filtered through filter paper and a 5-μm membrane filter (fig. S25, step five) to remove insoluble impurities, yielding a stable CNC dispersion. The CNC mass concentration was determined gravimetrically and was typically 2.0 wt %. When deviations occurred, the concentration was adjusted to 2.0 wt % by rotary evaporation. The final pH of the CNC dispersion was 2.6. Prior to use, the dispersion was ultrasonicated for 5 min to disrupt weak aggregates. To characterize the morphology and crystalline features of the as-prepared CNCs and to verify the reproducibility of the starting material, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was performed. For TEM sample preparation, the CNC dispersion was diluted to ~0.01 wt % and ultrasonicated for 5 min to minimize aggregation. A small aliquot of the diluted suspension was then drop-cast onto a copper grid and air-dried before imaging. TEM images revealed that the CNCs exhibited a characteristic rod-like nanomorphology, with particle lengths predominantly distributed in the range of 100 to 300 nm (fig. S26A). This size distribution is in close agreement with that reported for CNCs in the literature (63), thereby confirming the typical morphology of the as-prepared CNCs. Statistical analysis of a large number of particles further showed that the CNCs had nanoscale dimensions, with widths typically in the range of 5 to 20 nm and lengths of 100 to 350 nm (fig. S26B). In addition, the measured zeta potential of the CNC dispersion was approximately −50.4 mV (fig. S26C), indicating strong electrostatic repulsion between CNC particles in aqueous media and, consequently, good colloidal stability. These results collectively demonstrate that the as-prepared CNCs exhibit the expected morphology, size distribution, and colloidal stability, confirming the reproducibility and reliability of the starting CNC material.

## Preparation of the primitive and Step-5 hydrogel
To prepare the primitive hydrogel, 15 ml of an aqueous 2.0 wt % PVA solution and 15 ml of a CNC dispersion (2.0 wt %) were mixed and stirred at room temperature for 6 hours to obtain a homogeneous precursor solution. The resulting mixture was cast into polystyrene molds (55 mm by 55 mm by 5 mm) and allowed to undergo evaporation-induced self-assembly under ambient conditions. During solvent evaporation, the progressive increase in CNC concentration drove the formation of a chiral nematic liquid crystalline phase, leading to the emergence of long-range periodic order and the development of structurally colored photonic films. Owing to the slow evaporation rate at 20° to 25°C and a relative humidity of ~85%, the self-assembly process proceeded under near-equilibrium conditions, yielding uniform films with a dominant red structural color (fig. S1). To convert the photonic films into hydrogels, the films were immersed in a 0.05 M SC aqueous solution for 5 min, followed by a single freeze-thaw cycle (−20°C for 24 hours and 25°C for 3 hours). During immersion, water molecules infiltrated and saturated the polymer network. The subsequent freeze-thaw treatment induced crosslinking of PVA through crystallite formation (35). After rehydration and physical crosslinking, all samples exhibited a whitish, opaque hydrogel appearance (fig. S27), with a final hydrogel thickness in the range of ~0.15 to 0.25 mm, reflecting the disruption of long-range chiral order and increased light scattering in the swollen state. To prepare the Step-5 hydrogels, uniaxial tensile training was performed using either a Pvidi (PDV) motorized displacement stage, a PDV manual displacement stage (fig. S28), or an Instron universal testing machine. In all cases, parallel-plate clamps were used to ensure identical clamping geometry and minimize platform-dependent variations in the applied deformation field. The initial clamp-to-clamp distance was ~1 cm. Samples were stretched at a constant rate of 5 mm·min−1 to a target strain of 200% and held at this strain for 8 min to promote CNC reorientation and network relaxation under load. Subsequent cycles were carried out by selecting a fresh 1-cm segment from the central region of the previously oriented sample and subjecting it to the same stretching-holding protocol (200% strain, 8-min hold), thereby enabling a stepwise enhancement of CNC alignment along the loading direction (fig. S29). This procedure was repeated for a total of five cycles under identical strain amplitude and controlled moisture conditions. Experiments were conducted in a highly humid environment (about 80 to 85% relative humidity) to suppress water evaporation and maintain a nearly constant hydration state of the hydrogel network.

## Preparation of the Step-5 PVA hydrogels
Twenty milliliters of a 2 wt % PVA solution was cast into a 55-mm-diameter petri dish and dried at room temperature to form a uniform film. After immersion in 30 ml of 0.05 M SC saline solution for 5 min, the sample underwent the same freezing-thawing and swelling procedures as described above. The Step-5 PVA hydrogel was fabricated following the identical cyclic stretching protocol applied to the CNC-containing hydrogels.

## Sample characterizations
The fracture and surface morphologies were characterized using a Carl Zeiss helium ion microscope. The samples were dried and coated with conductive tape before observation. POM images of the hydrogels were captured under crossed polarizers using an Olympus BX53 microscope. IR-ATR spectroscopy was performed on freeze-dried hydrogel samples using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer. Mechanical testing was conducted on a texture analyzer (TA.TOUCH, Bosintech). Hydrogel samples were cut into dumbbell-shaped specimens with a gauge length of 30 mm and a width of 4 mm. The thickness of the hydrogels, measured with a caliper, was ~0.2 mm. Tensile tests were carried out at a stretching speed of 0.8 mm s−1, and for continuous loading and unloading tests, a speed of 1 mm s−1 was used. POM and birefringent images of the hydrogels were captured under crossed polarizers using an Olympus BX53 microscope. Quantitative birefringence of the samples was obtained using an Olympus BX53 optical microscope equipped with key optical components including a polarizer (U-PO3) and analyzer (U-AN360-3) from Olympus to ensure precise measurement. Separately, optical transmittance spectra of the hydrogels were measured under light-emitting diode white light illumination using an Ocean Optics USB4000 spectrometer directly coupled to the microscope, enabling seamless acquisition of transmission data. Raman spectroscopy and spatial Raman mapping were performed using a Raman microscope (Renishaw, UK) at a laser excitation wavelength of 785 nm. Stress-relaxation experiments were performed using an Instron 5943 universal testing machine. The creep behavior of the primitive hydrogel was assessed using a TA Instruments DMA 7100 under controlled humidity (saturated water vapor, ≈80% relative humidity) and temperature (≈20°C).

## DIC method
Speckle patterns were applied to the specimen surfaces using commercial speckle stickers to generate grayscale textures with sufficient contrast for correlation analysis. All DIC measurements were conducted under controlled environmental conditions at a temperature of 25°C and a relative humidity of ~80% to minimize moisture-induced deformation and ensure stable optical and mechanical responses of the hydrogel specimens. During the experiments, speckle images of the specimen surface were continuously acquired using a BASLER industrial digital camera at a sampling rate of 5 frames per second. The resulting image sequences were subsequently imported into a MATLAB-based video image correlation (VIC) program for postprocessing and analysis (64).

## GLCM texture analysis
To quantitatively assess nanodomain alignment and uniformity, GLCM analysis (65) was performed on Raman mapping intensity images. Images were first converted to 8-bit grayscale, and GLCMs were computed at a 1-pixel offset in four directions (0°, 45°, 90°, and 135°), followed by directional averaging to minimize anisotropic bias. From the GLCM, contrast, energy, and homogeneity were extracted to evaluate domain heterogeneity, local ordering, and global uniformity. All analyses were conducted in Python. Comparative analysis before and after FSSP processing was used to quantify the improvements in domain organization.

## X-ray scattering
WAXS experiments were performed using an Empyrean-100 system (Panacor Holland) with an x-ray source of 0.154-nm wavelength, operated at 40 kV and 200 mA. Scanning was conducted at a rate of 2° min−1 over the 2θ angular range of 5° to 60°. The test specimens were prepared by freeze-drying the hydrogel samples. The structural analysis was conducted using a Xeuss 3.0 SAXS/WAXS system equipped with an Eiger2R 1M detector featuring a pixel size of 75 μm. The instrument used an 8.05-keV Cu Kα x-ray source with a wavelength of 1.54189 Å and a beam spot size of 0.9 mm. Measurements were performed under vacuum conditions (<1 mbar), with the detector positioned 1800 mm from the sample. The samples to be tested were obtained by freeze-drying hydrogels. The exposure time was set as 300 s. 2D-SAXS patterns were analyzed using the Fit2D software package. During this process, valuable experimental resources and assistance were provided by W. Wang and the testing platform of Wuhan University of Technology. This process involved processing and visualizing the raw SAXS data to obtain detailed structural information about the samples. To enhance the identification of the scattering peak, the scattering intensity (I) was multiplied by q squared and plotted as Iq2 versus q.

## Measurement of the crystallinity of the hydrogel samples
The crystallinity of the initial hydrogel and Step-5 hydrogel after freeze-drying was measured using DSC (TA Instruments, Discovery DSC). During a standard DSC analysis, the freeze-dried hydrogel sample was placed in a hermetically sealed Tzero pan, alongside a reference Tzero pan containing no sample. The sample was then heated up from 25° to 250°C at a rate of 10°C min−1 under N2 environment. The heat-flow curve exhibited a peak between 150° and 250°C, indicating the typical melting of the crystalline domains within the sample.
