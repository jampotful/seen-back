# Neurogenomic and behavioral principles shape freezing dynamics and synergistic performance in Drosophila melanogaster

## Abstract
Collective behavior plays a vital role in detecting and evading predators, yet its neural and genetic underpinnings remain poorly understood. In Drosophila melanogaster, visual cues from conspecifics can alleviate freezing responses to threatening stimuli. Using a large-scale behavioral experiment combined with GWAS, we identify key loci, including Ptp99A and kirre, which are involved in visual neuron development and may influence visual responsiveness to conspecifics. Single-cell transcriptomics and functional assays confirm the modulatory roles of Ptp99A in gene expression in visual neurons and behavior. Furthermore, mixed-strain groups show enhanced freezing behavior compared to homogeneous groups, demonstrating a “diversity effect” where genetic diversity within groups induces flexible behavioral changes. Animal-computer interaction experiments using predatory spiders validate that variation in freezing durations among interactive individuals improves antipredator behavioral performance in fly groups. Agent-based simulations further support the hypothesis that behavioral synchronization among genetically diverse individuals improves group-level performance. We introduce genome-wide higher-level association study to find loci whose genetic diversity correlates with diversity effect, highlighting the potential roles of neuronal diversity. These findings demonstrate how genetic diversity fosters synergistic responses to threats, offering insights into the neural and genomic mechanisms underlying collective behavior in non-eusocial insects.

## Introduction
Group formation is one of the most common behaviors in social animals and plays an essential role in foraging, mating, and avoiding predators1,2 and often leads to notable behavioral patterns called collective behavior3. A large number of theoretical and empirical studies have revealed its ecological advantages and behavioral mechanisms, with examples shown in insect swarms, fish shoals, and bird flocks3–7. One of the key findings is that very simple rules of interindividual interaction: attraction, adjustment in speed, and repulsion govern seemingly complex dynamics of social collectives3,8. Such simple rules of individual agents can even lead to species coexistence9, suggesting its contribution to the ecological communities and evolutionary processes throughout the animal kingdom. Theoretical studies of collective behavior have often adopted a top-down approach by analyzing behavioral patterns at the group level, using variables such as position and speed to infer individual mechanisms. However, recent research has shifted focus towards a more biological perspective, examining how individuals perceive and respond to environmental and social signals10–14. These studies point out the importance of more experimental approaches for understanding the neural and genetic basis of how individuals perceive and react to social information.
Laboratory studies of collective behavior have relied primarily on small fish and eusocial insects such as zebrafish, ants, and bees due to their high degree of behavioral coordination and ease of experimental manipulation. In contrast, the limited behavioral coordination in non-eusocial insects has traditionally hampered them as a model to study collective behavior. Recent studies, however, have increasingly highlighted complex social behaviors, such as collective foraging15,16 and social learning17–19 for non-eusocial insects. Among these, the fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster), a model organism in genetics and neuroscience, may serve as a model system for studying group behavior20–22. Social aggregation of flies often arises through interindividual interactions mediated by visual and olfactory cues from the surrounding environment23,24. These aggregations are functionally significant; for example, flies in groups exhibit reduced fear responses to threatening stimuli compared to solitary flies25,26. While Drosophila are preyed upon by various predators, such as spiders, mantises, and dragonflies27, visual cues play a particularly crucial role in their predator avoidance strategies28. To investigate the visual mechanisms underlying such responses, the looming stimulus has been widely used as a model representing an approaching threat. Recognized for its cross-species applicability25,29,30, it serves as a simplified yet powerful tool to study collective predator avoidance in Drosophila.
In addition to the neurological mechanisms, there has been growing interest in the genetics underpinning group behavior. The social structures of fruit flies determined by network properties differ greatly among genetically distinct strains20 and are probably subject to natural selection31. Extensive screening of zebrafish found numerous mutant strains with defects in collective behavior32,33, confirming the idea that genetic variation controls interindividual interaction rules. Another recent perspective for exploring the genetic background of group behavior is utilizing the technological advances in genomics, as represented by genome-wide association study (GWAS). GWAS requires hundreds of samples with comprehensive information for phenotypes of interest and genotypes across genomes, often millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and detect associations between them. Recent GWAS have found genetic polymorphisms connected to eusociality in a unique wasp species34 and potential loci linked to sociability in guppies35. These studies suggested that animals’ intricate group behavior is likely encoded by their genomes and be impacted by specific genetic variants. However, identifying the genomic variants underlying group behavior of non-eusocial insects is difficult due to the paucity of model systems that can associate phenotypes and genotypes at the individual and group levels.
Therefore, the Drosophila genetic reference panel (DGRP)36, consisting of roughly 200 inbred strains of D. melanogaster, is a powerful tool to dissect genomic variations underpinning both individual and group behaviors. These strains are derived from a single population in North Carolina and represent both genotypic and phenotypic variation in the natural environment. The DGRP has been employed in GWAS targeting various physiological and behavioral phenotypes, such as activity levels37, aggression38, grooming behavior39, and circadian rhythms40. While these studies demonstrated the utility of the DGRP in the study of behavioral genetics, none focused on the emergent property of group-level traits. The unique feature of the DGRP, in which the individuals within a strain are considered genetically nearly identical, facilitates the quantification of individual- and group-level phenotypes linked to genomic variation. Recently, the genomic underpinnings of group-level phenotypes (e.g., plants productivity41 or resistance to herbivory42) and the role of genetic diversity within specific loci43 in enhancing group performance have been investigated. However, such exploration has rarely been applied to animal collective behavior. Indeed, specific genetic diversity linked to behavior could contribute to group performance, as shown by the effect of the Rover/sitter polymorphism on population fitness in Drosophila44. Given the growing understanding of how individual heterogeneity influences group dynamics44–48, leveraging the genome sequences further facilitates exploration into the genetic basis of the impacts of such diversity. The current study sought to clarify genomic loci underlying visually mediated group behavior of fruit flies, especially under a threatening environment. Combining a large-scale behavioral experiment with GWAS detected genomic loci relative to social responsiveness, which are involved in the development of visual neurons. Additionally, single-cell transcriptomic analysis and mutant behavioral screening demonstrated that differential regulation of lamina neurons influences the visual response to other conspecifics. We also found that genetic diversity among individuals led to nonadditive flexible changes in group behavior, and this emergent impact could be attributed to the loci involved in neuronal development. Taken together, these results show a conceptual breakthrough in the genomics study of group-level behavioral traits and shed light on the genetic mechanisms behind the collective flow of visual information in fruit flies.

## Results

## Genetic variation affects both solitary and group behavioral traits of flies
To investigate how genetic variation influences both solitary and social behaviors in flies, we conducted behavioral assays for 104 strains of D. melanogaster derived from the DGRP. Flies were placed individually or in groups (n = 6, sufficient to induce group effects26) for a 10-minute session, with looming stimuli (500 ms, repeated 20 times) presented in the latter 5 min of the trial, mimicking predatory threats25,26 (Fig. 1b). This setup allowed us to observe and quantify key behaviors, including locomotor activity, sociality, freezing responses to fear stimuli, and visual responsiveness to conspecific movements, representing fear response and its alleviation in the group (Fig. 1b; see Methods). Baseline locomotor activity was defined as the average moving speed during the initial 5 min without stimuli. Significant differences between strains (χ2103 = 6175.56, P < 0.001; Fig. S1a) and correlations with sex and social context (r2 = 0.56–0.68 and P < 0.001; Fig. S1b) aligned with high heritability of locomotor activity (H2 = 0.94–0.96). Group effects on moving speed differed by sex, with females slowing and males speeding up in groups (χ21 = 228.35, P < 0.001; Fig. S1b), suggesting sex-specific genetic influences on social response.
We quantified sociality in groups by calculating the average nearest neighbor distance (NND) among six flies at rest during the first 5 min. Out of 104 strains, most showed smaller NND than the visually impaired norpA mutant (norpAP12 in the DGRP208 background; see Methods for details) and pseudo-groups generated from random combinations of solitary condition data. Among females, all 104 strains exhibited smaller NND than norpA mutants, and 103 were closer than pseudo-groups; in males, 98 strains had smaller NND than norpA mutants, and 86 were closer than pseudo-groups (Fig. S2). These results confirm that flies exhibit social aggregation23, heavily influenced by visual information. Although a previous study suggested that females aggregate more than males23, the effect of sex was not significant (χ21 = 0.0027, P = 0.96), though a higher number of strains showed strong sociality among females than males (Fig. S2). Strain effects on NND were significant (χ2103 = 258.21, P < 0.001; H2 = 0.40 for females and H2 = 0.53 for males), consistent with prior findings on genetic impact on social networks20,31.
During the 5 min, some males showed chasing behavior, which is typically linked to aggression49 (Fig. S3a). However, this behavior was strongly correlated with chaining behavior (Fig. S3b), often seen in courtship of fruitless mutants50. While male-male courtship behavior in wild-type Drosophila warrants further investigation51, this behavior was infrequent and thus excluded from further analysis.

## Visual interaction alleviates fear response to threatening stimuli
To examine social interaction effects on fear responses, we analyzed changes in moving speed under looming stimuli. Solitary flies exhibited a sharp decrease in speed in response to stimuli, while grouped flies returned to baseline speed quickly (Fig. 1c). Freezing duration was measured as the speed reduction post-stimulus, with significant effects observed for strain, social context, and strain-by-social interaction (strain: χ2103 = 898.13; social: χ21 = 376.23; strain×social: χ2103 = 303.63; all P < 0.001; H2 = 0.68–0.84; Fig. S4).
Visual interaction was further examined by modeling the probability of freezing exit as a function of perceived motion cues from nearby flies within 10 seconds post-stimulus (Fig. 1d). The probability of freezing exit logistically increased with perceived motion cues (Fig. 1d), suggesting conformity in flies’ behavior triggered by conspecific visual cues26. To quantify this sensitivity, we used the logistic model intercept as a phenotype representing the threshold of visual reaction toward conspecifics, termed visual responsiveness (Fig. S5a). The significant effects of strain, sex, and strain-by-sex interaction on visual responsiveness (strain: χ2103 = 273.12, sex: χ21 = 13.54, strain×sex: χ2103 = 143.22, all P < 0.001; Fig. S5b, c) revealed genetic influence on the visual reaction (H2 = 0.53 for females and H2 = 0.51 for males). Notably, the majority of DGRP strains exhibited higher visual responsiveness compared to norpA mutants, supporting the difference in their visual responsiveness (Fig. S5b).
To further explore the mechanism of collective freezing, we developed a simple individual-based model (IBM) to simulate flies’ responses to repeated visual stimuli. In the simulation, one or six agents were placed in a virtual arena replicating the experimental setup. The observed pattern of agent’s behavior clearly mirrored those observed in actual flies, suggesting that visual cues support fast alleviation of fear response (Fig. S6b). These results provide mechanistic evidence that behavioral synchronization via visual information facilitates collective freezing.

## Genomic loci underlying social phenotypes of flies
We conducted genome-wide association analyses (GWAA) independently by sex for three phenotypes: freezing duration, NND, and visual responsiveness. GWAA for freezing duration was performed separately for solitary and grouped conditions. Genetic distances among the 104 strains showed broad genetic diversity representing DGRP strains (Fig. S9a). With the summary statistics resulted from the GWAA, we estimated the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability and the genetic correlations between the above phenotypes. SNP-based heritability estimates were overall lower than the corresponding H2 estimated from phenotypic variance (Fig. S9b), with genetic correlations higher than phenotypic correlations among traits (Fig. S9c, d), indicating shared loci for and/or different environmental effects on those phenotypes. GWAA results are displayed in Manhattan plots and Q-Q plots, whereas the number of genes adjacently located to the associated loci (P < 1.0 × 10–5) is shown in Venn diagrams (Fig. S10). Top hit loci are summarized in Supplementary Data 1.
Here we focus on the results of visual responsiveness, where we also performed meta-analysis of the GWAS results from both sexes (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Data 2). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) highlighted the involved functions of detected genes, including “appendage development,” “muscle structure development,” or “neuron projection morphogenesis” (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Data 3). Notably, kirre and Ptp99A were significantly associated with visual responsiveness in both sexes (Fig. 2c). A commonly detected SNP in Ptp99A (3 R:25292746) stood out as the most significant locus in meta-analysis (Fig. 2a) and revealed a clear genotypic effect (male: W = 397; female: W = 428; P < 0.001 for both, Wilcoxon’s rank sum test; Fig. 2d).Fig. 2Genome-wide association analysis (GWAA) suggests Ptp99A as a candidate gene underlying collective behavior of flies.a (Left) Manhattan plots showing associated loci with visual responsiveness across genome for each sex. ## Ptp99A affects gene expression in visual neurons and regulates social response
The two candidate genes, kirre and Ptp99A, are cell surface molecules (CSMs) and modulate the extension of lamina (L1–L5) neurons52,53. To investigate the impact of the candidate variant on the expression of Ptp99A, we analyzed a publicly available single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of developing visual neurons in D. melanogaster, containing gene expression data from female offspring of W1118 flies crossed with various DGRP strains (see Methods for details). Based on the annotation of neuronal cells (24–96 h after pupal formation), we found that Ptp99A was expressed in nearly all visual neuron types (Fig. S11a, b). Neurons related to motion detection54, such as L1, L2, Tm2, Tm8, and T4/T5, displayed genotype-dependent differences in Ptp99A expression throughout development (Fig. S11c). Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) identified coexpressing gene modules associated with translation (the brown module) and metabolism (the turquoise module), differentially expressed between genotypes across neurons (Supplementary Data 4, Fig. S11d, f).
To further validate the functional role of lamina neurons and Ptp99A in detecting motion cues from conspecifics, we employed the GAL4/UAS system to inhibit specific neuronal activities using tetanus toxin light chain (TNT). Silencing lamina neurons with the pan-lamina neuron driver55 (R9B08-GAL4 with UAS-TNT) significantly reduced visual responsiveness, although freezing level stayed constant (W = 373 and 143.5, P < 0.001 and P = 0.13, respectively, Wilcoxon’s rank sum test; Fig. 3). Similarly, conditional knockdown of Ptp99A (R9B08-GAL4 > UAS-Ptp99ARNAi) reduced visual responsiveness (W = 304 and 383, P = 0.0043 and P < 0.001, compared to R9B08-GAL4 and UAS-Ptp99ARNAi, respectively, Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test). In addition, silencing Ptp99A-expressing neurons (Ptp99A-GAL4 > UAS-TNT) significantly reduced visual responsiveness (W = 381, P < 0.001, Wilcoxon’s rank sum test), suggesting that Ptp99A influences the development of lamina neurons, potentially altering optical processing and social behavior. ## Genetic heterogeneity within a group enhances flexible changes in freezing behavior
To explore how genetic diversity within fly groups influences antipredator behaviors and the balance between vigilance and exploration, we combined individuals from 15 genomically diverse strains (Figs. S7a and S9a). Groups of six female flies were formed by pairing three individuals from two different strains, creating 105 unique combinations. The measured indices of each mixed-strain group were compared to the expected values, calculated as the average of single-strain groups that made up each mixed group. Mixed-strain groups displayed significantly lower moving speeds immediately after the stimulus (P < 0.001 for 0.5–1.5 s after stimulus, Wilcoxon’s signed rank test with Bonferroni correction; Figs. 4a and S12). To test which freezing duration best balances predator avoidance and exploration, we conducted animal-computer interaction (ACI) experiments with a jumping spider (Hasarius adansoni), a natural predator of fruit flies27,28. The spider was placed in a Petri dish atop a screen displaying a virtual fly (Fig. 4d). The fly paused for different durations (0, 1, 3, 6, or 12 s) upon proximity to the real-time-tracked spider. Both spider and fly positions were tracked over 5 min, and behavioral indices were calculated based on this data (Fig. S13). A spider attack on the fly was defined as movement exceeding 30 mm/s and approaching the fly within 1 cm within three consecutive frames. Principal component analysis (PCA) on the virtual fly’s travelled distance and the number of spider attacks revealed that a PC2 score indicated longer travel distances and fewer attacks, representing effective antipredator performance (Fig. S13c). The PC2 score showed that freezing durations of 3 seconds optimized this balance (χ24 = 38.91, P < 0.001; P < 0.001 for comparisons of 3–6 and 3–12 seconds; Fig. S13c). These results suggest an adaptive advantage of the observed pattern of behavioral switching in real flies (Figs. 1c and 4a).
Thus, using the time-series movement data of mixed groups of flies, we computed virtual behavioral performance metrics balancing vigilance and exploration across strain combinations (assuming the X = 3 s being the best timing of switching from freezing to exploration; see Fig. 4a and Methods). The behavioral performance was significantly higher for mixed-strain groups than expected (W = 1545, P < 0.001, Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test; Figs. 4b and S14a), driven by immediate reductions in moving speed following stimulus (Fig. S14b). We also found that greater differences in freezing duration, rather than visual responsiveness, between strains were correlated with higher performance (Figs. 4c and S15), indicating that behavioral synchronization among flies with varying freezing responses enhances rapid, efficient reactions to visual threats and their clearance. The diversity effect, defined as the difference between observed and expected performance values, was positive in many strain combinations. Notably, the number of strains showing positive diversity effects was highest with freezing duration thresholds of 2 and 3 seconds (Fig. S16). To further validate the ecological significance of the observed behavioral pattern, we conducted an additional ACI experiment in which a spider was exposed to a group of simulated flies (Fig. 4d). Interestingly, introducing individual agents with different freezing parameters resulted in a diversity effect on behavioral performance, only emerged when the flies synchronized their behavior (W = 2189 and 1195; P = 0.11 and P = 0.0056 for the conditions without and with a synchronization mechanism, respectively; Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test; Fig. 4d and S17). A simple IBM simulation of groups with different freezing parameters also reproduced this enhanced performance in mixed-strain groups (W = 487, P < 0.001, Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test; Fig. S6c), suggesting that genetic heterogeneity in freezing responses leads to collective antipredator performance through flexible behavioral switching.

## Genomic underpinnings of diversity effect
We conducted GWAA to identify loci linked to the diversity effect on group performance, treating it as a higher-level phenotype. Our approach, termed genome-wide higher-level association study (GHAS), focuses on higher-level (i.e., group, community, or population) properties based on genetic profiles determined by population genetic statistics calculated for particular sets of individuals (Fig. 1a). In the present study, we calculated the nucleotide diversity (π) as a metric of genetic diversity for 105 pairs of strains across the genome and found loci whose nucleotide diversities were positively correlated with the diversity effect. Manhattan and Q-Q plots for loci associated with the diversity effect are presented in Fig. 5a and S18a, respectively, and significant loci are listed in Supplementary Data 5. The top-hit locus spans the MFS14 gene (Fig. 5b), which codes Major Facilitator Superfamily Transporter 14, potentially involved in neural and metabolic functions. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the detected loci were functionally associated with metamorphosis, organ morphogenesis, neuron projection, and axonal development (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Data 6). ## Discussion
While the mechanism underlying collective behavior has long been debated in the field of behavioral ecology and/or physics, the knowledge about its genetic substrates has been limited especially in non-eusocial insects20,23,31. In this study, we concentrated on the transmission of visual information among individuals under predator-mimicking stimuli and identified genes linked to collective behavior in flies. The flies displayed freezing in response to looming stimuli, but the degree of freezing reduced upon detecting motion cues from conspecifics under group conditions (Fig. 1). We detected the genetic variants associated with this group effect using GWAS (Fig. 2) and found the candidate genes implicated in the development of visual neurons, such as kirre and Ptp99A; these molecules are known to control the interactions among lamina neurons52,53. The single-cell transcriptomic data of Drosophila visual neurons53 showed genotype-dependent differential expression of Ptp99A (Fig. S11). Functional investigation with the GAL4/UAS system additionally revealed that the KD of Ptp99A and silencing lamina neurons influence responsiveness to other individuals (Fig. 3b). These results indicated a critical role of visual, particularly lamina, neurons in the collective behavior of fruit flies, while other pathways, such as muscle development (Supplementary Data 3), can also be involved in the observed phenomenon.
The lamina is a component of the visual system of the fruit fly, serving as the initial layer of visual processing in the compound eye. This processing facilitates the detection of changes in light intensity, moving objects, and color discrimination. The detection of moving objects is one of the key functions occurring in the lamina58,59. Lamina neurons include L1–L5 monopolar cells, each of which projects to different layers in the outer medulla. L1–L3 neurons receive photoreceptor signals from R1–R6 neurons in the retina, while L4 and L5 neurons receive indirect input of photoreceptor signals through L2 and L1 neurons, respectively54,60. The photoreceptor signals are divided into two parallel motion circuits, ON and OFF pathways refs. 58,59, that send luminance signal increase and decrease, respectively, playing an essential role in edge detection and contrast enhancement54,61. Herein, we observed differential expression of Ptp99A in several neurons with the 3 R:25292746 genotype at various developmental stages (Fig. S11c and f). Although the neural mechanism is yet unclear, such genotypic difference might cause developmental disparities in the visual neurons. This may result in differential processing of optical information as well as visual responsiveness to moving conspecifics and, consequently, in emergent dynamics of the group behavior.
The heterogeneity of behaviors among individuals and its effect on group-level characteristics are currently under intensive focus44–48. In this work, we introduced an equal number of individuals with various genetic backgrounds into a group to test the effect of behavioral heterogeneity on collective freezing through interindividual interactions. The findings revealed that, in mixed-strain groups, the degree of freezing immediately after a stimulus was higher than expected (Fig. 4a), suggesting that behavioral heterogeneity within a group can lead to non-additive changes in group-level performance. Notably, differences in freezing duration—rather than visual responsiveness to conspecifics—were positively correlated with the performance of mixed-strain groups (Fig. 4c and S15). This indicates that heterogeneity in the fear response, coupled with adequate synchronization among individuals, produces emergent behavioral changes at the group level. This hypothesis was further supported by IBM simulations, which showed that conformity to the motion cues of other individuals reduced group-level freezing (Fig. S6b). Moreover, introducing interindividual variation in freezing parameters resulted in adaptive behavioral changes only when the agents were able to socially interact (Fig. S6c). Finally, using an animal-computer interaction experiment, we empirically demonstrated that phenotypic diversity in fear responses among flies within a group reduced the number of spider attacks, thereby increasing virtual survival probability. These results clarify that the emergent phenomena observed in this study arise through relatively simple interaction mechanisms.
Additionally, we established an analytical framework, GHAS (Fig. 1a), and identified loci where genetic diversity within the group is associated with an increase in behavioral performance. Some of the detected loci were also found to be associated with visual responsiveness in GWAS, including kirre and rsh (Fig. S18). Enriched functions of detected loci, such as imaginal disc development or neuron projection morphogenesis, were overlapped between the two analyses (Figs. 2b and 5c), suggesting their common genetic and/or neural mechanisms. Nevertheless, this finding may appear contradictory to the earlier result indicating that interindividual variation in responsiveness to other individuals does not improve group-level performance. To properly interpret these observations, more detailed investigations focusing not only on genome-wide genetic correlations but also on individual loci will be required. Moreover, while correlations between group-level genetic and phenotypic properties with similar approaches have been explored in plants41,43, such studies remain scarce in the context of animal behavior. From a methodological standpoint, further refinements would also be desirable going forward.
Finally, we must acknowledge the challenges in interpreting the functional significance of the observed behavioral patterns, particularly the nonadditive changes in freezing response and the complexities in extrapolating laboratory findings to natural evolutionary processes. While our study offers valuable insights into the neural and genetic mechanisms underlying group behavior in Drosophila, how these behaviors manifest in natural settings remains uncertain. In the wild, flies are exposed to a more complex array of environmental stimuli—encompassing not only visual but also olfactory, tactile, and auditory cues—as well as intricate interactions with various types of predators27. This complexity makes it difficult to fully elucidate the ecological and evolutionary significance of these behaviors. Despite these limitations, our study represents a significant step forward in understanding the genetic basis of group-level behavior in non-eusocial insects. By combining genome-wide association analysis, single-cell transcriptomics, and agent-based simulations, we identified key genomic loci and neural circuits linked to collective visual responses. Importantly, the observed diversity effect highlights how genetic heterogeneity can enhance flexible group behavior, potentially conferring ecological advantages under threat. Future research should aim to confirm these findings in more ecologically relevant environments and explore the evolutionary pressures that shape these complex behaviors.

## Methods

## Ethics
Experiments using transgenic Drosophila melanogaster stocks were approved under Chiba University Institutional Biosafety Committee protocol #G5-46.

## Fly strains and keeping conditions
A cohort of 104 strains derived from the DGRP was used in our experiment. This panel consists of 205 inbred and genetically distinct strains of D. melanogaster, representing natural genetic diversity within a population captured in North Carolina, US. Flies were kept at 25 °C with about 40–60% humidity in a 12 L:12D cycle, with standard food (Bloomington Formulation, Nutri-Fly™, #66-113, Genesee Scientific). A visually defective mutant strain, w[*] norpA[P12] (BDSC#9047)62, was used to test the behavior of blind flies and the contribution of visual information in our assay. The following lines were used for functional assay of lamina neurons and Ptp99A: R9B08-GAL4 (w[1118]; P{y[+t7.7] w[+mC]=GMR9B08-GAL4}attP2/TM3, Sb[1]; BDSC#41369), Ptp99A-GAL4 (w[1118]; Mi{GFP[E.3xP3]=ET1}Ptp99A[MB04947]; BDSC#24843), UAS-TNT (w[*]; P{w[+mC]=UAS-TeTxLC.tnt}G2; BDSC#28838), UAS-IMPTNT (w[*]; P{w[+mC]=UAS-TeTxLC.(-)V}A2; BDSC#28840), UAS-Ptp99ARNAi (y[1] v[1]; P{y[+t7.7] v[+t1.8]=TRiP.JF01858}attP2; BDSC#25840). All fly strains were obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BDSC), and the list of used strains with the stock number is summarized in Supplementary Data 7. Adult flies of both sexes aged at 2–4 days since eclosion were tested in the first behavioral screening with DGRP strains and norpA mutant (n = 20 per sex and social circumstance for each strain). Given the observed chasing behavior among male flies in several strains as described later, which likely impacts their social interaction, we used only female flies in a subsequent mixed-strain group experiment (n = 10 for each pair of strains) and mutant assay (n = 20 for each condition of strains).
To enable comparisons between the norpA mutant strain and DGRP strains, we backcrossed this mutant four times with DGRP208, a strain with intermediate behavioral phenotypes, while monitoring the norpA genotype using the following protocols. Genomic DNA was extracted from the whole body of a fly using the MightyPrep reagent (#9182, Takara Bio Inc., Japan) and amplified by PCR with MightyAmp DNA Polymerase (#R076A, Takara Bio Inc., Japan) and the primers targeting the mutated 240th residue63 (Table S1). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 59 °C for 15 s and 68 °C for 1 min. The amplified norpA cDNAs were digested with Cac8I (#R0579L, New England Biolabs Japan Inc., Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, resulting in products of different lengths depending on the norpA genotype. During the backcrossing, we successfully eliminated the white mutation which can also affect the flies’ visual properties64, through recombination. This resulted in a strain that isolates the effect of the norpAP12 mutation in the DGRP208 genetic background. The confirmed sequences of norpA and eye phenotypes are shown in Fig. S19.

## Behavioral assay under looming stimuli
Behavioral assays were conducted as previously described65. Briefly, flies were tested individually or in single-sex groups (n = 6) to avoid sexual behavior that could confound threat responses. We chose six individuals to be grouped since the previous study demonstrated the number to be enough to show group effect in the assay26. Flies were placed in arenas with sloped walls (30 mm in diameter, 2 mm deep), and looming stimuli were presented on a 13.3-inch monitor tilted 45° toward the plate, as detailed in our previous study65. The flies’ behavior was recorded at approximately 50 frames per second using a USB3 camera (DMK33UX290, The Imaging Source, Germany) controlled by a custom python script. Recorded videos were trimmed per arena and tracked using Tracktor software66, followed by additional error corrections with custom scripts65. To further reduce the calibration errors of tracking, the weighted moving average of coordinates over a time span (9 preceding time points including a time point of interest) was used for analysis. The coordinates were subsequently used to calculate the distance traveled from a previous time point, moving speed (traveled distance divided by frame intervals), and head angle (calculated by the movement between time points 50 ahead and behind the time point of interest) for each time point. These measures were averaged at 0.5-s intervals and used for subsequent analyses. Locomotor behavior was briefly dichotomized as walking when their speed was > 4 mm/s or stopping otherwise25. Freezing was defined when a fly that stopped 0.5 s after a stimulus continued to stop 10 s after the stimulus, otherwise referred to as freezing exit.

## Calculation and analysis of behavioral indices
The average moving speed during the first 5 min with no stimuli was assessed to be an index of baseline locomotor activity. To quantify the level of the sociality of flies, we calculated the NND at a time point when all the flies in a given arena were expected to be at rest (i.e., a time point when the average moving speed across flies were minimum during the 5 min). For comparison, pseudo-group data consisting of random combinations of single fly data were created and evaluated as a control with no social preference. In this process, an experimental date, time, and used plate and arena was randomly chosen from the information of constituting flies and used as covariates for the data. ## Statistical analysis for behavioral traits
Statistical analysis of behavioral data was performed using R 4.4.2 with the car, lmerTest, and lme4 packages when necessary. Effects of strains, sex, social circumstances, and their interactions on behavioral measures were assessed with the linear mixed model (LMM) with experimental date, time, used plate and arena assumed to be random effects. To assess the significance of fixed effects in the LMM, we applied a Type II Wald chi-square test. The broad-sense heritability of traits was estimated by the formula described in previous studies67,68 using mean squares for genotype (strain) and environment (residual error) obtained in analysis from variance (ANOVA). The results of ANOVA are shown in Table S2.

## Genome-wide association analysis
We first performed principal component analysis (PCA) to capture the genetic distance among strains, using a whole set of genotypes of 4,438,427 SNPs for 205 strains obtained from the DGRP2 website (http://dgrp2.gnets.ncsu.edu) with PLINK v1.969. Quality control of SNPs was carried out with conditions: (1) minor allele frequency >0.05 and (2) SNP call rate > 0.9, leaving 1,689,110 SNPs for subsequent analyses. Prior to GWAA, SNP-based heritability and genetic correlation among phenotypes were estimated by SCORE70. We then performed GWAA to identify loci responsible for the three phenotypes (i.e., freezing duration, NND, and visual responsiveness) of flies. The behavioral phenotypes were regressed with the top 10 PCs as covariates using GCTA v1.94.0 with --fastGWA-mlm flag71, based on a linear mixed model and a SNP-derived sparse genetic relationship matrix. The association analysis was performed independently for each sex, and loci with the P-values below the genome-wide significance level as P = 1.0 × 10–5 were detected to be candidates associated with the given phenotype. For visual responsiveness metrics, we have performed a meta-analysis of the GWAS results from both sexes using METAL72, and genome-wide significance level was set to P = 1.0 × 10–6. GO enrichment analysis for candidate loci was conducted independently for both sexes using the clusterProfiler package in R.

## Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of Drosophila visual neurons
Single-cell RNA-seq dataset of the developing visual neurons of D. melanogaster53 was reanalyzed. The dataset included expression data of visual neurons from female offspring of W1118 crossed with various DGRP strains across nine developmental stages (0–96 h APF). Due to differences in tissue composition and coverage53, we used the main dataset comprising 24–96 h APF. Each cell was annotated based on the previous study53, and cell clusters were visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). We specifically focused on motion-detecting neurons54, including R1/6, L1–5, Tm1–4, 9, and T4/5 cells. To compared the expression levels of Ptp99A between the genotypes (C and T alleles of 3 R:25292746) detected in GWAS, we used generalized linear mixed model with a gamma distribution, treating strains and replications as random effects, implemented with the glmer() function from the lme4 package in R. Finally, we conducted weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) using the hdWGCNA package73 in R. The 2,000 most variably expressed genes across the above neurons were selected for analysis, resulting in five major coexpression modules. Eigengene expression levels for each module were compared between the Ptp99A genotypes using Wilcoxon’s rank sum test.

## Mixed-strain group experiments and calculation of diversity effect
Among the 104 strains used in GWAS, we selected genomically diverse 15 strains (cf., Fig. S7a) and prepared groups of 6 female flies, composed of 3 individuals from each of the two different strains (i.e., 105 pairs of strain in total; n = 10 for each). Following the behavioral experiment, the average moving speed among individuals relative to that of first 5 min in a mixed-strain group was compared with the average values of the two strains that constitute the group. Subsequently, visual stimuli were considered as hypothetical predators, and virtual behavioral performance was defined based on the flexible behavioral changes observed in response to the stimuli. Assuming that freezing under predatory pressure and exploration in its absence are adaptive strategies, we calculated a weighted sum of moving speed, assigning a negative coefficient to the freezing term and a positive coefficient to the exploration term (Fig. 4a). Given that flies’ moving speed typically returned to baseline within 3 seconds after a visual stimulus and based on subsequent experiments with predatory spiders, the freezing term was defined as 0–2.5 seconds after stimulus, and the exploration term as 3–14 seconds after stimulus. Weights were applied to account for the differing durations of these time periods in the calculation (Fig. 4a). The difference between the observed performance level of mixed-strain groups and the expected performance level (calculated as the average values of single-strain groups for the two strains) was defined as the diversity effect (Fig. S14).

## Animal-computer interaction experiments with predatory spiders
To explore the adaptive significance of behavioral switching from freezing to exploration, we conducted an animal-computer interaction experiment using jumping spiders (Hasarius adansoni), a well-known predator of fruit flies27,28. A total of 20 trials were conducted with 14 wild-caught male spiders, starved for over 5 days, each placed in a Petri dish (84 mm diameter, 1.5 cm height) on a screen displaying a virtual fly. A black ellipse, 2 mm in length, was used to mimic a fly as it is simple enough to attract jumping spiders74. The experiment was performed under the same environmental condition as previous fly experiments, with the spider’s position tracked for 5 min using the same camera and a custom python script, yielding 1200 frames of data per trial (summarized into 0.25-s bins). The virtual flies’ movement followed a Lévy distribution with α = 1.5, with mean speeds set at three levels: slow ( ~2.5 mm/s), medium ( ~5.0 mm/s), and fast ( ~7.5 mm/s). To simulate the fly’s freezing response, its movement stopped for specified durations (0, 1, 3, 6, or 12 s) when the tracked spider approached within a proximity range (1 or 2 cm). Each spider was sequentially exposed to all conditions, with the order of presentation randomized to minimize potential bias. To prevent perpetual inactivity caused by a stationary spider, the virtual fly resumed movement for 3 seconds after each freezing period. A spider attack was defined as an instance where spider’s moving speed exceeded 30 mm/s and approached within 1 cm of the virtual fly in 3 consecutive frames. Given the spider’s strong attraction to the virtual fly at the highest speed tested (7.5 mm/s) and the shorter proximity range (1 cm) (Fig. S13a), these conditions were selected for the main analysis. Trials in which the spider attacked less than once on average across all freezing conditions were excluded from the analysis. Spider ID, trial, and experimental order (Fig. S13b) were included as random effects in LMM.
To further evaluate the behavioral performance of fly groups, we conducted an additional experiment using six agents in the arena with the same environmental and feeding conditions. In this experiment, we employed the same locomotion speed (7.5 mm/s) and proximity range (1 cm) as described above. Freezing durations were set to 1, 3, 6, and 12 s, and we recreated mixed-strain fly groups by combining agents with different freezing durations (three agents of one value and three of another). We also varied whether or not alignment in moving speed occurred among the agents, resulting in 20 distinct movement conditions. As the above single-agent experiment, each virtual fly group was then exposed to a spider for five minutes in a random order, and we assessed their behavioral performance based on a principal component score integrating the longer distance traveled by the flies and the fewer number of spider attacks, corrected by the order of experiment. A total of 10 individual spiders were used, each tested twice in this grouped-fly experiment. For seven of these spiders (m1–5, m15, and m16 in Fig. S17a), only the 3- or 6-s freezing durations were used in the first trial, meaning the number of experimental conditions for those spiders differed from that of the others.

## Identifying responsible loci for diversity effect with GHAS
The diversity effect was computed per combination of strains and may be explained by the genetic differences (diversity) between the two strains. To comprehensively explore the responsible loci for the diversity effect, we calculated the nucleotide diversity (π) at each locus (1000 bp windows) from the genome sequences of the two strains constituting the mixed group using vcftools75 and conducted an association analysis with the diversity effect, which we termed the GHAS. Genetic relatedness matrix of the analyzed strains was used as covariates. The same threshold as the above GWAA (P < 1.0 × 10–5) was applied to determine candidate loci, and GO enrichment analysis on the adjacently located genes ( ± 1 kbp) was performed using the R package clusterProfiler.
