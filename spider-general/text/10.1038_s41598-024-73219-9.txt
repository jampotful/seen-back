Diagnosing skin cancer using social spider optimization (SSO) and error correcting output codes (ECOC) with weighted hamming distance.

# Diagnosing skin cancer using social spider optimization (SSO) and error correcting output codes (ECOC) with weighted hamming distance

## Abstract
Skin cancer is a common disease resulting from genetic defects, and early detection is critical to improve treatment outcomes. Diagnostic programs that use computer aid especially those that use supervised learning are very useful in early diagnosis of skin cancer. This research therefore presents a new approach that integrates optimization methods with supervised learning to improve skin cancer diagnosis using machine vision approach. The presented method is initiated by data pre-processing that involves the removal of unnecessary data. Then, to segment the images, a combination of K-means clustering and social spider optimization technique is employed. The region of interest is then extracted from the segmented image and from this region a convolutional neural network extracts the significant features. To enhance the classification performance as compared with the standard classifiers, this research introduces a new concept of error correcting output codes coupled with a weighted Hamming distance in the group of gamma classifiers. The ability of the proposed approach in segmentation of skin lesions and classifying them was tested using samples from the ISIC-2017 and ISIC-2016 databases. The introduced method obtained state-of-the-art accuracy on both datasets (ISIC-2016: 97.10%, ISIC-2017: 95.17%). In particularly, the accuracy of the introduced approach for both these databases is at least 1.17% higher than the compared methods. This proves the high performance of the suggested method based on the usage of the convolutional neural networks for feature extraction and gamma classifiers with error correcting output codes for classification in skin cancer detection.

## Introduction
One common kind of cancer that starts in the skin is skin cancer and then, other body parts may experience an invasion by these cells. Skin cell Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) damage that is not repaired leads to cancer and is primarily caused by Ultra Violet (UV) exposure. Skin moles give rise to melanoma cancer cells, which inflame the surrounding area and elevate its temperature1. There are three (3) basic layers of the skin. Squamous, Basal, and melanocyte cells make up the first three outermost layers, which is where skin cancer first appears. Squamous cell and basal cell malignancies are other names for non-melanoma cancers. Non-melanoma Skin cancer reacts well to treatment and seldom spreads to other skin tissues. Compared to most other skin tumors, melanoma is more deadly2.
Traditionally, the diagnosis of melanoma was done by biopsies, the most conclusive test being excision of a small piece of tissue for histopathological examination3. New technologies have been developed to improve the techniques of lesion examination; dermoscopy is one of them, which uses a handheld dermatoscope to improve the visualization of subsurface structures using polarized light and immersion oil4. It is a non-invasive method that assists in locating features that have a likelihood of being melanoma but cannot alone confirm the presence of the disease. Biopsy is still a gold standard in diagnosing melanoma and it is the cornerstone of early diagnosis3.
The ABCDE symptoms, a helpful indicator for melanoma, are one typical and often utilized tip for melanoma diagnosis. Based on five characteristics—(A) symmetry, (B) order irregularity, (C) color variability, (D) diameter higher than 6 mm, and (E) evolution, or any form of change this feature can distinguish among a benign skin lesion and a malignant melanoma5. Cancer diagnosis must be done in a fast and correct manner, with the assistance of some professionals and a variety of necessary equipment. As a result, several computer support systems are deployed to assist the specialists in this field. Machine learning and deep learning techniques are now applied in a wide range of fields other than healthcare6. Non-invasive methods for detecting skin cancer are usually not accurate enough. Invasive sampling methods, although reliable, are not usually accepted by patients due to their difficulties. Recently, machine learning techniques have been used to classify the types of lesions and predict skin cancer through skin images. However, these techniques are far from an ideal model, and we need to use more efficient techniques to more accurately detect skin cancer through images. The majority of research focuses on categorizing lesions as benign or cancerous. However, precise lesion segmentation is essential for treatment planning for confirmed malignancies, therefore it would be beneficial to develop models that are capable of both segmentation and classification.
Therefore, to achieve an efficient model, several research gaps must be filled at the same time. These gaps include: accurate segmentation of lesion, efficient description of features for suspected regions, and also, accurate classification of features. Although some of these issues can be solved individually by taking advantage of recent developments in the field of deep learning; However, the review of recent researches shows that a comprehensive strategy has not been provided to meet these goals at the same time. Presenting a hybrid model to achieve these goals has motivated the current research.
This research presents an innovative image segmentation strategy based on combination of Social Spider Optimization (SSO) and K-means clustering for accurate segmentation of skin lesions. The optimization strategy used in this method minimizes the dependence of its performance on imaging conditions (such as: lighting, noise, distortion) and has made it superior to models based on machine learning. Also, the proposed model utilizes the significant capabilities of convolutional neural networks to effectively extract the features of the lesion. Finally, in order to solve the challenges related to the accuracy of lesion classification, in the proposed method, the combination of gamma classifications has been used in the form of an Error Correcting Output Codes (ECOC) model. This classification model, through the integration of ECOC with an innovative weighted distance measure, significantly improves the performance of classification models compared to individual approaches. Thus, the innovations of the current research can be summarized in the introduced segmentation algorithm as well as the hybrid approach used for feature classification. In brief, our principal contributions are described below:
Presenting a novel image segmentation algorithm based on SSO and K-means for identifying lesions in skin images.Introducing a light-weight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for efficient extraction of features from skin lesions.Presenting a novel hybrid classification algorithm based on gamma classifier ECOC using weighted hamming distance.
Presenting a novel image segmentation algorithm based on SSO and K-means for identifying lesions in skin images.
Introducing a light-weight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for efficient extraction of features from skin lesions.
Presenting a novel hybrid classification algorithm based on gamma classifier ECOC using weighted hamming distance.

## Related works
Majtner et al.7 pre-processed and extracted features from a dataset from the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) using CNN algorithms. The dataset included 379 test samples and 900 training samples that were categorized as malignant and benign. The system employed bounding box techniques, binary masking, and feature extraction using AlexNet. When feature reduction was done using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) approaches, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) yielded the best results with 86% precision and 99.9% specificity.
A melanoma diagnosis method was created by Vipin et al.8 with the goal of cutting down on time and human intervention. The 13,000 photos in the ISIC dataset that the algorithm used were reduced to 7353 after irrelevant ones were eliminated. A symmetric U-Net with maximum pooling and convolutional layers was employed in the segmentation step. Deep residual networks with recurrent neural network and CNN approaches were employed in the classification step. The system’s recall was 91% and its accuracy was 88.7%.
A CNN with a Fully Connected (FC) layer, two pooling layers of max. type, and two convolution layers was proposed by Sanketh et al.9 in order to diagnose skin cancer early, start treatment effectively, and lower death rates. The 2719 photos in the dataset—which was acquired from ISIC—were split into training and testing images. The model’s best performance was 98%.
The CNN introduced by Rahi et al.10 was created by combining max-pooling layers with convolution layers of two distinct strides to create nine layers total among the input and FC layer. There were additional dropout layers used. 2967 photos total—trained and test—were used to train the model. It obtained a 78.81% specificity and an accuracy of 84.76%.
Gulati et al.11 extracted features from the PH2 data, and used Visual Geometry Group (VGG-16) and AlexNet pretrained networks for transfer learning. In transfer learning, VGG-16 fared better than AlexNet, achieving 97.5% accuracy and 96.87% specificity.
Using 640 lesions photos, Daghrir et al.5 developed a new method using CNN, KNN, and Support Vector Machine (SVM) models. There were nine layers in the CNN design, and different preprocessing methods were used. The ultimate classification yielded an accuracy of 88.4%; CNN had the highest individual accuracy of 85.5%, followed by 71.8% accuracy achieved by the SVM.
Razmjooy et al.12 provided an optimization-based technique for melanoma cancer diagnosis using soft computing. They applied preprocessing methods to eliminate artifacts and noises, then used the World Cup Optimization (WCO) algorithm for weight and bias optimization, and applied morphological operations for post-processing.
Based on the notion of deep learning and simulation findings, Jadhav et al.13 suggested a boundary recognition system for skin cancer combining SVM and CNN learning models, reaching a 95.5% accuracy with an F-measure of 94.14%.
Despite a high computational cost, Rehman et al.14 proposed a skin lesion recognition and segmentation method that demonstrated improved segmentation and classification accuracy by using graph-cut and seed region growing strategies, speeded-up robust attributes, and Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptors.
Using ResNet152 for pretraining and combining with CNN in region- and mask-based modes, Jojoa Acosta et al.15 classified lesions in the ISIC-2017 data. Two phases of the architecture’s implementation were carried out, with the eVida M6 model—the suggested model—offering a precision boost of 3.66% over the top-performing models.
An SVM based in a kernel function with Gaussian Radial Basis Function (RBF) was used by Mustafa and Kimura16 to provide an intelligent technique for distinguishing melanoma in pictures. With the optimal parameters, the algorithm identified only six criteria for melanoma identification, with an accuracy of 86.67%.
Ilkin et al.17 established a hybrid classification algorithm for melanoma detection using bacterial colony algorithm. The algorithm, hybSVM, uses segmented images and the ABCDE rule to identify nine features. HybSVM proved to be successful and time-efficient as evidenced by its accuracy of 97.50% and 97.56% for the PH2 and ISIC datasets, when compared to Scikit-learn and ThunderSVM.
Arora et al.18 presented a diagnosis method that uses accurate feature extraction approaches to identify lesions as benign or malignant. They applied models such as quadratic SVM, linear discriminant, fine tree, and linear SVM to 100 images from the PH2 dataset.
The chaotic WCO, is a new metaheuristic methodology that Bi et al.19 presented to enhance skin cancer diagnosis systems. It utilizes contrast enhancement for segmentation and a SVM for melanoma boundary identification. The method’s precision is enhanced by choosing useful features and training them to the SVM. The results show the method has the highest effectiveness compared to four other methods. With low rates of erroneous acceptance and rejection, the approach yields the highest correct identification rate for ISIC and American Cancer Society (ACS) images.
Nawaz et al.20 used Fuzzy K-means Clustering (FKM) and faster region-based CNN (RCNN) to offer an intelligent solution for early-stage segmentation of cutaneous melanoma. The technique first preprocesses the photos to eliminate noise and issues with lighting before applying RCNN to produce a feature vector. The afflicted area of skin is then segmented using FKM. With an average accuracy of 95.6%, 93.1%, and 95.40% respectively on the PH2, ISIC-2017, and ISIC-2016 data, the strategy exceeds state-of-the-art approaches.
Tabrizchi et al.21 introduced a new model for early diagnosis of skin cancer based on dermoscopic data and utilizing VGG-16 network. The model improves accuracy in skin cancer detection by employing an enhanced architecture. A comparative study on the international skin image collaboration dataset showed that their model can outperform other techniques in terms of accuracy. Table 1 summarizes the studied works.

## Research methodology
This section first outlines the characteristics of the utilized data, then proceeds to explain the processes implemented in the introduced approach for diagnosing skin cancer based on these data.

## Data
Samples from the datasets ISIC-201622 and ISIC-201723 are used in this investigation. The datasets made available by ISBI address the problem of diagnosing skin cancer through analysis of skin lesions. Training and test samples comprise the two components of the ISIC-2016 database. There are 900 color photographs in this database that serve as training examples. It also contains 379 test samples, of which 304 are classified as normal and 75 as melanoma. However, the ISIC-2017 database has more samples than that—2000 training samples and 600 test samples—than any other database. This database’s test set includes 117 melanoma samples and 483 normal samples. The instances from both datasets have characteristics that make the detection procedure difficult, like the presence of tiny veins and hair strands, noise, reflection, and blurring. Notably, as of the time this article was written, the ISIC dataset included three more recent databases: ISIC-2018, ISIC-2019, and ISIC-2020. But they weren’t used in the testing procedure because of the large number of samples in these databases.

## Proposed method
For efficient skin cancer detection via machine vision techniques, it’s crucial to employ a precise strategy for eliminating image artifacts and pinpointing the Region of Interest (ROI). Concurrently, the deployment of an accurate feature extraction model to supply a robust classification model is highly significant. This research endeavors to fulfill these prerequisites in the issue of skin cancer detection by amalgamating optimization techniques and supervised learning. The proposed method executes the detection process through the subsequent steps:
Preprocessing.Segmentation and ROI detection: Utilizing a combination of SSO and K-means.Extracting features from ROI: Employing a convolutional neural network.Detection: Using gamma classifiers and ECOC with weighted hamming distance.
Preprocessing.
Segmentation and ROI detection: Utilizing a combination of SSO and K-means.
Extracting features from ROI: Employing a convolutional neural network.
Detection: Using gamma classifiers and ECOC with weighted hamming distance.
The sequence of steps in the proposed method is illustrated in a diagram in Fig. 1. As per this figure, the input images undergo preprocessing initially to eliminate extraneous information present within the input image, e.g., hair strands. Following this, each image is segmented utilizing an algorithm that combines SSO and K-means. A collection of segmented images with the ROI recognized as a contiguous region are the result of this stage. A CNN is then used to extract features from the ROI. As a result, the input image’s probable melanoma region is clipped and applied to the suggested CNN. The last FC layer of this CNN model defines the features of the ROI, leaving off the layers required for feature classification. Ultimately, a group of gamma classifiers are used to form an ECOC model for classifying the extracted features. This classification model improves the performance of the conventional ECOC model by utilizing a new weighted hamming distance and ascertains the status of the input sample in terms of susceptibility to skin cancer. The remainder of this section is devoted to elaborating each of these steps.

This preprocessing step’s goal is to remove unnecessary data and offset negative image characteristics, which will improve the detection system’s performance in the next stages. The existence of hair strands and blood arteries in the skin, which can cause errors in the identification process;Negative effects resulting from imaging, such as blurring, noise, or light reflection in the skin tissue.

## Image segmentation based on K-means and SSO
The second phase in the presented model involves the segmentation of preprocessed images and the identification of the ROI, accomplished using a fusion of the K-Means algorithm and SSO. The objective of this phase is to diminish the complexity of the problem, enabling the separation of the region suspected of being a lesion in the image as a cluster from other regions. The segregation of the lesion suspected of skin cancer can be achieved by analyzing the disparity in brightness intensity values. Consequently, by optimally adjusting the threshold values for each region, the accurate method of segregating regions in images can be ascertained. The proposed strategy models this phase as an optimization problem and employs SSO to solve it. The proposed method adopts an approach akin to the basic K-Means algorithm for image segmentation, with the distinction that the center of each region in the image is regarded as an optimization variable, and its optimal value is ascertained using SSO. Additionally, to expedite convergence, the initial population of SSO is formed based on the K-Means algorithm. In this section, the segmentation process is initially formulated as an optimization problem, followed by an explanation of its solving using SSO.
Consider an image, denoted as X, to be segmented into K non-overlapping segments. In any skin image, the differentiation between two tissue regions can only be achieved by examining the positional information and brightness intensity of the pixels that constitute the tissue. Consequently, a segment in image X can be characterized as a set of adjacent pixels that exhibit a smaller intensity difference with each other compared to other adjacent regions (possessing a common border). Conversely, two disconnected regions A and B with analogous intensity values can be regarded as two distinct segments. This definition implies that to identify each segment, both the positional information and brightness intensity of the pixels must be taken into account. In the optimization problem addressed in this research, an optimization variable is assigned for each ROI in the image, which determines the brightness intensity threshold corresponding to that region. Consequently, each solution vector in the proposed algorithm is encoded as a numerical vector of length K, where each value in this vector has search boundaries of (0, 255) and signifies the brightness intensity threshold of the pixels pertaining to its corresponding region.
In the proposed method, the membership of each pixel to a region is ascertained by calculating the difference in brightness intensity with the thresholds established in the solution vector. Consequently, each pixel in the image is associated with a region that exhibits the smallest absolute difference with the threshold corresponding to that region in the solution vector. In this manner, for each solution vector generated by the optimization algorithm, a candidate segmentation for the input image can be formulated. The primary objective of the optimization algorithm is to identify the optimal segmentation state among the generated candidate responses. To achieve this, the preeminence of a segmentation state over others must be ascertained using a fitness evaluation function. The proposed optimization model employs a fusion of three criteria: intra-cluster distance,’ ‘inter-cluster distance,’ and ‘entropy’ to determine the optimal segmentation state for each image. To evaluate the fitness of each solution vector based on these criteria, the image X is initially segmented into K regions based on the solution vector S. During this process, the absolute difference of each pixel with each of the K thresholds determined in S is computed, and that pixel is associated with the region that exhibits the smallest difference with the corresponding threshold. By applying this operator to all pixels, the image X is segmented into K regions to yield the matrix Y. The resulting segmentation solely considers the brightness intensity information of image X and disregards the positional information of the regions. Henceforth, the obtained regions are segregated from each other based on positional information. In this case, the image matrix Y is segmented into its constituent contiguous regions. Subsequently, if the area of a contiguous region exceeds 0.1 of the image area, a new unique identifier is allocated to that region to be recognized as a separate segment. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\left(x\right)$$\end{document} represents the probability of the occurrence of brightness intensity value x in a segment. The objective of the proposed segmentation algorithm is to establish a state of image segmentation that minimizes the entropy and intra-cluster distance of each segment while maximizing the inter-cluster distance. Minimizing entropy and intra-cluster distance implies forming segments with maximum pattern uniformity. Conversely, maximizing the inter-cluster distance ensures that a state of segmentation can be achieved where any difference between any two regions is maximized. The proposed method employs SSO to identify a segmentation capable of minimizing Eq. (4).
It is worth noting that a high convergence speed in a segmentation algorithm, based on optimization techniques, carries significant importance. In this context, in addition to the search strategy used, the way the initial population is formed is also important. In the proposed method, the K-means algorithm is utilized to determine a portion of the population. This allows for the provision of approximations of points adjacent to the global optimum for the optimization algorithm. Considering that setting the entire initial population based on the K-means algorithm can heighten the risk of the algorithm falling into a local optimum, the proposed method determines 1/4 of the initial population in SSO based on K-means, while the other solution vectors in the initial population are assigned random values. In this scenario, to determine each initial K-means solution vector, image X is converted into vector form, and the resulting vector is segmented into K clusters. ## Detection based on gamma classifiers and ECOC
The last stage of the suggested approach uses an ECOC model, which consists of a set of gamma classifiers, to identify skin cancer by classifying the features taken from the ROI. As a result, the structure of the suggested ECOC model is explained in this part before the gamma classifiers that were used are discussed.

## ECOC with weighted hamming distance
Using the ECOC model and a coding matrix, the complex classification issues is divided into a number of simpler classification tasks. In the coding matrix, the binary classifiers are represented by columns, while the target classes are represented by rows. The defined codes in each column of the coding matrix are used to train the binary classifiers. Each target category is represented as a binary code string in a unique way.
In the proposed method, the dense random scheme is used to construct the coding matrix. This strategy sets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L\:=\:10\times\:{\text{log}}_{2}\left(C\right)$$\end{document} as the total length of the distinctive code string for every target category. The gamma classifier is the classification model that the suggested approach uses. Using the dense random technique, a coding matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{M}_{C\:\times\:\:L}$$\end{document} is initially built in order to classify the retrieved characteristics. Every row in the matrix corresponds to a target category, and the codes found in one of the training coding matrix’s columns are used to train every gamma classifier model.
During the testing stage, each sample’s attributes are analyzed by a trained gamma classifier to create binary outputs, which are then used to predict the target in the test samples. Next, a code string is created by combining the classifiers’ binary output codes. The instance will ultimately belong to the category whose binary output from the classification algorithms has the lowest distance with the associated string after this code string is contrasted with the rows of the M.
The Hamming distance measure is used in traditional ECOC methods to calculate the disparity between two code sequences. All of the learners are equally important in this scenario. On the other hand, in the introduced model the disparity is determined using the weight values that are assigned to each binary classifier. The exhaustive technique is employed to provide weight for the learners, with each classifier’s weight being computed as a natural number within the interval [0,3].
Following this method of weighting the learners, the disparity between the binary classifiers’ output code sequence and the target category codes is computed using the Hamming function in conjunction with these weight values. The following is the definition of this weighted Hamming distance:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:WH\left(u,v\right)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{L}{w}_{i}\lambda \left({u}_{i},\:{v}_{i}\right),$$\end{document}
where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the weight given to the (i)th classifier, u and v are code strings, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:\left({u}_{i},\:{v}_{i}\right)$$\end{document} is a binary function that yields 1 in the event that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{i}$$\end{document} are not equal. Also, L denotes the length of code sequence associated to each target class.

## Gamma classifier
The generalized Gamma operator is used by the supervised Gamma classifier, which gets its name from the similarity operator. When a positive integer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}) and two binary vectors (x and y) are entered, this operator returns a value of 1 if the two vectors are similar, and 0 otherwise. A binary vector is required as input for the gamma operator. To work with real or integer vectors, a mechanism for representing these vectors in binary form is needed. Typically, the modified Johnson-Möbius code from26 is used in this work. The proposed method uses the mechanism defined in26 to implement the gamma classifier, and therefore, its details are omitted in this section.

## Implementation results
In this study, MATLAB software version 2020 was used to conduct experiments on two datasets, ISIC-2016 and ISIC-2017. To avoid the risk of getting a biased performance assessment due to the single random splitting of the dataset, the 10-fold cross validation was done on the entire data. While the initial model development and the tuning of hyperparameters were done using the training, validation, and the testing set, the 10-fold cross-validation was performed on the entire dataset to make the evaluation of the generalization ability of the model accurate. First, the whole dataset is divided into 10 parts with uncommon instances. Then at the start of each fold in cross-validation one of these parts (10%) is taken as the test set. For the rest of the 9 parts, one random part is taken as validation set (10%) while the other 8 parts (80%) are taken as training examples. By means of such selection, all the instances of the dataset are used to test the model at the end of the 10-fold cross-validation. At the end of each fold the evaluation criteria (accuracy, precision, recall and f-measure) are determined based on the predictions made for the test instances. The performance metrics presented in this section are the averages from the 10-fold cross-validation process in the end. This approach increases the validity and reliability of our study. While implementing the SSO optimization algorithm, we considered the population size as 100 and the number of iterations as 300. Also, for the K-Means algorithm, we have considered the number of regions to be 2.

## Discussion, limitations and future works
The findings show that the proposed method achieved superior performance compared to baseline approaches (CNN-only and Single GC) and achieved competitive results against recent methods (CWCO-SVM, DL-FKM, iVGG16) on the ISIC-2016 and ISIC-2017 datasets. The CNN plays a crucial role in feature extraction. Tables 3 and 4 show that the CNN-only approach achieves reasonable accuracy (ISIC-2016: 93.67%, ISIC-2017: 90.50%). This indicates the ability of the CNN to learn discriminative features from skin lesion images. While the GC on its own performs moderately well (ISIC-2016: 89.45%, ISIC-2017: 84.33%), in this case, the use of the ECOC-based GCs in conjunction with the CNN (CNN + GC) results in remarkable improvement (at least 7.65% in term of accuracy). This implies that the GCs are able to leverage on the informative features that are generated by the CNN in order to perform a better classification. The incorporation of ECOC with a weighted Hamming measure in the proposed method is therefore advantageous and leads to better performance than Single GC approach and also other methods (Tables 3 and 4). ECOC enables solving multi-class problems by using several binary classifiers and enhancing the model’s stability when there is noise and class imbalance.
The proposed method achieved state-of-the-art accuracy on both datasets (ISIC-2016: 97.10%, ISIC-2017: 95.17%). In comparison with the previously proposed approaches (CWCO-SVM19, DL-FKM20, and iVGG1621), the proposed method showed higher or similar accuracy, precision, recall, F-measure, and AUC rates. This indicates that feature extraction using CNNs and classification using gamma classifiers and ECOC are effective in the diagnosis of skin cancer.
Despite the significant performance in increasing the accuracy of skin cancer diagnosis, the proposed method has limitations:
Limited Dataset Size: Although the proposed models yield good performance, the study is restricted by the sample size of the ISIC-2016 and ISIC-2017 datasets.Computational Cost: Training deep learning models like CNNs and also, multiple gamma classifiers can sometimes be time consuming.Explainability: Although the presented method has a high accuracy, a difficulty appears in understanding the decisions made by the CNN model.

## Conclusion
Skin cancer, a prevalent global malignancy, underscores the critical need for early identification to significantly impact treatment outcomes. This approach involves image preprocessing to eliminate extraneous data, segmentation through a composite SSO and K-Means algorithm, feature extraction via a CNN, and the utilization of a Gamma classifier for detection. The process commences with image preprocessing to remove redundant information, followed by segmentation using a hybrid SSO and K-Means algorithm, feature extraction via a CNN, and subsequent isolation of the suspected melanoma region. The CNN model is then employed to extract features, which are subsequently classified using a Gamma classifier to determine the input sample’s susceptibility to skin cancer. The proposed method clearly demonstrates its efficacy in cancer detection with a precision of 91% on the ISIC-2016 dataset and 82% on the ISIC-2017 dataset. This research presents a promising advancement in the accurate detection of skin cancer, highlighting the potential for improved diagnostic capabilities in clinical settings.
