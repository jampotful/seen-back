# A comprehensive image dataset for the identification of lemon leaf diseases and computer vision applications

## Abstract
A comprehensive dataset on lemon leaf disease can surely bring a lot of potentials into the development of agricultural research and the improvement of disease management strategies. This dataset was developed from 1354 raw images taken with professional agricultural specialist guidance from July to September 2024 in Charpolisha, Jamalpur, and further enhanced with augmented techniques, adding 9000 images. The augmentation process involves a set of techniques-flipping, rotation, zooming, shifting, adding noise, shearing, and brightening-to increase variety for different lemon leaf condition representations. Each of these images was standardized to 800 × 800 pixels resolution, so that consistency may be maintained among the dataset. All images were labelled in the nine prefixed categories: anthracnose, bacterial blight, citrus canker, curl virus, deficiency leaf, dry leaf, healthy leaf, sooty mould, and spider mites. In the present study, a DenseNet-121 architecture was used, where 20 % of the dataset was kept for validation and the remaining 80 % for training. A trained model with a batch size of 32 was trained for 30 epochs, achieving an accuracy of 98.56 % with augmentation, and 96.19 % without it. The dataset will not only act as a benchmark in developing accurate machine learning models for early disease detection, but it will also contribute to the cause of sustainable lemon cultivation practices by facilitating timely and effective disease management interventions.

## Background
Lemon trees, as well as many species of citrus, are affected by a large number of diseases and pests that can seriously affect the yield and quality of the crop. The early detection and identification of disease help in the management of the disease to avoid widespread agricultural loss. Traditional diagnosis of diseases usually involves expert-based visual inspection, which is time-consuming, prone to errors, and expensive to conduct over large areas. We developed the dataset out of the need to address these challenges, which significantly affect agricultural productivity. The dataset is a response to the limited availability of comprehensive collections that focus on the distinct stages and types of lemon leaf diseases, crucial for the development of precise diagnostic models.
Our dataset is rather unique in many ways compared to existing datasets, such as that presented by H. T. Rauf et al. [1], containing images of citrus fruit, leaf, and stem depicting Black Spot, Canker, Scab, Greening, and Melanose diseases from Pakistani orchards. First, the dataset involves 1354 high-resolution images across nine classes, namely Anthracnose, Bacterial Blight, Citrus Canker, Curl Virus, Deficiency Leaf, Dry Leaf, Healthy Leaf, Sooty Mould, and Spider Mites, providing wider coverage of lemon leaf conditions. Second, unlike most existing datasets, ours has been tested using the DenseNet-121 model with high performance metrics that ascertain its strength for training and validation of state-of-the-art deep learning algorithms. To improve the variability in the dataset and make the model more robust, we used advanced data augmentation techniques.
By providing access to this dataset, we aim to support researchers and practitioners in advancing agricultural disease management, promoting transparency, reproducibility, and further exploration in precision farming.

## Data Description
The dataset comprises 1354 high-resolution images of both healthy and diseased lemon leaves of nine specific classes, including anthracnose, bacterial blight, citrus canker, curl virus, deficiency leaf, dry leaf, healthy leaf, sooty mould, and spider mites. These images were collected manually between July and September 2024 from orchards located in Charpolisha, Jamalpur, under the guidance of an agricultural expert. All images were captured using the camera of an iPhone 14 Plus; each photo was taken at a resolution of 800 × 800 pixels in JPG format. All images are properly labeled with their respective disease class, and hence, this dataset serves as an important source of information for machine learning and image processing tasks pertaining to lemon leaf disease detection.
During the data collection process, several challenges were encountered, such as:1.Inclement weather conditions, heavy rain, and strong winds produced murky images, and dust and debris often gathered on leaves and masked the symptoms of disease, generally making it challenging to capture clean and representative images.2.Due to this, different plants had to be revisited multiple times in order to capture images of different stages, thus increasing the time taken for the data to be collected. Also, diseases such as bacterial blight and citrus canker look so similar; hence, their labeling was quite difficult.3.Some diseases acted quickly because of the fast degradation of infected leaves; hence, the golden opportunity-that is very crucial for building an accurate disease detection model-of capturing early-stage infection was missed.

In contrast, datasets captured in controlled conditions may yield models that work well only under similar controlled conditions but fail when confronted with real-world variability. This complete dataset represents different conditions of lemon leaf diseases and would find applicability in the study of dynamics, disease development, and precision agriculture techniques for more sustainable farming.

## Experimental design
Leaf diseases have an effect on agricultural output, which in turn affects farmers' profitability and production capacity. Accurate plant disease identification requires computer-aided detection methods [13]. However, this study able to identify the disease of lemon leaf that will assist the agriculturalist. To create this dataset, we painstakingly gathered images from lemon farms, capturing a variety of symptoms of lemon leaf diseases under a range of climatic conditions. We carried out data preparation after data collection, which entailed arranging and cleaning the photos to improve their quality for analysis. Rigorous preprocessing steps were taken to make this dataset better for training. Data augmentation techniques used are flipping, rotation, and brightness adjustments that introduced more variation into the dataset. This dataset was then split into two subsets: 80 % for training and 20 % for testing. Deep learning models were trained based on these and tested with the test data to see their performance. By doing this step, the dataset was guaranteed to be free of noise and irregularities. In order to provide an organized basis for creating and assessing models for precise lemon leaf disease detection, we then divided the dataset into training and testing sets. ## Methods
The raw images collected from the lemon orchards were prepared at the pre-processing stage for the training deep learning models through several crucial steps outlined in Fig. 3. First, data cleaning was performed to exclude noisy, irrelevant, or duplicate images with a view to retaining only high-quality samples in the dataset that would be suitable for training. Further to that, the data were resized to ensure homogeneity within the set, with all images brought to a standard resolution of 800 × 800 pixels. Data labeling was done accordingly, classifying images into nine different classes each for anthracnose, bacterial blight, citrus canker, curl virus, deficiency leaf, dry leaf, healthy leaf, sooty mould, and spider mites. Other data augmentation techniques involved in this respect include image shifting, flipping, rotation, zooming, noising, Sharing and adjusting brightness levels to further increase the robustness of the dataset. These augmentations contributed to increasing diversity in the dataset through the generation of new training samples, thereby mitigating risks of overfitting that might occur during model training. Most importantly, the augmentation techniques are necessary to enhance the robustness and generalization of the deep learning model for real-world scenarios where there is a change in the conditions of the images. ## Data augmentation
Data augmentation can enhance model optimization and generalizability by generating fresh data samples from pre-existing data [14]. This method efficiently increases the amount of training data that is accessible without requiring additional data gathering, which is particularly helpful when working with tiny datasets. All things considered, data augmentation is essential to raising the effectiveness and dependability of machine learning models in a variety of fields and applications. Deep learning models benefit greatly from data augmentation, especially when it comes to visual object recognition tasks. It improves model generalization and reduces overfitting by expanding the training dataset with a variety of image variants.
Some of the techniques implemented here for this research involve flipping, rotation, zooming, shifting, adding noise, sharing, and adjusting brightness. These will introduce some variability into the dataset, hence enabling the model to generalize well with samples unseen and strengthening its ability to classify the lemon leaf diseases under different conditions; these include different orientations of leaves, variations in light, or angles.

## Model visualization
One version of the DenseNet network with four dense blocks is DenseNet121. Each dense block has six, twelve, twenty-four, and sixteen dense layers in order [15]. In order to classify lemon leaf illnesses, we thoroughly evaluated the DenseNet-121 architecture in this work. Images from seven distinct classes, each depicting a different lemon leaf disease, make up the dataset. We set aside 20 % of the data for validation and 80 % for training. We used data augmentation approaches to boost the training process, which broadened the training data's variety and enhanced the model's ability to generalize to cases that havenʼt been seen before. With a batch size of 32, the DenseNet-121 model was trained across 30 epochs. During training, the learning rate was dynamically adjusted using a learning rate scheduler. A variety of assessment criteria, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score (Eqs. (1) to (4)), were employed to assess the models' efficacy [16]. These measures provide a thorough evaluation of the models' performance and provide light on their categorization skills from several angles. Using both training and validation datasets, we also tracked the training process. ## Limitations
The lemon leaf dataset is limited by its geographic focus, potentially reducing its generalizability to other regions with different environmental conditions. It may also suffer from class imbalances, which could affect model performance on underrepresented diseases. Additionally, the use of a single image capture device further limits diversity, and the absence of data on non-visible symptoms restricts its comprehensiveness for broader disease detection tasks.

These leaves lose their moisture content but retain a subtle citrus aroma and flavor, often used in teas, culinary dishes, and herbal remedies. Dry lemon leaves are known for their mild, fragrant qualities, adding a delicate lemony note to infusions and spice blends [6]. | Image, table 1
Sooty mould | Sooty mold on lemon leaves is a fungal growth that appears as a black, powdery coating on the leaf surface. It usually develops when the leaves are covered with honeydew, a sticky substance excreted by pests like aphids or whiteflies. While the mold itself doesn't directly harm the plant, it can block sunlight, interfering with photosynthesis and weakening the lemon tree over time [8]. Proper pest control and regular leaf cleaning can help prevent sooty mold formation. | Image, table 1
Spider Mites | Spider mite infestation affects lemon leaves, causing yellowing, speckling, and stippling. In severe cases, they produce webbing. Untreated damage can weaken the tree, reduce fruit yield, and increase susceptibility to diseases. Control measures include regular leaf washing, insecticidal soap, or natural predators [9]. | Image, table 1
Bacterial blight | Bacterial blight in lemon leaves is a destructive disease caused by Xanthomonas axonopodis, causing dark, water-soaked lesions that enlarge, turn brown, and reduce fruit quality. Management involves pruning, improving air circulation, avoiding overhead irrigation, and using copper-based bactericides [10]. | Image, table 1
Healthy leaf | A healthy lemon leaf, vibrant green, glossy, smooth, and firm, supports photosynthesis and fruit growth and is used in cooking and teas due to its zesty aroma and mild citrus flavor [7]. | Image, table 1
