Abstract
Animals communicate with each other to convey messages containing information used in
many contexts (e.g., mating, territory defense, predator defense, foraging). Communication
occurs within visual, auditory, chemical, electrical, or mechanical (seismic) channels or with a
combination of these--multimodal signaling. During communication, aspects of the environment
may interfere with the transmission of the signals, which may affect the behavior of the animals
that are sending and receiving the messages. The work embodied in this dissertation examines
how different types environmental interference affect courtship and mating behavior of the wolf
spider, Schizocosa ocreata (Hentz) through a series of experimental studies. Interference may
not only affect the outcome of communication (mating success) but influence behavior during
the sending and receiving of messages. The types of environmental interference examined here
reflect aspects of the natural environment, including substrates with different signal transmission
properties (e.g., leaves vs rocks), noises that may occur during signaling (low frequency
anthropogenic noise, bird calls, cicada calls), and interference from other conspecifics signaling
within the communication network. In aiming to understand the effects of these potential
disturbances, the work also explores several theoretical frameworks concerning communication:
multimodal signaling, signaling with noise, and signaling within a communication network. In
Chapter 2, I found differences in transmission effectiveness of seismic signals among natural
substrates (leaves, rock, soil, wood) and a lower overall mating success on those that attenuate
vibration (rock, soil, wood). Male spiders also exhibited an increase in visual signaling when on
less effective substrates. These findings provide support for the backup signaling hypothesis and
potentially the alerting/enhancing/amplifying hypotheses for the function of multimodal
signaling. Chapter 3 is among the first studies to explore how airborne noise effect seismic

iii

communication, and suggests that the content of the sound is important, as mating success was
reduced with both white noise and bird calls, but not with cicada calls. Signaling behavior of
males was reduced with bird calls (which reflect the presence of a predator nearby). Female
orientation behavior increased with white noise. These findings suggest that the likelihood of
response is reduced though latency of female receivers to respond and mate in both cases. No
changes in spider behavior or other effects of cicada calls were detected. Finally, in Chapter 4
the communication of signaling males indicates both an inhibition and initiation of male
courtship with the presence of signals from other courting males, depending on stimuli from
females. The work in this dissertation provides several examples of the effects of environmental
interference on seismic signaling and how wolf spiders exhibit conditional behavioral plasticity
in their responses to noise disturbances.

Introduction
Animal communication involves not only one individual sending a message, but also
another receiving and responding to that message. Many definitions exist for communication,
but most incorporate the same key features (Fig. 1) — the sender (1), the signal/message (2), the
receiver (3), and the response (4) (Slater, 1983; Endler, 1993; Bradbury and Vehrencamp, 1998;
Greenfield, 2002; Partan and Marler, 2002). Signals are costly to produce (Dawkins, 1993), and
so selection acting on the signaler favors the most effective signaling behaviors and signaling
conditions (Endler, 1993). Selection acting on receivers often favors underlying sensory
mechanisms to improve signal reception (Arak and Enquist, 1993). Finally, the receiver’s
response should be favorable to the sender (Partan and Marler, 2002). Successful
communication therefore relies on effective transmission and reception of signals/messages.
Signals must be differentiated from environmental background noise, which makes signals
harder to detect and reduces the amount of information sent (Endler, 1992; Ryan and Rand,
1993). A general definition for noise is sound occurring simultaneously with a signal, making
detection more difficult (Rabin and Greene, 2002; Brumm and Slabbekoorn, 2005). What is
important to recognize is that “noise” may extend beyond airborne sound and interfere with any
type of signal (e.g., vibration, visual, chemical, etc.) and may apply to a wide range of
environmental conditions (e.g., lighting conditions, con- or heterospecific animals, or
anthropogenic influences). Noise may interfere with or interrupt communication at any point in
the process (sending, receiving, or responding). However, animals may alter signaling or
receiving behaviors to cope with noise at any point within the process of communication.
This dissertation examines how environmental interference affects animal communication
and how plasticity in behaviors may enhance communication. Animals may make behavioral

2

adjustments for environmental interference in several ways (Fig. 1): signalers may adjust the
message (a: 1 Æ 2), by increasing the magnitude of output, altering the pattern or syllables,
switching signaling location, or modifying the environment to best transmit the signal (Sorjonen,
1986; Rundus and Hart, 2002; Brumm and Slater, 2006; Halfwerk and Slabbekoorn, 2009).
Similarly, receivers may adjust their ability to receive the signal (b: 2 Æ 3) by moving to a better
location or adjusting position to receive the signal more efficiently, or changing receptor
sensitivity (Brownell and Farley, 1979; Barth, 1985; Narins et al., 1997; Rogers et al., 2010).
Based on the level of environmental interference, the receiver’s response may also vary (c: 3 Æ
4), such as latency to respond, number/intensity of responses or even choice of response
(Gerhard and Klump, 1988; Zuk and Kolluru, 1998). While the response of the receiver—in a
sense—becomes a signal, the communication process is not complete until the response of the
receiver is received by the signaler (d: 4 Æ 1). Once received, the signaler may then modulate
the initial signal by changing its message or reacting to the response in some other way. This
final step is critical in determining the success of signal transmission. For instance, if the
receiver is unable to acquire the message with environmental interference then the response may
be reflected in the signaler’s subsequent behaviors.
Environmental interference exists in many forms across all channels of communication.
Much research has focused on the acoustic channel, as noise in that channel is the most familiar
to human senses. Studies include measuring behaviors from both field experiments and
manipulative lab experiments, with noise sources arising from other signaling animals, natural
sources such as waterfalls, or anthropogenic noises created by cities and machinery (Gerhardt
and Klump, 1988; Brumm and Slater, 2006; Bee and Swanson, 2007; Dingle et al., 2008;
Lengagne, 2008; Francis et al., 2009; Halfwerk and Slabberkoorn, 2009). Typically, lab studies

3

control the decibel level of noise and measure sender or receiver behavioral responses. Similar
to acoustic noise, seismic noise from substrate vibration has recently become a frequent topic of
study (Polajnar and Cokl, 2008; Mazzoni et al., 2009). However, noise may exist in the visual
channel as well, e.g., wind moving vegetation, affecting detection of head bobbing displays of
lizards (Ord et al., 2007; Peters et al., 2008), and pollution increasing turbidity in water, making
color displays of fish harder to see (Seehausen et al., 1997). Environmental interference can also
be considered within the social context, as conspecifics signaling at the same time may
intentionally (or unintentionally) jam or block signals (Egnor et al., 2007; Groning and
Hochkirch, 2008).
The work included in this dissertation focuses on how environmental interference affects
animal signaling and receiving behavior in the well-studied wolf spider Schizocosa ocreata
(Hentz) from several perspectives. Chapter 2 identifies substrates that transmit or attenuate the
seismic portion of courtship signaling, and how communication behaviors (courtship, receptivity,
and mating success) vary across these substrates. Chapter 3 examines acoustic noise from the
environment as interference with behaviors involved in mating. Several types of noise were
chosen as representations of what the spiders encounter, including white noise (to represent
anthropogenic noises), predatory bird calls, and noisy cicada calls (loud calls occurring during
the breeding season that pose no threat or food option for the spiders). Finally, Chapter 4
focuses on the social environment, and how signaling of neighboring conspecifics effects
individual signaling. In addition, two appendices include data from several studies on spider
sound perception and behavior. Appendix I demonstrates that spiders hear airborne sounds via
substrate vibrations, already published as one of the studies in Lohrey et al., (2009). Appendix II
explores female reliance on visual cues in circumstances where seismic cues would not be

4

detected (attenuating substrates and noisy conditions). Since the data in this chapter cannot
disprove the null hypotheses, they are included as an appendix but not a chapter.

Short title: Substrate and multimodal signals of wolf spiders

Keywords:

wolf spiders, Lycosidae, multimodal signals, seismic signals, substrate

* Submitted to Animal Behaviour, July 2010

12

Abstract
Communication in complex environments poses challenges of potential loss of intended
messages, but some animals may compensate by using multimodal signaling. Courtship displays
of male Schizocosa ocreata (Hentz) wolf spiders are multimodal, consisting of visual and
seismic signals. The microhabitat of S. ocreata is complex, including leaf litter, wood/bark, soil,
and rocks. Results from laser vibrometer measurements indicate that leaf litter transmits male
courtship signals with greater efficacy. Mating success is significantly greater on leaf litter
(~85%) compared to other substrates (~30%), even though latency to male courtship did not vary
between substrates. Given these results, selection should favor spiders that increase the potential
of mating on non-conducting substrates. We tested whether spiders detect substrate differences
in a choice test, and found that males and females visited each substrate with equal frequency,
but spent significantly more time on leaf litter. We isolated courting male spiders on each
substrate and scored courtship behaviors to see whether signals varied with substrate. Males
used significantly more visual signals (waves and arches) on substrates that attenuated seismic
signals (soil and rocks), but other behaviors showed no differences. Taken together, these results
suggest that combined visual/seismic components of multimodal displays may serve as “back-up
signals”, ensuring reception under different environmental conditions. Results also suggest that
male S. ocreata have the flexibility to compensate for environmental constraints by choosing
microhabitats with greater vibration conduction properties, and/or by increasing visual signals on
substrates where seismic communication is less effective.

Introduction
Animal signaling behaviors are selected for successful transmission and accurate reception
(Endler, 1992; Greenfield, 2002), and some species have evolved signals that function best in
specific environments (Elias et al., 2004; Hebets et al., 2008). Alternatively, animals may adjust
their signaling in response to dynamic environmental conditions, e.g., by increasing the volume
of their acoustic signal to overcome noise (Manabe et al., 1998; Scheifele et al., 2005; Egnor and
Hauser, 2006) or increasing the size and frequency of visual signals under windy conditions that
affect leafy vegetation (visual background noise) (Ord et al., 2007; Peters et al., 2008). In
addition, animals may compensate for environmental noise by adjusting signaling behavior to
different frequencies (Slabbekoorn and Peet, 2003), or changing call/bout duration (Brumm and
Slater, 2006). Studies of communication efficacy often focus on questions that pertain to a
single modality (e.g., visual: Smith and Evans, 2009; chemical: Ugolini et al., 2009; auditory:
Verfub et al., 2009) rather than using a combined approach. However, when focusing on
specifics of one modality, the dynamic interplay between modalities may be lost. This is
particularly true with animals that exhibit multimodal communication. Multimodal
communication is defined by signals in more than one modality used to convey one (or more)
message(s) simultaneously (Partan and Marler, 1999, 2005; Candolin, 2003; Hebets and Papaj,
2005; Bro-Jørgensen, 2010). A recent paper by Bro-Jørgensen (2010) suggests that spatiotemporal variation in the environment may explain the use of multiple signals.
The wolf spider, Schizocosa ocreata (Hentz), lives in a complex leaf litter
microenvironment and utilizes multimodal signaling (visual, seismic, chemical) to communicate
during courtship. Males detect female species identity and sexual maturity via chemical cues in
the silk (Roberts and Uetz, 2004, 2005), and respond with visual and seismic courtship signals to

14

attract the female’s attention and persuade her to accept him as a mate (Stratton and Uetz, 1983;
Uetz, 2000; Uetz and Roberts, 2002). Much research has been conducted with S. ocreata,
making it an ideal subject for a study on the use of multimodal signals in a fluctuating
environment (Hebets and Uetz, 1999; Uetz, 2000; Uetz and Roberts, 2002; Taylor et al., 2005;
Delaney et al., 2007; Lohrey et al., 2009; Uetz et al., 2009). Previous studies have explored the
multimodal aspect of communication, finding that in isolation, either visual or seismic cues alone
elicit female receptivity, but if both signals are available there is a synergistic receptivity
response by females (Scheffer et al., 1996; Uetz, 2000; Uetz and Roberts, 2002; Gibson and
Uetz, 2008; Uetz et al., 2009). In addition, studies show that a conspicuous male visual trait
(tufts of bristles found on adult male forelegs) is subject to both sexual selection and natural
selection (Persons and Uetz, 2005; Roberts and Uetz, 2008). Larger tufts are a conditionindicating trait preferred by females (McClintock and Uetz, 1996; Uetz 2000; Uetz et al., 2002;
Uetz and Roberts, 2002; Persons and Uetz, 2005; Uetz and Norton, 2007), but more quickly
detected by predators (Pruden and Uetz, 2004; Roberts et al., 2007; Roberts and Uetz, 2008).
Additional studies show females exhibit greater receptivity to males that court more vigorously,
even when visual or seismic signals are isolated, as male courtship vigor may reflect male
condition (Delaney et al., 2007; Gibson and Uetz, 2008).
Two recent studies of S. ocreata show plasticity in male courtship behavior in response to
environment. In one study (Taylor et al., 2005), males changed their courtship depending on the
light conditions, i.e., spiders in a dark environment did not use the conspicuous visual signal of
wave and arch. In a second study (Lohrey et al., 2009), male spiders ceased courtship in
response to sensory cues from avian predators, including shadows, seismic disturbances, and
airborne bird calls. In addition, the latter study revealed that spiders “hear” airborne sound via

15

substrate vibrations, suggesting that the type of substrate might be an important factor
influencing courtship behaviors. These studies suggest that spiders are capable of responding to
changes in the environment with context-dependent behavioral flexibility in courtship behavior.
The current study explores how environmental constraints of substrate composition affect
courtship signaling of S. ocreata, and how spiders might compensate for limits on seismic
signaling imposed by microhabitat substrates. The deciduous forest floor environment of these
spiders is complex, and includes patches of different substrates, e.g., leaf litter, wood/bark, soil,
and rock (Uetz and Dondale, 1979; Cady, 1984). Recent pilot studies (Uetz, Roberts, Clark,
Gibson, Gordon, unpubl.) indicate that the active space of S. ocreata seismic signals varies with
substrate, as signals transmit more effectively on leaf litter compared to other substrates (soil,
rock, wood), where the signal is sharply attenuated. Similarly, the seismic signals of jumping
spiders, Habronattus dossenus, (which also live in a primarily leaf litter environment and use
multimodal signaling), transmit best on leaf litter (Elias et al., 2004). In a different example, S.
retrorsa wolf spiders (which live in a pine needle environment) have seismic signals that
transmit best on pine needles, suggesting that they are adapted to the environment in which they
live (Hebets et al., 2008).
We tested the seismic transmission of S. ocreata on leaf litter, rock, soil and wood
substrates, to confirm earlier findings (Uetz, Roberts, Clark, Gibson, Gordon, unpubl.). We also
conducted mating trials with pairs of S. ocreata isolated on one of the four substrates found in
their environment (litter, rock, soil, wood). As our results indicated differential success rates
across substrates, we used a choice test to determine if spiders are able to detect differences
between them. We predicted that both sexes of spiders would prefer the substrate most effective
in transmitting and receiving seismic signals (leaf litter) compared with other substrates, and

16

spend more time on that surface. In addition, we predicted that spiders would change their
behavior (latency to initiate courtship signaling, signaling rate) when isolated upon a substrate
that constrained seismic signal transmission.

Materials and Methods
General rearing methods for S. ocreata
Spiders were collected in the field from forest habitat at the Cincinnati Nature Center
(Rowe Woods near Milford, Clermont Co., OH) and from private forested land near New
Richmond OH, during fall 2006, 2007 and spring 2008, 2009. The spiders were allowed to
mature in the laboratory with a 13 h light:11 h dark cycle at 22oC and 60-70% relative humidity.
Spiders were housed in individual opaque round plastic containers (10 cm diameter, 8 cm depth),
with an ad libitum water supply, and fed at least one appropriately sized cricket twice a week.
To control for hunger, animals were fed a cricket the day before each experimental trial.

Mating Success on Different Substrates
Circular mating arenas (22 cm in diam.) were constructed from flower pots; each contained
one substrate type (leaf, wood, soil, rock) embedded in or placed upon a soil base. Prior to the
trial, unmated females were placed in the arenas overnight to lay silk to initiate male courtship.
The females were removed, allowed to hydrate, and then placed back into the arenas to acclimate
for 1 min. Unmated males were placed on the opposite side of the arena and allowed to court for
one hour. We used 15 pairs of spiders per substrate and spiders were used only once. The trials
were videotaped from above and stopped if/when copulation occurred. Videotapes were scored
for latency of males to initiate courtship and latency to copulate. In addition, female behaviors

17

were quantified, including likelihood and latency of orient/approach and receptive behaviors
(slow pivot, tandem leg extend, and settle) (Uetz et al., 2009). Latency of behaviors was
analyzed using a one-way ANOVA with substrate as the primary factor. A chi-squared
contingency analysis of frequency of occurrence of behaviors was used to test for independence
of behavior and substrate.

Seismic Signal Transmission on Different Substrates
The seismic transmission/attenuation properties of male courtship signals on the substrates
(leaf, wood, soil, rock) were tested in the 22 cm diam. circular mating arenas (above). Seismic
signals from a courting male (< 10 cm away) were recorded via a Polytec laser vibrometer
(Model: LDV-100, Polytec) and processed through an Oros noise and vibration analyzer (Model:
OR24, Dulles, VA, USA) creating a .WAV file. Simultaneous video recordings were made
using a Panasonic video camera (Model: PV-GS300, Panasonic Corporation of North America,
Secaucus, NJ). We used Raven Pro 1.3 (Cornell Laboratory of Ornithology, Ithaca, NY, USA)
to generate the average power in dB across all frequencies from the WAV file. We averaged
values for three 10 s intervals of courtship of each male, from at least 9 individuals per substrate,
using 3 samples of each substrate. Results were then analyzed with a one-way ANOVA with
substrate as the main factor of interest.

Substrate Choice
A choice arena was created by connecting four flower pots (10 x 10 x 11cm, L x W x H),
each filled with soil and topped (1-2 cm) with the same four substrates from the spider’s
environment (leaf, wood, soil, rock). The arenas were arranged in a randomized order and

18

surrounded by an acetate plastic barrier. Trials began by placing the test spider in a neutral zone
in the center of the arena to acclimate under a glass vial for one minute; the spiders were then
released and allowed to wander freely around the arena for one hour. The test spiders included
unmated adult male spiders (n=13) and unmated adult female spiders (n=9). Males were tested
without female silk present (“wandering males”) on the first day and the following day with
female silk present, laid as dragline silk overnight (“courting males”). The trials were
videotaped from above and later scored. We recorded the number of visits to each substrate, as
well as the duration of each visit. Spiders that did not visit all four substrates were excluded
from analysis (Persons and Uetz, 1996). The number of visits to each substrate by female and
male spiders (without silk) was first analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with substrate and sex
as the main factors of interest, using JMP ver. 7.0 (SAS Institute). A second analysis of courting
(silk-exposed) males was done with a one-way ANOVA with substrate as the main factor of
interest. Subsequent analyses used one-way ANOVA to analyze the mean visit duration and
mean proportion of total trial time (log or arc sin-transformed to normalize the data if needed)
spent on each substrate.

Male Courtship on Different Substrates
Individual unmated males were tested in square 10 cm arenas (flower pots filled with soil
topped with one substrate: leaf, wood, soil, or rock) in a repeated measures design over four
days, with each male experiencing one substrate per day in a randomized order. Males (n=21)
were placed for 10 min in the arena. The arena contained female silk laid over the previous 12 hr
to stimulate male courtship. Since male response to female silk can vary due to the age,
condition and reproductive state of the female (Norton & Uetz 2005; Roberts & Uetz 2005; Uetz

19

& Norton, 2007; Moskalik & Uetz, unpubl.), males were paired with the same female for each
trial and females were fed a cricket each day to keep feeding status the same. Trials were video
recorded using a Sony digital 8mm video camcorder (New York, NY, USA) and later scored for
visual behaviors using The Observer (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, The
Netherlands). Male courtship behaviors were scored as in previous studies (Delaney et al.,
2007). Courtship behaviors included: “wave and arch” (the spider raises the first leg, lifting the
tarsus towards and above the cephalothorax) and “jerky tap” (body bounces to the ground and
tapping with the legs) where the male is considered to be most actively courting. Other
behaviors that were also scored but not considered part of courtship included: stationary,
locomotion, grooming and chemoexplore. Courtship behavior data were analyzed using a
repeated measure MANOVA with substrate as the main factor of interest, using JMP ver. 7.0
(SAS Institute, Cary, NC, U.S.A). In addition, we scored the frequency of courtship and
analyzed the data with a chi-squared contingency analysis to test for independence of substrate
and presence/absence of courtship.

Results
Mating Success on Different Substrates
All males initiated courtship under 1 min, with no significant difference in latency among
substrates (F3,56=0.85, p=0.47). Males had greater mating success (over 85%) on leaf litter
compared to the other substrates (~30%) (G3=14.85, p=0.002) (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, for males
successful in mating, there was no significant difference in latency to copulate among substrates,
with the mean time under 5min (F3,25=0.519, p=0.78) (Fig. 1B).

20

Females exhibited significant differences among substrates in latency to orient/approach
the male, with shorter times on leaf litter (F3, 28=3.127, p=0.044) (Fig. 1B), although there was no
difference in the likelihood of orient/approach to the male (G3=0.44, p=0.93) (Fig. 1A). Females
were significantly more likely to exhibit receptivity on leaf litter (G3=10.158, p=0.017) (Fig. 1A)
but they did not display receptivity more quickly (F3, 19=0.807, p=0.51) (Fig. 1B). Of those trials
in which spiders mated, all females showed receptivity on leaf litter, while on other substrates at
least 25% of the females did not show any receptivity but still mated. While there was no
significant difference among substrates for likelihood of female aggression (G3=6.39, p=0.09),
there were no aggressive behaviors exhibited on leaf litter; when behaviors were pooled as
occurring on leaf and non-leaf substrates, female spiders were significantly more aggressive on
the non-leaf substrates (G3=5.69, p=0.002).

Seismic Signal Transmission on Different Substrates
Seismic male courtship signals consist of percussive and stridulation elements (Fig. 2A).
These signals transmit best on leaf litter, as measured by average power (F = 2129; p <0.0001)
(Fig. 2B). Only a faint level of the seismic courtship signal could be detected on wood and soil,
and there was no detectable signal on rock. Analysis of the signal on leaf litter shows most of
the signal energy is below 2 kHz. Interestingly, some substrates appear to filter out one
component or another of the seismic signal; i.e., some percussive signals can be detected on
wood, while some stridulation signals can be detected on soil.

21

Substrate Choice
The number of visits per trial was not significantly different among substrates or between
sexes (substrate: F3,80 =0.34, p=0.795; sex: F1,80 =3.26, p=.075; substrate*sex: F3,80 =0.25,
p=0.862) (Fig. 3A) although females on average made fewer visits than males to all substrates
(Fig. 3A). Courting males also showed no significant difference in the number of visits to each
substrate (F3,48=0.18, p=0.912) (Fig. 3A). Both wandering and courting males spent significantly
more time per visit on leaf litter (wandering males F3,48=5.29; p=0.003, courting males:
F3,48=5.34, p=0.003) (Fig. 3B). However, there was no significant difference in mean female
visit duration among substrates (F3,32=0.43, p=0.94). These data concur with the observation that
in the wild, males move more often than females (Cady, 1984), and therefore males would be
expected to make more, shorter visits to all substrates in this lab experiment. Combining number
of visits and visit duration within a trial, the mean proportion of total trial time for both females
and males (wandering and courting) was greatest on leaf litter (females: F3,32=3.42, p=0.029;
wandering males: Wilcoxon rank sums X2 = 16.32, p=0.001, courting males: Wilcoxon rank
sums X2 = 13.62, p=0.0035) (Fig. 3C).

Male Courtship on Different Substrates
Males isolated on individual substrates (leaf, wood, soil, rock) exhibited courtship with
equal likelihood (G3=0.05, p=0.306), and showed no difference in latency to begin courtship
(F3,10=3.31, p=0.066), initiating signaling on average within the first minute. During the trials,
there was no difference among substrates in the vigor of courtship, e.g., the number or mean
duration of bouts of “jerky tap” (the active state of courtship), the number of body bounces, or
the number of leg taps during jerky tap (Table 1). However, spiders exhibited significantly more

22

wave and arch displays on substrates known to attenuate their seismic signal (F3,10=4.8, p=0.001)
(Table 1, Fig. 4).

Discussion
In this study, we found that different environmental substrates affect S. ocreata seismic
signaling, and that some substrates effectively eliminate successful transmission of signals in the
seismic modality. Comparison of courtship communication behaviors on natural substrates that
affect transmission of spider seismic courtship signals revealed differences in several aspects of
the mating process, including choice of substrate by both males and females, male courtship
behaviors, female receptivity and mating success. Not only did S. ocreata of both sexes adjust
visit duration, with longer visits to the substrate most effective in conducting seismic signals
(leaf litter), but males also adjusted signaling behavior when on non-conductive substrates (soil,
rock) to employ more visual signals. One possible interpretation might be that males recognize
microhabitats and increase overall courtship effort to compensate. However, while visual signals
were increased, there was not an overall increase in courtship vigor, as active courtship behaviors
(jerky tap) did not change in duration or frequency on different substrates. Previous studies
found that during courtship of females, males display the tufts in a “wave and arch” behavior
more often than when signaling in male-male interactions (Delaney et al., 2007), indicating that
the trait is associated with courtship behavior.
Given the importance of substrate in mating success, selection for signaling efficacy might
therefore be expected to favor multimodal communication, as well as substrate recognition
and/or behavioral flexibility in signaling as well. Seismic signaling is considered the ancestral
(plesiomorphic) signaling channel for the family Lycosidae as well as the genus Schizocosa

23

(Stratton, 2005), although the relative use of multimodal communication has evolved
independently multiple times in this genus, and varies in the ocreata clade of Schizocosa wolf
spiders (Hebets and Uetz, 1999; Uetz, 2000; Uetz and Roberts, 2002; Hebets et al., 2006;
Hebets, 2008; Uetz et al., 2009). Variation in multimodal signaling is associated with the
presence or absence of visual displays and secondary sexual characteristics (pigmentation,
foreleg tufts) on the males, which enhance visual signals (Hebets and Uetz, 2000; Uetz 2000;
Uetz and Roberts, 2002; Stratton, 2005; Uetz et al. 2009). For example, in S. ocreata, males
have large foreleg tufts that enhance their visual signaling (e.g., leg tapping, wave and arch
displays), while in the sister taxon, S. rovneri, males do not have tufts and do not exhibit visual
signaling with the legs. Variation in reliance on different aspects of multimodal courtship
signaling in these two sibling species may be explained in part by the environments in which
they live. The environment of S. ocreata (with visual and seismic components) is a physically
complex forest litter, which has some substrates that attenuate seismic signals, while S. rovneri
(with primarily seismic components) inhabits a relatively simple flat flood plain litter which
transmits seismic signals more effectively over greater distances (Scheffer et al., 1996; Uetz et
al., 2009).
Interest in multimodal signaling has resulted in numerous hypotheses concerning the
origin, nature, and content of multimodal signaling (Hasson, 1991; Moller and Pomiankowski,
1993; Pomiankowski and Iwasa, 1993; Iwasa and Pomiankowski, 1994; Johnstone, 1995, 1996;
Rowe, 1999; Partan and Marler, 2005; Hebets and Papaj, 2005; Bro-Jørgensen, 2010): (1)
signals in different sensory modes contain the same information and/or may convey the same
message (redundant signals, backup signals); (2) signals in different sensory modes each provide
different information in the component modes (multiple messages); (3) signals in different

24

sensory modes contain information intended for different receivers or is used in different
contexts (multiple receivers); (4) multiple simultaneous signals result in a new message
(emergent message); (5) signals in different sensory modes are not equally reliable, but are
retained owing to relative cost and benefit (unreliable signal hypothesis); (6) signals in multiple
modes may enhance signal detection, discrimination or learning (alerting signals, enhancers,
amplifiers). In addition, a related hypothesis pertaining specifically to airborne bird song
suggests that compensation for signal loss caused by constraints of the environment can result in
changes in signaling behavior (acoustic adaptation hypothesis) (Ey and Fischer, 2009).
The use of multimodal signaling of S. ocreata wolf spiders supports several of the
previously mentioned hypotheses. Increasing visual signaling may ensure reception of the
message on substrates that effectively block seismic signaling. This behavior may fall under the
“signal enhancer” and “backup signal” hypotheses (Partan and Marler, 2005; Hebets and Papaj,
2005; Bro-Jørgensen, 2010), or may serve as an alerting signal to females, since the seismic
portion of the message is not being transmitted. This may explain the lack of a difference in
latency to mate when spiders successfully mated on seismic attenuating substrates.
Alternatively, increased use of the visual wave and arch signal may be an additional means of
highlighting courting male quality (amplifier)—as they expose the condition-dependent visual
trait of the leg tufts when the condition-dependent seismic component is obscured. In all these
cases, visual displays and tufts are potentially costly, as predators also use those cues (Pruden
and Uetz, 2004; Roberts and Uetz, 2008), and similar display behaviors are energetically costly
in S. ocreata and other spider species (Kotiaho et al., 1998; Cady, Delaney, Uetz unpubl.).
Given the potential fitness costs, an increase in visual signaling on different substrates suggests
an added means of conveying the message, ensuring backup signals. These results also extend

25

the acoustic adaptation hypothesis, which suggests animals adjust signaling to fit the current
environment (Ey and Fischer, 2009). While this hypothesis is traditionally used as an
explanation for birds adjusting auditory signals it can be extended to multimodal signalers of all
taxa as a means of compensating for environmental constraints in other modalities (i.e. visual).
An important finding of this research is that spiders are able to detect differences between
substrates in their environment and compensate for them through behavioral means. With regard
to how spiders might discern that they are on a non-conductive substrate, we can only speculate.
Results of previous studies show spiders can ‘hear’ airborne sounds as vibrations on a substrate
(Lohrey et al., 2009). Therefore substrates might differ in ambient noise, which would be
perceived clearly on leaf litter but less so on the other substrates (Gordon & Uetz, pers. obs.).
Furthermore, spiders would have increased ability to perceive their seismic environment on leaf
litter—whether for ambient noise, predators, prey detection or mating communication. Barth
(2002) has shown that the slit sensillae found on spiders legs are sensitive to deformations of
only a few Angstroms (10-8 cm), which suggests some have the sensory capability to detect very
sensitive levels of sounds.
Although males use more visual signals on substrates that attenuate their seismic signal, the
success rate of copulation was significantly higher on leaf litter, which transmits seismic signals
most effectively. Indeed, females oriented/approached the males more quickly and were more
likely to show receptive behaviors on leaf litter. While previous studies show that visual signals
alone are sufficient to elicit female receptivity (Scheffer et al., 1996; Uetz, 2000; Uetz and
Roberts, 2002), our study along with other recent research (Uetz et al., 2009) suggests a possible
synergistic interaction between seismic and visual signals. These findings show that spiders are
capable of an adaptive feedback response; i.e. if one signaling channel (seismic) is perceived as

26

blocked, they compensate for constraints by increasing signaling in another channel (visual).
This conditional plasticity in signaling behavior, combined with integration of visual and seismic
elements into multimodal signals, is a means to ensure reception of courtship messages.

Chapter 3:

Environmental Interference: impact of natural and anthropogenic noise on mating success
in the wolf spider, Schizocosa ocreata

Shira D. Gordon
George W. Uetz

University of Cincinnati, OH
Department of Biological Sciences
614A Rieveschl Hall
Cincinnati, OH 45221-0006

(Formatted for submission to Behavioral Ecology)

40

Abstract
Acoustic noise is abundant in the environment, often interfering with animal
communication. Animals cope with noise interference in a variety of ways, including raising
their signal volume (the Lombard effect), changing the pattern, frequency or duration of signal,
or changing the time of day when they call. We tested the effects of three different types of
acoustic noise on substratum-borne seismic communication and mating success in the wolf
spider, Schizocosa ocreata. We used artificially created white noise to simulate anthropogenic
noise, as other studies have done. In addition, we used recorded bird calls, which indicate the
presence of a known predator of wolf spiders (the Northern cardinal Cardinalis cardinalis) and
cicada calls (mixed Magicicada spp.), which represent neither predator nor prey but noisy
callers; both bird and cicada calls are out of the spider courtship frequency range. We found
spiders respond to these diverse types of environmental noise differently. Although white noise
and cicada calls did not affect male signaling, there was a longer latency to initiate male
courtship with bird calls. Females oriented toward males more often with white noise than no
noise but showed no change in orientation behavior with bird or cicada calls. Both white noise
and bird calls reduced receptivity of females and resulted in fewer mating events, while cicada
calls had no effect. Our data suggest that ground-dwelling wolf spiders are sensitive to different
types of airborne noises in the environment and have the ability to differentially respond to the
three types tested here.

Introduction
Studies of communication have explored the potential costs of environmental interference
from acoustic noise on senders and receivers. In this context, “noise” can be broadly defined as

41

sound occurring simultaneously with a signal, making perception of the signal and detection
more difficult (Rabin and Greene, 2002; Brumm and Slabbekoorn, 2005). Acoustic noise is
abundant in the environment, including anthropogenic city sounds, natural abiotic sounds (e.g.,
waterfalls), and biotic sounds (e.g., noisy animal neighbors), raising the basic background
decibel level in natural as well as human-dominated areas (Brumm and Slabbekoorn, 2005;
Barber et al., 2009). Environmental noise may affect the ability to find mates (Bee and
Swanson, 2007; Barber et al., 2009), and ultimately reproductive success of individual species
(Habib et al., 2007; Swaddle and Page, 2007), as well as species richness in the community
(Warren et al., 2006; Barber et al. 2009; Francis et al., 2009). However, animals may also adjust
their behavior in the presence of noise, e.g., by raising the decibel level of sounds they produce
(the Lombard effect), altering the duration or frequency at which they produce acoustic calls,
changing the length of calling, or shifting the time of day when calling (i.e., Gerhardt, 1983;
Brumm and Slabbekoorn, 2005; Patricelli and Blickley, 2006; Warren et al., 2006). There are
limitations on which behaviors animals can adjust based on their size or signaling options. For
example, some birds that signal with low sound frequencies may be unable to adjust the
frequency of their calls to compensate for noise (Hu and Cardoso, 2010), and some anurans are
unable to adjust their call structure frequency or temporal pattern (Lengagne, 2008).
Animals using seismic signaling must also cope with noise. Similar to acoustic signaling,
seismic or vibrational signaling is a common means to communicate in close proximity (e.g.,
arthropods on a single plant) or over long distances (e.g., large mammals) (Barth, 2002; Cocroft
and Rodriguez, 2005; O’Connell-Rodwell et al., 2001; Hill, 2009). In particular, arthropods rely
heavily on seismic (vibrational) signaling as their small size is not always conducive to efficient
auditory or visual displays (Greenfield, 2002; Virant-Doberlet and Cokl, 2004; Cocroft and

42

Rodriguez, 2005). Environmental interference with seismic communication may result from the
substrate, animal movement nearby, wind, other signaling animals, and other noises from the
auditory channel that cross the air-substrate boundary (Randall and Lewis, 1997; Cocroft and
Rodriguez, 2005; Rabin et al 2006; Hill, 2009; Mazzoni et al., 2009). For example, the wolf
spider Schizocosa ocreata responds to airborne bird calls conducted as seismic signals when on a
conductive substrate (paper) but not when on a vibration-attenuating substrate (granite) (Lohrey
et al., 2009).
Most studies of animal responses to noise have involved vertebrates, in particular birds,
because of the number and variation of songs and their amenability to operant experimental
learning approaches (Manabee et al., 1998; Dingle et al., 2008; Brumm et al., 2009; Halfwerk
and Slabbekoorn, 2009). Primates, marine mammals, and anurans also are well represented in
research studies (Scheifele et al., 2005; Egnor and Hauser, 2006; Bee and Swanson, 2007).
Fewer studies have tested how noise influences behavioral responses of arthropods (Polajnar and
Cokl, 2008; Mazzoni et al., 2009), despite much research on acoustic behavior and their ability
and sensitivity to receive sounds and vibrations (Hoy and Robert, 1996; Keil, 1997; Yager, 1999;
Barth, 2002; Hennig et al., 2004).
This study examines the effects of environmental noise interference in the form of natural
noises (birds and cicadas) and artificial white noise (to simulate anthropogenic city noise), at
several points in the signaler-receiver interaction during courtship and mating communication of
the wolf spider, Schizocosa ocreata (Hentz). Specifically, we test hypotheses that noise
negatively affects male signaling behavior, female reception of signals, female receptivity
response, and ultimately mating success. In addition to mating trials, we examined individual
male behaviors with/without white noise as well as during the onset of white noise, to identify

43

differences in male courtship behaviors (e.g., Brumm et al., 2009; Halfwerk and Slabbekoorn,
2009; Pohl et al., 2009). We used avian calls, as they indicate a predator presence previously
shown to suppress male courtship activity when played to courting spiders (Lohrey et al., 2009).
Finally, we tested the effects of periodic cicada calls on mating behaviors. These animals pose
no threat nor do they indicate the possibility of prey (Stölting et al., 2004), but do constitute a
loud source of noise (in years when they occur) during the S. ocreata breeding season. The main
frequency of both the bird calls and cicada calls is out of the range of seismic signaling of the
spiders and so any effects would be due to the sound content rather than signal obstruction as
with white noise.

Materials and Methods
Study species: Schizocosa ocreata
The brush-legged wolf spider, S. ocreata, lives in deciduous forests maturing and breeding
during the spring. Courtship of male wolf spiders begins when males encounter dragline silk of
mature females, which contains chemical cues sufficient to elicit male courtship even with the
female absent (Roberts and Uetz, 2005). This enables testing of male courtship independent of
female behaviors. Male courtship signals include a set of stereotypical behaviors that can be
quantified for number, duration, and latency of occurrence. These multimodal behaviors include
both low-frequency seismic signals (under 2kHz), produced by percussion (body bounces with
chelicerae striking the substrate), stridulation of the palps (Rovner, 1975), and visual cues (leg
waves/arches, body bounces, and taps) (Uetz and Denterlein, 1979; Gibson and Uetz, 2008; Uetz
et al., 2009). Male behavioral responses, while stereotypical, are not entirely fixed, as lighting
conditions and cues from avian predators (shadows, seismic disturbances, short airborne bird

44

calls) have been shown to affect signaling (Taylor et al., 2005; Lohrey et al., 2009). Females
may orient to the male and respond with receptivity displays (Uetz and Denterlein, 1979;
Scheffer et al., 1996) or aggression, potentially eating the male (Persons and Uetz, 2005). Signal
isolation studies illustrate the female’s willingness to mate with cues from only one modality
(visual or seismic) and a synergistic effect if both are present (Scheffer et al., 1996; Uetz, 2000).
Successful communication ultimately results in mating. In addition, females are more likely to
mate with males courting at faster rates (Delaney et al., 2007).

General rearing methods for S. ocreata
Spiders were collected from the deciduous forest habitat at the Cincinnati Nature Center
Rowe Woods (Clermont Co., OH; 39º07’28.00N, 84º15’00.0W) during spring 2008, 2009, and
2010. Spiders were allowed to mature in the laboratory with a 13 h light:11 h dark cycle at 22oC
and 60-70% relative humidity. Spiders were housed in individual opaque round plastic
containers (10 cm diameter, 8 cm depth), with an ad libitum water supply, and fed 1-2
appropriately sized crickets (Acheta domesticus) twice a week. To control for hunger, spiders
were fed one cricket the day before each experimental trial.

Sound playback
Three different types of environmental sounds were used (white noise, cicada calls, bird
song) (Fig. 1). White noise was generated using Matlab (The Mathworks, Natick, MA) at
different volume levels above the background (10 dB, 20dB, and 30dB) spanning the spectrum
of male courtship signals and beyond (0-4kHz) (Fig. 1B). Cicada (mixed species - Magicicada
septendecim, M. cassini, and M. septendecula) calls were recorded from the environment using

45

an omnidirectional Sennheiser microphone (Model: ME64, powered by K6-C) on a Marantz
recorder (Model: PMD660) and measured for decibel level with a sound level meter (Fisher,
Model: 11-661-6A) during the mass emergence of cicadas in southwest Ohio during the spring
of 2008. Cicada sounds (mixed species, predominately Magicicada septemdecim) were played
back at natural levels (~80dB, approximately 20dB above background noise) in lab experiments.
Northern cardinal (Cardinalis cardinalis) bird songs (approx. 1.5min of natural singing) were
obtained from the Borror Laboratory of Bioacoustics, The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH,
all rights reserved (codes: 13690, 3881, 757), as in a previous study (Lohrey et al., 2009). All
trials were conducted in a sound/vibration isolation room with a Dell multi-media speaker
(model: 0200DF) approx. 90cm above the arena. In addition, substrate vibrations were recorded
with a laser Doppler vibrometer (Polytech, LDV-100), monitoring any overlap in courtship and
noise signals (Fig. 1 C).

Mating trials
In round 15cm diameter plastic arenas, female spiders were placed overnight on filter paper
(to lay silk, which elicits male courtship). Females were removed after at least 12hr, allowed to
hydrate for an hour and reintroduced to their container. After a 1 min acclimation time (females
in the arena with males also in the room with the noise), males were introduced onto the filter
paper bottom arenas on the opposite side from the females’ location. The trials were run for 1
hour or until mating occurred. Each treatment ran with 20-24 pairs of spiders. Animals were
used at peak reproductive age (Females: 8-24 days post maturity; Males: 8-27 days post
maturity) (Norton and Uetz, 2005), chosen randomly within their age group, and never used in
any previous trials.

46

Male courtship with white noise
Male courtship behaviors were measured with and without white noise in two experiments.
In the first experiment, males were exposed to silence or constant levels of noise at 20dB above
background to observe if there was an overall difference in courtship behaviors with noise.
Males were given female silk to elicit courtship and allowed to court for 15min. Trials were
videotaped and later scored using The Observer (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen,
The Netherlands) at 1/3 speed for the number, total, and average duration of behaviors, including
courtship (jerky tap, taps and bounces), waves and arch, chemoexplore, groom, walking, and
stationary. In the second test males were first exposed to silence then noise after a 5 min
acclimation period (10dB, 20dB, or 30dB above background); the time spent in courtship was
scored for 30s before and after the onset of noise.

Trial analysis
All trials were videotaped and later scored for latency and occurrence of male courtship,
female orientation and receptivity, and mating. Trials were analyzed with the JMP statistical
package (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, U.S.A) using ANOVA or chi-squared tests with sound type as
the independent variable. If needed, data was log or square root transformed to become
normalized. Nonnormal data were analyzed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Results
White Noise
White noise overlapped with male courtship signal (Fig. 1). Over a 15min trial, there were
no significant differences in male courtship behaviors between noise treatments (no noise and

47

20dB constant white noise) (Table 1). In a second set of trials (white noise turned on mid-trial),
males reduced courtship significantly more often when comparing percentage of time spent in
jerky tap behavior 30s before and after the onset of noise (Fig. 2). In addition, cessation of
courtship was dependent on the level of noise, as males with 10dB of white noise showed no
difference in behavior, while those at 20dB and 30dB showed significantly reduced courtship
behaviors (10dB: t = 0.357, p = 0.73; 20dB: t = -5.034, p < 0.0001; 30dB: t = -3.6356, p =
0.0027) (Fig. 2).
White noise significantly affected mating of spiders, with increasing noise levels having
lower proportions of successful mating (G1,3 = 8.081, p = 0.045) (Fig. 3A). However, increased
white noise levels did not affect the proportion of females responding with orientation or
receptivity (orientation: G1,3 = 5.0, p = 0.1718; receptivity: G1,3 = 1.292, p = 0.7314) (Fig. 3A).
When data for all noise levels were pooled (noise or no noise) and compared, a significantly
higher proportion of females oriented to males in the presence of noise compared to no noise (no
noise: 36%, noise: 62%; G = 4.132, p = 0.042), while the proportion of females showing
receptivity displays did not change (no noise: 73%, noise: 76%; G = 0.101, p = 0.75).
There was no significant difference among treatments for the latency of males to begin
courtship (Fig. 3B). Female response latency data were adjusted for the initiation of male
courtship (by subtracting the latency of initiation of male courtship). Adjusted female latency to
orientation, receptivity, and mating were significantly longer with increasing noise levels (lat.
orient: Wilcoxon rank-sum: 7.86, p = 0.0051; lat. receptive: Wilcoxon rank-sum: 7.97, p =
0.0047; lat. mate: Wilcoxon rank-sum: 8.46, p = 0.0036) (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, while not
significantly different among treatments, there were 4 cases of cannibalism with some level of
noise present but no cases of cannibalism in the absence of noise.

48

Bird Calls
Male courtship used a lower frequency than the bird calls (Fig. 1). Males took twice as
long to initiate courtship when in the presence of playback of Cardinal calls (silence: 21 ± 4.9 s,
bird call: 61 ± 12,4 s; F = 4.80. p = 0.034) (Fig. 4B). Cardinal calls reduced mating success and
increased latency to mate for the pairs that did mate (% mated: G = 7.33. p = 0.0068; latency: F
= 7.41, p = 0.01) (Fig. 4). As with white noise, latency to female behaviors was adjusted for
initiation of male courtship by subtracting the latency to courtship from the female’s behavior.
Females showed no difference in the proportion that orientated or latency to orient with and
without Cardinal calls (% oriented: G = 0.036, p = 0.85; latency: F = 0.198, p = 0.66) (Fig. 4).
However, females were more receptive without bird calls present, with shorter latency to
receptivity (% receptive: G = 5.93, p = 0.015; latency to receptivity: F = 7.12, p = 0.013) (Fig.
4).

Cicada Calls
Male courtship signals were lower in frequency than the cicada calls recorded in the field
(Fig. 1). Cicada calls did not affect mating or any other associated behaviors of S. ocreata. No
significant differences were seen in the likelihood of or latency to mating events, male courtship,
female orientation, female receptivity, and aggressive displays (Fig. 5).

Discussion
Results of this study indicate that airborne environmental noise had a significant effect on
communication of epigeal wolf spiders. Increasing levels of airborne white noise reduced

49

mating success, as did bird calls (indication of a known predator of wolf spiders). Conversely,
despite their noise level, cicada calls had no apparent effect on communication or mating
success. These findings suggest that spiders perceived types of environmental noise and
responded accordingly. Interestingly, white noise and Cardinal calls affected communication
behaviors differently. Constant white noise did not effect male signaling, but spiders altered
behavior at the onset of noise. However, Cardinal calls affected male courtship behaviors by
increasing the latency for males to initiate courtship; these data suggest that males evaluate
environmental interference within their microhabitat during signaling.
Once the message is sent by males, females also behaved differently in the presence of
noise. With white noise, females re-oriented to the signaling male spider significantly more
often, while there was no significant difference in orientation when Cardinal calls were being
played. This implies that the signal message potentially lost some of its original content due to
masking or degradation but retained enough information to attract the female to identify the cue,
though with more difficulty. Orientation gave the females a chance to survey any visual cues or
improve reception of the seismic signals, as sensory information is weighted more strongly to the
front legs of spiders (Hergenröder and Barth, 1983). On the other hand, a signal such as a
Cardinal call did not block male seismic signals, which females receive and responded to (orient
to the male signal) with equal frequency and latency. In both types of noise, latency to female
receptivity increased with the level of noise, resulting in fewer matings. While both white noise
and Cardinal calls reduced mating success with longer latency and reduced receptivity, the basis
of reduction was different. Apparently, white noise made it more difficult to send and receive
messages. Bird calls did not hinder signal transmission, but did affected the behavior of the
animals—perhaps because of the perceived potential predation threat involved in the call. Our

50

results therefore suggest that wolf spiders have the capability of behavioral adjustment during
communication.
Mating and communication behaviors were not affected by cicada calls, although they
occur during the same time of year as the S. ocreata breeding season and could potentially add
noise to the communication of the wolf spiders. Cicada calls are known to affect the calling
behaviors of birds, as birds avoid singing higher pitched notes when cicadas are present (Dingle
et al., 2008). Because the cicada call is at a high frequency, and the spider courtship call is lower
frequency (Fig. 1), there is little overlap to block the spider signal. In addition, the spider signal
contains more structure than the cicada calls, which may compensate for signal loss and enable
recognition as with other animals (Endler, 1992). Moreover, the cicadas only constitute
interference in periodic years, while S. ocreata is an annual species.
Animals have developed many ways to cope with noise. Some animals have the ability to
change the components of the signal sent (e.g., switch songs, add syllables, change the sound
frequency, or increase the volume) (Patricelli and Blinkley, 2006; Halfwerk and Slabbekoorn,
2009; Warren et al., 2006). However, not all animals are able to adjust signaling in this manner.
In particular, arthropods often signal with sclerotized body parts in sound and frequency levels
that are scaled to their size (Bennet-Clark, 1998). In the case of S. ocreata, these spiders are not
able to adjust their stridulation sound frequency, even though the frequency they use to stridulate
effects female choice (Gibson and Uetz, 2008). The spiders potentially could adjust which
signals are used (add more visual or seismic components) but no such behavioral changes were
seen with and without noise. Previous work illustrates that spiders use more visual signaling
with environmental interference from substrates that attenuate seismic signals (Gordon and Uetz,
submitted). Behavioral plasiticity allows spiders to be flexible in mating tactics, and will

51

become more important for mating success and survival as the environment continues to rapidly
change with human activity. For example, our study shows that spiders reduce courtship activity
when a predatory bird call was played. The ability to reduce courtship when a threat is present
may have resulted from those males that could not adjust behavior being eaten before mating
while those that temporarily ceased courting were ultimately successful.
The results from this study demonstrate behavioral plasticity in wolf spider communication
in response to environmental noise interference. Understanding plasticity in animal behavior in
response to changing noise levels is especially important, as the environmental conditions to
which animals are adapted are becoming more restricted in location and noisier as they are closer
to roadways and other city noises. Natural habitat is being fragmented and reduced, putting
animals in closer proximity and requiring them to signal while other animals also signaling. In
addition, the increase in anthropogenic noise is creating fewer unaffected habitats (Barber et al.,
2009). With increasing noise in the environment, communication becomes more difficult
(Brumm, 2006; Bee and Swanson, 2007; Pohl et al., 2009). Wollerman and Wiley (2002)
identify four possible outcomes of signaling in noisy environments: correctly responding to a
call, correctly ignoring the call, or responding or ignoring incorrectly. They go on to
differentiate between detection and discrimination showing that for anurans, discriminating
between calls is even more difficult than mere detection. Our study shows that white noise is a
detection challenge impacting reproductive success for spiders. White noise was used in this
study to emulate the various types of anthropogenic noises that are becoming more abundant in
our environment (motorized vehicles, planes, generators) (Sun and Narins, 2005; Habib et al.,
2007; Bee and Swanson, 2007; Barber et al., 2009; Francis et al., 2009). Wolf spiders can detect
airborne sounds as vibrations in the substrates, as demonstrated by Lohrey et al. (2009). We

52

demonstrate that these airborne sounds, translated to the substrate as vibrations recorded in this
study, can affect the behaviors of arthropods living in the leaf litter, by creating seismic
interference. Not only does any seismic interference affect behavior but the type of seismic
interference is relevant and important. Our study is among the first to demonstrate the effects of
noise on arthropods that use seismic communication.

Chapter 4:

Male courtship and the effects of seismic signals from multiple courting males in the wolf
spider Schizocosa ocreata

Shira D. Gordon
George W. Uetz

University of Cincinnati, OH
Department of Biological Sciences
614A Rieveschl Hall
Cincinnati, OH 45221-0006

(Formatted for submission to Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology)

69

Abstract
Signaling between two individuals is rarely isolated and may be received by both intended and
unintended receivers within a communication network, forcing animals to contend with signals
from other animals. As a consequence, the signaler may need to adjust behavior to best transmit
its signal. However, information obtained from other signaling animals (eavesdropping) also
may benefit the signaler. We tested the effects of seismic signaling cues from other males on
behavior of the wolf spider, Schizocosa ocreata, by measuring focal male behavior when
courting (in the presence of chemical cues from female silk) or not courting (no silk) with and
without seismic cues from other courting males. We found that when courting in the presence of
female silk and seismic signals from multiple stimulus males, focal males reduced the vigor of
their courtship (rate of percussive signals). The opposite was true for the focal males that were
not courting (i.e. without female silk cues present); seismic signals from other courting males
induced courtship of a focal male where the female silk cue was lacking. A courting male may
reduce vigor in an attempt to reduce interfering or jamming signals. Alternatively, as vigor can
be a condition-dependent trait, reduction of vigor may be a means to evaluate condition relative
to the other males, thereby saving energy. The initiation of courtship by the non-courting focal
male (without female cues) is an example of eavesdropping on cues not intended for the receiver.
Using such cues may be advantageous to males, as they may encounter or attract a potentially
cannibalistic female nearby before her silk triggers a courtship response. These data highlight
the importance of considering the communication network of animals when trying to understand
their communication behaviors and systems.

70

Introduction
Animal communication involves the sending and receiving of signals intended as
territorial, mate attraction, or alarm calls between individuals (Endler, 1993; Bradbury and
Verhencamp, 1998; Partan and Marler, 2002; Rendall et al., 2009). The individuals involved
typically are not communicating in isolation, as the message is broadcast in public domain as
part of a communication network (McGregor, 1993, 2005). Within the network, senders have to
compete with the signals of other animals, in particular those of conspecifics (Rendall et al.,
2009). These calls are often noisy and can potentially confuse receivers by creating interference
via masking or jamming the signal, thereby impeding perception of the message (Gerhardt and
Klump, 1988; Bee and Micheyl, 2008; Groning and Hochkirch, 2008). Some animals will
intentionally jam rival signals or the response signal to their call (e.g., Australian bushcrickets:
Hammond and Bailey, 2003; leafhoppers: Mazzoni et al., 2009), while others will adjust their
signaling to avoid jamming each other (e.g., chaffinches: Brumm and Slater, 2007; reviewed in:
Egnor et al., 2007).
In addition, broadcasting signals in a network may reach other, unintended receivers also
located in the environment, often referred to as eavesdroppers (Peake, 2005). Eavesdropping
behavior is commonly thought of as an interspecific interaction where a predator or parasitoid
locates its prey or host by locating signals (Zuk and Kolluru, 1998; Djemai et al., 2004).
However, conspecific animals will eavesdrop to evaluate the relative condition of others during
aggressive displays (reviewed in: Peake, 2005; Dabelsteen, 2005) or will eavesdrop to help
identify the location of mates to intercept, or ‘sneak’, mating (Kiflawi and Gray, 2000;
Hammond and Bailey, 2003; Stoltz et al., 2008). For instance, poorer quality male Australian
bushcrickets are attracted to the courtship calls of higher quality males (Hammond and Bailey,

71

2003). Moving into close proximity with the high quality calling male, the lower quality male
may sneak copulation with the female also attracted to the call.
The role of competing signaling males in communication networks can have evolutionary
implications, as those successful at communicating within a competitive environment will be
more successful at mating. The wolf spider Schizocosa ocreata lives in a deciduous forest leaf
litter environment densely populated with males up to 4/m2 (Cady, 1984; Uetz and Roberts,
unpublished). In spring, these animals reach maturity for about a one month long mating season.
The male will court a mature female (or her silk) to persuade her to mate with him (Roberts and
Uetz, 2005). Male courtship is multimodal, with visual and seismic displays (Uetz, 2000; Uetz
and Roberts, 2002; Uetz et al., 2009). Females choose males based on these displays, with
choice criteria including vigor and rate of signaling behaviors, size of male secondary sexual
characteristics (tufts on legs), and sound frequency of seismic signals (Uetz and Roberts, 2002;
Delaney et al., 2007; Uetz and Norton, 2007; Gibson and Uetz, 2008). Male courtship signals
function primarily in female mate choice, because during male-male aggressive interactions, the
use of signals is less important than male size in deciding the winner (Delaney et al., 2007).
Whether males respond to signals of other courting males is unclear. Natural spacing of
males places them in close enough proximity to receive the signals of other males (Cady, 1984;
Uetz et al., unpublished). A study addressing eavesdropping of S. ocreata found evidence of
orientation to other courting males, but no evidence of social facilitation to initiate courtship by
the focal male (Roberts et al., 2006). However in that study, naïve focal males were only
exposed to a single courting male without the presence of female cues (silk). Subsequent studies
suggest that experience with a larger number of males may make a difference in the tendency to
eavesdrop (Clark and Uetz, unpublished).

72

Our study examines the effects of the social environment within the communication
network of S. ocreata on male courtship behavior. We approach this idea from two perspectives:
interference from multiple male signals and eavesdropping on other male signals. In the
interference study, we hypothesize that a courting male would adjust his signal when other males
court simultaneously (either to avoid interference or increase his signal to overcome the extra
noise). We also hypothesize that when the focal male had no cues from females (to stimulate
courtship) males would use eavesdropping on seismic courtship cues from multiple other
courting males as evidence of the presence of females, and subsequently begin their own
courtship. To find any change in the courtship behaviors of the focal male based on conspecifics
in the communication network, we focused on seismic cues, eliminating visual cues as well as
direct interaction between males.

Materials and Methods
General rearing methods for S. ocreata
Spiders were collected from the deciduous forest habitat at the Cincinnati Nature Center
Rowe Woods (Clermont Co., OH, 39º07’28.00N, 84º15’00.0W) during Fall 2008 and Spring
2010. Spiders were allowed to mature in the laboratory with a 13 h light:11 h dark cycle at 22oC
and 60-70% relative humidity. Spiders were housed in individual opaque round plastic
containers (10 cm diameter, 8 cm depth), with an ad libitum water supply, and fed at least one
appropriately sized cricket (Acheta domesticus) twice a week. To control for hunger, animals
were fed one cricket the day before each experimental trial.
Experimental design

73

Spiders were observed in round bottomless acetate cylinders (8cm diameter) used as
arenas. These were arranged with the focal male arena in the center, resting upon a velum
substrate connected to 3 other male arenas, separated by a distance of 2-3cm (Fig. 1). The velum
substrate was cut in a way to minimize reception of seismic signals between the courting
stimulus males, yet allow perception of their seismic signals by the focal male. All males were
visually and chemically isolated from each other so not to affect their behaviors. Trials were
conducted in a matched-pairs design where the focal male experienced either 3 courting males or
no courting males in a random order over two days. In the interference experiment, all males
were provided with filter paper that contained female silk (laid the night before) to stimulate
courtship. To control for female quality, the same female was used for trials when the male was
alone and with three other courting males. In the initiation experiment, the stimulus males were
given female silk, but the focal male was not exposed to female silk during the trial. In both
experiments, the focal male was placed in the arena after all stimulus males were observed to
exhibit courting. The trials lasted for 5 min and were videotaped for later analysis.

Data analyses
Videotapes of the focal male were scored using The Observer (Noldus Information
Technology, Wageningen, The Netherlands). Behaviors scored included several states:
stationary, chemo-explore, groom, walk, courtship (jerky tap), and various events: wave and
arch, body-bounce and leg taps. The number of times an animal did any of these behaviors was
recorded along with the latency to first performing the behavior and the mean/total time spent
doing it (where applicable). Trials were analyzed as matched pairs in the JMP statistical package
(SAS Institute, Cary, NC, U.S.A) with presence of additional males as the independent variable.

74

Data not meeting the assumption of normality were either normalized via log or square root
transformations, or tested with non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Behaviors that had
five animals or less responding for both treatments were excluded from analysis.

Results
Interference Experiment
Multiple males courting in the vicinity of the focal male affected the focal male’s courtship
behavior. The vigor of the focal male’s courtship, measured by the amount of body bounces and
tapping, was significantly lowered with the presence of other courting males (Body bounce: t =
2.12, p = 0.044, Tap: t = 4.41, p = 0.0002) (Fig. 2). There was no significant difference in the
latency to initiate courtship, or the amount of time in the courtship state between trials when
other males were present or absent (latency: Wilcoxon sign-rank = -7, p = 0.84; total time:
Wilcoxon sign-rank = 3.5, p = 0.93; mean time: Wilcoxon sign-rank = 7, p = 0.84). In addition,
when males courted alone, they performed more grooming behavior (Wilcoxon sign-rank = 14, p
= 0.016). No other behaviors varied between the two treatments (Table 1). While not explicitly
tested, there was no evidence of chorusing or synchronizing calls as signals sometimes
overlapped (Fig. 3).

Initiation Experiment
Focal males without female silk increased their courting behavior significantly in the
presence of seismic signals from three other courting males compared to when no other courting
males were present. They exhibited a higher number of bouts of courtship (jerky tap: Wilcoxon
sign-rank: 174, p <0.0001) as well as spent more total time in the courtship state (Wilcoxon sign-

75

rank: 171, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 4B). They increased the level of vigor within courtship bouts, as
measured by number of bounces, and exhibited a non-significant trend towards performing more
taps (Bounce: Wilcoxon sign-rank: 168, p = 0.0001; Tap: Wilcoxon sign-rank: 264, p = 0.16)
(Fig. 4A). Accordingly, the total amount of time the spiders spent walking was reduced with
other males present although there was no difference in the time they spent stationary (stationary:
Wilcoxon sign-rank: 125, p = 0.52; walking: Wilcoxon sign-rank: -398, p = 0.042) (Fig. 4B).
The mean amount of time spent in the courtship state did not vary between treatments (t = 0.19, p
= 0.85), but males spent significantly more time walking per bout then when alone (Wilcoxon
sign-rank: -447, p = 0.0013) (Fig. 4C). There was no difference in the latency to initiate any
behaviors (Table 2).

Discussion
Male S. ocreata responded to the presence of seismic courtship cues from neighboring
conspecific males, but responses varied depending on the context of sensory information at hand.
When the focal male was in the presence of female silk cues, seismic signals of other males
resulted in a reduction of courtship vigor--with fewer bounces by the focal male. Contrary to
expectations, there was not an increase in focal male courtship to compensate for the noise and
therefore signal over its interference level. When female cues were absent, other courting males
induced courtship of the focal male. In both of these experiments males responded to signaling
cues not intended for them, thereby eavesdropping on other male courtship behaviors. Together,
these studies show cues from eavesdropping can both interfere with and induce the same
behavior.

76

Based on work done with other animal systems, our findings on S. ocreata eavesdropping
behavior can be considered in three contexts: 1) increased signaling potential; 2) assessment of
other signalers; 3) obtaining new information (the traditional definition).

1) Increase signaling potential
Animals have many ways to increase their signaling potential, especially when other
signaling animals are considered. Sometimes animals will listen (or eavesdrop) to the calls of
other signaling animals and will respond by chorusing, synchronizing calling, intentionally
jamming with their own calls or reducing their signals to avoid signal overlap and interference.
There was no evidence of chorusing, synchronizing, or jamming behaviors in S. ocreata.
Instead, the focal male reduced courtship activity with cues from the other courting males.
Animals that chorus are often males competing with each other for females within a group
and chorus to minimize interference and maximize calling (Schwartz, 1987; Greenfield and
Schul, 2008). While S. ocreata spiders are competing for females, chorusing probably would not
increase reception of their signals. Spiders receive seismic signals with eight legs, not two ears,
which may affect directionality and interference. In addition, males use visual cues in a
multimodal display, creating an additional component that is not easily used in a chorus.
Animals that synchronize calls are either competing to be a lead signaling male, or are
competing with other groups of calling males to attract females to their group (Greenfield, 1994).
In S. ocreata, male and female spiders are in close proximity to each other during courtship and
courtship is persuasive rather than attractive, i.e. males are not trying to attract females from far
away. The active space of seismic signaling in these animals is limited to connected substrate,
and synchronizing calls to attract females would therefore seem unlikely. While the vigor of a

77

male’s display can change within a courtship bout (Gordon and Uetz, personal observation),
vigor reflects male condition (Gibson and Uetz, 2008) and so timing calls to produce a lead male
is a possibility. Females are more likely to mate with the first male they encounter (Clark and
Uetz, 1992; Scheffer et al., 1996). However, S. ocreata spiders do not have some of the key
elements suggested to make becoming in phase with each other easier (Greenfield, 1994); while
they do possess short calls, the calls are not at a regular rhythm (i.e., 1 to 3+ bounces in a row).
This flexibility in the amount of vigor used when calling suggests males may be able to
circumvent a lead calling male, which may be another reason why call synchrony is not
observed.
Animals will often jam signals to protect their own signal or the response signal from their
receiver (Hammond and Bailey, 2003), or alternatively jam in a mate or territory evaluation
(Tobias and Seddon, 2009). During courtship of S. ocreata, females are not known to give a
seismic response (aside from walking and movement noises), but instead give a visual and
sometimes a tactile response. Therefore, males cannot jam to block a seismic portion of the
intended female receiver’s response. However, this does not negate the possibility that signals
sent by different signaling males at the same time may jam each other, by blocking or decreasing
message detection/interpretation (Egnor et al., 2007; Groning and Hochkirch, 2008). Often to
cope with potentially jamming or interfering calls, animals will adjust their signals to avoid
overlap and jamming (Wasserman, 1977; Brumm and Slater, 2007), as overlapping calls are
usually considered less attractive (Schwartz et al., 2008). This behavior may be seen in our
experiments. When courting males are near other courting males their vigor decreases, as fewer
percussive elements are used ultimately reducing the amount of overlap in signaling. Active
courtship is energetically expensive (Kotiaho et al., 1998); decreasing vigor may save energy so

78

the spider can increase vigor when either other males are not around or for when the female is
located.

2) Assessment of other signalers
Animals will eavesdrop to assess signalers in the environment (Dabelsteen, 2005). Often,
this assessment is used for determining the relative status of individuals. For instance, before
engaging in aggressive displays, if animals observe an interaction between two individuals they
will treat the winner and loser of the fight differently (Oliveira et al., 1998; Freeman, 1987,
Earley and Dugatkin, 2005). Similarly, females may assess the condition of mates based on
nonrelated interactions they observe. In one such example, already paired female birds, (Parus
major), listen to territory defense of their mate, and based on his timing/calling ability are more
(or less) likely to make trips off of the territory to potentially seek an extra-pair partner (Otter et
al., 1999). In our study, one hypothesis explaining reduction of male courtship vigor is that focal
males may be evaluating their competition by eavesdropping. In this sense, reducing courtship
vigor enables more time for evaluation of neighbors. If the focal male can assess his own quality
via seismic signals, as female wolf spiders have been shown to do (Gibson and Uetz, 2008), then
he can evaluate whether the cost of signaling is worth the expense. However, the male continues
to court, as courtship duration showed no difference whether alone or with other courting males,
but the number of bounces decreases, increasing the inter-bounce interval. Similarly, a study of
fiddler crab visual signaling identifies longer inter-bout duration between waving in a group of
males, suggesting a reduction of vigor (McGregor et al., 2000).

79

3) Obtain new information
Eavesdropping is probably most commonly referred to as obtaining new information about
animals in the environment. It is in this context that predators or parasitoids eavesdrop on their
targets. However, conspecific animals also eavesdrop in this way (Kiflawi and Gray, 2000;
Hammond and Bailey, 2003; Stoltz et al., 2008). Males will sometimes obtain a ‘sneak’ mating
based on intercepting or eavesdropping on the signaling male. This occurs acoustically with
satellite male crickets that are attracted to calls of higher quality males and sneak a mating with
the female, who is also attracted to the call (Hammond and Bailey, 2003). Sneaking behavior
also occurs with visual eavesdropping, as some fish will observe the courtship of a larger male,
then sneak in between the male and female to release his sperm (Fu et al., 2001). In this sense,
we suggest that male S. ocreata might use courtship information of other males in the absence of
female cues to initiate their own courtship behaviors. Based on the courtship behaviors of the
other males, they may recognize that there is a female in the vicinity and act on it to increase
their chances of encountering the female as a potential mate. The mating system of S. ocreata is
monandrous and polygynous, where females typically only mate once but males will mate more
than once if given the opportunity (Norton and Uetz, 2005). In this sense, if additional males are
courting a female, the focal male may benefit from courting her as well.

Communication rarely occurs in isolation, as messages are broadcast in the public domain
as part of a communication network (McGregor, 1993, 2005). While signals from multiple
individuals might be considered background noise, eavesdropping can take many forms, some of
which are very subtle. Much work on eavesdropping is done on chorusing animals because they
are most certainly listening to the other animals signaling in their environment; these animals

80

include birds, anurans, and insects (in particular orthopterans and homopterans) (Gerhardt and
Klump, 1988; Greenfield, 1994; Bee and Swanson, 2007; Brumm and Slater, 2007; Greenfield
and Schul, 2008). Spiders, on the other hand, are not well studied in a social context. They do
not typically live in groups, with a few notable exceptions (D’Andrea, 1987; Uetz and Hieber,
1997; Avilés, 1997; Whitehouse and Lubin, 2005) and therefore, communication networks
among spiders have been studied minimally. Our study expands the literature on communication
networks and eavesdropping to include arachnids and seismic signaling. Male wolf spiders
clearly respond to seismic signaling cues not intended for them—i.e., eavesdropping on other
male courtship behaviors. However, this study is unique in that it demonstrates that
eavesdropping both inhibits and initiates the same behavior, suggesting behavior needs to be
considered within the communication network of the animal and in the context of other signals.

Conclusion
The research from this dissertation supports the conclusion that environmental
interference—in the forms of signal filtering and attenuation, various types of airborne noise, or
signals of neighboring conspecifics—affects seismic communication and mating success of the
wolf spider, S. ocreata. The work presented in this dissertation also demonstrates behavioral
plasticity, as studies show that these spiders have the ability to recognize different types of
environmental interference and adjust their signaling behavior accordingly. In addition, these
studies show that spiders can adjust their behavior to receive signals more clearly when needed.
Finally, the studies indicate how environmental interference from conspecifics can influence
eavesdropping behavior in the context of a communication network.
In order to understand animal responses to environmental noise interference during
communication, I focused on where animals might exhibit behavioral plasticity. In a diagram of
the four main aspects of animal communication (similar to previous diagrams e.g., Endler, 1993;
Partan and Marler, 2002; Bradbury and Vehrencamp, 1998), I illustrate the potential points of
behavioral modification between these aspects (Fig. 1a-d). Among acoustic noise-interference
studies, many test for variance in signaling behavior (a: 1 Æ 2) and accurate signaler location (c:
3 Æ 4) (Gerhardt and Klump, 1988; Brumm and Slater, 2006; Warren et al., 2006; Halfwerk and
Slabbekoorn, 2009). Understanding if/how/when signalers adjust behavior is vital to
understanding the effects of noise, but the consequences of their behavioral modifications should
be considered as well. Similarly, understanding receiver responses and ability to locate the
signaler (e.g., mate) is also key to understanding communication, but can only be used as
inferences as to what the outcome may be and not actually mating success. Fewer studies focus
on receiver modification of reception (b: 2 Æ 3) or actual communication outcome (d: 4 Æ 1).

96

Chapter 2 focused on substrate transmission properties and how substrate may interfere
with communication. Mating success was lower on substrates that did not transmit the seismic
courtship signal. Seismic analysis of the signal supports a large loss of transmission on non-leaf
substrates. These results can be explained using the communication diagram (Fig. 1). First, my
results suggest that both males and females recognized the differences between substrates (leaf,
wood, soil, rock) by choosing to spend more time on leaf litter. Male signalers adjusted their
signaling behavior to include more visual components when on substrates that do not transmit
their seismic signal as well (Fig. 2, 1 Æ2). Females did not adjust their reception behaviors; the
proportion of females that exhibited orientation behavior did not significantly vary (Fig. 2, 2 Æ
3). Females took longer to orient to the males on attenuating substrates indicating a potential
reduction of message transmission. However, spending more time on leaf litter may be a way to
increase reception. Next, a significantly lower proportion of females responded with receptivity
displays to the transmitted courtship message, which resulted in fewer mating events. When
females were receptive, there was no significant difference in the latency to the first receptive
behavior suggesting no change in female response behavior (Fig. 2, 3 Æ 4) but a lack of signal
reception of which to respond. Similarly, there was no significant difference in latency to mate
indicating that males received the female response (Fig. 2, 4 Æ 1). Together these results
suggest that the lower mating success stems from poor transmission of the seismic signal
reducing message transmission and therefore the percentage of receptive females.
Results from chapter 3 indicate that the type of airborne noise affects behavior differently
for both males and females. Males reduced their signaling behavior with bird calls, but there was
no change with white noise and cicada calls (1 Æ 2) (Fig. 3). Females showed behavioral
plasticity by varying their orientation responses. They oriented a higher proportion of times

97

when white noise blocked the seismic portion of the message but not with bird calls or cicada
calls (2 Æ 3). The increase in orientation was probably due to attempting to more accurately
receive signals. Female response (3 Æ 4) also changed with the type of interference. With white
noise and cicada calls females were just as likely to respond, while bird calls reduced the
proportion of receptive females. The latency to receptivity was longer for both white noise and
bird calls, which resulted in a longer latency to mate (4 Æ 1). Interestingly, while increasing
white noise levels had fewer matings, the percentage of females that gave a response was not
significantly different. This demonstrates behavioral plasticity in that with white noise latency
was probably increased due to signal degradation while with bird calls, the content and
information of the noise deterred a response; furthermore, the nature of cicada calls did not
trigger any change in behavior.
Chapter 4 tested the effects of signaling conspecific males on the behaviors of a focal male.
The results suggest that the focal male listens to conspecifics signaling in his environment. With
other males courting, he reduced courtship when he was courting female silk (Fig. 4A) and
increase courtship when no silk was present (Fig. 4B). These results demonstrate that the
communication network and contexts of other signals (i.e., chemical) should be taken into
consideration when trying to understand the behaviors of ground arthropods.
The broader impacts of this dissertation extend across several fields. Chapter 1 adds to the
literature on why animals use multimodal signals (Hasson, 1991; Møller and Pomiankowski,
1993; Pomiankowski and Iwasa, 1993; Iwasa and Pomiankowski, 1994; Johnstone, 1995, 1996;
Rowe, 1999; Partan and Marler, 2005; Hebets and Papaj, 2005; Bro-Jørgensen, 2010). Spiders
may use multimodal signals as a means to help convey their message in less than ideal signaling
conditions supporting the backup signaling hypothesis. Additionally, their signaling may be an

98

alerting or amplifying device. Chaper 2 focused on how animals change their signaling or
responding with airborne noise similar to other studies (Gerhardt, 1983; Brumm and
Slabbekoorn, 2005; Patricelli and Blickley, 2006; Warren et al., 2006; Lengagne, 2008; Hu and
Cardoso, 2010). This work is among the first to show the effects of airborne noise on seismic
communication. In addition, the experiments in this chapter are unique in that they show the
perspectives of sender, receiver, and outcome (mating success). Finally, the work in chapter 3
adds to the field of eavesdropping and communication networks (McGregor, 1993, 2005; Zuk
and Kolluru, 1998; Djemai et al., 2004; Peake, 2005). It shows how neighboring males both can
reduce and induce courtship behavior—which many studies do not demonstrate both types of
effects.

Appendix I: Male S. ocreata response to airborne sounds (bird calls)

Rationale
Lohrey (MS Thesis, 2007) found that male S. ocreata respond to airborne bird calls by
ceasing courtship. However, spiders do not possess any type of ear drum or tympanum, and may
not perceive airborne signals directly. Therefore, I tested if spiders were receiving these signals
as substrate vibrations, by playing bird calls to courting males on conductive (paper) or
attenuating (granite) substrates. Methods and results below are taken from the published paper
(Lohrey, Clark, Gordon, Uetz. 2009. Animal Behaviour. 77:813-821).

Methods
Experiment 4: Spider Perception of Acoustic Stimuli
To determine the manner of spider sensory detection of airborne stimuli, we
compared the responses of courting male spiders to the same avian airborne cues
on substrata that conduct airborne sound as seismic vibration (filter paper) and
those that do not (spiders were tested on granite slabs, eliminating the seismic
element of the bird calls). Previous studies (Hebets 2005) have shown that granite
is an effective vibration-isolation medium. Even so, we tested both substrates for
reception and conductance of airborne bird calls as substrate-borne vibrations
using a Polytec LDV-100 laser vibrometer. As in experiments 1–3, spiders were
tested in 15 cm diameter arenas with either a filter paper substratum or a granite
substratum (both containing female silk to elicit male courtship behaviours). We
played one of the three 3 s northern cardinal calls to courting males in the soundisolation room. To avoid spiders receiving near-field sound waves, a single tower
computer speaker was placed approximately 1 m away from the arena and
calibrated at the arena to natural bird call levels of 80 dB. Trials were scored for
cessation of male courtship (‘freezing’) as before.
Results
Experiment 4: Spider Perception of Acoustic Stimuli
Results of behavioural trials with airborne bird calls suggested that spiders
distinguish between avian acoustic stimuli and nonthreatening sounds, raising
questions about how spiders perceive such cues. We therefore tested spider
perception of airborne sounds on presumed conductive (filter paper) and
nonconductive (granite) substrata. Results of tests with the laser vibrometer

109

confirmed those of Hebets (2005), indicating that airborne bird calls can be
detected on filter paper, but hardly at all on granite (Fig. 10a, b). Spiders’
responses differed significantly between substrata (X2= 8.665, P = 0.0032). Males
on filter paper (airborne sound and substratum vibrations) ceased courting during
the bird call in 12/23 (52.17%) of the trials, while males on granite (airborne
sound only, no substratum vibrations) ceased courting in 2/20 (10%) of the trials.

Appendix II: Female response based on environmental substrate and signal transmission

Background and Hypotheses
Results of studies in chapters 1 and 2 indicate that females behave differently in response to
their environmental context. Females spent more time on substrates that effectively transmit
seismic signals (leaves) (Ch 1) and behaved differently toward males (orientation and receptivity
responses) when exposed to noise from different acoustic stimuli (Ch 2). As a follow-up, I tested
female reliance on visual cues when information from the seismic channel was restricted by an
attenuating substrate or noise. I hypothesized that if females can detect potential for limited
transmission of male seismic cues (either because the substrate does not transmit seismic signals
or because acoustic noise interferes with the seismic signal), then females will respond more
readily to visual male courtship cues. An alternative hypothesis is that if females lack
information from the seismic channel, then they will compensate for the missing channel by
reducing responsiveness to visual cues from courting males, as they need to be more alert for
potential predators or environmental disturbances. The null hypothesis is that females will act no
differently when the seismic channel is not transmitting well.

Materials and Methods
General rearing methods for S. ocreata
Spiders were collected from the forest at the Cincinnati Nature Center (Clermont Co., OH-Rowe Woods near Milford) during Fall 2008 and Spring 2009. The spiders were allowed to
mature in the laboratory with a 13 h light:11 h dark cycle at 22oC and 60-70% relative humidity.
Spiders were housed in individual opaque round plastic containers (15 cm diameter, 8 cm depth),

111

with an ad libitum water supply, and fed at least one appropriately sized cricket twice a week.
To control for hunger, animals were fed a cricket the day before each experimental trial.

Substrate Trials
Females (virgin, 8-21 days post adult molt) were placed in 6.5cm x 12cm arenas with a
paper or granite (painted white to control for color) substrate with an Ipod screen flush with the
narrow side of the arena. Arenas were cleaned with water and the paper was switched between
trials to clear silk and chemical cues. Females were allowed to acclimate in vials for 1 min.
Video playback of male courtship was begun on the Ipods and then the vial was removed. Three
exemplar male videos were used to avoid pseudo-replication. Trials were videotaped for 5 min
and scored for female behaviors. After two days, the same female was used in a second trial
(matched pairs design) but on the opposite substrate. The sample size was n = 41 females.

Noise Trials
Females (virgin, 8-21 days post adult molt) were placed in 15cm diameter circular arenas
with one side flattened to accommodate an Ipod. Females were allowed to acclimate and wander
around in the arena for 1 min before the Ipod started playing male courtship videos (as above).
Sound conditions were set before acclimation, to silence or 20dB above background of white
noise generated using Matlab (The Mathworks, Natick, MA) and standardized for intensity with
a sound level meter (Fisher, Model: 11-661-6A). Two days later, females were run again using
the opposite condition. Trials were videotaped for 5min and scored for female behaviors. The
sample size was n = 56 females.

112

Trial Analysis
Trials were scored in JWatcher for number, latency and duration of behaviors (orient +
approach, receptivity displays, stationary, walking, grooming). Data were then analyzed as
matched pairs using the statistical package JMP (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, U.S.A) with substrate
type or noise present/absent as the independent variables. In addition, trials from the first day
were analyzed in an oneway ANOVA, because in matched pairs behaviors must be present in
both days to be analyzed.

Results and Discussion
When presented with visual signals of male courtship on differing substrates (granite vs
paper) females showed no significant differences in any behaviors (Table 1). Similarly, females
showed no significant difference in behaviors to visual signals of male courtship with and
without white noise (Table 2). For both experiments, trials analyzed for just the first day also did
not produce significant data (data not shown). Both experiments indicate that females do not
compensate for a loss in seismic information by relying more heavily on visual courtship cues,
and thus the null hypothesis could not be rejected.
