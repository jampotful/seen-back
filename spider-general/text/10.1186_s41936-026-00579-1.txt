Venomics of the six-eyed sand spider, Sicarius
rugosus (Araneae: Sicariidae), from the
neotropical dry forest of Costa Rica
Cecilia Díaz1,2*, Fabián Bonilla2, Julián Fernández2 and Mahmood Sasa2,3
Abstract
Background Sicarius rugosus, the only member of the genus that inhabits Central America, is phylogenetically
related to South American Sicarius spiders. These originated from a common ancestor with sister African species. Like
Loxosceles, Sicarius exhibits venom phospholipase D activity due to a group of toxins known collectively as SicTox.
Methods A gel-assisted, bottom-up, proteomic analysis was performed to characterize the venom composition of S.
rugosus. Hyaluronidase activity was determined using zymography.
Results We identified several SicTox sequences, all classified as β-clade paralogs and sharing unique peptides with
proteins from S. patagonicus, S. peruensis, and other species. Enzymes such as metalloproteinases, including putative
astacins, carboxypeptidases, and angiotensin-converting enzymes, were detected in the venom. We also identified
probable serine proteinases and a toxin-processing peptidylglycine α-hydroxylating monooxygenase, which may
participate in venom peptide maturation. The venom displays in vitro hyaluronidase activity. Some likely peptide
toxins were also identified, including a sicaritoxin, which preferentially targets insects.
Conclusion This represents the first proteomic study of the rare six-eyed sand spider, S. rugosus, from the dry forest of
Costa Rica, confirming the presence of sequences similar to phospholipases D of other Sicarius and Loxosceles species,
as well as venom neuropeptides and enzymes characteristic of the family Sicariidae, which may participate in tissue
spreading and processing of other venom constituents.
Keywords Sicarius, SicTox, Venom, Knottin, Metalloproteinases, Hyaluronidase

Background
Spiders are almost exclusively venomous. They use
their venom primarily for predation and secondarily for
defense (Cooper et al., 2017; Evans et al., 2019). Except for
some genera, their main prey are insects and other small
arthropods (Hénaut et al., 2024). Compared to scorpions,
the other major venomous arachnids, the venom composition of a proportionally smaller number of spider
species has been studied (Herzig et al., 2019; Langenegger et al., 2019). However, both arachnid taxa display the
same general families of components: peptides, including cysteine-rich neurotoxins and non-disulfide-bridged

antimicrobial peptides (only found in some spider families); enzymes (i.e., proteases, hyaluronidases), and some
proteins without enzymatic activity, such as the CAPdomain superfamily allergens (Dresler et al., 2024). Other
small components include neurotransmitters, secondary
metabolites, polyamines, organic acids, and nucleotides
(Langenegger et al., 2019).
The Sicariidae is a small family of six-eyed spiders with
three described genera: Sicarius, Loxosceles, and Hexophthalma (Lopes et al., 2021; Magalhães et al., 2017). Due
to its medical importance, Loxosceles is the best studied
of the three. The most abundant toxins in sicariid venoms are phospholipases D, also known as sphingomyelinases D. These include many paralogs classified as α- and
β-SicTox, and denoted by Roman numerals and capital
letters (Binford et al., 2009; Magalhães et al., 2017). Venoms of Loxosceles laeta (Fernandes-Pedrosa et al., 2008)
and Loxosceles intermedia (Gremski et al., 2010) have
been characterized at the transcriptomic level, revealing that in addition to sphingomyelinase D, two other
components are highly expressed: astacin-like proteases
(metalloproteinases) and small peptides. Among the latter, neurotoxic ICK-peptides (knottins) account for more
than 50% of venom gland transcripts (Trevisan-Silva
et al., 2017), and they act by modulating insect voltagegated Na+ channels (Dongol et al., 2019). Hyaluronidases,
cysteine-rich secretory proteins (CAP superfamily), and
serine proteases exhibit low transcript levels (Gremski et
al., 2010, 2021).
The genus Sicarius comprises at least 21 species, all
associated with arid and semi-arid environments from
America and Africa (Magalhães et al., 2019). In the New
World, the genus is distributed from Central America to
Argentina (Magalhães et al., 2017). Still, all species occur
in restricted ecosystems, including the Mesoamerican
dry forest, the Caribbean coast of Colombia, the dry forest (caatinga) of Brazil, the Andean dry forests of Bolivia
and other countries, some xeric scrublands and deserts in
Chile, Argentina, and Peru, and in the Galapagos Islands
(Magalhães et al., 2019).
New World Sicarius also expresses sphingomyelinase
D, but most of them with significantly lower enzymatic
activity on choline-containing lipids than those observed
in venoms of most Loxosceles and African Sicarius species (Lajoie et al., 2015; Zobel-Thropp et al., 2010). This
likely reflects the fact that although these enzymes retain
the same catalytic amino acids in the active site, they differ in the substrate-binding region (Binford et al., 2009).
Interestingly, different affinities for phospholipid head
groups of SicToxs have been discussed in an evolutionary context, with respect to the presence or absence of
choline- and ethanolamine-containing phospholipids in
mammals and insects (Lajoie et al., 2015).

There have been attempts to characterize envenomation induced by some Sicarius species from the Brazilian caatinga, such as S. ornatus (Lopes et al., 2013) and S.
tropicus (Lopes et al., 2021), and a very recent proteomic
analysis of the Chilean S. thomisoides has been published
(Arán-Sekul et al., 2025). In addition to sphingomyelinases, other venom components identified in S. thomisoides include metalloproteinases (astacin-like), serine
proteinases, cysteine-rich proteins, and peptides that do
not belong to the knottin family (Arán-Sekul et al., 2025).
Sicariid sphingomyelinases paralyze insects (ZobelThropp et al., 2012); however, other venom components,
such as those previously identified in Loxosceles spp., also
have insecticidal activities (Castro et al., 2004). All these
toxins contribute to subduing and killing arthropod prey,
and like most spiders, South American Sicarius species
are generalists, preying on insects, centipedes, scorpions,
and other spiders (Bejar-Hermoza et al., 2023; Magalhães
et al., 2013; Taucare-Rios & Piel, 2020).
Sicarius rugosus is the only member of the genus that
inhabits Central America, and it has been reported in
tropical dry forests of Costa Rica, Honduras, and Guatemala (Cubas-Rodríguez & Brescovit, 2024; Cubas-Rodríguez & Herrera-Jordan, 2024). No other species from this
genus has been identified in North America or the West
Indies, where some Loxosceles, such as L. reclusa, are
abundant (Binford et al., 2008). The closest congener of
S. rugosus is S. vallenato (Magalhães et al., 2019), which is
associated with the tropical dry forest of Colombia (CalaRiquelme et al., 2017).
Venom of the elusive six-eyed spider S. rugosus has
been characterized only regarding its slight sphingomyelinase D activity (Binford et al., 2009) and its high toxicity
to crickets, compared to other spider venoms, including
some Loxosceles spp. (Zobel-Thropp et al., 2010). However, no information is currently available on venom
composition. Accordingly, this study aims to analyze the
venom of S. rugosus from Costa Rica, characterize its
peptides and enzymes, and compare them with those of
other Sicarius species from South America.

Methods
Specimens and venom extraction

Two S. rugosus adult specimens were collected in the
vicinity of the Palo Verde Research Station (Organization for Tropical Studies), at Bagaces, Guanacaste Province, Costa Rica. For each individual, venom (~ 30 µg of
protein content) was extracted using electro-stimulation
(30 V) and collected in capillary tubes. Venom samples
were lyophilized and stored at -70 °C until analysis. Procedures for this study were approved by the Biodiversity
Commission of Universidad de Costa Rica (CBio-882025; CBio-14-2026).

Electrophoresis and zymography

Venom samples (10 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE
(15%) under reducing conditions (5% 2-mercaptoethanol,
at 100 °C for 10 min), in a Mini-Protean system (Bio-Rad)
at 180 V, and proteins were stained with Coomassie blue
R-250. SDS-PAGE was also performed to obtain a concentrated protein band for mass spectrometry analysis,
avoiding the separation of bands in the lower gel. This
was achieved by stopping the electrophoresis as soon as
the migration front moved 3–4 mm into the resolving gel.
Zymography of venom hyaluronidase activity was
determined by the method of Cevallos et al. (1992) based
on SDS-PAGE in a 12% gel containing 0.5 mg/mL hyaluronic acid from rooster comb under non-reducing
conditions. Gels were washed with 1% Triton X-100 to
remove SDS and incubated overnight in 0.1 M NaCl,
0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.2. Gels were stained
with Alcian Blue 8GX for 4 h and washed with 6% acetic
acid.
Mass spectrometry (MS)

A protein band on SDS-PAGE containing the concentrated venom (not shown) and the most prominent
SDS-PAGE bands of completely separated venom were
subjected to reduction with dithiothreitol (10 mM)
and alkylation with iodoacetamide (50 mM). This was
followed by overnight in-gel digestion with sequencing-grade bovine trypsin (Sigma Chemical Co.) in an
automated workstation (Intavis). Resulting peptides were
analyzed by nESI-MS/MS using a nano-Easy® 1200 chromatograph and a Q-Exactive Plus® mass spectrometer
(Thermo). Tryptic peptides obtained from the separated
SDS-PAGE bands were analyzed as follows: Five µL of
each tryptic digest were loaded onto a C18 trap column
(75 μm × 2 cm, 3-μm particle size; PepMap, Thermo),
washed with 0.1% formic acid (solution A), and separated
at 200 nL/min (3 µm), 15 cm × 75 µm C18 Easy-spray®
analytical column. Buffer A was 0.1% formic acid, and
Buffer B was 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile. Gradient conditions were as follows: 1–5% B in 1 min, 5–25%
B in 30 min, 25–79% B in 6 min, 79–99% B in 2 min,
and 99% B in 6 min (total time, 45 min). Mass spectra
were acquired in positive mode at 1.9 kV, with a capillary temperature of 200 °C. Scanning was performed
from 400 to 1600 m/z, with a maximum injection time
of 100 ms, an AGC target of 3 × 106 (orbitrap resolution
of 70,000). Fragmentation of the top 10 ions with 2—4
positive charges was performed with an AGC target of
1 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 110 ms. The
resolution was 17,500, with a loop count of 10, an isolation window of 1.4 m/z, and a dynamic exclusion time
of 5 s. Tryptic peptides obtained from the concentrated
SDS-PAGE venom band were analyzed as follows: 10 μL
of tryptic peptides were loaded onto a C18 trap column

(75 μm × 2 cm, 3 μm particle size; PepMap, Thermo Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA), washed with 0.1% formic
acid (solution A). Separation was performed under a
flow rate of 200 nL/min using a C18 Easy-spray® column
(15 cm × 75 μm, 3-μm particle size). A gradient toward
solution B was developed over 110 min (1% B for 3 min,
1–26% B in 62 min, 26–99% B in 30 min, and 99% B for
15 min). MS spectra were acquired in positive mode at
1.9 kV, with a capillary temperature of 200 °C, using 1
μscan from 400 to 1600 m/z. Maximum injection time
was 50 ms, an AGC target of 1 × 106, and a resolution of
70,000. The top 10 ions with 2–4 positive charges were
fragmented with an AGC target of 1 × 105, a minimum
AGC of 2 × 103, a maximum injection time of 110 ms, a
dynamic exclusion time of 5 s, and a resolution of 17,500.
MS/MS spectra for all samples were processed to assign
peptide sequence matches to known protein families by
similarity to sequences in the UniProt/SwissProt database (Araneae, 2025) using Peaks X® (Bioinformatics
Solutions). Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a
fixed modification, while deamidation of asparagine or
glutamine and methionine oxidation were set as variable
modifications, allowing up to 3 missed cleavages by trypsin. Parameters for match acceptance were FDR < 0.1%,
and − 10lgP protein score ≥ 30. The false discovery rate
was estimated using decoy-fusion with Peaks X® (Bioinformatics Solutions). FDR was controlled at the peptidespectrum match level. Raw MS/MS files were deposited
in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the MassIVE
repository with the dataset identifier PXD075448.

Results
SDS-PAGE and hyaluronidase activity

By SDS-PAGE, all visible venom components were below
75 kDa, with some proteins of intermediate molecular
weight and peptides whose masses were equal to or lower
than 12 kDa (Fig. 1A). Two clear bands appeared around
65–70 kDa. Below them, a weak band around 50 kDa,
the expected size of sicariid hyaluronidases under reducing conditions (Gremski et al., 2021), was observed.
Hyaluronidase activity was confirmed by zymography
(Fig. 1B).
There is also a group of proteins with molecular
weights between 25 and 35 kDa, some of which may correspond to putative phospholipase D isoforms (see proteomic analyses of the bands below). An additional, very
thick, low-molecular-mass band (roughly divided into
two), containing peptides with molecular masses below
12 kDa, was observed. A couple of other low-concentration protein bands were present, corresponding to proteins of low (14–16 kDa) and intermediate (~ 50 kDa)
molecular weights, suggesting that this venom has a relatively small number of components (Fig. 1A).

After MS-analysis of the complete venom, we identified several putative phospholipase D paralogs, all of
which belong to the β-clade, groups I and II (Binford et
al., 2009). Those identified with higher coverage were
S. peruensis, S. patagonicus, and S. terrosus paralogs.
From all of the phospholipase D sequences retrieved,
we identified unique peptides corresponding to SpaSicTox-betaIIA1, SpaSicTox-betaIF1, SpeSicTox-betaIIA1,
SpeSicTox-betaIF1, StSicTox-betaIB1i, StSicTox-betaIF1,
and LcsSicTox-betaIC1 (Fig. 2; Table 1). There were
other potential paralogs in the venom, tryptic peptides
of which were not unique, but shared by different SicTox
(not shown).
A combination of peptide sequences that may correspond to LiSicTox-betaID1 from L. intermedia was
obtained. The same combination of tryptic peptides is
found in LarSicTox-betaID1 from L. arizonica, so the
presence of either one is possible (Fig. 2). They are the
only ones in the database potentially present in S. rugosus, whose masses are at least 2–3 kDa higher than
those of other proteins in the identified group. There is a
light band in the SDS-PAGE gel that corresponds to the
expected molecular weight (Fig. 1A). Also identified by
a combination of unique and common peptides was the
sequence of SicTox-βIC1 from Loxosceles cf. spinulosa
(Fig. 2).

Presence of other venom putative toxins

Table 2 displays S. rugosus venom components identified by gel-assisted bottom-up proteomics (GeLC-MS/
MS), not included in the SicTox family. We retrieved
some peptides, other putative enzymes, and some proteins with unassigned or unknown functions. We also
identified housekeeping proteins that may be present in
the venom glands or adjacent tissues. In the proteomic
analysis, we were unable to recover the hyaluronidase
sequence, although, as mentioned, the activity was present (Fig. 1B).
A putative sicaritoxin was identified. This is a neurotoxin from Loxosceles intermedia named U1-sicaritoxinLi1c, a knottin that is also toxic to insects (Castro et al.,
2004; Gremski et al., 2010). Two venom factors classified
as thyroglobulin type-1 domain-containing proteins were
also present in the venom (Table 2).
Most venom enzymes identified were putative proteases, primarily astacin-type metalloproteinases, but
sequences similar to angiotensin-converting enzyme
(ACE) and carboxypeptidase A were also present
(Table 2). A putative serine proteinase with similarity to
Loxosceles rufescens furin (a neuroendocrine convertase),
and an S8 peptidase-domain containing protein were also
evidenced. Two proteins without a characterized function were retrieved from S. rugosus venom. One was a
DUF885 domain-containing protein, which shares a peptide sequence with an angiotensin-converting enzyme,

suggesting it could be another metalloproteinase. The
other was a putative leucine-rich repeat protein, but little
information is available about that component (Table 2).
Other proteins recovered in the venom were a putative
hemolectin and a peptidylglycine α-hydroxylating monooxygenase. The latter is an enzyme that could participate
in the post-translational modification of toxins in S. rugosus venom. This enzymatic step occurs before amidation,
which is required by several peptides during maturation
(Delgado-Prudencio et al., 2019).
In a follow-up MS-analysis of venom SDS-PAGE
bands, we observed that putative S. rugosus ACE appears
in a double band corresponding to a molecular weight
range of 65–70 kDa (Fig. 1A). According to the UniProt
database, this mass range corresponds to putative ACE
homologs present in several spider venoms. In Costa
Rican sicariid spider sequences, we identified isoforms
with some amino acid differences. These were mainly
conservative substitutions of leucine/isoleucine for
valine. Interestingly, in this SDS-PAGE mass range, the
DUF885 domain-containing protein also appears, suggesting it could be an ACE (see above). In the gel range
of 25–34 kDa, we identified SicTox paralogs and putative astacins. Spider astacins display molecular masses
between 25 and 27 kDa, suggesting they correspond to
the lower band at this mass range (Fig. 1A). The upper
band (27–34 kDa) likely contains phospholipases D.
Moreover, in this additional MS analysis, we identified
venom factors in the band corresponding to peptides
below 12 kDa.

Discussion
The family Sicariidae is an ancient arachnid taxon that
probably originated in Western Gondwana and separated
during the second phase of the continental breakup, more
than 100 million years ago (Binford et al., 2008). Sicarius,
one of the main genera of the family, currently includes
species in southern Africa (mainly South Africa and
Namibia) and South America (Magalhães et al., 2019),
with a unique species, S. rugosus, inhabiting Neotropical
Central America (Cubas-Rodríguez & Brescovit, 2024;
Cubas-Rodríguez & Herrera-Jordan, 2024). The genus
Sicarius is found in subtropical deserts and dry tropical forests, exhibiting a low capacity to spread into other
biomes (Magalhães et al., 2019). These medium to large
sand-dwelling spiders are adapted to hide and ambush

their prey, which includes insects and other arthropods
(Magalhães et al., 2013).
Due to their elusive behavior and habitat preferences,
most New World Sicarius species are rarely seen and
do not represent a medical problem. In contrast, members of the related genus, Loxosceles, are well adapted to
disturbed environments, including human habitations
(Fischer & Vasconcellos-Neto, 2005), where they cause
serious accidents and a range of symptoms known as
loxoscelism (Gremski et al., 2023). The lack of reported
envenomations by South American Sicarius is also supported by the fact that most species show molecular differences that make their toxins non-toxic to mammals, as
those expressed by some Loxosceles and African Sicarius
(Binford et al., 2009; Lajoie et al., 2015).

Spider venoms contain various toxins, with the most
abundant protein families being cysteine-rich neurotoxins and several enzyme families. Some groups of spiders,
such as Theraphosidae, possess more than twenty types
of enzymes in their venoms (Dresler et al., 2024). Among
them, the most commonly found are oxidoreductases and
hydrolases. On the contrary, Sicariidae has few reported
venom enzymes, and most attention has focused on
phospholipase/sphingomyelinases D.
According to Delgado-Prudencio et al. (2019), enzymes
in arthropod venoms can be classified into six categories,
with sicariid phospholipases D belonging to the group of
directly toxic enzymes. Genes encoding these proteins
may have been recruited from other tissues and may have
undergone neofunctionalization in venom glands. Supporting that idea, a recent study on the composition of
the digestive tract enzymes of uloborid spiders, which
have no venom glands, revealed the presence of nontoxic sphingomyelinases D (Valladão et al., 2023). This
finding could suggest an earlier and perhaps more ubiquitous origin and function for this type of enzyme.
In addition to phospholipase D paralogs in S. rugosus
venom, we recovered putative pre-digestive enzymes,
spreading factors, and maturation, preservative, and

Page 7 of 10

multifunctional enzymes (Delgado-Prudencio et al.,
2022). Among them, there are proteinases (Fig. 3). These
enzymes are multifunctional, participating in prey predigestion and in diversifying the toxin repertoire by processing venom peptides (Dresler et al., 2024). Regarding
metalloproteinases, we found sequences for angiotensin-converting enzymes (ACE), carboxypeptidases, and
mainly, putative astacins. Putative serine proteases similar to furin were also present, which, together with carboxypeptidases A, could contribute to venom peptide
processing (Kuhn-Nentwig et al., 2019).
Invertebrate homologs of mammalian ACE are soluble
extracellular proteins with uncertain functions (Dresler
et al., 2024), although a role in neuropeptide processing
in insects has been suggested (Cajado-Carvalho et al.,
2016). ACE is also considered a spreading factor, similar to hyaluronidases, but unlike the latter, this proteinase has not been previously reported in sicariid venoms.
Hyaluronidases have been identified from several spiders,
including some Loxosceles species (Bordon et al., 2015).
Despite observing hyaluronidase activity, we were unable
to recover sequences corresponding to this enzyme in
the current analysis, probably due to very low concentration, or because they may differ from those deposited in
databases.
We also identified a putative peptidyl-glycine hydroxylating monooxygenase, which likely modulates neuropeptide maturation post-translationally (Dresler et al., 2024;
Langenegger et al., 2019). These enzymes are common in
scorpions and spider venom glands, as various peptide
toxins require processing before they become active (Delgado-Prudencio et al., 2019). Another oxidoreductase
present in S. rugosus venom is a putative glutathione peroxidase, a key antioxidant enzyme. In addition to other
members of this redox protein family, this component
could be important for venom protection against oxidative stress (Peiren et al., 2008).
Among non-enzymatic components, we identified a
putative hemolectin. Most lectins are proteins with affinity for carbohydrates and the capacity to agglutinate cells.
Some lectins and lectin-like proteins have been reported
in venoms of the families Ctenidae, Lamponidae, and
Theraphosidae (Dresler et al., 2024). The one identified
in S. rugosus venom matches a putative hemolectin from
Cupiennius salei. Like other lectins, they could agglutinate human and mouse erythrocytes; however, their biological function in spider venoms is unknown (Liang et
al., 1995).
All phospholipase D enzymes retrieved in this analysis
were paralogs of the β-clade, whose presence is characteristic of New World Sicarius and African Loxosceles. In
the recently published proteomics analysis of the Chilean
S. thomisoides venom, the authors not only reported the
presence of several South American Sicarius β-paralogs,

but also some α-paralogs of Loxosceles SicTox. That
study also showed that although several proteins reacted
immunologically with anti-Loxosceles laeta phospholipase D polyclonal antibodies, only one venom fraction
displayed significant sphingomyelinase D activity (AránSekul et al., 2020, 2025). This could explain the toxicological effects observed by this venom, which, together
with those characterized from South American species S.
ornatus and S. tropicus, could be potentially dangerous to
mammals (Lopes et al., 2013, 2021).
In contrast to the SicTox α-clade, the β-clade comprises
molecules with little or no sphingomyelinase D activity
and no dermonecrotic effects, which have been attributed to differences in key amino acids at the enzyme
binding site (Binford et al., 2009). Also, the putative SicTox identified in S. rugosus would belong to the I and II
groups, mainly in the IB, IF, and IIA categories. These are
typical paralogs identified in New World S. patagonicus,
S. peruensis, and S. terrosus. We recovered a unique peptide from SicTox-βIC1 of Loxosceles cf. spinulosa, a group
(β-IC) not commonly associated with American Sicarius
species, but present in S. thomisoides venom (Arán-Sekul
et al., 2025). This could be a remnant of enzymes present in the African sister species, which are probably not
expressed to a great extent in this venom. In any case, the
Southern African spinulosa group of spiders shows low
sphingomyelinase D activity, as seen in most New World
Sicarius (Binford et al., 2009).
The biological role of these SicTox with low affinity
for choline-containing lipids and poor dermonecrotic
activity has been described (Lajoie et al., 2015). They are
neurotoxic and cause paralysis in insects (Zobel-Thropp
et al., 2010, 2012), which would explain their rapid gene
duplication followed by adaptive divergence, even in
those proteins that lack sphingomyelinase activity and
are not toxic to mammals. Thus, it is not surprising that
several of these insecticidal paralogs are found in the
venom of S. rugosus, a species without apparent medical
importance, but very toxic to insects (Zobel-Thropp et
al., 2010).
The other characteristic group of components in
arthropod venoms is the cysteine-rich peptides, which,
in S. rugosus proteomic analysis, appear less represented
than enzymes. They have not been found in all sicariid
venoms, but some Loxosceles species do express them
to a great extent (Fernandes-Pedroza et al., 2008). Our
proteomic results are probably not conclusive evidence
of low expression in S. rugosus venom, since SDS-PAGE
shows a very prominent band of peptides below 12 kDa
(Fig. 1A). However, despite our efforts to identify most
of the venom components, we can only confirm the
presence of one, which probably belongs to the knottin
family. These peptides are mainly neurotoxic to insects
and other arthropods, modulating ion channel currents

(Castro et al., 2004; Gremski et al., 2010; Khamtorn et
al., 2022). Some reasons for inefficient identification of
low-mass components include their possible loss during
tryptic digestion or their invisibility in the analysis due
to the absence of homologous sequences in databases.
Regarding this last potential justification, it is evident
that compared to other families of spiders, venoms of the
Sicariidae have not been extensively studied beyond their
SicTox components, resulting in a lack of information
available for MS sequence comparison. This is a possible
limitation of our study.
Among the few cysteine-rich peptides reported in
the literature for sicariids, S. dolichocephalus sequences
U1-sicaritoxin-Sdo1a and U2-sicaritoxin-Sdo1a, deduced
from venom gland transcripts (Loening et al., 2013), and
three Loxosceles intermedia sicaritoxins (Li1c, Li1b, and
Li1a) are available in databases. All of them belong to the
family of knottins. One of the sequences identified in S.
rugosus displays some similarity to sicaritoxin-Li1c, an
insect neurotoxin. On the other hand, Arán-Sekul and
collaborators (2025) reported the presence of only two
peptides from an unknown family in the venom of S.
thomisoides.
Comparing SDS-PAGE banding patterns of S. rugosus
venom with those of S. thomisoides (Arán-Sekul et al.,
2025), two main differences were observed: (1) a thick gel
band containing small peptides in S. rugosus, absent in S.
thomisoides, and (2) a prominent 32–35 kDa band corresponding to a SicTox in S. thomisoides, absent in S. rugosus. An abundant region of peptides in S. rugosus venom
has been previously demonstrated by Zobel-Thropp and
collaborators (2010) using 2D-electrophoresis, which was
not observed in the African Sicarius hahni or Sicarius
damarensis.
In the natural environment, both phospholipases D
and knottin peptides could be neurotoxic to insects and
other arthropods. Since Sicarius species are all generalist
predators, these species may have diversified by preferentially expressing one of the two groups of toxins. This
is supported by the finding that even when several SicTox paralogs were identified in S. rugosus venom, unlike
peptides, the SDS-PAGE band containing phospholipases
D is not prominent. Most venomous arachnids, including
most spiders and scorpions, use neuropeptides as their
main weapons to paralyze and kill insect prey (Dresler et
al., 2024), and this is probably true for S. rugosus, as well
as other New World Sicarius.
On the other hand, SicTox with high sphingomyelinase
activity (probably α-clade paralogs), which are absent in
S. rugosus, but present in S. thomisoides, is biologically
intriguing. Previously, only Sicarius species from Africa
were known to express α-clade sphingomyelinase paralogs, which are toxic to humans (Binford et al., 2009).
SicTox-induced inflammatory dermonecrosis, as well

as vascular and renal alterations, seem directed toward
mammals and, alternatively, to some other higher vertebrates, as a defense against predators. Some large theraphosids feed on vertebrates (Nyffeler et al., 2022), and
members of the Sicariidae can also take vertebrates.
However, this behavior is infrequent and involves only
small animals such as geckos (Taucare-Rios & Piel, 2020).
There is limited information available on vertebrate
predators of Sicarius. For Loxosceles species, certain bats
and frogs have been identified (Fischer et al., 2006). It is
also reported that some closely related Sicarius species,
such as the South African endemic six-eyed sand spider, Hexophthalma spatulatus (previously classified as
Sicarius spatulatus), are preyed upon by mammals, birds,
and reptiles (Dippenaar-Schoeman et al., 2025). The same
could be true for the New World Sicarius species. However, it would require further investigation into their natural history to explain the absence of these highly toxic
sphingomyelinases in sicariid species such as S. rugosus.

Conclusions
In summary, by performing a proteomic analysis of the
venom of the rare Central American spider, S. rugosus,
we identified several SicTox paralogs, a knottin-type
cysteine-rich peptide, and enzymes, including putative
metalloproteinases, serine proteinases, and oxidoreductases. No cysteine-rich proteins from the CAP superfamily (potential allergens) or hyaluronidases were retrieved
in the proteome; however, hyaluronidase activity was
clearly observed by zymography. The presence of putative
β-clade SicTox phospholipases D is consistent with previously demonstrated S. rugosus sphingomyelinase activity,
which was extremely low, similar to most Sicarius species from the New World. On the other hand, the previously shown high toxicity on insects was corroborated by
sequence homology with these and other toxins identified in this venom.
