Effects of colour and scene dynamism on visual fatigue in animated films.
Argilés M, Fonts E, Pérez-Mañá L, Martinez-Navarro B, Sora-Domenjó C, Pérez-Cabré E, Sunyer-Grau B, Rovira-Gay C, Molins-Pitarch C, Quevedo-Junyent L. (2024). Scientific reports. . doi:10.1038/s41598-024-78329-y. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Effects of colour and scene dynamism on visual fatigue in animated films

## Abstract
AbstractAnimated films are highly popular in society, particularly among children and adolescents. During film production, selecting an adequate colour palette and scene dynamism is crucial for creating a visual impact on the audience. However, the influence of visual aesthetics, such as colour and scene dynamism on visual fatigue remains unexplored. In the present study, visual fatigue was assessed and compared in 30 young participants after viewing two popular films: Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse, an active, fast-paced film, and My Neighbour Totoro, a more relaxed film. Both films were viewed twice, once with the original colour palette and once in greyscale. Watching Spider-Man film induced a greater degree of visual fatigue than My Neighbour Totoro in two subjective questionnaires evaluating visual fatigue, and in the vergence facility and binocular accommodative facility tests. Moreover, important differences in the visual dynamics and colour aspects between both films were found. These results demonstrate that the high-paced and high-colour aspects of films directly affect visual fatigue. ## Introduction
This paper examines how visual strategies employed in animation shape both the aesthetic and narrative experience of cinematic works, while also significantly impacting viewer’s visual fatigue. Contemporary research in the field of visual perception and cognitive ergonomics suggests that prolonged exposure to certain visual features, including colour saturation, image complexity, flicker, and motion dynamics, can influence the level of visual fatigue experienced by viewers1–6. This phenomenon gains particular relevance in the context of animated films, a medium characterized by its unparalleled creative freedom and the possibility of experimenting with a broad range of visual aesthetics.
Visual fatigue, also known as eye strain or visual tiredness, refers to a group of characteristics linked to visual discomfort or tiredness in the eyes such as blurred vision, diplopia, headaches, redness of the eyes, and focusing difficulty1,7. It can be caused by prolonged near activities, such as reading, computer use or other electronic devices, and it can be accentuated by screens flicker4. Technological advances have resulted in increased image quality, and flickering TV images are less common. However, the frequency of visual fatigue has been growing, with a 50% prevalence among computer users7,8, and in up to 90% of digital device workers, who manifested eye strain signs9. Some studies have shown that binocular accommodative and vergence facility tests are related to visual fatigue, with lower performance in these tests associated with increased visual fatigue10–12.
The present research focuses on two distinguished examples of animated cinema: “Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse”, a masterpiece of technical and stylistic innovation produced by Sony Pictures Animation, and “My Neighbour Totoro”, a timeless classic from Studio Ghibli known for its gentle colour palette and focus on the beauty of everyday life. Both films provide ideal points of contrast and comparison to examine how differences in visual aesthetics can affect the viewer’s experience, from both physiological and psychological perspectives. These films were selected because of their distinctive stylistic and technical characteristics, which represent opposite ends of the animation spectrum. Spider-Man being a fast paced, brightly coloured animation film; in contrast to My Neighbour Totoro, a calmer and lighter alternative. Such comparison allows for a rigorous and quantitative analysis of how different visual attributes affect the human visual system.
Through a comparative analysis of these two films, this paper seeks to illuminate the relationship between aesthetic decisions in animation and their impact on visual fatigue, thereby contributing to a field of study that intersects art, perception science, and visual health. In doing so, we not only provide valuable insights for creators, critics, and viewers of animated films but also advance our understanding of the broader implications of visual aesthetics on our daily well-being.

## Materials and methods
Thirty-seven university students from the Centre de la Imatge, Tecnologia i Multimèdia (CITM) of Terrassa were recruited. This study was approved by the Review Board of the Polytechnic University of Catalonia (01/2024 UPC). All participants were informed of the purpose of the study and signed an informed consent to participate. Measurements started with the screening process for the inclusion criteria on February 22, 2024, and ended on March 7, 2024, over 12 days.
The inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined by certified optometrists. Participants with colour anomalies, determined with the Ishihara test, more than 0.00 log MAR near monocular visual acuity with their habitual correction, evaluated with ETDRS test, and more than 70” of stereoacuity (Random Dot Test) were excluded. In addition, all participants scoring ≥ 6.0 in the Symptom Questionnaire for Visual Dysfunctions (SQVD) were excluded. The SQVD is an instrument validated in Spanish and designed to identify visual symptoms associated with refractive errors, accommodative issues, and binocular dysfunctions13,14, and all participants scoring ≥ 6.0 were excluded. Following the initial screening in two classrooms, five participants were excluded from the study for not meeting the inclusion criteria, two others refused to participate. The total sample was 30 participants with an average age and standard deviation of 19.38 ± 2.24 years, 6 females and 24 males. Descriptive optometric values can be found in Supplementary Information Table S1.
Objective visual fatigue was evaluated immediately before and after the visualization of each film. Vergence facility and binocular accommodative facility are commonly used in optometric practice to evaluate the performance in changing vergence and accommodative demands over a certain period8. The vergence facility test, with 12 prism diopters Base-Out and 3 prism diopters Base-In, mounted in a flipper prism; and the binocular accommodation facility test, with + 2.00D and − 2.00 D flipper lenses, were the two tests used to assess visual fatigue. Cycles per minute were noted. A full cycle in the vergence facility test meant that the participant was able to see a single and clear vertical column of letters at 0.4 m with both prisms. Similarly, a full cycle in the accommodation facility test meant that the participant could see a single and clear rock chart with letters at 20/30 visual acuity at 0.4 m with both + 2.00D and − 2.00D lenses. All the measurements were conducted in a random order of presentation.
Upon completing the viewing of the the films, each participant answered the Computer Vision Syndrome Questionnaire (CVS-Q), validated in Spanish, and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for visual fatigue15–18. The CVS-Q assesses the frequency and intensity of 16 visual symptoms using a rating scale, the higher the score, the higher the visual symptomatology. The VAS scale ranged from 0 (indicating no visual fatigue experienced while watching the film) to 10 (representing extreme visual fatigue during the movie viewing). Participants were given the sentence “Please select a visual fatigue level between 0 and 10, 0 if you have not experienced any visual fatigue while watching the movie, and 10 if you have experienced extreme visual fatigue during the movie”. Furthermore, participants filled out a final questionnaire specifically at the end of the fourth day after viewing all film conditions. This questionnaire consisted of recalling and rating from 0 (least) to 10 (most) the amount of visual fatigue caused by each movie, and also selecting which movie caused them the greatest discomfort, asking “Rate the visual fatigue you experienced after watching each of the movies on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 means you did not experience any visual fatigue during the viewing of the movie, and 10 means extreme visual fatigue while watching the movie (if you did not watch a movie, please leave the box blank)”. All questionnaires were completed using an online survey provided by Google Forms (Google, Mountain View, California, USA).
The two chosen films were digitally altered to limit the time to 60 min, and converted to greyscale, using Final Cut Pro (Apple Inc, California, USA), so that participants viewed each film twice, one in colour and the other in greyscale. Each participant had a Cintiq Wacom monitor (22”, 1920 × 1080, 120 Hz, Saitama, Japan), at a viewing distance of 0.5 m, which was pre-calibrated for colour and contrast (see Supplementary Information, Figure S2 and Figure S3). Each participant was assigned a number during the entire study. All participants watched the films with headphones and were randomly assigned to the four conditions (normal colour and greyscale for the two movies) during the 4 days of the study over a two weeks period (two days per week). This period between viewing sessions was designed to minimise the effect of accumulated visual fatigue. All four viewings were conducted in the same room with the same illumination, half of the participants viewed a film in grey, and the other half viewed it in colour (see Supplementary Information, Fig S12, for pictures during one of the experimental sessions). The film condition of each participant was masked to the optometrist. The order of the movies was randomized using an Excel spreadsheet, in which participants were assigned to the four viewing conditions of the two movies. Then, participants were randomly assigned to these groups, ensuring that each order had a balanced number of participants. The measurement room was adjacent to the viewing room. Before the film viewing the objective visual fatigue was assessed in each participant, and then each participant went to see the film. After 60 min of film, they returned to do the post-visualization measures, and completed the online questionnaires. The timeline of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 1. The maximum time for each patient to do the objective measures after viewing the film was 10 min.

## Film selection
In our study, we selected two films renowned for their innovation and contributions to the realm of animation: Sony Pictures Animation’s Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse and Studio Ghibli’s My Neighbour Totoro. This choice is based on the contrast of their stylistic and technical approaches, providing an ideal comparative framework to explore the impact of visual aesthetics on viewer experience and visual fatigue.
Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse has been celebrated for its technical and narrative boldness, introducing a groundbreaking approach that blends 2D animation elements within a 3D environment, creating an unparalleled visual experience19. Conversely, My Neighbour Totoro is revered for its meticulous traditional animation, capturing the essence of nature and Japanese culture with a distinctive visual style20. The selection of these two films allows for an in-depth analysis of how different animation techniques and visual styles impact viewer perception and well-being.

## Visual aesthetics analysis between films: colour and scene dynamism
In this work, the primary objective was to evaluate the influence of the colour and scene dynamics of a film on the human visual system. For colour analysis, instead of using the RGB components of each pixel of the digital frames, a spectral colourimeter was employed to measure the illuminance and colour information received by the spectator. Since it is not feasible to describe the spectral pattern of the whole movie, we averaged the values for a set of frames. To evaluate the colour information and brightness of the two analysed films, an OHSP-350-F Portable Spectral irradiance colourimeter from HOPOOCOLOR (Hopoo Light&Color Technology Co.,Ltd., Hangzhou, China) was used in the experiments.
Both films were projected in their entirety and sequentially on a laptop screen, and an equivalent number of still frames were selected across the two films (every 5 minutes for “Totoro” and every 10 minutes for “Spider-Man”). The selection of frames was based on time distribution and regardless of the displayed information, so that the entire film was covered and a representative sample was obtained. The spectral colourimeter was positioned at a fixed distance from the screen (20 cm), reproducing a similar viewing angle (2ω=70 degrees) to the one participants had in the experiment. All additional lighting in the laboratory was switched off so that the spectroradiometer received the impinging light from the laptop screen alone (Lenovo ThinkPad E16 Gen 1, 16”, 1920 × 1200 pp, set up at high contrast). For every single frame, the colourimeter provided several parameters, including, among others, the illuminance in lux, correlated colour temperature (CCT) in Kelvin, x and y chromaticity parameters derived from the tristimulus values X, Y,Z of the CIE 1931 colour space21. For each frame, ten measurements were taken by the spectroradiometer, from which the mean value along with the standard deviation for each parameter was computed and taken into account for further analysis.
Regarding scene dynamism, the analysis was based on contemporary film studies which emphasise the significance of editing speed and scene duration in influencing visual reception. In this context, individual shots of the films were automatically detected, using a computational method for video segmentation, and shot identification detecting scene changes (see Figure S4 and Figure S5 in Supplementary Information). Key metrics of both films were analysed and compared including the Average Shot Length (ASL) and its distributions along the films, especially those related to the short-duration shots (see Table S7 in Supplementary Information). In addition, the concept of cluster was defined as a series of shots with a duration less than the median, edited successively, and defined according to the number of shots that constitute them. Afterwards, the frequency and duration of these clusters were analysed. These metrics provide insight into the visual load imposed on viewers by the rapidity of scene changes and the complexity of visual transitions.
Finally, qualitative observations were made to characterise the Visual Activity Index (VAI), composed of motion and movement effects22. Motion refers to the movement of characters or elements on a static background, and movement refers to camera movements or changes in the focal distance of the lens (zoom in and zoom out). Previous studies based on VAI index observed that camera movements tend to generate a greater visual change than the movement of elements or characters on a static background22,23. Therefore, it is considered that “movement” has a much higher impact on visual activity than “motion”. From this premise, we selected a random shot at the first half of each film and we performed a visual analysis of 250 consecutive shots, starting at the selected one. We visually classified each shot in terms of “no movement”, “motion” and “movement”, indicating if the movement was slow, medium speed or fast, and if the range of the movement was short or long (see description and characterization of methods used for analysing visual dynamism in films in Supplementary Information).

## Data analysis
Data distribution was explored using Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics are summarised with either mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range. A one-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to evaluate visual fatigue, followed by a Bonferroni post-hoc analysis. Additionally, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA, incorporating both film and colour conditions, was used to assess their interaction effects. Green-house correction was applied in case of non-sphericity assumption (Mauchly’s test, P < 0.05). A P-value of 0.05 (α = 0.05, β = 0.95) was considered as the cut-off of statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

## Results

## Visual fatigue under different film viewing conditions
The results obtained for the 30 participants showed statistically significant differences in vergence facility between the four film conditions. A one-way ANOVA of repeated eight measures with Greenhouse-Geisser correction, Mauchly’s test P < 0.001, revealed statistical difference between film conditions, F(3.29, 95.47) = 5.25, P = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.153. Pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni correction found a statistically significant difference between pre- and post-viewing of Spider-Man viewing, P < 0.001, as well as pre- and post-viewing of Spider-Man watching in greyscale, P = 0.049. In contrast, the binocular accommodative facility did not show statistically significant effects between film conditions, F(3.06, 88.80) = 1.21, P = 0.307, ηp2 = 0.040. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed a statistically significant difference between pre- and post-viewing of Spider-Man, P = 0.003 (Fig. 2). In addition, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA, using both film types and two colour conditions (colour and greyscale), indicated a significant main interaction effect on the changes in vergence facility, F(1,116) = 4.03, P = 0.047, ηp2 = 0.139. More information for descriptive values can be found at Supplementary information Table S2 and S3.
Box-plots for vergence facility and binocular accommodative facility for the pre-post measures in each film condition. G = Grey condition, *P < 0.05.
Regarding the post-viewing VAS scale, there was a statistically significant difference between the four film conditions, F(1.94,56.46) = 11.53, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.284. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed significant differences between Spider-Man and Totoro, P = 0.004, Spider-Man and Totoro G, P = 0.002, Spider-Man G and Totoro, P = 0.017, and Spider-Man G and Totoro G, P = 0.016. Similarly, the post-viewing CVS-Q showed significant differences between conditions, F(2.01, 60.59) = 7.63, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.241. Significant effects were found between Spider-Man and Totoro, P = 0.008, Spider-Man and Totoro G, P = 0.001, and Spider-Man G and Totoro, P = 0.025, but not between Spider-Man G and Totoro G, P = 0.081 (Fig. 3). More information for descriptive values can be found at Supplementary information, Tables S4 and S5.

The last questionnaire recalling which film conditions experienced the most visual fatigue was filled out by 24 participants (6 missed) and showed that participants experienced more visual fatigue during Spider-Man (41.70%), then Totoro in greyscale (29.20%), Spider-Man in greyscale (20.80%) and finally Totoro (8.30%). In addition, the analysis from rating all four film conditions at the end of the measures showed that Spider-Man experienced more visual fatigue, mean and standard deviation 2.20 ± 2.23, range from 0 to 7, then Spider-Man greyscale, 2.12 ± 2.40, range from 0 to 8, Totoro greyscale, 1.70 ± 2.31, range from 0 to 8 and Totoro, 1.58 ± 1.66, range from 0 to 5, without any statistical significance, F(3,69) = 1.28, P = 0.287, ηp2 = 0.053.

## Colour and scene dynamics between ‘Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse’ and ‘My Neighbour Totoro’
The comparative analysis of colour between Spider-Man and My Neighbour Totoro revealed distinct patterns in illuminance (measured in lux), with slightly darker values and higher variability observed in Spider-Man (Supplementary information, Table S6), although the differences did not reach statistical significance (Mann-Whitney test, P = 0.125). Similarly, colour temperature exhibited comparable trends, with greater variability observed in Spider-Man compared to Totoro, yet without statistical significance (Mann-Whitney test, P = 0.173). Analysis of the temporal variability for both measures and films demonstrated that Spider-Man exhibited peaks in illuminance and colour temperature, in contrast to Totoro, which displayed more stable measures over time. Figures including CIE 1931 diagram, illuminance, colour temperature, and time variations through film can be found in Supplementary Information Figure S1.
The obtained results related to scene dynamics revealed significant differences in the editing techniques of the two films. Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse features a much higher number of shots with very short durations compared to My Neighbour Totoro, which has fewer and longer shots. The median duration per shot in Spider-Man was 1,2 s, while in Totoro was 4,4 s, and the ASL was 5,54 and 2,04 fps, respectively. From these and other metrics shown in Supplementary Information Table S7 and Table S8, it can be inferred that the distribution of shot durations in Spider-Man gives place to fast scenes with very frequent visual changes. This is reinforced by a substantial proportion of shots (42%) lasting less than one second and containing numerous clusters of consecutive short-duration shots, enhancing the sense of action and urgency. On the other hand, My Neighbour Totoro uses longer shots and a slower pace, creating a more relaxed visual flow. Regarding VAI index, in Totoro, motion predominates, with few camera movements, while Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse has a significantly higher visual activity with many fast, long-range camera movements (Supplementary Information, Figures S6 to S11).

## Discussion
The findings of this study have significant implications for the production of animated films. The results demonstrate that the visual aesthetic characteristics of films, such as colour and scene dynamism, have a significant impact on visual fatigue in young people with normal vision. The comparative analysis between the films, Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse and My Neighbour Totoro, revealed notable differences in the colour palette and scene dynamism. Then, the selection of these films allows for an in-depth analysis of how different animation techniques and visual styles impact viewer perception and well-being. These differences are crucial for understanding the spectrum of visual and cognitive responses that animated films can evoke in viewers.
Vergence facility and binocular accommodation facility are related to visual fatigue; the more visual fatigue, the lower the performance in those tests10–12. At the physiological level, it was observed that watching Spider-Man caused a statistically significant decrease in vergence facility and binocular accommodation facility compared to Totoro, indicating greater objective visual fatigue. It is important to note that the differences in objective visual fatigue are low because those films were viewed in 2D, and some studies have noted more differences in virtual reality and 3D films24,25. A reduction of vergence facility of 3 cpm was found after viewing colour Spider-Man, which was much higher than after viewing Totoro in colour, less than 1 cpm. Binocular accommodative facility decreased 1 cpm after viewing Spider-Man in colour, and no differences were found in the other viewing conditions. In addition, the high variability in the objective tests explains the statistical but not clinical differences in this study. Although the change in these parameters is not clinically significant, it is important to note that some patients with low vergence and binocular accommodative facility can experience more visual fatigue after watching films such as Spider-Man9. Another factor to consider is the possible difference in visual fatigue after viewing these films in a cinema compared to at home. A larger visual field and less accommodative demand are present in a cinema. Future studies should clarify the relationship between these factors and different films on visual fatigue.
Moreover, the two subjective scales rating the visual fatigue after each film condition indicated that high scene dynamism was the major contributor to increased visual fatigue after watching films such as Spider-Man. Spider-Man and Totoro showed a statistical difference in the VAS and CVS-Q scales, but only in the VAS scale between the films in greyscale. Based on our data, both objective and subjective symptoms of visual fatigue indicated that Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse induces more visual fatigue than My Neighbour Totoro. We observed significant differences in vergence facility before and after watching Spiderman in greyscale; however, no significant changes were found in accommodative facility, likely due to the high variability in the measurements. Additionally, the subjective questionnaires did not reveal any significant differences between the same film in colour and greyscale, but they did show greater differences between Spiderman and Totoro.
Our detailed analysis of visual dynamism, utilising advanced computational methods for scene segmentation and shot length analysis, revealed significant differences between the two films. Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse features a considerably higher number of short-duration shots and frequent scene changes, contributing to its fast-paced nature. Specifically, the median shot duration in Spider-Man was 12 s, with 42% of the shots lasting less than one second. In stark contrast, My Neighbour Totoro exhibited a median shot duration of 44 s and significantly fewer short-duration shots. The rapid succession of images in Spider-Man likely overwhelms the visual processing system, leading to greater visual fatigue. These results are consistent with film studies that indicate a trend toward shorter and more frequent cuts in contemporary editing22,23. The fast pace of Spider-Man aligns with trends observed in contemporary action and animated films, where increased shot frequency is used to enhance the sense of action, urgency, and excitement26. However, animated films should be aware that although increasing the shot frequency could increase the excitement and joy during the film, it can also increase visual fatigue symptoms afterwards.
The VAI index, which evaluates both motion (movement of characters or elements on a static background) and movement (camera movements or changes in focal distance), also showed significant differences between the two films. Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse demonstrated significantly higher visual activity, with approximately 28% of the shots containing fast, long-range camera movements. In contrast, My Neighbour Totoro had only 7% of its shots with camera movements, predominantly slow and short-range. This finding is crucial because a high VAI index, particularly for fast camera movements, has been shown to directly affect visual fatigue22,27. The high frequency of these movements in Spider-Man indicates that viewers are subjected to continuous changes in visual stimuli, which can exhaust their visual processing system, and induce more visual fatigue.
In addition to visual dynamism, and probably to a more limited effect, illuminance and colour temperature have a higher temporal variability and more pronounced peaks in the case of Spider-Man colourimetric features compared to My Neighbour Totoro. Based on the colour analysis in this study, the exact contribution of colour to visual fatigue remains unclear. The results obtained in this study are in line with other previous works, where it has been seen that increasing colour saturation can be a source of visual discomfort, based on the response of some questionnaires and the measurement of brain activity28. However, although a significant main effect of colour and films was found on vergence facility, there were no subjective differences (VAS and CVS-Q) between films viewed in colour and greyscale. Hence, the independent contribution of colour to the overall visual fatigue needs thourough investigation in the case of animated films, and future work with more complex colour analysis would be valuable to clarify this relationship.
Overall, these findings have significant implications for the production of animated films. Filmmakers should also consider the potential impact of visual aesthetics on viewers’ visual health. While fast-paced editing and dynamic camera movements can enhance a film’s excitement and engagement, they also increase the risk of visual fatigue. Incorporating periods of visual rest, using longer shot durations, and reducing the frequency of intense visual stimuli could help mitigate these effects29. Furthermore, understanding the balance between artistic creativity and viewer comfort is essential for producing films that are not only visually impressive films, but also consider viewer well-being. Future research should continue exploring the long-term effects of repeated exposure to fast-paced animations on visual health and investigate strategies to optimise visual ergonomics in film production.
In summary, to the best of our knowledge, this study demonstrates for the first time that watching animated films with highly vibrant colours and high scene dynamism induces visual fatigue in a young population with a normal visual system.
