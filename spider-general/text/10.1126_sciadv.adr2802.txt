Blue light restores functional circadian clocks in eyeless cave spiders.
Wang J, Chang J, Wang K, Liang B, Zhu Y, Liu Z, Liang X, Chen J, Peng Y, Agnarsson I, Li D, Liu J. (2025). Science advances. . doi:10.1126/sciadv.adr2802. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Blue light restores functional circadian clocks in eyeless cave spiders

## Abstract
AbstractEvolution in profound darkness often leads to predictable, convergent traits, such as the loss of vision. Yet, the consequences of such repeated evolutionary experiments remain obscure, especially regarding fundamental regulatory behaviors like circadian rhythms. We studied circadian clocks of blind cave spiders and their sighted relatives. In the field, cave spiders exhibit low per expression and maintain constant activity levels. Curiously, their clocks are not permanently lost; exposure to monochromatic blue light restores both circadian gene expression and behavioral rhythms. Conversely, blocking blue light in sighted relatives induces an arrhythmic “cave phenotype.” Our RNA interference experiments suggest that clock genes regulate the rhythmicity of the huddle response, establishing a link between circadian gene networks and this behavioral rhythm. We demonstrate that circadian regulation is readily toggled and may play a latent role, even in constant darkness. Overall, our study expands understanding of circadian clock variations and paves the way for future research on the maintenance of silent phenotypes.

## INTRODUCTION
Organisms maintain circadian rhythms to synchronize essential biological processes with the external environment’s periodicity, ultimately enhancing their fitness (1). Circadian rhythms have evolved to coordinate physiological processes and behavioral activities with the 24-hour day-night cycle (2, 3). Light serves as a central modulator of circadian rhythms, playing a critical role in regulating organismal functionality (4, 5). Evolution in the dark provides a compelling example of predictable evolution, underlined by repeated convergent loss of eyes in multiple unrelated cave lineages. It has been widely assumed that animals—especially true troglobionts—residing in perpetually dark cave environments should experience relaxed selection for maintenance of circadian rhythms and gradual loss of the genetic mechanisms of circadian control (6). However, ongoing research has provided ambivalent evidence regarding this hypothesis, leaving its implications unclear (7). The adaptive evolution of circadian rhythms in cave-dwelling (hence, simply “cave”) animals thus remains obscure, especially in invertebrates. Among cave arthropods, circadian rhythms of nearly 30 species have been studied, revealing diverse circadian patterns (7). However, much of this research has focused on locomotor activity under laboratory settings. Our understanding of circadian rhythms in natural cave habitats remains elementary (8). Rigorous field experiments may offer greater insights into whether extended periods out of resonance with the external cycle, typically resulting in convergent elimination of the primary light sense mechanism in “blind” troglobites, lead to adaptive or neutral changes or permanent abolition of circadian clock function. Previous research on circadian rhythms in credibly the best studied system, Mexican blind cave fish (Astyanax mexicanus), identified alterations in circadian rhythms due to increased expression of light-inducible genes in cave populations (9). However, subsequent studies suggested that genes associated with light induction were not more likely to be up-regulated in cave fish (10, 11). Therefore, it remains unclear whether the circadian clock of cave animals tends to be repressed or permanently lost, and how factors such as the lack of eyes and environmental light stimulus affect clock entrainment and genetic mechanism underlying core clock function.
Previous research has shown that blue light, particularly at a wavelength of 450 nm, exhibits the lowest attenuation coefficient in karst caves (12). Moreover, some studies have demonstrated that exposure to monochromatic blue light induces the phase advancement of circadian rhythms in Drosophila and entrains diel activity cycles in lobster (13, 14). Furthermore, our recent study demonstrated that the eyeless cave Leptonetela spiders retain phototransduction pathway genes, including the blue-sensitive r-opsin Rh2, suggesting that detecting blue light may be adaptive even in eyeless organisms [see (15) for details] and thus plays some role in controlling activity patterns in cave animals (15). However, the specific influences of blue light on the circadian rhythms of cave animals remain unexplored.
Leptonetela (Araneae: Leptonetidae) spiders are primarily found in cave entrances (hence “entrance species”) or within deep caves. Cave species live in environments of constant darkness and are mostly eyeless (some species with dramatically reduced eyes), while their closely related entrance species are exposed to relatively dim light and generally have intact eyes (Fig. 1, A to B, and fig. S1, A to D) (16). The diverse optical phenotypes of Leptonetela spiders predict variation in circadian patterns of activity and rest. Thus, the coexistence of related cave and entrance species provides a unique opportunity for comparative behavioral and molecular expression studies addressing the interplay between the evolution of visual systems and circadian rhythms in animals living in light-deprived environments. All Leptonetela species, like most araneomorph spiders (Infraorder: Araneomorphae), exhibit a prominent “huddle response” behavior known to be under circadian control. The duration of the huddle response reflects activity patterns. Long huddle durations are associated with low-intensity behavior such as intermittent rest periods during the day in nocturnal species, while short huddle durations are seen during high-intensity behavior such as active foraging generally during the day in diurnal species (17, 18). This prior knowledge facilitates the investigation of circadian rhythms under natural cave conditions in the field and allows us to mitigate potential confounding effects resulting from transitioning spiders from caves to laboratory settings.

We use a comparative approach to investigate potential evolutionary changes in behavioral and gene-expression aspects of circadian rhythms in cave spiders. Despite living in permanently dark cave environments and lacking eyes, they retain sensory mechanisms to detect blue light (15). Consequently, we address five nested focal questions: (i) Do cave spiders display circadian rhythms in their dark natural habitats? (ii) If not, is that a temporary consequence of the absence of light cues, or a permanent evolutionary loss of circadian systems (behavioral or through loss of functional genetic pathways), and are circadian genes under natural selection in these systems? (iii) If cave spiders retain light perception and circadian genes, can their circadian rhythms be restored under artificial light exposure? (iv) By removing light, can we experimentally induce the “cave phenotype”—the loss of rhythmicity—in an otherwise rhythmic species with fully functional eyes (an entrance species)? (v) Does the knockdown of key clock genes in entrance species directly cause a loss of rhythmicity in the huddle response indicating a causal relationship between the circadian gene network and the huddle response in Leptonetela spiders?

## RESULTS

## Cave spiders remain constant activity
To determine whether cave spiders maintain functional circadian clock outputs despite the perpetual darkness of their cave environment, we assessed the circadian rhythms of the huddle response in two eyeless cave species (Leptonetela sublunata and Leptonetela tetracantha) and their eyed relatives (one entrance species, Leptonetela mengzongensis) within their natural cave habitats (fig. S1, C and D, and table S1). We found no significant differences in huddle durations across both light and dark phases (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, D and J), suggesting that these two eyeless cave species maintain consistent activity levels regardless of their location within the cave (entrance or deep cave) or the time of day. Notably, almost all individuals from these eyeless cave species exhibited markedly reduced huddle durations, with some individuals demonstrating a complete absence of huddle response behavior in situ (Fig. 1D). In contrast, their eyed relatives exhibited pronounced circadian rhythms in huddle response at the cave entrance, with significantly longer huddle durations during nocturnal periods compared to diurnal periods (P < 0.001; Fig. 1, D and J). However, this rhythmic pattern dissipated when entrance spiders were placed in deeper cave zones, where they remained constantly active, showing no significant differences in huddle durations between nocturnal and diurnal phases (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, D and J). This investigation highlights adaptive modifications in circadian rhythm functionality among the three Leptonetela species, suggesting a potential attenuation or loss of circadian regulation in response to the constant darkness of their subterranean habitat.

## Cave spiders show constantly low expression of per in the field
To determine whether cave spiders exhibit alterations or a loss in the molecular pathways underlying circadian rhythms in response to perpetual darkness, we compared expression levels of circadian genes in adult specimens from both eyeless cave species and their eyed relatives. Individuals from three Leptonetela species were entrained separately to a light-dark cycle at the twilight zone of the cave entrance (LD), and the samples were collected at 6-hour intervals. After 2 days of sample collection under LD conditions, the individuals were placed separately into constant darkness at the dark zone of the deep cave (DD), with collection continuing for a further two cycles. Results revealed that in cave species, per expression levels were generally low compared to those in their closely related entrance species and did not exhibit rhythmic oscillations under both LD and DD conditions (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, E and J). The maximal expression levels recorded for cave species did not exceed the minimal levels noted for the entrance species. Conversely, entrance species maintained a robust amplitude rhythm in per expression under LD conditions, peaking at Zeitgeber time 18 (P < 0.05; Fig. 1E). Notably, expression levels of per were significantly higher at night compared to daytime levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 1J). This rhythmic expression persisted under DD conditions although with a gradual diminution in amplitude over time (P < 0.05; Fig. 1E).

## Both cave and entrance spiders exhibit substantial alterations in the expression patterns of tim and cry across contrasting environments
To investigate whether the observed alterations in per gene expression rhythms were attributable to modifications in the light input pathway between eyeless cave species and their eyed relatives in natural habitats, we analyzed the expression levels of timeless (tim) and cryptochrome (cry) in both periodic and field-collected samples via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Our analyses confirmed pronounced rhythmic expression of tim in entrance species under LD conditions (P < 0.05; Fig. 1F). Under DD conditions, this species exhibited a rhythm of lower amplitude (P < 0.05; Fig. 1F). Expression levels of tim were significantly higher at night compared to daytime levels under both LD and DD conditions (P < 0.05; Fig. 1J). In contrast, no significant rhythmic variation in tim expression was observed in the two cave species under either LD or DD conditions (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, F and J). Notably, tim expression levels were significantly higher in the cave species compared to the entrance species under DD (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were detected between the two cave species at any time point (P > 0.05; Fig. 1F).
Prior research indicates that while the TIM protein is not directly light responsive, CRY (400 to 500 nm) that is activated by blue light (19) initiates TIM degradation (20). In this study, cry expression exhibited a significant high-amplitude rhythm in the entrance species under LD conditions and a rhythm of slightly lower amplitude under DD conditions (P < 0.05; Fig. 1G). Expression levels of cry were significantly higher in light compared to dark levels (P < 0.001; Fig. 1J). Unexpectedly, cry expression was arrhythmic under all conditions in the two cave species under DD, even after being entrained to a 12/12-hour LD cycle (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, G and J). Significant differences in cry expression between entrance and cave species were observed at nearly all time points under both LD and DD conditions (P < 0.05; Fig. 1G). These findings indicate a notable disruption of the light input pathway in cave Leptonetela species, certainly a consequence of eye loss, suggesting a profound impact of the cave environment on the circadian molecular mechanisms of these organisms.

## Expression of lark is significantly higher in eyeless cave species than in their eyed relatives
To investigate potential alterations in the molecular circadian rhythms across cave and entrance species, we executed a comparative transcriptomic assessment (tables S2 to S4). Using BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs) to assess the completeness of three de novo assembled transcriptomes (fig. S2A), we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between cave and entrance species, focusing on 2862 putative single-copy orthologous genes across the three Leptonetela species with high-quality alignments. Consequently, we separately identified 225 DEGs in the L. sublunata versus L. mengzongensis comparison and 715 DEGs in the L. tetracantha versus L. mengzongensis comparison (fig. S2B). Ultimately, we observed 106 overlapping DEGs in both cave species versus entrance species comparisons, with 77 DEGs (72.6%) annotated by the Swiss-Prot database (data S1). No Gene Ontology (GO) terms or Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were significantly enriched following corrections for multiple tests. Nevertheless, we found that the expression levels of the lark gene (analogous to RBM4 in mammals), which plays a pivotal role in circadian rhythm regulation, were significantly up-regulated more than twofold (log2FC = 1.4 and log2FC = 1.1 in the two comparisons, respectively) in cave species (fig. S2, C and D). Subsequently, we quantified the expression levels of the lark gene in the above periodic and field samples using qRT-PCR. Our investigation into the rhythmicity of lark expression under both LD and DD conditions revealed no significant oscillatory patterns in any of the three Leptonetela species (P > 0.05) nor significant differences between night and daytime conditions (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, H and J). However, lark expression was significantly up-regulated in entrance species under DD conditions compared to LD cycles (P < 0.001; Fig. 1K). In contrast, no significant differences in lark expression between LD and DD conditions were observed in cave species (P > 0.05; Fig. 1K). Notably, while lark expression did not oscillate in any of the spiders, it was up-regulated in cave species compared to entrance species under LD conditions (P < 0.05; Fig. 1H).

## Expression of double-time is strongly repressed in cave species
Could variations in the expression of per gene between cave and entrance species be attributed to alterations in the core clock expression pathway? Previous investigations have established that the PER protein is phosphorylated by the DOUBLETIME (DBT) kinase, leading to its degradation (21, 22). In addition, it has been found that in Drosophila melanogaster, the LARK protein can inhibit the DBT-mediated degradation of the PER protein (23). This raises the question of whether the expression pattern of dbt is modified in response to elevated lark levels in cave species. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the expression levels of dbt in the aforementioned periodic samples using qRT-PCR. Our analysis revealed that dbt expression did not exhibit rhythmic patterns between differing light conditions in any of the three Leptonetela species (P > 0.05; Fig. 1, I and J). Throughout, the eyeless cave species had lower dbt expression levels than their eyed relatives (P < 0.05; Fig. 1I). Entrance species showed reduced dbt expressions under DD compared to LD (P < 0.001; Fig. 1L), while expression levels did not change in the two cave species (P > 0.05; Fig. 1L). Together, our findings suggest that dbt expression is substantially suppressed in cave species, potentially linked to elevated lark levels. Hence, adaptive changes in circadian clock in response to dark cave environments may be modulated at the molecular level by intricate interlinked alterations in gene expression patterns. This highlights the complex interplay between genetic regulation and environmental adaptation in subterranean life forms.

## Clock genes of cave spiders are under purifying selection
To examine whether natural selection acts on clock genes in cave spiders, a study was conducted on four clock genes in five eyeless cave species and their five closely related entrance species. First, assuming a uniform rate of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions (ω) across all branches, model A (Table 1), which allowed ω to be estimated, outperformed model B, where ω was fixed at 1. For all four clock genes, ω was significantly less than 1 (P < 0.001) (model A versus model B in Table 1), suggesting strong purifying selection across all Leptonetela species. Second, allowing ω to vary between entrance and cave species (model C) did not significantly improve the data fit (P > 0.05; A versus C; Table 1), indicating that purifying selection is similar in both environments. Third, model C significantly outperformed fit model D, which assumed that the ω values for cave species were equal to 1 for the four tested clock genes, suggesting that the ω values for these genes in cave species were significantly lower than 1 (P < 0.001; D versus C; Table 1). However, when comparing model E, which allowed each branch to have its own ω value, with model A, no significant improvement in fit was observed for any of the clock genes except for the per gene (P > 0.05; A versus E; Table 1), suggesting minimal variation in ω among the cave species branches. In addition, the RELAX tool (v.3.3.1) was used to assess selection intensity in cave species (test branches) versus entrance species (reference branches) (24). The comparison of the alternative and null models showed no relaxation of selection pressure in cave species relative to entrance species (P > 0.05; table S5). Thus, purifying selection on clock genes is as strong in blind, arrhythmic cave spiders as it is in the eyed, rhythmic entrance spiders.

## The downstream rhythmic behaviors and physiological processes remain regulated by the circadian clock under DD conditions in the entrance species
To explore potential events in downstream rhythmic behaviors and physiological processes regulated by the circadian clock, we conducted a time-series transcriptome analysis on the entrance species (L. mengzongensis) under different light conditions (under LD cycle followed by DD condition) (fig. S3, A to C, and table S6), and we examined temporal variations in gene expression at each time point. This experiment investigated the putative first steps in cave adaptation in Leptonetela spiders when they lose critical light cues upon colonizing caves. Our results showed that 16,524 expressed unigenes were classified into 12 primary model profiles, and a total of 2459 genes revealed significantly time-dependent regulation (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A and table S7). GO terms annotation analysis confirmed the involvement of various GO terms, including cellular process (GO:0008152), metabolic process (GO:0008152), responses to stimuli (GO:0050896), etc. (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, KEGG pathway annotation revealed that some of the temporally regulated genes were associated with nervous system–dopaminergic synapse (map04728), lipid metabolism pathways (map00565), etc. (Fig. 2B), indicating that circadian control of neural activities and metabolic processes persists in entrance species, even under dark conditions (akin to conditions faced after cave colonization). In addition, our results identified two candidate genes, Nca and NFIL3, which exhibited notable time-dependent regulation (Fig. 2, C and D). Our annotation results revealed that the Nca gene is linked to the positive regulation of the circadian sleep/wake cycle (GO:0045938) and the negative regulation of neuronal signal transduction (GO:1902848). In contrast, the NFIL3 gene is associated with circadian rhythm (map04710), circadian rhythm-fly (map04711), and lipid metabolism (map00565). Together, these findings suggest that the rhythmic expression of clock genes, as well as the regulation of downstream rhythmic events, is temporarily preserved in eyed entrance species, even under constant darkness, and is not immediately disrupted. In addition, we examined the periodicity of expression of other clock genes (fig. S3D), which exhibited expression patterns similar to those of the previously analyzed clock genes, further supporting the reliability of our time-series transcriptome analysis.

## Blue light restored rhythmicity of huddle response and rekindled circadian genes expression in the cave spiders
Our previous research revealed that eyeless cave spiders retain the ability to detect blue light (15). To test whether the absence of circadian rhythms in these spiders is due to disrupted clock gene entrainment caused primarily by the lack of blue light, we conducted an experiment to assess whether exposure to monochromatic blue light–dark cycle (BL-D) and constant darkness in the laboratory (DD-L) could rekindle circadian genes expression and restore huddle response rhythms in two cave species. Behavioral assays demonstrated that under BL-D conditions, both eyeless cave species significantly reinstated circadian rhythms in their huddle behavior. They displayed longer huddle durations during the dark phase compared to the light phase (P < 0.001; Fig. 3, A and B). However, when transferred to DD-L conditions, the rhythmic oscillations of huddle response disappeared, mirroring the behavior observed in entrance species within deep cave environments. Under these conditions, no significant differences in huddle durations between the light and dark phases were observed (P > 0.05; Fig. 3, A and B).

To confirm whether blue light could establish circadian genes expression in eyeless cave spiders, we performed qRT-PCR analyses on periodic samples. Results showed significant circadian oscillations in the expression of per, tim, and cry in two cave species under BL-D conditions, with significant differences between the light and dark phases for these genes (P < 0.001; Fig. 3, C to H). These rhythmic patterns persisted even when transplanted to the DD-L conditions, although the amplitude gradually diminished over time (P < 0.05; Fig. 3, C to H). Significant differences in clock genes expression between BL-D/DD-L and LD/DD conditions were observed at most time points (P < 0.05; Fig. 3, C to H). No oscillatory patterns were detected in the expression of lark and dbt under either BL-D or DD-L conditions, with no significant differences between the light and dark phases (P > 0.05; Fig. 3, I to L). However, both cave species exhibited substantially lower expression levels of lark and higher levels of dbt under BL-D/DD-L compared to LD/DD at all time points. In addition, lark expression increased and dbt expression decreased under BB-L compared DD-L conditions (P < 0.001; Fig. 3, I to L). These findings collectively confirm that exposure to monochromatic blue light can effectively rekindle circadian gene expression and restore rhythmic behavior in cave species. This underscores that: (i) The circadian rhythm mechanism is preserved, although functionally silent in the cave environment, and (ii) blue light plays a crucial role in circadian rhythm entrainment in these cave spiders.

## The entrance species maintained constant activity after blocking blue light in the laboratory
To elucidate the impact of light, particularly blue light, on the circadian rhythms of cave spiders, we conducted an investigation into both huddle response and molecular circadian rhythms under conditions of full-spectrum light (FSL) and blue-light blocking (BBL) in a controlled laboratory setting. Our observations indicated that the entrance species exhibited distinct diurnal circadian rhythms in huddle response behavior under FSL conditions, as evidenced by significantly longer huddle durations during the dark phase compared to the light phase (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A). Conversely, under BBL conditions, the entrance species displayed constant activity and arrhythmic huddle response behavior, with no significant difference in huddle durations detected between light and dark phases (P > 0.05; Fig. 4A).

Further investigation into the molecular basis of these behaviors was performed by quantifying the expression levels of core circadian clock genes (per, cry, and tim) using qRT-PCR analysis of periodic samples. Under FSL conditions, the entrance species showed rhythmic oscillations in the expression of these genes, with significant differences observed between the light and dark phases for all three genes (P < 0.05; Fig. 4, B to D). In stark contrast, exposure to BBL conditions resulted in the abrogation of rhythmic expression patterns for per, cry, and tim (P > 0.05; Fig. 4, B to D), coupled with a significant reduction in expression levels of each gene under BBL compared to FSL conditions (P < 0.001; Fig. 4, B to D), and the elimination of rhythmic light and dark phases for these genes under BBL conditions (P > 0.05; Fig. 4, B to D). These findings underscore the pivotal role of blue light in the entrainment of circadian rhythms in entrance species, particularly through the modulation of light-sensitive genes tim and cry.
To determine whether blue light similarly influences the expression of the lark gene, we assessed the expression levels of lark and dbt under both FSL and BBL conditions using qRT-PCR. Results revealed that the entrance species did not exhibit oscillatory expression patterns of the lark and dbt in laboratory conditions, mirroring field observations, both under FSL and BBL conditions (P > 0.05; Fig. 4, E and F). Intriguingly, under BBL conditions, the entrance species exhibited significantly elevated expression of lark but reduced expression of dbt compared to FSL conditions at all time points (P < 0.01; Fig. 4, E and F), mirroring expression patterns observed in situ between cave entrance and deep cave environments. Consequently, these results suggest that the absence of blue light markedly up-regulates lark expression and subsequently down-regulates dbt expression in entrance species, a regulatory mechanism potentially shared by their cave counterparts residing in perpetually dark cave environments.

## Induction of clock genes depends on blue light intensity in entrance species in the laboratory
To further test the hypothesis that blue light modulates circadian rhythms in cave spiders, we examined the impact of varying blue light intensities on the expression of core circadian clock genes. The entrance species were subjected to a preexperimental entrainment period of 7 days in a 12/12 LD cycle under three distinct intensities of blue light (0 μW cm−2, analogous to the previously mentioned BBL condition; 1.0 and 45.0 μW cm−2). Subsequently, we checked the expression levels of the aforementioned clock genes in periodic samples using qRT-PCR. Our analysis revealed that the expressions of per, cry, and tim exhibited rhythmic oscillations in the entrance species under both 1.0 and 45.0 μW cm−2 blue light intensities, with significant differences between the light and dark phases for all three clock genes (P < 0.001; Fig. 4, G to I). Notably, the highest levels of expression for per, cry, and tim were observed under the 45.0 μW cm−2 intensity, while the lowest were recorded under the BBL condition among the three tested blue light intensities (P < 0.05; Fig. 4, G to I). Meanwhile, no rhythmic expression patterns were detected for lark and dbt under the BBL, 1.0 μW cm−2, and 45.0 μW cm−2 conditions (P > 0.05; Fig. 4, J and K). Intriguingly, under the 45.0 μW cm−2 condition, the entrance species exhibited the lowest expression levels of lark but the highest of dbt, and the converse was true under BBL, with significant differences at each time point among the three blue light intensities (P < 0.05; Fig. 4, J and K). These findings provide additional evidence that blue light substantial influences the circadian rhythms of cave spiders. Moreover, our results suggest that the absence of circadian rhythm observed in cave spiders could result from alterations in the light input pathway, particularly due to changes the expression of light-sensitive genes cry and lark, consequent to the absence of blue light in deep cave environments.

## Down-regulation of cry expression disrupts rhythmicity of the huddle response
To further verify the function of clock genes in the light input pathway that synchronizes the circadian pacemaker for the huddle response in cave Leptonetela spiders, we specifically knocked down the expression of cry in entrance species under an LD cycle. Our results revealed that rhythmic oscillations of cry were abolished after 4 days of RNA interference (RNAi) treatment, with expression levels remaining persistently low (P > 0.05; Fig. 5A), similar to those observed in cave species. Moreover, alongside the constant low expression of cry, the expression of tim and per also lost their rhythmicity, remaining consistently low under the LD cycle (P > 0.05; Fig. 5, B and C). Notably, following the knockdown of cry, entrance species also exhibited a loss of rhythmicity in the huddle response (P > 0.05; Fig. 5D). These findings suggest that the down-regulation of cry expression in entrance species disrupts both the rhythmic expression of key clock genes and the rhythmicity of the huddle response, highlighting the critical regulatory role of clock genes in controlling behavioral rhythms.

## DISCUSSION
Circadian rhythms are a ubiquitous feature of animal life, aligning activity and rest cycles with the 24-hour day-night cycle (25). The persuasive presence of these internal clocks across the animal kingdom underlines their pivotal importance in regulating biological processes in nature. However, the evolutionary consequences of living in perpetual darkness for circadian clocks remain poorly understood (7). In constant darkness, the absence of environmental cues, such as light, could lead to the disruption of these internal clocks. Moreover, animals that have evolved troglobiotic traits, such as eye loss, may have lost the ability to detect light cues altogether. A key question, therefore, is: What are the consequences of life in perpetual darkness and the loss of eyes on the rhythmic modulation of animal behavior and on the genetic pathways that govern circadian clocks? To address this and other fundamental issues surrounding cave adaptations and circadian rhythms, we interrogate five core questions: (i) Do cave spiders lack circadian rhythms in their naturally dark habitats? (ii) If so, is this due to the temporary absence of light cues or the evolutionary loss of circadian systems, and are circadian genes under natural selection in these systems? Previous studies have shown that eyeless cave spiders can still detect blue light (15), leading to the next question: (iii) Do they retain functional circadian genes and regulatory mechanisms, and can circadian rhythms be restored under artificial light? (iv) Can cave phenotypes be experimentally induced in normally rhythmic, visually oriented entrance spiders? (v) Does the periodic expression of clock genes directly regulate the rhythmicity of the huddle response indicating a causal relationship between the circadian gene network and this behavior?
To address the first core question, we examined the circadian rhythms of huddle response behavior in cave spiders within their natural habitats (fig. S1, C and D). Our results suggest that, although cave species retain the ability to detect light (15), they have lost behavioral circadian rhythms (Fig. 1D). Consequently, they permanently operate at “daytime” activity levels of typical diurnal animals, lacking the longer huddle durations characteristic of their rest at night. This indicates an adaptive modification of circadian clocks, where eyeless cave species in their natural habitat show no detectable rhythmic behavior. The diurnal huddle response pattern in all Leptonetela species is reduced compared to, for example, nocturnal orb-weaving spiders (17, 18). This difference suggests that the huddle response, which serves as a predator defense behavior and also reflects predation activity, exhibits phylogenetic plasticity. This plasticity likely reflects adaptive evolution to natural habitats, potentially enhancing survival and increasing fitness. On the one hand, this difference may result from cave adaptation of Leptonetela spiders. Most Leptonetela species inhabit cave entrances and deep caves, where nocturnal animals like bats and predatory spiders serve as their primary predators, while diurnal natural enemies are lacking (26). On the other hand, regional differences and differing environmental pressures may influence predation pressure. Leptonetela spiders are mostly distributed in the karst landforms of the Balkan Peninsula in Europe and southwest China (27) and may face different circadian changes in predation pressure compared to other regions. These findings promote the need for further research into the identities and behaviors of predators influencing both diurnal and nocturnal spiders. Understanding how these predator-prey interactions affect the circadian rhythms and predation behaviors of animals will help reveal their adaptive evolution in various environments. Furthermore, when eyed entrance species were transferred to deep cave environments, they exhibited arrhythmic behavior similar to that of eyeless, fully cave-adapted spiders. In contrast, the rhythmic huddle response in non-cave orb-weaving spiders persisted when they were transferred from an LD cycle to DD conditions (17, 18). Therefore, these findings lead us to hypothesize that these entrance species may experience a regression in circadian regulation of their huddle responses when exposed to cave environments.
To address our second core question, we investigated whether cave spiders exhibit alterations or a loss in the molecular pathways underlying circadian rhythms in response to perpetual darkness. Our results revealed that per expression levels in cave species were generally low in original habitats and lacked rhythmic oscillations (Fig. 1E). The maximal expression levels recorded for cave species were lower than the minimal expression levels found in their closely related entrance species (Fig. 1E). Transporting blind cave species to the cave entrance twilight zone—where their eyed relatives have typical circadian rhythms—did not restore activity oscillations. These findings suggest that cave spiders have adaptively lost rhythmic behavior in favor of “constant activity” throughout 24-hour day periods. Similar patterns have been previously reported for cave fish populations (A. mexicanus), from both behavioral (10, 28) and molecular perspectives (9), following long-term evolution in perpetually dark environments. For example, these cave fish show constant low expression levels of per1 (homologous to per in invertebrates) in natural populations (9) and sleep reduction (10, 28). Moreover, we observed a difference in the strength of the huddle response between entrance species and cave species, maybe resulting from the lower predation pressure in the cave environment, as reduced predation pressure is one of the major drivers of cave colonization (29). While this decreased predation pressure allows cave spiders to focus more on foraging, intraspecific competition and the scarcity of prey in resource-limited caves impose substantial foraging pressure. Consequently, the interplay of reduced predation pressure and heightened foraging pressure drives the constant activity of cave spiders, aiding their survival and reproduction. However, to our knowledge, this study presents the evidence for “constant behavioral activity” based on both behavioral and molecular data in cave animals under natural conditions.
Caves are generally considered extreme environments that in temperate environments are characterized by low abundance and diversity of organisms (30). Spiders, as predators in caves, face severe prey shortages and intense intraspecies or interspecies competition (30). Constant activity may increase fitness in cave Leptonetela spiders, forgoing rest in their search for sparse food. Our results support the previous view that ecological factors are the main driving forces shaping a species’ sleep phenotype, as observed in cave fish. Unexpectedly, transporting eyed entrance species L. mengzongensis to deep cave environments displayed arrhythmic behavior similar to that of fully adapted cave spiders. Furthermore, previous research has shown that environmental factors can exert an inhibitory or masking effect on the expression of an organism’s intrinsic circadian rhythms, a phenomenon known as the masking effect on circadian behavior (31, 32). This effect can obscure the circadian rhythms of behaviors such as the huddle response, making intrinsic rhythms unobservable under certain external conditions. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that a circadian masking effect is influencing our observations. We propose that this masking effect of circadian rhythms could represent an adaptive response to the ecological demands of life in the twilight zones of cave entrances, where inconsistent light cues may contribute to a regression in circadian control mechanisms.
The loss of circadian rhythms in cave spiders is likely a consequence of either (i) the lack of clock entrainment, such as light, or (ii) the actual repression of core clock function (9, 10), or (iii) both. To test this hypothesis, we compared the alterations in circadian genetic pathways, especially the light input pathways, between the naturally occurring cave and entrance species and conducted a transposing experiment exposing two cave species to natural (twilight) light of the entrance habitats. Previous studies have shown that the CRY protein is activated by light to trigger TIM degradation and then produce the characteristic circadian oscillations of PER in Drosophila’s clock system (33, 34). In natural habitats, the cave species displayed non-cyclic tim and constantly low-level cry expressions, while entrance species showed robust circadian rhythms in these two genes (Fig. 1, F and G). In addition, both cave species showed suppressed dbt expressions and higher lark expressions compared with the entrance species (Fig. 1, H and I). Previous investigations have suggested that the LARK protein may be light-inducible in mammals and arthropods (23, 35, 36). In Drosophila, the lark gene product can directly bind to the promoter region of the dbt gene that controls the accumulation and phosphorylation of the PER protein, promoting its transcription (23). Increased LARK protein can delay nuclear degradation of the PER protein, with overexpression causing behavioral arrhythmias (23, 36). Meanwhile, Huang et al. (23) also hypothesized that the LARK protein may be light inducible in addition to showing circadian variation, as they found a dbt transcript (dbt-RE) whose translation was light inducible and affected by LARK levels. On this basis, we hypothesize that the absence of light triggers elevated lark expression, which in turn inhibits dbt expression, leading to low levels of per in cave spider species. Field monitoring data indicated that temperature and humidity levels were relatively stable in both cave entrance and deep cave environments, with only light intensity at cave entrance exhibiting periodic variations (fig. S1, E and F). These results indicate that although circadian patterns exhibit lower activation levels in cave species, likely due to the absence of light in their natural habitats, the key genetic pathways regulating circadian clocks are still retained.
Intuitively, it might be expected that the circadian clocks of cave spiders have experienced relaxed selective pressure in perpetual darkness, while our results discussed above may suggest otherwise. In short, we identified purifying selection on all tested clock genes of cave spiders, suggesting that functional circadian clock systems are under strong functional constraints and must play an important role (Table 1). Except for the per gene, as shown in Table 1, all other tested clock genes exhibited similar levels of purifying selection in both cave and entrance species. However, the per gene showed branch-specific selection pressures, suggesting variability in its evolutionary constraints specifically among cave Leptonetela species. Parallel exploratory analysis using a partitioned model also failed to detect any evidence of relaxed selection within these genes (P > 0.05; table S5). Therefore, the low expression levels of per and constant activity of cave Leptonetela species are readily explained by the absence of environmental time cues but not the actual repression or loss of core clock function. In addition, the divergence time of the two cave species (L. sublunata and L. tetracantha) from their nearest sister clades of entrance species about 17.1 and 4.2 million years, indicating that entrance species independently moved into caves at 4 million ages (fig. S1A). This finding is unexpected given that the absence of light and circadian entrainment is not temporary but experienced through the lifespan of individuals, from one generation to another, spanning evolutionary timescales sufficient for the emergence of a plethora of troglobiotic traits that are intrinsically tied to life in perpetual darkness. In agreement with our results, Friedrich et al. (37) detected the expression of the complete circadian clock gene network in the troglobiont beetle, suggesting that these regulating pathways are genetically stable and that this stability may be critical for maintaining overall fitness.
The persistence of circadian regulatory systems in permanent darkness raises the question: How does evolution maintain light-triggered behavioral rhythms that remain unexpressed? It is plausible that key regulatory genes are preserved because of pleiotropy, suggesting that the circadian gene pathway may have alternative functions. We have also proposed that extraocular light detection may allow cave spiders to avoid arid environments near cave entrances, where survival rates are lower (15). This light detection could be linked to the same regulatory pathways, helping to maintain light-triggered mechanisms. Future research should focus on the fitness consequences of alterations or loss of circadian clocks and their regulatory genes. Such investigations may lead to the discovery of gene functions and interactions, as well as other physiological traits related to light detection or the regulation of behavior.
Our time-series transcriptome analysis enables us to distinguish genes directly regulated by circadian rhythms from those influenced by external or internal factors. By clustering gene expression profiles based on their temporal patterns, we revealed coregulated gene groups, providing deeper insights into the functional pathways governed by the circadian clock. Specifically, we observed time-dependent expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism, nervous system function, environmental adaptation, and signal transduction (Fig. 2, A to D). For instance, previous studies have shown that the Nca gene facilitates sleep by suppressing synaptic activity in neurons that promote arousal and wakefulness (38). Proper Nca function is essential for aligning sleep patterns with environmental LD cycles, optimizing behavior and physiology in response to daily fluctuations in light. In addition, Nca promotes sleep by inhibiting neurotransmitter release from circuits that promote activity and regulates motor control through dopaminergic pathways, preventing abnormal movements in both humans and Drosophila (38). Similarly, the NFIL3 gene has been shown to exhibit rhythmic expression in intestinal epithelial cells, orchestrating a circadian-regulated lipid metabolism pathway (39). This regulation is crucial for aligning metabolic processes with daily activities, optimizing energy utilization and storage, and maintaining metabolic homeostasis (39, 40). The interplay between circadian rhythms and lipid metabolism is vital for preventing metabolic disorders and managing energy trade-offs. These findings substantially enhance our understanding of the downstream metabolic processes and neural activities modulated by the circadian clock. They also help explain why entrance species maintain circadian clock gene expression in dark environments, even in the absence of rhythmic huddle response behavior.
To address our third core question, we tested whether the cave spiders still retain functional circadian clocks that could be triggered through extraocular detection of blue light demonstrated in our previous research (15). We conducted an artificial light induction experiment on two cave species in laboratory settings by exposing them to monochromatic blue light. The cave species exhibited robust circadian rhythms in all tested clock genes (per, tim, and cry) after exposure to BL-D conditions, even when subsequently transferred into DD-L conditions (Fig. 3, A to H). However, their behavioral rhythms disappeared in both species once transferred to DD-L conditions. These restored circadian clocks match those of eyed entrance species in their natural (twilight) habitats. Thus, we confirm that (i) through extraocular blue light perception (15), circadian clocks can be restarted and therefore that (ii) the expression of the arrhythmic cave phenotype results from the lack of environmental stimulus in the form of blue light. Previous research indicated that deep-sea organisms, despite the lack of sunlight, can still perceive residual blue light from bioluminescence or other sources, and even low levels of blue light can reset the internal clocks of these organisms, supporting the hypothesis that blue light plays a universal role in circadian regulation (41, 42). Our findings demonstrate that BL-D is capable of restoring disrupted circadian rhythms in eyeless cave spiders. Previous results (core question 2) further support the role of BL-D, showing that the cave phenotype is unaltered after entraining cave species to the twilight habitat of eyed entrance species. These entrance habitats have low blue light intensity, and we speculate that while light regulation of circadian clock genes is maintained in cave species, eye loss has resulted in reduced light perception. Together, these results demonstrate the need for further experimental studies in other animals to understand whether unexpressed but functional circadian clocks—truly “silent phenotypes”—are uncommon or perhaps represent another trademark of replicate convergent evolution in the dark. Our results could also find application in medicine as they invite potentially conducive exploration of the therapeutic potential of blue light for sleep disorders and cognitive impairments caused by circadian rhythm disruption.
To address our fourth core question, we further scrutinized the role of blue light in regulating the functional circadian clocks of cave Leptonetela species. In laboratory experiments, we were able to experimentally reproduce the cave phenotype not otherwise observed in these eyed species, simply by blocking blue light. In BBL experiments, these species showed (i) constant activity and reduced per expression (Fig. 4, A and B), (ii) disruption of rhythmic expression patterns for light-inducible genes cry and tim, and (iii) a notable reduction in expression levels of each gene compared to FSL (Fig. 4, C and D). This indicates that the absence of blue light interferes with the light input pathway of the circadian clock in entrance species. Moreover, under BBL condition, lark was present at substantially elevated levels, while dbt expression was reduced (Fig. 4, E and F). This suggests a previously unknown blue light sensitivity of the lark gene, necessitating further empirical validation to confirm its role and mechanism within the context of light perception and circadian regulation. Previous research has elucidated that light intensity plays a crucial role in modulating circadian rhythms (43). Similarly, our results confirm that the expression of circadian clock genes (per, tim, cry, lark, and dbt) is positively correlated with blue light intensity (BBL, 1.0 and 45.0 μW cm−2), but lark expression is negatively correlated with light intensity (Fig. 4, G to K). Clearly thus, typical circadian rhythms present in eyed entrance species can be repressed by removing blue light, reproducing the eyeless cave phenotype. Together, these results cement the critical role of blue-light intensity in regulating the expression and disruption of circadian rhythms in both eyed and blind spiders.
To address our fifth core question, we examined the rhythmic expression of clock genes and the daily patterns of the huddle response in entrance species following double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)–mediated knockdown. Our results indicate that down-regulation of the cry gene disrupts the oscillation patterns of other clock genes (per and tim), leading to a loss of rhythmicity in the huddle response. This finding suggests a causal relationship between clock genes and the huddle response. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that serotonin, which plays a key role in the light synchronization of circadian rhythms by influencing the light-induced degradation of the rhythmic protein TIM in Drosophila, markedly affects the huddle response in orb-weaving spiders Larinioides cornutus. This observation implies that the huddle response in these spiders also follows a circadian rhythm modulated by exogenous serotonin levels (44–46). Together, our findings further support the idea that the huddle response in cave Leptonetela spiders is regulated by the circadian clock. This suggests that the huddle response may serve as a behavioral readout for assessing circadian rhythms in cave-adapted, orb-weaving spiders.
Previous studies conducted under constant environments revealed the intrinsic adaptive value of circadian clocks but failed to address the frequent and complete loss of robust circadian rhythms in cave arthropods and fish living in constant environments (47). Here, we propose a potential hypothesis regarding the cave adaptation of the circadian clock mechanism in cave spiders: Cave spiders maintain constant activity due to reduced expression of per gene, which is attributed to the absence of blue light. This absence leads to alterations to light input pathways, characterized by (i) increased levels of the light-inducible gene lark, thereby repressing dbt expression, which controls the accumulation and phosphorylation of PER, and (ii) reduced expression of the light-inducible gene cry, which would disrupt the normal oscillations of TIM and PER (Fig. 5, E and F). We speculate that the mechanisms elucidated here may be more common in subterranean animals than previously thought. Here we demonstrate that (i) functional circadian clocks are silently retained in environment that permanently lacks the blue light cues necessary to engage them, and (ii) fully operational circadian clocks in eyed species evolving in lit environments can be “unplugged” by removing this critical blue light cue. The mechanisms elucidated in the current research have broad implications for understanding variation in circadian clocks among other animals exposed to varying light conditions, retention of light detection mechanisms despite evolutionary loss of eyes, and the evolutionary maintenance of a “silent phenotype” presumably invisible to natural selection. By extension, our key results also suggest that blue light merits critical evaluation as a potential adjunct therapy for circadian rhythm disorders in the future (48–50).

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Spider collection and maintenance
Three Leptonetela species were collected from three caves located in Guizhou Province, China (fig. S1B and table S1). The entrance-dwelling (hence simply “entrance”) species, L. mengzongensis (Fig. 1A), was collected from the twilight zone of the Mengzong Cave, while two cave-dwelling (hence, cave) species, L. sublunata and L. tetracantha (Fig. 1, B and C), were collected from the perpetually dark zones of Manwang Cave and Wengshuida Cave. We collected the spiders from their webs and individually housed them in 50-ml plastic tubes with moistened cotton to maintain humidity. Cave species were collected using head torches with dim light. For the field behavioral experiment, individuals were placed in their natural habitats. Subsequently, both entrance and cave species were transported to the laboratory at Hubei University, where they were maintained at 16° to 18°C under an LD 12:12 cycle (12 hours of light alternating with 12 hours of darkness) and constant darkness (DD). All studied individuals in the main text were adult females. The proportion of male individuals in our collected samples was too low (less than 5% based on field observations) to allow for meaningful inclusion in the analysis. The limited number of males prevented us from conducting reliable molecular and behavioral experiments across all three species. Consequently, we restricted our analysis to females only, as the insufficient sample size of males did not meet the statistical power required for valid comparisons. This study followed all relevant guidelines regarding the care and use of invertebrate study species (51).

## Behavioral assay
We conducted experiments to determine whether the huddle response of three Leptonetela species varied across the environmental light-dark cycle in twilight zone of cave entrance (LD) (fig. S1C) and the dark zone of the deep cave (DD) (fig. S1D). We used the “huddle response” assay, commonly used in many orb-weaving spiders, as huddle duration can indirectly reflect their activity levels (movie S1). Previous studies on orb-weaving spiders have shown that these species exhibit an active foraging period and an inactive period characterized by reduced aggressive and longer huddle durations when not foraging within a single circadian cycle (17, 18, 52). We gently transferred from spider individuals their containers into plastic bowls (4-cm diameter, 4.5 cm high) and lightly stimulated their pereiopods with a fine brush to initiate the huddle response. Upon stimulation, the spiders tightly folded their legs under the abdomen. Previous studies have shown that stimuli do not produce habituation for huddle response (44). We recorded the durations of the response using a stopwatch. Individuals that did not exhibit a huddle response after three attempts were assigned a score of 0 s; those that initiated a response but immediately broke out of it were assigned a score of 1 s, and response durations exceeding 3 min were recorded as 180 s. All dark observations were carried out under dim light conditions filtered with one-layer yellow Supergel filter 14.

## Huddle response behavior assay in natural caves and in the laboratory
We conducted field experiments at the collection sites of three natural caves in Guizhou Province, China, using plastic boxes and household items. The entrance and cave species under LD and DD conditions were individually placed in plastic bowls (4-cm diameter, 4.5 cm high) for at least 24 hours before testing in their original habitats. Ten spiders of each species were tested for 2 days in the twilight zone of the cave entrance before being transferred into the dark zone of the deep cave, where they were tested for huddle response for another 2 days. Testing for each species began at 08:00 a.m. (0.5 hours before dawn), with subsequent tests conducted every 4 hours. We also recorded the temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), and light intensity (l×) outside and inside the caves using a portable hygrothermograph (ASAIR) and illuminance meter (GMC-Instruments Co. Ltd.).
Adult individuals of two cave species, one with highly reduced eyes (L. sublunata) and one eyeless (L. tetracantha), were entrained under a periodic monochromatic BL-D (intensity: 45.0 μW cm−2) for 7 days in preparation for the experiment. After this, they were transferred to constant DD-L. Observations began at 08:00 a.m. (0.5 hours after lights on) and were tested every 4 hours. Ten spiders from each species were tested under BL-D for 2 days, followed by another 2 days under DD-L conditions to test their huddle responses.
Adult entrance individuals of L. mengzongensis were collected from natural caves, were brought into the laboratory, and maintained at 16° to 18°C. The experimental room was partitioned into two areas and carefully sealed against light leaks. We used two types of illumination in each chamber to provide a 12/12 LD cycle for at least 7 days before quantifying the huddle response. One chamber used artificial light with FSL (300 to 700 nm), while the other used a yellow filter to block monochromatic short wavelengths of BBL (Rosco Supergel filter 14; Rosco, Stamford, CT, U.S.A., 400 to 480 nm) (53). Entrance species were housed in these chambers, provided water twice a week, and fed fruit flies every other week. In both cases, 15 spiders were tested for 48 hours under LD conditions. Observations began at 08:00 a.m. (0.5 hours after lights on) and were conducted every 4 hours thereafter.
In addition, we set three different intensities of blue light (BBL, 1.0 and 45.0 μW cm−2) in the laboratory to determine the light responsiveness of the clock genes in entrance species (54). Entrance species were individually entrained on a 12/12 LD cycle for 7 days before the experiment under three different intensities of blue light in the laboratory. Observations began at 08:00 a.m. (0.5 hours after lights on) and were conducted every 4 hours thereafter.

## Taxon sampling and RNA extraction
Live adult female spiders were randomly sampled from three natural caves in Guizhou Province, China, including one entrance species (L. mengzongensis) and two cave species (L. sublunata and L. tetracantha), for comparative transcriptome analysis. The live spiders of the three Leptonetela species were brought into the laboratory and maintained at 16° to 18°C. Entrance and cave species placed under LD and DD conditions for at least 7 days, respectively, before the experiments. Total RNA was isolated from the cephalothorax of the three Leptonetela species using the TRIzol method (TransGen Biotech Co. Ltd), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each species had three biological replicates with three adults per replicate. Subsequently, RNA was quantified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and the quality and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). To select cDNA fragments of preferentially 200 to 250 base pairs (bp) in length, the library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA), and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (55). All libraries were commercially sequenced to generate paired-end reads of an average length of 150 bp on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 sequencing platform (Beijing Allwegene Technology Company Limited, China).

## Quality assessment, de novo assembly, and unigene annotation
We assessed the sequence quality of each RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) sample using FastQC (v.10.1) (56) and removed reads containing adapters, poly-N, and low-quality reads from the raw data using Trimmomatic (v.0.33) (57, 58). These clean reads were then de novo assembled into contigs with a minimum length of 201 nt for each of the three Leptonetela species by Trinity (v.2.8.5) with default parameters (59). The completeness of three de novo assembled transcriptomes was assessed by BUSCO using OrthoDB’s Arthropoda database of orthologous groups (n = 1013) (60). Clean reads from each sample were separately mapped to the Trinity contigs using RSEM (v. 1.3.1) (61). We applied a minimum expression filter of one fragment per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) and filtered out contigs with both FPKM values less than one. Contigs with the highest expression level in a contig cluster were selected as unigenes for downstream analyses. The unigenes were annotated using BLASTx (62) against multiple databases, including Swiss-prot, National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) nonredundant protein (NR), Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG), GO, Nucleotide Sequence Database (NT), KEGG Orthology (KO), and Protein family (Pfam) databases, with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−5. Similar unigenes numbers and the percentage of annotated unigenes suggest that these three transcriptomes are comparable.

## Ortholog identification
We determined the open reading frame for each unigene using TransDecoder (v.5.5.0) (63). Orthologous genes between each pair of species were identified using the INPARANOID programme (64). The pairwise orthologous genes of all three Leptonetela species were introduced into the MULTIPARANOID programme to generate multispecies orthologs. All putative orthologs were aligned by PRANK (v.170427) (65), and poorly aligned positions and divergent regions were removed by GBLOCKS (v.0.91b) (66) to achieve orthologous genes of the same length in three Leptonetela species. Alignments with an aligned region shorter than 100 nt were discarded (56).

## Differential expression analysis and GO annotation
To analyze the DEGs between cave and entrance species, we mapped clean reads from each species to single-copy orthologous genes to convert FPKM values using RSEM (v. 1.3.1) (61) to generate an expression matrix. For cross-species comparisons, we conducted a normalization process based on scaling factors generated from the interquartile range of conservatively expressed genes to avoid the influence of both highly and lowly expressed genes. Scaling factors were used to scale the raw counts of all genes in the expression matrix (57, 67). The raw counts of each sample were adjusted by the scaling factor as the final expression. All downstream analyses stemmed from pairwise transcriptomic comparisons (L. sublunata versus L. mengzongensis; L. tetracantha versus L. mengzongensis).
DESeq2 and edgeR, which showed low false-positive rates (68), were used for pairwise comparisons of differential expression analysis. The false discovery rate (FDR) was used as key parameters for DEG screening. Log2 fold change (Log2FC) and FDR values were computed for each gene. Genes with an FDR less than 0.05 and an absolute value of Log2FC more than 1 were considered differentially expressed. To identify candidate genes in regulating circadian rhythms, all DEGs were annotated using the GO database via DAVID Bioinformatics Resources (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) (v.6.8) and GOstats with the single-copy orthologous genes in three Leptonetela species identified by RNA-seq as the “background” (69, 70).

## Evolutionary analysis on clock genes in Leptonetela spiders
To confirm that clock genes in cave spiders are under purifying selection and potentially functional, and to explore whether there is differential selection between entrance and cave species, we used a likelihood-based approach to estimate ω values (nonsynonymous/synonymous rate ratio, dN/dS). The phylogenetic tree for Leptonetela species was based on our previously generated likelihood (ML) tree (15). In addition, raw transcriptome data of 10 Leptonetela species (Araneae: Leptonetidae) were downloaded from NCBI (PRJNA972491). We chose a compilation of conserved spider protein sequences as a source of query sequences (table S8). Nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) nucleotide substitution rates (ω) were estimated using the likelihood method in the CODEML program in PAML (v.4.1) (71). Likelihood ratio tests were conducted in the R program (v.4.0.5) (72). Furthermore, we used the RELAX program (v.2.5.48) to examine whether cave spiders have experienced relaxed selection pressure (24).

## Quantitative reverse transcription PCR
Three Leptonetela species were separately placed and entrained for 2 days under LD conditions before being transferred into DD for a further 2 days in the field. We sampled populations of the three Leptonetela species at 6-hour intervals. The spiders were placed in individual tubes and subsequently flash-frozen liquid nitrogen. Two cave species were entrained to a 12/12 LD cycle under BL-D for 7 days before being transferred to constant darkness. Individual spiders were then collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen at 6-hour intervals. In the laboratory, adult entrance species were maintained at 16° to 18°C and exposed to a 12/12 LD cycle under FSL and BBL conditions for 7 days before being transferred into liquid nitrogen, respectively. We extracted total RNA from multiple individuals of each species using TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech Co. Ltd), following the manufacturer’s protocol. We performed three biological replicates with four adults per replicate. A total of 2 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed to obtain cDNA using Hifair III First Strand cDNA Synthesis Super Mix (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.
Each species was analyzed in triplicate by qRT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, China). The specific primer of target gene was designed on the basis of the locations with common sequences in the three Leptonetela species derived from our de novo–assembled transcriptome (table S9). To check reproducibility, qRT-PCR was performed for each sample with three biological replicates, each including at least two technical replicates. The β-actin gene was chosen as a reference gene (73). Relative expression levels were compared directly using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and β-actin was used for normalization (74).

## Time-series transcriptome analysis of entrance Leptonetela species
Adult female individuals of the entrance species (L. mengzongensis) were entrained in a 12-hour light/dark cycle (LD: lights on 8 a.m. and lights off 8 p.m.) for 7 days before being transferred into constant darkness (DD) in the laboratory. Spiders were sampled at 6-hour intervals over 48-hour period, covering 1 day under LD conditions and 1 day under DD conditions (LD: CT0, CT6, CT12, and CT18; DD: CT24, CT30, CT36, and CT42). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, following the manufacturer’s protocol (TIANGEN BIOTECH, Beijing). Each time point included three biological replicates, with three adult individuals per replicate. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using Nanodrop 2000, RNA integrity was checked via agarose gel electrophoresis, and RNA quality was further verified using an Agilent 5300 (75).
Library construction and RNA-seq were performed on an Illumina HiSeqXten platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Majorbio (Shanghai, China). To ensure transcriptome sequence quality, low-quality, ambiguous, or poly-N and artificial sequences were trimmed from raw reads using TransRate (76). Clean reads were then de novo assembled into contigs using Trinity (v.2.8.5) with default parameters (59). To reduce sequence redundancy, the transcript sets were clustered using CD-HIT software, generating unigenes for further analysis (77, 78). The longest contig for each gene was retained as the unigene, and redundant contigs were removed. The completeness of the de novo assembled transcriptomes and unigene sets was evaluated using BUSCO (60).
Normalized gene expression levels, expressed as FPKM mapped reads, were calculated using RSEM (v.1.3.1) (61). Genes with FPKM ≥1 were considered expressed (79). Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) software was used to analyze unigene expression profiles under LD and DD conditions, each sampled at four time points (80, 81). The number of temporal expression patterns was limited to 50. For data preprocessing, expression levels at CT0 were set as the baseline, and fold changes for subsequent time points were calculated relative to this baseline. Fold-change data were log2-transformed for normalization, and temporal expression profiles were clustered into representative patterns using default parameters within STEM. Statistical significance of the profiles was evaluated with a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, with an adjusted P value (FDR) threshold of 0.05 to identify significant profiles. Candidate gene functions were identified through GO and KEGG pathway annotations (82).

## Ingestion of dsRNA in entrance Leptonetela species
Gene fragments of cry and egfp were amplified by PCR using gene-specific primer pairs (table S10) and used as templates to synthesize dsRNA for cry (dscry) and egfp (dsegfp) using the T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi System (Promega, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions (83). The integrity of the dsRNA was assessed using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). Adult female specimens of the entrance species (L. mengzongensis) were selected and individually placed in 2.5-cm petri dishes. The dsRNA concentration was adjusted to 20 ng/μl, and 200 μl of the solution was administered to each spider, with dsegfp serving as the negative control (84). The solution was refreshed every 12 hours to minimize contamination. The spiders were maintained under a 12/12 light/dark cycle for 4 days, after which huddle response were measured every 4 hours and samples were collected at 6-hour intervals over a 24-hour period. Total RNA was extracted from the samples to quantify the expression levels of the target genes.

## Statistical analyses
Data were compiled, and figures were generated using MS Excel 2013 and GraphPad Prism (v. 8.0.2). Spider individuals that died during the experiment were excluded from the analyses. Huddle durations at each time point were normalized by dividing by the sum of the mean values across all time points. Behavioral data for huddle durations during light and dark phases were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests. qRT-PCR data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan’s multiple comparison posttest and unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. The primary statistical analysis assessed circadian effects using cosinor analyses (85). All statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (version 24, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, U.S.A.). Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
