Abstract
Chemosensation is essential for foraging, predator avoidance, and reproductive behavior in terrestrial
gastropods. The gray field slug (Deroceras reticulatum) is a globally important agricultural pest, yet the
peripheral molecular mechanisms driving its chemosensory behavior remain poorly understood. Here,
we generated and analyzed de novo transcriptomes of the upper posterior tentacles (PT) and lower
anterior tentacles (AT), using non-sensory mantle tissue (MT) as a comparative baseline. Illumina
sequencing and de novo assembly yielded 142,526 transcripts, of which 28,310 were functionally
annotated. Tissue distribution and differential expression analyses revealed marked molecular
specialization between the two tentacle pairs. PT-enriched transcripts were associated with aerial
olfaction and photoreception, including neural signaling components, retinal-associated genes, and G
protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), whereas AT-enriched transcripts were associated with contact
chemoreception and substrate-associated sensing, with enrichment of structural remodeling
components, acetylcholine receptors, and metabolic enzymes. Targeted gene-family mining showed that
GPCRs dominated the annotated chemosensory repertoire. A multistep bioinformatic filtering pipeline
identified 67 PT-enriched and 38 AT-enriched candidate chemosensory GPCRs, including numerous
uncharacterized or orphan receptors. Non-GPCR candidate families included ionotropic glutamate
receptors, degenerin/amiloride-sensitive sodium channels, lipocalins, and Niemann-Pick type C2
proteins. qRT-PCR analysis of 17 high-priority candidate GPCRs largely supported the RNA-Seq
expression patterns. Together, these tissue-specific profiles provide a molecular resource for
understanding slug chemosensory biology and for prioritizing candidate targets for future behaviorbased or molecular pest-management strategies.

BACKGROUND
Terrestrial snails and slugs possess two distinct pairs of cephalic tentacles that serve important sensory
functions (Chase, 1986). The posterior tentacles (also known as the ocular tentacles) are elongated and
dorsally positioned, which likely enhance their ability to detect airborne odorants, and each bears a lightsensitive structure at its apex. In contrast, the anterior tentacles (also known as the peduncular
tentacles) are shorter, located near the mouthparts, and thought to be specialized for detecting
substrate- and food-associated chemical cues during locomotion (Brenzinger et al., 2021). These
tentacles constitute the principal chemosensory organs in gastropods and play essential roles in
feeding, orientation, and reproductive behaviors (Chase and Croll, 1981; Ito et al., 2000). Odor molecules
are detected by the olfactory epithelium covering the tentacle surface, and sensory information is
subsequently transmitted to the tentacular ganglion located beneath the tentacle tip and then relayed to
the procerebrum, the secondary olfactory center in the molluscan brain (Chase and Tolloczko, 1993;
Ucciferri and Wyeth, 2023).
Chemosensory perception in vertebrates and invertebrates is primarily mediated by large multigene
receptor families, many of which encode proteins with a characteristic seven-transmembrane (7-TM)
structure (Mombaerts, 1999). Among them, G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) represent one of the
Page 2/28

most ancient and expansive receptor superfamilies in animals (Krishnan and Schiöth, 2015). GPCRs
transduce extracellular signals across the plasma membrane through heterotrimeric G-protein
complexes and regulate a broad range of physiological and behavioral processes (Weis and Kobilka,
2018). Although vertebrate and arthropod chemoreceptors both possess a 7-TM structure, they are
evolutionarily unrelated, exhibiting opposite membrane topologies and distinct signal transduction
mechanisms (Hanlon and Andrew, 2015). The independent evolutionary origins of chemosensory
receptor systems underscore the importance of investigating these gene families across diverse
invertebrate lineages, including Mollusca, the second-largest animal phylum (Xu et al., 2025). In
particular, gastropod mollusks provide an important comparative framework for understanding the
evolution and diversification of chemosensory GPCRs (Rondón et al., 2024).
GPCR repertoires vary substantially among animal taxa. Approximately 800 GPCR genes have been
identified in the human genome (Fredriksson et al., 2003), whereas more than 700 have been reported in
the zebrafish, Danio rerio (Harty et al., 2015). In invertebrates, the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster
possesses more than 200 GPCR genes (Brody and Cravchik, 2000), whereas the nematode
Caenorhabditis elegans contains approximately 1,300 putative chemosensory GPCRs, representing over
5% of its genome (Vidal et al., 2018). In mollusks, however, GPCR-related studies remain relatively
limited. Previous investigations have characterized GPCR families in several molluscan species
(Morishita, 2017). For example, in the sea slug, Aplysia californica, numerous GPCRs have been identified
from the rhinophore, a specialized chemosensory organ (Cummins et al., 2009b). In the aquatic snail,
Biomphalaria glabrata, several ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs) have been implicated in odor
detection (Liang et al., 2016). More recently, comparative genomic analyses revealed substantial
expansion of chemosensory GPCR families in terrestrial gastropods. More than 600 Class-A GPCRs
were identified in Arion vulgaris, a terrestrial slug species inhabiting moist environments, whereas
reduced chemosensory repertoires were observed in species adapted to drier habitats (Nath et al.,
2025). These findings suggest that habitat diversification may have strongly influenced the evolution of
chemosensory receptor systems in terrestrial mollusks. Nevertheless, the molecular basis of
chemosensation in many terrestrial slug species, including Deroceras reticulatum, remains poorly
understood.
The gray field slug, Deroceras reticulatum (Müller) (Gastropoda: Pulmonata), is a globally distributed
terrestrial pest species native to Europe and now widespread throughout North America and other
temperate regions (CABI, 2022; Godan, 1983; Howlett, 2012; Tulli et al., 2009). This species causes
significant damage to a wide range of agricultural and horticultural crops (Howlett, 2012; Port and Port,
1989). Adult slugs can reach a length of 3.5–5 cm when crawling and are usually gray or cream in color
with characteristic reticulated dark markings. When disturbed, they secrete a milky-white mucus (Mc
Donnell et al., 2024). Eggs are generally deposited beneath stones, leaf litter, or within soil crevices, and
hatch within several weeks depending on environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity
(South, 1992). D. reticulatum is hermaphroditic, with individuals possessing both male and female
reproductive organs, although cross-fertilization is common (Runham, 1978).
Page 3/28

Current management strategies rely primarily on chemical molluscicide baits, supplemented by cultural
practices such as tillage while biological control agents such as parasitic nematodes in the genus
Pellioditis are used in some parts of the world (Barker, 2002, 2004; Dively and Patton, 2022; Glen and
Symondson, 2003; Tan and Grewal, 2001). Consequently, previous studies on D. reticulatum have focused
mainly on its ecology, pest status, and control measures (Arias, 1960; Howlett, 2005; Iglesias et al., 2002;
Lovett and Black, 1920). Early studies examined chemoreception and feeding behaviors of D. reticulatum
(Frain, 1980; Stephenson, 1979), but the molecular mechanisms remain poorly characterized, mainly due
to limited molecular resources for this species. Recent studies have advanced our understanding of slug
biology through whole-body transcriptome sequencing with complex neuropeptide repertoires (Ahn et
al., 2017) and functional characterization of the first molluscan ortholog of the vertebrate neuromedin U
receptor in D. reticulatum (Ahn et al., 2020). For potential applications in slug managements, GPCRtargeted bioactive peptides have been identified and shown to exert deterrent effects in this species
(Choi et al., 2024). In addition, immune-related gene expression profiles in D. reticulatum responding to
parasitic nematode infection have also been investigated (Hafeez et al., 2024). Despite these
advancements, molecular investigations into the specific physiological roles and sensory modalities of
the slug tentacles remain largely unexplored in D. reticulatum.
In this study, we analyzed the tentacle transcriptomes of D. reticulatum, generated using the Illumina
HiSeq 2500 platform. Posterior and anterior tentacles were excised separately, and their transcriptomic
profiles were compared with those of the mantle tissue through differential gene expression analysis.
Particular emphasis was placed on identifying GPCRs and other chemosensory-related genes enriched
in tentacular tissues. The expression patterns of selected candidate genes were further validated by qRTPCR. Ultimately, these tissue-specific molecular blueprints not only deepen our understanding of
gastropod neurobiology and sensory physiology, particularly chemoreception, but also provide a valuable
genetic foundation for identifying relevant chemoreceptor targets and developing next-generation
behavioral disruptors for sustainable pest management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Slug tentacle and mantle tissue samples, and RNA
isolation
Deroceras reticulatum were collected in Corvallis, Oregon, USA (44.567862 N, -123.288672 E). Prior to
tissue harvesting, individuals were maintained alive in plastic containers with lettuce and carrot, and
damp paper towel at 14°C. A pair of posterior tentacles (PT) and a pair of anterior tentacles (AT)
(Fig. 1A) were precisely excised from each slug using sterilized micro-dissection scissors (Fine Science
Tools Inc., Foster City, CA). As a comparative control, a piece of mantle tissue (MT)—devoid of tentacles
and internal visceral organs—was separately collected. The mantle is a saddle-shaped piece of tissue
located behind the slug head (Mc Donnell et al., 2024). To ensure sufficient biomaterial for downstream
application, the tentacle tissue samples were pooled from five individual slugs.
Page 4/28

Total RNA was isolated from the three tissue samples, PT, AT, and MT, using PureLink Total RNA Mini Kit
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. To eliminate potential
genomic DNA contamination, the isolated RNA samples were treated with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher
Scientific) for 30 min at 37°C. The treated RNA was subsequently purified by using the RNeasy MinElute
Cleanup Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) and eluted in a final volume of 50 µL of RNA storage solution.
RNA quantity was determined using a NanoDrop ND-2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific),
and RNA quality was evaluated via the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer
(Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). All isolated RNA profiles met the strict quality thresholds (RIN >
7.0) required for high-throughput transcriptomic sequencing. The comprehensive workflow
encompassing these experimental and bioinformatic procedures is schematically represented in Fig. 1B.

Illumina HiSeq sequencing
The cDNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San
Diego, CA). Briefly, ribosomal RNA was removed from total RNA and the remaining RNA was purified,
fragmented and primed for cDNA synthesis. First-strand cDNA was synthesized by priming the RNA
fragments with random hexamers and by transcribing with reverse transcriptase. RNA template of the
first strand cDNA was replaced by incorporating dUTP in place of dTTP to generate second-strand cDNA.
The cDNA then underwent an end repair process, adenylation of 3’ ends and subsequent ligation of the
adapter. The adaptor-ligated libraries were purified and enriched with PCR to create the final cDNA
library. After quality control and quantification, sequencing was performed by Psomagen Inc. (Rockville,
MD) using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform.
De novo transcriptome assembly
The raw sequence reads generated by Illumina sequencing were checked by FastQC for quality control
and trimmed by Trimmomatic tool (ver. 0.32) (Bolger et al., 2014) to remove adaptors and low-quality
reads. Overlapping high-quality reads were de novo assembled to create longer contiguous fragments
(contigs) using the Trinity (version r2014-07-17) (Grabherr et al., 2011). Transcript abundance was
estimated using RSEM (ver. 1.2.15) to generate FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million reads).

Functional annotation
The functional annotation of the assembled transcripts was performed using a multi-tiered pipeline
incorporating Gene Ontology (GO), UniProt, the NCBI Non-Redundant protein sequences database (NR),
Pfam, EggNOG, and the NCBI Nucleotide database (NT). In addition, manual annotations were conducted
for those of our interest, e.g. GPCRs, based on sequence similarity searches against the NCBI NR
database. These homology-based searches utilized the BLASTx algorithm with a stringent with a cut-off
E-value of 1e-5 run by Geneious 8.1.5 software (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand). The BLAST
hits were grouped into major gene families based on their putative biological functions in slug. The
Page 5/28

organism information obtained by the BLAST hit was collected to confirm the transcript’s sequence
similarity to closely related species.

Differential expression analysis of tentacle transcriptome
Transcript abundance was estimated using the RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization (RSEM) method.
Differential expression analysis was subsequently performed using the edgeR package in R (McCarthy et
al., 2012). Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were used to
quantify transcript abundance and to assess tissue-specific expression patterns. Differentially expressed
genes (DEGs) among PT, AT, and MT were identified through pairwise comparisons using a false
discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 and a fold-change cutoff of > 2.
Identification of chemosensory-related genes in D. reticulatum
The search for chemosensory-related gene sequences was based on sequence similarities to known
sequences among closely related species. De novo transcriptome sequences of D. reticulatum were
formatted into a local BLAST database using BioEdit (Hall, 2011). Chemosensory-related protein
sequences, including G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR), ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluR),
degenerin amiloride-sensitive ion channels (DEG/ENaC), lipocalin, Niemann-Pick type C2 (NPC2), and
sensory neuron membrane protein (SNMP), were retrieved from literature across diverse gastropod
species (Cummins et al., 2009; Nath et al., 2025; Rondón et al., 2024). These sequences were utilized as
queries in tBLASTn searches to identify homologous transcripts within the D. reticulatum transcriptome
database.

qRT-PCR
Two-step qRT-PCR was performed to assess the relative abundance of transcripts for selected targets. In
the first step, first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Verso cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher
Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNAs isolated from PT, AT, and MT (See
Section 2.1 above for details) were used as templates for the cDNA synthesis reaction. In the second
step, PowerUp SYBR Green master mix for qPCR (Applied Biosystems) was used to set up the PCR
reactions as described in the product’s manual. The qRT-PCR conditions were performed at 95°C for 10
min; 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min; followed by a melting curve analysis over the range of
60–95°C with 0.3°C/min increments, with the specific primers (Supplementary data 5 - Table S1 and Fig.
S1) on StepOne Plus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Among the three housekeeping genes
preliminarily tested, rpt6 (regulatory particle triple-A ATPase 6) was selected and used to normalize
signals, and relative abundance was calculated using the comparative Ct method (Supplementary Data 5
- Table S2 and Fig. S2). GraphPad Prism software (Boston, MA) was used to visualize the data. One-way
ANOVA was run using GraphPad Prism to estimate the significance of differences in the relative
expression level between samples. P values < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Illumina sequencing, assembly and annotation
A total of 228,377,020 paired-end raw reads with a GC content of 41.5% were generated. After trimming
the adapters and low-quality bases, a total of 227,620,062 clean reads were obtained, representing
99.7% recovery of the original sequencing reads (Supplementary data 1 - Table S1). More than 60% of
the clean reads from each sample were mapped to produce assembled transcripts (Supplementary data
1 - Table S2), resulting in a total of 142,526 transcripts with an average length of 614 bp (201–25,081 bp)
(Supplementary data 1 - Table S3). A total of 28,310 (19.9%) transcripts were annotated by at least one
method among GO, UniProt, NR, Pfam, EggNOG, NT, or manual annotation. Gene Ontology (GO) terms
were assigned to the BLAST-hit contigs using Blast2Go tool, resulting in the functional annotation of
19,074 transcripts (13.4%), UniProt annotation was assigned to 17,995 transcripts (12.6%), NR resulted
in the largest annotation of 27,522 transcripts (19.3%), Pfam resulted in 17,468 transcripts (12.3%),
EggNOG assigned 21,073 transcripts (14.8%), NT annotated 5,266 transcripts (3.7%), and manual
annotation added 179 transcripts (0.1%) (Supplementary data 1 - Table S4).

Differentially expressed genes across tentacles and mantle tissues

Global transcript distribution
To characterize the transcriptomic landscape of the slug chemosensory tissues, we evaluated the global
distribution of expressed transcripts across the PT, AT, and MT for both the total unigene assembly
(142,526 transcripts) (Fig. 1C) and the functionally annotated unigene subset (28,310 transcripts)
(Fig. 1D).
A global comparison of the total unigene pool revealed a massive, shared core of 67,828 transcripts
expressed across all three tissue types, reflecting fundamental cellular maintenance and widespread
metabolic pathways. Distinct tissue-exclusive signatures were prominent across the total assembly: the
mantle tissue contained the highest number of unique unigenes (19,699), followed by the posterior
tentacles (8,995), while the anterior tentacles possessed the fewest tissue-specific unigenes (3,622)
(Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the two tentacle pairs shared a distinct subset of 8,420 transcripts that were
entirely absent in the mantle control tissue, highlighting shared candidate genes potentially dedicated to
specialized tentacular or sensory functions. Within the total assembly dataset, 7,015 unigenes showed
no detectable expression across the three tissue groups (Fig. 1C).
Following functional annotation, the distribution of the 28,310 annotated unigenes was separately
assessed (Fig. 1D). Within this annotated subset, a core of 15,685 transcripts was shared ubiquitously
among all three tissue domains. Striking tissue-exclusive signatures persisted within the annotated
dataset, demonstrating persistent functional divergence: the mantle tissue maintained the largest unique
annotated signature (3,629), followed by the posterior tentacles (1,232), and the anterior tentacles (506).
Page 7/28

The PT and AT pairs shared a restricted subset of 1,150 annotated transcripts that were absent in the
mantle tissue. A negligible fraction (595 transcripts) of the annotated unigenes lacked expression across
these designated tissue groups (Fig. 1D).

Functional specialization of tissue-exclusive transcripts
Functional annotation of the top 30 transcripts exclusively expressed in each category highlighted the
distinct physiological specialization of each tissue domain (Supplementary Data 2). The poorly
characterized transcripts such as hypothetical proteins and uncharacterized proteins were excluded in
these lists. (i) Posterior tentacles (PT)-exclusive: Transcripts restricted entirely to PT were strongly
enriched in chemosensory, photoreceptive, and neural signaling components. These notably featured a
prominent array of nine GPCRs—including opsins (violet-sensitive opsin-like and melanopsin-like),
neuropeptide/peptide receptors (fMet-Leu-Phe, allatostatin-A, and adenosine A1-like receptors), and a
chemosensory receptor—alongside neuroregulatory rho GTPase-activating proteins (RhoGAP 35 and 15),
motor proteins (kinesin-like protein KIF22), solute transporters (sodium-dependent phosphate and
monocarboxylate transporters), a cyclic nucleotide-gated cation channel (CNGA/B family), and synaptic
vesicle-associated components (synergin γ-like) (Supplementary Data 2 - Table S1). (ii) Anterior
tentacles (AT)-exclusive: Transcripts restricted entirely to the AT prominently featured elements involved
in structural remodeling, neuroendocrine signaling, and synaptic transmission. These included
extracellular matrix components (collagen α-3(VI) and cell wall protein DAN4-like), proteases
(transmembrane protease serine 9-like and endothelin-converting enzyme 1), cholinergic signaling
machinery—comprising acetylcholinesterase-like (AChE), nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunit type A
(nAChR), and muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M4-like—alongside multiple GPCRs (growth hormone
secretagogue receptor type 1-like and allatostatin-A receptor-like), a Cys-loop ligand-gated ion channel,
and membrane transporters (sialin-like anion transporter and organic cation transporter) (Supplementary
Data 2 - Table S2). (iii) Mantle Tissue (MT)-exclusive: Distinct from both tentacle types, the MT-exclusive
profile was heavily dominated by muscular contraction and mucosal/immune defense machinery. This
domain was characterized by striated muscle-specific myosin essential light chains and myosin heavy
chains, alongside gastropod antimicrobial peptides (Incilarin A, B, and C), C1q domain-containing
immunity factors, and a mucin-5AC-like transcript. Additionally, the MT profile featured key physiological
regulators, including voltage-gated sodium channel β subunit 4, solute/ion transporters, and extracellular
matrix components such as fibulin-1-like and matrilin-like 85 kDa protein (Supplementary Data 2 - Table
S3).

expressed across all three tissue types, the top 30 transcripts showing higher expression in the PT
relative to the AT were represented by candidate genes involved in metabolic processing,
neuroendocrine signaling, and cell communication. These notably included three cytochrome P450
enzymes (CYP2J1-like and CYP2E1-like), two proteases (cathepsin L1-like), two GPCRs (metabotropic
glutamate receptor 2 and orexin receptor type 2), an ABC transporter (subfamily G member 2-like), a

GPCR gene family
The GPCR gene family emerged as overwhelmingly dominant across almost all tissue categories,
accounting for 1,193 of the 1,289 total chemosensory-related transcripts (92.7%) (Table 1). Strikingly, the
highest concentration of GPCR genes was identified in the shared tentacular transcriptome (PT & AT),
yielding 402 total GPCR genes. This prominent overlap underscores a substantial shared sensory
receptor repertoire unique to tentacular functions. Among tissue-exclusive profiles, the posterior
tentacles (PT only) maintained a substantially higher commitment to unique GPCR transcripts (264)
Page 10/28

compared to the anterior tentacles (AT only; 73), supporting a more specialized or diverse chemosensory
role for the upper tentacle pair. In contrast, while the mantle tissue possessed a large number of total
annotated transcripts (3,629), only 108 of them were classified as GPCRs (Table 1), further
demonstrating that these receptors are highly enriched within the sensory tentacular structures rather
than somatic mantle tissues.
To systematically isolate high-confidence, chemosensory-related candidates from the total pool of 1,193
annotated GPCR transcripts, we implemented a sequential filtering workflow based on transcript
localization and differential abundance (Fig. 2). Candidate GPCRs from these intersecting categories
were subsequently shortlisted using stringent downstream fold change (FC) filters to capture tissuespecific enrichment. From the 264 GPCR transcripts exclusively expressed in the PT, a subset of 35
GPCR genes met the criteria for robust transcript abundance with an FPKM > 2, isolating highconfidence, PT-specific sensory candidates (Fig. 2). Likewise, a subset of 9 GPCR genes was shortlisted
as exclusively and robustly expressed in the AT (Fig. 2).
For transcripts shared between the two pairs of tentacles, comparative differential expression analysis
partitioned candidates by regional preference. From the pool of 402 shared tentacular GPCRs (Table 1),
14 GPCRs were identified as significantly enriched in PT ( FC2> ), whereas 10 GPCRs were found to be
significantly enriched in AT ( FC2> ), compared to each other (Fig. 2). Similarly, 3 and 5 GPCRs were
identified as enriched in PT and AT, respectively, compared to MT. Finally, filtering the universally shared
core of 178 GPCRs revealed explicit localized elevations despite broad tissue distribution. Specifically,
15 GPCRs were enriched in PT (PT > AT; FC2> ) among those higher than MT, while 14 GPCRs were
enriched in AT (AT > PT; FC2> ).
Together, this multi-step curation pipeline successfully narrowed the broad transcriptomic dataset down
to discrete sets of putative chemosensory GPCRs highly enriched in PT (a total of 67 candidate GPCRs)
and in AT (a total of 38 candidate GPCRs), providing a targeted framework for subsequent functional
characterization (Fig. 2). Although the 65 GPCRs were found to be MT-enriched, they were excluded from
further investigation as they fell outside the sensory scope of this study. The comprehensive profiles of
these shortlisted tentacle-enriched candidate GPCRs and their transcript sequences are documented in
Supplementary data 3.

Non-GPCR gene families
Among the non-GPCR chemosensory gene families, distinct localized patterns were observed that
suggest specialized physiological and structural roles. A total of 94 non-GPCR chemosensory candidate
transcripts were identified as differentially expressed across the three tissue types, comprising 40
ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs), 34 degenerin/amiloride-sensitive sodium channels
(DEG/ENaCs), 12 lipocalins, 3 Niemann-Pick type C2 proteins (NPC2), and 4 sensory neuron membrane
proteins (SNMPs) (Table 1).

(A) iGluRs
Page 11/28

The 40 identified iGluRs displayed a high abundance in the mantle tissue and mixed-tissue profiles, while
only three iGluRs showed tentacle-specific expression with 2 in PT and 1 in AT (Fig. 3A). A majority of
iGluR transcripts were localized within the shared core across all three tissues (12 iGluRs) and
exclusively within the mantle tissue only (12 iGluRs). Additionally, 9 iGluRs were expressed in both the PT
and MT, while 4 iGluRs were found in the AT and MT comparison (Fig. 3A). This distinct leaning toward
mantle-containing profiles suggests that iGluRs may coordinate pervasive somatic sensory networks or
peripheral chemical feedback systems in the slug body wall. Intriguingly, among the tentacle-specific
transcripts restricted exclusively to distinct tentacular types, c123747_g1_i1 (glutamate receptor subunit
protein GluR7 precursor) was selectively expressed in PT only, whereas c88995_g1_i1 (glutamate receptor
ionotropic, kainate 4-like) was unique to AT only (Supplementary Data 4 – Table S1). Among those
expressed in multiple tissues, c96868_g1_i1 and c88280_g1_i1 were higher in PT and AT, respectively.
Taken together, this analysis indicates that iGluRs play a minor but highly specialized roles in divergent
tentacular sensing.

(B) DEG/ENaC channels
The DEG/ENaC ion channel family (34 transcripts total) was broadly distributed throughout the target
tissues but demonstrated substantial localized enrichment within the core ubiquitous transcriptome (12
transcripts) and mantle tissue (9 transcripts) profiles, implying substantial involvement in non-tentacular
tactile reception or localized somatic monitoring (Fig. 3B). However, highly localized sensory markers
were also present; c80151_g1_i1 (amiloride-sensitive sodium channel subunit alpha-like), c84991_g3_i2
(subunit beta-2-like), c74351_g1_i1 (amiloride-sensitive sodium channel subunit alpha-like), and
c74351_g2_i1 (acid-sensing ion channel 1-like) were heavily enriched in PT, exhibiting prominent
upregulated expressions in the PT compared to the AT; while c133010_g1_i1 (amiloride-sensitive sodium
channel subunit beta-like), c123636_g1_i1 (acid-sensing ion channel 1-like), and c100848_g1_i1 (amiloridesensitive sodium channel subunit beta-like) were higher in AT than PT, exhibiting prominent upregulated
expressions in the AT compared to the PT (Supplementary Data 4 – Table S2).

(C) Lipocalin, NPC2, and SNMP
The auxiliary transport and binding protein families, lipocalins (12 total), NPC2s (3 total), and SNMPs (4
total), were primarily restricted to interconnected tissue profiles (Fig. 3C-3E). Both lipocalins and NPC2
expressions were entirely absent from tissue-exclusive categories (PT only, AT only, or MT only). Instead,
lipocalins and NPC2s were predominantly localized within the ubiquitous core shared by all three tissues
(9 lipocalins and 3 NPC2s), highlighting their fundamental roles in systemic hydrophobic ligand binding
or general transport (Fig. 3C & 3D). The remaining lipocalins were found in shared tentacle-mantle niches
(2 in PT & MT, 1 in AT & MT) (Fig. 3C). By the fold change analysis of lipocalins, c78360_g1_i1 (fatty acidbinding protein, muscle-like) was higher in both PT and AT, compared to MT, while c76900_g1_i1 (fatty
acid-binding protein) was almost exclusively high in AT (Supplementary Data 4 – Table S3). In contrast,
SNMPs were explicitly absent from the universally shared core, appearing exclusively in the mantle
tissue (1 transcript) or within shared tentacle-mantle coordinates (2 in PT & MT, 1 in AT & MT) (Fig. 3E),
Page 12/28

revealing no particular tentacle-enriched NPC2 or SNMPs (Supplementary Data 5 – Tables S4-S5). Taken
together, these distribution profiles indicate an intricate tissue-specific sorting of non-GPCR candidate
chemosensory elements, including a majority of mantle-enriched ion channels and systemic transport
proteins, balanced with a minor numbers of tentacle-enriched ones.

qRT-PCR validation of selected GPCR transcripts
To validate the tissue-specific expression profiles obtained from the RNA-Seq analysis, a total of 17
candidate GPCR genes were selected for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. These genes
were chosen based on their distinctive transcript abundance patterns across the tissues: 5 were
predominantly expressed in the posterior tentacles (PT), 7 showed high expression in both the posterior
and anterior tentacles (PT & AT), and 5 were preferentially expressed in the anterior tentacles (AT)

the rpt6 gene was selected for target gene normalization due to its superior expression stability across
all tested tissue types (Supplementary Data 5 - Table S2 and Fig. S1). The PT tissue served as the
baseline control (set as 1.0) for all relative expression calculations. Relative transcript abundance was
calculated from three technical replicates per biological sample, with standard deviations represented as
error bars (Fig. 4).

Discussion
The chemosensory system is essential for survival, foraging, predator avoidance, and reproductive
success in terrestrial snails and slugs (Kiss, 2017). At the molecular level, detecting chemical signals
relies on specialized proteins belonging to medium- to large-sized multigene families present across
taxa in the animal kingdom (Nei et al., 2008). Although terrestrial gastropods are known to navigate
complex environments using airborne and substrate-borne chemical cues, the molecular mechanisms
underlying these behaviors remain poorly understood.
This study provides a comprehensive transcriptomic resource for one of the world’s most damaging slug
pests, Deroceras reticulatum, with a specific focus on its primary sensory structures: the posterior (PT)
and anterior (AT) tentacles. Prior to this work, publicly available sequence resources for D. reticulatum in
NCBI (PRJNA384817 and PRJNA167149) were limited in their representation of the chemosensory gene
repertoire because they were derived from whole-body transcriptomes. Building upon our previous
whole-body identification of slug neuropeptide repertoires (Ahn et al., 2017), our de novo assembly
generated 142,526 unigenes with an average length of 614 bp. Although our overall structural annotation
rate (19.9% of total transcripts annotated) reflects the typical limitations of working with non-model
gastropod genomes, the absolute number of fully annotated transcripts (28,310) represents a highresolution dataset vital for exploring terrestrial slug physiology. Nonetheless, the substantial fraction of
unannotated unigenes highlights a vast molecular diversity requiring further functional characterization.
Page 15/28

The presence of two pairs of tentacles is a defining morphological characteristic of the terrestrial
pulmonate order Stylommatophora, distinguishing them from aquatic Basommatophora, which possess
only a single pair (Croll, 1983). Molecular phylogenetic and microanatomical analyses suggest that this
dual-tentacle system evolved by the splitting of an original single ancestral pair as an adaptation to life
on land (Brenzinger et al., 2021). Our comparative transcriptomic data strongly reinforce this
evolutionary split, showing an underlying core of shared sensory transcripts paired with a robust
transcriptomic divergence between the individual tentacle pairs.
Pairwise differential expression analysis and functional annotations further illuminate the distinct
physiological specialization between the two tentacle pairs. Behavioral studies have shown that the
lower, anterior tentacles are positioned close to the ground for substrate contact chemoreception and
trail-following, whereas the upper, posterior tentacles project upward to specialize in airborne olfaction
and wind orientation (Ito et al., 2000; Kiss, 2017). This functional divergence is clearly reflected in our
data: the PT transcriptome is strongly associated with long-range neurosensory functions, as indicated
by the abundance of neural pathways, photoreception cascades (supporting the apical optical spot), and
expansive chemosensory receptor repertoires. In contrast, the AT transcriptome appears adapted for
localized mechanical interaction and chemical processing, enriched with structural remodeling proteins,
extracellular proteases, and synaptic transmission machinery. By demonstrating that the PT manages
aerial olfaction and vision while the AT is linked to tactile and contact chemoreception, our findings
underscore how the dual-tentacle configuration serves as a key sensory innovation enabling
stylommatophoran slugs to successfully navigate and live in complex terrestrial habitats.
Among the candidate chemosensory gene families identified in gastropods, GPCRs represent the
predominant group, matching trends observed in the sea hare A. californica (Cummins et al., 2009a), the
golden apple snail Pomacea canaliculata (Rondón et al., 2024), and the Mediterranean cone snail
Lautoconus ventricosus (Rondón et al., 2024). In D. reticulatum, GPCRs accounted for 1,193 of the 1,289
total annotated chemosensory-related transcripts (92.7%), with Class A rhodopsin-like receptors
comprising the vast majority (Table 1). Our multi-step filtration pipeline successfully narrowed this
extensive repertoire down to high-confidence sensory candidates based on tissue-specific localization
and abundance thresholds.
The large number of GPCRs identified from the D. reticulatum tentacular transcriptome suggests lineagespecific gene duplications. Although formal phylogenetic analysis remains to be performed to precisely
map these duplication events in D. reticulatum, similar expansions of the Class A GPCR family have been
documented in A. californica and other Mollusca, and are thought to reflect evolutionary adaptations to
shifting chemical landscapes (Nath et al., 2025). A terrestrial slug, navigating a multi-dimensional
chemical landscape containing for example volatile plant defense compounds, odors of plant and animal
decomposition, soil microclimate indicators, and specialized mucus pheromones and kairomones,
requires a highly adaptable and diverse receptor repertoire (Wang et al., 2026). Consequently,
investigating these lineage-specific duplications through dedicated phylogenetic and syntenic analyses
would represent a crucial next step to clarify the evolutionary trajectory of gastropod chemoreceptors.
Page 16/28

Moreover, because many tentacle-enriched candidates remain orphan receptors with unknown ligands,
systematic curation of these localized networks provides a strategic roadmap for downstream
deorphanization studies (Ahn et al., 2020). Such studies will be essential for uncovering the molecular,
structural, and evolutionary basis of chemosensation in terrestrial slugs.
Although GPCRs remain the primary focus of chemosensory research, our findings indicate that slug
peripheral sensation relies on a multi-tiered network of ion channels and auxiliary transport proteins. The
localized expression of specific ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs) and variant ionotropic receptors
(IRs) in the PT and AT suggests the presence of an ancient and conserved olfactory mechanism. In
insects and aquatic mollusks, IR networks act as precise, rapid detectors for volatile amines and acids
(Benton et al., 2009; Croset et al., 2010; Liang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2026; Yamagishi et al., 2008). The
identification of distinct, mutually exclusive iGluR/IR variants within individual tentacle types suggests
that D. reticulatum utilizes parallel ionotropic pathways to separate different classes of chemical inputs.
Similarly, the differential distribution of the DEG/ENaC ion channel family indicates a highly organized
sensory interface. Members of this superfamily function as non-voltage-gated mechanoreceptors, acidsensing ion channels, or salt taste receptors (Bianchi and Driscoll, 2002; Kaulich et al., 2023; Mano and
Driscoll, 1999). While widespread somatic expression across the mantle tissue suggest a role in general
neuromuscular coordination, the distinct enrichment of specific DEG/ENaC variants within either the PT
or AT points to targeted sensory tasks. These channels likely allow the slug to integrate mechanical
feedback (such as substrate texture or wind speed) with localized chemical cues, facilitating a
multimodal assessment of its surroundings.
Finally, our data clarify how terrestrial gastropods overcome the biophysical challenge associated with
detecting airborne chemical cues. Hydrophobic odorants must be captured and transferred across the
hydrophilic mucus layer overlaying the sensory dendrites (Zarzo, 2007). In insects, this function is
carryout by specialized odorant-binding proteins (OBPs) (Pelosi et al., 2005). Although classical insect
OBPs appear to be evolutionarily absent in Mollusca (Rihani et al., 2021), our analysis identified potential
functional analogs, including secreted Niemann-Pick type C2 (NPC2) proteins (Storch and Xu, 2009) and
specialized lipocalins (Zhu et al., 2021). These extracellular carriers likely act as solubilizing chaperones
within the tentacular mucus, capturing hydrophobic environmental lipid cues and delivering them to
nearby chemoreceptors. This highlights a compelling example of functional convergence in terrestrial
chemoreception.
In addition to its evolutionary insights, the tissue-specific receptor blueprint developed in this study
provides a valuable resource for applied agricultural biotechnology. D. reticulatum is a globally important
agricultural pest that causes extensive damage to a diverse range of crops (Howlett, 2012; Port and Port,
1989). Current management strategies rely heavily on broad-spectrum chemical molluscicide baits,
which are often limited by variable efficacy, rapid degradation under wet conditions, and toxicity to nontarget organisms (Dively and Patton, 2022; Mustapha et al., 2025). There is thus an urgent need to
develop new control tactics for this and other terrestrial gastropod pest species.
Page 17/28

Because peripheral GPCRs and ion channels are directly exposed to the external environment, they
represent promising targets for highly selective, next-generation behavioral disruptors and speciesspecific molluscicides. The high-confidence, tentacle-enriched candidates validated by our qRT-PCR
pipeline provide concrete targets for high-throughput chemical screening and for the design of
sequence-specific double-stranded RNAs (dsRNAs) for targeted RNA interference (RNAi) applications.
By identifying the specific molecular components that govern slug orientation and foraging behaviors,
these transcriptomic profiles establish a foundation for innovative, biorational management strategies
designed to disrupt chemosensory-mediated communication and protect vulnerable agroecosystems.
