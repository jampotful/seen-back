Cyanobacteriochrome-like GAF folds in phages revealed via AlphaFold proteomic modelling.
Meza-Padilla I, Doxey AC, Nissimov JI. (2025). Computational and structural biotechnology journal. . doi:10.1016/j.csbj.2025.09.020. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Cyanobacteriochrome-like GAF folds in phages revealed via AlphaFold proteomic modelling

## Abstract
AbstractAccurate protein structure prediction followed by structural homology detection enable the functional annotation of otherwise obscure viral protein-coding genes. Here we employ AlphaFold proteomic modelling and structural homology searches on the genome of CrV-01T, a representative freshwater cyanophage, to reveal previously unknown structural homologs. One of these cryptic viral proteins is found to be a cyanobacteriochrome-like GAF fold (CGF) protein. Cyanobacteriochromes (CBCRs) are known to regulate phototaxis, cyclic nucleotide metabolism and optimization of light harvesting in cyanobacteria. Phylogenetic analyses indicate that the CGF protein of CrV-01T was probably acquired from a cyanobacterial host. We then use experimentally determined CBCR structures to query the Big Fantastic Virus Database and discover that CGFs are present among many different bacteriophages. The GAF domain sequence, which is a hallmark of CBCRs, can still be detected in some of these divergent viral proteins. Remarkably, viral CGF proteins harbor an N-terminal extension that in most cases is predicted to contain a transmembrane α-helix, indicating that they may bind the host membrane after being synthesized in the virocell. The presence of CGF protein-coding genes in cyanophage genomes suggests novel ways in which viruses may manipulate the metabolism of cyanobacteria, the most abundant oxygenic phototrophs on Earth. Overall, the findings reported here emphasize the importance of applying structural homology detection methods when annotating viral genomes and highlight the potential of AlphaFold for exploring the dark matter of the aquatic virosphere.Keywords: Cyanobacteriochrome, Cyanophage, AlphaFold, Structural modelling, Structural homology, Virus ecology, Virus evolution

## Introduction
Viruses are the most abundant biological systems on Earth [1]. Not surprisingly, the virosphere most likely comprises the largest repertoire of genetic diversity on the planet [2], [3]. Coupled to the poor coverage of viruses in genomic databases, such an enormous diversity renders the functional annotation of viral genomes extremely challenging [4]. In many cases, protein-coding genes with no sequence homologs in genomic databases can comprise more than 90 % of a microbial virus genome [5]. A single viral genome can contain hundreds of protein-coding genes, and thanks to the development of high-throughput metagenomic methods, astronomical numbers of viruses are continuously being discovered [6]. The groundbreaking, artificial intelligence-based bioinformatics tool AlphaFold is able to computationally model the three-dimensional structure of proteins with unprecedented accuracy and efficiency [7]. Since the structure of proteins is more conserved compared to their amino acid sequence, structural modelling of viral proteins followed by structural homology search methods makes it possible to predict the function of large numbers of otherwise cryptic proteins at an unprecedented scale [8], [9].
Cyanobacteria are the most abundant oxygenic phototrophs on Earth and, along with microalgae, produce and fix around half of the planet's oxygen and CO2 [10], [11], [12]. Horizontally transferred genes involved in photobiological processes are common among the viruses that infect them (i.e., cyanophages). Exemplary cases include photosystem components and non-bleaching A (nblA) protein-coding genes in both marine and freshwater cyanophage genomes [13], [14], [15], [16]. Briefly, viral photosystem subunits help to maintain the photosynthetic efficiency of the cyanobacterial host [17]; while viral NblA proteins protect the virocell against high light intensities and increase the intracellular nutrients pool by disassembling the nitrogen-rich light harvesting complexes of cyanobacteria [18]. Yet another type of proteins involved in cyanobacterial photobiology is the cyanobacteriochromes (CBCRs), minimalistic photoreceptors that require only a GAF domain (a protein domain commonly found in various signaling pathways and sensory systems; abbreviated after cGMP-specific phosphodiesterases, adenylyl cyclases and transcription activator FhlA) to sense diverse wavelengths [19], [20]. They are known to play different roles in cyanobacteria, including phototaxis, cyclic nucleotide metabolism and optimization of light harvesting [21]. To the best of our knowledge, however, CBCRs have not been reported in viruses to date. This leaves a critical gap in our understanding of cyanobacterial virocells and raises the question of whether viruses encode CBCR-like proteins.
The aim of this study was to identify cryptic CBCR-like protein-coding genes in viruses and to explore the potential of AlphaFold for conducting structural homology-based viral genome annotation. To do this, we started by conducting proteomic structural modelling for the genome of CrV-01T, a representative freshwater cyanophage isolated from the Netherlands in 2012 that infects the invasive, bloom-forming, potentially toxic, nitrogen-fixer Raphidiopsis raciborskii (previously Cylindrospermopsis raciborskii) Cr2010 [22]. We predicted the structure of all 88 open reading frames (ORFs) with no functionally annotated sequence homologs in the NCBI non-redundant protein sequences (nr) database using AlphaFold 3 (AF3), which represented 71.5% of CrV-01T’s protein-coding genes. We then used Foldseek [23] and DALI [24] to stringently query the PBD and identify structural homologs for our predictions. This initial exploratory analysis revealed four structural homologs, including a CBCR-like GAF fold (CGF) protein. We further elaborated on this finding by leveraging structural bioinformatics tools and databases from the post-AlphaFold era.

## Methods

## Structural bioinformatics pipeline
The structural bioinformatics pipeline is outlined in Fig. 1. A proteome FASTA file containing all protein sequences encoded in the genome of CrV-01T was downloaded from the NCBI Protein database (GenBank accession number: MH636380) [25]. Structural modelling was conducted using AF3 through the AlphaFold server [7]. All protein-coding genes with no functionally annotated sequence homologs in the NCBI nr database were modelled using automatically generated seeds and the default number of ten recycles. For each run (i.e., each three-dimensionally predicted amino acid sequence), AF3 outputs five models ranked by their ranking score metric. In all cases, the top ranked models were used for downstream structural homology queries. The overall fold of AlphaFold models with a predicted template modelling (pTM) score higher than 0.5 is usually considered similar to that of the true structure [26; see also 27]. Thus, only the models with pTM scores higher than 0.5 were selected for downstream analyses. Furthermore, the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores of the structural models were visually assessed using UCSF ChimeraX 1.9 [28]. Foldseek [23] and DALI [24] were used to query the PDB [29] and identify structural homologs. Foldseek hits with an E-value < 0.001 are usually considered significant [8], as well as DALI matches with a Z-score > 2 (i.e., two standard deviations above expected) [18], [30], [31]. Moreover, only the hits with true-positive Foldseek probabilities (TPs) of 1, plus consistent results in both Foldseek and DALI were considered bona fide structural homologs. Manual curation followed to further evaluate the relevance of the matches by visual inspection [31]. Structural alignments between the viral models and their structural homologs were generated and visualized using the 3D superimposition tool of DALI.
Structural bioinformatics pipeline for the identification and assessment of structural homologs from cryptic viral open reading frames. See 2.1 for a more detailed description.
The initial screening described above led us to the discovery of a virus-encoded CGF protein. Foldseek [23] was then used to query the Big Fantastic Virus Database (BFVB; currently the largest repository of predicted viral protein structures) [8] with experimentally determined CBCR structures in different photostates downloaded from the PDB and identify other CGFs in viruses. Hits with an E-value < 0.001 [8] and a TP = 1 were considered significant. Finally, DeepTMHMM 1.0 [32], a deep learning protein language model-based algorithm, was used to search for transmembrane regions in the putative viral CGF proteins.

## Phylogenetic analyses
The amino acid sequence of CrV-01T ORF 65 (coding for the viral CGF protein identified after the proteomic modelling) was BLASTP searched [33] against the NCBI nr database to retrieve sequence homologs. In addition, we queried the genome of the closely related CrV-like virus Cr-LKS4 (GenBank accession number: OQ625504) with CrV-01T ORF 65, as it shares some identical ORFs. Hits with an E-value < 0.001 were considered significant. Since most of the CrV-01T ORF 65 BLASTP hits were cyanobacterial in origin, we hypothesized that the taxonomic assignment of two hits corresponding to other bacterial phyla (NDA96179, annotated in the database as a hypothetical protein from an Actinomycetota bacterium; and MFM7859517, annotated as a hypothetical protein from a Flammeovirgaceae bacterium) could be incorrect. Therefore, they were further investigated. Both proteins belonged to contig-level metagenome assembled genomes (MAGs) from lakes: ASM1002823v1 harboring NDA96179, and ASM4608718v1 containing MFM7859517. In both cases, the NCBI taxonomy check status was labeled as inconclusive. After downloading the genome assemblies, the contigs containing the CrV-01T ORF 65 hits were identified using the TBLASTN program with the ORF 65 hits as queries and the MAGs as databases parsed with their original sequence identifiers. NDA96179 was located in the RGPE01000254.1 contig of ASM1002823v1, while MFM7859517 was encoded by the JBJTZJ010009788.1 contig of ASM4608718v1. The two contigs were extracted and BLASTN searched against the NCBI core nucleotide database. As expected, the BLASTN queries significantly supported a different taxonomic assignment for these contigs. The top BLASTN hits for RGPE01000254.1 and JBJTZJ010009788.1 were mobile genetic elements: the cyanophage Cr-LKS4 (E = 2 × 10–72) and a plasmid of Anabaena sp. WA102 (E = 5 × 10–134; accession number: CP011457), respectively. Hence, we decided to annotate them accordingly in our study. The sequences were aligned using MUSCLE 3.8.31 [34] and visualized in SeaView [35]. Blocks of evolutionarily conserved sites were selected via Gblocks [36], and a maximum likelihood unrooted phylogenetic tree was computed using PhyML 3.1 [37] with the LG amino acid substitution model [38] and 100 bootstrap replicates.

## Results & discussion

## Proteomic modelling
We were able to identify structural homologs for four cryptic protein-coding genes of CrV-01T (Table 1, Fig. 2). These included a CGF protein (ORF 65), a portal protein (ORF 5), a head completion protein (ORF 22) and a Csf3 protein (ORF 58), which is a highly diverged Cas5 [39]. The latter is a noteworthy finding because, although the genome of CrV-01T contains an intact CRISPR array, its associated Cas protein could not previously be detected using traditional sequence-based approaches [22]. The remaining ORFs and their AF3 models were predicted with low confidence scores or did not have consistent Foldseek and DALI structural homology results (data not shown). Notably, the four ORFs for which we were able to identify structural homology had a significant structural similarity (E ≤ 2.14 × 10−4, TP = 1, DALI Z-score ≥ 9.5 in all cases) despite their very low amino acid sequence similarity (Fig. 2). We acknowledge that convergent evolution is a possibility in the sense that it is possible for sequences from different ancestors to converge towards the same or similar folds. Consequently, convergent evolution should not be ruled out. However, based on the data (Foldseek E < 0.001, true-positive homology probabilities of 1, and DALI Z > 8), we consider that the significant structural similarity between the structural models and their hits is most likely due to an evolutionary relationship [see [23], [40], [41]].

A final remark of the proteomic modelling was ORF 57 of CrV-01T (accession number: AXK90469), which AF3 was able to predict with high confidence (Fig. 3). It had no PDB matches detected by Foldseek or DALI and only two sequence homologs (cyanobacterial in origin) in the NCBI nr database. Although AF3 is not a homology modelling tool, it does incorporate multiple sequence alignment (MSA) and structural template search components, and shallow MSAs often result in low-confidence predictions [7]. Therefore, the generation of a high-confidence model for a protein with few sequence homologs and no detectable structural homologs is notable and underscores the exceptional training and architecture of the tool. The predicted fold of CrV-01T ORF 57 resembles a seahorse, so we decided to name it the seahorse fold. Indeed, AlphaFold models have led to the discovery of novel protein families and folds [42], such as the beta-flower fold in bacteria [43]. CrV-01T ORF 57 is identical in sequence to ORF 59 (WHL30625) of the CrV-like virus Cr-LKS4. In both viruses, this ORF is located immediately upstream of the viral Csf3 structural homolog. Moreover, the Csf3 structural homolog of Cr-LKS4 (ORF 60; WHL30626) is almost identical in sequence to ORF 58 of CrV-01T.

Freshwater cyanophages are one of the most societally relevant groups of aquatic viruses. This is because harmful algal blooms of toxic cyanobacteria have deleterious effects in freshwater environments and constitute a recurrent problem for humans and aquatic ecosystems [44]. Lytic cyanophages act in boom-and-bust cycles capable of reducing the abundance of HAB-forming microorganisms [45]. Hence, there is a growing need to gain insights into the genomic composition of these viruses to obtain a mechanistic understanding of how they interact with their hosts. The discoveries presented here are relevant because the ability to detect highly divergent structural and (anti)-defense proteins has important implications for the advancement of cyanophage therapy for lakes, particularly after the acquisition of virus resistance by toxic cyanobacteria [e.g., 46]. Moreover, the finding of a viral CGF protein hints at novel ways in which viruses may manipulate cyanobacterial metabolism, as discussed below.

## Viral cyanobacteriochrome-like GAF folds
The most intriguing finding of the proteomic modelling was arguably the CGF in CrV-01T (ORF 65). It should be noted that ORF 65 had significant Foldseek and DALI hits to CBCRs and bacteriophytochromes (see Supplementary Data 1 for the top 10 Foldseek and DALI hits). Foldseek’s top hit was a CBCR (E = 9.90 × 10−6, TP = 1), and DALI’s was a bacteriophytochrome (Z = 13.5). DALI compares protein structures by aligning two-dimensional C-α residue-residue distance matrices [47]. In contrast, Foldseek implements a 20-state alphabet describing tertiary interactions [23]. As such, while both tools agreed on the cyano/bacteriophytochrome nature of the hits, it is not surprising to obtain variations regarding top matches. The reader is referred to Fig. S1 for a visual comparison of structural and sequence alignments between Foldseek and DALI top hits. CBCRs contain only GAF domains, while bacteriophytochromes feature a PAS-GAF-PHY domain arrangement [19], [20], [48]. ORF 65 contains a single globular domain, which consistently aligned with the GAF domain region of its CBCR and bacteriophytochrome structural matches. Thus, ORF 65 was annotated as a putative CGF protein.
Since ORF 65 is a highly divergent CGF protein and studying its linear tetrapyrrole chromophore binding capacity is outside of the scope of the present study, it is important to discuss other functional roles that are also possible for this protein. For example, GAF domains can bind heme [49] or cyclic AMP [50], [51]. There are also known GAF domains that do not bind known ligands, and CBCR homologs that do not bind bilin [52], [53]. ORF 65 and related proteins (see below) may represent a new lineage of CGF proteins and/or have been exapted from their cellular counterparts altogether, as sometimes occurs with host-derived viral proteins [e.g., 31].
Interestingly, in addition to the globular domain, ORF 65 was predicted to have a long N-terminal extension (Fig. 4a). Since the extension was poorly modelled by AF3 (Fig. 2l), we resorted to other tools to gain insights into its potential structure and function. The extension was predicted by DeepTMHMM to contain a transmembrane α-helix with a very high probability (Fig. 4b), suggesting that this viral CGF protein may bind the cyanobacterial membrane after it is synthesized in the virocell. The first 41 residues were predicted to have an outside cell topology, phenylalanine 42 to leucine 62 formed the transmembrane region, and the rest were located inside the cell.

To our knowledge, CrV-01T ORF 65 is the first putative CGF protein that has been reported in viruses. It naturally raised the question: are CGFs widespread across the virosphere? To answer this, we first searched for amino acid sequence homologs of ORF 65. Only two homologs could be detected in viruses: Cr-LKS4, which is a CrV-like freshwater cyanophage isolated from Israel in 2017 that infects R. raciborskii [46], and a Cr-LKS4-like virus. The sequences of CrV-01T ORF 65 and Cr-LKS4 ORF 68 were identical. In contrast, we found multiple matches against hypothetical cellular proteins, allowing us to place ORF 65 within an evolutionary context (Fig. 4c; see Fig. S2 for the MSA). The hits were cyanobacterial in origin, indicating that ORF 65 was probably acquired from a cyanobacterial host. All of them presented the aforementioned N-terminal extension (Fig. S3) and many, including both viral hits, were predicted to harbor the transmembrane α-helix with the same outside cell topology (Figs. 4, S4). Specifically, CrV-01T and the two other viruses clustered together and further grouped with Aphanizomenon strains, a filamentous freshwater cyanobacterium. This may suggest that ORF 65 was horizontally transferred to CrV-like viruses from an Aphanizomenon-like host. In this regard, it is interesting to note that R. raciborskii, the known host of CrV-01T, was discovered during an Aphanizomenon bloom [54] (i.e., R. raciborskii and Apanizomenon coexist in nature). As with CrV-01T ORF 65, phylogenetic analyses indicated that the nblA gene of the freshwater cyanophage Ma-LMM01—a gene involved in photobiological processes—was probably acquired from a cyanobacterial host other than the one it is known to infect [18].
Since few viral sequence hits of CrV-01T ORF 65 were found, we subsequently queried the BFVD using Foldseek and experimentally determined cellular CBCRs in different photostates. The BFVD searches retrieved several viral CGFs, including ORF 97 (GenBank accession number: ACT65659) of the marine Prochlorococcus cyanophage P-SS2, isolated from Atlantic Ocean slope waters in 2001 [55]. Back in 2009, when the P-SS2 genome was published, ORF 97 was one of the two cyanobacterial ORFan genes detected in the virus, out of a total of 131 protein-coding genes [55]. As of the writing of this manuscript, 16 years later, a BLASTP search revealed only five sequence homologs in the NCBI nr database. All of them were hypothetical proteins from cyanobacteria (Synechococcus spp.). Note that one of the hits, MGA0983548.1, from a MAG (ASM4944787v1) with an NCBI taxonomy check status labeled as inconclusive was incorrectly annotated in NCBI as a Burkholderiaceae bacterium. However, as described above, after identifying and blasting the contig containing the hit (JBMTSB010000034.1), the correct taxonomic assignment of the sequence could be readily elucidated (Synechococcus sp. SYN20, E = 3 × 10–176). Interestingly, P-SS2 was found to be potentially capable of integrating into its host, rendering its genome as part of the cyanobacterial mobilome [55]; while CrV-01T recently lost the ability to form lysogens [22]. Commonalities between CrV and P-SS2 include a non-contractile tail (i.e., a ‘siphovirus’ morphology), linear genomes, similar number of ORFs and genome sizes (∼104 kb for CrV, ∼108 kb for P-SS2), and a lack of photosynthesis genes.
All viral CGF proteins included in this study had E-values < 0.001 and true-positive probabilities of 1. Some of them were annotated as GAF-domain containing proteins in BFVD and UniProt [56] (Fig. 5), further supporting their relatedness to CBCRs [19], [20], [21]. Moreover, the significant E-values and true-positive probabilities of 1 point against the possibility of convergent evolution. As in the case of CrV-01T ORF 65, all these viral proteins had an N-terminal extension, and most were predicted to contain a transmembrane α-helix (Fig. 5, S5). Unlike CrV-01T ORF 65 and its sequence homologs, which shared the same inside cell topology, DeepTMHMM predicted different topologies for certain viral CGF proteins. The globular (GAF) domain was predicted to be located inside the cell in some cases, while in others, outside.

## Conclusions
Cyanobacteriochrome GAF-like fold proteins are present among the Caudoviricetes, including cyanophages. The GAF domain sequence, hallmark of CBCRs, is still possible to be detected in some of these divergent viral proteins. Viral CGF proteins harbor an N-terminal extension that in most cases is predicted to contain a transmembrane α-helix. In CrV-like cyanophages, CGF protein-coding genes appear to have been transferred from a freshwater cyanobacterial host. As described earlier, CBCRs regulate diverse processes in cyanobacterial cells, including phototaxis, cyclic nucleotide metabolism and optimization of light harvesting. However, since these proteins are highly divergent, the possibility of them carrying out a function other than that of their structural homologs cannot be ruled out. In any case, the presence of CGF proteins in cyanophage genomes suggests novel ways in which viruses may manipulate the metabolism of their cyanobacterial hosts, the most abundant oxygenic phototrophs on Earth. Follow-up studies should apply AF3 proteomic modelling to a larger number of cyanophage genomes and experimentally investigate these computational findings, including transcriptomic analyses, bilin binding and photobiological studies, and structure determination efforts. Overall, the results reported here emphasize the importance of applying structural homology detection methods when annotating viral genomes and highlight the potential of AlphaFold for exploring the dark matter of the aquatic virosphere.

## Figure Fig. 4: CrV-01T open reading frame (ORF) 65 and amino acid sequence homologs. (a) Structural model of ORF 65 colored using a rainbow gradient, from the N-terminus in blue to the C-terminus in red. The predicted transmembrane residues are colored in black. (b) DeepTMHMM transmembrane probability plot. Per residue transmembrane α-helix probabilities are shown in red; outside and inside topologies are depicted in blue and pink, respectively. (c) Maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree of ORF 65 sequence homologs. Branch support values are shown next to the branches. The scale bar indicates 0.2 amino acid substitutions per site. Accession numbers are included after the strain names. Cyanophages are highlighted in bold. CrV-01T ORF 65 and Cr-LKS4 ORF 68 sequences are identical, so they are represented within the same branch of the tree. Asterisks indicate the hits with corrected taxonomic assignments (see 2.2). ‘M’ denotes the proteins with predicted transmembrane α-helices.
