Cryo-EM structure of the SRI-HtrI complex reveals the cytoplasmic coupling in an archaeal phototaxis system.
Lim GZ, Lin YE, Chen PC, Fu HY, Yang CS. (2026). Nature communications. . doi:10.1038/s41467-026-72092-6. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Cryo-EM structure of the SRI-HtrI complex reveals the cytoplasmic coupling in an archaeal phototaxis system

## Abstract
AbstractSensory rhodopsins (SRs) in haloarchaea form complexes with their cognate transducers (Htrs) to produce wavelength-specific phototactic responses, yet similar architectures mediate distinct behaviors: SRI mediates attraction, SRII drives repulsion, whereas SRM modulates both responses. Until now, structural insight was limited to the Natronomonas pharaonis SRII-HtrII system in a truncated form, without a full-length counterpart for comparison. Moreover, NpHtrII is distinct among HtrII transducers in lacking the large periplasmic domain retained in homologs from Halobacterium salinarum, Haloarcula marismortui and Haloarcula taiwanensis, leaving the canonical SR-Htr architecture unknown. Here, we report the cryo-EM structure of the Haloarcula taiwanensis SRI-HtrI complex, providing a near native, non-crystallized view of a full-length SR-Htr dimer with the cytoplasmic HAMP1 domain resolved. The structure reveals the intact homodimeric receptor-transducer assembly and visualizes the interface between the helix G (Arg215), SRI E-F loop (Pro154), and HtrI HAMP1. These findings fill the long-standing structural gap for a canonical SR-Htr complex and establish a framework for conserved receptor-transducer coupling across archaeal phototaxis systems.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Archaeal biology, Membrane proteins, Membrane biophysics

## Introduction
Haloarchaea rely on microbial rhodopsins (mRhos) to harvest solar energy and direct phototaxis toward favorable conditions1,2. The mRhos fall into two broad classes: ion transporters such as bacteriorhodopsin (BR) and halorhodopsin (HR)3,4, and sensory rhodopsins (SR) that act with cognate transducers (Htr) to mediate phototaxis. Among the latter, sensory rhodopsin I in complex with HtrI (SRI-HtrI) drives photo-attraction under orange light and repulsion under UV1,5,6, SRII-HtrII mediates blue-light repulsion7,8, and SRM-HtrM modulates both under green light9,10, with different species varying in their rhodopsin repertoires. Halobacterium salinarum (Hs) and Haloarcula taiwanensis (Ht) encode BR, HR, SRI, and SRII11–13, while Haloarcula marismortui (Hm) harbors additional BRII and SRM; whereas Natronomonas pharaonis (Np) retains only HR and SRII14, reflecting a blue-light preference.
Crystallographic analyses of NpSRII alone and NpSRII with truncated HtrII (1–114) revealed how receptor-transducer contacts may propagate conformational changes across the membrane15,16. Specific features such as a hydrogen bond between Tyr199 of SRII and Asn74 of HtrII, and a glycine hinge (Gly83) within the transducer, were proposed to stabilize the interface and enable flexibility for signal transduction17,18. Photoactivation is thought to drive outward movement of NpSRII helix F, inducing TM2 rotation and a horizontal shift in HtrII to propagate the signal, while the NpSRII-Ser154 on the E–F loop has also been proposed to interact with the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal region of HtrII (residues 91–95) upon photoactivation19. Spin-labeling and mutational studies further suggested that the first amphipathic helix of HAMP1 (AS1) continues from TM2 and relays light-induced displacements, while HAMP domains toggle between complementary x-da and knobs-into-holes packing states20,21. Together, structural insights into NpSRII, both alone and in complex with its truncated two-transmembrane regions of HtrII, have been extensively studied, establishing models for how NpSRII couples light activation to the NpHtrII HAMP conformational switching and downstream chemotactic signaling22,23.
However, current structural insights are derived exclusively from the NpSRII-HtrII truncated constructs, which mediate photo-repellent responses under blue light. Notably, NpHtrII is distinct among the HtrII family in that it lacks the large periplasmic (ligand-binding or sensory) domain that is otherwise retained in HtrII homologs from H. salinarum, H. marismortui24,25 and H. taiwanensis. In contrast, all known HtrI proteins naturally lack the large periplasmic domain, which may represent the canonical architecture of the SRI-HtrI signaling system. Consequently, the absence of this domain is expected for HtrI but distinguishes NpHtrII from other HtrII proteins. This architecture raises the possibility that structures derived from NpSRII-HtrII may not fully represent the broader family of sensory rhodopsin-transducer assemblies. In particular, no structural information is available for SRI or the SRI-HtrI complex, partly due to the instability of HsSRI under low-salt conditions26. As a result, whether similar structural principles govern the functionally opposite SRI-HtrI system and how HAMP domains contribute to photo-attractant signaling have remained unclear.
Here, we report the cryo-EM structure of the Haloarcula taiwanensis (Ht) SRI-HtrI complex at 4.92 Å resolution. This structure directly visualizes the SRI-HtrI assembly and unveils the HtrI HAMP1 domain that has never been structurally resolved before. Furthermore, it shows that the HAMP1 domain is engaged by interactions from the E–F loop and helix G of HtSRI and adopts a knobs-into-holes configuration in a non-activated state, opposite to the putative models for the dark-state NpHtrII HAMP1 in previous studies21. Our findings provide a structural and mechanistic framework for understanding how haloarchaea employ distinct conformational strategies to mediate photo-attractant versus photo-repellent signaling while retaining a conserved interaction between the E–F loop and HAMP1 domain.

## Results

## Visualization of HtSRI-HtrI in a near-native condition
To visualize the HtSRI-HtrI phototaxis complex under conditions closely approximating its native state, free from crystal lattice constraints and in a detergent-solubilized environment, we expressed a full-length fusion protein in which the C-terminus of HtSRI was connected via a 10-residue flexible linker (ASASNGASAH) to the N-terminus of its cognate transducer HtHtrI. The fused HtSRI-HtrI retained its characteristic absorbance maximum (Abs-max) and displayed similar light-driven photocycle kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Protein gel electrophoresis confirmed that the fusion remained intact upon detergent solubilization, where it assembles as a homodimer (Supplementary Fig. 1d), consistent with the canonical 2:2 receptor-transducer stoichiometry demonstrated for fused HsSRI-HtrI in native membrane27.
Single-particle cryo-EM reconstruction of the detergent-solubilized complex revealed a full-length HtSRI-HtrI homodimer, with the complete receptor and the transducer resolved up to residue 124 (Fig. 1). 2D classification of side views clearly showed a continuous density for the HtrI cytoplasmic tail extending from the detergent micelle (Fig. 1a–c), confirming that the full-length dimer complex remained intact in the solubilized state and during vitrification. Within the resolved HtrI cytoplasmic region, the first HAMP domain (HAMP1) was resolved (HtHtrI residues 56–104) and adopted a canonical four-helix coiled-coil configuration closely matching the compact gearbox model proposed for NpHtrII21 and the broader HAMP domain family28.

Comparison with the NpSRII-HtrII crystal structure (residues 1-82 of NpHtrII; PDB ID: 1H2S) shows that the overall helical arrangement is largely conserved between HtSRI and NpSRII except for helix E, while notable deviations are observed in the relative placement of the transducer helices (Fig. 2b). In HtHtrI, TM1 and TM2 are positioned slightly closer compared to their counterparts in NpHtrII, producing a narrower cytoplasmic opening toward the HAMP domain (Fig. 2b, inset). The absence of the intrinsic N-terminal region in the truncated NpHtrII crystallographic construct should not be overlooked when considering the observed difference in TM1-TM2 separation. This displacement also corresponds to a modest outward shift of helices E and F in HtSRI relative to NpSRII, altering the arrangements of the SR-Htr interface.
In addition, we observed a short protruding segment extending from the extracellular end of the NpHtrII transmembrane region that is absent in HtHtrI (Fig. 2b). This feature contributes to a more elongated extracellular profile in NpHtrII, whereas the corresponding region in HtHtrI appears more embedded within the membrane. These comparisons show that the general SR-Htr architecture is conserved, but with distinct differences in transducer positioning between HtSRI-HtrI and NpSRII-HtrII, suggesting further variations in their retinal-binding pocket and the interacting mechanisms at the SR-Htr interfaces.

## A distinct transducer binding interface in HtSRI-HtrI
The cryo-EM structure of the HtSRI-HtrI dimer complex further enables a structure-based investigation into the distinct interaction mechanism between SR and Htr (Fig. 3). Unlike the distinct NpSRII-HtrII24,25, which engages via hydrogen bonding between Tyr199 on Helix G of SRII and Asn74 on TM1 of HtrII (Fig. 3b), the HtSRI-HtrI complex lacks such hydrogen bond-mediated association at this interface (Fig. 3c). Although an Asn-Tyr pair is present near the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal region, the likelihood of a hydrogen bond-mediated association is scarce due to spatial separation (Fig. 3d). Notably, HtHtrI also lacks the glycine hinge corresponding to NpHtrII-Gly83; instead, an alanine occupies this position according to structural alignment (Fig. 3e), in agreement with protein sequence alignment (Fig. 3g).

Analysis of interfacial neighboring residues in ChimeraX revealed that helix G of HtSRI, particularly Phe199, together with aliphatic residues (HtHtrI-Ala20, -Leu24, -Gly45) (Fig. 3c), forms a cluster with the HtHtrI TM regions. The helix arrangements suggest similar observations at the interface in NpSRII-HtrII, but with an important difference: the canonical Tyr199-Asn74 hydrogen bond latch is absent, replaced by a hydrophobic knob centered on Phe199. Thus, the HtSRI-HtrI interface is possibly characterized by distributed van der Waals contacts rather than a discrete polar interaction. While these features differ from those described for the NpSRII-HtrII complex, the extent to which they reflect distinct signal transduction mechanisms remains to be evaluated with the limits of the currently available structural data. We performed site-directed mutagenesis of Phe199 to examine its contribution to the receptor-transducer interface (see below and Fig. 4c–g).

## Structural proximity of the HtSRI E–F loop and helix G to the HtrI HAMP1 domain
The structural arrangement between the SR E–F loop and cytoplasmic membrane-proximal region of Htr is visualized, consistent with the proximity previously observed from Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) between NpSRII-Ser154 and residues 91–95 of HtrII19. In the HtSRI-HtrI complex, we now resolve the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal domain and identify it as the HAMP1 domain. Pro154 (Fig. 3f) on the E–F loop of HtSRI (corresponding to NpSRII-Ser154) is positioned near Glu63 and Gln67 on HtHtrI (corresponding to NpHtrII-Thr92 and -Lys96), equivalent to the beginning of the HAMP1 AS1 helix (Fig. 3g). Based on the chemical properties of the residues and the modeled geometry, the interface is consistent with predominantly nonpolar packing between Pro154 and the membrane-proximal region of HAMP1, with nearby polar residues potentially contributing additional stabilization.

## Electrostatic properties of the HtSRI-HtrI interface and mutational analysis
To further characterize the transducer-rhodopsin interface at the current resolution, we computed the electrostatic surface potential using the Coulombic approximation in ChimeraX based on the current modeled geometry and visualized it with a red-white-blue gradient denoting negative, neutral, and positive values, respectively (Fig. 4a). The surface potentials ranged from −17.9 to +10.1 for HtSRI and from −23.8 to +13.6 for HtHtrI. A strongly electronegative patch extends along the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal surface of the HtrI helical bundle beginning near Glu56 and extending toward the solvent-exposed cytoplasmic face. In contrast, the transmembrane region of the SRI-HtrI interface appears largely neutral. At the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal interface, electrostatic complementarity is observed: Arg215 on HtSRI helix G is positioned near HtHtrI-Glu63 and -Ser60 (TM2) in the current model (Fig. 4b).
A HtSRI(R215E)-HtrI single-point mutant was generated in the fusion construct to probe the role of Arg215 in maintaining the transducer-rhodopsin interface. Substitution of the positively charged Arg with a negatively charged Glu resulted in a pronounced shift in the absorption maximum (Abs-max) of the complex, blue-shifting from ~590 nm in the wild-type HtSRI-HtrI to ~560 nm in HtSRI(R215E)-HtrI at both pH 5.0 (Fig. 4c) and pH 6.7 (Fig. 4d) when reconstituted in 0.02% LMNG. Notably, at pH 6.7, the Abs-max of HtSRI(R215E)-HtrI closely matches that of HtSRI alone (i.e., not complexed with HtrI) in 0.02% LMNG. No reliable absorption spectrum of HtSRI alone at pH 5.0 was obtained due to severe aggregations. In contrast, the HtSRI(F199A)-HtrI mutant, in which HtSRI-Phe199 (corresponding to NpSRII-Tyr199) was replaced with Ala, retained an Abs-max of ~590 nm comparable to that of the wild-type complex under the same conditions (Fig. 4c, d).
Flash-laser-induced photocycle measurements were further conducted to study the effects of mutations on the kinetics of HtSRI (Fig. 4e, f). Under both pH 5.0 and pH 6.7 conditions, the wild-type HtSRI-HtrI complex exhibited a measurable pH dependence in its photocycle recovery kinetics, with a longer apparent time constant (higher τ value, slower recovery) at pH 5.0 (~8.2 s) compared to pH 6.7 (~6.0 s) (Fig. 4g). This difference suggests that the photocycle dynamics of HtSRI-HtrI are sensitive to environmental pH to some extent over this range. While pH-dependent modulation of sensory rhodopsin I kinetics has been reported previously27,29, the magnitude of the effect observed here suggests that HtSRI may exhibit distinct pH responsiveness, warranting further investigation. Interestingly, the HtSRI(R215E)-HtrI mutant showed altered photocycle behavior. At pH 5.0, reliable kinetic fitting could not be obtained (Fig. 4e, g). At pH 6.7, the recovery kinetics of HtSRI(R215E)-HtrI were accelerated (lower τ value) relative to the wild-type complex, comparable to those observed for HtSRI alone under the same conditions (Fig. 4f, g). In comparison, the HtSRI(F199A)-HtrI mutant retained broadly similar photocycle kinetics at both pH values (Fig. 4e–g). The recovery time constants differed only modestly from wild-type (τ WT = 6.038 s and τ F199A = 5.756 s at pH 6.7, Fig. 4g) and did not exhibit the significant acceleration observed for the R215E mutant.
These spectral and kinetic analyses indicate that mutation of Arg215 has a pronounced effect on both the absorption properties and photocycle recovery of the HtSRI-HtrI complex, whereas substitution of Phe199 produces comparatively minor changes under the conditions tested.

## HAMP1 domain of HtHtrI
Building on the observed SR-Htr contacts, we next examined the architecture of the adjacent HAMP1 domain of HtHtrI, a crucial yet, to the best of our knowledge, previously unresolved element that mediates signal relay from the membrane interface toward the cytoplasmic transduction regions. The domain adopts the general HAMP fold, comprising a parallel four-helix coiled-coil bundle formed by dimerization of AS1 and AS2 helices connected through a short linker (Fig. 5a). Sequence alignment of the HtHtrI HAMP1 domain (residues 56–104) with the corresponding region of NpHtrII (residues 82–133) showed a conserved heptad repeat pattern, with hydrophobic residues occupying the first and fourth positions in the heptad repeat (denoted a and d positions in Fig. 5b) that stabilize the coiled-coil core21,30. Based on in silico predictions of side chain conformations within the limits of the achieved resolution, the relative arrangement of helices at the upper levels (layers 1–2) of HAMP1 are consistent with a knobs-into-holes-like packing mode, with hydrophobic residues including Phe88-Ile61 and Leu64-Leu91 residues occupying adjacent positions (Fig. 5c). At subsequent levels (layer 3–4), however, the bundle deviates from canonical packing, with the helical cross-section shifting from a rectangular to a rhombic arrangement, involving residues such as Thr68-Ile95 and Val71-Met98 (Fig. 5d, e).

## Comparison of retinal-binding pocket residues in HtSRI, NpSRII, and HsBR
As the retinal-binding pocket is central to the function of retinal-binding proteins, we examined residues located in proximity to the retinal based on the overall placement of transmembrane helices within the cryo-EM density. Inspection of the retinal-binding pocket revealed density for the β-ionone ring and the adjacent polyene carbons near the Schiff base in HtSRI, allowing reasonable placement of the chromophore headgroup (Fig. 1e inset). Structural alignment of HtSRI with higher-resolution NpSRII and HsBR crystal structures provides a framework to compare conserved and divergent features of the retinal-binding pocket (Fig. 6a–c). The primary and secondary counterions to the protonated Schiff base (Asp7631 and Asp201 in HtSRI; Asp75 and Asp201 in NpSRII; Asp85 and Asp212 in HsBR32), as well as aromatic residues Trp77, Trp171, Tyr174, Trp178 (HtSRI numbering), are conserved in all three proteins, consistent with their established roles in tuning characteristic spectral shift of mRhos. In NpSRII, Tyr174 and Thr204 form a hydrogen-bond pair that rearranges upon photoisomerization33, whereas both HtSRI and HsBR encode Ala at this site (Ala204 and Ala215, respectively), indicating that this specific interaction is absent outside SRII.

Hydrophobic and polar residues surrounding the β-ionone ring and polyene chain are generally conserved but show species-specific differences (Fig. 6c). In HtSRI, Met109 and His135 align with Met118 and Met145 in HsBR, residues known from mutagenesis to influence absorption maxima and photocycle kinetics34. At positions 131–132, HtSRI encodes Ser-Ala, retaining one hydroxyl group adjacent to the retinal β-ionone ring; in HsBR, Ser-Thr provides two hydroxyls, while in NpSRII, Gly-Ala eliminates them. Previous structural studies have shown that hydroxyl groups at this site promote red-shifted absorption, while their absence favors blue shifts16. On the extracellular side, HtSRI and HsBR share aromatic Tyr197 and Phe208, whereas NpSRII carries Ile197, removing an aromatic group from the chromophore environment. These features demonstrating the distinct retinal-binding pocket environment, together with the Arg215-mediated interactions, hinting at mechanistic differences between HtSRI-HtrI and NpSRII-HtrII that may underlie their divergent functional behaviors.

## Discussion
Our cryo-EM structure of the HtSRI-HtrI fusion complex provides direct visualization of a sensory rhodopsin-transducer homodimer resolved beyond the transmembrane region, which has not been resolved before since its initial discovery by spectroscopic identification more than four decades ago6. This fusion protein design follows the strategy previously validated for H. salinarum SRI-HtrI (60% similarity with HtSRI-HtrI, Supplementary Figs. 4, 5), which retained its ability to mediate attractant response despite covalent fusion of the two partners27. The structure reveals the intact homodimeric architecture (Fig. 1), the retinal-binding pocket (Fig. 1e inset, 6), and the HAMP1 domain (Fig. 5) in its near-native context (summarized in Fig. 7). Notably, the cryo-EM structure also reveals the interface between the SRI E–F loop and the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal HAMP1 region of HtrI (Figs. 3, 4), providing the direct visualization of this coupling site in an intact complex. These findings extend earlier crystallographic studies of truncated NpSRII-HtrII (1–114) constructs and offer a structural framework for understanding how receptor-transducer coupling translates into species-specific signaling outputs that drive phototaxis.

Summary of key elements along the signaling pathway from rhodopsin to the HAMP1 domain. a At the transducer-rhodopsin interface, HtSRI helix G engages through R215, possibly with HtHtrI-Ala59 and -Ser60. b Pro154 on E–F loop and R215 on helix G of the HtSRI are positioned close to Glu63 and Gln67 on HtrI, suggesting coupling with the HAMP1 AS1 helix at the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal region. c In the cytoplasmic region, the HAMP1 domain of HtHtrI exhibits knobs-into-holes packing across its helical bundle, with a gradual transition from rectangular to rhombic cross-sections.
To obtain a stable and homogeneous sample for high-resolution imaging, the HtSRI-HtrI complex was solubilized from E. coli membranes with n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) and exchanged into lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) detergent during Ni-NTA affinity purification. Owing to the intrinsic low-salt tolerance of HtSRI (Supplementary Fig. 2), the purified complex retained its blue coloration for about a week in DDM, but LMNG exchange markedly improved stability. The LMNG-solubilized complex maintained its spectral properties for at least four months when stored in the dark at 4 °C (Supplementary Fig. 1f). Bio-Beads treatment to maintain detergent concentration at 0.01% (w/v) further enhanced image contrast by minimizing filamentous micelle contaminants. These results establish that the cryo-EM reconstruction represents a functionally intact and structurally native-like HtSRI-HtrI signaling unit. The preserved photochemical activity, 2:2 stoichiometry, resolved transducer tail, and HAMP1 configuration all point to a physiologically relevant architecture that bridges prior biochemical and spectroscopic findings on archaeal SR-Htr complexes.
Although the full-length HtHtrI was only partially resolved, in the final structural reconstruction, 2D classification of side-view particles clearly showed the transducer tail extending from the detergent micelle (Fig. 1b, c), adding to the evidence that the complex remained intact. We attribute the absence of density for the distal cytoplasmic domain to its intrinsic flexibility. During classification, likelihood optimization was known to favor alignment of the more rigid transmembrane core within the detergent micelle, which dominates the signal-to-noise ratio, resulting in suboptimal averaging of dynamic cytoplasmic regions. Such underrepresentation of flexible domains is a recognized limitation in cryo-EM reconstructions of detergent- or nanodisc-embedded membrane proteins35. It was demonstrated that conventional uniform refinement can bias alignment toward the micelle and rigid core, thereby underfitting peripheral regions35. To address this, we performed local refinement with a static mask around the protein, which sufficiently improved density to reconstruct the model up to residue 124 of HtHtrI (Supplementary Fig. 3).
Our cryo-EM data now contextualize the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal helical arrangements that were previously inferred only through FRET and computational models. Earlier FRET measurements19 demonstrated that NpSRII-Ser154 approaches within 10–15 Å of residues 91–95 in the HtrII membrane-proximal domain upon photoactivation, implying a light-induced tightening at the SR-Htr interface. In our structure, the equivalent HtSRI-Pro154 engages directly with HtHtr-Glu63 and -Gln67 (Fig. 3g), forming a compact interface that gives rise to the predicted U-shaped conformation as in the NpSRII-HtrII complex36. However, as SRII-HtrII mediates a repellent response, SRI-HtrI drives an attractant response, suggesting that this conserved local interaction does not itself encode signal polarity, but rather serves as a mechanical coupling pivot. The proline-mediated anchoring of the E–F loop against the HAMP1 AS1 helix in HtSRI may therefore serve as a conserved coupling element in the sensory rhodopsin-transducer complex, yet can give rise to an opposite signaling output, potentially through differences in HAMP bundle conformational dynamics. These dynamic modulations have been proposed18,21 as the structural basis for functional divergence despite conserved local interfaces, consistent with the packing features observed in the HtHtrI HAMP1 domain.
The cytoplasmic membrane-proximal region plays a pivotal role in mediating communication between SRI and HtrI. Site-directed mutagenesis and functional analyses identify Arg215 as a key determinant of receptor-transducer coupling in the HtSRI-HtrI complex. Substitution of Arg215 with Glu induces a significant blue shift in Abs-max (Fig. 4c, d) and accelerates photocycle recovery (Fig.4e–g), yielding behavior closely resembling HtSRI in the absence of HtrI. In contrast, mutation of Phe199 (corresponding to the well-characterized NpSRII-Tyr199) results in only minor spectral and kinetic changes under the conditions tested, indicating that this transmembrane site does not play a dominant role in HtSRI-HtrI coupling. These observations support a model in which productive coupling depends critically on stabilization at the cytoplasmic membrane-proximal interface19. This conclusion is conceptually consistent with earlier studies demonstrating that ion-pumping mRho proteins can be rendered sensory-like through single-point mutations that suppress ion transport activity while enabling transducer coupling. In those systems, HsBR37 and Haloquadratum walsbyi middle rhodopsin (HwMR)38 were converted into sensory-like rhodopsins via targeted mutations that disrupt their inherent ion transport, revealing that retinal-proximal interactions within the transmembrane regions are sufficient to induce conformational changes that may be transmitted as a signal, but additional residues are required to geometrically position the transducer for efficient signal relay. In this framework, Arg215 in HtSRI appears to serve an analogous interface-stabilizing role at a distinct cytoplasmic membrane-proximal location.
Analysis of our HAMP1 domain structure highlights the generalizability of its conformational logic across archaeal and bacterial signaling systems. Current models of HAMP signaling can be broadly categorized into discrete and ensemble-based frameworks. Discrete-based models encompass the gear-box model28,39–42, involving transitions between complementary x-da and knobs-into-holes states, along with scissor and piston models that invoke helical rotations, tilts, or axial shifts39,43–46. These motions are not mutually exclusive and may occur in combination during signaling47. On the other hand, the dynamic bundle model under the ensemble-based framework suggests an ensemble of conformations with similar stabilities that yield biphasic outputs48,49. These studies underscore that HAMP domains can exploit multiple conformational modes to regulate downstream effectors. Previous studies utilizing EPR20,21,50 and NMR51 have characterized the HAMP1 domain of NpHtrII as inherently dynamic. In contrast, our structural resolution of the HtHtrI HAMP1 domain suggests a comparatively rigid conformation, highlighting potential differences in conformational dynamics among homologous HAMP domains. Our HtSRI-HtrI structure, which exhibits the knobs-into-holes configuration with a mixed rectangular and rhombic cross-sections in the HAMP1 domain (Fig. 5), is consistent with the view of multiple conformational states and may represent an intermediate in the signaling cycle. This further implies a transitional conformation of the HAMP1 domain, as shown in molecular dynamics simulations22 of the NpHtrII HAMP1 domain, where a rectangular cross-section was proposed to be a resting-like configuration, while a rhombic arrangement is associated with the signaling-like state. Together, these observations support a model in which HAMP domains transmit signals through structural transitions that integrate features of both two-state and dynamic bundle models21. Signal output may therefore arise from coordinated changes in conformation and dynamics, enabling opposite signaling states.
When the cryo-EM structure of HtSRI-HtrI was aligned with the NpSRII-HtrII(1–114) crystal structure, most TM helices of the photoreceptor regions were relatively well aligned except for helix E. Besides, prominent distinctions in the Htr placement relative to SR in HtSRI-HtrI and NpSRII-HtrII complex suggested mechanistic divergence between the two species. Investigations of the receptor-transducer interface (Figs. 3, 4) reinforced this hypothesis. In the NpSRII-HtrII complex, breakage of the Tyr199-Asn74 hydrogen bond upon retinal isomerization displaces helix F and rotates HtrII helices, thereby coupling to the HAMP1 domain21,33,52. A key feature enabling this relay is the Thr204-Tyr174 hydrogen bond in NpSRII, which strengthens upon K-state formation and transmits local changes in the retinal pocket to helix F and the transducer37. Mutation of either partner abolishes phototaxis signaling, underscoring its essential role. In contrast, our HtSRI-HtrI structure lacks this bond altogether, as Ala204 replaces Thr204, similar to bacteriorhodopsin and eliminating the hydrogen-bonded relay. Instead, HtSRI employs a hydrophobic cluster centered on Phe199 in contact with HtrI TM1, suggesting an alternative mechanism of coupling.
A characteristic property of retinylidene proteins, including microbial rhodopsins and animal visual pigments, is the spectral tuning of the retinal chromophore across the visible range. In H. salinarum, HsSRI exhibits absorption maxima between 580 and 590 nm (depending on the reconstituted lipid environment), corresponding to the yellow-orange region16,27, whereas SRII is blue-shifted, with maxima at 487 nm for HsSRII and 497 nm for NpSRII16 in native archaeal membranes. Although at the reported resolution, only the β-ionone head group and part of the polyene carbons were fitted in density maps, an all-trans retinal could be rigidly fitted into the HtSRI RBP without incurring significant clashes (Fig. 1e, inset). A similar observation was made in the ~5–6 Å halorhodopsin (HR) structure reported by the Henderson group, where density was evident for the retinal β-ionone headgroup, but the distal polyene chain was less well-defined53.
Furthermore, structural alignment with higher-resolution NpSRII and HsBR references provides a framework to compare conserved and divergent features of the retinal-binding pocket (Fig. 6a–c). Interestingly, HtSRI RBP showed higher similarity to that of HsBR (Fig. 6c), with 14 out of 18 residues conserved, while only 11 residues were conserved in NpSRII. Most notably, HtSRI Met109, Ser131, and Ala204 were conserved in HsBR, but varied in NpSRII. Mutagenesis in NpSRII demonstrated that single-site variants of V108M, G130S, and T204A (corresponding to HtSRI Met109, Ser131, and Ala204) produced only minimal spectral shifts (≤ 8 nm), whereas the triple mutant red-shifted the NpSRII absorption maximum by ~15 nm54. On the other hand, His135, Tyr197 in HtSRI were replaced by Phe and Ile in NpSRII, and Met and Phe in HsBR (Fig. 6c), highlighting the presence of species-specific residues within the RBP that may further contribute to the spectral differences among these mRhos.
Beyond the canonical SRI-HtrI and SRII-HtrII systems, our group previously reported a third sensory rhodopsin system in Haloarcula marismortui (HmSRM-HtrM), which introduced an additional layer of complexity into haloarchaeal phototaxis9. Unlike SRI and SRII, the SRM-HtrM complex attenuates both positive and negative phototaxis, most likely through its sole HAMP domain and competition with chemotaxis components, despite lacking the canonical methyl-accepting and CheA/CheW-binding domains of other transducers. This work established that SR-Htr assemblies have diversified through evolution to generate signaling outputs beyond simple attraction or repulsion. Similarly, HwMR was previously reported as a rhodopsin that retains SR-like features but functions as a light-driven magnesium transporter in the absence of any known cognate transducer38. Building on this, our team uncovered its function as a light-driven magnesium transporter and reported the crystal structures of the wild-type protein and its D84N mutant55,56. These studies underscore the remarkable versatility and conservation among microbial rhodopsins, spanning canonical signaling complexes, modulators of signaling, and ion-transport functions. Within this broader context, the HtSRI-HtrI structure presented here provides the direct structural basis for a canonical SR-Htr complex, expressed and analyzed in full-length, allowing mechanistic contrasts to be drawn with the NpSRII-HtrII system.
A practical outcome of our study lies in the biochemical stabilization of the complex. Structural analysis of the SRI system has long been hampered by its intrinsic instability under low-salt conditions, with H. salinarum SRI (HsSRI) reported to lose activity within 24 h26. By contrast, the H. taiwanensis SRI-HtrI (HtSRI-HtrI) fusion investigated in this study displayed tolerance to reduced salt and could be stabilized in LMNG detergent for months without loss of spectral properties (Supplementary Fig. 1). This combination of low-salt tolerance nature and detergent stabilization strategy of HtSRI-HtrI enabled cryo-EM analysis and may serve as a useful system for future structural and mechanistic investigations of the broader sensory rhodopsin family.
To date, the only structurally characterized member of the SR-Htr family has been NpSRII in complex with a truncated form of NpHtrII (residues 1–114 of 534; resolved up to Leu82; PDB ID 1H2S)15. Unlike other haloarchaeal transducers such as HsHtrII and HmHtrII, which retain large extracellular sensory domains possibly inherited from chemoreceptor ancestors24,25, NpHtrII is atypical in lacking this region altogether. This peculiarity raises the possibility that NpHtrII represents a specialized evolutionary branch rather than a canonical SR-associated transducer. The short extracellular TM helices protrusion of NpHtrII (Fig. 2b) also hinted at a special case of the SR-Htr system. As such, no typical full-length SR-Htr complex has yet been structurally resolved. Our cryo-EM structure of the HtSRI-HtrI complex, therefore, fills a critical gap, providing the visualization of a stable dimer complex in a near-native and functional state, establishing a framework for understanding how diverse SR-Htr pairs couple light detection to downstream signaling pathways.
Beyond their physiological role in phototaxis, SR-Htr complexes represent a versatile platform for optogenetic and synthetic biology applications. Their modular design (linking light-sensing rhodopsins to conserved two-component signaling cascades) can, in principle, be engineered for light-driven control of gene expression, rhodopsin-dependent kinase regulation and using SRs as optogenetic switches. By expanding the structural basis of how SRs interface with their cognate transducers, our findings provide a foundation for understanding the underlying mechanism for repurposing these systems for tailored cellular control.

## Methods

## Bacterial strains and plasmids
E. coli expression pET-21b vector was used to express mRho and mRho-transducer fused proteins. E. coli strain DH5α for general recombinant DNA cloning experiments and E. coli strain C43(DE3) for protein expression were grown in LB medium with 50 µg/ml ampicillin at 37 °C.

## Sequence analysis
The mRho protein sequences in the H. taiwanensis genome were identified by blastp analysis (/blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using Clustal W in MegAlign of the Lasergene or MUSCLE57 in Ugene58 software package to compare familial relations of mRho proteins between H. taiwanensis and related species.

## Cloning, expression, and purification of HtSRI-HtrI fusion proteins
For DNA manipulation, the sensory rhodopsin I gene (sop1) fragment was amplified with PCR from the total genomic DNA of the Haloarcula taiwanensis using two primers, a forward primer has NcoI restriction site (5′-gatatacatatgcgcgtctcgttgcgcgattcg-3′) and a reverse primer with an NdeI restriction site, following a linker sequence, encoding the amino acid sequence ASASNGASAH (5′-ctgccatatgcgccgacgcgccgttcgacgccgacgcgtccgccggcgtcactgtgg-3′). For the cognate transducer I gene, the forward primer has NdeI restriction site (5′-gatatacatatgactatttctagtgtcaagcagagc-3′), and the reverse primer has XhoI restriction sites (5′-ccgtcaattggaggtgacgacgcagcgcaccatcaccatcaccattagctcgaggtcg-3′). The cloned DNA fragment was digested with restriction enzymes, ligated into a full-length fusion sequence, and cloned into an NcoI/XhoI-treated pET-21b vector. Site-directed-mutagenesis experiments were conducted using the following primers: 5′-ggtgtcagcatcacctatgcagccctcgacctgct-3′ (HtSRI-F199A) and 5′-gtgtacttcttttacgccgagcggcaggtgttcgccacg-3′ (HtSRI-R215E).
The expression plasmid containing the HtSRI-HtrI gene was transformed into E. coli C43 (DE3). The transformed E. coli was cultured in 5 mL LB medium broth with ampicillin (final conc. 50 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 8 h. The culture was subcultured into 50 mL LB medium broth with ampicillin (final conc. 50 µg/mL) at 37 °C overnight. Subsequently, 16 mL of the culture was subcultured into an 800 mL volume of LB medium broth with ampicillin (final conc. 50 µg/mL) at 37 °C until the cell density reached between OD600 value of 0.4-0.6. IPTG was added to a final concentration of 1 mM to induce protein expression, along with all-trans retinal (solubilized in methanol), added to a final concentration of 3 µg/mL. The culture was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 4 h, then centrifuged at 6,759 x g (R10A3, Hitachi CR-21) for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended with high salt lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 4 M NaCl, 500 μM PMSF, 14 μM beta-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.8). and sonicated (S-3000, MISONIC, on/off pulse cycle with 5 sec intervals, total pulse time 5 min, 65 W) to lyse the cells. The lysate was centrifuged at 38,886 x g (R20A2, Hitachi CR-21) for 10 min at 4 °C to precipitate the cell debris. The supernatant was collected and then ultra-centrifuged at 237,662 x g (P70AT, Eppendorf Himac Technologies) for 1 h at 4 °C to separate the membrane from the mixture. The membrane pellet was solubilized in high-salt lysis buffer containing 2% n-Dodecyl beta-D-maltoside (DDM) and nutated at 20 rpm for 18 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation of the solubilized membranes at 38,886 x g (R20A2, Hitachi CR-21) for 45 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was treated with Ni-nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) agarose, which had been equilibrated with high-salt lysis buffer with Lauryl Maltose Neopentyl Glycol (LMNG) and imidazole concentration adjusted to 0.02% and 20 mM, respectively, for 4 h at 4 °C with slow rotation.
Target proteins were then eluted from the column with high-salt lysis buffer containing 250 mM imidazole and 0.02% LMNG. Imidazole was then removed with an Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter (50 kDa MWCO; Merck) via buffer exchange against FPLC buffer (50 mM MES, 100 mM NaCl, 0.01% LMNG, pH 6.0). The protein samples were concentrated to a final volume of 300 μL prior to loading onto a Superdex® 200 Increase 10/300 GL (Cytiva) size-exclusion column pre-equilibrated with FPLC buffer. The desired protein fractions were eluted at a flow rate of 0.65 mL/min and subsequently concentrated using a 50 kDa Amicon centrifugal filter.

## UV-visible spectroscopy
The purified HtSRI-HtrI samples were concentrated and exchanged to a buffer containing 4 M NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, and 0.02% DDM using an Amicon apparatus (Merck Millipore, Germany). UV-visible spectra were recorded at 298 K using a U-1900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan).

## Photocycle measurement
Flash-laser induced photocycle measurement was proposed by Goldschmidt et al.59 and practically described previously10,55. Briefly, transient absorbance changes were recorded using a custom-built cross-beam flash spectrometer equipped with an Nd:YAG laser (532 nm, 6 ns pulse width, 40 mJ per pulse). Purified samples were adjusted to an absorbance of 0.5 at their respective ground-state absorption maxima. Flash-induced difference spectra were collected at the wavelength corresponding to each protein’s absorption maximum. The observed traces represent time-dependent changes in optical density following photoexcitation, reflecting the loss and subsequent recovery of ground-state absorbance.

## Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection
To remove excess detergent, the HtSRI-HtrI samples were incubated with Bio-Beads at a 1:1 ratio of sample volume to Bio-Beads weight (v:w) for 1 h on ice with gentle agitation. The cryogenic electron microscopy was provided through the Cryo-EM Core Facility of the First Core Laboratory, College of Medicine, NTU. Sample grids (Quantifoil r1.2/1.3 Cu 300 mesh) were glow-discharged for 90 s with 30 mA current before loading onto the Vitrobot Mark IV (ThermoFisher Scientific). 3 µl HtSRI-HtrI adjusted to 1.5 mg/ml was adsorbed onto the grid for 5 s, followed by 2.5 s blotting, with blot force of 0 at 100% humidity and 4 °C. Grids were immediately plunged into liquid ethan and vitrified. EM data were collected on Glacios™ 2 Cryo-TEM equipped with a Falcon 4 detector with a pixel size of 0.74 Å at 200 kV and 190,000x magnification. The total dose was 45 e− Å−2 at an exposure time of 2.85 s.

## Data processing
A total of 5959 collected EER movies are processed using a single-particle workflow in CryoSPARC v4.6.0 as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 3. Patch motion correction and CTF estimation were conducted using the default settings with EM parameters mentioned above and an EER upsampling factor of 1. Motion-corrected micrographs were curated with a CTF fit resolution range limit of 2.539–5.000 Å, resulting in 5085 micrographs after exclusion. Autopicking with blob picker at a diameter size range of 150–250 Å resulted in 980,786 particles. Selected 2D classes were used as initial particles to generate templates for template-based autopicking using the template picker, resulting in 548,270 particles successfully extracted with a box size of 450 px (333 Å). Particles were classified into 50 classes with 2 final full iterations to remove junk particles and contaminations, leaving 71,872 particles sorted into 3 classes in 3D classification using a C1 symmetry. A 3D class with 27,819 particles showing visible protein features was selected to create template projections for another round of template-based autopicking. 719,783 were successfully picked, extracted with a 450 px box size and underwent two rounds of 2D classification. 103,177 particles were selected for 3D classification, followed by heterogenous refinement without imposed symmetry, followed by homogenous refinement with imposed C2 symmetry. Subsequent non-uniform refinement was conducted with imposed C2 symmetry, marginalized symmetry relaxation and per-group CTF parameters optimization. Local refinement was carried out using a soft mask to omit density corresponding to the detergent micelle. An initial model generated by AlphaFold3 was used to perform model refinement against a locally refined map using phenix.real_space_refine with global minimization, local grid search and secondary structure restraints. Protein structures were visualized and analyzed in UCSF ChimeraX 1.10.160.
