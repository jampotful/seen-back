# Integrative genomic, phylogenetic, and transcriptomic analyses reveal temperature-responsive transient receptor potential (TRP) genes in Pacific abalone Haliotis discus hannai

## Abstract
AbstractTemperature profoundly influences the physiology, survival, and distribution of marine ectotherms, including mollusks. Transient receptor potential (TRP) channels are conserved thermosensory proteins in metazoans, yet their evolutionary diversification and functional roles in gastropod mollusks remain unclear. In this study, we present a comprehensive phylogenetic classification and expression analysis of TRP-like channel genes in Pacific abalone (Haliotis discus hannai). Through the extensive mining of genome and transcriptome datasets, we identified 49 TRP-like genes and categorized them into nine families from two major groups: Group 1 (TRPA, TRPC, TRPM, TRPN, TRPS, TRPV, and TRPVL) and Group 2 (TRPP and TRPML), along with two unclassified TRP-like genes. Phylogenetic analysis incorporating sequences from lophotrochozoans, choanoflagellates, fungi, and green algae outlined a lineage-specific TRP-like gene expansion in mollusks. Spatial expression profiling revealed distinct tissue-specific patterns: TRPC-, TRPM-, and TRPP-like genes were enriched in sensory organs (i.e., the eyes and tentacles), whereas TRPM- and TRPV-like genes were expressed predominantly in respiratory and metabolic tissues (i.e., the gills and hepatopancreas). Under acute thermal stress, RNA sequencing and real-time quantitative PCR identified several thermoresponsive TRP paralogs, including TRPA1- and TRPV-like genes, exhibiting distinct transcriptional regulation. These results elucidate the evolutionary complexity and functional diversification of TRP channels in marine gastropods, and highlight the potential role of these molecules in thermal sensing and adaptation. This study provides a molecular framework for understanding TRP-mediated environmental responses in mollusks, contributing to broader insights into marine invertebrate resilience under climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02505-z.Keywords: Thermal adaptation, Gastropod, Mollusk

## Background
Temperature is a critical determinant of organismal growth and survival. Recent estimates have revealed a rapid rise in the surface temperature of the oceans over the past decades, contributing to more unstable and unfavorable environments, characterized by increased rainfall intensity, rising sea levels, coral reef destruction, and decreasing ocean oxygen levels and glaciers in the polar regions [1]. Combined with ocean hypoxia and acidification, ocean warming is a major external factor affecting diverse biological processes of marine ectotherms [2–4]. Increased temperatures promote cell metabolism, enhancing oxygen uptake and energy consumption in animals, along with oxidative stress and, consequently, molecular damage [5].
In vertebrates, the ability to sense external temperatures is assumed by somatosensory neurons, converting external temperature-related information into neural activity at the periphery transmitted toward the central nervous system. Somatosensory neurons contain specialized membrane proteins, including thermosensitive transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, responsible for thermal transduction over a wide range of temperatures [6, 7]. TRP channels are cation-permeable transmembrane (TM) ion channels that can be activated by specific temperature ranges, involved in stress responses [8, 9]. Originally discovered in Drosophila melanogaster [10], these channels have since been broadly identified across various animal phyla as well as in green algae, fungi, and choanoflagellates [11]. Currently, nine animal TRP families have been described: TRPA (ankyrin), TRPC (canonical), TRPP (polycystin), TRPM (melastatin), TRPML (mucolipin), TRPN (no mechanoreceptor potential C, or nompC), TRPS (soromelastatin), TRPV (vanilloid), and TRPVL (vanilloid-like) [8, 11]. TRP channels are divided into two major groups that diverged before the split between plants and animals. Group 1 (TRPA, TRPC, TRPM, TRPN, TRPS, TRPV, and TRPVL) predates the Cnidaria-Bilateria divergence, whereas Group 2 (TRPP and TRPML) emerged early during Eukaryote evolution [11]. Furthermore, a fungus-specific TRP family, TRPY/TRPF, has been identified [12] and is considered a distinct lineage outside the canonical Group 1/2 divide [11]. As most TRP families are ancient, widely expressed, and multifunctional, they are likely involved not only in the nervous and sensory systems, but also in cell death and proliferation, chemokine production, and vascular contractility [13, 14].
Mollusks are pivotal animals in almost all ecosystems, characterized by remarkable diversity and global distribution. Occupying a wide range of aquatic and terrestrial habitats, they serve as valuable models for studying the impacts of climate change [15–17]. For example, heat stress increases abalone mortality and coincides with altered gluconeogenesis or adenosine nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis [18], potentially hampering marine animal fitness. Shell formation in bivalves and gastropods is potentially depends on temperature changes, along with other factors, such as food supply, salinity, and hypoxia [19]. As mollusks are crucial elements in the food webs and support human activities, anticipating their responses and adaptation to global warming requires the understanding of the molecular mechanisms that enable survival across various temperature ranges. Although gene expression changes under heat stress, particularly in stress-tolerance genes (e.g., heat shock proteins) extensively studied in mollusks [20, 21], the underlying molecular and physiological mechanisms of temperature sensation and response remain mostly elusive.
The molecular identification of TRP family members and their expression profiling upon temperature changes would provide valuable insights into the TRP-mediated sensory system in marine invertebrates. Recently, genomic resource- and transcriptome dataset-based systematic studies have revealed complete or partial TRP-like transcript sets in bivalve mollusks and echinoderms [22–26]. Among the more than 70,000 described species of recent Mollusca, the World Register of Marine Species reports a marine gastropod-to-bivalve ratio of 4:1, representing a higher value than that of any regional fauna [15]. Thermal tolerance and range size are shaped mainly by life-history traits, larval dispersal, and metabolic constraints, rather than taxonomy per se [27]. However, gastropods beyond the juvenile stage are often characterized by narrower ranges and higher thermal sensitivity than bivalves. Abalones are a widespread group of marine gastropods that inhabit tropical and temperate waters worldwide [28]. Although considerable attention has been invested in establishing the physiological response and protective metabolism of abalones in the environment, particularly in the face of changing temperatures [28], reports on TRP-mediated sensory systems in gastropods are lacking.
In this study, we performed extensive data mining of transcriptome datasets and available abalone genomes, leading to the identification of 49 TRP-like genes in the Pacific abalone Haliotis discus hannai (Hdh). The spatiotemporal expression profiles of these Hdh-TRP-like genes, as determined from available RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis, allowed for the identification of several thermoresponsive Hdh-TRP-like genes. Our study provides a basis for future studies related to the molecular mechanisms of TRP-mediated thermal response and tolerance in gastropod mollusks, offering new insights into marine invertebrate adaptation to global warming.

## Methods

## Identification of TRP-like channel and sequence analysis
Homology-based searches were conducted to identify TRP-like channel genes in the Hdh genome database [29] and in transcriptome data obtained through RNA-seq analyses [30, 31], as detailed in the following section. Query protein sequences were collected from representative invertebrates (e.g., Crassostrea gigas, Mizuhopecten yessoensis, Lottia gigantea, Capitella teleta, Octopus bimaculoides, Drosophila melanogaster, and Caenorhabditis elegans) as well as vertebrates (Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, and Danio rerio). Sequence data were retrieved from public databases, i.e., NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for C. gigas, M. yessoensis, O. bimaculoides, H. sapiens, M. musculus, and D. rerio, UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org) for D. melanogaster and C. elegans, and the Joint Genome Institute MycoCosm repository (https://mycocosm.jgi.doe.g.ov) for L. gigantea and C. teleta. Similarity searches were performed using BLASTP with an E-value threshold of 1E-10 against the reference Hdh (Assembly: ASM4470709v1, Accession: GCA_044707095.1) and H. rufescens (Assembly: xgHalRufe 1.0.p, Accession: GCA_023055435.1) [32] available in the NCBI Protein database. Supplementary Material 2 contains detailed information regarding TRP and TRP-like sequences. To determine the genomic profiles, the predicted cDNA sequences were aligned with whole-genome sequences using BLASTN. The open reading frames (ORFs) of TRP and TRP-like cDNAs were predicted using the NCBI ORF Finder tool, and amino acid sequences were subsequently deduced. The initially identified TRP-like sequences were further validated using BLASTP searches against the NCBI non-redundant protein database. Proteins with strong sequence homology were analyzed using InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro) [33] and the Conserved Domain Search of NCBI to validate the presence of hallmark conserved domains characteristic of TRP channels. TM domains numbers and topologies of in TRP-like channels were predicted using DeepTMHMM (https://dtu.biolib.com/DeepTMHMM/) [34]. In addition, the domain organization of ankyrin repeats, typically present in certain TRP families, was examined using the Simple Modular Architecture Research Tool (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [35]. Protein sequences corresponding to Group 1 TRP channels (i.e., TRPA1, TRPC1, TRPC3-7, TRPM1-6, TRPV1-6 from H. sapiens; TrpA1, pain, pyx, wtrw, TRPC-like, TRPC-γ, TRPC, Trpm, Nan, Iav from D. melanogaster; trpa-1, trpa-2, trp1-3, gon-2, gtl-1,-2, ced-11, osm-9, ocr1-4 from C. elegans; TRPC1-4, TRPM1-4, TRPV1-2 from L. gigantea) and 39 Hdh-TRP channels were added to the pool of sequences, then aligned in MAFFT using the E-INS-i iterative refinement method and the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix on the NGPhylogeny server v7.407 [30]. The region corresponding to the sequences from last portion of the pre-TM1 to the last residue of the TRP helix was identified and selected. Finally, a second round of sequence alignment in MAFFT in Jalview was performed [36].

## Phylogenetic analysis of TRP-like channels
To classify the identified Hdh-TRP-like channels and their orthologs from other representative species, amino acid sequences corresponding to the predicted TM regions, along with 20 adjacent residues on both sides, were extracted using DeepTMHMM [34]. An initial phylogenetic analysis was conducted using maximum-likelihood trees based on these regions. Following this preliminary classification, sequences were assigned to the TRP channel families TRPA, TRPC/TRPN, TRPM/TRPS, TRPV/TRPVL, and TRPP/TRPML for subsequent phylogenetic analyses using full-length amino acid sequences. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MAFFT using the E-INS-i iterative refinement method and the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix on the NGPhylogeny server v7.407 [37]. The resulting alignments were examined using Jalview [36]. Incomplete sequences or poorly aligned regions were manually inspected and removed to ensure alignment quality. Maximum-likelihood trees were constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.12 [38] with the best-fitting amino acid substitution model (LG + G + I) selected automatically. Node support values were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess tree reliability. Potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily member 4 (KCNA4 or Shaker) sequences from M. musculus, D. melanogaster, L. gigantea, and C. teleta were included as outgroup sequences to construct phylogenetic trees, subsequently visualized and edited in FigTree (v1.4.4) (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

## Temperature setting and tissue sampling
In October 2024, adult Pacific abalone (n = 90; body weight 37.70 ± 0.14 g; mixed sex) were obtained from a local market in Gangneung, Gangwon-do, Republic of Korea. The abalone were acclimated in two flow-through seawater aquaria (400-L capacity) maintained at 18 ± 1.0 ℃ for two weeks prior to the experiment. During the acclimation period, the abalone were fed Saccharina japonica (kelp) ad libitum. On the day of sampling, the eyes, tentacles, gills, and hepatopancreas (n = 9; control group), were dissected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 ℃ until RNA extraction. Following control group sampling, the water temperature of one aquarium was gradually increased to 27 ℃ at a rate of 5 ℃/h, while the temperature of another aquarium was reduced to 9 ℃ at the same rate. At 6 and 24 h after exposure to these temperatures, tissue samples of the above-listed organs were collected (n = 9 per group). All samples were stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

## RNA-seq analysis
Two pooled samples were prepared, each consisting of tissue combined from three individuals of the control group in the tissue sampling section. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA quality was assessed using the 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were prepared using the CORALL RNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). mRNA was isolated using a commercial Poly(A) RNA Selection Kit (Lexogen). The isolated mRNA samples were then used for cDNA synthesis and fragmentation following the manufacturer’s instructions. Indexing was performed using Illumina indices 1–12 and the libraries were enriched via PCR amplification. Subsequently, the quality and fragment size distribution of the libraries were assessed using a TapeStation System or an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. The prepared libraries were quantified with a kit using the StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). High-throughput sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq XP platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with paired-end 150-bp reads. Raw sequencing data quality was evaluated using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were trimmed using Fastp [39], then clean reads were aligned to the red abalone (H. rufescens) reference genome (xgHalRufe 1.0.p; NCBI Accession: GCA_023055435.1) using STAR aligner [40]. Transcript quantification was performed using a tool Salmon program [41]. Read count normalization was carried out using the TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) + CPM (Counts Per Million) method, implemented in the count normalization tool for RNA-seq analysis (conorm, v1.2.0) of the Python package (https://gitlab.com/georgy.m/conorm). Data mining and graphic visualization were performed using the ExDEGA software (Ebiogen, Seoul, Korea). By default, ExDEGA performs gene selection using raw p-values. In this analysis, raw p-values were computed using t-tests implemented in Python’s SciPy library. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the temperature-challenged (27 °C–9 °C) and control (18 °C) groups were identified using fold changes (FCs) of ≥ 2 and P-values of ≤ 0.05. Gene expression data were normalized using the TMM method and converted to CPM. Z-score-transformed or log₂ FC values derived from the expression data were visualized as hierarchical clustering (HCL) heatmaps using MultiExperiment Viewer (MeV) v4.8.1. RNA-seq datasets were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accession number GSE305557).

## RT-qPCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each sample, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed to first-strand cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specific primers for abalone genes were designed using an RT-qPCR Assay Entry program (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA). Table S1 summarizes the primer sequences used in this study. SYBR-based RT-qPCR was performed using a QuantStudio 1 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under the following reaction conditions: 95 ℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 34 s. To assess the stability of the candidate reference genes under different temperature conditions, geNorm analysis was performed [42]. Relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method [43]. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis to ensure the single-product amplification of each primer pair (Fig. S1; Table S1). Hdh-EF1α was selected as the reference gene due to its strong expression stability across samples (Fig. S2).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, performed using SPSS software (version 28.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

## Results

## Overall relationships of TRP channels
We generated a complete set of Hdh-TRP-like channels by searching for entries that shared homology with known TRP channel sequences. To overcome the limitations of the vertebrate-biased taxonomy of the TRP superfamily, we ensured that the taxa in this phylogeny included not only lophotrochozoans (comprising abalones) but also choanoflagellates (unicellular flagellates closely related to animals), fungi, and the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. As a result, we identified 49 Hdh-TRP-like channels and further categorized them into two groups, i.e., Group 1 and 2 including seven (TRPA, TRPC, TRPM, TRPN, TRPS, TRPV, and TRPVL) and two (TRPML and TRPP) families, respectively (Fig. 1; Table S2). Based on sequence similarities, we divided the TRPP channels into two subfamilies: TRPP1 (Pkd1) and TRPP2 (Pkd2). However, we could not include two TRP-like channels in any families with significant phylogenetic support and thus designated them as unclassified TRP-like families. Table S2 contains the complete list of the Hdh-TRP-like sequences used in this study. Sequence analysis revealed the presence of six TM domains in all Hdh-TRP-likes, except for the Hdh-TRPP1-like subfamily, which contained 11 TM domains (Table S2). Group 1 TRPs except for TRPM, possessed multiple N-terminal ankyrin repeat domains; whereas Group 2 TRPs, including the TRPP and TRPML families, exhibited no ankyrin repeats. Our BLASTP analysis of the protein sequence alignments indicated that all Hdh-TRP-like channels displayed higher sequence identity with the molluscan orthologs than with other phyla, with the only exception of TRPM member-like 2 in the marine invertebrate chordate Branchiostoma lanceolatum (Table S3). TRP family member diversity and numbers were relatively more abundant in mollusks than in other phyla, including invertebrate model organisms and chordates (Fig. 2).

## Comparative phylogeny of Hdh-TRP channels across phyla

## TRPA family
We identified eight Hdh-TRPA1-like proteins in the first (vertebrate TRPA1 members) and second (invertebrate deuterostomes and Lophotrochozoa/Porifera/Cnidaria TRPA1 members) TRPA1 clades, respectively (Fig. S3). The third TRPA clade included the main arthropod TRPA members of the AS-TRPA subfamily. In this TRPA family, a high copy number of ankyrin repeats was present exclusively in the N-terminal intracellular domain (Table S2).

## TRPC and TRPN families
We subdivided Hdh-TRPC proteins into two major subfamilies: αTRPC and βTRPC. The αTRPC clade included Hdh-TRPC-like_7, human TRPC1/4/5, and TRPC channels from C. elegans and D. melanogaster TRPC (Fig. 1). The βTRPC clade consisted of various lophotrochozoan βTRPC subfamily members including six Hdh-TRPC-like and chordate βTRPC channels such as human TRPC3/6/7 types (Figs. 1 and S4). Various protostomian TRPCs have been phylogenetically characterized in these two subfamilies. The TRPN family, typically containing 28–29 ankyrin repeats and a TRP domain, was positioned close to the αTRPC and βTRPC clades (Figs. 1 and S4). A single Hdh-TRPN channel containing 29 ankyrin repeats and six TM domains was classified in the TRPN subfamily (Table S2).

## TRPM and TRPS families
We classified ten Hdh-TRPM channels within the canonical TRPM family (Fig. 1). Specifically, Hdh-TRPM-like_16 and 18 clustered within the αTRPM subfamily, including human TRPM1, TRPM3, TRPM6, and TRPM7 (Fig. S5). In contrast, we assigned Hdh-TRPM-like_15 and 17 to the βTRPM subfamily, comprising human TRPM2, TRPM4, TRPM5, and TRPM8. The remaining six Hdh-TRPM-like proteins formed a distinct TRPM-like clade that branched as a sister group to both the αTRPM and βTRPM subfamilies. Interestingly, several highly divergent TRPM-like proteins have been reported, including C. elegans cell death abnormal 11 (ced-11). This group, referred to as TRPM-related proteins (TRPS) or ced-11-like proteins, represents a sister lineage of the TRPM family [44]. We identified Hdh-TRPS-like_9 as an ortholog of C. elegans ced-11 (Figs. 1 and S5). Both the TRPM and TRPS channels are characterized by the presence of an N-terminal SMF/DprA-LOG (SLOG) domain and a C-terminal ADP-ribose phosphohydrolase (Nudix) domain [44]. We also identified the SLOG domain in the N-terminal regions of all Hdh-TRPM and Hdh-TRPS proteins, whereas the Nudix domain only in Hdh-TRPM-like_15 and Hdh-TRPS-like_9 (Fig. S6).

## TRPV and TRPVL families
We divided the TRPV-related genes into four or five subfamilies, depending on the placement of unclassified channels Hdh-TRP_21 and 22 within the TRPV-related group (Figs. 1 and S7). The Hdh-TRPV family comprised one TRPVA-like, one TRPVB-like, and six TRPVC/D-like channels. The TRPVL family, a sister clade of TRPV, included only Hdh-TRPVL-like_30. Unclassified Hdh-TRP_21 and 22 clustered near the TRPV and TRPVL families in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. S7).

## TRPP and TRPML families
We divided the TRPP family into two subfamilies: TRPP1 (also known as PKD1, PC1, or polycystin-1) and TRPP2 (PKD2, PC2, or polycystin-2) (Fig. S8; Table S2). We identified a total of eight Hdh-TRPP1-like channels, each harboring multiple PKD repeats, REJ modules, eleven TM segments, a β-barrel PLAT domain, and/or a TOP domain (Fig. 3). Because of their large size (1,778–4,715 amino acids) and low sequence similarity to other TRP families, we excluded Hdh-TRPP1-like channels from the phylogenetic analysis of the TRP superfamily. We characterized a single Hdh-TRPP2-like_20 channel, comprising 855 amino acids and containing six TM domains with cytoplasmic N- and C-termini, consistent with the typical TRP channel architecture. Overall phylogenetic analysis of the TRP superfamily supported classification of this channel within the TRPP2 subfamily (Fig. 1).

## Spatial expression patterns of Hdh-TRP-like genes
We performed RNA-seq analysis to examine tissue-specific Hdh-TRP-like transcript expression in adult Pacific abalone. Hdh-TRPM-like_16 and Hdh-TRPP1-like_49 were highly expressed in sensory (the eyes and tentacles), whereas Hdh-TRPV-like_23 and 24 as well as Hdh-TRPM-like_14 in respiratory/metabolic (the gills and hepatopancreas) tissues (Fig. 4). The expression of Hdh-TRPC-like_3, 5, 7, and 8, along with that of unclassified TRP_21, was notably higher in the eyes. Various TRP family members, such as Hdh-TRPM-like_13, Hdh-TRPA1-like_32, and Hdh-TRPN-like_41, were predominantly expressed in the tentacles. Hdh-TRPA1-like_34, Hdh-TRPM-like_11, Hdh-TRPV-like_27, Hdh-TRPP1-like_39, and 48 were highly expressed in the gills, whereas Hdh-TRPV-like_29 and Hdh-TRPML-like_46 were specifically and strongly expressed in the hepatopancreas.

## Hdh-TRP-like gene expression under temperature stress
To investigate the transcriptional response of Pacific abalone to acute temperature stress, we analyzed RNA-seq datasets from the eyes, tentacles, gills, and hepatopancreas 6 h and 24 h post-exposure to heat (27 °C) and cold (9 °C) conditions (Fig. S9). The heat shock protein ortholog genes (Hdh-HSP-like) of Pacific abalone were prominently up- and downregulated under heat and cold stress, respectively (Fig. S10), reflecting typical thermal stress responses. We identified a total of 10,686 DEGs (P < 0.05, |log₂FoldChange(FC)| > 1), including 5,966 up- and 4,720 downregulated transcripts (Figs. S11A-D). Among the 49 identified Hdh-TRP-like genes, seven and five were significantly up- and downregulated in the eyes under heat stress, respectively (P < 0.05, log₂FC > 1; Figs. S11A and B). Under cold stress, seven and three Hdh-TRP-like genes were significantly up- and downregulated in the eyes, respectively (Figs. S11C and D). Notably, Hdh-TRPV-like_29 and Hdh-TRPA1-like_31 were significantly upregulated under both heat and cold stress, while Hdh-TRPC-like_7 was downregulated (P < 0.05, log₂FC > 1). In the tentacles, Hdh-TRPA1-like_38 was significantly upregulated at 6 h post-heat stress, mirroring the response in the eyes; whereas Hdh-TRPM-like_10 and Hdh-TRPA1-like_36 were significantly downregulated (P < 0.05, log₂FC > 1; Fig. S11A). In the gills, 6 h post-temperature change, Hdh-TRPM-like_10, Hdh-TRPA1-like_34, and 36 were significantly downregulated under heat stress, whereas Hdh-TRPV-like_27 was significantly downregulated under cold stress (Figs. S11A and C).
To validate the RNA-seq results, we performed RT-qPCR analysis on selected Hdh-TRP-like genes. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, Hdh-TRPA1-like_38, Hdh-TRPC-like_2, and Hdh-TRPV-like_29 were significantly upregulated in the eyes and tentacles under acute heat stress (P < 0.05; Fig. 5). In contrast, Hdh-TRPA1-like_31, 34, and 36, along with Hdh-TRPS-like_9 were significantly (P < 0.05) upregulated in the eyes under cold stress.

## Discussion
TRP proteins are conserved cation channels that mediate responses to environmental stimuli, including temperature, osmolarity, and mechanical stress. In this study, we identified a complete set of TRP-like channel genes in Pacific abalone and performed a comprehensive phylogenetic analysis to elucidate their evolutionary relationships. Moreover, we examined the tissue-specific expression of these genes and their response to acute thermal stress. Our results provide new insights into the molecular evolution and functional diversification of TRP channels in marine mollusks, with implications for resilience and adaptation to warming oceans.
Genome-wide screening and transcriptome analysis identified 49 TRP-like channels in Pacific abalone, categorized into two major groups encompassing nine families. Group 1 included canonical metazoan TRP families, including TRPA, TRPC, TRPM, TRPN, TRPS, TRPV, and TRPVL, whereas Group 2 comprised the more divergent TRPML and TRPP families. This grouping mirrors previously proposed classifications based on phylogenetic and structural criteria [11, 45]. Within Group 2, we further subdivided the TRPP channels into TRPP1 (Pkd1) and TRPP2 (Pkd2) subfamilies, consistent with known vertebrate and invertebrate gene lineages [11, 46]. The identification of two unclassified Hdh-TRP-like channels, lacking robust phylogenetic support within existing families, suggests potential novel TRP subtypes or mollusk-specific expansions, as in other bivalve species [23, 24]. This study provides the first comprehensive phylogenetic characterization of the TRP channels in gastropod mollusks, using a broad taxonomic framework to mitigate the vertebrate-centric bias typical of previous TRP superfamily analyses [8, 47]. By including sequences from lophotrochozoans, choanoflagellates, fungi, and green algae, we have improved the resolution of TRP channel evolutionary history and diversification across metazoans and their unicellular relatives [26, 48]. Protein domain analysis confirmed that nearly all Hdh-TRPs shared the six TM domains common to TRP channels [49]. Only TRPP1 members contained 11 TM domains, reflecting their known structural divergence [46]. We observed multiple N-terminal ankyrin repeat domains in Group 1 members (excluding TRPM) that were absent in Group 2 TRPs, aligning with domain distribution patterns reported across protostomes and deuterostomes [26, 50]. Consistent with our phylogenetic analyses, among the Group 1 TRP families, TRPA channels represented a highly diversified and expanded family across both bilaterian and non-bilaterian phyla [26, 51–54]. BLASTP comparison revealed strong sequence identity between most Hdh-TRP-like channels and mollusk orthologs, indicating a conserved TRP-like repertoire within Lophotrochozoa. TRP-like diversity and expansion are more pronounced in mollusks than in other invertebrate model organisms and chordates, reflecting previous observations in cephalopods and bivalves [23–25, 55]. Interestingly, two oyster species (C. gigas and C. angulate) possess more TRP-like genes than other organisms [23], potentially reflecting the evolutionary adaptation of sensory and regulatory systems in marine mollusks to ensure a more robust response to temperature and other environmental factor-related fluctuations.
Our phylogenetic analysis revealed that Hdh-TRPA1-like genes were distributed across two of the three major TRPA clades. Two Hdh-TRPA1-like and molluscan TRPA1-like genes clustered within the first clade, including vertebrate TRPA family members (e.g., TRPA1) known for their roles in thermo- and chemosensation [56, 57]. The remaining six Hdh-TRPA1-like genes were placed in a second TRPA clade composed of invertebrate deuterostomes and early diverging metazoan lineages, including lophotrochozoans, cnidarians, and sponges. This phylogenetic placement implies a lineage-specific expansion of the TRPA family within mollusks, consistent with similar discoveries related to the bivalve species C. gigas and Chlamys farreri [23, 24]. A prominent feature of the identified Hdh-TRPA-like proteins was the high copy number of ankyrin repeats located within the N-terminal intracellular domain. Ankyrin repeat domains are conserved structural motifs that mediate protein-protein interactions, regulate ion channel function, and facilitate mechanical signal transduction [58, 59]. Their enrichment in molluscan TRPA1 channels, which often contain multiple copies likely arising from duplication events during evolution [29, 60], may represent an adaptive response to the complex environmental conditions of benthic and intertidal habitats, where highly specialized sensory systems are critical for the survival of marine mollusks.
Analysis of the TRPC gene family in Pacific abalone revealed two main groups: αTRPC and βTRPC. This division is consistent with the patterns observed in other animals, where two major TRPC lineages are widely conserved [8, 26]. In Pacific abalone, the αTRPC group comprises a single gene, Hdh-TRPC-like_7, which is closely related to human TRPC1/4/5 and to orthologs in the nematode C. elegans and the fruit fly D. melanogaster. This phylogenetic relationship suggests that αTRPC channels fulfill fundamental roles conserved throughout evolution. In contrast, the βTRPC group shows a clear expansion in abalone, with six genes forming a unique lophotrochozoan-specific subgroup. These are separate from the βTRPC group in vertebrates, such as human TRPC3/6/7, suggesting that their duplications happened independently in Lophotrochozoa. The presence of both αTRPC and βTRPC genes in protostomes supports the idea that these two groups already existed in early animal ancestors [61]. We also identified a TRPN gene, Hdh-TRPN-like_41, which is absent from mammals, but plays a critical role in mechanosensation in many invertebrates and lower vertebrates [11, 62]. Hdh-TRPN-like_41 displays the characteristic architecture of TRPN channels, such as 29 ankyrin repeats and six TM domains, essential for touch- and balance-related functions [11, 62]. The presence of a TRPN ortholog in Pacific abalone is consistent with the evolutionary history of the TRPN family, which originated early in metazoans and was independently lost in several lineages, including mammals [63]. These findings shed light on the evolutionary trajectories of the TRPC and TRPN families across protostomes and confirm the conserved mechanosensory function of TRPN channels in non-mammalian metazoans.
Identification and classification of TRPM and TRPS channels in Pacific abalone revealed both conserved and lineage-specific features. This is consistent with a previous finding identifying TRPM members as recurrent targets of genomic hotspots in various metazoan species [64]. The division of TRPM members into αTRPM-like and βTRPM-like subfamilies aligns with established classification of TRPM channels in mammals: αTRPM (TRPM1/3/6/7) and βTRPM (TRPM2/4/5/8), each exhibiting distinct functional specializations in sensory signaling, ion transport, and cellular homeostasis [8, 44]. The presence of both TRPM subfamilies in Pacific abalone suggests early divergence and functional conservation of these channel types across bilaterians. In addition, the discovery of a distinct TRPM-like clade branching as a sister group to both αTRPM and βTRPM subfamilies is in line with previous findings in invertebrates, where species-specific expansions and TRPM variants have been observed [44]. The TRPS family, defined as a sister lineage to TRPM, is relatively understudied, but has been recognized in several invertebrate species [44]. Its presence in mollusks, such as Hdh-TRPS-like_9 as an ortholog of C. elegans ced-11, supports the hypothesis that TRPM and TRPS divergence occurred early in metazoan evolution. Our domain analysis identified the presence of a conserved SLOG domain, thought to mediate protein–ligand interactions [8], across all TRPM and TRPS family members in Pacific abalone. Instead, the Nudix domain, commonly associated with ADP-ribose metabolism, was identified solely in Hdh-TRPM-like_15 and Hdh-TRPS-like_9. This selective retention suggests functional specialization in nucleotide signaling or metabolic regulation within the TRPM and TRPS channels, respectively [44, 65].
TRPV-related genes in Pacific abalone are divided into four or five distinct subfamilies, depending on the phylogenetic placement of two unclassified TRPs, Hdh-TRP_21 and Hdh-TRP_22. These two channels clustered near both the TRPV and TRPVL families, suggesting a potential intermediate or lineage-specific diversification event within the TRPV-related clade. Similar ambiguities in TRP channel classification have been described in other invertebrate systems, where taxon-specific expansions and divergent sequences complicate canonical subfamily boundaries [11, 26]. Within the core TRPV family, Hdh-TRPV-related genes were classified into TRPVA-like, TRPVB-like, and TRPVC/D-like subfamilies according to phylogenetic relationships and gene retention patterns of the vertebrate TRPV family [66]. The application of this subdivision to molluscan TRPV sequences underscores the evolutionary conservation of core TRPV subfamily structures beyond vertebrates. The TRPVL family, considered a sister group to canonical TRPV channels and typically underrepresented in vertebrates, is represented by a single gene, Hdh-TRPVL-like_30. TRPVL family has been suggested to represent an ancient lineage retained in certain invertebrate taxa, including mollusks and annelids [11]. The proximity of Hdh-TRP_21 and _22 to both TRPV and TRPVL clades raises the possibility of novel or previously unrecognized TRPV-like subfamilies in Pacific abalone, supporting the hypothesis of lineage-specific diversification events in molluscan TRP channel evolution.
Our analysis identified eight Hdh-TRPP1-like channels, relatively large receptor-like proteins bearing multiple PKD repeats, REJ modules, 11 TM segments, a PLAT β-barrel domain, and a TOP domain, which are consistent with the structural hallmarks of the TRPP1 subfamily observed across metazoans [67, 68]. Due to their significant size and low sequence similarity to other TRP superfamily members, these Hdh-TRPP1-like paralogs were excluded from overall phylogenetic reconstructions. In contrast, we discovered a single Hdh-TRPP2-like_20 channel with six TM domains, a coiled-coil domain, an EF-hand domain, and cytoplasmic N- and C-termini, in agreement with the canonical architecture of TRPP2 subfamily members [69, 70]. The conserved Hdh-TRPP2-like_20 channel may play a core cation-channel function essential for cell signaling, akin to that of TRPP2 in Ca2+ permeability and channel complex formation in vertebrates [69, 70]. Our results underscore the structural and likely functional divergence within the TRPP family in mollusks, including extensive TRPP1-like gene expansion to assume complex extracellular features as opposed to a conserved TRPP2-like channel. This pattern is indicative of a conserved evolutionary split between the TRPP1 and TRPP2 subfamilies in metazoan lineages.
The RNA-seq profiling of 49 Hdh-TRP-like genes across four tissue types of Pacific abalone revealed distinct tissue-specific expression patterns, suggesting diverse sensory and physiological roles. Notably, Hdh-TRPM-like_16 and Hdh-TRPP1-like_49 were highly expressed in sensory tissues (i.e., the eyes and tentacles), indicating potential key roles in sensory and neural regulation. Hdh-TRPM-like_16, an ortholog of TRPM3 in the Zhikong scallop, supports a conserved TRPM channel function in environmental and thermal sensing among mollusks [24]. Several TRPC-like genes (i.e., Hdh-TRPC-like_3/5/7/8) and Hdh-TRP_21 were highly expressed in the eyes, consistent with their proposed role in photoreception, as described in mollusks and vertebrates [24, 71]. Tentacle-enriched expression of Hdh-TRPM-like_13, Hdh-TRPA1-like_32, and Hdh-TRPN-like_41 suggests chemo- and mechanosensation-related functions, the latter aligning with the function of NOMPC (TRPN) in arthropods [72]. In the abalone gills, broad expression of TRPA1-, TRPM-, TRPV-, and TRPP-like genes suggests potential roles in environmental sensing and respiratory regulation, paralleling the functions of various TRP family members in mammalian respiratory systems [73]. The gill-enriched expression of Hdh-TRPV-like_27 in Pacific abalone is consistent with the increased levels of its ortholog TRPV4 in the gills of Pacific oyster [74], supporting a conserved role in sensing the environment across mollusks. Given that mammalian TRPV channels participate in hepatic Ca²⁺ signaling and inflammatory processes [75, 76], the pronounced and tissue-specific expression of Hdh-TRPV-like_29 in the hepatopancreas may reflect a conserved functional role in abalone. Hdh-TRPML-like_46 was also strongly expressed in the hepatopancreas, suggesting a potential role in lysosomal function and cellular homeostasis, consistent with the TRPML1-mediated regulation of lysosomal calcium and iron release, autophagy, and membrane trafficking processes essential for digestive and detoxifying organs [77, 78]. Taken together, these results imply the evolutionary conservation and neofunctionalization of TRP channels in molluscan sensory and metabolic tissues.
This study may advance our understanding of thermal stress responses in mollusks by elucidating the expression dynamics of TRP-like genes in key sensory and metabolic tissues. The differential expression of 49 Hdh-TRP-like genes in Pacific abalone under both heat (27 °C) and cold (9 °C) stress highlights the complex and versatile roles of these ion channels in mediating mollusk adaptation to thermal challenges. Our findings are consistent with observations in other mollusks, such as the Zhikong scallop and Pacific oyster [23, 24]. In particular, the significant upregulation of TRPA1-like, TRPC-like, and TRPV-like genes in sensory, respiratory, and metabolic tissues under acute high-temperature stress across all examined mollusk species underscores the pivotal role of these channels in coordinating sensory perception and physiological adaptation to thermal extremes. Insects use TRPA1 and TRPC channels to detect noxious heat stimuli and initiate behavioral responses such as movement to cooler microhabitats and cooling avoidance [79, 80]. Conversely, Drosophila TRPV family members Nan and Iav have specific mechanosensory roles and have not been shown to respond to heat or contribute to thermal sensing [81, 82]. Interestingly, the expression of Hdh-TRPV-like_29 was elevated under both heat and cold stress, suggesting its possible multifunctional role as a generalist thermosensor. This bidirectional response may indicate a broader thermal detection range or involvement in homeostasis during temperature fluctuations. In contrast, Hdh-TRPA1-like_31, 34, and 36 genes were significantly upregulated under cold stress, suggesting a specialized role in cold perception. This pattern aligns well with previous findings on the modality-specific roles of TRPA1 across different species, ranging from heat sensitivity in insects and ectothermic vertebrates, to cold sensitivity in rodents, and a lack of thermal sensitivity in primates [83]. The observed expression patterns suggest evolutionary plasticity within the TRP gene family, allowing different subtypes to adapt to specific thermal niches and physiological conditions. This molecular flexibility may be particularly advantageous for mollusks inhabiting highly variable intertidal or subtidal zones to cope with wide fluctuations in temperature and salinity. Further investigation of the functional properties of these TRP channels could help elucidate the precise mechanisms of thermal sensing and adaptation in marine mollusks.

## Conclusion
In this study, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the TRP gene family in Pacific abalone, identifying 49 Hdh-TRP genes belonging to two major phylogenetic groups and nine families. By expanding the taxonomic comparison to include lophotrochozoans, choanoflagellates, fungi, and green algae, we traced the lineage-specific gene expansion and diversification events that have driven the evolution of TRP channels in mollusks. Notably, the abundance and diversity of TRP channels in Pacific abalone, particularly within Group 1 families enriched with ankyrin repeat domains, highlight adaptive molecular innovations in complex benthic marine environments. Structural variations (e.g., expanded TM regions in TRPP1 subfamily members) further suggest functional diversification across TRP lineages. Expression profiling under acute thermal stress revealed tissue-specific and subtype-specific TRP responses, indicating a coordinated multi-organ sensory network. The distinct expression patterns under heat and cold stress likely reflect the unique thermal tolerance thresholds of abalone. Taken together, these findings underscore the evolutionary plasticity of TRP channels in marine mollusks, which balance conserved sensory frameworks with lineage-specific adaptations. Future studies employing functional assays, such as gene knockdown (RNAi/CRISPR) and electrophysiological analyses, will be pivotal for elucidating the specific roles of these TRP paralogs in thermosensation, chemosensation, and osmoregulation. Moreover, investigating TRP regulatory mechanisms across developmental stages and environmental stress conditions will deepen our understanding of the evolution and ecological significance of sensory systems in Pacific abalone.
