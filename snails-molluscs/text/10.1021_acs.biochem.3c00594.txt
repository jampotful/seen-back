# Glu1022.53-Mediated Early Conformational Changes in the Process of Light-Induced Green Cone Pigment Activation

## Abstract
Ligand-triggered activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) relies on the phenomenon of loose allosteric coupling, which involves conformational alterations spanning from the extracellular ligand-binding domain to the cytoplasmic region, where interactions with G proteins occur. During the GPCR activation process, several intermediate and equilibrium states orchestrate the movement of the flexible and rigid transmembrane (TM) segments of the GPCR. Monitoring early conformational changes is important in unraveling the structural intricacies of the loose allosteric coupling. Here, we focus on the lumi intermediate formed by thermal relaxation from the initial photointermediate, batho in primate green cone pigment (MG), a light-sensitive GPCR responsible for color vision. Our findings from light-induced Fourier transform infrared difference spectroscopy reveal its similarity with rhodopsin, which mediates twilight vision, specifically involving the flip motion of the β-ionone ring, the relaxation of the torsional structure of the retinal, and local perturbations in the α-helix upon lumi intermediate formation. Conversely, we observe a hydrogen bond modification specific to MG’s protonated carboxylic acid, identifying its origin as Glu1022.53 situated in TM2. The weakening of the hydrogen bond strength at Glu1022.53 during the transition from the batho to the lumi intermediates corresponds to a slight outward movement of TM2. Additionally, within the X-ray crystal structure of the rhodopsin lumi intermediate, we note the relocation of the Met862.53 side chain in TM2, expanding the volume of the retinal binding pocket. Consequently, the position of 2.53 emerges as the early step in the conformational shift toward light-induced activation. Moreover, given the prevalence of IR-insensitive hydrophobic amino acids at position 2.53 in many rhodopsin-like GPCRs, including rhodopsin, the hydrogen bond alteration in the C=O stretching band at Glu1022.53 of MG can be used as a probe for tracing conformational changes during the GPCR activation process.

## Introduction
Visual pigments belong to the family of light-sensitive G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and are broadly classified into rhodopsin, which is responsible for twilight vision, and cone pigments, which are sensitive to color vision. The common structures of visual pigments consist of seven transmembrane (TM) α-helices, which absorb photons by binding the chromophore, 11-cis-retinal, within the protein moiety via a protonated Schiff base (PSB) bound to a lysine residue.1−4 Atomic structures of both rhodopsin5−7 and its photointermediates,8−11 along with biochemical, spectroscopic, and computational studies,2,4 have provided critical insights into the molecular mechanisms such as photoabsorption, photoactivation, and signal transduction in rhodopsin. Thus, rhodopsin undergoes a series of conformational changes starting with cis-trans isomerization upon the photoabsorption of retinal, followed by batho, lumi, and meta-I, ultimately forming the active intermediate meta-II, which triggers the G protein signaling pathway.
Various spectroscopic measurements and X-ray crystallographic analyses have revealed that the batho intermediate, arising from retinal isomerization, does not undergo macroscopic structural changes. Instead, it experiences microscopic alterations in hydrogen bonding within the retinal binding pocket, resulting in a highly distorted all-trans retinal structure.8,12 The subtle structural shifts in rhodopsin during the initial photoreaction process were also captured in recently reported ultrafast time-resolved crystallography of rhodopsin conducted at room temperature.13 In contrast, the X-ray crystal structure9 and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopic measurements14,15 of the lumi intermediate showcase a relaxation of the distorted retinal structure. Simultaneously, they reveal conformational changes, primarily within the local TM2 and TM3 segments, associated with the transition from batho to lumi intermediates (Figure 1). Particularly noteworthy is the X-ray crystal structure, which visualizes the repositioning of the amino acid at position 2.5316 within TM2 (Met862.53). This event is the genesis of the local conformational shift between TM2 and TM3 (Figure 1).9 Consequently, the early structural transformations in TM2 and TM3, particularly within the retinal binding pocket observed in the lumi intermediate, in conjunction with the alteration in the side chain conformation of Met862.53, represent the initial stage of an activation pathway. This pathway amplifies in later stages.17,18
Comparison of X-ray crystal structures of the resting state (1U19)6 and batho (2G87)8 and lumi (2HPY)9 intermediates in BovRh. The photoisomerization of retinal from 11-cis to all-trans configurations results in a conformational change in the retinal binding pocket. Local structural perturbations are observed, especially the relocation of Met862.53 in TM2. These microscopic changes lead to macroscopic structural changes during the subsequent activation process.
For cone pigments, low-temperature and transient absorption spectroscopy measurements performed on chicken cone pigments have successfully pinpointed intermediates generated during the photoreaction process.19−22 This discovery confirms the existence of rhodopsin-like intermediates.19−22 Additionally, when analyzing the kinetics of the photoreaction in chicken cone pigments, it has been observed that the active intermediate meta-II exhibits a significantly shorter lifespan compared to rhodopsin.23−25 The short lifetime of meta-II intermediates is a common feature among cone pigments, and it partially accounts for the diminished sensitivity of cone photoreceptor cells.23−25 Recently, there have been reports on ultrafast time-resolved absorption spectroscopy of the green pigment in humans at room temperature. The findings reveal that, in contrast to the fast bleach reaction of the meta-II intermediate, the kinetics of meta-II intermediate formation are notably slower than those of rhodopsin.26 Nevertheless, our structural understanding of photoreactions within cone pigments remains limited, in contrast to rhodopsin. This limitation primarily stems from challenges in sample preparation, resulting in unresolved atomic structures, including their resting states.
We have successfully applied low-temperature FTIR spectroscopy to primate cone pigments, yielding valuable insights into structural changes within the resting state and batho intermediate.27−30 Our findings reveal that the chemical structure of the retinal is analogous to that of rhodopsin across all three cone pigments—blue (MB), green (MG), and red (MR). However, disparities emerge in the structural alterations of the protein component during the transition from the resting state to the batho intermediate, and these distinctions vary among the pigments.27−30 Furthermore, the hydrogen-bonding networks formed by hydrophilic amino acids and water molecules within the retinal binding pocket exhibit variations.30,31 These differences in hydrogen-bonding networks are interpreted as influential factors governing the electronic state of the retinal, thereby enabling photoabsorption at distinct wavelengths.
Recently, we conducted spectroscopic measurements of lumi intermediates of MB, revealing a fascinating phenomenon: the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) shifts into the ultraviolet (UV) region upon the deprotonation of the retinal Schiff base.32 Additionally, we observed that the lumi intermediate is photoreversible and capable of reverting to its resting state when exposed to UV light. Low-temperature FTIR spectroscopy of the lumi intermediate unveiled a protonation signal associated with a carboxylic amino acid, suggesting the transfer of the Schiff base proton to a nearby carboxylic acid (with the primary candidate being the counterion, Glu1133.28). It was also demonstrated that the deprotonation of the Schiff base accompanies a dynamic conformational shift in the α-helix in TM3. Conventionally, it has been believed that the deprotonation reaction of the Schiff base exclusively occurs during the formation of the meta-II intermediate within the photoreaction process of visual pigments.33,34 However, our results challenge this notion, indicating divergent photoreaction processes among the visual pigments. These disparities in photoreaction dynamics may emerge as pivotal factors contributing to distinct visual functions such as twilight and color visions. On the other hand, investigating potential differences in photoreaction dynamics based on structural changes among cone pigments remains unexplored.
In this study, we investigated the structural changes associated with the formation of the lumi intermediate of MG, employing low-temperature UV-visible and FTIR spectroscopic techniques.35 Our analysis unveiled photoabsorption changes reminiscent of those observed in rhodopsin, featuring a blue-shift of 50 nm, while no shift was observed in the UV region attributable to the Schiff base’s deprotonation reaction, a phenomenon observed in MB.32 Furthermore, FTIR spectroscopy unraveled the relaxation of retinal distortion, accompanied by a substantial conformational change in the α-helices—akin to the changes observed in rhodopsin and MB.32 Additionally, as seen in rhodopsin, we detected shifts in the hydrogen bonding involving protonated carboxylic amino acids. Nevertheless, this protonation signal’s source differed from that of rhodopsin, as inferred from site-directed mutagenesis experiments. These findings underscore that the early photoreaction process differs among MG, MB, and rhodopsin. Considering that the lumi intermediate that forms through the thermal relaxation from the batho intermediate (referred to as the BL intermediate in MB)36 serves as the key intermediate conveying structural changes linked to retinal photoisomerization, differences in the structural dynamics of the lumi intermediate among various visual pigments may be the determining factor leading to distinct visual functions.

## Materials and Methods

## Sample Expression and Purification of MG
The genes encoding primate green pigment (MG) (Uniprot ID: Q9XSX)37 were inserted into a pFastBac HT expression vector (Bac-to-Bac, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the nine amino acid epitope sequence as a Rho1D4 tag on the C-termini. This construct was expressed in Sf9 cells that were cultured in ESF921 (Expression Systems LLC) at 28 °C. After 48–72 h of incubation, the expressed cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000g for 10 min and then stored at −80 °C in 50% glycerol until further use. Cell pellets were lysed in a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2 (pH 7.5), and protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, Japan) following Dounce homogenization at least twice for cell disruption, then centrifuged for 25 min at 4 °C. The disrupted cells were homogenized with buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 M NaCl (pH 7.5), protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, Japan), and benzonase nuclease (Novagen) for increasing the solubilization efficiently to isolate nucleotides, followed by centrifugation at 75,000g for 25 min. This washing step was also conducted through centrifugation at least twice.
The purified membrane pellets were resuspended in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 30 μM 11-cis-retinal at the final concentration and then incubated overnight at 4 °C. Membranes containing regenerated MG were solubilized by 1% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (DDM, Anatrace), and stirred for 1.5 h at 4 °C. Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 158,420g for 30 min, and the clarified supernatant was loaded onto the 1D4 antibody bound to CNBr-activated agarose packed onto a gravity flow column, and then mixed for 4 h. Bound material was washed in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.02% DDM, and the purified MG sample was then eluted with a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, 0.02% DDM, and 0.2 mg/mL 1D4 peptide.

## Sample Preparation of BovRh
Bovine rod outer segment (ROS) membranes were isolated from the retinas in a buffer containing 10 mM MOPS (pH 7.3), 30 mM NaCl, 60 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM PMSF, 1 mg/mL aprotinin, 1 mg/mL leupeptin, and 1 mM DTT by a sucrose flotation method as described previously.38 A discontinuous sucrose gradient was used to purify the ROS. ROS were then washed five times with distilled water by centrifugation at 80,000g, followed by suspension in buffer containing 2 mM NaH2PO4 (pH 7.25), and 2 mM NaCl. The prepared sample was deposited on a BaF2 window and dried in a glass vessel that was evacuated by an aspirator to prepare a thin film. Before measurements, BovRh films were hydrated with 1 μL of H2O, D2O, or D218O.

## Sample Reconstitution for Spectroscopy
For the spectroscopy measurements, the purified sample was reconstituted into phosphatidylcholine (PC) liposomes using a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 0.75% CHAPS, and 1 mg/mL PC at a protein:lipid molar ratio of 1:30. Dialysis was performed in order to remove DDM, followed by centrifugation for 20 min at 222,000g at 4 °C. The lipid-reconstituted MG was finally suspended in a buffer containing 2 mM NaH2PO4 (pH 7.25) and 2 mM NaCl and then placed on a BaF2 window to prepare a dry layer thin film. MG films were hydrated with 1 μL of H2O, D2O, or D218O before measurements.

## Low-Temperature UV-Visible and FTIR Spectroscopies
The MG and BovRh hydrated films were placed in the cell of a cryostat (Optistat, Oxford Instruments) mounted in a UV-vis spectrometer (V-750, JASCO) and an FTIR spectrometer (Carry670, Agilent Technologies) and cooled to each temperature. Each difference spectrum was calculated from two spectra constructed from 128 interferrograms with 2 cm–1 resolution. To form the lumi intermediate, the sample was illuminated with >560 nm light (using an O-58 cutoff filter) for 2 min at 200 K. To revert the lumi intermediate back to the resting state, the sample was cooled to 77 K and illuminated first with 500 nm light with an interference filter for 2 min and then with >610 nm light (using a R63 cutoff filter) for 2 min. After this procedure, the sample was warmed and illuminated at 200 K to form the lumi intermediate repeatedly. An average of five to seven experiments were conducted for the spectra of the luminescence intermediate upon hydration of H2O, D2O, or D218O, respectively.

## Results

## MG Forms a Blue-Shifted Lumi Intermediate at 200 K, Like Rhodopsin
Figure 2a shows the lumi minus MG UV-visible absorption difference spectrum, acquired through 2 min light exposure at 200 K, using a >560 nm cutoff filter. This spectrum confirms the production of a spectral blue-shifted photoproduct, the lumi intermediate, a phenomenon akin to what has previously been reported for bovine rhodopsin (BovRh).39 A comparison of early photointermediates, batho and lumi, in human and bovine rhodopsins was conducted using FTIR and UV-visible difference spectroscopies.40 Despite minor variations in amino acid sequences, the structural transition to batho and lumi during the initial photoreaction process was found to be remarkably similar.40 Consequently, future comparisons with MG will be based on observations from BovRh. Furthermore, the λmax of the lumi intermediate was observed in the visible region, indicating protonation of the retinal Schiff base. This finding aligns with the results observed for chicken red pigment, which belongs to the long-wavelength-sensitive (L)-group,19,20 and MG.
(a) Light-induced UV-visible difference spectrum of MG at 200 K upon illumination at >560 nm. (b) Light-induced UV-visible difference spectrum of BovRh at 200 K with 500 nm illumination. Each gridline of the y-axis corresponds to 0.002 (a) and 0.01 (b) absorbance units. The spectral blue-shift after light illumination corresponds to the formation of the lumi intermediate for both MG and BovRh.
In contrast, the recently reported lumi intermediate of MB undergoes a deprotonation reaction of the Schiff base, implying that the photoreaction during the lumi intermediate’s formation differs between the short-wavelength-sensitive (S)-group and L-group. Subsequently, the lumi intermediate was exposed to 464 nm light via an interference filter for 2 min. Yet, no reversion from the lumi intermediate to the MG resting state was observed, as shown in Figure S1. This behavior mirrors that of BovRh, but not MB, which exhibits photochromic properties. Therefore, it is evident that MG, like BovRh, forms a spectral blue-shifted lumi intermediate at 200 K. This prompts an inquiry into potential discrepancies in the structural transition from batho to lumi intermediate between MG and BovRh. We subsequently performed structural analysis, utilizing low-temperature light-induced FTIR difference spectroscopy to investigate the structural alterations within the lumi intermediate of MG.

## Structural Perturbation of MG Retinal Binding Pocket upon the Formation of Lumi Intermediate
Figure 3 displays the FTIR difference spectra of lumi minus MG, measured at 200 K. As observed in Figure 2, the lumi intermediate of MG does not exhibit photochromic properties at 200 K. It thus cannot be spectrally integrated to obtain a high signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio and accurate spectra. In contrast, Furutani et al. have reported that the lumi intermediate of BovRh returns to its resting state through the batho intermediate upon light illumination at 77 K (Figure 3a).15

Consequently, we investigated whether the lumi intermediate photoreversibly reverts to the resting state at 77 K, for both MG and BovRh. The lumi intermediate formed in MG at 200 K through illumination (Figure 3, first spectrum) was subsequently cooled to 77 K. It was then illuminated first with 500 ± 10 nm light using an interference filter for 2 min, followed by exposure to >610 nm light for another 2 min. Subsequent to this procedure, the sample was warmed and illuminated at 200 K to recurrently form the lumi intermediate (Figure 3, second spectrum). The spectral feature of the second lumi minus MG difference spectrum corresponded with those of the first difference spectrum, notably featuring significant pair bands at 1648 (−)/1628 (+) and 1231 (−)/1186 (+) cm–1. It is worth noting that the spectral intensity of the second difference spectrum decreased to 65%. Intriguingly, subsequent cycles, spanning up to the fifth repetition, consistently yielded the same amplitude and identical spectral features. These findings indicate that the lumi intermediate in MG is also capable of reverting to the resting state upon illumination at 77 K, mirroring the behavior observed in BovRh. However, it is important to note that this reversion in MG is partial, in contrast to the complete reversion observed in the lumi intermediate of BovRh. The partial reversion of the lumi intermediate in MG suggests structural heterogeneity within MG.
Figure 4 compares the spectra between the lumie and batho intermediates of both MG and BovRh. In the lumi intermediate spectrum of MG, the 1558 (+)/1530 (−) cm–1 pair band is attributed to the C=C ethylenic stretch of the retinal. The upshift in the C=C stretch, associated with the formation of the lumi intermediate, corresponds to the spectral blue-shift observed in the UV-visible absorption spectrum. Within the 1100–800 cm–1 frequency range lies the hydrogen-out-of-plane (HOOP) vibration of retinal, a reflection of the torsional structure of retinal. In the batho intermediate spectrum of MG, several HOOP bands are noticeable on the positive side, whereas most of these bands either vanish or experience a reduced peak intensity in the lumi intermediate spectrum. These alterations are also observed in BovRh (Figure 4).

Black and red lines represent the measurements in H2O and D2O, respectively. The vibrational bands indicated by the tags represent the vibrational modes of the retinal. One distinction between MG and BovRh pertains to the 1012 (+) and 1024 (+) cm–1 bands corresponding to the bending vibrations of the methyl group at C13 and C9 of retinal. Specifically, the 1012 (+) cm–1 band originating from the methyl group at C13 (observed at 1016 (+) cm–1 in MG) exhibited an increased intensity in MG. This occurrence might be attributed to the overlap effect of the two vibrational bands, resulting from the heightened planarity of the retinal. Consequently, this finding implies that in the lumi intermediate of MG, the planarity of the polyene chain spanning from C9 to C13 positions of retinal is enhanced compared to that in BovRh.
The distorted structure of the retinal is influenced by the retinal binding pocket shaped by the surrounding protein environment. We have previously reported that the degree of conformational change in the protein moiety associated with batho intermediate formation is in the order MB > MG = rhodopsin > MR. This assessment was based on a comparative analysis of FTIR difference spectra of batho intermediates among the three (red, green, and blue) cone pigments and rhodopsin.27,30,35
This suggests that despite the amino acid identity in the retinal binding pocket being less than 50% between rhodopsin and cone pigment, MG and rhodopsin share similarities. Specifically, they both experience perturbations in the retinal binding pocket due to retinal isomerization.
Figure 5 centers on the amide-I region, representing a conformational change in the protein backbone, as depicted in the FTIR spectra of the batho and lumi intermediates in MG and BovRh, respectively. In BovRh, the formation of the lumi intermediate manifests a prominent amide-I band at 1656 (−)/1636 (+) cm–1. This shift is believed to correspond to changes in the movement of TM3, linked with a flipping motion involving the β-ionone ring of retinal, a phenomenon also observed in the crystal structure of the lumi intermediate in BovRh (Figure S2a).9 Notably, a prominent amide-I pair band at 1647 (−)/1627 (+) cm–1 is also evident in MG’s FTIR difference spectrum of the lumi intermediate (Figure 5). Together with the disappearance of the HOOP bands, it is plausible that, akin to BovRh, MG undergoes a conformational change within the α-helix as the distorted structure of retinal relaxes upon the formation of the lumi intermediate. It is worth noting that the amide-I band in the D2O spectrum in MG appears broader than in BovRh. In fact, the total area covered by the amide-I band in MG, when compared with BovRh, is calculated to be 1.6 times larger. This outcome may be attributed to a more extensive TM3 movement in the MG or accompanying adjustments in other helical structures.
FTIR difference spectra of lumi and batho intermediates of MG and BovRh measured at 200 K for lumi and 77 K for batho, respectively, in the amide-I region. Black and red lines represent the measurements in H2O and D2O, respectively. The tagged bands correspond to either the amide-I or C=NH stretching band of the Schiff base. The C=NH (D) stretching band associated with the retinal Schiff base appears in the frequency region, where the amide-I band is observed, a noteworthy observation. It is well-known that the shift values in the C=NH and C=ND stretching band frequencies, brought about by deuterium substitution, correlate with the hydrogen bond strength of the Schiff base.41,42 Previous research has reported that the transition from batho to lumi intermediates attenuates the Schiff base’s hydrogen bond strength. This is supported by the presence of the 1635 cm–1 band in the lumi intermediate spectrum of BovRh, which corresponds to the C=NH stretching band (1636 cm–1 band in Figure 5), and only a minor 4 cm–1 shift is observed upon deuterium substitution.14
In the lumi intermediate spectrum of MG, as depicted in Figure 5, a similar band is observed at 1627 cm–1 in the lower wavenumber region. Conversely, the corresponding C=ND stretching band resulting from deuterium substitution is not prominently visible. The C=ND stretching band at 1620 cm–1 is likely obscured, potentially canceled out by a larger band on the negative side near 1620 cm–1. However, taking into account that the difference in vibrational frequencies between the negative band linked to the resting state of BovRh and MG (1656 and 1647 cm–1, respectively) and the positive band corresponding to the lumi intermediate (1636 and 1627 cm–1, respectively) is consistent at 9 cm–1, it can be inferred that the hydrogen bond strength of the Schiff base in MG weakens during the transition from batho to lumi intermediates, mirroring the behavior observed in BovRh.

## MG-Specific Hydrogen Bond Change in the Protein Moiety of Lumi Intermediate
In rhodopsin, the displacement of the middle portion of TM3, caused by the flipping motion of the β-ionone ring of retinal, also results in a hydrogen bond alteration involving Glu1223.37. In Figure 6, the coupling band at 1740 (−)/1735 (+)/1727 (−) cm–1 observed in the batho intermediate of BovRh, which originates from the protonated C=O stretching vibration of Glu1223.37,43 appears at 1742 (+)/1725 (−) cm–1 with increasing intensity as the lumi intermediate forms. The pair band at 1773 (+)/1767 (−) cm–1 stems from the C=O stretching vibration of Asp832.50 in TM2,43 and this band is not significantly altered by the transition from batho to lumi intermediates. A comparison of the crystal structures of the batho8 and lumi9 intermediates of BovRh reveals that Asp832.50 experiences only a slight shift, while Glu1223.37 not only shifts but also undergoes a slight change in side-chain orientation (Figure S2b). These findings align with the FTIR spectroscopic evidence, indicating that the C=O stretching band from Glu1223.37 changes during the batho to lumi transition.

Black and red lines represent the measurement in H2O and D2O, respectively. (b) FTIR difference spectra of mutants (black) and wild-type MG (gray) hydrated with H2O. Each gridline of the y-axis corresponds to 0.00025 (a) and 0.0005 (b) absorbance units.
Conversely, in the MG spectrum, a pair band is observed at 1744 (−)/1738 (+) cm–1 for the batho intermediate and at 1748 (+)/1742 (−) cm–1 for the lumi intermediate. A comparison of amino acid sequences reveals that Asp832.50 is conserved in MG (designated as Asp992.50 in MG), but the glutamic acid at position 3.37 is replaced by isoleucine (I1383.37 in MG). Consequently, the pair bands observed in the batho and lumi intermediates of MG likely represent the C=O stretching vibration bands of Asp992.50. It is worth noting that these bands do not originate from lipid-derived C=O stretching vibrations, as evidenced by a distinct isotope shift observed during hydrogen/deuterium exchange (Figure 6a). Therefore, we attempted to confirm this assignment through site-directed mutation. Mutants were generated in which Asp992.50 was replaced with glutamate and asparagine. Among the two constructs, D99E, despite being expressed, did not bind retinal (Figure S3), and therefore only D99N was spectrally measured (Figure 6b). Interestingly, the corresponding pair band did not disappear in the D99N spectrum, indicating that this pair band did not originate from the C=O stretching vibration of Asp992.50. Further comparative analysis of amino acid sequences revealed that MG contains Glu1022.53 (equivalent to Met862.53 in rhodopsin), positioned one turn extracellular from the α-helix containing Asp992.50. Consequently, we measured the E102Q mutant, confirming the disappearance of the pair bands for both the batho and lumi intermediates (Figure 6b). Thus, the C=O stretching band of the protonated carboxylic acid observed in MG originates from Glu1022.53. In BovRh, the hydrogen bond strength of Asp992.50 remains unchanged during the transition from batho to lumi intermediates. In contrast, in MG, the hydrogen bond strength of Glu1022.53 reverses during the transition from batho to lumi, indicating a disparity in the structural changes between BovRh and MG during the transition to the lumi intermediate.
During the formation of the BovRh lumi intermediate, the local α-helical distortion caused by the retinal β-ionone ring flip affects not only Glu1223.37 but also Thr1183.33 in TM3. Indeed, the intensity of the O–H stretching vibration in Thr1183.33 decreases with a slight upshift during the transition from the batho to the lumi intermediates (Figure 7a). Thr1183.33 forms a hydrogen bond with the main chain of Gly1143.29 in the resting state. When batho forms, the O–H stretching band upshifts from 3464 to 3488 cm–1 due to the weakening of the hydrogen bond strength between Thr1183.33 and Gly1143.29 caused by a local positional change in TM3. The distance to Gly114 is further increased as TM3 undergoes a positional transition with the formation of the luminescence intermediate, further weakening the hydrogen bond and a higher upshift in vibrational frequency.

(b) FTIR difference spectra of lumi and batho intermediates of MG and BovRh in the X–D stretching band region. Red and blue lines represent the measurements in D2O and D218O, respectively. In MG, as observed in BovRh, Ser1343.33, corresponding to Thr1183.33, also experiences an upshift from 3426 to 3484 cm–1 upon forming the batho state in Figure 7a. This suggests that the hydrogen bond strength of Ser1343.33 is weakened. However, the O–H stretching band from Ser1343.33 disappears with the formation of the lumi intermediate in Figure 7a. This disappearance could be attributed to maintaining the same hydrogen bond strength in the resting state and the lumi intermediate or deuterium exchange occurring in Ser1343.33 during lumi formation. Similar events, where O–H stretching bands that are not hydrogen/deuterium exchanged, disappear during lumi formation, have also been observed in MB,32 indicating the possibility of a common alteration among cone pigments.
The local perturbation of α-helices and changes in the hydrogen-bonding network involve hydrophilic amino acids, as observed in the X–D stretching vibration region due to deuterium exchange analysis (Figure 7b). The vibrational bands, highlighted in green, correspond to the O–D stretching bands of water molecules, exhibiting isotopic effects due to 18O. In the case of MG, a distinctive feature is the observation of more O–D stretching bands originating from water molecules compared to BovRh when transitioning to the batho state. This suggests a greater number of protein-bound water molecules around the retinal binding pocket in MG than in BovRh. Figure 7b illustrates that water molecules’ observed O–D stretching vibrations are more prominent in MG than in BovRh, even in the lumi state. In BovRh, the perturbation of protein-bound water molecules induced by retinal isomerization significantly diminishes in the lumi state. In contrast, in MG, the perturbation of water molecules in the lumi state is comparable to that in the batho state.
The pair band at 2684 (+)/2670 (−) cm–1 observed in the batho minus MG difference spectra is known to be associated with a water molecule located near the retinal polyene chain. This is evident from the shift observed in the batho minus MG (9-cis bound form, which could be photochemically formed at 77 K) difference spectrum.31 Based on the vibrational frequencies, the 2683 (−)/2666 (+) cm–1 pair band observed in the lumi intermediate likely corresponds to the O–D stretching band of this water molecule. An upshift of the band from 2670 to 2684 cm–1 is observed with the formation of batho, indicating a weakening of the hydrogen bonding strength of the water molecule. Whereas a downshift of the band occurs in lumi, indicating the strengthening of the hydrogen bonding of the water molecule. Moreover, in the lumi intermediate spectrum, we observe a 2620 (+)/2600 (−) cm–1 pair band that exhibits a clear 18O isotope effect. However, this band’s intensity is significantly higher than other vibrational bands originating from water molecules, and the bands in the isotope spectrum (blue line) appear broadened and reduced in intensity. As a result, two potential scenarios arise: first, it contains the O–D stretching vibration bands of multiple water molecules, and second, it is likely that this pair band contains the O–D or N–D stretching bands of hydrophilic amino acids, distinct from water molecules.
In the context of the first possibility, the batho intermediate spectrum of MG reveals a positive 2308 cm–1 band attributed to the O–D stretching band from an internal water molecule, which is hydrated with a chloride ion—a characteristic feature of L-group cone pigments.31 Quantum chemical calculations further support this hydration pattern, indicating that at least three water molecules hydrate the chloride ion near the polyene chain of the retinal.44,45 Conversely, the lumi intermediate spectrum exhibits a broad band in a frequency region similar to the batho intermediate. However, there is no significant isotope shift due to 18O. Despite this, the resemblance in spectral characteristics to the batho intermediate suggests the persistence of a similar hydration water molecule in the lumi intermediate. An alternative interpretation is that the chloride ion binding pocket undergoes perturbation during lumi formation, resulting in weakened hydrogen bonding strength of the chloride ion hydration water. This is observed as a pair band at 2620/2600 cm–1. Prior low-temperature UV-visible spectroscopic studies on the red-sensitive cone pigment in chickens have proposed chloride ion dissociation from the protein moiety at the Meta-I intermediate formed after lumi.46 In essence, in lumi, the chloride ion is bound inside the protein, aligning with our current findings.
Considering the second possibility, an equation relating vibrational frequency to the converted mass of a diatomic molecule was employed for the 2620 (+)/2600 (−) cm–1 pair bands. The calculated vibrational frequency of the O–H stretching band before the shift aligns with 3515 (+)/3488 (−) cm–1, resembling the 3484 (+)/3426 (−) cm–1 band of Ser1343.33. These analyses suggest that Ser1343.33 is initially exposed to a hydrophobic environment in both the resting state and batho intermediate with no occurrence of deuterium exchange. However, a significant local conformational change in TM3, associated with the lumie formation, results in the deuterium substitution of Ser1343.33. As this phenomenon has also been observed in MB (Thr1173.33), further clarification is anticipated through future studies involving mutants.
Figure 7b also provides insights into the N–D stretching band of the lumi intermediate in MG. As depicted in Figure 5, the vibrational analysis of the C=NH and C=ND stretches suggests that, similar to BovRh, the hydrogen bond strength of the Schiff base in the lumi intermediate of MG is considerably weaker than that in the resting state or the batho intermediate. Furthermore, the Schiff base N–D stretching band of the lumi intermediate of BovRh has been previously assigned at 2337 cm–1 (Figure 7b). Given this information, it is reasonable to assume that the positive band at 2335 cm–1 can be attributed to the N–D stretching band of the MG lumi intermediate.

## Discussion
Understanding the early conformational changes in visual pigment activation is crucial for unraveling the molecular mechanism of the loose allosteric coupling, a concept often discussed in GPCRs.47 This phenomenon bridges the isomerization of retinal on the extracellular side to the major conformational changes necessary for the interaction with G proteins on the cytoplasmic side. In the case of rhodopsin, the perturbations of Met862.53 in TM2 and Glu1223.37 in TM3, which are associated with the formation of the lumi intermediate, result in an expansion of the retinal binding pocket’s volume. This expansion creates a passage connecting the extracellular-facing retinal binding pocket to the cytoplasmic side of the transmembrane bundle.17,18
Conversely, our recent findings indicate that during the formation of the lumi intermediate in MB, a significant perturbation of the α-helix, most likely in TM3, takes place, concomitant with the deprotonation of the Schiff base.32 Additionally, using the O–H stretching band of Thr1173.33 as a probe, we inferred that TM3 is primarily perturbed upon the formation of the lumi intermediate. The physiological significance of deprotonation during the early conformational change process in MB remains unclear. Nevertheless, at the very least, we believe it is reflected in the differences in photoreaction dynamics between rhodopsin and MB.
Having successfully acquired spectral data for the lumie intermediate of MG, a comprehensive spectral comparison among BovRh, MB, and MG has offered a detailed insight into the differences in early photoreaction dynamics (Figure 8). Some commonalities were found, such as the flip motion of the β-ionone ring and alterations in the torsional structure of retinal across all three pigments. However, when it came to the hydrogen bond strength of the Schiff base, MG exhibited similarities to BovRh, with no observation of the deprotonation reaction seen in MB. The way in which the α-helix’s conformational change coupled with the retinal’s structural transformation appeared analogous between BovRh and MG, except for the extent of α-helical movement (Figure 8). In BovRh, X-ray crystallographic evidence verifies that TM2/TM3 shifts outward due to the β-ionone ring’s flip motion in retinal. In this case, a hydrogen bond forms between Glu1223.37 and His2115.46, bridging TM3 and TM5. Since this hydrogen bond’s position counters the movement of TM3, it is believed to function as an inhibitor of TM3's motion. In contrast, in MG, the amino acids at positions 3.37 and 5.46 are isoleucine and cysteine, respectively, preventing the formation of a hydrogen bond between TM3 and TM5. Consequently, this absence of a hydrogen bond results in a significant α-helical movement in TM3.
Schematic illustrating the conformational changes in the resting state and lumie intermediate of MG (a), BovRh (b), and MB (c) as viewed from the intracellular side. In common with the three types of pigments, TM2/TM3 is expected to move outward, coupled with the flip motion of the β-ionone ring of retinal, upon lumi intermediate formation. However, MG lacks the interaction between TM3 and TM5 as BovRh does, suggesting a greater movement of TM3. Unlike MB, where a deprotonation reaction of the Schiff base occurs during the formation of the lumi intermediate, MG maintains the Schiff base in a protonated state, like BovRh. Additionally, MG is unique in having glutamic acid at position 2.53, and a change occurs with the formation of the lumi intermediate, weakening the hydrogen bond at Glu1022.53, causing it to move outward.
Furthermore, a specific change distinctive to MG was observed in the hydrogen bonding pattern of Glu1022.53 (compared to Met862.53 in BovRh and Leu852.53 in MB) during the transition from the batho to the lumi intermediate. Intriguingly, in rhodopsin-like GPCRs, the 2.53 position predominantly comprises bulky or aromatic residues, which are among the top 20% of amino acid residues crucial for activation.48 Additionally, previous NMR spectroscopy investigations and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations involving the β2 adrenergic receptor (β2AR) have demonstrated that Met822.53 plays a role not only in ligand binding but also in early conformational changes crucial for β2AR activation on time scales faster than milliseconds.49 Significantly, this time scale aligns precisely with the formation of the lumi intermediate in visual pigments. In addition, as described above, BovRh’s X-ray crystal structure reveals a relocation in the position of Met862.53 following the transition from batho to lumi intermediate. Consequently, the hydrogen bond alteration observed at Glu1022.53 during the transition from the batho to the lumi intermediate in MG is deemed a conformational change that initiates activation akin to other rhodopsin-like GPCRs.
As noted above, approximately 80% of rhodopsin-like GPCRs feature a hydrophobic amino acid residue at position 2.53, and it is believed that during the early stages of conformational change, this residue relocates to interact with Trp6.48, a pivotal element for activation, acting like a toggle switch.17 In contrast, only L-group cone pigments, including MG and the gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) receptor, possess a glutamate at position 2.53. The observation that the E902.53K mutation is responsible for congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism strongly underscores the significance of having an acidic amino acid at this position for the GnRH receptor.50 Furthermore, the X-ray crystal structure of the inactive form of the GnRH receptor reveals that the distance between the oxygen atoms of the carboxy group of Glu902.53 and the indole ring of Trp2806.48 is approximately 3.8 Å, while the distance between the carboxy group and the N–H group is roughly 5.1 Å.51 These distances are sufficient to enable interaction (Figure S4a). Additionally, we generated a model structure of MG using AlphaFold2,52 which similarly illustrated that Glu1022.53 and Trp2816.48 are within interacting distance (Figure S4b).
In the case of most rhodopsin-like GPCRs with a hydrophobic amino acid at position 2.53, alterations in hydrophobic interactions with the indole moiety of Trp6.48 contribute to the phenomenon of loose allosteric coupling. Conversely, in L-group cone pigments and the GnRH receptor, a one-point hydrogen bond between Glu2.53 and the N–H group of Trp6.48 may participate in this loose allosteric coupling. Although there are currently no reported instances of diseases resulting from mutations in Glu1022.53 of L-group cone pigments, we plan to investigate the effects of Glu1022.53 mutations on the photoreaction dynamics of the late intermediate in future research.
Exploring the amino acid at position 2.53 provides further insights into spectral tuning in cone pigments. Figure S5 illustrates that the λmax of the MG E1022.53Q mutant undergoes a 9 nm blue-shift compared to the wild-type pigment. In the predicted structure of MG constructed using AlphaFold2, the distance between the Schiff base and Glu1022.53 is approximately 6.2 Å. Typically, amino acids with high electronegativity near the Schiff base induce a spectral blue-shift in λmax due to the localized π-electrons within the retinal polyene chain. However, in our present study, a 9 nm blue-shift in λmax was observed despite the reduction in electronegativity caused by the Glu to Gln mutation. This shift is likely attributed to alterations in the torsional structure of the retinal or an increase in the electrostatic interaction between the Schiff base and Glu1293.28 (counterion) due to their proximity.
In Figure S6, a comparison of the fingerprint region where the retinal-derived vibrational signal appears in the FTIR difference spectra between the batho and resting states of E1022.53Q and the wild-type MG is presented. The spectral similarity between both spectra suggests that the torsional structure of the retinal in E1022.53Q closely resembles that of the wild-type. The low S/N ratio of the FTIR spectra of E1022.53Q limits the discussion of the N–D stretching band, representing the Schiff base’s hydrogen bond strength. Nonetheless, we can at least exclude the influence of the torsional structure of the retinal as a contributing factor to the spectral blue-shift in λmax observed in the mutant.
Previously, both quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) calculations and UV-visible absorption spectroscopy measurements of mutants have shed light on the difference of 2.53 amino acid residue that distinguishes UV- and blue-sensitive cone pigments.53 In UV-sensitive pigments, a hydrophobic amino acid occupies position 2.53, which lowers the pKa of the Schiff base, inducing its deprotonation in the resting state and resulting in short-wavelength absorption. Conversely, in blue-sensitive pigments, a polar amino acid resides at position 2.53, elevating the pKa of the Schiff base and leading to its protonation, allowing for visible light absorption. Notably, there is an exception concerning blue-sensitive pigments in humans and primates. These pigments belong to the UV-sensitive pigment group and contain a hydrophobic amino acid (Leu862.53) at position 2.53. Nevertheless, the Schiff base in these pigments remains protonated, implying the involvement of a distinct mechanism in raising the pKa of the Schiff base.
Therefore, the amino acid residue at position 2.53 not only contributes significantly to ligand binding and activation in numerous GPCRs but also assumes a multifaceted role in regulating the spectral tuning of short-wavelength-absorbing pigments through modulation of the pKa of the retinal Schiff base. Moreover, it is worth noting that hydrophobic amino acids often evade detection due to their insensitivity to infrared signals. In contrast, the C=O stretching band of glutamate remains distinguishable from those of other protein-originating infrared signals. Hence, by utilizing the C=O stretching band of Glu1022.53 in MG as a probe, we anticipate a more profound comprehension of the structural significance of the amino acid at position 2.53 in the activation mechanisms of rhodopsin-like GPCRs.

## Conclusions
We have found that the lumi intermediate of MG formed at 200 K exhibits a blue-shifted λmax akin to rhodopsin. However, the kinetics of early photoreaction differ between them.26 A comparison of light-induced FTIR difference spectra for MG and rhodopsin lumi intermediates indicates that MG, mirroring rhodopsin, undergoes a relaxation in retinal distortion during lumi formation, accompanied by a large conformational change in the α-helix. On the other hand, specific hydrogen bonding alterations of Glu1022.53 in TM2, unique to MG, and changes in hydrogen bonding involving internal water molecules were observed. Given that the amino acid at position 2.53 has been proposed to relocate early in receptor activation in rhodopsin and various other GPCRs, the direct observation of hydrogen bond changes at Glu1022.53 in MG-lumi stands as a crucial experimental discovery for a more profound understanding of the activation processes in other GPCRs. In the future, employing the infrared signal of Glu1022.53 as a probe and conducting structural analyses of subsequent intermediates, we aim to elucidate not only the macroscopic alterations in the protein moiety during photoactivation but also more microscopic changes including hydrogen bond alterations. This endeavor will contribute to advancing our understanding of the molecular-level activation mechanism.
