Abstract
Like vertebrate animals, some invertebrates have been shown to exhibit anxiety-like behavior while in
apparatus that allow choice between sheltered, darkened spaces and open, lit spaces. The behavioral
mechanisms by which invertebrates accomplish anxiety-like behavior, and whether those mechanisms
are similar across species, has not been fully studied. Across three experiments, we investigated possible
behaviors (photokinesis, phototaxis, and sheltering) that Great Ramshorn snails (Planorbarius corneus)
could use to accomplish anxiety-like behavior while in presence of the odor of a predatory fish. In
experiment one, we used a light/dark preference box to demonstrate reduced preference for exposed and
lit areas caused by the predator odor. In experiment two, we used an open field apparatus to demonstrate
an increase in refuge-seeking (time spent near a wall) in diffusely lit but not dark environments caused by
predator odor. In the same experiment we saw no effect of predator odor on photokinesis (difference in
movement speed in a lit versus dark environment). In experiment three, we conducted a second open field
study with a directional light source and found no evidence of phototaxis (movement direction with
respect to light), and no effect of predator odor on phototaxis. Thus, in our studies we found evidence for
refuge-seeking as a mechanism for anxiety-like behavior in the presence of predator odor and little
evidence for perhaps more computationally simple strategies of increased photokinesis and phototaxis.

Introduction
Anxiety has been described as excessive worry or apprehension across time about activities or events
and is prevalent in various psychiatric disorders (American Psychiatric Association 2013). The capacity
for anxiety, or at least for anxiety-like behavioral patterns, is conserved across vertebrate and invertebrate
species and appears to be ancient (Egan et al. 2009; Fossat et al. 2014; Mohammad et al. 2016; Zewde et
al. 2018). This may in part be due to the importance of the state during avoidance of predators and other
environmental dangers (Blanchard and Blanchard 1988; Steimer 2011; Perrot-Minnot et al. 2017).
Anxiety in animals can be assessed in the laboratory by measuring a variety of types of behaviors,
including social interactions, exploration of light-dark box, open field exploration, elevated plus-maze
exploration, shock-probe burying, and conditioned aversive contextual responses including fearpotentiated startle responses within a conditioned context (Graeff 2002; Menard and Treit 1999; Luyten et
al. 2011). Many tasks used to assess anxiety-related behaviors necessarily allow observations of
behavior in the presence and absence of anxiogenic stimuli (Lister 1990; Steimer 2011). Ethologically
nocturnal animals such as rodents tend to show a preference for dark enclosed spaces, and assessing
anxiety often involves measures of unconditioned approach and avoidance behaviors related to exposed,
open, or well-lit spaces (Denenberg 1969; Crawley 1981; Pellow et al. 1985). For instance, the light-dark
box is a box divided into a lit and open section and a darkened and enclosed section. An anxiety-like
behavioral state is inferred by measures of time spent in the lit, exposed section or sometimes by latency
to enter the light (Bourin and Hascoet 2003; Kulesskaya and Voikar 2014). The open field apparatus is an
open arena wherein an animal is allowed to freely explore for some amount of time. Anxiety-like states
are inferred by locomotor activity, reported most often as speed or amount of movement over time.

Moreover, time spent away from the walls of the apparatus and in the center are also measures of
anxiety-like behavior (Kulesskaya and Voikar 2014; Gould et al. 2009; Seibenhener and Wooten 2015).
Drugs known to reduce anxiety in humans increase the exploration of exposed, well-lit areas and/or
decrease the avoidance of such areas on the Light-Dark Box, Open Field Apparatus and similar
experimental apparatus (Merlo Pich and Samanin 1989; Chaouloff et al. 1997). Likewise, putative stressinducing experiences such as restraint, shock, and predator odor decrease exploratory behavior,
particularly of open and lit areas (Padovan and Guimaraes 2000; Staples 2010; Steenbergen et al. 1990).
Although open and enclosed areas of laboratory apparatus are most commonly used in studies of the
behavior of small mammals and especially rodents, they have been adapted to study behavioral patterns
indicative of anxiety-like states within invertebrate species. These have included species as diverse as
nudibranch mollusks (Hermissenda Crassicornicus) (Talk et al. 1999), crayfish (Procambarus clarkii)
(Fossat et al. 2014), fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) (Mohammad et al. 2016), and planaria flatworms
(Dugesia dorotocephala) (Zewde et al. 2018).
It is not clear by which behavioral or cognitive mechanisms that the various invertebrate species possess
the capacity for anxiety-like behaviors, or whether they are always similar to the mechanisms of
vertebrates. For example, a randomly moving organism could effectively reduce time in lit areas simply
by increasing movement speed in the light and slowing in the dark. An organism could avoid light by
actively turning and moving away from the source of the light. They could avoid light by being more likely
to refuge-seek while in the light than in the dark. We thus conducted the present set of studies to assess
these potential aspects of the anxiety-like state induced by predator odor in a gastropod mollusk species.
We first sought to demonstrate reduced preference for exposed and lit areas induced by predator odor in
a light/dark preference box. Then, in additional studies conducted in two sets of open field apparatus, we
sought to determine the mechanisms by which this anxiety-like behavior occurs. We assessed the effect
of predator odor on photokinesis (speed in a lit versus dark environment), refuge-seeking (time spent near
a wall) in lit vs dark environments and phototaxis (movement direction with respect to a light source).

Methods

Husbandry and Housing:
Great ramshorn snails (Planorbarius corneus) were laboratory bred and raised in 50 L tanks of artificial
pond water made with aged tap water and commercial aquarium electrolyte supplements (Seachem and
Marine Master). Pieces of dead and sterilized coral were placed in the tanks to supplement calcium
concentrations. The tanks were fitted with aquarium lights and were maintained at 21 ± 1°C on a 12 h: 12
h light: dark cycle (lights on at 7 am). The snails were fed iceberg lettuce (Lactuca sativa) ad libitum and
had half of their tank water replaced weekly. The snails were used as subjects when they were 10–12
weeks old.

Experimental Manipulation of Anxiety:
Page 3/14

The experiments were conducted in either control water or predator odor water. We made control water by
pureeing and straining (with a 1 mm grating) 300 g iceberg lettuce in 1.25 L aged tap water. This was
diluted to 10 g lettuce per liter of aged tap water in the tanks. We made predator odor water by pureeing
and straining 5 g of barramundi (Lates calcarifer) skin in 250 ml aged tap water. This was added to the
control solution to a final dilution of 20 mg pureed barramundi skin per liter of water.

Light/Dark Box Test:
We assessed preference to be in the light or dark using four rectangular (600 l x 60 w x 240 h mm) glass
tanks. We wrapped half of each tank with aluminum foil so that the top, sides, and bottom of half of the
tank was enclosed and protected from light. We placed the tanks side by side on a glass tabletop in a
darkroom and filled each tank with 6 L of either control or predator odor water. We positioned a 55 cm
long LED aquarium light 33 cm above the water surface across the center of the tanks so that it
illuminated the open half of each tank.
At 3 pm, we placed 40 snails in each tank at the center. The snails were allowed to freely explore for the
subsequent 16 hours. The snails were observed at 5, 6, 7, and 10 pm and then at 7 am the following day.
The number of snails in the open half of the tank was noted. Snails were scored as being in the open side
only if their entire foot was on that side. The light was turned off immediately after the 7 pm observation
so subsequently both the open and enclosed halves of the tanks were in the dark. the lights were turned
on for the 7 am observation, and the snails were then removed to holding tanks. The light/dark tanks
were emptied, cleaned, and prepared for the second day of the experiment.
We ran the experiment identically on the following day, with snails that received the control water on the
first day receiving the predator odor on the second day, and snails that received the predator odor on the
first day receiving the control water on the second day. After the 7 am observation the following day, the
lights were left on, and the snails were returned to their respective home tanks. At 9 AM, each snail was
checked for viability (that they were upright and clinging to the substrate).

Open Field Tests:
Our open field apparatus consisted of 14 cm square opaque plastic dishes that were filled to a depth of 1
cm with either control or predator odor water. Thirty-six such chambers were arranged on the floor in a
darkroom. Four 35 W fluorescent lamps were situated to the sides of the dishes providing indirect room
illumination. The snails were imaged using an infrared USB camera and computer. We conducted the
study between 1 PM and 6 PM in a single day.
We placed snails Individually in the center of each of the open fields and held them until they became
attached to the substrate. They were then allowed to move freely throughout the apparatus. After a 10minute acclimation period, the fluorescent lamps were turned on or off every 15 minutes for 1.5 hours.
Following the open field exposure period, we rinsed each of the snails in artificial pond water and placed
them individually into separate holding chambers.

A clean set of 14 cm square opaque plastic dishes served as the open field apparatus for the study
replication. The replication was run identically to the first study except that each snail was placed into an
open field containing the opposite water type. Snails that first received control water were placed into
predator odor water, and snails that had received predator odor water were placed into control water. After
a 10-minute acclimation period, the subject was then allowed to move freely about the open field for a
further 1.5 hours with the fluorescent lamps turning on or off every 15 minutes. The coordinates of each
snail were determined at each 30 second time point using ImageJ Manual Tracking plug-in.
We assessed phototaxic behavior (movement in a direction with respect to a light source) using six flat
bottomed round clear Pyrex dishes that were placed on a tabletop in a darkroom and filled to a depth of 2
cm with either control or predator odor water. The dishes were each set on the top and center of white
paper that had a circle printed on it with a radius of 7 cm. A 3 W LED lamp was diffused through opaque
white plastic to provide a light source to the side of the dishes 30 cm from the dish centers. The snails
were imaged from above using an infrared USB web-camera connected to a computer.
Between 3 and 6 PM across two days, we placed snails individually in the center of the dishes at random
orientation with respect to the light and held them in place until they became attached to the substrate.
They were then allowed to move freely about the dish until they crossed the 7 cm radius circle. They were
then returned to a home tank. We ran each subject only once. We used ImageJ with the recordings to
determine the location of the snail as they crossed the 7 cm circle and then calculated the angle to the
light source location with respect to the location of the center of the dish using Microsoft Excel. Thus, if
the crossing point was directly between the dish center and the light the angle was 0° and if directly away
the angle was 180°. We also recorded the time it took for each snail to cross the circle from the center.

Statistical Analysis:
We performed statistical analyses using the software SPSS. For Experiment 1, we compared number of
subjects in the open side of the light/dark test tank at each of the 10 time points. We conducted Chisquare tests for these observations due to their nonparametric nature. Even though this study has a
repeated measures design, we did not track the subjects across observations. We thus took a
conservative approach in the statistical analysis by treating each observation as independent. We applied
a Bonferroni correction to the critical alpha level by dividing it by ten, the number of comparisons we
tested. For Experiment 2, photokinesis during the Open Field Test was analyzed using a repeated
measures ANOVA on the rate of movement with the variables of water type (control or predator odor
water) and illumination level (light or dark). Sheltering near a wall was analyzed using a repeated
measures ANOVA on the percent time spent near a wall with the variables of water type (control or
predator odor water) and illumination level (light or dark). Being near a wall was defined as the center of
the snail as being less than 0.8 cm of the nearest wall. For Experiment 3, to assess phototaxis with
reference to a light source, we performed a MANCOVA with the angle and outward speed to the 7 cm
circle serving as the dependent measures and control water or predator water odor as a between-group
factor.

More subjects of the control water condition than of the predator water condition were in the open
chamber of the light–dark box At the 10 pm observation of Day One (X2 (1, N = 80) = 32.46, p < .001), and
at the 5 pm (X2 (1, N = 80) = 15.20, p < .001), 6 pm (X2 (1, N = 80) = 12.88, p < .001), and 7 pm (X2 (1, N =

Open Field Tests:
Photokinesis
The snails moved faster in the light than in the dark (F1,35 = 46.22, p < .001; Fig. 2). However, there was no
reliable difference in rate of movement of snails while they were in the control odor water vs predator
odor water (F1,35 = 0.02, p = .888). There was also no reliable interaction between water odor and light
(F1,35 = 0.48, p = .495).
Time near wall
There was a reliable interaction between water odorant type and lighting condition (F1,35 = 5.99, p = .019)
so that the subjects spent more time within .8 cm of the wall in the light if in water with predator odor
(Fig. 3). The snails were also more likely to be in proximity to the wall in the light than in the dark (F1,35 =
16.05, p < .001). There was no reliable main effect of odorant type (F1,35 = .406, p = .528).
Phototaxis
The subjects that received the control odor displayed minimal approach or avoidance behavior with
respect to the direction a light source. Their mean angle of movement with respect to the light source was
close to 90 degrees, indicating little average phototaxic or photophobic locomotion. The MANCOVA we
conducted on dependent measures of angle and speed and between-group factor of water odorant
(Control, n = 43; Predator, n = 52) did not reveal a significant main effect for group F(2, 92) = 0.843, p =
0.434. Subsequent univariate F tests revealed no significant main effect for group for the angle F(1, 93) =
1.59, p = 0.21 or speed F(1, 93) = 0.09, p = 0.76 scores. The mean angle and speed summary scores for the
control and predator odor groups are shown in Fig. 4 as vectors of angle towards or away from the light
source and overall outward speed represented as the length of the vector.

Discussion
Our initial goal with the current study was to assess the capacity of an aquatic gastropod mollusk
species to express anxiety-like behaviors by observing preference or avoidance of exposed, illuminated
areas of a light/dark preference box. We wanted to determine whether this behavior is altered by having a
predator odor in the water. Then, using studies of behavior in an open field with and without predator
odor, we aimed to unravel some of the underlying information processing or behavioral mechanisms
responsible for any observed anxiety-like behavior.
We found that Ramshorn snails indeed did express enhanced anxiety-like behavior in the presence of
predator odor. They reliably avoided the lit and exposed chamber of a light/dark box, and this avoidance
was enhanced while in the presence of odor from a predatory fish. Then using an open field apparatus,
we found that the snails expressed photokinesis, moving on average faster during periods of diffuse
illumination. Moving faster in the light and more slowly in the dark could be one mechanism for
avoidance of the lit areas in an environment. However, we found no effect of predator odor, or light by
predator odor interaction, on speed of movement. Photokinesis thus can’t explain the enhanced light
avoidance in the presence of predator odor that we observed. We did find that snails spent more time
near the wall of an open field when in illuminated rather than darkened surroundings, particularly with the
presence of predator odor. These findings suggest that the snails may be using a sheltering strategy,
spending more time in a more protected location while in the presence of predator odor in the light. In a
separate open field made of clear Plexiglas, we found that the snails did not express phototaxis. They
instead moved randomly with respect to a light source at a particular direction. This behavior was also
not reliably influenced by the presence of predator odor.
We conclude that the behavioral mechanisms of anxiety-like behavior of snails in an environment with
light and predator odor include sheltering behavior. This was seen in our studies by snails remaining in
protected areas including the darkened side of a light/dark preference test chamber, or near the wall of an
open field apparatus during diffuse light. With respect to our null findings related to the influence of
predator odor on photokinesis and phototaxis, further studies may be required to establish confidence
about them. For example, assessing effects of predator odor on photokinesis and phototaxis in a variety
of lighting conditions would resolve any floor or ceiling effects we may have encountered.
Scientists have argued for the existence of anxiety-like behavioral conditions in gastropod mollusks at
least since the initial demonstrations of aversive conditioning in Aplysia (Kandel 1983). Anxiety-like
behavior has been most clearly demonstrated in marine gastropod mollusks during aversive context
conditioning of Aplysia Californica (Colwill et al. 1988) and Hermissenda crassicornis (Talk et al. 1999).
In these studies, a context was paired with an aversive stimulus. Animals subsequently displayed anxietylike behaviors such as potentiated reflexes in the presence of the paired context, or avoidance of the
paired context. For example, (Colwill et al. 1988) demonstrated aversive context conditioning in Aplysia
leading to fear-potentiated siphon withdrawal reflex. Aplysia underwent training sessions in two distinct
contexts, receiving electric shocks in one context and none in the other. Context conditioning was then
Page 7/14

tested by mechanically stimulating the siphon. Aplysia tested in the same context in which they had
received electric shocks exhibited significantly greater siphon withdrawal than those tested in the control
context. In another study, (Talk et al. 1999) demonstrated aversive context conditioning leading to
avoidance of the paired context. They exposed Hermissenda to aversive high-speed orbital rotation while
restricted to either the lit or darkened side of a light-dark preference box. In a later preference test, if the
rotation had been administered in the lit context, the Hermissenda avoided the illuminated side. No such
avoidance of light occurred if the Hermissenda were exposed to rotation on the dark side of the box or
were not treated with rotation.
With respect to comparisons with mammalian species, anxiety in the open field in rodents and snails
appears be manifested with largely but not completely similar patterns of behavior. As we saw in snails,
exposure of rats to increased light levels as opposed to dark has been documented to increase time spent
near the edge of an open field (Valle 1970). Exposure of rodents to predator odor inconsistently enhances
fear responses, but as we saw here in snails, larger effects of predator odor in rodents occur within
anxiety-provoking environments that include bright lights (Morrow et al. 2002). One notable difference
between our observations with snails and earlier studies of rodents in the open field is that in rodents,
light decreases locomotor behavior (Valle 1970) whereas we found that light increases movement speed.
Other more recent studies have explored anxiety-like behaviors in invertebrate species including
pancrustaceans, and have aimed to establish neuropharmacological homology with mammalian anxiety.
Widely used anxiolytic drugs act on γ-aminobutyric acid type a (GABAa) receptors, or serotoninergic
receptors [51]. Multiple studies have shown a role of modulation of the same receptor types in anxietyrelated behavior in various pancrustacea species including crayfish, fruit flies, and amphipods (Fossat et
al. 2014; Mohammad et al. 2016; Perrot-Minnot et al. 2017). The only similar studies in mollusks that we
are aware of use lower concentrations of neuropharmacological agents and have an ecotoxicological
orientation. For example, (Hedgespeth et al. 2018) put aquatic snails in a water tank that also contained a
shelter under which they could hide. Addition to the tank of water taken from a tank that housed
predatory fish caused the snails to reduce their activity and to move away from open areas.
Administration of the common antidepressant and antianxiety drug sertraline (at doses relevant to
environmental contamination) had no effect on this behavior. The last common precursor of molluscan
and arthropod species occurred around 550 million years ago, around the same time as the last common
ancestor between mollusks and chordates. Commonalities in anxiety between mollusks, chordates, and
arthropods indicate that processes related to anxiety possess ancient evolutionary origins and have
probably functioned as essential survival mechanisms for organisms over millions of years.
