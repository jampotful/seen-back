Mapping the contribution of the C-linker domain to gating polarity in CNBD channels.

# Mapping the contribution of the C-linker domain to gating polarity in CNBD channels

## Abstract
Ion channels of the cyclic nucleotide-binding domain (CNBD) family play a crucial role in the regulation of key biological processes, such as photoreception and pacemaking activity in the heart. These channels exhibit high sequence and structural similarity but differ greatly in their functional responses to membrane potential. The CNBD family includes hyperpolarization-activated ion channels and depolarization-activated ether-à-go-go channels. Structural and functional studies show that the differences in the coupling interface between these two subfamilies' voltage-sensing domain and pore domain may underlie their differential response to membrane polarity. However, other structural components may also contribute to defining the polarity differences in activation. Here, we focus on the role of the C-terminal domain, which interacts with elements in both the pore and voltage-sensing domains. By generating a series of chimeras involving the C-terminal domain derived from distant members of the CNBD family, we find that the nature of the C-termini profoundly influences the gating polarity of these ion channels. Scanning mutagenesis of the C-linker region, a helix-turn-helix motif connecting the pore helix to the CNBD, reveals that residues at the intersubunit interface between the C-linkers are crucial for hyperpolarization-dependent activation. These findings highlight the unique and unexpected role of the intersubunit interface of the C-linker region in regulating the gating polarity of voltage-gated ion channels.

## Significance
Cyclic nucleotide-binding domain (CNBD) ion channels are involved in maintaining bioelectrical processes in various tissues and cell types. In this study, we use chimeric channels and alanine-scanning mutagenesis to determine the role of the C-terminal domain in gating polarity. Our results show that residues in the C-terminus, particularly those at the intersubunit interactions between the C-linker, greatly impact gating polarity. This work suggests that channel opening upon hyperpolarization-dependent activation not only depends on the coupling interface between the pore and voltage-sensing domain but can also be regulated by the cytosolic C-terminus.

## Introduction
The cyclic nucleotide-binding domain (CNBD) family of ion channels belongs to the voltage-gated ion channel (VGIC) superfamily (1,2). They are involved in various critical physiological processes ranging from pacemaking in the heart to photoreception in the retina (3,4,5). These ion channels constitute a diverse family that spans both plants and animals and can be further categorized into four subfamilies: cyclic nucleotide-gated, ether-à-go-go (EAG or KCNH), hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN), and voltage-gated plant K+ (plant Kv)—formerly known as “Plant Shaker” channels (1).
Like other members of the VGIC superfamily, these channels assemble as a tetramer in the cell membrane. Each subunit comprises the voltage-sensing domain (VSD; S1–S4 helices) and the pore domain (S5-P-S6). The characteristic feature of the CNBD family is the cytoplasmic C-terminal domain, which includes a C-linker region and a CNBD (6). Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures show that, despite remarkable differences in their functional response to voltage, their overall structures are remarkably similar (7,8,9,10,11,12,13,14,15). Recent studies have led to a greater understanding of how different structural elements of these channels combine to determine their voltage-dependent gating polarity (16,17,18). For instance, chimeragenesis studies suggest that the gating polarity is determined by three structural elements, the VSD, the pore, and the C-terminal domain (16). The VSD is the principal component and essential for hyperpolarization-dependent gating, whereas the HCN C-terminal and pore domain are the two secondary components. The HCN-VSD needs to be paired with either the HCN C-terminal domain or the pore domain to become primarily hyperpolarization-activated channels. Thus, the emergent view is that the cytosolic C-terminal and pore domains act in concert with HCN-VSD to drive hyperpolarization-dependent opening in wild-type channels (16).
Structural, computational, and functional studies show that the voltage sensor plays a specialized role in hyperpolarization-dependent activation in HCN channels (7,10,19,20,21,22,23,24,25,26). A negative membrane potential causes the S4 helix to move down and bend in the middle with the lower part to a horizontal position, similar to the S4-S5 linker found in domain-swapped channels. Voltage clamp fluorometry and mutagenesis studies have shown that residues that form the S4-S5 interface are critical for determining gating polarity in these ion channels (16,18,27).
Compared to the structural elements in the transmembrane domains, such as the VSD and pore domain, the role of the cytoplasmic C-terminus in gating polarity is less explored. Early studies on HCN channels show that deleting the C-linker does not shift the voltage dependence of channel opening (20,22). However, these studies did not fully capture the effects of channel gating, which requires measurements of absolute open probability. Subsequent studies suggest that the mutations in the C-terminus can prevent channel closure, indicating that these regions may impact the gating polarity of HCN channels (20,28,29). It has also been shown that interactions between the HCN domain, the VSD, and the C-terminus are needed for voltage- and cyclic nucleotide- dependent gating (30,31). Yellen and colleagues showed that crosslinking of residues in the S4-S5 linker with the C-terminal domain of HCN channels can lock the channel either closed or open depending on their position (29). Indeed, crosslinking of F359C on the S4-S5 linker to K482C in the A′ helix of the C-linker in the spHCN (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus HCN homolog) channel completely inverts the voltage dependence, making these channels open upon depolarization rather than hyperpolarization (17). Remarkably, DiFrancesco and colleagues identified a pathological mutant in human patients in the C-linker region that causes an about 40 mV negative shift in the activation range (32).
Despite such a wealth of evidence about the role of the C-terminal domain in gating polarity, we lack details about the molecular elements involved in the gating polarity in HCN channels. Here, we first tested a series of C-terminal domain swaps on the HCN-EAG chimera (HHHEH) background, which can open in response to hyperpolarization and depolarization. The ability of the chimeras to open upon either hyperpolarization or depolarization allows us to assess the relative impact of a perturbation on both forward and reverse activation pathways in the same construct without separate measurements to normalize for channel expression levels. Here, we show that the C-terminal domain’s phylogenetic origin profoundly influences these chimeric channels' gating polarity. We then select HHHEH2, a chimera that is mainly activated by hyperpolarization, for further alanine-scanning mutagenesis study since it has the necessary set of structural elements to stabilize the open pore upon membrane hyperpolarization. Thus, the effects of C-terminal domain mutations on hyperpolarization-dependent opening are not overshadowed by hyperpolarization-dependent opening driven by the HCN pore domain (16). This strategy was used previously to probe the role of a critical serine residue in the HCN S4 segment (33). The alanine-scanning mutagenesis reveals that the residues at the interface of the C-linker and C-terminal domain profoundly affect the gating polarity of this chimeric ion channel. Together, these studies map the role of the C-linker intersubunit interface in determining the gating polarity of these ion channels.

## Materials and methods

## Molecular biology
Chimeragenesis of C-terminal domain-swapped constructs used to generate HHHEH2, HHHES, HHHER, HHHEE, and HHHEA in the pUNIV vector was performed as described previously (16). Construct viewing and designing were done using Snapgene Viewer (v.5.3.2) and DNASTAR Lasergene (v.17). For generation of the HHHEK construct and all alanine-scanning mutagenesis constructs in HHHEH2, Fastcloning (34) was used. Primers were all designed and ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA). Sequences were all verified using Sanger sequencing by Genewiz Azenta Life Sciences (Beijing, China). Accession numbers retrieved from the NIH NCBI database are Mus musculus HCN1 (mHCN1): NCBI: NP_034538.2, Homo sapiens EAG1 (hEAG1): NCBI: NP_758872.1, Homo sapiens EAG related potassium channel 1a (hERG1a): NCBI: NP_000229.1, spHCN: NCBI: NP_999729.1, mHCN2: NCBI: NP_032252.1, Arabidopsis thaliana K+ channel 1 (KAT1): NCBI: NP_199436.1 (optimized for human expression), and AKT1: NCBI: NP_180233.1 (purchased from The Ohio State University Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center, Columbus, OH, USA).

## MSA
Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of the C-terminus and a structural comparison of the channels with cryo-EM structures were used to determine the starting point of the C-terminal domain for each channel for the C-terminal swap mentioned above along with the following additional sequences: human HCN1 (hHCN1): NCBI: NP_066550.2, hHCN2: NCBI: NP_001185.3, and rEAG: NCBI: NP_113930.1. MSA was generated using MUSCLE (v.3.8) (35,36), and coloring of aligned sequences was done using Seaview (v.4.4.2) (37,38).

## Electrophysiology
Capped cRNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription using linearized DNA and the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cRNA was then injected into the cytoplasm of Xenopus laevis oocytes using a Drummond Nanoject II microinjector. The amount of the injected cRNA was 5–45 ng with a volume of 9.2–64.4 nL. Oocytes were then incubated for 24–48 h at 17°C before recording.
Oocytes (Xenopus1, Dexter, MI, USA) were treated with collagenase A from Clostridium histolyticum (lyophilized from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). A two-electrode voltage clamp (TEVC) was used for all electrophysiological recordings in this study. The amplifier used for recordings was Dagan CA-1B, and the Digitizer used was Axon Instruments Digidata 1440A. The output current was low-pass filtered at 5 kHz and sampled at 10 kHz. The recording electrodes were pulled with a Sutter P-97 puller and filled with 3 M KCl with a resistance of 0.5–0.8 MΩ using 4-inch thin-wall glass with filament (1.5 outer diameter/1.12 inner diameter, TW150F-4, WPI). Recordings were performed with an agarose bridge with 3% agarose in 3 M KCl to separate the extracellular solution from the grounding electrode using Clampex (v.10.7) software.
All oocytes were recorded in the following extracellular solution: 100 mM KOH, 5 mM NaOH, 20 mM HEPES, 2 mM CaCl2∗2H2O (pH to 7.4 with methanesulfonic acid). Oocytes were stored in the following storage solution: ND96 with Ca2+ (96 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2∗2H2O, 1 mM MgCl2∗6H2O, 5 mM HEPES), 50 mg/L gentamycin sulfate, 50 mg/L ciprofloxacin, 100 mg/L tetracycline HCl, 10 mL of 100×/1 L (10,000 units P, 10,000 μg S) penicillin-streptomycin, 500 mg/L BSA, 100 mg/L Amikacin hydrate, 275 mg/L sodium pyruvate (pH to 7.4 with 1 M NaOH). Channels were blocked using 2 mM CsCl to confirm the inward current and 50 μM astemizole to confirm the outward current.
Voltage step protocols were selected by setting the tail potentials where the macroscopic current is closest to zero. Details of the exact holding potentials for each construct are described in Tables S1 and S2.

## Analysis of electrophysiological recordings
Peak tail currents were normalized to the maximum current amplitude for each construct to find the normalized tail currents. Normalization was done by taking the macroscopic tail current and dividing it by the maximum tail current value for each oocyte. I/Imax values are then averaged together with error bars representing the standard error using OriginPro (v.2020b 9.7.5.184). For visualization, the normalized tail currents versus voltage plots were fit to a sum of two Boltzmann where V is the voltage, OD is the offset of depolarization activation curve, MD is the maximum depolarization activation curve, D1/2 is the Vhalf of depolarization activation curve, BD is the slope of depolarization activation curve, OH is the offset of hyperpolarization activation curve, MH is the maximum hyperpolarization activation curve, H1/2 is the Vhalf of hyperpolarization activation curve, and BH is the slope of hyperpolarization activation curve to produce the following:I/Imax=OD+(MD−OD)1+eV−D1/2BD+OH+(MH−OH)1+eV−H1/2BH.
Ratio of the normalized tail current at −150 mV to the normalized tail current at 60 mV was used as the gating polarity index described previously (33) to evaluate the contribution of hyperpolarization and depolarization gating in each construct.

Non-bond interactions were generated using the Discovery Studio Viewer. The non-bond interaction contact map was then generated using MATLAB (v.R2022b). The detailed interaction map was drawn using Microsoft Visio (v.Microsoft 365).

## Results

## C-terminal swaps between members of the CNBD family influence gating polarity
As reported previously, the HHHEH chimera (Fig. 1; see Fig. S1 A for nomenclature) exhibits unusual voltage dependence—it is strongly activated by hyperpolarization, but we also observe small outward currents upon depolarization (16). Remarkably, when the C-terminus is deleted from the HHHEH chimera, the truncated channels (HHHEΔC) are depolarization activated (Fig. 1 C). These findings reveal that the HCN C-terminal domain functions as a cytoplasmic gating determinant that favors opening at hyperpolarized potentials and prevents opening at depolarized potentials in the absence of the HCN pore. C-terminal-deleted HCN in the presence of the HCN pore showed that this mutant does not close to the same extent as the wild-type channels when the baseline currents are not normalized (Fig. S2). To further probe the effect of the C-terminal domain on gating polarity, we generated a series of chimeras by swapping the C-terminal region between various members of the CNBD family in the HHHEH background (Fig. 1). Of these, mHCN1, mHCN2, spHCN, and KAT1 are all hyperpolarization-activated. In contrast, hEAG1 and hERG are depolarization-activated. The chimeras HHHEH2 and HHHES, where the C-terminus from mHCN1 is replaced by mHCN2 (H2) or spHCN (S), are primarily hyperpolarization-activated and show minimal depolarization-activated currents. The HHHEE chimera (with the EAG C-terminus) shows depolarization- and hyperpolarization-activated currents. These trends are consistent with the functional properties of the parent constructs. On the other hand, when we swapped the C-terminus of hERG ion channels into the HHHEH background, the resultant chimera (HHHER) was strongly hyperpolarization-activated with minimal depolarization-activated currents even though hERG is a depolarization-activated channel. Even more unexpectedly, the HHHEK chimera (C-terminus from the plant KAT1 channel) is completely depolarization-activated despite KAT1 being hyperpolarization-activated. Subsequent chimeragenesis using another well-characterized hyperpolarization-activated plant Kv channel, AKT1, confirms that complete loss of hyperpolarization activation is not unique to KAT1 (Fig. S3).
Given the structural similarity in the C-terminus of cryo-EM structures in CNBD channels, the above discrepancy in some chimeras is surprising. However, this seems to be related to the percent identity of the amino acid sequences between the CNBD subfamilies, since as the sequence identity between the C-terminus of swapped channels decreases relative to the HCN C-terminus, the preservation of the wild-type function in the HHHE-X chimera decreases. This percent identity matrix of the C-terminal domains of these CNBD channels reveals that the lowest percent identity values are between HCN and plant Kv channels and between HCN and KCNH channels (Fig. S4). This suggests that the chimeras with C-terminal domains from CNBD subfamilies that have lower percent identity values to the HCN subfamily may lack interactions important for stabilizing the open state of the channel at hyperpolarized potentials.
In the closed-pore cAMP-bound structure of the hHCN1 channel (PDB: 5U6P), the residues in the S4-S5 linker regions interact with the C-linker with a helix-turn-helix motif in the cytoplasm that connects the CNBD to the channel lining S6 segment. This linker has an “elbow-on-shoulder” configuration where the A′-B′ helices of one subunit are positioned below the S4-S5 linker and above the C′-D′ helices of its neighboring subunit (44). As a preliminary test of potential key residues, we introduced six mutations from mHCN2 into HHHEK, named HHHEK∗, based on residue differences observed in the MSA to determine if hyperpolarization activation can be restored. However, HHHEK∗ is still mostly depolarization-activated, similar to that in HHHEK (Fig. S5). This result indicates that these six residues are insufficient to make the HHHEK chimera open more above the baseline upon hyperpolarization.

Helical wheel plots in Fig. 3 C reveal that while the number of residues contributing to hyperpolarization activation on the A′ and B′ helices are about equal, the polarity index values of these residues on the B′ helix are larger. This suggests that the B′ helix has a larger contribution to hyperpolarization activation than the A′ helix. Surprisingly, most sites contributing to a loss of hyperpolarization upon mutation are facing away from the S4-S5 linker but toward the bottom side of the A′-B′ helices. For instance, mutants Y459A, L465A, P466A, R470A, K472A, I473A, Y476A, Y477A, and Y481A are primarily depolarization-activated and are located on the bottom face of the B′ helix (Fig. 3, B and C). In the structure of HCN channels, this bottom face of the B′ helix interacts with the neighboring C′-D′ helices, suggesting that the intersubunit interaction at the C-linker level is an essential pathway for maintaining hyperpolarization-dependent activation (Fig. S7; Video S1).
We should point out that although all the I-V recordings were obtained with the identical 1 s voltage pulses, it is clear that, in some cases, the currents were not saturated at the end of the pulse. This was done to circumvent the technical challenges of measuring currents at hyperpolarized and depolarized potentials using TEVC. We frequently observe slowly activating endogenous currents at these potentials, which contaminate our signals. In such cases, more thorough analysis may be needed in a different heterologous system to obtain their true conductance voltage relationships. Furthermore, our data also show that the instantaneous currents in two of the mutants are much larger than the background, suggesting that these channels have difficulty closing at all potentials. Interestingly, these residues are two helical turns away from each other but are adjacent to each other on the helical wheel map (Fig. 3 C).

## Inter- and intrasubunit interactions in the C-linker are essential for gating polarity in chimeric channels
Using the cryo-EM structure of hHCN1 (PDB: 5U6P) and Discovery Studio Visualizer (39), we generated a two-dimensional matrix of all non-bond interactions of residues in the A′-B′ helices. The corresponding contact maps of these intra- and intersubunit interactions are shown in Fig. 4. Residue interactions are marked as gray squares. Of these interactions, residues with a polarity index ≤1 identified from the Ala-scanning mutations in HHHEH2 are marked with yellow dots. These dots indicate residues that are important for maintaining hyperpolarization-dependent activation. Most intrasubunit interactions are in the B′ helix and are primarily hydrophobic (each type of interaction is shown in Fig. S8). These intrasubunit interactions may play critical roles for the A′-B′ helices to maintain their hairpin structure to stabilize the channel opening upon hyperpolarization activation. Notably, the majority of the intersubunit interactions that align with the residues with loss of hyperpolarization-dependent activation upon alanine mutations are between the B′ helix and the neighboring subunit’s C′-D′ linker and D′ helix. ## Discussion
In a prototypical Shaker-type VGIC, the voltage-sensing S4 segment, the S4-S5 linker, and interactions between S4 and S5 segments determine their gating polarity and voltage-dependence of channel activation. Although the N- and C-terminal cytoplasmic regions are involved in inactivation, they play a limited role in voltage-dependent gating. Other members in the VGIC family with large N- and C-terminal domains, such as TRP channels and BK channels, are weakly voltage dependent, but their voltage-dependent activation is profoundly modulated by N- and C-terminal cytoplasmic domains (45,46). For instance, calcium binding to the cytoplasmic RCK domain in BK channels can shift its voltage-dependent activation by as much as 50 mV (47). Similarly, residues in both N- and C-terminal regions have been implicated in temperature-dependent modulation of voltage sensitivity in TRP channel family members. Despite such significant effects on the voltage dependence of gating, these cytoplasmic domains do not influence the gating polarity in any of these ion channel families. They all remain depolarization-activated. This study shows that the residues in the C-linker region are crucial for determining the gating polarity of the CNBD family of ion channels. Substitutions of single amino acids in the A′ and B′ helices of the C-linker region switch the gating polarity of the bipolar HCN-EAG chimera. These high-impact residues are part of the inter- and intra-subunit interfaces between the CNBD domains, suggesting that the conformational changes in this region influence voltage-dependent opening.
One surprising result is that none of the sites on the top of the C-linker facing the S4 and S5 transmembrane helices are involved in maintaining gating polarity. This region of the C-linker is close to the critical intracellular gating interface comprising the S4 and S5 transmembrane helices. The lower part of the S4-S5 helices form a tight interface, and the mutations that presumably disrupt this interface destabilize the closed state. Crosslinking of the K482C (corresponding to K412 in hHCN1 and K454 in mHCN2) residue on the A′ helix in the C-linker to the F359C (F285 in hHCN1 and F327 in mHCN2) residue on the S4-S5 linker causes the spHCN channel to become depolarization-activated (17). Mutagenesis studies by Sanguinetti and colleagues (20,21) also suggest that specific interactions between the C-linker and S4-S5 loop affect channel opening. Note that most of the previous mutagenesis and functional studies were carried out before the structure was known, and, therefore, some of the positions were improperly assigned. Nevertheless, our finding that most of the high-impact residues are facing away from the transmembrane segments suggests that the direct interactions between the C-linker and the residues in the S4-S5 segment are not the primary determinant for hyperpolarization-dependent activation in this chimera. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that such interactions in the wild-type HCN channel still play a role in voltage-dependent gating.
Most of our high-impact residues for hyperpolarization-dependent activation face the CNBD and interact with residues in the neighboring C′ and D′ helices of the C-linker. This finding suggests that the conformational changes and interactions within the CNBD and C-linker tetramerization interface selectively influence hyperpolarization-dependent activation but not depolarization-dependent activation. Thus, we speculate that the interactions stabilizing the open pore conformation at hyperpolarized potentials may be different from those for the open pore at depolarized potentials. Alternatively, the EAG pore in the HCN-EAG chimera intrinsically favors the depolarized open pore conformation, and to drive opening at hyperpolarized potentials requires additional stabilization from the C-terminal region. Cowgill et al. (16) found that substituting HCN residues in the EAG pore domain reduces the depolarization-dependent opening but favors opening at hyperpolarized potentials. Therefore, we expect that, in the wild-type HCN background, the overall contribution of the C-linker and CNBD to hyperpolarization-dependent opening is less than in the HCN-EAG chimera.
Nevertheless, it is becoming evident that mutations in the C-linker region may have profound physiological consequences by altering the voltage-dependent opening of the channel. For instance, an E515K mutation in hHCN2 was found in a patient with sporadic idiopathic generalized epilepsy (32). This mutation, located in the C-linker region, causes a significant negative shift of voltage-dependent activation of the channel and a dramatic slowing of activation kinetics. In the structure of the human HCN1 channel, E446 (corresponding to E515 in hHCN2 and E488 in mHCN2) forms a salt bridge with R438 (R507 in hHCN2 and R480 in mHCN2) in the B′ helix in the C-linker. We have also found that disrupting the E488-R480 interaction by R480A mutation causes a loss of hyperpolarization-dependent activation (Figs. 2 B and 3 A).
While the primary focus of this study was on residues in the A′ and B′ helices of the C-linker region, other residues in the C-terminal domain, particularly those interacting with high-impact residues, may also influence hyperpolarization-dependent opening. In addition, intracellular cAMP concentration is not controlled in TEVC experiments in oocytes, and different CNBD channels have varying cAMP sensitivities. Therefore, the role of cAMP binding to the CNBD domain and its influence on channel gating would need to be further explored. Although our study provides a relative contribution of various residues in the C-linker on hyperpolarization-dependent gating, we have a limited understanding of the overall energetic contribution of the C-terminal domain. Future studies involving allosteric analysis of C-terminal deletion mutants will provide a quantitative estimate of the active contribution of this region to hyperpolarization-dependent channel opening.
