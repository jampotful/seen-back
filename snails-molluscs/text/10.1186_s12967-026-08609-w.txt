Effectiveness of a heparin-like glycosaminoglycan from snail mucus in a murine model of dry eye

# Effectiveness of a heparin-like glycosaminoglycan from snail mucus in a murine model of dry eye

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundDry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial ocular surface disorder characterized by tear film instability, inflammation, and epithelial injury, leading to discomfort and visual disturbance. Current treatments remain limited in efficacy and safety. A heparin-like glycosaminoglycan purified from the snail mucus of Achatina fulica (AFG) exhibits potential regenerative and anti-inflammatory properties. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effectiveness and mechanism of AFG in DED.MethodsThe effects of AFG on human corneal epithelial (HCE) cells were assessed by cell viability, proliferation, migration, and qRT–PCR analyses of proliferation- and mucin-related genes. In vivo, a benzalkonium chloride (BAC)-induced murine model of DED was used to evaluate corneal integrity, tear production, histological changes, inflammatory infiltration, and molecular expression following topical AFG treatment. Gene and protein expression levels were examined using qRT–PCR, western blotting, and immunofluorescence.ResultsAFG showed no cytotoxicity toward HCE cells and significantly enhanced their proliferation and migration by upregulating K14, KI67, MUC1, and MUC4. In the DED mouse model, topical AFG treatment markedly improved corneal integrity, increased tear secretion, and restored epithelial structure. AFG upregulated the expression of epithelial differentiation and repair markers (K12, K14, PAX6) while downregulating inflammatory genes (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, MMP-9, CD45) and reducing macrophage infiltration. These effects collectively promoted corneal epithelial regeneration and tear film stabilization.ConclusionsAFG alleviates dry eye disease by enhancing corneal epithelial proliferation, promoting mucin secretion, and suppressing inflammation. Its heparin-like glycosaminoglycan structure enables biocompatible and multifunctional activity, highlighting AFG as a promising candidate for topical therapy in corneal repair and dry eye management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08609-w.Keywords: Dry eye disease, Snail glycosaminoglycan, Corneal regeneration, Tear film

## Introduction
Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial ocular surface disorder characterized by disrupted tear film homeostasis and is often accompanied by symptoms such as discomfort, visual disturbances, redness, secretion, and eye fatigue [1]. The development of DED involves multiple key elements, such as increased osmolarity, inflammatory responses and ocular surface injury, as well as disruptions in neurosensory function [1]. In the current information age, DED causes an increasing health burden, and the global prevalence of DED is estimated to range from 5 to 50% [2]. According to the TFOS DEWS II classification, DED is divided into four subtypes: aqueous tear deficiency, reduced wettability, increased evaporation, and mixed type [1, 3].
In DED patients, tear film instability compromises the primary protective barrier of the ocular surface epithelium, disrupting the corneal epithelial microenvironment, which can further result in epithelial damage and exfoliation and progression to keratitis or corneal ulcers in severe cases. Therefore, restoring the homeostasis of the corneal epithelial microenvironment and promoting epithelial regeneration are critical for DED treatment. Current treatments for DED include gland transplantation, stem cell-based regeneration therapy, mucin secretion stimulators, pharmacological interventions (e.g., sodium hyaluronate eye drops), and assistive tools (e.g., scleral lenses) [4–6]. Among these, topical drug administration is cost effective and convenient; thus, it is usually suitable for DED treatment. However, poor tear film integrity may increase the susceptibility to ocular toxicity with long-term use [5]. Therefore, an ideal topical drug should be both safe and effective.
Complex compounds derived from animals have become an important source for drug development and innovation in ocular diseases [7, 8]. Recent studies have also shown that a snail mucus gel (SMG) extract from Achatina fulica not only promotes epithelial regeneration, cell migration, and proliferation but also has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and moisturizing properties [9, 10]. As the main bioactive component of SMG [11], A. fulica glycosaminoglycan (AFG) may potentially relieve DED. Given that the extract of SMG has multiple components, quality control during preparation is more challenging; in contrast, the pure polysaccharide AFG may be a better choice as a potential medicine [12, 13]. The structure of AFG is similar to that of heparin and heparan sulfate, which are composed of iduronic acid (IdoA) and N-acetyl-glucosamine (GlcNAc) with a repeating sequence of →4)-α-GlcNAc (1→4)-α-IdoA2S (1→(Fig. 5) [14] but without anticoagulant activity. The high density of negatively charged carboxyl and sulfate groups in glycosaminoglycan chains is believed to be crucial for maintaining tissue hydration [13]. For instance, one of the glycosaminoglycans, hyaluronic acid, can retain water molecules remarkably well, absorbing up to 1,000 times its molecular weight [15]. Sulfated glycosaminoglycans possessing moisturizing properties should be beneficial for DED.

Here, a purified snail glycosaminoglycan, AFG, was obtained from the snail mucus gel (SMG) of A. fulica, and its effect and mechanism of action on DED were explored. The results provide a theoretical basis and experimental evidence to support innovative treatment approaches for DED.

## Materials and methods

## Materials
AFG was isolated from the salivary gland of A. fulica, as previously reported [9]. The weight-average molecular weight (Mw) and number-average molecular weight (Mn) of the polysaccharide AFG were determined to be 92.7 kDa and 45.7 kDa, respectively. K12 (24789-1-AP), K14 (82824-1-AP) and PAX6 (12323-1-AP) antibodies were from Proteintech (China), CD45 (NB100-77417) antibody was from Novus (USA), and F4/80 (HA721520) and GAPDH (ET1601-4) antibodies were from Huabio (China). CoraL­ite® plus 488-goat anti-rabbit antibody (H + L) (SA00001-2) was from Proteintech (China). Multi-rAb® HRP-Goat Anti-Rabbit Recombinant Secondary Antibody (H+L) (RGAR001) was from Affinity (China). Benzalkonium chloride (BAC) was from Macklin (China). Fluorescein sodium was from Alcon (UK). The BCA protein assay kit was from Epizyme (China). The universal RNA purification kit, TRIzol reagent, color reverse transcription kit (with gDNA remover), and 2×color SYBR green qPCR master mix (Rox1 Plus) were from EZBioscience (USA). The hema­toxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (G1120) and RIPA lysis buffer (R0010) were from Solarbio (China). All other chemical reagents were obtained commercially and had high purity, and the dilution rates and concentrations of the antibodies are provided in Table S2.

## Cell viability assay
The effect of AFG on cell viability was studied using human corneal epithelial (HCE) cells, and a CCK-8 kit was used to determine the number of living cells. After cell counting, the HCE cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1000 cells per well) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, the HCE cells were treated with varying concentrations of AFG (0, 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 µg/mL) for 24 h. CCK8 reagent (10 µL) was added to each well, and after they were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was detected by a microplate reader (SynergyH4; Gene Company Limited, China).

## Scratch-wound healing assay
HCE cells were cultured in six-well plates until they reached 95% confluency. A sterile 200 µL pipette tip was then used to create a scratch in the center of each well. After the scratched cells were washed away with PBS, fresh culture medium or medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL AFG was added. Scratch healing was observed at 0, 12, 24, and 36 h using an inverted microscope (DMILLED, Leica, Germany).

## Animals
C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks, 20–30 g) were purchased from the Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Center (Shanghai, China). The mice were housed in an animal room with a regulated 12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water.

## Mouse dry eye model and therapeutic intervention
To create a dry eye model, we applied 0.2% benzalko­nium chloride (BAC) to the mice as eye drops for 7 days twice a day (9:00 AM and 9:00 PM). For each mouse, the right eye received BAC treatment, while the left eye was treated with PBS as a normal control (Normal group). The BAC-treated eyes were subsequently divided randomly into four groups (n = 10 each): the untreated group (Control Group), the PBS-treated group (PBS Group), and the 100 μg/mL sodium hyaluronate (SH)-treated group (SH Group) and the 200 μg/mL AFG-treated group (AFG Group). Eye drops were administered for 7 days four times a day. According to the experimental design, a subset of eyes from each group was randomly assigned to specific downstream assays, as certain analytical procedures were mutually exclusive and could confound subsequent evaluations. For instance, tear production measurements and fluorescein infiltration evaluations were conducted on separate subsets of eyes to avoid technical interference between protocols.

## Fluorescein infiltration of the cornea
Fluorescein sodium staining was performed on days 0, 3, 5, and 7 after modeling. After the mice were anesthetized with 1.25% avertin (15 mL/kg), 5 µL of 0.25% fluorescein sodium solution was applied to the corneal surface, which was carefully spread by manual blinking, after which the excess fluorescein was rinsed off with saline. Corneal fluorescence was photographed and scored under a microscope (YZ20T4, 66 Vision-Tech, China). The fluorescein infiltration pattern of each eye was scored according to the method of Murakami et al., in which the cornea was divided into three zones (superior, central, and inferior), each scoring 0–3 on the basis of the fluorescence density (total score range of 0–9) [16, 17].

## Tear production measurement
Tear production was detected on days 0, 3, 5, and 7 after modeling by the Shirmer I assay using phenol red threads (Zone-Quick; Yokota, Tokyo, Japan). After the mice were anesthetized, the threads were placed in the lower conjunctival fornix for 15 s. The length (mm) of the red portion was measured, which indicated the amount of tear secretion [18, 19].

## Eye and cornea collection
After 7 days of treatment, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anesthesia by an overdose of 1.25% avertin. Some of the eyes were harvested for histological analysis, and after collection, the eyes were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for paraffin embedding. Some of the eyes were dissected under a microscope to obtain corneas, and the samples were kept at − 80 °C for subsequent PCR and WB analysis.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
Fixed eyeball tissues were cut into 4 μm-thick paraffin slices. These sections were deparaffinized, followed by staining with hematoxylin for 5 min and counterstaining with eosin for another 5 min. After dehydration, the sections were sealed. For each section, 3 high-power fields, encompassing both central and peripheral corneal regions, were randomly chosen and examined under a microscope (ECLIPSE E100, Nikon, Japan) to evaluate the epithelial structure.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from HCE cells that were treated with AFG (50 µg/mL), from the control group at 48 h, or from the corneal tissue of mice after dry eye modeling and treatment using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration was quantified using a microspectrophotometer. The RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qRT‒PCR was subsequently performed using the following protocol: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The 2-∆∆Ct method was employed to quantify relative mRNA expression levels in the corneal tissue. The sequences of the gene-specific primers are provided in Table S1.

## Western blot (WB)
Corneal tissues were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The samples were homogenized, placed on ice for 30 min, and subsequently centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein were loaded for SDS‒PAGE, transferred to membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk for 1.5 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S2) at 4 °C overnight and then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The protein bands were detected using a gel imaging system (ChemiCaptureFlatMini.lnk 1.0.0.0), and densitometry analysis was performed using ImageJ (Version: 1.53t, Java 1.0_345) software.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) staining
Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer. They were then blocked with BSA for 1 h. The sections were treated with primary antibodies (Table S2), left overnight at 4 °C, and then exposed to secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and washed, the sections were mounted and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis and graphical representation were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.2 (San Diego, CA) and ImageJ (Version: 1.53t, Java 1.8.0_345). The data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test was employed, while unpaired t tests were used for comparisons between two groups. A P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results

## AFG enhanced the proliferation and function of HCE cells
First, the effect of AFG on the viability of HCE cells was studied. After 24 h of incubation, AFG at concentrations up to 1000 µg/ml did not decrease the number of living HCE cells, indicating that no cytotoxicity occurred. Additionally, 50 μg/ml AFG slightly promoted the pro­liferation of HCE cells (P < 0.05 vs. control, Fig. 1A). Next, the results of the scratch-wound assay revealed that AFG significantly promoted the proliferation and migration of HCE cells (P < 0.05 vs. control) (Fig. 1B, D). Additionally, the effects of AFG on the proliferation and mucous secretion function of HCE cells were studied by detecting the proliferation gene marker KI67 [20], the stem cell marker K14 [21], and the genes encoding ocular surface mucins (MUC1 and MUC4) [22]. AFG at 50 µg/mL significantly upregulated the mRNA expressions of KI67, K14, MUC1, and MUC4 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). These findings indicate that AFG could increase the proliferation, mucin secretion, and migration capacity of HCE cells without causing significant cytotoxicity.

Mucin is a key component of the tear film and is vital for lubrication, tear film stability, and viscosity [23]. Additionally, mucin facilitates the spread of the tear film over the corneal epithelium by regulating surface tension [24]. In DED patients, mucin is often reduced on the ocular surface. Since AFG increases mucin expression, it may be beneficial for relieving DED symptoms.

## AFG improves symptoms of dry eye disease in mice
The activity and number of corneal epithelial cells are essential for eye function, whose homeostasis is disrupted in dry eye disease. Therefore, the in vivo effect of AFG was further studied using a mouse dry eye model induced by benzalkonium chloride (BAC). BAC is the most widely used preservative in eye drops, and it is used as a fluorescein dye for diagnosis because it penetrates poorly into normal corneal epithelium. When used frequently at high concentrations over extended periods, BAC can cause tear film instability, ocular surface inflammation, corneal epithelial apoptosis, goblet cell loss, and squamous metaplasia, resulting in symptoms and histopathological changes that resemble those of human DED.
After modeling successfully (Fig. S1), the dry eyes of the mice were treated with eye drops of PBS, 200 µg/mL AFG (in PBS), or no treatment (control) (Fig. 2A). On days 3, 5 and 7, AFG significantly decreased the fluorescein staining score compared to the control (P < 0.05), with a stronger effect with duration of treatment. On day 7, the control group still scored high (about 7), while the score was less than 2 for the AFG group, indicating that cornea integrity was improved with AFG treatment (Fig. 2B, C). After 5 days of treatment, AFG also significantly increased tear secretion volumes compared to the control (P < 0.05). On day 7, the tear volume of the AFG group was approximately 4-fold that of the control group (Fig. 2D). The efficacy of AFG in improving both cornea integrity and tear secretion increased with number of doses, and on day 7, AFG-treated eyes were similar to the normal eyes (Fig. S3). In contrast, the PBS-treated group showed no significant difference compared to the control (P > 0.05). In addition, supplementary safety evaluations, including slit-lamp examination with fluorescein sodium staining and H&E staining of the cornea, further demonstrated the safety of AFG on the normal mouse cornea (Fig. S2). These findings demonstrate that AFG effectively alleviates dry eye symptoms in mice.

## AFG promotes corneal epithelial cell proliferation and mucin secretion
To study the mechanism through which AFG improves dry eye symptoms, corneal tissue samples from the dry eye mice were collected on day 7 for further analysis. H&E staining of the corneal sections revealed that compared with the control and PBS groups, the AFG group had more epithelial layers in both the central and limbal areas and maintained normal cellular morphology (Fig. 3A, B). To further investigate the regulatory mechanism of AFG on corneal epithelial cells, we studied its effects on the expressions of several key genes, including the terminal differentiation marker K12 [25], the squamous metaplasia marker Sprr1b [26], the proliferation-related markers Abcg2 [27, 28] and Ki67 [20], and Muc1 and Muc4, which are associated with ocular surface mucins [22]. Compared with the control treatment, the AFG treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expressions of K12, Abcg2, Ki67, Muc1, and Muc4 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C), indicating that the AFG treatment regulated differentiation and promoted the proliferation and mucin secretion of corneal epithelial cells. Additionally, AFG reduced Sprr1b expression, indicating that it is able to inhibit squamous metaplasia, which might prevent abnormal repair and scarring. These regulatory effects of AFG are beneficial for corneal repair after dry eye injury.

Next, the levels of several key proteins, including PAX6, which is important for maintaining corneal epithelial cell identity [29, 30], and K12 and K14, which are related to different degrees of differentiation [21], were detected by western blot analysis. K12 and K14 are expressed mainly in the superficial and basal layers of the corneal epithelium, respectively. Compared with the control group, the AFG group presented significantly elevated protein levels of K12, PAX6, and K14 (P < 0.05), and no significant differences were observed between the PBS group and the control group (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3D, E). The results indicated that AFG promoted corneal limbal stem cell proliferation and epithelial regeneration.
Taken together, these findings suggest that AFG promotes corneal epithelial cell proliferation, normal differentiation, and mucin secretion, thus accelerating corneal repair in dry eye.

## AFG reduces inflammatory responses in a dry eye model
Inflammation is another key driver of DED pathogenesis and is a hallmark symptom of DED. Initial damage leading to tear film instability may trigger a nonspecific innate immune response, resulting in chronic ocular surface inflammation. The effects of AFG on the expression of inflammation-associated genes, including Cd45 (a neutrophil marker), Mmp-9 (an inflammatory biomarker elevated in dry eye), and proinflammatory cytokines such as Il-1β, Tnf-α and Il-6 were studied [31–35]. qRT‒PCR analysis revealed that AFG significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of Mmp-9, Il-1β, Tnf-α, Il-6, and Cd45 (P < 0.05), with no significant differences between the PBS and control groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4C), indicating the potent anti-inflammatory effects of AFG. Immunofluorescence staining of corneal tissues further confirmed that compared with the PBS and control groups, the AFG group had significantly fewer CD45- and F4/80-positive [36] (a macrophage marker) cells, indicating a decrease in the infiltration of inflammatory cells, particularly macrophages (Fig. 4A, B). These findings suggest that AFG effectively reduces inflammation in dry eye models.

Overall, our findings showed that AFG promotes corneal epithelial repair and alleviates dry eye symptoms. The therapeutic effects are mediated primarily through the enhancement of corneal epithelial cell proliferation, promotion of mucin secretion, and suppression of inflammation, ultimately improving dry eye conditions (Fig. 5).

## Discussion
DED is a common disease caused by tear film instability, increased osmolarity, inflammation and ocular surface injury, as well as neurosensory irregularities [37]. These factors interact in a complex, dynamic manner, leading to dysfunction of the ocular surface and its adnexa [38, 39]. The regeneration of the corneal epithelium is a multifaceted process that involves cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and remodeling of the extracellular matrix and largely relies on limbal stem cells [40].
Snail mucus is composed mainly of polysaccharides (glycosaminoglycan and neutral polysaccharide), glycoproteins (mucin and lectin), antibacterial peptides, allantoin, and glycolic acid. Snail mucus can enhance cell migration and proliferation [10]. In addition, glycosaminoglycan can accelerate the healing of venous skin ulcers by enhancing re-epithelialization and granulation processes in adult epidermal keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts [41]. As the main active component of Achatina fulica snail mucus, AFG is biocompatible and biodegradable, and it promotes healing by enhancing epithelial regeneration [9]. In addition to collagen fibers, glycosaminoglycan is a major structural component of the extracellular matrix, with hyaluronic acid being among its key components [42]. It has been reported that hyaluronic acid extracts are safe and effective for repairing the skin epithelium [43]. Moreover, there are types of commoditized eye drops based on a glycosaminoglycan, named sodium hyaluronate (SH), which is also a natural polysaccharide with high adhesion and water retention properties [44, 45]. Several previous studies have verified that SH can promote corneal epithelial cell migration and proliferation [46, 47].
In this work, the effects of AFG from snail mucus on corneal epithelial cells, as well as on dry eyes in vivo, were investigated, and the underlying mechanisms were explored. AFG promoted HCE cell proliferation and migration but did not have a cytotoxic effect. The expression levels of the proliferation-related gene Ki67 [48] and the stemness-related gene K14 [49] were elevated in the AFG-treated group, indicating that AFG plays a vital role in promoting HCE cell proliferation. In a mouse dry eye model, H&E staining of corneal tissue sections revealed that AFG accelerated corneal epithelial repair by increasing the number of epithelial layers and improving morphology. The classic anticoagulant heparin also has proliferation and pro-healing activities in epithelial tissues, but its application may be accompanied by bleeding risk [50]. The mechanism of heparin may be linked to the increase in heparin-binding growth factors, such as epidermal growth factor [50–53]. Heparin-binding epidermal growth factor can promote the proliferation of corneal epithelial cells [54, 55]. This may help explain the ability of AFG, a heparin-like glycosaminoglycan, to promote cell proliferation. Moreover, we found that the effects of AFG are similar to those of SH on dry eye via accelerated corneal epithelial repair (Fig. S3).
Consistent with the in vitro results, AFG also increased the mRNA expressions of Muc1 and Muc4 in dry eyes in mice, highlighting its ability to improve tear film homeostasis through enhanced mucin secretion. Interestingly, these findings may differ slightly from the classic regulatory effects of heparin on mucins in other tissues. For example, in bronchial epithelial cells, exogenous heparin inhibits the expression of Muc5ac [56, 57]. Muc5ac is a secretory mucin that is involved primarily in signal transduction and functions differently from membrane-bound mucins such as Muc1 and Muc4 [58].
Macrophages play a central role in the pathogenesis of many inflammatory diseases. They are highly heterogeneous; under the regulation of the surrounding microenvironment, they can be polarized into M1 (proinflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) types [59]. The balance between M1 and M2 macrophage polarization is critical for controlling inflammation and tissue injury [60]. Previous research has shown that AFG can effectively promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thus reducing inflammatory responses [9]. In chronic inflammatory DED, the acute phase is followed by a prolonged adaptive immune response, exacerbating and perpetuating inflammation and creating a vicious cycle [61]. Sulfated glycosaminoglycans can bind to positively charged amino acids of inflammatory cytokines or chemokines [62], therefore suppressing inflammation [63]. For instance, heparin also possesses anti-inflammatory activity, but its application is limited because of bleeding risk. In this study, AFG significantly reduced the infiltration of inflammatory cells and the expression level of inflammatory factors, and effective anti-inflammatory treatment is essential to end the vicious cycle of inflammation. Recently, it was reported that a heparin-containing hydrogel inhibited the levels of inflammatory biomarkers, such as IL-1 and TGF-β, in injured HCE. Whether AFG-containing hydrogels are more effective at treating DED is worthy of further study.
In summary, AFG promotes corneal epithelial proliferation, regeneration, mucin secretion, and hydration and has anti-inflammatory effects in dry eye disease (DED). It enhances tear film homeostasis, restores the corneal epithelial microenvironment, and alleviates dry eye symptoms. This study is the first to explore the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of AFG in DED, offering new experimental insights and establishing its potential as a multifunctional therapeutic agent for clinical DED treatment. However, this study is limited by its reliance on the BAC-induced dry eye model. Further validation in additional animal models is necessary to investigate its broader functions, potential side effects, and clinical applicability.

Effect of AFG on the proliferation of HCE cells in the scratch assay. C. Effects of AFG on the expressions of KI67, K14, MUC1, and MUC4 mRNA in HCE cells. D. Effect of AFG on the migration of HCE cells in the scratch assay; representative images are shown. Data are presented as the means ± SD; n = 6 (for A) or 3 (for B, C and D); *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ****P < 0.0001 vs. control; one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test (for A), or unpaired t test (for B–C)

## Figure Fig. 2: Effects of AFG on the healing of dry eye in mice. A. Timeline of BAC-induced dry eye mouse model establishment and treatment. B. Representative photographs of mouse eyes with fluorescein sodium staining during the treatment process. C. Effect of AFG on corneal fluorescein sodium staining after different treatment periods. D. Effect of AFG on tear secretion in mice with dry eyes. The data are presented as the mean ± SD; n = 3 (for B and C) or 4 (for D); **P < 0.01 and ****P < 0.0001 vs. control; one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test

## Figure Fig. 3: Effects of AFG on the proliferation and function of epithelial cells in dry eyes. A. Representative images of H&E-stained corneas from dry eyes after 7 days of treatment. B. Effect of AFG on the regeneration of epithelial cells in the central cornea and corneal limbus of dry eyes. C. Effects of AFG on the mRNA expression levels of Abcg2, Ki67, K12, Sprr1b, Muc1, and Muc4 in corneal tissues measured by qRT‒PCR. D. Representative western blot images of K14, PAX6, and K12 proteins from corneal tissues with or without AFG treatment. E. Statistical analysis of the protein expression levels of K14, PAX6, and K12 by western blot. The data are presented as the means ± SD; n = 3; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001 vs. control; one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test
