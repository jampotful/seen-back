Lock-equipped six-helix DNA bundle-mediated siSTING delivery ameliorates Alzheimer's disease via cGAS-STING inhibition.

# Lock-equipped six-helix DNA bundle-mediated siSTING delivery ameliorates Alzheimer’s disease via cGAS-STING inhibition

## Abstract
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathological progression is intimately linked to microglial inflammatory activation mediated by the cGAS-STING pathway. Small interfering RNA targeting STING (siSTING) is a precise therapeutic to silence STING and interfere with this pathway, but its clinical translation is severely limited by serum nuclease degradation, inadequate blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, and suboptimal targeting. We constructed a lock-equipped six-helix DNA bundle for siSTING delivery. Prior to reaching AD lesions, siSTING was efficiently sequestered within the DNA bundle, ensuring its structural integrity; upon arrival at the lesion site, the carrier undergoes a conformational change in response to the high expression of CD64, thereby triggering the controlled release of encapsulated siSTING. Functional assessments showed this system significantly suppressed cGAS-STING activation, reduced cerebral Aβ plaque deposition, and promoted microglial polarization from M1 (proinflammatory) to M2 (anti-inflammatory). These effects ameliorated memory and learning deficits in AD model mice via enhanced neuroprotection, offering a novel platform for precise targeted therapy of AD.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04173-z.

## Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common neurodegenerative disorder, imposes a pressing public health burden amid global aging. With over 50 million people worldwide affected by AD or dementia and substantial medical costs [1, 2], current therapies only alleviate Aβ deposition, failing to arrest progression driven by microglial inflammation [3]. Targeting the core pathological mechanisms of AD is therefore an urgent unmet need. Mounting evidence identifies cGAS-STING-mediated microglial inflammation as a key driver: under Aβ stimuli, microglia become abnormally activated, releasing proinflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α) that exacerbate neuronal damage [4, 5]. The cGAS-STING pathway acts as a critical switch, wherein Aβ triggers STING activation and age-related mitochondrial DNA leakage sustains this ‘inflammation-damage’ cycle [6]. Previous studies confirm that inhibiting this pathway reduces Aβ deposition, improves microglial polarization, and enhances cognition [7], validating it as a pivotal therapeutic target.
Small interfering RNA targeting STING (siSTING) enables precise, low-off-target silencing of STING, outperforming traditional small-molecule inhibitors which lack blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration and specificity [8, 9]. However, the clinical translation of siSTING is hindered by serum nuclease degradation, poor BBB penetration, and suboptimal targeting [10], thus necessitating a specialized delivery vector.
DNA molecular machines offer a programmable strategy for delivering bioactive molecules by enabling stimulus-responsive protection and controlled intracellular release [11–13], features that are particularly advantageous for the delivery of fragile nucleic acids such as siRNA and protein [14–16]. In principle, such dynamic systems can align cargo activation with specific cellular states or pathological microenvironments, thereby improving therapeutic precision. In addition, these DNA molecular machines exhibit superior biocompatibility, as they are composed of natural deoxyribonucleotides whose degradation products are readily metabolized by the human body [17, 18], thereby reducing immunogenicity and toxicity risks. To date, DNA-based molecular machines have been primarily explored in oncology, where tumor-associated biomarkers or enzymes are exploited to trigger site-specific drug or gene release [19, 20]. However, their application in neurodegenerative diseases, especially for modulating neuroinflammation and innate immune signaling, remains largely unexplored. In the context of Alzheimer’s disease, where pathological progression is tightly linked to microglial activation states [21, 22], there is a critical unmet need for delivery systems that can selectively respond to inflammatory microglia and regulate intracellular signaling pathways.
In this context, we constructed a ‘lock-equipped six-helix DNA bundle functionalized with ANG peptide’ (L6HB-ANG), capable of sensing microglia-specific inflammatory markers, could provide a rational strategy for precise siRNA delivery to modulate the cGAS–STING pathway in Alzheimer’s disease. This carrier stably encapsulates siSTING during circulation, preventing nuclease degradation while facilitating BBB penetration. Upon reaching AD lesions, it undergoes a conformational change in response to highly expressed CD64, enabling precise release of siSTING. In the 5×FAD mouse model, this system efficiently crossed the BBB, accumulated specifically in inflammatory microglia, significantly inhibited cGAS-STING pathway activation, reduced Aβ deposition, and induced microglial phenotype transition from proinflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2, which ultimately improved learning and memory abilities in mice by enhancing synaptic plasticity. This system successfully overcomes the delivery bottlenecks of siSTING in AD therapy, providing a novel nano material-based platform for precise AD treatment (Fig. 1).

## Materials and methods

## Materials
All chemicals and solvents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used without additional purification. DNA strands supplied by Sangon Biotech underwent purification via high-performance liquid chromatography. Table S1 presents the sequences. FITC-Aβ and Aβ were obtained from ChinaPeptides Co., Ltd. Other reagents were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.
The BV2 cell line and primary microglia were generously provided by the School of Basic Medical Sciences, Southern Medical University. Cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. All culture reagents were obtained from Gibco (USA).
C57BL/6 and 5×FAD mice were purchased from Shulaibao (Wuhan) Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained in the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University. Animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee (LAEC‑2024‑174).

## Preparation of lock equipped six helix bundle (L6HB)
A one-step self-assembly strategy was employed to construct L6HB. DNA strands (L6HB‑1 to L6HB‑20, Table S1) and siSTING were combined to a final concentration of 1 µM in TAE‑Mg buffer (40 mM Tris, 20 mM acetic acid, 2 mM EDTA, and 50 mM magnesium acetate; pH 8.0). The solution was gradually cooled from 95 °C to ambient temperature overnight to allow hybridization. For the preparation of ANG‑functionalized L6HB (L6HB‑ANG), ANG and L6HB were combined at a molar ratio of 20:1, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. The distinct L6HB modifications utilized in this work are summarized in Table S2.

## AFM
An L6HB sample (10–20 nM, 8 µL in synthesis buffer) was deposited onto an aminopropyltriethoxysilane‑modified mica surface and left to adsorb for 5 min. AFM imaging was subsequently performed in liquid using a Fluid+ (Veeco) probe on a Bruker system.

## FRET analysis in vitro
A Cy3/Cy5 FRET pair was modified into L6HB structure as indicated (Table S2). To unlock the L6HB structure, a fourfold molar excess of CD64 protein was introduced and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. FRET was analyzed under excitation at 561 nm, while emission was recorded in the range of 600–750 nm.

## Gel electrophoresis
A 6% Native PAGE was used to characterize the formation of L6HB and L6HB-ANG. Electrophoresis was conducted in TAE‑Mg buffer on a Bio‑Rad system at 100 V for 1 h at room temperature, followed by GelRed (Biotium, USA) staining of the gels.
Naked siSTING and L6HB-ANG (siSTING labeled with Cy7) were incubated at 37 °C for 1, 6, 12, and 24 h in the presence of 10% fetal bovine serum or RNase A (2 U/mL). RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen, USA) was then added and incubated for 10 min to terminate the enzymatic degradation reaction. Finally, siSTING degradation was characterized using 6% PAGE and quantified using Image J.

## FRET analysis on the cell surface
Primary microglial cells were co-incubated with 5 µM Aβ for 4 h to induce high CD64 expression. L6HB bearing a Cy3/Cy5 FRET pair (100 nM, 5 µL) was separately added to primary microglial cells, CD64‑high primary microglial cells, and BV2 cells. The cells were then incubated for 10, 20, 40, 60, and 120 min, respectively. After incubation, cells were triple‑washed with PBS, followed by FRET imaging with a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan). The donor/acceptor fluorescence intensity ratio was analyzed with NIS‑Elements AR software (Nikon, Japan).

## In vitro BBB-penetrating efficiency detection
The in vitro BBB model was established following a previously described protocol [16]. When the TEER reached 150–200 Ω·cm2, Cy3‑labeled L6HB and L6HB‑ANG (200 nM, 20 µL) were placed in the upper chamber (Corning, USA) for 60, 120, 240–360 min. DAPI (CST, USA) was used to stain the nuclei. Cy3 fluorescence in the lower chamber was visualized using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan) and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA) with data processing in FlowJo (USA).

## In vivo BBB-penetrating efficiency detection
Mice received intravenous injections of Cy5-labeled L6HB or L6HB-ANG. At 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h, Cy5 fluorescence intensity in the brain regions was monitored using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system. One hour after injection, the mice were euthanized, and the brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested and imaged as described above. The fluorescence intensity and the distribution of Cy5 fluorescence (Ex = 649 nm, Em = 666 nm) were quantitatively analyzed using Living Image software (PerkinElmer, USA).
Fresh brain samples were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. After dehydration, the tissues were embedded in paraffin, cut into 4 μm sections, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. DAPI and IBA1 double staining was performed, and Cy5 fluorescence (Ex = 640 nm, Em = 662–737 nm) was visualized using confocal microscopy.

## Endosomal escape
BV2 cells were co-incubated with Cy7-labeled L6HB-ANG for 1 and 6 h. LysoTracker Green (Beyotime, China) and Hoechst 33258 were used to visualize lysosomes and nuclei, respectively. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan), and Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated using Image J.

## Knockdown efficiency of siSTING
BV2 cells were treated with PBS, free siSTING, siSTING encapsulated in Lipofectamine 3000, and L6HB-ANG. All groups received siSTING at a concentration of 50 nM. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for qRT-PCR analysis of knockdown efficiency.

## Protein and RNA detection
Primary microglia and BV2 cells were lysed on ice using Protein or Whole Cell Lysis Kits (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). Protein content was quantified with a BCA Assay Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). Equal protein samples were combined with 5× loading buffer (Fdbio Science, China), subjected to SDS‑PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were exposed to primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and then treated with HRP‑linked secondary antibodies (CST, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit and captured with a Gel Doc XR+ Documentation System (Bio Rad, USA). Densitometric quantification was conducted using Image Lab software (Bio‑Rad, USA), normalizing to β‑actin or GAPDH. All antibodies used are listed in Table S3.
Total RNA from BV2 cells was isolated with the SteadyPure Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, China). cDNA was generated using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan) on an ABI StepOnePlus system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Expression levels were normalized to ACTB, and relative quantification was determined by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method from triplicate experiments. Primer sequences are provided in Table S4.

## Flow cytometry
PBS, siSTING, L6HB, or L6HB‑ANG (200 nM, 20 µL) was added to the upper chamber in an in vitro BBB model (Corning, USA). After 24 h, the upper chamber was taken out, and BV2 cells were treated with 50 µM FITC‑Aβ for 6 h, collected, and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo (USA).

## Behavior tests
For the Morris water maze test, 5×FAD mice (4‑month‑old, male) were randomly divided into three groups (n = 8) and intravenously administered PBS, naked siSTING (400 nM, 200 µL), or L6HB-ANG (400 nM, 200 µL). Age‑matched wild‑type C57BL/6 mice (n = 8) were used as the control group and received 200 µL PBS only. Treatment consisted of 12 injections delivered every five days. After 60 days of treatment, the MWM video analysis system was used for training and testing. The test included five consecutive days of training to guide the mice to locate a hidden platform submerged below the water surface. On day 6, the probe trial was conducted with the platform removed. The monitoring system recorded the time when each mouse entered the water, tracked its swimming trajectory and escape latency, and further evaluated spatial memory and learning performance.
For the Novel Object Recognition test, mice were acclimated to the test chamber for 2 days before the experiment. During the familiarization phase, two similar objects were positioned at opposite corners of the chamber, and the mice were allowed to explore for 5 min. Exploration time for each object was recorded. During the testing phase, short‑term memory was evaluated 30 min after familiarization by substituting one of the familiar objects with a novel one, and the mice were permitted to explore for an additional 5 min. The exploration times for the novel object (N, s) and the familiar object (F, s) were recorded, and the discrimination index was calculated as (N − F)/(N + F).
For the Y‑maze test, mice were positioned in the start arm of the apparatus, and arm entries were recorded over a 5‑min period. An alternation was defined as consecutive entries into all three arms without repetition. The following parameters were analyzed: time spent in each arm; the percentage of time spent in the novel arm (novel arm time/total time ×100%); and the spontaneous alternation rate (number of alternations/[total number of entries −2] × 100%).

## Biosafety investigation
The biosafety of L6HB‑ANG was assessed through hematoxylin and eosin staining, hematological and serum biochemical analyses, and a hemolysis assay. For hematoxylin and eosin staining, the brain, liver, lung, spleen, heart, and kidney were collected on day 60 after intravenous injection of L6HB‑ANG (400 nM, 200 µL). For the hemolysis assay, red blood cells were collected from mice, washed with PBS, and diluted tenfold to prepare a total suspension of 1 mL. The cells were then incubated with PBS, 0.025% Triton X‑100, and L6HB‑ANG (1–500 nM) at 37 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 541 nm to calculate the hemolysis rate. The hemolysis rate (%) was determined using the equation: [(A_sample - A_negative control)/(A_positive control - A_negative control)] × 100%.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical evaluation was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Experimental data obtained from at least three independent replicates are presented as the mean±SD. Two-group comparisons used Student’s t-test; multiple groups used one-way or two-way ANOVA. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ns, not significant; * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, and **** p < 0.0001.

## Result and discussion

## CD64-responsive conformational transition of L6HB
L6HB adopts a compact six-helix bundle architecture composed of twenty-one component strands, within which siSTING was encapsulated during thermal annealing. To endow the nanostructure with specific CD64 responsiveness, the aptamer ZH-1c, which binds selectively to the membrane protein CD64 abundantly expressed on activated microglia during neuroinflammation [23], was integrated into two separate component strands (L6HB-17 and L6HB-18) to form dual locks on the sidewalls of the bundle (Fig. S1, S2). This dual-lock configuration maintains the bundle in a closed conformation under physiological conditions, while allowing localized unlocking upon recognition of CD64, leading to partial sidewall opening and exposure of the encapsulated cargo (Fig. 2A).
Native PAGE analysis revealed a slight retardation in migration rate after incubation with CD64, indicating complex formation between the aptamer locks and CD64 and a resulting relaxation of bundle compactness (Fig. 2B). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) further visualized this conformational transition: after CD64 treatment, the L6HB structures exhibited increased lateral dimensions and moderately reduced height, characteristic of a transition from a fully closed to a partially open state (Fig. 2C, D and S3).
The unlocking process in solution was further examined via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), achieved by tagging the two partially complementary lock strands with Cy3 (donor) and Cy5 (acceptor) (Fig. S4). In the closed state, the proximity of Cy3 and Cy5 enabled efficient FRET with strong Cy5 emission, whereas CD64 binding induced strand displacement, increasing fluorophore separation and thereby reducing FRET efficiency (Fig. 2E). The corresponding fluorescence spectra showed a marked decrease in the 650–700 nm emission peak, confirming the CD64-triggered unlocking and conformational transition of L6HB (Fig. 2F).
To ensure the integrity of siSTING during delivery, siSTING is partially hybridized with inward-facing strands of the six-helix DNA bundle, which is intended to structurally shield the siRNA within the bundle architecture. We examined the stability of siSTING in 10% FBS and RNase A environments, respectively. The results showed that free siSTING was almost completely degraded within 1 h (Fig. S5). In contrast, the stability of siSTING loaded in 6HB was significantly enhanced, and about 50% of siSTING remained stable at 24 h of incubation (Fig. S6). The above results indicated that the DNA nanocarrier was able to resist nuclease degradation of siSTING.

## Cellular validation of the CD64-responsive conformational transition of L6HB
To evaluate the CD64-responsive conformational switching of L6HB in living cells, we employed both primary microglia and immortalized BV2 cells. CD64, a membrane receptor predominantly expressed on cells of the monocyte-macrophage lineage, exhibits low basal expression in resting microglia [24]. Western blot results showed that Aβ stimulation significantly upregulated CD64 expression in primary microglia, while BV2 cells displayed relatively high basal levels even without stimulation, likely reflecting a constitutive inflammatory state associated with cell immortalization (Fig. 3A, B; Fig. S7).
To monitor the structural dynamics of L6HB on the cell membrane, the dual locks were labeled with a FRET donor-acceptor pair (Cy3/Cy5) as described above. The ratio of donor to acceptor fluorescence (D/A ratio) was used to assess the real-time conformational transition, where an increased ratio corresponds to reduced FRET efficiency and hence lock opening. Based on the distinct CD64 expression profiles, three experimental groups were designed to confirm that the activation of the dual-lock switch is specifically triggered by CD64. Upon incubation of L6HB with Aβ-pretreated primary microglia (high CD64 expression), pronounced fluorescence puncta appeared on the cell membrane, and the D/A ratio increased progressively, reaching a plateau at approximately 60 min, consistent with gradual unlocking of the structure (Fig. 3D). In contrast, L6HB incubated with primary microglia with low expression of CD64 exhibited minimal changes in D/A ratio over 2 h (Fig. 3C). Similarly, BV2 cells, which inherently express CD64, displayed a comparable time-dependent increase in D/A ratio, reaching a steady state after 60 min (Fig. 3E).
Together, these results demonstrate that L6HB undergoes a CD64-triggered dual-lock conformational transition at the cellular level, validating its responsiveness in microglial models and establishing a functional foundation for targeted therapeutic applications in neuroinflammation.

## Evaluation of BBB penetration and inflammatory microglia targeting of L6HB-ANG
BBB represents a major obstacle for brain-targeted delivery. Angiopep‑2 (ANG), a brain‑penetrant peptide binding to low‑density lipoprotein receptor‑related protein‑1 (LRP‑1), facilitates receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB given the highly expression of LRP-1 on brain endothelial cells [25, 26]. To enhance BBB permeability, ANG was attached to both termini of L6HB through DNA hybridization (Fig. 4A). Native PAGE confirmed the successful assembly of L6HB and the formation of L6HB-ANG, as evidenced by a distinct band shift after ANG conjugation (Fig. 4B).
We cultured bEnd.3 endothelial cells on Transwell membranes to establish an in vitro BBB model [27, 28]. Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements verified the establishment of an intact monolayer (Fig. S8), and both L6HB and L6HB-ANG maintained barrier integrity without cytotoxicity toward bEnd.3 or BV2 cells (Fig. S9, S10). To assess BBB permeability, Cy3-labeled L6HB and L6HB-ANG were introduced into the upper chamber, with fluorescence in the lower chamber being monitored. After incubation for 6 h, L6HB-ANG exhibited significantly stronger fluorescence than L6HB, indicating improved translocation across the BBB (Fig. 4C). When BV2 cells were seeded in the lower chamber, confocal imaging and flow cytometry revealed enhanced cellular uptake of L6HB-ANG relative to L6HB (Fig. 4D-F), showing a time-dependent increase over 6 h (Fig. 4G). These results demonstrate that ANG modification markedly enhances the BBB permeability of L6HB.
To validate these findings in vivo, Cy5-labeled L6HB and L6HB-ANG were intravenously administered to 5×FAD mice, and real-time fluorescence imaging was performed (Fig. 4H). Both structures showed brain accumulation within 1 h, but L6HB-ANG exhibited stronger and more sustained fluorescence, consistent with ANG-mediated LRP-1 transport (Fig. 4I). Ex vivo imaging confirmed higher fluorescence intensity in the brains of L6HB-ANG-treated mice, with no significant distributional differences in other organs (Fig. 4J and S11).
Immunofluorescence staining of brain sections further revealed that both L6HB and L6HB-ANG colocalized with activated microglia in the hippocampus and cortex. Notably, L6HB-ANG showed higher accumulation in microglia (Fig. 4K, L and S12), confirming its improved BBB penetration and selective targeting of inflammatory microglia in vivo. Collectively, these findings indicate that ANG functionalization significantly improves the BBB permeability and neuroinflammatory targeting capacity of L6HB.

## L6HB-ANG modulates microglial phenotype and promotes Aβ clearance in vitro
Previous studies have demonstrated that suppression of STING expression can reverse microglial activation and enhance Aβ clearance by microglia [29–31]. Endo/lysosomal escape is a prerequisite for efficient intracellular siRNA gene silencing. By co-localization experiments of intracellular L6HB with lysosomes, we found that L6HB was initially localized within endo/lysosomal compartments, a fraction of the siRNA signal was observed to dissociate from lysosomes at later time points (Fig. S13). Thus, part of siSTING can function as gene silencing in the cytoplasm.
To assess the silencing efficiency of siSTING, we first applied liposome and L6HB-mediated delivery in BV2 cells. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that both methods effectively reduced STING mRNA levels (Fig. S14). To further evaluate the functional modulation of microglia by L6HB-ANG under BBB-mimicking conditions, PBS, naked siSTING, L6HB, and L6HB-ANG were added to the upper chamber of the Transwell coculture system for 24 h. After removing the upper chamber, Aβ was introduced into the lower chamber to induce an inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 5A). Compared with the naked siSTING and L6HB groups, L6HB-ANG significantly reduced STING mRNA and protein expression in BV2 cells (Fig. 5B, C), reflecting its high BBB-penetration efficiency and superior gene-silencing performance. These results confirm that L6HB-ANG enables efficient siSTING delivery across the BBB and robust inhibition of STING expression in microglia.
To investigate whether STING downregulation by L6HB-ANG could regulate microglial polarization, BV2 cells were stimulated with Aβ to mimic an inflammatory microenvironment. qRT-PCR analysis showed that L6HB-ANG markedly reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) while upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10), indicating a phenotypic transition from M1 to M2 microglia (Fig. 5D, E). Western blot analysis further confirmed these results, showing reduced iNOS (M1 marker) and increased Arg1 (M2 marker) expression (Fig. 5F). These findings demonstrate that L6HB-ANG effectively promotes microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype under inflammatory stimulation.
To evaluate whether this phenotypic shift enhances microglial phagocytic capacity, FITC-labeled Aβ was added to BV2 cells following treatment. Confocal imaging and flow cytometry revealed significantly increased Aβ uptake in the L6HB-ANG group compared with other treatments (Fig. 5H and I). These results indicate that STING silencing by L6HB-ANG not only suppresses microglial inflammation but also facilitates Aβ clearance (Fig. 5G). Collectively, these findings suggest that L6HB-ANG downregulates STING expression, promotes microglial polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 state to the anti-inflammatory M2 state, and enhances Aβ phagocytosis, thereby exerting potential neuroprotective effects in vitro.

## L6HB-ANG rescues memory and learning deficits in 5×FAD mice
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of L6HB-ANG in AD, 4-month-old 5×FAD transgenic mice were randomly divided into three groups to receive PBS, naked siSTING, or L6HB-ANG via tail vein injection every five days, for a total of twelve administrations (Fig. 6A). After treatment, mice underwent various behavioral tests, including the Morris water maze (MWM), novel object recognition (NOR), and Y-maze tests, to assess their learning and memory abilities. Age-matched wild-type (WT) mice served as controls.
In the MWM assay, mice were subjected to training across five continuous days, and escape latency was recorded. On day 6, spatial memory was evaluated by removing the hidden platform (Fig. 6B). Representative swimming trajectories showed that 5×FAD mice treated with L6HB-ANG exhibited markedly shorter escape latencies compared with the PBS and siSTING groups (Fig. 6C, D). In the probe trial, L6HB-ANG treated mice showed increased residence time in the target quadrant and more frequent crossings at the previous platform region than the other treatment groups (Fig. 6E, F). Importantly, swimming speeds were comparable across all groups, excluding the influence of motor impairment (Fig. 6G). These results suggest that L6HB-ANG effectively rescues memory and spatial learning deficits in AD mice.
To further assess cognitive function, NOR and Y-maze tests were performed (Fig. 6H, J). In both tests, 5×FAD mice treated with PBS or siSTING displayed reduced discrimination of novel objects and lower spontaneous alternation rates, reflecting impaired recognition and working memory. In contrast, L6HB-ANG treatment significantly improved both behavioral parameters (Fig. 6I, K), indicating restored cognitive performance. Together, these results demonstrate that systemic administration of L6HB-ANG significantly alleviates memory and learning impairments in 5×FAD mice, highlighting its therapeutic potential in AD.

## L6HB-ANG alleviates Aβ burden and neuroinflammation in 5×FAD mice
Aβ deposition and microglial activation are hallmark pathological features of AD [32, 33]. To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of L6HB-ANG in vivo, immunofluorescence staining was performed to examine Aβ accumulation and microglial activation in the brains of 5×FAD mice. As shown in Fig. 7A-D, L6HB-ANG treatment markedly reduced Aβ plaques (green) and IBA1-positive microglia (red) in both the hippocampus and cortex compared with PBS- and siSTING-treated groups, indicating efficient Aβ clearance and suppression of neuroinflammation.
To further clarify the role of L6HB-ANG in microglial polarization, we analyzed the expression of phenotype-specific markers iNOS (M1) and Arg1 (M2) in the hippocampal region. As shown in Fig. 7E-F, strong iNOS (red) fluorescence was observed in the CA1 region of untreated 5×FAD mice, while L6HB-ANG treatment significantly reduced iNOS expression and increased Arg1 (green) intensity. These findings indicate that L6HB-ANG drives microglial polarization from the pro-inflammatory (M1) state to the anti-inflammatory (M2) state, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation. Neuronal loss in the hippocampus and cortex is a major pathological manifestation of AD [34]. Immunohistochemical analysis of NeuN-positive neurons revealed pronounced neuronal preservation in the CA1 region of L6HB-ANG-treated mice compared with the PBS and siSTING groups (Fig. 7G, H), confirming its neuroprotective effect.
Collectively, these results indicate that L6HB-ANG effectively reduces cerebral Aβ burden, suppresses microglial overactivation, promotes M1 to M2 polarization, and mitigates neuronal loss, thereby contributing to improved cognitive performance in 5×FAD mice.
To further assess the biosafety of L6HB-ANG, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs (Fig. S15), routine blood analyses, serum biochemistry, and hemolysis assays were conducted (Fig. S16-S18). No histopathological abnormalities or hematological changes were observed, confirming that L6HB-ANG exhibits excellent biocompatibility and systemic safety at the tested dosage.

## Conclusion
This study introduces a programmable DNA nanoplatform that enables precise and responsive delivery of siSTING for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease. By integrating a lock-equipped six-helix DNA bundle with Angiopep-2-mediated brain targeting, the system overcomes key translational barriers of siRNA therapy, including inefficient blood-brain barrier penetration and nonspecific tissue distribution. In the 5×FAD mouse model, L6HB-ANG achieved selective accumulation in activated microglia, where it released siSTING in response to CD64 signaling. This targeted silencing of cGAS–STING signaling attenuated neuroinflammation, facilitated microglial polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype, reduced amyloid pathology, and rescued cognitive function.
Importantly, unlike tumor microenvironments that often provide relatively stable physicochemical cues, the neuroinflammatory microenvironment in Alzheimer’s disease is highly dynamic and heterogeneous, with microglial activation states varying across time and space. This feature underscores the necessity of a cell-state responsive delivery strategy, in which siRNA release is selectively activated in disease-associated microglia rather than driven by nonspecific accumulation, thereby providing a mechanistic rationale for employing an intelligent DNA molecular machine in this context. This work establishes a structurally defined, stimuli-responsive carrier that couples molecular precision with biological intelligence. The modularity of this design suggests broad adaptability for delivering diverse therapeutic oligonucleotides to specific cell populations within the central nervous system, offering a generalizable strategy for tackling neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases.
