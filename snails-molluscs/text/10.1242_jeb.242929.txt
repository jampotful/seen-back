Abstract

24

The relationship between genotype and phenotype is nontrivial due to often complex

25

molecular pathways that make it difficult to unambiguously relate phenotypes to specific

26

genotypes. Photopigments, an opsin apoprotein bound to a light-absorbing

27

chromophore, present an opportunity to directly relate the amino acid sequence to an

28

absorbance peak phenotype (λmax). We examined this relationship by conducting a

29

series of site-directed mutagenesis experiments of retinochrome, a non-visual opsin,

30

from two closely related species: the common bay scallop, Argopecten irradians, and

31

the king scallop, Pecten maximus. Using protein folding models, we identified three

32

amino acid sites of likely functional importance and expressed mutated retinochrome

33

proteins in vitro. Our results show that the mutation of amino acids lining the opsin

34

binding pocket are responsible for fine spectral tuning, or small changes in the λmax of

35

these light sensitive proteins Most mutations caused a blue shift regardless of the

36

retinochrome background, with shifts ranging from a 12 nm blue shift to a 5 nm red shift

37

from the wild-type λmax. These mutations do not show an additive effect, but rather

38

suggests the presence of epistatic interactions. This work highlights the importance of

39

binding pocket shape in the evolution of spectral tuning and builds on our ability to

40

relate genotypic changes to phenotypes in an emerging model for opsin functional

41

analysis.

Introduction
Understanding how genotype influences phenotype is a fundamental goal of

50

biology. This relationship is far from trivial, as complex genetic pathways and

51

environmental effects often preclude clear connections. The phototransduction system

52

that converts light into electrical signals [1] offers a rare opportunity to examine

53

genotype-phenotype relationships because single amino acid substitutions can

54

drastically alter visual protein phenotype [2]. Furthermore, because photopigments – the

55

functional unit of phototransduction – only absorb a portion of the light spectrum, the

56

maximum absorbance (λmax) represents a directly measurable phenotype.

57

Photopigments form by covalent bonds between a retinal chromophore and the

58

binding pocket of an opsin apoprotein. The interactions of the chromophore with amino

59

acids in the binding pocket have been highlighted as critical for photopigment function

60

by theoretical calculations [3,4], mutagenesis [5–9] and quantum mechanics/molecular

61

mechanics (QM/MM) modeling [10–12]. Such interactions between the chromophore

62

and the amino acid residues within the binding pocket of the opsin are thought to be

63

responsible for modulating λmax, or spectral tuning [3,13]. Changing amino acid side

64

chains that effect the environment of the chromophore binding pocket may be

65

particularly important [14].

Extensive mutagenesis efforts have been directed towards identifying spectral

67

tuning sites in opsins [15–18]. In vertebrates, λmax differences between middle- and

68

long-wavelength-sensitive pigments involved in color vision are attributed to three [19–

69

21] or five [22,23] amino acid sites. These individual sites are responsible for small

70

nanometer shifts that cumulatively alter the protein function [7]. Whether the patterns of

71

amino acid changes responsible for spectral tuning in vertebrate visual opsins are

72

broadly applicable to the immense diversity of opsin proteins is unknown. This is

73

because the vast majority of experimental spectral tuning research has been done in

74

vertebrate systems (e.g., monostable Gt-protein coupled opsins) (reviewed in [24,25]),

75

with few exceptions in non-vertebrate species (e.g., bistable Gq-protein coupled opsins

76

[26–28]). Moreover, the crystal structure of invertebrate opsins, such as Gq-protein

77

coupled opsins (e.g., squid “rhodopsin”), have important differences compared to

78

vertebrate opsin (Gt-protein coupled opsins, e.g., bovine rhodopsin), such as greater

79

organization in the cytoplasmic region correlating to bistability, an interconvertibility

80

between the photopigment’s dark state and its photoproduct [29,30]. This longstanding

81

focus on vertebrate opsins has left us with a severe lack of understanding for how

82

spectral tuning functions in invertebrate opsins.

83

To identify amino acids involved in the spectral tuning of photopigments,

84

comparing the amino acid sequence and protein function of two closely related species

85

is an effective starting point. Here, we cloned and expressed in vitro a mollusc-specific

86

opsin, retinochrome, from two closely related scallops: the common bay scallop,

87

Argopecten irradians and the king scallop, Pecten maximus. Retinochrome is one of the

88

few examples of a non-vertebrate, non-visual opsin with well-characterized biochemical

89

and spectral properties [31–34]. Further, its ease of expression and predictable nature

90

in heterologous cell culture makes it a compelling protein to work with [31,35–37].

91

Retinochrome belongs to the “photoisomerase” opsin clade of

92

RGR/retinochrome/peropsin [38], which binds to the all-trans isomer of retinal as

93

opposed to 11-cis isomer commonly used by most visual opsins. The retinochrome

94

counterion that stabilizes chromophore binding has a notably different location than

95

other opsins [39]. Furthermore, the likely role of retinochrome is as a photoisomerase in

96

the retinoid visual cycle of molluscs, converting all-trans retinal to 11-cis retinal following

97

the absorption of light [40], which sets it apart from opsins that mediate

98

phototransduction. Whether a few amino acid substitutions cumulatively contribute to

99

differences in retinochrome λmax values, as seen in vertebrate visual opsins, is

100

unknown.

101

We hypothesize that changing the shape [41] or electrostatic environment [42] of

102

retinochrome’s binding pocket will shift the λmax value. This shift would be the result of

103

different biochemical properties of newly introduced amino acid residues, which can

104

affect noncovalent interactions within the photopigment. To test this hypothesis, we

105

performed reciprocal site-directed mutagenesis of retinochromes from the common bay

106

scallop, Argopecten irradians, and the king scallop, Pecten maximus. Using a protein

107

modeling approach, we identified three amino acid sites in the binding pocket and

108

created 14 mutants representing all possible combinations of the three sites of interest

109

in each background. Spectral analyses indicate that these three sites have differing

110

degrees of influence on phenotype and appear to have epistatic interactions with other

111

regions of the protein. Our results reveal a more complicated path from genotype to

112

phenotype than typically seen for opsins.

Results

114

Retinochromes have similar sequence, but differ spectrally, between scallop

115

species

116

Cloned retinochrome sequence of Argopecten irradians (GenBank KX550908)

117

and Pecten maximus (GenBank MW984523) were 308 amino acids. The two protein

118

sequences were 92% similar, with 25 different amino acid residues. There was high

119

sequence conservation within the binding pocket (99%), and most of the residue

120

differences belonged to the same biochemical class. Residues surrounding sites

121

important for the function of retinochrome, such as the lysine for chromophore binding

122

(pos 274) and glutamate counterion (pos 160), are conserved between scallop samples,

123

as are amino acids around these sites.

124

To determine the λmax of A. irradians retinochrome (Airr-RTC) and P. maximus

125

retinochrome (Pmax-RTC), we expressed both proteins in HEK293T cells and

126

incubated with all-trans retinal. The absorbance peak (λmax) of Airr-RTC was 510 nm

127

(Fig 1a, black curve), while the absorbance peak of Pmax-RTC was 492 nm (Fig 1b,

128

black curve). This is the first in vitro measurement of retinochrome λmax, and our

129

measurements are consistent with prior literature [33,43]. Differences between the dark

130

spectra minus spectral tracings of the photopigment after exposure to blue light (Fig 1a-

131

b, red curves) confirm the formation of a photopigment (Fig 1, insets).

Three amino acid changes indicate potential interaction sites lining the retinal
binding pocket
To narrow down the candidate list of 25 spectral tuning sites, we employed three-

135

dimensional protein models to identify potential ligand interaction sites. In total, 18

136

positions were identified between the two retinochrome models and were at the same

137

binding pocket locations of two RTC homologs with two exceptions. Positions 170 and

138

188 were only predicted to be interaction sites in the Pmax-RTC model, but not in the

139

Airr-RTC model (Fig 2a, 2b). However, the amino acid residue was conserved at

140

position 170 and thus was removed from the candidate list. In contrast, position 188

141

varied between a tryptophan in Pmax-RTC versus a tyrosine in Airr-RTC. While both of

142

these amino acids are classified as hydrophobic with similar pKa scores, the position of

143

the tyrosine’s hydroxyl group could allow a more direct polarity change in the binding

144

pocket [44], and therefore may affect the absorbance of the photon by the bound retinal

145

(Fig 2c). We selected site 188 as the first site for mutation.

146

To identify other sites that may be responsible for altering the shape of or

147

electrostatic environment within the binding pocket, we compared the 3D models of the

148

Airr-RTC and Pmax-RTC focusing on the location of the non-conserved amino acids.

149

We used UCSF Chimera to manipulate the 3D models, allowing us to measure the

150

distance between active groups of the amino acid residues of interest in relation to one

151

another as well as the retinal molecule. Many non-covalent polar interactions range

152

from 0.5 to 3.5 angstroms, thus this range was used as a criterion for identifying sites of

153

interest through bond measurements. Site 184 (Figure 2a, b) is a conserved methionine

154

in both Pmax-RTC and Airr-RTC but was predicted by COACH to function as a ligand

155

interaction site. A neighboring site which is not conserved is site 181. Site 181 is a

156

methionine in Airr-RTC and a leucine in Pmax-RTC. The primary difference between

157

these nonpolar side chains is the presence of the thioether of the methionine. This

158

thioether allows for oxidation of the residue and acts as a strong hydrogen bond

159

acceptor. Bond measurements show that the sulfur of Met181 is only 2.85 angstroms

160

from the side chain hydrogens of Met184 (Figure 2c). We selected site 181 as a second

161

site of interest due its potential in changing the polarity of an amino acid lining the

162

binding pocket, site 184.

163

Using the same logic, we also identified nonconserved position 193 as a site of

164

interest. Position 189, a leucine, was predicted by COACH as an interaction site for the

165

ligand (Figure 2a, b), yet was conserved between the RTC homologs. However, within

166

0.5 to 3.5 angstroms, position 193 differed as a valine in Airr-RTC or an alanine in

167

Pmax-RTC. The isopropyl group of the valine is much bulkier than that of the methyl

168

group found in alanine. Given the proximity of the hydrogens found on the carbon

169

groups of the leucine to that of the valine (between 2.735 angstroms and 3.202

170

angstroms) it is likely that the location of predicted interaction site 193 is altered in the

171

space of the protein (Figure 2c).

Discussion

202

The relationship between genotype and phenotype is a central unanswered

203

question in biology. Here, we provide the first experimental results with respect to

204

absorbance spectra of a molluscan photopigment. We investigated the genotype-

205

phenotype relationship by comparing retinochrome from two closely related scallop

206

species and, through site-directed mutagenesis experiments, altered amino acids

207

predicted to influence spectral absorbance. Because the A. irradians and P. maximus

208

retinochrome sequences are highly conserved around both the Schiff base binding site

209

and the counterion, the 18 nm difference in retinochrome λmax that we observed

210

between the two species must be attributed to non-conserved sites elsewhere in the

211

protein. We hypothesized that amino acids lining the binding pocket may in part be

212

responsible for spectral tuning of retinochrome λmax. We used bioinformatic methods to

213

identify three non-conserved amino acids that may alter the shape or electrostatic

214

environment of the opsin binding pocket, and reciprocally mutated those sites between

215

A. irradians and P. maximus to test their role in spectral tuning. These three sites are, in

216

part, responsible for spectral tuning of scallop retinochromes. However, our results

217

show that the relationship between amino acid changes and spectral shifts is not simply

218

additive, and indicates a role of intramolecular epistasis in retinochrome spectral tuning.

Predicted Ligand Interaction Sites Alter λmax
Through protein modeling and subsequent testing of 14 reciprocal site-directed

221

mutants, we found three sites that effect spectral tuning of retinochrome. However,

222

these results were not always predictable or additive in nature. Site 188, a tyrosine in A.

223

irradians and a tryptophan in P. maximus, was the only non-conserved predicted

224

interaction site based on the COACH analysis. Mutations created at this site resulted in

225

a -5 nm blue shift and a +5 nm red shift of λmaxes for Airr-RTC-ΔY188W and Pmax-RTC-

226

ΔW188Y, respectively. This mirroring effect between reciprocal mutants strongly

227

indicates an independent effect on spectral tuning at this location. The impact of

228

hydroxyl-containing amino acids, such as tyrosine, on λmax is well documented in both

229

vertebrate and invertebrate photopigments [15,44,45]. Such studies demonstrate that

230

the change in λmax is due to the potential dipole moments created by the oxygen of

231

hydroxyl groups in proximity of the ß- ionone ring of the chromophore, which cause a

232

red shift of the absorption maximum or dipole residues around the protonated Schiff

233

base that result a λmax shift in the opposite direction (e.g., OH-site rule [46]). The former

234

may be occurring in Pmax-RTC-ΔW188Y. Our Airr-RTC 3D model shows the tyrosine

235

hydroxyl group inserted into the aromatic ring of the retinal, while the tryptophan in the

236

Pmax-RTC model does not extend as far. This impossible orientation of tyrosine is likely

237

the result of the software’s limitation, which only provides 11-cis retinal as a ligand

238

option, rather than the all-trans retinal preferred by retinochrome. Likely, the straight

239

poly-carbon chain of the all-trans retinal would rest differently in the binding pocket of

240

the retinochrome, but the potential for dipole moments from the hydroxyl group could

241

still affect the electrostatic environment of the binding pocket. This hydroxyl group is

absent in the tryptophan at site 188 of wildtype P. maximus. Spectral analyses of the

243

single, double, and triple mutants of Airr-RTC with the Y188W mutation all had a blue

244

shift in λmax ranging from -7 nm to -14 nm as expected under the OH-site rule, while

245

spectra of Pmax-RTC single, double, and triple mutants with the W188Y mutation did

246

not show the reciprocal pattern. The Pmax-RTC W188Y single mutant, L181M and

247

W188Y double mutant, and L181M, W188Y and A193V triple mutant had slight red

248

shifts between +2 nm to +5 nm, while the double mutant containing W188Y and A193V

249

showed a blue shift of 2 nm. These conflicting changes in λmax indicate that other polar

250

groups may be involved in retinochrome spectral tuning. Future work should consider

251

the “dipole-orientation rule,” an extension of the OH-site rule, that takes into account

252

both location and orientation of polar groups, including water molecules, in modeling

253

approaches [47].

254

The remaining two mutant sites, 181 and 193, allowed us to investigate the

255

effects of residues in close proximity to predicted interaction sites rather than sites

256

directly interacting with the retinal chromophore. The results of our site-directed

257

mutagenesis experiments point to a hierarchy of effects. For instance, we observed the

258

same +2 nm (λmax 512 nm) shift in Airr-RTC with the M181L single mutation as with the

259

double mutant with M181L and V193A, yet the single mutant V193A had a large -12 nm

260

blue shift (λmax 498 nm). The lack of spectral shift in the double mutant between these

261

sites indicate that the effect posed by M181L may compensate for the alteration caused

262

by the mutation at V193A in Airr-RTC backbone. In Pmax-RTC mutants, single mutants

263

at same sites L181M and A193V had minor blue shifts (-4 nm and -2 nm, respectively),

264

while the double mutant of those sites showed no change in λmax (492nm). However,

this blue shift was maintained in Pmax-RTC mutants at the same sites. The mutation

with hydrophobic residues in bacterial rhodopsins have been shown to cause dramatic

267

blue shifts (up to 80 nm relative to wild type) [48], because the side chains of these

268

hydrophobic residues tend to be bulky due to the saturation of hydrogens and may

269

restrict movement of the retinal’s ß- ionone ring. Thus, the presence of hydrophobic

270

residues may alter the binding pocket geometry by pushing adjacent residues, such as

271

those lining the binding pocket, into different orientations.

A possible role for binding pocket shape in determining λmax

273

The effect of mutation V193A in Airr-RTC may exhibit a stronger effect on the

274

shape of the binding pocket. The single Airr-RTC mutants containing V193A showed a

275

large blue shift (-12 nm), with the exception of the double mutant also containing

276

M181L, which showed a slight +2 nm red shift. The decrease in size of side chains from

277

valine to alanine may be significant enough to reduce the bulging effect of this amino

278

acid residue on the proposed ligand binding site, creating variation in the region of the

279

protein and the shape or size of the binding pocket. However, when combined with

280

M181L mutation, the small change of λmax could suggest that the second mutation

281

compensates the effect of V193A. Reciprocal mutants at the site 193 in Pmax-RTC

282

(A193V) were expected to result in red shifts, mirroring that of Airr-RTC mutants, but

283

these mutants also had consistent and large blue shifts (-10 nm to -12 nm) with the

284

exception of the triple mutant (-5 nm). Here, the increase in side chain bulk (A to V)

285

producing the blue shift in λmax may indicate a weaken the interaction between the

286

ligand and retinochrome.

A scenario where mutations in the binding pocket weaken the interactions with

288

the chromophore may arise from changes in the distance between the amino acids in

289

the binding pocket and the bound retinal as well as the distance between interacting

290

amino acids. A change in distance can alter the strength of the interactions of these

291

components thus potentially changing the polar environment of the binding pocket and

292

the λmax of the protein. While mutations in Airr-RTC appear to shift towards the wild-type

293

λmax of Pmax-RTC, the λmax of the reciprocal mutants in Pmax-RTC may actually reflect

294

reduced interaction strength between binding pocket residues and chromophore, thus

295

shifting towards the native λmax of unbound all-trans retinal in the UV region. However,

296

being able to visualize the protein and binding pocket shape via crystal structure would

297

help us to understand how the shape is being affected by specific mutations.

298

Epistatic effect on spectral tuning

299

In numerous vertebrate visual opsins, spectral tuning is attributed to one or few

300

amino acid substitutions [17]. For example, three amino acids are responsible for red-

301

shifting in the evolution of old world primate trichromatic vision via additive effects

302

([49,50] but see [19]). We therefore had reason to expect one or particular combinations

303

of the three predicted sites for spectral tuning to be sufficient for shifting λmax from one

304

homolog to the to the other. However, our reciprocal site-directed mutagenesis rarely

305

mirrored the spectral tuning between Airr-RTC and Pmax-RTC backgrounds. In fact,

306

site 188 was the only mutant with equal blue and red shifts for Airr-RTC (-5 nm) and

307

Pmax-RTC (+5 nm), respectively, relative to their wildtype proteins.

308
309

Both triple mutants had λmax shifts in the expected direction, though neither shift
had a magnitude great enough to match the wildtype of the other species, e.g. Airr-

was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

310

RTC-ΔM181L-ΔY188W-ΔV193A λmax did not match the Pmax-RTC λmax. The Pmax-

311

RTC-ΔL181M-ΔW188Y and Pmax-RTC-ΔW188Y-ΔA193V double mutants had λmax

312

shifts that were close (within 1 nm) to the sums of λmax shifts for the single mutants.

313

Otherwise, the spectral changes for the double and triple mutant retinochromes relative

314

to the wildtype generally did not reflect additive changes of the single mutant effects.

315

Together, these results indicate that amino acid substitutions in these spectral tuning

316

sites may experience epistatic effects, the non-additive effect on protein function arising

317

from identical amino acid substitutions in different genetic backgrounds. Intramolecular

318

epistasis has been implicated in opsin evolution, likely resulting from trade-offs between

319

selection maintaining protein stability and spectral tuning [51,52]. Selection on amino

320

acid sites relating to properties unrelated to spectral tuning in retinochrome may

321

therefore hinder our ability predict changes to absorbance from these three residues

322

alone.

323

Scallop retinochrome as a promising model for understanding opsin function

324

In this study we were able to identify and successfully mutate three amino acid

325

sites that affect absorption maxima in scallop retinochrome. Our results demonstrate

326

that retinochrome is easy to clone, mutate, and express in heterologous cell culture,

327

making it as a promising model to examine the genotype-phenotype relationship in

328

opsins. To make retinochrome a more powerful system, we need detailed atomic-level

329

information to better model how all-trans retinal interacts with the protein and more

330

extensive sampling across molluscan species to describe the natural variants and

331

patterns of molecular evolution. This study and others [5,53,54] have shown that the

332

amino acid sequence does not provide a reliable predictor of absorption maxima (λmax)

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.26.445805; this version posted May 26, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

333

trends. In fact, λmax values are likely determined by changes in the topographical

334

distribution and orientation of the amino acid side-chains surrounding the chromophore,

335

making 3D structure of the photopigment essential. The application of Quantum

336

Mechanics/Molecular Mechanics (QM/MM) models is a powerful tool for studying

337

proteins beyond their structural characterizations [10,41,55,56]. However, the modeling

338

of opsin proteins is particularly challenging as it not only requires an atomic-level

339

representation to correctly describe the interaction between the chromophore and its

340

environment, but also a correct description of its light-responsive electronic structure.

341

Integrating experimental measurements of retinochrome mutants with model

342

refinements could yield more accurate and consistent QM/MM models. With such tools,

343

it will be possible to classify the effect of different noncovalent interactions, construct a

344

topographical map of their distribution, and ultimately, develop hypotheses on how the

345

λmax values and reactivity properties of the photopigment respond to these interactions

346

([57]).

347

Conclusions

348

We demonstrate the first example of expression, mutagenesis, and spectral

349

analysis of retinochrome from scallop species. We showed that the absorbance peaks

350

of scallop retinochromes fall within the blue-green range of the visible spectrum similar

351

to in vivo measurements of cephalopod retinochrome. We propose methods to identify

352

amino acid sites potentially responsible for spectral tuning of photopigments. Amino

353

acids near sites of import, such as the Schiff base binding site and counterion, have

354

long been recognized for their role in spectral tuning, but our results show that sites

355

elsewhere in the protein may be responsible for tuning in the λmax. While we uncovered

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.26.445805; this version posted May 26, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

356

sites for spectral tuning, we rarely observed mirrored changes in λmax for our reciprocal

357

mutagenesis studies, suggesting that retinochrome function may be influenced by

358

epistasis, thus complicating the predictability of genotype to phenotype relationships for

359

this opsin. Finally, our findings highlight the effectiveness of scallop retinochrome as a

360

model system for investigating the relationship between genotype and phenotype of

361

photopigments.

Materials and methods

363

Retinochrome Cloning and Insertion in Vector

364

Previously assembled transcriptomes (Serb et al., in prep) were used to identify

365

retinochrome sequences in Argopecten irradians and Pecten maximus. Based on those

366

transcripts, UTR-specific primers were designed to amplify the complete coding regions

367

from cDNA (Table 1). Scallop RNA was extracted from eye tissue using the RiboPure

368

RNA extraction kit (Ambion) and converted to cDNA libraries. PCR was carried out with

369

a reaction mixture equaling 50uL, containing, 5uL of 10x buffer, 1.5uL of 25mM MgCl,

370

4uL of 2.5mM dNTPs, 0.2uL Platinum Taq, 1uL of 10uM of forward and reverse primers

371

(Table 1), and 1uL of 3uM template cDNA. The thermocycler protocol used was as

372

follows, with variation in primer annealing temperatures: 95°C for 2min; 35 cycles of

373

95°C for 30s, primer temperature for 40s, and 72°C for 2min; and 72°C for 10 min. PCR

374

products were size-screened using a 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, bands of

375

expected size (923bp) were gel extracted (Qiagen Qiaquick Gel Extraction kit) and

376

cloned using chemically competent E. coli cells following the manufacturer’s protocol

377

(TOPO TA Cloning Kit with pCR2.1-TOPO). The identity of positive colonies from blue-

378

white screening was confirmed by Sanger DNA sequencing using an ABI 3730 Capillary

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.26.445805; this version posted May 26, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

379

Electrophoresis Genetic Analyzer at the Iowa State University DNA Facility. The genes

380

were then inserted into the expression vector p1D4-hrGFP II [59] to generate our

381

working plasmids for retinochrome from A. irradians (Airr-RTC) and P. maximus (Pmax-

382

RTC). These expression plasmids served as the templates for site-directed

383

mutagenesis.

Modeling and Site Identification

385

To identify amino acids lining the binding pocket which may be responsible for

386

altering the λmax of retinochrome, amino acid sequences and predicted 3D models were

387

compared. Amino acid sequences of A. irradians retinochrome and P. maximus

388

retinochrome were aligned using MAFFT v7.221 [60]. To identify sites hypothesized to

389

cause changes in λmax of retinochrome, amino acid sequences of A. irradians and P.

390

maximus retinochrome were submitted to GPCR-I-TASSER [61] to create 3D models of

391

each protein. The resulting models were then submitted to COACH [62,63], a meta-

392

server used to predict the active interaction sites within protein-ligand interactions.

393

COACH outputs a list of amino acid sites it has predicted to interact with the ligand

394

when bound based on proximity of the amino acid to the bound ligand model. This list of

395

predicted sites was compared to the alignment of Airr-RTC and Pmax-RTC, specifically

396

to identify predicted interaction sites that are also not conserved sites between the two

397

species, revealing one predicted interaction site which differed between species.

398

The second approach to identifying amino acids responsible for altering the

399

retinochrome binding pocket environment was based on the role of possible polar

400

interactions between amino acids and variation in the shape or electrostatic

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.26.445805; this version posted May 26, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

401

environment of the binding pocket plays a role in spectral tuning of the λmax of opsins.

402

3D models from COACH were loaded into UCSF Chimera v1.4 [64], a visualization

403

software for molecular analyses and model comparison. Using Chimera, predicted

404

interaction sites were differentially highlighted based on whether the amino acids were

405

conserved between A. irradians and P. maximus amino acid sequences. Non-conserved

406

amino acids outside of or far from the binding pocket were disregarded, as they are less

407

likely to affect the polar or shape of the binding pocket. The distances of the predicted

408

interaction sites to the active side chains of non-conserved amino acids were then

409

individually measured. Distances less than 3.5 angstroms (maximal hydrogen bond

410

length) were searched for, revealing two sites as targets for site-directed mutagenesis.

Cell Culture, Expression, and Pull-down

451

To express wild-type and mutant retinochrome proteins in vitro, 15 plates

452

(Corning Falcon Standard Tissue Culture Dishes, 10cm, ref. 353003; Tewksbury, MA)

453

of confluent HEK293T cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA) were transfected with 8 mg DNA

454

and 20 mL 293fectin Transfecting Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per plate,

455

according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bovine rhodopsin was used as a system

456

control using two plates of confluent HEK293T cells with equivalent amounts of DNA

457

and 293fectin as described above. Plates were incubated for 24 hours before the

458

minimum essential medium (MEM) was exchanged with new MEM containing 5 µmol

459

all-trans retinal for retinochrome and 5 µmol of 11-cis retinal for bovine rhodopsin. Due

460

to the addition of light sensitive retinal at this step, all subsequent culturing and

461

experimentation was conducted in a dark-lab environment under dim red light. The

462

plates were incubated for another 24 hours before cells were harvested by scraping the

463

plates twice with 5 mL bufferA (3 mmol MgCl2, 140 mmol NaCl, 50 mmol HEPES pH

464

6.6, aprotinin [10 mg/mL], leupeptin [10 mg/mL]). All subsequent centrifugation and

465

incubation steps were at 4°C or on ice. Cells were collected by pellet following

466

centrifugation (10 min at 1620 relative centrifugal force [RCF]) and resuspended in 10

467

mL buffer A. Cells were washed two times in total following the same protocol.

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.26.445805; this version posted May 26, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint (which

available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

468

After a second wash, cells were resuspended in 2 mL per plate of buffer A with 5

470

471

38360 RCF for 20 minutes and resuspended in solubilization buffer (buffer A plus 1% n-

472

473

plate. The solubilized cells were nutated for 1 hour at 4C. After the hour nutation, the

474

mixture was centrifuged for 20 min at 42,740 RCF. The supernatant was then added to

475

Spectrophotometry

484

Ultraviolet-visible absorption spectra (250–750 nm) of purified proteins was

485

measured at 15°C using a Hitachi U-3900 spectrophotometer (Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan).

486

Data analysis was performed on the mean value of five spectral measurements with the

487

software UV Solutions v4.2 (Hitachi). To test the proteins for photoreactivity, “dark”

488

absorbance was measured first for each protein, e.g., the naïve protein that has been

489

incubated and regenerated with the all-trans retinal chromophore. Retinochrome

490

proteins were tested independently with all-trans retinal because (1) retinochrome

available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

491

492

493

retinal when unbound to retinochrome apoprotein is 380 nm. Thus, any light-dependent

494

isomerization converting free all-trans retinal to 11-cis retinal will be undetectable in the

495

experimental system. Therefore, the most plausible explanation for any observed

496

change in spectral absorbance is due to a conformational change of the retinal

497

covalently bonded to the apoprotein.

498

For the “light” spectra, extracted proteins were bleached at different wavelengths

499

according to λmax identified from the “dark” spectra and then the absorbance was

500

measured. Extracted proteins were first exposed to light at ∼474 nm using two blue

502

503

504

performed, followed by an absorbance measurement. Mean spectra was plotted using

505

507

508

509

510

511

512
