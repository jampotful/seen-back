# Safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of an extract from the dried leaves of Hedera helix L. (common ivy extract) and thymol for all animal species and categories except poultry, ornamental birds and all aquatic animal species (FEFANA asbl)

## Abstract
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of an extract from the dried leaves of Hedera helix L. (common ivy extract) and thymol, when used as a sensory additive in feed for all animal species and categories except poultry, ornamental birds and all aquatic animal species. The EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP) concluded that the additive under assessment is safe up to the maximum proposed use levels in complete feed of 450 mg/kg for piglets and veal calves; 300 mg/kg for pigs for fattening, sows, rabbits and guinea pigs; 280 mg/kg for horses; 250 mg/kg for ruminants; and 600 mg/kg for dogs and cats. These levels are extrapolated to physiologically related species. For any other species, the additive is safe at 250 mg/kg complete feed. The use of the additive in animal feed under the proposed conditions of use is safe for the consumer and the environment. Regarding user safety, the extract under assessment should be considered as irritant to skin and eyes and respiratory sensitiser. Exposure of users by any route is considered a risk. The data provided indicate that the additive under assessment (common ivy extract and thymol) has the potential to be efficacious as a sensory additive in feed. However, the Panel notes that, considering the well‐known flavouring properties of thymol, it is not possible to conclude on the flavouring properties of the common ivy extract alone.

## INTRODUCTION

## Background and Terms of Reference
Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003 1 establishes the rules governing the Community authorisation of additives for use in animal nutrition. In particular, Article 4(1) of that Regulation lays down that any person seeking authorisation for a feed additive or for a new use of feed additive shall submit an application in accordance with Article 7. In addition, Article 10(2) of that Regulation specifies that, for existing products within the meaning of Article 10(1), an application shall be submitted in accordance with Article 7 within a maximum of 7 years after the entry into force of this Regulation.
The European Commission received a request from Feed Flavourings Authorisation Consortium European Economic Interest Grouping (FFAC EEIG) 2 for authorisation/re‐evaluation of 29 additives (namely dill herb oil, dill seed extract, dill tincture, dong quai tincture, celery seed oil, celery seed extract (oleoresin), celery tincture, hares ear tincture, caraway seed oil, caraway oleoresin/extract, coriander oil, cumin oil, taiga root extract (solvent‐based, sb), taiga root tincture, fennel oil, fennel tincture, common ivy extract (sb), opoponax oil, ginseng tincture, parsley oil, parsley tincture, anise oil, anise tincture, ajowan oil, Ferula assa‐foetida oil, anise star oil, anise star tincture, anise star terpenes and omicha tincture) belonging to botanically defined group (BDG) 02 – Apiales/Austrobaileyales when used as feed additives for all animal species (category: sensory additives; functional group: flavourings). During the assessment, the applicant withdrew the application for nine additives. 3 These additives were deleted from the register of feed additives. 4 During the course of the assessment, this application was split and the present opinion covers only one of the 20 remaining additives under application: common ivy extract (sb) from the dried leaves of Hedera helix L. for all animal species. During the assessment, the applicant requested a change in the species and withdrew the application for chickens for fattening, turkeys for fattening, laying hens, salmon and ornamental fish. 5 , 6
The remaining 19 additives belonging to botanically defined group (BDG) 02 – Apiales/Austrobaileyales under application have been assessed in separate opinions.
According to Article 7(1) of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, the Commission forwarded the application to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) as an application under Article 4(1) (authorisation of a feed additive or new use of a feed additive) and under Article 10(2) (re‐evaluation of an authorised feed additive). EFSA received directly from the applicant the technical dossier in support of this application. The particulars and documents in support of the application were considered valid by EFSA as of 24 June 2019.
According to Article 8 of Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003, EFSA, after verifying the particulars and documents submitted by the applicant, shall undertake an assessment in order to determine whether the feed additive complies with the conditions laid down in Article 5. EFSA shall deliver an opinion on the safety for the target animals, consumer, user and the environment and on the efficacy of the feed additive consisting of an extract from the dried leaves of Hedera helix L., when used under the proposed conditions of use (see Section 3.3.3).

## Additional information
An extract from the dried leaves of Hedera helix L. (common ivy extract) is currently authorised as a feed additive according to the entry in the European Union Register of Feed Additives pursuant to Regulation (EC) No 1831/2003 (2b natural products – botanically defined). ## ASSESSMENT
The additive under assessment consists of common ivy extract, obtained from the dried leaves of Hedera helix L. and thymol added as denaturant. It is intended for use as a sensory additive (functional group: flavouring compounds) in feed for all animal species except poultry and fish.

## Origin and extraction
Hedera helix L. (the common or European Ivy) is a clinging evergreen vine belonging to the family Araliaceae. It is native to Europe, the Mediterranean region and to Iran and subsequently introduced into parts of North and South America and Australia. The plant may reach 30 m in height when a suitable support such as a tree or wall is present; otherwise, it can form a dense ground cover. In the initial stage of growth, ivy produces long stems seeking a vertical surface to which it attaches using adventitious roots. The leaves typically are three to five lobed in the earlier stages of growth, becoming unlobed as the plant ages.
The additive is extracted at ambient temperature from the dried leaves (sourced from Europe) with a solvent mixture consisting of ethanol and water (70:30 v/v). The plant to solvent ratio is 1:5. After extraction, the liquid extract is recovered, subjected to a deep bed filtration and thymol added as a denaturant (2270–2490 mg/kg additive). 14

## Uses other than feed flavouring
‘Ivy leaf (Hederae folium)’ from H. helix L. is described in monographs of the European Pharmacopoeia 11.0 (PhEur, 2022) and of the European Medicines Agency (EMA, 2017a, 2017b) for medicinal uses.

## Characterisation of the additive consisting of common ivy extract and thymol

Isoquercitrin, sometimes called isoquercetin, is the monoglucoside of quercetin. Glucose exists in two isomeric forms (as pyranose and furanose), which are in an equilibrium in solution, depending on the pH. In crystalline form, they can exist in different isomeric forms. Isoquercitrin and isoquercetin are nearly identical, although they may differ because isoquercetin has a pyranose ring (quercetin 3‐O‐glucopyranoside) and isoquercitrin has a furanose ring (quercetin 3‐O‐glucofuranoside). Published literature considers them to be the same compound and uses the names interchangeably. In the current assessment, the compound is referred to as isoquercitrin.

The EFSA Compendium of botanicals 21 reports as substances of potential concern for human and animal health the occurrence of calcium oxalate and triterpene saponins, such as hederacoside (synonym: hederasaponin) B and C and β‐hederin in the leaves and the aerial parts of H. helix.
According to existing monographs (PhEur, 2022; PhEur Commentary, 2019; EMA, 2017b), the dried leaves from H. helix are known to contain (i) as main components bidesmosidic triterpene saponins (about 2.5%–6%) mostly having hederagenin, oleanolic acid and bayogenin (synonym: 2β‐hydroxyhederagenin) as aglycones; the main saponin being hederacoside C besides other hederacosides 22 (B, D, E, F,G, H and I), (ii) smaller concentrations of monodesmosides triterpene saponins such as α‐hederin, β‐hederin and hederagenin‐3‐O‐β‐D‐glucoside; (iii) flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol including their 3‐O‐rutinosides and 3‐O‐glucosides; (iv) caffeic acid and its derivates, such as caffeoylquinic acids (i.e. chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, 3,5‐dicaffeoylquinic acid); (v) the coumarin glycoside scopoline, (vi) polyacetylenes (falcarinone, falcarinol and 11,12‐dihydrofalcarinol); (vii) phytosterols, such as stigmasterol, sitosterol, cholesterol, campesterol, α‐spinasterol; (viii) volatile oil (in the fresh leaves 0.1%–0.3%) containing methylethyl ketone, methyl isobutylketone, monoterpenes (e.g. α‐ and β‐pinene, sabinene) and sesquiterpenes (e.g. germacrene D, β‐caryophyllene).
The applicant carried out an extensive literature search on the chemical composition of H. helix and their botanical preparations and the possible presence of substances of concern. 23 Three cumulative databases (LIVIVO, OVID and PubMed) were used. The keywords used covered different aspects of safety, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria were provided by the applicant. The literature search (no time limits) identified 61 references investigating the composition of preparations from H. helix. The results of the literature search confirmed the information in the EFSA Compendium. In addition, two references mentioned the potential presence of the coumarin glycoside scopolin and its aglycone scopoletin in ivy leaf (EMA, 2017b; Risk profile of Hedera helix (ivy) extracts, 2012 24 ). These two compounds were not detected in five batches of the additive under assessment (limit of detection (LOD): 0.10 μg/mL). 25 An HPLC investigation with high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) did not identify compounds with molecular mass and structure corresponding to polyacetylene. 26 No other substances of concern in preparations obtained from Hedera helix L. were identified.

## Safety
No studies to support the safety for target animals, consumers and users were performed with the additive consisting of common ivy extract and thymol.
Common ivy extract contains plant‐derived proteins, lipids and sugars (see Table 1) which are not of concern and are not further considered.
Among the secondary plant metabolites (see Table 2), up to 3.5% (w/w) of the additive consisted of triterpene saponins, up to 7.3% of their aglycones, 0.64% of phenolic acids and 0.40% of flavonoids. Triterpene saponins were individually identified and quantified. Individual flavonoids and caffeolylquinic and other hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives were also identified and quantified. The most abundant flavonoid is rutin, representing about 88% of the total flavonoid fraction, followed by nicotiflorin, which represents about 11% of the fraction.
Hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives (3,5‐dicaffeoyl quinic acid, 4,5‐dicaffeoyl quinic acid and chlorogenic acid) are ubiquitous in food and feeds of plant origin. They are readily metabolised and excreted and are not expected to accumulate in animal tissues and products. These compounds are not of concern at concentrations resulting from the use of the additive at the maximum proposed use levels in feed and are not further considered in the assessment.
Ethyl hexadecanoate and thymol, two volatile components identified in the additive under assessment, have been already assessed as chemically defined flavourings for use in feed and food by the FEEDAP Panel, the EFSA Panel on Food Additives, Flavourings, Processing Aids and Materials in contact with Food (AFC) superseded in 2008 by the EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials, Enzymes, Flavourings and Processing Aids (CEF). ## Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism and Excretion
No ADME studies were made with the additive under assessment. The literature search provided by the applicant identified several publications investigating the pharmacokinetics of the individual saponins (hederacoside C, hederacoside D and α‐hederin) and their aglycones (oleanolic acid and hederagenin) in laboratory animals when administered as single compounds, as mixtures or in the form of extracts/formulations. The studies considered relevant are briefly described.

## Triterpene saponins and their aglycones
The pharmacokinetics of hederacoside C (HDC) were evaluated in the rat after oral and intravenous administration (Kim et al., 2013). Rats were intravenously administered HDC at 3, 12.5 or 25 mg/kg body weight (bw). In parallel, rats received by gavage HDC or ivy extract (dried 30% ethanolic extract of H. helix leaf) at doses equivalent to 12.5, 25 or 50 mg/kg bw HDC. Blood samples were collected from animals up to 480 min after administration and plasma samples analysed by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS). HDC was detected in plasma soon after oral administration (5 min) and plasma Cmax was attained before 1 h for the three doses tested, both for the single compound (81, 171, 811 ng/mL) and for the ivy extract (89, 89, 827 ng/mL). The absolute bioavailability of HDC, administered as such or in the ivy extract, was very low, ranging from 0.118% to 0.250%. The intestinal contents collected 24 h after oral administration were also analysed; the percentage of the dose present ranged from 0.9% to 2.2% for HDC and from 0.15% to 2.4% for the ivy extract. In the 24‐h urine sample, the fraction of the oral dose of HDC excreted was 0.15%, 0.44% and 0.03%, of the low, medium, and high dose, respectively. Similar values were obtained after oral application of ivy extract: 0.12%, 0.18% and 0.04%. In the gastrointestinal tract 24 h after HDC dosing, an appreciable percentage of the hydrolysis products (α‐hederin: 5.8%, 5.1%, 16.8% and hederagenin: 5.1%, 4.5%, 12.7%) was present for the three administered doses. None of these compounds were detected in plasma. For the ivy extract, the percentage of the corresponding three doses of HDC recovered in the gastrointestinal tract was 0.149%, 2.4%, and 1.5%, respectively. The data show that the pharmacokinetic of HDC, administered as such or as a component of ivy extract, is very similar.
The pharmacokinetic parameters of hederacoside C, hederacoside D and α‐hederin were determined in the rat after oral or intravenous administration of a mixture of the three compounds (Yu et al., 2016). A comparison of bioavailability of the compounds was also made after oral administration of an ethanolic extract of H. helix containing 44, 63 and 34 mg/g hederacoside C, hederacoside D and α‐hederin, respectively. Rats were intravenously administered a mixture of 2.2 mg hederacoside C/kg bw, 3.15 mg hederacoside D/kg bw and 1.7 mg α‐hederin/kg bw or orally given the same mixture or the ethanolic extract containing hederacoside C, hederacoside D and α‐hederin at 22, 31.5 and 17 mg/kg bw, respectively. The compounds were analysed by ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC)‐MS/MS in blood plasma collected at several time points. After administration of the mixture or the ethanolic extract, the absolute bioavailability of the compounds did not differ and was lower than 1%. After oral administration of the mixture of the three compounds or the extract, no relevant differences were noted in t1/2, Cmax, AUC0 − t and bioavailability.
The pharmacokinetic parameters of α‐hederin and the nature of some deglycosilated and phase II metabolites were determined in the rat after oral or intravenous administration (Li et al., 2015). α‐Hederin was orally administered to rats at a single dose of 10 mg/kg bw or intravenously at 2 mg/kg bw. Blood samples were collected up to 24 h and plasma analysed by LC‐MS/MS. After oral administration, α‐hederin was only detected in plasma samples up to 6–8 h. The maximum plasma concentration of 14.5 ng/mL was reached at about 1 h. The bioavailability was very low (0.14%). After i.v. administration of α‐hederin, two metabolites were identified in plasma, arabinose‐3‐β‐hederagenin sulfate and hederagenin sulfate. Free hederagenin was not detected. After p.o. administration of α‐hederin, only the metabolite hederagenin sulfate was detected in plasma.
Yang et al. (2011) administered to rats a single oral dose of 400 mg/kg bw of Fructus akebiae extract containing about 70% hederagenin (corresponding to about 280 mg/kg bw hederagenin). Blood was collected at several time points, immediately pre‐dose and up to 300 min after dosing. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were taken before and 20 min post‐dosing. Plasma and CSF samples were analysed by ultra fast LC‐MS/MS. Hederagenin was detected in plasma within 5 min and its concentration peaked about 20 min after oral administration (Cmax of 47.73 ng/mL). At 20 min after dosing, hederagenin was present in CSF at 6.17 ng/mL. Plasma t1/2 was 44 min. The authors concluded that the absorption after oral administration is rapid and that the compound could cross the blood–brain barrier.
Yang and Wang (2022) tentatively identified by UPLC‐qTOF/MS 35 possible metabolites of hederagenin after oral administration to rats of a single dose of 51.21 mg/kg bw, once a day, for four consecutive days. Blood was collected at several time points up to 36 h after the last administration. The compound and several metabolites were detected at 9 h after the last administration, but not after 30 h. The following metabolic pathways were proposed by the authors: decarboxylation, dehydration, demethylation or methyl shift, deoxygenation, ring‐opening and double unsaturated link formation.
The presence of 6.7% of bayogenin was detected in the extract under application (Table 2). This aglycone is structurally similar to hederagenin, differing only by the presence of an additional hydroxy group in the A ring (see Figure 1). Pharmacokinetic studies with bayogenin are not available, but considering the similarity with hederagenin, a similar pharmacokinetic profile is expected.
For oleanolic acid, some studies on its pharmacokinetics in the rat (Jeong et al., 2007; Li et al., 2015) and in humans (Rada et al., 2015; Song et al., 2006) were submitted.
In a rat study, animals were administered oleanolic acid at doses of 0.5, 1 or 2 mg/kg bw intravenously and doses of 10, 25 or 50 mg/kg bw by gavage (Jeong et al., 2007). After oral administration, oleanolic acid was detected in the plasma only at the medium and high dose (limit of quantification, 2 ng/mL). Cmax was 74 and 132 ng/mL and Tmax was 25 and 21 min, respectively, for the doses of 25 and 50 mg/kg bw. The absolute oral bioavailability was 0.7%. The mean t1/2 was 46.5 and 65.3 min at 25 and 50 mg/kg bw, respectively. In the 24‐h urine sample, the percentage of the three orally administered doses of oleanolic acid excreted was lower than 0.06%. The poor absorption of oleanolic acid observed in vivo was confirmed in vitro, in an experiment with a Caco‐2 cell model. In the same study, oleanolic acid was also incubated with rat liver microsomes. After 1 h incubation, only 40% of oleanolic acid was present. Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) analysis of the incubation medium showed metabolites of oleanolic acid tentatively characterised as hydroxyl‐ and dihydroxyl‐oleanolic acid, although the exact hydroxylation position was not identified.
In another study in rats (Li et al., 2015), oleanolic acid was orally administered at a dose of 30 mg/kg bw and plasma and liver samples analysed by UPLC‐MS/MS. A plasma concentration peak of 228 ng/mL was observed at 1 h after administration. Cmax in plasma and in liver (210 ng/mL) was similar, indicating a rapid distribution in this organ. AUC0–24 h was 1507 ng/mLh in liver and 1602 ng/mLh in plasma. T1/2 was about 8 h, both for plasma and liver.
In humans, a single dose of 40 mg of oleanolic acid was given orally to 18 male volunteers in the form of capsules (Song et al., 2006). Blood samples were collected up to 48 h after administration and plasma was analysed by LC‐MS/MS. The mean values of Cmax, Tmax and T1/2 of oleanolic acid were 12.12 ng/mL, 5.2 h and 8.7 h, respectively. Rada et al. (2015) also investigated the pharmacokinetics of oleanolic acid in humans. Nine male volunteers ingested oleanolic acid in a meal that included 70 g of pomace olive oil containing 30 mg of dissolved oleanolic acid. Blood was collected up to 7 h post‐ingestion and serum was analysed by GC‐FID. Cmax was about 600 ng/mL, attained at 3 h and T1/2 was 4.6 h. Due to the very low absorption and the rapid excretion of the absorbed oleanolic acid, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that accumulation in tissues is very unlikely.
Studies on the metabolism of triterpene saponins and their aglycones in the target species were not available. In experimental animals, the available data showed that the saponins (mainly hederagenin glycosides) present in the extract are poorly absorbed after oral administration and are hydrolysed in the gastrointestinal tract to a certain degree (up to 12.7% for hederagenin). The absorption of the aglycone hederagenin is also very low, and it can be expected that this will also be the case for the related aglycone bayogenin. Only conjugated hederagenin (mainly as sulfate ester) was observed in the plasma after oral application. In vitro and in vivo studies with hederagenin have shown that, additionally to conjugation with sulfate, a considerable transformation and breakdown of the triterpene structure may occur. Thus, it is expected that pentacyclic triterpene saponins and their aglycones from the additive, if present in animal‐derived food products, would be at very low levels since absorption is low and the low amount absorbed is rapidly excreted.

## Toxicology

## Genotoxicity
For the assessment of genotoxicity of chemical mixtures, the EFSA Scientific Committee (SC) recommends that, first, the chemically defined substances are assessed individually for their potential genotoxicity using all available information, including read‐across and QSAR considerations about their genotoxic potential (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2019b). Then, genotoxicity studies performed with whole mixtures are taken into account if deemed relevant.
The FEEDAP Panel notes that no studies on the additive under assessment were provided by the applicant. Therefore, the potential genotoxicity of identified constituents was first considered. Then, a genotoxicity study performed with a H. helix extract similar to the additive under assessment was evaluated.

## Triterpene saponins and their aglycons
The possible mutagenicity and genotoxicity of oleanolic acid were evaluated using QSAR models. The VEGA models (www.vegahub.eu) for Ames test and in vivo micronucleus test provided results with good reliability, indicating a lack of activity.
The literature search provided by the applicant (see Section 3.3) identified several publications investigating the possible genotoxicity of triterpene saponins and their aglycones, and of preparations obtained from H. helix. The studies on the single compounds and those considered relevant for the assessment of the genotoxic potential of these preparations were evaluated by the FEEDAP Panel and reported below.
Oleanolic acid did not induce gene mutations in a bacterial reverse mutation assay (Ames test) performed with Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA 98, TA 100, TA 102, TA 1535 and TA 1537, at concentrations ranging from 50 to 5000 μg/plate in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (Gupta et al., 2022).
The saponins identified in the additive under assessment are pentacyclic triterpenoid saponins structurally similar to saponins detected in quillaia extract. Triterpenoid saponins in quillaia extract consist of glycosides of quillaic acid, which is a major pentacyclic triterpenoid aglycone of quillaia saponins. Based on this structural similarity of the aglycones, the FEEDAP Panel considers that applying read‐across from pentacyclic triterpenoid saponins from quillaia extract would be appropriate for the assessment of genotoxicity of saponins from common ivy. No concern for genotoxicity of saponins was identified by the EFSA Panel on food additives and flavourings (FAF) Panel in the scientific opinion on the re‐evaluation of quillaia extract as food additive (EFSA FAF Panel, 2019).
Negative results were also obtained in the frame of a master thesis investigating the potential to induce gene mutations of hederacoside C, α‐hederin and H. helix extract (Hedera helix ethanolic extract (80% native) containing 10.4% of hederacoside C) by a commercial kit for the fluctuation Ames test (EBPI's AMES‐384ISO) (Baghdadi, 2021). Two strains of Salmonella Typhimurium detecting frame shifts (TA98) and point mutations (TA100) were used in the absence of metabolic activation. Hederacoside C and α‐hederin were tested at concentrations ranging from 0.73 to 22 μg/mL, while H. helix extract was analysed at concentrations ranging from 7 to 210 μg/mL. Hederacoside C, α‐hederin and H. helix extract did not increase the frequency of gene mutations in any experimental condition applied.
The available information for triterpene saponins and their aglycones from common ivy do not raise concerns for genotoxicity.

## Flavonols
For the evaluation of the flavonoids present in the additive, rutin, isoquercitrin and nicotiflorin, the activity of the aglycones quercetin and kaempferol is considered relevant. Genotoxic effects have been detected for the flavonol aglycones quercetin and kaempferol in in vitro assays and in vivo assays with parenteral application of the compounds (for a summary, see the EFSA assessment of ginkgo extract, EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2024b). The effects depend on DNA intercalation after direct cellular contact of quercetin (and kaempferol after metabolic transformation to quercetin). It was demonstrated in several pharmacokinetic studies that no free quercetin will occur in the circulating system after oral uptake of flavonol aglycones or their glycosides. Thus, the flavonols occurring in the additive are of no concern for genotoxicity.

## Toxicological studies

## Triterpene saponins and their aglycones
Toxicological studies with triterpene saponins present in the additive and their aglycones are not available. The applicant made reference to the EFSA evaluation of triterpene saponins present in quillaia extract (EFSA FAF Panel, 2019) and provided two studies with ursolic acid, a compound which differs from oleanolic acid only for the position of one methyl group.
Geerlofs et al. (2020a) tested ursolic acid in a 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats. Doses of 100, 300 or 1000 mg/kg bw per day were administered to 20 male and 20 female Han Wistar rats by repeated oral gavage. Ursolic acid did not cause any deaths, abnormal body weights or abnormal pathology at all test doses. In addition, no changes were observed in behaviour, neurotoxicity, coagulation, haematology or clinical chemistry that is related to the administration of ursolic acid. The study indicates that oral dosing of ursolic acid for 90 consecutive days does not lead to toxic effects up to the highest dose of 1000 mg/kg bw per day. Therefore, the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) for ursolic acid is 1000 mg/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested.
The teratogenicity of ursolic acid was tested by Geerlofs et al. (2020b) in a study with 20 pregnant female Sprague Dawley rats by repeated oral gavage of 100, 300 or 1000 mg/kg bw per day from day 0 to day 14. The adult study was terminated at study day 15 and the developmental study was terminated at day 21 of gestation. No deaths or abnormal (reproductive) organs or body weights were observed up to the highest dose of 1000 mg/kg bw per day. The administration of ursolic acid resulted in no significant toxicological changes in either maternal or fetal subjects in terms of body weight, organ weights, food consumption, gross pathology, sex organs and maternal and fetal performances. This study indicates that oral dosing with ursolic acid is safe for adult female rats and their offspring and the NOAEL for ursolic acid is 1000 mg/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested.
The applicant also referred to the EFSA evaluation of quillaia extract (E 999), which identified a NOAEL of 1500 mg quillaia extract/kg bw per day (the highest dose tested) in a 2‐year study in rats (Drake et al., 1982). ‘Considering that the adverse effects reported were due to the presence of saponins in the extract, that saponins were present in quillaia extract Type 1 (around 20%) and using an uncertainty factor of 100, the Panel derived an acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 3 mg saponins/kg bw per day for E 999’ (EFSA FAF Panel, 2019).
In the current assessment, the NOAEL of 1000 mg/kg bw per day identified for ursolic acid is extrapolated to oleanolic acid, hederagenin and bayogenin.
For the glycosides, α‐hederin, hederacosides B, C and D, read across is applied from the NOAEL of 1500 mg quillaia extract/kg bw per day, corresponding to 300 mg saponins/kg bw per day (calculated considering that the extract contains 20% saponins). Although quillaia saponins differ in the sugar moieties from Hedera saponins, the corresponding sapogenins are pentacyclic triterpenes similar to those present in H. helix.

## Flavonol glycosides
The flavonoid content of common ivy extract consists of the glycosides nicotiflorin (synonyms: kaempferol‐3‐O‐rutinoside, kaempferol‐3‐rhamnoglucoside), rutin (synonyms: quercetin‐3‐O‐rutinoside, quercetin‐3‐rhamnoglucoside) and isoquercitrin (isoquercetin, quercetin 3‐O‐glucoside 32 ). For the evaluation of the flavonoids present in the additive, the activity of the aglycones quercetin and kaempferol is considered relevant. The two aglycones are structurally and metabolically related (kaempferol is metabolically transformed into quercetin), and read‐across would be possible. No data are available which would allow the identification of a reference point for quercetin or kaempferol.
However, the applicant submitted two studies with enzymatically decomposed rutin as test item, which consisted of 95% isoquercitrin. 33 In a 13‐week oral toxicity study, the test item was administered to Wistar rats at dietary concentrations of 0%, 0.2%, 1% or 5% (Hasumura et al., 2004). At the top dose, decreased blood cell count, haemoglobin concentrations and haematocrit were observed in males but not in females. Based on the results of the study, NOAELs for male and female rats were estimated to be 1% and 5% in feed, respectively, corresponding to 539 and 3227 mg/kg bw per day. In a chronic toxicity study (Tamura et al., 2010), the same test item was administered to Wistar rats at dietary concentrations of 0%, 0.04%, 0.2%, 1% or 5% for 52 weeks. Based on effects seen at the highest dose tested in males and females, the NOAEL for the test item was estimated to be 1% in both sexes (corresponding to 542.4 mg/kg bw per day for males and 674.0 mg/kg bw per day for females).
The FEEDAP Panel considers that the flavonoids present in the additive are structurally related to the main component of the test item and read‐across is possible. Therefore, from the NOAEL of 542 mg/kg bw per day identified in the chronic study with enzymatically decomposed rutin, a NOAEL of 515 mg/kg bw per day was calculated for the glycoside isoquercitrin considering that it represents 95% of the test item. Taking account of the relative molecular weight of the glycosides, 34 this NOAEL was extrapolated to the other flavonol glycosides present in the additive, resulting in NOAELs of 677 mg/kg bw per day for rutin and 659 mg/kg bw per day for nicotiflorin.

## Hedera helix extract
The possible teratogenicity of H. helix saponins was investigated in two studies (Baghdadi, 2021; Duffy et al., 1997) with different outcomes. The FEEDAP Panel notes that, although the results obtained by Baghdadi (2021) in the frame of a master thesis were not further published in a scientific journal, they were carefully considered in the current assessment as they were obtained with a test item similar to the additive under assessment.
Duffy et al. (1997) tested α‐hederin, which was applied subcutaneously to rats. Daily repeated doses > 10 μM/kg bw (7.5 mg/kg bw) of α‐hederin caused symptoms of maternal toxicity and embryonic malformations. Higher doses of 20 and 30 μM/kg bw (15 and 22.5 mg/kg bw) resulted in a 10‐fold increase in hepatic metallothionein and considerable disturbances of zinc homeostasis. According to the authors, the malformations are a result of the reduction of circulating Zn in the maternal serum and fetal tissue. EMA (2017b) questioned the relevance of the results of this study, considering the route of exposure and the mode of action. Compared to oral application, subcutaneous application provides a direct distribution of the compound to the systemic circulation, without prior liver passage.
Baghdadi (2021) used a commercial standardised ethanolic H. helix extract, which contained 10.4% hederacoside C together with several other hedera glycosides. These are mostly pentaglycosides, with ether and ester bindings to the aglycone. Although the characterisation of the test item is limited to the content of hederacoside C, and α‐hederin or bayogenin was not analysed, the FEEDAP Panel considers that the test item is similar to the common ivy extract present in the additive under assessment, because of the similar preparation of the extracts. Baghdadi applied the extract to pregnant female rats in daily oral doses of 7, 70 and 210 mg/kg bw between the 5th and 20th day of gestation. No maternal toxicity nor fetal malformations were observed.
The FEEDAP Panel is of the opinion that the discrepancies between the studies described above are due to differences in the pharmacokinetic behaviour of the hedera saponins when applied either orally (Baghdadi, 2021) or subcutaneously (Duffy et al., 1997), as already noted by EMA (2017b). The observed induction of metallothionein after s.c. injection of α‐hederin at doses > 7.5 mg/kg bw was most probably caused by the parent compound. Pharmacokinetic studies with α‐hederin orally applied to rats (Li et al., 2015) showed that the bioavailability was in the range of 0.1% and only the metabolite hederagenin sulfate could be detected in the plasma. Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that the teratogenic effects attributed to α‐hederin after s.c. application in the rat study by Duffy et al. (1997) are unlikely to occur after oral exposure to common ivy extract.

## Conclusions
The triterpene saponins and their aglycones and the flavonol glycosides present in common ivy extract do not raise concern for genotoxicity.
H. helix saponins are not teratogenic after oral exposure.
For oleanolic acid, hederagenin and bayogenin, the NOAEL of 1000 mg/kg bw per day for ursolic acid is selected as a group NOAEL. For the glycosides, α‐hederin, hederacosides B, C and D, read‐across is applied from the NOAEL of 300 mg saponins/kg bw per day identified for saponins of quillaia extract. For flavonol glycosides, the FEEDAP Panel identified a NOAEL of 515 mg/kg bw per day for isoquercitrin, derived from a study with enzymatically decomposed rutin. Taking account of the relative molecular weights of the flavonol glycosides, this dose is equivalent to 677 mg/kg bw per day of rutin and 659 mg/kg bw per day of nicotiflorin.

## Safety for the target species
Tolerance studies in the target species and/or toxicological studies in laboratory animals made with the additive under assessment were not submitted. In the absence of these data, the approach to the safety assessment of a mixture whose individual components are known is based on the safety assessment of each individual component (component‐based approach).
Thymol [04.006] is assessed based on the results of the tolerance studies carried out in the target species with the mixture of flavourings ‘Herbal’ (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023b).
For the other components of common ivy extract, subchronic oral toxicity studies are available, from which NOAEL values were identified.

## Components tested in tolerance trials with mixtures of flavourings: thymol
At the maximum proposed use levels of 250–600 mg/kg complete feed and considering the highest specified concentrations of thymol [04.005] in the extract (0.3%), the concentration of thymol would be 0.75–1.80 mg/kg complete feed. These concentrations are well below the concentration of 125 mg/kg complete feed considered safe for all animal species when thymol was tested in tolerance trials with the mixture of flavourings ‘Herbal’ (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023b). Therefore, no concern for the target species is expected from the presence of thymol in the additive.

## Triterpene saponins and their aglycone, flavonoids and volatile components other than those tested in tolerance trials
Based on considerations related to structural and metabolic similarities, the triterpene saponins identified in common ivy extract were allocated to the same assessment group, and their aglycones in a separate assessment group. Similarly, the identified flavonoids were allocated to one assessment group. Apart from thymol, the volatile compounds present in the tincture are ethyl hexanoate [09.193], a compound belonging to chemical group (CG) 1, and thymoquinone. The allocation of the components to the assessment groups is shown in Table 6.
For hazard characterisation, each component of an assessment group was first assigned to the structural class according to Cramer classification using Toxtree (version 3.1.0, May 2018 35 ). For some components in the assessment group, toxicological data were available to identify NOAELs. Structural and metabolic similarity among the components in the assessment groups was assessed to explore the application of read‐across, allowing extrapolation from a known NOAEL of a component of an assessment group to the other components of the group with no available NOAEL or, if sufficient evidence were available for members of a (sub‐)assessment group, to derive a (sub‐)assessment group NOAEL.
For triterpene saponins, the FEEDAP Panel identified a group NOAEL of 300 mg/kg bw per day, and for their aglycones, a NOAEL of 1000 mg/kg bw per day.
For flavonol glycosides, the following NOAEL values were identified: 515 mg/kg bw per day for isoquercitrin, 677 mg/kg bw per day for rutin and 659 mg/kg bw per day for nicotiflorin.
For CG 1, a group NOAEL of 120 mg/kg per day was identified from the toxicological data available for several compounds, which is applied to ethyl hexadecanoate [09.193] in the current assessment. For thymoquinone, toxicity studies were not available and read‐across was not possible. Therefore, the threshold of toxicological concern (TTC) approach was applied (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2019c).
For each component in the assessment group, exposure in target animals (expressed as mg/kg bw per day) was estimated considering the maximum proposed use level, the percentage of the component in the extract and the default values for body weight and feed intake according to the guidance on the safety of feed additives for target species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017b). For those compounds covered by specifications (bayogenin and hederacoside C, see Section 3.3.1), the indicated maximum specified concentration is used for the calculation of exposure. For the other components, the highest analysed concentration in the additive is used.
For risk characterisation, the margin of exposure (MOE) was calculated for each component as the ratio between the reference point and the exposure. For an assessment group, (i) when a group reference point is available, a group MOE was calculated for the combined intake; (ii) when different reference points are available for the components, the combined (total) margin of exposure (MOET) was calculated as the reciprocal of the sum of the reciprocals of the MOE for the individual substances (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2019a). An MOE > 100 allowed for interspecies differences and intra‐individual variability.

At the proposed use levels in complete feed, the MOE exceeds the value of 100 for all the intended species. Because glucuronidation is an important metabolic reaction to facilitate the excretion of the components of the extract and considering that cats have an unusually low capacity for glucuronidation particularly for aromatic compounds (Court & Greenblatt, 1997; Lautz et al., 2021), the use of common ivy extract as additive in cat feed needs a wider margin of exposure. The maximum proposed use levels in complete feed are safe for the target species: 450 mg/kg for piglets and veal calves; 300 mg/kg for pigs for fattening, sows, rabbits and guinea pigs; 280 mg/kg for horses; 250 mg/kg for cattle for fattening, dairy cows and sheep/goats; and 600 mg/kg for dogs and cats. These levels are extrapolated to physiologically related species. For other species (other than poultry, ornamental birds and all aquatic species), the maximum level of 250 mg/kg complete feed is considered safe.

## Safety for the consumer
There is no evidence for the use in food of common ivy extract from H. helix in the Fenaroli's Handbook of Flavour Ingredients (Burdock, 2009) or elsewhere.
No data on residues in products of animal origin were made available for any of the constituents of the extract. However, the FEEDAP Panel recognises that the triterpene saponins and their aglycones and flavonol glycosides and their metabolites are expected to be poorly absorbed and, if absorbed, extensively metabolised and excreted in the target species (see Section 3.2.1). Therefore, the FEEDAP Panel considers it unlikely that the use of the additive would result in a relevant increase of the intake of the individual constituents by humans consuming products of animal origin.
No safety concern is expected for the consumer from the use of additive at the proposed use level in feed for the target animals.

## Safety for the user
No specific experimental data were provided by the applicant regarding the safety of the additive for users.
A literature search related to the safety of preparations obtained from H. helix for the users did not retrieve any relevant publication. 36
The Commentary to the European Pharmacopoeia (PhEur Commentary, 2019) and the IPCS evaluation (IPCS, 1997) report that fresh ivy leaves can cause severe allergic contact dermatitis, with falcarinol, didehydrofalcarinol and 11,12‐dehydrofalcarinol identified as allergens.
The applicant provided a safety data sheet 37 for common ivy extract, which identified hazards for skin, eyes and the respiratory tract. The additive contains thymol, for which there is an opinion of the committee for risk assessment (RAC) proposing harmonised classification and labelling in accordance with the CLP Regulation (Regulation (EC) 1272/2008) 38 (ECHA, 2024).
The FEEDAP Panel concludes that the additive consisting of common ivy extract and thymol should be considered as an irritant to skin and eyes and as a dermal and respiratory sensitiser. Exposure of users by any route is considered a risk.

## Safety for the environment
Hedera helix L. occurs naturally and is found widespread in Europe. Thymol [04.006] is also naturally occurring in the environment at levels above those resulting from the use of the additive in feed.
The use of the additive consisting of common ivy extract and thymol in animal feed under the proposed conditions of use is not expected to pose a risk to the environment.

## Efficacy
In a sensory evaluation performed by five trained panellists, it was shown that the addition of the additive at 1000 mg/kg to the selected food matrix (crushed rusk) had a strong sensory impact. The aroma profile of the reference matrix changes from mainly roasty, earthy, buttery and sweet to dominantly olive‐like/phenolic, herbaceous, green and resin‐like. 39
The applicant provided three triangle tests performed in food with the additive under assessment (consisting of common ivy extract and thymol) at three concentration levels (25, 250 and 1000 mg/kg), 40 according to DIN EN ISO 4120: 2021‐06. The tests were aimed at verifying if the addition of the additive to a food matrix leads to flavour changes detectable by a triangle test. In a triangle test, the assessors are required to compare the test product against a control product. Each assessor is presented with three samples (two are the same and one is different) and asked to evaluate the samples and select the ‘different’ sample (ISO, 2021).
Rusk was selected as the food matrix for testing because of its neutral sensory profile, which would allow the panellists to detect subtle differences imparted by added flavourings, both orthonasally and retronasally. In each study, crushed rusk (40 g) was mixed with tap water (200 g) to prepare the ‘control’ sample to obtain a smooth palatable product (a porridge‐like slurry) suitable for testing. The ‘treated’ samples were prepared by adding the additive under assessment to the ‘control’ sample to achieve a concentration of 25, 250 or 1000 mg/kg. The samples were placed in glasses wrapped in aluminium foil to mask the slight colouring effect of the sample addition. The glasses were coded and presented as a triangle. The panellists had to identify the different samples in every triangle. Sensory evaluation was performed retronasally by 12 panellists. The rate of identification was 75% (9/12) for 25 mg/kg and 250 mg/kg and 92% (11/12) for 1000 mg/kg. From the results, it could be concluded that the ‘control’ and the ‘treated’ samples are different at α level of 0.01 for 25 mg/kg and 250 mg/kg and of 0.001 for 1000 mg/kg.
The data provided indicates that the additive under assessment (common ivy extract and thymol) has the potential to be efficacious as a sensory additive in feed. However, the Panel notes that, considering the well‐known flavouring properties of thymol, which is added to the extract (0.3% w/w), it is not possible to conclude on the flavouring properties of the common ivy extract alone.
