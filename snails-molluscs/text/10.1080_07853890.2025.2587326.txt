The lactate-lactylation axis in renal fibrosis: potential mechanisms in diabetic kidney disease.

# The lactate-lactylation axis in renal fibrosis: potential mechanisms in diabetic kidney disease

## Abstract
AbstractIntroductionDisturbances in energy metabolism are increasingly recognized as a key factor in the development of diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Among the pathological features of advanced DKD, renal fibrosis is both common and irreversible. With growing insight into metabolic reprogramming, lactate and its epigenetic derivative—lactylation—have gained attention as potential modulators of disease progression.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in databases including PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science using keywords such as ‘lactate’, ‘lactylation’, ‘diabetic kidney disease’, ‘renal fibrosis’, and ‘metabolic reprogramming’. Studies were included if they focused on the association between lactate/lactylation and DKD-related renal fibrosis, with priority given to preclinical (animal models, cell experiments) and clinical (human biopsy, cohort studies) evidence. Exclusion criteria were non-relevant studies, duplicates and articles with insufficient data.ResultsIn DKD, elevated lactate levels are associated with altered energy metabolism (enhanced glycolysis, impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation), inflammation activation (macrophage polarization, pro-inflammatory cytokine release), and excessive extracellular matrix deposition in renal tissues. Quantitatively, studies have shown that urinary lactate levels in DKD patients are 2.3–3.5 times higher than those in healthy controls, and lactate concentrations >2.5 mM can suppress mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in proximal tubular epithelial cells. Through lactylation modification, lactate regulates the activity of key molecules: histone lactylation (e.g. H3K14la) modulates the transcription of fibrosis-related genes, while non-histone lactylation (including PKM2, Fis1, Twist, Snail lactylation) affects glycolytic enzyme activity, mitochondrial function, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, collectively contributing to renal fibrosis.ConclusionThe lactate-lactylation axis is closely associated with renal fibrosis progression in DKD, and targeting this axis offers a promising therapeutic strategy to potentially slow fibrosis and preserve renal function in DKD. Consequently, inhibiting lactate dehydrogenase A, modulating monocarboxylate transporters, or targeting lactylation enzymes may provide novel treatment avenues. However, current evidence remains largely correlative, underscoring the need for large-scale cohort studies and early-phase clinical trials to validate its translational potential.Keywords: Lactate, lactylation, diabetic kidney disease, renal fibrosis

## Introduction
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is one of the common and fatal complications of diabetes patients. With the global prevalence of diabetes, DKD now accounts for nearly 40% of end-stage renal disease cases [1]. Notably, the excess mortality associated with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes is primarily concentrated in patients with chronic kidney disease, with a 2–3-fold higher all-cause mortality rate compared to diabetic patients without renal involvement [2–4]. Despite advances in clinical management, the global prevalence of DKD continues to rise alongside the diabetes epidemic, highlighting an urgent need to explore novel pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic targets.
Renal fibrosis is a major contributor to disease progression, and it is also a common pathological feature in the advanced stages of DKD. The main features include excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, tubular atrophy, and interstitial cell proliferation [5]. This fibrotic progression is driven by long-term hyperglycaemia-induced dysregulation of renal cell function, including epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [6], endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) [7], and activation of pro-fibrotic signalling pathways such as TGF-β1 [8], PI3K/Akt [9], and Wnt/β-catenin [10]. Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming serves as a fundamental driver of renal fibrosis in DKD. Under hyperglycaemic conditions, renal cells undergo a shift in energy production, with enhanced glycolysis and impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), leading to the accumulation of metabolic intermediates that further exacerbate inflammation and fibrosis [11–14].
Among the metabolic intermediates altered in DKD, lactate—the end product of glycolysis—has recently gained attention for its multifaceted roles in cellular metabolism and signalling. A key process governing lactate dynamics is the lactate shuttle mechanism, which refers to the transport of lactate between cells or tissues via proton-coupled monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) to maintain metabolic balance and acid-base homeostasis [15]. In the kidney, this shuttle facilitates lactate exchange between glycolytic cells and oxidative cells, potentially modulating intercellular communication in pathological states. Notably, accumulating studies have reported an association between elevated lactate levels and renal fibrosis in DKD [16,17]. For instance, in DKD renal tissues, lactate accumulation is associated with enhanced pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages—characterized by upregulated glycolytic enzyme expression and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine release—a process that further promotes excessive ECM deposition, a key feature of renal fibrosis [18].
Moreover, studies have confirmed that lactylation is an important way for lactate to function, contributing to tumour cell metabolic adaptation, macrophage polarization, angiogenesis, neuronal regulation, and other essential biological activities [19–21]. In DKD, lactate mediates lactylation of both histone and non-histone proteins, and these modifications collectively promote renal fibrosis [22,23]. In DKD, histone lactylation (e.g. H3K14 lactylation) alters chromatin accessibility to upregulate the transcription of fibrosis-related genes such as KLF5, which further drives EMT [22]. Meanwhile, non-histone lactylation targets metabolic enzymes (e.g. PKM2) and transcription factors (e.g. Snail1, Twist1), disrupting energy metabolism and activating pro-fibrotic signalling pathways [24–26].
Current therapeutic strategies for DKD primarily focus on controlling traditional risk factors, including strict blood glucose management (e.g. sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 [SGLT2] inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists [GLP-1RA]), blood pressure regulation (e.g. angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers), and modulation of the renin-angiotensin system (e.g. mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) [27–30]. While these interventions can slow DKD progression in early stages, they fail to halt or reverse renal fibrosis in advanced disease. Moreover, emerging therapies are increasingly exploring the potential of targeting metabolic reprogramming, a key driver of fibrotic progression in DKD. For instance, SGLT2 inhibitors reduce renal glucose reabsorption and subsequent aberrant glycolysis, while GLP-1RA receptor agonists alleviate renal lipid peroxidation [31,32]. However, neither directly addresses lactate metabolism or lactylation-related mechanisms, highlighting the need for more comprehensive metabolic interventions in current DKD management.
Given the pivotal role of metabolic reprogramming in DKD and the emerging relevance of lactate-lactylation axis, this review aims to: (1) systematically synthesize evidence on the association between lactate metabolism, lactylation, and renal fibrosis in DKD; (2) clarify the molecular mechanisms by which lactate and lactylation modulate renal cell function (e.g. energy metabolism, inflammatory response) in the context of DKD; (3) summarize potential therapeutic targets targeting the lactate-lactylation axis; and (4) highlight unmet research needs to guide future translational studies. By focusing on these objectives, we seek to provide novel insights into DKD pathogenesis and identify strategies to address the current limitations in fibrosis-focused therapy.

## The role of renal fibrosis in DKD
Renal fibrosis in DKD is not a static process driven by isolated linear pathways, but a dynamic network involving intercellular crosstalk, ECM remodelling, cellular senescence, and metabolic dysregulation—all of which converge to promote irreversible renal damage. These dynamic processes act as upstream triggers, modulating the activity of pro-fibrotic signalling pathways and renal cell functional states to accelerate fibrosis.

## The heterogeneity of fibroblasts
Notably, we cannot overlook a critical player in DKD fibrosis: renal fibroblasts. They are not a homogeneous population, but a complex coalition of cell subtypes with distinct origins, functions, and metabolic states. They mainly originate from activated resident interstitial fibroblasts, trans-differentiated pericytes, bone marrow-derived fibrocytes, and EMT/EndMT-derived cells [33]. Functionally, resident fibroblasts and pericytes are key ECM producers, bone marrow fibrocytes amplify fibrosis indirectly via immunomodulation, and EMT/EndMT-derived cells disrupt tissue barriers [34]. Metabolically, bone marrow fibrocytes are inherently glycolysis-dependent to support inflammation, while the others rely on enhanced glycolysis for ECM synthesis [35]. This heterogeneity explains why targeting a single source works poorly, pointing to subset-specific metabolic pathways as potential precision targets for DKD fibrosis.

## ECM remodelling
A key pathological feature of renal fibrosis lies in the irreversible dysregulation of ECM remodelling homeostasis [36]. This process not only involves excessive ECM accumulation, but more fundamentally, the disruption of the dynamic balance between anabolic and catabolic metabolism of ECM, ultimately leading to the formation of pathological scars [37]. Following renal injury, activated inflammatory responses drive infiltrating immune cells to secrete pro-fibrotic factors such as TGF-β and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), which then recruit and activate fibroblasts, resulting in hyperactive ECM synthesis [38]. If the injury persists, this positive feedback loop is continuously amplified, driving the progression of fibrosis [38]. Notably, activated myofibroblasts prefer glycolysis; this metabolic adaptation enables the rapid production of large amounts of ATP and the provision of biosynthetic precursors such as nucleotides and amino acids, leading to excessive synthesis of ECM components [39].
However, ECM accumulation in DKD is not solely due to overproduction—it is also exacerbated by impaired degradation capacity. ECM degradation primarily relies on matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), whose activity is regulated by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases [40]. A study has shown that Qianjin Wenwu decoction, a traditional Korean medicine, suppresses renal interstitial fibrosis in unilateral ureteral obstruction mice by enhancing ECM degradation through regulating MMP-9/TIMP-1 homeostasis, thus showing potential for treating DKD [37]. Additionally, advanced glycation end products in DKD can induce cross-linking of collagen fibres, rendering them resistant to degradation by MMPs [41].

## Targeting ECM degradation facilitates fibrosis resolution
It is particularly noteworthy that ECM degradation is indispensable for fibrosis regression. Fibroblasts, macrophages, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and tumour cells have been confirmed to possess the ability to degrade ECM [42–46]. Given that existing anti-fibrotic research often focuses on promoting the proliferation of tissue cells or inhibiting fibroblast activation, it is crucial to consider the obstacle posed by deposited ECM to tissue repair in anti-fibrotic therapy. Cells with ECM-degrading capacity have emerged as a novel therapeutic tool for fibrotic diseases. Upon reaching fibrotic organs, these cells continuously secrete proteases—such as MMPs—to break down deposited ECM, thereby promoting fibrosis resolution [47]. The latest research by Zhao et al. demonstrated that CAR-T therapy targeting PDGF receptor β on ECM-producing cells effectively alleviated renal and multi-organ fibrosis in chronic kidney disease models, with efficacy also verified in human kidney organoids [48]. However, there are still limitations to their safe clinical application in patients, including potential damage to non-fibrotic tissues and safety concerns related to post-degradation tissue regeneration.

## Cellular senescence
Cellular senescence is a key factor influencing the development and progression of DKD. Against the pathological backdrop of diabetes, hyperglycaemia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress interact intricately, forming a vicious cycle that continuously promotes renal cellular senescence [49]. Senescent or damaged renal cells act on neighbouring cells via autocrine and paracrine pathways, persistently releasing the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors. More importantly, even though senescent cells are arrested in the cell cycle, their active SASP secretion accelerates the abnormal accumulation of senescent cells in renal tissues, exacerbating renal injury [50].
Regarding the regulatory mechanisms of cellular senescence in DKD, recent studies have identified several critical intervention targets. GLIS family zinc finger 1 (Glis1) can alleviate age-related renal fibrosis in naturally ageing mice; Chen et al. found that Glis1 inhibits renal tubular epithelial cell senescence and renal fibrosis in DKD by reducing histone lactylation [51]. A study by Ishihara et al. further indicated that activating pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) may be a core strategy for preventing DKD. Specifically, PKM2 activation reduces the levels of senescence-associated molecules in the kidneys of CD-1db/db mice and inhibits renal fibrosis [52]. These studies confirm the close crosstalk between cellular senescence and metabolic reprogramming in DKD, and regulating the key factors involved is expected to further clarify the synergistic mechanism of cellular senescence and metabolic dysregulation in DKD.

## Metabolic dysregulation: from global reprogramming to lactate-mediated lactylation
Within the dynamic network of renal fibrosis in DKD, metabolic dysregulation acts as a pivotal node linking ‘cellular dysfunction–ECM remodelling–fibrosis progression’. It is not merely an accompanying pathological phenomenon, but may act through global metabolic reprogramming to serve as a core potential factor contributing to irreversible renal injury [11]. Under physiological conditions, renal tissues maintain homeostasis through tight regulation of energy metabolic pathways—such as OXPHOS, glycolysis, and fatty acid oxidation. However, in the DKD pathological microenvironment, sustained stimuli (hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, inflammatory factors) disrupt this inherent metabolic balance, and thereby induce metabolic reprogramming characterized by abnormal energy substrate utilization, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic pathway remodelling [53]. This reprogramming not only perturbs renal energy supply, but also accelerates renal fibrosis directly or indirectly by regulating cell survival, ECM synthesis/degradation balance, and inflammatory responses [54]. For instance, aberrant glycolytic activation elevates pro-inflammatory factor production in renal cells, while inhibited OXPHOS reduces ATP production and increases renal mitochondrial damage, collectively creating conditions for fibrosis development [55].
Notably, key metabolic intermediates from metabolic reprogramming are critical ‘metabolic links’ connecting metabolic dysregulation to DKD renal fibrosis. Among these, abnormal lactate accumulation and its mediated lactylation are particularly crucial and closely associated with the progression of fibrosis [56]. Under hyperglycaemia-induced metabolic disturbance, overactivated renal glycolysis causes significant lactate elevation both intracellularly and in the renal microenvironment. Lactate is no longer just a terminal energy metabolite; it may also act as a key molecule in epigenetic regulation. By mediating lactylation modification, lactate may potentially affect the expression of fibrosis-related genes, forming a pathological loop of ‘metabolic aberration–lactate accumulation–epigenetic modification–fibrosis acceleration’ [51]. For example, Zhang et al. showed that elevated H3K14 lactylation in DKD upregulates transcription factor KLF5, which inhibits E-cadherin transcription, promotes EMT, and ultimately exacerbates renal fibrosis [22]. This ‘metabolism–epigenetics’ crosstalk provides a framework for understanding metabolic dysregulation in DKD-related fibrosis and a rationale for developing targeted anti-fibrotic therapies.

## Metabolic reprogramming of key renal cells in DKD and its functional implications
The kidney, as a metabolically active organ, has cells in different functional regions that possess specific metabolic patterns to meet their own functional needs under physiological conditions. Changes in the renal microenvironment during DKD trigger an adaptive remodelling of cellular metabolic patterns in response to survival stress. This metabolic adaptation is not merely a passive stress response but actively feeds back to regulate cellular functions, driving the progression of DKD pathologies such as glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. The following sections will focus on several core cell types in DKD, systematically elaborating on the specific characteristics of their metabolic reprogramming and the association with disease progression (Table 1).

## Proximal tubular epithelial cells
Proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs), comprising 90% of the renal cortex, are densely populated with mitochondria and have high energy demands [57]. Under physiological conditions, these cells mainly rely on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for energy, supplemented by modest glycolysis [58]. In DKD, sustained hyperglycaemia triggers a well-established metabolic switch in PTECs, shifting energy production from FAO impaired by ∼37% towards glycolysis enhanced by ∼2.8-fold [59,60]. This is evidenced by reduced expression of FAO-rate-limiting enzyme carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A and increased expression of hexokinase 2 [32,61]. It is important to note that the impairment of FAO itself disrupts lipid homeostasis, resulting in intracellular lipid accumulation. This lipid overload in PTECs subsequently induces tubulointerstitial fibrosis via activation of the CD36/β-catenin pathway [60].
Notably, PTECs’ high energy demand for tubular transport is tightly linked to metabolic status. In DKD, the upregulation of SGLT2 in PTECs exacerbates the re-absorptive sodium and glucose, impairs Na+/K+-ATPase function, and intensifies cellular metabolic disturbances [62]. The resulting energy dysregulation—characterized by enhanced glycolysis and suppressed OXPHOS—causes accumulation of toxic intermediates, which activate cell injury pathways thereby accelerating renal dysfunction and fibrosis [62]. This pathogenic cascade is decisively demonstrated by a recent study showing that stachyose, a natural compound that binds and destabilizes SGLT2, effectively reverses this metabolic reprogramming and attenuates fibrosis in DKD models [11].

## Glomerular endothelial cells
In contrast to the proximal tubule, glomerular endothelial cells (GECs) primarily rely on glucose as their main fuel source, with fatty acids serving as alternative substrates [63]. In DKD, hyperglycaemia-driven metabolic disorders in GECs disrupt vascular integrity and promote glomerular sclerosis [64]. The GECs are covered by a negatively charged glycocalyx, composed mainly of glycoproteins, glycolipids, and proteoglycans, which is essential for maintaining normal endothelial function [64]. Under persistent hyperglycaemic conditions, the glycocalyx becomes thinner and sheds, leading to a series of pathological changes: on one hand, glycocalyx damage increases vascular permeability, promotes leukocyte adhesion and macrophage infiltration, thereby initiating and exacerbating inflammatory responses; on the other hand, it impairs the regulation of vascular tone, manifested as reduced activity of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, decreased bioavailability of nitric oxide, and ultimately resulting in vasoconstriction [65]. Studies have shown that the application of a novel heparanase inhibitor [66] and adiponectin [67] can mitigate glycocalyx damage in DKD and contribute to the restoration of glomerular filtration barrier function.
Functional studies show that GECs glycolysis is enhanced by ∼50% in DKD, with increased production of lactate that accumulates in the glomerular microenvironment [68]. This increase in glycolytic flux and its products promotes the infiltration of M1-type macrophages, ultimately accelerating renal inflammation [68]. Additionally, impaired mitochondrial function in GECs increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which activates the TGF-β pathway and drives EndMT [69]. The above processes—including GECs regulating macrophage phenotypes via lactate and GECs undergoing EndMT—collectively reflect the tight communication and functional coordination between cells and within GECs themselves, further driving the progression of DKD-related renal injury.

## Podocytes
Podocytes, as an essential component of the glomerular filtration barrier, have energy demands closely related to their differentiation stage. Undifferentiated podocytes primarily rely on glycolysis for energy. However, their energy metabolism undergoes marked changes during and after differentiation. Under normal differentiation, OXPHOS is gradually activated, characterized by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha-dependent enhancement of mitochondrial biogenesis and function, alongside reduced expression of glycolytic enzymes [70]. This shift to efficient oxidative metabolism provides the sustained ATP necessary to power the dynamic remodelling of the actin cytoskeleton—a process critical for foot process formation and slit diaphragm integrity [71]. In contrast, high glucose stimulation progressively arrests OXPHOS biogenesis, promoting a shift back to glycolysis accompanied by sustained lactic acidosis [70]. This metabolic reprogramming creates an energy-depleted state that disrupts cytoskeletal dynamics, ultimately leading to foot process effacement and breakdown of the filtration barrier [71].
Podocyte dysfunction serves as a core driver of DKD progression, with recent studies highlighting both podocytes’ intrinsic metabolic abnormalities and extrinsic regulatory strategies targeting them. Specifically, compromised glycolysis enhances ornithine catabolism in podocytes, whose metabolites activate the mTOR signalling pathway through the small GTPase Ras homolog, thereby inducing cytoskeletal remodelling in podocytes and ultimately promoting the progression of DKD [72]. Moreover, another study revealed that branched chain amino acid catabolic defects in glomerular podocytes, by regulating PKM2 depolymerization to reprogram glucose metabolism and promote podocyte apoptosis via nuclear co-transcriptional effects with DNA damage-inducible transcript 3, may drive the initiation of DKD [12]. Beyond these intrinsic metabolic pathologies, research has also explored intercellular regulatory approaches for DKD therapy. Zhang et.al found that exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells exert therapeutic potential for DKD by ameliorating Talin-1-mediated podocyte EMT, highlighting the value of intercellular communication in correcting podocyte dysfunction [73].

## Mesangial cells
Mesangial cells (MCs), located between the GECs and basement membrane, support glomerular structure by regulating the synthesis and degradation of mesangial matrix proteins. Additionally, their contractile and relaxant abilities allow them to modulate glomerular capillary haemodynamics, ensuring normal filtration function [74]. However, under pathological stimuli such as hyperglycaemia and hypertension, MCs become abnormally activated and secrete cytokines/chemokines that contribute to local inflammation—ultimately disrupting glomerular structure and function [75]. As the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier is compromised, this may eventually progress to glomerulosclerosis and renal fibrosis.
Notably, MCs’ energy metabolism is tightly coupled to their contractile and synthetic functions. Under physiological conditions, they rely on a balance between OXPHOS and glycolysis to sustain contractility [76]. In DKD, the metabolic disorders of MCs are highly context-dependent. Long-term hyperglycaemia reduces MC glycolysis by ∼20% (evidenced by lower extracellular acidification rates), and impairs their contractile and proliferative responses, which may lead to sustained renal fibrosis [76]. However, intermittent high glucose enhances aerobic glycolysis by ∼1.8-fold, increasing lactate production and activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to renal inflammation and cellular apoptosis [77].
Compelling evidence underscores the central role of aberrant MC-mediated intercellular crosstalk in orchestrating DKD pathogenesis. Specifically, excessive fructose intake exacerbates DKD by inducing abnormal Lrg1-mediated crosstalk between GECs and MCs via enhanced TGF-β1 signalling [78]. Additionally, intraglomerular crosstalk between MCs and podocytes disrupts podocytes’ physiological endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation processes and suppresses nephrin phosphorylation, ultimately causing podocyte injury in DKD [79]. Beyond that, exosomal miR-92a-1-5p from high glucose-stimulated PETCs promotes DKD by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and myofibroblast trans-differentiation in MCs [80]. Meanwhile, research on immune cells adds another layer to this mechanism. Zhu et al. reported that under high glucose conditions, macrophage-derived exosomes carrying TGF-β1 mRNA activate MCs via the TGF-β1 pathway. This activation promotes MC proliferation and the secretion of ECM and inflammatory cytokines, all contributing to DKD pathogenesis [81].

## Macrophages
Macrophages represent key immune cells in DKD, wherein two distinct populations coexist. Among them, tissue-resident macrophages originate during embryogenesis and are maintained through local self-renewal within renal tissues. Under physiological conditions, tissue-resident macrophages primarily utilize OXPHOS to sustain tissue homeostasis, clear cellular debris, and adapt to the local microenvironment [82]. However, under chronic pathological stimuli such as hyperglycaemia, hypertension, or metabolic disorders, they become overactivated and acquire a pro-inflammatory, pro-fibrotic phenotype [83].
As DKD progresses, pro-inflammatory cytokines released by leukocytes stimulate renal cells to produce various chemokines, which in turn recruit abundant infiltrating macrophages into the glomeruli and renal interstitium. This process establishes a self-perpetuating inflammatory cycle within the kidney [84]. Macrophages contribute to DKD pathogenesis largely through functional polarization, broadly categorized into M1 and M2 subtypes. The M1 phenotype promotes vigorous inflammatory responses and renal injury, whereas the M2 phenotype suppresses inflammation and facilitates tissue repair [85,86]. Notably, this phenotypic shift is closely linked to metabolic reprogramming. M1 macrophages in DKD exhibit approximately 2.2-fold enhanced glycolysis, as indicated by elevated lactate levels, accompanied by upregulation of glycolytic enzymes [87].
In recent years, growing research has elucidated key molecular events and potential therapeutic targets involved in the complex crosstalk between renal cells and macrophages in DKD. One study demonstrated that high glucose and inflammatory cytokines trigger abnormal opening of connexin 43 hemichannels in PTECs, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation and facilitating pathogenic communication between epithelial cells and macrophages [88]. Additionally, macrophage-mediated podocyte apoptosis has been identified as a critical mechanism in DKD progression [89]. Supporting this, Shi et al. reported that in DKD animal models, an increased proportion of M1-polarized macrophages promotes inflammation through the mTOR signalling pathway and compromises podocyte structural integrity [90]. On the therapeutic front, Chen et al. found that tea polyphenols attenuate ageing-related DKD by activating sirtuin 1, which promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, reduces lipid accumulation, and alleviates podocyte injury [91].

## Metabolic memory in DKD
A critical yet underdiscussed feature of DKD metabolic disorders is metabolic memory—persistent metabolic dysfunction in renal cells even after glucose control is restored [92]. A growing body of research indicates that persistent epigenetic modifications occur in different renal cell types during metabolic memory in DKD. Marumo et al. reported that in db/db mice, PETCs exhibit abnormal changes in histone acetylation (Kac) and DNA methylation. Even after subsequent blood glucose control with the anti-diabetic drug pioglitazone, these aberrant epigenetic modifications persisted [93]. Moreover, in high glucose-induced podocytes, epigenetic modifications were observed in the promoter region of the protein tyrosine phosphatase SHP-1, leading to sustained high expression of SHP-1 and maintenance of its phosphatase activity; even when systemic blood glucose was normalized with treatment, this epigenetically driven sustained expression of SHP-1 inhibited the insulin signalling pathway, causing persistent insulin resistance and subsequent podocyte dysfunction [94].
Similarly, Ye et al. found that TGF-β can upregulate the expression of pro-fibrotic and inflammatory genes in high glucose-induced rat MCs by altering histone methylation modifications at target gene promoters. MCs isolated from the glomeruli of DKD rats maintained their dysregulated epigenetic landscape even when cultured under non-diabetic conditions, promoting mesangial dysfunction [95]. Collectively, these findings suggest that initial metabolic perturbations like high glucose can establish metabolic memory via epigenetic mechanisms, which in turn drives the progression of DKD.

## Lactate metabolic imbalance and its impact on DKD
Traditionally, lactate was regarded as a harmful metabolic byproduct, toxic to renal cells and contributing to inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis. However, with ongoing research, lactate has gradually been recognized as an important metabolic intermediate and signalling molecule, interacting with specific receptors and regulatory mechanisms to create a pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic microenvironment, ultimately exacerbating renal injury progression.

## Lactate production
In DKD, renal lactate overproduction arises from tissue-specific metabolic reprogramming rather than global hypoxia. PTECs become a major lactate source under hyperglycaemic conditions, with high glucose inducing a 36.5% increase in lactate production in rat PTECs, an effect reversed by LDH inhibition with oxamate [96]. Pathological triggers of lactate overproduction include not only hyperglycaemia but also local acidosis. Renal LDH isoform A(LDHA)-mediated lactate generation creates a feed-forward loop promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and renal fibrosis in DKD [17].
Lactate generation exhibits remarkable regional and cellular heterogeneity. This is evidenced by higher lactate levels were observed in the renal cortex compared to the medulla in db/db mice [97]; furthermore, the spatial distribution of lactate-producing enzymes differs along the nephron, with LDHA (pyruvate-to-lactate) dominates in proximal segments and LDH isoform B (LDHB) (lactate-to-pyruvate) prevails distally, which establishes a nephron-specific lactate gradient that is disrupted in DKD [98].

## Lactate transport
The generated lactate is then transported out of the cell via proton-coupled MCTs to maintain cellular metabolic balance and acid-base homeostasis (Figure 1). The MCT family consists of non-glycosylated, multi-spanning proteins with 14 members, each exhibiting different transport properties and tissue distributions [99]. Among these, MCT1 to MCT4 are proton-driven transporters responsible for the transport of lactate, pyruvate, γ-hydroxybutyrate, and other endogenous compounds, as well as exogenous compounds such as salicylic acid, niguldipine, and pravastatin [100].
A mechanistic diagram of lactate production, transport, and reutilization. Under normoxic conditions, glucose undergoes glycolysis to produce pyruvate, which enters the mitochondria for OXPHOS to generate ATP. Under hypoxic conditions, lactate production increases and is exported from cells via MCT4. Lactate can then be taken up by other cells through MCT1, where it is either oxidized to pyruvate to enter the tricarboxylic acid cycle for ATP production or converted to glucose via gluconeogenesis, supplying energy for other tissues. Abbreviations: GLUT, glucose transporter.
In the kidney, the major MCT isoforms expressed are MCT1, MCT2, and MCT4. MCT1 and MCT4 are primarily localized to the basolateral side of renal tubular cells and are associated with the co-transporter protein CD147, which enhances their membrane expression and transport activity [101]. MCT1 primarily facilitates the reabsorption of lactate and the transport of pyruvate, whereas MCT4 is responsible for exporting lactate into the renal tubular lumen [101]. MCT2 is relatively less expressed in the kidney and mainly involved in the transport of pyruvate and other monocarboxylic acids [102].
The kidney is a central organ in lactate metabolism, typically responsible for clearing 20-30% of systemic lactate—mainly via coordinated reabsorption mediated by MCT1 and MCT4 in PTECs [103]. MCT1 exhibits specific transport kinetics for lactate, with a KM of 26.5 ± 2.2 mM and a Vmax of 72.0 ± 14.5 nmol·mg−1·min−1 [104]. Notably, MCT activity is strongly inhibited by intracellular acidosis, highlighting pH sensitivity as a key regulatory node [105]. However, extracellular acidosis establishes a powerful inward proton gradient that acts as a primary driving force, compelling MCTs to massively transport lactate and H+ from the interstitium into the cells, ultimately leading to intracellular lactate overload. Studies have confirmed that the net lactate removal by the kidney significantly increases with environmental acidification, with its contribution rising sharply from 16% at pH 7.45 to 44% at pH 6.75 [106]. This seemingly ‘enhanced’ clearance capacity is actually a double-edged sword. The excessive lactate influx far outpaces mitochondrial processing capacity, directly impairing mitochondrial function, exacerbating local hypoxia and acidosis, and forming a self-amplifying vicious cycle [107].
Furthermore, the role of MCTs in kidney disease has attracted increasing attention. Raimundo et al. reported that MCT1 expression in the renal medullary area of DKD rat models was positively correlated with blood creatinine levels, suggesting that MCT1 may serve as a potential early biomarker for DKD [108]. Kim et al. found that elevated MCT1 expression was associated with poor prognosis in clear cell renal carcinoma and could serve as a predictor of tumour progression [109]. Gerlinger et al. further demonstrated that silencing MCT4 in renal cancer tissues could partially reverse the aerobic glycolysis, leading to cell acidosis and reduced ATP production [110]. Similarly, Ma et al. showed that MCT4 activity was upregulated under high-lactate conditions, and inhibiting MCT4 expression might be an effective therapeutic target for ameliorating energy metabolic imbalance and inflammation in type 2 diabetes [111].

## Lactate reutilization
Lactate, in addition to being expelled from the cell, can also be recycled as an energy source under certain conditions. In muscles and other tissues, lactate can re-enter cells via MCT1, particularly when oxygen is abundant [112] (Figure 1). In this case, lactate is oxidized by lactate dehydrogenase into pyruvate, which then enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle to generate ATP [113]. Additionally, lactate is transported via the bloodstream to liver, heart, and other tissues, where it undergoes gluconeogenesis to produce glucose, which is subsequently released into the blood to supply energy to peripheral organs [114] (Figure 1). This process is particularly important during intense exercise or hypoxic conditions, as it helps maintain blood glucose levels and ensures a steady energy supply.
Beyond the systemic Cori cycle centred in the liver, an integrated cycle with synchronous lactate production and consumption exists in the intact kidney. The renal medulla consumes glucose via glycolysis to generate lactate; the renal cortex then takes up and oxidizes this lactate for energy production, while also producing glucose that is released back to the medulla to support further glycolysis and energy generation [115]. This lactate cycle is closely associated with renal function: lactate production rate is positively correlated with glomerular filtration rate, urine flow rate, and total Na+ reabsorption [116]. Lactate production rate decreases by 50% in non-filtering kidneys (with complete inhibition of filtration) and by 39% following furosemide treatment (which inhibits Na+ reabsorption), further confirming the dependence of the lactate cycle on renal function [116].
The renal lactate oxidation capacity exhibits a substrate-dependent characteristic. Glucose promotes lactate oxidation: in fed rats, the lactate consumption rate is 179.9 μmol/h·g dry weight when 2 mM lactate is the sole substrate, and increases to 300.6 μmol/h·g dry weight after adding 5 mM glucose—suggesting that glucose enhances the oxidative utilization of lactate [116]. Furthermore, lactate oxidation is affected by renal filtration function but not by inhibition of Na+ reabsorption. The lactate consumption rate in non-filtering kidneys (171.2 μmol/h·g dry weight) is significantly lower than that in normally filtering kidneys (298.8 μmol/h·g dry weight), whereas furosemide treatment (which inhibits Na+ reabsorption) does not alter lactate consumption rate. This indicates that the site of lactate oxidation may be associated with renal filtration and has no direct connection to the key sites of Na+ reabsorption [116].

## Lactate signalling and its biological effects
Lactate functions as a concentration-dependent signalling molecule, with distinct effects at physiological versus pathological levels. At physiological concentrations (0.5–2 mM), lactate serves as a preferred oxidative fuel in the renal cortex, where it is efficiently metabolized via the TCA cycle to generate ATP, even in preference to glucose [117]. Additionally, lactate acts as an endogenous ligand for the G-protein-coupled receptor 81(GPR81), which is predominantly expressed in adipose tissue, muscle, kidney, and liver. Studies have shown that GPR81 is sensitive to lactate within a concentration range of 0.1–30.0 mM, with partial activation occurring at levels of 0.2–1.0 mM. Thus, physiological lactate concentrations are sufficient to activate GPR81 [118]. Activation of GPR81 inhibits adenylate cyclase activity, reducing cAMP production, which in turn suppresses lipolysis in adipocytes [119]. However, at pathophysiological concentrations (>5 mM), GPR81 activation by lactate triggers endothelin-1 release, reducing renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate—effects abolished in GPR81-/- mice [120]. Furthermore, endothelin-1, signalling through the endothelin B receptor, drives EMT and promotes renal fibrosis [121].
Beyond its receptor-mediated signalling, lactate also plays a central role in hypoxic response through metabolic reprogramming. Under hypoxic conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) is activated and upregulates glycolysis-related genes (e.g. PKM2, LDHA), thereby driving robust lactate production [87]. In turn, lactate stabilizes HIF-1α protein and enhances its transcriptional activity by both inhibiting prolyl hydroxylase activity—which blocks the VHL-mediated degradation pathway—and through direct lactylation modification [122,123]. Thus, HIF-1α acts as the ‘switch’ for lactate generation, while lactate serves as a ‘stabilizer’ of HIF-1α. This metabolic synergy is further evidenced by lactate’s ability to activate the HIF-1α signalling pathway, which induces the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, thereby promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and ultimately facilitating new blood vessel formation [124].
Moreover, lactate also plays a notable role in the regulation of immune cell function. High-lactate concentrations modulate macrophage polarization, promoting a shift towards the anti-inflammatory phenotype that suppresses inflammation and facilitates tissue repair and regeneration [125]. Similarly, lactate inhibits the effector T cell activity while enhancing regulatory T cell function, thereby promoting immune resistance in tumours with high glycolytic activity [126].

## Lactate as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target
The metabolic changes in diabetic patients result in elevated levels of metabolites such as lactate in the kidneys, which in turn disrupt the metabolic balance of the organ. Amir et al. identified significantly dysregulated metabolites in DKD patients using two meta-analysis strategies, analyzed 44 studies (98 metabolite profiles), and found 17 metabolites (linked to pathways like glycolysis) to be dysregulated; further time-course study analysis revealed urinary lactate is among the top non-invasive early diagnostic biomarkers for DKD [127]. Moreover, multiple additional clinical studies have further corroborated the role of urinary lactate as a biomarker for DKD. Specifically, the Nord-Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT3) cohort study showed that patients with DKD exhibited 2.3-fold higher urinary lactate levels compared to controls [97]; the Singapore Study of Macro-angiopathy and Micro-Vascular Reactivity in Type 2 Diabetes (SMART2D) and the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) cohort studies found that, after adjusting for baseline clinical risk factors, patients in the third tertile (highest level group) of the urinary lactate-to-creatinine ratio had a faster decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate compared to those in the first tertile (lowest level group) [97]; studies in patients with type 1 diabetes also showed that during euglycemic and hyperglycaemic clamp tests, lactate levels in both plasma and urine were strongly correlated with glucose levels [97]. Further, Azushima et al. used gas chromatography-mass spectrometry to measure urinary lactate in mouse models and a cohort of 230 patients with type 2 diabetes, confirming that it was significantly associated with albuminuria and tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolites and was not interfered with by RAS inhibitors. Long-term follow-up (6.8 ± 1.5 years) showed that patients with high urinary lactate levels had a higher risk of progressing to end-stage renal disease and other adverse outcomes, and this significance remained after adjustment for clinical risk factors [16]. These results indicate that urinary lactate can not only reflect the presence of DKD but also predict its progression, providing strong support for its role as a clinical biomarker for DKD.
Therapeutic strategies targeting lactate metabolism represent a promising preclinical approach for DKD treatment. LDHA inhibitors, such as oxamate, reduce high glucose-induced lactate production in PTECs by 36.5% and redirect cellular metabolism towards OXPHOS, thereby improving mitochondrial respiratory function [96]. Lee et al. further revealed that silencing LDHA or using glycolysis inhibitors, such as 2-deoxyglucose and dichloroacetate, significantly reversed lactic acidosis, improved mitochondrial function in DKD rats, and alleviated renal fibrosis—findings that underscore glycolysis/LDHA inhibition as a viable therapeutic avenue [17]. Additionally, MCT1 inhibitors, such as α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamate, can prevent lactate-induced endothelial dysfunction, providing a rationale for addressing endothelial impairment in DKD [128]. Moreover, GPR81 antagonists deserve attention for their capacity to improve renal perfusion, a key mechanism to mitigate ischaemic injury in diabetic kidneys [120]. Notably, these interventions may also modulate protein lactylation by limiting lactate substrate availability, establishing a mechanistic link between metabolic correction and epigenetic regulation—an interaction that could be harnessed to optimize therapeutic outcomes in future DKD therapies [22].

## Mechanisms and dynamic regulation of lactylation in cellular processes
Among emerging post-translational modifications (PTMs), lysine lactylation (Kla) has garnered attention as a distinctive modification, characterized by the covalent attachment of lactate groups to lysine residues. Recent evidence suggests that Kla serves as a key mediator of lactate’s biological effects and couples cellular metabolism to epigenetic and proteomic regulation [129]. Unlike static PTMs, Kla represents a highly dynamic modification, enabling rapid adjustment of modification levels in response to metabolic shifts and thus facilitating adaptive cellular responses. This dynamic regulation is governed by three core functional modules: ‘writers’, ‘readers’, and ‘erasers’. The biological functions of lactylation rely on the spatiotemporal coordination of these modules, which is tightly linked to lactate metabolism—particularly the generation of lactyl-CoA, the direct substrate for protein lactylation (Table 2).
Enzymes involved in lactylation: writers, readers, and erasers.

## Lactylation ‘writers’
Lactylation ‘writers’ are enzymes that catalyze the covalent attachment of lactate groups to lysine residues on target proteins. Among these, histone acetyltransferases such as E1A-binding protein p300 (p300) [130] and General control non-derepressible 5 (GCN5) [131] have been confirmed as key regulators of histone lactylation—both modify histone H3 lysine 18 (H3K18). Additionally, Histone acetyltransferase KAT5 (KAT5) [132] and CREB-binding protein (CBP) [133], traditionally recognized for their roles in acetylation, mediate non-histone lactylation, respectively modifying Vps34 at K356 and K781, and MRE11 at K673. These findings highlight a dual functionality of acetyltransferases, suggesting their involvement in regulating both acetylation and lactylation processes.
Notably, although these ‘writers’ are widely expressed in various tissues, their enzymatic activity is not fixed but highly context-dependent, being significantly enhanced in metabolically active tissues or cell types. For example, in the DKD model, p300 mediates the lactylation of tripartite motif containing 65 (TRIM65) at K206, which reduces TRIM65’s ubiquitin ligase activity and impairs its dual regulatory roles in inhibiting ferroptosis and glycolysis, thereby promoting DKD progression [134]. Similarly, the high-lactate microenvironment in DKD enhances the basal binding between KAT5 and histone H3; molecular docking data reveal that GLIS family zinc finger 1 can directly interact with KAT5 at multiple sites, an interaction that reduces the binding ability of KAT5 to histone H3, thereby inhibiting histone H3K18la and ultimately alleviating the senescence of renal tubular epithelial cells [51]. Such tissue and cell type specificity indicates that lactyltransferases act as key metabolic sensors, converting nutrient availability into epigenetic and functional adaptations.
Importantly, p300 and GCN5 do not function independently; instead, they form functional complexes with enzymes involved in lactyl-CoA synthesis. These complexes supply L-lactyl-CoA—the critical substrate required for the reaction—and thereby regulate the overall lactylation ‘writing’ process in an indirect yet crucial manner.

## Lactyl-CoA biosynthetic pathways and their regulatory mechanisms
In eukaryotes, L-lactyl-CoA serves as the activated donor of L-lactyl groups for protein lactylation. It is predominantly synthesized within the cell nucleus and operates via a ‘local synthesis and on-site utilization’ mode, which ensures the specificity of histone lactylation while preventing interference with metabolic pathways in the cytoplasm or mitochondria. Additionally, L-lactyl-CoA biosynthesis exhibits a distinct metabolic dependence. Factors that elevate lactate production, such as high glucose levels and hypoxia, can promote L-lactyl-CoA synthesis, thereby enhancing the regulation of gene expression mediated by histone lactylation. Two primary biochemical pathways are responsible for L-lactyl-CoA production.
The nuclear synthesis of L-lactyl-CoA primarily relies on GTP-specific succinyl-CoA synthetase (GTPSCS), a heterodimeric enzyme composed of succinyl-CoA synthetase alpha subunit (SUCLG1) and succinyl-CoA synthetase beta subunit [135]. GTPSCS is abundantly expressed in tissues such as the kidney and liver. Endowed with a nuclear localization signal within its SUCLG1, GTPSCS is translocated into the nucleus to exert its catalytic function. Using L-lactate and CoA as substrates, GTPSCS consumes GTP to synthesize L-lactyl-CoA. D-lactate, present at negligible concentrations in mammalian cells (<20 μM), fails to act as a substrate for GTPSCS-mediated lactyl-CoA synthesis. Notably, a calculated KM value of 15 mM was determined for GTPSCS towards L-lactate, and this value aligns with the range of physiological lactate concentrations in tissues (0.5–40 mM) [136]. When intracellular lactate concentrations are significantly higher than those of succinate, the synthetic efficiency of L-lactyl-CoA can reach sixfold higher than that of succinyl-CoA—highlighting the regulatory role of metabolic status in product selection [136]. Furthermore, GTPSCS forms a stable complex with p300. Through the enrichment of local substrates, the newly synthesized L-lactyl-CoA is directly supplied to p300 via the substrate channelling effect, which then utilizes it for histone lactylation [135].
Unlike GTPSCS, the nuclear L-lactyl-CoA synthetic activity of acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2) depends on the activation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway [137]. Specifically, upon EGFR activation, ERK kinase phosphorylates the Ser267 residue of ACSS2, triggering its translocation into the nucleus. Once inside the nucleus, ACSS2 assembles with LDHA and the histone acetyltransferase KAT2A (KAT2A) to form a ‘LDHA-ACSS2-KAT2A’ functional complex, establishing a cascade reaction system: LDHA catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to L-lactate; ACSS2 subsequently uses this L-lactate and CoA as substrates, consuming ATP to generate L-lactyl-CoA; finally, KAT2A completes histone lactylation [137]. This pathway exhibits a high affinity for L-lactate (KM < 1 mM), enabling efficient operation even under physiological L-lactate concentrations [136]. Primarily stimulated by epidermal growth factor, it regulates histone lactylation levels locally and participates in processes like cell proliferation [136].
Beyond the well-established lactyl-CoA-dependent mechanism, alanyl-tRNA synthetases 1 and 2 (AARS1/AARS2) have been identified as metabolic enzymes that directly utilize L-lactate and ATP to catalyze protein lactylation [138]. This reaction mirrors their canonical aminoacylation activity: ATP-dependent formation of a lactyl-adenylate intermediate enables the direct modification of specific lysine residues on target proteins, circumventing the requirement for lactyl-CoA [138]. The existence of this parallel pathway fundamentally expands the known enzymatic repertoire for installing lactylation marks, highlighting a more complex regulatory landscape for this PTM.

## Lactylation ‘readers’
In organisms, proteins capable of recognizing lactylation marks and mediating interactions with other molecules are referred to as the ‘readers’ of lactylation. Although lactylation can occur and exert biological functions independently of these readers, their presence enhances the specificity and efficiency of lactylation-mediated effects, such as modulating gene transcription, signalling pathways, and metabolic processes [139]. While less characterized than writers/erasers, recent studies have identified candidate reader domains and their functional consequences.
Histone PTM readers encompass various domains such as the plant homeodomain (PHD), double PHD fingers (DPF) domain, bromodomain, and YEATS domain, among which multiple family members have been identified as specific readers for Kla. For instance, Zhang et al. used chemical proteomics to reveal that the double PHD fingers 2 (DPF2) protein, which contains a DPF domain, specifically recognizes histone H3K14la. The key residue D274 directly interacts with the Kla site to stabilize binding. Functionally, upon binding to H3K14la, DPF2 enriches at the promoter regions of tumour-related genes and mediates transcription in a DPF domain-dependent manner, ultimately promoting tumour cell proliferation [140]. In another study, Nuñez et al. reported that the PHD-bromodomain of TRIM33 acts as a reader for histone Kla modification. It engages with the lactyl side chain of H3K18la via glutamate residue E981, contributing to the activation of late-stage M1 macrophages. Notably, the binding affinity between TRIM33 and H3K18la is comparable to that for histone lysine acetylation [141]. Hu et al. demonstrated that the bromodomain of brahma-related gene 1 (BRG1) specifically recognizes H3K18la. Their interaction leads to enrichment at promoters of mesenchymal–epithelial transition-related genes, thereby facilitating reprogramming of induced pluripotent stem cells [142]. Wang et al. identified the YEATS domain-containing protein ALL1-fused gene from chromosome 9 protein (AF9) as a specific reader for H3K9la, suggesting it as a potential functional binder of H3K9la [143]. Additionally, research by Zhang et al. indicated that the bromodomain protein bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) is involved in regulating H4K8la modification. Silencing BRD4 significantly reduces H4K8la, exacerbating astrocyte polarization and leading to neuronal death [144].
Although several candidate readers have been identified, it is important to acknowledge that significant knowledge gaps remain: currently known readers focus exclusively on histone lactylation, while specific recognition proteins for non-histone lactylation have yet to be discovered; the recognition of lactylation by known reader domains lacks absolute specificity, and whether other potential domains such as Tudor or CHD are involved has not been systematically investigated; the dynamic regulatory mechanisms involving cofactors and the readers’ own post-translational modifications that modulate binding activity remain unclear; furthermore, the functional differences of the same reader across various physiological and pathological contexts await further elucidation.

## Lactylation ‘erasers’
In contrast, ‘erasers’ of lactylation modifications enable cells to dynamically transition between different states in response to internal and external environmental changes. Like other lysine acylation modifications, lactylation is reversible and is believed to be regulated by two major families of lysine deacetylases, including the histone deacetylase family (HDAC1-11) and the NAD-dependent deacetylase family (SIRT1-7) [145]. Recent studies have advanced our understanding of lactylation ‘erasers’. The functionality of these delactylases exhibits a high degree of multi-layered selectivity, which is manifested in various aspects such as enzyme families, substrate types, stereochemical specificity, and regulatory mechanisms.
Moreno-Yruela et al. confirmed that HDAC1-3 are potent histone lysine delactylases [146]. In terms of catalytic efficiency, HDAC3 exhibits the strongest activity when bound to the deacetylase activation domain of nuclear receptor corepressor 2 (NCoR2): its catalytic efficiency (kcat/KM) for lysine D-lactylation (D-Kla) is 3.5-fold higher than that for L-Kla, and its efficiency in removing histone Kla is comparable to that for Kac. In contrast, HDAC1/2 show lower overall activity but a more marked preference for D-Kla (kcat/KM ratio over 6-fold vs. L-Kla). For substrate selectivity, HDAC1-3 prioritize non-hydroxylated short-chain acylations (e.g. Kac) over hydroxylated Kla. HDAC3, for example, has a kcat/KM >105 M−1·s−1 for Kac but <2 × 104 M−1·s−1 for Kla. On histones, they preferentially target H3K9, H3K18, and H4K5/8/12, with minimal effect on H2BK5la, and all favour D-Kla over L-Kla. In dynamic regulation, HDAC3 requires association with the NCoR2/silencing mediator of retinoic acid and thyroid hormone receptors (SMRT) complex to enhance activity, while HDAC1/2 form nuclear multiprotein complexes relying on cofactors for substrate selectivity. Their activity is modulated by self-PTM (e.g. HDAC3 phosphorylation), acting via site-specific de-lactylation, and can be inhibited by broad-spectrum HDAC inhibitors or HDAC1–3-specific ones.
Complementing this finding, Sun et al. developed a fluorescence and luminescence probe to detect delactylase activity in live cells, providing evidence that SIRT1 may serve as a delactylase for non-histone proteins [147]. Subsequent research by Zhang et al. identified α-myosin heavy chain (α-MHC) K1897 as a substrate for SIRT1-mediated delactylation [148]. Additionally, Jin et al. identified that SIRT3 can remove lactyl groups from cyclin E2, further expanding the scope of lactylation regulation in cellular processes [149]. Notably, Du et al. identified that SIRT1 and SIRT3, but not SIRT2, act as potent ‘erasers’ for Kla in mammalian cells. Specifically, K228 lactylation of α-enolase 1 (ENO1) serves as a common target of SIRT1 and SIRT3, while K207 lactylation of PKM2 is selectively delactylated by SIRT1. This SIRT1-mediated delactylation of PKM2 primarily impairs its tetramer formation, alters its intracellular localization, reduces PKM2 enzymatic activity, and thereby inhibits glycolysis. Notably, there is minimal overlap between the Kla and Kac substrates targeted by SIRT1 and SIRT3, indicating that these two enzymes adopt distinct regulatory modes for the two acyl modifications [150].

## Correlation between lactylation detection methods and stoichiometry
Research on the stoichiometry of histone lysine L-lactylation (L-Kla)—defined as the ratio of L-lactylated lysine residues to total lysine residues at specific histone sites—is highly contingent on the advancement of detection technologies. Owing to methodological limitations, systematic quantitative data on L-Kla stoichiometry remain elusive. From the perspective of core detection methods, their supportive roles and constraints in stoichiometry research are primarily reflected in three aspects.

## High-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS)
Regarded as the gold standard for lactylation detection, HPLC-MS/MS enables direct capture of modified peptide signals, allowing the detection of L-Kla without prior enrichment [151]. This finding implies that the total abundance of L-Kla in cells is sufficient to support direct detection. Notably, HPLC-MS/MS can distinguish L-Kla from D-lactylation through chemical derivatization, thereby clarifying the core target of stoichiometry research [152]. Accurate quantification of L-Kla stoichiometry requires isotopically labelled L-lactylated standard peptides as references [136,152]. However, the preparation of such standards is costly and procedurally complex, and they have not yet been widely applied in L-Kla research. Consequently, specific quantitative values (e.g. modification percentages or molar concentrations) remain unavailable to date, and only qualitative descriptions such as ‘detectable’ or ‘non-trace’ can be used to infer the overall abundance of L-Kla [136].

## Antibody-dependent methods
Methods based on pan-L-lactylation-specific antibodies enable qualitative validation of L-Kla and its localization analysis at the tissue/cellular level [152]. Additionally, semi-quantitative inference of dynamic fluctuations in stoichiometric ratios can be achieved by comparing signal intensities—for instance, under metabolic stress conditions such as hypoxia, increased L-lactate production leads to a marked enhancement of antibody detection signals, suggesting a concurrent rise in L-Kla stoichiometry at the promoters of target genes [153]. However, these methods fail to provide absolute stoichiometric data. Furthermore, antibody signals are influenced by factors such as antibody affinity and sample processing conditions, making it difficult to establish a linear relationship between signal intensity and modification ratio. As a result, they can only reflect relative differences rather than quantifying the specific proportion of modified residues.

## Function-oriented assays
Analysis of the activity of lactyltransferases and delactylases, or localization of L-Kla across the genome via chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing, can indirectly reveal its ‘local stoichiometry’ characteristics. As before, the nuclear GTPSCS-p300 complex enriches L-lactyl-CoA locally through a ‘substrate channeling effect’, resulting in a significantly higher L-Kla stoichiometry at chromatin sites adjacent to the complex compared to the global level [135]. This ‘high local concentration but low global ratio’ feature explains why conventional HPLC-MS/MS struggles to detect a high global L-Kla stoichiometry, yet L-Kla can still exert critical biological functions by regulating specific genes.

## Lactylation modification in DKD
In DKD, elevated lactate levels drive histone lactylation, which regulates gene expression by altering chromatin accessibility; the dysregulation of this modification contributes to pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs. Additionally, lactate mediates non-histone lactylation, which modulates the activity of metabolic enzymes, signalling molecules, and transcription factors, thereby affecting cellular metabolic balance and tissue pathological development. Notably, histone and non-histone lactylation are not independent processes but form an interconnected regulatory network: they share upstream triggers, converge on downstream pro-fibrotic pathways, and even modulate each other’s functions—laying the groundwork for their coordinated role in DKD progression. However, we must acknowledge that there remains a gap in current evidence regarding DKD-specific lactylation modifications, and further efforts are needed to address this limitation.

## Histone lactylation and its impact on DKD

## Histone structure and epigenetic regulatory functions
Histones are essential components of chromatin in the cell nucleus, composed of four core histones—H2A, H2B, H3, and H4—that form nucleosomes to facilitate the tight packaging of DNA [154]. Histone H1, a linker histone located outside the nucleosome, plays a crucial role in stabilizing the DNA linker regions between nucleosomes, thereby promoting the further compaction of chromatin structure [155]. The N-terminal tails of histones are rich in amino acid residues such as lysine, arginine, and serine, which serve as key sites for PTMs. These modifications have been shown to regulate the transcription of genes involved in inflammation, tissue repair, and fibrosis, contributing to cellular homeostasis and functional adaptability [131,156].
In DKD, histone lactylation has emerged as a key mechanism linking metabolic dysregulation to pathological gene expression. For instance, Chen et al. conducted lactomics analysis of kidney tissues from DKD mice using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Their findings revealed that histone Kla and acyl-CoA synthetase family member 2 (ACSF2) are key mediators of mitochondrial dysfunction and may drive the progression of DKD [157]. Zhang et al. reported that increased lactate accumulation in DKD enhances histone H3K14la, which promotes the expression of KLF5, further exacerbating the EMT and renal fibrosis [22]. Moreover, Hu et al. demonstrated that insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 5 promotes NLRP3 inflammasome-induced EndMT and renal fibrosis in DKD by regulating glycolysis-mediated H3K18la [158]. Collectively, these investigations highlight that histone lactylation serves as a pivotal epigenetic regulator in DKD, integrating metabolic dysregulation with the activation of pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory gene networks, and thus aggravating renal damage and fibrosis in the disease (Figure 2).

## Chromatin context determines histone lactylation outcome
Histone lactylation, does not function in isolation but is critically shaped by the chromatin context—the core mechanism enabling its gene-specific regulatory effects. While lactylation enhances chromatin accessibility by neutralizing histone positive charges and recruiting transcriptional machinery (e.g. p300/CBP), its success in activating gene expression depends heavily on the local molecular environment [151]. Specifically, lactylation preferentially occurs at genomic regions pre-marked by activating modifications such as histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) [159]. In DKD, for instance, H3K18la in GECs shows genome-wide colocalization with H3K27ac and H3K4me3, and is specifically enriched at active CpG island promoters [160]. This suggests that regions with active histone marks provide a ‘permissive environment’, allowing lactylation to exert a synergistic effect that further opens chromatin and promotes pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory gene expression [158].
Conversely, various mechanisms operate across the genome to counteract the effects of lactylation, ensuring transcriptional specificity. Repressive chromatin states—such as those marked by H3K9me3 or H3K27me3—can resist lactylation-driven activation [161]. These modifications recruit proteins that sustain compact chromatin structure and limit access to transcriptional machinery, thereby maintaining the silencing of genes even under high-lactate conditions [161]. Furthermore, although direct crosstalk between lactylation and DNA methylation remains unclear in DKD, promoter DNA hypermethylation is a well-established repressor of gene expression [162]. It likely forms a physical and biochemical barrier by promoting chromatin condensation or recruiting repressive complexes, thereby inhibiting the deposition of histone modifications—including lactylation—or counteracting their gene-activating functions [163]. While these repressive and barrier mechanisms represent crucial aspects of gene-specific regulation, they remain insufficiently explored in DKD. Future studies examining whether and how lactylation may overcome or cooperate with DNA demethylation will be essential to fully understand epigenetic reprogramming in DKD.
Beyond these chromatin-based constraints, sequence-specific transcription factors may also contribute to gene-specific targeting of lactylation. Upon activation by upstream signals, these factors bind to enhancers or promoters and may recruit enzymatic complexes that promote lactylation, thereby precisely directing this modification to relevant pathogenic gene networks [164]. Thus, the biological significance of histone lactylation cannot be understood in absolute terms; its functional output is ultimately determined by the complex epigenetic background. This highlights the intricate interplay between metabolic signals and pre-existing chromatin states in dynamic cellular environments.

## Crosstalk and dynamic regulation between histone lactylation and acetylation
Beyond its individual functions, histone lactylation engages in a dynamic and competitive dialogue with acetylation to integrate metabolic signals into the epigenetic code. This interaction is characterized not only by competition for identical lysine residues but, more critically, by the sharing of core regulatory enzymes [151]. This implies that the relative abundance of lactate and acetyl-CoA can directly determine the modification status and transcriptional activity at specific genomic loci, forming a sensitive ‘metabolic switch’.
In DKD, histone lactylation and acetylation exhibit time-dependent and site-specific dynamic changes and interactions. In renal tubular epithelial cells induced by advanced glycation end products (AGEs), the level of pan-histone lactylation (Pan-Kla) significantly increased over stimulation time (4–24 h), with histone H3K14la being a core upregulated site. In contrast, pan-histone acetylation (Pan-Kac) showed no significant change during the early stage of stimulation (within 8 h), and the level of histone H3K14ac was even reduced [22]. These results suggest potential competitive binding between the two modifications at the same lysine sites. Furthermore, when lactate production was suppressed using the LDHA inhibitor oxamate, the AGEs-induced elevation in Pan-Kla and H3K14la levels was significantly attenuated, while early acetylation levels remained unaffected [22]. These findings indicate that the dynamic changes in lactylation—its rapid induction and reversibility by inhibition of lactate production—precede noticeable alterations in acetylation. Moreover, the temporal and site-specific crosstalk between these two modifications collectively influences the balance of histone modifications, thereby contributing to the regulation of EMT and renal fibrosis in DKD.

## Non-histone lactylation and its impact on renal diseases
Recent research has revealed that Kla occurs in various non-histone proteins, extending beyond gene expression regulation in the nucleus. Lactylation also modifies metabolic enzymes and other signalling molecules, thereby exerting extensive effects on cellular functions [165]. For example, key enzymes involved in glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism undergo lactylation, which alters their activities and impacts cellular energy balance.

## Lactylation of glycolytic enzymes
Study has shown that lactate enhances the lactylation of PKM2 by approximately 1.3-fold, inhibiting its transition from a tetramer to a dimer. This results in a 25% increase in enzyme activity and consequently reduces its nuclear distribution. This modification impairs the metabolic adaptation of pro-inflammatory macrophages [24]. Lactylation modifies key glycolytic enzymes: at K147 of aldolase A, completely abolishing its activity (100% reduction), and at K688 of platelet-type phosphofructokinase, leading to a 40% decrease in activity [166,167] (Figure 2).
Interestingly, lactylation serves as a self-limiting feedback mechanism for glycolysis. As a terminal product of glycolysis, lactate itself modifies and inhibits key enzymes in the pathway, potentially preventing excessive glycolytic activation and lactate overproduction. This feedback loop may be crucial for maintaining metabolic homeostasis under physiological conditions. However, in the sustained metabolic stress of DKD, this regulatory circuit might be disrupted or hyperactivated, contributing to pathological outcomes.

## Lactylation of mitochondrial-related enzymes
In an acute kidney injury (AKI) model, mitochondrial enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) K52la promotes the ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of the mitophagy receptor PHB2, thereby inhibiting mitophagy and exacerbating tubular injury and mitochondrial dysfunction [107]. Similarly, An et al. found that mitochondrial fission 1 protein (Fis1) K20la is significantly increased (∼1.5-fold). This modification promotes excessive mitochondrial fission, leading to ATP depletion, elevated ROS production, and apoptosis, thereby exacerbating renal damage [23] (Figure 2).
Thus, ALDH2 K52la and Fis1 K20la amplify renal injury through a synergistic mechanism of ‘disrupting mitochondrial structure and blocking damage clearance’, exerting pathological effects across energy metabolism, the inflammatory response, and cell survival. Moreover, they exhibit a progressive effect characterized by ‘early initiation and late-stage amplification’ during the disease course. Sharing common regulatory nodes such as ‘lactate metabolism’ and ‘SIRT3’ these two lactylation modifications provide a theoretical basis for the development of combined targeted therapies for AKI. Meanwhile, they also reveal the core role of the ‘mitochondrial protein lactylation network’ in the pathogenesis of AKI—rather than the independent effects of a single modification.

## Lactylation of transcription factors
HIF-1α, a key transcription factor that regulates glycolysis under hypoxic conditions, can also undergo lactylation modification following lactate uptake into prostate cancer cells via MCT1. This modification stabilizes HIF-1α, enhances transcription of KIAA1199, and promotes angiogenesis in prostate cancer cells [168]. Notably, the stability and activity of HIF-1α are collectively regulated by a network of PTMs including hydroxylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and acetylation—lactylation does not act independently but interacts dynamically with these canonical PTMs [169,170]. It disrupts oxygen-dependent HIF-1α degradation by interfering with hydroxylation and VHL-mediated ubiquitination, while synergizing with acetylation to enhance HIF-1α’s transcriptional activity [171]. This crosstalk enables HIF-1α to remain stable and functionally active even in lactate-rich microenvironments, thereby sustaining the transcription of target genes and fuelling pathological processes such as angiogenesis, forming a critical link between metabolic reprogramming and hypoxic adaptation.
The TGF-β signalling pathway is a central regulator of fibrosis, particularly in processes such as EndMT and EMT, which significantly impact fibrosis progression [172]. Snail1, an important transcription factor in the TGF-β pathway, undergoes lactylation, which facilitates its nuclear localization, enhances its binding to the TGF-β promoter, increases TGF-β production, and promotes EndMT, thus exacerbating cardiac fibrosis [25]. Similarly, Twist1, another transcription factor involved in regulating EMT and fibrosis under TGF-β signalling activation, is also regulated by lactylation [26]. Xu et al. discovered that lactate accumulation driven by PKM2 can induce Twist1 lactylation, promoting its translocation to the nucleus, modulating TGF-β transcription, and contributing to skin flap fibrosis [26] (Figure 2). Thus, lactylation of master EMT transcription factors represents a potent mechanism for amplifying pro-fibrotic signalling. By promoting their nuclear localization and enhancing transcriptional activity, lactylation effectively ‘locks’ these factors in an activated state. This initiates a vicious cycle: TGF-β signalling induces EMT and lactate production, which in turn hyperactivates Snail1 and Twist1 via lactylation, further driving TGF-β expression and EMT progression. In DKD, where both TGF-β and lactate levels are chronically elevated, this positive feedback loop may serve as a fundamental driver of sustained renal fibrosis. These findings suggest that targeting the lactylation of these specific EMT transcription factors could disrupt a critical node in the fibrotic signalling network.
Although non-histone lactylation is crucial for regulating inflammation, metabolic adaptation, and fibrosis, research specifically focused on its role in DKD remains limited. Further investigation of the mechanisms underlying non-histone lactylation within the unique metabolic environment of DKD is essential to uncover its distinct impact on disease progression.

## Therapeutic targeting of lactylation: translational promise and current challenges
Protein lactylation has emerged as an exceptionally attractive and novel therapeutic target, offering a unique strategic approach for intervening in a wide array of diseases driven by metabolic dysregulation. Its central role at the nexus of metabolism and epigenetics provides a unique opportunity to cripple disease-driving circuits at their source. By targeting this central node, a successful lactylation-directed therapy could simultaneously disrupt multiple downstream disease-driving pathways that are fuelled by high lactate, achieving a coordinated therapeutic effect that is difficult to attain with conventional single-target agents. Furthermore, because the lactylation-modified proteome is often disease-specific and enriched in pathological settings, it holds great potential as a source of novel biomarkers for diagnosis and patient stratification.
Despite this compelling therapeutic potential, the path towards clinical translation is fraught with considerable and unique challenges that must be rigorously addressed. First and foremost, the issue of specificity looms large. Lactylation shares its core ‘writers’ and ‘erasers’—with other well-established modifications, most notably acetylation. This makes the development of agents that selectively inhibit lactylation without globally disrupting the essential epigenomic and signalling landscape nearly impossible, posing a significant risk of intolerable on-target toxicity. Secondly, the intimate link between lactylation and cellular metabolism creates a complex adaptive system. Lactate, the precursor for lactylation, is a central metabolic hub molecule. Strategies aimed at reducing lactate availability would inevitably perturb fundamental energy production pathways across normal tissues, leading to widespread systemic side effects.
Beyond biological complexity, significant technical hurdles remain. The field currently lacks robust, non-invasive tools for real-time monitoring of lactylation dynamics in vivo, severely hampering our ability to assess target engagement and pharmacodynamic responses in clinical settings. Moreover, the functional outcome of lactylation is highly context-dependent. This heterogeneity necessitates sophisticated patient stratification strategies to identify those whose diseases are truly driven by lactylation, a task for which validated biomarkers are still absent. Consequently, the path to clinical translation is paved with concerns about safety and the imperative for precise patient selection. Overcoming these hurdles will require innovative approaches, such as developing allosteric modulators for lactylation-specific functions, advanced drug delivery systems for localized targeting, and a deeper functional mapping of lactylation networks across different diseases.

## Conclusion
DKD remains a major diabetic complication characterized by progressive renal fibrosis. Emerging evidence underscores the central role of metabolic reprogramming in this process, particularly through the lactate-lactylation axis. Specifically, histone lactylation influences the inflammatory response and fibrotic processes in renal cells by regulating gene expression and chromatin remodelling, while non-histone lactylation primarily impacts metabolic enzymes and transcription factors, promoting energy metabolism dysfunction and fibrosis in renal tissue, thus exacerbating renal damage and accelerating disease progression. However, it is important to note that research on lactylation in DKD is still in its early stages, with limited evidence available—especially concerning non-histone lactylation—and a particular scarcity of direct human data. Current understanding largely relies on preclinical models, and the translational relevance of these findings requires further validation.
The therapeutic targeting of the lactate-lactylation axis shows potential for DKD treatment, though clinical translation remains distant. Initial exploratory trials involving LDHA inhibitors, MCT modulators, or lactylation-specific agents are anticipated in the coming years, contingent upon successful preclinical outcomes and comprehensive safety assessments. Significant challenges include the development of specific lactylation modulators to minimize off-target effects, the identification of reliable non-invasive biomarkers for dynamic monitoring, and the refinement of renal-targeted delivery systems. Regulatory approval will necessitate rigorous demonstration of both efficacy and safety across diverse diabetic patient populations with varying degrees of renal impairment.
Future research should prioritize several key areas: elucidating cell-type-specific lactylation targets in human DKD tissues through advanced spatial omics technologies; developing highly selective lactylation regulators that avoid cross-reactivity with other post-translational modifications; validating lactylation-related biomarkers in urine or serum for prognostic use; and investigating combination therapies that integrate lactylation modulation with existing agents such as SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists to enhance anti-fibrotic effects. In summary, while the lactate-lactylation axis represents a promising frontier in DKD research, its therapeutic potential will depend on overcoming substantial mechanistic and translational challenges. A multidisciplinary approach integrating metabolomics, epigenetics, and clinical nephrology will be crucial to harness this pathway effectively for mitigating renal fibrosis and improving outcomes in DKD.

## Figure Figure 1.: A mechanistic diagram of lactate production, transport, and reutilization. Under normoxic conditions, glucose undergoes glycolysis to produce pyruvate, which enters the mitochondria for OXPHOS to generate ATP. Under hypoxic conditions, lactate production increases and is exported from cells via MCT4. Lactate can then be taken up by other cells through MCT1, where it is either oxidized to pyruvate to enter the tricarboxylic acid cycle for ATP production or converted to glucose via gluconeogenesis, supplying energy for other tissues. Abbreviations: GLUT, glucose transporter.

## Figure Figure 2.: Regulatory mechanisms of histone and non-histone lactylation. Under hypoxic conditions, glucose is metabolized via glycolysis to produce pyruvate, which enters the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle to generate ATP. The resulting lactate influences cellular processes through the following mechanisms: (i) Histone lactylation regulates the transcription of ACSF2 and KLF5, thereby modulating mitochondrial function and the EMT; (ii) Lactylation of transcription factors Snail1 and Twist1 enhances their interaction with TGF-β, promoting both EndMT and EMT; (iii) Lactylation of key enzymes involved in glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism (PKM2, ALDOA, PFKP, Fis1) and the transcription factor HIF-1α further modulates glycolytic and mitochondrial functions. Abbreviations: PKM2, pyruvate Kinase M2; ALDOA, aldolase A; PFKP, platelet-type phosphofructokinase; Fis1: mitochondrial fission 1.
