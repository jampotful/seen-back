Mechanistic study of CTHRC1 in promoting Wilms' tumor progression by regulating M2-type tumor-associated macrophages polarization.
Zhuo Y, Feng X, Zhang W, Du J, Sun X, Luo X, Wang W, Jiang H, Gu H. (2025). Journal of translational medicine. . doi:10.1186/s12967-025-06752-4. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Mechanistic study of CTHRC1 in promoting Wilms’ tumor progression by regulating M2-type tumor-associated macrophages polarization

## Abstract
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the role of Collagen triple helix repeat protein 1 (CTHRC1) in Wilms’ tumor (WT) progression and elucidate its molecular mechanism in promoting WT malignancy through regulation of M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (M2-TAMs) infiltration and polarization.MethodsBioinformatics analysis was conducted using public databases to examine CTHRC1 expression and immune cell infiltration in WT. Single-cell sequencing was employed to analyze expression patterns of CTHRC1 and M2-TAMs markers. CTHRC1 expression and M2-TAM infiltration were validated in WT tissues using RT-qPCR, Western blot, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence. In vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to investigate the biological functions of CTHRC1 in WT and its effects on M2-TAMs polarization. Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were used to explore potential signaling pathways. The TNFSF9-TNFRSF9 axis was further investigated through neutralizing antibody rescue experiments and co-localization analysis. The role of M2-TAMs in promoting WT progression via the PI3K/AKt pathway was examined using xenograft models and in vitro experiments.ResultsCTHRC1 and M2-TAMs were significantly overexpressed in WT tissues and positively correlated. CTHRC1 overexpression promoted WT cell proliferation, inhibited apoptosis, and induced M2-TAMs polarization both in vitro and in vivo. Transcriptome analysis revealed that CTHRC1 regulated M2-TAMs polarization through the TNFSF9-TNFRSF9 axis. CTHRC1 overexpression upregulated TNFSF9: expression and secretion in tumor cells, promoting its binding to TNFRSF9 on M2-TAMs. Neutralizing TNFSF9 or knockdown of TNFRSF9 significantly attenuated CTHRC1-induced M2-TAM infiltration and polarization. M2-TAMs promoted WT progression by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway in tumor cells. Inhibition of PI3K/Akt signaling reversed M2-TAM-mediated WT progression.ConclusionsCTHRC1 promotes WT progression by inducing M2-TAMs polarization through the TNFSF9-TNFRSF9 axis. M2-TAMs, in turn, enhance WT malignancy by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway in tumor cells. These findings provide new potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for WT diagnosis and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06752-4.

## Introduction
Wilms’ tumor (WT) is the most common malignant kidney tumor in children, accounting for approximately 7% of all childhood tumors and primarily affecting children aged 3–4 years [1–4]. Despite significant improvements in treatment modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, which have increased the 5-year overall survival rate to over 90%, approximately 25% of patients still experience recurrence or metastasis after initial treatment [5–7]. The prognosis for these patients remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of only about 30% [5, 8–10]. This highlights the urgent need for a deeper understanding of WT’s molecular mechanisms and the development of new therapeutic targets and strategies.
While molecular biology research has identified key genetic drivers of WT, such as WT1, CTNNB1, and WTX [11–13], and signaling pathways like Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/Akt that govern cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis [14, 15], Most studies have traditionally focused narrowly on intratumoral genetic/epigenetic alterations. Notably, the tumor microenvironment (TME), a dynamic ecosystem comprising immune cells, fibroblasts, vascular networks, and extracellular matrix, has emerged as a pivotal regulator of tumor behavior. It influences critical processes such as tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and treatment resistance [16–18].
TME consists of a variety of cellular and non-cellular components, including immune cells, fibroblasts, vascular structures, and the extracellular matrix, and is now recognized as a critical regulator of tumor behavior, It significantly impacts tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and treatment resistance [16–18]. Among the diverse immune cell populations in the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent one of the predominant infiltrating cell types and exhibit notable functional plasticity [19]. TAMs are generally categorized into classically activated M1 macrophages with anti-tumor properties and alternatively activated M2 macrophages that support tumor progression. M2-TAMs facilitate tumor growth, angiogenesis, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [20]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that an elevated M2/M1 ratio or increased M2-TAMs infiltration is closely associated with poor prognosis in various solid tumors [21, 22].The poor prognosis of WT is also profoundly influenced by the TME, making the investigation of WT-associated TME a critical priority for developing effective therapeutic strategies. Recent studies have further indicated that M2-TAMs may play a pivotal role in the initiation, progression, and therapeutic response of WT. Immune infiltration analyses have revealed a significant increase in M2-TAMs within WT tissues, along with elevated infiltration of total macrophages, T cells, and dendritic cells. These findings suggest that the WT microenvironment is highly complex and potentially immunosuppressive [23]. These findings further underscore the need for systematic investigation of TAMs in WT pathogenesis, particularly focusing on the mechanisms driving M2 polarization.
Collagen triple helix repeat containing 1 (CTHRC1) is a secreted glycoprotein that plays important roles in embryonic development, osteoblast differentiation, wound healing, and tumor progression [24–26]. CTHRC1 overexpression has been observed in various solid tumors, including colorectal, breast, ovarian, and pancreatic cancers, and is closely associated with tumor invasiveness, metastasis, and poor prognosis [27–29]. Recent studies have revealed that CTHRC1 can also reshape TAMs in the tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor immune evasion and malignant progression [30].
Recent evidence suggests that the PI3K/Akt pathway plays a crucial role in regulating the tumor microenvironment, particularly in tumor cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis evasion [31]. Studies have shown that M2-TAMs can secrete various growth factors and cytokines that activate the PI3K/Akt pathway in tumor cells, inducing malignant behaviors [32, 33]. Despite this, the interplay between CTHRC1, TAMs, and the PI3K/Akt pathway in WT remains poorly understood.
Based on these research backgrounds, the present investigation focuses on the molecular mechanisms of CTHRC1-mediated M2-TAMs polarization and its interaction with WT cells from the perspective of tumor microenvironment. Through systematic investigation at tissue, cellular, and molecular levels, the regulatory mechanism of the CTHRC1-TNFSF9/TNFRSF9-PI3K/Akt signaling axis in WT progression will be elucidated. Specifically, the clinical correlation between CTHRC1 expression and M2-TAMs infiltration will be analyzed, followed by exploration of the cellular mechanism by which CTHRC1 regulates M2-TAMs polarization via the TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 axis. Multi-omics approaches will be employed to identify and validate key signaling pathways, with the goal of providing novel molecular targets for WT diagnosis and treatment.

## Materials and methods

## Public database analysis of CTHRC1, M2-TAMs (CD163, CD206), and WT correlation

## Public database data selection
WT single-cell datasets were derived from the Human Cell Atlas (HCA) database (https://data.humancellatlas.org/). The TARGET-WT cohort from the TARGET database included 126 WT samples and 6 adjacent tissue control samples. The GSE73209 dataset from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) contained 36 tumor samples and 4 non-tumor fetal kidney tissue controls. The GSE66405 dataset included 32 samples, consisting of 28 WT samples and 4 normal kidney tissue controls. The GSE197147 dataset contained 5 WT samples.

## Differential expression analysis of CTHRC1 in WT
Gene expression matrices were obtained from GSE73209 and GSE66405 datasets in the GEO database, along with WT transcriptome data from the TARGET database. Transcriptome data underwent background correction, normalization, and log2 transformation. When multiple probes corresponded to a common gene, their average value was taken as the expression value. The “SVA” software package was used to eliminate batch effects and other unnecessary variations. The R package “ggplot2” was used to visualize the expression of CTHRC1 and M2 markers CD163 and CD206 in WT and control samples.

## Immune cell infiltration analysis
CIBERSORT (Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts) is employed to assess the relative abundances of different cell types in complex mixed tissue samples. In this study, the R software package “Cibersort.R” for CIBERSORT calculation and the expression matrix of the gene set of 22 cell types are utilized to estimate the infiltration levels of immune cells in WT samples. The samples are screened based on the criterion of P < 0.05. The percentages of each type of immune cell in the samples are calculated and visualized by using the “ggplot2” software package.

## Differential analysis of M2-TAMs in WT
Building on the research in Sect. 2.1.2, M2 expression in TARGET, GSE73209, and GSE6640 datasets was analyzed. Results were visualized using the R software package “ggplot2”.

## Correlation analysis of CTHRC1 and M2 markers (CD163, CD206) in WT
The Spearman correlation coefficient was systematically used to study the relationship between the infiltration levels of immune cells and the expression levels of CTHRC1 in the samples of WT. The analysis was carried out using the “spearman” method in the “cor()” function of the Stats tool in R language to ensure a reliable statistical assessment. After confirming the differential expression patterns of CTHRC1, CD163, and CD206 in the GEO database and the TARGET database, the Limma software package was used to precisely calculate the correlation coefficients between CTHRC1 and the M2-TAMs markers CD163 and CD206. Subsequently, the corrplot software package in R language was applied for visualization.

## Single-cell analysis of heterogeneous expression of CTHRC1 and M2 MARKERS (CD163, CD206)
Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis was performed using R (version 4.2.3) with the Seurat package (version 5.0.0). Three WT single-cell sequencing datasets were initially preprocessed by creating Seurat objects using the CreateSeuratObject function, followed by the addition of mitochondrial parameters through the PercentageFeatureSet function. Quality control criteria were established to select high-quality cells: nFeature_RNA between 200 and 7,500, percent.mt < 5%, and percent.HB < 3%, with concurrent removal of erythrocyte and mitochondria-associated genes. The three datasets were integrated using the Merge function, yielding 2,274 high-quality cells for subsequent analysis. Standard data processing procedures were implemented, including identification of highly variable genes using NormalizeData and FindVariableFeatures functions, data normalization via ScaleData, followed by dimensionality reduction through PCA and t-SNE analyses. Cell subpopulations were annotated based on the following marker genes: macrophages (CD163, MRC1, CD68), Wilms tumor cells (WT1, PODXL, PTPRO), erythroblasts (CA1, HBB, HBE1, AHSP, ALAS2), T cells (CD3D, TRAC, TRBC2, CD8A, GZMK, CCL5), NK cells (KLRD1, NKG7, KLRB1, FCGR3A, PRF1), mast cells (KIT, TPSB2, FCER1A, SLC18A2), and fibroblasts (MMP2, EMILIN1, SFRP2). Finally, the FindMarkers command was employed to analyze the expression patterns of CTHRC1, CD163, and CD206 to validate their heterogeneous expression across different cell subpopulations.

## Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction(rt-qpcr) analysis
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following these steps: 50 mg of tissue was homogenized and lysed in TRIzol, followed by chloroform separation, isopropanol precipitation, 75% ethanol washing, air drying, and dissolution in RNase-free water. RNA concentration was determined using a fluorescence detector.Reverse transcription was performed with the SureScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit in a 20 µL reaction containing 14 µL of total RNA, 1 µL of SureScript RT Enzyme Mix, 4 µL of reaction buffer, and 1 µL of primer. The program was 25 °C for 5 min, 50 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s, then held at 4 °C.Prior to PCR, cDNA was diluted 5-fold. The 10-µL PCR reaction contained 3 µL of diluted cDNA, 5 µL of 5×BlazeTaq PCR Mix, and 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (2 µM). Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s.The list of primers used for real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) is in Table S3 of the supplementary file.

## Western blot(WB) analysis
Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. Protein sampleswere mixed with 5×loading buffer and heat-denatured. SDS-PAGE gels of varying concentrations were prepared according to target protein characteristics. Electrophoresis was initially conducted at 80 V for approximately 30 min, then increased to 120 V for 90 min after samples entered the separation gel. Proteins were transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (300 mA, 1 h, 4℃), blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min, and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies (Cthrc1, β-actin). Following TBST washing, membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG 1:3000 or goat anti-mouse IgG 1:5000) at room temperature for 30 min. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit, captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system, and quantified by densitometric analysis using Image J software.

## Immunohistochemical analysis
Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol (75%), cleared in ethanol-xylene mixture (1:1) followed by pure xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at 3 μm thickness, floated in 20% ethanol, and mounted on charged slides after expansion in 47℃ water. Immunohistochemical staining procedure included: deparaffinization at 64℃, xylene clearing, rehydration through descending ethanol series, antigen retrieval using citrate buffer in a pressure cooker, blocking of endogenous peroxidase with 3% H2O2 and non-specific binding with 5% BSA. Cthrc1 primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4℃ followed by warming to 37℃, then sequential treatment with enhancement reagent, enzyme-labeled secondary antibody, DAB visualization, and hematoxylin counterstaining. After dehydration through ascending ethanol series and xylene clearing, sections were mounted and analyzed for IDO values using Image J software.

## Immunofluorescence analysis
For tissue immunofluorescence, sections were subjected to deparaffinization at 64℃ for 1 h, followed by sequential immersion in xylene (2 × 10 min) and rehydration through descending ethanol gradients. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer in a pressure cooker for 3 min. For cellular immunofluorescence, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and washed with PBS. Both tissue sections and fixed cells underwent membrane permeabilization with penetrating solution at ambient temperature for 20 min, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin at 37℃ for 30 min. For dual immunofluorescence staining, specimens were initially incubated with anti-CD163 primary antibody (diluted in 2% BSA) at 4℃ overnight, equilibrated to 37℃ for 30 min, and then labeled with Alexa Fluor® 555-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (37℃, 30 min). Subsequently, specimens were incubated with anti-CD206 primary antibody followed by Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (37℃, 30 min). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 min, room temperature, light-protected). All specimens were mounted with anti-photobleaching medium, and five random fields were captured using fluorescence microscopy for quantitative analysis of positive cell ratios.

## Cell culture
SK-NEP-1 cells and THP-1 cells were cultured in DMEM complete medium and RPMI-1640 complete medium respectively, both supplemented with 10% PBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Frozen cells were rapidly thawed in a 37℃ water bath, transferred to a centrifuge tube containing complete medium, and centrifuged at 1000r/min for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, cells were resuspended in complete medium, transferred to T-25 culture flasks, and maintained in a 37℃ incubator with 5% CO2.

## Polarization of THP-1 cells into M2-type tumor-associated macrophages
THP-1 cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 h to induce differentiation into M0 macrophages, followed by stimulation with IL-4 (20 ng/mL) and IL-13 (20 ng/mL) for 48 h to achieve M2-type TAM polarization.

## Construction and validation of CTHRC1 knockdown and overexpression lentiviral vectors
For CTHRC1 knockdown and overexpression studies, two lentiviral vector systems were employed: pLVshRNA-EGFP(2 A)Puro (8487 bp) for gene interference and pLV-EGFP-N (8791 bp) for gene overexpression, both containing essential elements including 5’LTR, 3’LTR, Amp(R), Puro(R), EGFP, and additional functional sequences such as U6 promoter, RRE, and PGK promoter, with the overexpression vector specifically including WPRE and cPPT sequences. CTHRC1 CDS was PCR-amplified and cloned into the vector using EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites. Lentivirus packaging was conducted in 293T cells (90% confluence) using a transfection mixture containing lentiviral vector (5 µg), VSVG vector (3.75 µg), Dr8.91 vector (1.25 µg), and Polyfect-V transfection reagent (20 µL). Viral supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection. For viral infection, target cells were seeded in 6-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well), and at 70% confluence, cells were infected in medium containing 8 µg/mL polybrene. Following fluorescence expression observation at 48–72 h post-infection, stable cell lines were established through 2-week puromycin selection, with knockdown efficiency and overexpression levels verified by RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis.

## CCK8 assay
Cells in logarithmic growth phase from five experimental groups (SK-NEP-1, sh-CTHRC1-NC, sh-CTHRC1, OE-CTHRC1-NC, and OE-CTHRC1) were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well, with six replicates for each group and blank controls. Cell proliferation was assessed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h by adding 10 µL of CCK8 solution to each well, followed by 2 h incubation. The optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

## Transwell migration analysis
The migration capacity of M2-TAMs was assessed using a Transwell assay system with CTHRC1-overexpressing SK-NEP-1 cells and anti-TNFSF9 neutralizing antibody intervention. Briefly, Matrigel was thawed at 4℃, and 50 µL was evenly coated onto pre-chilled Transwell inserts under ice-cold conditions, followed by drying at 37℃ for 30 min. Cell suspensions (400 µL, 1 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded into Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers and cultured for 48 h. Migrated cells were subsequently photographed under microscope (100×magnification) and quantified for statistical analysis.

## Flow cytometric analysis of cell apoptosis
For flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis, cells were harvested using EDTA-free trypsin with gentle dissociation, followed by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min. The collected cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS and resuspended in 1× Binding buffer. Subsequently, 100 µL of cell suspension was stained with 5µL Annexin V-PE and 5 µL 7AAD, followed by 15 min incubation at room temperature under light-protected conditions. After incubation, samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer with PE and 7AAD channels. Early apoptotic cells were identified as Annexin V-PE positive/7AAD negative, while late apoptotic cells were Annexin V-PE positive/7AAD positive. The data were analyzed using FlowJo software, and the percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated.

## Flow cytometric analysis of cell cycle
Cells were trypsinized into single-cell suspension and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, followed by washing with pre-cooled PBS. The collected cells were gently fixed with 1 mL pre-cooled 70% ethanol at 4℃ for at least 2 h or overnight. After centrifugation, approximately 50 µL supernatant was retained to avoid cell loss, and cells were resuspended in 1 mL pre-cooled PBS. Each sample was then stained with 0.5 mL propidium iodide solution and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min in the dark. Cell cycle analysis was performed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer, detecting red fluorescence at 488 nm excitation wavelength for 10,000 cells per sample in triplicate. The percentage of cells in each phase was analyzed using modfit-1 software. Flow cytometric analysis was completed within 5 h after staining.

## EdU assay
After cell adhesion, EdU detection was performed using EdU working solution (10 µM final concentration, 1:500 dilution from 10 mM stock) prewarmed to 37℃. Following 2 h incubation, cells were fixed and washed three times (5 min each) with washing buffer, permeabilized with 1 mL penetrating buffer for 10 min at room temperature, and then incubated with 0.5 mL Click reaction solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After three final washes, images were captured for analysis.

## Establishment of mouse xenograft model
Xenograft tumor models were established by subcutaneously injecting 5 × 106 cells suspended in 200 µL PBS into the right axillary region of 6-week-old C57 mice. Tumor growth was monitored and recorded three times per week. After four weeks, mice were sacrificed for measurement of tumor volume and weight, and evaluation of tumor capsule integrity to assess the effect of CTHRC1 on tumor growth. Portions of tumor tissues were preserved in liquid nitrogen for subsequent molecular mechanism studies.

## Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining
Tumor specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, processed through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin blocks for sectioning. The tissue sections were deparaffinized by heating at 59℃ for 60 min and rehydrated through a series of xylene and graded ethanol solutions. Subsequently, sections were stained with Harris hematoxylin for 5 min followed by counterstaining with 0.5% eosin for 2 min. After dehydration through ascending ethanol series and clearing in xylene, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam for histopathological examination.

## Flow cytometric analysis of CD206 and cd163 positive cells in mouse tumor tissues and peripheral blood
Blood samples were diluted 1:1 with physiological buffer and carefully layered onto lymphocyte separation medium, followed by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 min. Tissue specimens were enzymatically dissociated in 0.25% trypsin solution at 37 °C for 30 min following mechanical disruption, and single-cell suspensions were obtained by filtration through 0.22 μm mesh filters. Both cellular preparations were subjected to sequential washing procedures with phosphate-buffered saline via centrifugation at 1200 rpm and subsequently resuspended in 100 µL Binding Buffer. For immunofluorescence labeling, cell suspensions were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD206 and CD163 (5 µL each) for 20 min at ambient temperature under light-protected conditions. Following the addition of 400 µL Binding Buffer, flow cytometric analysis was performed using a BD FACSCalibur cytometer, acquiring 10,000 events per sample. Voltage parameters and fluorescence compensation were established using unstained and single-stained controls. All analyses were conducted in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability.

## Isolation of CTHRC1-regulated TAMs from mouse model
Tissue specimens were mechanically dissociated and enzymatically digested with 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by filtration through 0.22 μm mesh filters to obtain single-cell suspensions. After repeated centrifugation at 1200 rpm and PBS washing, cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium at a concentration of 107 cells/mL. Macrophages were isolated using a combination of Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation and discontinuous Percoll gradient centrifugation (25%, 35%, and 65% Percoll solutions, prepared by mixing Percoll with 8.5% NaCl at 9:1 ratio). The isolated cells were washed with PBS and seeded at 1 × 106 cells/mL in DMEM supplemented with antibiotics. After 4 h adherent culture at 37 °C, non-adherent cells were removed by PBS washing to obtain purified macrophage populations.

## Transcriptomic analysis
It starts with collecting the constructed tumor tissues from xenograft mice. First, the tissues are placed in a 0.25% trypsin solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min for enzymatic digestion, which disperses the tissues into single cells or small cell clusters. Then, the cell suspension is initially separated by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation.Next, gradient purification is carried out using Percoll solutions at concentrations of 25%, 35%, and 65%. Based on the differences in cell density, TAMs are separated from other cells. Finally, the purified macrophages are cultured for 4 h, and non-adherent cells are removed through adherent screening to obtain pure TAMs.During the sequencing stage, the RNA of TAMs is first extracted using TRIzol reagent. After quality inspection to ensure that the RNA concentration is greater than 50 ng/µL, the RNA integrity number (RIN) is greater than 7.0, and the OD260/280 ratio is greater than 1.8, the mRNA is enriched and a library is constructed. The library is sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq™ sequencing platform. The sequencing data are evaluated for quality using the FastQC software. The HISAT2 software is used to align the data to the reference genome, the StringTie software is used to assemble the transcripts, and the edgeR software conducts differential expression analysis according to the criteria of a fold change greater than 2 or less than 0.5 and a P value less than 0.05. Finally, the R software is utilized to perform pathway enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after washing the plates 5 times, 100 µL of appropriately diluted samples were added to pre-coated wells and incubated at 37℃ for 2 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of biotinylated antibody working solution was added, followed by 100 µL of enzyme conjugate working solution after washing steps. The reaction was visualized by adding 100 µL of TMB substrate solution and incubating at 37℃ in darkness for 30 min until a visible color gradient developed in the standard wells. The reaction was terminated with 100 µL of 2 M sulfuric acid, and the optical density was measured at 450 nm within 10 min. Sample concentrations were calculated using the standard curve equation.

## Co-culture of M2-TAMs and SK-NEP-1 cells
Conditioned media were collected from SK-NEP-1 cells, SK-NEP-1-OE-CTHRC1 cells, and SK-NEP-1-OE-CTHRC1 cells treated with either low (1 µg) or high (5 µg) doses of TNFSF9 for 24 h following 48 h culture. All supernatants were obtained by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, M2-TAMs were cultured with these different conditioned media mixed with complete medium at a 1:1 ratio, establishing four experimental groups: control group (M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC, using SK-NEP-1 conditioned medium), CTHRC1 overexpression group (M2 + OE-CTHRC1, using SK-NEP-1-OE-CTHRC1 conditioned medium), and two anti-TNFSF9 treatment groups (M2 + OE-CTHRC1 + anti-TNFSF9(L/H), using conditioned media from SK-NEP-1-OE-CTHRC1 cells treated with low/high doses of anti-TNFSF9). Cells were harvested for subsequent analysis after 48 h of treatment.

## Rescue experiment using anti-TNFSF9 neutralizing antibody
To investigate the effects of CTHRC1-induced TNFSF9 secretion from cancer cells on M2-TAMs, both proliferation and migration assays were conducted. Cell proliferation was assessed using CCK8 assay after 24-hour co-culture of CTHRC1-overexpressing SK-NEP-1 cells or control cells with M2-TAMs, while cell migration was evaluated using Transwell chambers. Both experiments were performed with or without anti-TNFSF9 neutralizing antibody intervention during the 24-hour co-culture period.

## Stimulation of TNFSF9 on M2-TAMs
M2-type tumor-associated macrophages were treated with Recombinant Tumor Necrosis Factor Ligand Superfamily, Member 9 (TNFSF9) protein at two concentration levels (3.928 ng/mL and 1 ng/mL) to investigate its dose-dependent effects on cellular proliferation and migration. The proliferative response was quantified using CCK8 assay, while migratory capacity was evaluated using Transwell migration assay, following established protocols.

## Immunofluorescence analysis of TNFSF9 and TNFRSF9 Co-localization
M2-TAMs were labeled with Dil (10 µM, 1 h) and co-cultured with CTHRC1-overexpressing SK-NEP-1 cells for 24 h. Following PBS washing, cells underwent pepsin-mediated antigen retrieval (37℃, 30 min), blocking with 3% H2O2 (room temperature, 20 min) and 5% BSA (37℃, 30 min). Double immunofluorescence staining was performed sequentially for TNFSF9 and Recombinant Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor Superfamily, Member 9 (TNFRSF9). Primary antibodies (1:100 dilution) were incubated overnight at 4℃ followed by 30 min equilibration at 37℃, and visualized using Cy5-conjugated and Alexa Fluor® 555-conjugated secondary antibodies (37℃, 30 min) respectively. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 min, room temperature, light-protected). Specimens were mounted with anti-fade medium and analyzed using inverted and laser confocal microscopy.

## Construction of TNFRSF9 knockdown vector
The TNFRSF9 interference vector was constructed by HonorGene Corporation using the pHG-LVsh plasmid vector (8593 bp). This lentiviral vector system contains multiple functional elements, including a Human U6 promoter controlling shRNA expression, a CMV promoter driving GFP reporter gene expression, and viral packaging elements such as 5’ LTR, RRE, and cPPT/CTS. Additionally, Puro(+) and Amp(R) resistance genes were integrated for cell line and plasmid selection. Subsequent lentivirus packaging, stable cell line establishment, and validation were performed following standard protocols.

## Migration assay of TAMs
To investigate whether CTHRC1-induced TNFSF9 promotes M2-type TAM migration through binding to TNFRSF9 receptor, THP-1 cells with downregulated TNFRSF9 receptor were first differentiated into M2-TAMs, and then co-cultured with CTHRC1-overexpressing tumor cells. The migration capacity of these M2-TAMs was subsequently assessed using Transwell assay according to previously described protocols.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 and GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). After confirming normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, independent-samples t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way ANOVA was employed for multiple group comparisons. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Bioinformatics workflows were implemented using R (version 4.2.3).Statistical significance was defined as follows: P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; P < 0.01 was considered highly significant; and P < 0.001 was considered extremely significant.The R language and the versions of the corresponding R packages are detailed in Table S4 of the supplementary file.

## Results

## Bioinformatics interrogation of the intricate associations between CTHRC1 and M2-TAMs (CD163, CD206) in Wilms tumor
CTHRC1 expression and its association with M2-TAMs in WT were analyzed using three public databases (TARGET, GSE73209, and GSE66405). Bioinformatics analysis revealed significantly elevated CTHRC1 expression in WT tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). To investigate the relationship between CTHRC1 and the tumor immune microenvironment, we analyzed M2-TAMs infiltration using TARGET and GSE73209 datasets. The analysis revealed significantly higher M2-TAMs infiltration in WT tissues compared to normal tissues, with GSE73209 dataset showing infiltration levels approaching 0.6 in tumor tissues, approximately threefold higher than normal tissues (approximately 0.2) (Fig. 1B). ## Effects of CTHRC1 on cell proliferation and M2-tams polarization in Wilms tumor
To investigate the function of CTHRC1 in Wilms tumor, we established a CTHRC1 lentiviral expression system. Fluorescence microscopy revealed that both CTHRC1 overexpression (OE-CTHRC1) and knockdown (sh-CTHRC1) lentiviruses achieved transfection efficiencies exceeding 90% in SK-NEP-1 cells (Fig. 3A). Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses confirmed that, compared to their respective negative controls, CTHRC1 expression was significantly downregulated in the sh-CTHRC1 group and upregulated in the OE-CTHRC1 group (Fig. 3(B-C)).CCK8 assays demonstrated that, compared to controls, CTHRC1 overexpression significantly enhanced SK-NEP-1 cell proliferation at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h (P < 0.0001), while CTHRC1 knockdown markedly inhibited cell proliferation (P < 0.0001). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that, compared to the OE-CTHRC1-NC group, the OE-CTHRC1 group showed a significantly increased S-phase fraction (43.24% vs. 32.24%, P < 0.0001), decreased G0/G1 phase fraction (44.90% vs. 55.03%, P = 0.0002), and reduced apoptosis rate (1.31% vs. 1.65%, P = 0.0237). Conversely, compared to the sh-CTHRC1-NC group, the sh-CTHRC1 group exhibited a significantly increased G0/G1 phase fraction (68.78% vs. 53.32%, P < 0.0001), decreased S-phase fraction (18.25% vs. 33.69%, P < 0.0001), and elevated apoptosis rate (5.47% vs. 1.81%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3 (D-F)).To further explore the role of CTHRC1 in the tumor microenvironment, we established a co-culture system of SK-NEP-1 cells with M2-TAMs. EdU staining showed that, compared to controls, M2-TAMs proliferation was significantly enhanced in the SK-NEP-1-OE-CTHRC1 + M2 group (P < 0.01) but suppressed in the SK-NEP-1-sh-CTHRC1 + M2 group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3G). Western blot analysis revealed that, compared to their respective controls, the expression of M2-TAMs polarization markers CD206 and CD163 was significantly upregulated in the OE-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.01) but downregulated in the sh-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3H).These results demonstrate that CTHRC1 promotes Wilms tumor progression by enhancing cell proliferation, regulating cell cycle progression, inhibiting apoptosis, and influencing the polarization and proliferation of tumor-associated macrophages, suggesting CTHRC1 as a potential therapeutic target for WT treatment.

## CTHRC1 promotes tumor growth in G401 cells and xenograft model
To validate the efficacy of CTHRC1 expression regulation, fluorescence microscopy revealed that the lentiviral transduction efficiency exceeded 90% in all G401 cell groups, including OE-CTHRC1, sh-CTHRC1, and their respective controls (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis demonstrated that CTHRC1 protein expression was significantly downregulated in the sh-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.0001) and upregulated in the OE-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.0001) compared to their controls. RT-qPCR results showed similar trends, with significantly decreased mRNA levels in the sh-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.05) and increased levels in the OE-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.0001). Notably, no significant differences were observed between negative controls (sh-CTHRC1-NC and OE-CTHRC1-NC) and untreated groups (P > 0.05), confirming that the vector backbone had no substantial impact on baseline CTHRC1 expression. These results collectively demonstrated the successful establishment of stable cell lines with differential CTHRC1 expression (Fig. 4(B-C)).To further investigate the biological function of CTHRC1 in vivo, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established. All groups successfully developed tumors, but with distinct growth characteristics. Compared to controls, the OE-CTHRC1 group exhibited accelerated tumor volume growth and significantly increased final tumor weight (P < 0.001), whereas tumor growth was notably suppressed in the sh-CTHRC1 group, showing reduced volume and weight (P < 0.001). Throughout the experiment, mouse body weights remained stable across all groups, indicating that the experimental interventions did not affect overall health status (Fig. 4D-H). H&E staining revealed that tumors in the Model group displayed densely packed cells with uniform morphology, suggesting active proliferation. Compared to the Model group, the OE-CTHRC1 group showed more compact cellular arrangement with increased heterogeneity, while the sh-CTHRC1 significantly decreased cell density (Fig. 4I).

## CTHRC1 regulates M2-TAMs infiltration and polarization in both tumor microenvironment and peripheral blood
To validate the expression level of CTHRC1 in the xenograft model, immunohistochemistry and Western blot analyses were performed. Results showed that CTHRC1 expression was significantly upregulated in the OE-CTHRC1 group and downregulated in the sh-CTHRC1 group (Fig. 5(A-B)). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of CD163⁺/CD206⁺ TAMs in tumor tissues was 5.06% in the Model group, significantly increased to 7.52% in the OE-CTHRC1 group, and notably higher than 6.20% in the OE-CTHRC1-NC control group (P < 0.001). Conversely, the proportion in the sh-CTHRC1 group decreased to 3.12%, significantly lower than 4.12% in the sh-CTHRC1-NC group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). Additionally, as monocytes serve as precursor cells of M2-TAMs, analysis of peripheral blood monocytes provided a research basis for investigating CTHRC1-regulated M2-TAMs polarization. The study showed that the proportion of CD163⁺/CD206⁺ cells in the Model group was 4.24%, significantly increased to 7.31% in the OE-CTHRC1 group, and higher than 5.12% in the OE-CTHRC1-NC group (P < 0.0001). Meanwhile, this proportion decreased to 2.49% in the sh-CTHRC1 group, significantly lower than 3.08% in the sh-CTHRC1-NC group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5D). These results indicated a positive correlation between CTHRC1 expression and M2-TAMs infiltration. To explore the effect of CTHRC1 on M2-TAMs infiltration, immunofluorescence double staining was first used to detect the expression of CD163 and CD206 in transplanted tumor tissues. In tissue sections, cell nuclei were stained blue with DAPI, while CD163 and CD206 were labeled red and green by Alexa Fluor 555 and 488, respectively, with co-expression regions displayed in yellow. Quantitative analysis showed that the proportion of CD163⁺/CD206⁺ cells in the OE-CTHRC1 group was significantly higher than that in the control group (P < 0.0001), indicating that CTHRC1 overexpression enhanced M2-TAMs infiltration. In contrast, this proportion significantly decreased in the sh-CTHRC1 group (P < 0.0001), suggesting that CTHRC1 suppression reduced M2-TAMs infiltration and polarization (Fig. 5E). Correlation analysis revealed a positive association between CTHRC1 expression levels and the positive rate of CD206⁺CD163⁺ cells across different groups (Model, OE-CTHRC1-NC, OE-CTHRC1, sh-CTHRC1-NC, sh-CTHRC1), showing that increased CTHRC1 expression promoted M2-TAMs polarization (Fig. 5F).

## Transcriptome analysis reveals key regulatory pathways and target genes mediated by CTHRC1 in TAMs
To investigate the molecular mechanism of CTHRC1 in regulating M2-TAMs, transcriptome sequencing was performed on tumor-associated macrophages isolated from mouse tumor tissues with CTHRC1 interference and overexpression. As shown in Table S1 (supplementary material file), the quality control data demonstrated uniform GC content (48.5-49.5%) across samples, with Clean Reads (39709344–43223082) accounting for 95.64-97.34% of raw data, indicating reliable sequencing quality. The distribution of differentially expressed genes between groups was visualized by volcano plots (Fig. 6A): 629 differential genes were identified between OE-CTHRC1 and OE-CTHRC1-NC groups (162 up-regulated, 467 down-regulated), while 1417 differential genes were found between OE-CTHRC1 and Model groups (568 up-regulated, 849 down-regulated); 463 differential genes were identified between sh-CTHRC1 and sh-CTHRC1-NC groups (170 up-regulated, 293 down-regulated), and 1665 differential genes were found between sh-CTHRC1 and Model groups (710 up-regulated, 955 down-regulated). A distinct bipolar expression pattern of these differential genes among groups was revealed by clustering heatmap (Fig. 6B). GO enrichment analysis bubble plots (Fig. 6C) indicated that the differential genes were primarily involved in biological functions such as extracellular region, cell surface, immune system processes, cell migration regulation, and cholesterol metabolism.Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 6D) revealed significant enrichment of differential genes in pathways including complement and coagulation cascades, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, osteoclast differentiation, TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and PPAR signaling pathway. KEGG pathway Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 6E) showed that 17, 37, 13, and 46 significant pathways were enriched in the four comparisons (OE vs. OENC, OE vs. Model, sh vs. shNC, sh vs. Model), respectively, with “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” being the only commonly enriched pathway. Through differential gene Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 6F), 87 common differential genes were identified, among which 6 genes (TNFRSF9, FAS, LIF, CCL5, CSF3, CX3CR1) were found to be associated with the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway, as shown in Table S2(supplementary material file). Opposite expression trends were observed for these genes under CTHRC1 overexpression and interference conditions: FAS, LIF, CCL5, and CSF3 were down-regulated during CTHRC1 overexpression but up-regulated during interference, while TNFRSF9 and CX3CR1 were up-regulated during CTHRC1 overexpression but down-regulated during interference, suggesting their potential roles as key factors in CTHRC1-mediated tumor immune regulation.

## CTHRC1 promotes M2-TAMs proliferation and migration by enhancing TNFSF9 secretion and its binding to TNFRSF9 on M2-TAMs
RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that, when compared with the M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC group, overexpression of CTHRC1 in the M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC group led to a significant decrease in the expression of FAS, CCL5, and CSF3 (P < 0.05). Concurrently, it caused a marked increase in TNFRSF9 expression (P < 0.01), while the expression levels of LIF and CX3CR1 remained unchanged (P > 0.05). In contrast, in comparison with the M2 + sh-CTHRC1-NC group, knockdown of CTHRC1 in the M2 + sh-CTHRC1 group notably elevated the expression of FAS, CCL5, and CSF3 (P < 0.05), and significantly reduced the expression of TNFRSF9 and CX3CR1 (P < 0.01). Only the expression of Lif showed no significant alteration (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that TNFRSF9 could be a potential downstream target of CTHRC1 (Fig. 7A).In SK-NEP-1 cells, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that, compared with the OE-CTHRC1-NC group, the OE-CTHRC1 group exhibited a significant up-regulation of TNFSF9 mRNA levels (P < 0.01). Western blot analysis further confirmed an increase in TNFSF9 protein expression, and ELISA results indicated a significant enhancement in its secretion levels (P < 0.01). Collectively, these data suggest that CTHRC1 positively regulates the expression and secretion of TNFSF9 (Fig. 7 (B-D)).In the co-culture system, relative to the M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC group, the M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC group displayed a higher cell density and closer connections among M2-TAMs. CCK-8 assay demonstrated a significant enhancement in cell viability (P < 0.01), and Transwell assay showed an increased number of migrated cells (P < 0.01). These promoting effects were dose-dependently attenuated by anti-TNFSF9 neutralizing antibodies, suggesting that CTHRC1 promotes the proliferation and migration of M2-TAMs through TNFSF9 (Fig. 7(E-G)).When compared with the untreated M2 group, both low and high doses of exogenous TNFSF9 treatment significantly enhanced the proliferation (P < 0.01) and migration (P < 0.001) of M2-TAMs in a dose-dependent manner, directly validating the promoting effects of TNFSF9 (Fig. 7H).Immunofluorescence co-localization analysis indicated that, compared with the OE-CTHRC1-NC group, the OE-CTHRC1 group had increased TNFSF9 expression in SK-NEP-1 cells and enhanced TNFRSF9 expression on the M2 - TAMs membrane. The co-localization at the cell membrane suggested a direct interaction between them (Fig. 7I).Both RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses showed that, compared with the M2 control and M2 + sh-TNFRSF9-NC groups, the M2 + sh-TNFRSF9 group had a significant reduction in TNFRSF9 expression (P < 0.01), confirming the successful knockdown (Fig. 7(J-K)).Finally, compared with the M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC and M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC/sh-TNFRSF9-NC groups, the M2 + OE-CTHRC1-NC/sh-TNFRSF9 group exhibited a significant decrease in the number of transmigrated cells (P < 0.001), indicating that reduced TNFRSF9 expression can suppress CTHRC1-induced M2-TAMs migration (Fig. 7L).In summary, these comprehensive results suggest that CTHRC1 regulates the function of M2-TAMs by promoting the expression and secretion of TNFSF9 in tumor cells, which then binds to TNFRSF9 receptors on M2-TAMs.

## M2-TAMs promote the progression of SK-NEP-1 cells through the PI3K/Akt pathway
CCK-8 assay showed that SK-NEP-1 + M2 co-culture significantly enhanced cell proliferation (P < 0.001), while this promotion was markedly inhibited by PI3K-specific inhibitor LY294002 (P < 0.001), suggesting PI3K pathway mediates M2-TAMs’ pro-proliferative effects (Fig. 9A).Apoptosis analysis revealed significantly lower total apoptosis rate (early apoptosis 0.97%+late apoptosis 1.90%=2.87%) in SK-NEP-1 + M2 group compared to SK-NEP-1 group (6.66%) (P < 0.001), which recovered to 5.00% after LY294002 treatment, demonstrating M2-TAMs suppress apoptosis through PI3K pathway (Fig. 9B).Flow cytometry analysis showed significantly increased S-phase (23.49%) and G2/M-phase proportions with decreased G0/G1-phase (47.35%) in SK-NEP-1 + M2 group compared to SK-NEP-1 group (P < 0.001), which was completely reversed by LY294002, indicating M2-TAMs promote cell cycle progression via PI3K pathway (Fig. 9C). Western blot quantification verified significantly higher p-PI3K/PI3K and p-Akt/Akt ratios in SK-NEP-1 + M2 group compared to SK-NEP-1 group (P < 0.001), while LY294002 treatment markedly reduced the phosphorylation levels of these key proteins (P < 0.001) (Fig. 9D). Further RT-qPCR validation confirmed significantly upregulated PI3K and Akt mRNA expression in SK-NEP-1 + M2 group, which was inhibited by LY294002 (Fig. 8E). These results systematically demonstrated that M2-TAMs promote Wilms tumor cell proliferation, suppress apoptosis, and accelerate cell cycle progression through activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, thereby driving tumor progression.

## Discussion
This investigation represents the first systematic elucidation of CTHRC1’s critical function in modulating the pathogenesis of WT. Our study revealed the crucial role of CTHRC1 in WT development and progression while systematically elucidating its complex molecular mechanisms through a dual-action pathway. On one hand, CTHRC1 directly acts on tumor cells, promoting malignant biological behaviors including proliferation, cell cycle progression, and anti-apoptosis. On the other hand, CTHRC1 can recruit and activate M2-TAMs through the TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 axis, remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment and facilitating tumor immune escape and malignant progression. Significantly, activated M2-TAMs can reciprocally secrete TNFSF9, activating TNFRSF9 reverse signaling, which subsequently activates the core PI3K/Akt pathway in tumor cells, further promoting tumor cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. This creates a CTHRC1-TNFSF9/TNFRSF9-PI3K/Akt positive feedback regulatory loop that drives the continuous malignant evolution of WT. The high expression of CTHRC1 in tumor cells can not only directly drive tumor progression but also indirectly promote the expression of TNFSF9 by upregulating the secretion level of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These pro-inflammatory cytokines significantly enhance the expression level of TNFSF9 through a cascade reaction, thereby promoting the specific binding of TNFSF9 to the TNFRSF9 receptor on the surface of M2-TAMs. It is worth noting that the M2-type genes within M2-TAMs can positively regulate the expression of TNFRSF9, further strengthening this signaling interaction (Fig. 10). When TNFSF9 binds to TNFRSF9, it activates TRAF1 and TRAF2 proteins, triggering PI3K phosphorylation and subsequent Akt signaling pathway activation, thereby promoting tumor cell survival. Additionally, the interaction between TNFRSF9 and TNFSF9 promotes M2-TAMs infiltration and polarization within the tumor microenvironment, further facilitating tumor growth and progression. This research not only unveils the critical role of CTHRC1 in WT pathogenesis but also significantly expands our understanding of the molecular basis underlying tumor-immune microenvironment interactions. These findings provide potential biomarkers and molecular targets for early diagnosis and targeted therapy of WT, demonstrating substantial clinical translational value. The translation of these findings into clinical practice will require the expertise of clinical laboratory scientists, who play an essential role in implementing novel diagnostic tests and ensuring accurate, timely disease diagnosis in modern healthcare systems [34].
Notably, while this study establishes a novel regulatory axis for CTHRC1 in WT, several mechanistic and translational aspects require further exploration. Although our in vitro and in vivo experiments validate the functional role of CTHRC1-TNFSF9/TNFRSF9-PI3K/Akt signaling, clinical validation in large-scale patient cohorts remains lacking. Previous studies have linked CTHRC1 overexpression to poor prognosis in gastric and hepatocellular carcinomas [35, 36], yet its prognostic significance in WT has not been systematically evaluated. Additionally, while we demonstrate macrophage polarization via TNFSF9/TNFRSF9, CTHRC1 may exert pleiotropic effects through other pathways such as TGF-β or IL-10 signaling, which are known to regulate macrophage plasticity [37, 38]. These alternative mechanisms could explain the observed phenotypic heterogeneity of TAMs in our single-cell datasets.
Importantly, our findings contrast with previous studies focusing on genetic alterations in WT1 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling [39], highlighting an unprecedented role for tumor-stromal interactions in WT progression. A recent study has reported correlations between CTHRC1 expression and M2-TAM infiltration in cancer [40], aligning with our single-cell observations. However, our study uniquely identifies the TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 axis as the mechanistic link, providing a targetable pathway for therapeutic intervention. Future investigations should address the potential crosstalk between CTHRC1-mediated immune remodeling and classical oncogenic pathways in WT.

Furthermore, while our cell cycle analysis reveals S phase-specific effects of CTHRC1, the functional consequences of prolonged S phase duration remain incompletely understood. Although this phenotype may enhance genomic instability [41–44], its contribution to tumorigenesis requires validation in genetically engineered mouse models. Additionally, the role of CTHRC1 in endothelial cell activation and angiogenesis warrants exploration, given TNFRSF9’s documented role in vascular remodeling [45]. These mechanistic gaps underscore the need for integrative multi-omics approaches to fully characterize CTHRC1’s tumor-promoting functions.
This investigation represents the first systematic elucidation of CTHRC1’s critical function in modulating the immunological microenvironment of WT. Our experimental observations demonstrate significant upregulation of CTHRC1 expression in WT tissues, with predominant localization to the tumor stromal compartment-a distinctive subcellular distribution pattern previously documented in various solid malignancies, including gastric carcinoma [35] and hepatocellular carcinoma [36]. These findings suggest that CTHRC1 potentially mediates tumor progression predominantly through reconstruction of the tumor microenvironment rather than via direct interaction with neoplastic cells. Significantly, tumor-infiltrating macrophages exhibit substantial phenotypic plasticity within the tumor microenvironment, with M1 macrophages demonstrating the capacity for phenotypic transformation to the M2 state under tumor cell-mediated stimulation [37, 38]. This observation indicates that M2-TAMs in WT potentially originate from both recruited monocytic populations and reprogrammed M1 macrophages. Tumor-associated macrophages play a complex dual role in the tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor growth and immune evasion while also participating in tumor surveillance, and this dynamic interaction is crucial for tumor progression and treatment response [46]. Comprehensive delineation of the molecular mechanisms governing macrophage polarization within the WT microenvironment may facilitate the development of novel therapeutic interventions targeting macrophage-mediated processes.
Cell cycle analyses reveal that CTHRC1 overexpression-induced diminution of G0/G1 phase populations potentially occurs through suppression of regulatory factors including RB(retinoblastoma tumor suppressor protein) and subsequent activation of Cyclin D-CDK4/6 complexes, thereby alleviating G1 checkpoint restrictions [41, 42]. Notably, CTHRC1 overexpression induced a marked elevation in S phase population, potentially through attenuation of S phase regulatory mechanisms and promotion of sustained DNA replication processes. The CTHRC1-mediated prolongation of S phase may enhance susceptibility to DNA replication errors and mutation accumulation, potentially constituting an additional significant mechanism underlying its tumor-promoting capabilities. Similar to our findings, other studies have demonstrated that natural compounds can selectively target cancer cells through cell cycle regulation, such as Moringa oleifera leaf extracts which induce cytotoxicity in leukemia cells by activating the P21 pathway independent of P53 [42]. Of particular significance, the minimal impact of CTHRC1 overexpression on G2/M phase progression suggests mechanistic specificity primarily directed toward S phase regulatory processes rather than G2/M phase transitions [43, 44]. These observations provide novel mechanistic insights into CTHRC1’s role in neoplastic transformation and progression.
Further mechanistic investigations revealed CTHRC1-mediated regulation of M2-TAMs through the TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 signaling axis, a finding of considerable therapeutic implications. The TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 pathway exhibits dichotomous functionality in tumor immunomodulation: while capable of enhancing T cell proliferation and cytotoxic responses to augment anti-tumor immunity, sustained TNFSF9 stimulation may induce T cell hyperactivation, culminating in apoptotic death and functional exhaustion [47]. Consequently, despite the pathway’s crucial role in potentiating tumor-specific T cell responses, its prolonged or excessive activation may elicit detrimental outcomes, necessitating careful consideration in the development of targeted immunotherapeutic strategies [48, 49]. Additionally, our novel observation of TNFSF9 expression and secretion by specific tumor cell populations establishes new avenues for investigating the regulatory mechanisms and biological significance of TNFSF9 expression patterns.
Notwithstanding these advances, our study has several limitations that warrant attention. The relatively small sample size of our single-cell analysis may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly regarding rare cell subsets. While we validated our results using multiple public datasets, direct comparison with other WT studies is challenging due to differences in experimental design and analytical pipelines. For example, a previous study of WT have focused on epithelial-mesenchymal transition [50], whereas our analysis emphasizes immune cell dynamics. Additionally, while we demonstrate the functional role of CTHRC1 in macrophage polarization, the precise contribution of tumor-derived TNFSF9 to systemic immune responses remains unclear. Future studies integrating spatial transcriptomics and functional imaging will be essential to address these limitations.
Finally, while our findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the CTHRC1-TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 axis, clinical translation must account for the pathway’s dual role in immune activation and exhaustion [47–49]. The development of targeted therapies with selective toxicity against cancer cells, such as the recently identified Moringa oleifera leaf extracts that selectively induce cytotoxicity in leukemia cells while sparing healthy cells [51], represents an important consideration for future therapeutic strategies. Strategies combining CTHRC1 inhibition with checkpoint blockade therapy may mitigate these risks, as previously demonstrated in preclinical models of prostate cancer [52]. These considerations underscore the importance of precision medicine approaches tailored to individual tumor microenvironments.
While our findings elucidate the mechanism through which M2-TAMs promote WT progression via PI3K/Akt signaling, several critical questions warrant further investigation. The tumor-promoting effects of M2-TAMs encompass multiple mechanisms beyond the immunosuppressive functions characterized in this study, including promotion of tumor angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metastatic dissemination [45, 53–55]. Previous investigations have demonstrated TNFRSF9 signaling-mediated proliferation of endothelial and smooth muscle cells, contributing to angiogenic processes in conditions including atherosclerosis and restenosis [45]. Consequently, the potential paracrine effects of CTHRC1-induced tumoral TNFSF9 secretion on endothelial TNFRSF9 receptors and subsequent contributions to tumor angiogenesis merit further investigation. Moreover, CTHRC1, as a secreted protein, may participate in epigenetic modification of WT-associated genes, potentially contributing to WT genetic susceptibility. Future research directions should encompass comprehensive investigation of the relationship between CTHRC1 and WT-associated genetic alterations, incorporating multi-level integrated analysis of its mechanistic contributions to WT pathogenesis.
However, this study still has some limitations and shortcomings. In the detection of CD206⁺/CD163⁺ peripheral blood monocytes, although mature TAMs do not exist in peripheral blood, monocytes—as precursor cells of macrophages—can migrate to tumor tissues and differentiate into TAMs under the influence of tumor microenvironment signals. Therefore, we analyzed the expression of specific markers (CD206⁺/CD163⁺) in peripheral blood monocytes, hoping to use them as surrogate indicators of M2 polarization. This is because the positive subpopulation of CD206⁺/CD163⁺ peripheral blood monocytes reflects their pre-activated state influenced by tumor signals. Although peripheral blood monocytes themselves are not tumor-associated macrophages, their state provides valuable information for evaluating the activity of M2 TAMs, which is of great significance in translational medicine research. While our data show enrichment of CD206⁺/CD163⁺ monocytes in tumor-bearing samples, the specific tumor-derived signals (such as cytokines, exosomes, or circulating tumor cells) driving monocyte differentiation into M2-TAMs remain unclear. We acknowledge the lack of mechanistic validation in this regard and plan to investigate the signaling interactions between M2-TAMs and monocytes in future studies.
In summary, this study provides a mechanistic framework for CTHRC1’s role in WT progression, but critical gaps remain in our understanding of its broader biological implications. Addressing these limitations will require interdisciplinary collaboration, combining advanced single-cell technologies, functional genomics, and clinical validation to fully realize the translational potential of CTHRC1 as a therapeutic target. Such collaborative efforts highlight the importance of supporting medical researchers across different cultural and geographical contexts, as scientific ability transcends boundaries and should be embraced rather than suppressed [56].

## Conclusion
The present investigation elucidates that CTHRC1, significantly upregulated in Wilms tumor tissues, functions as a critical mediator of tumor progression through orchestrating both direct oncogenic signaling and immunomodulatory mechanisms. Our molecular and functional analyses demonstrate that CTHRC1 induces M2-type tumor-associated macrophage polarization and infiltration via the TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 signaling axis, wherein CTHRC1-mediated TNFSF9 secretion by neoplastic cells engages TNFRSF9 receptors on M2-TAMs. These activated M2-TAMs subsequently promote tumor progression through PI3K/Akt pathway activation, establishing a CTHRC1/TNFSF9-TNFRSF9/M2-TAMs/PI3K/Akt regulatory cascade. This newly delineated molecular mechanism not only provides novel insights into the pathogenesis of Wilms tumor but also identifies potentially targetable nodes within the tumor-immune microenvironment interface, thereby presenting promising therapeutic implications for clinical intervention.
