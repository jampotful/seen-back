Ameloblastin amphipathic helix motif mediates ameloblast polarization and prismatic enamel formation

# Ameloblastin amphipathic helix motif mediates ameloblast polarization and prismatic enamel formation

## Abstract
Ameloblastin (Ambn) is a tooth-specific multifunctional protein essential for enamel biomineralization and the formation of its prismatic microstructure. To examine the function of the evolutionarily conserved cell-binding Ambn amphipathic helix (AH) motif, we deleted the hydrophobic residues within the Ambn AH motif in genetically engineered mice. Enamel in the homozygous (AmbnΔL76-P86) mutants had normal thickness but was hypo-mineralized and lacked prismatic structure. Micro-CT analysis further revealed that both the secretory and maturation stages of amelogenesis were delayed and proceeded slower than in the controls. Ameloblasts in the mutants were stunted and exhibited loss of cell polarity, as demonstrated by the mislocalization of Pard3, Claudin-1 and GM130 immunosignals. In the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants, a loss of Ambn-ameloblast distal membrane interaction was observed, with nuclear localization of β-catenin and p-Smad2/3, and a decrease in RhoA immunolabeling intensity, suggesting that changes in known signaling pathways may connect Ambn-cell interactions to the establishment of cell polarity. Together, these findings support a model in which AH-dependent Ambn engagement at the distal ameloblast membrane contributes to secretory-stage polarity and prism patterning, with downstream consequences for enamel organization and maturation-stage mineral density.

## Introduction
Dental enamel is a highly mineralized tissue that is entirely epithelial in origin.1 The molecular interactions that govern the establishment of terminally differentiated ameloblasts are tightly controlled, with reciprocal interactions between the epithelium and the underlying mesenchyme.2 Evolutionarily, prismatic enamel emerged about 200 million years ago in the late Triassic period.3,4 With the evolution of mammals, enamel structural complexity and hierarchy increased, with a resultant arrangement of calcium hydroxyapatite mineral bundles into a highly ordered hierarchical structure comprising prisms (rods) and inter-prisms (inter-rods).5–7
Unlike most epithelial cells, where the Golgi apparatus lies near the apical membrane to support directional secretion, ameloblasts are a uniquely polarizing cell type with their Golgi positioning towards the basement membrane (away from the anatomical apical membrane) during the presecretory stage of amelogenesis. This phenomenon has been described as a reversal of polarity.8–10 In the mouse, the basement membrane is degraded just prior to the onset of secretory stage, and the anatomical base serves as the functional apex through which secretion of proteins commences.11 Mammalian enamel is organized across multiple hierarchical levels of structure.12 At the level of rod-interrod organization, enamel architecture has been correlated with ameloblast morphology, particularly the presence and shape of Tomes’s processes, which are associated with spatially distinct secretion patterns.13–15 However, the molecular mechanisms that couple ameloblast morphology to these secretion patterns remain poorly understood.
Ameloblastin is the second most abundant extracellular matrix protein (EMP) after amelogenin, and in recent years has emerged as a multifunctional protein involved in extracellular matrix mineralization,16 cell adhesion and differentiation.17 The exon 5-encoded region of ameloblastin is part of a recently identified multitargeting domain in the N-terminal region. We have demonstrated that this domain encompasses the evolutionarily conserved cell-binding amphipathic helix-forming (AH) motif,18,19 in addition to the Ambn self-assembly20 and Ambn-Amel co-assembly motif.21 Genetically engineered animal models of Ambn truncation and deletion that result in a fundamental lack of true enamel22–24 and Amelogenesis Imperfecta involving AMBN16,24 further highlight the indispensability of this protein during amelogenesis. To date, nine different types of mutations in the AMBN gene have been associated with Amelogenesis Imperfecta.17 Of these, only a single frameshift mutation within the exon 5-encoded AH motif region resulting in nonsense-mediated decay of AMBN has been reported to cause enamel mineralization defects, including specific disruptions of enamel prismatic microstructure.16 An in-depth review of all AI cases involving AMBN is described elsewhere.17
Among the species with functional prismatic enamel, there is a high degree of conservation of residues in the N-terminal region of ameloblastin.25 Within the N-terminal region of Ambn exon 5 is the AH motif, spanning residues 69–86 of mouse Ambn.18 Amphipathic helices were first discovered in the context of apolipoproteins and antimicrobial peptides and are classical cell-binding motifs.26,27 We have shown that the Ambn AH motif mediates ameloblast cell membrane-Ambn interactions and is the bioactive region that can promote the elongation and polarization of cells in 3D cultures of ameloblasts lines.28–30 We reported that within the EMPs, the AH motif is unique to ameloblastin,28 and within vertebrates, only mammalian ameloblastin contains the AH motif.31 Subsequently, by evolutionary analysis of enamel structure and ameloblastin sequence from 53 vertebrates, we determined that the AH motif and prismatic enamel co-evolved in mammals.31
It has generally been accepted that ameloblast cell shape is what patterns the matrix structure and in turn the mineral organization.5,32,33 However, the molecular cues that establish and maintain the highly elongated shape and polarity remain incompletely defined. Here, we demonstrate that the highly elongated and polarized ameloblast morphology, a pre-requisite for matrix secretion and hierarchical patterning, arises consequent to interactions of the Ambn AH motif with the distal ameloblast cell membrane. Using a targeted deletion of hydrophobic residues within the AH motif, we generated a genetically engineered animal model that supports the hypothesis that a functional AH motif is required for the establishment of prismatic enamel structure. In our newly designed mutants, we observe the formation of full-thickness enamel, albeit with a loss of mineral density and a lack of prismatic organization. Unlike previous models, one copy of the deletion was sufficient to generate a phenotype. Importantly, these observations mirror findings from a case of Amelogenesis Imperfecta resulting from a truncation of AMBN within the AH motif region, further adding to the clinical relevance of this model.16 Finally, using this model, we reinforce the concept of multifunctionality of Ambn, with a selective disruption of ameloblastin-ameloblast interactions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) were used to compare the self-assembly properties of the recombinant mouse AmbnΔL76-P86, wild type (WT) Ambn (positive control) and Ambn Δ5 (negative control). Ribbon-like structures were observed in the recombinant WT Ambn samples, ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers in length (Fig. 1b, left). Recombinant AmbnΔL76-P86, while heterogeneous, exhibited a tendency to self-assemble and formed small fiber-like structures in the range of tens of nanometers (Fig. 1b, right, white arrow). Recombinant AmbnΔ5 neither self-assembled nor formed ribbon-like structures (Supplementary Fig. 1c). DLS analysis of recombinant AmbnΔL76-P86 protein solution showed a heterogeneous particle size distribution between 90 and 200 nm, which was comparable to the WT recombinant mouse Ambn (Supplementary Fig. 1d). In addition, DLS detected another population of ~10 nm particles with low dispersity. Taken together, this indicates that AmbnΔL76-P86 retains the ability to self‑assemble, albeit into assemblies that are less homogeneous than those of full‑length Ambn.
The effect of AH motif truncation on Ambn-ameloblast cell membrane interaction was examined in vitro using ameloblast lineage cells (ALC) with full-length and mutant recombinant Ambn proteins analyzed in direct cell spreading assays and corresponding peptides (called xAB2)34 in cell spreading competition assays. On average, 88.6% of ALCs cultured on recombinant WT Ambn-coated plates adopted a spread morphology, suggesting the presence of intact membrane interaction domains. In the case of recombinant AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant, disruption of the helix resulted in only 58.2% of ALCs adopting a spread morphology (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1c, left).
The loss of membrane interactions and the specificity of the LP region in mediating these interactions were confirmed using a cell-spreading competition assay with synthetic peptides (Fig. 1c, right). Only 34% of cells treated with full-length peptide adopted a spread morphology on recombinant WT Ambn-coated plates, suggesting that the successful xAB2 peptide-ALC membrane interaction (P < 0.001) was sufficient to inhibit ALC cell spreading. With mutant xAB2ΔL76-P86 as the competing peptide, 70% of ALC adopted a spread morphology on recombinant WT Ambn-coated plates (P < 0.001). The inability of the xAB2ΔL76-P86 peptide to inhibit cell spreading suggests that the mutant xAB2ΔL76-P86 does not interact with the ALC membrane.
Sanger sequencing confirmed the successful generation of an in-frame deletion of thirty-three base pairs that corresponds to the AmbnΔL76-P86 deletion, without any other off-target effects (Fig. 1d). Importantly, quantitative PCR analysis revealed no significant differences in the expression levels of AmelX and Ambn in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants compared to AmbnWT/WT (Fig. 1e, left and right) (P > 0.05). Additionally, RNA sequencing analysis revealed no change in the expression levels of the major extracellular matrix (ECM) genes involved in amelogenesis (Fig. 1f). Both Ambn isoforms were expressed at comparable levels in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants compared with AmbnWT/WT (Fig. 1g). These findings indicate that disruption of the AH motif does not affect the expression of key enamel matrix genes, establishing AmbnΔL76-P86 as a suitable model for studying the direct role of the AH motif in enamel formation.

## AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mouse enamel has normal thickness but a decreased mineral density
To assess the development of incisor enamel, we examined micro-CT reconstructions at both 14 µm (“low-resolution”) and 6 µm (“high-resolution”) isotropic voxel pitch. In false-colored, sagittal, low-resolution tomograms, incisor enamel in AmbnWT/WT and AmbnΔL76-P86+/− hemimandibles reached full thickness in secretory stage (Fig. 2d, e). Mature enamel was uniformly dense and highly mineralized (Fig. 2d, e). While the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− incisor enamel also reached full thickness, mature enamel was hypomineralized and the density distribution was not uniform but patchy (Fig. 2f). Confirming these observations, renderings of the enamel mineral density 15 µm below the surface of high-resolution reconstructions (colorized using a filled-contour map scheme) showed the typical gradual densification across the entire width of the incisor enamel for AmbnWT/WT and AmbnΔL76-P86+/− incisors, albeit with some delay in AmbnΔL76-P86+/− (Fig. 2g, h, vertical bands of color). Densification in AmbnΔL76-P86−/− incisors was delayed even further, no longer displayed the even progression across the entire width of the enamel (Fig. 2i, warped bands of color), and broke into patches of uneven density at about 1 000 µm.
Notably, measurements of incisor enamel thickness in low-resolution reconstructions confirmed that there was no significant difference between AmbnWT/WT and both AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants (n = 5; P > 0.05) (Fig. 2j). In AmbnWT/WT, the incisor mineral density gradually increases moving from the earliest formed enamel at region A to the erupted enamel at region D (Fig. 2k) (For anatomical landmarks identifying these regions, please see Supplementary Fig. 2). The average normalized mineral density of erupted enamel (region D) in the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mutant was only 74% that of AmbnWT/WT (Fig. 2k). The absolute mineral density of erupted enamel of AmbnΔL76-P86−/− was significantly lower compared to AmbnWT/WT (n = 5; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2l). No significant change in the mineral density of erupted enamel was observed in AmbnΔL76-P86+/− compared to AmbnWT/WT (n = 5; P > 0.05).
Plots of the mean maximum thickness of incisor enamel (in high-resolution reconstructions) as a function of arc length revealed that, in AmbnWT/WT, the secretory stage began at approximately −3 500 µm and concluded at −2 000 µm with a maximum thickness between 120 and 140 µm (Fig. 2m). In both AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants, the onset of secretory stage was delayed by ~500 µm. While the maximum thickness did not differ from AmbnWT/WT, it was reached only at an arc length of −1 500 µm in AmbnΔL76-P86+/− and approximately −500 µm in AmbnΔL76-P86−/−.
Plots of the mean mineral density as a function of arc length for AmbnWT/WT incisor enamel revealed the typical, rapid increase in mineral density between the end of secretory stage at −2 500 µm and ~0 µm, where a plateau was reached at 2.4 g/cm3 (Fig. 2n). In both AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants, onset of the maturation stage was delayed by ~500 µm, and densification progressed less rapidly (reflected in the widening of the color bands in Fig. 2h, i compared to Fig. 2g), ending around +2 000 µm. While the final density of mature incisor enamel in AmbnΔL76-P86+/− was the same as in controls, in AmbnΔL76-P86−/−, the final plateau was at a density of 1.7 g/cm3 (70% of AmbnWT/WT).

## Prismatic enamel microstructure is disturbed in AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants
Examination of the incisors under SEM revealed that enamel on the labial side of the AmbnWT/WT and AmbnΔL76-P86+/− incisors appeared smooth (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, d, e), while that of AmbnΔL76-P86−/− exhibited a rough appearance with spherical nodules on the surface and significant blunting of incisal tips (Supplementary Fig. 3c, f). Low-resolution cross-sectional back scatter electron (BSE) images of erupted enamel region in AmbnWT/WT reveal a uniform and highly mineralized enamel layer (Fig. 3a). Two regions were identified along the cross-section of erupted region, which were examined at a higher resolution. In both regions, a tight packing of crystallites and the typical, ordered rod-interrod enamel with evidence of decussation was observed in AmbnWT/WT (Fig. 3b, c). Substantial alterations of the enamel ultrastructure were observed in both AmbnΔL76-P86+/− and AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mice. In both regions of AmbnΔL76-P86+/−, interrod enamel areas were thicker, suggesting an increase in the interrod enamel at the expense of rod enamel (Fig. 3e, f, white arrows). Additionally, large empty gaps were observed in place of rod enamel crystallite bundles in AmbnΔL76-P86+/− (Fig. 3e, f, white arrowheads). Cross-sectional BSE of AmbnΔL76-P86−/− revealed the presence of large gray areas of low mineral density (Fig. 3g, black arrow). ## Ambn-ameloblast cell membrane interactions are lost in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants
In secretory stage of AmbnWT/WT labeling with a custom-made N-terminal Ambn antibody was specific to the Tomes’ process (TPs) and distal ameloblast membrane, including the secretory vesicles (Supplementary Fig. 4a, white arrows). This labeling pattern persisted in the transition stage (Supplementary Fig. 4b). In the maturation stage, however, the ECM labeled strongly for the N-terminal fragment, with a characteristic pattern of labeling reminiscent of the Ambn fragments persisting around the prism boundaries in the prism sheath35 (Supplementary Fig. 4c, white arrows).
Using the custom anti-Ambn antibody revealed that in the AmbnWT/WT samples, the N-terminal Ambn fragment specifically immunolocalized to the distal ameloblast membrane and along the long slender TPs, thereby resulting in a highly polarized distribution within the ameloblast (Fig. 4a). In the AmbnΔL76-P86+/−, a peculiar labeling pattern was present (Fig. 4b). Immunosignals were present both within the ameloblasts and in the ECM. The intracellular labeling in AmbnΔL76-P86+/− was confined to the supranuclear region, with no discernible labeling along the distal ameloblast membrane. In the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− samples, N-terminal Ambn labeling was confined intracellularly within the secretory stage ameloblasts (Fig. 4c). We confirmed the specific loss of membrane localization in the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mutants compared to AmbnWT/WT by co-labeling of β-actin and N-terminal anti-Ambn in secretory stage (Supplementary Fig. 5). In the AmbnWT/WT secretory stage, co-localization between β-actin (green) and N-terminal Ambn (red) was specific to the distal membrane and TPs (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). In the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− most of the N-terminal Ambn immunosignals (red) were within the cell, and only faint labeling was observed along the distal membrane, resulting in little to no co-localization between β-actin (green) and N-terminal Ambn (red) (Supplementary Fig. 5e–h). Overall, removal of the LP region in AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants resulted in a lack of Ambn immunolocalization to the distal ameloblast cell membrane in vivo.

## Ameloblastin-ameloblast distal membrane interaction predicates polarity development in vivo
We recently demonstrated that Ambn directly interacts with ameloblast cell membranes through the AH motif.28 Here, we examined the onset of ameloblast cell polarity and Ambn-ameloblast distal cell membrane interaction in vivo. Using stagewise co-labeling in AmbnWT/WT, we observed that the onset of Pard3 polarity in presecretory stage ameloblasts coincides with the establishment of ameloblastin distal membrane interaction (Supplementary Fig. 6). In the pre-ameloblast stage, the cells are shorter with the nucleus central in its positioning within the cell (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Immunosignals for Pard3 (red) in this stage were diffuse, and those for full-length Ambn (yellow pseudo-color) were intracellular and faint. The onset of polarity in presecretory ameloblasts was characterized by a massive expansion of cytoplasmic volume, resulting in cell elongation and the movement of the nucleus towards the proximal ameloblast membrane (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Immunosignals for Pard3 at this stage were highly polarized and restricted to the vicinity of the proximal ameloblast membrane (Supplementary Fig. 6b, red arrows), and this coincided with the immunolocalization of Ambn to the distal ameloblast membrane (Supplementary Fig. 6b, yellow arrows). The polarized Pard3 distribution within the ameloblasts, and Ambn immunolabeling along the distal ameloblast membrane and TPs continued into the secretory stage (Supplementary Fig. 6c).

## AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants exhibit loss of ameloblast polarity and blunted Tomes’ processes
We hypothesized that the lack of membrane interaction in the LP mutant would negatively impact ameloblast polarity development. To test this, ameloblast polarity was examined at the level of the cell membrane (Fig. 4) and at the level of intracellular organelles in the mutants compared to the AmbnWT/WT animals (Fig. 5). ## Dysregulated TGF-β1 signaling in AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants
To gain insight into possible signaling pathways involved in mediating cell polarization changes, we examined both the canonical TGF-β1 pathway and the MAPK pathways by labeling for p-Smad2/3 and p-Erk1/2, respectively. Labeling for p-Smad2/3 in the AmbnWT/WT revealed positive immunolabeling in the cytoplasm of the secretory stage ameloblast, consistent with the literature (Fig. 8d).38 We observed an increase in the nuclear translocation of p-Smad2/3 in the case of both AmbnΔL76-P86+/− (Fig. 8e, inset) and AmbnΔL76-P86−/− (Fig. 8f, inset). Labeling for p-Erk1/2 in the secretory stage ameloblasts of AmbnWT/WT was negative, and there was no change in the p-Erk1/2 labeling in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants compared to AmbnWT/WT (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c).

## A decrease in the secretory stage RhoA signal intensity in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants
RhoA immunosignals in the AmbnWT/WT and AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants were primarily present along the proximal ameloblast cell membrane (Fig. 8g–i). We did not observe any changes in the localization of RhoA immunosignals in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants. However, the signal intensity appeared reduced in the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mutants. This was confirmed using measurements of RhoA signal intensity at the proximal membrane. Normalized RhoA signal intensity comparison revealed a significant reduction in the RhoA intensity in the AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mutant compared to AmbnWT/WT and AmbnΔL76-P86+/− (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 11).

## Discussion
To examine the biological function of the evolutionarily conserved AH motif within the multitargeting domain of enamel matrix protein Ambn, we designed a mutation that selectively disrupts the AH motif. Our objective was to examine the specific effects of loss of a functional AH motif on enamel formation. We hypothesized that a disruption of the AH motif would affect ameloblastin-cell membrane interactions, and in turn impact ameloblast cell polarization and prismatic enamel formation. We deleted eleven amino acids within the AH motif that are primarily hydrophobic in nature (called LP region). We used recombinant proteins and peptides with similar deletions in in vitro and cell culture experiments to confirm disturbance to the cell-binding function. The in vitro experiments supported the design of our animal model and confirmed that the cell-binding AH motif was specifically targeted without significantly affecting the self-assembly properties of the protein.
Loss of a functional AH motif in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mouse mutants resulted in the formation of mature enamel exhibiting impaired prismatic organization with normal thickness but a significantly lower mineral density. Consistent with these findings, analysis of enamel thickness and mineral density along the incisor arc indicated delayed onset of the secretory and maturation stages in mutants, together with reduced enamel matrix accretion and densification rates relative to controls. Ameloblasts from the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants did not show any overt signs of pathology, except for a stunted morphology with defective cell polarization (Golgi positioning as well as cell membrane domains). A mouse model with disruptions of the highly conserved self-assembly (Y/F-x-x-Y/L/F-x-Y/F) motif using an amino acid substitution model (AmbnG/G) presents a completely different phenotype.20 The disruption of the self-assembly motif without concomitant changes in the AH motif in the AmbnG/G model did not alter ameloblast morphology or polarization. These observations collectively suggest that the Ambn multitargeting region contains domains with distinct functionality.
The finding of normal enamel thickness observed in AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants contrasts outcomes reported for other Ambn mutant models.22–24 Because enamel thickness is largely set by how much matrix is deposited during the secretory stage, we asked whether expression of the other major EMPs was maintained in the mutant. RNA sequencing indicates that the expression levels of AmelX and Enam genes are normal in our mutants. Evolutionary analysis and experimental work support key roles for amelogenin in expanding enamel matrix thickness and promoting the growth of elongated hydroxyapatite crystals.39–41 Enamelin (ENAM), the ancestral gene from which AMBN and AMELX genes arose, has been implicated in controlling enamel apatite crystal nucleation.42–45 Taken together, the preserved thickness in AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants is best explained by a functional uncoupling: disrupting the AH motif selectively compromises polarity-dependent prism organization, while the broader secretory-stage functions of ameloblasts that support matrix accumulation remain sufficiently intact to sustain overall thickness. Importantly, this interpretation supports the conclusion that the AH motif defines a non-redundant, polarity-related function within an otherwise multifunctional protein. In contrast, this polarity-associated role is less amenable to substitution by other EMPs because the AH motif is not present in the other major EMPs.28
Using co-labeling of a custom anti-Ambn antibody with β-actin, we demonstrated a loss of Ambn-ameloblast distal cell membrane interactions in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants. We postulated that the lack of membrane interaction would inhibit the development of ameloblast polarity. To test this, we first co-labeled Ambn and cell polarity protein Pard3 in developing ameloblasts from mice. In the case of wild-type ameloblasts, Pard3 labeling was shown to be specific to the proximal ameloblast cell membrane in association with the proximal tight junctions.36 Using systematic labeling in the presecretory ameloblasts, we show that the onset of Pard3 polarity (as shown by its asymmetrical labeling within the ameloblast proximal membrane) coincided temporally with the immunolocalization of Ambn to the distal ameloblast cell membrane. Our data corroborate recent reports that recombinant mouse Ambn induces an increase in the expression levels of Pard3 in an ameloblast cell line31 and results in polarized immunolocalization of Pard3 within the clusters of elongated cells in 3D cultures.29,30
Observations at both the intracellular organelle and cell‑membrane levels indicated impaired development of cell polarity in homozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant compared to AmbnWT/WT. In contrast, in the heterozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant, cell membrane and intracellular organelle polarity was affected to a lesser extent. Tight junctional protein Cldn-1 transcripts have been shown to be upregulated in the highly elongated presecretory stage ameloblasts compared to the shorter inner enamel epithelial cells.46 Cldn-1 localization in secretory ameloblasts is primarily along the distal membrane and TPs36,46 (Scheme 1). Removal of the LP region resulted in an increase in the cytoplasmic levels of Cldn-1 with little to no membrane localization, resulting in a potential loss of distal tight junction assembly. Tight junctions (TJs) are crucial for paracellular permeability and for restricting the intermixing of apical and basolateral membrane domains.47 Additionally, components of the Crumbs complex homolog, Crb3 have been shown to interact with Cldn-1.48 In secretory stage ameloblasts the proximal TJs have been shown to be structurally leaky while the distal TJs exhibit a more robust structure that likely function to regulate the movement of calcium ions.49,50 These observations collectively highlight that the sequestration of Cldn-1 in the cytoplasm of the mutants likely affects the permeability barrier function as well as the correct positioning of membrane domains in the mutant ameloblasts.Scheme 1Schematic representation summarizing Ambn-cell membrane interactions and the expression of polarization markers in AmbnWT/WT (top) and AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mutant (bottom)

The ability of epithelial cells to rearrange their membrane domains in response to changes in the ECM is well known and characterized.51–53 A unique characteristic that distinguishes ameloblasts is the reversal of polarity during the early stage of enamel development. Ameloblast reversal of polarity has been defined based on electron microscopy observations of the Golgi positioning.1,12 Using GM130 immunolabeling, we observed that the movement of the Golgi around the nucleus was impaired in the case of the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants, beginning from the presecretory stage. Ameloblast elongation was also impaired in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant animals. Notably, AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant cells were 19-23% shorter than those of AmbnWT/WT. Recombinant Ambn induced the selective elongation of ameloblast cell lines along the +Z axis in the independent 3D culture experiments.29,30 Here we show that disruption of the AH motif resulted in a significant impairment of this function both in vitro and in vivo. With overall enamel thickness preserved, our data link altered Golgi positioning and reduced elongation to changes in enamel quality and organization, including lower mineral density, a roughened (“sandpaper-like”) surface appearance, and evidence for disrupted enamel prisms. Together, these findings support a model in which the AH motif contributes primarily to the cellular organization needed for proper matrix organization, prismatic architecture, and enamel densification.
Of particular interest are observations in the heterozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants. In previously reported Ambn knockout models, heterozygous animals do not display an enamel phenotype, indicating that haploinsufficiency is not evident in those systems.22–24 In contrast, heterozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants do exhibit a distinct phenotype characterized by selective disruption of prism-interprism architecture, impaired AH-membrane interactions, incomplete cellular polarization, and altered cell-signaling, despite normal enamel mineral density. Importantly, transcript and protein analysis revealed no changes in the Ambn transcript levels or Ambn protein abundance in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants compared to AmbnWT/WT (Figs. 1 and 4). Together, these findings suggest that the phenotype observed in the heterozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants is not driven by haploinsufficiency, but instead reflects disrupted AH motif interaction at the distal membrane.
It is noteworthy that in cases of AI involving a truncation of AMBN within the AH motif region,16 two different phenotypic presentations and modes of inheritance were observed (hypoplastic AI with autosomal recessive inheritance and hypo-maturation AI with autosomal dominant inheritance). This diversity underscores the functional heterogeneity and multifunctionality of Ambn. We speculate that the phenotype observed in the heterozygous animals might also arise from altered behavior of the mutant Ambn protein within the extracellular matrix, potentially including disrupted interactions with wild-type Ambn that impair its scaffolding function.
Several Wnt pathway genes have been implicated in tooth development,54,55 and it has been shown that constitutive activation of β-catenin in ameloblasts results in enamel hypo-mineralization defects.56 Treatment of ameloblasts with pharmacological GSK3β inhibitor results in ameloblast polarity defects due to an enhancement of Wnt signaling.57 In both homozygous and heterozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants, the integrity of the ameloblast proximal adherens junction was compromised with potential changes in Wnt signaling secondary to the nuclear translocation of β-catenin.58 We also considered pathways previously linked to enamel mineralization and polarity. Hyperactive TGF-β1 signaling during amelogenesis has been linked to enamel mineralization defects in genetically engineered models of Smad2 overexpression.59 Additionally, a hyperactive MAPK pathway (characterized by an increase in the p-Erk1/2 levels) has been shown to drive the pathogenesis of ameloblast polarity defects in mouse models of Costello syndrome.60 Notably, we did not observe any changes in the p-Erk levels in the AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants; however, an increase in the nuclear localization of p-Smad2/3 suggests potential alterations in Smad2/3-dependent (canonical) TGF-β signaling.
Among these candidate pathways, the RhoA–ROCK signaling axis is particularly well suited to connect extracellular cues and cell-surface interactions with cytoskeletal remodeling, junctional stability, and other processes required for polarized organization in ameloblasts. Both RhoA as well as its downstream effector Rock have been extensively characterized in ameloblasts.61–64 Active RhoA immunosignals are upregulated in the elongated ameloblasts compared to the shorter cuboidal pre-ameloblasts.65 RhoA is also necessary for the correct polarized cortical distribution of E-cadherin in ameloblasts65 and both Rock isoforms are expressed in ameloblasts.63 Consistent with this role, treatment of ameloblasts with Rock inhibitor Y27632 resulted in a loss of polarized distribution of E-cadherin and β-catenin as well as disruptions to the directional secretion of Amel and Ambn.63
In prior work, we found that treatment of ameloblast cell lines with recombinant Ambn resulted in an upregulation of Rock1 and Rock2 isoforms when compared to denatured Ambn.31 In the current study, we observed reduced RhoA immunosignal intensity in secretory-stage ameloblasts from AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mutants. Collectively, these findings are consistent with the idea that AH-dependent Ambn-membrane interactions may influence RhoA-ROCK signaling, thereby affecting junctional organization and cell shape. While these data do not establish causality, the convergence of published mechanistic studies with our in vivo phenotypes makes the RhoA–ROCK axis a strong candidate for future functional tests.
In summary, we propose that interaction of Ambn with the ameloblast membrane via the AH motif specifies a unique apical membrane identity and participates in cell signaling interactions that function to establish a prismatic enamel structure through control of ameloblast morphology and polarization. The animal model presented here enables the elucidation of molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of human hereditary enamel defects involving Ambn AH motif. Heterogeneous phenotypic presentations in patients with Amelogenesis Imperfecta involving a truncation of the AH motif further illustrate the dynamic multifunctionality of this region.16,17 Finally, presenting the Ambn AH motif in engineered materials could provide a powerful design handle to promote ameloblast differentiation and polarized organization, support robust enamel matrix deposition, and drive more ordered biomineralization.

## Materials and methods

## Recombinant AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant protein design, expression and purification
An ameloblastin mutant was designed to delete the hydrophobic residues within the evolutionarily conserved AH motif, AmbnΔL76-P86. The details of the expression construct, and recombinant protein expression and purification are elaborated in Supplementary Information.

## Generation of AmbnΔL76-P86 mutant mice
Genetically engineered mice with a disruption of the amphipathic helix (AH) motif through deletion of hydrophobic residues (AmbnΔL76-P86) in a C57BL/6 background were generated at the Mouse Biology Program, UC Davis (CA, USA) using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. Heterozygous mice were transferred to the Department of Animal Resources, University of Southern California. Animals were handled as per approved protocols from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), University of Southern California (IACUC #10929). The AmbnΔL76-P86+/− mice were bred to generate AmbnΔL76-P86−/− mice which were used in the following experiments. Generation of homozygous mutants was confirmed using agarose gel electrophoresis. The mutant animals were maintained on a soft gel diet that was nutritionally balanced. Experimental replicates were performed using animals from different breeding cages (non-littermates). The following sample numbers were used: n = 5 for conventional micro-CT analysis, n = 4 for high-resolution micro-CT analysis of AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants, and n = 2 for AmbnWT/WT. For all immunofluorescence experiments, n = 3.

## Micro-computed X-Ray tomography (micro-CT)
Seven-week-old mouse mandibles were dissected, and the soft tissues were removed using a scalpel. Hemi-mandibles were mounted using wax, with the long axis of incisors and the axis of rotation oriented perpendicular to the X-ray and scanned using SkyScan 1174 (Bruker) operating at 50 kV, 800 μA. A 0.25 mm aluminum filter was used to selectively remove low-energy X-rays and improve image quality. Three-dimensional reconstructions at an isotropic voxel pitch of 14.1 μm (“low resolution”) were generated using NRecon (version 1.6.9.8). The reconstructed dataset was exported as DICOM and visualized using Amira (Thermo). Detailed descriptions of enamel mineral density and thickness measurements in low-resolution reconstructions, and of “high-resolution” micro-CT (6 µm isotropic voxel pitch), segmentation of reconstructions using convolutional neural networks (CNN) as reported previously,66,67 and subsequent analyses are outlined in the Supplementary Information.

## Scanning electron microscopy
Mandibles from 7-week-old mice were dissected, and the soft tissues were removed. The hemi-mandibles were dehydrated using an increasing ethanol gradient and embedded in epoxy resin (Pelco 2-Hour Epoxy Mount Kit, Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA). The embedded hemi-mandibles were sectioned at 1 mm increments from the first molar to the incisor tip. The cross-sections were ground through a series of ascending grits of silicon carbide papers (3M Wetordry Abrasive Sheet) and were polished using a 0.05 μm diamond paste (Buehler). The polished sections were etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 20 s, air-dried, and sputter-coated with a platinum-palladium mixture for 30 s. Samples were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI, OR, USA) at 10 kV.

## Histology and ameloblast height measurements
Post-natal 8-day-old (P8) samples were dissected and fixed (4% paraformaldehyde) overnight and decalcified for 10 days (in 10% EDTA with 0.2% paraformaldehyde). The decalcified mandibles were paraffin-embedded and sectioned to obtain 7-μm-thick sagittal sections that were stained using hematoxylin and eosin as per standard protocols. First and 2nd mandibular molars, along with the cervical loop, were used as anatomical landmarks to orient and identify stages of amelogenesis along the continuously erupting incisors. Presecretory and secretory ameloblast histology was examined, and the ameloblast cell height was measured and compared between AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants and AmbnWT/WT.

## Immunofluorescent labeling of P8 incisors and confocal scanning microscopy
P8 mandibular incisors were used for all the immunolabeling experiments. Paraffin sections of 7 μm thickness along the sagittal orientation were prepared to visualize incisor ameloblasts. A custom antibody was designed with residues 95–108 of mouse Ambn as the epitope, which was used to specifically immunolocalize the N-terminal Ambn fragment. The following primary antibodies were used: goat polyclonal anti-Ambn (AF3026-SP R&D Systems), custom rabbit polyclonal-anti-Ambn (Thermo), rabbit polyclonal anti-GM130 (R1608-7 Huabio), rabbit polyclonal anti-Cldn1 (28674-1-AP Proteintech), rabbit polyclonal anti-Pard3 (11085-1-AP Proteintech), mouse monoclonal anti-E-cadherin 555 conjugated (560064, BD Biosciences), rabbit monoclonal anti-β-catenin (ab32572 Abcam), rabbit monoclonal anti-p-Smad2/3 (ab202445 Abcam), rabbit monoclonal anti-p-Erk1/2 (4370T Cell Signaling Technology), mouse monoclonal anti-total RhoA (sc-418 AF488 SCBT, 488 conjugated), mouse monoclonal anti-β actin (ab6277Abcam, 488 conjugated). The following secondary antibodies were used: donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 647(705-605-003 Jackson Immuno), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (711-605-152 Jackson Immuno), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (A-21206 Thermo), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (711-545-152 Jackson Immuno). Detailed protocols for immunofluorescent labeling are outlined in Supplementary Information. The immunolabeled slides were examined using confocal scanning microscopy (Stellaris, Leica) and three-dimensional Z stacks were recorded with an optical pitch of 0.1–0.3 μm. Reconstructed Z stacks were visualized using maximum intensity projection on LasX (Leica). For measurement of RhoA signal intensity, ten regions of interest along the proximal ameloblast membrane were drawn on maximum intensity projections, and the normalized mean signal intensity was calculated and compared between AmbnWT/WT and AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants.

## Bulk RNA sequencing
Mandibular 1st molar enamel organs were isolated by microscopic dissection from P5 heterozygous and homozygous AmbnΔL76-P86 mutants as well as AmbnWT/WT mice (2 enamel organs per mouse). Detailed protocols are outlined in Supplementary Information.

## Cell culture—3D and 2D
Ameloblast lineage cells (ALC) were obtained from Prof. Toshihiro Sugiyama (University of Akita, Japan).68 ALC were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Corning) supplemented with 10% v/v heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5.0% CO2 atmosphere. For direct spreading and cell spreading competition assays, established protocols were used.69 Detailed methods are outlined in Supplementary Information. ALC were cultured in 3D following published protocols for 3D-on-top type culture.29,70 Detailed methods are outlined in the Supplementary Information. All cell culture experiments were carried out in triplicates and repeated thrice.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were carried out using three independent experimental repeats using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1). The limit for significance was set to P < 0.05. All experiments were repeated thrice. Both parametric (one-way ANOVA) and non-parametric (Kruskal–Wallis) tests were used based on the distribution of the datasets. Post hoc tests used were Dunn’s multiple comparison or Sidak’s multiple comparison.
