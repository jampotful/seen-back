# Transcription factor activator protein 4 (AP4)–mediated intrinsic control of axon regeneration

## Abstract
Activator protein 4 (AP4) is a basic helix-loop-helix leucine-zipper transcription factor, a direct transcriptional target of c-Myc and a key regulator of cell growth and apoptosis, abundantly expressed in cancer cells. Here, we reveal a role for the c-Myc–AP4 axis in axon regeneration and the intrinsic growth capacity of injured neurons. Through bioinformatic and functional analyses, we demonstrated that overexpression of AP4 in mice not only accelerated in vivo axon regeneration and functional recovery after peripheral nerve injury but also promoted robust axon regeneration and neuronal survival after optic nerve injury by activating mammalian target of rapamycin activity. Neuronal-specific knockdown of AP4 abolished the regenerative phenotype induced by c-Myc overexpression and phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) deletion. AP4 overexpression rendered the intrinsic growth capacity of c-Myc silencing injured retinal ganglion cells unaffected. These findings unveil a distinct aspect of the c-Myc–AP4 axis and highlight a previously unrecognized intrinsic role of AP4 in axon regeneration, with potential therapeutic implications.

## INTRODUCTION
Unlike the peripheral nervous system (PNS), injured central nervous system (CNS) neurons have limited intrinsic growth capacity that generally do not regenerate axons spontaneously, and the extrinsic hostile injury microenvironment further impedes axon regeneration after injury (1–9). Genetic ablation, neutralizing antibodies, and enzymatic degradation of extrinsic growth-inhibitory molecules such as neurite outgrowth inhibitor-A, myelin-associated glycoprotein, and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan have only induced modest axon regeneration across the lesion sites, and these approaches remain largely elusive (4, 10–12). This suggests that the loss of intrinsic growth capacity represents a major obstacle to CNS regeneration (5, 6, 13–15).
During early developmental stages, CNS neurons exhibit active and robust axonal growth by activating their intrinsic growth machinery. Once axons reach their target areas, the genes required for elongative growth are down-regulated, while those involved in synaptogenesis are gradually up-regulated, facilitating the formation of functional synapses with their targets (16). However, in adults, this intrinsic growth machinery fails to reactivate, leading to regeneration failure and limited functional recovery following CNS injury. Potent regenerative brakes, such as phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), suppressors of cytokine signaling 3, and (Krüppel-like factor 4), inhibit proregenerative spontaneous adaptive cell responses of CNS neurons (17–19), which are crucial for preventing the formation of misplaced or supernumerary structures. Nevertheless, manipulating these regenerative brakes has been shown to partially promote axonal regrowth in injured CNS neurons of adult mammals (15, 20).
Deletion of PTEN, a negative regulator of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), promotes sustained axon regeneration after optic nerve injury (18). c-Myc is required for the regenerative phenotype of PTEN deletion; however, the precise mechanisms through which c-Myc responds to injury remain unclear. Residual axon regeneration observed in codeletion of PTEN and c-Myc suggests that downstream targets of c-Myc may also contribute to the intrinsic growth capacity of adult retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) (13). The transcription factor activator protein 4 (AP4; also known as TFAP4) has been identified as a direct transcriptional target of c-Myc, playing a role in maintaining a c-Myc–induced anabolic transcriptional program, which is essential for the function of activated B and T cells during immune responses against microbial infections (21, 22). The c-Myc–AP4 axis has been extensively studied in the regulation of various cellular processes in cancerous and nonneuronal cells, including cell proliferation, differentiation, stemness, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis (23–27). However, studies exploring the functional roles of c-Myc–AP4 in the nervous system remain limited. During development, AP4 acts as a corepressor with geminin to inhibit the expression of dual specificity tyrosine-phosphorylation-regulated kinase 1A (DYRK1A) and its interacting partner phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase-interacting protein (PAHX-AP1), which is critical for normal brain development in humans. Notably, reduced AP4 expression leads to premature expression of PAHX-AP1 and DYRK1A in the fetal cerebral cortices of individuals with Down syndrome (28). In another study, Caenorhabditis elegans gene basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein 11 (hih-11) (an ortholog of mammalian AP4) has been linked to key regulatory mechanisms governing lipid metabolism and longevity. In response to starvation, HlH-11 protein underwent degradation, triggering the activation of lipid catabolism genes and subsequent remodeling of the lipid landscape through the mTOR signaling pathway (29, 30). In addition, AP4 has been identified as a key regulator in the terminal branching of Drosophila trachea; mutant carrying the cropped gene (an ortholog of mammalian AP4) displays truncated tracheal terminal branches (31, 32). Genes previously associated with branching morphogenesis in Drosophila, such as branchless (a homolog of mammalian Fgf) and pruned (a homolog of mammalian serum response factor Srf), play essential roles in axonal growth and branching in mammalian system (33–35). Collectively, these studies highlight the importance of AP4 expression in normal brain development and suggest its potential involvement in axonal growth. However, the role of AP4 within the transcriptional intrinsic growth program of injured neurons remains largely unexplored.
In this study, we focus on the role of AP4 in injury-induced responses by investigating whether and how it modulates the intrinsic growth capacity of injured neurons in the PNS and CNS. We found that AP4 was up-regulated after peripheral nerve injury (PNI) and was highly associated with the genes involved in axon regeneration using an unbiased weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) (2, 3, 36). Silencing of AP4 abolished the preconditioning effects completely, leading to impaired axon regeneration and functional recovery following PNI. Conversely, overexpression of AP4 promoted robust axon regeneration after PNI, induced extensive long-distance axon regeneration, and provided substantial neuroprotective effects for RGCs following optic nerve crush (ONC) injury. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that AP4 overexpression enhanced the intrinsic growth capacity of injury RGCs through activation of the mTOR signaling pathway. AP4 was indispensable for both c-Myc overexpression–induced and PTEN deletion–induced regenerative phenotypes. These findings establish the c-Myc–AP4 axis as a critical component of the intrinsic growth transcriptional program and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for promoting axon regeneration after nervous system injuries.

## RESULTS

## AP4 as a potential regulator of the injury-induced intrinsic growth program
We first examined whether AP4 is involved in the injury response and intrinsic growth program of injured neurons. We performed sciatic nerve transection (SNT) injury, and quantified the protein expression of AP4 in the injured (i.e., ipsilateral) lumbar 4 and 5 (L4/5) dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, which directly supplied the sciatic nerve, at various time points from days 1 to 8 post-SNT (2, 3, 9). We observed a gradual increase in AP4 protein expression in injured L4/5 DRG neurons, starting from day 2 and reaching its peak at day 5 post-SNT with a 3.16-fold increase (Fig. 1A). To evaluate the neuronal specificity of AP4 expression response to nerve injury, we performed double-labeling studies using antibodies against AP4 and βIII-tubulin to identify DRG neurons. Consistently, we found a significant increase in AP4 protein levels in βIII-tubulin–positive injured DRG neurons from days 3 to 5 after SNT, with expression returning to baseline levels by day 8 post-SNT (fig. S1). Next, we performed an unbiased genome-wide WGCNA (2, 3, 36, 37) to unravel the transcriptional modules that were coexpressed with AP4 in response to PNI. We built a WGCNA network on a microarray dataset consisting of preconditioned DRGs from adult C57BL/6 mice 4 days after PNI (GSE58982) (38). We used Tfap4 as a seed and identified the top 50 closely related genes ranked according to their topological overlap (TO) with the seed (Fig. 1B). Of these 50 genes that were coexpressed with AP4, 96% were up-regulated (Fig. 1C). WGCNA analysis uncovered that Tfap4 expression was highly associated with genes (30 of 50) involved in axon regeneration after injury, including classical regeneration-associated genes (RAGs) such as Klf6, Atf3, C1qa, Cdkn1a, Sprr1a, Gal, and Npy (table S1). These findings suggest that AP4 might function as a key regulator and indicate its potential role in the intrinsic growth program of injured neurons.

## AP4 is necessary for successful axon regeneration and functional recovery after PNI
To functionally characterize AP4 after PNI, we examined whether knocking down AP4 expression affected axonal regrowth in cultured DRG neurons by gene silencing using short interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting the transcript of AP4. The treatment of cultured DRG neurons with AP4-specific siRNA (AP4-siRNA) markedly reduced the expression of AP4 protein (Fig. 2, A and B) and resulted in a significant reduction in neurite outgrowth by nearly 50% (Fig. 2, C and D), as well as a 38.7% decrease in neurite branching (Fig. 2E). AP4-siRNA treatment had no effects on neuronal survival compared to DRG neurons treated with scrambled (scr)–siRNA (fig. S2A). In DRG cultures treated with AP4-siRNA, the mRNA and protein expression levels of AP4 were markedly reduced by 58 and 36%, respectively (fig. S2, B and C). We observed the relevant inhibitory effect of AP4-siRNA on axonal outgrowth and branching in axotomized DRG neurons using a second siRNA targeting AP4 transcript (fig. S3, A to D), which demonstrated similar knockdown efficiency (fig. S3E). This approach was used to rule out off-target effects associated with the first AP4-siRNA. Knocking down AP4 expression with the second siRNA did not affect neuronal survival (fig. S3F), indicating that the reduction in axonal growth is not therefore secondary to neuronal cell death.

We hypothesize that the up-regulation of AP4 is required for successful axon regeneration and contributes to the increased intrinsic growth capacity after a preconditioning peripheral lesion (2, 3, 6, 8, 9). We then purified DRG neurons prepared from ipsilateral/contralateral L4/5 DRGs 5 days after SNT and cultured them in the presence of AP4-siRNA for 48 hours (Fig. 2F). As expected, preconditioned DRG neurons displayed a marked increase in neurite outgrowth by 34.7% compared with uninjured DRG neurons (Fig. 2G). Gene silencing of AP4 completely abolished the growth-promoting effects induced by preconditioning peripheral lesion (Fig. 2, H to J), suggesting that AP4 is crucial for the intrinsic growth state of injured neuron.
To firmly establish the role of AP4 in axon regeneration in vivo, we selectively knocked down AP4 gene expression using adeno-associated virus serotype 2/9 (AAV2/9)–mediated delivery of short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting AP4 mRNA (fig. S4A). Consistent with our previous study (9), mice receiving sciatic nerve injections of control AAV2/9–scr-shRNA–enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) exhibited high transduction efficiency, with reporter gene (eGFP) expression strictly confined specifically to βIII-tubulin–positive DRG neurons (fig. S4B) and choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)-positive motor neurons (fig. S4C) in the ventral horn of the spinal cord. Notably, virtually no eGFP signals colocalized with SRY-box transcription factor 10 (SOX10)-positive Schwann cells (fig. S4D) in the DRG cryosections. AP4 mRNA expression was reduced by 52.2% in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)–sorted DRG neurons from AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice, confirming an effective and neuron-specific AP4 knockdown in mice (fig. S5).
We then evaluated in vivo axon regeneration following sciatic nerve crush injury in mice treated with AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA (Fig. 2K). In line with our in vitro results, the distal extent of axonal regrowth was significantly decreased in AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice (2.08 ± 0.15 mm) compared to control mice treated with AAV2/9–scr-shRNA (3.14 ± 0.08 mm) (Fig. 2L). This reduction in axonal regrowth was further validated by quantifying superior cervical ganglion 10 (SCG10)–positive regenerating axons at various distances beyond the crush site at day 3 postinjury (5, 9). In AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice, SCG10 immunoreactivity in longitudinal sciatic nerve sections was markedly decreased at 0.5 to 3.5 mm distal to the crush site compared to the control mice. SCG10 immunoreactivity was normalized to the crush site referred to as the regeneration index (Fig. 2M).
We extended these findings to functional recovery in animals, aiming to investigate whether the decreased rate of axon regeneration following AP4 gene silencing affects recovery after PNI. Two weeks before injury, mice received sciatic nerve injections of AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA to specifically knock down AP4 expression in DRG neurons. Starting from day 3 postinjury, a battery of functional recovery tests were performed every other day over a 60-day period (Fig. 3A). Sensory function was evaluated by pinprick assay, which measures the rate of in vivo axonal regrowth after PNI (2, 6, 8, 9, 39). Consistent with the nerve pinch test results, the complete recovery of sensory function was significantly delayed by 6 days in AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice when compared to control mice treated with AAV2/9–scr-shRNA (Fig. 3B). For motor functional recovery, control mice regained the toe spreading reflex by day 21 postinjury, whereas AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice showed a marked delay of 10 days, only fully regaining the toe spreading reflex by day 31 (Fig. 3C). In addition, mice with AP4 knockdown recovered 78.8% of their baseline hindlimb grip strength 1 month after injury. However, their grip strength remained significantly lower than baseline and were unable to return to preinjury levels throughout the entire 2-month observation period. In contrast, control mice restored hindlimb grip strength to baseline levels in about 1 month (Fig. 3D). The delayed motor functional recovery in mice with AP4 knockdown was further validated and quantified by weekly electromyography (EMG) recordings, which assess functional motor synaptic activities in muscles (1, 6, 8, 9). The compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes were significantly reduced in muscles proximal (gastrocnemius) and distal (interosseous) to the crush site. Notably, in AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice, the CMAP amplitudes of both the gastrocnemius and interosseous muscles did not return to baseline levels during the course of recovery (Fig. 3, E and F). These CMAP electrophysiology studies further support the conclusion that knocking down AP4 expression impairs motor functional recovery following PNI.

## Overexpression of AP4 promotes axon regeneration and functional recovery after PNI
Our gene silencing experiments strongly support the notion that AP4 is essential for axon regeneration. We therefore reason that overexpression of AP4 could be beneficial to axonal regrowth and functional recovery following PNI. To test this hypothesis, we intrathecally injected AAV2/9-AP4 (or AAV2/9-eGFP as a control) (Fig. 4A and fig. S6A) into adult C57BL/6 mice, which allowed stable expression of AP4 before sciatic nerve crush injury (Fig. 4B). Consistent with a previous study (40), over 70% of βIII-tubulin–positive L4/5 DRGs were transduced with AAV2/9-eGFP, indicating high transduction efficiency with minimal transduction in nonneuronal cells (fig. S6B). Intense eGFP-positive axonal fibers were observed in the ventral part of the spinal cord (fig. S6C). Following intrathecal injection of AAV2/9-AP4, GFP expression in DRG neurons, motor neurons, and sciatic nerves persisted for over 1.5 months (fig. S6, D to F). This prolonged expression period allowed us to assess sensory and motor functional recovery in AP4-overexpressing mice after PNI.

We first determined whether overexpression of AP4 could enhance the intrinsic growth capacity of axotomized DRG neurons. To do this, we performed SNT on mice 2 weeks after intrathecal injection of AAV2/9-AP4 (or AAV2/9-eGFP as a control). Five days postinjury, we harvested the L4/5 DRGs for ex vivo explant cultures (fig. S7A). The results showed that overexpression of AP4 greatly enhanced the intrinsic growth capacity of naïve DRG neurons by 85.4%. Conversely, knocking down AP4 completely abolished the growth-promoting effects of preconditioning peripheral lesion on axonal regrowth (fig. S7, B and C). We then assessed the rate of in vivo axonal regrowth in AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice using the sciatic nerve pinch test (2, 3, 5, 6, 9). Compared to control mice treated with AAV2/9-eGFP, the distal extent of in vivo sensory axonal regrowth increased by 57.8% in AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice (Fig. 4C). We further validated these results with immunostaining of SCG10 (5, 9), which showed accelerated axon regeneration in AP4-overexpressing mice. SCG10 immunoreactivity was detected in the far distal region of injured nerves at 4.5 mm from the crush site, and the regeneration index at 1 to 4.5 mm was significantly higher in AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 4D).
To further examine whether AP4 overexpression promotes sensory and motor functional recovery, we intrathecally injected AAV2/9-AP4 (or AAV2/9-eGFP as a control) into adult C57BL/6 mice 2 weeks before sciatic nerve crush injury. We then assessed their sensory and motor functional recovery by animal behavioral tests (Fig. 4E). The rate of sensory axon regeneration, measured by the pinprick assay, showed that control mice treated with AAV2/9-eGFP responded to initial pinprick stimuli at day 9 and fully recovered by day 21 postinjury. In contrast, AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice responded to pinprick stimuli as early as day 5 postinjury and achieved full recovery by day 17 postinjury (Fig. 4F). For motor recovery, toe spreading reflex assessment indicated that AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice showed an initial response at day 11, earlier than day 13 in control mice. Full recovery of toe spreading reflex occurred by day 19 in the AP4-overexpressing mice, compared to day 23 in controls (Fig. 4G). Motor function was further quantified by measuring hindlimb grip strength. The hindlimb grip strength of AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice was significantly higher than the control mice from 9 to 21 days postinjury (Fig. 4H). The accelerated motor recovery was validated by EMG recordings, showing a significant increase in CMAP amplitudes in the gastrocnemius (44 to 51%) and interosseus (34 to 72%) muscles of AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice between weeks 2 and 3 postinjury, indicating faster motor functional recovery compared to controls (Fig. 4, I and J).

## Overexpression of AP4 promotes sustained axon regeneration and RGC survival after ONC
To establish the role of AP4 as a key regulator to enhance intrinsic growth capacity of injured neurons, not limited to the PNS, we examined the AP4 expression before and after ONC injury. The transgenic Thy1-YFP-17 mouse line, in which yellow fluorescence protein (YFP) is predominantly expressed in RGCs, enables the isolation of a homogeneous population of YFP-positive injured RGCs at days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-ONC via FACS (fig. S8) (13, 41). Consistent with a prior finding that c-Myc, an upstream regulator of AP4, decreased after ONC (13), we observed a gradual decline in AP4 mRNA levels in purified injured RGCs from days 3 to 7 post-ONC (Fig. 5A). These results were further supported by immunohistochemistry with anti-AP4 antibodies, revealing a significant reduction in AP4 immunoreactivity in βIII-tubulin-positive RGCs at day 3 after injury (Fig. 5B). Intravitreal injection of AAV2/9-AP4 2 weeks before ONC restored AP4 expression in injured RGCs at day 3 post-ONC (Fig. 5C). Collectively, our results indicate that AP4 plays a crucial role in promoting CNS axon regeneration.

We next evaluated the growth-promoting effects of AP4 on the CNS, where axon regeneration is virtually nonexistent (4–7). Adult mice were intravitreally injected with AAV2/9-AP4 2 weeks before ONC, along with cholera toxin subunit B (CTB) conjugated to Alexa Fluor 555 to label regenerating axons anterogradely 3 days before harvesting the optic nerves for histological analysis (Fig. 5D). Our quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis confirmed that intravitreal injection of AAV2/9-AP4 resulted in robust overexpression of AP4 in RGCs, with a 17.9-fold increase in AP4 mRNA in FACS-sorted RGCs (fig. S9A). At 4 weeks post-ONC, AAV2/9-eGFP–treated mice showed no axon regeneration beyond the crush site (Fig. 5E). Notably, AP4 overexpression markedly enhanced the intrinsic growth capacity of RGCs, allowing CTB-labeled RGC axons to regenerate along the entire length of the optic nerve, with some reaching the optic chiasm (Fig. 5, E and F). RGC survival, quantified by RNA-binding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS) immunostaining (5, 6), increased to 32.6 ± 2.0% in AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice compared to only 18.9 ± 2.5% in control mice (Fig. 5G).

## AP4-induced axon regeneration requires the activation of mTOR
Myc is one of the most extensively studied oncogenes, and its deregulated form, c-Myc, is frequently involved in cancer (42, 43). The c-Myc–induced transcription factor AP4 was originally identified in a screen of p53 activating factors and as a downstream effector of c-Myc in regulating the cell cycle (25, 44). However, the c-Myc–AP4 axis has not been explored in the context of nervous system regeneration. c-Myc has been shown to inhibit the mTOR repressor tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2), leading to increased mTOR activity (45). This increased mTOR activity either through c-Myc overexpression or PTEN deletion enhances intrinsic growth capacity, resulting in robust axon regeneration after CNS injury (13, 15, 18). To investigate the novel role of c-Myc–AP4 axis and mTOR activity in CNS axon regeneration, we first examined neuronal mTOR activity 3 days post-ONC following genetic manipulation of c-Myc and AP4 expression. Using immunohistochemistry on longitudinal retinal cryosections, we stained for phosphorylated S6 ribosomal protein (pS6) to access mTOR activity 3 days after ONC (13, 18) (fig. S10A). Consistent with previous studies (18), nerve injury caused a marked decline in mTOR activity, with only 3.2% of injured βIII-tubulin–positive RGCs retaining considerable levels of mTOR activity 3 days after ONC in AAV2/9-eGFP–treated mice (fig. S10B). In stark contrast, AP4 overexpression restored mTOR activity in injured RGCs (10.1% of pS6–βIII-tubulin–positive RGCs) to a level comparable to that of uninjured and intact RGCs (11.5% of pS6-positive RGCs), outperforming the effect of c-Myc overexpression (7.7% of pS6–βIII-tubulin–positive RGCs). Notably, AP4 overexpression could activate mTOR activity even when c-Myc was knocked down; however, c-Myc overexpression failed to rescue mTOR activity when AP4 was knocked down in RGCs following ONC injury (fig. S10C).
Next, we examined whether mTOR activation is essential for the AP4-induced intrinsic growth capacity in injured neurons using the specific mTOR inhibitor, rapamycin (18). Mice treated with AAV2/9-AP4 received intraperitoneal injections of rapamycin [or 5% Tween 80/5% polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 400 (PEG-400) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as vehicle control], and we assessed the extent of axon regeneration 2 weeks after ONC. In addition, mice treated with either AAV2/9-eGFP or AAV2/9-AP4 received vehicle injections, following the same dosing paradigm as the rapamycin group (Fig. 6A). Rapamycin treatment completely abolished the AP4-induced growth-promoting effects, as evidenced by the absence of regenerating axons at the crushed site 2 weeks post-ONC (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis of CTB-positive regenerating axons confirmed that AP4-overexpressing mice treated with rapamycin exhibited levels of axon regeneration similar to those of AAV2/9-eGFP controls, where axon regeneration was virtually absent (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the prosurvival effects induced by AP4 overexpression were eliminated following rapamycin treatment, resulting in an RGC survival rate of 21.9%, comparable to the 22.9% observed in AAV2/9-eGFP controls (Fig. 6D).

## AP4 is required for c-Myc–induced growth-promoting effects
In cancerous and nonneuronal cells, c-Myc serves as an upstream regulator of AP4, regulating its expression to either promote or inhibit transcription, cell proliferation, and migration (21, 22, 25, 46). To elucidate the role of the c-Myc–AP4 axis in regulating the intrinsic growth capacity of neurons and facilitating axon regeneration, we performed a series of gain- and loss-of-function experiments to assess axon regeneration 2 weeks after ONC (Fig. 7A) (5). As expected, overexpressing c-Myc alone with AAV2/9–c-Myc markedly increased its mRNA levels in FACS-sorted RGCs by 403-fold (fig. S9B) and was sufficient to promote significant axon regeneration beyond the crush site at 2 weeks post-ONC (13). To determine whether AP4 is necessary for c-Myc–induced axon regeneration, we coinjected AAV2/9–c-Myc and AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA intravitreally to simultaneously overexpress c-Myc and silence AP4 in injured RGCs 2 weeks before ONC. Intravitreal injections of AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA significantly reduced AP4 mRNA levels in the injured RGCs by 71% (fig. S11A), and this AP4 silencing completely abolished the regenerative effects induced by c-Myc overexpression. Conversely, the robust axon regeneration driven by AP4 overexpression was unaffected by the suppression of its upstream regulator c-Myc (Fig. 7, B and C), partly because AP4 overexpression could sustain mTOR activation in the injured RGCs even at lower expression levels of c-Myc expression (fig. S10, B and C). The c-Myc mRNA expression was significantly decreased in the injured RGCs by 68% following intravitreal injections of AAV2/9–c-Myc–shRNA (fig. S11B). Overexpression of c-Myc or AP4 alone induced significant neuroprotective effects on RGC survival. However, when RGCs were knocked down for AP4 expression after ONC (AAV2/9–c-Myc + AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice), the neuroprotective effect of c-Myc on RGC survival was abolished, reducing to a level comparable to controls (AAV2/9-eGFP–treated mice). In contrast, overexpressing AP4 was able to induce neuroprotection even in the presence of c-Myc knockdown (AAV2/9–c-Myc–shRNA + AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice) (Fig. 7D), maintaining mTOR activity at levels similar to uninjured mice (fig. S10, B and C). In conclusion, these results underscore the essential role of AP4 within the c-Myc–AP4 axis in promoting axon regeneration through modulation of mTOR activity.

## c-Myc–AP4 axis is essential for PTEN deletion–induced axon regeneration
It is well established that PTEN deletion, a negative regulator of mTOR signaling pathways, promotes sustained axon regeneration after ONC (18). Previous studies indicated that c-Myc deletion significantly reduced the number of regenerating axons in mice with shRNA-mediated PTEN silencing. However, some residual regenerating axons persisted even after c-Myc deletion (13), which prompted us to further investigate whether AP4 plays a role in PTEN deletion–induced axon regeneration.
To delete Pten in RGCs, we intravitreally injected AAV2/9-Cre into adult Pten-floxed (Ptenfl/fl) mice (i.e., Pten−/−) 2 weeks before ONC (5). Simultaneously, we injected AAV2/9–c-Myc–shRNA and/or AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA intravitreally into the Pten−/− mice to silence c-Myc and AP4 expression in RGCs (Fig. 8A) (5). Consistent with findings from us and others (5, 13, 18), Pten−/− mice exhibited a substantial extent of axon regeneration, with regenerating axons extending up to 1 mm distal to the crush site 2 weeks after ONC (Fig. 8B). Target silencing of c-Myc or AP4 individually resulted in a significant reduction in the number of CTB-positive regenerating axons in Pten−/− mice, although some residual axons remained visible beyond the crush site. Simultaneous silencing of both c-Myc and AP4 completely abolished the regenerative phenotype observed in Pten−/− mice (Fig. 8C). In line with a previous study (13), silencing c-Myc did not affect RGC survival in Pten−/− mice. However, knockdown of AP4, either alone or combined with c-Myc silencing, completely eliminated the promoting effect of Pten deletion on RGC survival (Fig. 8D). Together, we conclude that the c-Myc–AP4 axis is crucial for both the regenerative capacity and neuronal survival promoted by Pten deletion.

## DISCUSSION
Following PNI, peripheral neurons retain the ability to regenerate axon spontaneously, albeit at a slow rate of regrowth, resulting in suboptimal motor functional recovery in patients with proximal PNI primarily due to the failure of target reinnervation and the formation of functional synapses within the so-called “critical period” (1, 2, 8). Therapeutic strategies aiming at accelerating axon regeneration through gene manipulations of RAGs, such as Hsp27 overexpression or Fmn2 silencing (1, 2, 9), as well as pharmacological treatment with bioactive small molecules such as metaxalone or Lycium barbarum polysaccharides (6, 9), have shown promising results in improving both sensory and motor functional recovery after PNI. Understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms that underlie the intrinsic growth capacity of injured PNS neurons could offer valuable insights to broaden current knowledge and develop strategies for promoting axon regeneration in the CNS.
Our unbiased WGCNA robustly supports the role of AP4 in activating the intrinsic growth program of injured neurons. Among the top gene candidates, 60% of the closely related neighboring genes that were highly associated with AP4 regulation following injury were significantly enriched for axon regeneration. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that the increased expression of several well-known RAGs, including C1qa, Sprr1a, Atf3, Cdkn1a, Socs2, and Gal, were highly correlated with the up-regulation of AP4 following injury. For example, C1q has been implicated in promoting optic nerve regeneration through controlled inflammation, mediated by intraocular injection of zymosan, cyclic adenosine 5′-monophosphate (cAMP) analog, and the Zn2+ chelator N,N,N′,N′-tetrakis(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine (also known as TPEN) (47). Similarly, galanin (Gal) expression markedly increases following PNI and is necessary for the regenerative phenotype in peripheral neurons (48). Both Sprr1a and Cdkn1a (also known as p21) enhance axonal outgrowth (49, 50) by modulating the actin cytoskeleton (49, 51). Socs2 has also been shown to stimulate neurite extension (52). While a recent study suggests that Atf3 might negatively regulate RGC survival after ONC (53), it remains a well-established RAG essential for successful regeneration after PNI (37, 54), and it synergizes with Pten deletion to promote optic nerve regeneration (55). Manipulating these RAGs individually in neurons has been demonstrated to enhance regenerative capacity and drive significant axon regeneration (48–50, 52, 54, 56). Our gain- and loss-of-function assays combined with bioinformatics analysis confirm that AP4 is crucial for the intrinsic transcriptional program underlying axon regeneration. Specifically, gene silencing of AP4 abolished the preconditioning effects in injured DRG neurons. Collectively, these data suggest that the up-regulation of AP4, together with other RAGs, forms part of the intrinsic transcriptional molecular machinery that primes injured neurons into an active growth state and facilitates axon regeneration following injury.
The expression of AP4 in nonneuronal cells is directly regulated by its upstream transcription factor c-Myc, which binds to the first intron of AP4 containing c-Myc–binding site (25). Multiple studies have established AP4 as a direct downstream effector and mediator of c-Myc signaling pathways (57), and AP4 is required for c-Myc–driven regulation of several critical signaling pathways (57), including p21 (25, 44), p53 (58), Wnt/β-catenin (59), and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling (60). In cancerous cells, AP4 is necessary for several c-Myc–driven cellular processes, including cell growth, proliferation, senescence, survival, migration, and metabolic regulation (61). In Drosophila, c-Myc regulates the transcriptional activity of AP4 and enhances its expression to promote the formation of terminal branching in the trachea (31), a morphological process structurally analogous to neuronal neurite formation (62). In neuronal cells, injury-induced up-regulation of c-Myc in DRGs peaked at day 1 post-PNI but was significantly down-regulated in RGCs at day 3 post-ONC (13), indicating a strong correlation between c-Myc expression and the regenerative capacity of CNS neurons. Interestingly, while c-Myc levels peaked at day 1 after injury and returned to baseline in mouse DRGs (13), c-Myc remained elevated at 7 days post-PNI in rat DRGs (63). In our study, we showed that AP4 expression increased gradually with a 4-day delay, reaching its peak at day 5 postinjury. This distinct temporal pattern highlights their complementary roles within the c-Myc–AP4 axis, where c-Myc likely functions to initiate regenerative programs early after injury, while AP4 executes downstream effects that are essential for sustained axon regeneration. Notably, our results demonstrated that DRG neurons lacking AP4 expression lost their intrinsic regenerative capacity, failing to extend neurites both in vitro and in vivo, which resulted in delayed sensory and motor functional recovery following PNI.
In adult RGCs, both mRNA and protein levels of AP4 were significantly decreased following ONC. This reduction supports the hypothesis that the down-regulation of AP4, along with c-Myc (13), contributes at least in part to the lack of intrinsic regenerative capacity observed in adult CNS neurons. Our results showed that overexpression of either AP4 or c-Myc individually restored the regenerative growth of injured RGCs; however, the intrinsic growth capacity of AP4-overexpressing RGCs remained unaffected in the absence of c-Myc. Conversely, the suppression of AP4 expression abolished the regenerative capacity of c-Myc–overexpressing RGCs, further highlighting the essential role of AP4 in facilitating successful CNS regeneration.
The pS6 is a reliable indicator of mTOR signaling pathway activity, which is a critical determinant for the intrinsic regenerative capacity of neurons in both the CNS and PNS (18, 64). During development, mTOR activity markedly decreases, as the intrinsic growth potential of injured neurons diminishes (14). Neurons exhibiting high endogenous mTOR activity, characterized by pS6-positive RGCs, exhibited enhanced axon regeneration and neuronal survival compared to those with low mTOR activity (65, 66). The tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC1/TSC2) functions as a negative regulator of mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), thereby restraining its activity (67). AKT, a key upstream regulator, activates mTORC1 by inhibiting TSC, serving as a master regulator of cellular growth and protein synthesis (68). PTEN, a lipid phosphatase, converts phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate to phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, thus impeding AKT recruitment and downstream effector activation (69). Modulation of the PTEN/AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway is known to induce robust axon regeneration (18, 70–72). Although the precise mechanisms through which AP4 directly influences mTOR signaling pathways remain unclear, AP4 is known to affect AKT activity (60, 73), potentially regulating mTOR activity in nonneuronal cells. In addition, c-Myc has been shown to activate the mTOR signaling pathway (74, 75). Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that increased mTOR activity is required for AP4-induced CNS axon regeneration, as rapamycin—a potent inhibitor of mTOR—completely abolished the growth-promoting effects of AP4 (76). Further investigation is warranted to elucidate interplay among PTEN-mTOR, c-Myc–AP4, and other key intrinsic growth signaling pathways to optimize CNS axon regeneration.
In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence demonstrating the potent proregenerative effects of AP4, which actively promotes robust axon regeneration following ONC by modulating mTOR activity in injured RGCs. We observed that AP4 overexpression alone can induce CNS axon regeneration to a level comparable to that achieved by overexpressing its upstream regulator, c-Myc. These findings advance our understanding of the intrinsic growth program fundamental to successful CNS regeneration. Furthermore, our results emphasize the importance of deciphering the transcriptional program that governs intrinsic regenerative growth in injured neurons. These valuable insights hold promises for the development of safe and effective therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing functional recovery after nervous system injuries.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Animals
All experiments and euthanasia procedures were conducted in compliance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines. The animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Sub-Committee in City University of Hong Kong (reference number: A-0205) and Department of Health, Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR). Adult male C57BL/6 mice (8 to 12 weeks old) were used in all in vitro and in vivo experiments. Ptenfl/fl mice (with C57BL/6J background) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (6, 18). The Thy1-YFP17 mouse line, which extensively expressed the reporter gene YFP in DRGs and RGCs, was originally obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and provided by C. K. S. Leung at the University of Hong Kong (77).

## Sciatic nerve injury
Sciatic nerve crush and SNT were performed under 2.5% isoflurane anesthesia, as previously described (2, 5, 6, 9, 39). Briefly, the left sciatic nerve was first exposed at the mid-thigh level. For sciatic nerve crush injury, the exposed nerve was crushed with 5/45 smooth forceps (Fine Science Tools) for 15 s just distal to the sciatic notch, where the external rotator muscles are located. For SNT, the nerve was transected using ophthalmic microscissors at the same position where the sciatic nerve crush was performed. After the injury, the overlying muscles and skin were sutured in two layers using 5-0 epineural suture (Ethilon).

## Immunohistochemistry of DRG cryosections
L4/5 DRGs directly supplying the sciatic nerves were harvested at days 1, 2, 3, and 5 post-SNT, postfixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), cryoprotected, and embedded in optimum cutting temperature (O.C.T.; Tissue-Tek). To quantify AP4 immunoreactivity, 5-μm-thick serial cryosections of DRGs were blocked with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) and then incubated with primary antibodies against AP4 (1:300; Proteintech) and βIII-tubulin (1:400; BioLegend). Cryosections were subsequently incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 and Alexa Fluor 647 (1:300; Molecular Probes). Fluorescence images were captured at 20× magnificently using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E epifluorescence microscope equipped with a motorized stage. The fluorescence intensity of AP4 was quantified automatically using a customized General Analysis 3 pipeline in NIS-Elements software (Nikon) (5, 9, 36). For each mouse, at least four nonoverlapping sections (spaced at least 25 μm apart) were quantified, and the average fluorescence intensity of AP4 was calculated from four to six mice per time point. All analyses were conducted by an experimenter blinded to the surgery groups.

## Weighted gene coexpression network analysis
WGCNA was performed as previously described (2, 3), using gene expression profiling data detailed in Painter et al. (38). The primary microarray expression profiles were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus with accession number GSE58982 (38). Briefly, genes present in at least five samples were selected, and the absolute Pearson correlation coefficients between each gene and every other gene were computed, then weighed, and used to determine the TO— a measure of connection strength or shared neighborhood. A pair of nodes in the network is considered to have high TO if they are both strongly connected to the same group of nodes. In general, genes with high TO are more likely to be part of the same biological pathway. Genes were clustered on the basis of TO, and modules of highly interconnected genes were identified. Network neighborhood analysis provides a set neighborhood of an initial set of nodes. The top 50 nearest neighbors were added to the seed gene (Tcfap4; Illumina probe ID: ILMN_2706477) based on TO as a measure of connection strength.

## Primary dissociated DRG cultures
Primary dissociated DRG cultures were prepared from adult C57BL/6 mice (8 to 12 weeks), as previously described (2, 9, 39). Briefly, DRGs were dissected and subjected to mild enzymatic digestion in a solution containing collagenase and Dispase II (Roche Diagnostics). The cells were then trypsinized and mechanically dissociated using three flame-polished Pasteur pipettes of decreasing diameters. A total of 2000 DRG neurons were plated onto eight-well chamber slide (Millipore) coated with poly-d-lysine and laminin (Sigma-Aldrich). The cultures were maintained in full Neurobasal (NB) medium (Gibco) supplemented with B27, 200 mM l-glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin, nerve growth factor (NGF) (50 ng/ml; Gibco), glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) (2 ng/ml), and 10 μM arabinosylcytosine (Ara-C) (Sigma-Aldrich).

## In vitro gene silencing of AP4 and neurite outgrowth assay
scr-siRNA (QIAGEN, #1027284) or AP4-siRNA (Sigma-Aldrich, SASI_Mm02_00337023) was applied to the cultures at a concentration of 100 nM 1 hour after plating (78). The cultures were then incubated for 24 hours. The medium was replaced with freshly prepared full NB medium containing B27 and all supplements, and either scr-siRNA or AP4-siRNA was reapplied to the cultures 1 hour after the medium change. The cultures were incubated for another 24 hours before proceeding to neurite outgrowth assay.
For immunostaining, primary DRG neurons were fixed using 4% PFA at room temperature for 15 min. The fixed cultures were blocked with 0.5% BSA/0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 1 hour and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against βIII-tubulin (1:800; Sigma-Aldrich) and AP4 (1:300). After three washes with PBS, the cultures were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:300; Molecular Probes) and Cy3 (1:300; Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 hour at room temperature.
Neurite outgrowth assays were performed as previously described (2, 9, 39). A total of 30 nonoverlapping images were taken at ×10 magnifications using an epifluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse 90i) equipped with a motorized stage. Total neurite length and branching number were quantified using the automated WIS-NeuroMath software (Weizmann Institute of Science). The AP4 protein expression level in each DRG neurons was measured by fluorescence intensity using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/). Cell bodies of DRG neurons with fluorescence intensity greater than 100 arbitrary units were considered as AP4-positive. All imaging parameters were kept consistent across all experimental conditions. Data were obtained from three independent experiments repeated in duplicates.

## Cell viability assay
One thousand DRG neurons were plated onto poly-d-lysine and laminin-coated 96-well plates (BD Falcon) and incubated with AP4-siRNA as described above. After 48 hours of incubation, cell viability was assessed using the WST-1 cell proliferation assay kit (Clonetech). WST-1 reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured after 2 hours using a microplate reader (BMG Labtech). The absorbance values were normalized to background controls (wells containing only full NB medium without cells). Data were obtained from three independent experiments repeated in quadruplicate.

## Western blot analysis
To collect protein lysates from DRGs, L4/5 DRGs directly supplying the sciatic nerve were dissected at various time points post-SNT from both ipsilateral and contralateral sides, with tissues from two mice pooled into the same sample. The tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer, and protein concentration was determined (2, 9, 79). To obtain protein lysate from DRG cultures, 30,000 DRG neurons were plated onto poly-d-lysine and laminin-coated 35-mm petri dish (BD Falcon) and incubated with either scr- or AP4-siRNA as described above. After 48 hours of incubation, the cultures were washed with PBS and lysed in a protein lysis buffer containing protease (Roche Diagnostics) and phosphatase (Sigma-Aldrich) inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method (Pierce).
Twenty micrograms of total protein from each sample were loaded onto 4 to 12% bis-tris gradient gel (Invitrogen), separated by electrophoresis, and transferred onto 0.2-μm polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against AP4 (1:500; Proteintech) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Following three washes in TBS-T, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000; Pierce) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate Kit (Pierce) with the Fujifilm LAS-4000 luminescent image analyzer. Band intensity was quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad), with AP4 band intensity normalized to GAPDH. Data were obtained from four independent experiments (2, 9, 79).

## In vivo knockdown of AP4 expression
To silence AP4 expression, AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA (PackGene) was injected directly into the sciatic nerve at a viral titer of 5 × 1012 viral genome (vg)/ml using a polished glass micropipette connected to a microinjector (Harvard Apparatus) 2 weeks before sciatic nerve crush injury (9). The shRNA expression was driven by the U6 promoter, while the reporter gene (eGFP) was expressed under the control of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. AAV2/9–scr-shRNA (PackGene) served as a negative control (see table S2 for shRNA sequence). Successful knockdown of AP4 was confirmed by immunohistochemistry and qPCR analyses, as previously described (9).

## Purification of DRG neurons by FACS
DRG neurons were purified using FACS as previously described (9), with minor modifications. Two weeks after sciatic nerve injection of either AAV2/9–scr-shRNA or AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA, L4/5 DRGs were harvested, enzymatically digested with a mixture of collagenase and dispase, and mechanically dissociated in dissociation buffer (1× PBS with 10% fetal bovine serum) using three flame-polished Pasteur pipettes with decreasing diameters. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer (BD Falcon) to obtain a single-cell suspension for FACS (9). All AAV2/9 vectors used in this study carried an eGFP reporter gene.
Cell sorting was conducted using the Sony SH800Z Cell Sorter. Cells were identified on the basis of cell size [forward scatter (FSC)] and cell surface properties (side scatter), with doublets or cell aggregates excluded on the basis of the FSC height (FSC-H):FSC area (FSC-A) ratio. Nonviable cells were excluded by gating out cells with high Zombie Yellow fluorescence, detected using the Zombie Yellow viability dye (BioLegend). For each experiment, dissociated DRG cell suspension from adult C57BL/6 mice without AAV injections served as negative controls to establish gating parameters for eGFP-positive cell detection. Sorted eGFP-positive cells were immediately lysed and used for qPCR analysis. In each sample, four DRGs from one mouse were pooled, representing one biological replicate (n = 1).

## Total RNA extraction and qPCR analysis
Total RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qPCR analysis were performed as previously described (5, 6, 9, 80, 81). Briefly, total RNA was extracted from cultured DRG neurons after 48 hours of incubation using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix (Invitrogen). For each sample, triplicate qPCR reactions were performed using the KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR Kit (KAPA) on a QuantStudio 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The Ct values from each replicate were recorded, and relative fold change of each gene was calculated using the formula 2−ΔΔCt. Gapdh served as an endogenous control for normalization. The complete list of primers used in this study is provided in table S3.

## Sciatic nerve pinch test
To evaluate the distal extent of sensory axonal regrowth, sciatic nerve pinch test was performed at day 3 post–crush injury as previously described (2, 5, 6, 9). Briefly, the left sciatic nerve was exposed under 2.5% isoflurane anesthesia. The sciatic nerve pinch test was performed under light anesthesia (e.g., 1% isoflurane). A series of gentle pinches was applied staring from the most distal point of the nerve and moving proximally toward the injury site. The distance from the crush site to the most distal point on the nerve where the mouse exhibited the first withdrawal reflex upon pinching was recorded. All measurement was done blinded to the treatment groups.

## Quantification of regenerating axons
Adult C57BL/6 mice (8 to 12 weeks old) were perfused transcardially with 4% PFA. The ipsilateral sciatic nerves were dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA, cryoprotected, and embedded in O.C.T. (Tissue-Tek). Longitudinal cryosections of the sciatic nerve at 12 μm in thickness were prepared and immunostained with primary antibodies against SCG10 (a marker for regenerating sensory axonal fibers; 1:5000; Novus Biologicals), followed by secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (1:300; Molecular Probes) (5, 9). Images of the entire sciatic nerve were taken at ×20 magnifications using a Nikon AXR confocal microscope equipped with a motorized stage and resonant scanner. The images were stitched and maximally projected using NIS-Elements software. The fluorescence intensity of SCG10 along the entire nerve length was quantified using ImageJ [National Institutes of Health (NIH)] software. The fluorescence intensity of SCG10 immunoreactivity was calculated by averaging the pixel intensity over 100 neighboring pixels and normalized to that at the crush site (5). For each mouse, at least three nonoverlapping sections spaced 48 μm apart was analyzed, and data were obtained from four mice per treatment group.

## Animal behavioral assessments and electrophysiological studies
One week before sciatic nerve crush injury, adult male C57BL/6 mice (8 to 12 weeks old) were first trained and habituated in three independent sessions (30 min per session, with 2-day intervals). Baseline values of each behavioral assessment were recorded after the final habituation session. Sciatic nerve crush injury was then performed 1 day after these baseline measurements. Sensory (pinprick assay) function was assessed first, followed by evaluations of motor function (toe spreading and grip strength tests) (2, 39). All animal behavioral and electrophysiological assessments were performed on every alternate day for 2 months in AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA–treated mice or for 1 month in AAV2/9-AP4–treated mice, starting from day 3 postinjury. All behavioral and electrophysiological studies were performed blinded to the treatment groups.

## Pinprick sensory assay
Mice were first habituated for 30 min in wire mesh cages before testing. During the assessment, a size 000 Austerlitz insect pin (Fine Science Tools) was gently applied to the lateral plantar surface of the hindlimb ipsilateral to the crush injury, which was divided into five regions—from the most lateral toe (score = 5) to the proximal heel (score = 1). If a brisk withdrawal reflex was observed after application of the pin, then the mouse was scored for that area. A score of 0 was assigned if no withdrawal reflex was observed during the assessment. To confirm the score, the insect pin was applied twice with a 30-min interval between applications (1, 2, 6, 8, 9).

## Toe spreading test
The toe spreading test was used to evaluate motor functional recovery following PNI. Briefly, the mice were gently covered with a piece of cotton and lifted by their tails, allowing their hindlimbs to be exposed for assessment. The toe spreading reflex was scored as follows: 0 for no clear spreading of all toes, 1 for intermediate spreading of toes but failed to sustain the reflex for 2 s, and 2 for full toe spreading with the reflex successfully sustained for at least 2 s. Each mouse was tested twice with a 30-min interval between assessments to confirm the score (1, 2, 6, 8, 9).

## Grip strength test
Grip strength was measured using a grip strength meter (Bioseb). The forelimbs of the mice were rested on a plastic bar, while their hindlimbs were gently placed onto a T-bar connected to the grip strength meter. The mice were gently pulled away, and the grip strength was recorded in grams. The average of at least five independent measurements were calculated for each mouse (1, 6, 8, 9).

## EMG recording
The mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg) and then placed on a heat pad set to 37°C to maintain the body temperature. Baseline EMG recordings were taken 1 day before sciatic nerve injury using custom-made monopolar Teflon-coated electrodes. Weekly EMG measurements were performed for 2 months, starting from day 14 postinjury. CMAPs were recorded by inserting recording electrodes into the proximal (gastrocnemius) and distal (interosseous) muscles, with reference electrodes placed in the Achilles tendon. Stimulating electrodes were inserted into the sciatic notch, and grounding electrodes were positioned at the base of the tail (1, 6, 8, 9). EMG signals were first sampled at 5 kHz using a data acquisition system (Blackrock Microsystem), amplified 1000-fold, and passed through a band-pass filter with cutoff frequency set at 10 Hz (low) and 3000 Hz (high). The amplified signals then underwent additional filtering: a low-pass filter at 2000 Hz to prevent aliasing and removal of dc offset. CMAP amplitudes were calculated from the peak of the negative deflection to the peak of the positive deflection using Spike2 software. For each mouse, mean CMAP amplitude was obtained from three to five measurements at each time point.

## Intrathecal injection of AAV2/9-AP4
The open reading frame of AP4 was cloned into the pFB-CMV-IRES-eGFP vector and packaged into AAV2/9. Consequently, AP4 expression was driven by the CMV promoter (40). Two weeks before sciatic nerve crush injury, a small laminectomy was performed between L5 and L6 vertebrae to expose the thecal sac. Then, 1 to 3 μl of AAV2/9-AP4-eGFP (or AAV2/9-eGFP as a control) (Virovek) at a viral titer of 1 × 1013 vg/ml was intrathecally injected into the spinal cord using a 10-μl Hamilton syringe fitted with a 32-gauge injection needle. The injection rate was carefully maintained at 200 ng/min to prevent injury to the spinal cord. The expression of eGFP in the spinal cord and DRG neurons at 2 weeks postinjection confirmed successful transduction of AAV2/9 in PNS tissues.

## DRG explant cultures
Five days after SNT injury, L4/5 DRGs directly supplying the sciatic nerve were dissected, carefully cleaned of spinal and peripheral root, and plated onto poly-d-lysine (Sigma Aldrich)–coated eight-well chamber slides (Millipore) with a thin layer of Matrigel (BD Biosciences). DRG explants were cultured in full NB medium (Gibco) supplemented with B27, 200 mM l-glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin, NGF (50 ng/ml; Gibco), GDNF (2 ng/ml), and 10 μM Ara-C (Sigma-Aldrich). After 48 hours, the explants were fixed with 4% PFA, blocked with 0.5% BSA/0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 1 hour, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody against βIII-tubulin (1:800; Sigma-Aldrich). Following PBS washes, explants were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:300; Molecular Probes). For each explant, four nonoverlapping images were taken at ×4 magnifications using an epifluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ni-E) equipped with a motorized stage. Total neurite length was measured from each explant using the automated WIS-NeuroMath software (Weizmann Institute of Science) (3, 6, 82). Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments repeated in duplicate.

## Intravitreal injections of AAV2/9 and ONC
Two weeks before ONC, 1 μl of AAV2/9-AP4, AAV2/9–c-Myc, or AAV2/9-eGFP (as control) (OBIO) was intravitreally injected into the left eye of adult C57BL/6 mice at a slow rate of 0.2 μl/min, with a viral titer of 5 × 1012 vg/ml. To perform simultaneous in vivo knockout of Pten and knockdown of AP4 and/or c-Myc, a mixture containing 1 μl each of AAV2/9-Cre (OBIO), AAV2/9–c-Myc–shRNA (OBIO), and AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA (PackGene) was intravitreally injected into the left eye of adult Ptenfl/fl mice (on a C57BL/6J background) at the same viral titer (5 × 1012 vg/ml), 2 weeks before ONC (5, 6).
The ONC was performed as described (5, 6). Briefly, adult mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), and the left optic nerve was exposed intraorbitally. The nerve was crushed with 5/45 smooth forceps for 5 s, ~1 mm from the optic disc (5, 6). For rapamycin (Selleck Chemicals) treatment, it was dissolved in ethanol at 20 mg/ml to prepare a stock solution and then diluted in 5% Tween 80/5% PEG-400 in PBS. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of rapamycin at 6 mg/kg on the day of AAV injection and every 2 days thereafter. Control mice received injections of the vehicle solution (5% Tween 80/5% PEG-400 in PBS) without rapamycin following the same dosing paradigm.

## Purification of RGCs by FACS
On days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-ONC, the retinae were carefully dissected from Thy1-YFP17 mice and enzymatically digested with papain (20 U/ml; Worthington) for 30 min at 37°C with gentle shaking. To assess mRNA expression levels of c-Myc and AP4 following overexpression or knockdown of these genes, retinae were harvested 2 weeks after intravitreal injection of AAV2/9-AP4, AAV2/9–c-Myc, AAV2/9-AP4-shRNA, or AAV2/9–c-Myc–shRNA and then subjected to enzymatic digestion. The digestion was halted by adding ovomucoid solution, and the retinae were resuspended in a dissociation buffer (NB supplemented with l-glutamine and B27). Mechanical dissociation was performed using P1000 and P200 pipettes. Following filtration through a 70-μm cell strainer, the retinal cell suspension was processed with FACS to specifically isolate YFP-positive RGCs from Thy1-YFP17 mice or GFP-positive RGCs from mice injected with the respective AAV constructs (15, 18).
FACS was performed using the Sony SH800Z Cell Sorter. The dissociated retinal cells were initially gated on the basis of FSC (cell size) and side scatter (cell surface characteristics), with cell aggregates excluded on the basis of the FSC-H:FSC-A ratio. Dead cells were eliminated by gating out events with high Zombie Yellow fluorescence signals, as detected using the Zombie Yellow viability dye (BioLegend). In each experiment, retinal cells from C57BL/6 mice lacking YFP expression in RGCs were used as negative controls to set the detection gate for sorting YFP-positive RGCs. The sorted RGCs were then immediately processed for RNA extraction and subsequent qPCR analysis. In each sample, two retinae from one mouse were pooled, representing one biological replicate (n = 1).

## Immunohistochemistry of retinal cryosections
PFA-fixed retinae were longitudinally sectioned into 20-μm-thick cryosections and immunostained with primary antibodies against anti-AP4 (1:200; Proteintech), anti-pS6 (Ser235/236) (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology), and anti–βIII-tubulin (1:500; BioLegend). The sections were then incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (1:300; Molecular Probes) and Alexa Fluor 647 (1:300; Molecular Probes) (5, 15, 18). Images were taken at ×20 magnification using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E epifluorescence microscope equipped with a motorized stage. Multiple serial images were captured and automatically assembled into a single composite image via automatic tiling using NIS-Elements software (Nikon).
To quantify AP4 immunoreactivity, the ganglion cell layer (GCL) was manually outlined to define the region of interest (ROI), and the fluorescence intensity of AP4 within this ROI was measured using ImageJ (NIH) software. The AP4 fluorescence intensity in the GCL was averaged across three to five sections per mouse, with each section spaced at least 100 μm apart. The AP4 immunoreactivity in the ipsilateral retina was then normalized to that of the uninjured contralateral retina from the same mouse (5, 13).
On day 3 post-ONC, the percentage of pS6-positive RGCs was quantified from both ipsilateral and contralateral retinal cryosections. To determine this percentage, the total number of pS6-positive and βIII-tubulin–positive RGCs within each entire retinal section was manually counted using the Cell Counter plugin in ImageJ (NIH) software. The counts were then normalized to the total number of βIII-tubulin–positive RGCs. The percentage of pS6-positive RGCs was calculated by averaging data from three to five sections per mouse, with each section spaced at least 100 μm apart (13, 15, 18). The average immunoreactivity levels of AP4 and the percentage of pS6-positive RGCs were determined from at least four to six mice per treatment group. All analyses were performed with investigators blinded to the treatment conditions.

## Quantifications of regenerating axons and RGC survival
Three days before tissue harvesting, 2 μg (1 μl in PBS) of anterograde tracer CTB conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (CTB-555) was intravitreally injected into the injured left eyes to label regenerating axons. On day 14 (for the 2-week time point) or day 28 (for the 4-week time point), the entire optic nerves were dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA, and incubated sequentially in increasing concentrations of ethanol (50, 80, and 95%) for 20 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. The nerves were then stored in 100% ethanol overnight, followed by incubation in 100% hexane for complete dehydration. Last, the nerves were cleared using a 1:2 mixture of benzyl alcohol and benzyl benzoate (BABB). The cleared nerves were mounted on slides with the BABB clearing solution and imaged using a Carl Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with AiryScan Fast mode and a motorized stage or a Nikon AXR confocal microscope with motorized stage and large field-of-view capability. Optical sections were acquired at 1.7 μm in thickness with ×20 magnification. The images were stitched and maximally projected using Zen Blue (Carl Zeiss) software.
To quantify regenerating axons, the number of CTB-555–positive axons extending distally from the crush site was counted in three to five 10-μm-thick optical sections. The nerve diameter was measured at the same location where counting was performed. The total number of regenerating axons (Σad) extending to a distance (d) was calculated using the formula:Σad=πr2× average axons/mm/twhere r is the nerve radius and t is the section thickness (10 μm) (5, 6). The average number of regenerating axons at each nerve segment was derived from data obtained from at least four to six mice per treatment group.
RGC survival was assessed on day 14 or 28 post-ONC as described (5, 6). Briefly, both injured ipsilateral and uninjured contralateral eyes were harvested, postfixed in 4% PFA, cryopreserved, and sectioned into 20-μm-thick longitudinal retinal cryosections. The sections were blocked with 0.5% BSA and 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) and then incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-RBPMS antibodies (1:500; Abcam), a pan-RGC marker. After PBS washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 (1:300; Molecular Probes). Images were taken at ×40 magnification using a Nikon AXR confocal microscope equipped with a motorized stage and resonant scanner. RBPMS-positive RGCs were manually counted in every fifth section, spaced at least 100 μm apart, within both injured ipsilateral and uninjured contralateral retinae (three to five sections per retina) of the same mouse, using the Cell Counter plugin in ImageJ (NIH). The percentage of RGC survival in the injured retina was calculated relative to the uninjured contralateral retina of each mouse (5, 6). Data were obtained from at least four to six mice per treatment group.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as means ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t test was used; for comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Bonferroni tests was applied as appropriate. In all neurobehavioral and electrophysiological experiments, two-way ANOVA with repeated measures was used, followed by post hoc Bonferroni tests. Similarly, for quantification of regenerating axons, two-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Bonferroni tests was used. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All graphs and statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software.
