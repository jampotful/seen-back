Abstract

23
24

Discrepancies in mitochondrial and nuclear genetic data are often interpreted as

25

evidence of hybridisation. We re-examined reports of hybridisation in three cryptic stingless

26

bee species in the genus Tetragonula in South East Queensland, Australia (T. carbonaria, T.

27

davenporti, and T. hockingsi). Previous studies on this group using microsatellite markers

28

proposed that occasional hybrids are found. In contrast, we find that allele frequencies at

29

neutral regions of the nuclear genome, both microsatellites and random snps, reliably separated

30

the three species, and thus do not support hybridisation. We found no inter-species variation in

31

PCR amplicons of the nuclear gene EF1alpha, but low and moderate species-specific

32

polymorphisms in the nuclear gene Opsin and the mitochondrial 16S respectively, with no

33

cases of mito-nuclear discordance at these genes. We confirm that nuclear divergence between

34

these species is low, based on 10-26kb of non-coding sequence flanking EF1alpha and Opsin

35

(0.7-1% pairwise difference between species). However, we find mitogenomes to be far more

36

diverged than nuclear genomes (21.6-23.6% pairwise difference between species). Based on

37

these comprehensive analyses of multiple marker types, we conclude that there is no ongoing

38

gene flow in the Tetragonula species of South East Queensland, despite their high

39

morphological similarity to one another and the low nuclear divergence among them. The

40

mitogenomes and draft nuclear genomes provided for these species will be a resource for

41

further molecular studies on this group, which are important pollinators in Australian natural

42

and agroecosystems.

Introduction

44
45

Allopatry is key to speciation, but species boundaries may be tested when populations

46

that diverged in allopatry come back into secondary contact through changes in habitat or

47

climate, or as a result of anthropogenic movement over geographic boundaries (Sánchez-

48

Guillén et al., 2013, Gloag et al. 2017; Brookes et al. 2020). The process of hybridisation is

49

closely tied to the designation of species in population genetic terms (Paterson 1978). When

50

species are considered conceptually as recombining gene pools (Ayala et al. 1994),

51

hybridisation is the process of gene flow across two such species gene pools. High levels of

52

hybridisation in local areas of overlap indicate that the species in question would be better

53

viewed as subspecies, which, by definition, have independent geographical distributions but

54

hybridise freely where and when they do overlap (Walter 2003, Ford 1974). Such situations

55

are often seen when closely related lineages of the same species are brought back into sympatry

56

(Feder et al. 2003), but the pattern is also sometimes seen in more distantly related lineages

57

(e.g. Shipman et al. 2019). The frequency of hybridisation, or gene flow, between gene pools

58

is therefore key to understanding the limits of a given species, and to our understanding of

59

speciation.

60

Accurate assessment of the frequency of hybridisation under natural conditions is

61

therefore the first step in shedding light on the process of speciation. The more recently two

62

populations have diverged, the more likely they have not yet become separate species. When

63

two subspecies come back into secondary contact, gene flow is generally re-established (Feder

64

et al. 2003). Hybrid detection between close relatives is especially sensitive to the molecular

65

methodologies used (e.g. number and type of markers), and more likely to be susceptible to

66

sampling error because parent species are similar in appearance with respect to one another.

67

An Australian species complex of stingless bees (Meliponini) in the genus Tetragonula

68

illustrates these challenges well. The genus Tetragonula includes three cryptic sister species

69

(T. carbonaria. T. hockingsi and T. davenporti), indigenous to eastern Australia, that are part

70

of a monophyletic clade known as the “Carbonaria group” (Franck et al., 2004). These bees

71

can be kept readily in wooden hives, are popular pets, and are effective pollinators of some

72

tropical and subtropical crops (Dyer et al. 2016, Heard 1994). This has led to many thousands

73

of colonies being kept by beekeepers in Eastern Australia. Today, the distributions of all three

74

species overlap in parts of south-east Queensland (Fig. 1). Beekeeping and other human

75

activities, such as land use change, have greatly increased the overlap of their distributions in

76

recent decades, particularly through the southward spread of T. hockingsi. Unfortunately, the

77

historical distributions of the species are poorly known due to their cryptic morphology.

78

Queens, males and workers (females) cannot be reliably identified based on

79

morphology alone (Dollin et al. 1997). Rather, species are typically identified by the structure

80

of their brood comb, with T. carbonaria producing a neat spiral of adjacent brood cells in a

81

layered comb and T. hockingsi producing clusters of cells in a form known as semi-comb (Fig.

82

1). Tetragonula davenporti shares the semi-comb brood structure of T. hockingsi, and is known

83

from only a handful of managed colonies in a sub-coastal pocket of southern Queensland

84

(Franck et al. 2004, Brito et al. 2014), but is believed to persist in the wild in nearby forested

85

areas (P. Davenport pers. Comm 2019; R. Gloag unpublished data).

86

Relatively little is known about the mating behaviour of these bees, because it occurs

87

during the nuptial flight. Genetic data indicates that queen’s mate only once (Green & Oldroyd

88

2002), and observations of tethered virgins indicate that male genital detachment is likely the

89

mechanism by which multiple mating is avoided (Smith 2020). Male mating congregations,

90

which form outside colonies with virgin queens preparing for nuptial flights, can contain a mix

91

of both T. carbonaria and T. hockingsi males (Cunningham et al. 2014). These two species are
4

perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

92

also known to engage in interspecific nest usurpations, in which an invading colony kills a

93

resident queen and installs its own in the nest (Cunningham et al. 2014), a behaviour which

94

may increase the chances that males are attracted to interspecific queens.

95

Previous efforts to establish if, and how regularly, gene flow occurs between these

96

species, have returned equivocal results. First, microsatellite markers have been used to look

97

for evidence of hybrids. Frank et al. (2004) identified some South-East Queensland colonies

98

of Tetragonula as being of hybrid origin based on microsatellite data, where hybrids were

99

defined as samples having a posterior probability of assignment of less than 90% to a cluster

100

in an analysis using the genetic assignment program STRUCTURE (Pritchard et al. 2000). The

101

same three Australian Tetragonula species were re-investigated by Brito et al. (2014), with

102

microsatellite data and two mitochondrial genes. Three instances of hybridisation in South-

103

East Queensland were reported within the zone in which T. carbonaria and T. hockingsi

104

overlap. This was based on a threshold of 95% posterior probability of belonging to a cluster

105

in a Bayesian clustering analysis of the microsatellite data. Brito et al. (2014) also included

106

two hives reported to be T. davenporti in their study. One of the T. davenporti hives clustered

107

with T. carbonaria in a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) of genetic distance based on the

108

microsatellite data, and the other clustered slightly outside of the ‘T. carbonaria’ cluster. They

109

suggested that T. hockingsi represents two genetic clusters, one each in northern and southern

110

Queensland, based on their microsatellite and mitochondrial data. Their reported T. hockingsi

111

x T. carbonaria hybrids were from South East Queensland and they suggested that this

112

hybridisation was due to the anthropogenic movement of northern T. hockingsi into the

113

southern region.

114

Second, both nuclear and mitochondrial markers have been used to look for evidence

115

of introgression and thus past interbreeding across Australia’s East Coast Tetragonula species.

116

These efforts were hampered by the amplification of nuclear copies of mitochondrial genes

117

(numts) (e.g. for cytochrome b (cytb); Franck et al. 2004). Recently, several numts of

118

Cytochrome Oxidase Subunit I (COI) were identified in T. hockingsi and T. carbonaria

119

(Françoso et al. 2019), which cast into question even the designation of T. davenporti as a true

120

species. Surprisingly, the genuine COI sequences of T. hockingsi and T. carbonaria are highly

121

diverged (16.5 %), relative to available nuclear genes (Cunningham 2014, Francosco 2019).

122

This pattern of high mitochondrial divergence but low nuclear divergence might be consistent

123

with recent or ongoing gene flow and highlights the need for new analyses to understand the

124

species status of these bees. This will in turn reveal the likely impact of continued movement

125

of managed Tetragonula hives around Australia.

126

To resolve whether ongoing gene flow occurs between T. carbonaria, T. hockingsi and

127

T. davenporti we sampled these species in sympatry in South East Queensland. We compared

128

microsatellite data, using the same markers as previous studies, to a genotyping by sequencing

129

approach that yielded 2,049 snp markers with less than 5% missing data. We also look for

130

evidence of mitochondrial introgression, by considering two nuclear gene sequences and one

131

mitochondrial gene sequence of each species. We then use Illumina sequencing to reconstruct

132

mitochondrial genomes and confirm that our mitochondrial sequences are not pseudogenes.

133

Finally, we assembled draft nuclear genomes for these three species as a resource for further

134

genetic analysis of the species complex. This comprehensive approach leads to new insights

135

about the extent of hybridisation in these bees, and their species status.

Neutral nuclear markers

154

Microsatellites: We first assessed species status and evidence of hybridisation using seven

155

microsatellite loci (Green et al. 2001), as used in previous molecular studies of this group

156

(Franck et al. 2004, Brito et al. 2014). We used PCR reactions of 2 µl DNA template, 0.5 U

157

MyTaq polymerase (Bioline, Australia), 0.1 µM of forward primer, 0.2 µM of reverse primer,

158

0.2 µM M13 labelled primer with different fluorescent dyes (6-FAM, VIC, PET or NED) and

159

1x buffer, with reaction conditions of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 10

160

cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, annealing at 45 °C for 25 s, and 1 min extension at 72 °C, then 40

161

cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, annealing at 55 or 52°C for 25 s, and 1 min extension at 72 °C, and the

162

final extension was at 72 °C for 2 min (see Cunningham et al. (2014) for locus-specific

163

annealing temperatures). Loci were then pooled with one locus per dye, cleaned with 1U

164

Exonuclease I and one unit of Antarctic Phosphatase, as above, and genotyped by Macrogen

Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Microsatellite peaks were analysed using the microsatellite

167

Single nucleotide polymorphisms: We generated genome-wide snp data using a genotyping-

168

by-sequencing method. We adapted a protocol and adaptor regime based on the methods of

169

Elshire et al. (2011), Poland et al. (2012) and Petersen et al. (2012), with barcodes based on

170

Caporaso et al. (2012). Full details of the method are provided in supplementary data and can

171

be found online at http://www.jameshereward.org/GBS.html. We pooled 288 individuals per

172

sequencing lane, and sequenced the libraries with PE150 Illumina sequencing at Novogene

173

(Beijing, China).

174

The sequence data were demultiplexed, assembled, and snps called using STACKS

175

(Catchen et al. 2013). We then filtered the vcf using vcftools, with the data first being filtered

176

to a minor allele count of three (one heterozygote and one homozygote), which allows the

177

conservative removal of singleton snps that are likely to be errors, without discarding rare

178

alleles (Linck & Battey 2019). We set a minimum depth of 5 and kept only biallelic snps. We

179

filtered the data for missing data in three steps. First, any marker missing more than 50% data

180

was discarded (i.e. 50% of individuals were not genotyped at that marker), to remove the

181

markers most affected by missing data. Second, any individual that had missing data at more

182

than 50% of the markers was discarded, to remove the individuals that had bad quality

183

genotyping. Finally, any marker missing more than 5% data was discarded to produce a final

184

dataset with relatively little missing data (~3%).

185

For both our microsatellite and snp datasets, we performed principal component

186

analyses (PCA) using the adegenet package in R (Jombart, 2008) and STRUCTURE analyses

187

(Pritchard 2000). For the PCA we used the full snp dataset (2,049 snps), but for STRUCTURE

188

only one snp per locus was retained (1,935 snps). STRUCTURE is an individual-based

Mitogenome sequencing and assembly

229

The “Carbonaria group” of Tetragonula are now known to have several pseudogenes

230

(or “numts”, nuclear insertions of mitochondrial genes) (Françoso et al. 2019; see also Franck

231

et al. 20014, Brito et al. 2014). To ensure we were amplifying only the true 16S gene and to

232

provide a reference genome for each species for future work, we conducted whole genome

233

sequencing on one individual of each of the three species and reconstructed the complete

234

mitochondrial sequence. Sequencing libraries were made using the NebNext UltraII DNA kit

235

for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Mass., USA), and PE150 sequencing conducted

236

by Novogene (Beijing, China).

238

240

241

coverage into one file (this includes the nuclear genome, which had between 23x and 51x

Results

273

Tests of hybridisation using microsatellites and snps

274

PCAs using both microsatellite and snp datasets separated samples into three non-

275

overlapping clusters consistent with their species assignment (<5% missing data, Fig. 2A). The

276

microsatellite PCA placed T. davenporti closer to T. carbonaria than to T. hockingsi (Fig. 2A),

277

but the snp PCA indicated an equal distance between the three clusters (20% of the variance

278

explained by each of the first two components, Fig. 2B). The loading plot of the snp data

279

indicated that many of the 2,043 snp markers were contributing to the clustering in the PCA,

280

rather than a few specific loci (Fig. 2C).

281

STRUCTURE analyses using both microsatellite and snp datasets (N= 63 colonies, one

282

worker per colony) returned no evidence of hybrids, with all individuals showing close to 100%

283

assignment to a species’ cluster (Fig. 3). Notably, STRUCTURE results were sensitive to

299

300

highly conserved (no variation). The nuclear gene EF1alpha had identical sequence for all

301

302

303

representative of T. carbonaria it also had an Opsin sequence representative of T. carbonaria

304

(Fig. 4). However, one colony that was labelled T. carbonaria during field collections returned

305

sequences and nuclear markers consistent with T. hockingsi and was assumed to be the result

306

of either labelling error, or a recent interspecific colony takeover. Further, one colony suspected

311

We were unable to fully resolve the AT-rich control region of the mitochondria, but we

312

313

314

carbonaria and T. davenporti, and 23.6% across T. davenporti and T. hockingsi (Fig. 5). Using

315

316

317

relative to M. bicolor and all other bees for which mitogenome sequences are available

318

(Supplementary Fig. 1) with 12S, 16S, NAD1, CYTB and NAD6 reversed and inserted in

319

between NAD3 and NAD5.

321

From this sequencing, we investigated further the lack of interspecific difference at EF1alpha.

322

We took the EF1alpha sequence and used it as a ‘bait’ to pull out the regions of the genome

323

surrounding the 768bp PCR product. We managed to select at least 10kb in each species that

324

aligned around the PCR product using MAFFT (Katoh and Standley 2013). Duplications were

325

not present as only one hit was found in each assembly. Variation was detected around the

326

327

we sequenced. We thus confirmed that the pairwise difference across these three bee species

328

was indeed extremely low in this region of the nuclear genome (0.7-1%, Fig. 5), relative to that

329

detected in the mitochondrial genome. Similarly, we recovered 26kb around the Opsin gene,

330

which returned the same low nucleotide difference between species (0.7-1%).

Discussion

337

Testing for hybridisation

338

Hybridisation and gene flow are central to our understanding of species and speciation,

339

but accurately detecting hybridisation depends on the markers and analyses used. In the case

340

of Australia’s Tetragonula stingless bees, two previous studies concluded a low incidence of

341

hybrid colonies in an area of sympatry between sister species (2-3% of all sampled colonies;

342

Franck et al. 2004, Brito et al. 2014). In this study, we use multiple additional molecular

343

markers to analyse a new sample of colonies from the same area of sympatry (South-East

344

Queensland) and find all markers reliably segregate the three species, T. carbonaria, T.

345

hockingsi and T. davenporti. We cannot discount rare occurrences of hybrid colonies based on

346

63 hive samples, but we can conclude that there is no gene flow between the species in this

347

region beyond the F1 generation from our data.

348

The lack of gene flow between Tetragonula species indicates that these species either

349

do not mate, or that interspecific mating produces inviable or infertile offspring. Beekeepers

350

regularly keep multiple species in close proximity, providing opportunities for interspecific

351

mating; for example, at one property that we sampled, colonies of all three species had resided

352

together for at least nine years. In stingless bees, mating occurs on the wing close to the queen’s

353

colony. Males typically aggregate out the front of a colony that is undergoing the re-queening

354

process (i.e. has a virgin queen), presumably attracted by a pheromone (Vollet-Neto et al.
15

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.03.08.982546; this version posted September 16, 2020. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

355

2018). Male aggregations may attract dozens to hundreds of unrelated males from the

356

surrounding area (Vollet-Neto et al. 2018; Cameron et al. 2004). Virgin queens then fly into

357

the aggregation and mate with just one male, before returning to the nest (Green & Oldroyd

358

2002). Even if aggregations sometimes contain males of more than one Tetragonula species, it

359

may be that males are only sufficiently attracted to conspecific queens to attempt actual mating.

360

Alternatively, queens may be crucial to the recognition process at the point a male makes

361

contact with them, as in Aphytis lingnanensis parasitoids (Fernando and Walter 1997). Further,

362

interspecific mating may occur, but produce inviable diploid brood due to genetic

363

incompatibility. This latter scenario has been reported in sister species of honeybee that have

364

come into sympatry recently (Apis cerana and Apis mellifera). Interspecific mating between

365

these honeybees is common, but all hybrid offspring die at the early larval stage (Remnant et

366

al. 2014; Gloag et al. 2017). In this case, mis-mated queens may be quickly replaced. For

367

example, workers of at least one stingless bee species (Scaptotrigona depilis) will execute

368

laying queens that have made inbred matings and are producing diploid male brood (Vollet-

369

Neto et al. 2017). Clearly, further investigation into the mating of Tetragonula species is required

370

to clarify the interactions between males and females in mixed species swarms.

371

Australian Tetragonula highlight two common reasons why it can be difficult to

372

identify hybridisation between sister taxa. First, Tetragonula have nuclear pseudogenes of

373

several common mitochondrial marker genes (Françoso et al. 2019; Franck et al. 2004). Such

374

pseudogenes can complicate efforts to assess hybridisation through mito-nuclear discordance,

375

because they may be mistaken for valid haplotypes that are shared across species and thus

376

mistakenly taken as evidence of introgression. Even where researchers aim to avoid

377

pseudogenes, by checking for stop codons or frame-shifting indels in their sequences, a subset

378

of pseudogenes may still be missed. The reconstruction of whole mitogenomes from short read

379

data, using a depth cut-off to avoid nuclear copies, as in this study, may be necessary to identify
16

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.03.08.982546; this version posted September 16, 2020. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

380

true mitochondrial genes. A further issue is that the pattern expected from mito-nuclear

381

discordance (a sample returning one mitochondrial sequence and a different nuclear one) is the

382

same as one gets when samples are mislabelled. Rates of mislabelling have been estimated at

383

around 4% in published genetic datasets (Zych et al. 2017), so any cases of mito-nuclear

384

discordance at these rates probably deserve additional scrutiny. We detected one instance of

385

mislabelling across two of our datasets, by virtue of having conducted multiple genetic tests on

386

the same individuals. Both pseudogenes and “mis-labelling introgression” have likely led to

387

mistaken assignments of hybridisation in many taxa, and inflated perceptions of the extent to

388

which species hybridise.

389

Second, where nuclear markers suggest low divergence between species, the

390

interpretation of STRUCTURE analyses must take care to avoid biasing group assignment or

391

over-interpreting structure plots (Lawson et al. 2018). In our dataset, the inclusion of four

392

individuals per colony (full sisters) in a STURCTURE analysis led to the assignment of some

393

individuals as putative hybrids. When this pseudo replication was removed, however, by

394

including just one individual per colony, no hybrids were evident. Likewise, missing data had

395

a large effect on the likelihood of inferring Tetragonula hybridisation in our dataset. Our

396

original microsatellite dataset had low levels of missing data (<5%) and every individual was

397

assigned at close to 100% into a species group. When we subsampled this dataset to 20%

398

missing data, however, some samples appeared to be hybrids, indicating that STRUCTURE

399

analyses can be sensitive to such levels of missing data. Notably, in this test on the effect of

400

missing data, we subsampled our microsatellite data randomly across markers. In real datasets,

401

microsatellites tend to go missing in a much less random way, generally with one or two loci

402

most affected. The impact of missing microsatellite data is therefore likely to be even greater

403

in practice than in our simulation. STRUCTURE assignments using microsatellites were the

404

basis of hybrid identification in previous studies of Australian Tetragonula and may have been
17

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.03.08.982546; this version posted September 16, 2020. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

405

sensitive to these issues (Franck et al. 2004; Brito et al. 2014). In our study, snp data was much

406

less sensitive to the effect of missing data and vastly outperformed microsatellite data to test

407

for hybridisation. Indeed, in taxa where nuclear genome divergence is low, using many markers

408

may be the only way to confidently assess hybridisation rates. In the absence of direct parentage

409

analysis to assess hybridisation, such a multi-marker nuclear genome assay is the best approach

410

to infer whether gene flow between species is ongoing (Twyford & Ennos 2011).

411
412

Mitochondrial divergence between Tetragonula species

413

Our mitogenome data support previous studies indicating a high mitochondrial

414

sequence divergence between species of the ‘Carbonaria complex’ (Cunningham et al. 2012;

415

Francosco et al. 2019). This mitochondrial divergence contrasts with the low divergence we

416

observed in two nuclear genes and their flanking regions, around 0.7-1% bp difference vs. 21-

417

23% in the mitogenomes. We investigated the lack of variation in the EF1alpha PCR product

418

further to see whether it was restricted to this 700bp fragment. We were able to recover the

419

sequence flanking this region from our whole genome assemblies. These larger fragments did

420

exhibit variation and recovered the same phylogeny as the mitogenomes. The discrepancy in

421

divergence might indicate a particularly high mutation rate for mitochondria in this clade. The

422

ratio of mitochondrial to nuclear mutation rate varies substantially across metazoans (from at

423

least 0.8x to 40x, Allio et al. 2017). A mutation rate in Tetragonula mitochondria that was

424

around 20 times that of their nuclear genome would be within the range reported for insects,

425

albeit at the high end (Allio et al. 2017; Francosco et al. 2019). Several other Hymenoptera

426

have high rates of mitochondrial divergence, compared to other insects (Castro et al. 2002;

427

Kaltenpoth 2012). This pattern of mito-nuclear substitution rate difference could also be

428

interpreted as being evidence for a relatively deep divergence time for these species (evidenced

18

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.03.08.982546; this version posted September 16, 2020. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

429

in the divergent mitochondria) that was later followed by a period of nuclear gene flow, erasing

430

nuclear divergence. Under this scenario, past gene flow between diverged populations must

431

have occurred in a similar fashion between all three species, because they share a common ratio

432

of mitochondrial to nuclear divergence. Distinguishing between these two alternatives requires

433

a better understanding of rate variation in mitochondrial and nuclear genomes of stingless bees

434

relative to other Hymenoptera, and population genomic studies to elucidate the evolutionary

435

history of this group.

436

Tetragonula davenporti remains the most mysterious of the three Tetragonula species

437

considered in this study. It has been confirmed from only one property in the Gold Coast

438

hinterland (originally sourced from forested areas nearby) and one wild specimen from the

439

Queensland-New South Wales border (R. Gloag, unpublished data). It is essentially cryptic in

440

morphology with T. carbonaria and in nest structure with T. hockingsi. Nevertheless, the

441

distinctive mitogenome of T. davenporti reported here lends strong support to its status as a

442

valid species. The natural range of T. davenporti is currently unknown, and the difficulty in

443

identifying it morphologically to species means that its presence among managed colonies is

444

also unknown. South-east Queensland is an area of high endemicity, so it may well be endemic

445

in this area.

446

This study investigated managed colonies mostly from Brisbane and SE Queensland

447

because this is where hybridisation is most likely between these three species and has been

448

reported previously. However, T. carbonaria and T. hockingsi also co-occur in parts of Far

449

North Queensland, and T. hockingsi is increasingly being transported by beekeepers into more

450

southern parts of T. carbonaria’s range (pers. obs. TJS). Examining wild populations of this

451

genus may reveal additional species within the ‘carbonaria complex’. Comparing wild

452

populations to those that have been transported anthropogenically will provide further insight

453

into the impact of human movement on the evolution of this group. Finally, we hope the
