Abstract
Visual patterns are widely deployed as non-chemical insect deterrents, yet the visual mechanism remains unresolved. We
tested whether spatial aliasing, arising from a mismatch between stripe geometry and compound-eye sampling, disrupts
landing control. Using trained honey bees (Apis mellifera) and three-dimensional trajectory tracking, we quantified search
and landing/entry performance across stripe scale and orientation. Stripe scale produced a non-monotonic behavioural
response: disruption was greatest at intermediate stripe widths (~ 0.95–0.475 cm), where search times peaked and performance deteriorated. On a floor-mounted landing target, failures peaked at intermediate widths (up to 80% at 0.475 cm). On
a wall-mounted target, horizontal stripe patterns yielded 70–90% off-target entries at these intermediate widths, whereas
vertical stripe patterns at the same widths produced only 10–20% off-target entries. In contrast, when stripe width entered
an ultra-fine range (≤ 0.23 cm; down to 0.175–0.075 cm), error rates decreased (e.g., wall off-target entries fell to 20%,
and floor failures dropped to ~20%), consistent with patterns becoming poorly resolved and thus less disruptive. In the
near-field, as viewing distance decreases and the angular scale of the pattern increases, landing performance becomes
increasingly constrained by optic-flow quality: orientations that degrade coherent edge-motion cues during descent disrupt
approach stability and touchdown/entry localization, increasing unstable approaches and off-target landings or entries.
Together, these findings provide experimental evidence that spatial aliasing can impair both target localization and terminal
landing manoeuvres, and they establish quantitative design rules for pattern-based, chemical-free deterrents.

Introduction
Spatial aliasing is a sampling-related process that can give
rise to visual artefacts when spatial frequencies exceed the
resolving capacity of an animal’s visual system, leading
to misrepresentation of pattern structure. In simple terms,
when repeating features such as stripes become smaller than
what the eye can reliably distinguish, the pattern may no
longer be perceived accurately. Instead of appearing as regular stripes, it can produce misleading perceptual signals,
such as distorted edges, false motion cues, or mislocalised

In compound-eyed insects such as bees and flies, spatial resolution is constrained jointly by the spacing between
ommatidia (interommatidial angle, Δφ) and by optical blur
within each ommatidium (acceptance angle, ρ). This optical blur arises from the limited angular sensitivity of each
ommatidium, which is in turn shaped by the optics of the
diffraction-limited facet lens and rhabdom. The acceptance angle therefore acts as a low-pass optical filter and
can attenuate fine spatial structure before it is sampled by
the ommatidial array. As a result, some spatial frequencies
that would otherwise give rise to aliasing may be reduced
or removed by optical filtering. Consequently, spatial aliasing is most likely to arise not simply when pattern elements
fall below ~2Δφ, but when they remain above the blur
limit imposed by ρ while still being sampled too coarsely
for reliable reconstruction. In this sense, a useful working
distinction is between a blur-dominated regime (θ ≤ ρ),
an intermediate aliasing-prone regime (ρ < θ < 2Δφ), and

a resolvable regime (θ ≥ 2Δφ) (Reichardt 1969; Srinivasan
and Lehrer 1988; Lawson and Srinivasan 2020).
Although spatial aliasing is well established in visual
neuroscience, its behavioural consequences remain poorly
understood, particularly in ecologically relevant tasks such
as insect landing. During flight, insects rely on visual cues
such as optic flow to control approach speed and landing
timing (Chahl et al. 2004; Mauss and Borst 2020). If spatial aliasing distorts these signals, it may interfere with both
target localization and the control of landing manoeuvres.
A recent modeling study demonstrates that striped stimuli
can induce aliasing-driven motion errors and explicitly calls
for behavioural verification of these motion errors and their

effects on landing-related behaviour (Mouy 2025). Because
bees rely strongly on optic flow during approach and landing, such distortions may disrupt both target localization
and landing control. To aid interpretation, we provide a
schematic illustration of the geometric definition of visual
angle and how stripe-width-dependent sampling can produce blur-dominated, aliasing-prone, or resolvable visual
representations (Fig. 1).
Importantly, spatial aliasing may influence behaviour through at least two related but distinct mechanisms.
First, undersampling can lead to static spatial ambiguity, in
which stripe structure is not reliably encoded, resulting in
errors in target localization. Second, aliasing can generate

Because both interommatidial angle (Δφ) and acceptance angle (ρ) vary across the honey bee retina, the effective spatial limits differ between frontal and ventral viewing
regions. At a given retinal region, Δφ describes the angular
spacing between neighbouring ommatidial axes, whereas ρ
describes the angular sensitivity of each ommatidium and
therefore the degree of optical blur imposed before spatial sampling (Laughlin and Horridge 1971; Labhart 1980;
Seidl and Kaiser 1981; Rigosi et al. 2017). The relationship
between these two parameters is critical for aliasing. When
optical blur is broad relative to the sampling interval, fine
spatial structure may be attenuated before it can generate
aliased signals, corresponding to a more blur-limited or
oversampled condition. Conversely, when angular sensitivity is narrow relative to ommatidial spacing, spatial detail
may remain available to the sampling array but be sampled
too coarsely for reliable reconstruction, corresponding to a
more undersampled and aliasing-prone condition (Laughlin
and Horridge 1971; Srinivasan and Lehrer 1988; Lawson
and Srinivasan 2020). Previous anatomical, optical, physiological, and modelling studies have shown that both Δφ
and ρ vary across the honey bee eye, with regional differences in ommatidial sampling density and angular sensitivity (Labhart 1980; Seidl and Kaiser 1981; Stürzl et al.
2010; Rigosi et al. 2017). We therefore interpret θ using
region-specific criteria, distinguishing vertical-plane stimuli, which are primarily viewed by frontal eye regions, from
floor-plane stimuli, which are primarily viewed by ventral
eye regions. This approach does not attempt to reconstruct
the full ommatidial mosaic stimulated during each trajectory; rather, it uses representative frontal- and ventral-view
parameters as an operational framework for classifying the
angular scale of the stimulus during approach.
Landing in flying insects is a visually guided behaviour
that depends strongly on optic flow and related motion cues
during approach and touchdown (Srinivasan et al. 2000;
Evangelista et al. 2010; Baird et al. 2013). During approach,
insects use optic flow—the motion of visual texture across
the retina generated by self-motion—to estimate their velocity and distance to the surface (Chahl et al. 2004; Mauss
and Borst 2020). However, because the angular sensitivity of each ommatidium is limited in part by diffraction at
the facet lens, imposing an optical blur on incoming spatial
structure, aliasing-related perceptual errors may arise when
fine repetitive patterns are not fully suppressed by this filtering but remain undersampled, leading to failures in edge

detection or motion tracking. Such errors have been suggested to delay descent, increase search time, and reduce
landing accuracy (Srinivasan and Lehrer 1988; Lawson and
Srinivasan 2020). Here, we explicitly test how stripe geometry and viewing conditions interact to produce these behavioural effects and whether they follow the predictions of an
aliasing-prone regime.
Striped patterns have repeatedly been reported to reduce
landings by biting flies, making them a useful ecological
context for examining stripe-induced perceptual disruption.
Although several functions have been proposed for zebra
stripes, including predator confusion, thermoregulation, and
fly deterrence (Caro et al. 2014; Melin et al. 2016), the flydeterrence hypothesis is particularly relevant here because
tabanid flies are visually guided ectoparasites that can transmit pathogens and reduce host welfare (Foil 1989; Egri et al.
2012; Caro et al. 2019). Experimental studies have shown
that striped surfaces can reduce tabanid landings, and narrower stripes are often more effective, implicating limits in
insect visual sampling and motion computation (Egri et al.
2012; Caro et al. 2019, 2023; How et al. 2020). However,
the underlying mechanisms remain difficult to disentangle,
with proposed explanations including polarisation-based
cues, contrast-related visual effects, aperture effects, and
aliasing (Egri et al. 2012; How et al. 2020; Caro et al.
2023). Importantly, most previous work has focused on
attraction, orientation, or landing frequency rather than on
the approach-to-touchdown sequence itself. Direct behavioural evidence showing how stripe geometry alters visually
guided landing performance therefore remains limited.
Both biting flies and honey bees possess compound
eyes with finite spatial sampling, and both can therefore be
susceptible to spatial aliasing when viewing repetitive patterns. The severity and spatial scale at which aliasing occurs
are expected to vary across taxa and viewing directions
because they depend on both the interommatidial angle,
which constrains spatial sampling resolution, and the acceptance angle, which imposes optical low-pass filtering that
attenuates fine spatial structure before sampling (Srinivasan and Lehrer 1988; Anderson 1979; Lehrer et al. 1985).
Accordingly, the stripe widths most likely to induce aliasing—and thus to perturb approach and landing—need not
coincide between flies and bees, even if aliasing arises from
the same sampling principles. Such differences may in part
reflect variation in compound-eye design, including neural
superposition versus apposition architectures. Despite these
quantitative differences, the visual mechanisms supporting
motion detection and optic-flow–based control are broadly
shared across flying insects (Srinivasan et al. 2000; Evangelista et al. 2010).
We therefore used honey bees (Apis mellifera) as a model
system to experimentally test how controlled variation in

stripe scale and orientation reshapes landing behaviour.
Compared with biting flies, bees can be reliably trained to
approach and land on predefined visual targets, enabling
repeated trials and high-resolution trajectory analysis
under standardized conditions (Baird et al. 2013; Ehmer
and Gronenberg 2002). Their visual system is among the
best characterized in insects, with well-established measurements of ommatidial organization, interommatidial
angles, angular sensitivity, spectral sensitivity, and visual
acuity (Laughlin and Horridge 1971; Labhart 1980; Seidl
and Kaiser 1981; Lehrer et al. 1985; Srinivasan and Lehrer
1988; Rigosi et al. 2017), as well as extensive behavioural
and neurobiological evidence for optic-flow-based motion
processing and landing control (Chahl et al. 2004; Mauss
and Borst 2020). Honey bees have also served as a primary
model for studies of optic flow regulation and landing kinematics, providing a robust framework for quantifying how
stripe-induced aliasing propagates from sensory sampling
to motor execution during landing (Srinivasan et al. 2000;
Evangelista et al. 2010).
Here we test whether stripe-induced landing disruption
can be explained by spatial aliasing and how this mechanism interacts with optic-flow geometry during the final
descent. Using honey bees as a tractable landing model, we
present black-and-white stripes with systematically varied
scale and orientation on both horizontal and vertical targets,
and quantify approach and landing behaviour with threedimensional trajectory tracking. We interpret behavioural
outcomes in relation to region-specific sampling limits in the
honey bee compound eye, which vary across the visual field
and thus predict when a given stripe scale should become
resolvable, aliased, or effectively blurred during approach
(Stürzl et al. 2010). We show that landing performance
exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on stripe scale, with
maximal disruption at intermediate scales consistent with an
aliasing regime and recovery for ultra-fine patterns that fall
below spatial resolution. We further demonstrate that orientation-dependent optic-flow reliability modulates terminal
control, exacerbating disruption when edge-motion cues
are degraded. Together, these results provide behavioural
evidence linking sensory sampling constraints to landing
failures and establish quantitative principles for designing
stripe-based, chemical-free visual deterrents.

Materials and methods
Experimental insects and training
The experimental subjects were honey bees (Apis mellifera), maintained on the campus of National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan. The experiments were conducted

13

outdoors and scheduled during periods of clear weather and
stable foraging activity. Bees were trained to associate a
50% sucrose solution with a feeding station 50 m away from
their colonies and were gradually guided to forage from
the feeder positioned inside the experimental apparatus.
Training to the apparatus was considered stable when bees
repeatedly returned directly to the feeder without search
behaviour across consecutive trips.

Experimental setup
The experimental apparatus consisted of three transparent
acrylic rectangular boxes (each 60 cm × 30 cm × 30 cm).
The boxes could be combined along their long sides to form
a single elongated chamber (180 cm × 30 cm × 30 cm)
(Fig. 2). Transparent acrylic barriers were placed at both
ends to prevent bees from passing through, with one barrier
featuring a 12 cm-diameter circular entry hole centered on
the 30 × 30 cm end panel (hole centre 15 cm above the floor
and midway laterally), thereby setting the initial flight elevation. The striped stimulus pattern was placed at the third
acrylic box of the apparatus, either at the bottom (horizontal
placement) or mounted on the front wall by replacing the
far-end barrier (vertical placement) (Fig. 2a).
A 3D tracking system recorded flight trajectories and
landing behaviour using two GigE monochrome cameras
(XCFS-BG62, 1/1.8″, CS-mount), each fitted with a Kowa
4.4–11 mm varifocal C-mount lens via a 5-mm C-to-CS
adapter, and mounted to view the landing volume from
two angles. Video acquisition was performed using Media
Recorder 4 (NSE-OBS-MR4; Noldus Information Technology bv, Wageningen, The Netherlands), and trajectories
were tracked and reconstructed in EthoVision XT Base
(NSE-EV-BASE) with the Track3D module (NSE-EV3DM; Noldus Information Technology bv). Stereo views
were synchronized and integrated to yield 3D coordinates in
the Track3D workflow.
Stereo calibration. After each recording session, a black
3D calibration frame (XCAL-5070, 50 × 50 × 70 cm) bearing 47 pre-localized white fiducial points was placed in the
recording volume and recorded from fixed viewpoints by
both cameras. Using the Track3D calibration routine (direct
linear transformation with bundle adjustment), we estimated the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of each camera and computed the 3D reconstruction transform. Focus
and focal length were locked after calibration and remained
unchanged during data acquisition.

ensure that search time measurements were not confounded

Dataset definition and inclusion criteria
The experimental unit was the bee. Within each condition,
each individually marked bee completed the full coarseto-fine stimulus series, contributing one trajectory dataset
per width (or check size); therefore, datasets across widths
from the same bee were treated as within-bee repeated measures and were not regarded as independent replicates. A
dataset denotes one complete, stereoscopically recorded
approach-to-endpoint episode by a single focal bee, spanning from landing onset to the endpoint. Landing onset
was defined as the first frame after the bee entered the third
acrylic box (for both the horizontal-floor and vertical-cube
configurations). Endpoints were defined as: (i) horizontalfloor stimuli (HLSP/HTSP/HCP), the first contact with the
50% (w/w) sucrose droplet; and (ii) vertical-cube stimuli
(WVSP/WHSP), crossing the entry aperture into the black
acrylic cube (i.e., entering the cube). In our experiments,
all included trials reached the relevant endpoint (i.e., no
aborted trials were analysed). For both configurations, outcomes were classified as either successful (direct touchdown on the sucrose droplet or direct entry at the aperture
upon arrival) or unsuccessful (off-target touchdown/stop
followed by walking to the droplet or to the aperture before
feeding/entry). Datasets were included only when uninterrupted stereo visibility and reliable 3D reconstruction were
maintained throughout the analysed interval, without interference from other bees or external visual obstruction (e.g.
leaves entering the camera field and partially occluding the
bee trajectory) during recording. Sample sizes (n) refer to
bees that completed the full stimulus series within a condition (e.g., HLSP: n = 30 bees; HTSP/WVSP/WHSP/HCP: n
= 10 bees per condition). For HLSP, behavioural endpoints
were quantified using all qualifying datasets (n = 30 bees),
whereas kinematic analyses were performed on a subset of
bees/datasets that satisfied the strict stereo-visibility and
3D-reconstruction criteria (n = 10 bees), as detailed above.

Data analysis
For all angular analyses, landing onset was defined as the
first frame in which the bee entered the third acrylic box,
because this point marked the beginning of the final approach
trajectory toward the target region. To relate the physical

stimulus widths reported in centimetres to the visual scale
experienced by the bee, we computed, for each frame t, the
angular period of the grating (θ) from the reconstructed 3D
trajectories and the calibrated pose of the stimulus plane.
Here, θ denotes the angular period of one complete black–
white stripe cycle measured perpendicular to the stripe orientation, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
For behavioural interpretation, θ was evaluated relative
to region-specific visual parameters, reflecting the spatial
heterogeneity of the honey bee compound eye. Because
the reconstructed flight trajectories do not specify the exact
ommatidia stimulated at each moment, we did not apply a
full ommatidial mosaic map. Instead, we used representative parameter sets for the two viewing regions most relevant to the behavioural task: frontal-view parameters
for approaches to wall-mounted stimuli and ventral-view
parameters for approaches to floor-mounted stimuli. We
used the frontal- and ventral-view parameterization from
the honey bee eye model of Stürzl et al. (2010) because
it provides a practical region-specific framework for linking compound-eye geometry to the viewing conditions in
our behavioural assay. This modelling framework was not
treated as the sole source of primary biological measurements; rather, we interpreted its thresholds in light of previous anatomical, optical, and physiological measurements
of honey bee ommatidial organization and angular sensitivity (Laughlin and Horridge 1971; Labhart 1980; Seidl
and Kaiser 1981; Rigosi et al. 2017). These parameter sets
were used to define operational visual regimes based on the
relationship between the instantaneous angular scale θ, the
acceptance angle ρ, and the interommatidial sampling interval Δφ. The blur-dominated regime was defined as θ ≤ ρ,
where the stripe structure is expected to be strongly attenuated by optical blur; the aliasing-prone regime was defined
as ρ < θ < 2Δφ, where the pattern is not fully suppressed by
blur but remains undersampled relative to ommatidial spacing; and the resolvable regime was defined as θ ≥ 2Δφ. Thus,
the regime boundaries should be interpreted as region-specific analytical thresholds rather than as exact perceptual
boundaries for every ommatidium.

or white; equal widths, 50% duty). Because the full spatial
period of the square-wave grating consisted of one black bar
plus one adjacent white bar, the full physical period was 2w.

the stimulus plane; the bar-orthogonal angular period of one
complete black–white cycle was

Angle binning and histograms. For each frame of each
reconstructed approach, we computed the instantaneous
angular period (θ) along the bar-orthogonal viewing direction. We summarized θ as percentage-time occupancy in
five bins: A (θ≤ρ), B (ρ<θ≤Δφ⊥), C (Δφ⊥<θ≤1.5Δφ⊥), D

Angular analysis links behavioural disruption to an
aliasing-prone regime
Intermediate stripe widths cause bees to traverse a range of
angular periods (ρ < θ < 2Δφ) during descent in which spatial
structure is not fully suppressed by optical blur but is still
undersampled relative to ommatidial spacing. We therefore
define this range as a region-specific aliasing-prone regime
(“aliasing band”), bounded below by the acceptance angle
(ρ) and above by twice the interommatidial angle (2Δφ),
with both parameters varying between ventral and frontal
visual fields.
Using ventral-view binning, aliasing-band occupancy,
defined as the combined percentage of approach time spent
in bins B–D, varied systematically with stripe width in HLSP
(Fig. 7a). At coarse widths (20.8–2.975 cm), B–D occupancy remained at 0%, indicating that bees spent essentially
no approach time within the aliasing-prone regime. Occupancy increased at 1.98 cm (12.3 ± 3.2%, mean ± S.E.M.),
peaked at 0.95 cm (55.2 ± 3.1%), and remained elevated at
0.475 cm (34.7 ± 2.8%), before declining at 0.23 cm (16.0
± 2.0%). This pattern coincided with the strongest behavioural disruption at intermediate stripe widths. Similar nonmonotonic patterns were observed across the other stimulus
configurations (Fig. 7b–e). In HTSP, B–D occupancy was
highest at intermediate widths, peaking at 0.7 cm (53.5 ±
2.5%) and remaining elevated at 0.35 cm (37.4 ± 3.0%),
before decreasing at finer widths (0.175 cm: 10.4 ± 2.2%;
0.075 cm: 1.4 ± 0.3%). In WVSP and WHSP, B–D occupancy also peaked at intermediate widths, reaching 61.8 ±
5.6% at 0.475 cm in WVSP and 44.8 ± 7.0% at 0.475 cm
in WHSP. In HCP, aliasing-band occupancy peaked at
0.475 cm (46.4 ± 2.2%) and declined at finer check sizes

(0.23 cm: 17.0 ± 3.3%; 0.115 cm: 4.9 ± 1.3%; 0.057 cm:
2.9 ± 0.8%). The complete A–E occupancy distributions,
including the corresponding blur-dominated and resolvable
regimes, are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. Across all
five configurations, B–D occupancy peaked at intermediate
widths or check sizes and declined again at finer scales. This
non-monotonic occupancy pattern supports the interpretation that behavioural disruption was associated with substantial time spent within an aliasing-prone angular regime.
However, differences between WVSP and WHSP indicate
that aliasing-band occupancy alone does not fully explain
orientation-dependent landing performance, suggesting that
optic-flow cue geometry also modulates terminal landing
control.

Discussion
Effects of spatial aliasing on visual search and target
localization
Our results show that repetitive stripe patterns can substantially disrupt honey bee landing by impairing visual search
and target localization during the approach and descent.
As stripe width decreased, the time required to locate the
sucrose increased over an intermediate range of spatial
scales, consistent with a sampling-limited regime in which
periodic structure can be misinterpreted and edge localization becomes unreliable (Srinivasan and Lehrer 1988; Lawson and Srinivasan 2020). When stripes became ultra-fine,
search times decreased and stabilized, consistent with stripe
elements approaching or falling below retinal resolvability
and thus becoming less effective at generating disruptive
aliasing-related signals. Although other visual cues in the
apparatus may still have contributed to landing, ultra-fine
stripe patterns likely provided progressively less effective
patterned visual information. Figure 4 further shows that
prolonged search reflected repeated active movements over
the sucrose location rather than inactivity. Taken together,
the behavioural non-monotonicity indicates that disruption was strongest at intermediate angular scales during
approach.
These results are therefore more consistent with an intermediate aliasing-prone regime than with a criterion based
on 2Δφ alone (Mouy 2025). The present behavioural data
do not allow these mechanisms to be fully separated, but the
observed increase in search time and landing errors is consistent with spatial ambiguity, whereas the repeated flight
adjustments and extended trajectories suggest a contribution
from degraded motion cues.

Optic flow and pattern geometry in landing control
While spatial aliasing can impair initial localization, optic
flow is a primary cue for terminal landing control, particularly the regulation of in-flight deceleration and the timing
of touchdown. The dominant visual signals differ between
the two geometries used here: for floor-mounted targets,
bees descend toward a downward goal and rely mainly on
ventral optic flow; for wall-mounted targets, bees approach
a frontal aperture and depend more strongly on frontal cues,
including angular expansion near the target and the spatial
stability of edges around the aperture. Figure 5 illustrates
how these geometry-specific optic-flow constraints interact with pattern orientation and aliasing to shape landing
outcomes. Because floor- and wall-mounted tasks necessarily used different targets (sucrose droplet vs aperture), we
restrict statistical comparisons to within-geometry contrasts
(e.g., HLSP vs HTSP; WVSP vs WHSP). This controls for
target type while isolating the effects of stripe orientation
and scale.
For floor-mounted patterns, longitudinal stripes (HLSP)
produced weaker and less stable ventral motion cues during descent than transverse stripes (HTSP), consistent with
the higher landing-failure rates observed under HLSP. This
reduces the reliability of ventral optic-flow cues required
for regulating deceleration and timing touchdown, thereby
increasing landing failures (Srinivasan et al. 2000; Baird
et al. 2013). Conversely, in HTSP, where stripe boundaries are oriented perpendicular to the approach direction,
edges sweep consistently across the ventral retina, producing a more stable ventral optic-flow gradient and supporting
comparatively accurate landings, even when stripe widths
become fine.
A similar orientation dependence was observed for
wall-mounted patterns, where horizontal stripes (WHSP)
produced substantially more off-target entries than vertical
stripes (WVSP), suggesting less stable frontal motion cues
during approach. In contrast, in WHSP, the target region contains rapid successive edge transitions; when stripe widths
fall into an aliasing-susceptible range, these transitions may
be mis-sampled, producing ambiguous or erroneous motion
and expansion cues and increasing off-target entries and
delayed entry (Lawson and Srinivasan 2020). These orientation effects are expected to be most consequential during
the terminal phase, where bees regulate approach dynamics
using optic flow.
Furthermore, bees have been shown to maintain an
approximately constant angular velocity (~ 500°/s) during
landing by adjusting forward speed to preserve optic flow,

13

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

supporting smooth touchdown (Srinivasan et al. 2000).
When striped patterns distort optic flow through aliasing
and/or unfavourable edge geometry relative to self-motion,
the optic-flow signal becomes unreliable, undermining this
control strategy and increasing the probability of failed
touchdown.
Aliasing may also generate misleading motion signals
that contribute to unstable trajectories and repeated flight
adjustments at intermediate stripe widths. Because bee optomotor responses are frequency-dependent, the consequences
of undersampling are unlikely to depend only on whether a
pattern falls within an aliasing-prone range, but also on the
specific motion signals generated by the interaction between
stripe geometry, optical filtering, and flight trajectory. The
present study was designed to provide behavioural evidence
for disruption across stripe scales and orientations, rather
than a formal quantitative model of motion-detector output.
Nevertheless, the behavioural signatures observed here—
especially unstable approach trajectories, repeated flight
adjustments, and orientation-dependent terminal disruption—are consistent with the possibility that aliasing-related
motion artifacts contribute to the landing errors.

Spatial aliasing as a visual disruption mechanism
and its applications
Together, our experiments provide behavioural evidence
that spatial aliasing can disrupt landing by impairing target
localization and interacting with optic-flow–based control.
By mapping disruption across stripe scale and orientation,
we delineate a finite parameter range that yields strong
interference, thereby providing a quantitative framework
for pattern-based, chemical-free deterrent design.
Previous studies on striped deterrent patterns have rarely
evaluated whether stripe geometry falls within a biologically
relevant aliasing range. An important implication is that
acceptance angle must be considered together with interommatidial angle when evaluating whether a given stripe pattern is likely to generate behaviourally relevant aliasing,
because optical blur can attenuate or suppress fine spatial
frequencies before they are sampled by the compound eye.
Although narrower stripes are often reported to reduce landings on zebra-like patterns (Egri et al. 2012; How et al.
2020), key retinal parameters (e.g., interommatidial angle
Δφ) are seldom reported, limiting inference about whether
the approaches fall within an aliasing regime. Our framework provides a tractable way to test whether approaches
traverse the putative aliasing band and to identify the stripewidth range that maximizes behavioural interference: both
overly coarse and ultra-fine patterns reduce disruption,
whereas intermediate spatial frequencies within the aliasing
range elicit strong effects. These aliasing-driven perceptual

13

errors are expected to interact with optic-flow–based landing control. Because insects regulate deceleration and
touchdown timing using optic flow, any pattern that reduces
the reliability of edge-motion signals or motion contrast can
further compromise terminal control, especially at close
range. Although tested here in honey bees, the results generate testable predictions for striped deterrent systems in other
insects, including zebra-like patterns in biting flies. Under
this interpretation, stripe-based deterrence could arise from
a combined disruption of sampling-limited perception (spatial aliasing) and motion-control signals (optic flow), potentially acting alongside other proposed visual factors such as
contrast-based disruption or polarization interference (Egri
et al. 2012; Horváth et al. 2019; Caro et al. 2023). These
insights motivate comparative tests across species and support the design of bio-inspired deterrent surfaces.
Future modelling linking optical filtering, spatial sampling, stripe geometry, and flight trajectories will help clarify how aliasing alters motion signals during landing. Such
modelling would help test whether the disruptive intermediate stripe widths identified here are expected to generate
specific low-frequency motion artifacts capable of perturbing optomotor control. We therefore regard formal prediction of aliasing-related motion signals as an important next
step beyond the scope of the present behavioural study.
In summary, this study demonstrates that intermediate
stripe scales can disrupt visually guided landing behaviour
in honey bees, particularly when combined with unfavourable pattern orientations during approach. These findings
provide a quantitative framework for understanding how
spatial sampling limits influence insect landing behaviour
and may aid the design of pattern-based, chemical-free
deterrents.
