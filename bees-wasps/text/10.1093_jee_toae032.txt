Providing rigor in bee colony strength auditing methods.
Cook D, Hauxwell C. (2024). Journal of economic entomology. . doi:10.1093/jee/toae032. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Providing rigor in bee colony strength auditing methods

## Abstract
AbstractThe primary method used to audit honey bee (Apis mellifera Linnaeus, 1758 [Hymenoptera: Apidae]) colony strength for almond pollination services, Nasr et al.’s (1990) frame-top cluster count method, is a subjective visual audit that relies on an auditor’s spot assessment and may lack rigor and repeatability. We created novel, open-source software for the analysis of frame-top cluster count photographic assessments to improve methodological rigor and repeatability. We evaluated 2 existing visual audit methods, created 3 novel audit method variations, and determined between-method conversion factors using linear modeling. The software has potential applications in apiological research, apiarist and orchardist colony auditing, as well as training future generations of apiarists in auditing techniques. The software enhances the rigor and repeatability of Nasr et al.’s (1990) frame-top cluster count population assessment. In this article, we introduce the novel open-source software and between-method regression equations and review the tested visual assessment methods and their application.

## Introduction
Bee colony strength is an important measure for bee brokers, apiarists, and orchardist during almond pollination events (Goodrich 2017, Somerville and Frost 2018). The “strength” of a honey bee (Apis mellifera L.) colony is described synonymously with colony population, and population measures often assume a valid and operational colony that is disease free, contains an active, laying queen, and contains adequate brood and stores (Goodman 2015, Goodrich 2017). In-field assessment of bee colony population is a challenging task confounded by several factors such as a mobile population of bees both within and outside the hive, diurnal cycles in behavior and hive environment, and environment-influenced behavioral changes (Eckert et al. 1994, Melicher et al. 2019).
Empirical methods of colony measurement involve the separation of bees from comb and surrounding hive structures, the subsequent weighing of the bees, and measurement of comb areas for brood (capped and uncapped), honey, or pollen (Farrar 1937, Delaplane et al. 2013). These empirical methods tend to be highly disruptive to the colony, time consuming to conduct, and need to be undertaken in favorable conditions where the external temperature is high enough to avoid exposure damage to the brood and bees through chilling (Nasr et al. 1990, Delaplane et al. 2013, Somerville and Frost 2018). Empirical methods are time sensitive and require measurements to be performed before the bees start foraging to capture the entire population (Nasr et al. 1990, Delaplane et al. 2013). For these reasons, empirical methods of colony measurement largely remain tools of scientific experimentation and research diagnostics and are rarely used by beekeepers or commercial auditors (Delaplane et al. 2013, Somerville and Frost 2018).
Apiarists, bee brokers, and pollination auditors often use rapid, less-disruptive methods that rely on primarily visual assessment of bees (Goodrich 2017, Somerville and Frost 2018). Rigorous visual auditing methods in the field include frame-by-frame assessments of the bee population and comb constitution or, more commonly in in-field almond bee colony auditing, the more rapid but less rigorous frame-top “cluster count” of bees (where no frames are removed for inspection) (Nasr et al. 1990, Delaplane et al. 2013, Somerville and Frost 2018, Dainat et al. 2020).
The frame-by-frame assessment method was originally developed by Imdorf et al. (1987) and later renamed the Liebefeld method (Dainat et al. 2020). The Liebefeld method removes each frame and visually estimates area of bee coverage and brood (open and capped cells) area (Imdorf et al. 1987). The Liebefeld method is commonly measured in “Frames of Bees” (FOB) in the apicultural industry, where 1 FOB is documented as a minimum of 75% bee coverage on the 2 sides of a single full-depth Langstroth frame (Somerville and Frost 2018, Dainat et al. 2020). Like the original Liebefeld method, apiarists estimate total number of adult bees based on perceived density, rather than a fixed density as used in later work by Delaplane et al. (2013) who assume a bee density of 1.24–1.77 bees/cm2, dependent on the region in which the bees are kept (Burgett and Burijam 1985, Gris 2002, Delaplane et al. 2013).
The frame-top “cluster count” method was created by Nasr et al. (1990) to create time and labor efficiencies over the Liebefeld assessment during in-field colony strength auditing, particularly in almond pollination events. Nasr et al.’s (1990) method requires the inspection of the mass of bees on only the top of the wooden frames (without removing any frames) in each box and is measured in “Number of Frames” (NOF), where 1 frame is equal to 1 wooden frame top (of a standard 35 mm Hoffman frame) being completely covered with bees (Nasr et al. 1990, Chabert et al. 2021). Note that the unit terms NOF and FOB are taken directly from Nasr et al.’s (1990) paper and are maintained here for clarity and continuity.
The Liebefeld method was used by Sheesley and Poduska (1970) in one of the first comparisons of colony strength to almond pollination efficacy and is likely the origin of the colony standards used today for almond pollination (Nasr et al. 1990, Somerville and Frost 2018).
The apiarists, auditors, and brokers in the almond industry in Australia and the United States almost exclusively use Nasr et al.’s (1990) method when auditing beehives for strength during almond pollination events and report these results in the unit NOF. Pollination contracts commonly specify colony standards in FOB based on Sheesley and Poduska’s (1970) work using the Liebefeld method (Somerville and Frost 2018). The conversion between Nasr et al.’s (1990) method and the Liebefeld method requires a calculation (Equation (1)) as explicitly stated in Nasr et al.’s (1990) method; however, this conversion is frequently omitted in Australian apicultural industry colony assessment guides (Nasr et al. 1990, Somerville and Frost 2018, Cook et al. 2022). Since NOF and FOB are not equivalent, this may lead to confusion in the specification of colony standards, which refer to “frames” without stating which method is used.
In addition to these 2 audit methods, a third visual assessment method was recently described by Chabert et al. (2021) that assesses bees visible between the comb frames when viewed from the bottom of each hive box and named Interframe bees (IFB). Chabert et al.’s (2021) method partially addresses some of the difficulties in the use of Nasr et al.’s method such as cluster breakup, cluster scattering over time, temperature dependence, and the disruption of the cluster by hive devices such as queen excluders or hive mats (a layer of cloth or plastic that reduces unwanted comb build up and heat loss) (Goodman 2015, Somerville and Frost 2018, Chabert et al. 2021). However, Chabert et al.’s (2021) method also requires additional lifting, hive manipulation, or disassembly of some hive configurations such as fixed bottom hives, which are likely time consuming and disruptive to the colony (Chabert et al. 2021, Cook et al. 2022).
The Liebefeld, Nasr et al. (1990), and Chabert et al. (2021) methods rely on visual inspection and immediate grading by the auditor and suffer from a lack of persistent evidence or assessable record of the audit result, resulting in low assessment repeatability and a lack of result verification. Unlike the Liebefeld method, Nasr et al.’s (1990) and Chabert et al.’s (2021) methods do not account for the varying density of observed bees, which logically should impact a score based on area coverage of bees, and so are more subjective than the Liebefeld method, lacking a degree of methodological rigor and repeatability due to their more subjective nature (Imdorf et al. 1987, Nasr et al. 1990, Chabert et al. 2021). Due to the issues described in Nasr et al.’s (1990) applied audit method, a need for reducing subjectivity and increasing the rigor and repeatability of audit assessments may be required. To this end, we created software in the programming language Python 3 (Van Rossum and Drake 2009) that works in tandem with a photographic audit process (Cornelissen et al. 2009, Delaplane et al. 2013, Bargen et al. 2019, Cook et al. 2022) and enables repeatable and rigorous (randomized or multi-auditor) assessment.
The software is not designed for the Liebefeld method, but has applications in both Nasr et al.’s (1990) and Chabert et al.’s (2021) methods as well as our novel variations. The software provides measurement methods for:
Nasr et al.’s (1990) manual visual approximation of the frame-top cluster (NOF)
Modification of Nasr et al.’s method using a user-defined oval (NOF_o) or rectangle (NOF_r) to set bee cluster area. These methods allow bee density to be set, rectifying density measurement issues
IFB measurement as per Chabert et al. (2021)
A novel “frame-top bees” linear coverage with bee density setting (NOF_l). This method combines Nasr et al.’s (1990) method with a modified top-of-the-frame version of Chalbert et al.’s (2021) IFB method
The software provides rapid visual assessment of the frame-top cluster, recording of audit scores and audit method, automatic image scaling, and downstream processing software-ready file output (Comma Separated Value) within a single piece of software. To increase the usefulness of the software, the described audit methods were tested on a series of images to test the correlation between methods and describe the regression equation for the cross-conversion between methods. Provision of these regression equations, in addition to Equation (1) (Liebefeld method to Nasr et al.’s [1990] method), allows the cross-conversion of measurements between all methods described. A video walkthrough of the software has been created to show the features, operation, and software application in the context of hive auditing (https://youtu.be/k9dWufk_S24).

## Materials and Methods
To assess auditor correlation and between-method correlation and calculate the intermethod conversion equations, a photoset of hive frame-top images (n = 244) of single brood box hives (without supers/honey boxes) from a previous experiment (Cook et al. 2022) was loaded into the software and graded by 3 auditors of varying experience. Each auditor followed a set process (software guided) for image assessment as shown in Fig. 1. Auditors performed 1 audit method at a time across the entire photoset before moving onto the next audit method. To assess interauditor variability, no calibration was performed by the auditors.
Procedure for software auditing of bee hives from photographic records.
Visual estimation as per Nasr et al.’s (1990) (NOF) was performed by keyboard input of a score between 0 and 9 where 9 is the maximum coverage of all 9 frames in the hive box and a score of zero represented an empty hive. Scores were integer only, with scores rounded to the nearest whole number. NOF scores had no further calculations performed on them.
NOF_o (oval), NOF_r (rectangle), NOF_l (line), and IFB methods required the auditor to scale the image. Auditors input hive configuration data, such as hive type, number of frames in the hive box, and hive box depth in the software’s configuration, and drew a single scaling configuration line along the length of the central frame in the hive with the assumption that the central frame suffered the least length distortion from parallax error (Fig. 2). The configuration line length allows calibration and scaling of the image based on known hive dimensions (Fig. 2). The software provides for several common hive configurations and their sub-variations, including Langstroth (Australian and Mexican variants), Dadant, Warre, National (UK), Commercial (UK), and Smith, each with frame configurations of 10, 9, 8, 6, 5, and 4 frames. Hive depth configuration options are also provided including full, 3/4, 1/2, W.S.Pender (WSP), and ideal depths for each hive type.
Setting the frame length using the button on the left panel. Metrics are calculated by determining the frame length (dashed line, centre image) in the image and scaling the pixels to this measurement based on a known hive-type frame-length. By using the centre frame, perspective distortion issues are negated. Frame scaling calculation results are shown in the bottom right for user information.
The area-based audit methods of NOF_r and NOF_o required the auditor to first estimate the density of bee coverage by percentage and then drew a shape over the largest predominant bee mass (Fig. 3). The score for this area is then calculated by comparing the user-drawn area to the total possible area score for the hive configuration, derived from the configuration line and known box measurements.
Cluster count area (dottted elipse) with 50% density setting, and frame configuration length line (dashed).
The line-based assessments of IFB and NOF_l do not provide for density measurement and require the user to draw a line of varying length over contiguous masses of bees either between frames (IFB) or on top of frames (NOF_l). A maximum of 10 lines could be drawn per image to suit our 9-frame test hives. The score is then calculated by adding the total length of the lines and comparing the user-drawn length to the total possible combined frame length score for that hive configuration. It should be noted that the Chabert et al.’s (2021) method is designed for the bottom of the hive box; however, our auditors applied it to the top of the hive box to use the same photo sets and test the method application alongside our novel methods.
After all audits were complete, scores for each audit were merged into a single file in Microsoft Excel version 2105 using the image number (a date-time stamp) as an index and checked for record completeness (Microsoft Corporation 2018). Date and time data from the timestamp were used to determine the mean, maximum, and minimum photographic audit timings between hives. Data were exported to R version 4.1.0 with R Studio, version 1.4.1103, and used for all downstream analysis (RStudio Team 2020).
Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to calculate the correlation between auditors and between-method types as the data were normally distributed, had a linear relationship, and contained no outliers. A linear model (base R function “lm” [RStudio Team 2020]) was used to compare data in pairs using the auditor as a random effect. The linear model was used to produce regression equations for conversion from one method to another and calculate an adjusted R2 value to demonstrate model fit.

## Results
Pearson’s correlation coefficient between auditors varied by auditor pairs with auditors 1 and 2, r = 0.83, auditors 1 and 3, r = 0.71, and auditors 2 and 3 showing the lowest correlation at r = 0.51. All correlations had a P-value < 2.2e-16. Correlations between methods were also calculated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and are shown in Table 2. All correlations had a P-value < 2.2e-16. The regression equations and adjusted R2 model fit of these linear models are summarized in Table 3. All linear models between method pairs were found to be significant with a P-value < 2.2e-16. The duration of software assessment was not recorded; however, auditors reported that the NOF audit was the most rapid (estimated at 3–5 s), with NOF_r and NOF_o being the next fastest (estimated at 20–30 s) and NOF_l and IFB being the slowest (estimated at 45–60 s) due to the number of measurements required. The physical photographic audit process speed was calculated from the interimage timestamp intervals, with the mean timing between images being 35 s, the minimum 11 s, and the maximum 4 min and 2 s.

## Discussion
We designed software to allow rapid, rigorous, and repeatable assessments of photographic frame-top beehive audits. In testing this software, we found significant differences in correlation between auditors of varying experience levels, ranging from r = 0.83 to 0.51 (P-value < 2.2e-16), which may demonstrate the inherent subjectivity of the visual audit methods tested. Despite interauditor assessment variations across the data set with auditors of varying experience, a statistically significant correlation of the assessment methods was possible. We show a significant correlation between the Nasr et al.’s (1990) manual assessment method (NOF) and our novel methods of assessment using geometric shapes and density estimates. The rectangular (NOF_r) and oval (NOF_o) shape estimation methods closely correlated with the NOF assessment (r = 0.78–0.833, P-value < 2.2e-16) and provided additional rigor when using the software through the marked identification of both the primary cluster and its density. These drawing methods may lend additional methodological structure to an auditor’s assessment of the frame-top bee cluster, increasing rigor and repeatability.
The top-of-the-frame, line-based drawing method, NOF_l provides a similar correlation with the NOF assessment (r = 0.816, P-value < 2.2e-16) but provided improved correlation with both the NOF_r and NOF_o methods (r = 0.889–0.898, P-value < 2.2e-16) despite the lack of bee density assessment. This suggests that a structured assessment process using drawing methods increases the rigor (and therefore repeatability) of frame-top bee cluster assessment.
Our top-of-box modification to Chabert et al.’s (2021) method showed a significantly lower correlation with both NOF assessment and our novel shape assessments (r = 0.303–0.56, P-value < 2.2e-16), suggesting that the method is either not readily transferable to the hive top (and must be enacted exactly as described in their method) or is not an accurate assessment method compared with the other assessment types. However, Chalbert et al.’s (2021) method may be flawed in its application when used with fixed bottom hives commonly found in both Australian and American contexts without significant in-field modification of the hive, such as dismantling the fixed bottom brood box prior to assessment, rendering the method less practical in the field.
Nasr et al.’s (1990) assessment of cluster size remains the fastest method of assessing the population of a beehive; however, measurement is subjective, is not reassessable, and requires training and experience to ensure accuracy (Delaplane et al. 2013, Somerville and Frost 2018). Assessment repeatability and spot checking become possible when using a photographic audit method, and this may provide a degree of surety to orchardists when hiring hives for a pollination event (Goodrich 2017). Our software provides for linking the assessment score to the image providing additional rigor through the ability to verify, repeat, and review the measurement using multiple assessors and may be a simple, more cost-effective solution when compared with having multiple auditors on site during inspections (Delaplane et al. 2013). Further cost and time efficiencies may be made by having less experienced in-field assessors who only photograph the frame-tops within the hive (taking an estimated 20–30 s per hive) and then transfer the images to qualified or experienced auditors elsewhere for assessment. This workflow presents the opportunity for innovation by using image-based machine learning systems to assess images (with human-expert verification) to allow far greater numbers of images to be audited in shorter time periods, and a larger, nonexpert team of auditors in the field, further reducing auditing costs.
Shape-based cluster analysis provides further rigor when shape data are retained with the photographic data for later review, but reduces assessment speed due to the need for additional drawing operations. Auditors reported that rectangular (NOF_r) and oval (NOF_o) shape-based cluster analysis becomes difficult when the cluster breaks from a single solid mass to a more scattered arrangement. Density settings become critical during cluster breakup to estimate the number of bees; however, this becomes a little better than the Nasr et al.’s (1990) method due to subjective density assessment. Density variation over the frame-tops can make estimation complex or unreliable, introducing additional subjectivity. Shape-based assessment provides the opportunity for further downstream analysis such as cluster location within the hive, which may hold clues for brood area location and size, and therefore queen fecundity.
Provision of the between-method regression equations in Table 3 and Equation (1) allows the cross-conversion of data to and from industry “standard” Liebefeld measures and any of our novel methods. Integration of these regression equations into the software allows for a rapid comparison of hive strengths to industry standards, removing uncertainty regarding the strength metric in use and highlighting the need for explicit method reporting. Further to this, the software opens the assessment of hives to growers and nonexperts and allows for an independent audit of colony strength from the primary photographic data source.
While this software does not completely address the population assessment challenges inherent in apicultural industry methodologies, it does offer a systematic approach to standardizing population assessment methodologies in bee research. The work presents an open-source platform (licensed under Creative Commons CC BY-SA 4.0) that allows researchers and industry to enhance their assessment methods through the use of or modification and expansion of the software platform, thus providing prospects for further methodological development or additional features to provide rigor and repeatability. The software is provided at https://github.com/illuminateddan/Cluster-Count-Audit-Software/.
