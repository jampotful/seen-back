Olfaction is essential for nest recognition in solitary Hymenoptera
Sylvie P. Vandenabeele, Thomas Schmitt (2023). Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/10288589. doi:10.1073/pnas.2304703120. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Olfaction is essential for nest recognition in solitary Hymenoptera

## Abstract
AbstractFlying insects are believed to rely primarily on visual cues for orientation, with chemical cues often being overlooked. In the case of solitary bees and wasps, being able to return successfully to their nests and provision their brood cells is paramount for the survival of the species. While vision has been shown to be involved in pinpointing the nest location, our results confirm that olfaction is important in nest recognition. The large diversity in nesting strategies observed among solitary Hymenoptera makes them an excellent model to comparatively study the use of olfactory cues from the nesting individual for nest recognition. We have analyzed the chemical profiles of three nesting bees (Osmia spp.) and one wasp (Sceliphron curvatum) and that of their nest entrances. A striking match in the identified chemicals was revealed between each nest and its occupant. When the chemicals were removed from the nest, a clear behavioral response could be observed for Osmia cornuta. This shows the importance of olfactory cues in complementing visual orientation for precise homing in a solitary species, thereby opening up various promising biological questions in the fields of sensory perception and complementation, or the trade-offs of nest aggregation and associated costs.Keywords: Osmia, nest recognition, olfactory cues, cuticular hydrocarbons, nesting behavior
To be able to navigate the environment and localize key places in space is essential for successful foraging, mating, or nesting in insects, with vision often being at the basis of such navigational abilities (1). One fascinating phenomenon observed in solitary species that form nesting aggregations is the ability of females to return to their nest even when it is located among hundreds of other similar-looking nest entrances (e.g., ref. 2). As discussed in other studies (e.g., refs. 3 and 4) and despite some controversy (e.g., ref. 5), we provide evidence that olfaction is involved in nest homing and probably complements vision in achieving precise nest recognition.

## Results
The chemical profile analysis of each nest type showed a strong overlap in hydrocarbons with that from the cuticle of the corresponding nesting bee or wasp (Fig. 1). Several hydrocarbon compounds could be identified from both sources including alkanes, alkenes, and methyl-branched alkanes (Table 1). These findings are in congruence with data from a recent study on Osmia bees (4).

The behavioral observations revealed that in normal conditions (i.e., undisturbed nest), Osmia cornuta females often made a direct and fast return to their nest (mean duration from approach to entry ± SD = 3.9 ± 1.8 s). In contrast, once olfactory cues had been removed (i.e., odorless nest entrance), the bees appeared reluctant to enter their nest (mean duration from approach to entry ± SD = 620 ± 503.3 s). In most cases, they landed near their nest, inspected the entrance with their antenna, sometimes even inspected other nests around their own nest, and then left the nesting area again (movies S1–S4). When the bee finally entered the nest once again after treatment, the behavior returned to normal. However, in one case, the female abandoned its nest.

## Discussion
The removal of hydrocarbons present at the nest entrance disrupted the nest-homing behavior of Osmia cornuta females. Although the females were able to return to the vicinity of their nests, most likely using visual cues for navigation, they did not enter their nest as before nest odor manipulation. Instead, their reentry was delayed, with one bee even abandoning its nest. This clearly indicates the strong potential for hydrocarbons present at the nest entrance to be used as olfactory cues for precise and fast nest recognition, at least within short range distance. It therefore supports the idea that olfaction could complement vision and be at the basis of a multimodal sensory system used by some solitary species for nest homing (e.g., refs. 3 and 6).
These findings actually open up to a range of biological and evolutionary questions. For example, do all solitary species rely to the same extent on olfactory cues for nest recognition? Does the nesting strategy have an influence? If olfactory cues may help nesting females to return efficiently to their nests, may it not also be used by antagonists to localize their hosts (e.g., ref. 7)? Our approach of combining comparative chemical ecology studies with behavioral assays is a good starting point to elucidate such questions and should encourage future studies to be undertaken particularly on solitary Hymenoptera given their diversity in species and nesting strategies.

## Materials and Methods

## Chemical Analyses.
For this study, samples of the nest entrance from four nest types (mud nest, trap-nest, cylinder nest, and vase-cell nest) were collected as well as the individuals occupying these nests which included three solitary bees (two Osmia cornuta and one Osmia rufa) and one mud-dauber wasp (Sceliphron curvatum). The sample collection took place in Würzburg (Germany), Hofstetten-Flüh (Switzerland), and Kaiserstuhl (Germany). The chemical profiles of these nest samples and nesting individuals (whole bodies) were determined by gas chromatography/mass spectrometry. Hydrocarbons could be identified based on diagnostic ions and retention times.

## Behavioral Experiment.
Behavioral observations were performed before and after removing hydrocarbons present at the nest entrance. First, the behavior of Osmia cornuta bees (n = 4) returning to their nests before nest manipulation was recorded (control). Then, the nest entrances were wiped with hexane (manipulation) and further observations were made. The duration between the time when each bee approached its nest and when it finally entered inside was calculated.
