# The life history of recessive deleterious alleles as seen through the eyes of a honey bee (Apis mellifera)

## Abstract
AbstractDeleterious mutations arise continually in natural populations, yet their full life histories—from origin to elimination—are rarely observed directly. Our understanding of mutational fate in the wild comes from indirect inference rather than from tracking a single mutation through time and space. Haplodiploid systems provide a rare opportunity to observe negative selection in action: because males are haploid, recessive alleles are immediately exposed to selection rather than masked by heterozygosity. Haplodiploidy has long been hypothesized to accelerate the purging of deleterious variation. Here, we leverage the honey bee as a model system to document the complete trajectory of a spontaneous deleterious mutation, from its origin in a single queen to its extinction. The mutation alters eye pigmentation in haploid males, enabling us to quantify its molecular, physiological, and behavioral consequences through integrated genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses. By following this mutation across generations, we directly observe how haplodiploidy exposes recessive alleles to immediate purifying selection and how mating structure and colony demography influence their persistence. This study represents one of the few documented cases of a naturally occurring mutation tracked through its full evolutionary course, offering a rare empirical view of how selection operates in real time within a social, haplodiploid genome.Keywords: eye mutation, cinnabar, Hymenoptera, genome-wide association study, purifying selection, multiomic

## Introduction
Spontaneous mutation, while acting as the source of all genetic diversity, relies on the subsequent actions of genetic drift, selection, and gene flow to impact a population (Kimura 1968; Whitlock 2000; Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007; Charlesworth 2009). Because most newly arising mutations that alter the protein-coding sequence are effectively neutral or slightly deleterious (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007), their persistence or removal is determined by the interplay among environmental context, population dynamics, individual fitness effects, and the genomic landscape in which they occur (Kondrashov and Crow 1991; Charlesworth 2009). When their carriers can be identified, deleterious mutations offer a window into the functional importance of the genomic regions they disrupt. However, such opportunities are typically confined to tractable model systems (Baird et al. 1988; Kerem et al. 1989; Narod and Foulkes 2004; Häfliger et al. 2020; Winkler et al. 2020).
Ever since Hamilton (1967), it has been hypothesized that haplodiploid populations are particularly susceptible to the impacts of deleterious mutations. Diploids carry two copies of each gene locus, allowing expression of deleterious recessive mutations to be masked by the wild-type copy (Kondrashov and Crow 1991). This benefit is not afforded to haploids, which can only express their single copy. The deleterious mutation hypothesis claims haplodiploid populations have a greater efficiency in purging (and fixing) deleterious (and beneficial) recessive mutations (Hamilton 1967; Goldstein 1994; Richerd et al. 1994; Smith 2000). Breeding experiments support this hypothesis where haplodiploid populations experience less inbreeding depression compared with diplo-diploid populations (Henter 2003).
Despite theoretical expectations and indirect experimental support (e.g. fewer per-chromosome deleterious variants in haploid males [Miller and Sheehan 2023]) for the deleterious mutation hypothesis, there are few documented cases in which the full life history of a deleterious mutation (emergence, phenotypic expression, transmission, and eventual fate) has been observed in a natural population. In diplo-diploid organisms, recessive deleterious mutations are masked in heterozygotes, rendering them phenotypically cryptic and difficult to track without controlled crosses, long-term pedigree data, or genomic sequencing. These limitations have constrained most empirical tests of the deleterious mutation hypothesis to statistical inferences rather than direct observation.
Honey bees are a powerful study system to overcome these challenges and to document the role of deleterious mutations (Winston 1991; Kennedy 2020; Shultz et al. 2026). First, while causal mutations are rare to identify in nature, largely due to the aforementioned deleterious effects, which can be difficult to detect without an easily identifiable phenotype—the large number of closely monitored honey bee colonies in the United States makes identification tangible. In 2025, the United States Department of Agriculture recorded 2.63 million colonies kept by beekeeping operations with at least five colonies (NASS 2025). There is ample opportunity for scientists to collaborate with beekeepers enthusiastic about identifying phenotypic anomalies. Second, the haplodiploid system is streamlined for mutation detection; drones must express any maternally inherited mutations because they are hemizygous. Drones arise from unfertilized eggs, only acquiring their maternal chromosomes, while queens and workers arise from fertilized eggs (Winston 1991). Haplodiploidy may benefit future colonies through the purging effect supported by the deleterious mutation hypothesis (Goldstein 1994). Additionally, honey bees participate in a unique mating scheme: honey bee queens are hyperpolyandrous and their unfertilized eggs develop parthenogenetically into drones, producing offspring from eggs randomly fertilized by one of the ∼12 males with which the queen mates (Tarpy et al. 2004). It is with this system that we can better understand the lifespan of recessive mutations in nature.
In this study, we tracked the life history of a deleterious recessive eye mutation in honey bees using a multiomic approach. We identified an eye color mutation that only manifests in a subset of sons from a mutant queen and analyzed its genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic profiles, as well as physiological and fitness outcomes. While the first honey bee genetic map (using mutant phenotypes) was constructed in the 1960s (Rothenbuhler and Kerr 1968), eye mutations in honey bees have been sparsely studied. It remains unclear whether such mutations result in blindness or impaired vision (Witherelli 1972; Gribakin 1988). This research provides new insights into the biological and behavioral consequences of eye mutations in honey bees, specifically the potential fitness consequences for drones, which rely substantially, though not exclusively, on vision to locate queens during mating flights. By collaborating with beekeepers across the country, we identified colonies with rare mutant phenotypes and tracked a mutation from its identification to its eventual disappearance with the death of the mother queen. Our findings shed light on the dynamics of recessive mutations in natural populations and offer valuable insights into the processes of mutation, selection, and gene flow in a haplodiploid species. This case study highlights the importance of multiomic approaches in studying the fate of genetic mutations and provides a compelling example of how purifying selection can shape the genetic structure of a population over time and document the impact of a single deleterious mutation on eye color pigmentation.

## Results and discussion

## Emergence ratios and morphology of eye color variants
Following a community science program (see Materials and Methods) in 2021, we received a wild-type queen from a beekeeper in Georgia state (B.T. with assistance from J.M.) whose haploid drones were either wild type or exhibited an eye pigmentation variation which we call ivory, following Dustmann (1968) (Fig. 1a). The queen's diploid daughters also had wild-type eyes. We hypothesized that ivory was the result of a single recessive mutation carried by the queen. To test this, we tracked the emergence rate of drones by prompting the queen to produce unfertilized eggs during the beekeeping seasons of 2022 and 2023 (see Materials and Methods). At emergence, each phenotype was counted and recorded. Ivory and wild-type drones did not emerge at a 1:1 ratio (Fisher's exact test P = 0.03) as predicted, but nearer to a 1:3 ratio (Fig. 1b).

Ivory mutants do not emerge at a 1:1 ratio with their wild-type brothers, as assessed across three trials (Fisher's exact test; P < 0.05). c) Box plots visualizing estimates of ten reproductive (mucus gland length, seminal vesicle length, sperm count, live sperm count, dead sperm count, sperm viability) and morphological (body mass, head width, thorax mass, thorax width) traits between ivory and wild-type brothers (ANOVA; P > 0.05 for all comparisons). Raw data points represent a single individual.
This result suggests (i) the phenotype is driven by a single allele that is partially lethal or incompletely penetrant, (ii) more than a single gene is underpinning the ivory phenotype, or (iii) wild-type workers are laying eggs which develop into drones and confound our results. While we observed minimal mortality during development and there were no larvae of different developmental stages intermixed with emerging drones, we cannot exclude the potential for larval nor embryonic lethality. Our genomic sequencing data do not support the hypothesis that worker-laid drones are contributing to the skew in emergence ratio, given that wild-type drones are no less related to each other than ivory (Fig. S1). We discuss this and the remaining possibilities of incomplete penetrance and multiple alleles in subsequent sections.
To fully characterize the phenotypic effects in ivory mutants, we measured traits beyond eye pigmentation. Eye mutants in Drosophila have been documented to have additional phenotypic differences from wild type (e.g. mutants dor [Merrell 1947] and white [Xiao et al. 2017]). We measured ten traits associated with body size and reproduction in ivory mutants and their wild-type brothers of the same approximate age. We found no statistical differences in any measured morphological or reproductive phenotype between ivory and wild-type drones for univariate approaches (P > 0.05 for all comparisons; see Materials and Methods; Fig. 1c). We also found a high degree of similarity between the two morphological character matrices (Rho = 0.76; Z = 3.57; P = 0.001; see Materials and Methods).

## Ivory drones do not learn the location of their natal colony
Drones begin orientation flights as early as five days after emergence, taking short trips around their natal colony to learn its location (Winston 1991; Reyes et al. 2019). By eight days old, ∼50% of males will have completed orienting to their natal colony, and most will have done so within ten days (Reyes et al. 2019). After sexual maturity near day 13, drones begin to make multiple mating flights each day until they successfully mate (and die after copulation) or die from other causes. We found that ivory and wild-type drones exit the colony at relatively similar times and rates (but fewer absolute numbers of ivory), with most flights occurring after 1:30 PM EST each day (Fig. 2a). We found no marked ivory drones returning to nor leaving the colony at or beyond nine days postemergence (Fig. 2b).

The absence of the ivory phenotype on day nine is likely not the result of drones drifting into nearby colonies, a common occurrence for drones (Neumann et al. 2000; Reyes et al. 2019). We observed only a single ivory drone in a nearby colony in our yard during regular colony inspections over the 2023 season. We also repeated the above experiment in a host colony set 150 m away from other accessible colonies to limit drift (Currie and Jay 1991). We introduced 51 ivory and 192 wild-type newly emerged and marked drones into the isolated host colony. We returned ten days later, allowing them time to orient, and collected all remaining marked drones from within the colony. We found that only 1/51 ivory versus 83/192 wild-type drones remained (Fisher's exact test P < 0.001; Fig. 2c).
We hypothesized that ivory drones cannot learn the location of their colony and therefore do not return during their first orientation flights. To test this, we gathered eight day old ivory and wild-type drones (Reyes et al. 2019) from inside their natal nest (see Materials and Methods). We placed marked drones on a platform in an open field 175 m from their natal nest, fed them diluted honey ad libitum (similar to Menzel et al. 2005), and allowed them to feed or fly for 30 min while another researcher concomitantly recorded the number and phenotype of drones that returned to the entrance of their natal colony (Fig. 2d). Following 30 min of collection, all but two drones (both ivory) flew from the platform. At their natal colony, 32% of the wild-type drones returned, and no ivory returned (Fisher's exact test P = 0.0003; Fig. 2d).

## Ivory drones respond incorrectly to light
We hypothesized that ivory drones have impaired vision due to a lack of pigment (Witherelli 1972; Gribakin 1988; Ferreiro et al. 2018). Accordingly, we predicted that ivory drones would respond incorrectly to light compared with wild-type drones. To test this prediction, we performed a simple phototactic assay on ivory and wild-type drones, both with and without their ocelli occluded (Fig. 3a). Honey bees use their ocelli to detect photic stimuli and support navigation under sunlight (Frisch 1986; Kastberger 1990). Occluding the ocelli therefore provides a baseline for an impaired phototactic response. No drones with occluded ocelli were phototactic, and significantly more nonoccluded wild-type drones were phototactic than nonoccluded ivory (Fisher's exact test P = 0.009; Fig. 3b).
Ivory and wild-type light response. a) Graphic representing a simple phototaxis assay: placing wild-type and ivory drones with and without occluded ocelli on a small platform in the center of a dark room and recording their light response over five minutes. b) Bar plots counting the number of drones of each phenotype and their ocelli status, as well as their light response.
Taken together, our navigation and light response data indicate that ivory drones suffer vision impairment. Honey bees use visual cues during orientation flights to improve homing, to identify landmarks during navigation, and to orient themselves in the air (Gries and Koeniger 1996; Capaldi and Dyer 1999). Drones specifically use their eyesight in addition to pheromone signals to navigate during mating flights (Gries and Koeniger 1996; Slater et al. 2021; Otis 2026). We find that ivory drones are unable to learn the location of their natal colony and “disappear” during orientation.

## Genome-wide association study identifies a single peak on chromosome 4 associated with ivory
We next mapped the location, identity, and function of the ivory-causing allele. To identify the mutation(s) underlying ivory, we implemented two independent genomic mapping methods. We first performed a Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) using skim-seq data for 50 ivory and 50 wild-type individuals following previously developed methods (Shultz et al. 2026). We identified a single significant peak (−log10(P)>8) corresponding to a 3-Mb (136 to 2,999 kb) region near the centromere of the acrocentric chromosome 4 that was associated with ivory (Fig. 4a; Table S1).

To map the associated region more finely, we performed pool-seq on pools of ivory and wild-type drones (N = 60 in each pool). We aligned to new long-read builds of ivory and wild-type genomes (see Materials and Methods; Shultz et al. 2026). The most highly differentiated region was, again, the 3-Mb region of chromosome 4 (Fig. 4b). This region contains 65 protein-coding and 75 total genes (Table S2). We found no fixed single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the region (FSTmax = 0.81); however, we found a single fixed indel in drones with ivory eyes. The indel is absent in wild-type drones, from the genomes of wild-type honey bees in two previous large genomic databases (Harpur et al. 2014; Dogantzis et al. 2021), and from wild-type drones in two previous genomic studies (Shultz et al. 2024, 2026). Ivory is also independent of a white-eye-causing mutation recently discovered on chromosome 11 (Shultz et al. 2026). We can conclude that ivory is not likely to be incompletely penetrant and is exceptionally rare (allele frequency <0.001), perhaps found only in the focal colony of this study.

## Two paths to ivory: a frameshift mutation in chromosome 4 and incorrect processing of cinnabar RNA
The indel associated with ivory is 12 nucleotides long, replacing an 11-nucleotide sequence in reference and wild type, and is in the exon of a protein-coding gene (LOC551858; cinnabar; kynurenine 3-monooxygenase; Fig. 4c). It has predicted effects on both the transcript and the protein. Firstly, it changes the predicted RNA-binding protein sites, three of which (maca, sans-fille (SNF), and SNRNP70K) contribute to or are inferred to contribute to RNA splicing in Drosophila (Park et al. 2004; Hu et al. 2009; Zhu and Fukunaga 2021) (Fig. 4c). Secondly, it causes a premature stop codon during translation (Fig. 4c).
We explored the impact of the ivory-causing mutation at the transcript level using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). We predicted above that the ivory-causing allele may influence splicing and create a premature termination codon. To test each, we designed primers targeting the end of the gene encoding kynurenine 3-monooxygenase as well as two primers spanning intronic regions (Fig. 5a). Kynurenine 3-monooxygenase transcripts are expressed in the eyes of wild-type and ivory drones but significantly less so in the ivory drones (Fig. 5b; ANOVA; F1,13 = 49.12, P < 0.001). We also find evidence that the transcripts of kynurenine 3-monooxygenase may be mis-spliced in ivory. There is a greater proportion of intron-retaining transcripts in ivory eyes relative to wild type: we estimated the percent intron retention (see Materials and Methods) at intron 3 and found 13.2% intron retention in the ivory phenotype to 1.4% in wild-type eye samples. In intron 5, ivory-eyed samples had 13.5% intron retention compared with 4.4% in wild-type samples (Fig. 5c). Intron retention can result when mutations occur at splice junctions (Monteuuis et al. 2019). At the transcript level, the ivory-causing allele impacts the transcript population by both mis-splicing and by reducing expression.

Our transcript-level results and the predicted effects of the ivory allele on translation (Fig. 4) suggest that the kynurenine 3-monooxygenase protein is also downregulated and may be absent in ivory drone eyes. We tested this prediction using data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry. There are 18 proteins upregulated in ivory-eyed drones compared with wild type (Fig. 5d; Table S3; −log10(P) >8). None of their coding regions can be found in the associated region on chromosome 4. Of the 18 proteins upregulated in ivory, three cluster into Gene Ontology (GO) groups significantly associated with the GO:0030431-sleep biological process and IPR050975-sleep regulator (Table S4). This includes XP_006566259.1 (CG7781), XP_003250814.1, and XP_006570001.1 (CG17218; crooked). We also found that kynurenine 3-monooxygenase is the only protein that is upregulated in wild-type eyes and completely absent in ivory (Fig. 5d and 5e). Kynurenine 3-monooxygenase is likely downregulated through nonsense-mediated decay due to the actions of mis-splicing and the introduction of a premature termination codon (Aoufouchi et al. 1996; Chang et al. 2007; Andrade et al. 2021).

## Kynurenine 3-monooxygenase is upstream of pigment production
Kynurenine 3-monooxygenase is essential to eye pigment production in Drosophila and other insects (Ryall and Howells 1974; Lorenzen et al. 2002; Xu et al. 2020). It converts kynurenine into 3-hydroxykynurenine upstream of the production of ommochrome (Fig. 6). If kynurenine 3-monooxygenase protein is downregulated, we would then expect that kynurenine would be elevated and 3-hydroxykynurenine would be reduced in the eyes of ivory-eyed drones relative to wild type. Using Liquid Chromatography-High Resolution Mass Spectrometry (LC-HRMS/MS) in an untargeted metabolomics analysis, we quantified the relative abundances of metabolites in the ommochrome synthesis pathway. We found that kynurenine is significantly elevated (analysis of variance [ANOVA] P < 0.001; see Materials and Methods) and 3-hydroxykynurenine is significantly depleted (ANOVA P < 0.001; see Materials and Methods) in the eyes of ivory-eyed drones relative to wild-type. Additionally, there are significant differences in all downstream metabolites in ivory-eyed drones relative to wild type (Fig. 6; P < 0.05; see Materials and Methods). Our results are consistent with a previous metabolite study by Dustmann (1968), who found that drones with white eyes had excess levels of kynurenine and depleted levels of 3-hydroxykynurenine. Dustmann (1968) referred to these drones as “ivory mutants” and we adopted that usage here.

Knockout of cinnabar (kynurenine 3-monooxygenase) in Drosophila melanogaster produces a characteristic red-eye phenotype. This phenotype arises because Drosophila synthesizes and deposits red pteridine pigments (drosopterins) in eye pigment cells alongside ommochromes (Ziegler and Harmsen 1970; Kim et al. 2013). In contrast, honey bees also express pteridines in their eye tissue, but these pigments do not contribute to external eye coloration (Dustmann 1968). Consequently, disruption of ommochrome synthesis in Drosophila, including mutation or knockout of cinnabar, results in red eyes (Clausen 1924; Paton and Sullivan 1978) but ivory in the honey bee.
Previous work in Drosophila has also shown that cinnabar (cn) mutants can exhibit partial lethality. Martelli et al. (2024) reported ∼30% mortality from egg to adult in homozygous cn (cn +/+) individuals, compared with ∼8% mortality in heterozygotes (cn +/−). In our experiment, drone emergence occurred at approximately a 1:3 ratio. This pattern is consistent with ∼66% early mortality of ivory hemizygotes within the first 24 h after fertilization, followed by removal of nonviable eggs by workers and subsequent replacement oviposition by the queen. Such early mortality would create a survivorship bias in the observed emergence ratio.
Several studies have examined whether the accumulation of upstream metabolites in the ommochrome pathway contributes to neurotoxicity in Drosophila eye color mutants. However, these investigations did not detect increased mortality in cinnabar mutants exposed to kynurenic acid. Further targeted studies of specific intermediates in the ommochrome biosynthesis pathway will be required to determine whether metabolite buildup contributes to early developmental lethality in Hymenopterans.

## Conclusion
By following a single, naturally occurring deleterious mutation from its origin in a queen to its extinction with her death and the death of her carrier sons, this study provides a rare, empirical view of purifying selection unfolding in real time. Such complete life history documentation of a spontaneous mutation is exceptionally uncommon in natural populations, particularly outside of classical model organisms. More broadly, this case study provides empirical support for the long-standing deleterious mutation hypothesis in haplodiploid systems. Rather than relying on population-level statistics or inbreeding depression assays, we document the precise route by which a recessive mutation is exposed, expressed, and removed.
The ivory mutation illustrates how haplodiploidy accelerates purifying selection by immediately exposing recessive alleles to selection in males. Although the mutation produces no detectable effects on morphology or reproductive anatomy, it severely compromises vision, disrupting orientation, homing, and ultimately mating opportunity. Selection acts on behavioral exclusion from mating opportunity. We suggest that the mutation may be early-lethal. This finding shows that deleterious fitness effects can arise from subtle sensory impairments rather than overt developmental defects and that these effects may only be visible when behavior is closely examined.
At the molecular level, we show that a single frameshifting indel in cinnabar (kynurenine 3-monooxygenase) disrupts eye pigmentation through mis-splicing, reduced transcript abundance, loss of protein expression, and downstream metabolic collapse of the ommochrome pathway. The convergence of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic evidence provides a mechanistic link from genotype to phenotype to fitness consequence. Notably, cinnabar underlies eye color variation across insect orders underscoring the deep conservation of pigmentation pathways across the class. Mutations in cinnabar have been repeatedly implicated in eye and body pigmentation (e.g. studies in wing and cuticular pigmentation of Lepidopterans and Hemipterans [Reed and Nagy 2005; Berni et al. 2022]) across diverse taxa, highlighting the strong evolutionary constraint and predictability of pigmentation pathways. For example, mutant cinnabar individuals in the western tarnished plant bug (Lygus hesperus), the brown planthopper (Nilaparvata lugens), and D. melanogaster are associated with a red-eye phenotype (Paton and Sullivan 1978; Xue et al. 2018; Heu et al. 2022), while mutant kmo individuals in the silkworm (Bombyx mori) are characterized by a white eye phenotype (Quan et al. 2002). Interestingly, eye color phenotypes in the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella) depend on the type of mutation induced in the kmo ortholog, Pxkmo (Xu et al. 2020). The recurrence of this gene as a mutation target suggests that relatively few loci underlie major shifts in pigment production, likely due to the central position of cinnabar in the ommochrome biosynthesis pathway. The consistent identification and subsequent studies on cinnabar allude to its capacity to generate visible phenotypic effects with limited pleiotropic consequences.
Finally, our findings highlight the value of community science partnerships for identifying rare genetic variants. As managed honey bee populations continue to expand, and genomic tools become more accessible, similar efforts will enable the discovery and tracking of additional spontaneous mutations. This will provide a richer empirical foundation for understanding how mutation and selection operate in real populations, and how social and haplodiploid genomes shape evolutionary outcomes.

## Materials and methods

## Identification of a mutant phenotype
During the summers of 2021 and 2022, we sought mutant-producing honey bee colonies through a community science effort. We were contacted by beekeepers located in Georgia (B.T. and J.M.), Kentucky, and Minnesota who identified mutant-producing wild-type queens in their apiaries. The Minnesotan queen did not survive transportation, and the Kentucky queen was maintained by its owner, who performed other in situ experiments in collaboration with us (Shultz et al. 2026). The Georgian queen was paint-marked and introduced into a single-deep colony in the Purdue Research Apiary and was maintained at our facility until her death in 2023.

## Emergence ratios and drone collection
The Georgian colony was headed by a wild-type queen who produced wild-type daughters, a proportion of wild-type sons, and a proportion of sons with an ivory-colored mutant-eye phenotype. We refer to the mutant phenotype throughout the text as ivory. There was no strong observable difference within the ivory nor wild-type color patterns within each category. We presumed that the queen was heterozygous for the mutation leading to the ivory eye phenotype and that it was a single, recessive mutation underlying the phenotype.
To empirically evaluate this mutation, we compelled the queen to lay drones by caging her on a comb and determined the emergence ratios of the wild-type:ivory phenotypes. We used queen-excluder cages to isolate the mutant-producing queen onto drone foundation (frame with large-sized cells) to produce drones during the beekeeping seasons of 2022 and 2023. Each time, the queen was left for 24 h on the comb to lay eggs. After 24 h, the queen was freed into the hive body, and the drone foundation remained caged to prevent the queen from laying additional eggs. We monitored the frame for excess larval mortality regularly. On the day of emergence, we counted and recorded the phenotype of each drone. All drones from each frame emerged within a 4-h period of one another, and we did not observe exaggerated mortality in any phenotype during any stage of larval development. Subsets of drones reared via this method were used for all experiments and methods listed below. Where necessary, newly emerged drones were painted-marked on their thoraces and returned to their natal (or in some cases, host) colony, providing us with a robust method of tracking their emergence age (adult day 1) and colony of origin.

## Morphological and reproductive assessment
To test if wild-type and ivory drones varied in any reproductive or morphological traits beyond eye color, we measured other physical quantities for each drone phenotype. Ivory and wild-type brothers (N ≈ 60 of each phenotype) were collected from their natal colony and shipped from Purdue University to the North Carolina State Queen & Disease Clinic by the United Parcel Service overnight in two separate plastic cages containing 50% sucrose (w/v) absorbed into a cloth wick, sugar fondant (powdered sugar and corn syrup), a TempQueen (Mann Lake) QMP lure, and ∼300 attendant workers per cage. Each cage was shipped with a thermal sensor (iButton collecting at 15-min intervals) to monitor temperature conditions during shipment, since it is known that extreme temperatures can affect sperm viability (McAfee et al. 2025). Upon arrival, the drones were separated from workers and kept at 30 °C with a 50% sucrose-soaked cotton ball for up to 3 h while dissections were performed.
To measure the morphological and reproductive traits of reproductively mature drones, individuals were dissected and measured following the methods of Metz and Tarpy (2019). Only drones determined to be reproductively mature were retained for subsequent analyses (N = 40 ivory; N = 32 wild type). In brief, drones were weighed whole, and their thoraces, legs, and wings were weighed together. Photographs were taken of the head and thorax, as well as the dissected seminal vesicles and mucus glands. We then immediately measured each drone's body mass (mg), head width (mm), thorax width (mm), mean seminal vesicle length (mm), mean mucus gland length (mm), total sperm count, and sperm viability. The seminal vesicles were placed into 1.0 mL of buffer (Metz and Tarpy 2019) and homogenized with a pair of forceps. Spermatozoa were incubated in 1:100 diluted SYBR-14 and propidium iodide (PI) (Invitrogen Live/Dead Sperm Viability Kit, Thermo Fisher) for a minimum of 5 min and a maximum of 2 h. Spermatozoa were labeled live (SYBR-14) or dead (PI) and imaged for counts of total sperm stored in the seminal vesicles and sperm viability (live sperm count/total sperm count) using a fluorescent cell count (Nexcelom). Photographs were analyzed using ImageJ (Schneider et al. 2012) to measure body parts.
We performed univariate analyses and multiple-comparison tests to compare the characters of interest (above) across phenotypes. Total sperm count was transformed by natural log and sperm viability was transformed by the arcsine of the square root of the proportion for the analyses, but not for visualization. Univariate comparisons of each character of interest were performed using ANOVA (Sokal and Rohlf 1995). In all correlation calculations, Spearman's ranked correlations were used (Sokal and Rohlf 1995) and matrices were compared using the Mantel test (Sokal and Rohlf 1995). Individual correlation constants were compared between phenotypes using an arcsin-transformed paired t-test for unequal sample sizes as described by Levy (1977) and implemented by Moore et al. (2019). Principal components were generated from covariance matrices of the loading variables (Bro and Smilde 2014) and assessed by multiple regression (Sokal and Rohlf 1995). Multiple comparisons (e.g. for the univariate measures and correlation constant comparisons) were evaluated for significance using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 10% (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Statistical comparisons of metabolite abundances between ivory and wild-type drones were performed using Progenesis QI (nonlinear dynamics), which applies a one-way ANOVA with post hoc pairwise t-tests on normalized ion abundances to identify significantly differentially abundant features between groups.

## Behavioral experiments: ivory mutation phenotyping and phototaxis
In May 2023, we performed a preliminary investigation into the flight behavior of ivory drones: we watched the ivory colony from 9 AM to 7 PM each day for a week documenting the number of drones leaving and entering the colony (Fig. 2a). To characterize the ivory eye phenotype, we age-marked drones from the ivory colony for use in three separate experiments. In the first assay, we observed the drone orientation flights from their natal colony (Fig. 2b). Drones make orientation flights at ∼5 to 7 days of age (Currie 1987). We monitored the time of day they began flying and the count of each phenotype leaving and returning to the colony each day on days 5, 6, and 9. We performed a second assay spanning day 1 through day 10. One-day-old drones were transferred to a host colony (N = 192 wild type; 51 ivory) 150 m away from any other colonies (Fig. 2c). After 10 d had passed, the number of each phenotype in the host colony was counted, and the proportion was calculated. Concomitantly with the second assay, we performed a third assay on day 8. For this assay, age-marked drones of each phenotype (N = 56 wild type; N = 39 ivory) were removed from their natal colony at 10:30 AM, prior to peak flight for matings (Currie 1987). Drones were marked with a second color to indicate their use in the assay and then transported 175 m from their natal colony to a platform (Fig. 2d). They were allowed to refuel on a honey:water mixture and then fly off. The number and phenotypes of drones returning to the natal colony were tracked for 30 min after leaving the platform.
To measure differential light response between phenotypes, we performed a phototaxis assay on brother drones. We collected 125 drones from their natal colony (N = 65 wild type; N = 61 ivory) and kept them in a cage with a honey:water mixture. We then randomly selected a proportion of each phenotype and occluded their ocelli with opaque paint (wild type N = 41 unmarked, N = 24 occluded; ivory N = 36 unmarked, N = 25 occluded). Occluding the ocelli served as a negative control (Kastberger 1990). We allowed the drones to acclimate, caged, on a small platform in a dark room. After a 5-min acclimation period, we opened the cage lid and turned on the track lighting above the cage. Over 5 min, drones were allowed to fly to the light source and were collected immediately upon response to light with their phenotype and ocelli status recorded (Fig. 3a). At the end of 5 min, drones still on the ground were collected and recorded. We attempted an additional small trial comprised of drones with both the ocelli and the compound eyes painted, but found they preferred to self-groom to remove the occlusion.

## Sample size and whole genome sequencing
To identify the genotype(s) underlying the ivory phenotype, we followed standards previously established by Shultz et al. (2026). This study established standards to determine sample size in a GWAS in haploid honey bee brothers. Sample size was calculated by determining the number of haploid individuals needing to be sequenced from each group (ivory and wild type), such that the only fixed mutational difference between them was the causal mutation (Shultz et al. 2026). In brief, DNA was extracted from individual (skim-seq) and pooled samples (by phenotype) and sent for sequencing. For individual sequencing, 50 ivory and 50 wild-type drones (100 total) were run pair-ended on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 to ∼5× depth by Gencove. Genome imputation was performed by Gencove using software adapted from Rubinacci et al. (2023), following methods by Li and Stephens (2003). For pooled sequencing, two groups of 60 ivory and 60 wild-type drone thoraces were ground to create a tissue homogenate. Extracted DNA was run with 150 bp pair-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 to 100× depth and a 350-bp insert size by Novogene (CA, USA). Pooled reads were aligned to Amel_HAv3.1 (Wallberg et al. 2019).

## Long-read sequencing
Extracted DNA from a whole drone (N = 1 individual ivory drone; 1 wild type) was sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II using 8M Single Molecule, Real-Time cells (PacBio sequencing; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Data were aligned with Burrows-Wheeler Aligner's Smith-Waterman Alignment (Li and Durbin 2010) to the most recent honey bee reference genome (Amel_HAv3.1). Minor faults in the assembly were resolved by comparing against short read wild-type data using Pilon v1.22 (Walker et al. 2014).

## Genome-wide association study
As described in Shultz et al. (2026), we performed a GWAS on both skim-seq and pool-seq output to identify a region associated with the ivory phenotype. For individual sequencing, samples were filtered through PLINK (Purcell et al. 2007) to include filtered bi-allelic SNPs. They were then passed to GCTA (Yang et al. 2011) to construct genomic relationship matrices and run a mixed-linear model association. A GWAS was repeated for the pool-seq sequences, measuring differentiation sites across the two phenotypic pools through POPOOLATION2 (Arnold et al. 2005; Kofler et al. 2011).

## Mutation scanning
We used Integrative Genomic Viewer (Robinson et al. 2011) to visually locate mutations fixed in ivory and absent in wild-type pooled and long-read sequences. Both sequencing types were also used to scan for segregating structural variation in addition to SNPs and indels.

## Identifying RNA-binding proteins
We used RBPMap to predict RNA Binding Protein (RBP) binding sites in the mutant and wild-type unprocessed transcripts (Paz et al. 2014). We used D. melanogaster motifs (Apr 2006 [BDGP R5/dm3]). We compared motifs in ivory and wild type at the target site: chromosome 4: 2,756,111 to 2,756,122 and took any significant motif (P < 0.05).

## RNA extractions and cDNA synthesis
To understand transcriptomic effects of the mutation, RNA was extracted from each phenotype, and the gene of interest was targeted for gel electrophoresis. The pairs of eyes (N = 5 wild type; N = 5 ivory) and individual thoraces (N = 5 wild type; N = 5 ivory) were homogenized in 1,500 μL TRI reagent (Zymo) using a micropestle. RNA was extracted from each sample (Zymo Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit) and eluted with 30 μL DEPC water. Samples were quantified on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen by Thermo Fisher) and then diluted to 10 ng/μL for qPCR and to 50 ng/μL for PCR. RNA was then converted into cDNA using a SensiFast cDNA synthesis kit (Thomas Scientific BIO-65053). Samples were run on a T100 Thermal Cycler according to the kit manufacturer's guidelines. cDNA was synthesized at 25 °C for 10 min, 42 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 min, and then held at 4 °C until moved to −20 °C for storage.

## Gene expression via ΔΔCt method
RNA samples were used to compare normalized expressions across two regions of the gene of interest (see Table S5), as well as for a housekeeping gene. eIF3-S8 is a commonly used housekeeping control gene used in honey bee assays (Grozinger et al. 2003; Whitfield et al. 2003; Richard et al. 2008). A standard 20 μL qPCR was run on a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR system. Four biological replicates for each phenotype (ivory and wild type) and two technical replicates were run across two 96-well plates. Samples were run at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, an annealing temperature of 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. A melt curve from 55 °C to 95 °C was employed to ensure nontemplate controls were not contaminated. Ivory and wild-type eyes and thoraces were analyzed via ΔΔCt method for gene expression in R (R version 4.2.2 through RStudio 2023) (R Core Team 2021). Relative gene expression data were processed and visualized using tidyverse (Wickham and Vaughan 2023), and reshape2 (Wickham 2007) packages.

## Intron retention analysis
RNA samples were used to determine the percentage of intron retained across two regions of the gene of interest (each region spanning two exons; see Table S5). Individual cDNA samples from a single ivory (N = 1 eye pair, N = 1 thorax) and a single wild-type sample (N = 1 eye pair, N = 1 thorax) were converted to PCR products (2X Phusion Flash; Thermo Fisher) in a standard 20 μL PCR reaction. A PCR reaction was run on a Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler at 98 °C for 10 s, followed by 36 cycles of 98 °C for 1 s, annealing at 60 °C for 5 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. The reaction was held at 72 °C for final extension for 1 min and then at 4 °C until pickup. Samples were then transferred to ice until they were run on a gel. Ten microliters of PCR product were run per reaction well on a 2.5% gel in TBE buffer for ∼1.5 h at 60 V. Samples were compared against a 50-bp DNA ladder (Thermo Fisher: SM0371). Samples were imaged on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ and analyzed with ImageJ for peak fluorescence of bands (Schneider et al. 2012). Bands in each run were compared for the amount of fluorescence following methods outlined by Stael et al. (2022).

## Proteomics
To test whether the mutation affected translation and protein formation, we sent 12 individuals for proteomic analysis (N = 6 wild type; 6 ivory). Eyes of each drone were dissected into a 2-mL screw cap DuraTube (UltiDent Scientific) for bead-beating homogenization using four 2.8 mm ceramic beads in 4% SDS in 100 mM Tris pH 8. Samples were homogenized with a Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin Technologies) for 30 s at 6,500 rpm in three cycles, with 30 s ice-down between each cycle. A portion of each sample was reduced and alkylated by heating to 95 °C for 5 min in 40 mM chloroacetamide and 10 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride and loaded onto 10% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Protein Gels (Bio-Rad). The whole lane was excised and performed in-gel digestion (PMID 16920818) with Trypsin-ultra (New England Biolabs), and resulting peptides were cleaned with C-18 STop And Go Extraction tips (PMID 12498253). Peptides were eluted with 40% (v/v) acetonitrile in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. Samples were evaporated using Vacufuge (Eppendorf) and the final dried peptides were resuspended in buffer A consisting of 0.5% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Peptide concentrations were estimated by UV absorbance at 205 nm using NanoDropOne (Thermo Scientific, A205 nm, scopes). Approximately 100 ng of peptides were loaded onto timsTOF Pro2 (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to NanoElute UHPLC (Bruker Daltonics) with Bruker's PepSep column (10 cm × 75 μm inner diameter, 1.9 μm particle size with 20 μm emitter) using a DIA-PASEF method as described in PMC10806398. The sample injection order was randomized.
Data were searched against a custom honey bee database and common protein contaminants using DIA-NN version 1.8.1 (PMID31768060). Library-free search mode was enabled, using trypsin protease specificity and one missed cleavage. Other search parameters include one maximum number of variable modification, N-terminal methionine excision enabled, and cysteine carbamidomethylation enabled as a fixed modification. Peptide length ranged 7 to 30, precursor charge ranged 1 to 4, precursor m/z ranged 200 to 1,800, and fragment m/z ranged 300 to 1,800. Precursor FDR was set to 1%.
The data were normalized across samples (assuming most proteins do not change across phenotypes) and statistical analyses were performed in Fragpipe-analyst (Hsiao et al. 2024). The target spectral library was quantified using an in-house script modified from the ProteinGroup.txt file from DIA-NN (Demichev et al. 2020). The label-free quantification (LFQ) data were filtered to remove contaminant proteins, reverse sequences, proteins identified solely by the posttranslational modification site, proteins identified by a single peptide, and inconsistently identified proteins within a phenotype. The LFQ data were log2 transformed, grouped into phenotypes, and missing data were imputed via the “Missing not At Random” method (random draws from a left-shifted Gaussian distribution of 1.8 SD with a width of 0.3). Differential expression analyses were conducted through protein-wise linear models and Empirical Bayes statistics. Pairwise comparisons were made across differentially expressed proteins (adjusted P-value of 0.001 Benjamini–Hochberg method and log2 fold change of 1) between ivory and wild type. The GO and functional annotation analysis compared proteins upregulated in ivory to a background of all honey bee proteins detected in the analysis using DAVID (Huang et al. 2009; Sherman et al. 2022).

## LC-HRMS/MS metabolomics
Liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry techniques (LC-HRMS/MS) were performed on wild-type versus ivory drones (N = 8 heads from each phenotype) in an untargeted metabolomics analysis. Following a protocol established by Matyash et al. (2008) with adjustments in brief, a two-phase extraction was performed on whole drone heads (McAfee et al. 2024). Samples were homogenized in a 2 mL homogenization tube preloaded with a mixture of 1.4 and 2.8 mm zirconium oxide beads, 300 μL of ice-cold methanol supplemented with 0.02% w/v butylated hydroxytoluene (to prevent oxidation/isomerization of reactive compounds), and 1 ppm of internal standards (L-methionine-methyl-d3, L-phenyl-d5-alanine, caffeine-(trimethyl-13C3), and ferulic acid; Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were homogenized with a Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, USA) for 20 s at 4,000 rpm in three cycles, with a 30 s ice-down between. Tissue homogenates were then transferred to 1.7 mL standard tubes and 750 μL of methyl tert-butyl ether was added. Samples were shaken for 10 min at 4 °C, 200 μL of LC-MS water was added, and phase separation was induced through a 10 min 10,000 rpm centrifugation period. The remaining polar fraction was dried in a speed vacuum and resuspended in 50% aqueous methanol. The suspension was centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 rpm, resulting in a supernatant that was transferred to LC-MS vials (Thermo Fisher) and used in LC-HRMS/MS analysis.
The supernatants from individual samples were analyzed for metabolome profiling using an Impact II high-resolution mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) and a Vanquish Horizon UHPLC System (Thermo Scientific). Chromatographic separation was induced in an Inertsil Ph-3 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 2 μm; GL Sciences, Torrance, CA, USA; injection volume 2 μL). A gradient elution was done with a mobile phase (solvent A: water containing 0.1% v/v formic acid; solvent B: methanol containing 0.1% v/v formic acid). Chromatography was conducted over an 18-min interval at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min: 1 min period with 5% B, then a linear increase from 5% to 35% B until minute 8, followed by an increase from 35% to 99% B until 10.5 min, then a holding period at 99% B until 14 min, and finally a return to 5% B until 18 min. As a quality control (QC) step, 20 μL aliquots from each sample were pooled into a single QC sample. This sample was injected every 5 samples to verify instrument performance.
To analyze metabolites, data-dependent acquisition was conducted to acquire precursor and fragment ion information using positive ionization (ESI+) mode. Scans on the mass spectrometer were performed with the following configurations: a spray voltage of 4,500 V, nebulizer pressure of 2.2 Bar, source temperature set at 220 °C, and a cycle time of 700 ms. For each MS/MS scan, the range was 65 to 1,400 m/z and a collision energy of 20 V from 100% to 250% was employed.
Raw data processing was performed using Progenesis QI software (V3.0.7600.27622) incorporating METLIN plugin V1.0.7642.33805 (NonLinear Dynamics, Newcastle Upon Tyne, United Kingdom) and entailed peak picking, peak alignment, all ions normalization, and searching against databases. Level 2 annotations were performed as previously described (McAfee et al. 2024). Kynurenine, 3-hydroxykynurenine, and tryptophan were successfully matched against experimental MS/MS fragmentation in METLIN. Formylkynurenine was matched against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (Fig. S2).
