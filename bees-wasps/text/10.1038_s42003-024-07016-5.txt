Knockout of a single Pax6 gene (toy but not ey) leads to compound eye deficiency and small head in honeybees
Xiaofen Hu, Fu-Ping Cheng, Zhi-Xian Gong, Kaixin Qin, Tingting Shan, Wenwen Li, Lizhen Zhang, Weiyu Yan, Zhi Jiang Zeng, Zilong Wang (2024). Communications Biology. https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-024-07016-5. doi:10.1038/s42003-024-07016-5. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Knockout of a single Pax6 gene (toy but not ey) leads to compound eye deficiency and small head in honeybees

## Abstract
AbstractThe compound eyes are crucial to honeybees, playing pivotal roles in color recognition, orientation, localization, and navigation processes. The development of compound eyes is primarily mastered by an evolutionarily conserved transcription factor Pax6. In honeybees, there are two Pax6 homologs: ey and toy. To gain a deeper understanding of their functions, we knock out both homologs using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Intriguingly, we observe that toy knockout mutants have smaller heads without compound eyes and exhibit brain atrophy, while ey knockout mutants develop normal compound eyes, most of which die before/during their metamorphosis from pupa to adult. By comparing the head transcriptomes of four stages (larva, prepupa, pupa, and adult) in toy-knockout mutants versus normal controls, we identify significantly perturbed genes related to DNA binding transcription factors, neuron differentiation, and insect visual primordium development. Additionally, we find the interaction network of toy in honeybees differs obviously from that of D. melanogaster. Our findings suggest the two Pax6 genes serve distinct functions in honeybees and toy takes over the central function of ey in master-regulating the development of honeybee compound eyes. This adds new evidence for breaking the simplified view that some of conservative developmental toolkit genes function as all-or-nothing master regulators.Subject terms: CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing, Development, Biological metamorphosis, Entomology

## Introduction
Honeybees (Apis mellifera) are important pollinators in both natural and managed habitats1, which play an essential role in maintaining complex and adaptive ecosystems and the production of agricultural crops worldwide2. They have a powerful visual perception system in which the compound eyes play major roles in searching food, recognizing colors, determining time and location, and homing. Honeybees are often used as a model for studying insect vision, perception, and cognition3. The visual system of honeybees has been studied for more than 100 years4,5. People have a relatively clear understanding of the whole process of the compound eye development of honeybees. Development of honeybee eyes begins as a thickening of the hypodermis along the lateral regions of the head, which takes place before the larva is hatched from the egg6. At the beginning of the third larval instar, the primordial visual cells start to differentiate7, and the optic lobe anlage (OLA) forms as a single neuroectodermal layer8. In the fifth instar, significant growth of the optic lobes occurs, with the lobula and medulla becoming distinct optic ganglia8. In the early cocoon-spinning phase, an upfolding in the outer OLA extends towards the upper edge of the medulla; while the retinal placode shows minimal development until the later cocoon-spinning stage8. Throughout the cocoon-spinning and prepupal stages, the lamina continues to overlay the medullar cortex, with optic nerve fibers extending from the retina to reach the lamina8. In the prepupal stage, the optic lobe in the compound eyes and nerves accounts for a large proportion of these rapidly developing organs in the head. This period is the most important period for the external morphology and volume increase of the compound eyes6. After completing the pupal molt, the optic lobe architecture is fully established, featuring concentric disks of lamina and medulla overlying the lobula, separated by inner and outer optic chiasmata8. The lamina connects with the retina via incoming optic nerve fibers. During the last stage of adult development, honeybees finally form complete structures of compound eyes, visual and olfactory boutons of projection neurons in the mushroom bodies significantly increase in their size9. Adult maturation has been found in honeybees at neuronal and behavioral levels10, and gradually acquire phototransduction and light perception abilities.
Besides the studies on the development of the compound eyes, the physiological functions of honeybee visual system, visual relevance learning and memory, and visual neurophysiology were also well reported11–13. However, there was little research on the key functional genes involved in the development of honeybee compound eyes and their molecular regulatory mechanism.
The DNA binding transcription factor paired box protein 6 (Pax6) is a master control regulator for eye morphogenesis in a wide range of animal species14. The Pax6 gene is a member of the Pax gene family; the Pax6 protein contains a paired box domain and a homeobox domain15–17 and shows functional conservation in bilaterian animals18. The Pax6 gene was originally identified in humans19 and mice20 via genetic analyzes of the loss-of-function phenotypes observed in the eyes: human aniridia and mouse small eye. There are two Pax6 homologs in Drosophila melanogaster and some other holometabolous insects (such as Bombyx mori14, Tribolium castaneum21) and crustaceans (such as Daphnia pulex22, Exopalaemon carinicauda23), possibly due to an evolutionary duplication event22. For the research on the molecular development of compound eyes, many basic and classical works have been conducted in D. melanogaster24–26, among which the roles of Pax6 genes (ey and toy) in the compound eye development were studied in detail. It was found that targeted expression of either ey or toy induced formation of ectopic compound eyes27,28 and both Pax6 genes were necessary for initiating the eye developmental pathway in Drosophila27,29. The mutations of ey and/or toy in Drosophila led to abnormal eye morphogenesis, showing variable phenotypes from lack of compound eyes to small eyes, and to wildtype eyes30–33. Additionally, Pax6 genes are involved in the development of the central nervous system (CNS) and brain in Drosophila34, and ey and toy have different functions in Drosophila head development, but could partially substitute for each other35. The ey gene has also been reported to be important for the formation of compound eyes in other arthropods, including T. castaneum21, E. carinicauda23, and Daphnia magna36.
We noticed that the two Pax6 homologs showed divergent functional patterns in the development of compound eyes in different invertebrates, such as D. melanogaster31, T. castaneum21, and E. carinicauda23. We found that there were also two Pax6 homologs in honeybees. However, there was little knowledge about honeybee Pax6 genes and their roles in the development of compound eyes. Considering the strong visual perception ability of honeybees, we are curious about the position and role of these two Pax6 genes in the developmental pathway of honeybee compound eyes. Do both Pax6 genes (ey and toy) in honeybees play important regulatory roles in the development of compound eyes? Or do they have their own unique genetic functions and regulatory networks in honeybees?
Previously, we have reported a high-efficiency CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing method, which allows one-step generation of biallelic knockout mutants in honeybee37. In this study, we extend previous work to investigate the roles of Pax6 genes in the development of compound eyes in honeybees. We employed the high-efficiency CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology to knock out ey and toy in honeybees. We found that the morphological development caused by the two Pax6 knockouts were quite different. Toy-knockout resulted in compound eye loss and small heads, while ey-knockout was lethal, but they have developed normal compound eyes similar to the wildtype in appearance. Subsequently, we identified the affected downstream genes and their related gene networks after knocking out toy. This research offered fresh perspectives into the roles of Pax6 genes in the development of social insect compound eyes.

## Results

## Two Pax6 genes in A. mellifera genome
Three genes (gene IDs: LOC102654776, LOC724238, and LOC724146) were annotated as Pax6 gene in the current Western honeybee reference genome (assembly Amel_HAv3.1). Two genes (LOC102654776 and LOC724238) were confirmed as Pax6 gene by searching against Drosophila homologous proteins using Blastx, the other gene (LOC724146) was confirmed as Hbn gene. One Pax6 gene (LOC724238) was located on the LG1 linkage group (GenBank accession number: NC_037638.1), and the other (LOC102654776) was located on the LG6 linkage group (GenBank accession number: NC_037643.1). Based on the phylogenetic relationship of arthropod Pax6 proteins, honeybee Pax6 genes of LOC724238 and LOC102654776 were named as eyeless (ey) and twin of eyeless (toy), respectively (Fig. 1A). Notably, we found that the arthropod Toy proteins were tightly clustered, while the arthropod Ey proteins were more dispersed in the phylogenetic tree. Consistently, the distance of arthropod Toy proteins was much smaller than that of arthropod Ey proteins (Fig. 1B). In the phylogenetic tree, honeybee Pax6 proteins encoded by ey and toy were clustered closely with those encoded by T. castaneum ey and toy, respectively; honeybee Pax6 encoded by toy was also clustered closely with fruit fly Pax6 proteins encoded by Drosophila toy genes, but the one encoded by A. mellifera ey gene showed more distance from the Pax6 proteins encoded by Drosophila ey genes (Fig. 1C). Sequence analysis showed that the A. mellifera ey gene is about 110 kb long in the genome, harboring 10 exons and 1,653 bp open reading frame (ORF) encoding 550 amino acids, while the A. mellifera toy gene is about 43 kb long in the genome, containing 10 exons and 1,545 bp ORF encoding 514 amino acids (Fig. 1D). Both A. mellifera Ey and Toy proteins contain paired box (PAX) and homeobox (HOX) domains, and the two functional domains (PAX and HOX) are highly conservative in different arthropod species (Fig. 1E).

## Ey-knockout leads to death of honeybees
Previously, we have established a high-efficiency CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing method37 by injecting the complex formed by sgRNA and Cas9 protein as the delivery into the region of zygote formation at the 1–2 cell embryonic stage. In this study, we applied this method to perform the experiments of knocking out two Pax6 genes in honeybees. We also conducted a sham control experiment by injecting the solvent (i.e. nuclease-free water for diluting the editing materials) into eggs. The growth and metamorphosis of larvae hatched in the later stage are no longer affected by the injection damage. The larvae could develop into normal pupae and adults (Supplementary Fig. S1), with no difference in development compared to the undamaged eggs.
We conducted two batches of editing experiments, injecting honeybee eggs with the A. mellifera ey gene specific sgRNA. Consequently, we obtained nine and seven ey gene edited mutants in the first and secondary batches, respectively (Supplementary Data 1 and Fig. 2A). Through PCR amplification, direct Sanger sequencing after PCR, and/or Sanger sequencing after TA cloning of the target sequence, we identified thirteen editing types in these mutants, including six deletion types and seven insertion types (Fig. 2B). Among them, ten types led to frameshift mutations, two types resulted in one amino acid deletion but no frameshift, and one type resulted in three amino acids insertion also without frameshift. Among the 16 gene-edited mutants, five were homozygous and biallelic knockout mutants with frameshifted ey gene, which were dead at the early or late pupal stage. Seven individuals were heterozygous and biallelic edited mutants, two of which developed into adults and the others were dead at the early or late pupal stage. Four individuals had three alleles of the edited ey gene, which were also dead at the early or late pupal stage. Altogether, most (87.5%) of 16 mutants died during the development from pupa to adult, only two (B1A1E and B1A2E) were developed into adult. The B1A1E mutant had no frameshift in both alleles, one allele resulted in one-amino acid (AA) deletion, and the other allele resulted in three-AA insertion. The B1A2E mutant had frameshifts in both alleles; one allele had a 5-nucleotide deletion, and the other allele had a 17-nucleotide insertion. Notably, normal structure of compound eyes (uncolored for early pupa or browned for late pupa) were observed on the heads of ey gene-edited mutants (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Data 1).

## Toy is required for the development of honeybee compound eyes and the brain
We employed the same sgRNA used in previous studies37 to edit the toy gene by injecting honeybee eggs. We found that the compound eyes of the toy gene mutants were absent while other body parts developed normally (Figs. 3A,B). Therefore, to systematically study the effect of toy knockout on the development of honeybee compound eyes, we collected gene-edited mutants without compound eyes at four time points, and carried out morphological analysis and transcriptome analyzes using these samples. We collected 15 toy gene editing mutants of 6th-day larvae (n = 4), 2nd-day prepupae (n = 3), 3rd-day pupae (n = 4) and 1st-day adults (n = 4) (Supplementary Data 2). Meanwhile, we collected 12 normal wildtype individuals without injection as controls (three samples per time point) at the four corresponding developmental time points.

We confirmed toy gene-editing types of these 15 mutants by direct Sanger sequencing after PCR amplification or Sanger sequencing after TA cloning. Among these mutants, the PCR products of L6E3, PP2E2, PP2E3, P3E1, and P3E3 were sequenced by the first-generation sequencing technology, and their sequencing peaks were all homozygous at and around the editing sites (Supplementary Fig. S3), indicating that these five toy gene editing honeybees were all edited as one mutation type. While the first-generation sequencing peaks of the other 10 samples were heterozygous, we further performed TA cloning and sequencing for them. The results suggested that L6E1, L6E4, PP2E1, P3E4, A1E1, and A1E4 contained two mutation types, L6E2, P3E2, A1E2, and A1E3 contained three mutation types. We predicted the coding protein sequences of their edited toy genes. It was found that except for an editing type containing a 27-bp insertion in the A1E3 individual, all the above toy editing types had frame shift mutations and could not encode normal Toy protein (Supplementary Fig. S4).
We observed the morphological changes of toy gene-knockout honeybee mutants at the above four developmental time points. We found that after toy gene knockout, the body of gene-edited 6th-day larva was almost unchanged compared with that of normal larva, but the head of gene-edited larva was sharper and smaller than that of normal individual, and the head-chest junction was constricted, appearing thinner than that of normal individual (Supplementary Figs. S5A and S5B). The body-to-head junction of normal individual was plumper and smoother than that of edited mutant. The morphological changes of the edited mutant of 2nd-day prepupa were similar to those of gene-edited 6th-day larva. The body of edited 2nd-day prepupa had no changes compared with normal one, but the head of edited 2nd-day prepupa appeared obviously smaller than the wildtype. The bulging structure of developing compound eyes could not be visible on the head of the mutant (Supplementary Figs. S5C and S5D). The gene-edited mutants of 3rd-day pupa and 1st-day adult also showed obvious changes in their heads: the size of the head became significantly smaller, and they had no compound eyes (Fig. 3).
To better understand how the mutant head become smaller, we carefully observed the shape changes of the 3rd-day pupal mutant compared with the normal individual in more detail, and further carried out a comparative histomorphological analysis of the head sections between the mutant and the normal individual. It was found that the 3rd-day pupal mutant had no deformity in the body, and its body was similar to that of normal pupa (Fig. 3A). The head of the mutant was significantly smaller than that of the wildtype individual, with obvious compound eye loss. The structures of other head appendages (including ocelli, antennae, maxillary palp, mandible, galea, glossa, etc.) were basically similar to those of the control individual (Figs. 3A and B). In the normal 3rd-day pupa, compound eyes appeared as oval brown regions, while the mutant had none of these regions (Fig. 3B). Through the histomorphological observation, it was found that the head structure of normal individual was complete, mainly including compound eyes, brain, hypopharyngeal glands and other structures. However, the head of the mutant was significantly smaller, including the hypopharyngeal glands and an atrophic brain (Fig. 3C). The visual lobes were obviously absent in the brain, while atrophic mushroom bodies and antenna lobes could be observed. We also carefully observed the morphological changes of the head of the adult mutant. The normal honeybee had compound eyes, ocelli, antennae, mouthparts (including maxillary palp, mandible, galea, glossa, etc) on the head. The mutant had no compound eyes but had ocelli, antennae, mouthparts, and its brain was significantly atrophic (Fig. 3D). Compared with the normal honeybee, these organs (ocelli, antennae, and mouthparts) of the mutant developed smaller.

## Overview of the A. mellifera head transcriptome in the different stages
Illumina RNA sequencing generated an average of 50.8 million (M) clean reads with an accumulated length of 7.5 million bases (Mb) per sample. The average mapping rate of clean reads on the A. mellifera reference genome was 94.8%, the average unique mapping rate was 92.4% (Supplementary Data 3). The gene body coverage of each sample was almost symmetric between the 5’ end and 3ʹ end of the genes (Supplementary Fig. S6). The results indicated that the RNA quality was high and the randomness of the reads was good, suggesting that the sequencing data of the samples was suitable for further bioinformatics analyzes. We performed hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA) using the whole gene expression data of 27 samples (including 12 uninjected wildtype controls and 15 toy gene-edited samples) (Fig. 4A). In the HCA plot, we found that these 27 samples could be clustered into three categories, i.e., all larval samples were clustered together, all adult samples were grouped together, and prepupal and pupal samples were gathered together (Supplementary Fig. S7A). For the second-day prepupal and the first-day adult samples, the toy gene-edited samples were completely separated from the control samples; the gene editing samples and the control samples in sixth-day larval and the third-day pupal stages roughly grouped into two different clusters, but there were some exceptions. In the PCA plot, the 27 samples were also grouped into three categories, and the samples from the same stage were also grouped together (Supplementary Fig. S7B), which was well in agreement with the hierarchical cluster analysis.

## Landscape changes of honeybee head transcriptome throughout normal development
To reveal the head transcriptomic landscape changes of normal honeybees across the four time points of 6th-day larva, 2nd-day prepupa, 3rd-day pupa, and 1st-day adult, we first identified the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) specifically upregulataed at each developmental time point. As a result, we identified 346, 367, 265, and 1,125 DEGs specifically upregulated at the larval, prepupal, pupal, and adult stages (Supplementary Fig. S8A), respectively. Subsequently, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyzes using these genes. The top 10 GO or KEGG terms in each developmental time point were shown in Supplementary Fig. S8B.
At the larval stage, the top 10 GO or KEGG terms are carboxylic acid metabolic process (GO:0019752), oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), alpha-amino acid metabolic process (GO:1901605), small molecule biosynthetic process (GO:0044283), Valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation (dme00280), Carbon metabolism (dme01200), Fatty acid metabolism (dme01212), fatty acid beta-oxidation using acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (GO:0033539), Pentose and glucuronate interconversions (dme00040), and larval serum protein complex (GO:0005616). These enriched terms are essentially related to the metabolism and biosynthesis processes of small molecules. At the prepupal stage, the top 10 enriched GO terms are mitotic cell cycle (GO:0000278), chromosome (GO:0005694), ATP binding (GO:0005524), regulation of cell cycle (GO:0051726), DNA replication (GO:0006260), double-stranded DNA binding (GO:0003690), spindle elongation (GO:0051231), plasma membrane bounded cell projection organization (GO:0120036), cell fate commitment (GO:0045165), pattern specification process (GO:0007389). These GO terms are predominantly associated with mitosis, the cell cycle, and DNA replication. At the pupal stage, the 265 specific upregulated genes are enriched in cell morphogenesis (GO:0000902), cell-cell adhesion mediated by cadherin (GO:0044331), pupal development (GO:0035209), regulation of tube architecture, open tracheal system (GO:0035152), Toll binding (GO:0005121), extracellular space (GO:0005615), apical plasma membrane (GO:0016324), peptidase activity (GO:0008233), extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198), animal organ morphogenesis (GO:0009887), ant etc. These GO terms mainly focus on cell morphology and pupal development. At the adult stage, we obtained 1125 co-upregulated genes compared to other stages. These genes are enriched in Oxidative phosphorylation (dme00190), inorganic molecular entity transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0015318), contractile fiber (GO:0043292), active transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0022804), homeostatic process (GO:0042592), rhodopsin mediated signaling pathway (GO:0016056), muscle system process (GO:0003012), phosphate-containing compound metabolic process (GO:0006796), chemical synaptic transmission (GO:0007268), system process (GO:0003008), and etc. Therefore, this suggests that oxidative phosphorylation, transmembrane transporter activity, homeostatic and system processes are involved in the development and formation of adult honeybee head organs.

## Differentially expressed genes between the toy-knockout groups and their controls
In this study, we compared the head transcriptomes of toy gene knockout mutants with those of normal controls and obtained the DEGs at the above four developmental time points. Using these DEGs, we reconducted correlation analyzes on the individuals between toy gene knockout mutants and the normal controls at each developmental time point. We found that the DEGs could divide the samples into two divergent clusters, representing the gene-knockout group and the normal control group (Supplementary Fig. S9), which was slightly different from the clustering using whole transcriptomic data in the clustering of whole transcriptomes (Supplementary Fig. S7A). We detected 83, 1267, 46, 83 up-regulated genes and 165, 264, 221, 429 down-regulated genes in the gene-edited samples of 6th-day larvae, 2nd-day prepupae, 3rd-day pupae, 1st-day adults, respectively (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Data 4). Among these down-regulated genes, 5 genes were common to the four time points, and 42 genes were shared in three different developmental periods (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Data 5). Among the up-regulated genes, none of them were shared by more than three developmental time points; among these down-regulated genes, eya, lz, gl, scro, and dpr7 were common to the four time points. All these five genes in the gene-knockout groups showed significant down-regulation compared to the normal controls at each time point (Fig. 4D). It was reported that Pax2 genes are expressed in developing eyes of spiders and scorpions, and the genes seem to play more important roles than Pax6 genes in chelicerates38. We also check the expression file of Pax2 between normal individuals and toy knockout individuals. We found that the mutants still expressed a considerable amount of Pax2 in the prepupal and pupal stages (Fig. 4D).
We employed qRT-PCR to validate gene expression in the transcriptome. We selected 13 genes with different classes of expression patterns, most of which had a known biological function involved in eye development. The mRNA expression trends of the qRT-PCR-tested genes were consistent with those detected by RNA-seq in the four developmental time points (Supplementary Fig. S10 and Supplementary Data 6).

No KEGG pathway was enriched using these 41 genes.

## Expression profiles of the genes having interaction with toy gene
To identify the gene set having interaction with toy gene in honeybee, we performed a weighted co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using all 27 transcriptomes of gene-edited mutants and normal controls collected in this study, and reconstructed a co-expression network related to toy gene. Using the interaction weight of 0.05 as the filter criteria, we detected 15 genes, including ey, eya, Mir29b, gl, CG11294, hbn, Hmx, ap, CG11085, scro, Rgk2, RunxB, CG43778, lz, and LOC114577038 (Fig. 6A), had highly synergy with the phenotypic changes between the edited mutants and normal controls. All 15 genes were significantly down-regulated in the 6th day larva. Except for CG43778, 14 other genes were significantly down-regulated between edited and normal individuals at more than three developmental stages (Fig. 6B).

We performed a protein-protein interaction network and functional enrichment analysis of Toy using D. melanogaster as the model species in the STRING database (version 12). With confidence of 0.4 as the lowest interaction score, 18 proteins were predicted to be interacted with Toy, including Ey, Eya, Optix, Tio, So, Six4, CG4328, Dmrt99B, Fd59A, Hipk, CG13331, Zif, Tim9a, Tim9b, CG31229, SdhD, Wsck, and Fd3F (Fig. 6C). We checked the differential expression profiles of these 18 protein-coding genes in our honeybee transcriptome (Fig. 6D). We found that ey, eya, Optix, so, dmrt99B, fd59A, HipK, and tio were down-regulated at least in one period after toy editing. The expression of CG31229, CG4328, fd3F, SdhD, Six4, Tim9a, Tim9b, Wsck, and Zif had no difference between edited mutants and wildtypes. There is no gene in honeybee corresponding to fruit fly CG13331 gene. These results suggested that although toy plays key regulation in the development of compound eyes in both A. mellifera and D. melanogaster, there were still certain difference between the detailed functions of A. mellifera and D. melanogaster toy.

## Discussion
In this study, we knocked out two Pax6 genes in honeybee to investigate their roles in the development of compound eyes. In fruit fly, both ey and toy genes play early decisive roles in eye development, and are required for the development of all structures derived from eye-antennal disks30,31,39. Drosophila ey and toy should be considered together as carrying out the equivalent functions of the single vertebrate Pax6 gene32. There were also some studies on Pax6 loss of function by gene knockdown or knockout in other invertebrates, including red flour beetle T. castaneum, water flea D. magna, and ridgetail white prawn E. carinicauda. In red flour beetle, knock down of single or both Pax6 genes affect larval eye development strongly but adult eye development mildly21, and the Pax6 genes ey and toy were required for global patterning of the ocular segment in the Tribolium embryo40. In water flea, a gene-editing targeting exon 10 of the ey gene led to a typical phenotype of the eyeless mutant, i.e., eye deformity31. In ridgetail white prawn, the surviving ey-knockout embryos and larvae exhibited severe abnormal eye morphology, while no mutant phenotype was found in toy-knockout individuals23. But in spider Parasteatoda tepidariorum, both Pax6 genes were not considered to be the upstream master regulators in eye development41. In our present study, the developmental and phenotypic changes caused by toy and ey gene-knockouts in honeybees were quite different. Toy gene-knockout mutants showed the loss of compound eyes and small heads, while ey gene-knockout mutants had normal compound eyes and most of them died before their metamorphosis from pupa to adult. Based on the morphological observations, we could preliminarily speculate that ey possibly lose its master role in the development of compound eyes but was essential for the metamorphosis of the entire individual, and that only one Pax6 gene (toy) played the role of master regulator in the development of compound eyes.
We further performed the transcriptome analysis of the toy knockout mutant in honeybees. We found that the expression of ey gene had a very obvious change in the absence of toy gene. It indicated that the ey gene was still in the regulatory network of the toy gene in honeybees, but its function has dramatically changed. At the same time, the deletion of the ey gene could lead to a large number of mutant pupae that could not develop into adults and died during the pupal stage. This phenomenon did not occur in the toy gene deletion mutants. From the results, it could be inferred that the deletion of the ey gene might have a certain degree of lethality, mainly affecting the transformation from pupal stage to adults. The specific mechanisms of its impact also required more experimental research and exploration. The results of honeybee Pax6 gene knockouts showed that the functions of two honeybee Pax6 genes, toy and ey, were different from those of D. melanogaster, T. castaneum, and other species, and these Pax6 genes exhibited unique characteristics in honeybees. This differentiation may be related to the fact that D. melanogaster adult compound eyes develop from imaginal disks42, T. castaneum adult compound eyes develop from a so-called “eye placode” in the lateral head epidermis42, while compound eye development in honeybees seems to be tightly linked to optic lobe/brain development6,8. This major difference could be linked to variations in the expression and function of underlying genes.
The Pax6 gene is a functionally conserved gene, which is conserved from flatworms to humans, especially in two conserved structural domains, PAX and HOX. Pax6 has two or even multiple copies in many animals. They exhibit a certain degree of redundancy in their functions in fruit flies35. We found in honeybees that the functions of these two Pax6 genes do not exhibit a redundant relationship with each other, but rather that the function of ey gene mastering the development of compound eyes has changed although ey contains two conserved domains. We also found that Toy proteins in arthropods are more conserved than Ey proteins, indicating that the function of Ey in arthropods is more susceptible to be changed. The results in the present study supported that toy took over the central function of ey in master-regulating the development of honeybee compound eyes. Our findings provide additional evidence for breaking the simplified view that some of conservative developmental toolkit genes function as all-or-nothing master regulators.
It was reported that Pax6 was ancestrally linked and shown to have interchangeable functions with Pax238,43. Pax2 genes are expressed in developing eyes of spiders and scorpions, and these genes seem to play more important roles than Pax6 genes in chelicerates38. We found that the mutants of compound eye and optic lobe deficiency still expressed an amount of Pax2 in the prepupal, pupal, and adult stages, especially a considerable amount in the prepupal and pupal stages, indicating that Pax2 is expressed in tissues other than compound eyes and the expression level is not low. We also found that the differences in the expression of Pax2 between normal individuals and toy gene knockout individuals were only significant in the prepupal stage, but not significant or slight significant in the other three stages. This phenomenon suggests that the function of Pax2 may be related to the development of other tissues in the head. However, based on the current results of our toy gene knockout experiments, it could be concluded that Pax2 has no substitutive or compensatory effects on the toy gene, but the expression of Pax2 gene were slightly disturbed after knockout of toy gene in honeybees.
We found that after the toy gene was knocked out in honeybees, the genes affected in more than three developmental times of 6th-day larvae, 2nd-day prepupa, 3rd-day pupa and 1st-day adult were all down-regulated. Of them, five genes, including eya, lz, gl, scro and dpr7, were closely related to either the development of compound eyes or nerve development. Eya encodes a transcriptional cofactor that physically interacts with several other retinal determination proteins. Eya gene was required at an early stage in development of the D. melanogaster compound eyes44, and was proposed to control the initiation of the morphogenetic furrow via regulation of Dpp signaling within the out margin of eye-antennal disc45. Lz encodes an alpha-subunit of the transcription factor complex core binding factor, which plays an important role during eye development contributing to programmed cell death46, eye morphogenesis47,48, eye cone cell differentiation and R7 cell development49–51. Gl encodes a zinc finger transcription factor involved in cell fate specification, which is required for correctly patterning the retina, rhabdomere formation, and for the expression of phototransduction proteins52,53. Scro is an NK-2 homeobox gene that possibly acts as a regional specification factor and plays an essential role for the development of the optic lobe45,54. Dpr7 is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily. They form a complex interaction network with Dpr-interacting proteins (DIPs), acting as neuronal surface labels in the specificity of synaptic connections between neurons and target cells55. These five genes were down-regulated in all the four time points after toy editing. These results indicated that toy gene knockout had greatly affected the development network of compound eyes and brain in honeybees.
Interestingly, among the genes commonly down-regulated at three developmental times, two non-coding RNAs including Mir29b and LOC114577038, were detected in the co-expressing interaction network of toy gene. After toy knockout, Mir29b was significantly down-regulated and showed a strong interaction with toy. This microRNA Mir29b was reported playing important regulatory roles in many biological processes. For examples, Mir29b mediates epigenetic mechanisms of HBG gene activation to induce fetal hemoglobin56, contributes to multiple types of muscle atrophy57, regulates aberrant methylation in in vitro diabetic nephropathy model of renal proximal tubular cells58, and inhibits prostate tumor growth and induces apoptosis by increasing Bim expression59. It was the first time to report that the microRNA Mir29b was involved in the development of compound eyes. The other commonly down-regulated non-coding RNA LOC114577038 was located at the 0.3-kb downstream site of scro gene, and showed strong correlation with scro gene in our expression data. This suggested that LOC114577038 might play roles in regulating the expression of scro during the development of honeybee compound eyes. The role of these two non-coding RNAs in the development of compound eyes is worthy of further study. Our results suggested that epigenetic regulation is involved in the development of the compound eyes in honeybees.
Another incredible phenomenon is that there are far more up-regulated genes in the 2nd-day prepupal mutants. To explore the potential causes of this phenomenon, we examined the developmental characteristics of normal honeybees at four developmental times by performing GO/KEGG enrichment analysis on genes that were specifically up-regulated at each developmental time (Supplementary Fig. S8). We found that: (1) the specific developmental characteristics of 6th-day larvae were related to nutrient metabolism; (2) the characteristics of 2nd-day prepupae were specifically associated with cell proliferation, including the processes of mitosis, the cell cycle, and DNA replication; (3) the characteristics of 3rd-day pupae were cell morphology and pupal development; and (4) During the adult stage, each organ underwent a process of maturation, and all organs developed their normal biological functions. At the prepupal stage of honeybee, the various organs of the head rapidly grow into pupal morphology, accompanied by significant changes in size and morphology6. Among them, the developing compound eyes and related optic nerves constitute a large proportion. Therefore, prepupal stage is the most important period for the increase in the external morphology and size of compound eyes. However, the knockout of toy gene led to the development defects of compound eyes and optic lobes in the mutants, providing more developmental opportunities for other head organs. The phenomenon of organ developmental competition is consistent with the experimental results of Nijhout & Emlen60 and Klingenberg & Frederik61, which indicate that the removal of imaginal disks leads to competition development in other organs or structures. It was reported that insects only feed in the larval stage, where they accumulate enough nutrients to ensure the normal development of organs throughout the prepupal and pupal stages62. Under conditions of limited and fixed nutrition, the developmental competition of organs appears more obvious in individuals with compound eye deficiencies. Prepupal mutants with compound eye defects provide more nutritional support and developmental opportunities for other tissues and organs, resulting in the significant upregulation of a large number of genes related to the development of these organs and tissues. Consistently, we observed that the enriched terms at the prepupal stage of mutants included the developments of muscle structure, respiratory system, and cuticle (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Data 7).
In summary, we find that toy is more conservative than ey in insect evolution and knockout of toy but not ey leads to small-headed honeybees without compound eyes, which provides fresh insights into the role of Pax6 genes in honeybee compound-eye and brain development, and highlights the divergent developmental regulation of compound eyes among insects.

## Materials and methods

## Experimental animals
The experimental honeybees (Apis mellifera) were reared in the apiary of Honeybee Research Institute, Jiangxi Agricultural University, China (28.46 °N, 115.49 °E).

## Gene identification and sequence analysis
The coding sequences and predicted protein sequences of Pax6 were identified from the annotation of the honeybee reference genome63,64 and confirmed by searching against Drosophila homologous proteins using Blastx. The phylogenetic tree of Pax6 proteins was reconstructed with 1000 bootstraps using MEGA-X software. Multiple alignment for the Pax6 sequences were first conducted using clustalw implemented in MEGA, with the default alignment parameters. Distance module in MEGA was then used to calculate pairwise distances for the well-matched sequences. The parameters were also set as default and analyzes were conducted using the Poisson correction model. All ambiguous positions were removed for each sequence pair using the pairwise deletion option. There was a total of 1009 positions in the final dataset. The domain architectures of Toy and Ey were identified and annotated using SMART. Multiple alignment of Pax6 protein sequences were performed by Clustal-Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/) and were viewed in MView (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/mview/).

## Preparation of sgRNAs and Cas9 protein
We employed the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing method established previously37 to generate ey- and toy-knockout mutants in honeybees, respectively. The sequence of ey sgRNA target site was 5ʹ-GGTCGAGTCTGGAAGCGGTCAGG-3ʹ (Underlined is the PAM sequence), which was searched by the CCTop online tool65 and was located in the exon 3 of LOC724238 gene. For toy gene, the sgRNA target site was 5ʹ-GACCATTACCAGACTCTACAAGG-3ʹ, which was the same as previously reported37. A PCR-based approach was used to produce sgRNAs of ey and toy. A specific oligonucleotide encoding a T7 polymerase-binding site and the sgRNA target sequences of ey or toy was designed as the forward primers (F-sgRNAAmEy: 5ʹ-TAATACGACTCACTATAGACCGCTTCCAGACTCGACCGTTTTAGAGCTAGAAATAGC-3ʹ for ey; F-sgRNAAmToy: 5ʹ-TAATACGACTCACTATAGACCATTACCAGACTCTACAGTTTTAGAGCTAGAAATAGC-3ʹ for toy) and a common oligonucleotide encoding the remaining sgRNA sequences was designed as the reverse primer (R-Common: 5ʹ-AAAAAAAGCACCGACTCGGTGCCAC-3ʹ). The two pairs of primers for ey and toy were annealed by PCR to synthesize template DNA. The PCR reaction mixture (80 μl) contained 40 μl of 2 × Pfu Mastermix (Transgene), 37 μl of H2O, 1 μl of 5 μM forward and reverse primers (F-sgRNAAmEy or F-sgRNAAmToy and R-Common) and 1 μl of 50 ng/μl pYSY-sgRNA plasmid (YaoShunYu, China). PCR was performed at 95 °C 3 min, 35 cycles of (95 °C 30 s, 58 °C 30 s, 72 °C 30 s), 72 °C 10 min and 4 °C ∞ . PCR products were purified by a PCR clean-up (Axygen) kit. In vitro transcription was performed with the MAXIscript SP6/T7 RNA in vitro transcription kit (Ambion, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.
The Cas9 protein (TrueCut™ Cas9 Protein v2) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Shanghai, China).

## Microinjection and rearing
Before microinjection, the limitation and release of honeybee queen, as well as the timing of embryo collection, followed previously established procedures37. The honeybee queen was placed in a quiet, clean, egg-free, built-up comb and allowed to lay eggs. After two hours, the queen was released. The plastic plugs with the laid eggs were then taken off from the comb, and the attached eggs were prepared for injection.
The concentration of microinjected sgRNA and Cas9 protein per delivery were 150–200 ng/μl and 200 ng/μl, the injection site was the region of zygote formation near the ventral side of embryonic cephalic pole, the injection angle between the needle and the side of the egg was adjusted to less than 30 degrees, and the needle with 4 μm internal diameter was inserted following the direction from the anterior side to the posterior side37. The injection time was 0.1 s, the injection pressure was 600 hPa, and the balance pressure was 50 hPa. These injection conditions were the same as previous studies37,66. The integrity of the egg should be ensured after injection. A transparent spot was found at the injection site of the egg, which then disappeared slowly. If the egg protoplasm overflowed during microinjection, the egg was removed, as it would die because of damage. The embryos were incubated in plastic boxes at 35°C (relative humidity 85%) with a small amount of 16% (vol/vol) sulfuric acid to prevent mold formation66. We conducted a sham control experiment by injecting the solvent (i.e. nuclease-free water for diluting the editing materials) into eggs using the same microinjection and rearing ways.
When we reared each batch of microinjected experimental eggs, we collected the eggs of the same honeybee queen in the same way at the same time, raised them under the same conditions, and treated them as normal developmental controls.

## Collection and identification of mutants
For ey gene, we carried out two batches of microinjection experiments. Each batch of 30–40 eggs were microinjected. Nine pupated mutants were obtained in the first batch, and 7 pupated mutants were obtained in the second batch. All the mutants were verified by PCR, direct Sanger sequencing, and/or Sanger sequencing after TA cloning at the target editing site of ey gene. PCRs for target genes were performed following the instructions of the TransDirect Animal Tissue PCR Kit purchased from Transgen Biotech (Beijing, China). The fragment (1442 bp) flanking the editing target site in ey was amplified using a pair of specific primers (forward: 5ʹ-TTTCGATGCTTTTCGATCTC-3ʹ, reverse: 5ʹ-ATAATGCACATCGCTGGTCT-3ʹ). The PCR reaction mixture (20 µl) contained 10 µl of 2× TransDirect PCR supermix ( + dye), 0.4 µl of forward primers, 0.4 µl of reverse primers, 5.2 µl of ddH2O, 4 µl of embryonic lysis fluid. PCR was performed at 94 °C 10 min, 35 cycles of (94 °C 30 s, 52 °C 30 s, 72 °C 1 min), 72 °C 10 min and 12 °C until the PCR products were taken out. The PCR products were ready for Sanger sequencing or inserted into TA vectors for Sanger sequencing.
For toy gene, we carried out four batches of microinjection experiments, each batch of 30–40 eggs were microinjected. We finally collected 18 mutants at four developmental times from these four batches of samples, including 6th-day larvae (n = 4), 2nd-day prepupae (n = 3), 3rd-day pupae (n = 6) and 1st-day adults (n = 5). All these mutants were also verified by PCR, direct Sanger sequencing, and/or Sanger sequencing after TA cloning at the target editing site of toy gene. The fragment (446 bp) flanking the editing target site in toy was amplified using a pair of specific primers (forward: 5ʹ-GCCGGTGTGTGTTTATTCAA-3ʹ, reverse: 5ʹ-TGCAAAAGTGACATCCTTGCT-3ʹ) reported previously37. The PCR reaction mixture and cycling condition were same as the above condition of ey target sequence. Two pupal mutants and one adult mutant were ready for making slices and the morphological observation; the other 15 mutants were ready for RNA extraction and transcriptomic analyzes. Meanwhile, we collected 12 normal samples corresponding to the four developmental times to perform RNA-seq and transcriptomic analyzes, which were treated as normal controls.

## Slicing, staining and morphological observation
We conducted morphological observations on toy-knockout mutants at both the pupal and adult stages. As controls, we included uninjected wildtype pupa and adult for comparative morphological analysis. For tissue section analysis, we used the heads of 3rd-day pupal mutants and normal wildtype pupae to prepare frozen tissue sections. For slicing, we cut off the entire heads of 3rd-day pupa samples and immersed them in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for at least 48 h. Afterward, the heads were sectioned into frozen slices with a thickness of about 7 μm. The frozen slices were air-dried at room temperature and then subjected to fluorescence staining using a 10 ng/ml PI staining solution. The slices were completely covered with the staining solution and subsequently observed and photographed under an OLYMPUS MVX10 fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

## RNA extraction and resequencing
Total RNA was extracted from the liquid nitrogen stored samples using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen). The RNA integrity of the total RNA samples was detected by agarose gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Fig. S11). The RNA concentration and quantity of the total RNA samples were determined by using the RNA 6000 Nano Kit on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, US). The complementary DNA was prepared for each embryo sample using the Illumina mRNA sequencing kit (Illumina, CA, US) and the Clontech SMART cDNA Library Construction Kit (Invitrogen). Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeqXTen platform (Illumina, CA, US).

## Mapping and quality control of RNA-seq data
Before mapping, reads with just adapters, reads in which unknown bases represented more than 10% of the total sequence and low-quality reads in which the percentage of low-quality bases (quality no more than 10) exceeded 50%, were removed from the raw data. The remaining clean reads were used for subsequent bioinformatics analyzes. Quality control on the clean data was performed by the production of a base composition chart and quality distribution chart using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). We used Hisat267 to map clean reads to the A. mellifera genome assembly version 3.1 (Amel_HAv3.1). Prior to this, exons and splice sites were extracted from the genome annotation GTF files and used to index the genomes. The mapping statistics and the coverage of the gene body by the RNA-seq reads were calculated by using bam_stat.py and geneBody_ coverage2.py, respectively, implemented in the RSeQC package68.

## Transcriptome reconstruction and annotation
We employed StringTie69 to reconstruct the transcriptome for each A. mellifera head sample and to estimate the relative abundances of genes/transcripts in these transcriptomes. We filtered the low-expression transcripts with the criterion of fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) > 1.
The gffcompare program (https://github.com/gpertea/gffcompare) was employed to evaluate the transcript assemblies (via comparison with A. mellifera reference annotation) and to perform the basic tracking of assembled isoforms across multiple RNA-seq experiments. Hmmer2go (https://github.com/sestaton/HMMER2GO) was used to identify the possible open reading frames (ORFs) of the assembled transcripts. We identified putative homologs to the D. melanogaster database using BLAST with an E-value threshold of 1e-5, which was implemented in the KOBAS-i online annotation tool68.

## Differential expression analysis and GO and KEGG analysis
Transcript abundances were estimated by running StringTie with the -eB options. Read count information was extracted from the above transcript abundance files by prepDE.py for subsequent differential expression analysis. We used the R statistical package DESeq270 from the Bioconductor repository to test for differential expression among the head samples from different days.
To determine the functional enrichment of the candidate genes, the enriched GO terms and the KEGG pathways were characterized on the Metascape online annotation tool71 with default setting. In the default setting, p-values were calculated based on the cumulative hypergeometric distribution, and the multi-test adjusted p-values (q-values) were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to account for multiple testing. To effectively explain the functions of differentially expressed genes, D. melanogaster was set as input and analysis species.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
cDNAs were generated using Reverse Transcriptase Kit reagents (Transgen, Beijing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Twelve differentially expressed genes and GAPDH were selected for qRT-PCR analysis, and RpS3 and RpL18 were used as internal controls to normalize the data, which were confirmed as stable gene using GeNorm by analyzing the transcriptomic data. The primer pairs are provided in Supplementary Data 8. Real-time PCR was conducted using an ABI 7500 real-time PCR detection system (ABI, CA, USA). The cycling conditions were as follows: a preliminary cycle at 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 30 s. The specificity of the PCR products was verified by a melting curve analysis for each sample. For each unigene, three biological replicates (with three technical replicates per biological replicate) were performed. The control and target unigenes for each sample were run in the same plate to eliminate interplate variations. The Ct value for each biological replicate was obtained by calculating the arithmetic mean of three technical replicate values. The relative mRNA expression was determined using the comparative 2-(ΔΔCt) method. The statistical analysis of gene expression was performed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using R version 4.0.1.

## Gene or protein network analysis
Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to build gene co-expression networks with the WGCNA package (version 1.47). The analysis included the following steps: (1) preparing the data; (2) creating the network; (3) grouping genes in modules; (4) associating modules and phenotypes (wildtype and toy gene-knockout); and (5) choosing the module associating with toy-knockout phenotype and visualizing the network. After the construction of co-expression modules, the modules in which ey, toy, and eya located were selected. The top 16 genes with the highest weight values in the module were chosen.
STRING (v12.0, https://cn.string-db.org/) and network analyst computational software were utilized to determine gene-gene and gene-protein interaction for Drosophila toy gene.

## Statistics and reproducibility
Data statistical analyzes were performed using R software version 4.0.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) or spreadsheet of WPS office version 12.1.0.15712 (Jinshan office, Zhuhai, China). We utilized the DESeq2 statistical package from the Bioconductor repository in R to perform differential expression analyzes comparing toy-edited mutants and normal controls. Student’s t tests were performed to compare the genetic distance within or between Arthoprod Ey or/and Toy (in Figs. 1B and C) and the relative expression of genes in the qPCR test between toy-edited mutants and normal controls (Supplementary Data 6); tests were two-sided with an α level of 0.05. P-values were adjusted with FDR method.
