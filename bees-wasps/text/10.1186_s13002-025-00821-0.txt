Pollination as a biocultural process: integrating local ecological knowledge of family farmers in northern Argentinian Patagonia for inclusive conservation.
Grimaldi PA, Ladio A. (2025). Journal of ethnobiology and ethnomedicine. . doi:10.1186/s13002-025-00821-0. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Pollination as a biocultural process: integrating local ecological knowledge of family farmers in northern Argentinian Patagonia for inclusive conservation

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundPollination is a complex and biocultural significant ecological process, essential for the reproduction of cultivated and wild plants. In family farming, pollination is crucial due to the economic and cultural dependence on pollinators. This study examines local ecological knowledge about pollination in three localities of northern Patagonia, Argentina.MethodsSemi-structured interviews and participant observation were conducted with 30 family farmers in Villa Llanquín, Río Villegas, and El Manso. The interviews included open- and closed-ended questions about perceptions and practices related to pollination. In addition, free listing and visual stimulus techniques were used to further explore local ecological knowledge. The data were analyzed qualitatively through discourse analysis and quantitatively using indices, descriptive statistics, and generalized linear models to investigate the relationship between LEKP and cultivated area, management practices, seniority, and plant species richness.ResultsSixteen local names were identified to refer to 15 pollinators. The insects with the highest cultural significance index were the honeybee Apis mellifera and bumblebees Bombus spp. About 97% of the farmers recognized the pollination process, and 93% emphasized its importance for horticultural production and the environment. Management practices carried out by farmers were indirect in 67.2% of cases and direct in 32.8%. Both types of practices favor pollinators and promote biodiversity. Finally, the importance of reflexivity and methodological flexibility in ethnobiological work was discussed.Discussion and conclusionThese results underscore the importance of local ecological knowledge in pollinator conservation and biodiversity promotion. This study emphasizes the necessity of contextualized and specific approaches to understand the socioecological dynamics that influence local knowledge.Keywords: Agriculture, Family farming, Pollination, Local ecological knowledge, Argentine Patagonia

## Background
Pollination is a complex ecological process that goes beyond the transfer of pollen from the anthers to the stigma of a flower. It is mostly a mutualistic interaction, in which plants and animals collaborate for mutual benefit: Plants ensure their own reproduction, while animals obtain resources, primarily nectar and pollen. In addition to its biological relevance [1], pollination is revealed as a biocultural phenomenon [2, 3] with significant social, cultural, and economic implications [1, 4]. This essential process for ecosystem health [1, 5] is indispensable for the reproduction of both, cultivated plants, which provide food [6–11] and multiple resources, and wild plants, which sustain biodiversity [3]. Pollination fundamentally contributes to people’s livelihoods [1, 3, 12].
The global decline of pollinators, a phenomenon documented by the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment in 2005 and the IPBES initiative [13–15], poses serious threats to both, the economy [16–18] and the environment [15, 19]. In family farming, this decline could have a deeper, more complex impact. The loss of pollinators would affect not only food production [18] but also alter the sociocultural dynamics of communities that depend on crop and wild plant pollination [20, 21]. Globally, agricultural productivity would be compromised due to the dependence of crops on pollination (for more details see 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11). The impact could be devastating in countries such as Mexico, where 85% of culturally important species depend on animal pollination [12], or in Anolaima (Colombia), where 64% depend on animal pollination [22]. Furthermore, a recent study in Patagonia, Argentina [3], showed that family farmers use 426 species in their food system, 86% of which depend on animal pollination, indicating their potential vulnerability.
Growing concerns about pollinator conservation have led to the need to integrate communities’ Local Ecological Knowledge (LEK) into the design of more robust strategies [2, 23, 24]. LEK is defined as a complex and dynamic system of knowledge, practices, and beliefs that evolves and is transmitted culturally over generations [25]. The LEK about pollination (LEKP) ranges from the perception of the intricate relationships between plants and animals, to the identification and classification of the various actors involved in the pollination process. LEKP also encompasses environmental management practices that benefit pollinating insect populations and the pathways and mechanisms by which this knowledge is acquired and transmitted.
Despite the growing recognition of the importance of LEKP, significant gaps remain in our understanding of how farmers, especially those practicing family farming, perceive this process. While research by Osterman et al. [26] suggests that pollination is generally recognized as a key factor in agricultural production worldwide, studies on LEKP vary considerably by region [26–29]. These studies reveal a wide range of situations, from communities with high knowledge levels, such as Mufulira, Zambia (96.2% knowledge of pollination, 30) and Spain (92.7% recognition of pollination as essential, 29), to communities with more limited knowledge in other areas, such as the rural district of Moshi, Tanzania (only 52% of farmers identified the role of bees in pollination, 27), and/or the case of farmers in Gozamin district of Ethiopia, where 77% lack pollination knowledge [4]. Multiple studies have documented this variation [31–36], underscoring the need for further research on the particularities of LEKP in different agricultural contexts.
Research on LEKP among family farmers shows that it is influenced by several sociocultural variables. Educational level is one of the most explored variables. Several authors have noted its relationship to various aspects of LEKP, including knowledge of pollination [4, 30, 32], pollinating insects [4], bees [34, 35], and the perception of the socioecological benefits of pollinators [30]. Other variables related to these LEKP-linked themes have also been documented, including gender [32, 35], age [30], linkage/dialog with rural extension workers, media access and consumption [4, 34], agricultural experience (seniority), and time of residence in the location [30, 34].
In Patagonia, the region where this study was conducted, research on LEKP is notably absent. In contrast, a significant body of work exists on the LEK of other vital subsistence resources. These studies have consistently shown that knowledge of cultivated plants is positively associated with the size of productive spaces, the number of management practices, and an individual’s age (see [20, 37, 38]). Consequently, it is crucial to determine whether these same variables positively influence LEKP in order to understand the factors that shape its vitality.
Furthermore, despite their importance in pollination, knowledge of floral rewards and their relationship to agricultural practices has received little attention in farmer research. These rewards, which include mainly nectar and pollen, are the resources that attract and sustain mutualistic interaction. Ali et al. [39] working with cucurbit growers in southern Punjab, Pakistan, found that approximately 57% recognized nectar as the main resource. In contrast, Misganaw et al. [4] found that farmers in a region of Amhara, Ethiopia, cited both pollen and nectar as resources. In Argentina, Baranzelli et al. [40] conducted an educational workshop and observed that, afterward, 50% of students cited (after the workshop was conducted) pollen as the main foraged resource. These results show that knowledge of floral rewards varies significantly depending on the geographic context or the human group with which one is working. Local ecological knowledge about these rewards can provide valuable insights to complement scientific research. Failing to consider this knowledge can have negative consequences for biodiversity conservation and agricultural practices because farmers may unknowingly implement practices that harm pollinators by not providing them with necessary resources.
Farmer’s habitat management practices are one of the elements highlighted when addressing pollinator conservation and care [23]. These practices include protecting and restoring habitats, increasing the quality and quantity of floral resources, reducing intensive mechanical practices, reducing chemical inputs, and promoting nesting sites [41]. Many of these management practices are currently used in local systems [23] and overlap with practices affecting other environmental components, such as vegetation or soil [42]. For example, one management practice is creating or leaving crop edges, which help to increase the quality and quantity of resources available to pollinators. These species edges (usually wild) can be the result from tolerating, inducing, protecting, or planting plants. Casas et al. [43] described all these practices in the context of domestication, but they can also be part of creating and maintaining crop edges.
In this paper, we characterize the LEKP of family farmers in northern Patagonia. Specifically, we document how farmers perceive the pollination process, including their understanding of floral structures and their interactions with insect visitors. We explore the productive and ecological importance they attribute to this process, identify the organisms they recognize as pollinators, and detail their knowledge of the resources these visitors seek, such as pollen and nectar. Furthermore, we describe the management practices they carry out that may directly or indirectly benefit these pollinators. Finally, we quantitatively analyze whether this knowledge is related to key sociocultural and farm variables, such as the number of practices, the farmers’ experience, the cultivated area, and the richness of plant species on their farms.

## Methods

## Study area
This study was conducted in three locations in northern Patagonia, Argentina (Fig. 1). The collaborating family farmers are mostly Creoles descended from European immigrants, mainly Spanish. Some are descendants of the indigenous Mapuche people, and a few are first-generation immigrants from neighboring countries, mainly Chile.
Locations of the collaborating farmers who participated in this study
The families that participated in this study live in two areas with different environments. Those who live and work in drier regions inhabit the locality of Villa Llanquín (40°53′40″ S, 71°02′11″W), which is located in the Patagonian steppe ecoregion [44]. This region is dominated by grassy and shrubby-grassy vegetation. In contrast, farmers in the wooded region are from the localities of Río Villegas (41°34′41″S, 71°30′06″W); located at the intersection of National Route 40 and Provincial Route 38 and El Manso (41°35′48″S, 71°31′05″W); a place developed along Provincial Route 83. Both sites are within the boundaries of Nahuel Huapi National Park. These sites belong to the Patagonian Forest ecoregion [44], dominated by plant species of the genus Nothofagus.
The ecological diversity of Patagonia is largely driven by strong gradients in humidity, altitude, temperature, and soil, which influence both plant [44] and animal communities [45]. Among the fauna, the pollinator assemblage is particularly diverse, reflecting this environmental variation. Across these gradients, the dominant insect pollinators include Hymenoptera, Diptera, Lepidoptera, and Coleoptera [46], while the region’s flora also supports a community of avian pollinators [47]. However, to date, no pollinating bats have been recorded in the study area [48].
Villa Llanquín is located 40 km from San Carlos de Bariloche. It is a town located on National Route 9, bordering the Limay River. The main activities are cattle raising and horticulture for family consumption. Only the surplus is sold. In recent years, this area has also gained relevance in the tourism industry, leading many families to rely on tourism as their main source of income. Production is done under cover due to frost and strong winds or in the open air [38].
Both Villegas and El Manso belong to the commune of El Manso, which is located approximately 70 km from Bariloche and 30 km from El Bolsón. These are major cities in the region where people go for medical assistance and to buy supplies and food. Both places are located in the Manso River valley, which provides a mild microclimate for growing crops under cover due to frost and for producing fine fruits and other fruit trees. In both Río Villegas and El Manso, livestock activity predominates [49]; however, tourism has increased in importance in recent years. Among the main fine fruit crops are: raspberries, currants, and cherries [49]. Many farmers also produce sweets and other products derived from these fruits. They collect and market mushrooms and make salted preserves [50].
The locations in which farmers carry out their activities are environmentally very different (Steppe versus Andean Patagonian Forest), but their production systems are similar. In all cases, except for some instances where the producers are elderly and must hire personnel for heavy tasks such as soil preparation, farmers rely on family labor for production [38]. Their practices are mainly agro-ecological, based on diversity and the interrelationship between the various components of nature, as well as solidarity among farmers [3, 51].

## Data collection
Initially, the farmers were visited to establish bonds of trust and become familiar with their farms through participant observation techniques [52]. During these visits, various activities were carried out, including informal discussions about events or topics not strictly related to the research, walks through the productive spaces, and field activities such as pruning, irrigation, harvesting, and weeding. After these visits, the focus shifted to conducting interviews, which included open and closed questions.
A total of 30 interviews were conducted, 19 in the El Manso Valley and 11 in Villa Llanquín. Each collaborator was visited an average of two times, with each visit lasting between 1 and 2 h. Educated Prior Informed Consent (EPIC) was requested at the beginning of each visit in all cases. The EPIC consisted of clearly and concisely stating the names of those present, the purpose and objectives of the study, the activities to be carried out, and the methods for establishing the return of the results. Permission was also requested to take audio and photographic recordings. People of both genders, ranging in age from 29 to 83, were interviewed. The snowball technique [52] was used to select the collaborators. At the end of the talk, the collaborator was asked if he/she knew someone who was willing to collaborate and could provide his or her name and location.
During the interviews and visits to farmers, we discussed the following topics:
Which are the animals that visit their flowers?
What do animals look for in flowers?
What is “pollen” and “nectar” as perceived by the collaborators?
Is there any benefit to the plant during the animals visit to the flowers?
If they think it is important for animals to visit flowers? And why?
What are the practices carried out on farms that benefit the animals that visit their flowers?
What does a flower look like? What are its parts, and how are they linked to animals?
To determine the LEKP with respect to the variety of animals that visit flowers, the free listing technique was also used [53]. First, the collaborators were asked to count and describe the insect or bird they were referring to. Then, during the walks through the productive areas, they were asked again about the name of an animal they came across. Finally, for butterfly and fly species, photos taken during the walks or specimens collected in the productive areas were shown.
At the end of the interview process, each collaborator played a game. This allowed for the triangulation of information, which is based on analyzing the same question or topic using different methods [54]. The purpose was to deepen farmers’ knowledge about flowers and their interaction with animals, and to counteract the limitations of any isolated approach [54, 55]. A visual stimulus technique [52] using cardboard figures symbolizing different flower structures was used as a triangulation methodology [52]. In this instance, the starting point was assembling the flower to better understand the LEKP, emphasizing what happens inside the flowers when pollinators interact with them. The task consisted of the collaborator interacting with the figures, as shown below:
The contributors were asked to think of a flower from a plant that they liked or remembered.
The collaborators were asked to use the cardboard figures to create a flower similar the one they had thought of.
Once assembled, the contributors were asked about: the names of the different parts of the flower they had assembled, where the animals were seen, where the insects or birds collected pollen and/or nectar, and where the fruit was formed, if the collaborators had mentioned during the interview that the fruit came from the flowers or was related to them in some way.

## Data analysis
The data analysis was qualitative and quantitative. The analyzed variables were as follows:
Total pollinator richness The species or groups (local ethnocategories) that were cited by the collaborators during the interviews were counted.
Relative frequency of pollinators The relative frequency of the species or groups (local ethnocategories) cited was calculated as a percentage of the total number of contributors (N = 30).
Biogeographic origin of pollinators Each pollinator species or group was classified based on its origin as either native or exotic to the region. This classification was determined using information from the iNaturalist website and the BiodAr portal [56].
Cultural Significance Index (CSI). This index addresses cultural significance by taking the weight of the different terms in the formula into account. To calculate the CSI, we modified the index proposed by Turner [57]. Inserting the Correction Factor (CF), which represents the informants’ consensus, allows the CSI to reflect the species’ popularity among collaborators.
f = Frequency of mention: It takes on a maximum value of 3 and a minimum value of 1, according to how many times the species is mentioned during the interviews.
Value of 3 for animals mentioned in more than 75% of the interviews.
Value of 2 for animals mentioned between 40 and 74% of the interviews.
Value of 1 for animals mentioned in less than 40% of the interviews.
o = Order of salience: The position in which the animal is mentioned during the free listing.
It takes values from 1 to n according to the position in which the species is named during the free listing.
c = Awareness and observation: It considers whether the animal is recognized and observed by the collaborator as a floral visitor.
Value of 2 for known and frequently observed animals.
Value of 1 for poorly known and observed animals.
CF = Correction Factor. It measures the degree of consensus among study participants. CF is the number of times an animal is mentioned divided by the number of mentions of the animal with the most mentions. The maximum value it can take is 1, in the case that the species mentioned is cited by all the collaborators.
The maximum value of the first CSI component, “Ʃ (f × (1/o) × c)” can take depends on the number of species or groups cited by the collaborators. In the case that all 30 collaborators cite the same species, with a frequency of mention of 3, it has always been mentioned first and a knowledge and observation value of 2, the maximum value it can take is ((3*1*2) + (3*1*2) + … + (3*1*2)) 180.
Perception of pollination To learn about family farmers’ perception of pollination, a qualitative and quantitative analysis was conducted. The qualitative approach focuses on analyzing the discourse of the collaborators [52]. Discourse that is part of a group reveals shared beliefs, values, and social representations. Therefore, analyzing of individual discourse reflects the construction of collective thinking [58]. In addition, the information was reinterpreted by constructing ethical categories [58]. A collaborator was considered to have identified the pollination process when they used examples and in their own words to indicate that an animal was responsible for transporting “something” or “pollen” between flowers for fertilization, which is fundamental for later producing fruit and/or seeds. Key fragments of discourse were selected to reveal the farmers’ perceptions. They were coded by assigning each sentence its author’s letter P (participant) followed by a number.
Local ecological knowledge about floral rewards To determine the family farmers’ knowledge of what “animals take from the flowers” (floral rewards), we carried out a discourse analysis. This process was similar to that used for the perception of pollination [58].
Importance of the pollination process To determine the importance that family farmers attribute to pollinators for production and the environment, a closed question with three response options (“Very important,” “Important,” or “Not important”) was used. To determine the reasons behind their responses, a discourse analysis was carried out similarly to how the perception of pollination was determined [58].
Management practices The collaborators’ responses were ethically recategorized according to Table 1. The type of practice was categorized as follows:
Direct: Practices intentionally carried out in the production areas that, according to the collaborators, positively impact on the insects that visit the flowers.
Indirect: Practices carried out in productive areas that have a positive effect on pollinators from an academic point of view, but were not directly mentioned by the collaborators for this purpose.
Type of direct and indirect practices carried out by family farmers that benefit pollinators
*According to Blancas et al. [54]
The practices were classified into subcategories, and their relative frequencies were calculated. These subcategories were adapted from the classification of Blancas et al. [59], a widely used model in agroforestry systems [60] that were applied to the study of pollination practices for the first time.
Cultivated area The surface area of each productive space was estimated using the Google Earth tool. They were measured in square meters.
Seniority To determine the seniority as farmers, the interview responses were recategorized as follows:
Ep: Experienced farmers, i.e., those who have been engaged in the activity for over 10 years.
Av: Advanced farmers, i.e., those who have been involved in horticulture for 5–10 years.
In: Beginning farmers, i.e., those who are engaged in horticulture for between 2 and 4 years.
Pr: Beginning or novice growers, those who have been horticulturists for between 0 and 1 year.
Plant species richness To estimate the richness of cultivated and wild plant species in the productive areas, vegetation sampling was carried out. To this end, orchards and greenhouses were visited, and all the species present that could potentially be resources for pollinators were listed. Poaceae species were not considered since they are mostly wind-pollinated. The plant specimens are deposited in the herbarium of the Universidad Nacional del Comahue (codes in progress). The insects are deposited in the offices of the Ethnobiology Group at INIBIOMA.
Knowledge about flowers and their structures To determine family farmers’ knowledge of flowers, their structures, and their links with animals, we analyzed the diagrams created during this stage, along with the information discussed. To this end, recordings and photos were taken of the most representative diagrams. The most representative diagrams were selected based on criteria such as clarity, richness of detail, and diversity of perspectives among the participating farmers. This visual stimulus technique [53] allows access to cognitive representations through the interaction between graphic and verbal elements.
The practices and variables in Table 1 were analyzed quantitatively, using descriptive statistics. The proportions of the different categories referring to management practices were analyzed using Python’s “pandas” [61] and “matplotlib” [62].
To analyze the factors influencing LEKP, we fitted a Generalized Linear Model (GLM). The response variable was the richness of pollinator species or groups mentioned by each family farmer. Given that our response variable consisted of count data—which violates the assumptions of traditional linear regression—we specified a GLM with a Poisson error distribution and a log link function. Based on existing literature, we selected the following predictor variables, as they are known to influence knowledge acquisition and retention: cultivated area, management practices, seniority, and plant species richness.
To rule out correlation between the different variables, a Spearman correlation analysis was performed. We used the Python package “statsmodels” [63] to adjust the GLM; setting the significance level to 0.05.
Our data exploration revealed no significant differences relevant to the questions of this study, so we decided to use the combined dataset.

## Results and discussion

## Characteristics of family farmers
The family farmers who participated in this study exhibited differences in species richness, cultivated area size, horticultural experience, seniority, and educational level. The species richness of their productive spaces ranges from 29 to 134 species, including cultivated and wild plants. Among the main species are those linked to vegetable gardens such as tomatoes, potatoes, lettuce, chard, and squash; fruit trees such as apples, pears, walnuts, plums, and cherries; and berries such as raspberries, blueberries, and blackberries, among others (for a detailed list of species, see 37 and 50). Their plots range in size from 18 to 3092 square meters, averaging 861.3 square meters for the steppe productive spaces and 1228.28 square meters for the forest productive spaces. Of the 30 collaborators, 13 are considered experts, six are considered advanced, four are considered intermediate, and seven are considered beginners or novices. The educational level of farmers is very variable; most have primary education, some have secondary education, and only a few have tertiary education.

## Total pollinator species richness
A total of 16 local names were recorded for different animals cited as pollinators or referred to as “bugs that go to the flowers” (Table 2). The scientific name or group of the animal to which reference was made was known for 14 of the cited animals. The species referred to could not be identified for two Hymenoptera and one Coleoptera. The dipteran cited by the collaborators is estimated to belong to the muscid group. Of the identified animals, 12 were insects and two were birds. Of the insects, 100% of the collaborators mentioned Apis mellifera and “moscardones” (bumblebee) (Bombus species). The “black with yellow” species, B. terrestris and B. ruderatus, were distinguished from the orange species B. dahlbomii (“Mangangá”). The least mentioned groups were butterflies with 67%, flies with 0.17%, and beetles with 0.06%. Wind was also mentioned by 0.1% of people as a means of transport pollen from one plant to another. No mammals were mentioned.

Similar results regarding the recognition of animals or insects as pollinators were recorded by other authors in different production systems around the world [4, 30, 32, 64]. In these studies, farmers primarily recognize bees, particularly the genus Apis, but also other species as pollinators. Other animal groups are less visible in local perceptions as pollinators. Bees stand out in farmers’ views as floral visitors because they are conspicuous, easily recognized, large, and widely represented in technical brochures as pollinators. It could also be due to the importance of certain bees to farmers given their long cohabitation with the species [65–67], as in the case of B. dahlbomii.
In addition, we observed that the animals most visible as pollinators coincide with ecological studies in Patagonia. Several authors demonstrate that bees are the most prevalent group of pollinators in this region of the country [67–69, among others], followed by hummingbirds [70] and flies [71]. This last group of insects generally presents low visibility as pollinators; however, it is a very important group for a great diversity of plant species [72], especially in cold regions of the world [73], such as northern Patagonia.

## Biogeographical origin of pollinators
Of the 14 animals mentioned as pollinators and of which it was possible to identify the scientific name of the species or group to which they belong, 11 pollinators belong to native species or groups, and three are exotic. In the case of bumblebee, 69% of the collaborators recognized the black and yellow blowflies as exotic and the orange blowfly as native. All of the cited Lepidoptera belonged to native species.
Family farming in Patagonia depends on the contribution of both exotic and native pollinator species. Grimaldi et al. [3] observed that exotic bees are the most representative for cultivated and wild plants used for food purposes in Patagonian family farming. These species are A. mellifera, B. terrestris, and B. ruderatus, all of which were cited by the collaborators as pollinators. In addition, the authors showed that native pollinators include Sephanoides sephanoides, a bird also mentioned by all the collaborators.

## Cultural significance index (CSI)
Table 2 shows the different species mentioned and their respective CSI scores. While most of the cited pollinators are insects, although our results show that other animals are also pollinate for farmers. The species with the highest CSI are exotic species such as A. mellifera (CSI = 140.2) and bumblebee (B. terrestris and B. ruderatus, CSI = 90.14). In contrast, native species present marginal values (e.g., native butterflies with ISC ≤ 5.29), with exceptions such as hummingbirds (S. sephanoides and Chlorostilbon lucidus, CSI = 22.99). The importance of Apis and exotic Bombus species may be due to their economic utility (e.g., honey production, in the case of Apis) and their conspicuousness to observe due to their size and abundance in the environment (as are the cases of Bombus and Apis).
Exotic hymenopterans stand out for their high CSI, while their native counterparts (e.g., Ruizanthedella mutabilis, CSI = 0.007) are less frequently mentioned and have a lower CSI. Despite their diversity, butterflies show minimal CSI (e.g., Argopteron aureipennis, CSI = 0.007), possibly because they are not widely considered pollinators.
The results show disparity in the cultural valuation of pollinators. None of the named pollinators reached the maximum CSI value. This discrepancy in results might stem from a disconnect between a species’ perceived ecological role and its actual visibility. For instance, a species may be frequently mentioned (frequency of mention = 3) without always being considered primary or highly salient (o > 3), and may be even observed with low frequencies (c = 1).

## Perception of pollination by farmers
Ninety-seven percent of the farmers identified the process of pollination, and 50% provided examples or explanations of how pollen transport between flowers occurs. The other 50% referred to the process using the term coined by biological sciences “pollination.” This process is known as a reciprocal interaction between the organisms involved, that is, an interaction between plants (specifically their flowers) and insects (or other animals). Phrases such as “It fertilizes the plant… it acts as a courier from plant to plant” (P22), “The crossing of pollen that insects make from one flower to another so that the fruit reproduces” (P14), and “… they are good for the plants. But they take something from the plants and leave something else… these bugs make an exchange” (P4), and “They have a relationship, how do you say, of reciprocity” (P3). These phrases allow us to observe how pollination is perceived among family farmers.
The richness of farmers’ knowledge on pollinators and pollination is likely the product of a dynamic and multifaceted learning process. Indeed, research in this same Patagonian context has shown that local ecological knowledge—in that case, regarding harmful arthropods—is acquired and transmitted through a diverse array of channels. These include direct personal experience, intergenerational learning, engagement with government extension agents, media exposure, and peer-to-peer exchange with other farmers [74]. Although our study did not directly investigate these transmission pathways, it is highly plausible that the same complex network of formal and informal learning underpins the sophisticated knowledge of pollination we observed.

## Importance of pollination process
All the collaborators mentioned that pollination is important. For 93% of the farmers, pollination was key to horticultural production, as well as to many plants outside their productive spaces. There, “the bug carries pollen from one flower to another” to facilitate “flower setting” (fruit and seed formation). Statements, such as “Everything is an ecosystem, it is a wheel that leads to the fruit coming well” (P2), “It is very important for plants, their reproduction, and for the bugs to have food and be able to live” (P3), and “It is also important for the forest plants, yes also, that is why it is important the presence of bugs, birds, wind, it is like a chain” (P11), suggest that farmers know the importance of pollination. They do not see pollination only as a “contribution” or a “service” provided by nature, but rather as a process on which they depend on and are part of.
Our results, therefore, stand in contrast with many global studies [4, 27, 33–36], demonstrating a profound understanding of pollination among these farmers. They conceptualize it not as a simple input, but as a core ecological relationship that underpins the health of their entire agroecosystem, highlighting a deeply relational worldview.
These findings underscore the perils of assuming that academic and local knowledge discourses are interchangeable. They emphasize the crucial need to value and understand local knowledge within its unique context [34]. Assuming that different knowledge systems (hegemonic academic and local) understand and/or describe a system or process in the same way can lead to misunderstandings [75]. Local ecological knowledge about pollination, like all LEK, is based on local perceptions that are anchored in ways of interpreting biological processes according to cultural norms and valuations [76]. Failure to describe the process as ecologists do does not directly imply a lack of knowledge.

## Local ecological knowledge about floral rewards
Ninety-three percent of respondents indicated that animals look for food resources in flowers: “It is for their food, and it is good for the bees to make honey” (P13), and “Because they need it to live. It is part of their food, or they process it to make it their food” (12). Thirty-six percent named both pollen and nectar as floral resources. Fifty percent named only pollen as a resource and 14% named only nectar. The collaborators observed pollen as a yellowish powder, although they stated that its color can vary depending on the plant species that the pollinators visit. In addition, the collaborators stated that pollen is collected and transported by honeybees or blowflies on their legs as if they were “little balls.” Nectar is known as an “agüita” or a honey, which is found in the center of the flowers. It has a sweet taste, and bees collect it to make honey because they like it.
Very little research has examined farmers’ knowledge of floral rewards; most existing studies have focused exclusively on honey production systems [77–79]. These studies primarily investigate whether farmers recognize pollen and nectar [4, 36, 39], often overlooking crucial related dimensions. Key questions such as why these resources are collected, how they are identified, where they can be found, and details such as pollen color and nectar taste remain largely unaddressed. Exploring these aspects of local knowledge reveals the diverse meanings ascribed to floral rewards from a local perspective. Ignoring these critical dimensions of Local Ecological Knowledge (LEK) risks fragmenting a fundamentally holistic knowledge system. This fragmentation, in turn, hinders the development and adoption of more inclusive and respectful conservation strategies [66, 76].

## Management practices
On average, farmers carry out of six practices/person (sd = 2.2) related to pollination. On average, they perform two direct and four indirect practices. Most of the management practices that family farmers carry out to favor pollinators are indirect (67.2%), while the rest are direct (32.8%) (Fig. 2). The most frequently performed practice is soil preparation (15.87%, an indirect practice), followed by tolerance of certain plants (14.28%, a direct practice). The least frequently performed practices are crop rotation (6.87%, an indirect practice) and induction (2.12%, a direct practice).
Management practices carried out by farmers
Within each type of practice, 43.55% of the direct practices correspond to tolerance, 38.7% to seeding, 11.3% to plant protection, and 6.45% to induction. Among the indirect practices, 23.62% are due to soil preparation, 18.11% correspond to interseeding and spontaneous growth without management, 15.75% to organic management, 14.17% to seed production, and 10.24% to crop rotation.
The “tolerance” is a practice that can be involved in various processes in family farming systems, such as managing harmful arthropods [74] and managing woody species in agroforestry [60]. In this work, we introduce three additional practices with a broader scope to the framework in which they were originally proposed and described, i.e., domestication. These practices are induction (e.g., of Alstroemeria aurea or Trifolium pratense), protection (e.g., Maytenus boaria or Schinus johnstonii), and seeding (e.g., Diplotaxis tenuifolia or Calendula officinalis). For example, farmers propagate A. aurea vegetatively, through its rhizomes to increase the number of individuals. M. boaria grows around productive areas and receives care such as pruning, watering, and protection against herbivory. Farmers propagate the seeds of D. tenuifolia, also known as “wild arugula” and sow them inside their greenhouses because they consume and commercialize this species. Thus, the plants that receive some of these management practices are linked to maintaining diversity in crop borders, protecting the soil, protecting against winds, and ornamenting the orchard, among other purposes. Thus, tolerance, induction, protection, and seeding are practices associated with pollination. These practices increase the quality and quantity of resources, provide nesting sites, and reduce the use of synthetic chemicals.

## Local ecological knowledge about pollinators and their relationship to different variables
The GLM analysis indicated that none of the selected predictor variables—cultivated area, management intensity, age, or plant diversity—were significant predictors of pollinator knowledge (LEKP) (p > 0.05, Table 3). This suggests that these factors are not the main determinants of an individual’s knowledge about pollinators in this community. Our finding aligns with Pozzi and Ladio [80] in Patagonia, who also reported a lack of relationship between LEK about fauna and socio-demographic factors. Yet, it diverges from Eyssartier et al. [37], who found that garden size and plant richness did predict knowledge about plants in the same region, highlighting that the drivers of knowledge may be subject-dependent. Ultimately, this highlights the complex, context-specific nature of LEK. The drivers of ecological knowledge may not be universal, and other variables could explain the patterns in our study. For example, international research points to the importance of formal education [4, 34] and access to extension services [35] in shaping pollination knowledge. Exploring these social and educational pathways represents a key avenue for future research to understand the dynamics of LEKP in our region.

## Local ecological knowledge about flowers
In 100% of cases, the contributors cited flowers as the plant structure from which fruits and seeds, i.e., reproductive structures, are derived. Figure 3 shows three diagrams of flowers assembled by three contributors. We can see aspects in common among them. All flowers have colored structures on the periphery called “petals.” These structures are mentioned in all cases as protective structures that give visibility to the flowers, making the flowers conspicuous to people and insects. The figures also show that the flowers have a central part, where the pollinators go to look for pollen and nectar. The collaborators did not mention the names of the floral structures referred to the gynoecium and the androcecium. In addition, the nectaries are generally located in the central part of the flowers. Although the ovary can be in different positions, flowers are generally represented (from an academic perspective) as superior ovary flowers (hypogynous flower). This is when the ovary is positioned above the sterile floral parts, such as petals and sepals. The part identified by the collaborators as the part of the flower where fruit formation takes place, corresponds precisely to the area where the ovary would be in a hypogynous flower. The area where the fruit is formed, from the local point of view, is in the lower part of the center of the flower, above the “stick” (pedicel).

For the collaborators, the green cardboard structures symbolized leaves, and different structures were used to refer to the branches. Having structures to use as starting and reference point allowed the collaborators to rethink the task and complete the scheme they were being asked to carry out. This simple game, conceived from the compartmentalized academic knowledge about assembling a flower and decontextualized from its origin, is another example of the importance of openness and the ability to reformulate the activities carried out with the collaborators.
Farmers do not use terms such as “stamens,” “gynoecium,” “ovary,” “stigma,” etc., when describing flowers. This does not indicate ignorance about the role of flowers and how they function in relation to the animals that visit them. The various local and academic names of flower parts are not mutually exclusive. They represent different ways of understanding the same phenomenon. Farmers have a deep knowledge of the function of flowers and their relationship with pollinating insects, even if their terminology differs from that of scientific botany [36].
Initially thought of as a triangulation stage, it became one of the fundamental stages of fieldwork. It allowed us to understand, from a local perspective, how flowers are observed and understood, and their importance in relation to insects. It facilitated dialog between different knowledge systems, improving our interpretation of the entire research process and our chosen approach [81].

## Final thoughts
Following Vandebroeck et al. [82], our work shows that research with communities must transcend mere data collection. It should be a dialogic process in which reciprocity and reflexivity structure the relationship between researchers and collaborators. In this study, interactions after the interviews became spaces for knowledge triangulation, in a reflexive and bidirectional manner rather than mere closing the research protocol. Topics such as pollination and floral morphology were reinterpreted through dialog intertwining academic and local knowledge, allowing for a holistic understanding of ecological interactions and their importance.
Ethics in fieldwork cannot be reduced to static protocols [82], but rather must be integrated into the daily relationships, where “learning to listen” takes precedence over data extraction. Post-interview stages (e.g., triangulation) serve as spaces where researchers and community members co-produce narratives about nature [83], including floral structure, its role in the environment and its relationships with other organisms, such as pollinators.
However, reflexivity requires more than an intellectual exercise, it demands a dynamic ethical commitment. As Furlan et al. [75] and Sutherland et al. [84] warn, omitting this principle generates interpretive biases or misleading, especially when social techniques are applied without adapting them to local dynamics. Therefore, openness to the unexpected, as proposed by Moon et al. [81], is both an option and a methodological necessity.

## Conclusion
This study is the first in Argentina to explore LEKP among family farmers, highlighting the importance of pollinators in these systems. The study underscores the complexity and richness of local knowledge regarding pollinators and their significance in family farming. We identified 16 local names for various pollinating animals. Among them are animals with great cultural importance, such as Apis mellifera, Bombus species, and hummingbirds. Given these findings, it is crucial to implement biocultural strategies that recognize and promote the value of pollinating animals beyond the bee group. Developing lines of participatory conservation could positively influence the revaluation of native pollinators of all orders, aligning their ecological importance with culturally meaningful narratives.
Remarkably, 97% of farmers recognize the pollination process, and 93% highlight its relevance for fruit, seed, and horticultural production. These results contrast with those from other communities in different regions of the world. Without delving into the specifics of the methodologies employed used in the cited studies, we believe that there are misunderstandings that could be clarified with alternative methodological tools: Are farmers unaware of pollination (and pollinators), or do they simply not engage with local discourses that reveal the links between people and pollinators? Our results show that only 50% of farmers used the term “pollination” to refer to the process. Had we asked closed questions in academic language, the results might have been different. Building rapport, establishing bonds of trust, discussing related topics, and acquiring different ways of naming or referring to a topic, are some of the essential steps to achieving approximations that try to reduce misunderstandings.
We observed that farmers’ management practices, both direct and indirect, have great potential for environment conservation and biodiversity promotion. Many of these practices increase the quantity and availability of resources, diversify nesting sites, and help protect or create healthy habitats. Practices that promote these benefits include tolerance, induction, protection, and seeding. These practices are described for the first time as influencing plant–pollinator interactions.
A very important moment of the fieldwork emerged during the last stage of the interview. What was intended as a space for triangulation became a space for dialog. The post-interview dialog between the collaborators and the authors addressed diverse topics related to pollination, bees, and flowers. This study highlights the need for contextualized and specific approaches. Approaches where reflexivity, recognition of the epistemic value of the collaborators and reciprocity (i.e., the exchange of knowledge that enriches both the academia and communities), are the principles that guide ethnobiological work and that of other related disciplines. This will allow us to better understand the ecological and social dynamics that influence this knowledge.
Finally, our results allow us to reaffirm the idea that pollination is a biocultural process involving an intimate interrelationship between humans and non-humans. This human–plant–pollinator interrelationship depends on the knowledge of the people who manage the environment and other components for their agro-food production, thereby promoting conservation.
Our findings on local pollination knowledge are not only of academic interest but also hold significant value for conservation initiatives. This locally-grounded information serves as a critical foundation for effective, community-based pollinator conservation [85]. Additionally, understanding the pollination processes recognized by farmers is essential for developing successful participatory programs. Integrating local ecological knowledge with scientific approaches is a key step toward creating sustainable strategies that support both regional biodiversity and the livelihoods of family farmers.
