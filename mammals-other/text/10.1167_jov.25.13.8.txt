Visual field of the ferret ( Mustela putorius furo ), rat ( Rattus norvegicus ), and tree shrew ( Tupaia belangeri )
Jacob M. Morris, Esteban Fernández‐Juricic, Caryn E. Plummer, Bret Alan Moore (2025). Journal of Vision. https://doi.org/10.1167/jov.25.13.8. doi:10.1167/jov.25.13.8. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Visual field of the ferret (Mustela putorius furo), rat (Rattus norvegicus), and tree shrew (Tupaia belangeri)

## Abstract
AbstractTo describe the visual field of three common model species in vision science to understand the organization of their visual perceptual experience and contribute to continued studies of visual processing. Visual fields were measured using an ophthalmoscopic reflex technique in four common ferrets, four albino rats, and six northern tree shrews. Animals were anesthetized to avoid stress and the midpoint between their eyes was centered inside a spherical space. A rotating perimetric arm was manipulated in 10° increments around the head. At each increment, direct ophthalmoscopy was used to visualize limits of the retinal reflex for each eye, the overlap being the binocular visual field. Mean binocularity in the horizontal plane was 63.7 ± 5.1°, 79.1 ± 7.4°, and 53.6 ± 12.0° in the ferret, rat, and shrew, respectively. Maximum mean binocularity was 69.0 ± 1.6° in the ferret, 90.0 ± 3.1° in the rat, and 53.6 ± 12.2° in the shrew, located at 10° above, 40° above, and at the horizontal plane, respectively. Binocularity extended to 160°, 200°, and 180° in the sagittal plane in the ferret, rat, and shrew, respectively, from at least below the nose to above the head in all animals. Establishing the extent of the visual field accessible to the retina provides insight into the egocentric perceptual experience of animals. In describing the visual field, we provide a reference for the representation of the visual space in different cortical and retinal regions, many of which represent specific subregions of the visual field.Keywords: rodent, binocular field, blind area, ferret, rat, shrew

## Introduction
Sight begins with photons of light entering the eye. Visualization of light particles is limited to an individual's visual field, the three-dimensional region around the head from which visual information can be received. The visual field is shaped by the orientation of the eyes in the orbit, adnexal and other craniofacial structures (e.g., hair, feathers, skin folds, long muzzles, or beaks, which can dramatically influence the configuration of visual space), and the optics of the eye itself (Allman & Martin, 1999; Heesy, 2004). Three principal components of visual fields have been described: the monocular visual fields, the binocular visual field, and the blind area. Monocular visual fields describe the regions of space from which visual information can be received by either eye individually (Walsh & Walsh, 2020; Wolter, 1972). The binocular visual field refers to the overlapping portion of the two monocular fields where individual images from separate eyes are visualized together but from different perspectives, often combined into a single fused image. Binocular vision plays an important role in visual processes including binocular fixation and stereopsis (Jones & Lee, 1981; Niechwiej-Szwedo, Colpa, & Wong, 2023; Read, 2021; Sheedy, Bailey, Buri, & Bass, 1986). Last, the spaces around the head where there is no retina to initiate a visual signal constitute the blind areas (Martin, 2007).
Once light enters the eye and interacts with the retina, the visual system displays sophisticated relationships between numerous visual components, such as the topography of retinal photoreceptors and ganglion cells, intraretinal and cortical processing, and even leading to behavioral outputs (Baden, Euler, & Berens, 2019; Blakeslee, Jacobs, & McCourt, 1985; Moore et al., 2017; Moore, Tyrrell, Pita, Bininda-Emonds, & Fernández-Juricic, 2017b; Rolls & Cowey, 1970; Tyrell & Fernández-Juricic, 2017; Vallortigara, Rogers, Bisazza, Lippolis, & Robins, 1998; Warwick, Kaushansky, Sarid, Golan, & Rivlin-Etzion, 2018; Woolsey, Carlton, Kaas, & Earls, 1971; Yokoi & Komatsu, 2010). Within the brain, multiple brain regions contain neurons that are responsive to specific regions of the visual field, sampled by the photoreceptors in the retina (Lee, Tran, Turan, & Meister, 2020; Rajendran Nair, Camarillo, Lu, & Thomas 2024; Sibille et al., 2022). Often, nearby neurons in the brain signal changes in adjacent visual field locations (Ito & Feldheim, 2018; Rumberger, Tyler, & Lund, 2001; Sibille et al., 2022). The mapping of nearby retinal neurons onto related regions of the brain is referred to as retinotopic mapping.
To better understand the visual system of a given species, complete visual mapping, from the visual field to retinotopy in the brain, is important. Avian species have long been a topic of interest for visual field studies, beginning with in the early 1980s (Martin, 1984) after the implementation of the retinal reflex technique for visual field measurements. To the present date, the visual fields of over 180 bird species across 32 families have been recorded (Cantlay et al., 2023; Martin, 2017; Potier et al., 2018; Potier et al., 2023). Using the same technique, a single study on two species of fish (Pita, Moore, Tyrrell, & Fernández-Juricic, 2015), has been performed, although no amphibian, reptilian, or mammalian studies have been published to date. Surprisingly, considering the importance of understanding the visual system in model species for human studies, there are no published records of visual field information for several common laboratory animals used as model species for retinotopic mapping and cortical vision studies.
The present study evaluated the visual fields of the Northern tree shrew (Tupaia belangeri), the common ferret (Mustela putorius furo), and the Sprague–Dawley albino laboratory rat (Rattus norvegicus). Considering the phylogenetic, anatomical, ecological, and developmental differences between these three species, we predicted significant differences in their visual fields. Considering the use of binocular vision for visual acuity in mammals, and the relatively high visual acuity in tree shrews and ferrets compared with rodents (Sajdak et al., 2019), we predicted that binocularity would be greater in tree shrews and ferrets than in rats. Between tree shrews and ferrets, we predicted the ferret would have the largest region of binocular overlap, owing in part to the more frontal orientation of their orbits (Antonelli, Leischner, & Hartstone-Rose, 2022). Additionally, considering the prominent anatomical positioning of the eyes and anti-predator vigilance behavior of rats (Davis, Roma, & Hienz, 2016), we predicted the total field of view would be greater in this species compared with the tree shrew and ferret. Providing estimates of the principal components of the retinal visual field will allow visual science researchers to have a more complete view of visual processing and ultimately a better understanding of the organization of the visual perceptual experience in these species.

## Materials and methods
This study was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Max Planck Florida Institute of Neuroscience, with adherence to ARVO Statement for the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research.
Six (3 male, 3 female) Northern tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri), four (all females) common ferrets (Mustela putorius furo), and four (2 males, 2 females) Sprague–Dawley albino laboratory rats (Rattus norvegicus) underwent in vivo visual field estimations. Of the six Northern tree shrews, one juvenile animal was included. All other animals included were healthy adults of similar age and body mass. Rats were not aged, but rather weighed to achieve morphometric consistency. The mean body mass was 180.0 ± 12.7g (range, 165–195g) for tree shrews, 521.0 ± 13.9g (range, 502–534g) for ferrets, and 305.0 ± 4.8g (range, 300–310g) for rats. The estimated mean intranodal distance (distance between left and right optic nerve heads per subject) was 17.9 ± 1.5 mm (range, 15.26–19.1 mm), 19.3 ± 0.9 mm (range, 18.5–20.6 mm), and 13.9 ± 1.9 mm (range, 12.1–16.5 mm) in shrews, ferrets, and rats, respectively. At the time of enrollment, all animals underwent ophthalmic examinations by a board-certified veterinary ophthalmologist to evaluate for evidence of ophthalmic disease.
Visual field measurements were conducted under scotopic conditions in anesthetized animals using the ophthalmoscopic reflex technique described by Martin (1984). The measurement of visual fields in anesthetized individuals is not common (but see Martin, 1986a). We chose to anesthetize individuals to minimize stress, particularly in tree shrews and ferrets, when trying to fixate their heads in a stationary position within the visual field apparatus. In tree shrews, anesthesia was induced with intramuscular injections of ketamine (50 mg/kg) and midazolam (5 mg/kg) followed by a subcutaneous injection of atropine (0.5 mg/kg). Anesthesia was induced in ferrets with an intramuscular injection of ketamine (12.5 mg/kg) followed by a subcutaneous injection of atropine (0.2 mg/kg). In rats, anesthesia was induced with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (75mg/kg) and xylazine (7.5 mg/kg) and maintained with additional doses as necessary. Tree shrews and ferrets were subsequently intubated with appropriately sized cuffed endotracheal tubes. Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane gas inhalant at concentrations ranging from 1% to 3% carried by 100% oxygen at 0.25 to 0.50 L/min. Tree shrews and ferrets were supported with 5% dextrose solution (1 mL/h) and lactated ringers administered at 1.5 mL/kg/h. All animals received anesthetic monitoring including rectal temperature, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, and capnography.
Individual animals were placed in a visual field perimeter apparatus with their heads centered within the apparatus in all three dimensions. Head position was maintained at the natural resting position for these animals, determined by examining each species in their enclosures and through photographs and videos of them in their natural environment. The resting position for the tree shrews, rats, and ferrets occurred with the dorsal bridge of their nasal bones aligned in a ventral orientation approximately 30°, 35°, and 40° from the horizontal plane, respectively. Eyelids were manipulated manually to achieve normal anatomical positioning such that the superior eyelid margins rested at the level of the superior perilimbal cornea during data collection. The inferior eyelid was left unmanipulated and naturally assumed normal anatomic positioning throughout periods of data collection. Corneas were intermittently lubricated with a balanced saline solution between measurements.
Once animals were positioned within the visual perimeter, each eye was examined with a Welch Allyn 3.5 V coaxial ophthalmoscope positioned at the level of the perimeter arm. The alignment of the animal's head within the perimeter was such that the rim was centered at the midpoint between the interpupillary distance and the positions of the visual fields were described in reference to this point. The perimeter coordinate system followed conventional latitude and longitude with the equator aligned at the animal's median sagittal plane. The perimeter arm was rotated in 10° increments in the sagittal plane, with 0° denoting above the head, encircling the animal in a rostral direction such that 90° was the forward horizontal plane, 180° was directly below the head, and 270° is horizontally caudal (as diagrammatically depicted in Martin, 1984). At each elevation, the ophthalmoscope was moved along the perimeter arm and the limits of the retinal visual fields (defined by the positions at which the retinal reflex was no longer observed using direct ophthalmoscopy) were determined as a function of elevation from the median sagittal plane, to the nearest 1°. Estimations of internodal measurements were obtained with electronic digital calipers (Neiko 01407A) at the level of the dorsomedial orbital rim. The visual field measurements were then corrected to a hypothetical viewing point of infinite distance (optical infinity) following Martin (1984), based on the diameter of the visual field apparatus perimeter. The correction factor, x, was determined by the equation: tanx=a(sinB)b-(cosB), where a = half the separation of the nodal points, b = the perimeter radius, and B = the vertical degree elevation from the horizontal plane. Using the corrected data, digital plots of the principal components of the visual field (blind area and monocular and binocular visual fields) were constructed.

## Statistical analysis
We produced figures showing the mean ± standard deviation width of the binocular field and blind areas for each species across the different elevations. These figures included all measurements obtained during sampling. To analyze these data statistically, general linear mixed models were run, including species, elevation, and the interaction between species and elevation as independent factors. The identity of the individual was included as a random effect (i.e., random intercepts model) because the same individual was represented in the dataset at multiple elevations. In these mixed models, only elevations where each species was represented with data were included to allow for the model convergence. Additionally, a general linear model was run to establish if there were differences among species in the vertical extent of the binocular field. In this model, only one data point per individual was included. We checked for the homogeneity of variance and normality of residuals assumptions of all models. All analyses were run in R (R Core Team, 2024), using the afex package (Singmann, Bolker, Westfall, Aust, & Ben-Shachar, 2024) for the linear mixed models, and the emmeans package (Lenth, 2024) for the least square means and post-hoc tests (under the t distribution and correcting for multiple comparisons). All R-code (Supplementary Material S1) and data files (Supplementary Material S2 and S3) used for the statistical analysis and Figure 1 was done in Excel, Word, and Adobe Illustrator. Figures 2 and 3 are available for download. We used generative artificial intelligence (Microsoft Copilot, 2025) to generate only the heads of the animals in Figure 1. ## Results
In vivo retinal visual field measurements were successfully performed in five Northern tree shrews (two males, three females), four common ferrets (four females) and four albino laboratory rats (two males, two females). One male Northern tree shrew experienced and unpreventable, anesthetic related death at the time of data collection and was excluded from the analyses. The mean age of tree shrews was 774.0 ± 600.7 days (range, 167–1,511 days), and 66.5 ± 0.6 days (range, 66–67 days) in ferrets.
In the natural resting position, the mean extent of the cyclopean visual field (i.e., visual field coverage around the head excluding the blind area) in the horizontal plane (i.e. 90°) encompassed 306.5 ± 2.9° in the Northern tree shrew, 271.0 ± 3.9° in the common ferret, and 295.4 ± 9.7° in the albino rat (Figure 1). The visual space in this horizontal plane consisted of two monocular fields of 126.4 ± 6.1°, 103.6 ± 2.7°, and of 108.2 ± 6.3°, and a binocular field of 53.6 ± 12.0°, 63.7 ± 5.1°, and 79.1 ± 7.4° in the Northern tree shrew, common ferret, and albino rat, respectively. In the horizontal plane, the blind area spanned 53.5 ± 2.9°, 89.0 ± 2.9°, and 64.6 ± 9.7° in the Northern tree shrew, common ferret, and albino rat, respectively. The mean configuration of the visual field components in the horizontal plane is shown in Figure 1.
The mean width of the binocular field for each species relative to elevation around the head is shown in Supplementary Material S4a. The maximum mean binocularity was located at elevation 90° in the Northern tree shrew, elevation 100° in the common ferret, and 130° in the albino rat. The mean width of the blind area for each species relative to elevation around the head is shown in Supplementary Material S4b. The maximum mean blind area was located at elevation 270° in the Northern tree shrew, elevation 290° in the common ferret, and 270° in the albino rat (Supplementary Material S4b).
The linear mixed models, considering the elevations with representation from each species, identified species, F (2, 10) = 45.07, p < 0.001, elevation, F (15, 145) = 89.41, p < 0.001, and the interaction between these two, F (30, 145) = 5.00, p < 0.001, as significant factors influencing binocular field width. Across the sixteen elevations included in the model, albino rats (67.4 ± 2.7°) had significantly wider binocular fields than common ferrets, 35.4 ± 2.4°; t (10) = 7.57, p = 0.001, and Northern tree shrews, 32.6 ± 2.8°; t (10) = 8.86, p < 0.001, without significant differences between the latter two species, t (10) = 0.87, p = 0.671 (Figure 2). However, the direction of the significant differences changed depending on elevation owing to the significant interaction effect (means and post hoc tests reported in Supplementary Material S4a). For instance, at the top of the head (0°) and at the horizontal plane (90°), albino rats had wider binocular fields than the other two species, but at elevation 130°, the differences among all pairs of species were all significant (Supplementary Material S4a).
The linear mixed models, considering only the elevations with representation from each species, identified species, F (2, 10) = 37.71, p < 0.001, elevation, F (5, 46) = 76.92, p < 0.001, and the interaction between these two, F (10, 46) = 3.68, p = 0.001, as significant factors influencing blind area width. Across the six elevations considered in this model, common ferrets (79.7 ± 4.0°) had significantly wider blind areas than Northern tree shrews, 35.4 ± 3.6°; t (9) = 8.23, p < 0.001, and albino rats, 41.7 ± 4.1°; t (10) = −6.66, p < 0.001, without significant differences between the latter two species, t (10) = 1.16, p = 0.500 (Figure 3). However, the direction of the significant differences varied depending on elevation because of the significant interaction effect (means and post hoc tests reported in Supplementary Material S4b). For instance, the differences among species were more pronounced at elevations 290° through 310° than the other two elevations (Supplementary Material S4b).
In the Northern tree shrew, binocularity extended from 60° below to 110° above the horizontal plane (Figure 1a). In common ferrets, binocularity extended from 50° below to 100° above the horizontal plane (Figure 1b). In albino rats, binocularity extended from 60° below to 130° above the horizontal plane (Figure 1c). The vertical extent of the binocular field differed significantly among species, F (2, 10) = 12.49, p = 0.002. Common ferrets (142 ± 6°) had significantly smaller binocular field vertical extent than Albino rats, 182 ± 6°; t (10) = 4.87, p = 0.002, and Northern tree shrews, 170 ± 5°; t (10) = -3.53, p = 0.014, without significant differences between the latter two species, t (10) = 1.60, p = 0.289.

## Discussion
The present study is the first to provide visual field representations in mammals based on the retinal reflex technique, a well-established method that has largely been used to study avian visual fields (Baumhardt, Moore, Doppler, & Fernández-Juricic, 2014; Brewer et al., 2023; Cantlay et al., 2023; Fernández-Juricic et al., 2011; Martin, 2017; Moore, Doppler, Young, & Fernández-Juricic, 2013; Moore, Pita, Tyrrell, & Fernández-Juricic, 2015; Moore et al., 2017, Potier et al., 2018; Potier et al., 2023; Tyrrell, Moore, Loftis, & Fernández-Juricic, 2013; Tyrrell & Fernández-Juricic, 2017). Similar to birds, we found interspecific variation in visual field configuration in the three mammals studied. At the horizontal plane, the differences in visual coverage among the three species spanned about 35°, with Northern tree shrews having the largest coverage and common ferrets, the smallest, and albino rats being closer to the upper values of coverage. However, albino rats had the widest binocular fields on average, followed by Northern tree shrews and common ferrets. Finally, the common ferret showed wider blind areas on average, followed by albino rats and Northern tree shrews. The degree of difference between species in these visual field parameters changed depending on elevation around the head.
The visual field of the tree shrew had a small region of dorsofrontal binocularity and two large peripheral monocular visual fields. When compared with the tree shrew, ferrets had larger regions of binocular overlap and a much larger blind area. Variations in orbital positioning can dramatically influence the configuration of an animal's visual space (Heesy, 2004). We suggest that the large lateral fields in the tree shrew are a consequence of more laterally positioned orbits, whereas the larger binocular fields in the ferret are due to the their more frontally positioned orbits.
The albino rat has orbits positioned approximately 60° lateral to midline and 40° dorsal to horizontal axis (Hughes, 1979). In the current study, we found that rats had the greatest maximum mean binocularity (approximately 90°) of the three species. We suggest that this finding is influenced largely by shallow orbits resulting in a natural anterior protrusion of the globes seen in the albino rat when compared with the tree shrew and ferret. It is possible that that albino rats necessitate an increased region of binocularity to navigate their natural environment. A previous report found that dark agouti rats have wide binocular fields, with a maximum binocular field of 85° (Hughes, 1979). The slight difference in binocular field width between these two rats could be related to albinism, resting pupil width, or even their relatively close degree of phylogenetic separation.
Some degree of overhead binocularity (i.e., vertical extent of the binocular field above the head) was noted in all three species, which are ground dwelling and subject to attacks by aerial predators (Majumder & Agarwala, 2015). We suspect that the overhead binocularity may reflect an evolutionary response for a heightened ability to detect approaching aerial predators supplemented by vigilance with wide lateral fields. Rats had the most overhead binocularity of the three species studied, which is consistent with them having a more generalized foraging style (i.e., wide variety of foraging methods including scavenging), but likely more predation pressure.
The retinal topography of the Northern tree shrew is characterized by a distinct temporal visual streak and area centralis with a peak ganglion density of nearly 20,000 cells/mm2 spanning to the nasal aspect of the retina (Norton, Savier, & Sedigh-Sarvestani, 2022). Considering the structural organization of the tree shrew retina in combination with its visual field configuration, it is likely that the tree shrew binocular and monocular visual fields also have high localized spatial resolving power (as indicated by the ganglion cell density) (Figures 4a, 4d).
The common ferret possesses a retinal ganglion cell distribution that resembles a prominent area centralis accompanied by a horizontal visual streak in the temporal, peripapillary retina (Henderson, 1985; Isayama et al., 2009). Maximum ganglion cell densities are estimated to range from 3,300 to 5,200 cells/mm2 in the area centralis (Henderson, 1985; Isayama et al., 2009). There are three morphologically distinct ganglion cells that vary in size and resemble the alpha-, beta-, and gamma- ganglion cells found in the cat retina (Henderson, 1985; Isayama et al., 2009). Considering the characteristics of the ferret retina, it is likely that binocular field has high spatial resolving power owing to its alignment with the area centralis, whereas the visual streak may provide some higher spatial resolving power in the peripheral monocular visual fields (Figures 4b, 4e).
The retinal ganglion cell distribution in albino rats is much less specialized with smaller differences in density across the retina when compared with the two other species. However, the dorsotemporal portion of their retina contains the greatest density of retinal ganglion cells (Dreher, Sefton, Ni & Nisbett, 1985; Reese & Cowey, 1986). Considering the structural design of the rat retina in combination with orientation of the visual field, it is expected that rat spatial resolution is highest within the binocular visual field (Figures 4c, 4f). Additional studies evaluating retinal topography and visual field measurements on the same individuals may provide a more accurate picture of regions of visual acuity possessed by each of these species (Kano, Naik, Keskin, Couzin, & Nagy, 2022).

## Implications for animal models in vision science
Our study species are important model species in vision science, particularly in cortical mapping of the central visual system. Northern tree shrews share many similarities to the visual system of humans owing to their relatively close phylogenetic relatedness to primates, their cone-dominated retina, the columnar nature of orientation sensitivity in the primary visual cortex, diurnal lifestyle, and the demonstration of visually guided behaviors, making them popular animal models for studying retinal pathology and central visual processing (Baldwin et al., 2013; Lyon, Jain, & Kaas, 1998; Petry & Bickford, 2019; Samorajski, Ordy, & Keefe, 1966). The ferret has been used extensively in the study of visual development owing to the columnar nature of orientation sensitivity in the primary visual cortex, the relatively immature state of the brain at birth, and the delay in eye opening thereafter (Gilardi & Kalebic, 2021; Sharma & Sur, 2014), which allowed exploring changes that occur in the visual centers as a result of the onset of vision (Gilardi & Kalebic, 2021; Lempel & Nielsen, 2019). Rodents, such as the albino rat, have relatively poor overall visual acuity and do not exhibit the range of visually guided behaviors seen in diurnal mammals (Prusky, West, & Douglas, 2000). Yet, they have been used extensively to study the pathogenesis and therapeutics of various retinopathies (Dagher et al., 2004; Kern, 2007; Kowluru & Chan, 2007; Daniel et al., 2021) and aspects of central visual processing (Blue & Parnavelas, 1983; Lashley, 1930; Huppé-Gourgues, Jegouick, & Vauche, 2018; Matteucci, Bellacosa Marotti, Riggi, Rosselli, & Zoccolan,2019; Saïdi, Mbarek, & Chaouacha-Chekir, 2011).
Despite these advances, retinotopic mapping of visual fields in the brain is commonly a gap in understanding processing in the visual system. Retinotopic maps are commonly used to segment the visual system of animals, but some maps exhibit biases for certain regions of the visual field; for instance, emphasizing the central visual field at the expense of the periphery (Sedigh-Sarvestani et al., 2021). These biases in visual field coverage are related to the functional contributions of the area to visual perception serving to optimize information processing (Sedigh-Sarvestani & Fitzpatrick, 2022). These maps are measured using specialized visual stimuli projected onto a monitor in front of the experimental animal. The present study provides knowledge of the extent of the visual field in three model species, which can be used to help position the monitors to cover the full visual field and improve retinotopic mapping studies.
There are only a few studies (Bishop, Kozak, Levick, & Vakkur, 1962; Blakeslee et al., 1985; Diao, Wang, & Xiao, 1983; Hanke, Römer, & Dehnhardt, 2006; Heesy, 2004; Hughes, 1977; Lane, Allman, & Kaas, 1971; Sedigh-Sarvestani et al. 2021; Warwick, et al., 2018; Woolsey et al., 1971; Yu, Rowley, Price, Rosa, & Zavitz, 2020) detailing the visual fields of mammals, many of which are performed with estimations involving representation of cortical visual fields, or behavioral studies. We demonstrated that the ophthalmoscopic technique (Martin, 1986b; Martin, 1999; Martin, 2007; Martin, 2009; Martin, 2014) is a viable method of determining the absolute boundaries of the retinal visual field and allows for the reconstruction of a three-dimensional visual space. Understanding the complete visual space of these animals may allow for a clearer interpretation of the neuroscientific work focused on the visual cortex of these animals. For instance, optical imaging studies in the tree shrew primary visual cortex show a seemingly enlarged representation of the central 25° of the visual field (Bosking, Kretz, Pucak, & Fitzpatrick, 2000). Now we can better understand the relative over-representation of this region with our results. The tree shrew has a binocular field covering approximately 54° of the central visual space. The over representation of the central 25° of the visual field previously described (Bosking et al., 2000) may likely represent a central nervous system effort to maximize visual processing within the central portion of the binocular field at the expense of peripheral visual processing.

## Study limitations
This study is not without limitations, primarily with regard to the effects of anesthesia on eyelid and globe position, pupil diameter, and the elimination of eye movements. Sedation or anesthesia can pose a potential additional risk to the study subject. However, using the chemical restraint can be the safest option for animals that naturally are highly anxious (i.e., tree shrews), or there is no way to safely restrain the subject to successfully obtain the measurements. Despite a rigorous anesthetic and monitoring protocol, one patient died under anesthesia in the present study. No particular cause of death was determined. Anesthesia also can alter eye and eyelid position, as well as pupil size. Although some deviation in eye positioning likely occurred, the impact was minimal because there was no apparent difference in optic axis position after evaluation using the retinal reflex technique described by Martin and Prince (2001). Eyelid positioning was corrected manually, restraining the superior eyelids to normal anatomic positioning (in which the eyelid margins rested just rostral to limbus) during data collection. Additionally, the pupil has a large range of normal sizes in the natural setting depending on photopic or scotopic conditions of the environment. However, because we did not measure pupillary diameter on each individual, it is possible that these alterations from the natural setting allowed for discrepancies in the degree of visual field accessible to the retina when compared with an alert animal. Finally, because animals were placed under general anesthesia, their range of eye movements were not accounted for in this study. It is possible that the extent of the visual fields may be underestimated by not accounting for convergent and divergent eye movements. Additional studies are needed to investigate what effect body, head, and eye movements have on the dynamic visual field in these animals. As the field moves toward studying visual processing in freely moving animals (Parker, Brown, Smear, & Niell, 2020; Skyberg & Niell, 2024), it is important to understand the statistics of the visual field as constrained by eye anatomy, in order to form an understanding of the dynamic visual processing that occurs when anatomy and active sampling are combined in the natural paradigm.

## Conclusions
Our study validates the use of the ophthalmoscopic reflex technique, which has been extensively used with other vertebrates, in mammals. We quantified the extent of different components of the visual field of three common model organisms in an effort to contribute to the ongoing study of egocentric visual perception. We found differences in the extent of each of these components (binocular, lateral, and blind) depending on the plane around the head within each species. Additionally, we found differences among the three species in binocularity (highest in albino rats, followed by Northern tree shrews and common ferrets), visual coverage (highest in Northern tree shrews, followed by common ferrets and albino rats), and extent of the blind area (highest in common ferrets, followed by albino rats and Northern tree shrews. These differences could be associated with the ecology and behavior of each species.

## Figure Figure 4.: The binocular visual field of the Northern tree shrew (A, D), common ferret (B, E), and albino rat (C, F) as it relates to retinal topography and visual acuity. The top images (A–C) display the binocular visual field in blue of each individual as determined by these experiments. The bottom row (D–F) demonstrates the projection of the retinal topographic maps (Norton, Savier, & Sedigh-Sarvestani, 2022; Isayama et al., 2009; Norton, Savier, & Sedigh-Sarvestani, 2022; Dreher, Sefton, Ni & Nisbett, 1985; respectively) projected into the visual space. Areas of increased neuronal density are denoted by darker shades of purple. The projection of the retinal ganglion cell topography into the visual space allows for the theoretical regions of visual acuity within the binocular visual field (Norton, Savier, & Sedigh-Sarvestani, 2022; Dreher, Sefton, Ni, & Nisbett, 1985; Isayama et al., 2009).
