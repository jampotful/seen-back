# Extracting full information from OCT scans—signs of early age‐related macular degeneration within inner retinal layers by local neighbourhood statistics. Part I: Methodology

## Abstract
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesAssociations between the occurrence of early age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) and alterations in retinal layer thicknesses have been reported based on classical processing of optical coherence tomography (OCT) data by noise removal and subsequent image segmentation. However, speckle noise within OCT data itself bears a substantial part of the total information. For this reason, an omics‐type approach was designed for full exploitation of OCT data, which was able to identify signs of early AMD throughout the retina as a whole.MethodsA nested case–control study was designed with 200 early AMD cases and 200 healthy controls. For every participant, within a randomly selected OCT scan and a randomly selected column therein, manual grading was performed for 26 retinal feature positions. At each position, a total of 3792 descriptors were computed, based on nonlinear transformations of OCT data, first‐order neighbourhood statistics and Haralick features. Equivalence and differences between cases and controls were tested for every descriptor at each graded position. Results of multiple testing were expressed in terms of false and true discovery rates controlled by the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure.ResultsIn terms of the amount and disparity of true discoveries, overall non‐equivalence of early AMD and healthy groups was found. Strong difference signals were observed at the internal limiting membrane and two central retinal positions, particularly for descriptors emphasising speckle noise.ConclusionsBetween retinae of healthy controls and early AMD patients, significant differences were observed at the level of local neighbourhood statistics within the OCT data. Thus, independent evidence was obtained for AMD affecting not only the outer retinal layers but also the retina as a whole, even in the early stages of the disease. Within OCT data, both cartoons and speckle bear essential parts of total information. A constructive, completely documented, traceable and repeatable approach was pursued without invoking artificial intelligence methods.

## INTRODUCTION

## Scientific background
Age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) is a progressive retinal disease without a known cure and the leading cause of blindness in the elderly. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Main risk factors include age and smoking, 5 as well as a strong, multifactorial genetic predisposition. 6 , 7 Long before any signs of AMD become noticeable clinically, the disease starts with accumulation of small deposits of extracellular debris within the outermost layers of the retina, evolving into different types of lesions such as drusen or subretinal drusenoid deposits. 8 Such early lesions often remain unidentifiable in fundus images, 9 but can be made visible by optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging 10 ; see Figure 1. This has become the standard tool for noninvasive imaging of the living retina, generating high‐resolution cross‐sectional images of tissue microstructure. OCT imaging devices use coherent near‐infrared light to identify the position of reflective substructures within the retina by interferometry. 11 In general, the features visible in OCT images correspond well with histologically defined structures in the retina and choroid.

The question arises whether AMD in its early, preclinical stage can be recognised by the occurrence of lesions alone, or whether other, independent biomarkers can be found. Meanwhile, there is a growing body of literature concerned with associations between early or intermediate AMD and alterations of retinal thickness, either as a whole or of certain retinal layers. 12 , 13 , 14 In these studies, OCT data were analysed by the classical paradigm of image processing, namely, conversion of raw data into a standard image representation (by forming the 4th root of the raw data and greyscale binning, referred to as gimdat mode), application of a denoising procedure and subsequent segmentation of the layers. For these processing steps, built‐in software provided by the manufacturers of the OCT device is employed. However, the quality of the segmentations obtained in this way is often very poor, and it is highly questionable whether reported thickness differences exceed the range of systematic error of the segmentation procedures. 15
In the present investigation, we pursued an independent approach, applying an ‘omics’‐type methodology in line with Haralick et al. 16 and Larue et al., 17 which is fundamentally different from layer segmentation and subsequent thickness measurements. Notice first that the considerable amount of multiplicative noise (speckle) that is always contained within OCT data is inherent to the interferometry method. As pointed out, for example, by Schmitt et al., 18 the role of speckle noise within OCT images is ambiguous. Far from being just a degradation of an ideal image, the speckle itself bears a substantial part of the total information, which has been almost completely neglected in the literature to date. ‘The question (…) regarding whether speckle is the signal or the noise in OCT can now be answered succinctly: it is both', see Schmitt et al., 18 p. 99. Consequently, in order to extract the full information from OCT data, one has to include denoised data (‘cartoon’) as well as the speckle into the evaluation.
Here, this principle will be realised within the framework of a nested case–control study, employing a constructive approach of local neighbourhood statistics. Besides the above‐mentioned standard visualisation, raw OCT data were subjected to a number of different nonlinear transformations. Namely, after application of Rudin‐Osher‐Fatemi total variation (TV) regularisation, 19 cartoon and speckle were separated. As a result, 12 different image modes were obtained. Then we computed first‐order statistics and Haralick features in local windows of different sizes around the positions of features in the retina and choroid. In contrast to the mostly undocumented commercial segmentation algorithms, we pursued a fully documented, externally repeatable approach without ‘black boxes’. Finally, we arrived at a set of more than 3500 descriptors as computer‐generated endpoints, which were statistically tested at every position for equivalence, and subsequently for difference. This statistical approach is different from the papers cited above. Instead of (highly conservative) alpha‐level correction, we accounted for multiple testing through control of the false‐discovery rate (FDR) by the Benjamini–Yekutieli step‐up procedure. 20 Consequently, the results are expressed as numbers or percentages of true discoveries instead of the significance of single tests.

## Aims of the study
In summary, the present investigation aims to establish independent biomarkers for early AMD, which are neither related to the observation of drusen or other lesions, nor to comparative analysis of retinal layer thicknesses. To achieve this goal, we introduced an ‘omics’‐type description of OCT data, preparing meaningful, sufficiently large and diverse sets of local neighbourhood descriptors. Based on these descriptor sets, we performed multiple statistical tests for equivalence as well as for differences between early AMD cases and healthy controls. Then, biomarkers for early AMD will be obtained by evaluating the rates of false and true discoveries within whole ensembles of statistical tests. This approach is realised in the framework of a nested case–control study with 200 pairs of early AMD patients and healthy controls, which were matched by seven parameters, including the main non‐genetic AMD risk factors. Indeed, the analysis revealed that even in the earliest preclinical stages of the disease, signs of AMD can be observed throughout the retina as a whole, including its inner parts distant from the position of lesion formation—see Table 1.

## Outline
The investigation was organised into two closely related papers, the present one being devoted to the methodology. We describe in detail the case‐control setting (Section 2.1), OCT data acquisition and grading of the retinal features (Section 2.2), generation of image modes (Section 2.3) and the construction of the local neighbourhood descriptors (Section 2.4). The results of manual grading and the structure of the obtained descriptor sets are presented and discussed in Sections 3 and 4. The manuscript closes with a short conclusion. A working instruction for the grading procedure is provided as an Appendix.
The statistical analysis will be presented in the parallel manuscript. 15 For the convenience of the reader, the content is also outlined here. After shortly summarising Part I (Sections 1 and 2), two key ingredients of the statistical approach are explained in detail, namely, the TOST (‘two one‐sided subtests’) methodology for equivalence testing 21 and the handling of multiple tests by controlling the rates of true and false discoveries, rather than by alpha‐level correction (Section 2.5). Then, the plan for equivalence and difference testing between early AMD patients and healthy controls is detailed (Sections 2.6–2.8) and the outcomes of the statistical analysis are presented (Sections 3.1–3.3). Subsequently, we discuss the results and set them in perspective (Section 4) followed by a conclusion (Section 5). Full classification of all tests in terms of discoveries and results of sensitivity analysis are provided as appendices.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Nested case–control study design

## The LIFE Adult cohort
The Leipzig Research Centre for Civilization Diseases (LIFE)‐Adult study is a population‐based cohort study, for which 10,000 randomly selected inhabitants of the city of Leipzig (Saxonia, Germany) between 40 and 79 years of age were recruited and deeply phenotyped between 2011 and 2014. 22 , 23 During the baseline examination, ophthalmological imaging data were generated for more than 9000 adults. The current research employed OCT volume scans of the macula region. The LIFE Adult study follows the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the responsible institutional ethics board of the Medical Faculty of Leipzig University (approval numbers 2632009‐14122009, 263/09‐ff, 201/17‐ek). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data use was approved by the institutional review board of the Leipzig Research Centre for Civilization Diseases (LIFE).

## Definition of early AMD and healthy patients
For the LIFE Adult Baseline cohort, the author (FGR) performed OCT‐based AMD grading for both eyes, using the Three‐Continent Classification (TCC) scheme from Klein et al. 24 This scheme defines five severity steps of AMD (No, mild early, moderate early, severe early and late). Early AMD in the sense of this work comprises the mild early, moderate early and severe early severity steps. Additionally, eyes classified by TCC as No‐AMD but with apparent lesions on OCT imaging were counted as early AMD as well. Consequently, No‐AMD eyes in this study satisfy the TCC condition of No‐AMD, which is strengthened in the sense that lesions are completely absent. Thus, a person was classified as No‐AMD if and only if both eyes were No‐AMD, and as early AMD if and only if both eyes were early AMD or one eye was early AMD and the fellow eye was No‐AMD.

## Selection of cases and controls
To realise a nested case–control study design, all participants classified as No‐AMD or early AMD were identified with the following eight phenotypes available: (1) sex (binary), (2) age (years), (3) body mass index (BMI, six classes, following the World Health Organisation definitions 25 ), (4) smoking (three classes: non‐smoker, smoker or ex‐smoker), (5) socioeconomic status (SES, three levels derived from the SES index 26 : low, 3.0≤SES≤9.2; medium, 9.2<SES≤15.3 or high, 15.3≤SES≤21.0), (6) diabetes (three classes, following American Diabetes Association definitions: 27 no diabetes, prediabetes or diabetes), (7) non‐high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol level (mmol/L) and (8) report of any current statin medication. From the early AMD group, 200 cases were selected at random. Each case was then matched with a single control from the No‐AMD group using criteria 1–7 and applied in the order listed above. Sex, BMI, smoking, socioeconomic status and diabetes were exactly matched. For age, a ±1 year tolerance was accepted. With respect to non‐HDL cholesterol, the desired tolerance of ±1 mmol/L could not be realised in about one‐third of cases. However, both groups proved to be well‐balanced with respect to their current intake of statin medication. Baseline properties are summarised in Table 2. Except for age and smoking, known AMD risk factors were purposefully excluded from the matching procedure. In particular, genetic risk factors, as described by Winkler et al. 7 were not considered.

## OCT imaging and grading of retinal features

## Generation of OCT raw data
Optical coherence tomography data were generated with a commercially available spectral‐domain device (Spectralis HRA + OCT, equipped with camera head with serial number 04514, software modules Heidelberg Eye Explorer 1.7.0.0, Acquisition Module 5.4.7.0 and Viewing Module 5.4.6.0; Heidelberg Engineering, heidelbergengineering.com). This device works with a super luminescent diode at a central wavelength of 870 nm. For each participant, volume scans of the macular area with a field size of 20 deg (temporal‐nasal) × 20 deg (superior–inferior) were acquired for both eyes. Each volume scan (C‐scan) consists of 97 equally spaced rectangular slides (B‐scans), each composed of 512 columns (A‐scans) of 496 pixels in size. Scan depth in tissue was about 1.9 mm, resulting in a pixel depth of 3.87 μm while the axial optical resolution amounts to 7 μm, see the user manual. 28 The real‐time eye‐tracking function of the device was enabled, thus obtaining an average of 10 measurements per A‐scan. No special imaging modules were used. Raw data were exported and used following the specification of the export function. 29 For the subsequent image analysis, the use of raw data is indispensable, see Section 2.3.

## Selection of B‐scans and A‐scans
For each participant, one eye was randomly selected. From the volume scan of this eye, a single B‐scan was selected at random. In order to randomise a single column (A‐scan) from each B‐scan, a list of equally distributed numbers within the range [9, 504] was generated, and from the B‐scan under consideration, the column with the next available number from the list was selected. Then this number was marked as used. However, if the selected column, by visual inspection, was located within a vessel shadow or intersected an AMD lesion, then it was dropped, and the column with the next available number was taken from the same B‐scan. Summing up, for each participant, a single A‐scan without disturbances from vessels or lesions was made available for manual grading.

## Definitions of retinal features and manual position grading
Based on the nomenclature introduced by Staurenghi et al., 30 a total of 26 features from the vitreous body, retina and choroid were specified for manual grading with respect to feature positions. Sixteen positions thereof were situated inside of the retinal or choroidal layers or OCT reflectivity bands while 10 marked respective borders. Grading was organised so that automated evaluation was possible. Besides the B‐scan, the grader was provided with an intensity profile of the selected A‐scan and a grey‐level inverted cutout of the environment; see Appendix. Any missing features were documented. A complete list of features and working instructions for position grading is provided in Appendix.

## Nonlinear transformations of OCT data
By nonlinear tranformations of the measured intensities, 12 different image modes were generated from the original OCT data. The modes bear the proper names rawdat, gimdat, lograw, rawreg, rawspe, gimreg, gimspe, logreg. logspe, rawsvg, gimsvg and logsvg, Intensity value matrices used for the calculations are denoted by I0,I1,…,I12.

## Image modes immediately derived from raw data
For each B‐scan, the raw data I0 constitute the rawdat image mode with continuous values in the range 0,1. Missing values are exported as NaN (‘not a number’) values and subsequently replaced by zero. From I0, we generate the gimdat mode as the usual image representation of OCT data, which is obtained as I1=I04 with subsequent binning to 256 grey levels. Further, after replacing possible zero values in I0 by 0.5⋅Minc∈I0c>0, thus obtaining I2, we generate the lograw mode by the logarithmic transformation I3=−log2I2. lograw takes continuous values in the range 0∞.

## Separation of cartoon and speckle by TV regularisation
The occurrence of speckle noise can be formally modelled by the equation I=C⋅S where I, C and S denote the really obtained image, the ideal (undisturbed) image (‘cartoon’) and the speckle, respectively. In order to reconstruct C, we apply to I a well‐established standard procedure in mathematical image processing, namely, TV denoising, see Rudin et al. 31 This procedure can be understood as a type of filtering, which generates a coarsened, cartoon‐like version of the input data I while its original edge structure is conserved. Formally, cartoon and speckle are then obtained as C=TVI and S=I/C=I/TVI.
For the realisation of the TV denoising procedure, as described in Bredies et al., 19 two parameters must be specified: the so‐called regularisation parameter α and the number of iterations which is fixed to K=40 in all cases. Applying the TV procedure with α=0.04 to rawdat and replacing possible zero values by 0.5⋅Minc∈TVI0c>0, thus obtaining I4 as cartoon, we generate the rawreg mode. The corresponding speckle mode (rawspe) is defined as I5=I0/I4. Analogously, applying the TV procedure with α=0.75 to lograw and replacing possible zero values by 0.5⋅Minc∈TVI3c>0, thus obtaining I6 as cartoon, we generate the logreg mode. Again, logspe calculated as I7=I3/I6 defines the corresponding speckle mode. All four modes, rawreg, rawspe, logreg and logspe take continuous values in the range 0∞. Finally, the mode gimreg is generated by the application of the TV procedure with α=0.08 to gimdat, replacing possible zero values by 0.5⋅Minc∈TVI1c>0, thus obtaining I8 as cartoon, and subsequent binning to 256 grey levels. The corresponding speckle mode gimspe is calculated as I9=0.6667⋅I1/I8, with subsequent binning to 256 grey levels.

## Adaptive greyscale binning
We obtain three further image modes by application of an adaptive greyscale binning procedure. To this end, after sorting all entries of a given data matrix I in ascending order, we calculate the 5%‐ and 95%‐quantiles Q5andQ95 of the sample, perform a histogram equalisation by mapping the values from the interval Q5Q95 to 0,1 and bin the resulting values to 256 grey levels. For all entries x∈I, the mapping is given by (1) Hx=0|x<Q5x−Q5/Q95−Q5∣Q5≤x≤Q951∣Q95<x.
As a result, we obtain a greyscale image HI with level ranges adapted to the frequency statistics of the original data I. That way, the rawsvg and logsvg modes are calculated from rawspe and gimspe modes by I10=HI5 and I11=HI7 and subsequent grey‐level binning. The gimsvg mode is obtained as I12=HI1/TVI1 and subsequent grey‐level binning by using, in difference to the calculation of gimspe, α=0.75 for the TV denoising procedure. Summing up, we arrive at a family of 12 image modes, 6 thereof are continuous data matrices and further 6 modes constitute greyscale images, see Figure 2 and Table 3.

## Construction of descriptors
Following the highly successful paradigm of ‘radiomics’ in automated MRI, CT and PET image analysis, 17 , 32 we aim to describe different retinal features using a large set of descriptors involving brightness, texture and correlation measures based on local neighbourhood statistics. Surprisingly, in spite of promising early work by Baroni et al. 33 and Quellec et al., 34 this paradigm has never been applied to the evaluation of retinal OCT images. To date, only a few publications exist concerning texture classification in OCT‐based atherosclerosis and cancer imaging, for example, Raupov et al. 35 However, the construction of appropriate descriptors is well‐understood in computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography and can be carried over to OCT imaging.

## Local window sizes
We consider nine different formats of rectangular windows (or submatrices) W, centred around a given position of interest. The possible sizes are p1×p2 with p1,p2∈3,5,7, see Figure 3.

## Neighbourhood statistics for continuous data
From the p1·p2=n values cij contained in a window W of p1×p2 pixel size, we calculate the following 12 quantities: (1) sample mean: d001=∑i,jcij/n, (2) empirical variance: d002=Kn·∑i,jcij−d0012/n−1, (3) sample skewness: d003=Kn·∑i,jcij−d0013/∑i,jcij−d00123/2, (4) sample kurtosis: , (5) minimum: d005=Mincij, (6) maximum: d006=Maxcij, (7) range: d007=d006−d005, (8) median: d008=Mediancij, (9) energy: d009=∑i,jcij2, (10) root mean square: d010=∑i,jcij2/n1/2, (11) interquartile range: d013=Q75−Q25 where Q75 and Q25 denote the 75% and 25% quantiles of the sample cij, respectively and (12) number of outliers: or cij>Q75+1.5·d013/n. In definitions (2), (3) and (4), Kn denote appropriate positive scaling factors.

## Neighbourhood statistics for greyscale‐binned data
From the grey‐level values cij contained in a window W of p1×p2 pixel size, we generate a histogram with 2q classes of equal width where q∈4,5. Denoting the frequencies within this histogram by zk, we calculate two further quantities: (1) Shannon entropy: d011q=−∑zk>0zk·log2zk and (2) uniformity: d012q=∑kzk2.

## Haralick features for greyscale‐binned data
Based on the so‐called grey‐level co‐occurrence matrix (GLCM), Haralick et al. 16 proposed a set of descriptors which is now widely used for the classification of textures in nearly all areas of image analysis. In the context of retinal OCT data, Haralick features prove to be particularly useful for the analysis of image modes where speckle has been enhanced or separated. The definition of a GLCM G=gkl of size 2q2q relies on the grey‐level histograms for W generated above: its entries gkl count the number of horizontal or vertical adjacencies between pixels from histogram classes k and l in W. Since there is no preference for a certain adjacency direction, we obtain a symmetrical GLCM. Finally, G is normed using division by the sum of all entries. We calculate 21 Haralick features which, via G, depend on q∈4,5. The features are (1) d101q—autocorrelation, (2) d102q—cluster tendency, (3) d103q—cluster prominence, (4) d104q—cluster shade, (5) d105q—contrast, (6) d106q—correlation, (7) d107q—difference entropy, (8) d108q—difference variance, (9) d109q—dissimilarity, (10) d110q—Haralick energy, (11) d111q—Haralick entropy, (12) d112q—homogeneity, (13) d113q—information measure of correlation 1, (14) d114q—information measure of correlation 2, (15) d115q—inverse difference, (16) d116q—maximum probability, (17) d117q—sum average, (18) d118q—sum entropy, (19) d119q—sum of squares, (20) d120q—sum variance and (21) d121q—maximal correlation coefficient. Cluster tendency is defined as in Aerts et al., 32 Supplement, p. 18. For the precise definitions of all other features, see Löfstedt et al., 36 p. 5, Table 2.

## Definition and labelling of descriptors
A descriptor δ is defined by: (a) the image mode used (12 modes), (b) the size of the window W around the position of interest (9 sizes), (c) the statistical quantity to be calculated (12 quantities for continuous data/35 quantities for grey‐level data, of which 23 depend on q) and (d) in the case of greyscale‐binned data, the number 2q of histogram classes (2 possible values of q). We add a further descriptor for the immediate evaluation of the data at the position of interest, which is formally effected by the calculation of d001 in a window of 1×1 pixel size. Thus, we arrive at 109 or 523 descriptors for every image mode with continuous or grey‐level data, respectively, and at 3792 descriptors in total. For all case and control patients, every descriptor is evaluated at each graded retinal feature position.
All descriptors are labelled by seven‐digit numbers. The first two digits encode the image mode, the middle three digits the quantity and the last two digits the window size and the value of q, if applicable. More precisely, the leading digits 01, 02, …, 12 encode the image modes rawdat, rawreg, rawspe, rawsvg, gimdat, gimreg, gimspe, gimsvg, lograw, logreg, logspe and logsvg, respectively. The statistical quantities are identified by the three‐digit subscripts 001, …, 121 used in the definitions above. The last digits 00, 01, 02, …, 09 encode the window sizes 1×1, 3×3, 3×5, …, 7×7, either without specification of q or with a choice of q=4. Again, the last digits 11, 12, …, 19 encode the window sizes 3×3, 3×5, …, 7×7 but with a choice of q=5. For example, the descriptor δ1001112 uses the image mode 10 (logreg) for the calculation of the quantity 011 (Shannon entropy) in a window encoded by 12 (size 3×5 pixels by the use of q=5), thus evaluating a grey‐level histogram with 32 classes.
The subset of all descriptors based on the image mode m is denoted by Sm. Within Sm, we arrange the descriptors in ascending order of their labels which are read as integers. In this context, descriptors may be denoted alternatively with subscripts, for example, δi.

## Mutual dependence/independence of the descriptor subsets
For every pair m1m2 of image modes, we calculate a correlation matrix Cm1m2 whose elements are Pearson correlation coefficients ϱij for the vectors Δi and Δj defined as follows. For the descriptor δi from subset Sm1, Δi contains the value differences between cases and controls calculated for all positions included in statistical analysis and all matched pairs available for the respective position—see Section 3.1, Table 4. Analogously, Δj is defined for the descriptor δj from subset Sm2, calculating the case‐control differences for the same extent of positions and matched pairs. Furthermore, the overall correlation matrix C is calculated in a completely analogous way, but varying now the descriptors δi, δj throughout all descriptor subsets S1, …, S12. ## Remarks about implementation
Generation of image modes and calculation of descriptors were implemented as MATLAB procedures. The numerical realisation of the TV denoising procedure used for separation of cartoon and speckle follows Bredies et al. 19 and Chambolle and Pock. 37 Procedures were tested on MATLAB 9.6.0.1072779 (R2019a) and require the MATLAB Image Processing Toolbox (mathworks.com/products/matlab and mathworks.com/products/image). No particular attempts for optimisation of runtime behaviour were made.

## RESULTS

## Data obtained by manual grading
After the selection procedure described above, for each participant, a single column of OCT data was made available for manual grading. Thus, a total of N=400 columns were graded with respect to 26 retinal and choroidal feature positions using the procedure described in the Appendix. Note that this grading was only a preparatory step for the generation of the endpoints. The feature positions in themselves bear no information about the possible appearance of early AMD.
Further, we note that not every feature was clearly distinguishable in each B‐scan. Moreover, detachment of the vitreous body was present for less than one‐half of the participants. As a consequence, for the majority of patients, six positions could not be graded, namely, Positions 1–3, 19, 24, and M (see the descriptions in Appendix). In summary, we included 20 positions in the subsequent statistical analysis. This led to a total of 3179 matched pairs of single locations from cases and controls that were divided into 20 position groups. The results of manual grading are summarised in Table 4.

## Correlation structure of the descriptor sets
After specification of the positions to be analysed, the values of all 3792 descriptors were calculated at all analysed positions for each participant. Before continuing the statistical analysis of descriptors, we assessed the mutual correlation between single descriptors δi and δj and the general correlation structure of the descriptor set S as a whole, as well as of the subsets S(1), …, S12 of descriptors based on the different image modes. In order to assure the validity of the subsequent statistical analysis, the descriptor sets S, S(1), …, S12 must admit the following properties. First, the overall dependence between mutual different descriptor sets, as measured by the quantities Rm1m2 (see Section 2.4.6 above), must be, at most, low to moderate. The same must be true for the values Rm,m and R which may be understood as cumulative measures of intra‐correlation within single descriptor sets. Second, all descriptor sets must show a sufficiently high dispersion and variation of signs and magnitudes of the single correlation coefficients.
To perform the assessment of correlation, for every descriptor δi all 3179 value differences between cases and controls originating at matched pairs of single locations are stored in an ordered vector Δi. Next, for every pair δiδj of descriptors we calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient ϱij of the vectors Δi and Δj and assemble the correlation matrices C and Cm1m2 for all pairs m1m2 of descriptor subsets. Further, the quantities Rm1m2 are calculated, which may be understood as the weighted means of the unsigned entries of the matrices Cm1m2. These are shown in Table 5. ## DISCUSSION

## Can we really extract additional information from OCT data by neighbourhood statistics?
In the introduction, the hypothesis that OCT data contain significantly more information than can be detected by examining its usual visualisation (i.e., gimdat mode) was stated. In particular, we hypothesised that the speckle noise inevitably arising with OCT data acquisition contains a substantial part of the total information.
To test this main assertion, we investigated four groups of image modes. The first group comprises three basic modes: the raw data themselves (rawdat), its usual greyscale representation gimdat and its logarithmic inversion lograw. Hereof, we derived three regularised modes where noise was suppressed and removed (rawreg, gimreg and logreg) and three modes exclusively containing the pure speckle (rawspe, gimspe and logspe). Finally, we generated three further modes where pure speckle was subjected to histogram equalisation and subsequent greyscale binning (rawsvg, gimsvg and logsvg). Based on these 12 image modes, we generated 12 individual sets of descriptors, which were finally combined into a large total set. Now we discuss whether the properties of the whole descriptor set as well as the sets related to individual image modes support the hypotheses formulated above.

## Implications of the observed correlation structure for the whole descriptor set
Indeed, the results show that in order to extract full information from OCT scans, the use of raw data and application of nonlinear transformations, which accentuate different intensity bands, is indispensable. This is proven in Figure 4a, showing at a glance the high diversity and little redundancy within the whole descriptor set obtained in the present study. The entries of the overall correlation matrix C are mostly of moderate to small magnitude, appearing grey or dark grey in the visualisation. Moreover, large subsets of highly to perfectly correlated descriptors are missing. The mean unsigned correlation within C is measured by R=0.2042, and the signs of correlation coefficients show considerable dispersion. Thus, the general use of the different image modes is well justified, and the construction and application of the whole descriptor set, containing a sufficiently large variety of descriptors, is meaningful.

## Implications of the observed correlation structure for the individual descriptor sets
The internal correlation structure within the subsets Sm can also be read from Figure 4a where the submatrices Cm,m can be identified as blocks around the main diagonal, and exemplarily, from Figure 4b–d where C5,5 and C5,8 are pictured as typical examples. Even here, the internal structure is highly differentiated, comprising single descriptors or descriptor clusters showing both strong and weak mutual correlations. Typically, the values Rm,m in Table 5 amount to 0.5. Moderate R‐values are to be expected since descriptors, which use different window sizes but are related to the same statistical quantity, always form clusters with moderate to strong correlation. Within the subsets Sm, the signs of the correlation coefficients again show considerable dispersion throughout Cm,m. Consequently, the subsets Sm in themselves are meaningful and sufficiently diverse to justify its comparative investigation. Note that in view of the internal correlation structure within the sets Sm observed here, it is appropriate to employ a procedure with most general assumptions for the determination of false and true discovery rates, namely, the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure, see Wagner et al. 15
Further, from Table 5 and Figure 4a, it is obvious that all 12 descriptor subsets Sm, 1≤m≤12, contain mutually different pieces of information. Closer inspection of the correlation between subsets Sm1 and Sm2 with m1≠m2 reveals that the mean unsigned correlation between descriptors involving different image modes is moderate at best (only R1,2 and R9,10 exceed 0.5), and even very weak with Rm1m2<0.1 in 13 pairs of modes. The mutual differences between the three basic modes rawdat, gimdat and lograw were moderate to strong (R1,5=0.3555, R1,9=0.1913 and R5,9=0.2490). Between corresponding basic and regularised modes (cartoons), higher correlations were observed (R1,2=0.6445 for the rawdat–rawreg pair, R5,6=0.3822 for gimdat–gimreg and R9,10=0.5169 for lograw–logreg). On the contrary, corresponding basic and speckle modes were, in terms of R, nearly independent (R1,3=0.1345 for the pair rawdat–rawspe, R5,7=0.1069 for gimdat‐gimspe and R9,11=0.1315 for lograw‐logspe). For the speckle modes with adaptive greyscale binning, the same was true. Summing up, the main hypothesis is confirmed by analysis of mutual correlation of the different descriptor subsets.

## Limitations of the presented approach
The methodology presented has some inherent limitations. First, manual grading of retinal positions is a very expensive procedure, thus performed only once in the present study. Note that due to the moderate sample size (N=200 pairs), data were lacking for some retinal positions (1–3, 19, 24 and M); thus, these had to be excluded from subsequent analysis. Second, in view of the fact that contrast jumps within OCT data frequently extend over segments of several pixels (as a typical example, see the intensity profile for the ILM contrast jump in Figure 5), one has to expect a considerable amount of inter‐observer and even intra‐observer variation for the graded positions. Third, all obtained descriptor values are strongly device‐dependent (and depend upon the acquisition protocol as well). As a consequence, statistical analysis of the descriptors should focus on the behaviour of whole descriptor sets rather than individual results for single descriptors. Indeed, the statistical analyses in Wagner et al. 15 controlled the true and false discovery rates for whole subsets of descriptors, thus taking into account this limitation and likely overcoming the device dependency at least in part, see section 4.7 of Wagner et al. 15

## CONCLUSION
In the framework of a nested case–control study with 200 early AMD cases and 200 healthy controls from a large population cohort, manual grading with respect to 26 retinal feature positions was performed within a randomly selected OCT B‐scan and a randomly selected column (A‐scan). At each graded position, a total of 3792 descriptors was computed, based on nonlinear transformations of OCT data, first‐order neighbourhood statistics and Haralick features. We established a constructive, completely documented, traceable and repeatable approach without invoking artificial intelligence methods. For the subsequent statistical analysis, 20 positions were selected. Within the descriptor set as a whole, high diversity and little redundancy were shown. Furthermore, the descriptor subsets Sm related to individual image modes show sufficient diversity in themselves to justify its comparative investigation. Statistical analysis revealing strong signals of AMD throughout the whole retina will be presented in the parallel manuscript. 15
