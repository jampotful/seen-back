Management of anterior lens luxation in a bornean sun bear (<i>Helarctos malayanus euryspilus</i>): A case report.

# Management of anterior lens luxation in a bornean sun bear (Helarctos malayanus euryspilus): A case report

## Abstract
AbstractBackground:A 23-year-old female Bornean sun bear presented with symptoms of right eye discomfort, including frequent blinking and scratching. Ophthalmic examination revealed a dilated right pupil, anterior lens displacement, and early-stage bilateral cataracts. Corneal edema developed in the right eye.Case Description:An intracapsular lens extraction was performed to remove the right dislocated lens and ruptured capsule 3 weeks later. Postoperative care included topical eye drops, oral anti-inflammatory drugs, and antibiotics. Mild eyelid swelling, ocular rubbing, and decreased appetite were noted during the first postoperative week. By day 18, the eyelid swelling had resolved and the eye was fully open; however, a persistent corneal opacity rendered the right eye blind.Conclusion:This case illustrates the complexities of ophthalmic surgery in a Bornean sun bear, particularly in managing postoperative complications and visual outcomes. This underscores the importance of early detection and timely surgical intervention in wildlife medicine to improve prognosis and preserve ocular health.Keywords: Cataract, Eye surgery, Intracapsular lens extraction, Lens luxation, Sun bear

## Introduction
The Malayan sun bear (Helarctos malayanus), which is found in Southeast Asia and Sumatra (Crudge et al., 2019) classified as vulnerable by the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (Scotson et al., 2017). Sun bears are omnivores that primarily feed on fruits and invertebrates and occasionally hunt for small vertebrates (Sethy and Chauhan, 2018; Wong et al., 2002). Sun bear activity patterns vary, being diurnal in rainforests and nocturnal in agricultural land to avoid human encounters (Guharajan et al., 2018). Adults are poached for paws and meat as a culinary delicacy (Mills and Servheen, 1994); gall bladders and bile are extracted as traditional Asian medicines (Gomez et al., 2020); and fur coats, skulls, claws, and canine teeth are used as decorative and cultural ornaments (Meijaard, 1999). The cubs are sold as pets (Krishnasamy and Stoner, 2016; Krishnasamy and Zavagli, 2020). The Bornean sun bear (Helarctos malayanus euryspilus) is a smaller subspecies that only inhabits Borneo (Meijaard, 2004). The Bornean Sun Bear Conservation Centre (BSBCC) is located in the Kabili-Sepilok Forest Reserve Sabah, which houses 42 rescued sun bears. Sun bears can live up to 30 years in captivity, typically 10–15 years longer than their wild counterparts. Therefore, captive sun bears are susceptible to aging diseases that are not commonly seen in the wild population (You et al., 2023). In the BSBCC, 51% of the captive bears are aged >10 years, and 6 individuals are aged >20 years.
Ophthalmic diseases in captive ursids are common but not widely reported or published. In a retrospective study of common diseases in 512 bears in zoos in the United States, 17 bears (9 sun bears, 3 spectacled bears Tremarctos ornatus, 2 Asiatic black bears Ursus thibetanus, 1 American black bear Ursus americanus, 1 polar bear Ursus maritimus, and 1 brown bear Ursus arctos) had ocular diseases (Blake and Collins, 2002). In China, 47% of rescued bears (140 Asiatic black bears and 5 brown bears) from the bear bile industry had ophthalmic pathologies, such as corneal ulcers, retinal anomalies, cataracts, lens luxation, and eyelid abnormalities (Reynard et al., 2013). Hartley and Oliveira (2022) treated ocular conditions such as conjunctivitis, conjunctival masses, corneal opacity and ulcer, keratitis, lens luxation, cataracts, and retinal detachment in sun bears. Successful cataract surgeries by phacoemulsification or extracapsular lens extraction were performed in 10 Asiatic black bears, 1 juvenile sun bear, and 3 adult sun bears in China, Vietnam, and Cambodia, respectively (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). Complete case reports on surgical intervention for ocular diseases in Ursidae are limited, and to the authors’ knowledge, none have been reported for the Bornean sun bear.

## Case Details
One captive female Bornean sun bear (23 years old, 35 kg) at the BSBCC has right eye discomfort. The bear frequently rubs the skin around its right eye with its paws and claws. No history of eye illness or trauma was known. The bear was sedated with tiletamine-zolazepam 120 mg (Zoletil; Virbac, Carros, France) and xylazine 40 mg (Ilium Xylazil; Troy Laboratories, Glendenning, Australia) using a remote drug delivery system (blowpipe) for the initial ophthalmic examination, including a slit lamp examination (SL-14; Kowa, Nagoya, Japan), ultrasound (Eco 3 Expert Vet, Chison, Jiangsu, China), and fluorescein corneal staining. The right cornea was clear and without an ulcer. The right lens was anteriorly displaced into the anterior chamber, and the pupil was widely dilated behind the lens (Figs. 1 and 3A). The left lens had early cataracts. Sedation was reversed with yohimbine 0.15 mg/kg (Yohimbe; Equimed, Miami, Florida, USA) intramuscularly, and the bear was monitored during recovery in its cage. Recovery was uneventful.

Diffuse right corneal edema was observed 2 days after the eye examination. With the tentative clinical diagnosis of inflammation and increased intraocular pressure (IOP), meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg once daily (SID) (Melonex; Intas Pharmaceuticals, Ahmedabad, India) and mild pressure-lowering oral glycerin 15 ml (Cap Kaki Tiga; Syarikat Wen Ken Drug, Johor Bahru, Malaysia) twice daily (BID) were attempted for 5–7 days. During positive reinforcement training, neomycin and dexamethasone eyedrops (Neodeca; Duopharma, Klang, Malaysia) were attempted BID, often successfully. However, there was noticeable pain, as evidenced by her dull, inactive demeanor, hyporexia, blepharospasm, and increased frequency of gentle stroking of the right face and lids.
Because of logistics for procuring microsurgical equipment and other lens extraction necessities that were not available on-site, lensectomy could only be performed 21 days after lens luxation diagnosis to reduce ocular pain and preserve the eye globe. Sedation was induced with the protocol as before, and then intubation (endotracheal tube ID 8.0 mm) to maintain anesthesia on isoflurane (Isorane; Piramal Pharma, Sangareddy, India). The procedure, from intubation to extubation and reversal, required 3 hours. An intravenous drip of 5 ml/kg/hour was maintained throughout the anesthesia with Hartmann’s solution (Infusol; Ain Medicare, Kota Bharu, Malaysia).
The eyes were examined with direct ophthalmoscopy and slit lamp before surgical preparation. There was no change to the previous findings except that the cornea on the right was even more cloudy, and the luxated anterior lens could not be visualized. A decision was made to forego the ultrasound examination to avoid further lens shifting. IOP was obtained with a rebound tonometer (iCare Tonovet Plus; Vantaa, Finland) at all the different species settings (Table 1). No corneal ulcers were present on fluorescein dye testing.

The anesthetized bear was positioned for right lens extraction surgery by placing it on dorsal recumbency, tilting the head slightly to the left so that the right cornea was as horizontal as possible. A combination of towels, cushions, and masking tape ensured that the bear’s head was immobilized and that the airway was safe. The left cornea was protected from desiccation using a non-medicated lubricant ointment (Duratears; Alcon, Petaling Jaya, Malaysia). The right palpebral skin was shaved. The right eye surgical field was then prepared for aseptic surgery by thorough lavage of the eyelid fornices with sterile 0.9% NaCl, including the visceral and parietal surfaces of the third eyelid. When no more mucus or debris were visible, the lavage was replaced with 1% diluted povidone solution. Eyelids were swabbed with neat povidone iodine solution (Septidin 10%, Pahang Pharmacy, Klang, Malaysia). A periorbital local anesthetic injection was made through the partly prolapsed third eyelid with lignocaine (Locana, 2% w/v; LBS Laboratory LTD, Bangkok, Thailand) at 2 mg/kg. The bear was carefully positioned under a portable, desk-mounted operating microscope (Appasamy Associates, Madras, India) with magnifications of 0.4, 0.6, 1.0, and 1.6x available. Then, a sterile cloth fenestrated drape was placed, and the opening was reduced with towel clamps to form a small opening over the eye. A lateral canthotomy was performed for better exposure to the globe. Then, a Lieberman V-shaped speculum with 15-mm blades was used to retract the eyelids. A sterile 60 cm × 60 cm ophthalmic drape (Teknomek, Tekirdağ, Turkey) with an adhesive clear window was then placed, and an opening was made in the adhesive window with scissors for optimum exposure of the globe. At this time, discission lines were observed in the corneal stroma, indicating that the opacity was caused by endothelial touch. A Colibri forceps was used to steady the eyeball, while a 2.75-mm disposable keratome (Appa Blade Slit Blade 2.75mm; Appasamy Associates, Madras, India) was used to apply sufficient pressure continuously along the sclero-cornea junction to create a near-full-thickness corneal incision for 210°. At the midpoint of this incision, a keratome was used to enter the cornea, and 2% sodium hyaluronate gel (Hyotek, Teknomek, Tekirdağ, Turkey) was placed against the endothelium, and 1.4% sodium hyaluronate gel (Coevisc, Appasamy Associates, Madras, India) was instilled to prevent the anterior chamber from collapsing. The corneal opening was completed with left and right corneal scissors. The cornea was wet with sterile Balanced Salt Solution (BSS, Ocusol, Ubi Techpark, Singapore) by an assistant throughout the surgery. Because the corneal cloudiness did not decrease despite venting of the anterior chamber, the Coevisc gel was carefully used as a dissection tool in the chamber to delineate the location of the lens. The fairly intact lens material was extruded, but not the capsule, with this technique. The extracted lens appeared biconvex and round with a central axial length of 6 mm. The lens was white. Alternating BSS with Coevisc delivered with a 23G Simcoe to protect the endothelium, the torn thick lens capsule was gently nudged out of the anterior globe. Once the capsule was removed, the corneal sutures were placed. The corneal incision was divided into 4 segments, and positioning sutures with 7–0 polyglycolic acid ophthalmic sutures (Usiol, Lexington, USA) were first placed, followed by 9–0 monofilament polyglycolic acid (Luxcryl PGA, An-Vision GmBH, Luxembourg) in a continuous pattern confined to each segment. By this time, the cornea had cleared somewhat, and intraocular structures such as the anterior iris were easier to visualize. The vitreous appeared to have a smooth surface. Before closure of the final segment, the anterior chamber was cleared of viscoelastic with BSS using Simcoe; this also likely removed the lens endonucleus that may have slipped out during the time the capsule was torn, a duration suspected to have been about 20 days. When only clear BSS existed, triamcinolone acetonide, 0.1 ml (Shincort, 40 mg/ml; Shincort, YSP. Industries, Kajang, Malaysia) was introduced into the anterior chamber through a 26 G blunt cannula and rinsed slowly into the anterior chamber with BSS through the Simcoe to detect any escaped vitreous. There did not appear to be any vitreous, but the triamcinolone was retained on parts of the anterior iris that appeared inflamed. These were left in situ to promote the anti-inflammatory effect. After completing the final closure, 0.25 ml of gentamicin (Garasent, 40 mg/ml; Duopharma, Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia) was injected subconjunctivally in the dorsal conjunctiva, and 0.1 ml of triamcinolone was injected subconjunctivally at a different site. The lateral canthotomy site was sutured in two layers with 6–0 polyglycolic acid (Usiol, Lexington, KY, USA) with a standard closure. Figure 2 shows the surgical steps.

Perioperative medications included meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg SQ (Melonex; Intas Pharmaceuticals, Ahmedabad, India), tramadol 3 mg/kg (IM) (Acugesic; Duopharma, Klang, Malaysia), amoxicillin 15 mg/kg (IM) (Amoxi; Vemedim, Subang Jaya, Malaysia), and mecobalamin 1 ml (IV) (Methycobal; Eisai, Tokyo, Japan).
Postoperative management involved oral medications: paracetamol 250 mg (Progesic; Xepa-Soul Pattinson, Melaka, Malaysia) once only, prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg (Prednisolone; Idaman Pharma, Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia) BID for 7 days, then decreased to 0.5 mg/kg SID for 7 days, amoxicillin and clavulanic acid 625 mg (Curam; Sandoz GmbH, Kundl, Austria) BID for 14 days, papain 300,000 USP (Beazyme; Duopharma, Bangi, Malaysia) BID for 14 days; oral supplements: astaxanthin (Bio-life; Unison Nutraceuticals, Ayer Keroh, Malaysia), vitamins B1, B6, and B12 (Flavettes Neuroforte; Duopharma, Bangi, Malaysia), silymarin (Legalon; Madaus GmbH, Köln, Germany), and 0.5% chloramphenicol eyedrops (Nicol; Duopharma, Klang, Malaysia) four times a day topical for 17 days.
The bear’s appetite was reduced for the first week and then normalized by day 8 postoperatively. The right eyelids were mildly swollen after the surgery. The bear’s right eye was closed most of the time, and it was scratched with its claws and paws. The right eyelids started to partially open on day 12 and were fully open on day 18 postoperatively. The right eye conjunctivae were mildly red. The left eye appeared either large or bulging, and in view of the elevated IOP of 32 mmHg obtained when under anesthesia, it was decided to start with topical 0.005% latanoprost (Latanost; Duopharma, Klang, Malaysia) SID, dorzolamide/timolol (Cosopt; Santen Pharmaceutical, Ishikawa, Japan) BID, and oral glycerin 15 ml (Cap Kaki Tiga; Syarikat Wen Ken Drug, Johor Bahru, Malaysia) BID. The left eye appeared normal after 10 days, and anti-glaucoma medications were discontinued. The IOP was not taken again after the initial IOP measurement at surgery because the anesthetic risk was unwarranted, and the bear seemed clinically responsive to the anti-glaucoma treatments. Topical hyaluronic acid (Hylo Gel, Ursapharm, Saarbrücken, Germany) was administered to the right eye for lifelong eye care. The right eye menace response was negative, the cornea remained opaque, and the eye contents were not visible despite subtle corneal changes over time (Fig. 3).

## Discussion
Lens disease is common in Ursidae and has been reported in the Asiatic black bear, sun bear, sloth bear, brown bear, giant panda, and Andean spectacled bear (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). The records from bear rescue centers in China and Vietnam showed that cataracts (28.1%) and lens luxation (12.6%) were the most common ocular abnormalities among the 135 Asiatic black, sun, and brown bears. Among the 17 cases of lens luxation, 9 had concurrent cataracts (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). In Hong Kong, captive giant pandas older than 10–12 years commonly have incipient or immature cataracts. Mature and hypermature cataracts are frequently observed in giant pandas older than 25 years (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). Hartley and Oliveira also reported three out of four cases of hypermature cataracts in giant pandas complicated by lens-induced uveitis and secondary lens luxation. Another prevalence study in dogs (45 eyes) reported that 77.8% and 22.2% of lens luxation are secondary to hypermature senile cataract and trauma, respectively (Ali and Mostafa, 2023). Anterior lens displacement commonly results in glaucoma and uveitis, whereas posterior lens displacement can cause retinal damage (Ali and Mostafa, 2023).
This case report shows the development of cataract in one old sun bear, and the potentially unnoticed cataract condition causes lens-induced uveitis (LIU) or secondary glaucoma, leading to secondary lens luxation. LIU occurs at all stages of cataracts due to chronic and sustained leakage of lens protein (Wilkie et al., 2006). Hence, the release of inflammatory mediators in the posterior chamber weakens the lens zonules, leading to lens displacement (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). LIU can be subclinical in patients with cataract but is mostly clinically significant in patients with hypermature cataract (Wilkie et al., 2006; Malik et al., 2014; Ali and Mostafa, 2023). LIU is diagnosed on the basis of decreased IOP, cataractous condition, presence of episcleral injection, aqueous flare, and miosis (Adkins and Hendrix, 2005). Anterior uveitis was pronounced in this case before the surgery and was characterized by a lowered IOP. The reduction in IOP is associated with the release of prostaglandins and the increase in uveoscleral aqueous outflow through the ciliary stroma and musculature (Leasure et al., 2001). Secondary glaucoma can also be caused by infiltrating inflammatory cells or cytokines produced in uveitis cases, affecting the function of the trabecular meshwork (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). The unusually increased and decreased IOP in the left and right cataractous eyes of this bear demonstrate the different immunologic responses to uveitis. Rupture of the lens capsule can be spontaneous, traumatic, or accidental intraoperatively (Andrews et al., 2023). Spontaneous lens capsule rupture has been reported in dogs with diabetes and progressive cataracts (Wilkie et al., 2006) and in humans with senile hypermature cataracts and Alport’s syndrome (Scott, 1953; Andrews et al., 2023). Diabetes mellitus was ruled out by blood glucose measurement. The extracted lens is mildly cataractous and far from the hypermature stage. No remnants of the zonules were visible on the capsule. Therefore, the bear must have had gradual, chronic, and spontaneous dehiscence leading to anterior lens luxation. The cause of the rupture of the lens capsule was unknown, but since it appeared following lens luxation, trauma within the cage is a likely explanation.
Anterior lens luxation is an emergency requiring immediate surgical intervention to reduce complications, minimize pain, and improve the chances of retaining vision (Ali and Mostafa, 2023). Wildlife conservation centers are often located in rural or wildlife-friendly areas, far from established clinics and surgical facilities, and typically only provide basic and emergency procedures. The logistical arrangement of the microsurgery equipment was challenging but feasible with portable ophthalmic tools and equipment. Constant endothelial touch from the anteriorly luxated lens causes intense pain with additional corneal edema (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). Damage to the corneal endothelial cells is irreversible, resulting in decompensation and may progress to phthisis. An anteriorly displaced, swollen lens can physically impair aqueous drainage at multiple points, resulting in secondary glaucoma (Ali and Mostafa, 2023). However, secondary glaucoma caused by lens luxation was not common in bears, with 5.6% of cases (IOP > 30 mmHg) observed in China and Vietnam (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022).
The suggested surgical interventions in the case of anterior lens luxation are ICLE or phacoemulsification based on the surgeon’s preference (Ali and Mostafa, 2023). In one 36-year-old giant panda with left eye anterior lens luxation and secondary glaucoma, lensectomy could not be performed because of the increased anesthesia risks and pre-existing health concerns. Couching was attempted and successfully dislocated the lens back to the vitreous chamber, converting the condition into posterior lens luxation. The animal was conditioned to look up in her cage using food treats, then latanoprost was applied to induce myiosis to trap the posteriorly displaced lens. The animal was managed medically for several months with a combination of prostaglandin analogs and anti-inflammatory agents to control glaucoma and uveitis. The patient’s left eye was completely lost to vision after a year due to several episodes of anterior lens re-luxation and pressure spikes. In another case, couching was unsuccessful in a 31-year-old giant panda because the lens could not be sustained at the posterior position. ICLE and subtotal anterior automated vitrectomy were eventually performed, but the animal did not recover vision from the eye, which was potentially caused by secondary glaucoma (Hartley and Oliveira, 2022). Therefore, couching or trans-corneal lens reduction may be an alternative short-term solution to manage anterior lens luxation and avoid corneal decompensation. In dogs, a posteriorly luxated lens also often results in vision loss due to retinal atrophy or detachment, vitreal hemorrhage, vitreous syneresis, or persistent corneal edema (Ali and Mostafa, 2023).
The case reported in this paper may not have had a favorable outcome, such as restoring vision to the bear, due to factors beyond our control at the time. However, it demonstrates that minimal but sufficient specialized equipment can be sufficient to perform potentially vision-saving cataract surgeries on bears at this facility. Although the easier option would have been enucleation with minimal external intervention, the fact that we were able to preserve the eye despite initial postoperative challenges shows that the knowledge gained in surgical and postoperative care can be applied to future cataract surgeries. This case is important because it highlights the rectifiable weaknesses in our approach to conservation—training, early detection, and the rapid mobilization of off-site resources can all contribute to better outcomes for conserved and rescued animals. Building, maintaining, and expanding networks, such as the one we have started for the eye health of bears at BSBCC, is critical for effective veterinary care. As the existing captive sun bears age, we anticipate more ocular abnormalities, particularly cataracts and lens luxation. Operant conditioning of all the sun bears to accept basic ophthalmic examinations (ocular light examination, tonometry, and slit-lamp examination) and the administration of topical eye drop medications will also be a key factor in the success of ocular case management.
