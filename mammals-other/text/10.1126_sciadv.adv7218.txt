Experimental quantification of nuclear quantum effects on the hydrogen bond of liquid water.

# Experimental quantification of nuclear quantum effects on the hydrogen bond of liquid water

## Abstract
Nuclear quantum effects (NQEs) significantly influence material properties upon isotopic substitution, particularly with light atoms such as hydrogen. While water is rich in hydrogen, its hydrogen-bonded structure exhibits only moderate NQEs. Simulations ascribe this to competing zero-point energies (ZPEs): Intermolecular ZPEs stabilize hydrogen-bonds, while intramolecular ZPEs destabilize them. However, experimental validation has been lacking due to the difficulty in quantifying NQEs. The air/water interface provides an ideal platform to quantify NQEs in liquid water using surface-specific vibrational spectroscopy. By analyzing the excess/depletion of interfacial HOD, H2O, and D2O molecules with one free OH/OD group and the other H-bonded OH/OD group, we found that the intermolecular ZPE destabilizes the hydrogen-bonds by 0.74 ± 0.20 kilojoule per mole upon isotope substitution from H to D, while the intramolecular ZPE stabilizes them by 0.78 ± 0.33 kilojoule per mole. This near-complete cancellation explains the overall moderate NQE in liquid water. The interface thus allows for quantifying NQEs in water.

## INTRODUCTION
Quantum-mechanical effects rule the world of electrons, yet nuclear quantum effects (NQEs) are pivotal to explaining the behavior of light nuclei, such as hydrogen atoms. NQEs influence molecular interactions through the zero-point energy (ZPE) of vibrational modes, which is mass dependent. Consequently, when considered quantum mechanically, ZPE modifies macroscopic properties of materials upon isotopic substitution of light atoms by affecting chemical bonding. This phenomenon arises from the interplay between vibrational frequencies and atomic masses in quantum systems. For example, in the case of water, H2O has a 4°C lower melting point and a 7°C lower temperature of maximum density than D2O (1). The change in the melting point has been explained by the softening of the hydrogen-bond (H-bond) network due to the different ZPE of H2O and D2O. In contrast to the melting temperature, the critical temperature of H2O is higher than that of D2O (1). Furthermore, the surface tensions of H2O and D2O are almost the same (2). These different trends demonstrate that NQE in water cannot be rationalized solely by the softening of the H bond network.
Simulations have suggested that these complex NQEs can be explained by two competing NQEs in liquid water (1, 3–5); the intramolecular ZPE increases the OH bond length and thus increases the dipole moment of water (Fig. 1A) (6), making H bonds stronger. At the same time, the intermolecular ZPE destabilizes the H bond network, reducing the H bond strength (Fig. 1B). The radial distribution function of liquid H2O and D2O obtained by x-ray, γ-ray, and neutron scattering measurement shows that the OH covalent bond is elongated as compared to the OD bond, while the H∙∙∙O H bond is ∼4% shorter than the D∙∙∙O H bond (7), consistent with simulations (8). However, the intermolecular and intramolecular contributions to the differences in the H bonding free energy upon isotope substitution from H to D (denoted as ∆Einter and ∆Eintra, respectively) have not been experimentally detected for liquid water. This knowledge gap can be traced to the ultrafast fluctuations of H bonds in liquid water that make the individual detection of ∆Einter and ∆Eintra challenging, impeding a unified understanding of NQEs in water.

To experimentally probe these contributions to NQEs, knowledge of the free energy of a water molecule with a non–H-bonded, free OH/OD (OHf/ODf) group and an H-bonded OH/OD (OHHB/ODHB) group is required. For example, the difference in free energy of a HOD molecule with OHf and ODHB groups and a HOD molecule with ODf and OHHB groups gives access to ∆Einter because the change in the amplitude of the dipole moment of the HOD molecule due to the NQE is negligibly small (see Fig. 1C and the Supplementary Materials). In contrast, the free energy difference of HfOHHB + DHBODHB and HHBOHHB + DfODHB is given by the sum of ∆Einter and ∆Eintra (see Fig. 1D and the Supplementary Materials). Hence, in an environment where HOD, D2O, and H2O molecules experience H bond fluctuations as in liquid water and at the same time have OHf/ODf groups, one can experimentally estimate ∆Einter and ∆Eintra separately, allowing us to discuss the presence of the competing NQE in liquid water and opening up a path to unveil the complex NQE of liquid water.
These seemingly unrealistic configurations of liquid water at ambient conditions are actually abundant at the air/isotopically diluted water interface, where one OH/OD group is H bonded and the other OH/OD group can be free (9). At the interface, water exhibits a unique combination of properties that both distinguish it from and connect it to bulk water. Studies have shown that the density of H-bonding OH/OD groups in interfacial water is lower than in the bulk (10, 11). However, this difference in density does not translate to other properties. These interfacial H bonds maintain strengths comparable to those in bulk water (12). Furthermore, they undergo fluctuations that mirror those observed in the bulk (13, 14). Adding to this similarity, the vibrational relaxation dynamics of interfacial water molecules closely resemble those of their bulk counterparts (15). These findings paint a nuanced picture of interfacial water, highlighting how it maintains many bulk-like characteristics despite its distinct structural arrangement at the interface. Surface-specific heterodyne-detected sum-frequency generation (HD-SFG) spectroscopy is well suited to probe these interfacial H2O, D2O, and HOD molecules because it can probe the topmost one to two layers of water with OHf/ODf groups at the air/water interface (9, 16), and it can spectrally discriminate between HfODHB, HHBODf, HfOHHB, and DfODHB molecules (12, 13, 17–19), unlike the conventional SFG (20, 21). In this article, we quantify the free energy difference of these isotopically substituted species from their relative population at the air/isotopically diluted water interface by measuring the HD-SFG spectra in the OH and OD stretch region. Our estimated values of ∆Einter and ∆Eintra provide the first experimental evidence that the intermolecular ZPE and intramolecular ZPE largely cancel out, making NQEs in water very small.

## RESULTS
We performed HD-SFG measurements at the air/isotopically diluted water interface with varying H2O-D2O concentrations. We recorded the HD-SFG data in both the OH and OD stretch regions. The imaginary part of the second-order SFG susceptibility in the yyz direction [Imχyyz(2)] in the OH stretch region are displayed in Fig. 2 (A and B), where the z axis forms the surface normal, and the yz plane forms the incident plane of the beams. The Imχyyz(2) data were obtained by correcting the measured HD-SFG spectra for the Fresnel factor (see the Supplementary Materials) (22, 23). The pure H2O SFG spectrum shows the 3700-cm−1 sharp positive peak, 3600-cm−1 positive shoulder, 3450-cm−1 negative broad band, and 3200-cm−1 negative shoulder (24, 25). The 3700-cm−1 sharp peak arises from the free OH stretch mode of HfOHHB molecules [HfO(HHB) stretch mode] (9). The 3600-cm−1 shoulder arises from the antisymmetric stretch mode of HHBOHHB molecules (see also the Supplementary Materials) (12). The 3450-cm−1 negative band arises from the OHHB group (24). The 3200-cm−1 shoulder peak results from intermolecular vibrational coupling (IVC) of the OHHB stretch modes (26) together with the Fermi resonance (FR) of the stretch mode and bending mode (see the Supplementary Materials) (23, 27).

We note that NQEs shift the equilibrium to lower the value of K to 3.86 (28). Irrespective of the exact value of K, when H2O is mixed with D2O, the HOD spectral feature appears in addition to H2O and D2O spectral signatures. The deduced HOD spectral shape (13, 17) without NQEs (see the Supplementary Materials) is also shown as the black broken line in Fig. 2 (A and B). As the concentration of D2O increases, the free OH peak slightly redshifts, the 3600 cm−1 shoulder component vanishes, and the IVC + FR peak disappears (16, 17). The redshift of the free OH peak occurs because the frequency of the free OH stretch mode of HfODHB molecules [HfO(DHB) stretch mode] is lower than that of the HfO(HHB) mode due to the absence of intramolecular vibrational coupling in HOD (see the Supplementary Materials) (12); thus, the observed free OH peak reflects a population-weighted average of the HfOHHB and HfODHB molecules. The shoulder peak and the IVC + FR peak disappear because they are present only for H2O molecules (12, 26, 27). The connection between the peak assignments and the molecular structure of water is summarized in Fig. 2 (C and D).
A similar trend can be seen in the OD stretch region (Fig. 2, E and F). The pure D2O spectrum shows the 2730-cm−1 sharp positive peak arising from the free OD stretch mode of DfODHB molecules [DfO(DHB) stretch mode], the 2650-cm−1 shoulder, the 2510-cm−1 negative band arising from the ODHB group, and the 2370-cm−1 negative IVC + FR peak (17). When H2O is added to D2O, the free OD peak redshifts because of the lower frequency of the free OD stretch mode of DfOHHB molecule [DfO(HHB) stretch mode]. Furthermore, the 2650-cm−1 negative shoulder peak and the IVC + FR peak disappear. The variation of the SFG spectra in the OD stretch region upon the isotope dilution is thus qualitatively consistent with the OH stretch region. The minor differences in the spectral features in the OH and OD stretch modes are discussed in the Supplementary Materials.
To quantify the variation of the spectral features, we fit the Imχ(2) spectra with five Gaussian bands (see Fig. 2C). These Gaussian bands represent the peaks of the IVC + FR, the OHHB/ODHB peak, the 3600-/2650-cm−1 shoulder peak, the HfO(HHB)/DfO(DHB) stretch peak, and the HfO(DHB)/DfO(HHB) peak. The details of the fitting procedure and the obtained parameters can be found in tables S1 and S2 in the Supplementary Materials. The variation of [OH], [OD], [H2O], [D2O], and [HOD] based on a purely statistical distribution of isotopes based on Eq. 1 (K=4) is displayed in Fig. 3A, while the trends of the experimentally observed SFG amplitudes for the five peaks are displayed in Fig. 3, B to F. Note that the fitting parameters with the Gaussians to the D2O spectrum are consistent with previous fits (29).

First, we focus on the spectral feature in the <3650-cm−1 region of the OH stretch spectra and in the <2680-cm−1 region of the OD stretch spectra, which include the IVC + FR peak, the OHHB/ODHB stretch peak, and the 3600-/2650-cm−1 shoulder peak. Figure 3 (B to D) displays the amplitude variations of the OHHB/ODHB peaks, the IVC + FR negative peaks, and the shoulder peaks, respectively. Figure 3B shows that the OHHB (ODHB) amplitude decreases with the OH (OD) fraction, and the variations of the OHHB and ODHB peak amplitudes overlap well. This agreement of the amplitude variations indicates that NQEs do not affect the isotope distribution of the OHHB/ODHB groups of the interfacial water, as expected, given the small NQE in H-bonded bulk water. The amplitude variations further agree with the diagonal line representing the OH/OD concentration (dashed line in Fig. 3A). Hence, the data indicate that the OH band arises from all interfacial groups OH—irrespective of being located within H2O and HOD molecules—and the amplitudes vary according to that expected for a statistical isotope distribution.
Figure 3C shows that the amplitude variations of the IVC + FR peaks in the OH and OD stretch regions overlap. The variations of the IVC + FR peak amplitude follow not the trend of OH/OD concentrations, but the trend of H2O/D2O concentration estimated from Eq. 1. This result illustrates that the degrees of the IVC are proportional to the concentration of H2O/D2O. Also, the amplitude of the FR scales with the concentration of H2O/D2O as is discussed in the Supplementary Materials. Hence, our results indicate that the IVC + FR contribution reflects the H2O/D2O concentrations, and the subsurface populations of H2O and D2O are the same as in bulk.
Figure 3D shows that the amplitude variations of the shoulder peaks in the OH and OD stretch regions overlap and follow the H2O/D2O concentration trend. Because the shoulder peak arises mainly from the antisymmetric OH stretching of the H2O molecules with two H-bond donors (13, 23), this result further supports the notion that the H2O and D2O concentrations in the subsurface region are identical to that in the bulk.
Subsequently, we focus on the amplitude variation of the free OH/OD peaks above 3650 cm−1 in the OH stretch region and above 2650 cm−1 in the OD stretch region. The amplitude variations of the HfO(DHB) and DfO(HHB) are shown in Fig. 3E. The amplitudes shown in Fig. 3E are normalized by the free OH/OD amplitude in the deduced HOD data without NQE shown in the black broken lines in Fig. 2 (A and E) (see the Supplementary Materials). The amplitudes of the HfO(HHB) and DfO(DHB) peaks are displayed in Fig. 3F. The variations of the HfO(HHB) and DfO(DHB) peak amplitudes (Fig. 3F) overlap very well, whereas the variations of the HfO(DHB) and DfO(HHB) peak amplitudes deviate substantially (Fig. 3E). Hence, Fig. 3E suggest a marked NQE on the population of interfacial HOD molecules, while Fig. 3F manifests that the populations of the interfacial H2O and D2O are comparable and behave nearly ideally.

## DISCUSSION
The H-bonding equilibria shown in Fig. 1 (C and D) underlie the interfacial populations of the species contributing to the free OH/OD peaks in Fig. 3 (E and F). These read[HfODHB]⇌[DfOHHB](2)[HfOHHB]+[DHBODHB]⇌[DfODHB]+[HHBOHHB](3)where [DHBODHB] and [HHBOHHB] are the H-bonded bulk or subsurface D2O and H2O species. The free energy difference between HfODHB and DfOHHB estimated from the equilibrium in Eq. 2 can tell us the free energy change of the H bond of water upon isotope substitution from H to D due to the intermolecular ZPE, ∆EO⋯H/D HBinterZPE. The free energy difference estimated from the equilibrium in Eq. 3 leads to the sum of ∆EO⋯H/D HBinterZPE and ∆EH2O/D2O HBintraZPE, where ∆EH2O/D2O HBintraZPE denotes the free energy change due to the intramolecular ZPE (see the detailed discussion in the Supplementary Materials).
Figure 3G displays the ratios of the populations of the DfOHHB and HfODHB species as well as the populations of the HfOHHB and DfODHB species. The figure shows that the DfOHHB population is, on average, 26 ± 6% lower than the HfODHB population, meaning that the HfODHB is energetically favored (by 0.74 ± 0.20 kJ/mol) over DfOHHB species. Because this free energy difference is approximately equal to ∆EO⋯H/D HBinterZPE, one can conclude that ∆EO⋯H/D HBinterZPE=0.74±0.20 kJ/mol.
Similarly, we computed the free energy difference of HfOHHB + DHBODHB and DfODHB + HHBOHHB from the populations of the HfOHHB and DfODHB species in Fig. 3F. On average, the population of HfOHHB is somewhat lower (98 ± 5% of DfODHB) than the population of DfODHB, indicating that the free energy of HfOHHB + DHBODHB is 0.04 ± 0.13 kJ/mol higher than DfODHB + HHBOHHB. As the free energy difference of HfOHHB + DHBODHB and DfODHB + HHBOHHB is equal to ∆EO⋯H/D HBinterZPE+∆EH2O/D2O HBintraZPE, ∆EH2O/D2O HBintraZPE=−0.78±0.33 kJ/mol. That means the H bond is stabilized upon isotope substitution from H to D because of the intramolecular ZPE. Our data suggest that >90% of the free energy difference of the H bond caused by the intermolecular ZPE upon the isotope substitution is canceled by that because of the intramolecular ZPE in liquid water.
The free energy difference in the O-H∙∙∙O and O-D∙∙∙O H-bond in HOD molecule of 0.74 ± 0.20 kJ/mol obtained from the current study of liquid water is much smaller than the energy difference of 3.0 ± 1.5 kJ/mol when a H-bond energy is assumed to be ~21 kJ/mol—the average value for H bonds in liquid water (30–32)—which was estimated from the data of the inelastic tunneling spectroscopy of HOD molecules at 5 K (33). This apparently strong temperature dependence of the O-H∙∙∙O and O-D∙∙∙O H-bond energy difference suggests that lowering the temperature reduces the intermolecular ZPE significantly, partially because of the increase in the uncertainty of the hydrogen atom position and partially because of the broad distribution of the H-bond strengths in liquid water, as opposed to the narrow distribution at cryogenic temperatures. The very broad negative HD-SFG peaks centered at 3400 cm−1 in the OH stretch region and at 2500 cm−1 in the OD stretch region manifests that the liquid water samples a large variety of the H-bond strengths (11, 16, 23, 34).
Our findings indicate negligible NQE effect on the population and structure of H2O and D2O at the air/water interface, while the NQEs play a crucial role in determining the orientation of HOD molecules. In particular, the population of the OD and OH groups of HOD molecules with non–H-bonded groups can differ by 26%. Hence, the analysis of aqueous interfaces using OH/OD groups of isotopically diluted water, often done to avoid vibrational coupling in the bulk (35–37) and at interfaces (38–40), should be performed carefully.
In conclusion, we performed the HD-SFG spectroscopic measurement at the air/isotopically diluted water interfaces at various H2O-D2O concentrations to understand the role of the intra-/intermolecular ZPE on the H bond of liquid water. We found that the intramolecular ZPE stabilizes the H bond by 0.78 ± 0.33 kJ/mol upon the isotope substitution from H to D, while the intermolecular ZPE destabilizes the H bond by 0.74 ± 0.20 kJ/mol. Thus, more than 90% of the free energy difference arising from the NQEs in liquid water is canceled out, making the NQEs in liquid water rather moderate. Such a vibrational SFG approach can be used for ice surfaces (41–43), which allows us to explore the intermolecular/intramolecular ZPEs of ice at lower temperatures.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## HD-SFG measurement
For heterodyne-detected SFG measurements, the experimental setup is described elsewhere (10, 13). In short, we used a Ti:Sapphire regenerative amplifier system (Spitfire Ace, Spectra-Physics) pumped by a seed Ti:Sapphire oscillator laser system (Mai Tai SP, Spectra-Physics) as the laser source. A portion of the power was used to generate a narrowband visible pulse (bandwidth ~12.5 cm−1 at a central wavelength of 800 nm) using a 4f pulse shaper. The rest of the power was used to pump a commercial optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS SHBS-400, Spectra-Physics) system, from which the signal and idler beams were collimated and propagated to a silver gallium disulfide (AgGaS2) crystal for the generation of a broadband mid-infrared (IR) pulse (bandwidth of ~300 cm−1). The visible (ω1) and infrared (ω2) pulses were first focused on a 20-μm-thick y-cut quartz plate to generate the local oscillation (LO) signal. Then, the ω1, ω2, and LO pulses were focused again on the sample surface to generate SFG, all at incident angles of 45 degrees. A phase modulator was placed before the sample to delay the LO pulse relative to the ω1 and ω2 pulses by ~2 ps. The SFG and LO signals created an interference pattern on a liquid-nitrogen-cooled charge-coupled device camera (Princeton Instruments, PyLoN). All heterodyne measurements were performed using the ssp polarization combination, where ssp denotes s-polarized SFG, s-polarized visible, and p-polarized IR beams. To avoid strong IR absorption by CO2 and water vapor, SFG measurements were conducted in a N2-purged environment. The phase information of the complex χeff(2)ssp spectra was obtained via Fourier analysis of the interferogram and normalization by a z-cut quartz crystal. The pure H2O and D2O HD-SFG spectra in OH and OD stretch regions, respectively, have always been inspected at the beginning and end of the day to ensure that frequency shifts caused by laser fluctuations are eliminated.
