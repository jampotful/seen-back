Distinct oxytocin populations support seismic social communication and militant behaviors in a naturally solitary wild rodent.
Zilkha N, Hamade R, Chuartzman SG, Sofer Y, Kashash Y, Meir R, Fishman R, Biton IE, Ben-Dor S, Kimchi T. (2025). iScience. . doi:10.1016/j.isci.2025.114151. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Distinct oxytocin populations support seismic social communication and militant behaviors in a naturally solitary wild rodent

## Abstract
SummaryThe blind mole rat (BMR) is a solitary and highly aggressive subterranean mammal that avoids social interaction yet communicates with conspecifics using long-distance seismic signals. Here, we identify a previously undescribed oxytocin (OT) neuron population in the anteroventral periventricular nucleus (AVPV) of the BMR hypothalamus, alongside OT+ neurons in the paraventricular (PVN) and supraoptic nuclei. Using neuroanatomical mapping and ethologically relevant social paradigms, we show that OT+ neurons in both the AVPV and PVN are activated by non-contact social encounters. However, they are differently recruited by the social signals, with PVN neuronal activation associated with defensive behaviors, and AVPV neuronal activation associated with seismic communication. AI-based behavioral analysis further links OT circuitry activation with efficient drumming behavior. These findings indicate an unexpected role for oxytocin in solitary social signaling and suggest that in BMRs, the oxytocinergic system has undergone unique adaptations to support a non-affiliative, context-specific social lifestyle.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Behavioral neuroscience

## Introduction
Social isolation is generally perceived as an aversive and maladaptive state for most mammals, including humans, often leading to severe behavioral and physiological consequences.1,2,3,4 As a result, social neuroscientists have focused almost exclusively on gregarious animals, while largely neglecting inherently solitary species, even though over 20% of mammalian species exhibit solitary lifestyles.5 Rodents such as mice and rats, which constitute the most widely used models in neuroscience, form complex social groups,6,7,8,9 and even display empathy and helping behaviors.10,11,12 When subjected to forced social isolation, these animals exhibit physiological stress responses and brain-wide changes that can lead to illness or death.1,13,14,15 In contrast, solitary animals not only tolerate social isolation, but they often require it to thrive,5,16 and in some cases show acute stress or aggression when exposed to conspecifics.17,18 Notably, even in humans, typically considered a highly social species, some individuals actively choose solitary lifestyles, highlighting that sociality exists along a spectrum shaped by both biological and environmental factors.4,19
Among the most conserved and studied neuromodulators of social behavior is oxytocin (OT), a nonapeptide produced by neurons in the paraventricular (PVN) and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus.20,21,22 OT regulates a broad range of social behaviors in mammals, from social recognition to mating and parental care.23,24,25,26,27 However, recent evidence also implicates OT in aggressive and agonistic interactions, including aggression in laboratory rats,28 and mice.29,30,31 Moreover, it was recently demonstrated that OT+ PVN neurons are necessary and sufficient for promoting dyadic aggression and establishing dominance hierarchy in groups of wild-derived female mice.32 These findings support the broader notion that OT enhances the salience of social cues (whether affiliative or agonistic), and mediates diverse species-specific social behaviors, rather than uniformly promoting prosociality.22,33,34
Despite growing recognition of OT’s pivotal role in social behavior of mammalian social species35 including mice,22,36 rats,20,37 monkeys,35,38 and humans,4,39,40 its function in solitary mammalian species remains unknown. To address this gap, we turn to the blind mole rat (BMR; Nannospalax galili, Figure 1A), a strictly solitary subterranean rodent that evolved a remarkable suite of physiological and behavioral specializations.16,41 BMRs spend their entire lives alone underground, in self-excavated tunnel systems. They are congenitally blind and display extreme territoriality and aggression toward conspecifics outside the brief mating season.16,42,43,44 If two individuals meet in the wild, it typically results in violent aggression with the risk of serious injury or death.41,45,46

Despite their asocial nature, BMRs use unique rhythmic head-drumming to produce seismic signals for long-range communication with conspecifics.47,48 The seismic signals are detected mainly by “jaw listening behavior.”16,49,50 This specialized communication system is used to find mates and signal their territory,16,47,49,51,52 as well as for underground spatial orientation.51,53 The coexistence of extreme social avoidance with an evolved, species-specific repertoire for social communication raises important questions about the underlying neural architecture: How does the BMR brain support a solitary lifestyle while enabling such specialized, context-dependent social behavior, and what are the neural circuits and molecular regulators that mediate this balance?
Here, we combine comparative neuroanatomy, ethologically relevant social paradigms, and functional neural mapping to explore how the oxytocinergic system contributes to the unique social behaviors of the BMR. We describe three anatomically distinct OT-expressing subpopulations in the hypothalamus, including a previously undescribed population in the anteroventral periventricular nucleus (AVPV). This AVPV OT+ cluster was selectively activated during a non-physical social encounter and correlated with seismic communication behavior. In contrast, OT+ neurons in the PVN were associated with defensive and threatening social responses. These findings suggest that in this species, which chooses to live alone throughout life, oxytocinergic neurons support context-dependent social avoidance, and may have been evolutionarily repurposed to mediate non-affiliative and agonist social contexts.

## Results

## Ex vivo imaging reveals unique craniofacial and neuroanatomical adaptations for subterranean lifestyle and seismic communications
For anatomical characterization of the BMR’s skull and brain, we conducted a whole-head ex vivo imaging using micro-CT and MRI (see methods, Figures 1B and 1C, Videos S1, S2 and S3).

Micro-CT scans (Video S1) revealed cranial and dental specializations for a fossorial lifestyle, including robust jaws and two sets of chisel-shaped incisors optimized for gnawing through compact soil.54 The lower jaw exhibits two distinctive posterior protrusions: a condylar process that contacts the tympanic bulla, supporting “jaw listening,”16,50 and an alveolar process that stabilizes the incisors against fracture during forceful digging,55 a morphology unique to the Spalacidae family.54 The nasal and incisive bones form a wide elongated rostral portion of the skull that facilitates bulldozing and blocking behavior.56 (Figure 1B and Video S1).
MRI scans (Videos S2 and S3) showed additional unique specialization to the BMR lifestyle. Sagittal views highlighted an enlarged harderian gland cavity, which secretes aggression-dampening pheromones to deter intruders.57 Coronal and sagittal images revealed hypertrophied masseter muscles surrounding the jaw, supporting powerful biting and gnawing.58 Well-developed vomeronasal organ (VNO) canals and main olfactory epithelium (MOE) were also evident, indicating reliance on chemosensory cues for underground communication.59 Finally, MRI scans showed flattening along the brain’s ventral plane and an absence of optic tracts, consistent with advanced visual system degeneration16,60 (Figure 1C and Video S2).

## Comparative analysis of the oxytocin system uncovers a previously unknown neuronal population
To examine whether OT sequence evolution corresponds with social lifestyle, we compared OT DNA sequences across >20 rodents representing 3 diverse social lifestyles (solitary, facultatively solitary, social) within the Myomorpha suborder (Figure 1D). We further compared the full OT protein sequences across >40 rodent species, representing solitary, facultatively solitary, and social lifestyles, as well as eusocial lifestyle (Figures 1E and S1B). Neither the OT gene nor the OT protein sequence segregated according to social strategy.
We next compared hypothalamic OT expression patterns between BMRs and social rats (Rattus norvegicus), which are evolutionarily related and have comparable brain size (Figures 1F–1Q, and S2A–S2D).61,62 BMRs exhibited significantly fewer OT+ neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN; Figures 1F–1H), and a marginally significant reduction in the supraoptic nucleus (SON; Figures 1J–1L), although overall OT+ neuron density did not differ significantly due to species-specific dispersion patterns (Figures 1I and M).
Remarkably, we also discovered a previously unreported population of OT+ neurons in the BMR’s anteroventral periventricular nucleus (AVPV), which was nearly absent in rats (Figures 1N–1P). The density of AVPV OT+ neurons was also significantly higher in BMRs compared to rats (Figure 1Q). No obvious sex differences were observed in the number of OT+ neurons across the PVN, SON, or AVPV of the BMR (Figures S2E–S2G).

## Social stimulation elicits robust seismic communication and defensive responses in a solitary rodent
Using our custom-designed paradigm that allows non-contact (and visually independent) social interactions, we phenotyped the behavioral response repertoire of male BMRs to a same-sex intruder conspecific (Figures 2A and 2B; see methods).

Across trials, we identified six distinct behaviors that were categorized into three functional classes: (1) seismic communication, including head drumming and jaw listening16,49; (2) defensive behaviors, including tube blocking and self-grooming42,63; and (3) agonistic behaviors, including attacks on the divider and open-mouth threats63,64 (Figures 2C–2L, S3, and Video S4).

A representative video shows deep learning based automatic tracking of the head/snout position of the BMR in relation to its center body, enabling detailed and accurate analysis of head drumming behavior. A representative frame from the video is shown in Figure S6B.
Exposure to a social stimulus, even without physical contact, resulted in a dramatic shift in behavioral repertoire. BMRs in the social group exhibited significantly higher levels of social activity compared to controls, without a corresponding change in general locomotion (Figures 2C–2F, S3A and S3B). Specifically, animals in the social group produced significantly higher levels of seismic communication behaviors compared to control animals (Figures 2G and 2H). All socially exposed animals performed seismic communication behaviors, compared to fewer than 40% of controls, and did so with significantly greater duration and shorter latencies (Figure S3C).
Defensive behaviors were also elevated in the social group. Animals exhibited these behaviors more frequently, spent more total time on them, and initiated them with shorter latencies (Figures 2I–2J, and S3D). Finally, BMRs showed a marginally significant increase in agonistic behaviors, with significantly shorter latency to initiation (Figures 2K–2L, and S3E). Interestingly, observing the stimulus animals showed that they performed more defensive grooming behavior, compared to the resident BMRs (Figure S3F). No significant differences were found between stimulus and resident BMRs in the other behaviors quantified (Figure S3F), nor were there any sex differences in a separate cohort of males and females (Figure S3G).

We next asked whether these behavioral traits are reflected in oxytocinergic activity in the hypothalamus.

## Social encounters selectively activate paraventricular nucleus oxytocin neurons linked to defensive behavior
Resident BMR brains were stained for oxytocin (OT) and cFos to assess neuronal activation (Figures 3 and S4). Quantification of OT+ and cFos+ neurons in the PVN revealed a significant increase in OT+/cFos+ double-labeled neurons in the social group compared to controls, with no difference in the total number of OT+ or cFos+ neurons between groups (Figures 3B–3D). No differences in OT+/cFos+ co-expression were observed in the SON (control = 757.3 ± 392.5, social = 557.4 ± 266.2, p > 0.3).
To investigate whether this activation was behaviorally relevant, we examined correlations between PVN OT+/cFos+ neuron count and specific social behaviors within the social group. We found a significant negative correlation between PVN activation and the number of defensive behaviors, but no significant association with agonistic or seismic communication behaviors (Figures 3E–3G).
To further examine individual variability in behavioral response, we categorized BMRs into sub-groups based on high or low levels of agonistic or defensive behaviors, as previously described in BMR studies (Ganem & Nevo, 1996). We found significant differences in PVN OT activation between these behavioral phenotypes (Figures S4A–S4D). Specifically, animals exhibiting either higher agonistic or higher defensive behavior had lower levels of OT+/cFos+ activation in the PVN (Figures S4B and S4D).
These results imply a functional role for PVN OT neurons in constraining or modulating context-specific aggression and defense toward conspecifics.

## Social encounters recruit a previously undescribed anteroventral periventricular nucleus oxytocin neuronal population in the blind mole rat linked to seismic social communication
As described above (Figures 1N–1Q), our anatomical analysis revealed a previously unreported population of OT+ neurons in the hypothalamic AVPV of the blind mole rat. To assess whether this population is engaged during social interactions, we quantified OT+ and OT+/cFos+ neurons following a non-contact social encounter (Figures 4 and S5). We found that the number of OT+/cFos+ neurons in the AVPV was significantly elevated in the social group (Figure 4D), and this activation was marginally negatively correlated with the number of seismic communication events (Figure 4G). A marginally significant increase in the number of OT+ neurons in socially exposed animals compared to controls was also detected (Figure 4B).

Together, these results suggest that this species-specific, uncovered, AVPV OT+ population may contribute to the modulation of seismic social communication, a species-specific social phenotype in this solitary rodent.

## AI-based analysis of social seismic communication reveals a phenotype associated with oxytocinergic circuitry
To further investigate the specific features of social seismic communication in blind mole rats, we applied a deep learning model to perform a detailed, frame-by-frame behavioral analysis during signaling bouts (Figures 5 and S6; Video S4). This pose-tracking approach followed the BMR’s posture across time (Figures 5A and S6A), enabling the construction of a continuous behavioral plot from which head-drumming events (defined as seismic signal production) could be identified and characterized (Figures 5B–5C, S6B; see Methods).

This AI-based analysis extracted six quantitative parameters describing the head-drumming repertoire (Figure S6C). The behavior showed consistent structure across animals: the average duration of a single drumming bout was 0.41 ± 0.04 s, comprising an average of 2.68 ± 0.35 drums per bout. To integrate these features, we calculated a composite drum efficiency score, which reflects signaling that is temporally controlled, moderate, and efficient (see methods). Although no detectable correlations were found between single drumming features and the OT system, we found that the composite head drum efficiency score was significantly and positively correlated with the number of OT+ neurons in the PVN (Figure 5D). Moreover, animals with higher drum scores exhibited significantly more OT+ PVN neurons and higher activation of OT+ AVPV neurons (Figures S6D–S6G).
These findings suggest that individual variation in seismic communication behavior is anatomically and functionally linked to the OT circuitry, supporting a role for oxytocinergic architecture in shaping social signaling in the solitary BMR.

## Discussion
Our study explores the involvement of the oxytocinergic system in regulating solitary social behavior in the blind mole rat (Nannospalax galili), a naturally wild, subterranean, and non-social rodent. While oxytocin (OT) is a highly conserved neuropeptide implicated in a wide range of social behaviors across mammals, its role in solitary species has remained largely unexplored.20,22,35
We show that, despite its solitary lifestyle, the BMR exhibits structured and diverse behavioral responses to non-physical social encounters, including the rapid initiation of species-typical seismic communication (head-drumming and jaw-listening), elevated defensive behaviors (tube blocking), and modest increases in agonistic behaviors (open-mouth displays and divider attacks),42,43,49,52,63,64 with no changes in non-social activity. All components of this repertoire were quantified in a semi-naturalistic setting, revealing substantial individual differences in the diversity, intensity, and structure of social behaviors, reflecting the natural variability expected in truly wild animals.18,65,66,67,68,69 This variability is a key advantage of the BMR as an animal model, enabling the study of richer, ethologically relevant behavioral dynamics.
These behaviors were associated with the activation of distinct oxytocinergic populations: both a previously unknown population of OT+ neurons in the AVPV as well as OT+ neurons in the PVN were activated during social encounters, but OT+ AVPV activation was inversely associated with seismic communication, while OT+ PVN was inversely correlated with defensive behaviors. Individuals displaying the highest levels of defensive and agonistic behaviors exhibited the lowest PVN OT+/cFos+ co-expression, suggesting that these neurons may serve to modulate, rather than promote, aggressive responses.70
A similar pattern of PVN OT neuron activation has been reported in wild mice during aggressive interactions; however, in that context, PVN OT neurons were found to be necessary and sufficient to drive attack and dominance behaviors.32 These observations raise the possibility that while PVN OT neurons are engaged during social conflict in both species, the functional consequences of their activation may diverge depending on ecological context.5,21,33,71 In the BMR, OT activity may contribute to inhibiting or constraining escalation during potentially harmful aggressive encounters with conspecific, whereas in social rodents, it may support structured aggression used for social regulation.28,31,72 Future studies involving circuit-level manipulations and real-time imaging, on larger cohorts of BMRs, will be essential to determine the causal role and potential interaction between AVPV and PVN OT neurons in the BMR, particularly in relation to dopaminergic systems implicated in social motivation and salience.73
These results contribute to a growing body of research demonstrating that solitary mammals are not inherently asocial but express nuanced, context-dependent behavioral repertoires supported by ecological and neurobiological adaptations.5,17,74,75 The AVPV OT+ population we identified provides compelling evidence for the evolution of a specialized oxytocin circuit potentially mediating a unique subclass of social interactions, in a species where social interactions are both rare and life threatening. In social rodents, the AVPV has been implicated in sex-specific regulation of reproductive behaviors, including maternal care and OT release, as well as in male aggression and social conflict.32,76,77,78
Ecologically, our findings emphasize how the functional recruitment of distinct OT neuron populations may support behavioral specialization in solitary contexts.5,51,79,80,81 The BMR’s use of seismic communication reflects an energetically efficient strategy suited to subterranean life, where direct social contact is rare and potentially dangerous.47,49,51,52 The dissociation between AVPV-linked communication and PVN-linked aggression suggests that oxytocinergic systems have been selectively shaped to support precise and adaptive behavioral responses to consequential social events.82,83,84 This notion regarding functional differentiation between distinct OT populations is supported by extensive research describing specific roles and different projections assigned between the PVN and the SON,22,72,85 as well as between subtypes of parvocellular and magnocellular OT neurons.22,82,83,86
More broadly, our results align with a rethinking of solitary living as an ecologically contingent and evolutionarily derived strategy.5,79,80 Across mammalian taxa, solitary lifestyles often arise from selective pressures such as resource dispersion, burrow construction costs, predation risk, or thermoregulatory constraints.5,51,79,80 Even in species long considered nonsocial, individuals can exhibit situational and seasonal sociality or maintain selective affiliative relationships.74 The BMR exemplifies this ecological potential for latent social behavior, showing that social responsiveness is preserved and finely regulated within a solitary framework.
Taken together, our study offers a neuroethological framework for understanding how deeply conserved neuromodulatory systems such as oxytocin, can be evolutionarily repurposed to support solitary life strategies.

## Limitations of the study
While our study provides compelling evidence that the oxytocinergic system of the BMR is involved in processing social seismic signals, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, an analysis of changes in OT neuronal activity was performed by evaluating cFos protein expression. As cFos is not expressed in all neurons that are activated by a given stimulus, it may only represent a subset of the active neuronal populations. Second, our study is confined to evaluating the effects of social stimuli on behavior and neural activity in a short linear tube that is much simpler than the elaborate tunnel system of the BMR in nature. Further studies in a more complex labyrinth-like system may provide a much deeper understanding of the social behavioral repertoire and the role of OT neurons in the regulation of social behavior in nature. Finally, functional manipulations of OT expression/activity are required to ascertain the role of specific neuronal populations in governing distinct social repertoires.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Animals
Adult male and female blind mole rats (Nannospalax) were captured from the upper Galilee, Israel, under permit from the Israel Nature and Parks Authority (age at capture ∼1 year). As this species is classified as an agricultural pest, no special conservation permit was required. In accordance with their solitary lifestyle, animals were housed individually in custom-designed cages comprising two clear side-boxes (27 × 35 × 29 cm) connected by a narrow Plexiglas tunnel (20 × 6 cm; Figure S1). Conditions were maintained at 20–22 °C on a 12h light/dark cycle. Animals were fed daily with fresh vegetables (carrots, beets, lettuce). Adult male Wistar rats (8–9 weeks) were obtained from Envigo (Rehovot, Israel) and kept in standard laboratory cages with food and water ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Weizmann Institute of Science (approval numbers: 04610724-2, 06670823-2, 01430222-3).

## Method details

## Oxytocin sequence identification
Oxytocin (OT) gene and protein sequences were examined across a range of rodent species representing four distinct social strategies16,18,87,88: solitary,5,16 facultatively solitary,87,89 social,22,90 and eusocial.88,91,92 For each species, protein sequence searches were conducted using BlastP93 with the mouse OT sequence (NP_035155.1) against the NCBI NR database. If no annotated protein was found, TBlastN was performed against the species’ genome (if available), or against transcriptomic data from the NCBI Short Read Archive (SRA). Relevant sequences were assembled into contigs using Sequencher v5.4 (Gene Codes Corporation, Ann Arbor MI, USA), followed by manual curation and annotation. Final sequences are provided in Data S1, and their sources are detailed in Table S1.

## Phylogenetic analysis
Multiple sequence alignments were generated using Muscle v.3.8.31.94 Phylogenetic trees were constructed using PhyML v.3.095 and visualized with iTol v.7.96

## Social behavior assay
BMRs were acclimated to the animal facility for at least 6 weeks prior to testing. On test day, one side-box of the home cage was replaced with a clean box connected via a 60 cm tunnel that included a perforated divider (20 cm behind the stimulus animal; Figures 2A and 2B). This allowed olfactory, auditory, and seismic communication, but not physical contact. A “resident” BMR was habituated for 90 minutes in the experimental room before being exposed to either a conspecific “intruder” confined to the tube (“social” group) or to an empty tube (“control” group). Behavior was video recorded for 15 minutes at 24 frames per second by StreamPix 8 (Norpix, Montreal, Canada) and analyzed using EthoVision XT15 or Observer XT15 (Noldus, Wageningen, Netherlands).

## Immunohistochemistry
Ninety minutes after the start of the behavioral assay, animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of phenobarbital (∼500 mg/kg). Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, and animals were perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA solution and sectioned coronally at 30 μm using a vibratome (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, United States). Sections were stained with primary antibodies: mouse anti-OT (1:2000, provided by H. Gainer) and rabbit anti-cFos (1:1000, Cell Signaling). Secondary antibodies included goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, Invitrogen) and donkey anti-rabbit Cy5 (1:200, Jackson).

## Image analysis and cell quantification
Sections were imaged with an Olympus VS120 Virtual Slide Microscope and analyzed using OlyVIA 2.9. Regions of interest (PVN, SON, AVPV) were identified using the Paxinos and Watson Rat Brain Atlas, 3rd ed.97 Quantification was performed on evenly spaced sections using Imaris 9.5 (Bitplane) and automated with a modified StarDist v0.9.1 neural network, as previously detailed.32

## AI-based behavioral analysis
A custom Python pipeline was developed to track posture landmarks in BMRs during social encounters. Annotated features included the body center, snout, jaw, limbs, low abdomen, pelvic back, spine base, back, head, and 3 sections of the buccal ridge, an unpigmented facial region distinct to the BMR (Figure S6A).98 At least 50 video frames were manually annotated, representing both left and right profiles, to generate a pose estimation model using the YOLOv8 framework,99 enabling accurate high-throughput tracking.
Head-drumming behavior was quantified by plotting the displacement between the snout and body center across frames (Figures S6A and S6B). Extracted parameters included total drumming duration, number of drum events, bout duration, number of drums per bout, intra-bout frequency, and inter-bout interval. These variables were standardized and combined into a single “drum efficiency score” calculated from the following weighted components: drums per bout (–1), total drumming duration (–1), total head drums (+1), inter-bout interval (+1), and intra-bout frequency (–1). Higher scores reflect more controlled, moderate, and efficient seismic signaling.

## Ex-vivo micro-CT and MRI scanning
All images were acquired ex-vivo at the Weizmann Institute pre-clinical imaging center. An adult female BMR was euthanized using an i.p injection of Phenobarbital (∼500mg/Kg BW). The head of the animal was removed and fixed in 4% PFA solution for 4 days prior to the scanning.
Micro-CT imaging was performed using a SkyScan 1276 system (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) with acquisition parameters: 65 kV, 200 μA, 1.0 mm Al filter, 81.8 μm pixel resolution, 360 degrees rotation, step-and-shoot mode with 60° rotation steps, and three scan sets for full skull coverage. The total acquisition time was 33 minutes. Projections were reconstructed using NRecon v1.7.4.6 with beam hardening correction, ring artifact reduction, and post-alignment. 3D reconstructions were generated using Amira v5.2.2.
MRI was performed on a 9.4 T BioSpec 94/20 USR system (Bruker, Germany) equipped with AVANCE NEO electronics and a 72 mm 1H transmit/receive volume coil. T2-weighted multi-slice RARE sequences were acquired with a repetition time (TR) of 6000 ms, echo time (TE) of 23.9 ms, RARE factor of 2, matrix size of 320 × 320, and fat suppression. For transverse scans, 38 slices (0.45 mm thickness, 0.1 mm gap) were acquired with 38 signal averages and a field of view (FOV) of 2.0 × 3.5 cm2, corresponding to an image acquisition time of 5h 4min. For coronal scans, 30 slices with identical spacing were acquired with 24 averages, corresponding to an image acquisition time of 3h 12min.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
Number and density of OT+ cells, cFos+ cells, and OT+/cFos+ cells were analyzed using Student’s t test. Behaviors during the social encounter were analyzed using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test. Correlations between behaviors and OT or OT/cFos expression were calculated using Spearman's correlation. Significance was defined as p≤0.05. Analyses were performed using Statistica 14 software (TIBCO Software), or GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad). All the statistical details of experiments can be found in the results, figures and figure legends.
