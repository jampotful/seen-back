# A comprehensive study of c-Kit–expressing amacrine cells in the mammalian retina

## Abstract
Amacrine cells assemble complex circuits in the inner retina, where they integrate, modulate, and relay spatial and temporal information to retinal ganglion cells. Nevertheless, their organization and functional diversity are still not fully understood. In this study, we identify a distinct group of amacrine cells that express the stem cell factor receptor—c-Kit. Using combined techniques of anti-CD117 (c-Kit) immunolabeling, confocal imaging, and computational algorithms, we characterize the morphological features and spatial distribution of the c-Kit–expressing amacrine cells. Our results show that c-Kit-expressing amacrine cells are GABA-positive neurons with medium to wide dendritic fields that stratify within the specific strata in both ON and OFF sublaminae of the inner plexiform layer (IPL). The density recovery profile of c-Kit cells reveals regulated intercellular spacing, supporting the notion that they represent a single amacrine subtype with significant variation in their dendritic structures. Notably, a subset of these cells also bears ascending processes projecting into the outer plexiform layer (OPL). This distinct anatomical feature raises the possibility that c-Kit-expressing amacrine cells contribute to a long-range circuit across retinal layers and participate in a novel feedback pathway. Further analysis demonstrates that c-Kit–expressing amacrine cells are distinct from AII, starburst, nGnG, vGluT3-positive, VIP-positive, and dopaminergic amacrine cells. Our findings provide a new insight into the cellular diversity of amacrine cells and suggest a previously unrecognized functional role for c-Kit–expressing amacrine cells in the mammalian retina.

## Introduction
Amacrine cells are the principal inhibitory interneurons of the inner retina. They receive excitatory input from bipolar cells and inhibitory input from other amacrine cells, and in turn, provide feedback to bipolar cells through reciprocal synapses within the inner plexiform layer (IPL). Amacrine cells also interact with ganglion cells, the retinal output neurons, via both chemical synapses and gap junctions. Through their extensive network of chemical and electrical synapses in the IPL, amacrine cells play a critical role in integrating and encoding spatial, temporal, and directional visual information before it is transmitted to the brain via ganglion cells1–3. By performing these functions, amacrine cells contribute to a wide array of visual processes, including contrast enhancement, temporal filtering, motion detection and even circadian regulation. This remarkable functional diversity underlines their central role in shaping retinal output and visual perception4–6.
Notably, amacrine cells represent the most diverse class of retinal neurons, with at least 63 molecularly distinct types identified in the mammalian retina7. They are broadly classified into narrow-, medium- and wide-field types based on the dendritic field size and stratification in the IPL3,8,9. Amacrine cells contain almost every known neurotransmitter. In mammalian species, the vast majority (> 85%) of amacrine cells utilize either GABA or glycine as their neurotransmitter; but there is a subset of glycinergic amacrine cells also expresses vesicular glutamate transporter 3 (vGluT3) and co-releases both glutamate and glycine in the IPL10–13. Only a small population of amacrine cells lacks both GABAergic and glycinergic properties and is referred to as nGnG (non-GABAergic, nor-glycinergic) amacrine cells14,15. Moreover, certain subtypes of GABAergic and glycinergic amacrine cells co-release neuromodulators such as dopamine, acetylcholine or neuropeptides, such as vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)5,16,17. Among the best-characterized subtypes are cholinergic starburst amacrine cells, essential for direction selectivity, and dopaminergic amacrine cells, which play a key role in retinal adaptation to changes in ambient light. Despite extensive research, many amacrine cell subtypes remain poorly understood, particularly in terms of their intrinsic properties, morphological features, and physiological roles. In the present study, we applied a specific antibody against the c-Kit receptor tyrosine kinase to identify and characterize a distinct subpopulation of amacrine cells in the mammalian retina.
c-Kit receptor tyrosine kinase, also known as the stem cell factor receptor or CD117, is a single transmembrane glycoprotein that contains intracellular tyrosine kinase domains. It belongs to the platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) receptor subfamily of receptor tyrosine kinases18–20. It is expressed in various cell types, including stem cells, mast cells, germ cells, and melanocytes. In the nervous system, c-Kit expression is restricted but prominent, particularly in regions such as the cerebellum, where it is found in GABAergic interneurons21,22. We used the specific c-Kit antibody (anti-CD117) as a molecular biomarker in combination with confocal imaging and customized algorithm analysis to characterize the morphological features and spatial distribution of c-Kit-expressing amacrine cells (hereafter referred to as c-Kit-amacrine cells) in the mouse retina. Here we report for the first time that c-Kit-amacrine cells are GABA-containing neurons in the retina. They are distinct from AII, starburst, nGnG, vGluT3-positive, VIP-positive, and dopaminergic amacrine cell subtypes, comprising approximately 2.5—3.5% of the total amacrine cell population. These findings lay the groundwork for future studies investigating the potential roles of c-Kit and c-Kit-amacrine cells in retinal physiology.

## Results

## Identification and topographic analysis of c-Kit-amacrine cells in the mouse retina
The anti-CD117, which specifically recognizes the extracellular domain 494 amino acids of mouse c-Kit protein, was used to label c-Kit-expressing neurons in the mouse retina. Confocal z-stacked images of retinal whole mounts, captured from the proximal INL to the distal IPL, revealed a distinct population of amacrine cells selectively labeled by anti-CD117 (Fig. 1A). CD117-positive cells had somal diameters of ~ 7–8 µm and fine processes stratifying within the IPL. Their densities were quantified across different regions of the dorsal and ventral retina. In the dorsal retina, the average densities (cell/mm2) in the central, mid-peripheral, and far-peripheral regions were 779 ± 31, 724 ± 23 and 649 ± 26, respectively. In the corresponding regions of the ventral retina, the counts were 864 ± 37, 794 ± 31 and 676 ± 26 cells/mm2 (mean ± SEM; n = 4 animals, ages 2–3 months, Fig. 1B). The results indicate that the central ventral retina contains ~ 10% more c-Kit-amacrine cells than the central dorsal retina, a statistically significant (p < 0.05, n = 4). To assess whether c-Kit-amacrine cells represent a single subtype or comprise multiple subtypes, we analyzed their spatial arrangement using density recovery profiles (DRPs), measuring of retinal cell mosaic regularity23,24. CD117-positive somata were segmented with Cellpose 3 and processed through a custom Python pipeline to extract soma size, centroid coordinates, and fluorescence intensity, generating real-image mosaics (Fig. 1C). Random simulations were created to match area, geometry, and density, constrained by soma size (Fig. 1D), and DRPs were computed for both real and simulated mosaics (Fig. 1E). In a random distribution, the DRPs remain flat at the mean density (Fig. 1E, red dashed line); any deviations below this baseline indicate regulated intercellular spacing. In contrast, CD117-positive cells showed an initial dip, reflecting an exclusion zone, followed by a gradual return to mean density at a radius of ~ 40–45 µm (Fig. 1E, vertical dashed line). This pattern indicates nearest neighbors ≥ 40 µm, and a regular, anti-cluster mosaic, consistent with a single, regularly distributed population.
Quantitative analyses of the regularity index further supported this notion. Voronoi-domain patterns generated from real-image mosaics displayed a spatial distribution of CD17-positive cells (Fig. 1F). Real-image mosaics showed greater regularity than density-matched random simulations, specifically, the Voronoi-domain regularity index (VDRI) was 2.54 ± 0.28 compared with 1.81 ± 0.23, and the nearest-neighbor regularity index (NNRI) was 2.34 ± 0.18 in real mosaics compared with 1.77 ± 0.14 in simulations (both p < 0.05, Fig. 1G). These indices, which are sensitive to exclusion zone size and widely applied in mosaic analyses, confirm regulated spacing beyond chance. Taken together, the presence of a prominent exclusion zone and significantly elevated regularity indices indicate that c-Kit–amacrine cells form a single, regularly spaced mosaic, akin to canonical tiling subtypes such as starburst amacrine cells, W3 retinal ganglion cells, and bipolar cells24–27. It is noteworthy that the formation of a regular mosaic reflects orderly spacing within local retinal regions but does not imply uniform density across the entire retina. Thus, the ~ 10% higher cell density observed in the central ventral retina likely represents a regional variation in cell number superimposed on an otherwise well-organized mosaic architecture.

## Dendritic arborizations and morphological features of c-Kit–amacrine cells
The dendritic projections of c-Kit-amacrine cells in the IPL were examined in retinal cross-sections. The IPL is a distinctly laminated structure subdivided into five equal-thickness strata (s1–s5) with s1–s2 forming the OFF sublamina a and s3–s5 forming the ON sublamina b28. Confocal z-stacked images of retinal cross-sections labeled with anti-CD117 reveal that the somata of c-Kit-amacrine cells are located in the proximal INL. As a population, their dendritic processes collectively arborize across strata s1, s3 and s4/s5 of the IPL, spanning both OFF and ON sublaminae (Fig. 2A), even though individual cells exhibit variability in their specific stratification patterns. An enlarged single focal plane image further illustrates that the stratified dendritic arborization is tightly confined to the upper regions of strata s1 and s3, the lower region of s4 and the s5 stratum of the IPL (Fig. 2B). Notably, many processes from anti-CD117 labeled amacrine cells extend ascendingly into the OPL (see arrows in Fig. 2A), resembling the morphology of interplexiform cells29–31. Ascending processes were traced plane-by-plane through z-stacks toward the OPL in retinal whole mounts to confirm continuity with c-Kit-positive somas. Approximately 385 ± 27 processes per mm2 were identified at the distal plexus of the INL (Fig. 2C, see circles), corresponding to an estimated 42% of c-Kit–amacrine cells bearing an ascending projection. These projections form neuropil clusters with narrow arborization fields in the OPL, as visualized in confocal z-stacked images of the OPL in retinal whole-mounts (Fig. 2D). In addition to the OPL, INL and IPL, anti-CD117 labeling was also observed in a few somas and processes within the ganglion cell layer (Fig. 2F). No anti-CD117 immuno-reactivity is detected in photoreceptors, horizontal cells, bipolar cells, displaced amacrine cells, and Müller glia cells.
To assess dendritic morphology and potential functional diversity among c-Kit-amacrine cells, confocal image stacks were acquired with a fine z-steps of 0.1 µm, enabling accurate three-dimensional reconstruction of their fine neuronal processes within the dense retinal neuropil. Single-cell dendritic arbors were traced across tangential planes in the IPL and reconstructed using the Fiji cell-tracing plugin. Representative 3D reconstructions and corresponding tracing renderings (Figs. 3a – e), illustrating the dendritic diversity observed among c-Kit-amacrine cells. Typically, these cells exhibit oval somas positioned 7–9 μm above the distal IPL border, with a few somas at the border. Each soma gives rise to a primary apical dendrite descending vertically toward strata s3 and s4/s5, with some branches in s1 (25/38 cells, Fig. 3a–d). The dendrites arborizing in s3 and s4/s5 form multiple branches, each bearing numerous varicosities that create mesh-like or varicose terminal fields. These primary dendrites are confined to the ON sublamina (s3 and s4/s5), with no arborization observed in the OFF sublamina (s1), suggesting that these neurons predominantly receive input from rod bipolar cells. A subset of c-Kit-amacrine cells with ascending projections also exhibit fine dendritic arborizations forming a loose mesh with varicosities in strata s3 and s4/s5. These cells could be further classified based on the presence or absence of s1 arborization (representative images see Fig. 3e, f), potentially reflecting whether they receive input from ON-bipolar cells alone or from both ON- and OFF-cone bipolar cells. Their dendritic processes could often be traced for ~ 100 μm before merging into the dense plexus. Notably, the ascending processes of these cells without s1 arborization typically originate from the dendrites in s3 and s4/s5 (see Fig. 3e), in contrast to dopaminergic amacrine cells, whose ascending processes arise from the s1 stratum32 Overall, these results reveal dendritic structural heterogeneity among c-Kit-amacrine cells.
We observed that complete profiling of dendritic arbors is often limited by overlapping branches, tangled varicosities, or tissue disruption from retinal cross-sections when using cell-tracing methods. To obtain more comprehensive information on dendritic arborization and field size, additional quantitative analyses of branch length and junctions were applied.

Amacrine cells are generally classified as narrow-, medium-, or wide-field according to the diameter of their dendritic fields3 AII amacrine cells exhibit narrow dendritic fields (~ 70 μm), starburst cells display medium fields (~ 150–200 μm), and dopaminergic cells possess wide fields extending beyond 300 μm. Based on these distinctions, dendritic branch lengths were categorized as 30–100μm, 100–300μm and > 300μm. To avoid artifacts, branches shorter than 30 μm, below the reported minimum length for mouse amacrine cells, were considered as short paths and excluded from subsequent Analyze Skeleton plugin analyses. No branches longer than 500 μm were detected. Across the s1, s3, and s4/s5 sublaminae, most dendritic branches fall within the 100–300 μm range, with relatively few extending 300 μm (Fig. 4C, Total # of Branches). We also noticed a subset of shorter branches (30–100 μm) was present in all three plexuses. These shorter segments likely represent secondary or tertiary arborizations originating from broader primary dendrites as detected by the plugin. Together, these findings indicate that c-Kit–amacrine cells display features of medium- and wide-field types. Still, additional study is necessary to definitively rule out the presence of a distinct narrow-field c-Kit-amacrine cells subtype.

## c-Kit-amacrine cells are GABA-positive neurons
The majority of amacrine cells are either GABAergic or glycinergic neurons, while a small subset lacks both neurotransmitters and is referred to as nGnG amacrine cells (for review, see2,8,15). To determine whether c-Kit-positive amacrine cells are GABA-positive neurons, we performed double labeling of anti-CD117 and anti-GABA in retinal whole mounts. As shown in Fig. 5A, most c-Kit-amacrine cells exhibit colocalization of the two antibodies, with only a few cells displaying very weak anti-GABA signal (see arrows in Fig. 5A). Compared to strongly GABA-immunoreactive amacrine cells, c-Kit–amacrine cells show weaker anti-GABA labeling, reflected in lower green fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5B, see asterisks). Therefore, we quantified the degree of colocalization between anti-GABA and anti-CD117. The Pearson correlation coefficient matrix was applied to assess the relationship between antibody labeling intensities and morphological features. The correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between anti-GABA and anti-CD117 fluorescence intensities, but no significant correlation between labeling intensity and morphological parameters such as cell area or eccentricity (Fig. 5C). ## c-Kit-amacrine cells are distinct from AII, starburst, nGnG, vGluT3-positive, VIP-positive and dopaminergic amacrine cells.
To distinguish c-Kit–amacrine cells from other well-characterized narrow-, medium-, and wide-field amacrine cell types, we performed double-labeling using anti-CD117 in combination with specific antibodies that mark these neuronal subtypes. Starburst amacrine cells, which constitute a substantial subset of GABAergic neurons, are located in both the INL and ganglion cell layer (GCL), with their dendrites stratifying in the OFF sublamina (s2) when the somas are in the INL. These cells can be specifically labeled with an antibody against choline acetyltransferase (anti-ChAT). In addition, dopaminergic amacrine cells, another class of GABAergic neurons, are positioned at the boundary between the INL and IPL, with dendritic arborizations confined to the s1 stratum of the IPL, and can be specifically labeled with anti-TH. Figure 6A shows representative images of retinal whole mounts labeled with anti-CD117, anti-ChAT, and anti-TH, highlighting three distinct groups of amacrine cells. Furthermore, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP)–expressing amacrine cells are a subset of GABAergic interneurons with subtype-specific dendritic morphologies and stratification patterns. VIP-1 amacrine cells are wide-field neurons with dendritic arbors spanning sublaminae s1, s4, and s5 of the IPL, whereas VIP-2A and VIP-2B cells are medium-field neurons primarily localized to s3 and s4. VIP-3 displaced amacrine cells, also classified as medium-field, are found in s4 and s517. Double immunolabeling with anti-CD117 and anti-VIP demonstrates no colocalization between c-Kit–positive and VIP-positive cells in the INL (Fig. 6B), supporting the conclusion that these cells represent distinct amacrine cell populations.
Because some c-Kit-amacrine cells exhibited substantially low levels of GABA immunoreactivity, we speculated that these neurons might correspond to the nGnG amacrine cell population, which ramifies primarily within the OFF sublamina (s1–s3) of the IPL through their dendritic arborization14,15. To investigate this possibility, we performed double-labeling using an antibody against protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 17 (anti-Ppp1r17) in combination with anti-CD117. Anti-Ppp1r17 has been used as a biomarker to label the second most densely distributed amacrine cells, referred to AC51-70 cells, including nGnG1 and nGnG3, which are characterized by narrow-field dendritic profiles33,34. AII amacrine cells are the most populated glycinergic amacrine cells with narrow-field processes. The antibody for cytoplasmic protein Disable-1 (anti-Dab1) is a specific marker for labelling AII amacrine cells in mouse retina35. Double labeling of anti-CD117 with anti-Dab1 shows that the two antibodies labeled different groups of amacrine cells (Fig. 7B), confirmed that AII amacrine cells are not c-Kit-positive neurons. Furthermore, the c-Kit-amacrine cells are also not immunoreactive to the another a group of glycinergic amacrine cells, vesicular glutamate transporter 3 (vGluT3)-positive amacrine cells. vGluT3 amacrine cells release both glutamate and glycine with their dendritic arborization across both ON- and OFF- sublaminas of the IPL, laterally extending about 100 µm. In summary, these results indicate that c-Kit-amacrine cells are represent a distinct class of amacrine neurons.

## Discussion
Amacrine cells constitute one of the most diverse and functionally specialized classes of retinal interneurons, playing pivotal roles in shaping visual signals prior to their transmission to higher visual centers. Over the past decades, advances in molecular labeling, genetic manipulation, electrophysiology, and high-resolution imaging have significantly expanded the classification and understanding of amacrine cell subtypes. In this study, we identify and characterize a distinct population of amacrine cells in the mouse retina that express c-Kit receptor tyrosine kinase, using the anti-CD117 as a molecular marker. The precise characterization of amacrine cell morphology and spatial organization is critical for elucidating their diverse roles within retinal circuits. Recent advances in image analysis tools have significantly improved our ability to quantitatively assess complex neural structures with greater accuracy and reproducibility. In this study, we utilized high-resolution confocal imaging of immunolabeled retinal whole mounts and cross-sections to examine the morphology, spatial distribution, and dendritic stratification of c-Kit-amacrine cells. To support objective and high-throughput quantification, we implemented two complementary image analysis platforms: Cellpose3 for automated cell segmentation and spatial pattern analyses, and Fiji for skeleton-based morphological analyses. Through plexus-by-plexus analysis of dendritic arborization and receptive field characteristics, we provide detailed morphometric profiles, assessed anti-cluster, regulated spatial distribution, and calculated averaged cell densities across the INL. Our findings are consistent with the possibility that c-Kit-amacrine cells represent a single population that exhibits notable diversity in dendritic architecture. Within this population, we identified at least four morphological types: (1) ON-stratified, (2) ON–OFF bistratified, (3) cells with ascending projections to the OPL, and (4) cells lacking such projections. The morphological diversity points to underlying heterogeneity in synaptic inputs and outputs in c-Kit-amacrine cells. Such heterogeneity may correspond to distinct functionals roles for integrating local signals from rod and cone pathway, modulating ganglion cell activity, shaping contrast sensitivity, integrating temporal signals, or mediating adaptation to changing light conditions. These diverse functions could be further governed by multiple functional states driven by intrinsic factors, such as c-kit gene expression dynamics and the receptor signaling, as well as extrinsic influences from environment, including dark–light adaptation-dependent modulation. Consequently, while c-Kit provides a unifying molecular marker, its expression likely encompasses a previously underappreciated subclass of amacrine cells that contribute in complementary ways to retinal signal processing, network plasticity and visual adaptation. Our results are consistent with existing amacrine cell classification frameworks based on molecular and architectural profiling7,24,36, while also emphasizing the potential for morphological and functional heterogeneity of this newly recognized group of amacrine cells.
Despite their morphological diversity, c-Kit-amacrine cells are arranged in a highly regular mosaic across the retina. This spatial organization suggests that, while individual cells may differ in dendritic architecture or synaptic connectivity, they share common developmental and positional cues that maintain uniform local spacing and minimize overlap. Interestingly, c-Kit-amacrine cells exhibit a modest (~ 10%) difference in density between the dorsal and ventral retina (see Fig. 1B). Such regional variation does not contradict their regular mosaic organization, as mosaic regularity reflects local spacing uniformity rather than global density constancy. Since the retina is not functionally uniform, many retinal neurons show gradients in density, morphology, or gene expression along this axis to adapt to different visual demands. The dorsal–ventral gradient likely reflects functional and developmental asymmetries inherent to retinal organization. In mammalian species, the ventral retina, which views the brighter upper visual field, often contains higher densities of certain neuron involved in detecting objects from above with processing information related to shape, color and object identification, whereas the dorsal retina, sampling the dimmer lower field, emphasizes processing spatial information, motion detection, and guiding actions based on visual input. Furthermore, the dorsal retina is also specialized for processing red-green light. This contrasts with the ventral retina, which is more involved in object recognition and processing short-wavelength (blue) light37,38. A similar principle may underline the regional variation in c-Kit-amacrine cells, suggesting they contribute differentially to local circuit functions that optimize visual performance across luminance gradients. It is also possible that gradients of molecular cues guiding neurogenesis, migration, or survival during development may contribute to slight differences in the final distribution of c-Kit-expressing neurons. While their local spacing remains highly ordered, regional density variations likely reflect adaptive and developmental patterning across the retinal axis. Thus, the 10% dorsal–ventral density difference in c-Kit-amacrine cells likely reflects functional and developmental retinal asymmetries, not randomness or irregularity. Their regular mosaic indicates that local spacing cues are preserved, even if overall cell density varies slightly across the retina.
The potential roles of c-Kit-amacrine cells in retinal signal processing can also be inferred from their dendritic receptive field sizes, which provide insights into the spatial domains of their synaptic integration. Our analysis reveals that most of these neurons exhibit medium- to wide-field morphologies, with dendritic arbor extending laterally over 100 µm. In mammalian species, amacrine cells are broadly classified into GABAergic, glycinergic and nGnG populations14,15. Glycinergic amacrine cells are typically narrow-field neurons, with vertically oriented to several sublaminae of the IPL, mediating local interactions across parallel circuits between the ON- and the OFF-sublamina39,40. The most extensively studied narrow-field amacrine cells are AII amacrine cells, which function as critical interneurons that relay rod-mediated scotopic signals into cone pathways via a dominant glycinergic input to the inner retina41,42. In contrast, GABAergic amacrine cells, which often co-express neuromodulators such as acetylcholine, dopamine, or serotonin, typically exhibit medium- to wide-field morphologies, with dendritic processes extending laterally across large portions of the IPL. These cells are thought to contribute to lateral inhibition and direction-selective processing in retinal circuits. Although a number of wide-field amacrine cell types have been described, detailed characterization remains limited. Confocal imaging and double-labeling with anti-GABA indicate that c-Kit-amacrine cells are GABA-positive neurons (see Fig. 5). When integrated with computerized skeleton analysis, these findings support their classification within the medium- and wide-field amacrine cell subtypes. Notably, one well-characterized wide-field subtype, the dopaminergic amacrine cell, is morphologically distinct from c-Kit-amacrine cells in terms of somatic size and location, and the pattern of dendritic and axonal arborization in the OPL and IPL (see Figs. 2E, 3e,f & 6A). While the neural connectivity and long-range feedback mechanisms of dopaminergic amacrine cells have been extensively studied, c-Kit-amacrine cells may represent a previously unrecognized medium- and wide-field amacrine cells capable of linking distal and proximal retinal layers through an inter-retinal connection.
Notably, approximately 42% of the c-Kit-amacrine cells exhibit morphological features consistent with interplexiform cells that possess ascending processes that extend from the IPL toward the OPL. This distinctive arborization forms a structural loop connecting the distal and proximal synaptic layers, suggesting a potential role for these amacrine cells in conducting long-range feedback within the retina. Among the best-characterized interplexiform cells are dopaminergic and glycinergic cells; however, studies on glycinergic interplexiform cells have been largely limited to lower vertebrates31. Thus, this subset of c-Kit-amacrine cells provides a promising model for investigating interplexiform cell function in mammalian retina.
Previous studies have reported c-Kit expression in various retinal cell types, though its precise localization and functional significance have remained debated. Early evidence indicated that c-kit mRNA and its receptor were present in amacrine cells, while its ligand, stem cell factor (SCF), was localized to ganglion cells22. However, subsequent work revealed that SCF is primarily expressed in Müller glia, whereas c-Kit receptor expression occurs in retinal ganglion cells, where it may mediate neuroprotective effects against glutamate-induced excitotoxicity43. In addition, c-Kit has been detected in retinal progenitor cells during both development and adulthood44. These progenitors, located in the ONL and INL of the adult retina, exhibit self-renewing, clonogenic, and multipotent properties, suggesting a potential role for c-Kit signaling in retinal maintenance and regeneration. The persistence of c-Kit–expressing neurons in the retina likely reflects an evolutionarily conserved mechanism that supports neural development, maintenance, and functional specialization. Given c-Kit’s established roles in cell proliferation, migration, and survival, it may have originated as a broadly utilized signaling molecule in early metazoans and was subsequently adapted for specialized functions in the nervous system45. Its retention in distinct neuronal populations could provide advantages such as neuroprotection, synaptic plasticity, and activity-dependent modulation, thereby contributing to the evolution of complex neural circuits and adaptive visual behaviors in vertebrates. Building upon these insights, our findings reveal that c-Kit is strongly expressed in a distinct subpopulation of amacrine cells in the adult retina, exhibiting morphological features consistent with medium- and wide-field types. A small number of c-Kit-positive somas and processes were also detected in the ganglion cell layer, suggesting that certain ganglion cells may express c-Kit as well. These results provide new perspectives on the spatial distribution, dendritic stratification, and synaptic organization of c-Kit–expressing neurons in the adult retina, laying a foundation for future studies exploring how this amacrine cell subpopulation modulates ganglion cell excitability and visual processing through subtype-specific synaptic connectivity.
Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, our analysis of dendritic morphology relied on computer-assisted cell tracing and modeling tools, which, while powerful, may introduce biases or inaccuracies. Automated reconstruction of 2D/3D skeletons in Fiji can occasionally misidentify fine processes or fail to capture very thin dendritic branches, potentially underestimating overall dendritic complexity and branch length. Moreover, reconstruction from confocal images is inherently constrained by imaging resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and sampling depth, which may limit the precision of morphological measurements. Thus, while our approach provides a systematic and reproducible assessment of c-Kit-amacrine cell architecture, further validation with complementary techniques, such as the c-Kit-Cre mouse line and intracellular dye injection, will be important to fully capture the diversity of their dendritic morphologies.

## Methods

## Animal preparation
Wild-type 129 SvEv mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). The mice were kept in the animal facility under a 12-h dark/light cycle. The illumination levels in the mouse housing room were ~ 200 Lux. The mice, weighting 20–30 g, used in the experiments were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (200 mg/kg) and Xylazine (10 mg/kg) followed by cervical dislocation. All procedures were performed in accordance with National Institutes of Health and the Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals, approved by the Committee on Animal Research (IACUC) of Florida Atlantic University.

## Immunofluorescent labeling and confocal imaging
Retinal tissue preparations followed previously described protocol46,47. Briefly, eyes were removed immediately from the mice after euthanized and placed into a cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The cornea and lens were removed; the eyecups remained for further processing.
For retinal cryostat sections, the eyecups were placed in the fixative for another 15 min, dehydrated in graded sucrose solutions (10%, 15%, 20% and 30%), and immersed in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C. The dehydrated eyecups were embedded in OCT compound (Ted Pella, Redding, CA), frozen overnight, and then sectioned at 12–20 μm on a cryostat. Frozen sections were collected on slides, air dried, and stored at −80 °C.
Immunofluorescent labeling was performed on retinal cryostat sections and flat-mounted retinas that were removed from eyecups after fixation. Retinal sections and flat-mounts were rinsed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween and 0.3% Triton X-100 (PBST-T), and then treated with a blocking solution consisting of 10% normal goat or donkey serum and 0.1% sodium azide in PBST-T. The sections and flat-mounts were then incubated in the primary antibody mixture with 3% goat or donkey serum and 0.1% sodium azide in PBST-T either for 2 h at room temperature or for 5 days at 4 °C, respectively (see Table 1 list of primary antibodies, concentration and their sources used in this study). The specificity of the anti-CD117 was confirmed by pre-absorption with Recombinant Human CD117/c-kit Fc Chimera (R&D Systems, Cat. #10,961-SR), which completely abolished the antibody’s immunoreactivity in both retinal sections and whole-mount tissue preparations. ## Quantification of cell number, ascending processes, dendritic structures, and morphological subtypes with fiji (ImageJ)
The number of CD117-positive amacrine cells was quantified in flat-mounted retinas. Images measuring 320 × 320 µm were acquired from four distinct retinal locations: dorsal, ventral, nasal, and temporal. To ensure representative sampling along the radial axis, a minimum of six images were captured within each of these four locations, resulting in a total of at least 24 images per retina. The immunofluorescently labeled cells were manually counted from these images using a cell counter plugin in Fiji (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health). The averaged cell numbers were determined from four retinas obtained from different animals and are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test in Microsoft Excel.
Ascending processes of CD117-positive amacrine cells were quantified by sequentially tracing each process plane-by-plane through confocal z-stacks toward the OPL in retinal whole mounts to confirm continuity with CD117-positive somas. This approach ensured accurate identification of individual processes within the imaging field (320 × 320 µm). Process density was then calculated at the distal plexus of the INL.
The dendritic morphology of CD117-positive amacrine cells was analyzed using image processing techniques in Fiji. To ensure accuracy, high-quality raw confocal images were preprocessed to isolate dendritic structures. This involved converting the raw images to binary format using a thresholding algorithm that distinguished dendrites (foreground) from the background noise. Manually fine-tune the threshold to retain all true dendritic branches and exclude background artifacts. Misidentified connections or breaks were corrected using the drawing tools to ensure structural accuracy. Inspect the skeleton to ensure it accurately represents the original dendritic structure. To enable precise morphometric analysis, a scale factor was applied to each image to convert pixel measurements to microns. Dendritic parameters, including total length, branching complexity, and number of junctions, were quantified using the ‘AnalyzeSkeleton (2D/3D)’ plugin, with the pruning method set to ‘Shortest Branch’ to minimize redundant loop artifacts. A subset of samples was manually traced to evaluate the error rate and validate the automated measurements, allowing fine-tuning of parameter settings for optimal accuracy.
To analyze dendritic morphology of individual CD117-positive amacrine cells, confocal image stacks were acquired with fine z-steps of 0.1 µm to ensure high-resolution 3-dimensional reconstruction of fine neuronal processes in retinal cross-sections. This small axial increment minimized under sampling and facilitated accurate tracing of thin or overlapping neurites within the dense retinal neuropil. Single-cell dendritic arbors were traced across tangential planes in the IPL using the Fiji cell-tracing plugin, with the dendrites were manually traced by clicking points along their paths, following each branch to its terminus. Careful navigation through all planes of the z-stack ensured accurate 3-dimmensional reconstruction for subsequent quantitative analyses.

## Cell segmentation and profile analysis
Cells were segmented from the anti-CD117 fluorescence channel using Cellpose (v3.1.1.2)36, running on an NVIDIA 5090 GPU with the “cyto3” model and estimated initial diameter of 30 pixels, which corresponded to the average soma size of CD117-positive neurons under our imaging conditions (pixel size: 0.31 µm/pixel). The flow and cell probability thresholds were set to the default values (0.4 and 0.0, respectively) to ensure accurate cell boundary detection without over-segmentation. For each auto-detected mask, a custom Python pipeline computed geometric centers (centroids), mean fluorescence intensity, soma areas, fitted-ellipse major/minor axis lengths, and ellipse eccentricity. These feature values were then normalized and assembled into a feature matrix for downstream statistics. To quantify the correlations among features, we constructed a pairwise Pearson correlation matrix and the corresponding p-value matrix: specifically, for each unordered feature pair, we applied scipy.stats.pearsonr from the Python SciPy package to obtain the correlation coefficient (r) and its two-sided p-value, which were then filled into the matrices accordingly.
The density recovery profile was calculated based on the centroids of segmented cells using a method previously published23 Nearest neighbor regularity index (NNRI) and Voronoi domain regularity index (VDRI) were calculated with a customized Python program following established method48.
