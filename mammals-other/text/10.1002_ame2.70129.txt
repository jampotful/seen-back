Tree shrew model of early diabetic retinopathy reveals microvascular dysfunction and identifies phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 as a novel therapeutic target.

# Tree shrew model of early diabetic retinopathy reveals microvascular dysfunction and identifies phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 as a novel therapeutic target

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most significant manifestation of diabetic microangiopathy. The existing tree shrew model of DR has dysfunctional retinal short wave sensitivity (SWS) cones and retinal ganglion cells, but it remains unclear whether the retinal microvessels are also compromised. In this study, we established a tree shrew diabetes model to investigate the characteristics of retinal microvascular disease observed in early human DR.MethodsA high‐fat and high‐sugar diet combined with streptozotocin was used to establish the tree shrew diabetes model. After 20 weeks of sustained high glucose levels, we measured the thickness of each retinal layer and the number of ghost pericyte cells and acellular capillaries, and examined the ultrastructural changes in the retina. We also performed RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) and evaluated the protein expression levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Bcl‐2‐related X protein (Bax).ResultsThe tree shrew model exhibited the characteristics of diabetes, including hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and insulin resistance. The retinal nerve fiber layer and ganglion cell layer exhibited significant thinning (36% and 30%, respectively). Retinal capillaries exhibited ghost pericytes and acellular capillaries, whereas the retinal ultrastructure exhibited signs of damage. VEGF and Bax protein levels in the retina were significantly upregulated. RNA‐seq revealed downregulation of phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1). Overexpression of PSAT1 in retinal microvascular endothelial cells restored their lumen formation ability and mobility in a high‐glucose environment, and reduced the expression of VEGF.ConclusionOur results indicate that the tree shrew may be a suitable experimental animal model for studying the pathogenesis of early DR. Furthermore, PSAT1 may be a promising molecular target for DR treatment.Keywords: diabetic retinopathy, phosphoserine aminotransferase 1, retinal microvessels, tree shrew

## INTRODUCTION
Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a significant complication associated with diabetes mellitus and a primary cause of vision impairment in working‐age individuals. 1 In 2021, it was estimated that 10.5% of individuals aged 20–79 years globally had diabetes, corresponding to ~536.6 million people. Projections suggest this figure could increase to 12.2% by 2045, affecting ~783.2 million. 2 Approximately one in five individuals with diabetes is affected by DR 3 ; therefore, it is important to develop new therapies that can prevent or delay the onset and progress of this condition. 4 , 5 , 6
Although numerous studies have explored the molecular mechanisms involved in the onset of DR, the specific mechanisms remain unclear and require further elucidation. Understanding the pathogenesis of early DR is particularly important. However, investigation of the mechanisms and treatment options for DR requires dependable, practical, and suitable animal models.
The existing animal models of DR have significant disadvantages that limit their usefulness, including the genetic differences between animals and humans, variations in ocular structure, challenges in maintenance, and high cost. Moreover, many of these models fail to fully replicate the complete pathophysiology of neuronal and vascular changes at various stages of DR. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The lack of an effective animal model for DR has been a major obstacle in understanding its pathogenesis and in the development of novel treatment strategies. Therefore, there is an immediate need for animal models that closely mimic the onset of this disease in humans and are suitable for evaluating therapeutic approaches.
The tree shrew presents a promising alternative due to its small size, rapid reproduction, ease of maintenance, and large eyeballs. Their retinal anatomical structures are similar to those of humans, 13 , 14 and they possess a well‐developed visual system, color vision, and eye structure, making them comparable to primates and important models for human visual research. 15 , 16 , 17 Diurnal tree shrews have cone‐dominant retinas (95%), with the highest cone density located in the central retina. 18 The tree shrew retina has no fovea, but the density peak of retinal ganglion cells (RGC) corresponds to the area centralis and is similar to the area of central vision. 17 These animals are dichromatic, with 90%–96% red‐sensitive cones and 10% blue‐sensitive cones, depending on the retinal location. 19 , 20
Previous studies have indicated that tree shrew models of DR exhibited a retinal phenotype characterized by cone degeneration and RGC dysfunction, allowing the investigation of retinal degeneration induced by hyperglycemia during the early stages of DR. 18 Together these findings suggest that tree shrews could be a useful model for translational research aimed at preventing vision loss or restoring vision in patients with DR. However, further investigation is needed to determine whether retinal microvessels are also damaged in the tree shrew model of DR.
The aim of this research was to investigate whether tree shrews with hyperglycemia experience early‐onset DR and, if so, to examine the underlying mechanisms. We investigated the feasibility of using tree shrews fed a diet rich in fats and sugars, in conjunction with streptozotocin (STZ) treatment, as a possible DR animal model. The retinal pathology of the diabetic tree shrew model was examined at 20 weeks. A retinal phenotype consistent with early DR was found, including thinning of both the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and the ganglion cell layer (GCL). It also exhibited characteristic vascular abnormalities, particularly the emergence of acellular capillaries and ghost pericytes, as well as retinal ultrastructural damage and upregulated expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Bcl‐2‐related X protein (Bax). Additionally, RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) analysis revealed significant downregulation of phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1) expression. Overexpression of PSAT1 in retinal microvascular endothelial cells (RMEC) restored their mobility and lumen formation ability in a high‐glucose environment while decreasing the expression of VEGF. These results support application of the tree shrew as an animal model to study the early stages of DR. They also suggest that PSAT1 may serve as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and treatment of DR.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Animals
A total of 22 adult tree shrews (Tupaia belangeri chinensis, male, aged 2–3 years, and weighed between 150 and 180 g) were obtained from the Center of Tree Shrew Germplasm Resources (Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Kunming, China). They had normal eye health, exhibited no evident signs of tumors or illness, and adhered to the regulatory standards for this species (T/CALAS 08‐2017 and T/CALAS 09‐2017). The animals were categorized into two groups: a DR group (n = 16) and a control group (n = 6). All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee, Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (approval number: DWSP202108002).

## DR induced by a high‐fat, high‐sugar diet combined with STZ
To induce DR, a high‐fat and high‐sugar diet consisting of basic chow supplemented with 1% cholesterol, 10% lard, 10% white sugar, and 10% fructose was fed to the tree shrews for 1 week. After this period, 100 mg/kg of STZ (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was injected intraperitoneally, and then the same diet was fed an additional week. The control group was fed a standard diet and injected with 0.1 mmol/L sodium citrate buffer. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels were assessed weekly from tail blood samples using a Yuyue blood glucose meter, with levels >11.1 mmol/L regarded as indicative of diabetes. Body weight was measured weekly. Blood samples were collected at week 8 to determine fasting insulin (FINS) levels. Animals in both the DR and control groups were killed at week 20, and blood samples were collected for biochemical analysis of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C).

## Histopathological analysis
For histopathological analysis, the eyeballs were quickly removed after the tree shrews were euthanized and fixed in FAS (Servicebio, China) eyeball fixation solution for 24 h. The eyeballs were sectioned (3 μm thick), dehydrated, cleared, embedded, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Retinal morphology was observed using a scanning microscope. The thickness of the RNFL, GCL, inner nuclear layer (INL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), and outer nuclear layer (ONL) was measured using CaseViewer software. Measurements were taken from 10 randomly selected positions of the retinal paraffin sections.
To observe changes in retinal capillaries, the eyeballs were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The retinas were peeled off and digested with trypsin to isolate the retinal vasculature. The samples were stained with periodic acid–Schiff (Servicebio) and air dried, and the morphology of retinal capillaries was observed under a microscope. The number of pericyte ghosts and acellular capillaries was counted using morphometric analysis software (Olympus, Germany). Ten fields were randomly selected from each retinal capillary section at 400× magnification for counting and averaging.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to examine the ultrastructure of the retina. Eyeballs were meticulously excised and immersed in a fixation solution for TEM analysis. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated, embedded, polymerized, and sectioned prior to TEM analysis; images were obtained for further analysis.

## Western blot analysis
Retinal tissues obtained from tree shrews were homogenized and lysed on ice for 20 min in a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich) consisting of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 14 000g for 10 min at 4°C. The protein concentration was then assessed using the Bicin‐choninic Acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime, China), and the loading volume was normalized to 30 μg. The samples were subjected to separation via 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. These membranes were incubated in 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h to block nonspecific binding and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against VEGF (ab32152, Abcam) and Bax (GTX635715, GeneTex) at 4°C. After having washed with Tris Buffered Saline Tween (TBST) buffer, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti‐rabbit or goat anti‐mouse immunoglobulin G secondary antibody for 1 h. The proteins were detected using ECL Western blot substrate (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the resulting immunoblot bands were evaluated using an imaging analysis system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK). The results were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## RNA extraction and RNA‐seq
Total RNA was extracted from tree shrew retinopathy tissues using the TaKaRa RNAiso Plus kit, as recommended by the manufacturer. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and was further confirmed using RNase‐free agarose gel electrophoresis. After the total RNA was extracted, the eukaryotic messenger RNA (mRNA) was enriched using Oligo (dT) beads. After enrichment, the mRNA was fragmented into smaller sequences and reverse transcribed to produce complementary DNA (cDNA) using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB 7530, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The purified double‐stranded cDNA fragments underwent end‐repair and base addition, and were subsequently ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. The ligation reaction mixture was purified using AMPure XP Beads (1.0×). The ligated products were subjected to size selection via agarose gel electrophoresis before amplification using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The resulting cDNA library was sequenced by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Quality control and filtering of low‐quality data were conducted using Fastp to obtain clean reads. The reference genome used in this study was KIZ, version 3: TS_3.0 from the Tree Shrew Database (http://www.treeshrewdb.org/). A comparative analysis was performed utilizing HISAT2 software based on established reference genomes.

## Differentially expressed gene analysis
Differential analysis of gene expression was conducted using DESeq2. 21 Genes that exhibited significant differential expression were filtered based on p <0.05 and an absolute fold change (FC) of ≥2.

## Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and genomes pathway analyses
Enrichment analysis was conducted on the differentially expressed genes identified after 20 weeks of sustained hyperglycemia in tree shrews. This comprised both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) assessments. The functional pathways were filtered with a p < 0.05 criterion. GO terms that satisfied this threshold were considered significantly enriched, whereas KEGG pathways that met this requirement were categorized as significantly enriched pathways.

## Validation of mRNAs using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis
To confirm the RNA sequencing results presented in this research, three genes that exhibited significant differential expression were chosen for validation using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR). The primers used in the qRT‐PCRexperiments were shown in Table 1. This technique was conducted using six purified RNA samples, including three from the experimental cohort and three from the control cohort. First, the GoScript Reverse Transcription Mix and random primer kit were used to reverse transcribe total RNA into cDNA, with glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the internal reference. The TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) reagent kit was used for qRT‐PCR evaluation of the relative expression levels of retinal mRNA in the two groups. ## PSAT1‐overexpressing cells
Under optimal growth conditions, RMECs were seeded into six‐well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and incubated for 48 h; then they were rinsed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Culture medium containing polybrene (4–5 μg/mL) was then added to enhance transfection efficiency, and lentivirus packaged with the PSAT1 gene overexpression construct was inoculated at a multiplicity of infection of 30. The recombinant PSAT1 lentivirus was provided by GenePrarma. After 4 h, an additional 1 mL of polybrene‐containing medium was added, and the cells were incubated for 24 h before being replaced with complete medium with no polybrene. After 48 h of incubation, RMECs infected with the PSAT1‐overexpressing lentivirus were transferred to six‐well plates. When the cells reached 60% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with complete medium containing G418 (recombinant lentivirus with Neo resistance) and cultured until the control wells (not inoculated with lentivirus) showed complete cell death. The experimental group cells (PSAT1‐overexpressing RMECs) were collected and verified using Western blot analysis.

## Cell proliferation assay
Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) per the instructions provided by the manufacturer. RMECs were maintained at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96‐well plates and allowed to incubate for 24 h. After this period, the culture medium was replaced with serum‐free medium for 12–24 h, during which the cells were treated with media containing various glucose concentrations (5.5, 25, 35, 50, and 65 mmol/L) for 24, 48, and 72 h periods. Once the initial culture medium was removed, a mixture of the CCK‐8 reagent solution was added, and the cells were incubated for another hour. Absorbance readings were taken at 450 nm using an enzyme marker, and cell viability curves were produced. A glucose concentration of 50 mmol/L was selected for subsequent experiments.

## Cell wound scratch assay
RMECs were seeded into six‐well plates at a density of 6 × 105 cells per well. After the formation of a monolayer, a cross‐scratch was made in the center of each plate. The cells were rinsed with PBS thrice to clear the area of scratched cells. They were then allocated to two groups: a control group that received 5.5 mmol/L glucose and a high‐glucose group that received 50 mmol/L glucose for additional incubation. ## Luminal formation experiment
Matrigel was removed from −20°C storage and thawed at 4°C overnight. A precooled 96‐well plate and tips were prepared. After the Matrigel had melted, 70 μL was added to each well, taking care to avoid air bubbles. The 96‐well plate was incubated for 30 min. Cells were digested, resuspended in a 5.5 or 50 mmol/L medium, and inoculated at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL. After 4 h in a 37°C incubator, the medium was removed, and paraformaldehyde was added to fix the cells for 20 min. Luminal structures were examined microscopically, and images were obtained.

## Statistical analysis
Data were compared between the control and DR groups using Student's t‐test. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons involving multiple groups. Before applying t‐tests and ANOVA, we performed normality tests. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 7.0, software. A p‐value <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. The experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

## RESULTS

## A high‐fat and high‐sugar diet combined with STZ injection induces sustained hyperglycemia and metabolic disturbance in tree shrews
Among the DR group, 88% exhibited signs of diabetes, with FBG levels exceeding 11.1 mmol/L for >4 weeks (Figure 1A). Additionally, the DR tree shrews exhibited increased food and water intake, as well as elevated urine output. Particularly, a statistically significant weight loss was observed after 14 weeks (Figure 1B). The FINS level in the DR group increased at 8 weeks, indicating the presence of insulin resistance (Figure 1C). At 20 weeks, the levels of TC, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C were higher in the DR group than in the control group (Figure 1D–G).

## Histological analysis revealed DR‐associated vascular dysregulation and thinning of the retinal layer
Histological analysis of the retinal capillary patch revealed increased vascular density, disorganized vessel arrangement, and a significant increase in acellular capillaries in the DR group. Pericyte ghosts were observed after pericyte degeneration (Figure 2A,B,E). Retinal layers typically become thinner due to accelerated retinal cell loss associated with diabetes. 22 HE staining of the retina in DR animals revealed clearly visible structures in each layer, with no significant morphological differences compared to the control group. However, the DR group exhibited significant decreases in the thickness of both the RNFL and the GCL (Figure 2C,D). Particularly, the thickness of the RNFL was reduced by 36% and that of the GCL by 30% compared to the control group (Figure 2F). No significant differences were detected in the thickness of the IPL, INL, and ONL.

## Ultrastructural damage in the retina
TEM of the retina in the control group revealed normal overall morphology and structure of the blood vessels (Figure 3A,B). In the DR group, pericytes exhibited moderate cytoplasmic edema, with the mitochondria exhibiting significant swelling, local matrix lysis, broken cristae, and dissolution. Endothelial cells exhibited moderate edema, irregular nuclei (N), and increased heterochromatin. Mitochondria (M) were significantly swollen, with enlarged size, matrix dissolution, fractured cristae, and damage to individual membranes. The rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) appeared slightly dilated with minimal degranulation (Figure 3D). Finger‐like projections of endothelial cells extended into the lumen, and numerous ingestion vesicles (indicated by black arrows) were observed (Figure 3C). Local narrowing of the vascular lumen (Cap) was observed, and the compact area of tight junctions appeared shorter and locally blurred, with a slight reduction in protein filaments and local loosening of junctions. Ganglion cells also exhibited damage (Figure 3E,F), characterized by areas of significant edema, swelling of intracellular organelles, irregular nuclear shapes (N), patchy and scattered heterochromatin, and slight local widening of the perinuclear space. Mitochondria (M) were significantly swollen, with enlarged size, matrix dissolution, and largely broken or absent cristae, along with vacuolation. The RER was moderately dilated and slightly degranulated. Overall, retinal blood vessels in the DR group exhibited some damage.

## Transcriptomic profiling reveals dysregulation of retinal gene expression in tree shrews with DR
To assess differential gene expression, we performed expression profiling after 20 weeks of hyperglycemia. The results showed the mRNAs clustered into distinct groups (Figure 5A), suggesting that genes with similar expression patterns may share common functions or participate in related metabolic and signaling pathways. A total of 860 differentially expressed mRNAs were identified between the DR and control groups, with 446 mRNAs upregulated and 414 downregulated. Only mRNAs with a FC ≥2 and an adjusted p‐value (p.adj) < 0.05 were considered significant. This analysis revealed that 178 mRNAs exhibited significant differential expression in the retinas of diabetic tree shrews (Figure 5B), of which 93 were upregulated (yellow) and 85 were downregulated (red). The 860 differentially expressed mRNAs were significantly enriched in 267 GO terms and 13 KEGG signaling pathways. GO analysis revealed that the differentially expressed mRNAs in the retina participate primarily in metabolic processes, and metabolic processes specific to single organisms. KEGG analysis revealed these mRNAs were predominantly enriched in the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, peroxisome proliferators‐activated receptors (PPAR) signaling pathway, and pathways related to insulin resistance (Figure 5C,D).

To validate the differentially expressed mRNAs identified using RNA‐seq, three specific mRNAs were selected: myosin binding protein C2 (Mybpc2), PSAT1, and ST6 N‐acetylgalactosaminide α‐2,6‐sialyltransferase 2 (ST6GALNAC2). Consistent with the RNA‐seq data, the expression level of Mybpc2 was significantly upregulated, whereas PSAT1 and ST6GALNAC2 were significantly downregulated (Figure 6A). Additionally, we verified the expression level of the PSAT1 gene in RMECs from tree shrews. ## PSAT1 overexpression restores cell mobility and lumen formation ability and downregulates VEGF expression in a high‐glucose environment
The results of Western blot analysis showed that PSAT1 expression in PSAT1‐overexpressing RMECs was significantly higher than that in normally cultured RMECs; also RMECs were transfected with the negative control (NC) lentivirus (Figure 7A). The CCK‐8 cell proliferation assay indicated that after 72 h of incubation, the cell viability of RMECs in the high‐glucose (50 mmol/L) group and in the NC group was significantly lower than that in the control group cultured in 5.5 mmol/L glucose. However, the high‐glucose group transfected with PSAT1 exhibited improved cell viability (Figure 7B), indicating that PSAT1 expression can enhance the proliferation of RMECs.

Cell migration assays revealed that RMECs cultured in high glucose exhibited significantly reduced mobility compared to those cultured under normal conditions. However, increasing the expression of PSAT1 restored the mobility of RMECs in the high‐glucose environment (Figure 7C–K). Lumen formation occurred in both normally cultured RMECs and PSAT1‐overexpressing RMECs exposed to high glucose (Figure 7L,N). These results indicate that PSAT1 exerts a protective effect on the tube‐forming function of RMECs in a high‐glucose environment.

## DISCUSSION
In this study, we developed an effective model of diabetic tree shrews by administering STZ along with a diet rich in fats and sugars. The animals used in this model exhibited hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, weight loss, polydipsia, polyuria, and lipid metabolism disorders. Additionally, cataracts developed in the diabetic tree shrews but not in age‐matched control tree shrews. After 20 weeks, the diabetes model demonstrated early signs of DR, characterized by thinner RNFL and GCL, as well as unique vascular anomalies. Acellular capillaries, pericyte ghost cells, and damage to the retinal ultrastructure were observed significantly, along with elevated levels of VEGF and Bax proteins. The levels of TC, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C were significantly elevated in the diabetic animals, whereas TG levels showed no significant change. We infer that TG levels may be normally distributed and influenced by various factors, including environmental and genetic variables, resulting in substantial biological variation among individuals. HDL‐C is recognized as a protective factor against cardiovascular diseases, and several studies suggest that higher HDL‐C levels correlate with better outcomes. However, excessively high HDL‐C levels may shift from being anti‐inflammatory to pro‐inflammatory, potentially increasing oxidative stress. 23 Furthermore, compared to humans, tree shrews exhibit a higher basal HDL‐C to LDL‐C ratio, 24 which may render them more susceptible to hyperglycemic stimulation. VEGF induction is a critical factor in pathological retinal angiogenesis. 25 Neurodegenerative changes and vascular issues are the main pathological characteristics of DR. 26 Hyperglycemia‐induced retinal neurodegeneration may precede microvascular dysfunction and contribute to the pathogenesis of DR. Moreover, the loss of nerve cells results in thinning of the RNFL and GCL. 22 , 27 Previous studies have indicated that early progression of DR is associated with neurodegeneration and abnormalities in the retinal microvasculature, including increased permeability of the blood–retinal barrier, loss of vascular endothelial cells and pericytes, the appearance of pericyte “ghosts” and acellular capillaries, thickening of the vascular basement membrane, subsequent capillary occlusion, and apoptosis of retinal neuronal cells along with glial changes. 28 , 29
Our findings revealed a significant thinning of the RNFL and GCL in diabetic tree shrews at 20 weeks, suggesting the onset of early neurodegenerative changes characteristic of DR. Furthermore, these animals exhibited a significant expansion of retinal capillary pathology, as evidenced by increased acellular capillary formation and pericyte ghost accumulation. Research by Gorbatyuk et al. 18 has indicated that tree shrew models of DR exhibit retinal phenotypes marked by cone degeneration and RGC dysfunction, thereby facilitating the investigation of retinal degeneration induced by hyperglycemia in the early stages of DR. Our findings provide additional evidence that retinal microvascular impairment occurs during the initial phase of DR.
DR is influenced by multiple factors and is characterized by intricate, underlying pathophysiological processes. Prolonged periods of high blood sugar, inflammatory responses, increased concentrations of advanced glycation end products, and oxidative stress are key contributors to the development of DR. 30 However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In the present study, we employed RNA‐seq to investigate the potential molecular mechanisms of DR. This revealed 860 differentially expressed mRNAs between the DR and control groups, which were significantly enriched in 267 GO terms and 13 KEGG signaling pathways. Particularly, the pathways were primarily associated with metabolic processes, including single‐organism metabolic processes, the MAPK signaling pathway, the PPAR signaling pathway, and pathways related to insulin resistance (Figure 5).
RNA‐seq analysis indicated that PSAT1was significantly downregulated in the DR group. PSAT1 is a key enzyme in the serine biosynthesis pathway, and it has been implicated in breast cancer oncogenesis, tumorigenesis, cancer cell proliferation, and glycogen synthesis. Particularly, PSAT1 overexpression has been shown to significantly enhance insulin signaling in diabetic mice. This suggests a novel role for PSAT1 in regulating insulin sensitivity, possibly by directly inhibiting intracellular signaling pathways related to AKT phosphorylation. PSAT1 also plays a crucial role in regulating metabolic processes in diabetes. In the present study, PSAT1 expression was found to be downregulated under high‐glucose conditions in RGCs (data not shown), consistent with observations in RMECs. Due to the high degree of differentiation and limited proliferative capacity of RGCs, which restricts primary culture, further investigations were performed using RMECs to enable more in‐depth analysis. The retina is a highly complex tissue comprising multiple cell types that interact through intricate mechanisms. Overexpression of PSAT1 in RMECs restored the mobility and lumen formation ability of these cells in a high‐glucose environment, while decreasing the expression of VEGF (Figure 7). These effects contributed to the maintenance of normal vascular morphology, which is essential for preserving the blood–retinal barrier, regulating blood flow, and supporting retinal metabolic demands and function under hyperglycemic stress.
Previous studies have reported significant downregulation of PSAT1 in DR patients and high‐glucose‐treated human retinal pigment epithelial cells (ARPE‐19), 31 which aligns with our findings in a tree shrew DR model and in RMECs and RGCs under hyperglycemic conditions. As the core cellular component of the retinal vascular inner wall, RMECs play vital roles in maintaining the blood–retinal barrier, regulating blood flow, and participating in immune responses. Modulation of PSAT1 expression in RMECs may influence photoreceptor glucose metabolism via intercellular signaling, thereby affecting metabolic homeostasis in the retina. As an enzyme involved in serine biosynthesis, PSAT1 is involved in metabolic pathways (glycolysis) and amino acid biosynthesis pathways (serine), 32 participating in controlling blood glucose. 33 The by‐product of the PSAT1‐catalyzed reaction, α‐ketoglutarate (α‐KG), has been reported to stimulate insulin secretion. 34 Indeed, PSAT1 upregulation has been demonstrated to enhance insulin signaling and insulin sensitivity both in vitro and in vivo under normal conditions. 35
In conclusion, previous studies and our results suggest that PSAT1 may be involved in the pathological processes of DR and may represent a potential molecular target for DR treatment. Future studies might explore adeno‐associated virus‐mediated overexpression of PSAT1 in RMECs or the retinal tissue as a viable therapeutic strategy. Alternatively, the administration of PSAT1‐derived metabolites, such as α‐KG, which may act as a paracrine signal to enhance insulin sensitivity and support retinal glucose homeostasis, represents another promising approach for mitigating or treating DR. Our findings also indicate that the tree shrew is a promising animal model for research on early‐stage DR. However, the specific mechanisms by which PSAT1 influences DR progression remain to be fully elucidated.
This study has several limitations. First, the absence of specialized ophthalmic equipment limited our evaluation scope. Techniques such as optical coherence tomography provide detailed information on the structure of the retina, whereas fluorescein fundus angiography enables the identification of neovascularization (NV), 36 and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) visualizes the dynamic aspects of the retina. 37 Wide‐field swept‐source OCTA can distinguish intraretinal microvascular abnormalities from NV. 38 Future studies should incorporate these ophthalmic imaging technologies to further validate the retinal vascular pathology in our model. Second, the relatively brief hyperglycemic period (20 weeks) meant that retinal vascular lesions were not detected in the tree shrews, with only diabetic cataracts observed in the imaging results. Long‐term continuous stimulation, such as sustained hyperglycemia, elevated VEGF levels, and lipoprotein exposure, may be necessary to visualize clear vascular changes. 39 We will continue longitudinal monitoring of hematological parameters and retinal alterations in these animals. Additionally, due to the limited sample size (n = 4 per group) for histological and protein analyses, future studies should include larger samples to validate these preliminary findings.
