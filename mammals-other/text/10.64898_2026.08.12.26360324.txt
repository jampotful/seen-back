Abstract

50

Introduction

51

Deafness is the most common human sensory deficit. Cochlear implantation is the primary

52

intervention for severe-to-profound deafness. Currently, over 7000 people have bilateral cochlear

53

implants (CIs) in the United Kingdom (UK), most of whom are children. Patient feedback suggests

54

that for children with bilateral CIs, everyday communication is challenging and tiring, with extra

55

effort required to integrate information from two ears, especially in noise, and that current

56

rehabilitation techniques are not engaging, or appropriate to their lifestyles. To address these issues,

57

researchers developed the Both EARS (BEARS) training package comprised of three virtual reality

58

games to improve sound localization and listening in noise.

59

Objectives

60

This protocol describes the design and methodology of a multi-center phase III randomized

61

controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate whether use of the BEARS training package alongside usual care

62

compared to only receiving usual care improves speech-in-noise perception, hearing experiences,

63

vocabulary and quality of life and reduces listening effort in children and young people (aged 8 -16

64

years (inclusive) with bilateral CIs.

65

Methods

66

This RCT is currently underway in 16 clinical CI departments in National Health Service or university

67

hospitals across the UK. The intervention involves 3 months of spatial-listening training delivered via

68

the BEARS training package in addition to any routine rehabilitation. The control is ‘usual care’

69

(routine rehabilitation clinical care pathway). The primary outcome is the difference between the

70

intervention groups in speech-in-noise perception score at 3 months derived from the spatial speech

71

in noise (SSiN-VA) test. Recruitment closes at the end of the day on 31st July 2026, and end of data

72

collection is 31st October 2026. Data analyses will be reported by 31st March 2026.

Introduction

81

Cochlear Implants (CI) are one of the most successful medical devices available. They are surgically

82

implanted and contain an electrode array inserted into the cochlea that bypasses the damaged

83

cochlear hair cells and stimulates the auditory nerve directly. CIs are the main hearing device

84

intervention for people with severe/profound sensorineural hearing loss. In the UK there are currently

85

18,300 adults and 6,600 children using at least one CI and there are over one million worldwide [1].

86

With respect to deaf children, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) has

87

recommended bilateral CIs since 2019 [2], because the benefits of hearing through two ears greatly

88

outweighs the use of only one. There are currently 5,500 children with bilateral CIs in the UK with

89

approximately 400 new children bilaterally implanted annually [1].

90

The primary intention of CIs is to provide access to speech (to develop spoken communication and

91

acquire language skills for children) for the deaf listener. Speech communication is crucial for

92

cognitive, social and language development and often takes place in complex noisy environments.

93

For listeners with normal hearing, subtle differences in timing and level of sounds reaching each ear

94

provide directional and localization cues for ‘spatial hearing’ [3-6] which contribute to the separation

95

of speech from noise and the ability to attend to a particular speaker [7-9].

96

Children and teenagers spend substantial time in noisy environments, impacting their speech and

97

language perception, educational attainment, and social well-being. Hearing loss further adds to

98

these challenges and increases the risk of emotional and behavioral difficulties that can influence

99

social relationships [10,11]. Although language development [12-14], sound localization and tracking

of moving sound sources [15-20], speech-in-noise perception [14,15,21] and listening effort [22] are

101

better for people with bilateral CIs than for those with a unilateral implant [23,24], these skills

102

remain far below those with normal hearing. People with bilateral CIs often struggle to understand

103

speech in noise even with visual cues [25].

104

Our ‘Living with CIs’ Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE) group reported that

105

everyday communication was challenging and tiring due to the extra effort required, especially in

106

noise [26]. Bilateral CI users reported how it can be hard to balance sounds from two implants and

107

they described sounds from the second implant as ‘annoying’, ‘distracting’ and ‘lop-sided’, leading to

108

its rejection.

109

The lack of protocols for fitting bilateral CIs, ecologically valid outcome measures, and resources for

110

spatial-hearing training contribute to these listening difficulties. The current ‘usual care’ rehabilitation

111

assessments and techniques completed with bilateral CI patients are reportedly not engaging, do not

112

reflect real-world hearing difficulties experienced by users and do not feature specific training to make

113

the most of having two implants [26,27]. However, a large body of research demonstrates that sound

114

localization can improve with training, underpinned by plasticity-driven changes in the auditory

115

pathways for children and adults [20, 28-45]. Additionally, research has found that computer-based

116

speech training can improve sentence recognition [46] and that simple activities in which users

117

interact with sounds through movements and vision can result in significant hearing improvements

118

[47]. Pediatric CI users have also reported that computer-based rehabilitation assessments would

119

better fit their lifestyles and support their learning [26].

120

To help address the everyday hearing challenges faced by children and young people with bilateral CIs

121

and to reduce current gaps in rehabilitative support for this population, we developed the Both Ears

122

(BEARS) training package of virtual-reality (VR) games [27] to train spatial hearing. A participatory

123

design approach was used to develop the training package [26], improve its relevance to the target

124

population and enhance the likelihood of sustained use.

The BEARS training package comprises three games addressing different hearing functions: speech-in-

126

noise perception, music listening and sound-source localization. Each game is based on an audiovisual

127

task performed through a VR interface. The BEARS training package uses a head-mounted display,

128

such as the Meta Quest 2, or an iPad for those with balance issues or who have a head circumference

129

of less than 50 centimeters. Audio is delivered, in both cases, using circumaural headphones (AKG

130

K240 Studio). Different audio playback options were widely explored during the participatory process

131

[26] including using wireless audio transmission. Due to the high variability across CI manufacturers,

132

and potential latency and quality issues with streaming audio via Bluetooth, the choice of the

133

circumaural headphones was ultimately taken, and the brand and model were chosen together with

134

the various stakeholders.

135

By providing training using videogames that are engaging, relevant and appealing to young people, it

136

is expected that positive brain changes can occur to maximize spatial-hearing skills. This has the

137

potential to improve auditory performance in real-life scenarios, which would have positive

138

repercussions in social, emotional, and academic aspects of life. To date, no training program has been

139

implemented and evaluated for young people with bilateral CIs. This manuscript describes the design

140

and methods of a large RCT that aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the BEARS VR training package

141

in improving hearing abilities and listening skills in children and teenagers with bilateral CIs.

142

An independent, parallel mixed-methods process evaluation sub-study, that collects data from

143

participants, caregivers, and clinicians, will run alongside the trial. Designed to support interpretation

144

of the trial results, the process evaluation will inform optimization of the intervention and identify

145

strategies for successful implementation [48], guided by Normalisation Process Theory [49,50]. The

146

process evaluation will assess the quality of trial implementation including fidelity, dose and reach,

147

the participant experience of using BEARS and contextual factors associated with variations in

148

outcome, as well as gathering clinician experiences of implementing the trial. The process evaluation

149

will make recommendations for intervention delivery and will be reported separately.

Interventions

211

Intervention arm: BEARS training package

212

The BEARS training package is a suite of three videogames designed specifically for young people with

213

bilateral CIs. The hardware is either:

214

-

A head-mounted VR display device (Meta Quest 2) with AKG K240 Studio headphones; or

215

-

An iPad with AKG K240 Studio headphones, for those participants with a smaller head

216

circumference (<50 cm) or with reported balance conditions.

217

The BEARS training package comprises three games addressing different hearing functions: speech-in-

218

noise perception, music listening and sound-source localisation. Each game is based on an audiovisual

219

task, and participants are automatically guided through on-screen visual prompts to support the

220

gameplay with feedback given on their performance and progress through levels of increasing

221

difficulty.

222

The three games are:

223

1. Target training game: Initially developed to train normal hearing listeners to localize sounds [40],

224

it has subsequently been adapted for CI users. Through a series of increasingly difficult levels, the

225

player is trained to localize sounds relying initially on audiovisual cues. The visual signal disappears

226

gradually as the levels increase, transforming the task into a purely auditory challenge. Steadman

227

et al. have shown how virtual auditory displays can be used for creating training environments

228

where users learn to localize sounds using modified localization cues [45]. The use of audiovisual

229

stimuli helps with task familiarization in the lower levels, and the gamification approach

230

contributes to improving engagement and attainment.

231

2. Speech-in-Noise training game: Follows many of the same principles as the localization game. It

232

is based on the principle that computer-based training of word perception in noise can improve

233

speech perception [52]. Players will be introduced to a virtual scenario simulating everyday

234

situations, e.g., café. A series of increasingly difficult speech recognition tasks will be presented,

235

challenging the players to interact with the environment (e.g., rotate their head), localize the
10

medRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.12.26360324; this version posted August 13, 2026. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted medRxiv a license to display the preprint in

236

speaker and identify the words, using a range of background noises and reverberant acoustic

237

conditions.

238

3. Spatial music game: This is based on a ‘Musiclarity’ application created within the 3D Tune-In

239

project [53]. The game aims to enhance perception and localization of musical instruments and

240

lyrics in a range of immersive and interactive soundscapes.

241

The BEARS training package design allows for the training to be self-administered, played anywhere

242

and at any time. There is no upper limit to the frequency of use of the BEARS training package. During

243

each session of use (minimum 2x 30-minute sessions once a week), all three games need to be played.

244

The device steers participants through the three games and participants receive an instructional guide

245

that describes how to use the Meta Quest 2 headset [54].

246

The participants will also be provided with a training diary to document any issues they have with the

247

BEARS training package hardware or software, and to document when the games had been played,

248

helping to ensure compliance with the minimum training. Use of the BEARS training package requires

249

all participants randomized into the BEARS intervention arm to switch to an extra program added onto

250

their CI sound processors. This program removes microphone preprocessing modifications (e.g.,

251

directional microphone, wind noise reduction). Once they have finished using the games, they then

252

return to their everyday program.

253

Control intervention: Usual care

254

Usual care describes the routine rehabilitation received by participants via their CI center. During a

255

review appointment the clinician will check that all external and internal equipment is working and

256

check CI usage logs. Patients can attend their CI center at any time to discuss any equipment issues.

257

This will enable their CIs to keep working optimally according to the British Cochlear Implant Group

258

(BCIG) quality standards (QS) [55]. There is no limit to the level of contact between the patient or their

259

family/legal representative and the implant center, in line with BCIG QS. Any contacts will be recorded

260

in the patient’s medical notes and appropriate sections of the trial’s case report forms (CRFs).

Randomization

315

Randomization will be performed by the PI or delegated member of the trial team at local sites using

316

317

the trial [57]. This has been validated by the trial statistician. Following confirmation of eligibility and

318

319

320

Type of randomization

321

Participants will be randomized 1:1 using a concealed fixed minimization process according to the

322

minimization factors listed below. This will ensure that balance across the two study arms will be

323

achieved. The minimization algorithm will incorporate a random element to maximize balance in the

324

stratifying variables between the randomized groups.

325

Minimization factors will include: 1) sex at birth (male; female); 2) school age group (primary 8-11;

326

secondary 12-16); 3) school setting (mainstream day school; weekday specialist boarding); 4) onset

327

of deafness (congenital; progressive/acquired); 5) device allocation (VR headset; iPad).

328

Sealed Envelope software ensures that site clinicians are unaware of the next assignment in the

329

random sequence. For the sites, clinicians’ allocation concealment is not possible during the

330

intervention period because the equipment/training aspects of the BEARS intervention need to be

331

provided to the participant and their family at the end of their baseline visit (Visit 2b).

332

Responsibility for enrolling participants and randomizing them to study arms lies with the PI and other

333

trained individuals (e.g. clinicians/research nurses) named on the site delegation log.

334

Once a participant has been allocated to a study arm and following baseline data collection, the

335

participant/family will be informed of the intervention allocation by a delegated site team member.

Discussion

674

This RCT protocol has been designed to analyze whether using the spatial-listening training delivered

675

via the BEARS training package for 3-months alongside usual care compared to only receiving usual

676

care improves speech-in-noise perception, hearing experiences, vocabulary and quality of life in

677

young people with bilateral CIs.

678

The underlying premise is that the BEARS training package will facilitate improvements in hearing

679

abilities and listening skills, particularly in background noise, which in turn could improve self-

680

confidence for engaging in communication without the fear of embarrassment. It is expected that

681

vocabulary will be expanded, there will be improved concentration and attention, reduced anxiety

682

and improved quality of life. Additionally, the measures used in the trial are intended to assess the

683

direct and longer-term health and well-being outcomes as well as unpicking the mechanism of

684

change.

685

The immersive VR format allows for home-based training, empowering children and young people

686

with CIs to take more ownership of their rehabilitation and reducing the reliance on outpatient-

687

based services. Moreover, the multi-modal format of the BEARS VR training package, which

688

incorporates both audio and visual components, may be better than auditory interventions alone for

689

generalization and retention [82].

690

The embedded health economic analysis in this RCT will provide evidence regarding the value for

691

money of the BEARS compared to usual care alone in terms of health and quality-of-life benefits to

692

CI users and the potential impact of this on their use of health care services. Importantly, it also

693

incorporates the impact that these improvements in hearing and participation could have on local

694

education support used by children and young people in the study.

695

Alongside the RCT, in-depth qualitative interviews and online, open-text surveys will capture the

696

perspectives of children and young people regarding their experiences of listening in daily life and

697

the impact of the BEARS training package on their real-life listening experiences. Qualitative findings

698

will also contribute to our understanding of how the BEARS training package may lead to perceived

699

changes in everyday listening for users of bilateral CIs. Focus groups with clinicians at the research

700

sites conducted as part of the Process Evaluation will inform implementation strategies, and more

701

generally, insights gathered from our qualitative research may inform new policy interventions,

702

changes to clinical practice, and highlight opportunities to remove barriers to access and help

703

promote well-being among children and young people with bilateral CIs.

704

Additionally, the trial’s use of iPad-based spatial speech-in-noise assessments highlights an

705

opportunity to scale more efficient and accessible methods for spatial hearing assessment in routine

706

clinical practice. Spatial speech-in-noise assessments (e.g., speech tests presented in noise where

707

speech and noise come from different locations) provide a more realistic picture of how bilateral CI

708

users function in noisy, real-world sound environments compared with standard pure tone

709

audiometry [61]. However, these assessments are time consuming and have historically required

710

expensive, maintenance-intensive multi-speaker arrays that impose significant space and

711

operational costs. This has resulted in variable use of speech testing across the UK [83], which raises

712

concerns about equality of access and uniformity of care. Delivery of the iPad-based speech tests in

713

the BEARS trial demonstrates the possibility to use simple equipment to assess complex listening

714

skills. Refining and translating these tools for clinical practice presents an opportunity to increase

715

access to speech testing across geographical locations.

716

This is the first NIHR-funded multi-center hearing research study and is the largest known

717

randomized controlled trial in children and young people with CIs. We are confident that the

718

bespoke measures and the findings in this study will not only contribute to future audiological

719

clinical practice and interventions but will also enable identification of further existing research gaps.
