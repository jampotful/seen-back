# First Free Anterolateral Thigh Flap for Facial Reconstruction Following Bear Mauling in Bhutan

## Abstract
Summary:Approximately 70% of Bhutan is forested, providing habitat for the Himalayan black bear. A farmer who was mauled by a bear experienced significant tissue loss affecting his left forehead, eyebrow, upper and lower eyelids, and cheek. Immediate management included wound irrigation, antibiotic prophylaxis, and soft-tissue reconstruction using an anterolateral thigh fasciocutaneous flap for coverage. Flap reconstruction facilitated ocular salvage, with retention of excellent visual acuity and a cosmetically acceptable, functional facial outcome. The flap also provided protection and function of the parotid duct, temporomandibular joint, and the oral and nasal cavities. This case highlights the value of a multidisciplinary team approach from ophthalmology, ear, nose, and throat, and plastic and reconstructive surgery in the management of severe composite facial defects. The recent establishment of plastic and reconstructive surgical services in Bhutan allowed for timely microsurgical intervention that would not have been possible in earlier years, demonstrating a significant advancement in local reconstructive capacity and patient outcomes.
Bear mauling is a rare yet devastating trauma mainly involving the head and neck region. The National Hospital in Bhutan treated 34 patients who had been mauled by bears between 2015 and 2019, of whom 29 sustained facial injuries.1
Until 2024, Bhutanese patients with large tissue defects were referred outside of Bhutan for reconstruction and rehabilitation, whereas minor wounds were left to heal by secondary intention, requiring a protracted healing period and resulting in contracted and deforming scars.1–3
Free and local flaps have been used to cover facial defects caused by bear maulings in other Asian countries.1–7 However, before 2024, free flaps were only available in Bhutan during visiting surgical camps.
In 2024, the first Bhutanese plastic and reconstructive fellow returned to Bhutan after training in India and Australia. A plastic surgical and reconstructive team was formed with 4 nurses and 1 nurse anesthetist. This article describes the first major facial defect reconstructed with an anterolateral thigh (ALT) free flap performed by a local surgeon in Bhutan.

## CASE PRESENTATION
A 44-year-old farmer from Wangdiphodrang, central Bhutan, was attacked by a Himalayan black bear while collecting his cattle in the forest at dusk. He was taken to a local hospital within 3 hours, where first aid and moist dressings were applied, and was then transferred to the National Referral Hospital in Thimphu. Upon arrival there the following morning, he was conscious and oriented, with stable vital signs.
The patient had sustained a left-sided scalp laceration and loss of soft tissue measuring 20 × 25 cm of the left side of the face, exposing the parotid gland, temporomandibular joint, and left globe. The facial nerve was missing from the nerve trunk. He had lost part of the zygomatic bone and the anterior maxillary wall (Fig. 1), with 3 punctures into the oral and nasal cavity. The lacrimal system was severely damaged. He had also sustained an incomplete open rupture of the left quadriceps tendon with a large osteochondral fracture of the medial trochlear facet while using his knife to defend himself (Fig. 1). The patient provided consent for wound debridement and ALT flap cover.
The patient sustained scalp laceration, with a 20 × 25 cm soft tissue defect to the left side of his face, revealing loss of all branches of the facial nerve, the zygomatic bone, partial loss of the anterior maxilla, loss of lower eyelid, and exposure of the temporomandibular joint and parotid duct, affecting full functional units 3b, 3c, 3d, and partial 4a, full 4b, 4c, and 4d.8
The ear, nose, and throat team completed wound debridement, and the plastic surgical team raised the ALT flap. As the left facial nerve branch terminal could not be located, nerve graft reconstruction was not possible. The left facial artery and vein were used as recipient vessels for microvascular end-to-end anastomosis with 10-0 nylon. The flap was partially split to accommodate the left eye, allowing the ophthalmology team to reconstruct the periorbital soft tissues.
The patient presented with a complete loss of the lower eyelid, whereas the upper eyelid had an intact posterior lamella and lid margin. For reconstruction of the lower eyelid posterior lamella, a Hughes procedure (tarsoconjunctival flap advancement) was performed. The upper eyelid was everted, and a 15-blade incision was made through the conjunctiva and tarsus, 4 mm above the eyelid margin. A tarsoconjunctival flap was raised by dissecting the conjunctiva from the Müller muscle using conjunctival scissors. The flap was then rotated inferiorly to cover the lower eyelid defect, with the superior border of the tarsus forming part of the new lower eyelid margin. The edges were secured to the medial and lateral canthus, and the lower portion of the flap was anchored to the lower eyelid using 6-0 polyglactin sutures. Following posterior lamella reconstruction of the lower eyelid, the anterior lamella of both the upper and lower eyelids was reconstructed using a full-thickness skin graft.
Postoperatively, the patient rested in intensive care for hourly flap monitoring. Low molecular weight heparin infusion was given as anticoagulation therapy for 5 days, followed by aspirin for 21 days. He was kept hyperhydrated, warm, and pain free. He was able to eat, speak, and breath normally postoperatively and was discharged on day 16.
Six weeks after his injury and following the second stage of the Hughes procedure, the patient was treated for chemosis of the lower conjunctiva (Fig. 2).
ALT flap, split to accommodate the patient’s left eye.
Stage 2 of the Hughes procedure was performed after 6 weeks, which involved separating the upper and lower eyelids by dividing the flap at the level of the newly created lower eyelid margin (Fig. 2). Division of the patient’s tarsoconjunctival flap revealed chemosis of the lower conjunctiva, which was treated topically.
Nine weeks after his injury, the patient returned to ophthalmology with 2 primary concerns. First, his eye was watering, and second, the flap was obscuring his visual field. At this stage, he did not want further surgical treatment (Fig. 2). He reported no facial pain and no difficulties with mastication, and stated that he was not self-conscious in his village. He reported some resistance when opening his mouth widely and was given a home program of gentle vibration and light scar massage with temporomandibular range of motion. The patient’s facial sensation on his unaffected side was normal (as per Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments), and the flap was assessed to have protective sensation in all areas (monofilament 4.31).9 At 9 weeks postinjury, the patient reported continued watering of his left eye and some visual field obstruction from the flap (Fig. 3).
Two weeks after his injury and following the second stage of the Hughes procedure, the patient’s eye was watering. Separation of the patient’s eyelid revealed chemosis of the left eye.
Six months after the injury, the patient presented with narrowing of the opening that had been created in the flap to accommodate his left eye, and the lateral canthus was surgically revised to increase his field of vision.
Ten months post injury, the patient had a surgical revision to the lateral canthus to improve his field of vision. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the patient at 9 weeks postinjury with continued watering of his left eye and some visual field obstruction from the flap, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E688.)
One year following the injury, debulking of the periorbital portion of flap and an upper eye lid skin graft were performed to increase the eye opening and facilitate blinking. Following that, the patient had no visual field obstruction or tearing (Fig. 4).
The donor site, which was grafted, is large and recessed, but does not concern the patient, as most of it is covered by his gho (traditional Bhutanese robe worn by men). He reports no alteration in leg strength or range of motion and is able to carry out his farming duties, as no muscle or bone was harvested from his thigh.

## DISCUSSION
Bear attacks, though mostly nonfatal, target the head and neck. The nonretractable claws of a bear are capable of removing bone and overlying tissue.10 The Himalayan bear is largely vegetarian and, in this attack, did not bite the patient. Further, the patient presented quickly for wound cleaning and antibiotic prophylaxis, and retention of vascular structures allowed for early coverage of the wound; these factors contributed to the patient remaining free of infection.2
Early debridement and timely closure of complex facial defects are critical to preventing further loss of vital structures, including the oral and nasal mucosa, underlying osseous framework, and dentition. Delayed management, as documented in previous case reports of secondary intention healing, can result in severe contracture and disfiguring cicatricial changes.8 Prompt reconstruction in the periorbital region is particularly important for the preservation of the globe and maintenance of visual function.

## CONCLUSIONS
This study describes growth in the capacity of the health system of Bhutan in managing major deforming and disabling facial trauma. The availability of microsurgical free flaps for early wound coverage can save patients from infection, visual loss, loss of further structures, and deforming scars, as demonstrated in this case.
