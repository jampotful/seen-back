Abstract

9

Batesian mimicry, with its highly colorful species and astonishing mimic-model resemblance,

10

is a canonical example of evolution by natural selection. However, Batesian mimicry could also

11

occur in inconspicuous species and rely on subtle resemblance. Although potentially

12

widespread, such instances have been rarely investigated, such that the real frequency of

13

Batesian mimicry has remained largely unknown. To fill this gap, we developed a new

14

approach using deep learning to quantify the resemblance between putative mimics and

15

models from photographs. We applied this method to quantify the frequency of Batesian

16

mimicry in Western Palearctic snakes. Potential mimics were revealed by an excess of

17

resemblance with sympatric venomous snakes compared to random expectations. We found

18

that 8% of the non-venomous species were potential mimics, among which all were imperfect

19

mimics. This study is the first to quantify the frequency of Batesian mimicry in a whole

20

community of vertebrates, and shows that even concealed species can be reliably identified

21

as potential models. Our approach should prove useful to detect mimicry in other communities,

22

and more generally it highlights the benefits of deep learning for quantitative studies of

23

phenotypic resemblance.

1. Introduction

25

Batesian mimicry occurs when palatable or harmless species resemble an unprofitable (e.g.

26

unpalatable or dangerous) model species, adopting some of its characteristics, and

27

consequently gaining protection from predators (Bates 1862). Mimicry is a canonical example

28

of evolution through natural selection; it was already discussed by Darwin with Wallace

29

(Darwin 1887) and still constitutes a proliferous field of research in ecology and evolution

30

(Sherratt 2002; Nishikawa et al. 2015; Joshi et al. 2017; Bosque et al. 2018). Nevertheless,

31

some fundamental aspects of this defensive strategy remain poorly known, notably its

32

frequency in plants and animals, mainly because detecting mimicry is no simple matter.

33

Historically, research on Batesian mimicry has long been confined to a few spectacular

34

cases, like hoverflies (Penney et al. 2012), butterflies (Kitamura and Imafuku 2015) or

35

kingsnakes (Brodie and Janzen 1995), which possess conspicuous colors and show a

36

resemblance between models and mimics so striking that it cannot be fortuitous. This

37

perfection itself is a double-edged sword. It served as an evidence of the Darwinian evolution,

38

but it also provided a distorted picture of what mimicry can be. Indeed, neither

39

conspicuousness nor high resemblance is a necessary condition for Batesian mimicry. Actually,

40

even when their overall appearance is inconspicuous or camouflaged, venomous species may

41

eventually be spotted by predators, which can recognize species using cues that have not

42

necessarily evolved as specific signals. Harmless and palatable species can mimic these cues

43

to gain additional protection from mimicry while being themselves cryptic (Barlow and Wiens

44

1977; Mound et al. 2005; Gianoli and Carrasco-Urra 2014; Corcobado et al. 2016). Moreover,

45

experimental evidence suggest that an imperfect resemblance between a mimic and its

46

model, known as imperfect mimicry, can still provide protection against predators (Dittrich et

47

al. 1993). Thus, most examples found in literature do not reflect the potential diversity of

mimicry in nature. Focusing on these classic cases is likely to bias our perception of mimicry,

49

making us underestimate its frequency.

50

If we want to fully understand the role of mimicry in shaping phenotypic evolution in

51

natural communities, we need to go beyond the few exceptionally perfect cases to expose

52

common and imperfect products of natural selection. The detection of mimicry within a

53

community requires a quantification of the resemblance between species. In mimicry studies,

54

two main categories of methods have been traditionally used to estimate resemblance. The

55

first methods analyze predefined features; for example morphometric measures, the

56

geometry of color patterns or the distribution of orientations and spatial frequencies in light

57

changes in images (Penney et al. 2012; Taylor et al. 2016; Troscianko et al. 2017; Endler et al.

58

2018). These methods usually attempt to model physiological perceptual processes and thus

59

can provide information on the role of these processes in the evolution of mimicry. However,

60

by being confined to the studied processes, they may be limited for capturing the high

61

dimensionality of the feature space relevant for assessing visual resemblance (Zhang et al.

62

2018). The second category of methods relies on psychological tests with human subjects (e.g.,

63

Cuthill and Bennett 1993). These methods can provide more accurate assessments of overall

64

perceptual resemblance; however, extensive tests and very large sample size are necessary to

65

mitigate the individual variations between evaluators (Ishihara et al. 2001; Kitayama et al.

66

2003).

67

Artificial Intelligences, and especially deep convolutional neural networks (hereafter

68

DNNs), offer new opportunities to model perceptual resemblance (Jozwik et al. 2017; Cichy

69

and Kaiser 2019). For example, a psychophysical experiment conducted with thousands of

70

humans showed that DNN’s measure of resemblance between complex stimuli (e.g., pictures

71

of a crowd) predict the human averaged perceived resemblance better than any previous

73

74

illumination (Jozwik et al. 2017). Thus, DNNs are highly suited to exploit the nearly

75

inexhaustible resource of non-standardized photographs posted on the World Wide Web.

76

DNNs have revolutionized many scientific fields in recent years, including biology (e.g. in

77

genomics: Zhou and Troyanskaya 2015, medicine: Shen et al. 2017) but the potential of DNN

78

as a model of human and animal perception, which can handle very large databases of non-

79

standardized data, has been overlooked in visual ecology thus far.

80

Several cases of Batesian mimicry between venomous and non-venomous snakes of

81

the Western Palearctic (Europe, North Africa and the Middle-East) has been reported in

82

literature, sometime based on experimental validation or mimic behavior but mainly using a

83

human subjective assessment of the visual resemblance between potential model and mimic

84

(Gans 1961; Werner and Frankenberg 1982; Werner 1983; Valkonen et al. 2011a; Aubret and

85

Mangin 2014; Valkonen and Mappes 2014). None of the venomous model species in the study

86

area possesses highly conspicuous colors, and most of them are cryptic in their environment

87

(Turk 1958; Heatwole and Davison 1976; Werner 1983). However the zigzag dorsal patterns

88

of vipers has been shown to elicit avoidance in bird predators (Valkonen et al. 2011b) in

89

addition to providing camouflage (Santos et al. 2014). In spite of these case studies, no

90

attempt has been made to evaluate the extent of mimicry in the Western Palearctic snake

91

assemblage. Western Palearctic snakes thus appear as a good model to measure the

92

frequency of mimicry in situations more complicated than most classic examples described in

93

the literature. Here we incorporated DNN into a workflow to identify potential mimics among

94

all 122 snake species known in the Western Palearctic at the time of the study.

To identify candidate mimics, we developed a method that quantifies the visual

96

97

98

trained DNNs to recognize the candidate (venomous) models before (ii) using the DNNs to

99

assess the resemblance between candidate mimic (non-venomous) and candidate models. To

100

identify candidate mimics, we (iii) combined the information on resemblance with information

101

on distribution to detect non-venomous species that exhibit more phenotypic resemblance to

102

one or several models that live in sympatry than expected by chance. We found that 8% of the

103

non-venomous species were candidate mimics, among which almost all were imperfect

104

mimics to a human eye. A global test of the hypothesis that mimicry affected the evolution of

105

phenotype in the Western Palearctic snake assemblage was highly significant. We discuss this

106

result in the light of the known ecology of Western Palearctic snakes and of the validity of

107

modeling non-human predator vision using DNNs and regular images.

110

We analyzed all snake species of the Western Palearctic (after the taxonomic list of Geniez

111

2015), 122 in total, with 35 venomous species and 87 non-venomous species. Table S1 lists all

112

(3) Methodological workflow

149

DNNs were first trained to recognize the different venomous species of the Western Palearctic

150

plus the “foreign” class (Figure 1A). Trained DNNs were then asked to classify pictures of non-

151

venomous snakes as venomous species plus the “foreign” category (Figure 1B). DNNs

152

provided, for each image, a probability of being attributed to each venomous species class.

153

The “foreign” class avoids forcing the networks to attribute an image to one of the potential

154

model even if it does not resemble any of the Western Palearctic venomous species. For each

155

non-venomous species, attribution probabilities were then averaged over all available

156

pictures to build a matrix of resemblance between non-venomous and venomous species with

157

averaged attribution probabilities as entries. These probabilities were transformed into

158

resemblance scores, i.e. in inversed ranks of probability (1/rank, in ascending order; Figure

159

1C). Thus, the classes of venomous snakes to which one particular nonvenomous snake was

160

attributed most often, i.e. ranking first or second, had the highest resemblance score. This

161

step allows to reduce the prediction variability that can arise between DNNs due to differences

162

of architectures or training algorithms (Sameen and Pradhan 2017; Kim et al. 2019). Next, we

163

deleted from this matrix all non-venomous species for which the “foreign” class received the

164

highest resemblance score, thus discarding species that were clearly not mimics, as well as the

165

foreign species class. In parallel, we built a sympatry matrix of the same size as the

166

resemblance matrix, which gives the proportion of the geographic distribution of each non-

168

previous study (Miller et al. 2019), we considered that the proportion of sympatry reflected

169

the potential extent of selection for mimicry. This, in fact, is an approximation as for a few

170

allopatric species pairs, distribution ranges may be close enough to allow predators

171

associating both snake species. Yet most allopatric pairs are distant by hundreds or thousands

172

of kilometers in our dataset, which limits this possibility (Pfennig and Mullen 2010). The last

173

step of our workflow was to calculate “total scores” by summing, for each non-venomous

174

species, the resemblance scores weighted (Hadamard product) by sympatry scores.

175

Our method did not include phylogenetic correction for the measure of the

176

resemblance because all the venomous and non-venomous snakes belong to two distant and

177

monophyletic clades. The resemblance between these clades thus cannot be attributed to

178

phylogenetic relationship.

179

Candidate mimics and models were identified statistically by comparing total scores to

180

a null distribution. This null distribution was generated by randomly shuffling the venomous

181

species in the sympatry matrix before calculating the resemblance score for each non-

182

venomous species, and by replicating this operation 50,000 times (Figure 1C). Species for

183

which the total scores fell in the right tail (e.g., the 5% highest scores) of the null distribution

184

were more similar to sympatric venomous species than expected by chance and were thus

185

considered candidate mimics (and their sympatric venomous species considered candidate

186

models).

187

We tested the same hypothesis of an excess of resemblance multiple times; thus, we

188

tested whether significant results could have arisen by chance. We used the Fisher’s method,

189

also named Fisher's combined probability test (Fisher 1925), to test if there was, across all the

190

non-venomous species, a significant excess of resemblance with the sympatric venomous

(4) Image pre-processing

198

Snake images were squared (by cropping around snakes) and downsized to 299 x 299 pixels.

199

The venomous snake dataset was split into two: 90% of images were used for training and

200

10% for validation (i.e., to monitor performances while tuning training hyperparameters). We

201

used an image augmentation procedure to improve invariance to noisy variations and to limit

202

overfitting: before every training epoch, images were rotated with an angle sampled between

203

0° and 180°, randomly flipped horizontally and vertically, up to 20% zoomed in or out, and

204

RGB color channel randomly shifted by 0.2. Images of non-venomous snakes were squared

205

and downsized only.

(5) DNN training and predictions

208

Training a DNN to recognize snake species from scratch would have required a very large

209

dataset of images to achieve good performances. Following other studies (e.g., Norouzzadeh

210

et al. 2018), we rather used “transfer learning”, in which a DNN pre-trained on a very large

211

dataset is fine-tuned with a new, smaller dataset. In this study, we transferred the Xception

212

network (Chollet 2017) pre-trained on ImageNet (a dataset of 14 million images belonging to

213

more than 20 000 classes; Deng et al. 2009). We tested other networks also trained on

214

ImageNet (ResNet, Alexnet), which all yielded qualitatively similar results but reached lower

216

3. Results

249

After training, DNNs were able to recognize the species of a Western Palearctic venomous

250

snake with an accuracy of 77% ± 0.004 SE (evaluated on the validation dataset; random guess:

251

2.78% accuracy) and a top-3 accuracy of 92% ± 0.015 SE (probability that one of the three

252

classes with the highest prediction matches the correct class), indicating that DNNs learned

253

the most distinctive traits of each species. This is a remarkably high performance considering

254

the limited size of the training dataset, and the extreme resemblance among several vipers,

255

which makes their identification challenging even for experts when the geographic origin is

256

unknown (Geniez 2015). In comparison, a previous DNN-based analysis of species recognition

257

based on 35,200 training images from the Internet representing 289 species of reptiles

258

reached 45.9% and 80.9% top-1 and top-5 accuracy, respectively (Van Horn et al. 2018).

259

Comparing weighted resemblance scores to the null distribution, with a 5% threshold,

260

our method identified seven non-venomous species out of the 87 (8%) as candidate mimics of

261

venomous snakes: these species have a higher resemblance with their sympatric venomous

4. Discussion

270

We propose here a new automatic method to infer potential mimetic pairs and to evaluate

271

the frequency of mimicry within an entire group of organisms based on non-standardized

272

photographs. We applied it to quantify for the first time the extent of mimicry in an

273

assemblage of vertebrates at a continental scale (see Wilson et al. 2015 for a related work

274

with invertebrates). Although the hypothesis of mimicry would have to be validated

275

experimentally for each particular species pair, our analysis confirms the existence in

276

Western Palearctic snakes of a pattern typical of Batesian mimicry (i.e. pairwise resemblance

277

between sympatric pairs of unrelated nontoxic and toxic preys), and suggests that this

278

defensive strategy has influenced their phenotypic evolution. Finally, our analysis confirms

279

previous researches in demonstrating that DNNs can reach high level of accuracy even when

280

trained on small datasets (Hansen et al. 2018; Körschens et al. 2018; Mathis et al. 2018).

281

Several arguments from the natural history of snakes support the validity of our

282

method to identify mimics and models. Firstly, six of the seven species identified as potential

283

mimics by our method have been mentioned as such in the literature (Gans 1961; Werner and

284

Frankenberg 1982; Werner 1983; Valkonen et al. 2011a; Aubret and Mangin 2014; see Figure

285

2). Secondly, the mimicry hypothesis is supported for all the detected species by additional

evidence of mimicry, based on behavioral or/and acoustic traits. The putative mimics Natrix

287

helvetica, N. maura, Coronella austriaca, Spalerosophis diadema and Telescopus fallax

288

displace their maxillary bones to produce a triangular head shape when disturbed, increasing

289

visual resemblance with their viper model (Werner and Frankenberg 1982; Werner 1983;

290

Valkonen et al. 2011a; Aubret and Mangin 2014). In addition, Natrix maura also produces a

291

hissing sound similar to the sympatric Vipera aspis and V. berus (Aubret and Mangin 2014)

292

while the non-venomous Dasypeltis sahelensis and its sympatric venomous Echis pyramidum

293

possess the same modified scales on the flanks that are rubbed to produce a similar warning

294

signal when disturbed (Gans 1961). Finally, even if it has never been reported yet as a potential

295

mimic in the literature, Hemorrhois nummifer shows the same behavioral and acoustic

296

warning signal as its sympatric vipers, Montivipera xanthina and Macrovipra lebetina (authors’

297

personal observation).

298

Remarkably, we found that even well camouflaged species (e.g., vipers of the genus

299

Pseudocerastes; Figure 2) could be a model for non-venomous species. The lack of any pattern

300

or color signaling venomousness in this species confirms that mimicry can be effective without

301

conspicuous traits, and advocate for a better separation between the concepts of Batesian

302

mimicry, communication signaling and conspicuousness. The link between these three

303

concepts seems to represent a historical legacy of the easier detection of mimicry when it

304

involves conspicuous aposematic models. The method presented here, which uses DNN-based

305

resemblance coupled with information on geographic co-occurrence, avoids a biased selection

306

favoring conspicuous organisms when studying Batesian mimicry.

307

An additional advantage of our DNN-based approach is its ability to suggest mimicry

308

even when resemblance is imperfect. Most of the species identified by our method are

309

imperfect mimics, at least from our human point of view, as their colors and patterns match

313

snakes, only the “back-up signal” hypothesis is currently supported by ecological evidence.

314

This hypothesis suggests that imperfect mimicry can be reinforced by complementary signals

315

(Johnstone 1996). It is convincing especially for mimics that show similarities in behavioral,

316

morphological and acoustic warning signals with their sympatric venomous species (Gans

317

1961; Werner 1983; Valkonen et al. 2011a; Aubret and Mangin 2014). In addition to the eleven

318

hypotheses, one characteristic of the studied snakes could help explain how imperfect

319

mimicry can evolve. Some model species are highly variable, which may prevent predators

320

from associating a specific phenotype to venomousness (Kikuchi and Pfennig 2010) and thus

321

make them more careful and less likely to attack snakes with ambiguous phenotypes. This

322

explanation is plausible at least for the three candidate models Vipera ammodytes, V. berus

323

and V. aspis, which exhibit significant intraspecific and often intra-population variation in

324

coloration (Figure S2). More research is needed to evaluate the validity of these different

325

explanations and to determine why imperfect mimicry seems ubiquitous among Western

326

Palearctic snakes.

327

Although additional cues comfort the mimicry hypothesis for all detected species, our

328

method can only be used for suggesting candidate mimics and models, and a proper

329

demonstration of mimicry would require additional data. For instance, mimetic species are

330

likely to have similar size and ecology than their models. This information was not provided

331

by our photographs but may influence the plausibility of the mimicry hypothesis for some

332

species (see below). Moreover, most studies of interspecific resemblance, including our

333

method, are fundamentally limited in their ability to distinguish between mimicry and other

334

factors of resemblance. For example, phylogenetic relatedness is a classical cause of

335

resemblance; however this hypothesis does not apply to our study as venomous and non-

336

venomous snakes belong to two distant, reciprocally monophyletic clades. Resemblance could

337

also arise by ecological convergence because of similar abiotic environmental constraints

338

(Rajpurohit et al. 2008), or similar selective pressures for camouflage (Stevens et al. 2013).

339

These factors could lead to overestimating the frequency of mimicry in our results. This issue

340

potentially affects all mimicry studies that do not test predator behavior in a natural

341

environment. However, because most studies focus on conspicuously colored and presumably

342

aposematic species (Brodie and Janzen 1995; Cheney and Marshall 2009; Penney et al. 2012)

343

researchers usually assume that resemblance can only be attributed to mimicry. This

344

assumption is seldom tested although even conspicuous colors may have camouflage function

345

(Mochida et al. 2015; Barnett et al. 2018). In Western Palearctic snakes, body colors are not

346

conspicuous. It would thus be interesting to perform predation tests in natural conditions to

347

investigate whether the features copied by potential mimics are those effectively used by

348

predators to recognize models (e.g., as in Kristiansen et al. 2018), and thus to confirm that

349

resemblance is effectively driven by mimicry. Nevertheless, as discussed previously all

350

identified mimics use behavioral or acoustic imitation in addition to color pattern and, as

351

resemblance in several ontogenetically independent traits can hardly be attributed to

352

ecological convergence, to our view mimicry remains the most likely explanation.

353

The ability to detect imperfect and non-conspicuous mimicry makes our method less

354

conservative compared to methods entirely based on human detection, for evaluating the

355

frequency of mimicry. Even so, we may have failed to identify potential mimic-model pairs.

356

First, the 5th percentile threshold in the null distribution used to interpret resemblance scores

357

is entirely conventional and may overlook some mimicry systems. We found two species

The second species, Rhagerhis moilensis, resembles the sympatric Cerastes cerastes in

362

coloration but has a different size and shape (Geniez 2015). Mimicry hypothesis is implausible,

but cannot be discounted for this species. Therefore, the 5th percentile threshold should be

interpreted cautiously and we recommend not drawing firm conclusions without examining

366

and models because, for the non-venomous polymorphic species (e.g. Leviton 1970; Kark et

367

al. 1997; Mebert 2011), we could not test mimicry for each morphotype separately. Instead,

368

we had to analyze all the morphotypes together, and test mimicry at the species level. This

369

complicates the detection of mimicry as only some morphotypes of these polymorphic species

370

may mimic venomous snakes (e.g., in Hemorrhois ravergieri, Werner and Frankenberg 1982,

371

see below). Testing mimicry for each morphotype is possible knowing the precise geographical

372

distribution of each morphotype, but this information was not available for most polymorphic

373

species. These two limits may lead to an under-estimation of the mimicry frequency in natural

374

communities.

study (Gans 1961; Werner and Frankenberg 1982; Werner 1983). One of them, Hemorrhois

378

and Frankenberg 1982). Our method failed to identify it as such probably because it exhibits

379

a strong polymorphism, with only some of its variants thought to mimic vipers (Werner and

380

Frankenberg 1982). A second species, Dasypeltis bazi, could have been missed by our

381

approach because of the limited number of pictures in our dataset (six in total, this rare and

387

Our study highlights the benefits of DNNs for studying resemblance between

388

phenotypes in ecology and evolution. DNNs have been previously used in ecology to analyze

389

big data, e.g., to identify species from camera trap images (Norouzzadeh et al. 2018).

390

However, their capacity to model biological perception has been thus far overlooked (Cichy

391

and Kaiser 2019). A growing body of literature in humans has shown that DNNs predict the

392

perceived resemblance between complex stimuli better than all other models, even those

393

specifically tuned with neurophysiological data (Yamins et al. 2014; Kriegeskorte 2015;

394

Kubilius et al. 2016). Of particular relevance for the study of camouflage and mimicry, one

395

study showed that whichever perceptual task a DNN is trained for (e.g., recognizing objects

396

from images, segmenting foreground in video frames), DNN accurately predicts perceptual

397

similarity even near the discrimination threshold (Zhang et al. 2018).

398

In mimicry systems (and more generally in most communication systems) the

399

relevant observer is not human. Are the performances of DNNs at reproducing human

400

perception also relevant to other animals? These performances are based on three

401

characteristics (Kriegeskorte 2015). The first one, the capacity of DNNs to solve high-level tasks

402

(Zhang et al. 2018), is shared by all animals. The second is their general architecture (a stack

403

of convolution, activation and pooling functions) which reproduces operations performed

404

sequentially and iteratively by neurons in the different human visual areas (Güçlü and van

405

Gerven 2015). Similar operations are also found, beyond humans, in at least all Vertebrates

17

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/789206; this version posted October 1, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

406

(Renoult et al. 2019). The third is the structure of input data. In this study, we used RGB images

407

that match human trichromatic vision (ie based on three cone types). Other animals have

408

different cone types (e.g., two in carnivores, four in birds), tuned to different wavelengths.

409

How these differences affect DNN models of perceptual space is a nearly unexplored avenue

410

of research (but see Bergeron and Fuller 2018). Yet indirect evidence suggests that the impact

411

would be relatively minor. Indeed, studies on bird plumages that used both RGB images and

412

bird-specific cone stimulations showed no qualitative differences (Dale et al. 2015; Miller et

413

al. 2019). Accordingly, several authors have argued that RGB images provide major insight into

414

ecology, evolution, and behavior in nonhuman species (Seddon et al. 2010; Bergeron and

415

Fuller 2018). In addition, in our particular case, the use of ranks to express relative

416

resemblance makes our mimicry-detection method insensitive to how precisely the similarity

417

measure produced by DNNs scales to similarity perceived by animals.

418

A potentially important limitation of RGB images, however, is to eliminate wavebands

419

to which humans –but not other vertebrates– are blind. The ultraviolets (UV), in particular,

420

may be problematic. Regarding our study, the main predators of snakes in the Western

421

Palearctic are non-primate mammals and raptors (Valkonen et al. 2011b): they have a very

422

limited UV vision and they most certainly do not use UV cues for foraging (Jacobs 2009; Lind

423

et al. 2013, 2014). Thus, UV-based mimicry seems unlikely in snakes. More generally, however,

424

one should keep in mind that DNNs will never detect resemblance from information not

425

present in the data.

426

Last, in our study we analyzed non-standardized images. Although standardization

427

removes irrelevant information from the data, we think that non-standardized images are

428

ecologically more relevant. Indeed, predators experience substantial variation in perceptual

429

conditions, in relation to snake position, background, shading, light intensity and color. This

18

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/789206; this version posted October 1, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was

430

variation could affect the evolution of mimic phenotypes; for example, it has been suggested

431

to explain imperfect mimicry (relaxed selection hypothesis, Kikuchi and Pfennig 2013). While

432

RGB pictures from the Internet may not precisely match variation experienced by predators,

433

drastically limiting all variation (by standardization) would make the dataset un-natural.

434

Standardized images would be nevertheless useful in other contexts, e.g. to determine which

435

visual features contribute the most to deceive predators.

436

To conclude, Artificial Intelligence, here represented by DNNs, has a great potential for

437

the study of mimicry. We used it to detect candidate Batesian mimics and models among all

438

Western Palearctic snakes. We found that Batesian mimicry is a likely defensive strategy for

439

8% of non-venomous snakes. Although there is still room for methodological improvement,

440

DNNs already allow to measure resemblance while accounting for the high dimensionality of

441

visual phenotypes, simultaneously accounting for colors, texture, patterns and proportions,

442

and without requiring standardized data that may be difficult to collect for many species.

443

Through their ability to detect imperfect and non-conspicuous mimicry, DNNs further avoids

444

the bias of many mimicry studies towards colorful and spectacularly resembling species.

445

Finally, beyond mimicry, the ability to handle complex visual patterns makes DNNs highly

446

promising tools for modeling resemblance in general, with broad perspectives of applications

447

in evolution and ecology.
