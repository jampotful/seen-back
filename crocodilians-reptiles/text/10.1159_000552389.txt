# Annulated Surprise during Cataract Surgery: A Case Report

## Abstract
AbstractIntroductionWe report a pediatric case in which a retrolenticular Armillifer armillatus infestation mimicked a white cataract.Case PresentationA ten-year-old girl presented with a white cataract of her right eye during a humanitarian mission in the Democratic Republic of Congo. Fundoscopy was inaccessible. During surgery, an annulated worm-like parasite was found in the retrolenticular space. Further anamnesis was remarkable for regular snake and crocodile meat consumption, and a clinical diagnosis of ocular pentastomiasis was made. The parasite was morphologically and molecularly identified as A. armillatus. After surgery, fundoscopy showed diffuse retinal atrophy and the vision did unfortunately not improve. Human pentastomiasis is a rare, yet emerging zoonosis in rural tropical areas. Consumption of undercooked, infected snake (or rarely other reptile) meat may lead to visceral pentastomiasis, with uncommon ophthalmological involvement. Ocular pentastomiasis can be a devastating cause of vision loss in endemic regions.ConclusionThis case illustrates that a retrolenticular location of the parasite was likely responsible for a white cataract in an otherwise quiet, white eye, highlighting intraocular pentastomiasis as a potentially underdiagnosed cause of vision loss in endemic regions.

## Introduction
Pentastomids are a group of obligatory parasites residing as adults in the upper respiratory tract of terrestrial carnivorous vertebrates, in many cases reptiles. Visceral pentastomiasis is an often-overlooked zoonotic disease caused by the larval stage of these parasites in which humans serve as intermediate, dead-end hosts. Almost 90% of human infections are caused by Armillifer armillatus, utilizing large snakes as final hosts, or Linguatula serrata, harbored by dogs as final hosts, each with their own geographical distribution (tropical Africa and Asia for Armillifer spp. and temperate regions for L. serrata) [1, 2].
Infection occurs by ingestion of respiratory or enteral secretions from the final host containing infective ova. Once ingested, the larvae hatch in the intestinal tract and invade the viscera. Common sites include the peritoneum, mesentery, liver, spleen, and pleura [1, 3]. Human pentastomiasis usually remains asymptomatic. However, severe cases may occur, ranging from a self-limited nasopharyngitis in linguatuliasis to B symptoms, chronic cough, abdominal pain, or even death in Armillifer infections [4, 5].
Ocular involvement has also been described for both linguatuliasis and Armillifer infections, although uncommon. With a decreasing frequency the parasite has been found in the anterior chamber, the ocular adnexa, and the posterior segment [3].

## Case Presentation
A 10-year-old girl presented to our clinic in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). She reported a painful, red right eye 1 year before presentation, followed by progressive vision loss. The pain was described as dull, with no history of trauma or apparent infection. Visual function of the right eye was limited to light localization in all four visual field quadrants, and no relative afferent pupillary defect was present. Best corrected visual acuity of the left eye was 1.0. Slit-lamp examination of the right eye was unremarkable apart from a white cataract (shown in Fig. 1). The intraocular tension was normal. Fundoscopy of the right eye was impossible, with no access to a B-scan or indirect ophthalmoscopy. Slit-lamp examination and fundoscopy of the left eye were unremarkable.
Preoperative image: right white cataract in an otherwise quiet, white eye. Credits: Dr. Richard Hardi.
Differential diagnoses of leukocoria in children include conditions such as retinoblastoma and persistent fetal vasculature. In this case, the patient’s age, the history of previously normal visual acuity until 1 year prior to presentation, and the presence of a dense white cataract on slit-lamp examination made these diagnoses less likely, and the right eye was setup for cataract surgery. After performing capsulorhexis and total aspiration of the soft, non-calcified lens, a foreign body approximately 9 mm in length with an annulated structure was discovered beneath the macroscopically intact posterior capsule. The foreign body was located in the retrolenticular space, embedded in a mildly vascularized, white, cotton-like material (shown in Fig. 2). A clinical diagnosis of intraocular pentastomiasis due to an Armillifer parasite was made, and after opening of the posterior capsule using Vannas scissors, the parasite was removed using viscoexpression and forceps (shown in Fig. 3). After removal of the parasite, the procedure was completed without vitreous manipulation or intraocular lens implantation. Given the opened posterior capsule and the limited availability of appropriate lens options (only 3-piece IOLs), primary implantation was deferred.

The postoperative examination showed diffuse retinal atrophy, and visual improvement could not be achieved. The patient was subsequently referred to a pediatrician to exclude systemic pentastomiasis, with abdominal ultrasound performed to detect any cysts and plain radiography to identify any possible calcified parasites. In the absence of serological tests for pentastomiasis, diagnosis generally relies on morphological identification or histopathology. The results of this evaluation remain unavailable, and it is unclear whether systemic antiparasitic therapy was attempted, as the patient presented toward the end of the humanitarian mission, precluding follow-up [6].
The parasite was sent for morphological and molecular analysis to the Bernhard Nocht Institute for Tropical Medicine (Hamburg, Germany). The organism was identified as Armillifer armillatus by having 20 body annulations and by positive pentastomid-PCR and sequencing, thus confirming the diagnosis of ocular Armillifer pentastomiasis.

## Discussion

## Signs and Symptoms
Ocular pentastomiasis can cause a broad range of signs and symptoms depending on the affected site. A case series focusing on ophthalmological pentastomiasis in the DRC described 19 cases of unilateral ocular pentastomiasis. In most cases, the parasite resided in the ocular adnexa, with no permanent symptoms reported after extraction. In cases of intraocular involvement, however, the parasite may cause retinal detachment, iritis, and secondary glaucoma, thus significantly limiting visual prognosis even after surgery [3, 7]. In the case discussed above, we describe a white cataract concealing an intravitreal pentastomid for the first time.

## Cataract Formation and Retinal Atrophy
The exact cause of cataract formation in this case remains unknown and is likely multifactorial. Possible mechanisms include a mechanical cause, in which pressure on the posterior capsule can disorganize the lens fibers as postulated in eyes with ocular toxocariasis [7]. While no macroscopical defects were observed, microtrauma caused by the parasites hooks or movements could disrupt the integrity of the posterior capsule, leading to an influx of aqueous, causing an intumescent cataract. Another possible cause is a change in the lens metabolism due to chronic intraocular inflammation [8].
The diffuse retinal atrophy may have resulted from direct toxic or migratory damage by the parasite, similar to diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis [9]. Furthermore, the chronic inflammation may lead to retinal fibrosis, atrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium and arterial narrowing, compromising the blood supply to the retina [10].

## Mode of Infection and Prevention
Human infection with Armillifer pentastomids may occur when infective ova shed by snake feces and excretions into the environment are ingested. Another way of human infection is by eating undercooked snake meat contaminated by pentastomid ova. In the case described here, the patient admitted eating snake meat on a regular basis. A survey conducted on snakes sold for human consumption in the Kole District (DRC) showed that 87.5% of Bitis vipers (n = 40) and 92.3% of pythons (n = 13) were infected with Armillifer spp. [2]. The water used for washing kitchen utensils used during preparation of the snake meat is frequently recycled, with subsequent items also being washed in the same water. Additionally, the discarded snake remains are often disposed of in the garden further spreading ova.
Prevention should focus on increased awareness regarding foodborne illnesses and prioritize hygiene practices when interacting with snakes and snake-related meat or items, including thorough hand washing after handling snakes. Consumption of undercooked or raw snake meat should be avoided, and special attention should be paid to how waste is discarded. In developed countries, pet owners, veterinarians, and zookeepers can possibly be also infected [11].

## Treatment
Human pentastomiasis usually remains asymptomatic and no definitive treatment has been proposed thus far. Suggested options range from the use of ivermectin, praziquantel, or mebendazole to traditional Chinese medicine [12, 13] without good evidence. Exploratory laparotomy in patients suffering from acute abdominal symptoms allows not only the diagnosis of pentastomiasis but also an improvement of the patient’s condition after removal of parasites [1]. Regarding ocular pentastomiasis, prompt surgical removal is advisable for preventing further organ damage as the nymphs remain mobile and keep on feeding on the contents of the eye.

## Conclusion
Ocular pentastomiasis can be a devastating cause of vision loss. This case illustrates, for the first time, a white cataract concealing an intraocular pentastomid larva. Our report underscores intraocular pentastomiasis as a possibly underdiagnosed etiology of cataracts or vision loss in general in parasite-endemic regions. Informing human populations at risk about this disease and subsequent complications, as well as promoting food and meal preparation hygiene, might prevent some cases of blindness.
