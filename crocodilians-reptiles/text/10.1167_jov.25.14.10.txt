Do you see what I see? Linking involuntary nonretinal (phantom) vision and mental imagery in aphantasia.
Keogh R, Kay L, Meagher C, Pearson J. (2025). Journal of vision. . doi:10.1167/jov.25.14.10. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Do you see what I see? Linking involuntary nonretinal (phantom) vision and mental imagery in aphantasia

## Abstract
Recent theories propose that, like endogenous and exogenous visual attention, voluntary and involuntary forms of phantom vision (e.g., mental imagery and dreams) are related and hence depend on overlapping mechanisms. However, the relationship between voluntary and involuntary phantom vision remains largely unknown. Here, we assess this relationship by examining how voluntary visual imagery relates to involuntary forms of phantom vision (specifically, visual illusions) in a unique population with no voluntary visual imagery (aphantasia). In our first study, we presented individuals with aphantasia with seven different visual illusions (Hermann grid, Ponzo illusion, Kanizsa triangles, Ebbinghaus illusion, watercolor effect, neon color-spreading, and rotating snakes). Compared to both a large group of undergraduates and an age-matched control sample, the only illusion in which individuals with aphantasia reported a significant reduction was the neon color illusion. In a large online follow-up study, we used the method of adjustment to obtain a more precise measure of the neon color-spreading illusion in individuals with aphantasia and those with visual imagery. We found that this measure of neon color was lower in those with aphantasia than in those with visual imagery, as were their subjective ratings of the illusion. Importantly, there were no differences between the groups for catch/mock neon color “illusion” trials or a separate color adjustment task. Together, these data provide evidence that individuals with aphantasia experience the neon color illusion at a lower intensity, supporting the hypothesis that some forms of voluntary and involuntary phantom vision depend on overlapping mechanisms.

## Introduction
Mental imagery, synesthesia, dreams, hallucinations, perceptual filling-in, and many illusions are conscious visual experiences without a corresponding retinal stimulus, which can be referred to as phantom vision (Pearson & Westbrook, 2015). Multiple forms of phantom vision can range from having some relation to incoming sensory stimuli (e.g., illusions, perceptual filling-in, or misperceptions) to those that are completely unrelated or unconstrained by incoming retinal stimulation (e.g., hallucinations and dreams) (Pearson et al., 2016; Rogers, Keogh, & Pearson, 2021). Phantom vision can also be thought of as occurring on a spectrum from highly volitional (e.g., visual imagery) to highly involuntary (e.g., illusions and dreams).
The relationship between these forms of phantom vision has been of interest to many researchers (Pearson & Westbrook, 2015). Some research has shown that patients with Parkinson's disease who experience visual hallucinations are also more likely to report misperceptions when viewing potentially ambiguous stimuli (Shine, Halliday, Carlos, Naismith, & Lewis, 2012). A link between visual hallucinations and pareidolia (specifically, seeing faces in visual noise) has also been found in these individuals (Mamiya et al., 2016; Yokoi et al., 2014). Additionally, patients with Parkinson's disease who have visual hallucinations have stronger and more vivid visual imagery than those individuals without hallucinations (Shine et al., 2015). There is also evidence that individuals with schizophrenia and schizotypal personality disorder have more vivid visual imagery than controls (Marothi & Keri, 2018; Sack, van de Ven, Etschenberg, Schatz, & Linden, 2005). Work has also linked voluntary visual imagery with intrusive memories or flashbacks in posttraumatic stress disorder (Bryant & Harvey, 1996; Morina, Leibold, & Ehring, 2013).
Research in nonclinical populations has also found a link between the vividness of visual imagery and some forms of phantom vision. For example, one study found that individuals with more vivid visual imagery were more likely to report detecting a Gabor patch in noise than those with weaker imagery (Dijkstra, Mazor, Kok, & Fleming, 2021). Another study found that individuals with more vivid visual imagery were also more likely to report seeing faces in visual noise (pareidolia) (Salge, Pollmann, & Reeder, 2021). Taken together, these studies of clinical and nonclinical populations suggest that various forms of voluntary and involuntary phantom vision are related, suggesting potential shared mechanisms. Neuroimaging work has demonstrated that visual imagery can engage low-level sensory regions (Dijkstra, Bosch, & van Gerven, 2019; Pearson, 2019), highlighting some potential neural overlap with visual illusions. However, a recent study found that while both visual imagery and the neon color illusion could be decoded from early visual cortex, imagery was decodable from deep layers of V1 while neon color was decodable from mostly superficial layers (Bergmann et al., 2024). Studies investigating the experience of phantom vision in individuals without visual imagery would provide stronger evidence for a shared mechanism underlying different forms of phantom vision (Pearson & Westbrook, 2015).
Since the 1800s, we have known that some individuals report entirely lacking visual imagery (Galton, 1880). It was not until 2015 that a name was given to this phenomenon: aphantasia (Zeman, Dewar, & Della Sala, 2015). Since the naming of aphantasia, there has been an explosion of interest in this unique population (Keogh, Pearson, & Zeman, 2021). Our research has shown that the self-reported lack of visual imagery in this population is also reflected in several different tasks. Multiple studies have used binocular rivalry to measure imagery. Binocular rivalry is an illusion in which two different images are presented, one to each eye, but only one is perceived. Studies have shown that when people who report having imagery imagine an image prior to viewing binocular rivalry the congruent image is more likely to dominate perception. When individuals with aphantasia complete this task, there is evidence of a lack of priming in the binocular rivalry imagery paradigm (Keogh & Pearson, 2018, Keogh & Pearson, 2024). Several physiological measures have also been used to assess the lack of imagery abilities in individuals with aphantasia. In one study, participants with aphantasia read fear-inducing stories while their skin conductance levels were measured. While individuals with imagery showed a skin conductance response to reading, the group with aphantasia showed no skin conductance response to imagined fearful stories (Wicken, Keogh, & Pearson, 2021). Another study assessed the pupillary light response to imagined images. Studies have shown that when individuals imagined images of different brightnesses, their pupils constricted and dilated in response to these imagined images (Kay, Keogh, Andrillon, & Pearson, 2022; Laeng & Sulutvedt, 2014). Interestingly, when individuals with aphantasia attempted to imagine stimuli of different brightness, there was a lack of a pupillary light response (Kay et al., 2022). Another study assessed motor responses to imagining and viewing hand movements in aphantasia. Interestingly, individuals with aphantasia lacked a motor evoked potential (MEP) response to both voluntarily imaging movement and viewing movement (Dupont, Papaxanthis, Madden-Lombardi, & Lebon, 2024).
Some preliminary research into the experience of phantom vision in aphantasia has been conducted. Questionnaire studies have shown that individuals with aphantasia report dreaming less frequently than control populations (Dawes, Keogh, Andrillon, & Pearson, 2020; Milton et al., 2021; Zeman et al., 2020). In addition, individuals with aphantasia report having fewer visual intrusions of past traumatic events (Dawes et al., 2020). An exploratory report found that individuals with aphantasia were less likely to endorse that they had seen images when looking at a full computer screen of flickering light (Konigsmark, Bergmann, & Reeder, 2021). It should be noted that these studies have relied on subjective reports and lack any catch trials and, as such, may potentially be measuring response biases.
In addition, these studies all investigated highly unconstrained involuntary forms of phantom vision (dreams, hallucinations, and intrusive memories/flashbacks). If all forms of phantom vision are related, we might expect that individuals with aphantasia would also exhibit a reduction in the experience of more constrained forms of phantom vision, such as visual illusions. Here, we explored the experience of visual illusions in individuals with aphantasia. First, an exploratory study was run to assess whether there was any evidence of differences in the experience of several different illusions in a sample of individuals with aphantasia. Here, we compared the experience of 16 individuals with aphantasia with that of 16 age-matched controls. Additionally, as this was an exploratory study, we wanted to ensure that there were similar reporting levels in a more representative, larger sample to ensure our results were not being purely driven by our small sample.

## Experiment 1—Qualitative illusions

## Participants
In total, 169 undergraduate participants (mean age 19.49, range 18–38) completed this study for course credit. Participants had a vividness of visual imagery questionnaire (VVIQ) mean score of 58.29 (SD = 10.31, range 33–80; see Supplementary Figure S2). These participants completed this study for course credit.
Sixteen individuals with self-diagnosed aphantasia (mean age 31.06, range 18–51) completed this task with $20 AUD compensation for their time. These participants had contacted the lab wanting to participate in research on aphantasia. All participants self-identified as having aphantasia and had VVIQ scores of 32 or less (mean VVIQ score 17.88, range 16–23; see Supplementary Figure S2 for VVIQ scores).
Sixteen age-matched controls (mean age 32.88, range 18–60) were recruited from the undergraduate sample, plus friends and relatives of the individuals with aphantasia. All participants identified as having imagery (VVIQ mean 66.87, range 45–80). Participants were paid $20 AUD for their participation in this study.

## Exclusion criteria
Participants in the undergraduate group were excluded from the study if they scored less than 33 on the VVIQ. Participants were also excluded if they answered any of the catch/foil questions incorrectly (see supplementary materials for a full list of questions asked).

## Apparatus
This study was completed on participants’ own computers online using Qualtrics.

## Stimuli
This task was exploratory, and we chose seven different visual illusions. We aimed to use illusions that spanned multiple types of phantom vision (e.g., form, size, color, and motion). The illusions viewed were the following: Hermann grid, Ponzo illusion, Kanizsa triangles, Ebbinghaus illusion, watercolor effect, neon color spreading, and rotating snakes.

## Procedure
Participants were presented with seven different illusions (once each) and first asked to describe what they saw (open text responses), and then they were asked a series of questions about each illusion, one of which was related to the illusion itself and intended to ascertain whether participants experienced the illusions. The rest of the questions were catch trials that were not important to the illusion. For example, for the Herman grid, the critical illusion question was, “I see dots in this image” (yes or no response), and a catch question was, “Do you see any colors in this image? (Click all that apply).” See Supplementary Materials for a full list of questions for each of the illusions. The study was self-paced with no timing. The critical questions for each of the illusions are presented in Table 1.
Critical questions for each illusion.
These questions were included for two reasons: (a) to identify and exclude anyone who was not reading/performing the questionnaire faithfully (e.g., if any color other than white, black, or gray was chosen in the above catch question, this participant was excluded) and (b) to attempt to obscure what exactly the individuals were supposed to see in the illusion and ensure participants were not just saying yes or no to everything or showing a response bias.
The open-text responses were included as a general check. The lead author read over each response to ensure the participant was paying attention (i.e., it was used to exclude any participants who did not write a sensible response, e.g., if random words or letters were typed in this section).

## Discussion
This exploratory study found evidence that individuals with aphantasia reported experiencing the neon color illusion less than both age-matched controls and the large undergraduate sample. There was also evidence that individuals with aphantasia reported seeing the rotating snakes illusion less than the large undergraduate sample, but not age-matched controls, suggesting this difference was driven by the age of participants. This aligns with previous work showing that older participants are less likely to experience the rotating snakes illusion (Billino, Hamburger, & Gegenfurtner, 2009).
Taken together, these findings suggest that the neon color illusion was the only illusion that individuals with aphantasia consistently reported experiencing less than people with visual imagery. Clearly, this study had several limitations due to its exploratory nature. First, we relied on participants’ responses to a singular question about the illusion to assess whether they experienced it. Second, we had a relatively small sample of participants in our aphantasia group. Lastly, there were no catch trials to rule out differences in response biases between the groups. It should be noted, however, that the individuals with aphantasia did not universally report experiencing all the illusions less frequently than all the control groups, which one might expect if our findings were driven by response biases. To address these shortcomings, we ran a second experiment with a large online sample of individuals with aphantasia, using a method of adjustment task that included catch trials.
In addition to the neon color illusion, we ran a study that aimed to assess another form of nonretinal color representation in individuals with aphantasia.
Previous work has shown that when individuals see grayscale images that have an implied or expected color (e.g., a gray banana), the implied color of these objects can be decoded from classifiers trained on real color (Teichmann, Grootswagers, Carlson, & Rich, 2019), and this decoding has also been shown to occur in low-level visual regions of the brain (Bannert & Bartels, 2013; Vandenbroucke, Fahrenfort, Meuwese, Scholte, & Lamme, 2016). Further, it has been shown that when individuals are presented with a color diagnostic object (e.g., banana) and asked to adjust that image until it appears gray, they adjust the color to the opponent color (e.g., a blue/purple banana) (Hansen, Olkkonen, Walter, & Gegenfurtner, 2006; Olkkonen, Hansen, & Gegenfurtner, 2008), suggesting a form of nonretinal phantom color. Taken together, these studies support the idea that when individuals view grayscale images with an expected color, there is a color experience (or phantom vision). Alternatively, it has been argued that any observed effects of color diagnostic images are driven by semantic or nonsensory associations. If it is the case that this effect is due to phantom color vision, we should expect to see a reduction in the color adjustment effect. However, if this is purely semantic or it relies on different mechanisms of imagery in early visual areas, then there should be no difference between the two groups. To investigate this, we ran an online color adjustment task in addition to the neon color adjustment task with individuals with aphantasia.

## Experiment 2—Neon color spreading illusion and color diagnostic images experiment

## Participants
Due to this task being online, we had several exclusion criteria. First, if participants did not complete this task on a computer (e.g., completed the study on a phone or tablet), they were excluded. To ensure participants engaged in the task and understood the task in the color diagnostic adjustment task, we excluded people who did not move the slider more than 5 points, as this would mean the fruit was still colored (i.e., not gray), no matter the type of monitor on an individual's computer. As the task was to adjust the image until it was gray, this would indicate participants not understanding the task or that the color on their screen was not appropriate. If participants did not complete the questionnaires or stopped the task before completing it, they were excluded from the study. Control participants were excluded if they had VVIQ scores lower than 32, and aphantasic participants were removed if they had VVIQ scores above 32. The range of VVIQ scores for both groups are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.
In total, 163 individuals with aphantasia participated in this study. All participants were part of our Future Minds Lab Aphantasia Database and were contacted by email. Participants were reimbursed by being able to enter a gift card lottery, which was drawn at the end of the data collection ($200 AUD). Four participants were removed for using iOS on a handheld device, and 11 were removed for not moving the banana/broccoli slider past five values from the starting point. Fifteen people were removed for not completing the questionnaires, and four were removed for not completing all the tasks. Five were removed for scores higher than 32 on the VVIQ, resulting in a total of 124 aphantasic participants (mean age 40.67, range 18–72, 64 females, 58 males, 1 nonbinary [assigned female at birth], and 1 undisclosed) and a VVIQ mean of 17.75 (see Supplementary Figure S3).
In total, 157 control participants were recruited from the undergraduate pool at the University of New South Wales and MTurk. University students were given course credit for participating, and MTurk participants were given $4.50 USD (1/2 hour). Thirty-one participants were removed for not moving the banana/broccoli slider past five values from the starting point, and 5 were removed from the study completely for scores less than 32 on the VVIQ, resulting in a total of 121 control participants (mean age 31.41, range 18–72, 73 females, 47 males, and 1 undisclosed) and a VVIQ mean of 60.93 (see Supplementary Figure S1).

## Apparatus
This study was completed on participants’ own computers online using Qualtrics (for the demographic questions) and Inquisit 5 (Millisecond) for the adjustment tasks.

## Stimuli

## Neon color illusion
The neon color illusion comprised four sets of concentric circles. In half of the trials, the concentric circles were white and presented on a black background. In the other half of the trials, the concentric circles were black and presented on a white background. The reason for the white and black background was that during piloting, some individuals reported they experienced a greater neon color effect on a white versus a black background, while others reported the opposite. For this reason, we decided to include both options.
For the neon color illusion, a quarter of each of the circles was colored in either green or red to form an illusory square (see Figure 2A). For the catch/mock trials, the stimuli were the same, except the concentric circles were rotated so that their colored elements did not form an illusory square, and an actual colored square (i.e., colored pixels) was added in place of where the illusory one would be (see Supplementary Figure S1 for an example of mock stimuli). The neon color illusion and mock stimuli were presented on the left side of the computer screen. On the right-hand side, a colored square (red or green) matching the hue of the illusory square was presented. Participants were able to adjust the saturation of the square on the right-hand side of the screen by pressing the up and down arrows. On each trial, the right-hand square began at a random saturation level between 20% and 66.6%.

## Color diagnostic images
Photos of a grayscale broccoli and banana were used as the neutral color diagnostic images. The saturation of these colors was applied offline in 1% increments using Adobe Illustrator. The colors were saturated with either congruent (green for the broccoli and yellow for the banana) or incongruent/opponent (red for the broccoli and purple for the banana) colors. The beginning saturation level began at a random saturation between 17% and 20% (three possible increments), counterbalanced between congruent and incongruent colors.

## Procedure

## Neon color method of adjustment task
Participants were first presented with the neon color illusion (see Figure 2A) on the left and were asked to adjust the saturation of the colored square on the right until they felt it matched the saturation of the illusory square. Once they felt the colors matched, the participant pressed the space bar. Following this, a slider scale appeared on the right-hand side, replacing the colored square. Participants were asked to adjust the slider scale to subjectively rate how intense they thought the illusory square in the neon color illusion was. See the supplementary materials for the exact wording of the explanation of this task.

## Color diagnostic image adjustment task
Participants were presented with an image (broccoli or banana) that was either congruently colored (e.g., green broccoli) or colored in the opponent color (e.g., red broccoli). Participants were instructed to adjust the color of the image (using the up and down arrows) until the image appeared to them to be a neutral gray color. See the supplementary materials for the exact wording of the explanation of this task.
Participants completed the two tasks in a blocked counterbalanced fashion. The study was self-paced with no timing. Participants first completed four neon color practice trials, and once this practice was completed, three blocks of each study were run, with the neon color adjustment task always being completed first. For the first block, participants completed 30 white and 30 black background illusion tasks (48 real and 12 catch/mock trials), followed by 8 color diagnostic adjustment trials (4 banana and 4 broccoli, half of which began with the congruent color and half being incongruently colored). For the last two blocks, participants completed 40 neon color adjustment tasks (20 black, 20 white, 32 real, and 8 catch/mock) and 8 color diagnostic adjustment trials (4 banana and 4 broccoli, half of which began with the congruent color and half being incongruently colored). This resulted in participants completing a total of 140 neon color trials (70 black background, 70 white background; 112 real and 28 catch/mock trials) and 24 color diagnostic trials (12 banana, 12 broccoli; 12 beginning congruently and 12 incongruently).

## Results—Neon color spreading illusion
Although the age range of our two participant groups was the same, the mean age of the aphantasic group was significantly older than the control group (mean age 40.67 and 31.42, respectively), so age was added as a covariate for all following analyses. Figure 2B shows the method of adjustment results for the control (gray bars) and individuals with aphantasia (teal bars) for the neon color spreading illusion, while Figure 2C shows the results for the mock neon color image. A 2 (Condition: illusion vs. mock) × 2 (Background: white vs. black) × 2 (Group: aphantasia vs. control) repeated-measures analysis of covariance was run on the method of adjustment values. There was a significant interaction between condition and group when controlling for age, F(1, 242) = 25.372, p < 0.001, as well as a significant interaction between background color and group, F(1, 242) = 18.576, p < 0.001. The three-way interaction between group, condition, and background was nonsignificant, F(1, 242) = 3.884, p = 0.050. Post hoc analysis using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons found that individuals with aphantasia reported the color of the neon color illusion as being less bright than the control participants in both the white and black background conditions (both p < 0.005) but not in the mock condition (both p > 0.999).
Figure 2D shows the subjective intensity rating for the control (gray bars) and individuals with aphantasia (teal bars) for the neon color illusion, while Figure 2E shows the results for the mock neon color image. A 2 (Condition: real illusion vs. mock) × 2 (Background: white vs. black) × 2 (Group: aphantasia vs. control) repeated-measures analysis of covariance was run on the subjective ratings. There was a significant interaction between condition and group, F(1, 242) = 28.110, p < 0.001, and a significant interaction between the background color and group, F(1, 242) = 5.300, p = 0.022. There was no significant three-way interaction between group, condition, and background, F(1, 242) = 0.247, p = 0.620. Post hoc analysis using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons found that individuals with aphantasia reported the color of the real neon color illusion as being less bright than the control participants in both the white and black background conditions (both p < 0.001) but not in the mock condition (both p = 0.999).

## Results—Color diagnostic images
To assess whether the implied color of an object affected color adjustment differentially in aphantasic and control participants, a 2 (Starting position: congruent vs. incongruent) × 2 (Fruit: banana vs. broccoli) × 2 (Group: aphantasia vs. control) repeated-measures analysis of covariance (age added as a covariate) was run on the color adjustment end values. There was a significant three-way interaction between starting position, fruit, and group, F(1, 242) = 9.533, p = 0.002, see Figure 3. Post hoc analysis using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons showed there were no differences between the two groups for any of the conditions (all p > 0.415). All other comparisons were significant, except for the aphantasia group's incongruent broccoli condition, which was not significantly different from the incongruent banana condition for the aphantasia group.

## General Discussion
Here we found that individuals with aphantasia reported experiencing the neon color illusion less intensely than their imager counterparts in two separate studies. Importantly, this difference is unlikely to be due to a general response bias, as those with aphantasia did not report experiencing any other illusions significantly less when controlling for age (Experiment 1). Additionally, in Experiment 2, there were no significant group differences in the method of adjustment results for the mock neon color stimuli or for the color diagnostic experiment between the aphantasic and control participants. Taken together, these findings suggest that it is unlikely that the reduced experience of the neon color illusion can be explained purely by response biases.
These findings, that the neon color illusion is experienced less intensely in individuals with aphantasia, suggest that visual imagery, or the mechanisms underlying it, may play a part in the experience of the neon color illusion. This provides novel evidence in support of theories proposing that some forms of voluntary and involuntary phantom vision are related and likely depend on overlapping mechanisms (Pearson & Westbrook, 2015). Another possibility is that an underlying process involved in both imagery and the neon color illusion is impacted in individuals with aphantasia.
It has previously been suggested that the experience of perceptual filling-in, such as in the case of the neon color spreading illusion, is a combination of feed-forward and feed-back connections in low-level color regions of the brain (Devinck & Knoblauch, 2019; Gerardin, et al., 2018). Recent work using laminar functional magnetic resonance imaging has found that while the content of visual color imagery and the neon color illusion can both be decoded from V1, there seems to be a difference in which layers represent this information (Bergmann et al., 2024). It may be that in individuals with aphantasia, the feed-forward connections that give rise to the illusion are intact, but the feedback connections that lead to this illusion may be impaired or reduced in individuals with aphantasia. A recent study also found evidence of reduced feedback of auditory information in the early visual cortex in individuals with aphantasia when compared to blind and sighted individuals with visual imagery (Montabes de la Cruz et al., 2024). These findings suggest that aphantasia may be characterized by impaired or reduced visual feedback connections in the brain, and as such, any illusion or cognitive function that is dependent on these connections may be impacted by having aphantasia. This may be reflected in previous findings showing that more unconstrained forms of phantom vision, such as dreaming and hallucinations, which rely more on top-down mechanisms, are reduced in aphantasia. While nighttime dreaming frequency is reduced in aphantasia as a group, some individuals with aphantasia still report having dreams. It could be the case that the type of aphantasia an individual has might be related to the experience of phantom vision. It has been shown that, in addition to reduced visual imagery, as a group, individuals with aphantasia report reduced imagery in other modalities (Dance, Ward, & Simner, 2021; Dawes et al., 2020; Takahashi et al., 2023). However, this reduction in other imagery modalities is not universal, with a cluster study finding that around 25% of participants with aphantasia report not having imagery in any modality (multisensory aphantasia) while around 30% of participants fall into having only visual aphantasia (i.e., they have retained imagery in at least one other modality) (Dawes, Keogh, & Pearson, 2024). It could be the case that the neural mechanisms underlying aphantasia differ for those who have multisensory aphantasia versus visual aphantasia. Future studies could assess whether individuals with multi-ensory aphantasia show greater reductions in functions that are heavily reliant on top-down connections.
Why was the neon color illusion the only illusion that showed a reduction in the aphantasia group? It might be the case that only color filling-in illusions are related to imagery, but it should be noted that another filling-in illusion was included in Experiment 1: the watercolor illusion. For this illusion, we did not see a significant difference between the aphantasia and control groups, but for all groups, the illusion was only rarely reported (only 38% of the large cohort of undergraduate students reported seeing the illusion; see Figure 1). It may be that in more controlled settings, with fixed viewing distances and monitor settings, the illusion may be stronger and, therefore, result in a difference between the two groups. In other words, we may have seen a floor effect for the watercolor illusion in Experiment 1, potentially due to suboptimal conditions such as computer monitor luminance, which may impact the experience of the watercolor illusion (Gerardin, Dojat, Knoblauch, & Devinck, 2018). Future studies should aim to map out whether all filling-in illusions, when properly controlled for, are reduced in aphantasic individuals or whether this is only specific to the neon color illusion. Additionally, future studies should map out this pattern of results and whether it extends to the full range of phantom vision, like apparent or phantom motion.
Taken together, these findings suggest that there is a link between voluntary and some forms of involuntary phantom vision, much like endogenous and exogenous visual attention.

## Figure Figure 1.: Qualitative illusion results. Bar graphs show the proportion of undergraduate (dark gray), aphantasic (teal), and age-matched control (light gray) participants who reported seeing each of the seven visual illusions. Bars represent mean values for each group, and error bars represent ± SEM.

## Figure Figure 2.: Online experimental procedures. (A) Neon color spreading illusions method of adjustment (MOA) experiment. Participants were presented with either a real or mock neon color spreading illusion, which could be red or green, and presented on a white (left) or black (right) background, on the left-hand side of fixation. A red or green square was presented on the right-hand side of fixation, and participants adjusted the brightness of this image until it matched the brightness of the perceived color in the real or mock neon color image. After this, participants were asked to indicate how intense the color in the neon color images was using a slider. (B) Data show the MOA values for the aphantasia (teal) and control (light gray) groups when viewing real neon color illusions. (C) Data show the MOA values for the aphantasia (teal) and control (light gray) groups when viewing the mock neon color illusions. (D) Data show the subjective intensity ratings for the aphantasia (teal) and control (light gray) groups when viewing real neon color illusions. (E) Data show the subjective intensity ratings for the aphantasia (teal) and control (light gray) groups when viewing mock neon color illusions. For all plots, bars represent mean values for each group, and error bars represent ±SEM. Each dot represents the data of an individual participant.

## Figure Figure 3.: Color diagnostic fruit adjustment experiment. Participants were presented with a fruit that was congruent (i.e., yellow banana or green broccoli) or the incongruent orthogonal color (i.e., purple banana or red broccoli), and they were instructed to change the image (by pressing the up and down arrows) until the image appeared to be gray. The gray line indicates a completely gray image; values above the line indicate the image is saturated with the congruent color, while values below the line indicate the image is saturated with the incongruent color. Boxplots represent aphantasia (teal) and control (gray) group data. For all boxplots, whiskers show min to max values, and the box shows the interquartile range and median. Each point represents an individual participant's data.
