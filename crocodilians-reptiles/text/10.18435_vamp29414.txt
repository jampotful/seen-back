Abstract: The American alligator responds to physical threats with a suite of behaviors including the defensive tail swipe, a lateral and vertical sweep of the tail. Despite the large size of the alligator tail, which accounts
for roughly half the length and a quarter of the mass of the entire animal, the tail swipe had a mean duration
of only 0.2 s, about the duration of a human eye blink. During this interval the posterior tip of a sub-adult
(about 2 m in length) alligator’s tail may be displaced over a meter, resulting in high terminal velocity values
(mean = 10.1 m/s) and, high impact force values (mean = 1.2 kN). Kinematically, the defensive tail swipe
consists of two phases; an initial phase during which the base of the tail is elevated vertically, and a terminal
phase during which the posterior (laterally compressed) portion of the tail is accelerated. The magnitude of
displacement of both phases is variable, as is the site of maximal vertical elevation along the length of the tail.
The initial elevation phase may be a counter-torque strategy for head strikes; a functional role in aiming or
ground clearance of the posterior tip is also possible. A better understanding of the kinematics of the alligator
tail may provide insight into the interpretation of tail structures in other archosaurs.

INTRODUCTION
Archosaurs (herein dinosaurs, birds, and crocodylians) have
a wide variety of caudal vertebral, and/or tail, morphologies (Hone et al. 2021). The diversity of tail spines, clubs,
or blades described from dinosaurs (e.g., Mayr et al. 2002;
Arbour and Zanno 2018; Kareem et al. 2024) has led to
renewed consideration of the behavioral use of these tail
appendages (e.g., Sereno et al. 2022; Bertozzo et al. 2025).
Examination of the mechanics of lateral tail sweeping can
also be applied to the slender elongate tails of sauropods
(e.g., Myhrvold and Currie 1997; Lategano et al. 2024).
In extant non-avian archosaurs (crocodylians), the tail is
dragged passively during terrestrial locomotion (Willey et
al. 2004), but during aquatic locomotion it generates propulsion through lateral undulation (Fish 1984), and is used
to regulate buoyancy (Uriona and Farmer 2008; Grigg and

Krishner 2024). While describing crocodylian foraging,
Gadow (1901:447) noted that “[s]ome species are said to
make use of their powerful tails for hitting the victim and
even jerking it into the mouth.” Though incidences of
caudal foraging appear to be quite rare, tail swipes are a
frequently described portion of the crocodylian defensive
repertoire; in alligators, the greatest magnitudes of tail
displacement and speed occur during defensive swipes with
the tail. Though anecdotes and numerous videos of the
alligator tail swipe exist, the kinematics of this defensive
behavior have not been previously described. The present
study, like all previous reports, is restricted to the alligator’s
defensive use of the tail while on land; the defensive behavior of swimming or submerged crocodylians has received
little attention.

the total length of the animal, a proportion that decreases

tail, there are transitions in cross-sectional area, vertebral,
and muscular features (Mallison et al. 2015). The large
cross-sectional area and musculature in the tail base of
A. mississippiensis, combined with its relatively modest

kinematics of the tail (but see Philen and Neu 2011; Fish et
al. 2021); in particular, the influence of these longitudinal
differences in tail structure on the mechanics of tail swipes
has not been examined.
The present study was undertaken to describe the basic
kinematic features of the defensive tail swipe in the
American alligator. The exploration of these kinematics
may inform the ongoing exploration of tail form and function in the extinct archosaurs.

MATERIAL AND METHODS
Live Animals

Six sub-adult (157–192 cm total length, 11.8–24.2 kg
mass) Alligator mississippiensis were obtained from the
Louisiana Department of Wildlife and Fisheries. The
alligators were housed communally in a 29 m2 facility that
featured three submerging ponds, natural light, and artificial lights on a 12:12 cycle. The facility was maintained at
30–33° C; warm water rain showers were provided every
20 minutes, which helped maintain the facility at > 75%
relative humidity. The alligators were maintained on a diet
of previously frozen adult rats. The husbandry and use
of the live alligators followed all relevant guidelines and
regulations, and were approved by the IACUC of A.T. Still
University (Protocol #226, approved March 2022). The
authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Data Collection

An individual alligator had its mouth taped shut, then
was placed in an isolated 3 x 3 m enclosure. A digital video
camera (Action Camera, YI Technology) was positioned
overhead; a second digital video camera was positioned on
the floor perpendicular to the trunk of the alligator. Both
cameras recorded at 30 frames/s. The same researcher elicited
all of the tail swipes. This was done by crawling on all fours
to approach the unrestrained alligator. Care had to be taken
to avoid the rapid (and forceful) head strikes. The researcher
would extend a hand-held force pad sensor (Quelingsport,
Sibo Technology) as a visual “target” for the alligator; the
sensor had a mean distance to the alligator of 0.34 m (s.d. =
0.11). If this visual target failed to elicit a tail swipe, the re-

search would use light tactile contact (normally at midbody)
which consistently induced a response. To avoid overstimulation of the alligator, only one tail swipe was recorded at a
time, and the animal was given one hour of quiet isolation
between behavioral trials. A total of 107 tail swipes were
recorded; during 28 of these trials there was “good” contact
with the force pad (swipes were excluded if the tail made
only glancing contact with the force pad, or if the force
pad moved before or during tail contact). The recorded tail
swipes were roughly evenly divided among the six alligators.

Analysis

The video records were exported into kinovea (kinovea.
org) for quantification. Using kinovea the tail was divided
into 10 segments of equal length, then the center of each
segment tracked frame-by-frame through the defensive
strike. The resulting data were exported to Excel for integration with the impact forces and final analysis.

RESULTS
The Alligator Tail

For the present study, the tail was defined as including
all of the axial tissue posterior to the midpoint of the
acetabular plane. The six alligators used for this study had
tail lengths of 89–112 cm, which corresponded to a range
of 51.4–54.0% of the alligator’s total body length. The alligator tail is effectively bipartite, the anterior “base” being
roughly circular in cross-section while the posterior end is
laterally compressed (Fig. 1). Herein, the transition point
between the two regions is defined as the spot where the
dorsal “keel” of the posterior portion divides into two low
diverging ridges (Fig. 1). On the alligators examined in this
study, this transition point occurred at 53.8–58.0% of the
total length of the tail (measured from the anterior base).

Kinematics of the Defensive Tail Swipe

The defensive tail swipes were rapid events; the longest duration of a swipe (defined from first movement to
contact) was 0.3 s, the mean duration was 0.2 s. When the
total displacement of each segment was quantified during the swipe (Fig. 2), three key features were evident: 1)
despite variation in the initial position of the tail, the basic
kinematics of the swipe were fairly consistent; 2) the individual and mean curves all had an upward deflection point
(indicating increased displacement) that occurred proximal
(anterior) to the transition point on the tail; 3) the anterior
portion of the tail moved more when tracked in the lateral
perspective, the displacement observed from the lateral
perspective was greater throughout the swipe (Fig. 2).

tail in the anterior-posterior and medial-lateral axes (anterior-posterior was defined as the X-axis, and medial-lateral

Impact Force of the Defensive Tail Swipe

The defensive tail swipes impacted the force pad with a
mean force of 1.19 kN (s.d. = 1.0 kN). There was a positive
relationship between the terminal velocity of the tail tip
during the strike and the impact force (Fig. 10); though
this relationship explained little of the variation in impact force recorded (R2 = 0.28). During the defensive tail
swipes, the initial elevation of the tail had no clear relationship with the tail impact force (Fig. 11).

DISCUSSION
Though commonly referenced (e.g., Greene 1988),
crocodylian defensive tail swipes do not appear to have
been previously studied. The present study was limited by
three factors: 1) all of the alligators studied were sub-adults,
so care must be taken extrapolating the reported findings to
larger animals; 2) the tail swipes were recorded at standard
(30 fps) rates rather than using high-speed, this introduces
some uncertainty in the reported velocities; and 3) while
the hand-held force sensor provided a good target for the
alligator; any inaccuracy in the sensor was compounded by
movements of the researcher holding it.
The defensive tail swipe is remarkable in that it occurs
in approximately 0.2 s, covering a distance of over 0.7
meters. As is evident in the kinematic traces, the tail tip is
generally accelerated throughout the tail swipe (notice the
increasing separation of the terminal tail segments in Figure
4.) The alligator tail tip had a mean terminal velocity of
12.8 m/s during the defensive swipes; multiple trials were
determined to have terminal velocities over 20 m/s (Fig. 8).
This terminal velocity is faster than most snake strikes (e.g.,
Penning et al. 2016) but slower than venom spitting in
cobras (Westhoff et al. 2010). On a more phylogenetically
relevant comparison, the peak velocities recorded during
the defensive tail swipe of A. mississippiensis are similar
(though less than) those reported from simulations of dinosaur tail swipes (Conti et al. 2022).
The mean force of impact recorded during the defensive
tail swipes was 1.2 kN. This is less than the impact forces
of colliding bighorn sheep (Huang et al. 2017) or Olympic

The impact force of the alligator tail is comparable to the
snapping turtle bite force (ranged from 0.008–1.9 kN)
reported by LaGrange et al. (2023), and similar to the
bite forces reported for similar sized A. mississippiensis by
Erickson et al. (2003).
Recognizing the limitations of our methodology described
above, the results of the present study suggest that the velocity and impact force of the alligator defensive tail swipes
are biologically relevant. This seemingly simple conclusion
is important given the increasing attention being paid to
the biomechanics and behavioral significance of the dinosaur tail (e.g., Arbour and Snively 2009; Conti et al. 2022).
Analyzing the kinematics of the defensive tail swipe
revealed an unexpected finding, the initial elevation of the
tail base. As noted herein, this elevation or arching of the
tail base generally coincided or directly followed a defensive
head strike (Fig. 2). In a frozen frame (Fig. 2B) the alligator is positioned similar to what has been described as the
HOTA (Head Oblique Tail Arched) posture (e.g., Garrick
et al. 1978; Walsh et al. 2022). This is unexpected, since
the HOTA is a posture adopted while the alligator is floating at the surface of water and is a slowly developing social
signal, not the rapid displacement seen in this study.
At the present time, the functional significance of the
initial tail elevation is not clear. Arching the tail may serve
to stretch and/or preload some of the tail musculature (e.g.,
Holzer et al. 2023). If the tail elevation was directly related
to muscle mechanics we would expect greater consistency
in the location of the elevation (Fig. 5) since the musculature of crocodilians is discontinuous along the tail (e.g.,
Mallison et al. 2015), as well as a stronger relationship
between tail elevation and impact force (Fig. 11).

The rapid elevation of the tail base may serve as a countertorque to offset the rapid elevation and lateral displacement of the (very heavy) head of A. mississippiensis. Similar
counter-torques have been described from a variety of
vertebrates (e.g., Massaro et al. 2016; Tingle and Garland
2021). A coupling between the head strike and tail elevation could explain the variation observed in the tail elevation (Fig. 5).
In nature the defensive tail swipe would be performed
over a heterogeneous substrate. Elevating the heavy anterior
end of the tail prior to the arcing acceleration of the posterior tip could minimize friction/obstruction with environmental surface features. The alligator showed clear ability to
“target” the force pad (and/or the researcher) with the tail
during the defensive tail swipes. The current study could
not resolve whether this targeting was completed prior to
the onset of the tail swipe, or whether there were mid-air
adjustments of the posterior portion of the tail to enhance
targeting. In either case, the elevation of the anterior portion of the tail may contribute to the alligator’s ability to
aim the posterior tip.
The results of the present study suggest that defensive
head and tail movements of A. mississippiensis may be, at
least partially, torque coupled. It would be informative to
see the extent to which A. mississippiensis could modify the
trajectory of the tail tip to better track a moving target,
and how that ability might relate to variation in trunk and/
or head mobility (see Molnar et al. 2015; Jagnandan and
Higham 2017). A better understanding of the underlying
biomechanics of the defensive tail swipe in crocodylians has
the potential to broaden our understanding of the behavioral significance of the tail among other archosaurs.
