Endemism and regionalization patterns of squamate reptiles in Amazonia

# Endemism and regionalization patterns of squamate reptiles in Amazonia

## Abstract
AbstractThe world is keeping a close eye on Amazonia, due to its importance as a centre of biodiversity and its essential role in regulating global climate. To ensure the provision of climatic services, landscape and species diversity need protection. However, little is known about Amazonian megadiversity patterns. This lack of information is more acute for squamate reptiles (lizards, snakes, and amphisbaenians). Here we propose a regionalisation and endemism scheme for Amazonian squamate reptiles, based on a taxonomically verified checklist. We recognise 14 areas of endemism and 22 bioregions, determined by range restricted species and exhibiting high spatial heterogeneity. This contrasts species richness patterns, which are apparently defined by spatial homogeneity, without a marked gradient. While there is little support for the river barrier hypothesis in defining the areas of endemism, rivers, together with vegetation, geology, relief, and climatic variables, collectively explain spatial patterns of species composition. Our findings suggest that conservation efforts should include the borders of Amazonia, and create a connected network of conservation areas to protect endemism hotspots harbouring rare and threatened species and to function as a buffer against the expansion of habitat destruction to deeper parts of Amazonia.Subject terms: Biogeography, Biodiversity, Herpetology, Conservation biology

## Introduction
Amazonia is a continental scale mosaic of ecosystems and vegetation types that includes tropical rainforests, deciduous and seasonal forests, flooded areas, savannahs, mangroves, as well as very distinct upland environments like the tepuis1. It harbours the world’s largest and continuous rainforest (about twice the size of the Congo Basin forest; the second largest in the world), encompassing about 7.5 million km2, and harbouring about 25% of the world’s catalogued biodiversity2–4. The detection of regions of high conservation value depends heavily on patterns of species diversity and endemism derived from biogeographic studies5. Thus, without detailed knowledge of how many species occur in Amazonia, and where they are distributed, conservation efforts in the region are doomed to fail6. Moreover, time for the exhaustive description and documentation of the entire Amazonian biotas is quickly running out. As a result, science needs to urgently describe objective biodiversity patterns and their potential explanatory factors, as a means to provide informed priorities for conserving the untapped biodiversity of megadiverse regions of the planet, even in the face of incomplete data5.
The so-called Wallacean shortfall (gaps in our knowledge on the distribution of organisms7) is still large in Amazonia and, consequently, for most groups of taxa, the region remains as ‘terra incognita’ (‘unknown land’)8–13. Although many areas in Amazonia are of difficult access (mostly its northern and western parts), in the last three decades the region has experienced an increase in field sampling, with the establishment of many new regional collections. Data on species diversity across the region has already been collected, but has not been compiled and revised comprehensively. The best way to circumvent the Wallacean shortfall is first to carefully compile and revise available biological material housed in biological collections6,8,14, detect the widest sampling gaps, and then perform planned biodiversity inventories and informed taxonomic revisions15,16. Specifically, biological collections enable us to revise the identification and distribution of species, essential for identifying biogeographical patterns, processes, and regions of high conservation concern5,17. Advances in the use of molecular methods have dramatically improved our ability to uncover cryptic species, suggesting that species richness in Amazonia may be four to five times higher than currently known13. Due to the high rates of destruction of habitats, many of these species are going extinct before we even know they exist18.
Reptiles are one of the most remarkable components of Amazonian biodiversity, having successfully colonised all environments in the region, from the canopy of the tallest trees to lakes, rivers, and open sandy habitats19,20. They play important roles in natural systems, as predators, prey, grazers, seed dispersers, and commensal species, serving as indicators for environmental health21. As a result, reptiles have accumulated a vast diversity of morphological, behavioural, ecological, and life history strategies, earning them a central role as model systems for evolutionary, biogeographical, and ecological research22. In addition, reptiles generally have narrower ranges than other vertebrates, making them good indicators of speciation and biogeographical processes, while also being especially susceptible to anthropogenic threats; therefore representing a group of high conservation concern21–24.
Syntheses and broad analyses on species richness, composition, and endemism patterns using quantitative methods for Amazonian reptiles are scarce even for well-studied tetrapod groups such as birds, mammals, and amphibians25–30. In addition, persistent knowledge gaps on squamate distribution hamper broader analyses and general conclusions on biogeographical patterns of Amazonian terrestrial tetrapods. To illustrate, areas of endemism delimited by river barriers currently represent the most accepted and discussed biogeographical hypothesis for Amazonian tetrapods, despite being criticised based on recent studies29,31. However, beyond river basins and drainage systems, other factors such as topography (e.g., noticeable along the Andes and in the Guiana and Brazilian Shields), distinct geological formations (e.g., sedimentary basins and shields), vegetation structure, historical climatic stability, and current climatic nuances should also be considered to explain the composition and endemism of the region6,23,29–31.
Our study is based on the compilation of a dataset of squamate reptiles distribution for Amazonia, aiming to decrease the Wallacean shortfall in the Neotropics, and performing quantitative analysis of biogeographical patterns for this group in Amazonia. For that, we use a revised point-locality database of squamates plotted in grids with a resolution of 1 × 1° at the 30th degree of latitude, searching for coincident distributional patterns, proposing a testable and comprehensive regionalisation scheme for the richest and most diverse region on the planet, and evaluating the river barrier hypothesis of endemism as a major determinant of biodiversity patterns. In summary, we aim to: (1) identify Bioregions (BRs; geographically defined regions according to the clustering of compositional dissimilarities32,33), (2) Areas of Endemism (AoEs; areas defined by congruent restricted distributions of three or more species, consequently common areas of biotic differentiation23,34 and their phylogenetic structure, (3) the best predictors which could explain the patterns found (environmental variables affecting the distribution of species richness, and the definition of BRs and AoEs33,35; Table 1), and (4) evaluate the conservation status of biogeographic units recognised (BRs and AoEs) by assessing deforestation rate and protection level of each area. Additionally, we assess how protected areas of Amazonia include threatened or poorly known species (assessed as Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, and Data Deficient by IUCN [International Union for Conservation of Nature36]). In this same approach, we also include a dataset of species with five or fewer records (and not evaluated by the IUCN) that, when properly assessed by IUCN, may reveal new additions to the list of threatened or Data Deficient species. To avoid repetitive words along the text, we use abbreviations previously defined in the literature for Bioregions and Areas of Endemism (BRs33 and AoEs29, respectively), as well as well-established abbreviations for the methods applied (Biotic Elements [BEs]37–40; Endemicity Analysis [NDM]29,41–43; Parsimony Analysis of Endemism [PAE]29,44,45.

## Results

## Species richness
Based on the overlap of range polygons, species richness of squamate reptiles varies from seven to 171 species per grid cell, considering all species occurring in Amazonia (780 species), and from zero (absence) to 84 species when only endemics were considered (536 species) (Fig. 1). About 69% of Amazonian squamates are endemic to the region. The highest numbers of species in both datasets were found along the main course of the Amazon River, the middle and lower course of the Madeira and Purus rivers, and in the southern Guiana Shield, while the lowest species richness was recovered along the Eastern Andean foothills (for all squamate taxa), and throughout the borders of Amazonia (for endemics only) (Fig. 1). Species richness and sampling effort based on raw point localities are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

## Species composition and biogeographical regionalisation: Bioregions (BRs)
Based on a presence-absence matrix obtained by overlapping all species ranges onto the gridded area, we calculated the turnover component of beta diversity among grid cells with the Simpson derived pairwise dissimilarity index. Then, we clustered the dissimilarity values among cells with the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) for the identification of bioregions (BRs). We identified 22 BRs (Fig. 2) with the criteria of over 95% of explained similarity and non-increasing silhouette values. Alto Napo-Putumayo) (Fig. 2). A general west–southeast–northeast division of the Amazonian region was then recognised. The validation analysis of the BEs through random subsampling of 10% of the species across 100 iterations revealed high stability (>75% of the cells were consistently classified in the same BR) for nine BRs, moderate stability (50–75%) for 11 bioregions, and lower stability (<50%) for only two BRs (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 2). BR 3. Solimões harbours the highest species richness and the highest number of Amazonian endemic species (n = 282, and n = 173, respectively), while BR 19. Alto Mantaro has the lowest number of species and Amazonian endemics (n = 3 each) (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 2). BR 1. ## Areas of Endemism (AoEs) and phylogenetic endemism
Fourteen AoEs were recognised (Fig. 3), based on continuous and overlapped grid cells of the areas recovered in at least two methods (Biotic Elements [BE], Endemicity Analysis [NDM], and/or Parsimony Analysis of Endemism [PAE]; Supplementary Methods). Four AoEs were mostly restricted to the Guiana Shield (1. NE Guiana; 2. S Guiana; 3. C Guiana; and 4. NW Guiana); three to Western Amazonia (5. Neblina Mountain; 6. Guaviare; and 11. Caquetá); six to the Andean foothills and adjacent lowland areas (7. Piedemonte; 8. Alto Marañon; 9. Mantaro Valley; 10. Huallaga-Ucayalli; 12. Manu-Urubamba; 13. Alto Mamoré); and one AoE in the South-western Amazonia (14. Guaporé) (Fig. 3). Except for a small portion of the S Guiana AoE, South-eastern Amazonia did not present any AoE.

The AoEs, in general, comprised large areas, but four of them are limited to very specific landscapes and/or environments (5. Neblina Mountain [in the western border between Brazil and Venezuela]; 6. Guaviare, and 7. Piedemonte [both in Colombia]; and 9. Mantaro Valley [in Peru]; Fig. 3). Considering only a visual inspection of AoEs distribution, none was delimited by any interfluve. For example, although the S Guiana AoE is mostly north of the Amazon and east of the Rio Negro, it also harbours areas south of the Amazon west of the Rio Negro; Guaporé AoE has areas on both sides of the Madeira River; and Caquetá AoE is on both sides of the Japurá River. All remaining AoEs are located near or along the headwaters of the main Amazonian rivers, not being delimited by river courses (Fig. 3).
The AoE Alto Marañon harbours the highest number of Amazonian endemic species (n = 193), followed by the AoE C Guiana (n = 112) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 3). The AoEs Mantaro Valley and Guaviare have three and eight Amazonian endemic species, respectively. The highest number of exclusive endemic species is also found in the AoE Alto Marañon (n = 122) but is now followed by the AoE S Guiana (n = 47). The AoEs Mantaro Valley and Guaviare have the lowest number of exclusive endemics (n = 3 each AoE) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 3).
The analysis of phylogenetic structure considering the composition of exclusive endemic species of each AoE showed that five AoEs (Neblina Mt., Mantaro Valley, Caquetá, Alto Mamoré, and Piedemonte) exhibited significant phylogenetic clustering (Mean Pairwise Distance - MPD z-scores < −1.96). It indicates that endemic species in these areas are more closely related than expected by chance, and suggesting in-situ diversification from a limited number of ancestral lineages (Table 2). The remaining AoEs showed phylogenetic structure not significantly different from random expectations. Mean terminal branch lengths, which indicate the average time since the most recent known speciation events for endemic species, varied considerably across AoEs (Table 2). Neblina Mt. exhibited the longest mean terminal branch length (12.83 ± 5.74 Mya), suggesting its endemic species are relatively older or phylogenetically distinct. In contrast, Piedemonte and Alto Mamoré showed the shortest mean terminal branch lengths (3.45 ± 1.89 Mya and 3.62 ± 0.95 Mya, respectively), potentially reflecting more recent speciation events in these areas.

## Predictor variables
To explain the patterns of species richness, BRs, and AoEs, we used a model selection approach by testing all possible combinations of seven predictor variables (128 models), retaining the ones with delta-AICc < 2. Using generalised least squares, we found three top-ranked models (delta-AICc < 2) for predicting species richness in Amazonia when considering all species (Supplementary Data 3). The best model (Akaike weight = 0.32) included vegetation (NDVI), relief roughness, precipitation, geology units, temperature, and river barriers. The standardised effect sizes of each variable (Fig. 4) showed that vegetation had a significant positive effect on species richness (t₆₃₇ = 3.71, p < 0.001), while temperature, precipitation, geology, and relief roughness had significant negative (t | = 2.09–5.20; p < 0.05). River barriers had a non-significant effect on species richness, suggesting that this variable had marginal importance in the best model (t₆₃₇ = 1.66, p = 0.097). For endemic species richness, we found five top-ranked models (delta-AICc < 2) (Supplementary Data 4). The best model (Akaike weight = 0.26) included the same variables as for all species, and the standardised effect sizes showed the same positive and negative effects, except for relief roughness and geology which were not significant for endemic species richness (p > 0.05).

For bioregions (BRs), using a multinomial logistic regression model selection, we identified a single best model (delta-AICc = 0, Akaike weight = 1; Supplementary Data 5). This model included river barriers, climate stability, vegetation, precipitation, temperature, and relief roughness; geology was the only variable absent. Variable importance strongly supported this finding, with all predictors having maximum importance values (1.0), except for geology which showed negligible importance (9.89 × 10−10). The heatmap (z-tests) revealed that these environmental variables had significant but varied effects across different bioregions (Fig. 4), with vegetation and climate stability showing the strongest influence in multiple areas.
For the AoEs, we found two equally supported models (delta-AICc < 0.00002, Akaike weights ~0.5 each; Supplementary Data 6). Both models included geology, vegetation, precipitation, relief roughness, and temperature. Differences between those models were that river barriers appeared in one of the two equally supported models, and climate stability in the other. Variable importance was very high (>0.999) for geology, temperature, vegetation, relief roughness, and precipitation, with climate stability and river barriers showing intermediate importance (~0.5). The heatmap (Fig. 4) showed that the significance and direction of environmental effects varied considerably across different AoEs, with geology, relief roughness, and vegetation having particularly strong influences in multiple areas. Climate stability and river barriers presented a non-significant effect on this, evidencing marginal importance in the best models.

## Conservation status of bioregions (BRs) and Areas of endemism (AoEs)
We found higher deforestation levels in BRs Tocantins-Xingu, Middle Tapajós-Madeira, South-eastern Arc, and South-western Arc (BRs 6, 13, 15, and 20, respectively), all in Southern Amazonia in Brazil and Bolivia, varying from 17.66 to 30.26% of habitat loss (Figs. 5–6; Supplementary Table 2). On the other hand, nine BRs presented 5% or less of deforestation: Western Guiana, Eastern Guiana, Solimões, and Central-western Amazonia (BRs 1–3, 7), in North-eastern and Western Amazonia; Ecuadorian Andes Foothills, Peruvian Amazonas, Cajamarca, Urubamba, and Alto Mantaro (BRs 5, 10, 11, 18, 19), along the Andean foothills or adjacent areas in Ecuador and Peru (Figs. 5–6; Supplementary Table 2). The BRs with the highest levels of protection were Lower Tapajós-Madeira (BR 8), in Central-South Amazonia in Brazil (37.91%), Western Guiana (BR 1) in North-eastern Amazonia in Venezuela, Brazil, and Guyana (35.02%), and Ecuadorian Andes Foothills (BR 5; 32.96%) (Figs. 5–6; Supplementary Table 2). Alto Mayo and Alto Mantaro (BRs 14, 19), also along the Andean foothills, are totally uncovered by protected areas (0% of protection); South-eastern Arc and South-western Arc (BRs 15, 20) have only 1.27% and 8.74% of their areas under protection (Figs. 5–6; Supplementary Table 2). Middle Mamoré and Alto Grande (BRs 21, 22) in South-western Amazonia in Bolivia, and South-eastern Arc (BR 15) in South-eastern Amazonia in Brazil had the higher rates of deforestation inside protected areas (9.49%, 7.99%, and 7.47%, respectively) (Supplementary Table 2). The most threatened BRs, with the highest deforestation rates, at the same time with the lowest levels of protection were South-eastern Arc, Tocantins-Xingu, and South-western Arc (BRs 15, 6, 20), all in southern Amazonia in Brazil and Bolivia (Figs. 5–6; Supplementary Table 2).

AoEs with the largest portion inside protected areas (above 50%) were Neblina Mountain (93.62%), NE Guiana (54.31%), and Caquetá (50.83%) (Figs. 5, 8; Supplementary Table 3). On the other hand, Piedemonte (13.94%) and S Guiana (11.81%) were AoEs with low levels of protection, with Mantaro Valley (in Peru) showing 0% of protection (Figs. 5, 8; Supplementary Table 3). Guaviare and Alto Mamoré showed the highest levels of deforestation inside protected areas (6.0% and 7.37%, respectively), followed by Guaporé (4.23%) (Supplementary Table 3). About 1/3 of the area without any protection in Guaviare, and more than 1/5 of Guaporé were already deforested (34.23% and 23.63%, respectively) (Figs. 5, 8; Supplementary Table 3).
In total, 61 species in Amazonia are listed in the red list of the IUCN as Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, or Near Threatened (n = 914 records), and 69 species as Data Deficient (n = 500 records). Other 125 species have five or fewer records in Amazonia (rare species, n = 313 records) (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables 2–3). The highest number of threatened and DD species were found in the AoE Alto Marañon, with 36 and 19 species, respectively (n = 55 species in total, with n = 728 records) (Figs. 7–8; Supplementary Tables 2–3). However, only 14 threatened species and ten DD species occur in protected areas (n = 77 and 15 records, respectively; 25.45% of the total species) (Figs. 7–8). All threatened species in the AoEs Huallaga-Ucayalli, Manu-Urubamba, S Guiana, and C Guiana are fully unprotected (absent from conservation areas), as well as all DD in Guaporé and NE Guiana (Figs. 7–8; Supplementary Tables 2–3). The AoE C Guiana has the highest number of rare species (≤5 records, and not assessed by the IUCN) (n = 29 species; n = 62 records), with the vast majority of them inside protected areas (86.20% of the species; 82.26% of the records) (Figs. 7–8; Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables 2–3). Six AoEs do not have any threatened species, while five AoEs do not have any DD species (Figs. 7–8; Supplementary Tables 2–3). A summary of deforestation, protected areas, the distribution of the AoEs and BRs, as well as the threatened, Data Deficient, and rare species for each country is in Supplementary Figs. 11–17).

## Discussion
Amazonia harbours the highest richness of terrestrial vertebrates in the world23, and squamate reptiles represent approximately a third of these species46. However, despite the relevance of Amazonia and the role of reptiles on global biodiversity, to our knowledge our study is the first to produce a taxonomically verified checklist of this group for the whole Amazonian region, presenting numbers of this biodiversity: 780 reptiles are known to occur in Amazonia, of which 536 (~69%) are endemic to the region.

## Distinct richness patterns: reptiles tell a different story
The highest species richness was found along the middle course of the Amazon River (also known as Solimões River), in the southern Guiana Shield, and along the middle and lower courses of the Purus and Madeira rivers. This area has high NDVI (potentially high tree cover density and productivity), warm-wet and less seasonal climate, and low relief (flatter landscape) on ancient substrates (old crystalline/consolidated sedimentary terrains). The pattern we found is distinct from those reported for amphibians, birds, and mammals, where the highest number of species was found in the westernmost Amazonia, areas closer to the Andes30,47–51, but similar to those reported by Ribeiro-Júnior & Amaral6 for lizards in the Brazilian Amazonia. Lamoreux et al.52 and Roll et al.23 showed little differences in richness patterns between squamates and other tetrapods at the global level, and in Amazonia, these studies supported the idea that hotspots of species richness were highly correlated among all groups. Differences between our study and the ones mentioned above may be explained by the fact that reptiles were analysed in detail by us and specifically for the region using a database of occurrences built based on an unprecedented effort of gathering and revising specimens (and records) of squamates in Amazonia. All these allowed us to face, test, and present an alternative hypothesis for the distribution of species richness of Amazonian squamates. We showed that reptiles exhibit a particular pattern, and using other terrestrial vertebrates to infer the distribution of reptiles could lead to the omission of relevant areas of high species richness in the region.
Endemic species richness peaked in parts of the south-western and central Amazonia, where warm-wet and high-productivity sectors are more predominant. A progressive decline in richness was found toward Andean foothills and shield margins, where areas become more seasonal, cooler, and productivity drops53–57. Thus, both total and endemic squamate richness are overwhelmingly driven by vegetation cover and warm, humid climates, corroborating the energy-richness hypotheses23,58–62. While relief and geological age modulate total species richness, they do not shape endemic richness at the broad scale. This suggests that, for Amazonian reptiles, energy and moisture gradients set the stage for diversity, with habitat contiguity and substrate age fine-tuning local species pools62,63. The combination of favourable and relatively stable current environmental conditions in those areas acts to create a ‘refugium’ scenario in the region49,64–66.

## Endemism and its hidden signatures
Together with species richness patterns, local endemism also plays a fundamental role in the documentation and interpretation of biodiversity; both metrics being central concepts in studies with a historical imprint of all biological entities47,52,67. Although species richness and endemism richness are mostly congruent in Amazonia, both measures are not necessarily coincident with hotspots of range-restricted species, like the AoEs47,68. Our results confirm this finding, with Amazonia having 14 AoEs for reptiles, but only one in agreement with an area holding high levels of species richness (the S Guiana AoE). All other 13 AoEs are either located in areas presenting the lowest number of species (like the AoEs near or along the Andean foothills in Peru and Bolivia), or in transitional regions between high and low levels of species richness (like the C Guiana AoE). The mismatch between AoEs and richness patterns warns against the use of richness alone as biodiversity metric in priority setting exercises, which could result in neglecting narrow ranged, intrinsically vulnerable and species and lineages.
The distribution of the AoEs for squamates, in general, resembled the pattern presented for birds in Amazonia by Oliveira et al.29. These two groups of tetrapods have been shown to display more congruent endemism patterns with each other than with those of mammals and amphibians52. Regionally, the Guiana Shield houses 56.4% of the total area of the AoEs (four AoEs); seven AoEs are nearby and under the direct influence of the Andes (35.4% of the total area of the AoEs); one is near the border with the Brazilian Shield (~5%); and another one in the relict and isolated Neblina mountain complex (~1%). This means that while ~97.9% of the area of the AoEs for reptiles are somehow associated with the Andean foothills or the Brazilian and Guiana shields, only ~2.1% are located exclusively in the typical Amazonian lowland forest (the Caquetá AoE). Similar patterns of concentration of endemism have also been detected for Neotropical snakes69.
In contrast to species richness patterns, showing peaks along spatially homogenous Amazonian lowlands, the AoEs were confined to areas with higher spatial heterogeneity, central factors for the genesis, accumulation, and maintenance of endemism51,69–71. This is even more clear when we compare the best predictors influencing the delimitation of each AoE belonging to a same major Amazonian region, like the Guiana Shield or the Andean foothills. While the presence of igneous and metamorphic rocks (geology type 1) had a strong effect on delimiting the NE Guiana and C Guiana AoEs, this same variable had very little effect on the other two AoEs (S Guiana, and NW Guiana), with the presence of consolidated sedimentary rocks (geology type 3) in NW Guiana having a higher influence on this AoE. Lower temperatures and vegetation greenness (NDVI) had a strong influence on the Mantaro Valley AoE, while for two neighbour AoEs along the Andean foothills in Peru, higher temperatures and vegetation had major effects in delimiting the Huallaga-Ucayalli and Manu-Urubamba AoEs. Therefore, environmental heterogeneity, defined as the interplay of geology, relief, vegetation, temperature, and precipitation, plays a major role in shaping endemism patterns in Amazonian squamates.
Geology and relief are predictors related to changes that occurred on a deeper timescale, associated with paleo-endemism in the Guiana Shield, as well as in some parts of Andes51,69,72,73. Our analysis of phylogenetic structure supports that, with the top five AoEs with higher in-situ diversification and the oldest endemic species being somehow associated with the Guiana Shield or the northern Amazonian Andean foothills. On the other hand, temperature and vegetation are predictors more associated with neo-endemism in the Neotropics69. Four of the five AoEs with more recent speciation events, involving multiple colonisation events from different lineages, were areas where geology had little effect on delimiting them (Alto Mamoré, Caquetá, Manu-Urubamba, and Huallaga-Ucayalli). Instead, higher influences of vegetation, temperature, and precipitation were found.

## Beyond river barriers
River barriers had the second lowest importance in explaining AoEs patterns (climate stability had the lowest), evidencing little support for the river barrier hypothesis. Consisting of rivers acting to delimit AoEs in Amazonia, this hypothesis was first proposed by Wallace74 based on the distribution of 21 species of primates. The Negro, Amazon, and Madeira rivers were originally used to divide the region into four large areas, each of them having endemic species (four AoEs). The modern interpretation of this hypothesis includes most of the largest tributaries of the Amazon River (e.g., the Tocantins, Xingu, Tapajós, Purus, and Branco rivers), defining each interfluve as a distinct AoE25,28,75–77. The discrepancy between the river barrier hypothesis and the AoEs we found for reptiles should not be seen as surprising. Although it has been used to define AoEs in squamates before19,78–82, the role of rivers in delimiting endemism had never been quantitatively tested considering the whole Amazonia and all squamate species. Santorelli et al.31 tested this hypothesis on a regional scale and showed that for more than 99% of the species belonging to 14 taxonomic groups (including lizards and snakes) the Madeira River could not be used to delimit AoEs. Even for birds, the most studied tetrapod group, rivers failed to define limits to the AoEs or to represent significant biogeographical barriers in the region29.
The rivers only appeared as an important predictor in analyses of species composition patterns. Collectively and equally with all other variables, river barriers contributed to delimit the bioregions of reptiles in Amazonia. However, considering the effect of this predictor on each bioregion separately, river barriers had a strong influence on delimiting only one Bioregion (Eastern Guiana). Large-scale quantitative analyses on species composition in the region were performed for amphibians30 and birds29, and major breaks in spatial turnover were coincident with the Amazon/Solimões and Madeira rivers for amphibians, with the addition of the Negro and Branco rivers for birds. Little agreement is found between the pattern we recovered for reptiles and these studies. Reptiles showed breaks coincident with the Pará River (or Parauaú), the lower Negro River, the Essequibo River, and in part of the middle course of the Amazon River. The hypothesis that the robust width of the lower Amazon River would represent a major break dividing Northern from Southern Amazonia19,29,75,83 was not supported in our analyses. Instead, the Pará River was the boundary between these regions. Also, the limit between Eastern and Western Amazonia for reptiles was gradually transitioning in altitude from one side of the Madeira to the other, not following the course of this river as a break. The landscape dynamism of the Amazon and Madeira river basins over time84,85; the floods favouring dispersion by carrying reptiles from one side of the river to another86; and the transition from dense to open forests (including patches of savannahs in between) along the same bank of the rivers, happening on both sides of these rivers31,87,88, could offer us insights about why the breaks in spatial turnover of reptiles are not coincident with river courses.
Rivers in westernmost Amazonia, along the Andean foothills and adjacent lowlands, were not coincident either with the limits of the bioregions. Actually, for most of the areas in this region river barriers showed a low effect on delimiting them. Instead, bioregions along and near the Andean foothills were defined by heterogeneous, fragmented, and isolated upland landscapes formed by Andean uplift53,89–91. The altitudinal gradients with very distinct vegetation and climatic conditions between lowlands and highlands, and among the inter-Andean valleys (separated physically by higher areas of mountains), provided conditions to generate and maintain not only endemism (as we discussed for the AoEs patterns) but also for groups of species to distinctively occupy this region51,53,55,91–95.

## Conservation priorities in a vulnerable landscape
Effective conservation actions require detailed information about species distribution and the threats affecting them21. However, choosing one concept in biodiversity measurements to consider an area as a priority is the main challenge in conservation efforts47. It is even more demanding when large and complex landscapes like Amazonia are being targeted, where low congruence among biodiversity metrics (AoEs, species composition, or species richness) is recognised. In this case, conservation efforts may consider providing an effective way of minimising actions and costs at the same time they maximise the results48,96. For example, the Amazonian borders have nine of 14 AoEs, and 18 of 22 BRs, while the central portion of Amazonia houses the highest species richness of reptiles, five AoEs, and four BRs. Conservation initiatives focused on creating a connected network of ecological corridors and a mosaic of protected areas along the border would not only protect the areas in this region but also create a buffer zone preventing (or suppressing) major advances of habitat destruction to extend to central areas97–99.
A closer view of deforestation, protected-area coverage, and reptile diversity across the AoEs and BRs shows many contrasts in conservation risk and urgency. Although deforestation in general remains moderately low grouping all AoEs and BRs together (under 10% of total area), several areas already exceed critical thresholds for tropical forest loss100–102. A safe boundary of accumulated deforestation of 10% would allow the original vegetation to recover; between 10 and 20% actions are needed to stop deforestation; beyond 20% deforestation, the vegetation mortality accelerates, causing a domino effect in the region102. Unprotected lands suffer from the worst scenario, with up to 30.6% deforestation, placing them under immediate threat. For example, the AoEs Guaviare and Guaporé, and the BRs Tocantins-Xingu, Middle Tapajós-Madeira, and South-eastern Arc have the highest conservation priority due to intense threat (all accumulated more than 20% habitat loss). Together they combine high deforestation levels with low protected-area coverage, at the same time they house 18 exclusive endemic species, 10 threatened, eight Data Deficient, and 14 rare species (≤5 records). High deforestation rates over those areas place local assemblages at acute risk of habitat loss and warrant urgent protection measures to prevent species extinctions6,102,103.
The AoEs Piedemonte, Huallaga-Ucayalli, and Alto Mamoré, and BRs Alto Napo-Putumayo, Huallaga, Alto Madeira-Beni, Lower Mantaro, South-western Arc, and Middle Mamoré have moderate threat that similarly warrant “middle-ground” attention (10–20% deforestation). These areas house 100 exclusive Amazonian endemics, 28 threatened species, 62 Data-Deficient, and 41 rare species. Based on low levels of protection, we also included the AoEs Alto Marañon and S Guiana, and BRs Morona and Cajamarca in this group. The S Guiana and Alto Marañon AoEs hold the richest squamate fauna among all AoEs (305 species; 150 exclusives; 37 threatened; and 24 rare species), but they count with only 11% of protected areas in the S Guiana AoE, and 23% in Alto Marañon. These highly productive forests in transitional zones6,30 between the Andes and Amazonia (AoEs Alto Marañon, Alto Mamoré, Piedemonte, and Huallaga-Ucayalli; BRs Alto Napo-Putumayo, Huallaga, Alto Madeira-Beni, Lower Mantaro, and Middle Mamoré) and between the Amazonian lowlands and the Guiana and Brazilian shields [AoE S Guiana AoE, and BR South-western Arc]) combine exceptional diversity, increasing habitat loss, and lower levels of protection, making them especially vulnerable to major threats. Calls for new reserves or initiatives of extension of the existing protected areas to encompass remnants in those areas could guarantee habitat protection, as well as safeguard dozens of exclusive, rare, and IUCN-threatened species103,104.
By contrast, the AoEs NE Guiana, C Guiana, NW Guiana, Neblina Mt., Caquetá, and Manu-Urubamba, and the remaining BRs had minimal habitat loss, and either very high levels of protection (e.g., 93% in the Neblina Mt.) or moderate to low coverage of protected areas (e.g., 10.8% in BR Cajamarca). Two exceptions are BRs (Alto Mayo and Alto Mantaro) in Peru, which are fully absent of any protected area. Despite most of the areas mentioned above could be considered well-protected refugia, they can also be considered knowledge gaps. Together they have 171 rare and 77 Data Deficient species. Therefore, even low deforestation rates in those areas can imperil species that little is known about them. Intensified survey efforts should be a priority to resolve the conservation statuses of those species105.
An optimal network of conservation units can be done through international frameworks, such as ‘transboundary conservation areas’106, from the north-western portion of Amazonia in Venezuela and Colombia, along the Ecuadorian and Peruvian Andean foothills, to the Amazonian southern border in Bolivia and Brazil. However, until collaborative governance to create a well-connected system of protected areas along the borders of Amazonia does not happen, each country has to act at the national level. Virtually, all Amazonian countries have national legal systems and specific laws to establish and maintain ecological corridors (e.g., BRASIL107), and all efforts should be made to implement them108. For example, the two AoEs in Colombia (Guaviare and Piedemonte) are under pressure by an interlinked context of land grabbing, coca crops, and cattle ranching109–111, while the BRs and AoEs in Bolivia and Brazil are being cleared for soybean production for world markets, cattle ranching, logging, unofficial roads, and land grabbing6,112–119. Together, Colombia, Bolivia, and Brazil respond to the highest levels of deforestation among all AoEs and BRs in Amazonia (Supplementary Fig. 11–17). Thus, stronger governmental control in the face of the threats imposed by illegal economic forces and groups acting in those countries is urgently needed, especially inside protected areas104,116.
New perspectives and efforts to protect Amazonia can now find support in our study to consider priority areas for squamate reptiles in future initiatives. We presented the regions harbouring the highest species richness, the location of areas of endemism, and the structure and distribution of the reptile communities in the region. We showed how vegetation, geology, relief, and climate influenced the delimitation of those patterns, and the role of the river barrier hypothesis in the definition of priority areas for squamates in Amazonia. We also presented the conservation status of those areas. In this context, the integrative use of endemism, species composition patterns, environmental predictors, and anthropogenic threats becomes a powerful tool for identifying priority regions that combine high irreplaceability (from a biogeographical perspective) with high vulnerability (from a conservation perspective)120. Lastly, but not less important, the results here presented are based on the reliability of our point locality record database, grounded on more than 100,000 verified and unique records, the vast majority obtained from the direct examination of vouchers deposited in many different institutions. This kind of dataset is an invaluable source of information for conservation efforts and biogeography14. Uncovering and drawing distribution patterns based on large-scale and verified datasets allowed us to improve the understanding of the evolutionary history of species and their relationship with the environment5,14,121–123. Given the continental proportions of Amazonia, the large number (and size) of pristine areas, and the recent high rates of species discovery, we can certainly say that the species richness and diversity of squamates are still far from completely known11,14. While we acknowledge certain limitations of the methods employed—particularly the use of minimum convex polygons (MCPs)124,125 to delineate species ranges—this framework provides a transparent, reproducible, and widely adopted approach that enables consistent comparisons across taxa (e.g., IUCN36,126). Although Species Distribution Models (SDMs) could in principle yield finer-scale insights, applying them to all Amazonian species would require species-specific modelling decisions, validation steps, and sufficient occurrence data, which remain unavailable for many narrowly distributed taxa127. Such heterogeneity could introduce additional biases and reduce comparability, making MCPs the most practical and coherent choice for this large-scale synthesys126.
Nevertheless, despite the intricate ecological and evolutionary processes that govern squamate diversity in Amazonia, we believe that the major patterns here presented would not be dramatically modified with the inclusion of new descriptions in future biogeographic studies. Our study and data-driven results provide an important scientific background for acting and conserving the richest, largest, and most diverse block of tropical forests on the globe.

## Methods

## Study area and species dataset
Amazonia was defined based on the vegetation map of South America128,129, with adaptations from MCT130 for pristine vegetation. The map was delimited by a forested matrix (Broadleaf Evergreen Forest) that includes Montane forests east of the Andes, Flooded forests along rivers, and patches of Semi-evergreen forests. Within the limits of the forested area, the following vegetation types are found: Dry Forests; Shrublands; Shrublands to Grassland mosaic; and Grasslands128,129. These limits are highly coincident with the Amazonian region in Olson et al.131, and have been used previously in the literature6,29,132–135 (Supplementary Fig. 4).
Taxonomy and species lists follow the catalogue of living reptile species (Reptile Database136), including for lizards the currently recognised subspecies and the new species under description. Records were obtained from examined specimens deposited in herpetological collections, published taxonomic revisions, and species lists (Supplementary Data 2). Specimens directly examined by the authors are listed, with collection acronyms and voucher numbers6,14,132–135. Because amphisbaenia is the most underrepresented squamate group in herpetological collections and the least studied in taxonomic revisions, for this group only we used GBIF distribution records137 to complement the collection and literature records. We gathered all available records, removing duplicates and records without specific localities and/or vouchers. Then, all the GBIF’s coordinates were checked for matching with respective localities or included for localities missing coordinates.
Our dataset includes 100,966 records for 780 species (2327 records of 37 species of amphisbaenids; 30,607 records of 361 species of lizards; 68,032 records of 382 species of snakes); 536 species are Amazonian endemics, with a total of 22,147 records (20 amphisbaenids, with 224 records; 294 lizards, with 15,532 records; and 222 snakes, with 6391 records). All species included in our database have at least one record within the limits of the study area. Endemic taxa were defined as those with ranges fully or largely coincident with the limits of the study area, including taxa with marginal records in transitional areas with surrounding regions (suggesting a possible historical association with a once-continuous Amazonian landscape)138.
We used point records for the endemism analyses, and Minimum Convex Polygons (MCPs) for species richness and species composition patterns (analyses are described below). MCPs were produced using the R package SpeciesGeoCodeR139 to delimit the geographical ranges of species with more than two records (species with one or two records were represented as points, turned in grids with presences).

## Biogeographical analyses
All analyses were conducted within a defined gridded area that encompasses the boundaries of Amazonia. This grid was produced using a cylindrical equal-area Behrmann projection, with a resolution corresponding to 1 × 1° at the 30th degree of latitude69,140. Because the analyses we used produced results sensitive to cell sizes, before the decision we tested cell sizes varying from 0.5° to 2°. A 1° grid map produced less fragmented patterns and at the same time, it presented the best resolution between the sizes tested. In addition, a 1° grid map was also used in other studies with biogeography of Amazonian vertebrates29, allowing reasonable comparisons between the patterns found for vertebrates.
We mapped species richness by overlaying the ranges of species across the gridded area, where any overlap between a species range and a grid cell was considered a presence in that cell, without applying any overlap threshold. This process was conducted in two stages: the first included all squamate species, and the second included only Amazonian endemic species, thereby assessing endemic species richness.
We analysed patterns in species composition through the identification of bioregions (BRs)30 for squamate reptiles. We produced a presence-absence matrix with all species by overlaying species ranges onto the gridded area. From this matrix, we calculated the turnover component of beta diversity among grid cells with the Simpson-derived pairwise dissimilarity index141. The turnover metric as measured here is less influenced by variation in species richness and is more reflective of patterns of endemism, vicariance, and regionalisation32. We then used the recluster R package142 to set the optimal cut-off value for clustering grid cells. We used the UPGMA to cluster the cells based on the turnover values, due to its good performance in clustering dissimilarity matrices32. UPGMA is a hierarchical agglomerative clustering method that sequentially merges clusters based on the mean distance between all pairs of objects in different clusters142,143. To minimise the variation in the clustering related to equal turnover values among cells or the presence of zeros, recluster produces several trees (default n = 50) by reordering the areas (grid-cells) in the presence-absence matrix142 and generating a consensus dendrogram. We selected the number of clusters that reached at least 95% of explained dissimilarity144 and in which silhouette values and width values became stable30. Finally, we mapped the delimited BRs onto the gridded areas and displayed the corresponding consensus dendrogram. To assess the statistical robustness of the bioregionalization, we implemented a species subsampling validation approach. We created 100 subsampled datasets by randomly removing 10% of species, then reran the bioregionalization procedure for each subsample145. For each grid cell, we calculated a stability score representing how consistently it stayed clustered with its original bioregion members across subsamples. Bioregion-level stability was calculated as the average stability of all cells within each bioregion.
For endemism patterns, we applied three biogeographical methods, using the dataset of endemic species: Biotic Elements Analysis (BEs), Endemicity Analysis (NDM), and Parsimony Analysis of Endemicity (PAE). Biotic Element Analysis was performed using the prabclus R package37,38. This analysis aims to identify groups of species with distribution ranges that are more similar to each other than to those of other groups39, while also testing for non-random congruence among these ranges. Notably, exact overlap in species ranges is not a requirement, allowing for a gradient in species richness within each biotic element. We followed Azevedo et al.40 in utilising the geco coefficient146, which is particularly designed for biotic element analysis to measure dissimilarity among grid cells from the presence-absence matrix. We tested for the non-random congruence of species ranges by comparing the observed inter-range distances to those expected under a null model of random distribution147,148, while keeping the range sizes of the species fixed in the test simulation since we were using species ranges instead of locality records. We then clustered the dissimilarity matrix using UPGMA via the hprabclust function149, which identifies clusters of species rather than clusters of grid cells, differing from the bioregion analyses. Smaller clusters are considered part of the noise component and are not assigned to any biotic element. We used the default values for the threshold to define clusters (cutdist = 0.4), and for the minimum cluster size (nnout = 2), which aligns with the criteria for cluster formation used in NDM and PAE40. We then mapped each biotic element by overlaying the ranges of the classified species and including only cells with more than two species of the respective biotic element. Lastly, we applied a similar approach to validate the Bioregions for our detected Biotic Elements, measuring how consistently species from the same original BE clustered together across the 100 rounds of subsampling 10% of the species.
The NDM analysis was performed in VNDM41–43 using the dataset of endemic species. Imprecisions of georeferenced points (‘observed presences’) and adjacent empty cells that could be assumed presences (‘assumed presences’) are intuitively absent in our analysis because species ranges were used to run the model, and biases due to incomplete sampling are minimised. Thus, we used ‘observed presences’ = 0% and ‘assumed presences’ = 0%. The search was repeated 100 times, retaining areas with scores equal to or above 2.0 and with two or more endemic species. The results included biogeographic units with a cut-off of at least half of the similarity between endemic species through the ‘consense areas’ = 50%, and comparisons using the flexible criterion of ‘against any of the other areas in the consensus’. We used the endemicity scores as a measure of the robustness of the resulting consense areas.
The PAE was implemented in TNT150,151, using the dataset of endemic species. We used search procedures designed for analysis of large data matrices (‘New Technology search’), with a tree-search of 1000 random-addition sequence trees. Parameters followed Oliveira et al.29 with TBR Branch Swapping retaining 99 trees per replicate and a sequence of 50 cycles of sectorial search through the entire trees (with sectors below 75 taxa analysed through RAS + TBR); tree-drifting with 200 iterations; ratchet with 500 iterations; and 100 rounds of tree-fusing. The biogeographic units were defined based on clades unambiguously supported by at least two non-homoplastic species, identified in the strict consensus tree. We then hierarchically grouped these biogeographic units by visually examining the PAE dendrogram, creating a nested classification of areas44. Bootstrap was calculated using WinClada/Nona with 1000 replications152,153.
Casagranda et al.45 compared the three methods (BEs, NDM, and PAE), presented the strengths and limitations of each approach and highlighted that the use of only one of them could lead to the identification of endemism patterns that would be merely methodological artifacts. Because it would be difficult to distinguish whether the identified endemism could result from properties of the methods154–156, and they are usually applied to address the same problems, here we delimited the final biogeographic units of endemism through the overlap of the results of all approaches (BE, NDM, and PAE), following the qualitative criteria of two or more coincident and continuous areas among methods. The combination of at least two overlapped areas with a distinct logical basis to identify co-occurrence patterns of species aims to produce a rigorous and consistent way to detect the final biogeographic units for the Amazonian squamates, minimising method/approach bias29,40,157. These biogeographic units were defined as areas of congruence of endemism, in short, and for standardised nomenclature purposes named Areas of Endemism (AoEs).
To explore the degree of phylogenetic relatedness of species endemic to each Area of Endemism (AoE), we analysed the phylogenetic structure of the species endemic assemblage using a sample of 100 phylogenies from Tonini et al.158. Missing species described after 2016 were added to the analyses following methods in Tonini et al.158. For each AoE, we calculated the Mean Pairwise Distance (MPD) among its endemic species and compared observed values to null models generated by randomly sampling species from the entire pool of AoE endemic species (999 randomisations)159. This approach produces standardised effect sizes (z-scores) indicating whether species within each AoE are more closely related (clustered, negative z-scores) or more distantly related (overdispersed, positive z-scores) than expected by chance160. We considered z-scores below −1.96 or above 1.96 as statistically significant (p < 0.05). We also calculated mean terminal branch lengths for endemic species in each AoE as a proxy for the timing of speciation events. All analyses were conducted across 100 phylogenetic trees to account for phylogenetic uncertainty, and mean values with standard deviations are reported.

## Predictor variables and model selection
To investigate species richness patterns and the distribution of the AoEs and BRs we selected the following set of seven predictor variables: river barriers; geology; relief roughness; climate stability; vegetation; precipitation; and temperature. These variables were based on previous studies that showed their influence on the distribution and diversity patterns of terrestrial tetrapods in the Neotropics6,29,30,33,35,40. Here we used all those predictors combined in analyses to test for the predictors of species endemism, composition, and richness in Amazonia. Below we describe the predictors and the hypotheses we are testing.
i) River Barrier: Major Amazonian rivers are hypothesised to act as geographic barriers that limit the dispersal of terrestrial vertebrates, thereby contributing significantly to the delimitation of the Areas of Endemism and Bioregions for squamates in Amazonia30,74,79,80. Rivers with higher water discharge and wider channels are expected to represent stronger breaks in reptile spatial turnover28,161. Based on this hypothesis, distinct Areas of Endemism and Bioregions are expected to occur on opposite banks of the major rivers of the region28,30,74,79,80,161. In our study we defined river barriers based on the banks of the largest Amazonian rivers: Tocantins; Xingu; Tapajós; Madeira (Guaporé); Purus; Juruá; Ucayali; Putumayo; Japurá (Caquetá); Negro; Orinoco; Branco; and Essequibo25,30,162 (Supplementary Fig. 5). To measure the degree of isolation of each interfluve, we calculated the sum of annual water discharge (in m3/s) of the rivers that separate each interfluve, weighted by the length of the rivers at the respective interfluves30,163.
ii) Geology: The present-day distribution of reptiles in Amazonia is expected to be structured by chronolithostratigraphic units that reflect the region’s complex geological history. These units, which vary in age, composition, and long-term landscape stability, have likely influenced historical patterns of habitat connectivity, speciation, and endemism49,66. Given that many Amazonian vertebrate taxa, including squamates, exhibit distributions aligned with edaphic and geomorphological discontinuities28,161, chronolithostratigraphic units are considered spatially and temporally explicit predictors of biogeographic structure. Thus, distinct geological units are expected to harbour distinct reptile assemblages, reflecting historical trajectories of isolation, environmental stability, and vicariant processes. We delimited five main chronolithostratigraphic units based on the Geological Map of South America164:
1. Igneous and Metamorphic Rocks (Precambrian – Archean and Proterozoic); >541 million years; ancient crystalline rocks, including gneisses, granites, migmatites, and schists. Major geological units include the Guiana and Brazilian Shields.
2. Consolidated Sedimentary Rocks (Paleozoic – Ordovician to Permian); 541–252 million years; consolidated marine and continental deposits such as sandstones, siltstones, and limestones, formed in ancient seas and river systems. Main geological units include the Amazon and Solimões Basins (e.g., Juruá and Maecuru Formations).
3. Consolidated Sedimentary Rocks (Mesozoic – Triassic to Cretaceous); 252–66 million years; fluvial, lacustrine, and eolian sediments, mainly reddish sandstones and claystones, with some basalt intrusions. Principal units include the Parecis Basin and Alter do Chão Formation.
4. Unconsolidated Sedimentary Rocks (Cenozoic – Paleogene and Neogene); 66–2.6 million years; poorly consolidated sedimentary deposits such as clays, sands, and conglomerates, associated with the evolution of the Amazon River system. Key units include the Solimões and Içá Formations.
5. Surface Deposits (Quaternary – Holocene and Pleistocene); <2.6 million years; recent unconsolidated sediments, including alluvium, fluvial terraces, and organic deposits. Main units occur in the floodplains and terraces of the Amazon, Madeira, and Negro Rivers.
iii) Relief Roughness: Topographic heterogeneity, as captured by relief roughness, is expected to influence reptile diversity and distribution across Amazonia. Areas with greater relief roughness may promote environmental gradients, microhabitat diversity, and reduced landscape permeability, all of which can foster population isolation and, over evolutionary timescales, increase endemism and species turnover165,166. Conversely, low-relief areas may facilitate gene flow and support wider species distributions. Accordingly, relief roughness is hypothesised to be positively associated with beta diversity and the presence of range-restricted reptile species in the region. We calculated relief roughness (the largest elevation difference, in metres, between a focal cell and its immediate neighbours) to characterise topography and terrain heterogeneity167,168.
iv) Climate Stability: Past climate fluctuations have played a key role in shaping current species distributions30,169,170. Areas with higher long-term climatic stability are hypothesised to have functioned as refugia, maintaining suitable microclimates over geological timescales and enabling long-term population persistence and diversification170,171. In contrast, climatically unstable areas may have experienced greater environmental turnover and local extinctions, leading to lower endemism and broader species ranges. Thus, climate stability is expected to be positively associated with reptile endemism and beta diversity. We used the Climate Stability Index (CSI172) as a measure of long-term climate stability in the region. This comprehensive index incorporates climate data spanning from the Pliocene (3.3 Ma) to the present, representing both warm and cold cycles throughout the Pleistocene and Pliocene. This approach captures the dynamic climate history that has shaped Amazonian biodiversity over millions of years.
v) Vegetation: Vegetation structure is expected to influence reptile distributions across Amazonia by modulating habitat structure, microclimatic conditions, and resource availability. Vegetation gradients may also act as environmental filters, favouring species according to habitat specialisation and influencing species composition20,173. Here we measured the vegetation predictor using the Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)174, which estimates the “greenness” of the vegetation and could serve as a proxy for forest cover and productivity.
vi) Temperature and Precipitation: Climatic gradients are expected to shape reptile distribution patterns across Amazonia by influencing physiological tolerances, activity periods, and habitat suitability. Variation along these variables is expected to be associated with species turnover and the presence of climate-restricted endemics, reflecting climatic filtering and niche specialisation across Amazonia’s environmental heterogeneity23,62. In our study, we used climatic variables (temperature and precipitation) derived from the CHELSA v2.1 dataset175,176. To account for collinearity among these variables and to summarise major environmental gradients, we used the first principal component axis (PC1) from Principal Component Analyses (PCA) of bioclimatic temperature and other for bioclimatic precipitation variables. Temperature PC1 (explaining 59.34% of the variance) primarily represents an overall temperature gradient and low seasonality, while precipitation PC1 (explaining 63.7% of the variance) represents a moisture gradient with a more pronounced seasonality component (Supplementary Fig. 6).
We aggregated and resampled all layers to 0.1-degree resolution before calculations. Then, we extracted the median values of each variable for each grid cell in Amazonia to match the resolution of the biogeographical analyses described above, except for geology, for which we extracted the dominant (number of cells) chronolithostratigraphic unit (Supplementary Fig. 7). The selected variables presented variance inflation factors smaller than five, indicating low collinearity problems for the subsequent analyses (river barriers = 1.3920; climate stability = 2.3193; relief roughness = 2.5480; vegetation = 1.3637; precipitation = 1.2758; temperature = 1.4805; geology = 1.2476).
To identify the most relevant variables influencing the distribution and classification of the AoEs and BRs, we performed a model selection of Multinomial Log-linear Models177 of all possible combinations of variables (128 models) using the MuMIn package in R178. We compared the delta-AICc (Akaike’s information criterion corrected for small sample sizes) of all models to rank their importance, penalising for complexity179. We also assessed model importance using the Akaike weights for each model to infer their relative likelihood given the data set. Lastly, we quantified the relative importance of each predictor by calculating the sum of model weights over all models including each explanatory variable in the MuMIn package30,33,180. We displayed z-score coefficients for each variable in a heatmap, where each cell shows the model-averaged coefficient divided by its standard error (z-statistic) for a given AoE or BR. When multiple models had substantial support (ΔAICc < 2), we averaged coefficients across those models and calculated corresponding z-tests to incorporate model-selection uncertainty.
For species richness (a continuous response variable, in contrast with AoEs and BRs), we performed a model selection using Generalised Least Squares (GLS) models against the same set of variables and using the same model selection procedure as above181. To account for spatial autocorrelation, we used the exponential spatial correlation structure, which best explained the spatial structure in our data (lowest AIC in preliminary model selection), and therefore, was applied in the models182. To visualise the effects of environmental variables, we extracted standardised effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals from the best-supported models to illustrate the relative importance and direction of each environmental predictor on both total and endemic species richness.

## Conservation analyses
We examined the level of protection of AoEs and BRs using the National Natural Protected Areas database183, restricted to the boundaries of each AoE and BRs. Only protected areas under national authorities (federal jurisdiction) and categorised as Strict Protected Areas were considered, because they represent areas that do not allow any kind of exploitation, and their statuses are more difficult to change (in theory) by political and regional actions in the future68,103. This category of protected areas is also more important for reptile conservation because this group shows a relatively high sensitivity to habitat loss and disturbance, common in sustainable use protected areas68,184–187.
Based on the intersection of protected areas, AoEs, and BRs, we obtained unique polygons referring to the protected and unprotected portion of each biogeographical unit. We calculated the level of deforestation using the Deforestation Year data from the JRC Tropical Moist Forest188. This database comprises Landsat imagery (30 m resolution) with deforestation measured from 1982 to 2022, recording the year when the forest cover has been deforested for the first time (followed or not by regrowth). After downloading 13 tiles188, we generated a binary mosaic image for the Amazon region, with undisturbed (0) pixels or deforested (1) pixels. We considered deforested all pixels presenting deforestation at any time during the studied period. We then calculated the number of deforested and undisturbed pixels (zonal statistics) for every polygon inside the limits of the AoEs and BRs, obtaining the total deforestation percentage, inside and outside protected areas. To correct the boundaries of the AoEs (those located along the borders, with areas outside the limits of Amazonia) we excluded portions that were not inside the study area before we calculated deforestation and protection rates. For a visual perception of the data, we created maps showing all the layers mentioned above and all the results from different methods here applied using QGIS189.
Additionally, we used three datasets (1. species assessed by the IUCN as Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, and Near Threatened; 2. species assessed by the IUCN as Data Deficient; and 3. species with five or fewer records in Amazonia and not assessed by the IUCN)36 to perform Kernel density analyses, and we overlap the distributional maps with layers of protected areas and AoEs. We aimed to check the coverage of the protected areas over the distribution of threatened species, Data Deficient species, and those with five or fewer records, as well as the representativeness of those patterns inside the AoEs. Kernel analyses were performed in QGIS189, using a two-degree ratio and quartic kernel function. We also produced bubble charts using the rate of deforestation, levels of protection, and size of the geographic units (AoEs and BRs) to have a visual inspection of the status of protection of each AoE and BRs. All codes used in this study are available in Azevedo190.
