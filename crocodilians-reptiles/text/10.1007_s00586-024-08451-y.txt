Abstract
Purpose To explore the impact of the snake-eye appearance (SEA) on the efficacy of anterior cervical discectomy and fusion
(ACDF) in treating Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy (DCM).
Methods A total of 316 Patients were divided into three groups, those with SEA were in the SEA group, and those with the
absence of SEA were in the non-SEA group. Meanwhile, in the non-SEA group, patients with grade 1 or 2 increased signal
intensity (ISI) on T2-weighted MRI were in the ISI group, and the remaining patients without ISI were in the non-ISI group.
The modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association scale (mJOA) and the neurologic functional improvement rate (IR) were
measured. The number of patients who achieved upper-limb motor weakness improvement was recorded and compared.
Propensity score matching (PSM) was used among the three groups.
Results The postoperative JOA and IR were similar in the SEA and non-SEA group; however, they were significantly lower
than in the non-ISI group after PSM(P < 0.05). Besides, only 32.1% of patients in the SEA obtained an improvement of
upper-limb motor weakness, which was the lowest in three groups after PSM (60.7% in the non-SEA group and 78.6% in
the non-ISI group, P < 0.05).
Conclusions We suggested that patients with SEA tend to achieve an inadequate neurological recovery after ACDF. Besides,
a SEA may be an unfavorable prognostic factor for upper extremity motor function recovery.

Introduction
Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy (DCM) is a progressive
spine disease and one of the leading causes of spinal cord
dysfunction. It is characterized by degenerative changes in
the spine that result in compression of the spinal cord and
adjacent structures, leading to long-term disability and significant neurological deficits [1]. The prevalence of DCM
is increasing globally due to the aging population, making
accurate diagnosis and optimal treatment strategies crucial.
The diagnosis of DCM requires a combination of clinical

and imaging findings. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
is a valuable diagnostic tool for DCM as it provides highresolution visualization of neural, osseous, and ligamentous
structures. It not only allows observation of the compressed
spinal cord lesion but also detects changes in signal intensity
within the spinal cord parenchyma [2–5]. The increased signal intensity (ISI) on T2-weighted MRI has been associated
with gray matter changes in the spinal cord and is indicative of severe myelopathy symptoms and poor neurological function recovery following decompression surgery [6,
7]. Recently, ISI has been extensively studied, and various
classification methods have been used in clinical research
[8–10]. Among the different manifestations of ISI, snakeeye appearance (SEA) was firstly described in patients with
DCM using delayed CT-myelography [11, 12]. SEA is not
only observed in DCM due to chronic cord compression but
also found in some cases of cervical spondylotic amyotrophy [13]. It is a unique neuroimaging finding defined as a
bilateral, nearly symmetrical high signal intensity of the

European Spine Journal (2025) 34:78–85

grey matter on axial T2-weighted MRI (Fig. 1). Due to the
small dots representing the high signal intensity located in
the anterior horn, SEA can sometimes be easily overlooked.
Although the underlying mechanisms of SEA are not fully
understood, Mizuno J and colleagues hypothesized that it
may be associated with cystic necrosis caused by mechanical compression and venous infarction. Their autopsy findings also suggested that SEA is an unfavorable prognostic
factor for the recovery of upper-extremity motor weakness
[14].
The natural history of DCM varies widely and is unpredictable in individual patients. However, surgical intervention is generally considered when neurological deficits
progressively worsen. Anterior cervical discectomy and
fusion (ACDF) is a reliable surgical treatment for DCM. It
involves fusion to reconstruct and stabilize the spinal column while restoring cervical alignment. Although the efficacy of ACDF in treating DCM has been well established,
the impact of SEA on clinical outcomes following ACDF
and whether SEA disappears after decompression remain
unclear. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effect of
SEA on neurological function recovery after ACDF.

Methods
Participants: This retrospective study included patients with
DCM who underwent ACDF between June 2016 and February 2020. All patients underwent pre- and postoperative
cervical X-ray and MRI (1.5 Tesla). Inclusion criteria were
as follows: (1) patients underwent anterior cervical surgery
and minimum 2-year follow-up. Exclusion criteria included:
(1)patients who were diagnosed as ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament; (2)patients were with ankylosing
spondylitis, cervical trauma, cervical infection; (3) patients
underwent revision cervical surgery; (4) patients with postoperative MRI showed that the spinal cord of surgical segments was not decompressed adequately. The patients were
divided into three groups: SEA group (patients with snakeeye appearance), non-SEA group (patients with grade 1 or
grade 2 ISI but without snake-eye appearance), and non-ISI
group (patients without ISI, serving as the control group).
SEA was defined as a symmetric small high signal intensity
dot in the anterior horn. Patient characteristics such as age,
sex, BMI, symptomatic duration, surgical level, and C2-7
cobb angle were recorded and compared.
Clinical Parameters and Analysis: At the 2-year followup, MRI was performed to assess the decompression status and observe the SEA signal. The modified Japanese
Orthopaedic Association score (mJOA) was used to assess
improvements in motor function of the upper and lower
limbs. In addition, the mJOA and the neurologic functional

Discussion
Although ACDF can effectively halt the progression of
clinical deficits and improve long-term neurological function in the treatment of DCM, several factors can influence
clinical outcomes. Factors such as longer duration of symptoms, higher severity of preoperative myelopathy, older age,

European Spine Journal (2025) 34:78–85

smoking, and BMI have been shown to impact outcomes [15,
16]. Additionally, preoperative MRI features can also serve
as predictors of clinical outcomes. For example, a larger
number of high signal intensity segments on T2-weighted
images, low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, a combination of T1 and T2 signal intensity changes, and a higher
signal intensity ratio have been associated with poorer neurological function recovery after surgery [17–21]. These
imaging features likely reflect irreversible damage to neural tissue, such as gliosis, myelomalacia, cystic necrosis,
and syrinx formation [4, 22]. Particularly when present at
multiple levels, signal intensity changes suggest cavitation
or necrosis in the spinal cord and are indicative of poorer
surgical outcomes [23]. In our study, despite achieving full
decompression of all surgical segments after ACDF, the
non-ISI group exhibited a significantly higher IR than the
non-SEA group at the 2-year follow-up after PSM. Consistent with previous studies, we suggest that patients with ISI
tend to have inadequate neurological function recovery.
To minimize observation biases in this study, we
employed PSM, resulting in well-balanced baseline characteristics between groups. Although patients with SEA
have limited pathological changes in the spinal cord, some
authors have suggested that these patients may experience
less improvement in the JOA score compared to those with
other ISI patterns. However, we did not find a significant
difference in neurological function improvement between
the SEA group and the non-SEA group. In our opinion, the
status of neurological function recovery is not only related
to preoperative factors but also closely associated with the
adequacy of decompression. In this research, all patients
in both the SEA and non-SEA groups underwent complete
decompression confirmed by MRI at the 2-year follow-up.
Therefore, our results suggest no significant difference in
the improvement rate (IR) between the SEA and non-SEA
groups. Nonetheless, the IR was significantly lower in the
SEA group than in the non-ISI group, both before and after
PSM. Based on these findings, surgeons should engage in
open dialogue with patients whose preoperative MRI shows
SEA. Such patients may experience inadequate neurological function recovery similar to those with preoperative ISI.
Histopathological and clinical studies have extensively
examined the characteristics and significance of ISI. Various classifications have been utilized to categorize ISI and
assess clinical outcomes. Some authors have suggested that
patients with well-defined border ISI have a significantly
poorer prognosis compared to those with ISI showing fuzzy
borders [24]. However, there is limited research exploring
the relationship between SEA and clinical outcomes after
ACDF for DCM. In our study, although the IR in the SEA
group was not as high as in the non-ISI group, the JOA
score in the SEA group significantly increased after ACDF.

Unfortunately, only approximately 32.1% of patients in the
SEA group achieved recovery of upper limb motor function,
which was the lowest among the three groups. This might
be attributed to the fact that SEA is typically observed in
the ventral horns of the spinal cord, which primarily contain lower motor neurons. Thus, the presence of SEA on
T2-weighted MRI may indicate a less favorable recovery of
upper limb motor funtion.
Due to the limited research on SEA, the underlying
pathophysiological concept of SEA remains unclear. However, since its initial report in 1985, SEA is no longer considered a rare phenomenon on MRI. When extending over
several segments, SEA may also manifest as “pencil-like”
hyperintensities on sagittal T2-weighted images [25, 26].
The presence of ISI is often associated with a wide range
of compressive pathologies or severe compression. In our
study, SEA was observed not only at the compressed level
but also at the adjacent caudal level to the most severely
compressed spinal segment in three patients. In addition to
patients with DCM, SEA has been observed in a patient with
progressive posttraumatic myelopathy associated with spinal instability and a teenage girl with spontaneous spinal
ischemia [27, 28]. Autopsy studies have shown that SEA
is associated with cystic degeneration, primarily occurring
in the central portion of the gray matter near the ventrolateral posterior column. Fibrous thickening of small veins
with narrowed lumen surrounding the cystic necrosis has
also been described. This change in small veins is considered a consequence of chronic mechanical compression on
the spinal cord [23]. Nevertheless, whether SEA represents
an irreversible change in the spinal cord remains uncertain
[14]. Although adequate decompression was achieved in our
study, and cervical spine alignment was well reconstructed
after ACDF, SEA-related ISI persisted at the two-year follow-up in all patients in the SEA group (Fig. 2). Thus, unlike
edema, we reached a consensus that SEA-related ISI represents an irreversible change. On the other hand, we did not
observe the evolution of SEA, either in patients with singlesegment SEA or multi-segment SEA. This lesion appeared
to be limited after thorough decompression.
Given that the SEA-related ISI is a unique neuroimaging finding characterized as the nearly symmetrical small
high signal intensity of the spinal parenchyma resembling
the face of a snake on axial T2-weighted MRI, the high
signal intensity in the anterior horns appears as two small
highlighted dots. Previous studies have indicated that the
clinical presentation of SEA can be subtle, and conventional
or dermatomal somatosensory evoked potentials may fail
to detect this type of lesion [29]. Surgeons may inadvertently overlook SEA-related ISI due to carelessness. In the
SEA group, focal SEA was observed on axial T2-weighted
MRI in 21 patients, while SEA-related ISI on both axial and

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the current study. Firstly, this is a single-center retrospective study,
which is inherently subject to biases associated with this
study design. Specifically, the high-resolution MRI systems used may vary in fundamental properties and working
parameters. Additionally, the high-intensity signal in the ISI
group was not categorized. However, the primary aim of
this research was to assess the clinical outcomes after ACDF
in treating DCM with SEA, as the comparison of neurological function recovery with a distinct type of ISI was previously established. Furthermore, the sample size was limited,
and the follow-up period was relatively short, which may
affect the observation of SEA changes on MRI. Therefore,
further studies are warranted to address these limitations.
