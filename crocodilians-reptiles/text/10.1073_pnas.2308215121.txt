# A bifurcation integrates information from many noisy ion channels and allows for milli-Kelvin thermal sensitivity in the snake pit organ

## Abstract
SignificanceMany organisms exhibit exquisite sensitivity to signals which are weak and statistically insignificant at the level of single molecular receptors. To be useful, information from many of these receptors must be integrated into an amplified response. Here, we study a particularly striking example of this information integration, thermosensing in pit vipers. In our minimal model, temperature-dependent channel currents drive a dynamical system poised near a bifurcation. There, the system achieves arbitrarily large amplification, and most of the information in molecular receptors is efficiently transmitted into a collective response even if additional noise corrupts the signal or readout. A simple feedback motif robustly maintains the system close to the bifurcation, potentially suggesting a more common design principle for sensory systems.Keywords: signal amplification and information integration, self-organized criticality, scaling analysis, TRP channels, pit organ
Many biochemical pathways serve primarily to amplify a weak signal—one which is statistically significant only after integrating input from many individually noisy molecular sensors. A striking example of this is the pit organ, used for thermal imaging by infrared sensing snakes (1–4). This organ consists of a small opening near the nostril, which forms the aperture of a pinhole camera (1, 5, 6). Incident radiation heats the pit membrane, a thin (≈10 μm) tissue which lies behind this opening (3). A few thousand sensory neurons project primary trigeminal afferents into the pit membrane (2, 6), where resultant temperature changes are detected and transmitted further downstream as changes in the action potential (AP) frequency (2–4). Snakes use this organ for nocturnal ambush hunting, and they can detect and localize the tiny radiant heating from warm-blooded prey at distances up to ≈1 m (7).
Careful experiments with single nerve fibers combined with thermal modeling (2, 6) suggest that the nerve terminals can reliably detect temperature changes as small as 1 mK. At the same time, more recent experimental evidence has demonstrated that the molecular sensor is a TRPA1 thermo-sensitive ion channel (8). While these channels are among the most sensitive single proteins characterized, they are still three orders of magnitude less sensitive than nerve fibers, opening with increasing temperature according to a sigmoid whose width is about ΔT≈1 K (8). In addition, these sensors must operate in cold-blooded snakes, whose body temperature varies by thousands of milli-Kelvin with ambient changes.
How can the pit organ reliably measure temperature changes with three orders of magnitude higher accuracy than individual channels? An individual TRPA1 channel provides very little information about milli-Kelvin (mK) changes in temperature: It only changes its probability of opening by 0.1%. In principle, it is possible to obtain an arbitrarily more accurate measurement of temperature by averaging over a large number of channels, and/or for a duration much longer than a single channel’s (de)correlation time of opening, τc, over which it cannot make a new, independent measurement. Averaging over N channels for a time τtot allows for Nτtot/τc independent measurements and could thereby reduce the error in a measurement of temperature by a factor of Nτtot/τc, as the variance of the mean decreases like the inverse number of independent measurements. These considerations suggest that a measurement of temperature with mK accuracy must somehow integrate over at least a million individual channel events. These numbers, though impressive, are not necessarily discouraging. While the density of TRPA1 channels in these nerve terminals has not been measured, it is reasonable to think their number might range in the hundreds of thousands to millions. With a correlation time of τc = 1 to 10 ms (9), there is in principle enough information to make mK accurate measurements in 10 to 100 ms.
Still, it remains to be understood how neurons effectively integrate and amplify the information from these many individual channels into a readout in the form of increased firing of action potentials (APs): Even if the required accuracy of 1 mK can be achieved by averaging over a million individual channel events, preserving this information requires that the readout sufficiently amplifies the signal above the readout noise.
In this paper, we propose a model for how this integration and amplification could be carried out mechanistically. In our model, amplification arises from the diverging susceptibility at a dynamical bifurcation. This amplification is driven by positive feedback between individual transient receptor potential (TRP) channels which are known to be sensitive not only to temperature but also to voltage (10–13). We will argue that a generic model for the voltage dynamics of a single terminal nerve mass that incorporates this positive feedback naturally exhibits a dynamical bifurcation. In the vicinity of the bifurcation, the rate of action potential firing can have an arbitrarily steep dependence on temperature, and the statistics of the times between consecutive action potentials contain an order-one fraction of all available information distributed among individual channels. A key prediction of our model is that the coefficient of variation (CV) in the distribution of interspike times is not constant but decreases for larger AP frequency. This trend in the CV is indeed observed in experiments with single nerve fibers of pit vipers (2). Quantitative comparison between our model and these data indicates that in the absence of stimulus the real system is located very close to the bifurcation point. While proximity to a bifurcation point typically requires fine-tuning of parameters, we will show how a simple feedback mechanism from the order parameter, the action potential frequency, onto a control parameter of the bifurcation naturally poises the system near this transition, even when background conditions fluctuate dramatically. While molecular details are likely varied, our proposed feedback mechanism might illustrate a broader design principle for the high sensitivity found in diverse signaling systems made up of many noisy sensors.

## Model and Results
We consider a minimal model for the voltage dynamics of a single terminal nerve mass as illustrated in Fig. 1. Voltage increases (the membrane depolarizes) if cations flow through open TRP channels. We take the opening probability po to be a sigmoid function in membrane potential difference V,

We assume that when a channel is open, a current Ic flows through it.* Together with the voltage-dependent opening probability, this naturally leads to positive feedback: A channel opening event leads to an inward current that raises the voltage across the membrane and thereby increases the opening probability of other channels. This negative feedback term quantifies the effects of active ion transporters, other channels, and passive diffusion along concentration gradients across the membrane (17).

Near this bifurcation, tiny changes in temperature are thus strongly amplified into large changes in the AP frequency.

## Noise Broadens the Bifurcation.
In the absence of noise, our deterministic dynamics predicts an arbitrarily steep relationship between temperature and AP rate near the bifurcation. However, noise in the voltage dynamics is expected to suppress the singular behavior (diverging slope) in AP frequency and to reduce the maximal possible amplification by broadening the bifurcation region (Fig. 2). To get quantitative insight, we consider two broad categories of noise; intrinsic noise, which is driven by the stochastic nature of the individually noisy molecular sensors, and extrinsic noise which includes all other sources. Since the intrinsic noise arises from the stochastic opening of TRPA1 channels, we take it to be binomial in the number N of these channels, with opening probability po and a correlation time of τc. Extrinsic noise is likely dominated by the stochastic opening of other channels as well as thermal charge and heat fluctuations that provide correlated noise to all parts of the terminal nerve mass. We model this extrinsic noise phenomenologically via a characteristic correlation time τe and strength Ie and assume for now that all noise processes are faster than other dynamic features. J(α) thus quantifies how efficiently information about temperature available in the channels is passed down to the level of AP firing. The expression for the information rate, Eq. 13, provides a lower bound on the actual information rate: Higher-order terms stemming from changes in the n≥ 2-th cumulants/moments of the interspike time tAP can only increase the information but are expected to be small. Including the second-order term changes the information rate by <1%; see SI Appendix, section S4. Since in our scheme, information about the signal (temperature) influences the AP timings only via the membrane voltage, the information rate ι˙ contained in the full voltage dynamics V(t) provides an upper bound on I˙, and we indeed numerically find that the information fidelity J(α)≤1; see Fig. 3.
That being said, Eq. 13 shows that information about the signal is transferred efficiently by the proposed signaling system: The available information ι˙ enters linearly and a substantial fraction of it (roughly 2/3 if J(α)=Jmax is maximized over α) is captured by the timing of action potentials; see Fig. 3. More generally, since iN∼N, the information rate scales like ∼N/(1+Nm/Next), and extrinsic noise starts to become relevant for Nm≳Next. This argument provides a rough estimate for the optimal number of channels that is as small as possible but simultaneously maximizes the information that can be obtained about changes in temperature: Nopt∼ρIc2τc/(Ie2τe).
As shown in Fig. 3, the information fidelity is small in the irregular regime where information about temperature is transduced into small changes in the membrane potential, but without meaningfully altering the AP frequency which is near 0. In contrast, in the regular regime, it saturates for α≳1 at a value of 2/3. In this regime, the variance in the frequency of APs is extremely low, Fig. 3C, and information about changes in the temperature is encoded in tiny changes in the frequency, Fig. 3D2. Reading out this information thus requires extremely precise measurements of the interspike times, and already small noise in the readout of the AP frequencies strongly suppresses the information for large α: If we assume that the variance in the interspike times is not only due to noise in the voltage dynamics/the firing of APs but also due to the fact that the interspike times cannot be read out precisely, we can effectively replace the variance in the interspike times by VartAP→VartAP+protAP2 for a readout noise that scales with the average time between two APs, Fig. 3B. Since VartAP is very small away from the bifurcation in the regular regime, this additional contribution to the variance strongly increases the overall variance there and the information fidelity J(α)=M′(α)2/(M(α)S(α)+proM(α)2) develops a distinct maximum close to the bifurcation, whose position only slightly depends on the strength of the noise. (Similar results are also obtained for a constant, extrinsic readout noise VartAP→VartAP+σro2, for which J(α)=M′(α)2/(M(α)S(α)+σro2/τs2); see SI Appendix, section S8). Taken together, the accessible information is highest for α≈0, suggesting that poising the system in the vicinity of the bifurcation is desirable from an information point of view and for being able to sense small changes in the signal.

## Order-Parameter Feedback.
If changes in the signal always needed to be measured with respect to the same background signal (temperature) and in conditions where the underlying physical properties such as the membrane capacitance or resistance or the channel characteristics are mostly constant, one could imagine that, e.g., evolutionary processes had fine-tuned these properties so as to poise the voltage dynamics at the transition. However, snakes live in deserts, where the ambient background temperature can vary quite considerably on the channels’ natural scale of milli-Kelvin. Membrane channel properties like the conductance are furthermore known to depend on various cellular factors like pip lipids in the membrane or intracellular calcium (13, 25–29), which are affected by numerous intracellular processes. It thus seems impossible to achieve proximity to the bifurcation by simply fine-tuning certain channel and membrane properties.
Inspired by early work on self-organized criticality (30, 31) and recent studies extending these ideas to discontinuous transitions in the framework of so-called self-organized bistability (32, 33), here, we propose a specific type of feedback motif, which robustly tunes the dynamical system arbitrarily close to the transition—without the need for fine-tuning. The idea is that in systems with a critical or bifurcation point where an order parameter, O, exhibits singular behavior, i.e., a diverging susceptibility, with respect to the control parameter, C, feedback from the order onto the control parameter, which can be as simple as dC/dt=signdO/dCγ−βO, acts to maintain the system in the vicinity of this critical point, Fig. 4B. Due to the diverging susceptibility at the critical or bifurcation point (bif), dO/dCbif→∞, a small change in the control parameter triggers a large change in the order parameter and, conversely, a change ΔO∗ in the fixed point O∗=γ/β of the feedback affects the control parameter only marginally, ΔC∗∼ΔO∗(dO/dC)−1, thus providing robust tuning to the vicinity of the bifurcation.

In our case, the frequency of APs serves as a collective variable with singular behavior at the bifurcation point; see Fig. 2. We implement feedback from the AP frequency onto the control parameter, the voltage at half-maximum V12 or, equivalently, the distance to the bifurcation δV12, by decreasing V12 by d− at rate γ in the phases between two APs and deterministically increasing it by d+ when an AP is fired. This feedback is reminiscent of threshold adaptation in pyramidal cells of the weakly electric fish, where firing of action potentials leads to a shift in the voltage at half maximum (34) and of order-parameter feedback suggested in the context of self-tuned critical oscillations of hair cells (35). Simulating the dynamics with feedback shows the desired adaptation property: When a temperature step T0→T0+δT is applied after the system has equilibrated to the original temperature, the AP frequency changes transiently but then returns to pre-stimulus level γd−/d+; see Fig. 4 and compare with ref. 3. This behavior is similar to the response of the signaling protein CheA in Escherichia coli chemotaxis where a step change in input leads to a transient followed by near-perfect adaptation to the same input-independent steady state (36). Similar kinds of homeostatic (or control-theoretic) mechanisms also appear to be relevant in other contexts ranging from neural plasticity (37–39) and regulation of ion channel conductances (40) to sensitivity in the auditory system (35, 41) and can also be based on derived quantities such as the order-parameter autocorrelation function (42–44). While we currently do not have a precise mechanistic explanation for how order-parameter feedback in snakes could be implemented physiologically, it seems plausible that it is mediated by intra-cellular calcium and pip lipids. In particular, TRP channels are known to be regulated by pip lipids (13, 25–29, 45), which themselves are regulated by calcium dynamics (28, 29) and thereby plausibly depend on the firing of action potentials.

## Comparison to Experimental Data.
How well can our simple model capture experimental data on snake thermosensing? To address this question, we compared our model to published data in ref. 2 for AP firing in single nerve fibers of the trigeminal nerve of pit vipers. The authors not only determined mean firing rates but also the distribution of interspike times, both in the absence of stimulus and also while giving calibrated step changes in incident radiation. Since the CV is a function of α only, the measured CV in the nonstimulated case, CV0≈0.51, allows us to infer the value of α=α0≈0.17 at steady-state. Notably, this value of α0 lies well within the region dominated by the bifurcation, see also Fig. 2A. We then use the mean interspike time in the absence of stimulus f0≈8.4 Hz to deduce τs≈0.023 s.
A key qualitative prediction of our model is that the coefficient of variation should decrease monotonically with AP frequency, as the system moves from the irregular barrier-crossing dominated regime to the regular and roughly periodic regime. This is also a key feature seen in experiments, visible as the narrowing of the histogram (symbols connected by lines) for smaller average interspike time in Fig. 5A, and quantified in Fig. 5A, Inset. The value of τs inferred from the data without stimulation also allows us to make a quantitative prediction for the shape of the CV vs. AP frequency curve, which does not contain any new fit parameters. This predicted curve (line) is compared to the measured CVs in the experiments (dots), demonstrating reasonable agreement in Fig. 5A, Inset. The curve passes through the point with lowest frequency by construction. We observe that our theory consistently underestimates the variance in the distributions after stimulation. One reason for this discrepancy could be that these experimental distributions were measured in adapting systems, and so contain both intrinsic variation at fixed parameters and variation from the time evolution of the bifurcation parameters themselves. The latter is not included in our predicted CVs. Nonetheless, when comparing the measured distribution of interspike times (symbols connected by lines) with the prediction of the distribution of tAP from our model (histograms), we find good agreement; see Fig. 5A.

In order to constrain other model parameters, we turn to a second set of experimental data in ref. 2, namely time courses of the frequency of AP firing while the temperature of the membrane is varied. More specifically, simultaneous fits of the frequency time series for three different rates of temperature increase in the pit membrane (compare figure 4 in ref. 2) are used to determine the scaling a between α and the temperature change δT, α=:aδT+α0, and the timescale for adaptation (see SI Appendix, section S10 for details and Fig. 5C for the fits); we find a=1.67×103 K−1 and as characteristic timescale for adaptation τadapt≈0.23 s.
The observed overall good agreement between the different sets of data and our model lends credence to the model and the inferred parameters, and we use them to estimate the amplification (log-slope) as dlog(fAP)/dT≈1.6×103 K−1. Similarly, we find that the Fisher information rate is given by I˙≈80mK−2s−1 in the absence of any stimulus. This estimate implies that a temperature change of δT=1 mK can be detected statistically significantly against the background in less than 100 ms. Importantly, the fits are to data for single unit preparations (single fibers) and not for the whole nerve. These fits thus indicate that a single fiber can achieve mK sensitivity and roughly 1,600-fold amplification. Note that the estimate for the Fisher information rate is for the case without adaptation and therefore does not take into account that, first, the system adapts relatively quickly and, second, that in the case of feedback, the distance to the bifurcation itself exhibits fluctuations around its steady-state value (Fig. 4C and Discussion). It serves as a reasonable estimate for the true Fisher information rate with adaptation. Importantly, this system seems to achieve mK temperature sensitivity in well under 100 ms.
The inferred phenomenological parameters (a, τadapt) can be used to deduce parameters of our mechanistic model, with a few additional guides from existing literature. We take the width of the sigmoid opening probability as function of temperature and voltage to be ΔV≈30 mV and ΔT≈1 K (8, 11). These numbers and the experimentally inferred value for τs imply that the channel decorrelation time τc should be smaller than 0.8 ms. These experiments also constrain Neff but not its individual contributions. If we take τc=0.7 ms and assume that roughly 1% of the channels are open at the bifurcation, ρ=0.01, we find that the number of channels is of the order of N≈107 and Ic/cmem≈75 V/ms. If we allow the channel decorrelation time τc to be smaller, the inferred values for Ic/cmem and N decrease correspondingly. These findings indicate that either TRP channels in the snake pit organ exhibit very fast decorrelation times τc≪1 ms and/or the organ itself exhibits a particularly small capacitance. It could be that the implied fast channel kinetics reflect evolutionary pressure at the single channel scale toward lower effective noise and hence more reliable sensing. Note that for τc⪆0.26 ms the inferred value of τrest is smaller than τc, suggesting that the system is in the regime where channel noise cannot be considered as white. In order to do the parameter inference, we thus use Eq. 11.

## Discussion
Here, we have presented a plausible model for sensitive thermosensing in snake pit organs. Our model explains how information distributed in many individual ion channels can be read out into an amplified, collective response which can naturally be a thousand times more sensitive than individual channels. In our model, the collective response takes advantage of the diverging susceptibility near a dynamical bifurcation. The system uses a motif inspired by self-organized criticality (30, 31, 46) to maintain itself in the narrow, highly amplified region close to the bifurcation—even while ambient conditions vary over much larger temperature changes, albeit on slower timescales.
Using information theory, we trace how information is lost as it is integrated from many channels into AP firing. We find that proximity to the bifurcation is not needed in principle to preserve information. The full voltage dynamics V(t) preserve most of the information that is contained in individual channel currents, and an order one fraction of this information is also preserved in the interspike times, at least in the regime of regular action potentials. However, the further the system is from the bifurcation, the smaller changes in the frequency of AP firing a downstream sensory would need to be able to distinguish to read out this information. Only very near the bifurcation is information about the signal accessible even with an imprecise readout mechanism or in the presence of additional noise in the firing of APs; see Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2.
Is there a natural way for the voltage dynamics to maintain itself close to the bifurcation where information about temperature is most accessible? Motivated by work on self-organized criticality (30, 31) and control theory (46), we suggest that feedback from the order parameter, the AP frequency, onto the control parameter, the voltage at half maximum V12(T), robustly self-tunes the system to the bifurcation. In the most simple case, V12(T), a single-channel property, gets increased (decreased) on average if the AP frequency, a collective property of the system, is above (below) a certain “target value.” This negative feedback is particularly robust since the diverging susceptibility at the bifurcation point not only leads to high signal amplification but also ensures that the control parameter stays close to its critical value even if the target value varies, see Fig. 4B.
Nonetheless, due to the intrinsic noise in the time between two APs (and the ensuing stochasticity in the feedback), the control parameter exhibits fluctuations around its steady-state value (blue shaded in Fig. 4C). These fluctuations fundamentally limit the maximal sensitivity of the system: Only temperature changes that (transiently) shift the distance to the bifurcation by considerably more than the typical deviation of the control parameter from the steady-state value can be detected. As expected, we find a tradeoff between the speed of the adaptation/feedback and the sensitivity: A more sensitive response requires a larger adaptation time or, conversely, a maximal adaptation time limits the possible sensitivity; see SI Appendix, section S9. Furthermore, the control parameter constitutes an unbiased estimator of temperature at steady-state and therefore its precision can be at most the Fisher information (Cramer-Rao bound). Indeed, as we show in SI Appendix, section S9, the typical fluctuations in the control parameter due to intrinsic noise in the feedback scale exactly inversely to the information fidelity and are thus minimal close to the bifurcation if AP firing is subject to additional noise, cf. Fig. 3. For the parameter values as inferred from the comparison of our model with the data in ref. 2 we find a SD of the adapted temperature (voltage) of roughly σδT≈0.16 mK (σδV12≈0.005 mV); see also the blue shaded area in Fig. 4C, corresponding to ±2σδV12 around the adapted value of δV12.
Feedback from an order onto the control parameter also appears to have relevance in other biological homeostatic mechanisms. First, when zooming out from the level of a single neuron to networks of neurons, homeostatic plasticity (or spike-rate homeostasis) has been suggested as a means to self-organize the networks close to a critical state with benefits for information processing (38, 39). It corresponds to a negative feedback mechanism where the activity of a neuron, a collective property of the neuronal network, suppresses the local synaptic (coupling) strength. One particular model is the so-called LHG model (38, 47), where the synaptic coupling strength (or control parameter) constantly increases in the background while it gets reduced when a presynaptic AP is fired. It has been shown that this system exhibits self-organized quasi-criticality (48), sawtooth oscillations that hover around the critical point—similar to what we see for the control parameter in the case of feedback; see SI Appendix, section S9. Similarly, it has been shown that an anti-Hebbian learning rule, where the connectivity matrix evolves according to the neurons’ activities can lead to a self-organized dynamical critical state with infinite susceptibility to external inputs (49, 50). Finally, feedback from the collective output of the signaling system has also been discussed for self-tuned criticality in the context of hair cells/the auditory system (35), for threshold adaptation in pyramidal cells (34), and beyond neural systems, for instance, as a way to achieve robust adaptation in the context of E. coli chemosensing (36).
How plausible is such a type of feedback in the context of snakes? While we are not aware of specific evidence that the opening probability of single channels changes when AP frequency is modulated, it has been suggested that TRP channels act as integrators of calcium signaling (28): TRP channels are activated by membrane phosphatidylinositol-4,5-biphosphate PIP2, which in turn is suppressed by calcium influx, for instance following an AP. One could thus imagine a scenario in which pip lipids are constantly produced but are rapidly degraded whenever an AP is fired. In this case, the AP frequency, the order parameter, would directly feed back onto single channel activity, the control parameter, in a negative feedback loop, as desired. One way to test this hypothesis could be to artificially elicit action potentials in isolated trigeminal nerve fibers of pit vipers, e.g., by injecting currents, and to investigate whether this modification will lead to a (temporary) decrease in AP firing when the injection of currents is paused.
While the pit organ is ideal for understanding the integration of molecular information since the purpose of the signaling system is very specific and well defined, the general motif of embedding microscopic sensors in a dynamical system operating close to a bifurcation or critical point may be more common in sensory systems, as also suggested in other contexts (35, 51–63) Indeed, other biological systems also amplify weak signals, through mechanistically diverse mechanisms that nonetheless share common features. For example, E. coli are able to respond to 1 to 10% changes in ligand concentration to climb shallow chemical gradients (64). This system must also operate over several orders of magnitude in background concentration (65), a range which is four to five orders of magnitude larger than their sensitivity. In another example, in the inner ear, faint sounds are mechanically amplified by active processes tuned close to a Hopf bifurcation (35, 66, 67). Proximity to the bifurcation enables the ear’s enormous dynamic range and frequency discrimination and explains a wide array of observed nonlinearities and the emission of sound in quiet environments (35, 68, 69).
One drawback of being near critical points is that there the dynamics suffer from long equilibration and autocorrelation times. It has been argued that due to this so-called “critical slowing-down” the rate of information is minimized at criticality even though the susceptibility there is highest (70). While our system exhibits some characteristic features of critical slowing down on the level of the voltage dynamics (the restoring force is zero at the bifurcation), the interspike times are either uncorrelated without adaptation (71) or anti-correlated (with adaptation) and the rate of accessible information is highest at the bifurcation; see Fig. 3.
One reason why information is most accessible at the bifurcation is due to its relative robustness to noise. In contrast to the regime of regular firing of APs, the bifurcation regime is inherently noisy and information is contained in highly amplified changes in the AP frequency. Additional readout noise thus does not considerably change the sensing capability of the system. While this robustness against readout noise (and noise in the feedback, see before) is certainly promising, there are many other sources of noise that we have not considered here and that might exhibit different robustness properties. A careful characterization of these would likely require to take into account the spatial distribution of channels within the nerve ending and to determine fundamental limits for temperature sensitivity in the pit membrane (72). Furthermore, we here assumed that extrinsic noise is delta-correlated. This assumption is likely not true for temperature fluctuations in the pit membrane that plausibly have equilibration timescales much longer than the typical time between two action potentials; see also ref. 73 for a discussion of noise timescales in the context of AP dynamics. Finally, while TRP channels seem to have an intrinsic voltage dependence, one could also imagine a system where TRP channels are temperature- but not voltage-gated and instead trigger the opening of other voltage-gated channels. Preliminary analysis of such a system suggests that the addition of non-TRP channels does not change the bifurcation diagram. However, additional channels do lower the available information—the effective noise level roughly doubles if current fluctuations through TRP and non-TRP channels are of the same order of magnitude; see SI Appendix, section S11.
Strikingly, the pit organ appears to have evolved independently in snakes and in vampire bats, which use a very similar structure to find blood vessels in the mammals whose blood they feed on (74). Vampire bats adapted TRPV1 channels rather than TRPA1, even though both TRPA and TRPV family channels are present in trigeminal neurons of these species (75). This observation may suggest that both types of TRP channels, and perhaps all TRP channels, can be coupled together into a bifurcation like the one we propose. Indeed, the voltage dependence that is crucial for modulating positive feedback in our model is shared by all TRP channels, including both hot- and cold-activating and nonthermally sensitive TRP channels (11), possibly indicating a common mechanism for amplification among distinct TRP subfamilies.

## Details about Stochastic Simulations.
To check the validity of the approximations leading to our analytical results for the interspike times, we also perform stochastic simulations of a time-discretized version of the full system, Eq. 2, in the case of no extrinsic noise Next=∞. To this end, we evenly partition the 2log2(N) channels into 2x batches and discretize time in units of a 2x-th of the channel correlation time τc. During the simulation, we keep track of time t, the voltage V(t), the voltage at half maximum V12(t), the number of open channels Ni(t) in every batch i=1,…,2x, and, if convenient, the times at which APs are fired. At each timestep, we redraw the number of open channels Ni in a single batch i from a binomial distribution with probability po(V(t),V12(t)) depending on the current value of the voltage V and voltage at half maximum V12 and with number of trials 2−xN (we go through the batches in a deterministic, periodically repeating sequence i=1,2,…,2x,1,2,…). We then update the voltage by −Δtτrest(V−Vrest)+IcΔtcmemN∑i=12xNi, and time by Δt=2−xτc. In the case of feedback, we additionally update the voltage at half maximum by −d−. If the voltage crosses a threshold value Vth, which we chose to be Vth=Vrest+ΔV2ρ(1+121−4ρ) in order to ensure that close to the bifurcation it is both above the unstable fixed point, if existent, and below the upper stable fixed point, an AP is fired, and the system is reset: V=Vrest and the number of open channels Ni in each batch i=1,…,2x is redrawn from a binomial distribution with probability po(Vrest,V12(t)) and number of trials 2−xN. Furthermore, in the case of feedback, the voltage at half maximum is increased by d+. To generate Figs. 2A and 4 A and C, we chose x=8 and simulated the system for 226 timesteps for Fig. 2A and 223 timesteps for Fig. 4 A and C. For Fig. 2A, we started to record the state of the system after 0.1×226 steps and recorded every 2x-th step. As initial conditions, we chose V(0)=Vrest and drew the number of open channels in each batch randomly as we do after each system reset. For Fig. 2A, we set V12=V12bif−ΔV(δT/ΔT). For Fig. 4 A and C, we chose the initial condition for V12 to be the expected steady-state voltage at half maximum corresponding to a frequency f0=8.4 Hz, and in the middle of the recording decrease V12 by ΔV(2mK/ΔT)=0.06 mV (corresponding to a temperature increase of 2 mK). As one can see from Fig. 2A, the agreement between the analytical prediction from the rescaled dynamics, Eq. 7, and the stochastic simulation results is excellent. In order to generate the distribution of interspike times in Fig. 5A, we thus simulate the rescaled dynamics, Eq. 7, instead of the full system, Eq. 2. During the simulation, we keep track of time s, the voltage u(s), and the interspike times. We chose a timestep Δs=0.001 and start the system at s=0,u(0)=−500. At each timestep, we randomly draw ξ from a standard Normal distribution and increase u(t) by Δs(α+u2)+Δsξ. Once u exceeds u=500, we record the time s, reset it to s=0 and set u=−500. We simulate the system until we have recorded 221≈2.1×106 interspike times. For Fig. 5A, we used α=0.16768 for the case of no stimulus (blue) and α=11.48 for stimulus intensity I=64 (gray). To compare the distribution of these interspike times to the experimental data, we go back to real time t=τss and therefore multiply the simulated interspike times by τs, which in this case is τs=0.023 s.
