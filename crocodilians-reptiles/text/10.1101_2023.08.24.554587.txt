Abstract

26

Several vertebrate taxa, cetaceans, sirenia, pinnipeds, and sea snakes have adapted to

27

the marine aquatic environment. In the species of these taxa, marine adaptation has

28

resulted in shifts in the absorption spectra of opsin pigments and/or the degeneration or

29

duplication of opsin genes. Thus, marine adaptation has strongly affected the evolution

30

of opsins. In sea turtles, however, the effect of adaptation from freshwater to marine

31

environments on opsin evolution has not been studied. In this study, we determined the

32

high-throughput RNA sequences extracted from eyes of two sea turtles (green turtle:

33

Chelonia mydas, loggerhead: Caretta caretta) and two freshwater turtle species

34

(three-keeled pond turtle: Mauremys reevesii, softshell turtle: Pelodiscus sinensis) and

35

investigated the amino acid evolution and expression of the opsin gene. We found that

36

most of the sea turtle lineage-specific amino acid substitutions did not alter amino acid

37

properties and did not include previously known substitutions for turning absorption

38

spectra of opsin pigments, suggesting no adaptive amino acid substitutions in the opsins

39

during marine adaptation in sea turtles. Instead, the blue-sensitive opsin (SWS2) gene

40

expression was higher in sea turtles than in freshwater turtles. These results suggest that

41

sea turtles may have adapted their vision to the blue light-rich marine environment by

42

increasing SWS2 expression.

Introduction

47

Several taxa of terrestrial vertebrates have returned to the sea and adapted to the aquatic

48

environment. The cetaceans, sirenia, and the hydrophiin sea snakes are fully adapted to

49

aquatic environments and no longer depend on terrestrial habitats (Heatwole 1999;

50

Sanders and Lee 2010). These three groups of species are entirely aquatic, giving birth

51

to their young in the water and spending their entire lives in the water. Some vertebrate

52

taxa spend part of their life above water: pinnipeds and amphibious laticaudins sea

53

snakes come ashore to spawn, breed, and rest but forages in the water. Sea turtles spend

54

most of their lives underwater but lay their eggs on land.

55

Sea turtles (Chelonioidea superfamily) are an ancient diverged species

56

(Bowen and Karl 2007) that originated in the Late Jurassic (Naro-Maciel, et al. 2008;

57

Joyce, et al. 2013). Seven sea turtle species are currently recognized, with loggerhead,

58

green, and leatherback turtles found in tropical and subtropical waters worldwide.

59

Molecular phylogenetic analysis indicates that sea turtles form a monophyletic group,

60

which is sister to the freshwater turtles (Thomson, et al. 2021). In other words, sea

61

turtles likely adapted from freshwater to a marine environment.

62

Visual adaptation is a prime example of molecular evolution by natural

63

selection (Davies, et al. 2012). Vertebrate visual pigments comprise a light-absorbing

64

component (chromophore) and a protein component called opsin (Shichida 1999). The

65

spectral sensitivity of a photoreceptor is determined by the chromophore type and the

66

chromophore's interaction with the amino acid residues in the retinal binding pocket

67

where the chromophore lies (Yokoyama 2000). Several spectral tuning positions in the

68

amino acid sequence of opsins have been reported, and these positions have been used

69

to predict the absorption spectra of visual pigments from opsin gene sequences

70

(Yokoyama and Radlwimmer 1998, 1999; Yokoyama 2000; Hunt, et al. 2001;

71

Yokoyama 2008; Yokoyama, et al. 2008). Vertebrates have two types of photoreceptor

72

cells that differ in structure and function: cone cells, which are active in bright light and

73

contain cone visual pigments (SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS) for color vision, and rod cells,

74

which contain RH1 pigments for scotopic vision (Yokoyama 2000; Bowmaker 2008;

75

Davies, et al. 2012).

76

The transition from terrestrial to marine environments has affected the

77

evolution of opsins in marine mammals and sea snakes. A correlation between foraging

78

depth and spectral sensitivity of visual pigments has been reported in cetaceans

79

(McFarland 1971; Fasick, et al. 1998; Fasick and Robinson 1998; Fasick and Robinson

80

2000; Bischoff, et al. 2012). Shallow-diving species have a peak absorbance (λmax) of

81

RH1 comparable to that of land mammals (500 nm), while deep-diving species have a

82

blue shift in λmax around 479 nm (Fasick and Robinson 2000; Bischoff, et al. 2012). A

83

correlation between foraging depth and the spectral sensitivity of RH1 pigments has

84

also been reported in pinnipeds. Deep-diving elephant seals have a λmax of 483 nm,

85

while other shallow-diving pinnipeds have a λmax similar to land mammals (Southall,

86

et al. 2002; Levenson, et al. 2006). A correlation between foraging depth and spectral

87

sensitivity of visual pigments and the amino acid substitutions responsible for this has

88

also been reported in sea snakes (Seiko, et al. 2020). The blueshifts of LWS pigments

89

have occurred stepwise and may have adapted to the deep-sea or open-ocean

90

environment where blue light is dominant (Seiko, et al. 2020). In Hydrophiini, a

91

substitution causes a long wavelength shift in the SWS1 pigment, and the

92

polymorphism at this position has persists among Hydrophiini species (Simões, et al.

93

2020).

94

In addition to spectral shifts, opsin genes have been lost or duplicated in

95

marine mammals and sea snakes during adaptation to the marine environment. All

96

cetaceans have lost the SWS1 gene (Fasick, et al. 1998; Peichl, et al. 2001; Griebel and

97

Peichl 2003; Levenson and Dizon 2003) at an early stage of evolutionary transition

98

(Meredith, et al. 2013). All pinnipeds also lost the SWS1 gene (Peichl and Moutairou

99

1998; Peichl, et al. 2001; Newman and Robinson 2005; Levenson, et al. 2006),

100

indicating that cetaceans and pinnipeds convergently degenerated SWS1 gene during the

101

evolutionary transition. Furthermore, cetaceans have independently lost LWS multiple

102

times in both toothed and baleen whale lineages (Meredith, et al. 2013). No gene loss

103

has occurred in sea snakes, while a possible duplication of the SWS1 gene has been

104

suggested (Rossetto, et al. 2023).

105

Marine adaptation has strongly affected the evolution of opsin in cetaceans,

106

pinnipeds, and sea snakes. In sea turtles, however, the effect of adaptation from

107

freshwater to marine environments on opsin evolution has not been studied. In this

108

study, we examined the amino acid evolution and expression of the opsin gene and

109

showed the effects of marine adaptation on the opsin gene in sea turtles.

Phylogenetic relationships of opsin gene sequences

113

We used these sequences of loggerhead turtle SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS, and RH1

114

sequences in the database (Table S1). Using these opsin sequences as queries, we

115

searched the opsin sequences of Testudines species from the database by blastn (Table

116

S1). We also used the most similar outgroup sequences as outgroup sequences for

117

phylogenetic analysis (Table S1). Some opsin gene sequences were not in the database

118

for green and softshell turtles. Therefore, we assembled eye-derived RNA-seq reads and

119

isolated opsin sequences from each assembled sequence by blastn using the loggerhead

120

turtle opsin sequence as queries (Table S1). We then constructed phylogenetic trees

121

from the sequences of SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS, and RH1, respectively, using the ML

122

method (Fig. 1). As for the phylogenetic relationships among turtle species, the

123

topologies were the same as those of previous studies (Thomson, et al. 2021), except for

124

SWS2.

126

Sea turtle lineage-specific amino acid substitutions

127

To search amino acid substitutions that played an adaptive role in the adaptation of sea

128

turtles to the marine environment, we estimated and placed sea turtle lineage-specific

129

amino acid substitutions on the phylogenetic trees of the respective opsin genes (Fig. 1).

130

Most of these sea turtle lineage-specific amino acid substitutions did not alter amino

131

acid properties. In addition, the sea turtle lineage-specific substitutions did not include

132

previously known substitutions for turning absorption spectra of opsin pigments. These

133

results suggest that no adaptive amino acid substitutions may have occurred in the

134

opsins during marine adaptation in sea turtles. The only amino acid substitution in the

135

RH2, position 164, substituted from alanine to serine in green and loggerhead turtles,

136

was estimated to be a long-wavelength shift of the absorption about 7 nm (Yokoyama

137

and Radlwimmer 1998, 1999; Yokoyama 2000; Hunt, et al. 2001; Yokoyama 2008;

138

Yokoyama, et al. 2008). This substitution may be related to the ecology of green and

139

loggerhead turtles but not to marine adaptation since it is alanine in leatherback turtle.

141

Expression patterns of opsin genes

142

To examine opsin gene expression patterns in green, loggerhead, three-keeled pond, and

143

softshell turtles, RNA-seq reads from each species' eyes were mapped to each species'

144

opsin gene. We calculated the TPM (Transcript Per Million) from the mapping results.

145

Then we calculated the relative expression levels of the opsin genes (Fig. 2). The

146

expression patterns of two sea turtle species (Figs. 2A and 2B) and two freshwater turtle

147

species (Figs. 2C and 2D) were similar, except for SWS2. SWS2 expression tended to be

148

higher in the two sea turtle species. Indeed, we compared the expression levels of each

149

opsin gene between sea turtles and freshwater turtles (Fig. 3) and found that only SWS2

150

was significantly higher in sea turtles than in freshwater turtles (Fig. 3B). These results

151

suggest that sensitivity to blue light may be higher in sea turtles than in freshwater

152

turtles.

154

Discussion

155

In cetaceans and the hydrophiin sea snakes, which are fully adapted to aquatic

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.08.24.554587; this version posted August 25, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-ND 4.0 International license.

156

environments and live their entire lives underwater, the transition from terrestrial to

157

marine environments has affected opsin evolution. Even in pinnipeds and amphibious

158

sea snakes, which are not fully adapted to marine habitats and come ashore to spawn,

159

breed, and rest, the transition to marine environment has significantly affected the

160

sensitivity of opsin pigments. Therefore, we expected the effect of the transition from

161

freshwater to marine environment on opsin evolution in sea turtles.

162

In previous studies of cetaceans, pinnipeds, and sea snakes, marine adaptation

163

has resulted in shifts in the absorption spectra of opsin pigments and the degeneration or

164

duplication of opsin genes. In sea turtles, however, no gene degeneration, duplication,

165

and amino acid substitutions affecting the sensitivity of opsin pigments have occurred

166

during marine adaptation. Furthermore, we found no amino acid substitution affecting a

167

significant shift in the opsin pigments sensitivity of all turtles analyzed in this study.

168

Most turtles forage in the water and come ashore to bask in the sun. Therefore, they use

169

vision in both terrestrial and underwater light environments. Therefore, opsins are likely

170

conserved due to the functional constraints for using vision in two light environments.

171

The evolution of opsin expression patterns during marine adaptation has

172

previously been studied in sea snakes, where opsin expression levels tended to be the

173

same in sea snakes and ancestral land snakes (Seiko, et al. 2020). In this study, the

174

expression of SWS2 was significantly higher in sea turtles than in freshwater ones. The

175

ocean, especially the open ocean, is dominated by blue light (Kirk 1994). Therefore, sea

176

turtles may have adapted their vision to the blue light-rich marine environment by

177

increasing SWS2 expression.

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.08.24.554587; this version posted August 25, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

178

Previous studies have reported shifts in the absorption of opsin pigments,

179

degeneration, and duplication of opsin genes during marine adaptation. This study

180

shows that the evolution of opsin gene expression may also be part of marine adaptation.

181

Future comparisons of opsin gene expression levels in marine vertebrates and their

182

ancestral species will reveal the evolutionary role of gene expression in marine

183

adaptation.

Samples

187

Fertilized eggs of the green sea turtle Chelonia mydas were collected on Chichijima

188

Island with the permission of the Tokyo Metropolitan Government during the laying

189

periods of 2020. Fertilized eggs of three-keeled pond turtle Mauremys reevesii and

190

Chinese softshell turtle Pelodiscus sinensis were purchased from turtle farms in Japan,

191

in 2020. Eyeballs were excised from fresh late stage embryos of the three turtle species

192

and used for RNA extraction. Eyeballs were excised from dying juveniles of the

193

loggerhead turtle Caretta caretta hatched from the eggs laid by captive females at

194

Kushimoto Marine Park, Japan, in 2020 and used for RNA extraction. All animal

195

experiments were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of

196

the Faculty of Science, Toho University (permits 20–51–449). Three individuals were

197

analyzed for each turtle species.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

203

Total RNAs were extracted from eyes of green, loggerhead, three-keeled pond, and

204

Chinese softshell turtles using TRIzol RNA Isolation Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific,

205

Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with purification

206

using RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s

207

protocol. RNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic

208

Isolation Module and the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New

209

England Bio Labs, Ipswich, MA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Short

210

cDNA sequences (paired-end 150 bp) were determined from the libraries using the

211

Illumina HiseqX platform (RNA-seq).

213

Phylogenetic analysis

214

Sequences of the SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS, and RH1 gene of turtles and outgroup

215

species were collected by blastn (Camacho, et al. 2009) from the NCBI nucleotide

216

database using the loggerhead turtle SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS, and RH1 sequences as

217

queries (Table S1). SWS2 and LWS sequences of green turtle and SWS1 and SWS2

218

sequences of softshell turtle were not found in the database. Therefore, we assembled

219

RNA-seq reads and isolated these opsin sequences from each assembled sequence by

220

blastn using the loggerhead turtle opsin sequence as queries. All opsin sequences

221

obtained were aligned with GENETYX ver. 20.0 software (Genetyx Corporation, Tokyo,

225

Expression analysis

228

RNA-seq reads from each of three individuals of green, loggerhead, three-keeled pond,

229

and softshell turtles were mapped to the sequences of SWS1, SWS2, RH2, LWS, and RH1

230

from each species. Reads showing similarity (90%) with 90% read lengths were mapped

231

to opsin sequences and expression levels were calculated using CLC Genomics

232

Workbench ver. 11 (https://www.qiagenbioinformatics.com/). TPM (Transcript Per

233

Million) was used for normalized expression values.
