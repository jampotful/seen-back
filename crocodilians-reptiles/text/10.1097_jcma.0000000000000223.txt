# Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan: An epidemiological survey from 1990 to 2016

## Abstract
Background:Venomous snakebites are common during hot seasons in Taiwan. However, rarely is venom spat directly into the subject’s eyes, causing eye injury. Despite being uncommon, analytical data regarding venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan have been lacking. This study thus aimed to conduct an epidemiological survey on Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan during the past decades to improve future care of such patients.Methods:Registered records of cases with snake venom injuries at the Taiwan National Poison Control Center from 1990 to 2016 were retrospectively reviewed, enrolling those with records of cobra venom-spit eye injuries and excluding exotic species. Demographic data, clinical symptoms, snake species, ocular conditions, management, and outcomes were recorded and analyzed.Results:A total of 39 cases suffering from Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia were enrolled. The overall incidence rate was 1.6 cases per million people from 1990 to 2016. Among the included cases, most were unilaterally injured (82%), male (95%), aged 18 to 59 years (90%), injured during catching (51%), and injured in spring and summer (92%). Ocular symptoms occurred in 90% of the cases, majority of which included ocular pain (90%) and redness (85%). Conjunctivitis (67%) and corneal injury (59%) of involved eyes were common. Immediate water irrigations were done in all cases, most of whom received further topical eye drops, including antibiotics, corticosteroids, and vasoconstrictors. Although topical or intravenous antivenoms were administered in 11 cases, no obviously superior outcome was observed. Most cases (77%) were symptom free after the acute stage.Conclusion:Although Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan is uncommon, the risk for transient ocular symptoms and corneal/conjunctival injury does exists. Prompt ocular irrigation and professional ophthalmic care after envenomation help prevent serious ocular sequelae. Moreover, superior outcomes were not achieved with the use of antivenom. Nonetheless, further studies are required to clarify the role antivenoms play in venom-spit ophthalmia.

## 1. INTRODUCTION
Venomous snakebites, which induce significant morbidity and mortality, are a common emergency throughout the world.1–6 Given the subtropical to temperate climate in Taiwan, venomous snakes are not a rare occurrence, thereby necessitating appropriate care for venom-related injuries.6–13 Injuries caused by venomous snakes are usually in the form of snakebites, which produce various clinical manifestations from mild local reactions to severe, life-threatening systemic reactions. However, other types of local venomous snake injuries, such as venom-spit ophthalamia, should not be neglected.
Six major venomous snakes have been identified in Taiwan, including the Taiwan cobra (Naja atra),14 Taiwan banded krait, Hundred pacer snake, bamboo viper, Taiwan Habu, and Chain snake (Table 1).12,13,15

Most of the snake venom-related injuries in Taiwan occur from April to October.6,11,13,15,17–19 Snake envenomation may involve multiple organs (Table 2), including the eyes. Mortality may occur after a venomous snakebite due to shock or inadequate paralysis treatment, myolysis, coagulopathy, renal failure, or extensive tissue injury. When available, the definitive and emergent therapy for snake envenomation has been intravenous administration of the appropriate antivenom as earlier as possible.20–22 However, the use of an antivenom for venom ophthalmia has still not been recommended.
Reported clinical manifestations in cases bitten by cobra snakes4,6,12,17,23,24
Venom ophthalmia, commonly induced by the spitting cobra, is a rare type of snake venom injury. According to reported studies, spitting cobras found in Asia and Africa are capable of ejecting their venom in a spray for distances over 2 to 3 m.25 This behavior is mainly considered to be a defensive maneuver, and the venom is squeezed as a jet out of the fangs, forming small droplets upon hitting the targets.25,26 When the snake venom enters the eyes, immediate and intense painful and burning sensation, followed by production of ocular discharge, conjunctival congestion, eyelid swelling, blurred vision, photophobia, and temporary blindness potentially develop. Corneal ulceration and scarring, and even endophthalmitis, had also been reported as complications from African spitting cobra venom.27 Delay or lack of appropriate treatment may result in corneal opacity, hypopyon, or blindness. Following ocular envenomation, immediate copious irrigation for decontamination is the key to alleviating severe injuries.28 No evidence for the significant beneficial effects of systemic envenoming has been demonstrated in human patients.
In Taiwan, a few patients who sustained spitting cobra injuries and developed venom ophthalmia had been noted. Despite being uncommon, the suffering and potential for long-term visual acuity impairment brought by venom ophthalmia should be a cause for concern. Furthermore, the use of an antivenom and its mode of administration has still remained controversial. Therefore, a detailed review of the clinical presentations and management of Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan is important for improving future care quality. We hypothesized that the use of an antivenom did not shorten symptom-free days of cobra venom-spit ophthalmia. Thus, our study aimed to analyze the epidemiology, clinical presentations, management, and the outcomes of Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan from 1990 to 2016.

## 2. METHODS
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (IRB no: 2017-05-001CC) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
A retrospective review of the medical records was conducted for all snake venom-injured patients who had been admitted to the Taiwan National Poison Control Center (TNPCC)29 from 1990 to 2016. Any case with a record of ophthalmic injury due to cobra venom spit in the TNPCC database was included into our analysis. Cases involving spitting cobras other than Nata atra, as well as those that were exotic or unidentified, were excluded.
Patient data, including gender, age, involved sites, event upon envenomation, month of occurrence, signs and symptoms, management and medications, and final diagnoses, were recorded for analysis.
The annual incidence rate was calculated using the 2016 population data from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI) given that its coverage reached >99%.13,30,31 Incidence rates were calculated as the number of cases per million people divided into northern, central, southern, and eastern regions of Taiwan.
Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD, while categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Data were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2016 and statistically analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 20.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparing continuous variables as appropriate, while the χ2 test was used for comparing proportions of categorical variables among groups. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were drawn using SigmaPlot (Version 12.0, Systat Software, Inc. San Jose, CA, USA).

## 3. RESULTS

## 3.1. Subjects and seasonality
A total of 41 cases were found to have sustained venom-spit injuries. Two cases were excluded after determining that their injuries were not caused by Naja atra but by Naja melanoleuca and Naja sumatrana. Thus, a total of 39 cases coming from six medical centers and 26 local hospitals in Taiwan were ultimately enrolled and analyzed.
Among the enrolled cases, 37 (95%) were males (Fig. 1 A) and 35 (90%) aged between 18 and 59 years old (mean age, 44 ± 12 years old) (Fig. 1 B). Most of the cases presented with unilateral eye injuries (n = 32, 82%) (Fig. 1 C), while most of the envenomation occurred while catching the snake (n = 20, 51.3%) (Fig. 1 D).

Most of the cases mainly occurred in spring and summer and markedly decreased in autumn, while none occurred in winter (Fig. 2A and B).

## 3.2. Clinical presentations
Among the 39 enrolled cases, only two (5.1%) were asymptomatic, while most developed ocular symptoms (n = 37, 94.9%) (Fig. 3A). Extra-ocular symptoms were infrequent. One case also developed tongue and mouth numbness after some of the venom was sprayed into his mouth. Two cases developed headache and vomiting, one case exhibited chest tightness, and one other case experienced local redness and swelling on his thumb due to a simultaneous cobra bite.
Distributions of general symptoms (A), ocular symptoms (B), and management (C) in the 39 cases of venom-spit ophthalmia.
The most common ocular symptoms were pain and redness. Other symptoms included ocular swelling, discharge, blurred vision, and foreign body sensation (Fig. 2 B). The acute (within 3 days) and subacute (3 days to 1 month) ocular diagnoses are listed in Table 3. Accordingly, the most common acute ocular problem was conjunctivitis (n = 26, 66.7%) followed by corneal epithelial defect or erosion (n = 12, 30.8%). More than 76% of the enrolled cases had no ocular problem during and after the subacute stage (Table 3).

## 3.3. Incidence
The overall incidence rate of venom ophthalmia in Taiwan was 1.6 cases per million people from 1990 to 2016. The annual incidence rate of venom-spit ophthalmia was 0.06 case per million people per year (Table 4).
Incidence rate of venom ophthalmia in different regions of Taiwan from 1990 to 2016
A comparison among the different districts in Taiwan showed that the incidence rate was the highest in eastern Taiwan (6.2 case per million people) and lowest in northern Taiwan (0.8 case per million people) from 1990 to 2016. Incidence rates were 2.6 and 2.0 cases per million people from 1990 to 2016 in central and southern Taiwan, respectively (Table 4).

## 3.4. Management and outcomes
According to the medical records, all enrolled cases received immediate ocular water irrigation. After hospital arrival and examination by an ophthalmologist, some of the cases received further saline irrigation. Different types of ophthalmic medications were used among enrolled cases, including topical antibiotics, corticosteroids, vasoconstrictors, and cycloplegics. An antivenom was provided as a local instillation in six cases and as an intravenous injection in five cases (Fig. 3 C). None of the enrolled cases developed anaphylaxis. The patient who suffered local redness and swelling of the thumb after being bitten received intravenous antivenom within 1 h after thereafter. One other patient suspected to have suffered a simultaneous upper limb bite did not receive an antivenom injection due to the absence of local signs. A total of 16 cases (41.0%) were hospitalized for 1–2 days.
The conditions of patients who did or did not receive an antivenom were compared and shown in Table 5. Accordingly, most of the cases (n = 28, 71.8%) did not receive antivenom treatment. Among the 11 cases (19.2%) who received antivenom treatment, six received local instillations and five received intravenous injections. Cases receiving local antivenom instillations tended to have more clinical symptoms and ocular involvements than those receiving intravenous injections or no antivenom. Moreover, those receiving local antivenom instillations had a significantly higher proportion of ophthalmic watering or discharge compared to those receiving intravenous injections or no antivenom (p < 0.05) (Table 5). The proportions of all other symptoms, corneal injuries, conjunctival injuries, and symptom-free days did not significantly differ between the three management groups (p > 0.05) (Table 5). No mortality was recorded among the enrolled cases, and none had any record of long-term problems in visual acuity or other ocular conditions.

## 4. DISCUSSION
The present study investigated 39 cases of Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia from 1990 to 2016 in Taiwan. To the best of our knowledge, the present study has been the largest case series report regarding venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan.
Previous reports of venom snake injuries in Taiwan have mainly focused on snakebites. Accordingly, cobra snakebites had been the most mentioned with a high risk for surgical intervention (Table 6).6,7,11–13,15–19 The present report has been the first to investigate cobra venom-spit ophthalmia in Taiwan, with our findings showing that cobra venom-spit ophthalmia had obviously better outcomes than cobra bites.
Reported venomous snake bites and the requirement of surgical treatment in Taiwan from 2007 to 2018
Published reports have rarely investigated cobra venom-spit ophthalmia (Table 7).23,26,28,32–37 Moreover, reported cases have been distributed across the world, including Africa, Asia, North America, Europe, and Oceania. The present study identified 39 cases from 1990 to 2016, making it the largest case series on venom-spit ophthalmia. Although the present study included a different cobra species (Naja atra) compared to other reports, a comparison between our study and previously published reports revealed no substantial difference in management. Almost all authors suggested prompt and effective ocular irrigation to remove the spat venom from the ophthalmic area,28,36 a finding consistent with that presented herein.
Published case reports of venom ophthalmia and their managements by spitting cobra from 1976 to 2017
The pathogenesis of venom-spit ophthalmia is related to the contents of the cobra’s venom, which could directly injure the cornea and conjunctiva. As previously reported, venoms from spitting cobras have a variety of toxins and enzymes.39–40 Accordingly, cardiotoxins, polypeptides that may interfere with the integrity of cellular membranes, play a major role in inducing ocular toxicity.41 The presence of collagenases or proteinases in the venom may further worsen corneal edema into liquefaction and even opacification.42 All of the aforementioned processes may impair visual acuity later in the future. Therefore, the immediate removal of the venom from the eyes is key to alleviating severe ocular injuries caused by cobra venom. Although some of our cases developed erosion, epithelial defects, inflammation, infections, eyelid swelling, and visual acuity, all of them recovered without obvious sequelae (Table 5). The good outcomes achieved herein may be attributed to immediate water irrigation followed by professional ophthalmic care in all cases.
Permanent blindness is the most serious complication of venom-spit ophthalmia. Accordingly, studies have reported that two cases in Africa and one in Europe developed blindness after ocular envenomation in 1976 and 1984, respectively.32,38 However, with the advancements in general knowledge and medical care, blindness has no longer been a problem following venom-spit ophthalmia after 1990. This is notable considering that the cases included herein occurred in or after 1990. Considering that prompt water irrigation had been well known and routinely used for acute ophthalmic injuries due to chemicals or organics and that an excellent medical care system (NHI) existed in Taiwan,30,31 our reported cases achieved good prognosis, with more than three-fourths being symptom free after 3 days.
Immediate and aggressive irrigation of the involved eyes has been key in the management of venom ophthalmia. Given that cardiotoxins may not be tightly bound to the cell membrane, immediate irrigation with water or saline may efficiently reduce ocular symptoms.41 Mydriatics and anti-inflammatory agents have been used in patients with anterior chamber inflammation to reduce the risk of posterior synechiae formation and improve patient comfort. Studies have suggested applying topical antibiotics to patients with corneal erosions to prevent secondary bacterial infection.23,28,35,43 All aforementioned management modalities, which have been commonly used during the past decade,23,28,34,35 have also been provided to our present cases.
Although some studies have reported the use of topical antivenom in cases involving venom-spit ophthalmia,43,44 none have strongly recommended either intravenous or topical antivenom.28,45 One study showed that topical antivenom induced rapid and effective pain relief following ocular envenomation.34 Moreover, the present report showed that some clinicians attempted to use topical or intravenous antivenom in cases having more significant ophthalmic symptoms. Although the present study found no obvious benefit with antivenom treatment compared to the absence thereof, further studies are needed before definitive conclusions can be established regarding the use of local antivenom on injured eyes. Moreover, the retrospective design, relatively small population size, and varying ophthalmic injury severity may have influenced our results. Therefore, further studies accounting for venom-spit ophthalmic injury severity will be required before clinical recommendations can be made.
Some limitations of the present study need to be noted. First, considering the retrospective nature of the study wherein data regarding clinical information and management were simply collected, clinical management could not be standardized, while ophthalmic injury severity could not be adequately scored. Moreover, comparisons among groups were difficult to interpret. Second, although eye drops had been frequently used for venom-spit cases, drug names had not been indicated in 20 of the cases (51.3%). Moreover, further analysis on the relationship between therapeutics and outcome could not be done. Third, regular long-term follow-up on ocular problems was lacking.
In conclusion, the present study showed that Naja atra venom-spit ophthalmia is uncommon in Taiwan. However, the risk for transient ocular symptoms and corneal/conjunctival injury does exists. Prompt ocular irrigation after envenomation followed by professional ophthalmic care helped prevent serious or long-term ocular sequelae. Moreover, the use of an antivenom did not show significantly superior benefits in the studied cases. Nevertheless, further studies will be required to clarify the role of antivenom treatment in venom-spit ophthalmia.
