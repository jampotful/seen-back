Abstract

20
21

A severe lack of distribution data for aquatic reptiles in northern Australia leaves many taxa

22

vulnerable to extirpation and extinction. Environmental DNA (eDNA) technologies offer

23

sensitive and non-invasive genetic alternatives to trapping and visual surveys and are

24

increasingly employed for the detection of aquatic and semi-aquatic reptiles. However, at

25

present, these studies have largely applied species-specific primers which do not provide a

26

cost-effective avenue for the simultaneous detection of multiple reptilian taxa. Here, we

27

present a 16S rRNA metabarcoding assay for the broad detection of aquatic and semi-aquatic

28

reptile species. This assay is tested on water samples collected at multiple sampling sites at

29

two tropical locations: 12 marine/estuarine sites in Roebuck Bay, Western Australia, and 4

30

estuarine sites in Cooktown, Queensland, Australia. A total of nine reptile taxa were detected

31

from 10 of the 16 sampled sites, including marine and freshwater turtles, aquatic and semi-

32

aquatic/terrestrial snakes, and terrestrial skinks. However, inconsistencies in the detection

33

of previously observed aquatic reptiles at our sampled sites, such as saltwater crocodile and

34

sea snakes, indicates that further research is required to assess the reliability, strengths and

1. Introduction

44
45

The aquatic and semi-aquatic reptile fauna of northern Australia are highly distinctive,

46

exhibiting life history traits and physical adaptations to extreme climates and, in the case of

47

semi-aquatic species, seasonal, but increasingly inconsistent, water availability (Pusey,

48

2011). Collectively these taxa form an integral component of the region’s aquatic and

49

riparian food webs across multiple trophic levels; the saltwater crocodile (Crocodylus

50

porosus) often fills the role of apex predator in these ecosystems. There are over 90

51

recognised aquatic and semi-aquatic reptile species in Australia, including marine and

52

freshwater turtles, crocodiles, monitor lizards, water skinks, water dragons, sea snakes, sea

53

kraits and other semi-aquatic snakes (Uetz, Freed and Hošek, 2018). Whilst their terrestrial

54

counterparts have not historically experienced population declines in Australia – largely

55

reflecting their inhabitance of arid, inland areas less disturbed by anthropogenic influences

56

(Fox, 2008), aquatic and semi-aquatic reptiles species are under greater threat from

57

pollution, urban development, over-harvesting, fisheries bycatch, invasive species (such as

58

the toxic cane toad Rhinella marina), and climate change; the latter affecting the frequency

59

of bushfires, coastal erosion and coral reef degradation (Böhm et al., 2013; Doody et al.,

60

2014; Milton, 2001; Wilcox et al., 2015). In addition, latitudinal niche shifts to mitigate

61

changing temperatures have prehistorically been much more challenging for ectotherms,

62

such as reptiles, which have a lower climatic tolerance and reproductive ability (Rolland et

63

al., 2018). The viability of reptile species that exhibit temperature-dependent sex

64

determination may be compromised with projected temperature shifts in the next 50 years

65

(Tomillo et al., 2015). Thus, the continual monitoring of aquatic and semi-aquatic reptile

66

species, particularly in northern Australia, is an increasing necessity.

68

Typical survey techniques for the detection of aquatic reptiles include snorkelling, trapping,

69

satellite tracking, aerial surveying, seining and bycatch reports, although many of these

70

invasive methods can be limited in northern Australian waterbodies by the threat posed by

71

saltwater crocodiles (Australian Government, 2011). In comparison to other faunal groups,

72

there is a severe lack of data on aquatic reptile distributions across catchments in northern

73

Australia (Fox, 2008). The majority of aquatic reptile surveys in this region have targeted

74

the saltwater crocodile; two thirds of consolidated aquatic/semi-aquatic reptile distribution

75

records are attributed to this species (Fox, 2008), leaving data deficiencies for other reptilian

76

taxa.

77
78

The coastal and offshore waters of northern Australia are a biodiversity hotspot for sea

79

snakes (Elapidae: Hydrophiinae) with at least 32 species, of which 4 are nationally endemic

80

(Uetz, Freed and Hošek, 2018). Two of these endemic species, Aipysurus foliosquama and

81

A. apraefrontalis, were elevated to ‘Critically Endangered’ in 2011 under Australia’s

82

Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act 1999, given sustained

83

declines on the Ashmore and Hibernia Reefs, with a total estimated area of occupancy

84

totalling only 227 km2 (Guinea, 2003; Guinea & Whiting, 2005; Lukoschek, Beger,

85

Ceccarelli, Richards, & Pratchett, 2013). There is a lack of robust distribution data for sea

86

snakes globally; 34% of all sea snakes are listed as Data Deficient (DD) on the IUCN Red

87

List (Elfes et al., 2013). Data deficiencies for sea snakes in northern Australia include the

88

Arafura sea snake (Aipysurus tenuis), Zweifel’s sea snake (Enhydrina zweifeli), the faint-

89

banded sea snake (Hydrophis belcheri), the fine-spined sea snake (Hydrophis czeblukovi),

90

the slender-necked sea snake (Hydrophis melanocephalus) and the northern mangrove sea

91

snake (Parahydrophis mertoni; Elfes et al., 2013). Other DD aquatic reptile taxa in northern

92

Australia include freshwater turtles, such as Irwin’s snapping turtle (Elseya irwini), the Gulf

93

snapping turtle (Elseya lavarackorum), and the northern yellow-faced turtle (Emydura

94

tanybaraga; Van Dyke, Ferronato, & Spencer, 2018), and the marine flatback turtle (Natator

95

depressus).

96
97

Whilst a high level of data deficiency does not necessarily correspond directly to elevated

98

extinction risk, insufficient information in regards to population trajectories, distribution and

99

taxonomy creates a lot of uncertainty around extinction risk, conservation priorities and

100

legislation (Böhm et al., 2013; Bland and Böhm, 2016). Robust temporal and spatial

101

distribution records provide the baseline upon which species ecology and conservation

status can (and must) be developed. However, given the sheer number of DD reptile taxa,

103

that may or may not be threatened, it remains economically challenging to conduct in-depth

104

surveys. Furthermore, DD taxa may require more specialised trapping techniques,

105

taxonomic expertise and may react poorly to trapping/handling. In addition, rare and cryptic

106

taxa may never be detected in timeframes that are required for management decisions, such

107

as in relation to coastal development assessments.

108
109

Environmental DNA (eDNA) technologies offer a sensitive, cost-effective and non-invasive

110

genetic alternative to individual species and multi-taxon surveying in marine, freshwater

111

and terrestrial environments (Thomsen et al., 2012; Bohmann et al., 2014; Evans et al.,

112

2016; Olds et al., 2016). To date, reptile eDNA studies have largely applied species-specific

113

markers to amplify individual species from mixed environmental samples (Davy, Kidd and

114

Wilson, 2015; De Souza et al., 2016; Halstead et al., 2017; Baker et al., 2018; Feist et al.,

115

2018; Ratsch, Kingsbury and Jordan, 2020; Rose, Fukuda and Campbell, 2020). The first

116

reptile eDNA study, published in 2014, developed a diagnostic PCR to detect the Burmese

117

python (Python bivittatus), a semi-aquatic, invasive species in Florida (Piaggio et al., 2014).

118

The python’s elusive nature, cryptic colouration and occupation of aquatic habitats that were

119

logistically difficult to survey, prompted an eDNA approach. Piaggio et al., (2014)

120

developed a P. bivittatus-specific mitochondrial cytochrome b assay that was applied to

121

water samples from field sites in south Florida, successfully detecting the species where it

122

had been previously observed.

123
124

Advances in high-throughput sequencing now allow the simultaneous amplification and

125

sequencing of multiple taxa through universal or broad-taxonomic PCR assays (referred to

126

as eDNA metabarcoding), proving a more efficient approach to genetic surveying. However,

127

the use of eDNA metabarcoding for the detection of reptile assemblages has not yet been

128

thoroughly tested. Kelly, Port, Yamahara, & Crowder (2014) applied vertebrate-specific

129

mitochondrial 12S rRNA primers to detect green sea turtle (Chelonia mydas) in a large

130

mesocosm, but were unsuccessful, despite successfully amplifying the intended target with

131

species-specific primers. Conversely, Lacoursière-Roussel, Dubois, Normandeau, &

132

Bernatchez (2016) had more success in using COI metabarcoding assays to detect three

133

species of snake and two species of turtle across lakes and rivers in Canada. Our primary

134

objectives in this study were to design a metabarcoding assay that is able to simultaneously

2. Materials and methods

139
140

2.1 Field sampling

141
142

A total of 22 one-litre surface water samples were collected at 12 sites in Roebuck Bay,

143

Western Australia in August 2018 and 20 one-litre surface water samples collected at four

144

sites near Cooktown, Queensland in March/April 2020 (Figure 1; Table S1). Roebuck Bay

145

is a semiarid, tropical, marine embayment characterised by intertidal sand, mudflat and

146

mangrove habitats. Surface water samples were collected during the dry season at eight

147

ocean sites, two estuarine creek sites, and two intertidal/mangrove sites. There were a

148

number of marine turtle and sea snake species observed during sampling across the majority

149

of sites, whilst saltwater crocodiles have been previously observed in the vicinity of the two

150

sampled creek sites (Table S1). The Cooktown region is comprised of a variety of tropical

151

landscapes such as sandy beaches, tidal estuaries, freshwater wetlands and rainforest

152

hinterland. Surface water samples were collected in the wet season at four estuarine creek

153

sites that outflow into the ocean; two of which contained mangrove vegetation and two that

154

were connected to a sandy wetland system. The Cooktown surface water samples were

155

collected using a large pole to mitigate saltwater crocodile risks in the area. Water samples

156

were individually filtered across Pall 0.45µm GN-6 Metricel® mixed cellulose ester

157

membranes using a Pall Sentino® Microbiology pump (Pall Corporation, Port Washington,

158

USA). Filter membranes were immediately frozen and stored at -20°C prior and post-

159

transportation to the Trace & Environmental DNA (TrEnD) Laboratory in Perth, Western

160

Australia.

161
162

2.2 In-silico design

163
164

Mitochondrial DNA is typically targeted for eDNA metabarcoding assays due to both

165

template copy number and reference data. Two Indo-Pacific sea snake databases were

166

curated for the mitochondrial 16S rDNA and cytochrome b gene regions by Sanders, Lee,

167

Bertozzi, & Rasmussen (2013) and provided for this project. The cytochrome b database

5

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.09.29.319525; this version posted October 1, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-ND 4.0 International license.

168

was substantially larger (361 sequences, 1104bp length) than the 16S database (50

169

sequences, 531bp length), however the cytochrome b region presented no conserved regions

170

that were consistent across all sea snake sequences and that would be suitable for primer

171

binding. Conversely, the 16S region exhibited a few conserved regions (approximately 30-

172

50bp length) flanking larger hypervariable regions. Additional 16S rDNA sequences of a

173

number of Australian aquatic and semi-aquatic reptiles (Table 1) were downloaded from

174

NCBI GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/) and aligned using the MUSCLE

175

plugin in Geneious 10.0.6. Primer pairs were designed in conserved regions using the built-

176

in primer design tool in Geneious; the final primer pair chosen by visually inspecting

177

alignments for a target region with maximum variation and under a length of 280bp. The

178

length constriction allows for the sequencing of barcode indexes (on the 5’ end of a

179

forward/reverse primer within a fusion-tagged primer setup) on a single-end Illumina MiSeq

180

sequencing run (up to 325bp). The forward primer was modified to include a degenerate

181

base allowing for annealing to polymorphic sites in the reptile alignment. The resulting

182

primer pair (herein referred to as the 16S Reptile assay) are AqReptileF-degenerate: 5’–

183

AGACNAGAAGACCCTGTG-3’ and AqReptileR: 5’–CCTGATCCAACATCGAGG-3’;

184

with a G/C content between 50.0-55.6%, TM between 52.0-55.5 and hairpin TM of 32.4.

2.3 In-vitro testing on reptile tissue and eDNA water samples

187
188

In-vitro testing of the primers to assess PCR amplification and optimise the annealing

189

temperature was firstly conducted using tissue extracts (1/10 dilution) from saltwater

190

crocodile (C. porosus) and flatback turtle (N. depressus). The primers were then further

191

tested on the filtered water samples collected at Roebuck Bay, Western Australia, and

192

Cooktown, Queensland. DNA was extracted from half of the membrane using a DNeasy

193

194

µl of ATL lysis buffer, 60 µl of Proteinase K and a 3-hour digestion at 56°C. Blank controls

195

were processed in parallel with all samples to detect any cross-contamination.

196

Environmental DNA extracts were then stored at -20°C.

197
198

A gradient PCR determined an optimum annealing temperature of 52°C. Each qPCR

199

reaction was carried out in 25µl containing: 1X AmpliTaq Gold® PCR buffer (Life

200

Technologies, Massachusetts, USA), 2mM MgCl2, 0.1mM dNTPs, 0.2µM each of forward

3. Results

241
242

3.1 Primer design

243
244

The 16S Reptile assay was designed using sea snake, sea krait, crocodile, marine turtle,

245

freshwater turtle and monitor lizard 16S rDNA reference sequences (Table 1). Target length

246

of the amplified fragments ranged from 212bp (sea snake and sea krait) to 275bp (crocodile).

247

There were no mismatches with the primers, except for the monitor lizard reference

248

sequences (Varanus indicus and Varanus panoptes) which both exhibited one mismatch

249

towards the 5’ end of the forward primer. It is unlikely that this will hinder potential

250

amplification of monitor lizards with the 16S Reptile assay, but may impact on efficacy of

251

detection when other (matching) reptile templates are more abundant.

252
253

The average pairwise percent identity for sea snakes across the target region was 94.2%

254

(across 30 SNPs); the pairwise percent identity for congeneric sea snakes averaged 96.4%

255

(min 93.4%, max 100%), providing enough variation to distinguish closely-related taxa.

256

Across the two congeneric crocodiles and two congeneric monitor lizards, the pairwise

257

percent identity was 95.3% (across 13 SNPs) and 88.8% (across 25 SNPs), respectively. For

258

marine turtles (superfamily: Chelonioidea) the average pairwise percent identity was 90.9%

259

(across 48 SNPs); and for freshwater turtles (family: Chelidae and Carettochelyidae) 74.0%

260

(across 127 SNPs).

287

288

289

assignments had a high percent identity match (>98% with 100% query coverage). Only the

290

northern tree snake (D. calligaster) had a lower percent identity match of 95.8% (with 100%

291

query coverage). However, the queried eDNA sequence provided no other hits above 90%.

292

This indicates that the eDNA sequence detected may represent intraspecific differentiation

293

from the NCBI reference sequence of D. calligaster, or a closely-related taxon that is yet to

294

be barcoded and shared on a publicly-accessible database. For both the genus and family

295

296

differentiation (£1% percent identity difference) between closely-related taxa to confidently

297

assign at a species level. However, for both queried eDNA sequences there were no percent

298

identity matches above 98%, indicating that the detected sequences potentially represent

299

taxa that are yet to be barcoded.

4. Discussion

302
303

The in vitro eDNA testing of our 16S Reptile metabarcoding assay successfully detected

304

two marine turtle species at 6/12 Roebuck Bay sites, where they were visually observed in

305

the area upon water sampling (Table S1). Flatback turtle (Natator depressus) was detected

306

widely across ocean, creek and intertidal sites at Roebuck Bay, whilst green turtle (Chelonia

307

mydas) was only detected at site 4 (ocean), despite being observed at creek and intertidal

308

sites within Roebuck Bay. The principal detection of marine reptiles at Roebuck Bay was

309

not unexpected, given that the surveyed sites were predominately marine-based with the

310

exception of two estuarine sites. In comparison, the Cooktown sites, which were located

311

solely within estuaries, provided a greater detection range of freshwater and also terrestrial

312

reptile species. Two freshwater turtles (the saw-shelled turtle [M. latisternum] and an

313

Australian short-necked turtle [genus: Emydura]) were detected at three of the Cooktown

314

sites; these eDNA detections coincide within their known distribution ranges across northern

315

and eastern Australia. Terrestrial skinks and a northern tree snake were additionally detected

316

at the Cooktown sites, which likely reflects DNA shed into the sampled coastal creeks from

317

drinking, skin shedding or other activities.

318
319

A notable detection at Cooktown was that of an Indo-Australian water snake

320

(Homalopsidae). The family Homalopsidae is comprised of over 50 aquatic and semi-

321

aquatic species that typically inhabit mangrove forests, tropical tidal wetlands and coastal

322

waters from Southeast Asia to northern Australia (Alfaro et al., 2008). The detected

323

Homalopsidae eDNA sequence however could not be resolved to a species or even genus-

324

level, as there were no high percentage matches (>98%) typically required for a species

325

assignment, and of the remaining Homalopsidae hits (>90%) there was not enough

326

resolution to confidently distinguish taxa even at the genus level. This indicates that the

327

detected Homalopsidae eDNA sequence at Cooktown represents an Indo-Australian water

328

snake that has not yet been barcoded for the 16S region and is potentially an undescribed

329

species. Targeted herpetological surveying at this site is recommended to resolve this

330

Homalopsidae eDNA detection.

331
332

A discrepancy in the performance of the 16S Reptile assay however, is that it did not detect

333

any sea snake (Elapidae: Hydrophiinae) species, despite visual observations at 5 out of the

12 Roebuck Bay sites (Table S1). Additionally, it failed to detect any saltwater crocodile,

335

despite previous observations in both the Roebuck Bay and Cooktown areas. This lack of

336

detection could be attributed to the non-specificity of the assay and/or a low shedding rate

337

of reptiles in the environment. Despite the fact that the assay was designed to preferentially

338

amplify reptile taxa, reptiles only accounted for 8.1% of the average proportion of

339

sequencing reads per sample, however this varied greatly between samples. The detection

340

of the Indo-Australian water snake (Homalopsidae) for example, accounted for 24.2% of the

341

total sequencing reads from Cooktown Site 1. Therefore, whilst the 16S Reptile assay does

342

detect other taxonomic groups (bony fish, amphibians, bivalve molluscs and mammals), it

343

is not exhibiting consistent preferential amplification of these groups above reptiles.

344
345

Further optimisation of this assay to reduce non-target amplification is ideal, although this

346

may be complicated given that the primers are located in a highly-conserved region of the

347

16S rRNA gene. Furthermore, the inclusion of mismatches increases the risk that rare reptile

348

variants will be excluded from amplification; additionally, the placement of mismatches on

349

3’ ends may compromise the efficiency of qPCR assays (Wilcox et al., 2014). Alternatively,

350

the development of discrete assays for each major reptilian order (i.e. turtles, crocodiles,

351

tuatara, and squamates [lizards and snakes]) may be more effective in refining specificity,

352

but retaining broad-taxonomic amplification. Another possibility is the use of blocking

353

primers, which preferentially binds and restricts amplification of a targeted taxonomic

354

group. Blocking primers have been successfully used in conjunction with metabarcoding

355

assays to increase the specificity of amphibian and bony fish amplicons by reducing the

356

amplification of human DNA (Valentini et al., 2016; Sasso et al., 2017).

357
358

The shedding rate of reptiles may also undermine our ability to detect them via eDNA

359

methods. It has recently been suggested that reptiles may have a relatively lower shedding

360

rate because of their keratinised scales and reduced urine production, and subsequently are

361

less detectable than other mucus-covered organisms, such as fish and amphibians (Raemy

362

and Ursenbacher, 2018; Adams et al., 2019). Whilst this is yet to be explicitly tested, it may

363

explain the inconsistent amplification of reptiles in aquatic environments across multiple

364

studies. For example, giant garter snake (Thamnophis gigas) mesocosm experiments

365

reported positive eDNA detection in tanks with snake skin and snake feces, however no

366

detection with live snakes in tanks, nor at field locations, despite capture of the species with

367

traps (Halstead et al., 2017). The collection of water within one metre of eastern massasauga

rattlesnakes (Sistrurus catenatus) only produced a positive eDNA detection for 2/100 water

369

samples (Baker et al., 2018). Conversely, Lacoursière-Roussel, Dubois, Normandeau, &

370

Bernatchez (2016) successfully used metabarcoding to detect redbelly snake (Storeria

371

occipitomaculata), northern watersnake (Nerodia sipedon), milksnake (Lampropeltis

372

triangulum), snapping turtle (Chelydra serpentine) and wood turtle (Glyptemys insculpta)

373

in rivers and lakes in Canada; however the wood turtle was not detected in 4 rivers that

374

produced positive detections via species-specific qPCR and visual surveying. Overall, there

375

has been a lot more success in the detection of wild turtles than snakes with eDNA, primarily

376

with species-specific assays (Kelly et al., 2014; De Souza et al., 2016; Feist et al., 2018),

377

with a push to quantify turtle abundance and biomass using eDNA (Adams et al., 2019). In

378

regards to crocodiles, there is only one published study at present that has attempted to

379

amplify crocodile eDNA. However despite observing West African crocodile (Crocodylus

380

suchus) and the Nile monitor (Varanus niloticus) in the water at their field sites in

381

Mauritania, they were unable to amplify any crocodile or other reptilian eDNA using a

382

metabarcoding approach (Egeter et al., 2018). A low shedding rate many therefore limit

383

eDNA detection, despite a well-designed assay that is capable of amplifying the targeted

384

taxa. It is possible that changing the eDNA substrate/method may help to improve detections

385

by enriching fractions for target taxa – for example a plankton-tow might assist in retrieving

386

reptile eDNA (Koziol et al., 2018). Nonetheless, an increase in sampling density may be the

387

most feasible approach to increase eDNA sensitivity for aquatic reptiles.

388
389

Another limitation to the implementation of eDNA metabarcoding for broad-reptile

390

surveying is potential reference database gaps for DD taxa. At present, only 27.6% of reptile

391

species have been barcoded for the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase I (COI) gene; a

392

short, standardised gene region that has historically formed the primary barcode sequence

393

for animal species (Ratnasingham and Hebert, 2007). Only four sea snake species (Elapidae:

394

Hydrophiinae) have been barcoded for this region, and only two of which are distributed in

395

northern Australia. Our assay design targeted the mitochondrial 16S rDNA gene region,

396

given the development of a 16S Indo-Pacific sea snake database with a high representation

397

of northern Australian sea snakes (Sanders et al., 2013). The development and

398

implementation of eDNA metabarcoding for reptile species, particularly for DD taxa, should

399

ideally be tailored to available reference sequences for the targeted taxonomic group. As

400

such, the development of discrete taxonomic assays, as discussed previously for refining

401

specificity, may be a superior approach to ensure high-resolution assignments (i.e. to a

402

species-level). Ultimately however, it will be easier to implement broad metabarcoding

403

assays with either a standardised barcode region, a complete suite of barcoded gene regions,

404

or ideally, a complete mitochondrial genome for each representative species.

405
406

The inconsistent amplification and detection of aquatic reptilian eDNA, despite positive

407

visual and trapping detections at survey sites, indicates that at present eDNA methodology

408

provides an unreliable estimate of diversity and community composition between sites. We

409

recommend that aquatic and semi-aquatic reptile shedding rates into various substrates (e.g.

410

water, sediment, soil) are substantially tested before eDNA approaches, in particular

411

metabarcoding, are further applied as reptile survey tools. This will provide greater insight

412

into inconsistencies in amplification between taxonomic groups and whether assays need to

413

be tailored to accommodate this i.e. the use of species-specific assays for taxa with low

414

shedding rates. An alternative approach to detecting reptiles with potentially low shedding

415

rates, would be to explore sampling volumes and subsequent filtering methods, i.e.

416

increasing our standard water replicate volumes from 1L up to 50L. Here, we present a 16S

417

rDNA primer assay for the broad detection of aquatic and semi-aquatic reptile species in

418

northern Australia. However, constrictions around suitable primer binding regions that can

419

simultaneously amplify deeply diverged reptile lineages has resulted in non-target

420

amplification of other closely-related metazoan groups, such as amphibians. If a higher level

421

of specificity is desired, we further recommend that reptile eDNA metabarcoding assays are

422

developed at an order level or lower, and consider the coverage of reference databases for

423

various gene regions. Taken together, we advocate that this 16S reptile assay is a valuable

424

addition to the metabarcoding assay ‘toolkit’ and like many of the other assays developed

425

(Miya et al., 2015; Elbrecht and Leese, 2017; Taberlet et al., 2018; Nester et al., 2020) will

426

be useful when designing or screening environmental samples for reptiles and other taxa.
