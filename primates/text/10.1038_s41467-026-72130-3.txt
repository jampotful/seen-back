Retinoic acid drives cell fate specification, maturation and retinal regionality in human retinal organoids.

# Retinoic acid drives cell fate specification, maturation and retinal regionality in human retinal organoids

## Abstract
Retinoic acid (RA) is a key morphogen in human retinal development, activating transcriptional programs that drive retinal progenitor differentiation and photoreceptor development, ensuring proper spatial organisation within the neural retina, essential for vision. Despite its well-established role in retinal patterning, the concentration-dependent effects of RA on human retinal cell fate specification and the regional definition of the primate macula and peripheral retina remain poorly understood. Here, we show that temporal and dosage-dependant modulation of RA during human retinal organoid differentiation induces distinct changes in retinal cell abundance, maturation, and organisation. Single-cell transcriptomics and protein analysis reveal that RA dosage influences the relative abundance and maturation of photoreceptors and retinal interneurons. Spatial transcriptomics analyses demonstrates that low RA levels biases retinal organoids toward a macular-like regional identity, whereas high RA levels promotes peripheral-like development. Collectively, our findings emphasise the critical role of RA signalling in retinal maturation and regional specification. This study elucidates mechanisms involved in human retinal development and we anticipate that controlled RA modulation in retinal organoids provides a strategy to refine disease modelling of inherited retinal disorders and enhance the specific generation of photoreceptors suitable for transplantation and regenerative therapies.

## Introduction
Retinal development is a complex spatially-organised process dependent on signalling pathways that have been extensively characterized in various model organisms. However, the intricacies of human retinogenesis remain comparatively less understood. The advent of human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived retinal organoids (hROs) has provided a powerful in vitro model to investigate human retinal development. Despite their promise, the utility of hROs is highly dependent on the precise optimization of culture conditions and a thorough evaluation of how closely the differentiated tissues recapitulate the structure and function of the native retina. Achieving accurate cellular patterning, regional specification, and a molecular identity that reflects distinct developmental stages is essential for establishing hROs as a reliable model for studying human retinal development.
Morphogens regulate cell fate decisions by activating intracellular signalling pathways in a concentration-dependent manner, ultimately leading to the expression of specific transcription factors regulating cellular identity. In particular, retinoic acid (RA) has been identified as a key morphogen involved in orchestrating retinal development. The role of RA has been elucidated as early as the stages of optic vesicle regional specification and optic cup invagination, where RA levels, regulated by aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs), contribute to the establishment of dorsal-ventral patterning in the developing retina1. Following optic cup invagination and the formation of the neural retina and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), retinogenesis is initiated in the central retina and progresses towards the periphery. This spatial and temporal progression leads to the stepwise differentiation and maturation of retinal cell types, ultimately establishing the distinct central and peripheral retinal regions2,3. Within this developmental sequence, retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), cones, horizontal cells, and amacrine cells differentiate during early retinogenesis, while rods, bipolar cells, and Müller glia emerge at later stages.
The role of RA in retinogenesis was first demonstrated in vitro in rat retinal explants where RA supplementation led to an increase in photoreceptor cells4. In zebrafish embryos, RA led to a premature generation of rods and an increase in expression of opsins5,6. In addition, RA can direct photoreceptor cell fate decisions, by facilitating the differentiation of retinal progenitors cells (RPCs) into rods at the expense of cones7. In the chick retina, fibroblast growth factor 8 (FGF8) signalling and a lack of RA signalling promoted the formation of a rod-free zone composed exclusively of cones, analogous to the fovea at the centre of the primate macula8. In mammals, RA levels are spatiotemporally regulated, particularly through its degradation via cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes expressed in the central retina at key developmental stages8–10. These studies highlight the critical role of RA in regulating the balance between progenitor cell proliferation and differentiation. Proper timing of this transition is essential to ensure the successful generation of photoreceptors in appropriate numbers during retinal development.
Key studies have started to elucidate the role of RA signalling in human retinal development. Differentiation of hROs in the presence of exogenous RA has demonstrated its enhancing effects on RHODOPSIN expression11 and 9-cis retinal, an isomer of the RA precursor retinaldehyde has also been shown to accelerate rod differentiation12,13. Similarly to the response of mouse PSC-derived ROs14, hROs cultured without RA supplementation showed an increase in cone opsin expression15. RA also plays a role in specifying subtypes of cone cells16,17 and its inhibition has also shown to cause a reduction in retinal proliferation and an increase in cone L/M-OPSIN expression10. Additionally, exogenous RA has also been shown to decrease RPE in ocular organoids18. Despite these insights, our understanding of RA’s influence on human retinal development remains incomplete.
To comprehensively characterize the role of RA in human retinal development, we investigated the effects of dose-dependent RA gain- and loss-of-function in hROs. Prior to RA exposure and modulation, we profiled early-stage hROs enriched in RPCs and generated using our spontaneous, growth factor-free differentiation protocol. Leveraging spatial transcriptomics, we mapped the expression and spatial distribution of key components of the RA signalling pathway. This analysis revealed the presence and localisation of RA signalling genes at this critical early stage of retinal development. By applying RA modulation at early, mid, and mid-to-late stages of hRO development, we defined the stage-specific roles of RA in regulating RPC proliferation, cell fate specification, and cell maturation. Early treatment with all-trans RA promoted the maintenance of RPC proliferation, whereas inverse agonism of RA signalling using a pan-retinoic acid receptor (RAR) inverse agonist primarily drove the specification of RGCs. During mid stages of development, RA treatment not only sustained RPC proliferation but also promoted rod photoreceptor differentiation. In contrast, organoids with low RA levels or RAR inverse agonism favoured the specification of RGCs, cones, and bipolar cells, while reducing the generation of Müller glia.
Finally, low RA levels were associated with accelerated maturation of multiple retinal cell types, including photoreceptors. Transcriptomic analysis also revealed the upregulation of region-specific retinal markers, reflecting features of both the central and peripheral domains of the human retina. These findings underscore the importance of precise modulation of RA signalling in orchestrating the timing, identity, and regional specification of retinal cells during human retinogenesis.

## Results

## Early hRO development matches early human retinogenesis
To understand when retinal cell specification starts in vitro and what signalling pathways govern early retinogenesis, we generated hROs using our previously established differentiation protocol19,20 (Fig. 1A). This spontaneous unguided protocol relies on endogenous cell signalling and offers a retinal development background that has not been manipulated by growth factors. First, we analysed early-stages hROs from 6−12 weeks in culture to understand when RPCs start to differentiate into specific retinal cell types. Here, we analysed RGCs and cone photoreceptors specifically, which, together with starburst amacrine cells and horizontal cells, are the first-born cell types of the retina. hROs at 6 weeks showed the presence of few RNA-binding protein with multiple splicing (RBMPS)+ RGCs, and RXRG+ cones (Fig. S1A). At 10-weeks, a distinct population of RGCs and cones were present in the developing neuroblastic layer (NBL) of hROs (Fig. 1B). At 12 weeks, cone photoreceptors and RGCs had expanded within the developing hROs (Fig. S1B). To capture the relationship of these nascent cells and RPCs we performed 10x Visium spatial transcriptomics on 10-week-old hROs. Recently, Zuo et al., performed scRNA-seq on the foetal retina at various stages of development (9 to 24 post-conception week (pcw)21. Due to the large spot diameter of the Visium spatial technology (~5 cells per spot), we performed spot deconvolution of hROs using the Zuo et al., 11-pcw foetal dataset for annotation. Pearson Correlation was performed between the pseudo-bulk transcriptomes of the hROs at 10 weeks and Zuo et al. human foetal retina timepoints which showed that hRO development at 10 weeks most closely resembled the foetal retina at 11-13 pcw (Fig. 1C, PSC line 1 in two z-positions A and B, PSC line 2 in two z-positions A and B) validating hROs as a good representation of the native retinal tissue. Furthermore, cell type proportions under each spot based on gene expression showed that RPCs, cones and RGCs were the most predominant cell types in the NBL (Figs. 1D, E and S1C, D). Marker gene expression plots for the key retinal cell types, including RPCs, RGCs, horizontal, photoreceptors precursors and Müller glia-like/late progenitors is shown in Fig. S1E-I. Immunostaining of the same hROs confirmed the presence of SOX2+ RPCs, NEUN/RBPMS+ RGCs, CRX+ photoreceptor precursors and RXRG+ cones with similar localisation to the spatial dataset (Figs. 1F, G and S1J–L). Immunostaining of the foetal retina at 10 pcw confirmed the presence of these cell populations in the native retina (Fig. S1M-R). These findings possibly indicate that early-stage hRO development recapitulates key aspects of human retinal development, including age and cell type composition.
Next, CellChat was used to characterise signalling networks and communication patterns underlying retinal morphogenesis at this critical stage where cell fate specification is commencing. The CellChat package screens against a curated signalling molecule database of ~3300 known molecular interactions, with outgoing cell interactions referring to the signalling molecules (ligands and co-factors) serving as a signal source to a cell population that receives incoming signalling activity (receptors and cognate factors) from other cells in the communication network. Here we denoted all outgoing communications as ‘ligands’ and incoming communications as ‘receptors’ for simplicity.
Overall CellChat predicted biologically meaningful intercellular communications including paracrine signalling, non-protein signalling, traditional cell-cell contact and ECM-receptor signalling22,23. Our data showed that RPC-mediated cell signalling was the most prevalent, likely due to their abundance at 10 weeks. RPC-Cone interactions were also observed, followed by less abundant RGC and horizontal cell interactions (Fig. 1H). Particularly, RA signalling was the most prevalent pathway between interactions involving RPCs, cones and horizontal cells. The formation of RA, a lipophilic molecule involved in paracrine signalling24, is dependent on aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) enzymes oxidising retinaldehyde into RA, with ALDH1A1 being evident as an outgoing signalling (‘ligand’) for both RPC-RPC and RPC-cone interactions. Additionally, the expression of transcripts that produce RA interacting proteins, such as the RA-responsive element CRABP2 and nuclear receptors RXRG and RORB were identified as RA ‘receptors’ receiving incoming signalling present at this stage (Fig. 1I). The presence of ALDH enzymes 1A1 and 1A3 was validated with immunostaining, confirming this expression pattern (Figs. 1J, K and S2A). Interestingly, the Secreted Phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) and Midkine (MDK) pathways, previously seldom connected to retinal development, were also prominent signalling interactions detected at this stage. SPP1 was primarily expressed in RPCs and RGCs with RPCs communicating through SPP1 signalling to both RPCs and cones via Integrin-beta 1 and Integrin-alpha V (ITGB1 and ITGAV) receptors. MDK signalling appeared restricted to RPCs (Fig. 1L, M) and while it is also known to interact with Integrins the most prevalent receptor for MDK at this stage was protein tyrosine phosphatase zeta 1 (PTPRZ1). Interestingly, these pathways have been shown to be downstream of RA signalling25–31 demonstrating that RA likely also plays a leading role during early stages of human retinogenesis. Overall, this data illustrates the expression patterns of the major signalling pathways present in 10-week-old organoids, utilising the spatial context to infer communication between the different cell types present within samples.

## Transcriptome of early hROs reveals RA signalling dynamics
Following on from the evidence that RA-mediated signalling is present at this stage, we sought to characterise canonical RA pathway genes in hROs (Fig. S2B). Trafficking of vitamin A (retinol)/retinol binding protein 4 (RBP4) complexes through the cell membrane into the cytoplasm is dependent on the STRA6 receptor32, of which expression was evident at 10 weeks (Fig. S2C). The intracellular retinol binding protein RBP1 (also known as CRBP1) (Fig. S2D) was strongly expressed across the present retinal cell types33. We also examined hROs for the presence of retinol dehydrogenases, responsible for the oxidation of retinol to retinaldehyde34. RDH10 and RDH11 were the most abundant, although less abundant than RBP1 (Fig. S2E,F), whilst RDH8 and RDH12 were not expressed at this age.
Both spatial transcriptomics and immunostaining showed the presence of ALDH1A1/3 enzymes, which are responsible for RA synthesis (Figs. 1K, L and S2A). CRABP2, a protein which binds to free cytosolic RA, and shuttles it into the nucleus to bind to retinoid receptors via RAR/RXR dimeric complexes or ROR monomers to drive the transcription or repression of genes via RA or RO response elements (RAREs/ROREs) was also identified (Fig. 1I)35. The most prevalent nuclear receptors in retinal cells at this stage were RARA, RXRG and RORB (Figs. 1I and S2G). RA can also bind to CRABP1 (Fig. S2H), which has been implicated in modulating intracellular RA concentrations, delivering RA for catabolism via cytochrome P450 family 26 (CYP26) and mediating non canonical RA signalling35,36. The spatial transcriptomic data showed very little CYP26A1/B1 expression in 10-week-old hROs (Fig. S2I,J). CYP26C1 which is expressed in the zebrafish retina is also not present at this stage37. Immunostaining for CYP26A1 over a time-course of 6, 10 and 12 weeks also confirmed the absence of CYP26A1 at 6 weeks, low levels at 10 weeks, and increased expression at 12 weeks (Fig. S2K-M). CYP26A1 expression increases at 26 weeks and appears to be localized to the CRALBP+ processes of Müller glial cells (Fig. S2N). Collectively, these findings reveal a dynamic retinoic acid (RA) signalling cascade during hRO development, offering a comprehensive and previously uncharacterised expression pattern of RA signalling in early human retinogenesis.

## Early stage RA modulation preferentially gives rise to RGCs
Next, to elucidate the role of RA in retinogenesis, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments in hROs and analysed its effects on retinal cells. Based on the findings showing RA signalling in the 10-week-old hROs, we postulated that modulation between 6–10 weeks in culture (early-stage; Schematic Fig. 2A) would mainly affect a population of early RPCs. We tested our original protocol with no exogenous RA (RA- (control condition)) against all-trans RA addition at 10 µM (RA10µM), 1 µM (RA1µM), or 0.1 µM (RA0.1 µM). Additionally, hROs were also cultured with the addition of the pan-RAR inverse agonist BMS-493 (hereafter named BMS) at 0.5 µM or 1 µM (BMS0.5/1 µM). To first assess the impact of various treatments on the overall viability of hROs, we performed a live/dead assay using a SYTOX dye and flow cytometry. At 10 weeks of differentiation, no significant differences in cell viability were observed between RA-treated and other treatment groups, with the exception of the BMS1µM condition, which exhibited a notable increase in SYTOX⁺ dead cells (Fig. 2B).
Next, we investigated cell proliferation via 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assays. At 10 weeks, hROs treated with RA10µM exhibited a significantly higher proportion of EdU⁺ proliferating cells (9.1 ± 2.9%) compared to RA- (5.1 ± 0.5%) and BMS1µM-treated organoids (3.5 ± 1.7%) (Fig. 2C, D). This suggests that in elevated RA levels there are more cells entering or progressing through S-phase pointing out to a larger pool of proliferative progenitors in this condition.
Finally, to determine whether early modulation of RA signalling influences cell fate decisions, we first evaluated the differentiation of RGCs and photoreceptor precursors. hROs were generated using the H7.BRN3b-P2A-mcherry human embryonic stem reporter line38 and two different CRX.RFP reporter lines to track RGCs and photoreceptors emergence, respectively (Fig. 2E, F). At 10 weeks, flow cytometry revealed a significantly higher proportion of BRN3B.mCherry+ RGCs in BMS1µM-treated hROs (67.9 ± 3%) compared to RA10µM-treated organoids (36.4 ± 9.7%) (Fig. 2G), indicating that low levels of RA promote early RGC specification. In contrast, early RA modulation had minimal impact on photoreceptor differentiation. The proportion of CRX.RFP+ photoreceptors was comparable across conditions (Fig. 2H). These findings suggest that early RPC differentiation is preferentially biased toward the RGC lineage, while photoreceptor specification likely occurs at later stages. Next, we analysed the differentiation of rods (NRL +) and cones (CRX + /NRL-) using immunostaining followed by image quantification using Cellpose39 (Fig. S2O). RA- hROs exhibited the highest percentage of NRL+ rods while no differences were observed in CRX + /NRL- cones across treatments (Fig. 2I, K, L). SOX2 + /SOX9 + /VSX2+ RPCs were observed in the high RA conditions while SOX2-/SOX9-/VSX2+ bipolar cells were more numerous in the RA- and BMS1µM hROs (Fig. 2J, M). The presence of bipolar cells at this early 10-week timepoint in BMS hROs denoted a premature differentiation of these cells.

## Mid stage RA modulation influences retinal cell fates
We next sought to understand whether the effects of RA manipulation were time-dependent. Mid-stage treatments from 10–16 weeks were performed, encompassing the peak of cell differentiation in hRO development. Again, a range of treatments modulating RA levels over these periods were performed: RA10µM, RA1µM decreasing to 0.5 µM (RA1µM, original protocol), RA0.1µM, BMS0.5µM, and BMS1µM (Schematic Fig. 3A). Following treatment, BMS conditions resulted in marked disorganisation and loss of laminar structure in hROs. To assess whether this phenotype was associated with cytotoxicity, we performed flow cytometry analysis for cell viability. Notably, mid-stage treatments did not induce significant toxicity at 16 weeks, with an average of 8% SYTOX+ dead cells across all treatment groups (Fig. S2P). Although BMS-treated hROs exhibited a trend toward increased SYTOX+ cells, this was not statistically significant. To investigate the molecular response of retinal cells to RA modulation, scRNA-seq was performed on 16-week-old hROs treated from week 10 onwards with RA (10 µM and 1 µM), RA- (no exogenous RA), and BMS (1 µM) (Figs. 3B and S3A). Annotation was performed via transfer learning43 using selected studies from the ScEiaD meta-ocular atlas44 capturing the presence of the major retinal cell types that reliably expressed known markers (Fig. S4). Integration of all samples demonstrated strong overlap and mixing across all treatment conditions, indicating robust cell type annotation irrespective of condition (Figs. 3B and S3B). Analysis of cell type proportions revealed treatment-dependent differences (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, UMAP projections reflected the developmental plasticity and immature precursor state of mid-stage hROs at 16 weeks, with partially intermingled clusters most prominently in RA10µM and RA1µM conditions, and to a lesser extent in RA- and BMS1µM (Fig. 3D).
ScRNA-seq revealed that RPCs accounted for approximately 9% of the total cell population in RA10μM and RA1μM conditions, but this proportion was reduced to 3% in RA- and BMS1μM-treated hROs (Fig. 3C). Immunostaining was performed to identify bipolar cells, Müller glia and RPCs (Fig. 3E, F). An average of 5 ± 2% Ki67+ RPCs was observed in RA-treated hROs, compared to only 2 ± 1.5% in BMS-treated organoids (Fig. S3C). We next assessed the differentiation of RGCs. Along with horizontal and RPE cells, retinal RGCs appeared underrepresented in the scRNA-seq data potentially due to technical artefacts, with fewer than 5% of cells detected. Therefore, to accurately quantify RGCs following treatment, we quantified BRN3B.mCherry+ RGCs via flow cytometry. Consistent with early-stage observations, enhanced RGC differentiation was observed under low RA conditions. BMS1μM-treated hROs contained 36.9 ± 8.1%, compared to only 18.8 ± 5.5% in RA10μM-treated organoids (Fig. S3D). These results further support the role of RA in maintaining RPC identity and suppressing premature RGC differentiation in a time independent manner.
Next, the impact of mid-stage RA modulation on late-born retinal bipolar and Müller glia populations was assessed. ScRNA-seq revealed that an average of 16% of cells in both RA- and BMS-treated conditions were bipolar cells, compared to 6% in RA-treated hROs. Consistent with these findings, a significant increase in VSX2 + /SOX9-(RPCs) bipolar cells was observed as RA concentrations decreased (~14% in both RA- and BMS-treated conditions) (Fig. 3E, G). When examining differentially expressed genes (DEGs), bipolar markers VSX1 and VSX2 were significantly upregulated with BMS treatment, not only in terms of expression levels but also in the number of cells expressing these markers (Fig. 3H). Therefore, our data supports that RA inverse-agonism promotes bipolar cell specification. Immunostaining with rod-bipolar specific PKCA further demonstrated the presence of bipolar cell at this stage (Fig. S3E).
A significant reduction in Ki67-/SOX9+ Müller glia population was observed in BMS-treated cultures, with Müller glia decreasing to 19.7 ± 8% compared to 28.5 ± 7.3% in RA1μM-treated hROs (Fig. 3F, I). This decrease mimicked the scRNA-seq data where these cells decrease from 30% in RA to 23% in RA- and BMS conditions. Albeit weak, possibly due to onset of expression at this stage, Glutamine Synthetase (GS) validated the identity of Müller glia cells, in 16-week-old hROs (Fig. S3F). Other cell types such as PROX1+ horizontal and bipolar cells and Calretinin+ amacrine cells (Fig. S3G, H) were also present at this stage confirming the scRNA-seq data. The presence of these cell types at this developmental stage was confirmed in the 16 pcw foetal retina (Fig. S3I–L).
DEG analysis revealed important genes involved in RA regulation (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Data S1–3), including CYP26A1 and DHRS3, which were upregulated across cell types of RA-treated hROs, whilst being downregulated in RA- and BMS1μM conditions. These findings confirm the presence of a feedback mechanism in the human retina in which elevated environmental RA levels induce the expression of CYP26A1 and DHRS3 to maintain RA homeostasis. RPCs and Müller glia were the two cell types that appeared to have the most direct response to RA with a greater upregulation of CYP26A1 and DHRS3 in these cells. Interestingly, MDK and SPP1 which were identified as key signalling pathways in 10-week-old retinal organoids were also modulated by RA with upregulation of both these markers across multiple cell types. MDK was upregulated in photoreceptors, Müller glia and bipolar cells whilst SPP1 was upregulated in photoreceptors, Müller glia and RPCs (Supplementary Data S1–3). Other genes significantly upregulated in response to RA included, HOXB2 and the long non-coding RNA HOTAIRM1 (HOXA Transcript Antisense RNA, Myeloid-Specific 1). These two genes have been implicated in RA-regulated neural differentiation45,46, and seldom described in the context of retinal development47. HOXB2 was the only differently expressed HOXB gene detected in multiple cell types and enhanced expression was more evident in RPCs and RGCs. High HOTAIRM1 expression was evident in RA-treated photoreceptor and bipolar cells.
Overall, RA inverse agonism administered during mid-stage retinal development showed a reduction in RPCs and Müller glia and promoted the generation of RGCs and bipolar cells. These findings reveal an underappreciated role that RA signalling plays in directing cell fate decisions of other interneurons beyond photoreceptors during retinal development.

## Loss of RA signalling drives cones at the expense of rods
An early cell fate decision in retinal development involves the specification of photoreceptor precursors into cone or rod lineages. To assess whether treatments influenced the overall abundance of photoreceptor cells, we first interrogated our scRNA-seq datasets. Comparative analysis across experimental conditions revealed no significant differences in the proportion of photoreceptor populations (Fig. 3C). To independently validate these findings, we performed flow cytometry quantification of CRX.RFP+ photoreceptor precursors in hROs. We then examined treatments for evidence of cone specification. Key cone genes, including RXRG and ARR3, showed a clear enhancement for cones in BMS1µM (Fig. 4B). Immunostaining for the cone-specific marker ARRESTIN3 (ARR3) demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in ARR3+ cone cells as RA levels decreased in culture (R² = 0.96) (Fig. 4C,D). RA10µM-treated hROs contained 2.4 ± 2% ARR3+ cells, which increased to 6 ± 4.6% and 9.1 ± 5.6% in RA0.1 µM and RA- conditions, respectively. A more pronounced increase to 16.8 ± 6.8% was observed in BMS1µM-treated hROs (Fig. 4D). Next, we asked if the increase in cones occurred at the expense of rod cells. Quantification of NRL+ rod photoreceptors showed a decrease in the proportion of these cells in low RA conditions, with only 10.1 ± 6.9% rods in BMS1µM hROs compared to 24.5 ± 8% in RA10µM-treated organoids (Fig. 4E). Our findings indicate that RA levels play a critical role in modulating photoreceptor fate decisions between cones and rods. Specifically, higher concentrations of RA promote rod photoreceptor differentiation, whereas lower RA levels favoured cone specification.
UMAP projections of cone and rod photoreceptor populations revealed some intermingle of cones into the rod cluster, potentially reflecting their immature and plastic state (Figs. 3B and 4F). Indeed, rod-specific NRL transcripts were present within cones in all conditions (Fig. 4B). This feature confounded our conclusions that NRL+ rods were increasing in RA-treated samples. To investigate this plasticity further, and assess the proportion of rods specifically, we examined our data for the presence of plastic photoreceptors which we defined as cells that were both NRL+ and RXRG+ (Figs. 4F and S5A–C). Rod and cone plastic precursor populations expressing both markers were observed across all conditions with BMS1µM treated hROs containing the fewest number of these plastic photoreceptors (Fig. S5A). Of note, this plastic population was not distinct to either rods or cones, with cells in the rod population expressing RXRG and cells within the cone population expressing NRL (Figs. 4F and S5D-F). When examining NR2E3 expression, a rod specific gene downstream of NRL, within this plastic NRL + /RXRG+ population, we observed that RA10µM hROs had less mean NR2E3 expression (Fig. S5G,H). Recently, NR2E3 has been described to regulate RXRG48.
Other studies have also reported this phenomenon, and we detect low levels of NRL expression in other cell-types in single-cell studies of the human retina (Fig. S5B) and organoids (Fig. S5C)21,49,50. Thus, we next evaluated double positive (NRL + /RXRG+) plastic cells using immunostaining (Fig. 4G). Our data showed a small population of NRL + /RXRG+ double-positive cells (<4 %) in all culture conditions, with a significantly greater population detected in RA10μM (3.8%) when compared to RA- (1.7%) (Fig. 4H). Finally, we quantified NRL+ and RXRG- rod cells to eliminate NRL expressing cones and obtain the correct percentage of rods cells. A significant increase in rods was observed in RA10µM and a significant decrease with BMS1µM (Fig. 4I). These findings confirmed that high RA levels gave rise to rods at the expense of cones with the opposite being true for RA inverse agonism.

In line with our previous published protocol20, 16-week-old RA1µM hROs were yet to develop brush border (BB) regions, encompassing the connecting cilia and nascent outer segments of photoreceptor cells. In contrast, these BB structures were clearly visible in untreated (RA-) hROs (Fig. 5A, B). This was confirmed with immunostaining for long/medium-wavelength (L/M)-OPSIN expression in cone outer segments (Fig. 5C,D). The presence of BB regions in RA- hROs suggested a more mature photoreceptor phenotype relative to their RA treated counterparts. We compared the transcriptional profiles of rod and cone photoreceptors from 16-week-old hROs to those of human foetal retina across developmental timepoints, reported by Zuo et al.21. Transcriptomic similarity analysis showed that rods and cones from RA- and BMS-treated hROs more closely resembled the foetal retina at 20-24 pcw (Fig. 5E). This suggested that reduced RA signalling promoted a more mature transcriptional state in developing photoreceptors.
Volcano plots showing DEGs in cone cells highlighted the upregulation of mature M-OPSIN (OPN1MW) in BMS1µM when compared to RA-. In agreement, RA1µM and RA10µM showed a significant downregulation of OPN1MW as well as a downregulation of short-wavelength (S-Opsin) cones (OPN1SW) (Fig. S6A). We also observed downregulation of the mature rod opsin (RHO) in RA treated rods (Fig. S6B). To validate the observed cone-opsin phenotype, we performed immunostaining for both (S)-OPSIN and (L/M)-OPSIN cone subtypes (Fig. S6C, D). Quantification revealed increased proportions of both cone types in RA- conditions, with 37 ± 17% S-OPSIN+ and 39 ± 16% L/M-OPSIN+ cones. In contrast, RA10μM treatment had significantly reduced populations to 7 ± 10% and 13.8 ± 11.4%, respectively (Fig. S6E-F). These results indicated that RA removal and inverse agonism promoted cone maturation independent of the subtype, particularly enhancing opsin expression. A possible mechanism for the opsin upregulation observed is through NEUROD1 and THRB, which were also upregulated in low RA conditions (Figs. 4B and S6A) and have been shown to pattern opsins in the rodent retina51.
Phosphodiesterases PDE6A, PDE6B, PDE6C and PDE6H genes related to maturation and phototransduction in rods and cones were upregulated in RA- and BMS treated hROs. Furthermore, cilia-specific gene PCDH15, ion channel KCNV2, and calcium-regulated genes such as GUCA1B/C were also enhanced. The proteoglycans IMPG1/2 which are normally expressed in the interphotoreceptor matrix and structurally supports photoreceptor cell outer segments were also upregulated in rods and cones in RA- and BMS conditions (Fig. 5F). PROM1 (CD133) and NT5E (CD73) were downregulated in RA treated hROs. These markers identify photoreceptor cells, with PROM1 marking nascent photoreceptor disk membranes in all photoreceptors and NT5E specifically labelling maturing rods and commonly used to purify this population from the retina52–54 (Fig. S6A, B).
To validate the transcriptional evidence of enhanced photoreceptor maturation, we performed immunostaining for key phototransduction proteins. Rod-specific markers GNAT1 and PDE6A were detected across all treatment conditions. However, in RA treated hROs, PDE6A and GNAT1 were predominantly present within rod cell bodies while under low RA conditions, PDE6A expression extended more prominently into the developing outer segments (Fig. 5G). This protein shift from cell bodies to outer segments is normally observed as cell mature during development. Similarly, immunostaining for the cone-specific phototransduction marker PDE6H was more prevalent in the brush border regions of RA- hROs (Fig. 5H), further supporting that reduced RA signalling enhances photoreceptor maturation.
Complementary to RA signalling, we performed cell-cell communication analysis on our scRNA-seq data to explore other pathways associated with retinal maturation. Examining the total communication probabilities, we observed an increase in overall signalling activity with decreasing exogenous RA (Fig. S7A,B). Consistent with this trend, there was generally a linear increase in the strength of both outgoing and incoming interactions (Fig. S7C). While cell-cell adhesion pathways such as NCAM, CADM and CDH are consistently regulated by photoreceptors, reduced RA dosage revealed the induction of signalling related to establishing proper photoreceptor function. For example, synapse formation and rhodopsin maturation via NRXN55, as well as neurotransmission and neurite and axon targeting through Glutamate and CNTN, respectively (Fig. 5I). A heatmap summary of incoming signalling showed an increased number of unique interactions occurring as well as an overall increase in strength in BMS1µM hROs (Fig. S7D).
Finally, we established whether other retinal cells were also responding to BMS treatment by showing improved maturation. In BMS1µM and RA- hROs, all cell types exhibited outgoing signalling toward bipolar cells and Müller glia involving numerous signalling pathways. In contrast, RA1µM and RA10µM conditions showed reduced signalling communications (Fig. S7A–E). Signalling pathway analysis of bipolar cells also revealed an increase in pathways related to synapse formation and neurotransmitter secretion (Fig. S7F).

## Sustained RA signalling leads to developmental stagnation
Next, we prolonged the treatment period from 16 to 26 weeks (mid-to-late stages) to investigate whether hROs subjected to sustained high levels of RA signalling would maintain their developmental delay or if they would still mature over an extended time-frame (Fig. S8A).
At 26 weeks, RA-treated hROs contained very few proliferative Ki67⁺ RPCs, which typically co-express VSX2 and SOX9. To assess bipolar cell populations, we quantified VSX2 + /SOX9- cells, which increased from 3 ± 3.1% in RA10µM-treated hROs to 6.5% in BMS1µM-treated hROs (Fig. S8B, E). Interestingly, similar to the documented decline in RGCs during hRO maturation56, the overall proportion of VSX2+ cells decreased between 16 and 26 weeks, indicating that bipolar cells are also progressively diminished as organoids age. Müller glia cells (SOX9+) were also quantified, and no significant differences were observed between treatment groups, with samples containing approximately 30% Müller glia on average (Fig. S8B, F). This is different from the mid-stage treatment in which at 16 weeks BMS1µM had significantly less Müller glia (~21%) than RA conditions which averaged at approximately ~30%. The increase in Müller glia cells from 16 to 26 weeks with RAR inverse agonism suggests that the final stage of Müller glia specification happened between 16 and 26 weeks. This increase was independent of the treatment and Müller glia were still able to differentiate in the presence of BMS.
Consistent with our findings from mid-stage (10–16 weeks) treatments, extended RA influenced photoreceptors differentiation. Quantification of NRL+ cells, showed 20.6 ± 7.3% NRL+ cells in RA10µM hROs and a drop to 8 ± 9.2% NRL+ cells in BMS1µM hROs (Fig. S8C, G). hROs grown in high RA (RA10µM) showed 3.8 ± 2.4% ARR3+ cones while BMS1µM treatment led to a notable increase of 30.7 ± 14.0% (Fig. S8D, H). The differences in cone-rich, rod-rich hROs were also evident when visualising whole hROs. Immunostaining with ARR3 and RHODOPSIN showed numerous cone ISs in RA- and BSM1µM while rod inner segments (ISs) were more prevalent in RA10µM treatment. Furthermore, in RA10µM RHODOPSIN was localised to ISs indicating immaturity, whereas in RA- RHODOPSIN was expressed in the outer segments (OSs), suggesting that these photoreceptors were more mature (Fig. S8I). These data confirmed that high levels of RA continued to prohibit cone development and promote rod specification over a prolonged treatment period.
Next, we sought to assess the effects of sustained RA treatment on cone-opsin maturation. At 26 weeks the proportion of S and M/L Opsins cones increased with prolonged treatments when compared to 16 weeks. The proportion of S-OPSIN-expressing cones was highest in RA1µM hROs (37 ± 10.6%) (Fig. S8J) increasing from 14% at 16 weeks. Interestingly most treatments, expect for RA10µM, promoted the expression of L/M-OPSIN with an average of 33% of the cones expressing it. In RA10µM hROs only 14% of its cones expressed L/M-Opsin showing that high levels of RA hinder L/M-OPSIN cone maturation (Fig. S8K). Finally, in low RA hROs the percentage of L/M-OPSIN cones was significantly higher than S-OPSIN cones demonstrating a shift in cone subtype specification when RA is absent (Fig. S8L).
Overall, sustained modulation of RA signalling until 26 weeks of development resulted in phenotypes comparable to those observed with mid-period treatments between 10 and 16 weeks. The exception was Müller glia, for which no significant differences were detected across conditions, and the previously observed reduction in cell numbers with BMS treatment was no longer evident. These findings, combined with evidence that RA-related genes are modulated in response to treatment, suggest that Müller glia possess the capacity to self-regulate RA signalling. This self-regulation likely contributes to their ability to mitigate the effects of RA pathway modulation. ## Expression of hallmark macula markers in low RA conditions
The establishment of regional identity in the primate retina is characterized by the early differentiation and maturation of cells in the central macula, while peripheral retinal regions remain more proliferative and immature. A gene known to exhibit regional variation is CALB1, a calcium-binding protein typically expressed in horizontal cells, but also reported in cones of the peripheral retina in both macaques and humans57–59. Notably, CALB1 is absent from macular cones, making it a useful marker for peripheral cone identity. Another regionalised gene, PCP4 has been demonstrated to be upregulated in the cones of the macula60.
In our study, CALB1 was among the most significantly upregulated genes in RA10μM hRO photoreceptors (Fig. S6A, B). In the RA10μM condition, UMAP visualization revealed CALB1 expression mainly within the cone and rod photoreceptor clusters (Fig. 6A). Immunostaining confirmed co-localization of CALB1 with ARR3+ cones only in RA10μM (Fig. 6B, head arrows). In contrast to CALB1, PCP4 emerged as one of the most significantly downregulated genes in cones both at RA10µM and 1 µM concentrations whilst it was significantly upregulated in cones treated with BMS1µM (Fig. S6A). UMAPs also highlighted the increased expression of PCP4 in RGCs and cones mainly in RA- treatment (Fig. 6C). These findings were further supported by immunostaining of ARR3 with PCP4, confirming the expression of PCP4 in ARR3+ cone cells amongst other cells (Fig. 6D). To further illustrate these regional differences, key genes were visualized in their spatial context (Figs. 7E and S9C, D). For both 16 and 26 weeks hROs, PDE6H and PCP4, identified as macula-enriched markers, exhibited increased expression and broader spatial distribution in RA- and BMS1µM-treated hROs. Conversely, the peripherally enriched markers such as SPP1, NGR1 and TF were enriched across retinal cell types and showed increased expression in RA treated hROs compared to those treated with BMS1µM. PDE6H is a key gene expressed in mature cone cells, so its enrichment in the cone-rich hROs generated under the BMS1µM condition is expected, especially given their increased maturity. PCP4, which is also a marker for other interneurons, provides a more representative feature of macular regionalisation, as it has been shown to also be upregulated in macular cones60. SPP1, a marker for both RGCs and Müller glia, has also been reported to be selectively expressed in peripheral Müller glia61.
An increase in the mean expression of PDE6H and PCP4, was evident from 16 to 26 weeks in RA- conditions, reflecting increased maturation. However, in RA10µM-treated hROs, retinal cells remained stationary both in the proportion of cells that expressed these two genes and in the mean expression of these two genes (Fig. 7F). We initially postulated that this stationary phenotype may be linked to proliferation, as both early-stage (6–10 weeks) and mid-stage (10–16 weeks) RA treatment maintained proliferating RPCs. However, at 26 weeks RA-treated hROs contained very few proliferative Ki67+ RPCs (Fig. 7G). Sustained high levels of RA, therefore, does not maintain proliferation but stagnates the differentiation of retinal cells, leading to immaturity and a more peripheral identity.
Together, these results demonstrate that modulation of RA signalling orchestrates the emergence of region-specific retinal identities, recapitulating in vitro aspects of macular and peripheral patterning observed in the human retina, where high and low RA levels are established in a developmental wave through the balance between ALDHs (RA-synthesizing enzymes) and RA-degrading CYP26A1.

## Discussion
This study establishes RA signalling as a central morphogenetic axis in hRO development, governing proliferation, cell fate specification, maturation, and regional identity. We profiled the perturbation of RA using spatial transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, and protein analysis to delineate the multifaceted roles of RA signalling in human retinogenesis.
Spatial transcriptomic analysis of 10-week-old hROs revealed endogenous signalling pathways mediating communication between RPCs and early differentiating retinal cells. Among the most prominent pathways were RA, SPP1, and MDK signalling. Notably, SPP1 and MDK signalling have not been previously characterised in early retinal development. SPP1, also known as osteopontin, is known for its neuroprotective role in mature RGCs but its early developmental presence is unexplored62,63. MDK, a RA-inducible gene, has been associated with cell cycle regulation and Müller glia proliferation post-injury64–67. Our data suggest that PTPRZ1 is the key receptor mediating MDK signalling in hROs, potentially regulating RPC proliferation and differentiation via RA-induced MDK expression.
Focusing on RA signalling, we identified robust expression of multiple canonical RA pathway genes (e.g. RBP1, ALDH1A1, CRABP1, RORB) in the absence of exogenous RA, indicating intrinsic RA activity. The absence of RA-degrading enzymes (CYP26A1, CYP26B1) and high ALDH1A1 expression further support that high levels of RA are present in the human retinal environment at this stage. RORB emerged as the predominant RA receptor, consistent with its known role in promoting rod and cone differentiation68,69. These findings underscore the intrinsic capacity of hROs to initiate and sustain RA signalling and reveal previously unappreciated roles for SPP1 and MDK in early human retinal development. Further investigation into these pathways may uncover novel mechanisms regulating human retinogenesis.
Modulation of RA signalling in hROs revealed its critical role in regulating RPC proliferation and differentiation in a stage-specific manner. RA supplementation sustained RPC proliferation across early (6–10 weeks) and mid (10–16 weeks) developmental stages, while inverse agonism via BMS493 reduced RPC numbers and accelerated differentiation. These findings mirror the spatial dynamics of the retina, with RA-treated hROs resembling the proliferative peripheral retina and RA-deprived hROs mimicking the differentiating central retina67,70.
RA also influenced cell fate decisions in a time-dependent manner. Early RA inverse agonism promoted RGC specification, while photoreceptor differentiation was more sensitive to RA during mid-stage development. High RA concentrations reduced CRX+ photoreceptor populations and consistently favoured NRL+ rod differentiation, whereas RA deprivation promoted cone specification. Bipolar cell differentiation was enhanced under low RA conditions regardless of developmental stage, and Müller glia appeared largely resistant to RA modulation, potentially due to their intrinsic regulation of RA via CYP26A1 and DHRS3.
Transcriptomic analysis revealed dynamic feedback regulation of RA-associated genes. Elevated RA levels upregulated RA transport and degradation genes (RBP1, CRABP2, CYP26A1, CYP26B1), while RA deprivation induced expression of RA-synthesizing enzymes (ALDH1A3), confirming a bidirectional feedback mechanism governing RA homeostasis in these cells.
These findings highlight the nuanced, stage- and cell-type-specific roles of RA in human retinal development and underscore the importance of tightly regulated RA signalling in orchestrating RPC proliferation, cell fate specification, and maturation.
Our scRNA-seq data revealed unexpected NRL transcript expression in cone cells, despite NRL’s established role in rod specification71,72. We also observed RXRG expression within the rod population. This led to the identification of a population of ‘plastic’ photoreceptors co-expressing CRX, NRL, and RXRG, with their abundance being RA concentration-dependent. While transcript contamination during dissociation may contribute to this phenomenon49,73,74, RA may also directly induce NRL expression ectopically in cones75. These plastic cells could also represent early rod photoreceptor precursors which express RXRG prior to NR2E3-mediated inhibition of RXRG and thus cone-fate commitment48. Protein-level analysis showed minimal NRL + /RXRG+ cone cells, supporting the likelihood of transcript-level artefacts and underscoring the need for proteomic validation alongside transcriptomic profiling.
RA also influenced cone subtype specification in a time-sensitive manner. Mid-stage RA treatments yielded balanced S-OPSIN and L/M-OPSIN cone populations, whereas RA reduction increased both subtypes. Notably, prolonged low RA exposure led to a predominance of L/M-OPSIN cones at 26 weeks. Transcriptomic data revealed upregulation of thyroid hormone (TH) pathway genes (NEUROD1, THRB, DIO2), which are known to promote L/M-OPSIN fate76,77. DIO2, responsible for converting the inactive thyroid hormone thyroxine (T4) to active triiodothyronine (T3)78, was enriched in Müller glia, suggesting a potential role for these cells in local T3 activation and cone subtype regulation.
Beyond specification, RA levels also modulated retinal maturation. Consistent with previous studies5,11, RA deprivation accelerated photoreceptor maturation, evidenced by earlier outer segment formation, increased opsin and phototransduction gene expression, and enhanced intercellular signalling. Importantly, maturation extended to bipolar cells, which showed increased synaptic markers and neurotransmitter pathway activity under RA-deprived conditions. This suggests that premature RPC differentiation under the guidance of morphogenetic RA signalling gradients, may drive broader retinal maturation.
However, accelerated maturation via BMS treatment disrupted organoid lamination. Optimising BMS dosage to 0.5 µM better preserved hRO architecture while maintaining cell specification and maturation, potentially offering a more physiologically relevant model of human retinal development with further optimisation.
RA signalling also orchestrated spatial patterning within hROs. Low RA conditions promoted expression of macular-enriched markers (PCP4, PDE6H), while high RA favoured peripheral markers (CALB1, SPP1)21,57–61. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed that RA deprivation broadened macular marker distribution, whereas elevated RA reinforced peripheral-like features. These regional identities were not solely attributable to cone or rod abundance but reflected broader transcriptional reprogramming across retinal cell types. However, we emphasise that these regional ‘macula-like’ and ‘peripheral-like’ phenotypes refer to transcriptomic profiles enriched for gene signatures and cell type analysis only as key features of the retina, as typical morphological features of the macula and fovea, such as foveal pit is not present in current organoids. Moreover, the absence of RPE in hRO models may limit macula-like region formation. Future work should explore combinatorial signalling modulation and incorporation of additional cell types such as RPE, microglia, and endothelial cells to enhance hRO structural and functional fidelity. Integration with other organoid systems, such as neural assembloids, may further support lamination and RGC survival and connectivity. Modulating RGC proportions during early and mid-stages via BMS treatment for example could promote optic nerve-like structures and improve inter-organoid communication. While this study highlights RA’s role in establishing regional identity, it is unlikely that RA alone governs the complex spatial organisation of the primate retina. Comparative spatial analysis with human foetal retina, particularly macular and peripheral regions, will be essential to uncover additional mechanisms.
Accurate characterisation of cellular identity and maturation in hROs is essential for advancing disease modelling and therapeutic development. This study demonstrates that even subtle changes in RA levels can significantly influence the specification and maturation of all major retinal cell types. The persistent challenge of generating hROs with macular-like features highlights the need for refined methodologies79. RA modulation offers a promising strategy to bias hROs toward cone-rich or rod-rich phenotypes, enabling tailored models for macular degenerations or peripheral retinal disorders such as Retinitis Pigmentosa.
Moreover, targeted RA control may reduce intra- and inter-batch variability, improving reproducibility and suitability for high-throughput drug screening. hROs cultured without exogenous RA reflect endogenous signalling and spontaneous differentiation, resulting in heterogeneous populations with both cone- and rod-enriched regions. However, variability in spontaneous differentiation across cell lines underscores the importance of precise signalling control to optimise hROs for translational use.
The directed differentiation of hROs into bespoke photoreceptor populations also holds significant potential for the development of stem cell-derived therapies. The developmental stage of transplanted cells determines their capacity for integration, with post-mitotic photoreceptor precursors, rather than fully mature photoreceptors, being optimal for survival, integration, and visual function rescue following transplantation19,80–82. BMS-induced precocious differentiation, resulting in the accelerated emergence of post-mitotic photoreceptor precursors, may enable their earlier harvesting potentially shortening culture duration and reducing labour and production costs. The upregulation of surface markers such as CD7352, previously used to isolate rod populations also facilitate and improves the isolation of enriched photoreceptor subsets52–54. Given that therapeutic applications may require millions of cells per patient, improving yield is critical. These findings collectively underscore the translational potential of RA-guided hRO differentiation strategies for both disease modelling and regenerative therapies.
Together, our spatiotemporal map of retinal organoid development offers mechanistic insight into retinal cell fate specification, maturation, and regional identity. By modulating the RA morphogenetic gradient, we demonstrate how hROs can recapitulate key features of human retinal development, providing a powerful platform for both basic research and translational applications. Importantly, our findings reveal that RA signalling influences the broader retinal cellular landscape beyond photoreceptors. As such, the choice of RA modulation should be carefully considered by researchers aiming to model a wide range of retinal diseases. The integration of precise signalling control with additional cell types and organoids will be essential to further improve the fidelity of hROs. Ultimately, these models offer a versatile platform to advance our understanding of human retinal development and to accelerate translational research in vision science.

## Experimental model and subject details
Experiments described in this study have been performed across four PSC lines including two WiCell human embryonic stem cell lines (WA09, CRX.tdTomato and WA07, A81.BRN3B-P2AmCherry) and two human iPSC lines (HPSI0314i-hoik_1 from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC) and 149Br.CRX.mcherry). The 149br.CRX.mcherry line was generated by insertion of an IRES-mCherry reporter at the c-terminus of CRX (exon 4) using CRISPR/Cas9 mediated HDR in the 149br iPSC cell line83. All cell lines are routinely authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling through CellBank Australia to confirm their identity and prevent misidentification. In addition, all cell lines are regularly tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination using standard PCR‑based assays.

## Maintenance of human induced pluripotent stem cells
For maintenance culture hPSCs were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. hPSCs were grown and expanded in feeder-free conditions using Essential 8 media (A1517001) on 6 well plates coated with Geltrex (A1413302) at a concentration of 1:100. Media was replaced daily, and cells were passaged at 70% confluency via 5–10 min 37 °C incubation with Versene Solution (0.48 mM) (15040066) to detach clumps of cells (All Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell clumps were resuspended at a ratio of 1:6–1:12 in E8 with 10 µM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632 dihydrochloride, Stem Cell; 72307) and seeded in fresh Geltrex-coated Falcon 6 well plates (Corning; 353046). For differentiation, hPSCs were grown to 90-100% confluency.

## Differentiation of retinal organoids from hPSCs
Retinal organoids were differentiated as previously described19,20 with some modifications. Briefly, at 90–100% confluency (denotated day 0), hPSC media was replaced with Essential 6 (E6; A1516401) daily for two consecutive days. On day 2, E6 media was replaced with pro-neural induction media (PIM, Advanced DMEM/F12; 12634028, 1X N2 supplement; 17502001, 1x L-Glutamine; 25030081, 1X MEM-NEAA; 11140050, 10 U/mL Pen-Strep; 15070063 and 0.25 µg/mL Amphotericin B; 15290026 [all Thermo Fisher Scientific]). Optic vesicles displaying neuro-retinal epithelium were manually isolated using a needle under EVOS XL microscope (Thermo Fisher) between day 25–35 and transitioned to 3D suspension culture in low-binding 96 U-bottom well plates (Sigma Aldrich; CLS7007) in ALT70 media (Advanced DMEM/F-12, 2X GlutaMAX Supplement; 35050061, 1X B-27 Supplement minus Vitamin A; 12587001, 10 U/mL Pen-Strep and 0.25 µg/mL Amphotericin B, [All Thermo Fisher Scientific] containing 100 µM taurine [Sigma Aldrich; T8691] and 10% FBS [Bovogen; SFBS]). At 10 weeks, retinal organoids were transferred into low-binding 24 well plates (Sigma Aldrich; CLS3473) and supplemented with the various concentrations of all- trans retinoic acid (RA) (Sigma-Aldrich; R2625) or BMS-493 (Stem Cell; #73974) tested in this study. At 13 weeks and until experimental end-point, media was replaced with ALT90 (Advanced DMEM/F-12, 2X GlutaMAX Supplement, 1X B-27 Supplement minus Vitamin A, 1X N2 Supplement, 7 mM glucose (G6152), 10 U/mL Pen-Strep, 0.25 µg/mL Amphotericin B, 1X Lipid Mixture, [All Thermo Fisher], 100 µM taurine, and continued supplementation of RA. Media was replaced every 2 days and cells maintained at 37 °C 5% CO2.
For experimental conditions, exogenous RA (10/1/0.1/0 µM) or BMS-493 (0.5/1 µM) was added to conditions at 6 to 10 weeks for manipulation at early-stage differentiation, from 10 to 16 weeks for a mid-stage manipulation and from 10 to 26 weeks for mid to late-stage treatment. For RA treatment depicted as RA1µM, RA was reduced from 1µM down to 0.5 µM starting at 13 weeks (as per our previously described differentiation protocol). Endogenous RA concentrations during vertebrate embryogenesis are in the low-nanomolar range (~1–10 nM), whereas the 0.1–10 µM doses applied here are supraphysiologic and commonly used to probe RA-dependent pathways in vitro; interpretations are therefore made relative to RA- (no exogenous RA) conditions and constrained exposure windows. Experimental validation throughout this study was performed using four PSC cell lines described above.

## Dissociation of organoids into single cells for single-cell RNA sequencing
ScRNA-seq data generated are derived from one differentiation batch with pooled samples of five to six organoids differentiated from the H7 A81.BRN3b.mCherry reporter line were collected for each condition in a 1.5 ml tube and washed twice with PBS. Retinal organoids were dissociated into single cell suspension using the Neurosphere Dissociation Kit (P) (Miltenyi Biotec; 130-095-943). Enzymatic digestion was performed as per manufacturer protocol for 10 min at 37 ◦C with intermittent agitation, followed by gentle mechanical dissociation, and a further incubation for 5 min at 37 ◦C. The cell suspension was further dissociated into single cells by pipetting before the enzymatic reaction was stopped by washing with HBSS. The cell suspension was filtered through MACS SmartStrainer 30μm (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-041-407) before being pelleted by centrifugation at 300 g for 10 min at room temperature. The cell pellet was resuspended in ALT90 and maintained on ice.

## Single cell RNA-sequencing procedure and data pre-processing
Each single cell suspension of dissociated retinal organoid was assessed for viability using 0.4% Trypan Blue staining on a Countess II Automated Cell Counter (Invitrogen) and concentration was adjusted to 1000 cells/µl. Cell suspension was loaded on a single-cell-G Chip (10x Genomics) for a target output of 10,000 cells per sample. Single-cell droplet capture was performed on the Chromium Controller (10x Genomics). cDNA library preparation was performed in accordance with the Single-Cell 3’ (v3.1 Dual Index) protocol. Libraries were evaluated for fragment size and concentration using Agilent HSD5000 ScreenTape System. Samples were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 instrument according to manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina). Sequencing was carried out using 2 × 150 paired-end (PE) configuration with a sequencing depth of 40,000 reads per cell. The sequences were then processed by Azenta, GENEWIZ. Basecalling was performed by Illumina RTA software in sequencer, further demultiplexing was performed by Illumina bcl2fastq 2.20. Counts were generated using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger (v7.2.0)84 and reads were mapped to the GRCh8 genome. Empty droplet filtering was performed using DropletUtils (v1.22.0)85. After standard cell filtering, doublet detection was performed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4)86. Gene expression counts for each cell were divided by the total counts for that cell and multiplied by a scale factor of 10,000 (Table 1). Normalised counts were then natural log-transformed with a pseudocount of 1. ## EdU assay
Click-iT™ Plus EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Thermofisher; C10632 [Flow Cytometry], C10640 [Imaging] was used to perform the EdU assay. The day before collection, organoids were incubated in 10 µM of EdU diluted into media for 16 h overnight before moving into fresh media before dissociation and fixation for flow cytometry or fixation for immunohistochemistry. Click-iT reactions were then performed as per manufacturer’s instructions on fixed dissociated cells or on sectioned slides prior to any staining.

## Confocal image acquisition
All images, except for spatial transcriptome slides, were acquired on a DMI8 Stellaris 8, a inverted laser scanning confocal microscope. A series of Z optical sections, approximately 1.0 μm apart, throughout the depth of the section were taken and combined into a stack to give a maximum projection image for images of organoid sections. For 3D imaging, optical sections and depth was variable. LAS X image software was used. Imaging for each batch of experiments was performed in close temporal proximity using consistent confocal settings throughout.

## Cell segmentation and quantification using CellPose
Cell segmentation was performed using a custom Python-based image analysis pipeline built around Cellpose39, a deep learning-based cell segmentation tool. We developed custom segmentation models specifically optimized for retinal organoid images by iteratively training on manually annotated datasets of nuclear and cytoplasmic/membrane markers across various retinal cell types88. Image processing involved generating maximum intensity projections, channel normalization, model-based segmentation, and boundary refinement. Optimized segmentation parameters were used for each cell type, with validation and confirmation subsequently performed by manual inspection (Fig. S3E). Measurements from segmented masks included morphological features, position data, and fluorescence intensities across channels which enabled a robust quantification of all images.
All cell quantifications in this study were performed using this method. Normalisation for cell proportions was performed against DAPI unless otherwise stated.

## Flow cytometry and analysis
H7 A81.BRN3b.mCherry and 149br.CRX.mCherry/WA09, CRX.tdTomato PSC-derived organoids were dissociated at various time points of culture into a single cell suspension using a Neurosphere Dissociation Kit as described in the dissociation for scRNAseq. Cells were resuspended in HBSS and SYTOX Green (Thermofisher; S34860) was added to the samples at a final concentration of 1 μM for 5 min at room temperature before running on the LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and subsequent analysis. For flow cytometry analysis of EdU+ cells, organoids were dissociated as above before fixing and permeabilisation prior to Click-iT reaction was performed as per manufacturer’s instructions.
Cells were analysed using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences). Small debris, cell fragments and aggregates were excluded from analysis by forward and side scatter (measuring cell size and granularity, respectively) whilst doublets were gated out using forward scatter area vs height. 15,000 events were captured on average per experiment. Background fluorescence was measured using age matched organoids derived from a control PSC line. These cells were used to set gating parameters between positive and negative populations whilst unstained controls were used to set gating parameters for Sytox staining. For EdU gating, stained samples showed non-specific staining compared to unstained samples so gating was performed to capture the small, brighter population of cells. Example representative scatterplots of gating strategies can be seen in (Fig. S10).

## Differential gene expression analysis of scRNA-sequencing data
To discover genes that are perturbed with varying RA dosage, differential gene expression between RA conditions for a given cell type was calculated using FindMarkers from the Seurat package (v5.2.1)43 using the Wilcox test. Relative expression is calculated as the log2FC using RA- as the baseline. Gene set enrichment analysis for each cell type between RA conditions was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (v1.30.0)89 against the Gene Ontology Biological Process gene sets using the top 100 differentially expressed genes ranked by average log2FC.
To assess the similarity in cell identity between cell types in the Zuo et al.21 foetal retina and the retinal organoids, cell-type specific differentially stable genes were independently calculated for each dataset using the Cepo package (v1.10.2)90 Genes that were shared between the foetal retina and retinal organoid samples were retained. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was computed to quantify the similarity between cell identity of Rods and Cones in the reference against the retinal organoids. 9 pcw was excluded as there were insufficient rod cells to reliably compute differential stability scores.

## Comparison of macula and peripheral gene expression between RA conditions
Putative marker genes for each cell type dissected from the macula and peripheral regions were obtained from Zuo et al. where a comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis of the developing human retina across 9 to 24 pcw was conducted. Using the curated list of marker genes from Zuo et al., a regression model was fitted for each cell type, with ‘location’ (macula vs. periphery) as the explanatory variable. A two-sided Wald test was then performed to assess whether regional location had a statistically significant effect on gene expression. Then, a negative binomial model was fitted to account for gene expression variations, incorporating both ‘location’ and ‘age’ as explanatory variables in the full model and ‘age’ in the reduced model. A likelihood ratio test was performed to identify DEGs due to location independent of age. The intersection between DEGs identified from the regression model and likelihood ratio test was taken to derive the final list of putative location-specific marker genes for each cell type.
To assess the degree of macula- or periphery-like identity across whole cell type populations, the region-specific marker genes derived for each cell type were leveraged. For cells assigned to each cell type, the mean log-normalised expression of the respective markers was calculated for each cell. Markers of the NRPC and PRPC subtypes were combined under the parent category ‘RPC’ to match the level of classification used in this study. This analysis was performed for each RA condition and statistical significance was calculated using a two-sided t-test.
To compare the gene expression of the above macula and periphery marker genes across conditions, we visualised for each condition the mean log-normalised counts and percentage of spots expressing the putative location marker for each cell type found in our spatial transcriptomics data. Macula and periphery markers were considered ‘shared’ if they were differentially expressed in more than one cell type in the list derived from Zuo et al. (Supplementary Data S4). Top representative markers chosen for visualisation were chosen based on criteria that they had an average log-normalised gene expression greater than 0.5 and were expressed in at least 20% of total spots in each condition.

## Single-cell atlas integration and cell type annotation of scRNA-sequencing data
Reference human retinal studies from Lukowski et al.91, Cowan et al.92, Lu et al.47, Menon et al.93, Sridhar et al.94, Gautam et al.95 were downloaded from the scEiaD ocular meta-atlas database44. Datasets were chosen based on sufficient coverage of retinal cell types within and across datasets. The integrated reference map was created using the default Seurat v5 integration pipeline43 under the R (v4.4.1) environment. k = 20 neighbours were used when using reciprocal PCA to determine anchors between datasets. Cell type labels from the original publications were used when aligning datasets.
Gene expression normalization, highly variable gene selection, scaling and PCA reduction was performed on each scRNA-sequencing sample as per the standard Seurat pipeline. Then, the transfer learning approach was applied to classify cells in the query scRNA-seq datasets based on the integrated human retinal reference. FindTransferAnchors() was run to identify shared features between the query and the reference, followed by TransferData() to assign cell type labels. The automated label transfer was performed after PCA and UMAP dimensionality reduction using the top 2000 highly variables genes rather than single marker genes.
To ensure high-confidence cell labels, we incorporated an additional step to assess the annotation quality after Seurat transfer labelling. Specifically, we used scReClassify96, a semi-supervised learning method for post-hoc identification and reclassification of mis-labelled cells. Briefly, an adaptive sampling approach was used wherein a support vector machine base classifier was iteratively trained to estimate the mislabelling probability of each cell. During each iteration, cells with a high mis-classification probability were excluded, and this improves the classification accuracy of the model by refining the quality of the cell type labels during training. An ensemble classification model was built by combining the predictions from multiple base classifiers. Finally, cell type labels with the highest prediction probability were assigned to each cell. Additionally, we performed sample correction using the default Seurat v5 integration pipeline43 and visualised the results to confirm that cell type clusters grouped consistently across samples.

## Spot deconvolution and cell type annotation of spatial transcriptomics
To estimate the cell type proportions for each spot on a Visium slide, we first identified the foetal retina developmental stage most comparable to 10-week retinal organoids. For each Visium slide, multiple organoids were pooled together as a single composite sample. For each slide and each foetal retina stage, the top 80% most highly variable genes were selected separately. Then, the gene expression was averaged across spots or cells to generate transcriptome-wide pseudo-bulk expression profiles. Pearson correlation was calculated using common genes between each Visium slide and each foetal developmental stage from the Zuo et al. retinal atlas. The developmental stage with the highest average correlation across all Visium slides was selected as the most appropriate reference. Then, the CARD algorithm97 with default spot and gene filtering parameters was used, keeping spatial locations where at least 100 counts were detected and including genes where at least expressed in 5 spots. A list containing the union of genes aggregated across organoids for each slide replicate in in Supplementary Data S5 as derived from the CARD algorithm.
To generate a gene expression matrix for retinal organoids profiled by Stereo-seq, UMIs from all DNBs were aggregated and summed. Thus, data collected at 16 and 26 weeks was analysed at bin60 resolution (30 μm x 30μm, approximately single cell) and bin100 resolution (50 μm × 50 μm), respectively. To annotate each spot at 16 weeks, matching 16 weeks scRNA-seq data was used as the reference for each condition and the Seurat transfer learning approach was applied to classify cells in the query scRNA-sequencing datasets using FindTransferAnchors() and TransferData(). To annotate the 26 weeks spatial data, previously generated in-house retinal organoids at 30 weeks derived from the UCLOOi017-A-1 (UCL) cell line was used to perform cell deconvolution with CARD97. Derivation of these retinal organoids is previously described in Kim et al.98 and Fernando et al.99. To visualise the dominant retinal cell populations across the entire Stereo-seq slide, the major cell type occupying each spot was assigned according to the largest proportion present.

## Inferred cell-cell communication analysis of spatial transcriptomics using CellChat
After cell deconvolution, the major cell type occupying each spot was assigned according to the largest proportion present prior to analysis. Cell-cell communication signalling pathways were analysed against the curated repositories of ligand-receptor interactions from CellChatDB (v122, v223), which included NeuronDB100, and CellPhoneDB (v4.1)101, including more than 3000 protein and non-protein interactions (e.g. metabolic and synaptic signaling). CellChat v2 has been updated to enable inference of spatially proximal cell-cell communication between interacting cell groups from spatially resolved transcriptomics. Our analysis was performed considering the physical distances between spots as recommended by the tutorial for spatial transcriptomics datasets. Communication probabilities were calculated for each organoid replicate slice, and interactions found in at least one organoid slice were kept. Chord diagrams displaying the inferred cell-cell signalling pathways was generated using netVisual_chord_gene().
Separately, for scRNA-seq at 16 weeks, stratified sampling was performed for each sample, retaining 500 randomly selected cells, or all cells if fewer than 500, to mitigate the impact of class imbalance. Relative proportion of cells was hence not considered due to the prior sampling approach when computing the communication probabilities. The inferred cell-cell signalling pathways was generated using netVisual_chord_gene(). netAnalysis_signalingRole_scatter() was used to visualise the total incoming and outgoing cell-cell interaction probabilities for each cell type across conditions. Information centrality was calculated using netAnalysis_computeCentrality() at pathway level, and the aggregated cell-cell communication network for each cell type using netAnalysis_signalingRole_heatmap(). Values were min-max scaled per row (ie. per signalling pathway) to enable cross-comparison.

## Statistical information
All means are presented ± SD (standard deviation); N, number of independent differentiations batches or experiments performed; n, number of images and organoids examined in total, where appropriate. Organoids were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Statistical analysis is based on at least three independent experiments. Representative images (e.g., confocal microscopy) are shown from one experiment, and similar results were observed in at least three independent experiments (differentiation batches) and independent biological replicates (organoids) across multiple cell lines.
Statistical significance of bar plots was assessed using Graphpad Prism 10 software while statistical significance of boxplots was assessed in R using stat_compare_means function from the ggpubr package and denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001. Appropriate statistical tests were applied including ANOVA with relevant post hoc correction; Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Exact p values and statistical tests are indicated in the figures or figure legends.
