A key amino acid site associated with rhodopsin mammal evolution to diurnal vision.
Fernández-Sampedro MA, Ramon E, Aguileta G, Bosch-Presegué L, Garriga P. (2025). Scientific reports. . doi:10.1038/s41598-025-29872-9. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# A key amino acid site associated with rhodopsin mammal evolution to diurnal vision

## Abstract
AbstractRhodopsin is a photoreceptor protein found in the vertebrate retina used as a landmark for vision evolution studies at the molecular level. Here, we examined the biochemical and functional performance of modern rhodopsin from three different mammal species— bovine, murine and human—to analyze their visual pigment evolutionary relationships. We selected these species for their relevance in vision research, their different position on the phylogenetic tree and their diverse ethology regarding nocturnal (mouse) and diurnal (bovine and human) life. We report on the important role of the amino acid at position 290 as a key player in the active rhodopsin conformation stability. Our spectroscopic analysis shows that the retinal release process for mouse rhodopsin (L290) is significantly slower, meaning a more stable and durable active state, compared to the human and bovine cases (I290). This finding is supported by the faster retinal release rate observed in the L290I mutant mouse rhodopsin, where the nocturnal mutated pigment behaved like diurnal rhodopsin. The result suggests a potential link between the amino acid at this position and the activity pattern (nocturnal or diurnal). This association was also observed when comparing the sequences of 79 mammal species at position 290, and better appreciated in more specialized primate and rodent orders. Moreover, we propose an evolutionary mechanism in rhodopsin specialization for diurnal and nocturnal life, implying a compromise between the prevalence of damage protection under bright light in nocturnal therian mammals (L290) and dark adaptation under dim light in diurnal therian mammals (I290).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29872-9.Keywords: Rhodopsin evolution, Visual pigments, Chromophore regeneration, Retinal release, Nocturnal vision, Molecular evolutionSubject terms: Biochemistry, Neuroscience, Zoology

## Introduction
Habitat adaptation is crucial for survival in response to changes in the immediate environment of the species, e.g., nocturnal or diurnal life due to temperature changes or the presence of predators. One feasible approach to provide insights into habitat adaptation towards diurnal or nocturnal life is to understand the differences in the biochemical and functional performance of visual pigments in different species. These findings will also provide novel clues for the molecular evolution of different species from their ancestors. For this purpose, combining sequence and phylogenetic analysis of visual pigments with experimental studies is an interesting approach. Human diseases related to the visual pigment rhodopsin are typically characterized using bovine rhodopsin as a prototypical model in structural and functional studies1. In vision studies, mouse rhodopsin has been widely used as a model for both in vitro and in vivo assessment2. Functional and biochemical studies from different mammal species must give us information on whether using bovine and murine models is a good approach of physiological relevance for studies regarding human visual diseases phenotypes and molecular mechanisms. In view of this, the comparison of human pigments with the behavior observed for the bovine and murine rhodopsins can provide new insights into structure–function relationships, showing substantial differences and deviations in both inactive conformation and activation mechanisms among different animal models3. Moreover, this characterization must provide important information regarding visual pigment evolution and nocturnal/diurnal life adaptation. Mouse, cow (bovine) and human rhodopsins, are positioned differently on the phylogenetic tree and exhibit different nocturnal/diurnal life ethology (or dominant temporal niche). Additionally, the analyzed phylogenetic tree reflects the past evolutionary differences, as previously described4. Mouse has been categorized as a nocturnal animal and not remarkable changes in the nocturnal behavior in its rodent ancestors have been reported4. On the other hand, human species is a diurnal mammal whose more distant ancestors were nocturnal, but it is still under discussion whether the ancestral primates (our closest ancestors) were nocturnal5,6 or not nocturnal7. However, cow is not totally diurnal and it has been classified as a “crepuscular, cathemeral or arrhythmic animal”4 or alternatively as “mostly diurnal with some crepuscular activity”8. Moreover, the shift from a nocturnal to a diurnal phenotype occurred at a closer time in the bovine ancestor than in the human case4.
Herein, the sequences of the three rhodopsins were analyzed, revealing that position 290 could have played an important role in the activity pattern (nocturnal or diurnal) of the different rhodopsins, comparing mouse rhodopsin (L290) to human and bovine species (I290). We have compared the amino acid located at position 290 for 79 mammal pigments phylogenetically differentiated, and a high correlation with the nocturnal and diurnal activity pattern was found. L290 is also found in the inferred ancestral therian sequence, supposed to be nocturnal.
Wild-type (WT) human, bovine and mouse, and L290I mouse rhodopsin were purified and further characterized. The analysis of the spectral and biochemical properties of the three rhodopsin species, and the effect of the L290I mutation in a nocturnal species background, such as mouse, suggests that the introduction of leucine at 290 position is an important adaptation in scotopic vision of early therian species, whereas L290I is one of the critical amino acid changes associated with diurnal niches along therian evolution. Furthermore, the L290I substitution is likely to be the result of independent analogous changes, as it appears in therian mammals of different phylogenetic branches that had independently readapted to diurnal vision.

## Results

## Sequence and phylogenetic analysis
Major variants in the visual pigments system can be detected in nocturnal versus non-nocturnal animals, therefore the three corresponding sequences (human, bovine and murine) were aligned to pinpoint the amino acids that are equal in both human and cow, but different in mouse (Fig. 1A). The sequence variations found from nocturnal to diurnal niche in these species were V158A, I162V, F260A, L290I, S299A, L309M, D332E and A337V. Among them, positions 260, 290 and 299 are closely associated with the retinal chromophore binding pocket by either direct or indirect interactions through other amino acid residues9.

We compared positions 260, 290 and 299 in 79 phylogenetically distinct mammalian pigments (accession numbers shown in Supplementary Table S1), and observed a strong association with the nocturnal/diurnal activity pattern and the amino acid exclusive to position 290 (Table 1). Nocturnal mammals predominantly present leucine at position 290, while diurnal mammals primarily exhibit isoleucine in their pigments (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, L290 is also found in the inferred ancestral therian sequence, which is supposed to be nocturnal (Table 1). Furthermore, position 290 was found to be positively selected in the evolutionary branch from early mammals to therian species10. Based on the rationale, the context and the methodology outlined in a preliminary report11, we studied position 290 as a relevant site for nocturnality. Some species were difficult to classify because they do not show a clear nocturnal or diurnal activity pattern, therefore, aquatic and subterranean species were excluded from the analysis.

## Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopic characterisation of rhodopsin
Cloned rhodopsin genes from human, bovine, and mouse were expressed in COS-1 cells and immunopurified as described under Materials and Methods. The purified proteins were analyzed at 20 °C by means of UV–vis spectrophotometry in the wavelength range from 250 to 650 nm. Some differences can be detected in the visible chromophoric band wavelength maximum (λmax) of the purified protein spectra (Fig. 3). Besides the common band representing the opsin apoprotein for the three species at 280 nm, the λmax of the spectral chromophoric characteristic band in the visible region is slightly shifted. Thus, human rhodopsin spectra show λmax at 495 nm, mouse at 502 nm and bovine at 498 nm, pointing to subtle differences in the retinal binding pocket of the three pigments. To check the role of position 290 in the nocturnal/diurnal activity pattern, we constructed L209I mutation in the mouse opsin gene by site-directed mutagenesis. The UV–visible spectroscopic characterization of this mutant shows a slight blue-shift of 2 nm to 500 nm, when compared to WT mouse rhodopsin. The photobleaching and acidification behavior across all analyzed rhodopsins did not show relevant differences, as shown in the UV–vis spectra (Fig. 4).

## Dark state thermal stability
The kinetics of the thermal stability decay process were measured at 48 °C by following the decrease in the absorbance at λmax (Amax) for each protein, over time. The temperature used on the thermal decay experiments, 48 °C, was selected according to previous experimental studies14. The relative thermal decay half-life (t1/2) of human WT rhodopsin was about 3 times longer than that of the bovine WT, similar to previously observed results (Fig. 5)15 (human WT t1/2 = 84.5 ± 6.6 min and bovine WT t1/2 = 27.8 ± 6.8 min). Mouse rhodopsin (65.8 ± 5.4 min) showed about 2.4-fold higher thermal stability than bovine rhodopsin, which is also a similar relative thermal decay half-life to that obtained in a previous study16.
Thermal stability of purified rhodopsin proteins at 48 °C. Average and S.E, with the normalized value in brackets (bovine WT taken as 1.0) are the following: human WT: t1/2 = 84.5 ± 6.6 min (3.1), mouse WT: t1/2 = 65.8 ± 5.4 min (2.4), bovine WT: t1/2 = 27.8 ± 6.8 min (1.0).

## Dark state chemical stability
In the dark state, the protonated Schiff base linkage between 11-cis-retinal and opsin, in bovine rhodopsin, is not reactive to hydroxylamine at neutral pH, due to a compact intradiscal conformation and a well-protected retinal binding site. However, in the activated state, Meta II, the ligand binding pocket is readily accessible so the hydroxylamine quickly cleaves the Schiff base linkage17,18 and rapidly forms a stable retinal oxime with all-trans-retinal that absorbs around 360 nm19,20.
The chemical reactivity in the dark state was measured as the reduction in λmax in the visible spectral region over time, as described under Materials and Methods. Hydroxylamine reactivity of the human WT rhodopsin (0.001306 min−1) is quite similar with a ratio of 1.1 ratio when compared to that of bovine WT (0.00118 min−1) meanwhile mouse WT (0.001950 min−1) is slightly faster, a 1.6 ratio when compared to bovine rhodopsin.

## Retinal release rate
The decay of the active state Meta II conformation was monitored by following the retinal release from the binding pocket. This process involves the hydrolysis of the Schiff base bond and subsequent release of all-trans-retinal from the pocket21. We have determined the Meta II decay process, and the associated retinal release, by means of a fluorometric assay in which tryptophan fluorescence increase is recorded over time after illumination as described under Materials and Methods. Mouse rhodopsin showed an almost twofold slower retinal release process (t1/2 = 25.76 ± 0.37 min) than that obtained for the other two species (Fig. 6). When comparing human (t1/2 = 12.09 ± 0.72 min) and bovine retinal release (t1/2 = 13.90 ± 0.80 min), human rhodopsin apparently has a slightly slower t1/2 although our statistical analysis did not find it to be significant (p > 0.05). However, a similar difference was found to be significant for human and bovine rhodopsin in a previous study15. Besides, this small difference is consistent with a previous study where Meta II decay of human rhodopsin was faster when compared to bovine22. Our results show faster retinal release (reflecting a faster Meta II decay) in human WT (diurnal species) and bovine WT rhodopsin (diurnal/crepuscular species), in contrast with the slower rate found for mouse WT (nocturnal species) retinal release. Interestingly, the L290I mutation introduced in the mouse rhodopsin background reduces twofold the half-life time, reverting the retinal release rate from 25.76 ± 0.37 min of WT mouse rhodopsin to a 14.61 ± 1.55 min, similar to the values obtained for bovine and human WT rhodopsin.
Meta II decay of photoactivated purified rhodopsin samples at 20 °C. The t1/2 of retinal release was measured. Illumination was carried out at t = 0 min. Average data and S.E. are the following: human WT: t1/2 = 12.09 ± 0.72 min, bovine WT: t1/2 = 13.9 ± 0.8 min, mouse WT: t1/2 = 25.76 ± 0.37 min, mouse L290I: t1/2 = 14.61 ± 1.55 min.

## Discussion

## Genotype–phenotype relationship
From our sequence analysis, we found three amino acid positions, 260, 290 and 299 that may be relevant in rhodopsin evolution (Fig. 1A).
The most relevant change related to nocturnality is the amino acid substitution at position 290, which was found to be positively selected in the evolutionary branch from early mammals leading to therian species10. This change could be explained by ecological diversification during the Mesozoic era, which corresponds to the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck4,23,24 and correlates with a faster Meta II decay rate. Our amino acid sequence analysis at position 290, in 79 phylogenetically differentiated mammal pigments, reveals a strong association with the nocturnal/diurnal activity pattern and the leucine to isoleucine amino acid substitution, where L290 is also found in the inferred rhodopsin sequence of ancestral therian species, which are supposed to have a nocturnal activity pattern (Table 1 and Fig. 1B).
In the case of primates (Fig. 2A), although the diurnal or nocturnal nature of the common ancestor is still a debatable matter, previous studies have pointed to the theory of a nocturnal activity pattern in the common ancestor12,13], In this theory, the evolution to diurnal pattern would be produced whether in the ancestor of Haplorrini (where the Tarsius species, would have been secondary readapted to nocturnality) or in the ancestor of Simiiformes. In any case, a secondary return to nocturnality, in species from genus Aotus13 and Carlito (tarsier), is widely accepted25. Although apparently following markedly independent paths in the adaptation of their scotopic vision25, and exhibiting different geographical distributions, both species possess L290. In rodents (Fig. 2B), independent adaption to diurnality from a nocturnal ancestor4 is exemplified by the genus Marmota and Octodon, and despite differing geographical distributions, both possess I290.
Another diurnal organism, the Zebrafish (Danio rerio), was found to have an L290 residue in the rhodopsin sequence, however the phylogenetic evolution and physiological differences with mammals, such as body temperature, make direct comparisons with mammal rhodopsin sequence difficult. Moreover, L290I change in zebrafish rhodopsin, resulted in a faster active Meta II state decay26. This result would be consistent with an amino acid change from early therian (nocturnal) to nowadays species with a diurnal activity pattern phenotype. Changes in the rate of retinal release were found to depend primarily on the chromophore positioning inside the binding pocket, and on the accessibility of bulk water during Schiff base hydrolysis27. These results indicate that L at position 290 could be one of the important adaptations in scotopic vision of early therian species, meanwhile L substitution to I could be a critical amino acid change associated with diurnal activity pattern niches along therian evolution. Furthermore, the L290I substitution is likely to be caused by independent analogous changes, because it appears in therian mammal species of different phylogenetic branches that had independently readapted to diurnal vision (Table 1).

## Functional and biochemical characterization of modern rhodopsin
In order to analyze the visual pigment evolution at the molecular level, we have conducted a functional and biochemical characterization. Recombinant purified rhodopsins were analyzed by means of UV–vis spectrophotometry, and slightly differences were observed in λmax of the visible chromophoric band (Fig. 3). Thus, human rhodopsin shows a λmax at 495 nm, mouse at 502 nm and bovine at 498 nm. These results suggest that subtle differences are present in the retinal binding pocket of the three pigments.
Previous studies have emphasized the important role of the retinal release process in both the molecular basis of vision and the visual process adaptation to different environments28,29. According to our Meta II stability analyses (Fig. 6), a relation may be inferred between the retinal release performance of the human, bovine and mouse rhodopsin and the nocturnality/diurnality activity patterns of these three species. Thus, human WT and bovine WT show a faster Meta II decay (t1/2 = 12.09 ± 0.72 and t1/2 = 13.9 ± 0.8 min, respectively) whereas mouse WT exhibits a more stable Meta II active state intermediate with a twofold longer retinal release process (t1/2 = 25.76 ± 0.37 min). Other studies involving immunopurified rhodopsin have resulted in a Meta II decay rate of 33 min in dogs 30, a cathemeral domestic animal that probably preserves nocturnal/crepuscular characteristics from its recent wolf ancestor 31, and 23.6 min for the hippopotamus28, a specie with a peak activity during night-time and a retina specialized to enhance dim-light vision compared with other animals of the same order32.
An interesting study, recreated the inferred ancestral rhodopsin of early amniote, mammalian and therian species. The obtained Meta II decay half-life times were 16.5 min, 22.5 min and 27.5 min, respectively10. The higher Meta II active state stability of mammalian and therian rhodopsins, could be related to the hypothesized preceding mesopic, or intermediate light conditions, bottleneck affecting early mammals4, or a nocturnal bottleneck to avoid predators with daytime activities10. This interpretation is supported by another study implicating different visual genes where an increased adaptation to dim-light conditions was observed in the case of ancestral mammals towards the branch to theria from an inferred diurnal amniota33. Therefore, the findings reported from both inferred ancestral rhodopsin and early amniota, mammalia and theria visual pigments are consistent with our results and with the proposed relationship between retinal release and nocturnal/diurnal activity patterns.
Meta II active state decay is related to the deactivation of the rhodopsin visual signal. However, the physiological meaning of the long-lasting Meta II state, and its evolutionary meaning, is unclear10,16. In fact, a simple explanation cannot be easily found, as studies conducted in rod cells have shown that arrestin, a protein responsible for rhodopsin desensitization, produces a very fast shut-off of the pigment signal after rhodopsin phosphorylation by rhodopsin kinase34, in the order of 80 ms after dim light illumination35, or 36 ms in theoretical studies36, despite the background signal generated by the phosphorylated and arrestin-bound photoproducts37. These shut-off times highly differ from reported Meta II decay times35,38, even when we consider the stability conferred for the detergent and the temperature used in our measurements, in contrast to the native physiological conditions. In this sense, the shut-off was observed in the order of 3 s after illumination and in the absence of rhodopsin kinase39, or in the order of 50 s and in the absence of arrestin40. Thus, the earlier suggested effect of a slower retinal release rate, increasing Gt activation by a long-lived Meta II conformation, and consequently increasing rod sensitivity41, was found to be improbable due to the fast shut-off produced by the Rho kinase and arrestin35 that was found to be faster than the Meta II decay under physiological conditions39. A deeper analysis, using in vivo approaches, is needed to unravel the physiological meaning of these photoproducts relative half-lives related to their belonging to nocturnal/diurnal activity pattern niches.
At this juncture, it is important to emphasize that the overall amount of photoactivated Rho molecules exposed to comparable light intensity varies among species and reflects the diurnal/nocturnal vision patterns. The necessary light intensity for bleaching 50% of rhodopsin in the mouse retina is approximately 100–200 cd/m2, comparable to standard room illumination. However, the intensity required to achieve the same degree of rhodopsin bleaching in the human retina is around 25,000 cd/m2, akin to intense sunshine42. Mammals have developed various physiological and morphological adaptations to achieve these differential levels. Diurnal animals typically possess large retinas and small pupils, whereas nocturnal animals exhibit smaller retinas and larger pupils4. Additionally, adaptations include a greater presence of cones in diurnal species and increased rod density, along with the presence of the tapetum, a tissue behind the retina that reflects light back to the photoreceptors, in nocturnal species8.
Some evolutionary adaptations for nocturnal vision have primarily concentrated on maximizing light intensity reaching the rod outer segments (ROS) and, consequently, the visual pigments to improve photoresponse in low-light conditions. Animals adapted for dark vision exhibit increased susceptibility to light-induced damage, while diurnal species demonstrate greater resistance to light exposure43. This was evidenced by experiments comparing diurnal cone-dominant rodents with rod-dominant rats and mice44. A comprehensive comparison of diurnal macaques and nocturnal owl monkeys (Aotus), both of which are rod-dominant primates, reveals that under identical light exposure, the retinal damage was severe in the nocturnal species, while the diurnal species experienced minimal harm44. Additionally, nocturnal animals are not exclusively limited to scotopic light conditions; they can exhibit occasional activity during the day, as for example, they tend to have eye lenses transmitting UV4. This suggests the potential presence of distinct physiological and molecular protection mechanisms in nocturnal species, such us the presence of a UV-absorbing compound in the aqueous humor of the eye, i.e. ascorbic acid4.
In addition, one of the harmful key factors implicated in the toxicity of the visual system process is the accumulation of all-trans-retinal45,46. The all-trans-retinal excess is suggested to cause a toxic effect closely related to that observed in age-related retinal macular degeneration and other retinal diseases47–49. On the other hand, all-trans-retinal was demonstrated to re-bind to the binding pocket of half of the phosphorylated opsin population when Meta II was stabilized by arrestin42. The entrance was similarly noted in the instance of purified pigment in detergent solution50. Interestingly, higher concentrations of monomeric arrestin in mouse, were found in the ROS under dark conditions when compared to human38.
Hence, considering all these facts, the slower Meta II decay found in nocturnal mammals could also help to improve the protection of rod cells by buffering all-trans-retinal concentration, as arrestin would stabilize half of the photoactivated rhodopsin pool42. In contrast, a slower decay of photoproducts would result in a slower regeneration rate that could respond less accurately to light changes and movement detection21,51. Thus, these differences in Meta II release rates seen in diurnal mammals may be linked with the beneficial effect of a faster dark adaptation under dim-light conditions10 and a more relaxed pressure in the protective necessity. This could bring faster adaptation also when moving from a bright light environment to a dim light one.
Analysis of rhodopsin sequences highlights the possible role of the amino acid in position 290 in nocturnal or diurnal activity patterns in therian mammals, especially in the primate and rodent orders (Table 1 and Fig. 2), suggesting that this location deserves a deeper analysis. The experimental results of Meta II stability show a twofold lower retinal release half-life for the L290I mutant (Fig. 6) compared to mouse WT, suggesting that this mutation on a nocturnal pigment background acquired a behavior similar to a diurnal rhodopsin, such as bovine rhodopsin.

## Structural analysis
Regarding the structural correlate of our results, it should be emphasized that there are many examples where one or a few amino acid substitutions can generate a functional change representing an adaptative response52. However, the observed change in decay rate is striking in our case, since the L290I change represents a conservative substitution from a physicochemical standpoint. The main difference in our case should be sought in the position of the branching of the side chain in isoleucine allowing specific interactions with surrounding amino acids.
Despite the location near the opening between TM helix 1 and 7, the proposed retinal entry site53, position 290 is also proximal to the Schiff base linkage environment in the three-dimensional activated rhodopsin26 (Fig. 7A). As observed in the active Meta II conformation, these positions correspond to F287, F293 and F294, as well as neighboring positions T289 and P291. Meanwhile, in the rhodopsin dark state conformation, these positions correspond mainly to F286, T289, P291 and F294 and neighboring T289 and P291. These amino acids are also highly conserved in all the 79 analyzed therian mammal species (except minor variations corresponding to L287). In fact, T289P was found to be a mutation causing retinitis pigmentosa (RP) in humans54. Also, comparison of dark state versus Meta II conformation contact surface shows a closer contact of amino acid at position 290 with aromatic amino acids at positions 287, 293 and 294 (Fig. 7B and C).

Importantly, as the retinal chromophore is covalently bound, in the inactive dark state, to K296 in TM7, changes in the interaction energy affecting the Schiff base linkage, upon retinal isomerization, may be determinant for the retinal release rates. In this way, in the dark state, F293 presents a strong interaction with the K296, and a weaker interaction with F29455. F293 is located in a network including position 39 at TM1, site of a natural mutation causing RP (M39R). This fact, together with M39 location near the proposed entry retinal opening, has been related to a faster retinal release rate observed for the M39R RP mutation56.

## Conclusions
In summary, the L290I mutation has been shown to cause an important change in the retinal release rate of mouse rhodopsin compared to human and bovine visual pigments. Besides, there may be an important relation between activity pattern (nocturnal or diurnal) and the amino acid at this position, which is more evident in the more specialized orders of primates and rodents. We propose a systematic explanation for the observed difference in Meta II decay in nocturnal versus diurnal therian mammals by inferring slower Meta II kinetics in species with L290 and faster kinetics in species with I290. Although nocturnal animals tend to maximize the light (including UV wavelengths) that reaches their rod cells for vision in dim-light conditions, they can occasionally be exposed to bright, potentially harmful light, requiring protective mechanisms. For instance, the sequestration of all-trans-retinal by a more stable Meta II photointermediate arises as a possible mechanism, even at the cost of a worse dark adaptation. On the other hand, diurnal animals tend to better protect their visual system by different processes that limit the amount of bright light arriving at rod cells and consequently rhodopsin. Rod regeneration is limited in bright light environments by the supply of fresh 11-cis-retinal. When diurnal animals are under dim-light conditions, the limitation relies on all-trans-retinal release from photoactivated rhodopsin, expecting a faster visual cycle regeneration and thus an improved dark adaptation.
In conclusion, a compromise between the prevalence of damage protection under bright light in nocturnal therian mammals (L290), and dark adaptation under dim light in diurnal therian mammals (I290) is proposed to have an important influence on rhodopsin specialization (Fig. 8). However, it should be noted that some animals exhibit less clear activity patterns due to low visual specialization or a high degree of plasticity under both nocturnal or diurnal conditions (such as cathemeral animals). Additionally, other amino acids, other than 290, may contribute to adaptation to different light conditions through epistatic interactions.
Schematic representation of the proposed relationship between the amino acid at position 290 in rhodopsin and vision specialization. Nocturnal species maximize environmental light transmission to photoreceptors, by different methods, in contrast with diurnal species. In nocturnal species, all-trans-retinal sequestration by a more stable Meta II photointermediate associated with the presence of L290 arises as a possible adaptation to a damage protection mechanism under bright light. In diurnal species, an improved dark adaptation as a result of faster all-trans-retinal release related with the I290 would be expected.

## Materials and methods

## Sequence alignment and phylogenetic trees
Amino acid alignment of WT rhodopsin in three different species: mouse, human and cow (bovine) was done using CLUSTALW. Nocturnal (mouse) and non-nocturnal (human and bovine) rhodopsin amino acid differences are highlighted in grey (Fig. 1A). Phylogenetic trees (Fig. 2) of primate (A) and rodent (B) orders were constructed with species analyzed in Table 1. The 79 therian rhodopsin sequences were obtained from NCBI GenBank and Uniprot. Amino acid residues in position 290 (corresponding to the position 7.37 in the Ballesteros classification57) are shown on the right side of each species.
To build both trees, all the primate and rodent rhodopsin amino acid sequences of the species listed in Table 1were aligned using MEGA 1258 with default parameters. The aligned sequences were used to generate a phylogenetic tree by Maximum Likelihood method. The empirical nucleotide equilibrium frequencies were estimated, assuming the GTR model (GTR, Generalized Time Reversible, the most general neutral, independent, finite-sites, time-reversible model possible) with four among-site rate categories. Bootstrap sampling with 100 replicates was implemented to infer node support values. We rooted the phylogeny using Ictidomys tridecemlineatus (Fig. 2).

## Opsin genes and mouse L290I mutant
Mouse rhodopsin gene cloned into the pIRes eGfp vector was kindly provided by Dr. Elvir Becirovic (Ludwig- Maximilians-University of Munich) and cloned human Rho gene into the pCDNA3 vector was kindly provided by Dr. Robert J. Lucas (University of Manchester). Synthetic bovine rhodopsin gene has been cloned into the pMT4 vector59.
In order to introduce the selected mutation into the mouse opsin gene, site-directed mutagenesis was used on the original gene. The oligonucleotide primers used are Fwd: CGGCCCCATCTTCATGACTATCCCAGCTTTCTTTGCTAAGAG, Rev: CTCTTAGCAAAGAAAGCTGGGATAGTCATGAAGATGGGGCCG (Sigma, Spain).
The mutated genes were obtained by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification employing Pfu Ultra II Fusion DNA polymerase (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with the site-directed mutagenesis QuikChange II protocol (Stratagene). At the end of the reaction, 1 µl DpnI restriction enzyme (10 U/µl, Thermo Fisher Scientific- LifeTechnologies, Spain) digestion was performed with 2 h incubation at 37 °C in order to eliminate the methylated template vector. Then the DNA was precipitated by 1 h incubation at -80 °C with 100 µl ethanol 100% and 5 µl sodium acetate (pH 5.5) and subsequently subject to centrifugation. Finally, the precipitated DNA was resuspended and transformed into competent DH5α E coli cells.

## COS-1 cell line (monkey kidney cells)
COS-1 cells (ATCC, LGC standards, Barcelona, Spain) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Lab clinics, Spain) and split every 48–60 h by removing the old media and washing the plates with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4) to remove the fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma, Spain). Then, PBS was added again with 0.8–1% of trypsin for cell splitting, and the plates were kept at room temperature for 4–5 min until the cells started to detach helped by gently pipetting. Finally, new media with FBS that stops the trypsin effect was added, and the contents were divided into new plates with fresh media60.

## Protein expression in eukaryotic COS-1 cells
The expression and purification were performed as previously described60. Plasmids encoding the opsin genes were transiently transfected into COS-1 cells in 150 mm dishes at ≃85% confluence, by using 0.1 mL of polyethyleneimine 25 kDa (PEI, Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) and 30 μg of plasmid DNA per plate in 3 mL Opti-MEM (Life Technologies, Madrid, Spain). Cells were harvested 48 – 60 h after transfection, and double-washed with PBS by centrifugation for 20 min at 4, 000 rpm. Then, the cells were either stored at -80 °C or incubated with exogenous 11-cis-retinal for further visual pigment purification.

## Recombinant protein purification
From this point, all experiments were conducted in the dark or under dim red light (Kodak number 2 filter). The pigments were reconstituted with 10 μM 11-cis-retinal (kindly provided by Dr. R. Crouch (National Eye Institute, National Institutes of Health, USA) in PBS by nutating overnight at 4 °C in intact cells (chromophore stock solution was prepared in 100% absolute ethanol). Then, the cells were solubilised for 1 h with 1% (w/v) n-Dodecyl-D-maltoside (DM, Anatrace, Maumee, OH, USA) in PBS pH 7.4, with PMSF and protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, Spain). This solubilised sample was ultracentrifuged at 35,000 rpm for 35 min, and the pellet was discarded. The pigments were purified by immunoaffinity chromatography on 1D4-Sepharose 4B (mAb rho-1D4 (Cell Essentials, Boston, MA, USA). For this, the supernatant was incubated for 3 h by gently nutating at 4 °C. All procedures, up to and including binding of the receptor to the immunoaffinity matrix, were performed at 4 °C. Therefore, subsequent washes and elutions were performed using cold buffer on ice. The resin was washed four times with PBS 0.05% DM. The receptors were eluted for 2 h in the same buffer containing 100 μM rho-1D4 9-mer peptide (TETSQVAPA, synthesized by Serveis Cientificotècnics (Universitat de Barcelona, Barcelona, Spain). The elutions were repeated three times for each purification in order to obtain more purified protein60.

## UV–vis spectrophotometric rhodopsin characterisation
All measurements were made on a Cary 100 Bio spectrophotometer (Varian), equipped with water-jacketed cuvette holders connected to a circulating water bath. Temperature was controlled by a Peltier accessory connected to the spectrophotometer. All spectra were recorded in the 250 nm-650 nm range with a bandwidth of 1 nm, a response time of 0.1 s, and a scan speed of 600 nm/min. Purified samples were photobleached with a 150 Watt power light using a 495 nm cut-off filter for 30 s; an absorption spectrum was immediately recorded. Acidification was performed immediately after photobleaching by the addition of 2N H2SO4 to a final sample pH of 1–2 and the acidified spectrum was measured after 1 min, in order to let the protein spectrum stabilize60.

## Thermal and chemical stability of rhodopsin
Rhodopsin thermal stability in the dark was studied by means of UV–vis spectroscopy at 48 °C by monitoring the rate of absorbance loss at λmax, and the increase of A380 as a function of time. Scan spectra from 250 to 650 nm were monitored every 2.5 min60. The chemical stability was measured by adding hydroxylamine (NH2OH, Sigma, Spain) to the sample and monitoring the decrease in the λmax absorbance band with time at 20 °C. NH2OH from a stock of 1 M adjusted to pH 7, was added to the sample to a final concentration of 50 mM and successive scan spectra were recorded every 2.5 min to measure the loss of Amax and formation of stable oxime with all-trans-retinal, around A36020,56. In both experiments, samples were kept in the dark until the end of the experiment when they were photobleached. For each experiment, three different replicates were carried out. In addition, all the measured spectra were normalized to a value of A = 0 at 650 nm.

## Meta II decay determined by fluorescence spectroscopy
Meta II decay was recorded by fluorescence spectroscopy at 20 °C, as previously described17,60. All fluorescence spectra were carried out by exciting the samples for 2 s at 295 nm, with a bandwidth slit of 0.5 nm and measuring Trp emission at 330 nm16. Rhodopsin in PBS 0.05% DM was kept at 20 °C until a stable baseline was obtained and subsequently illuminated for 30 s. The t1∕2 values for the Trp fluorescence increase (resulting from retinal release from the receptor) were derived from fitting the obtained curves to an exponential function. For each experiment, three different replicates were carried out.

## Statistical analysis
ANOVA was used to test differences between the analyzed rhodopsins.
