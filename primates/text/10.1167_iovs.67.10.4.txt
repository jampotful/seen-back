# Ocular Component Growth From the Cornea to the Sclera in Non-Human Primate Emmetropization

## Abstract
PurposeTo quantify ocular parameters and their relationships during normal ocular development in rhesus monkeys.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included rhesus monkeys with a normal visual experience and no experimental interventions (N = 210). Retinoscopy, biometry (Lenstar), optical coherence tomography (OCT; SPECTRALIS), and tonometry were performed in both eyes at age 24 ± 2 days. Twelve monkeys were followed longitudinally up to age 362 days. OCT scans were analyzed for retinal, choroidal, and scleral thickness. Data were analyzed using t-tests and linear mixed-effects models to assess sex and age effects. Associations between parameters were examined.ResultsAt baseline, there were no significant interocular differences in spherical equivalent refraction (P = 0.30) or axial length (P = 0.98). Females weighed less than males and exhibited thinner central corneas, greater corneal powers, shallower anterior chamber depths, thinner lenses, and shorter axial lengths (P < 0.05 for all). Longitudinal analyses showed that spherical equivalent refraction became less hyperopic with age, but axial length and retinal, choroidal, and scleral thickness increased in a non-linear manner. Choroidal and scleral thickness showed significant variability across monkeys with modest correlation coefficients.ConclusionsThis study established normative in vivo trajectories of ocular parameters, including retinal, choroidal, and scleral thickness, during early development. Rhesus monkeys were initially hyperopic and underwent coordinated refractive and structural changes consistent with early emmetropization. Refraction, axial length, and retinal thickness followed expected patterns, but choroidal and scleral thickness exhibited substantial variability and only modest associations with age.
Across many species, including humans, refraction is typically hyperopic at birth with a wide distribution among individuals.1–4 Early postnatal development is characterized by a coordinated process of ocular growth in which visual feedback guides optical power and axial length toward optimal retinal focus.1,2,5–7 During this period, the two eyes grow in a synchronized manner, progressively shifting refraction toward emmetropia.4,7–11 This process of emmetropization involves coupled structural and optical changes of the cornea, anterior chamber depth, crystalline lens, and axial length.11,12 Deviations from this coordinated growth are associated with the development of refractive errors. Thus, defining normal trajectories of these ocular parameters is essential for understanding emmetropization and the genesis of refractive errors.
Acquiring detailed ocular measurements in human infants is challenging. Given the close anatomical similarities between non-human primate and human eyes, rhesus monkeys provide a highly relevant model for studying early ocular development.13–17 Therefore, primate models offer a valuable opportunity to characterize how optical components contribute to the earliest phases of emmetropization. Primate studies have been instrumental in defining how visual experience influences eye growth.1,18 For example, experimental manipulations of image quality in infant monkeys confirmed that emmetropization is visually guided, with defocus inducing predictable, direction-specific changes in axial elongation and refraction.19–21
Many prior studies of refractive development in non-human primates have relied on control eyes from experimental paradigms in which visual input was manipulated in one eye, raising the possibility that such interventions may have influenced otherwise normal growth patterns in the contralateral eye.22 Longitudinal data describing typical, unmanipulated ocular development are comparatively limited. Qiao-Grider et al.15 characterized normal eye growth from infancy to adulthood in rhesus monkeys with unaltered vision in both eyes, with a focus on anterior ocular structures and their relationships with axial length. Although anterior ocular changes during emmetropization are now relatively well described,15,23–28 developmental changes in posterior ocular structures are less well characterized, particularly for the choroid29,30 and sclera.31 A recent longitudinal study using optical coherence tomography (OCT) in rhesus monkeys assessed postnatal retinal maturation and demonstrated significant increases in retinal thickness and changes in foveal morphology over the first year of life.21 In myopia, excessive axial elongation is characterized by thinning and remodeling of the choroid and sclera,32–35 but retinal thickness remains relatively stable.36 Therefore, defining normal choroidal and scleral thickness changes with age, particularly during early development when axial length is rapidly increasing, is critical for interpreting structural alterations associated with myopia.
Prior longitudinal non-human primate studies of normal growth relied on A-scan ultrasound measurements to estimate axial parameters, an approach with relatively limited precision and resolution for distinguishing individual posterior tissue layers.37–41 Advances in ophthalmic imaging now enable substantially more precise, layer-specific, in vivo measurements using optical biometry and OCT. The present study leveraged these high-resolution modalities to quantify on-axis ocular structure from the cornea to the sclera during early postnatal development in rhesus monkeys. By establishing normative trajectories of anterior and posterior ocular parameters and examining their relationships during early emmetropization, this work provides a comprehensive in vivo framework for coordinated primate eye growth that is directly translatable to human visual development.

## Methods
This retrospective study included data from 210 infant rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta). Monkeys were obtained at approximately 2 weeks of age and reared in a primate nursery, as described previously.19,38,42 Cross-sectional baseline data were analyzed for all monkeys, and longitudinal data were available for 12 representative monkeys (Fig. 1) for up to 362 days. Monkeys were reared under fluorescent lights (correlated color temperature = 3500K; Philips TL735; Philips Lighting, Eindhoven, The Netherlands), maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, with an average cage-level illuminance during the daily light cycle of 480 lux (range, 342–688). All monkeys had unrestricted vision for both eyes. Rearing and experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Houston and adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

## Ocular Measurements
Baseline cross-sectional data were collected at 24 ± 2 days of age. For all monkeys, there were no indications of ocular pathology, and the ocular media were clear. All measurements were taken between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM to minimize the effects of diurnal variations. Intraocular pressure (IOP), refraction, biometry, and OCT imaging were measured at baseline, and, for longitudinal monkeys, they were subsequently repeated every 2 weeks for 5 months and every 1 to 2 months thereafter. Refraction, anterior segment parameters, axial length, and, in some cases, choroidal thickness have been presented for these monkeys as part of other studies, where they served as controls.42–46
For each measurement session, monkeys were sedated with an intramuscular injection of 30-mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride and 0.3-mg/kg acepromazine. Eyes were cyclopleged with two drops of 1% tropicamide approximately 20 minutes apart. IOP was measured using an iCare TONOVET tonometer (iCare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland). Three measurements, each an average of six individual readings, were collected and averaged. Refraction was measured by two experienced examiners using streak retinoscopy, which was defined as the mean spherical equivalent of the spectacle plane refractive correction for a vertex distance of 14 mm. Previous analysis in our lab for one cohort of monkeys showed a 95% limit of agreement of approximately ±0.60 diopter (D) for central spherical equivalent refractive errors with good agreement between examiners.47 A non-contact biometer (Lenstar LS 900; Haag-Streit, Köniz, Switzerland) was used to assess corneal power, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and axial length, measured from the anterior corneal surface to the inner limiting membrane (ILM). The display resolution of the Lenstar biometer is 10 µm. Axial length was recorded in the Lenstar short eye mode, and axial separations for each measurement were manually delineated by adjusting the cursor positions in the optical A-scan, as described previously.38 At least five biometric measurements were captured for each eye and averaged.
For OCT imaging, a custom-made gas-permeable contact lens was inserted to maintain optical surface quality. Retinal, choroidal, and scleral thicknesses were derived from spectral-domain OCT using a SPECTRALIS imaging platform (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) and the enhanced depth imaging mode of the instrument to improve the visibility of the retinal, choroidal, and scleral layers. Two horizontal 30° line scans with 100-frame averaging centered on the fovea were captured at each time point for each eye.48 Axial resolution of the SPECTRALIS OCT is approximately 3 µm.49 Raw data (*.vol files) were exported and analyzed using custom semi-automated MATLAB software (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), which further enhanced the visibility of the retinal, choroidal, and scleral interface.50,51 A lateral magnification correction based on each monkey's biometry was applied, accounting for contact lens parameters that were used for OCT scan acquisition.52 This custom software automatically segmented the internal limiting membrane, Bruch's membrane, and choroidal–scleral interface via a machine learning program (Fig. 2).50 An experienced observer manually corrected the segmentation, if required. The sclera was segmented manually by an experienced observer. The center of the fovea (minimum retinal thickness of central 1 mm) was automatically identified by the MATLAB code based on the Gaussian fit. Choroidal thickness was defined as the distance between Bruch's membrane and the outer border of the choroid, and it was measured perpendicular to the choroidal–retinal pigment epithelium interface. Scleral thickness was defined as the distance from the outer border of the choroid to the outer border of the sclera and was measured perpendicular to the choroidal–scleral interface. The average choroidal and scleral thickness of the 300-µm transverse region centered at the deepest point of the foveal depression is reported.

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 21 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA) and Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all parameters and are provided as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise noted. Spherical equivalent refraction (SER) was defined as sphere + cylinder/2. Baseline distributions of all ocular measurements other than SER and lens thickness did not deviate significantly from normality (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, P > 0.05). In contrast, SER and lens thickness showed mild positive skew with a leptokurtic distribution (P ≥ 0.002). Therefore, all continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD except for SER and lens thickness, which are described as median and interquartile range (IQR). When data were assessed for normality by gender, all baseline parameters were normally distributed (P > 0.05). Paired t-tests were used for within-subject intraocular comparisons, and independent-samples t-tests were used to compare measurements between female and male monkeys. For longitudinal analysis, repeated measurements of all ocular parameters were approximately normally distributed, and model assumptions were checked by examining the distribution of residuals from each fitted model. Residual normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (P > 0.05), together with inspection of skewness and kurtosis values and Q-Q plots, and no meaningful violations were detected. To characterize individual trajectories and to accommodate unequal measurement intervals and between-animal heterogeneity, linear mixed-effects models were fitted with subject-specific random effects. Because ocular growth is not linear with chronological age, age was transformed using the natural logarithm to examine age-related effects on various ocular parameters.
To identify functional forms that best described age-related changes, data from each monkey were fitted with both linear and non-linear models. Linear fits used simple linear regression, whereas non-linear fits included logarithmic regression to capture rapid early changes followed by asymptotic behavior. Model performance was evaluated using standard goodness-of-fit statistics (R2) and visual inspection of fitted curves relative to the observed data; logarithmic models generally provided the most biologically plausible and statistically adequate description of various ocular parameters, whereas body weight had a linear fit with age. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
At age 24 ± 2 days, the mean monkey weight was 0.59 ± 0.07 kg. Baseline right and left eye data are shown in Table 1. Paired t-tests showed that refraction, axial length, central corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and retinal, choroidal, and scleral thickness were not significantly different between right and left eyes (P > 0.05 for all). Corneal curvature and the axial length to corneal radius (AL/CR) ratio showed significant differences between right and left eyes of approximately 0.005; this interocular difference was likely driven by a small difference in corneal power (approximately 0.13 D). Given the absence of an interocular difference in axial length and the very small magnitude of the effect, the difference was deemed clinically insignificant. ## Longitudinal Data
Among the 12 monkeys (eight females and four males) with longitudinal data, body weight, refraction, biometry, and two OCT scans were available at every time point. IOP was available for seven monkeys. Follow-up duration for body weight, refraction, and biometry was 236 ± 100 days; for retinal and choroidal thickness, 247 ± 89 days; for scleral thickness, 112 ± 41 days; and for IOP, 214 ± 105 days. For OCT, the ILM, Bruch's membrane, and choroidal–scleral interface were visible in all scans of all 12 monkeys. However, scleral thickness was not included at all for one monkey and for some individual scans at some time points for other monkeys because the outer scleral boundary was out of the scan frame or not clearly visible. Ocular metrics at baseline did not differ among the 12 monkeys included in longitudinal analysis and the remaining 198 monkeys for any parameter (all P > 0.05), except for SER, which was 0.88 D less hyperopic in the longitudinal subset (+2.96 ± 1.10 vs. +3.84 ± 1.45; P = 0.04).
For the 12 monkeys followed longitudinally, females weighed significantly less than males (P < 0.001), had shorter axial lengths (P < 0.001), greater corneal powers (P = 0.001), smaller AL/CR ratios (P < 0.001), thinner central corneal thicknesses (P < 0.001), and shallower anterior chamber depths (P < 0.001) at baseline. Unlike the full cohort, the choroids of the 12 monkeys in the longitudinal group were thinner in females at baseline (P < 0.001). No significant differences between males and females were observed for SER, lens thickness, or retinal or scleral thickness for the longitudinal set at baseline (P > 0.05 for all).

## Relationships Among Ocular Components
Relationships among SER, corneal power, anterior chamber depth, and lens thickness for longitudinal data are shown in Figure 7. A statistically significant negative trend was observed for SER with axial length (R2 = 0.31, P < 0.001). There was a strong negative linear association between corneal power and axial length (R2 = 0.91, P < 0.001), with longer axial length associated with less corneal power. Anterior chamber depth also showed a strong positive correlation with axial length (R2 = 0.69, P = 0.001), such that eyes with longer axial lengths had greater anterior chamber depths. Lens thickness showed a significant, although weak, positive correlation with axial length (R2 = 0.04, P = 0.01).

## Discussion
This study comprehensively quantifies changes in ocular components from the cornea to the sclera using high-resolution biometry and imaging, providing insight into ocular growth during emmetropization in non-human primates. Monkeys were reared under normal visual conditions without experimental manipulation. At 3 weeks of age, ocular measurements were similar between fellow eyes of each monkey, with only minor interocular differences. There was high inter-individual variability across monkeys for most parameters. With increasing age, there was a decrease in SER associated with an increase in axial length and a decrease in corneal power. Retinal, choroidal, and scleral thickness increased with age, whereas IOP was stable. Sex had a significant association with body weight and several ocular parameters.
Ocular growth trajectories from the present study are consistent with those reported by Qiao-Grider et al.15 in similarly aged rhesus monkeys, which exhibited non-linear changes in biometric components with age. The present study extends prior work by showing that retinal,21 choroidal,29 and scleral thickness also increased non-linearly with age. For the ages examined here, body weight increased linearly and IOP remained stable with age. Refractive error measurements were obtained using retinoscopy, consistent with the methodology of Qiao-Grider et al.,15 who reported a refractive error of +1.58 D for monkeys 500 days of age.15 Based on our data measured over approximately 300 days, the estimated refractive error at 500 days of age was +1.50 D, indicating strong agreement between datasets. Of note, although the overall trend in both datasets was toward a reduction in hyperopia with age, individual monkeys exhibited variation in the magnitude and timing of refractive change. Similar variability has been reported previously in normally developing rhesus monkeys15 and likely reflects natural inter-individual differences in the emmetropization process. Although some degree of measurement variability is inherent to retinoscopy, the observed range of refractive trajectories is consistent with previously reported ocular development.
Prior work by Bradley et al.53 demonstrated that the majority of ocular growth in non-human primates occurs within the first year, including substantial reduction in mean corneal power (a 71% decrease, from 58 D at birth to 53 D at 1 year), a decrease in mean spherical equivalent refractive error (from +7.1 D at birth to +2.5 D at 1 year), and an increase in mean axial length (from 13.1 mm at birth to 17.5 mm at 1 year). Additional studies have reported that rapid phases of ocular growth are largely completed by 3 to 4 months of life in monkeys.15,20,53 This course of emmetropization, with a shift toward low hyperopia, is conserved across a wide range of species, including humans.3,10,53–56
Consistent with findings reported by Patel et al.21 in young monkeys, retinal thickness increased during the first year of life. In the present study, retinal thickness increased from approximately 164 µm in the first month to 207 µm by 13 months of age. The estimated central retinal thickness at 627 days is 217 µm, closely aligning with values reported by Patel et al.21 for the central 50-µm region (207 ± 14 µm). This increase in retinal thickness is thought to reflect centripetal migration of retinal cells and changes in cell morphology.21 These findings are further supported by histologic data showing age-related increases in photoreceptor packing density and progressive elongation of inner and outer segments of cones within the fovea.57–60
In agreement with previous studies in non-human primates, postnatal choroidal thickness increased gradually with age.29,30,61 These early increases in choroidal thickness, likely reflecting normal maturation, appear to have relatively little association with the refractive status.29,30 Choroidal thickness is anticipated to continue increasing up to 1.5 years in monkeys, coinciding with the period in which axial length reaches an asymptote.30 Similarly, age-related increases in choroidal thickness have been reported in non-myopic children 3 to 14 years old.62 Given that visual signals are relayed from the retina to the choroid through localized retinal mechanisms63 and emmetropization functions to bring the retinal image to focus, the choroid may serve as a modulatory structure. Specifically, small adjustments in choroidal thickness may serve as a cue for larger defocus-driven changes in axial length. In addition, increasing choroidal thickness may also support the metabolic and vascular demands associated with rapid ocular growth during early development.29 Choroidal thickness has therefore been proposed as a potential biomarker for predicting future myopia. However, our data show substantial variability across monkeys, consistent with observations in human populations. The predictive value of choroidal thickness also remains uncertain, as findings from animal studies are mixed. Guggenheim et al.64 found no association between choroidal thickness and subsequent myopia in chicks, whereas Nickla and Totonelly65 reported that thinner choroids were associated with faster ocular growth in untreated eyes. Taken together, these findings suggest that, although choroidal thickening is a normal feature of ocular maturation, its utility as an independent predictor of future refractive development remains unclear.
Although choroidal thickness increased with age, the relatively weak correlation and substantial inter-animal variability suggest that age accounts for only a portion of the observed variance. Similar variability has been reported in humans.66 Choroidal thickness may vary among individuals due to differences in ocular growth trajectories, axial dimensions, refractive development, and local retinal signaling mechanisms involved in emmetropization. In addition, small measurement-related variability associated with image quality and segmentation of choroidal boundaries cannot be entirely excluded. Collectively, these factors likely contribute to the broad distribution of choroidal thickness values observed across animals and the relatively modest association between choroidal thickness and axial length.
Our data demonstrate an increase in scleral thickness during early postnatal development, parallel with overall ocular growth.67 The observed increase in scleral thickness may reflect the development of mechanical support required for ocular growth. Downs et al.68 measured scleral thickness in perfusion-fixed eyes of four normal monkeys (mean age of 5.75 years) with an IOP of 10 mm Hg and estimated scleral thickness in the foveal region to be about 307 µm. Based on our data, the estimated scleral thickness at a comparable age is 345 µm. Using transmission electron microscopy, Paidimarri et al.31 found that posterior scleral thickness was 217 ± 10.1 µm at 150 days. Based on our data, the estimated scleral thickness for 150 days of age is 297 µm. Although the estimated scleral thickness from our study differs from prior reports, these variations might be due to methodological differences in the measurement of scleral thickness. The current study measured scleral thickness in vivo using OCT, whereas previous studies implemented histological and ex vivo techniques that may be subject to fixation-related artifacts.
Based on lifespan differences, it is estimated that 1 year of a monkey's life is approximately equivalent to 3 human years.69,70 The developmental changes observed in rhesus monkeys are broadly consistent with those reported in human infants and children.11,12 Rozema11 recently demonstrated that most biometric components of the human eye follow a bi-exponential growth pattern characterized by an initial phase of rapid growth followed by a slower phase associated with emmetropization and long-term ocular maturation. Similarly, here, axial length and anterior chamber depth increased rapidly early in life before slowing with age, but corneal power decreased as part of coordinated anterior segment development.71 In addition, increases in retinal and scleral thickness have been reported during human ocular development.72,73 Lee et al.72 demonstrated that retinal layer development follows a non-linear trajectory extending through adolescence, and Shen et al.74 reported increasing scleral thickness during the first 2 years of life before stabilization. Rhesus monkey eyes are smaller than human eyes throughout development, with axial length increasing from approximately 14 mm in infancy to 19 to 20 mm in adulthood compared with approximately 17 mm and 23 to 24 mm in humans, respectively. Despite these differences in absolute size, both species exhibit comparable age-related patterns of ocular growth and emmetropization. The findings suggest that rhesus monkeys and humans share similar developmental patterns of ocular growth and tissue remodeling, supporting the utility of the rhesus monkey as a model for studying emmetropization and refractive development.
Sex-related differences in ocular structure were also evident in the current dataset. Female monkeys exhibited shorter axial lengths than males.17 More broadly, sex-based differences in ocular biometry in monkeys mirror those observed in human populations, with females also having lower central corneal thickness, lens thickness, anterior chamber depth, and axial length, as well as higher corneal power, than males.75,76 Here, although the choroidal thickness of the entire cohort was similar in both females and males at 24 days of age, longitudinal data indicated that female monkeys developed thinner choroids than males, suggesting that sex-related differences may emerge over time and persist into adulthood, as observed in humans.77 These sex-based differences in choroidal thickness have been attributed to variations in pulsatile ocular blood flow, sex hormones, and choroidal hemodynamics, with males typically exhibiting greater choroidal thickness.73,77–79
Systemic factors were also associated with ocular growth. Our cross-sectional and longitudinal data showed that monkeys with higher body weight exhibited greater axial length and lower corneal power compared to those with lower body weight. In humans, Ojaimi et al.80 and Northstone et al.81 reported linear associations between axial length and birth weight and an inverse association between corneal power and body weight.
Monkeys in this study were reared under controlled nursery conditions with artificial lighting and regulated visual environments. Consequently, the ocular growth trajectories reported here may not fully reflect development in free-ranging primates exposed to natural light cycles and more variable visual experiences. Notably, none of the rhesus monkeys in the present cohort developed myopia during the observation period. In contrast, studies of cynomolgus monkey colonies in the Guangdong82 and Yunnan83 provinces in China reported a high prevalence of spontaneous myopia, with mean refractive errors ranging from approximately −1.25 to −1.50 D. In free-ranging rhesus macaques from Puerto Rico, Fernandes et al.84 described a broad refractive error distribution, including myopia. These differences likely reflect a combination of environmental, genetic, and colony-specific factors, including variation in light exposure, visual experience, husbandry practices, and age structure. Although caution is warranted when extrapolating findings from nursery-reared animals to natural populations, the controlled conditions of the present study minimized environmental variability and provided a valuable normative framework for characterizing ocular growth and emmetropization in infant rhesus monkeys.
Some limitations of this study should be considered. First, although the sample size for cross-sectional analyses was large, longitudinal data were available for only a subset of monkeys, which may limit the ability to fully capture individual growth trajectories. Second, although in vivo imaging with optical biometry and OCT provides high-resolution, layer-specific measurements, segmentation of posterior boundaries remains technically challenging and may introduce measurement variability. Third, all animals experienced normal visual input, but environmental conditions in a nursery setting may not fully replicate natural visual experience. Fourth, anesthesia and tropicamide may affect IOP85 and choroidal thickness.86,87 However, these effects are expected to be consistent across animals and age and unlikely to introduce systematic bias. Prior work indicates that refraction remains similar with and without tropicamide in rhesus monkeys.86 Finally, although rhesus monkeys provide a highly relevant model for human ocular development, species differences in developmental timelines should be considered when extrapolating these findings to humans.

## Conclusions
The present study provides a comprehensive characterization of ocular growth trajectories from the cornea to the sclera during early postnatal development using high-resolution in vivo techniques. Rhesus monkeys were initially hyperopic and exhibited coordinated optical and structural changes consistent with emmetropization. Refraction and anterior and posterior structures followed expected developmental shifts, but choroidal and scleral thickness showed substantial variability and only modest associations with age. These findings establish normative benchmarks for early primate ocular development and provide a translational framework for interpreting structural changes associated with myopia.
