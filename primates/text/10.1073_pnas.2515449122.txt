# A complete spatial map of mouse retinal ganglion cells reveals density and gene expression specializations

## Abstract
SignificanceRetinal ganglion cells (RGCs) transmit visual signals to the brain, and their diversity supports specialized visual functions. Using gene expression mapping and machine learning, we charted the spatial distribution of all known RGC types in the mouse retina. Local distributions for most types confirmed mosaicism. While many types’ global distributions were even, others showed elevated density in regions linked to specific functions, such as sky detection or binocular vision. We also found location-dependent gene expression differences within types, independent of cell density. These spatial patterns did not align with primate retinal gene profiles, suggesting divergent evolutionary paths. Our findings reveal previously unrecognized retinal specialization and provide evidence for the lack of homology between the mouse retina and human macula.Keywords: visual neuroscience, spatial transcriptomics, machine learning, retina
More than a hundred years ago, neuroanatomists described the basic information flow of the retina, conceptualizing light detection in the outer retina by photoreceptors (1, 2), and sequential propagation of information through intermediate layers to retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), which project to the brain (3). In addition to the differential specialization of its layers along the “vertical” axis, research going back to Thomas Young demonstrated that the en face or “horizontal” aspect of the human retina is inhomogeneous, where high-resolution vision in the central retina is supported by the macula and its center, the fovea, which are anatomically and functionally distinct from the peripheral retina (4, 5).
The classic definition of these and other retinal specializations requires locally increased photoreceptor and/or RGC packing and/or differential retinal thickness (6). Arguably the best-known specialization is found in species with frontal-facing eyes, termed the “area centralis.” This is a small region of the retina with high RGC density which experiences minimal optic flow during locomotion and is used for binocular fixation and depth discrimination (6–11). For animals with lateralized eyes, minimal optic flow occurs on the part of the retina that samples from frontal visual fields, which will generally be on the temporal, or for mice, the dorso-temporal retina (6). For these species, the “Terrain Hypothesis of Vision,” which was developed based on insights from histology, optics, analysis of lifestyle, ecological niche, and eye height from the ground predicts the retinal specialization termed area retinae temporalis (ART) (6). Refs. 7–9 and 12–17. Relevantly to the mouse, the terrain hypothesis further predicts the absence of lateral streak, a structure common to taller species that regularly view an unobstructed horizon. In addition to gross anatomical specializations, the retina is known to vary in its molecular or type spatial distributions (15, 17–31). In accordance with efficient coding principles, s-Opsin expression and enhanced contrast sensitivity in the laboratory mouse photoreceptors clearly demark the ventral retinal domain that samples from the sky (19–23, 32, 33). Some RGCs are sensitive to this photoreceptor topography, which influences their center-surround receptive field organization and response properties (34–36).
The murine retina does not contain areas with significant differences in thickness and only slight differences in total RGC counts (24, 37, 38). A slight area centralis that does not relate to optic flow (8, 16) exists just ventral to the optic nerve head (15, 24). Although the most numerous functional RGC type, termed W3, is more common in the area centralis (24), a more nuanced examination reveals not all RGC types cover the retina uniformly or favor this central region. Most notably, the alpha sustained (αS) RGCs exhibit higher density in the dorso-temporal retina (15), are genetic and functional orthologs of primate midget RGCs—which are highly enriched in the macula/fovea—leading to previous studies proposing the ART to be the rodent homolog of the primate central retina (12, 15, 16).
Despite decades of intense research, the field managed to map the spatial distribution of only 17 of the mouse RGC types (15, 16, 24, 26–30, 39, 40), out of ∼45 distinct RGC types in the mouse retina identified by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) (41–46). A limited number of unique genetic markers for RGCs challenges traditional mapping approaches that rely on immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Cre-line labeling. These approaches are further complicated by nonspecific labeling, requiring the researchers to resort to labor-intensive approaches like analyzing morphological or functional differences to distinguish between RGC types in a single project (29, 47, 48). As an alternative, some studies have used scRNAseq on small retinal regions, successfully differentiating RGC type distributions between the primate fovea and periphery, suggesting glimpses into RGC topographies in the mouse (31, 46, 49–51). In a more granular approach, two recent tour-de-force studies sequenced individual cells via PatchSeq, revealing functional correlates of some RGC types found in genetic studies (48, 52). Unfortunately, these methods lack the spatial resolution or the throughput to comprehensively characterize RGC coverage across the retina.
Recent advances in high-plex spatial transcriptomics at subcellular resolution offer a promising path forward toward mapping all cells in a single tissue (53, 54). A recent demonstration of these techniques in cross-sections of the mouse retina successfully identified all major retinal cell classes (e.g., photoreceptors, RGCs), but did not clearly characterize their spatial distributions (31). Here, we developed an en face cryosectioning technique to preserve spatial distribution patterns (55) combined with a machine learning method for dimensionality reduction to select a high-quality gene panel. This enabled type inference from puncta counts measured with 10X Genomics Xenium (54). Using this approach, we were able to uncover the spatial maps of all 45 RGC types identified in single-cell studies (42).
Extending rigorous, unbiased geospatial methods with machine learning, revealed four interpretable RGC type groupings with distinct retinal coverages. Specifically, about one-third of types preferentially sample either from the ART, or from a previously unrecognized, sky-facing, horizon-avoiding specialization (30).
Finally, molecular maps showed that in addition to different distributions, these groups varied in their genetic profiles and we identified several nonmarker, spatially variable, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within RGC types. We further focused on five gene families related to synaptic transmission and membrane excitability, comparing their spatial distributions between mice and primates. We found low correspondence in gene expression between mouse ART and anatomical area centralis to primate macula. Although the expression of the voltage-gated sodium channel gene family showed a conserved positive correlation between central and peripheral vision across species (44, 46, 50), while GABA receptor (GABAR) expression was anticorrelated. These findings provide detailed type and molecular maps of the mouse retina, revealing spatial specialization zones and their potential relevance to human vision.

## Results

## Spatial Transcriptomic Mapping of All 45 RGCs.
The retina contains several distinct cell classes, all of which must be accurately distinguished to enable reliable type inference (Fig. 1A). To classify RGCs based on expression profiles with the initial 300 genes available on 10X Genomics Xenium, we employed the GraSP algorithm for dimensionality reduction. This algorithm uses neural network ensembles, each trained on datasets containing 50% target and 50% distractor cells (Fig. 1B) (54). We integrated published single-cell datasets (42, 44, 45) and ranked genes by their importance in classifying all 130 retinal types. This procedure yielded a list of 225 genes that preserved class and type separability, which were supplemented with 75 additional genes of interest encoding synaptic proteins and voltage-gated ion channels.

Retinas from 5 adult C57BL/6J mice (3 females and 2 males) were sectioned into 20 μm thick horizontal (en face) slices and mounted on 10X Genomics Xenium slices (Fig. 1C) (55). Following transcriptomic profiling, the tissue was stained with anti-RBPMS antibody and tomato lectin (TL) to label RGCs and vasculature, respectively (Fig. 1D), which were used to register IHC images from each tissue slice to the Xenium slide and manually oriented by the location of a temporal cut made during dissection and further refined by the dorsal–ventral gradients of the Opn1sw and Opn1mw opsin genes (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (19–23).
Baysor segmentation (56) assigned transcripts to cells using nuclei priors (Fig. 1F). To differentiate between RGCs and other retinal cells, we employed a supervised hierarchical deep neural network for automated classification, CuttleNet (Fig. 1G) (54). CuttleNet consists of a “head” module that identifies cell classes and dynamically routes them to the corresponding “tentacle” subnetwork to predict specific type identities (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3) (54). The two-stage inference was important, as the amalgamated scRNAseq training data did not contain identical gene sets between studies (42, 44, 45). The performance of Baysor and CuttleNet’s “head” was assessed first by examining relative inferred RGC type counts compared to the scRNAseq dataset. While enrichment methods applied in scRNAseq studies complicate the estimation of the relative abundance of RGC type proportions, we found a good correlation (R=0.85) between our type counts and Tran’s dataset (Fig. 1I) (42), see also refs. 31 and 41.
For validation of CuttleNet’s “tentacles,” we compared puncta counts with normalized gene expression values of single-cell datasets. Using z-scored gene expression vectors of all 300 genes for each cell class in each data source (42, 44, 45), we computed pairwise distance vectors that revealed clear separation between cell classes and RGC types (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Next, we directly examined RGC inference accuracy by comparing binary cell class predictions with manual classification based on RBPMS IHC, demonstrating a precision of 89.5% and a recall of 98.5% (Fig. 1G, Inset, n=255 RGCs). This approach also allowed us to verify Baysor segmentation, which we found to be more accurate in correctly identifying individual cells in the ganglion cell layer relative to Xenium’s built-in segmentation and other algorithms (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) (57–64). The segmented cells varied in size; the largest cells in the dataset belonged to three types of alpha RGCs (T42, T43, and T45), and the smallest-sized cell was W3 (T1), consistent with the known properties of these RGC types (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) (48, 65).
We further examined type assignment using genes that nearly exclusively mark specific types: Zic1, expressed in Tran’s type 6 (T6) (42) and Kcnip2, which largely, but not exclusively, marks T45 (42, 47) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). 96.4% of T6 RGCs classified using CuttleNet contained at least one Zic1 puncta, very similar to the 96.5% T6 cells expressing this gene in Tran’s dataset (42). Similar analysis showed that Kcnip2 is expressed in 72% of inferred T45 cells, which is lower than 85 to 100% expression levels found in previous work (42, 47) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This discrepancy could be due to the relative rarity of T45 in the scRNAseq training data compared to T6, suggesting that inference accuracy is higher for more common RGC types.
Following RGC type classification and slice registration, we projected each retina onto a normalized Cartesian grid centered at the optic nerve head (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S5), allowing us to map the local and global distributions in all 45 genetically defined RGC types (Fig. 2), further characterized below (66).

## Local RGC Statistics and Mosaics.
To assess the local spatial distribution of RGC types, we computed the Voronoi Domain Index (VDRI), the effective radius, and the Nearest Neighbor Regularity Index (NNRI, Fig. 3A–D) (14, 51, 67–71). We computed the distribution of all types in 14 independent study regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Statistical significance was tested using bootstrap analysis, where we replaced cells corresponding to a given type with the same number of cells randomly sampled from the entire RGC population in the region (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Relative to simulating complete spatial randomness, this empirical null distribution incorporates tissue processing artifacts, blood vessels (67, 70), and other hidden variables, such as variable homotypical soma sizes over the retina (15, 65, 66).

(Scale bar, 0.1 mm.) The spatial autocorrelation (Middle) and the distribution of nearest neighbor distances (Right) for T6, as a function of the distance from the origin cell, used to compute the effective radius (Re) and Nearest Neighbor Regularity Index (NNRI), respectively. (B) As in (A), but for T10, showing less regular spatial distribution. (D and E) Black, observed NNRI (D) and VDRI (E); Gray, indices computed from bootstrapped populations. See SI Appendix, Fig. S8 for local statistics of all 45 RGC types.
We observed that 18 RGC types out of the 26 that were well represented in the study regions had effective radii that were significantly larger than predicted by random distributions (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Correspondingly, the NNRI values in the majority of RGC types were higher than expected by chance, although VDRI calculations identified only 6 RGC types that were significantly different from tessellation patterns in random populations (Fig. 3 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These results suggest that the most common RGC types, for which we could derive a reliable estimation of their local statistics, have an ordered distribution. This information aligns with mosaicism, which is a regular spacing of cells that approximately tile the retina. An important exception was T10, which was common in our sample, yet had a statistically random distribution (Fig. 3 E and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). T10 is known to represent a mixture of On-DSGCs (48), explaining the lack of ordered spatial statistics. Segmentation or inference errors could influence local indices (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), but the evidence for mosaics in many RGCs is a strong indicator of the ability of spatial transcriptomics to identify the local spatial arrangements of most RGC types.

## Geospatial Statistics Identify Type Specializations in Distinct Visual Fields.
The mouse visual system supports a variety of behaviors and functions. For example, predation in mice often involves head and eye movements that position the image of the prey within the binocular zone, which covers approximately 40 degrees of the forward-facing visual fields (Fig. 4 A and B) (8, 16). As in many other rodents, panoramic visual coverage of the sky is facilitated by lateralized eyes and an enhanced overhead binocular zone, actively compensating for head motion to detect airborne predators (Fig. 4A) (6–9). Previous work linked visual requirements with the spatial distribution of individual RGC types in the mouse. In particular, ipsilaterally projecting RGCs, including the alpha OFF sustained (αOFFS) (T42) and alpha ON sustained (αONS) (T43) densely cover the dorso-temporal retina (Fig. 2) and signal prey—with hunting success dramatically reduced with their ablation (8, 16).

Given that we have mapped the full RGC population, we next asked whether other regions of the retina are preferentially sampled by specific RGC types. To perform this analysis, we first confirmed that globally, statistically significant spatial clusters (SSSCs) were present in all types, using Moran’s I, a test for spatial randomness based on autocorrelation (72). To locate the positions of the SSSCs, we applied Kulldorff’s scan statistic (73–75), which evaluates the relative incidence of an observed population within and outside exploratory circles (Fig. 4C) (73–75). We next quantified the relationship between SSSCs and different ethologically relevant subregions of the retina by intersecting SSSC regions with masks representing the binocular sky, binocular ground, peripheral sky, and peripheral ground (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). For each mask and RGC type, we calculated the following metrics: the overlap of the SSSC and the mask (true positive, TP), the part of the mask outside of the SSSC (false negative, FN), the SSSC outside of the mask (false positive, FP), and the part of the study area with neither SSSC nor mask (true negative). The F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was then computed to measure the exclusivity of each type’s SSSC to a specific visual field (Fig. 4 C and D).
Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of F1 scores across retinal regions revealed four groups, each composed of RGCs with similar spatial distributions (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Our findings show that the coverage of 65% (29/45) of RGC types extends over most of the rodent retina. These types corresponded to two groups with a somewhat stronger preference either for the region typically occupied by the sky or the ground. Conversely, group 3 consisted of seven RGC types with a clear preference for the ventral retina (thus sampling from the visual sky), including half of the ipRGCs types. The other nine types, assigned to group 4, included the other remaining ipRGCs and demonstrated a pronounced preference for binocular visual field in the dorso-temporal retina (Fig. 4 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11).

## Spatially DEGs within RGC Types Are Most Pronounced on the Dorso–Ventral Axis.
RGC characteristics can be shaped by their retinal position (34, 35, 39, 76). As the spatial RGC groups we identified cover different retinal regions, these groups could vary in gene expression. To explore this question, we chose to focus on the 83 genes encoding proteins involved in synaptic transmission and membrane excitability, only 8 of which were type markers selected by GraSP. These gene families were well represented in our panel and convey specific and known functions in neurons. DEG analysis was performed only on genes with mean expression levels >1 puncta/cell in at least one RGC group (Fig. 5A). We found that 6 out of 50 genes that passed the inclusion criteria had significantly different mean expression values (ANOVA, corrected for multiple comparisons), suggesting above-chance genetic similarity between the spatial RGC groups.

Next, we focused on individual RGC types and asked whether their gene expression varies as a function of retinal topography. To identify DEGs that display within-type spatial variability (77), we pooled RGCs of a given type within and outside of different spatial masks (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Most genes in our dataset (260 out of 300) had similar expression across the retina and types (Fig. 5 B and C). This was anticipated as GraSP-based selection found the most reliable type markers. However, a small portion of genes showed statistically different expression levels within at least one RGC type (0.9% of all gene/RGC type combinations). We found the sky vs. ground division was responsible for over half of the DEGs (Fig. 5B). Despite the limited size, and engineered bias favoring marker genes of our gene panel, our DEG analysis predicts that several RGC types may have variations in their properties over the retina. Among these are T6 with 7 DEGs and T8, T14, T16, T17, and T36, with 5 to 6 DEGs.
Several genes demonstrated spatial variation in multiple RGC types, in which case their spatial expression pattern tended to align between types, with most DEG patterns centered in the dorsal-temporal retina (Fig. 5D). Rarely, spatially variable genes were found in other retinal locations–for instance, Cosh had higher expression levels in the ventral retina (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). It is conceivable that this variable gene expression may relate to other observed murine spatial retinal specializations (8, 16, 34, 35).

## Retinal Specializations Transcriptomically Differ Between Mice and Primates.
The mouse is the primary animal model of human eye diseases, including macular degeneration and glaucoma, which affect central and peripheral vision differently (78–80). This raises the question of how closely the ART-the proposed evolutionary correlate of the macula in mice-or the mouse area centralis-an anatomical correlate of the macula based on higher RGC density and location near the optical axis-transcriptionally resemble the central retina of primates (6, 12).
To investigate this, we first examined in an unbiased manner whether any part of the mouse retina differs in RGC type composition based on type densities alone. We first divided the mouse retina into a 400 μm grid and calculated vectors representing the relative densities of RGC types at each spatial location. Next, we clustered these vectors using an ensemble of hierarchical clustering algorithms (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). This analysis identified two distinct spatial clusters with different RGC type compositions (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, one of these clusters occupied the dorso-temporal region, closely matching the predicted topographical position of the ART (Fig. 6B).

To compare gene expression between the ART-like cluster vs. the rest of the retina with that of the primate central vs. peripheral retina, we correlated pooled puncta counts from all RGCs in both spatial clusters against gene expression data from primate scRNAseq datasets (Fig. 6 C and D). As above, we limited the analysis to synaptic proteins and voltage-gated channels that had orthologous genes in the primate datasets. We saw only modest correlations between mouse and primate glutamate receptors and calcium or potassium voltage-gated channels (Fig. 6D). However, sodium voltage-gated channel and inhibitory synaptic receptors (GABA and Glycine) subunits significantly correlated and anticorrelated, respectively, between the mouse ART:periphery and primate macula:periphery (Fig. 6D). These observations suggest that even though the behavioral role the ART and primate macula play is potentially similar, their functional transcriptomic profile is not related.
Next, we repeated the same DEG analysis on the mouse area centralis. We found fewer DEGs than from the unbiased ART cluster (Fig. 6C). We next regressed the mouse area centralis and primate macula, again finding low correlations in functional gene sets. Although, as in the ART, the sodium voltage-gated channels were weakly positively correlated between mice and primates (Fig. 6D). Detailed analysis showed that the correlations in the voltage-gated sodium channel gene family were primarily driven by Group 3 types, whose most dense distribution is found far from the ART and area centralis and thus do not reveal specific specializations to these regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S14).
The analysis above focused on gene families involved in neuronal circuit function and excitability. Although genetic orthologs exist between rodent and primate RGCs, the fovea has unique anatomy, photoreceptor distribution, and circuitry that are not replicated in the mouse retina. Consequently, the genes regulating signal processing in RGCs located in the mouse ART may not correspond directly to their primate counterparts.
To test whether similarity could be detected in other neural or metabolic pathways, we performed additional experiments using the 10X Genomics Xenium 5k panel. As in the previous DEG analysis, we examined the transcriptomic profiles of the entire RGC population in the ART, AC, and other retinal regions. Because some genes in the 5k panel were only weakly expressed in RGCs, we restricted our analysis to the top 75% of strongly expressed genes in both mouse and human datasets (n=3,429).
Correlation of DEGs between ART or AC and the rest of the mouse retina with central vs. peripheral human retina did not yield significant results (Fig. 7A). The low correlation may reflect true species differences in expression patterns but could also arise from “contamination” by broadly expressed genes, such as those in basic metabolic pathways.

To minimize this effect, we next compared pathway-level correlations between RGCs in ART:periphery in mice and macula:periphery in humans. Pathways were included if they contained at least 10 genes and shared less than 10% of their gene content with other pathways in the Xenium 5k panel. After correcting for multiple comparisons, none of the 12 pathways meeting these criteria showed statistically significant correlations across species (Fig. 7A). The strongest trend was observed in fibroblast growth factor signaling (R2 = 0.20), possibly reflecting its conserved role in retinal development and nasal-temporal patterning (81). Similar analyses of the AC region produced comparable, low-correlation results (SI Appendix, Fig. S15), supporting the conclusion that transcriptomic profiles of mouse RGCs do not recapitulate those of the primate central retina.

## Discussion
We used spatial transcriptomics to create a detailed atlas of RGC type distributions in the mouse retina. By optimizi ng en face slicing (55), we preserved large, intact regions of the ganglion cell layer. To address the high cellular and molecular density in our dataset, we implemented a Bayesian segmentation algorithm (56) and invented machine learning approaches for dimensionality reduction and inference (54) trained on single-cell RNA sequencing (42, 44, 45) and fine-tuned with spatial puncta distribution data. This allowed us to identify and map all RGC types in the retina. While the majority of RGCs exhibited relatively uniform spatial distribution, about one-third showed a strong preference for either ventral or dorso-temporal retinal zones. Interestingly, gene expression also varied across the retina but was largely uncorrelated with the spatial profiles of the RGC types expressing those genes. Finally, the topographic distribution of genes of interest expressed in primate central vs. peripheral retina was regressed against both the behaviorally homologous area retinae temporalis and anatomically homologous area centralis. In both cases, we found weak correlations between the genetic profiles of the primate central vs. peripheral retina, suggesting divergent specialization between the mouse ART and the primate macula.
We found close agreement between our results and published RGC type distributions (Fig. 2). In the temporal retina, we reproduced the high densities of αONS, alpha ON transient (αONT), and alpha OFF sustained (αOFFS) RGCs, corresponding to T41, T43, and T42 respectively (15). The ventral retina is enriched in several types: W3 cells (24) genetically correspond to multiple Tran RGC types (T2, T3, T4, T6, T21, T23, T30, and potentially T1 and T13) (42, 48, 52), as well as FminiOFF (T3), FminiON (T4), and FmidiOFF (T28) (26). As expected, FmidiON (T38) cells showed a dorsal bias (26), and J-RGCs (T5) exhibited a central elevated density pattern (39). The intrinsically photosensitive RGCs (ipRGCs) showed distinct regional patterns: M1 ipRGCs (T33, T40) displayed a bimodal distribution with peaks in both ventral and temporal retina, M2 ipRGCs (T31) showed a dorso-temporal peak, M4 ipRGCs (T43) had a centro-temporal peak, and M5 ipRGCs (T22) were strongly concentrated in the ventral retina, all in line with previous work (29, 82). Of the ∼17 murine RGC types historically mapped, the only type we observe conflicting results with is the αOFFT, T45. This type was described as uniformly distributed with a slight temporal bias (15, 83). Instead, we found a modest ventral-temporal peak, which aligns with a proposed physiological role of T45 as a detector of approaching aerial predators (25). Moreover, the observed RGC type proportions in our dataset were more similar to those from scRNAseq studies (41, 42) compared to the previous spatial transcriptomic classification of retinal cross-sections (31). Intriguingly, we recapitulated the increased T16 and T21 prevalence relative to scRNAseq datasets observed by this group. Last, the RGC maps generated in this study closely align with the distributions reported by Nimkar and colleagues in a recent spatial transcriptomic investigation. Despite substantial differences in techniques and analytical methods, this consistency supports the accuracy of our type identification and the topographical organization of various RGC populations (66).
Efficient coding theory proposes that sensory systems evolve to encode environmental information accurately while minimizing resource consumption (33). According to this theory, homotypic RGCs are expected to tile the retina without redundant sampling (84). Consistent with this prediction, numerous studies have demonstrated that retinal cell classes are organized in nonrandom mosaic patterns (15, 24, 27, 28, 70, 85–92). Comparing our local spatial analysis with ref. 66, the presence of mosaics was identified using NNRI analysis in 23 of 29 RGC types with strong representation in the two datasets. Mosaicism was rejected in three types (T7, T10, and T29), and the two studies differ in only three cases: Our analysis detected a significant difference in NNRI values from shuffled data in T15 and T20, but failed to detect it in T39. The absence of regular mosaics can be explained by the inclusion of distinct RGC types with transcriptomically unseparable profiles within a single genetic type, as is the case for T10, which represents multiple subpopulations of On-DSGCs (48). In addition to NNRI, we quantified the VDRI and the effective radius-three local spatial metrics commonly used to characterize cellular mosaics. Ideally, all three measures should consistently identify the presence of an organized local pattern (see, for example, ref. 69). However, in our dataset, the NNRI proved to be the most sensitive indicator of spatial regularity, while the other two metrics often failed to show statistically significant deviations from spatial randomness (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). One plausible explanation for this discrepancy is the increased variability of the null distribution in the VDRI analysis, likely stemming from the irregular boundaries of our regions of interest and from Voronoi domains intersecting these edges. Such artifacts are inherent to the slicing approach used in this study and could be mitigated in future work by analyzing cell distributions in intact or minimally perturbed tissue.
Alternatively, some RGC types may not form regular mosaics (67–71). Like all nocturnal animals, mice rely on vision that performs effectively under scotopic conditions. In such environments, redundant visual information becomes an advantage, as the ability to detect and discriminate rare photons can be critical for survival. Increased sensitivity in dim light can be achieved through various adaptations, such as clustering photoreceptors, exemplified by the “macroreceptors” of Scopelarchus guntheri (7, 93). Another strategy is to enhance receptive fields via gap junctions or a reduction in surround inhibition (6, 7, 93–95), potentially limiting the utility of ordered spatial distributions for nocturnal animals with poor optics (6, 7, 96).
What is a type? This is a fundamental question not only in the study of the retina, but in neuroscience in general. Given our challenge to the idea that mosaics define types, we wanted to delve deeper into this question. To date, the three complimentary (48) techniques to define types include morphology (3, 65, 97, 98), physiology (84, 99–101), and genetic profile (12, 42–47, 49–51). Individual RGC types’ morphology (30, 39, 102, 103) and physiology (34, 76) can vary as a function of cell position in the retina. scRNAseq studies dramatically increased the number of putative RGC types than was appreciated in morphological or functional examinations. Potential “overcounting” of RGC types could be explained by spatially variable genetic profiles of RGCs. Spatially variable gene expression has been reported within the same “type” in the retina (12, 46, 49–51), and other brain regions (104–111). In this scenario, a single cellular phenotype could be erroneously divided into multiple types in scRNAseq analysis, which has no access to topographic information. Our examination of RGC coverage and DEG analysis concretely disproves the possibility that scRNAseq overestimated the number of RGC types in the mouse. First, while retinal coverage of several RGC types could form a continuum (for example, types belonging to specialization group 3 with types from group 4), these RGCs either had radically different gene expression patterns or matched characterized distributions of specific RGC populations. Second, in addition to the presence of mosaics in many genetically defined RGC types, we show that the number and identity of DEGs are not sufficient to drive erroneous type assignment.
Unexpectedly, we found that many of the spatially variable genes were expressed differently within a narrow region of the dorso-temporal retina, a zone that had statistically different RGC type distributions, closely matching the expected position of the ART (6, 8, 12, 16). Yet, despite previous proposals linking the ART to the primate central vision based on function and orthologous RGC type topography (8, 12, 15, 16), our findings show that genes encoding synaptic receptors, voltage-gated channels, and multiple signaling pathways do not show matching differential expression between mice and primates. The only strongly correlated gene family was the voltage-gated sodium channels, and a detailed analysis shows that this difference is driven by a small population of RGCs in the ventral retina.
Looking ahead, this work opens several promising avenues for future research. First, the use of more advanced transcriptomic techniques that do not require en face sectioning will eliminate slicing artifacts and allow mapping of larger retinas. Second, our gene panel was designed to classify all retinal cell types, including bipolar and amacrine cells. However, current segmentation methods did not adequately resolve the inner nuclear layer to our satisfaction (57–64). Improved segmentation would clarify the spatial relationships between photoreceptors, bipolar, amacrine, and ganglion cells within the same tissue, enabling the development of more accurate models of retinal physiology. Such analysis could shed light on how the specializations identified in the RGC layer are organized in relation to upstream circuits. This, in turn, would provide deeper insights into the computational strategies employed across different retinal regions, which is crucial for understanding vision in mice and, eventually, humans.

## Materials and Methods
After cryosectioning, molecular profiling was performed with the 10X Genomics Xenium platform using a custom 300-gene panel. Cell boundaries were predicted with the Baysor algorithm (56) and RGCs were classified into 45 types based on reference scRNA-seq datasets. Local spatial distributions were derived from regions of interest, while global statistics were computed across the entire dataset of 130,575 RGCs. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the Model-based Analysis of Single-cell Transcriptomics (MAST) framework (77) implemented in the Seurat package. Human and macaque retinal scRNA-seq datasets were restricted to genes with verified mouse orthologs using BioMart mapping.
