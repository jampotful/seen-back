# Inner Limiting Membrane Peel Extends In Vivo Calcium Imaging of Retinal Ganglion Cell Activity Beyond the Fovea in Non-Human Primate

## Abstract
PurposeAdaptive optics scanning light ophthalmoscopy (AOSLO) paired with intravitreal injection of a viral vector coding for the calcium indicator GCaMP has enabled visualization of neuronal activity in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) at single cell resolution in the living eye. However, the inner limiting membrane (ILM) restricts viral transduction to the fovea in humans and non-human primates, hindering both therapeutic intervention and physiological study of the retina. To address this issue, we explored peeling the ILM before intravitreal injection to expand calcium imaging beyond the fovea in the living primate eye.MethodsFive eyes from three Macaca fascicularis (aged 3–10 years; 2 males, 1 female) that were immune suppressed with cyclosporine, underwent vitrectomy and ILM peel centered on the fovea prior to intravitreal delivery of 7m8:SNCG:GCaMP8. RGC responses to visual flicker were evaluated using AOSLO calcium imaging 1 to 6 months after intravitreal injection.ResultsCalcium activity was observed in RGCs throughout the ILM peeled area in all eyes, representing a mean eight-fold increase in accessible recording area relative to a representative control eye. RGC responses in the ILM peeled and control eyes were comparable and showed no significant decrease over the 6 month period after the procedure. In addition, we demonstrated that activity can be recorded directly from the retinal nerve fiber layer.ConclusionsPeeling the ILM is a viable strategy to expand viral access to RGCs for gene therapy, and when paired with GCaMP imaging has the potential to advance visual neuroscience, preclinical evaluation of retinal function, detection of vision loss, and assessment of therapeutic interventions.
The retina is a highly specialized tissue in the back of the eye that plays a critical role in the capture and processing of visual information. Advances in methods to study retinal function have enhanced our ability to understand retinal physiology,1,2 disease,3 and to evaluate and optimize therapeutic interventions.4,5 In vivo measures of function allow us to gain insight into retinal activity in an intact tissue with the integrity of both circuitry and bodily systems maintained.6 This is particularly important for preclinical development where the immune system may be provoked by the intervention.5 Furthermore, in vivo recording enables indefinite longitudinal follow-up. The non-human primate (NHP) is the gold standard animal model for both retinal physiology and preclinical testing of novel therapies, owing to the close similarity between the visual7,8 and immune systems of NHPs and humans.9,10 In vivo measures of function are, therefore, particularly appealing in NHPs, where numbers are few but the potential for translation is high.
The ideal in vivo functional testing paradigm would evaluate neuronal activity at the cellular scale throughout the retina. Multielectrode array and patch clamp electrophysiology in retinal explants allow recordings with high temporal resolution, but rapid degradation of the explant precludes longitudinal measurements in a single individual. This complicates assessment of pathologies and therapies, particularly when the time course is uncertain.11,12 Electoretinography enables repeated non-invasive measurement of retinal responses in vivo,13,14 but does not readout activity at the cellular scale. Behavioral testing can complement optical and electrophysiological studies,15 but does not provide direct insight into function at the retinal level or at the cellular scale.
By pairing adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (AOSLO), which enables high-resolution retinal imaging,16 with viral vector mediated expression of the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP, it is now possible to optically read out retinal ganglion cell (RGC) activity at the cellular scale in NHP in vivo.6,17,18 However, viral transduction of the ganglion cell layer (GCL) after intravitreal injection of an adeno-associated virus (AAV) has previously been spatially restricted to a ring of RGCs serving the most central foveal cones in primates.19 This is because the inner limiting membrane (ILM), a thin border between the retina and vitreous fluid formed by astrocytes and end feet of Müller cells, creates a barrier to viral penetration outside of the foveal pit.20 Peeling the ILM before intravitreal injection has reliably expanded viral transduction of Green Fluorescent Protein in retinal neurons,21–23 and this approach has been proposed as a strategy to dramatically expand the area of RGCs transduced by gene therapies. Peeling of the ILM has been associated with mild edema caused by the surgical procedure itself, swelling of the arcuate nerve fiber layer,24 dissociated optic nerve fiber layer,25 and intraretinal edema26 that, although minor, could be worsened by viral transduction.27
In this study, we dramatically expand the area of the NHP retina accessible for optical recording of function in vivo by removing the ILM before intravitreal injection of the viral vector coding for GCaMP. Not only does this approach expand the potential usefulness of this optical recording platform in vivo, but it also provides insight into the impact of the ILM procedure on the retina at the cellular scale in the living eye. We obtained RGC responses at eccentricities that were previously inaccessible, in multiple animals, over months and present the first optical recordings from the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL). Additionally, a comprehensive assessment of concomitant inflammation was performed to better understand the outcomes associated with pairing the ILM peel procedure with a viral vector injection.

## Materials and Methods

## Experimental Design
Five experimental eyes and one control eye from three Macaca fascicularis (two male, one female) were used in this study. All five experimental eyes were vitrectomized and underwent peeling of the ILM centered on the fovea (Figs. 1A–B). Six to eight weeks later, an intravitreal injection of a viral vector was performed to produce GCaMP expression in the ganglion cell layer (Fig. 1C). Three of the five experimental eyes received an intravitreal injection of viral vector AAV2.7m8-SNCG-GCaMP8s, while the two eyes of macaque M3 received the GCaMP8m variant. One control eye did not undergo vitrectomy or ILM peeling (Table 1). We did not perform a vitrectomy without an ILM peel as an additional control because previous studies have established that vitrectomy alone was insufficient to extend viral transduction.21 Confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (cSLO) confirmed the presence of GCaMP in the area corresponding with the region of the surgical ILM peel (Fig. 1D). cSLO, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and color fundus photography were performed longitudinally for up to 6 months after the procedure. Functional assessment in vivo was conducted using fluorescence AOSLO in combination with a red LED flicker stimulus (Figs. 1E–G). ## Animal Welfare
The macaques used in this study were housed in pairs in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care–accredited facility in compliance with the University of Rochester University Committee on Animal Resources. They had free access to water and primate biscuits, providing a complete and nutritious diet. To supplement this, they were given various treats multiple times daily, such as nuts, dried fruit, vitamin chews, and a large variety of fresh fruits and vegetables. They were also given high-value treats during training, including Ensure, marshmallow snacks, and fruit gummies. An animal behaviorist and veterinary technicians provided novel enrichment items multiple times a week, including items such as grass, oranges with peels still on, treat-filled forage boxes, grapevines, water play, and popcorn balls. Daily enrichment included mirrors, puzzle feeders, durable chew toys, daily television time and/or music, and regular visits from staff to offer treats and socialization. All macaques were cared for by the Department of Comparative Medicine veterinary staff, including three full-time veterinarians, five veterinary technicians, and care staff who monitored animal health and comfort at least three times daily. The animal behaviorist regularly recorded and analyzed primate behavior to monitor any signs of stress. This study was carried out in strict accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals and the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. The protocol was approved by the University Committee on Animal Resources of the University of Rochester (PHS assurance number: D16-00188[A3292-01]).

## Immunosuppression
All macaques in this experiment received subcutaneous cyclosporine injections daily. Blood trough levels were collected to titrate the dose to 150 to 250 ng/mL, and this level was maintained for the duration of the experiment. The timings of immune suppression relative to the ILM peel surgery and viral vector injections are detailed in Table 1.

## Animal Preparation for ILM Peel Surgery
The surgical subject was fasted overnight with free access to water. The animal was sedated using ketamine (100 mg/mL) and medetomidine (20 mg/mL), then intubated and kept at proper anesthetic depth via 1.5% to 3% isoflurane throughout the procedure. The animal was positioned in dorsal recumbency on a padded surgical table. A Bair Hugger warming system was placed over the animal to maintain body temperature. Vital signs including temperature, heart rate and rhythm, respirations, end tidal CO2, blood pressure, SpO2, and reflexes were monitored continuously and recorded every 15 minutes. Pupil dilation was accomplished using 1% atropine ophthalmic solution. An aseptic preparation was performed on the surgical eye and surrounding area using 5% betadine solution and flushed with sterile 0.9% saline. The subject's eye and body were then draped in a sterile fashion.

## ILM Peel Procedure
The ILM peel procedures performed in NHP closely followed the protocol used in human surgery. A standard 3-port pars plana vitrectomy was performed through 25G cannulas using the Constellation vitrectomy system (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA), OPMI Lumera 700 operating microscope, and RESIGHT viewing system (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany). Triamcinolone was injected intravitreally to visualize the vitreous. A posterior vitreous detachment was induced using aspiration from the vitrector or a soft tip cannula. The peripheral vitreous was trimmed. Brilliant Blue G Ophthalmic Solution (DORC, Zuidland, the Netherlands) was placed over the macula and the ILM was stained for 2 minutes (Supplementary Movie 1). A fluid–fluid exchange was performed to remove the dye. The stained ILM was then peeled 360°, in an approximately 5- to 6-disc diameter area (13–24 mm2) centered on the macula, using ILM peeling forceps (Alcon) (Supplementary Movie 1). The three ports were removed at the end of the surgery and the sclerotomies sutured. The animal was given an intravitreal injection of the antibiotics vancomycin (1 mg/0.1 mL) and ceftazidime (2 mg/0.1 mL). The immunosuppressant cyclosporine (150–250 ng/mL), anti-inflammatory meloxicam (5 mg/mL), analgesic buprenorphine SR (3 mg/mL), and an anesthetic reversal of antisedan (5 mg/mL) were administered postoperatively.

## Intravitreal Injection of the Viral Vector
Intravitreal injection of AAV2-7m8.SNCG.jGCaMP8s.WPRE.SV40 (100 µL at titer 2.62 × 1013 GC/mL) or AAV2-7m8.SNCG.jGCaMP8m.WPRE.SV40 (100 µL at titer ≥2.46 × 1013 GC/mL), was performed as previously described in Yin et al. (2011).28 Briefly, the eye was sterilized with 5% diluted betadine before the vector was injected into the vitreous through the pars plana using a tuberculin syringe and a 30G needle. All viral vectors were synthesized by the University of Pennsylvania Vector Core. Neutralizing antibodies to AAV2 were 1:25 or lower in all four injected animals before the study. After intravitreal injection of the vector, each eye was imaged weekly with a conventional scanning light ophthalmoscope (Heidelberg Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Franklin, MA, USA) using the blue autofluorescence modality, to determine the onset of expression, and to monitor eye health. Animals received 2 mg/0.05 mL of triamcinolone intravitreally (Kenalog-40) after the injection to treat symptoms of uveitis.

## Animal Preparation for Imaging
All animals were fasted from 4 to 18 h before anesthesia induction. Anesthesia induction began with 5 to 20 mg/kg ketamine, 0.25 mg/kg midazolam, and 0.017 mg/kg glycopyrrolate intramuscularly. The pupil was dilated with a combination of tropicamide 1% and phenylephrine 2.5%. In cases of minimal pupil dilation within the standard time, phenylephrine 10% and/or cyclopentolate 1% drops were administered. Flurbiprofen ophthalmic drops are given to the target eye, to prevent any discomfort from prolonged use of an eye lid speculum. Both eyes were covered with a hydrating ophthalmic gel (GenTeal; Alcon). The target eye then had the lid speculum placed to keep the eye open during imaging and a contact lens was placed to ensure corneal protection. The fellow eye was taped closed with porous tape, to protect the cornea from drying.
The animal was placed in a stereotaxic cart. Before intubation, an oxygen mask with 1% to 2% isoflurane, was placed over the monkey's face to allow for adequate sedation for intubation. An intravenous drip of lactated Ringers with 5% dextrose was maintained at 5 mL/kg/h for the duration of imaging. The monkey was intubated and maintained at a surgical plane of anesthesia with isoflurane 1.0% to 2.5%. A Bair Hugger warming system was placed over the monkey to maintain body temperature. Monitoring was conducted as previously described.
After a surgical plane of anesthesia had been established, a Rocuronium constant rate infusion (800 µg/mL) was given with an initial bolus dose of 100 to 400 µg/kg, followed by a dose range from 200 to 600 µg/kg/h. The lowest effective dose of rocuronium needed to control eye movement in each animal was administered. Once respirations ceased, the monkey was maintained on a ventilator until imaging was over and the infusion was turned off. Once a peripheral nerve response was established, an intravenous dose of glycopyrrolate 0.01 mg/kg was given. Five minutes after the glycopyrrolate, neostigmine 0.05 mg/kg was given intravenously. The animal was monitored for indications of breathing against the ventilator and then removed from the ventilator once able to breath without assistance. The animal was removed from the isoflurane no sooner than 15 minutes after the neostigmine injection to ensure stability off the ventilator. The animal was then allowed to wake up and extubated once all reflexes had returned.

## Clinical Imaging
cSLO, OCT, and color fundus photography were performed before and after the procedure using a commercially available Spectralis HRA (Heidelberg Engineering) and fundus camera (Topcon 50EX). The blue autofluorescence modality on the Spectralis HRA was used to capture GCaMP fluorescence using the 488nm excitation wavelength and a longpass filter at 500 nm. GCaMP fluorescence was also captured using a fundus camera equipped with filters selective for GCaMP (excitation 472/30 nm emission 520/28 nm). We collected 30° and 55° cSLO images centered on the fovea before and after ILM peel, before and after intravitreal injection, and approximately every 30 days thereafter. OCT data from the optic nerve head in addition to slit lamp photographs (Zeiss) were recorded at the same timepoints to document the time course and severity of any inflammation.

## Assessing Ocular Inflammation
A modified Hackett–McDonald scoring system29 was used to evaluate ocular inflammation. A veterinary technician evaluated pupil reflex and conjunctival discharge before pupil dilation. A comprehensive set of slit lamp images were recorded, randomized and graded by a masked ophthalmologist (V.G.). The scoring system is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

## Calcium Imaging Adaptive Optics cSLO
AOSLO data were collected using a previously described AOSLO instrument.30,31 An 847-nm diode laser (QPhotonics, 100–136 µWmm−2) was used as a wavefront sensing beacon in combination with a Shack-Hartman wavefront sensor and a deformable mirror (ALPAO) for measuring and correcting the optical aberrations of the eye. A 796-nm superluminescent diode (Superlum, 57 µWmm−2) was used to image the cone photoreceptor layer using a 2 airy disc (20-µm) pinhole to collect the reflected light. Simultaneously, a 488-nm laser (Qioptiq, 57 µWmm−2) focused on the RGC layer was used to excite GCaMP and emitted fluorescence was collected using a 7.5 airy disc (75 µm) pinhole. This excitation laser produced a light intensity of approximately 100 nWmm−2 at the photoreceptor layer. Laser powers were calibrated before every imaging session to ensure consistency across sessions. To correct for longitudinal chromatic aberration between the 796-nm source, the 488-nm source, and the emitted fluorescence, motorized stages were adjusted to optimize the source and detector positions in vivo before each experiment. The adaptive optics control facilitated fine focus on the retinal ganglion layer. We recorded GCaMP fluorescence from RGCs within each 2.54° × 2.54° field of view as a 7° uniform visual stimulus was presented through a Maxwellian view system.32 An LED (Thorlabs, Newton, NJ, USA) with a 661/20-nm emission filter was used as the visual stimulus to avoid the GCaMP excitation spectrum. The visual stimulus consisted of 20-second contrast increments and decrements against a mean light level (3 µWmm−2 or 15.0 log quanta·cm−2·s−1, 0.33 Michaelson contrast). Because we are using a large 2.5° × 2.5° field of view, some photoreceptors received light from both the 488 nm imaging laser and the 661 nm visual stimulus, reducing the effective Michaelson contrast, particularly at high eccentricities where the Henle fibers are shorter and there is more overlap between the 488-nm excitation laser and the photoreceptors.
Trials began with a 60-second system background period with no laser emission, followed by a 120-second light adaptation period to the 488-nm imaging laser and the mean luminance of the visual stimulus before the 20-second increments and decrements began. The stimulus was designed primarily to yield robust responses despite the relatively low signal to noise ratio of in vivo calcium imaging. Measuring the response over the full 20 seconds for most cells gave us a larger window of integration for quantification of ON and OFF responses and increased the likelihood that we would identify differences in response strength between the control and ILM–peel eyes. Each trial was repeated twice at each retinal location. Total exposure to all laser sources was kept below the Rochester exposure limit,33 designed to be two-fold more conservative than the 2014 American National Standards Institute standard.34

## Data Analysis
The intensity profiles shown in Figures 1A–B show image brightness along the dashed lines, spanning 25° through the animal's foveal center. Each intensity profile was independently normalized by scaling the intensity value to the maximum pixel intensity within each image, resulting in values from 0 to 1. To reduce noise, a 50-point moving average filter was applied to smooth the trace.
To quantify the area of GCaMP expression, we used FIJI/ImageJ36 to manually segment the bright region corresponding to extrinsic fluorescence in 25 degree cSLO images (blue autofluorescence modality). The contrast of the images was adjusted to maximize the discriminability of this region relative to retinal autofluorescence, and the boundary was then manually traced with the freehand tool. Degrees were converted to mm by scaling a model human eye (24 mm) to the individual NHP axial length (17.7 mm). The measure function in FiJI was used to calculate the segmented area in square millimeters. These segmentation masks closely corresponded with the extent of the ILM peel captured in the surgical video. An example of this correspondence is shown in Figures 1B and 1D.
Adaptive optics calcium imaging data was stored as an uncompressed AVI video format. Stimulus information was written frame by frame to a csv file, providing an accurate record for analysis. The reflectance videos of the photoreceptor mosaic were registered offline using a strip-based cross-correlation algorithm35 that corrected translational motion between frames and intraframe warping due to retinal motion. Each frame of the GCaMP fluorescence video was coregistered to the photoreceptor reflectance video. The frames of the fluorescence video were temporally integrated to form a high signal-to-noise ratio z projection image that was used to create an RGC segmentation mask using FIJI.36 RGC segmentation masks were imported into MATLAB and used to tabulate individual cell traces for each imaging location. These cell traces were filtered using a 50-frame moving median filter. The instantaneous z-score values were calculated as the GCaMP response amplitude (signal) minus the mean amplitude of the last 20 seconds of the 120-second prestimulus baseline adapted response (noise), all divided by the standard deviation of the noise. Cell responses were classified as ON center or OFF center and time averaged in the corresponding regions to calculate an overall cell z-score. Final z-scores were computed using only the relevant (ON or OFF) response periods for each cell. The stimulation paradigm and example responses are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. The criterion for a responsive cell was set to 2.5 standard deviations above the noise. Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB two-sample t test, with group spread reported as the mean ± SD.
To record from the RNFL near the optic disc we used the same imaging and stimulation paradigm that was used for RGCs. We integrated a large area encompassing multiple passing fibers to enable extraction of population-level responses.

## Results

## ILM Peel Enables Functional Recording From RGCs at Previously Inaccessible Eccentricities In Vivo
To assess the impact of ILM peel on the spatial extent of GCaMP transduction in the GCL, we directly compared the pattern of expression in the ILM peeled eye with the fellow eye of the same animal (M1), which received an intravitreal injection but no ILM peel (control). Figures 2A and 2B show a 5-fold increase in the area of GCaMP expressing RGCs in the ILM peeled eye (12.9 mm2) relative to the control (2.5 mm2). In the ILM peeled eye, RGCs were transduced with GCaMP out to 9.2° superior to the fovea, whereas in the control eye the maximum eccentricity was just 3.5°. The increased fluorescence relative to the autofluorescent background observed in Figure 2B extended to the limit of the ILM peel identified by retinal landmarks in the surgical video. Furthermore, imaging with a fluorescence fundus camera equipped with filters selective for GCaMP excitation and emission confirmed that the elevated fluorescence was the extrinsic fluorophore GCaMP.

To assess the responses of RGCs in the ILM peeled and control eyes, we performed calcium imaging AOSLO while the retina received periodic visual stimulation at 0.025 Hz. Figure 1G shows an example fluorescence trace from a single ON cell in the ILM peeled eye. The magnitudes of single cell responses recorded in the ILM peeled and control eyes are shown in Figure 2C, represented as z-scores. The distribution of z-scores from individual RGCs in the ILM peeled and control eyes were not significantly different 3.5° (P = 0.61), suggesting that the ILM peel did not adversely impact RGC physiology at this eccentricity. Importantly, only in the ILM-peeled eye was it possible to obtain functional recording from RGCs at eccentricities beyond 3.5°. Figure 2C shows responses collected from RGCs at 9.2° from the foveal center. This was the first time in vivo calcium imaging had been performed outside the parafovea. No RGCs fluoresced beyond 4° from the fovea in the control eye (M1 OS). The z-scores from M1 OD at 9.2° were found to be significantly lower than those at 3.5° in the same eye (P = 0.0004).
Because functional recordings were obtained in vivo, it was possible to return to the same region of the GCL to longitudinally monitor RGC responses over a period of months (Fig. 3). For M1 OD, RGC response amplitudes at 9° did not significantly change over time (Figs. 3A, 3B; paired t test; P = 0.86, P = 0.42), but the amplitudes at 3° significantly increased after 5 months (Figs. 3C, 3D; paired t test; P = 0.02). Compared to the non-ILM peeled fellow eye at 3.5° (Figs. 3E, 3F) that was injected first, OD had significantly higher amplitudes (paired t test; P = 0.021). Although responses varied from week to week, no long-term decrease in the calcium responses of RGCs were observed, suggesting that RGC function was not significantly compromised by the ILM peel procedure.

## Optical Readout of RGC Activity Is Possible From the RNFL
For the first time, we have shown that it is possible to record not only from RGC somas, but also from the RNFL near the optic disc (Fig. 5). We evaluated calcium responses from the RNFL in both the ILM peeled and control eyes of M1. The investigated locations were outside of the ILM peeled area and as a result lacked fluorescing RGC somas, but did contain GCaMP-expressing axonal fibers (Figs. 5 A, 5C). The averaged signal from the integrated region (Figs. 5B, 5D) corresponded with an OFF type response in both cases, but in the control eye the magnitude of the response was reduced by one-half (3.3 ± 0.02 in M1 OD and 1.4 ± 0.02 in the control) (Fig. 5E).

## Structural Imaging Reveals Minor Morphological Changes After ILM Peel
To ensure we had a full understanding of any structural changes in the retina resulting from the ILM peel, we closely monitored all eyes over time with fundus photography and OCT (Fig. 6). Alteration of the foveal contour was visible in the B-scan OCT images of all subjects, with the extent of this alteration varying between individuals. The foveal pit diameter of M1 OD (Fig. 6A) and M3 OD (Fig. 6E) appears slightly reduced compared with before the ILM peel, whereas the foveal contours of M2 (Figs. 6B, 6C) and M3 OS (Fig. 6D) were more dramatically altered. These changes in foveal contour persist throughout the observation period (6 months after ILM peel). No macular holes were created, but disruption to the photoreceptor layer was noted in M2 OS and improved over time.

Swelling of the arcuate nerve fiber layer also known as concentric macular dark spots (CMDS),24,37 were observed in the color fundus images of three of the five eyes (Figs. 6B–6D, white arrows) surrounding the fovea and aligned with the sweep of retinal nerve fibers toward the optic disc. Shallow dimples in the RNFL were observed after ILM peel in OCT-B scan data from all animals, consistent with previous reports of disassociated optic nerve fiber layer.38,39
To further investigate potential damage to the GCL or RNFL, we used fluorescence AOSLO imaging (Fig. 7A) to investigate retinal locations containing both darkened spots in color fundus and disassociated optic nerve fiber layer in the OCT-B scan. Our findings confirmed that dimples present in the RNFL (Figs. 7B, 7D) correlated with the absence of small (<100 µm) pockets of RGCs (Figs. 7C, 7E). This RGC loss was variable between animals. Animal M1 OS (Fig. 7D, left) had only one hypofluorescent region near the edge of expression (Fig. 7D, right), whereas other eyes had them close to the fovea, predominantly in the temporal retina. To test the connection between CMDS and the RNFL dimples visible in OCT, we investigated patches of CMDS with fluorescence AOSLO. We found that these CMDS regions were not associated with missing RGCs. For example, in M2 OS (Figs. 7F, 7G) the CMDS region does not appear on the OCT as a dimple in the RNFL, nor in the AOSLO data as hypofluorescent.

A board-certified ophthalmologist evaluated ocular inflammation using a Modified Hackett–McDonald scoring system (Table 2)29 in all four eyes of animals M2 and M3 following intravitreal injection of the viral vector. In M2, inflammation was characterized principally by conjunctival congestion, swelling, and decreased corneal transparency with pigment noted on the anterior capsule of OS up to 12 weeks after injection. M3 primarily experienced decreased corneal transparency in both eyes, with OS scoring higher on the Modified Hackett-McDonald scale, indicating greater severity. Additionally, M3 developed mild conjunctival congestion and swelling, which persisted up to 7 weeks after injection. Despite temporary inflammation, GCaMP expression was maintained in all eyes for the duration of this study.

## Discussion
Surgical removal of the ILM before intravitreal injection of a viral vector coding for GCaMP expanded the mean area of the retina accessible for optical recording from RGCs eight-fold across the five primate eyes evaluated in this study. These are the first optical measurements of RGC activity at eccentricities beyond a small (<3°) foveal ring that have been achieved in vivo and the first demonstration that optical recording of calcium responses from the RNFL is possible. Longitudinal recordings of calcium responses from RGCs over weeks and months showed variation but no significant long-term decline, suggesting that RGC function was not significantly compromised by the ILM peel procedure. This result is promising for future applications of ILM peeling in gene and cell therapies. In addition, we used AOSLO to investigate previously reported structural defects associated with the ILM peel procedure and observed a variable number of small hypofluorescent regions of RGC loss, more common in temporal retina and visible as depressions in the OCT images of the GCL. This minimal loss of discrete patches of RGCs may be an acceptable tradeoff, given the expanded region accessible for calcium imaging in vivo or for therapeutic applications.
The advances presented in this paper have implications for both retinal neuroscience, preclinical assessment of retinal function, and the delivery of new therapies that could use the ILM peel procedure. The ability to functionally survey large areas of the GCL beyond the fovea both in vivo and at the cellular scale could be valuable in identifying and interrogating rare classes of primate RGCs and understanding how these cells change with eccentricity. From a preclinical standpoint, the cellular scale assessment of retinal function in vivo has potential to advance our ability to characterize disease models and detect and track restored RGC function that might be spatially and temporally sporadic, for example, in the development of cell therapies. In this case, peeling the ILM would expand the available surface area for therapeutic investigation by eight-fold. Lastly surgical peel of the ILM may be a viable strategy to expand the region of the retina that can be transduced by gene therapies or receive RGC transplants. Our results suggest that RGC function is not severely impacted by this intervention in the months after surgery and for example, in the case of, optogenetic gene therapy targeting RGCs,17,40 ILM peel could substantially increase the patients’ restored visual angle and, therefore, the potential benefit derived from this therapy.

## Variability in RGC Responses Between Subjects
We observed a substantial variability in z-scores between animals, with subject M1 responding with significantly higher z-scores relative to other experimental eyes. A variety of factors could be behind this, but we note that M1 was significantly older (10 years as opposed to 4 years) and had fewer inactivating antibodies to AAV2 than the other animals (Table 1). Age can have an impact on innate immunity, and could have affected the efficacy of viral transduction or the degree of inflammation impacting anterior segment optics. In M1 there was also a much shorter time between ILM peel and the first intravitreal injection, minimizing any potential obstruction of viral access to the GCL by glia-mediated scar formation; although the ILM does not regrow, over time Muller glia produce a basement membrane that covers most of the exposed retina.41

## Functional Responses Were Obtained From the RNFL
We were able to extract functional responses from RGC axons in addition to those recorded directly from RGC somas. This was possible both in the control eye and the ILM peeled eye, but with double the Z score in ILM peeled eyes relative to the control (Fig. 5). This finding could be explained by the increased number of GCaMP-expressing RGCs in the ILM peeled eye and, therefore, a greater number of GCaMP-expressing axons traversing the field of view. We note that the RNFL trace was dominated by an OFF response that may be explained by the stimulus design. A high photopic mean light level can drive stronger OFF responses than ON. An enlarged, panretinal stimulus could further increase the measured RNFL response by activating additional RGCs. This finding raises the possibility of RGC responses being partially contaminated by signals from the overlying RNFL; further work is required to understand the relative magnitudes of soma and RNFL responses, a ratio that is likely to vary with retinal position and focal plane. Recording directly from the RNFL may be of interest in preclinical models of glaucoma or assessing axon regeneration of transplanted ganglion cells in vivo.42 Future work may focus on segmenting responses from individual axon bundles to better understand organization of functional responses traveling to the optic disc.

## The Ocular Inflammation Associated With Viral Vector Injections in Immunosuppressed ILM Peeled Animals Does not Prevent Functional Recording
Inflammation was observed in the weeks after viral vector injection in the eyes of M2 and M3 (Table 2; inflammation was not evaluated in M1). The duration and extent of inflammation varied between immune-suppressed animals, but we did not observe a substantial increase in inflammation relative to a previous report from a nonvitrectomized, non–ILM-peeled primate eye intravitreally injected with vector AAV2:CAG:GCaMP6s.43 Relative to this animal, M2 scored higher in overall inflammatory response, whereas M3 scored lower with both M2 and M3 recovering faster. The present study used a ganglion cell–specific promoter SNCG44 instead of the ubiquitous promoter CAG, a different serotype (7m8 instead of AAV2) and GCaMP generation (GCaMP8 instead of GCaMP6) relative to the previous study, making a direct comparison challenging, but the general similarities suggest that inflammation does not preclude adoption of this technique in a preclinical context.

## Surgical Outcomes Associated With Peeling the ILM
This study demonstrates the feasibility of using an ILM peel to expand the imaging area for the functional assessment of macaque RGCs and also provides new insight into outcomes associated with the surgery itself. Consistent with clinical literature, the most obvious consequence of ILM peel is an altered foveal contour (Fig. 6), consistent with previously reported increase in central macular thickness.45 CMDS and disassociated optic nerve fiber layer were also observed, the latter being associated with small pockets of RGC loss and occurring more frequently in temporal retina where the RNFL is thinner.46
Some RGC loss after ILM peeling is consistent with immunohistochemical studies of excised ILM, suggesting that RGCs can be removed during the ILM peeling process.47 In this study, M1 (Fig. 4A; 10 years old) showed only a single focal spot of hypofluorescent RGC loss, compared with more numerous hypofluorescent regions of RGC loss present in the temporal retina of younger NHPs (Figs. 4B–4E; ∼4 years old). Electron microscopy studies have demonstrated that the ILM basement membrane undergoes significant age-related changes.48 In the human eye, the ILM thickens from 70 nm at fetal stages to 300 nm by age 22 years, reaching up to 2 µm by age 90 years. In addition to thickening, the ILM also becomes progressively stiffer and undergoes changes to its biochemical composition that may further decrease retinal adhesion in older animals. Although the numbers of animals are limited, we speculate that these age-related changes in ILM adherence may explain why the older animal, M1, showed more limited RGC loss relative to the younger animals in the study.
Determining the impact of ILM peeling alone on visual function is challenging as the surgical procedure is typically performed as a treatment for retinal problems rather than on otherwise healthy eyes. One study examining ILM peeling during idiopathic epiretinal membrane removal found no statistically significant difference in visual acuity between patients who underwent ILM peeling and those who did not.49 Studies examining structural retinal changes after ILM peeling have found that arcuate nerve fiber swelling24 and dissociated optic nerve fiber layer50 appearance have not been correlated with decreases in visual acuity, although studies suggest that there may be decreases in sensitivity.51 The primary benefit of ILM peeling lies in expanding the visual field that is treatable with intravitreal gene therapies from the central 3° of the fovea to 12° in patients with profound vision loss. For patients with central retinal degeneration, it may be prudent to restrict the ILM peel only to the area requiring treatment to minimize any risk to the remaining retina. In the scenario that this treatment is being offered to patients with no central vision, the changes in the foveal contour or the loss of small patches of RGCs are of relatively little concern.

## Alternatives to ILM Peeling
Alternative methods to overcome the barrier to viral transduction posed by the ILM in primates have been suggested including enzymatic digestion of the ILM with a nonspecific protease,20,52 photodisruption,53,54 or sub-ILM injection.55 Enzymatic digestion has the advantage of not requiring surgery, but has not been attempted in NHPs, where the ILM is thicker than in small animal models. Because ILM thickness is eccentricity dependent in primates, it is not straightforward to titrate the correct dose of enzyme, risking substantial retinal damage. Photodisruption of the ILM has been tested successfully in bovine retinal explants53 using indocyanine green, and recently this work has been extended to an in vivo rabbit model56; however, further testing is necessary before it can be applied to NHP. Last, an alternative surgical strategy is sub-ILM delivery of the viral vector that may yield similar results while keeping more of the structure intact. Because the ILM is composed of Muller cell foot plates, its removal has the potential to cause Muller cell dysfunction.57 ILM delivery is, therefore, desirable, but is surgically very challenging given the thickness of the ILM and there is a risk of delivering the viral vector subretinally or subhyloid, neither of which would target the GCL.

## Conclusions
This study demonstrates not only that the ILM peel approach allows functional recording from a larger area of the GCL in primate, but also that the recordings remain stable over time and are comparable to those in an eye with no ILM peel. As such, this technique may be of value for the preclinical evaluation of retinal function, both for detecting vision loss and assessing the impact of therapeutic interventions. For cell-based therapies where single-cell resolution is needed, a larger area to assess retinal function would greatly increase the chance of success given the potential sparse integration and uncertainty of the locus of cell delivery. Last, these data suggest that ILM peel is a viable approach for expanding the area of the GCL that is treatable by intravitreal delivery of gene therapies.
