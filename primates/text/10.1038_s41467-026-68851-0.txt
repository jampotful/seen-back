# Physiological basis of resolution acuity in vision

## Abstract
AbstractVision is the primary sensory modality for many animals, especially humans and other primates. A fundamental constraint on visual resolution acuity is set by the size and spacing of cone photoreceptors, notably in the fovea of the retina where cones are the smallest. To optimally utilise this cone array, neurons in the retina and lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) must have receptive field centres comprised of only one cone, yet such receptive fields have never been observed directly. Here we comprehensively map parafoveal LGN receptive fields in male macaques using an adaptive optics microstimulator and align them to the underlying cone mosaic. The receptive field centres of parvocellular LGN neurons were most often defined by signals originating from a single cone photoreceptor, a finding confirmed by biophysical light capture modelling and spatial frequency tuning data. Our results demonstrate that visual acuity originating at the fovea is mediated by LGN neurons working at the limit of cone photoreceptor spacing. Revealing this physiological basis of spatial resolution reconciles longstanding anatomical and perceptual data over what mechanism limits acuity prior to cortical processing, underscoring the centrality of optical correction for attaining peak visual resolving power.Subject terms: Thalamus, Retina, Neurophysiology

## Introduction
The visual system of humans and other primates evolved anatomical specialisations in both the eye and the brain that emphasise the veridical detection of high spatial frequencies, a capability that defines resolution acuity. In the eye, the hallmark is the fovea, a small locus in the retina where cone photoreceptors have an exceptionally high density1,2, where other retinal cells are displaced to allow less optical distortion and scatter for the photoreceptors3,4 and where the centre of gaze is anchored for most eye movements5,6. To take advantage of the extra visual information that comes from this dense array of foveal photoreceptors, the cerebral cortex considerably expanded the amount of cortical tissue devoted to these ocular adaptations, particularly in primary visual cortex (V1)7–10. The presence of all these features underscores the importance of the fovea, but high acuity visual information can only be utilised if the physiological signals from individual cones are passed directly to V1.
There is ample evidence from retinal anatomy that a signal from one cone photoreceptor can travel through a bipolar cell to a single midget retinal ganglion cell, availing a one-to-one pathway from a photoreceptor to an output neuron of the retina11,12. This circuit is considered responsible for transmitting the highest spatial-frequency information available from the cone mosaic spacing and exists in two functionally distinct forms—ON-centre and OFF-centre—mediated by dedicated bipolar cells. While there is strong psychophysical evidence that resolution acuity appears closely related to the distance between cones in the fovea13–17, there is no direct evidence that the receptive fields of any neurons prior to V1 are scaled to support perception at the resolution afforded by the cone mosaic spacing. In contrast to resolution acuity, there are visual hyperacuity capabilities—like vernier acuity tasks—that enable spatial discrimination sensitivity of extended objects to exceed that of the cone mosaic18. Human hyperacuity performance suggests there is a clear role for cortical processing that can improve spatial resolution independent of the limits imposed by the retina. So there remains a question about how neural signals from the retina determine the high visual acuity enjoyed by primates.
Normally, the optics of the eye diminish the high spatial frequency content of images delivered to the retina, in part because the optics are never perfect19–21 and in part due to diffraction, which imposes a physical limit on the contrast of images with increasing spatial frequency22. To present images to the retina with high contrast at spatial frequencies exceeding ~20 cycles/°, investigators have resorted to using interference fringe patterns that bypass the eye’s optics23 or adaptive optics correction of high-order aberrations24. To physiologically map the receptive fields from the foveal retina, we used an adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscope (AOSLO, Fig. 1a) and recorded from parvocellular neurons in the macaque lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), which receive their primary input from single midget retinal ganglion cells25,26.

## Results

## Single cone-sized receptive fields
We used the AOSLO to take cone-resolved images of the retina and to identify the location of the fovea in the retina of all macaques studied (n = 3). Our AOSLO system was also capable of simultaneously delivering a movie stimulus containing red (710 ± 12 nm) and green (543 ± 11 nm) wavelengths while imaging the retina in infrared (842 ± 25 nm). The stimulus wavelengths and intensities were chosen such that the green light equally drove M and L cones, while the red light stimulated L cones preferentially by a factor of 19. The M and L cones represent at least 90% of the cones in the macaque retina and are much more likely to comprise single cone receptive fields than S cones27,28. To map the receptive fields of LGN neurons, we used binarized uncorrelated noise movies in which the two colours were modulated independently at 30 Hz. These stimulus movies subtended from 0.18°–0.32° on the retina and had a resolution of 32 × 32 stimulus pixels (each stimulus pixel was composed of a 4 × 4 set of imaging pixels) (Fig. 1b). The stimuli were thereby set to have spatial frequencies ranging from 50–88 cycles/°, with each stimulus pixel being smaller than a cone at any eccentricity studied. When a stimulus pixel had both red and green channels turned on, the photopic luminance was ~280 cd/m2, appearing as a vivid yellow and its contrast ratio against the background light was greater than 250:1 (see ‘Methods’). These movie stimuli were effective at driving neural responses during each frame (Fig. 1c) and even more so for specific pixel conditions, as exemplified by the heightened spike activity in Neuron 1 for two ON pixels (Fig. 1d) where the firing rate increased from 22 to 33 spikes/s at the maximal response over baseline (Fig. 1e). Spatial resolution of the imaging and the stimuli could be varied depending on distance from the fovea, to accommodate the fact that cone diameters increase substantially within 5° of the foveal centre1. AOSLO images were 512 × 512 pixels and ranged from 0.73°–1.28° in width; consequently, to accurately measure the eccentricity of the receptive field locations on the retina, we created montages for each eye (Fig. 2a, b).

We made extracellular recordings in the LGN of anesthetized macaques undergoing neuromuscular blockade to quell eye movements. To minimise any remaining motion artifacts that would enlarge the receptive fields due to respiratory and cardiac pulsation, all stimuli were delivered with real-time retinal stabilisation29,30. Recordings were made in both parvocellular and magnocellular layers, as identified by post-mortem histology (Supplementary Fig. 1). During retinal imaging and stimulation, continuous electrode recordings were made and the corresponding AOSLO movies contained a fiducial cross to indicate the location of each movie frame on the retina. Action potentials from single neurons were identified by offline spike sorting (see ‘Methods’). We calculated chromatic spatiotemporal receptive fields by spike-triggered averaging (STA) of movie frames occurring up to the time of each spike. Example STAs from well-characterised parvocellular neurons (n = 34, 18 ON and 16 OFF) highlight the typical features of the receptive fields. Neuron 1 was an ON cell responding equally to red and green stimuli, with a peak latency of two frames, a response duration less than 150 ms and a receptive field diameter of ~2.7 μm in the red channel (Fig. 2c). Neuron 2 was an OFF cell also driven by both colours, but had shorter latency and duration (Fig. 2d), while Neuron 3, an ON cell, was solely activated by the green channel (Fig. 2e). These STAs are like those generated by midget retinal ganglion cells when stimulated with uncorrelated noise, having similar temporal dynamics and no prominent receptive field surrounds28,31. We used an elliptical 2D Gaussian fitting procedure to estimate receptive field centre sizes for the STAs (see ‘Methods’). Across our population of parvocellular neurons, the receptive field centre diameters were 4.2 ± 1.0 μm (mean ± SD, equivalent visual angle: 1.06 ± 0.27 arcmin), representing cells with fields located 0.42°–4.11° from the foveal centre. In this eccentricity range, these receptive field diameters are anatomically consistent in size with macaque cone inner segments1, which are considered the apertures for light capture by individual cones.

## Receptive fields map to the cone mosaic
We next sought to determine if the activity patterns seen in the STAs corresponded to cones in the retina as imaged by the AOSLO during our recordings. Doing so would confirm that the LGN neurons were receiving inputs primarily from one cone and validate that the spike waveforms with high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) were from single cells (Fig. 3a). We superimposed the STAs from each LGN recording on the cone mosaic, including multiunit and magnocellular recordings, so that the number and location of the cones involved could be identified (Fig. 3b). STAs having single isolated peaks (ON cells) or troughs (OFF cells) were typical for high SNR recordings (n = 23), while a few low SNR recordings had several clearly separated islands in their maps, occasionally containing both ON and OFF fields (Fig. 3b). These cases were analysed as multiunit recordings from separate parvocellular neurons (see ‘Methods’), providing additional receptive field data (n = 11). When magnocellular neurons were encountered, they were weakly driven by our noise stimuli, but still yielded STA maps, though with fields much larger than parvocellular neurons (Fig. 3b).

To complete the alignment of the STAs to the cone mosaic, we had to account for chromatic aberrations introduced by the optics of each animal’s eye. Transverse chromatic aberration (TCA) causes the red and green wavelengths to land on the retina transversely offset from the IR channel, which was used for stimulus targeting. TCA varies across the retina and from eye to eye and therefore had to be measured at the time of stimulus delivery and corrected for in our analyses (see ‘Methods’). The location of each mapped receptive field with respect to the cone array, once corrected for TCA, showed that the parvocellular receptive field centres were often aligned to one specific cone, even when the neurons were driven by both colours (Fig. 3c, d). The presence of TCA in our recordings is evident in Fig. 1d, where the single red and green pixels that optimally drove Neuron 1 were clearly offset, but were aligned with the receptive field centre after TCA correction. Notably, as the cones increased in size with eccentricity, the size of the receptive fields increased concomitantly. These results demonstrate that parvocellular LGN neurons within ~4° of the central fovea carry signals from the retina that primarily represent either ON or OFF contrast information from single cone photoreceptors.

## STA prediction from the light capture model
Given the small scale of the LGN receptive fields, as well as the inherent noise in the STAs and derived receptive field sizes, we used a Monte Carlo simulation incorporating a biophysical model of cone light capture32 to assess the likelihood that we mapped receptive fields originating from one cone (Fig. 4a). This approach seemed justified because the 2D Gaussian fits of the STAs were often slightly elliptical (Fig. 3d, median anisotropy = 1.29), suggesting there could have been some contributions from adjacent cones in the STAs. We simulated the size and shape of mapped receptive fields expected to result from either 1, 2 or 3 cone photoreceptors contributing to a receptive field centre and considered red and green wavelengths independently because longitudinal chromatic dispersions would lead to differences in stimulus size on the retina (see ‘Methods’). Model parameters included cone size, placement of the stimulus movie relative to the cone mosaic, jitter in the stimulus delivery due to tracking errors and STA noise level (Fig. 4b). Movie placement was important because the STA maps were expected to be very sensitive to the position of a stimulus pixel landing on the centre of a cone. The cone sizes simulated were derived from cone spacings measured in our AOSLO images at the sites on the retina where receptive fields were mapped, which ranged from 2.5–7.5 μm (Supplementary Fig. 2). As there may be an optimal focal position for light capture in cones32, we also considered various defocus levels relative to the cone inner segment that could affect absorption by neighbouring cones (Supplementary Fig. 3). Each simulated receptive field STA was fit with an elliptical 2D Gaussian to quantify its size along a major and minor axis.

The simulations were compared to the mapped receptive field sizes (major and minor axes) computed from the wavelength driving the largest STA response in each LGN neuron (n = 22 red STAs, n = 12 green STAs). Compared to the minor axis of the simulations, most of the receptive fields from the STA data were closest to the 1 cone case, regardless of eccentricity or wavelength (Fig. 4c, d). Because both 1 and 2 cone stimulations have the same minor axis width, it is not surprising that the receptive field minor axes were not distinct from 2 cone simulations. The data can be disambiguated when compared to the major axis of the simulations (Fig. 4e, f), where the STA data move upward due to their ellipticity, but now often shift away from the 2 cone simulation data. To ascertain whether the mapped STAs were likely to include input from 1, 2 or 3 cones, we calculated their Euclidean distance from the simulated data in the corresponding 0.1 μm cone spacing bin. From this biophysical simulation, we found that 26 of 34 LGN cells (76%) were most likely to have a single cone receptive field centre, with 6 having a two cone centre and 2 a three cone centre. Four of the two cone centre cases were <1° from the fovea, where the simulations converged and stimulus pixel size was most likely to extend beyond 1 cone. These data support the idea that the majority of foveal parvocellular neurons have input restricted to one cone and that some neurons may be partially driven by 1 or 2 additional neighbouring cones, which is consistent with cone gap-junction coupling in the macaque retina33 or convergence onto a single midget retinal ganglion cell.

## Heightened spatial frequency tuning
If adaptive optics correction of ocular aberrations is indeed required to reveal higher spatial frequency sensitivity in LGN neurons compared to normal optics, then stimulation with traditionally employed sinusoidal gratings should shift spatial frequency tuning curves to higher frequencies. In a subset of neurons mapped with noise stimuli (n = 5), we delivered drifting grating stimuli to probe their spatial tuning characteristics. Gratings varied in spatial frequency from 6.3–31.6 cycles/° (limited by aliasing at the stimulus pixel resolution) and were delivered at different orientations spanning 180° at a drift rate of 2 cycles/s using only the green channel. Raster plots of the spike activity showed that LGN neurons were phase-locked to the grating drift rate and that parvocellular neurons responded to spatial frequencies exceeding 20 cycles/° (Supplementary Fig. 4). Tuning curves were constructed from the amplitude of the Fourier transform of neural responses and were fit with a difference of Gaussians model to quantify the characteristic spatial frequency (Fc) of the receptive field centres (see ‘Methods’). The 4 parvocellular neurons stimulated with drifting gratings had a mean Fc of 22.1 cycles/° (Fig. 5a), a spatial frequency which was ~4× higher than previous studies of parvocellular neurons within 5° of the fovea conducted with normally corrected optics34,35. This result is consistent with the degree of improved spatial frequency sensitivity found in humans when measured with and without adaptive optics correction of monochromatic light36,37. We also estimated Fc from the elliptical Gaussian fits of the STA-mapped receptive fields (averaging the major and minor axes). The distribution of Fc values from our parvocellular neurons had a median of 23.1 cycles/°, closely matching the Fc found by fitting spatial tuning curves (Fig. 5b). These grating results confirm the observations using noise stimuli that, in the absence of ocular aberrations, parvocellular LGN neurons near the fovea typically mediate signals representing one cone receptive field centres.

## Discussion
In the parvocellular pathway of the visual system, which is thought to transmit high-resolution visual information, anatomical and physiological data have been at odds concerning the extent of convergence of photoreceptor inputs onto these neurons38. The anatomy of midget retinal ganglion cells suggests that signals from individual cones should be available39,40, while receptive field mapping experiments have usually found the field centres to be several times larger than one cone at parafoveal eccentricities41–44. Here we have reconciled these two approaches by showing that the parvocellular LGN neurons most often have receptive field centres comprised of one cone, when the optics are suitably corrected and uncorrelated spatial noise stimuli are used.
Our results have several implications about the early visual system. One is that midget retinal ganglion cells—out to at least 4° eccentricity—are likely to transmit signals predominantly devoted to one cone, given their dendritic field size and dedicated bipolar cells39–41,43,45–47. Such an arrangement will enable these neurons to signal cone-opponency at high spatial frequencies. A second point is how dependent the outcome of receptive field mapping is on the type of stimuli used. In two earlier studies where optical imperfections were essentially mitigated, it was still found that receptive field centres were commonly larger than a single cone for cells near the fovea23,48. It is likely that their use of gratings and small spot stimuli more systematically engaged lateral contributions from neighbouring cones through intraretinal circuitry, thereby extending the field sizes observed. The uncorrelated noise stimuli used in this current study would not strongly drive receptive field surrounds and thereby would just produce fields with prominent centres, as evident in ex vivo studies28,31. Finally, the occasional presence (24%) of receptive fields with 2 or 3 cone inputs is consistent with the existence of gap junctional coupling between cone pedicles in the macaque retina, allowing some degree of current to flow among neighbouring cones when stimuli larger than a cone are used33,49,50. The existence of multicone receptive fields is also consistent with some intraretinal convergence of signals onto single midget retinal ganglion cells via amacrine cells and to a small degree by nearby midget bipolar cells11. This finding gives a more comprehensive account of foveal cone signals, indicating there are exceptions to the commonly held idea that they have an exclusively private line for relaying visual information to downstream thalamic and cortical neurons.
Our study shows that the images falling on the retina are captured at the sampling resolution of the photoreceptor mosaic, thereby providing the highest fidelity visual sensory input to the brain. These LGN signals not only transmit optimal spatial sampling of the retinal image, enabling resolution acuity that shows a strong correlation with cone density51, they also provide the strongest chromatic signals if one assumes random wiring between receptive field centres and opposing surrounds for L and M cones28,52. For higher-level visual functions such as hyperacuity or processing of dynamic signals in the presence of eye movements, the single cone receptive fields again provide the best possible sensory input to inform the brain about the object viewed. Vernier acuity, for instance, requires sampled inputs to be slightly offset from another, but now we know that those tasks more certainly arise from pooling at the cortical level rather than any pre-cortical level with lower sampling resolution. Finally, for normal vision, the blur from natural aberrations or slight amounts of uncorrected defocus means images are generally oversampled by the cone mosaic, which serves to mitigate aliasing by photoreceptor array geometry53.
The STAs exhibited slight variations from an exact mapping onto the cone mosaic, pointing to a few limitations of the study. One was the small differences in green versus red receptive field sizes in cells that responded to both wavelengths: this could have been due to defocus differences between the two channels, to longitudinal chromatic aberration (LCA) that was not corrected precisely for each animal or to a larger error in aberration correction for green compared to red light54. Where the receptive field outlines did not fall on the centre of a cone, this could have been due to TCA noise or to an undetected shift in eye position before TCA was measured following stimulation. All of these confounds were incorporated in the light capture simulations, which likely overestimate the degree of contributions from additional cones to the receptive field due to various noise sources.
Knowing that most parvocellular LGN receptive fields have centres arising from a single cone establishes that there is relatively little photoreceptor signal convergence occurring between the foveal retina and V1. Because foveal signals are responsible for what humans consider to be everyday vision, our results showcase the importance of providing the best optical correction available, given that this pathway always lies waiting to transmit visual information with a resolution limited only by cone spacing. Neural models of vision and the design of visual prosthetics can now more accurately consider the behaviour of the parvocellular system that constitutes the single largest visual processing stream from the retina to the brain.

## Methods

## Animal preparation and surgical procedures
Experiments were conducted on 3 adult male Macaca fascicularis (weight range 7.8–10.2 kg, age range 6–13 years), using procedures approved by the UAB Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and in accordance with NIH guidelines. Animals were prepared for the experiments using methods already established26,48. Anaesthesia was induced with ketamine HCl (10 mg/kg) given intramuscularly. Following endotracheal intubation, animals were ventilated with 1.5% isoflurane in a 1:1 mixture of N2O and O2 to maintain anaesthesia during all surgical procedures. We monitored the electrocardiogram waveform, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, blood oxygen (SPO2), end-tidal CO2, urine output and inspired/expired levels of anaesthetic gases throughout each 2–3 day experiment. A solution of 5% dextrose in 0.45% saline was given intravenously at 2–5 mL/kg/hr.
Animals were placed in a stereotaxic frame, which itself was mounted on a gimbal to allow 3-dimensional positioning of the eye with respect to the adaptive optics ophthalmoscope’s beam and focal plane. The gimbal also allowed either eye to be rotated horizontally ± 15° and vertically ± 10°, with the pivot axes centred on the pupil plane of the eye being imaged. To gain electrode access to the LGN, we made a 10 mm diameter craniotomy at a location suitable for a dorsal approach (7–17 mm mediolaterally, 2–12 mm anteroposteriorly). The craniotomy exposure was kept moist with sterile balanced salt solution (Alcon Laboratories), saturating a sterile gauze sponge. After all surgical procedures were completed, we shifted to intravenous sufentanil citrate anaesthesia (4–30 µg/kg/hr; Akorn) and lowered the isoflurane percentage to 0.2–0.4%. Before electrophysiological recordings were made, we established neuromuscular blockade with vecuronium bromide (60 µg/kg/hr; Sun Pharmaceutical Industries) to minimise eye movements.
Lens accommodation was inhibited using topically applied 1% cyclopentolate hydrochloride (Cyclogyl, Alcon Laboratories) and pupil dilation was achieved via instillation of 1% tropicamide (Akorn). Ocular multi-antibiotics (Bausch + Lomb) were applied to prevent infection over the course of the experiment. Eyes were fit with rigid gas-permeable PMMA contact lenses (Essilor Contact Lens Division) to protect the cornea and minimise the spherical correction required during retinal imaging. Clarity of the optics was continuously monitored throughout the experiments. Contact lenses were exchanged with clean ones every 24 h or more frequently, as needed. The sclera was kept moistened with balanced salt solution given topically during pauses between recording episodes in the LGN (about every 2 h).

## Retinal imaging
As depicted in Fig. 1a, we used a multiwavelength AOSLO to image and stimulate cones within a 512 × 512 pixel imaging field on the retina55. The AOSLO removes most of the optical aberrations present in the eye, enabling nearly diffraction-limited imaging of the cone photoreceptors. Retinal images were acquired at a frame rate of 30 Hz, with adaptive optics correction refreshed at 15 Hz. Imaging sampling resolution during stimulus delivery was variable, ranging from 400–704 pixels/°, resulting in image field widths between 1.28° and 0.73°, respectively. Visual angle as projected on the retina was calibrated with an optical model eye containing a rectilinear grid with 100 µm spacing, which also served to correct geometric distortions caused by the sinusoidal path of the raster-scanned beam. The light source was a supercontinuum 100 MHz pulsed laser (SuperK Extreme, NKT Photonics) whose output was limited by bandpass filters (Semrock, IDEX Health & Science) before being directed into three separate optical channels: infrared (IR) (842 ± 25 nm), green (543 ± 11 nm) and red (710 ± 12 nm). The IR channel was used for retinal imaging, optical correction of wavefront aberrations, eye tracking and stimulus target selection, while green and red channels were used for stimulus delivery. The beams from each wavelength were co-aligned within the AOSLO and delivered to the eye through the raster scanning mirrors (Fig. 1a). The green channel wavelength was chosen because it equally stimulates L and M cones, allowing for the variable functional weighting of these cones to be measured regardless of stimulus intensity28,56,57. We note that S cones are 2 log units less sensitive to the green channel than the L and M cones. To help identify cone type, we took advantage of the differential sensitivity of M cones to the red light, which is 1.28 log units lower than L cones. We set the intensity of the red channel to allow equal light capture probability in L cones for both red and green channels. Consequently, M cones would be 1.28 log units (~19×) less likely to be driven by stimuli in the red channel, an outcome that could theoretically distinguish M cones from L cones. Across the experiments, peak power levels in the three channels ranged as follows: IR = 212–285 µW, red = 3.25–7.00 µW, green = 10.8–20.0 nW. These beam powers were measured at the position of the cornea with a power metre (detector head 918D-SL, meter 1936-C, Newport).
The multiwavelength nature of the AOSLO meant chromatic dispersion of the visual stimuli within the eye needed to be taken into account so that we could determine where on the retina each stimulus landed with subcellular accuracy. LCA must be corrected to ensure the red, green and IR wavelengths come to focus on the same axial plane. LCA was corrected in the AOSLO directly using average data for human eyes because they exhibit a small degree of variation58,59. TCA correction is more challenging because it depends on the position of the stimulus beams relative to the pupil and to the unique achromatic axis of each eye60–63. The presence of TCA meant shorter wavelengths can readily land on a different set of cones than longer wavelengths, thus requiring direct measurement of TCA offsets at each stimulation site on the retina, as described previously59. Briefly, after all the movie stimuli were presented (see below), TCA was measured by presenting a static 128 × 256 pixel image containing vertical stripes of either IR, red or green wavelengths, alternating in every third image column. This TCA image pattern was delivered at 2–3 log units higher intensity in order to measure reflected images of the cone mosaic from each wavelength. The reflected TCA images were decomposed into individual wavelength images to compute the TCA offsets in the imaging plane. There was a 20 min recovery time after the TCA measurements to allow cones to recover from the intense light before the next recording site was studied. Corrections for TCA were not applied during stimulus movie presentation, but were used to align receptive field maps to the AOSLO images of the cone mosaics.

## Electrophysiological recordings
Single epoxylite-coated tungsten microelectrodes were used for extracellular recordings (5–8 MΩ; Frederick Haer Company). Each electrode was encased within a stainless-steel guide tube mounted on a stepper-motor micromanipulator (Sensapex). The guide tube was lowered to ~5 mm above the LGN based on the estimated stereotaxic position. At this point, the electrode was advanced alone into the LGN. Differential amplification of recorded extracellular potentials was done using a 10× head stage amplifier followed by 100× amplification (Model 1800, A-M Systems), with the guide tube serving as the reference electrode. Analogue potentials were bandpass filtered between 0.3 and 3 kHz before being split into 2 lines, with the first line being passed to a digital data acquisition system that sampled at 25 kHz (Spike2 version 7.10c and Power 1401, Cambridge Electronic Design). The second line was fed to an oscilloscope (MDO3054, Tektronix) and an audio monitor (Model 3300, A-M Systems). To synchronise the physiological signals with the visual stimuli generated by the AOSLO, four timing signals from the stimuli were recorded as time-stamped events in the same digital data file: frame start time, stimulus delivery ON and video recording ON and OFF signals (all sampled at 5 kHz). In one animal, we included a photodiode signal recording, sampled off the stimulus beam at 50 kHz, to confirm stimulus delivery and timing.
To find a neuron to record as the electrode was advanced, we initially used a retinoscope to sweep large spots of light across the fundus. We listened to the audio monitor for neural activity and stopped advancing the electrode once we identified activity phase-locked to the swept stimulus. Using the oscilloscope, we could monitor the waveform to confirm we had achieved a good SNR. To refine the retinal location within ~2° that drove the spikes, we next flashed a bright spot onto a translucent tangent screen viewed by the animal through a beam-splitting pellicle while listening for a phase-locked response. This tangent screen contained a grid calibrated to give visual field position within 1°. The animal’s head was then repositioned using the stereotaxic gimbal to place that retinal location within the AOSLO beam. Finally, we determined the retinal location at which to centre stimulus movies by manually probing the imaged field with a flashing 3 Hz green stimulus (~5 arcmin) until a phase-locked response was encountered over a small set of cones. Stimulus target sites ranged from 0.42–4.11° in eccentricity across the 3 animals used.

## Visual stimuli
Stimulus movie frames were consistently 128 × 128 imaging pixels, which was one-quarter the size of the imaging field (1/16th of the imaging area). To modulate pixel intensities, the light in each channel passed through dedicated optical switches formed by acousto-optical modulators tuned to each wavelength (Brimrose). The temporal frequency response of these switches limited our ability to change stimulus intensity on every imaging pixel during the raster scan. Efficient switching could only be done every 4 imaging pixels. Thus, a single stimulus pixel was always 4× the size of 1 imaging pixel. For visual stimulation, uncorrelated noise movies were created consisting of binarized 32 × 32 stimulus pixels, containing both red and green channels modulated independently. Under these conditions, the mean power contained in each frame was 1/32nd of the peak power, yielding a range of 100–218 nW for the red channel and 0.34–0.63 nW for the green channel. We note that stimuli were presented on a background light level, arising from a combination of the infrared imaging light and some leak through the optical switches of the stimulus wavelengths (see Supplementary Table 1).
These stimulus powers were chosen to approach equiluminance and be at a level comparable to prior studies. Luminance was estimated by superimposing a steady halogen source, filtered at 540 ± 5 nm, onto the red AOSLO stimulus field and adjusting the red power level until a prominent yellow was perceived by three of the authors with normal colour vision. We measured the luminance of the halogen source (the red stimulus field was turned off) with a spectrophotometer (SpectraScan PR-670, Photo Research) and measured the matching power of the red stimulus field with the power meter (with the green source turned off). These measurements resulted in an estimated luminance range of 73–135 cd/m2 in each channel for a typical movie frame with a 0.32° width. We note that luminance values for the scanning beam of the AOSLO were not measured using the spot-metering of the spectrophotometer because the device assumes a static extended source, not a pivoting scanning beam. Our luminance levels were similar to those used in a prior study without adaptive optics correction41, but are higher than those in other earlier studies34,35.

The spatial extent of the stimulus movie frames ranged from 0.18°–0.32° on the retina, depending on the imaging field size used. As a result, stimulus pixels subtended 0.34–0.60 arcmin (~1.5–3 µm), just smaller than a single cone photoreceptor at the retinal locations studied. These dimensions produced movies with spatial frequencies ranging from 50–88 cycles/°. The uncorrelated noise movies were delivered in 5 s epochs, alternating with 5 s epochs of correlated noise (data to be used in another study). The total time of uncorrelated noise stimulation for the LGN cells analysed ranged from 7.1–17.8 min, most often being 8.25 min (15,000 frames).
Once a retinal location was selected for stimulation as described above, a reference frame was taken to allow us to deliver stimuli that were stabilised on the retina30. This stabilisation corrected for any eye motion present from cardiac and respiratory pulsations that remained after neuromuscular blockade. Movies were stabilised on the retina at the frame rate of 30 Hz by reading the incoming retinal image, comparing it to the reference frame and then predicting the movement of the eye within 2 ms prior to stimulus delivery as the raster scan approached the targeted delivery site. During stimulus delivery, motion-stabilised and unstabilized videos of the retinal images were saved concurrently. An 11 × 11 imaging pixel fiducial cross was automatically inserted into the recorded movies during acquisition to indicate the centre of each stimulus frame. The centre pixel of the fiducial cross was used along with the measured TCA offset values to determine where the light in the red and green channels of the movies fell on the retinal surface, so that the position of the receptive fields (described below) could be registered to the cone mosaic.
In one of our animals, we presented movies consisting of drifting grating stimuli to examine how LGN neurons would respond to traditional stimuli under AOSLO conditions. These movies were constructed from Gabor patterns of drifting sinusoidal gratings with orientations spanning 180° at 7.4° intervals and spatial frequencies ranging from 7.8–32.1 cycles/°. The Gabor envelope was a circular 2D Gaussian with a sigma that was 0.2× the stimulus field width. The gratings drifted at a speed of 2 cycles/s. Every 2 s, the grating orientation and spatial frequency changed pseudorandomly, for a total movie length of 96 s to present each grating set once. For the subset of neurons recorded with grating movies, only the green channel was on for this stimulus and these movies were presented 1 or 2 times.

## Histology
At the end of each experiment, the animal was given a lethal dose of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg IV). After EKG cessation, a bilateral thoracotomy was performed. We removed the brain and placed it in 1–2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB), storing it at 4 °C overnight. The next day, we blocked out a section containing the LGNs, placed the block in 1% paraformaldehyde plus 30% sucrose in 0.1 M PB and stored it at 4 °C until the tissue block sank. To cut sections, the tissue was embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek) and then cut frozen with a sliding microtome into 70 µm coronal sections before being mounted out of PB onto gelatin-coated slides to be air dried.
Alternate sections were processed for Nissl substance or cytochrome oxidase histochemistry. Nissl staining solution was prepared with a chrome alum-gallocyanine technique69. Gallocyanine (1.5 mg/mL; MilliporeSigma) and chromium potassium sulphate (50 mg/mL; Fisher Scientific) were boiled in distilled water for 20 min, cooled and then filtered before adding enough water to restore the specified concentrations. Sections were submerged in this solution for several hours at room temperature until sufficient staining contrast was reached and then rinsed in 0.1 M PB. Sections reacted for cytochrome oxidase histochemistry70 were placed in 0.1 M PB containing cytochrome c (0.3 mg/mL, product C2506), diaminobenzidine (0.5 mg/mL, D5637) and catalase (0.2 mg/mL, C40). All these reagents were from MilliporeSigma. Sections were submerged in the cytochrome oxidase solution in the dark for 6–8 h, at which point they were removed and briefly rinsed with 0.1 M PB. From storage in PB, Nissl and cytochrome oxidase stained sections were then dehydrated via submersion in a series of graded ethanols in water (50, 70, 95 and 100%), followed by 1:1 ethanol: xylene and 100% xylene before being coverslipped with Permount resin (Fisher Scientific).

## Spike sorting and STA generation
To identify action potentials, we used off-line waveform templating (Spike2, Cambridge Electronics Design). From the raw electrode traces, we selected voltage peaks above the typical noise level in the recordings and extracted a ~2 ms waveform surrounding each peak. These spike waveforms were sorted by principal component analysis with k-means clustering. Clustering often revealed two or more waveforms. One cluster usually had waveforms with larger peak amplitudes than the others, indicating better separation from background activity and commonly interpreted as arising from an isolated single neuron. To confirm this idea, we checked interspike interval histograms for the presence of an absolute refractory period. We also computed the SNR of the average spike waveform in each cluster, as an SNR < 2.5 is more likely to be a multiunit recording71. The SNR was defined as the peak-to-trough amplitude divided by twice the standard deviation (SD) calculated away from the peak72. The time of each spike was set to be at the peak amplitude of each spike waveform.
We mapped the LGN receptive fields by computing the STA stimulus from the red and green stimulus channels separately73. STAs were generated for each set of distinct waveforms by reverse correlating spikes with the stimulus movie frames and averaging the correlated frames across spikes. Before computing the STA, the stimulus movies were corrected for small frame misplacements caused by image stabilisation errors (jitter) and for any failed stimulus delivery. Frame misplacements were known from the fiducial cross position and any affected frames were adjusted to correctly position the frame as delivered. Occasional frames that were entirely dropped due to image stabilisation failure were assigned as a blank frame.
The STAs were computed for 7 frames prior to the spike, one frame during the spike and 2 frames delivered after the spike. The binarized movie frames were coded as 1 when a pixel was turned on and −1 when a pixel was turned off. Each STA frame was an average of the frames when a spike occurred. Thus, the STA contrast magnitude represents the probability of each pixel being associated with a spike, with positive values for ON responses and negative values for OFF responses.
Each uncorrelated movie, when averaged, had a small amount of spatial structure due to the short movie lengths and this could have led to an artifact in the STA. Such artifacts were removed by subtracting the average stimulus from the computed STA. Finally, to more accurately represent the light as delivered to the retinal surface, we convolved the final STAs with the optical point spread function of the AOSLO, after rescaling the STAs to the imaging resolution of 128 × 128 pixels. Each STA revealed the receptive field size, position, wavelength preference, latency and response type (ON or OFF) for a given spike waveform.

## Receptive field identification and quantification
From the spike sorting step, our data included 101 distinct waveforms from the LGN recordings made in the 3 animals. An STA was computed for each of these waveforms as described earlier. In some instances, an STA showed no evidence of a receptive field, likely due to it being outside the stimulated area. If any STAs recorded at the same site appeared similar, the STA drawn from the waveform with more spikes had lower noise and was the one chosen for further analysis. Our analysed data set then ended up including 56 STAs with unique candidate receptive fields.
Qualitatively, these STAs exhibited three general patterns that drove neurons: a single small area, multiple small areas or a large area spanning about half the STA width. The STAs with single areas were considered to represent the activity of single LGN neurons (39 of 56 STAs), an interpretation supported by the high SNR of the source spike waveforms. The remaining STAs with multiple activating areas could be interpreted in two ways: either as a single neuron receiving input from several spatially separated cones or as a multiunit recording combining activity from several neurons. Three lines of evidence suggest the latter explanation applies to these STAs: (1) some of the STAs with multiple active areas had mixed ON and OFF behaviour, which is unlikely from the known classification of LGN receptive field centres being either ON or OFF exclusively, (2) the active areas were distinctly separated on the cone mosaic, which implies input originating from signals transmitted by multiple non-adjacent retinal ganglion cells and (3) the SNR of the average waveforms producing these STAs was low, which is associated with the difficulty of isolating multiple small-amplitude spikes. Consequently, we classified the STAs with multiple active areas as multiunit recordings. Anatomically, the STAs containing single or multiple small active areas were confirmed to be in the parvocellular LGN layers based on electrode recording depth and on track reconstructions in the histological sections (Supplementary Fig. 1). From the same criteria, the STAs with the single large active areas were associated with the magnocellular LGN layers and classified accordingly (n = 10).
From the multiunit recordings, based on the criteria above, we considered each distinct active area to represent the receptive field of one LGN neuron. Because the AOSLO allowed us to map LGN receptive fields with near diffraction-limited imaging, it provided a unique opportunity to unveil how individual neural signals contribute to the STA of a multiunit recording. There were 17 multiunit STAs that had 30 distinct active areas, thereby potentially adding 30 cells to our set of 39 single-unit LGN recordings. The cells were classified as either ON or OFF based on the receptive field in the STAs.
The focus of this paper is on parvocellular neurons whose receptive fields were mapped comprehensively. Therefore, we excluded cells with receptive fields that appeared truncated at the edge of the stimulus frame, that had evidence of stimulus delivery failure (e.g. poor stabilisation) or were likely to be magnocellular. Finally, we also excluded some cells based on a low SNR of the receptive field in the STA.
We quantified four values associated with each receptive field using elliptical 2D Gaussian fitting (amplitude, major and minor ellipse diameters and ellipse angle), computed with the lsqcurvefit function in MATLAB version 2023a (Mathworks). The fit diameters were defined to be equal to the 1 SD level along each axis. The SNR of each receptive field was computed by calculating the ratio between the amplitude of the Gaussian fit and the root mean square of the pixel values > 3 stimulus pixels from any receptive field centre. These outlying stimulus pixels were further than 2× the radius of any receptive field centre of the parvocellular neurons. Only receptive fields with SNR higher than ~4 were considered for inclusion, yielding a final population of 18 parvocellular ON cells and 16 parvocellular OFF cells.
To find out which cones were responsible for the receptive fields, we mapped the STAs onto the cone mosaics. This required applying the appropriate TCA offset correction (measured as described above) for the stimulus field for each wavelength. The primary red and/or green STA frames were first centred on the stimulus delivery fiducial cross in the IR channel. The STA frames were then shifted according to the TCA offset values measured during the experiment, placing them at the location where the red and green stimulus movies were delivered.

## Quantification of cone spacing
Estimation of the cone photoreceptor inner segment diameters in our data was derived from cone spacing measurements made from the AOSLO images at the sites on the retina where receptive fields were mapped (Supplementary Fig. 2). First, the centres of at least 50 cones encompassing the receptive field sites were identified by finding the peak reflectivity of each cone. This was done by Fourier filtering the cone mosaic image of interest, followed by thresholding and peak detection. The identified cone positions were then manually reviewed and corrected when necessary. An average cone image was then computed from the identified cones, which were no further than ~0.2° from the receptive field centre. Finally, to estimate the cone spacing, a radial average was calculated from the centre of the average cone image out to 25 imaging pixels using 2D linear interpolation along a radius measured at 1° angular intervals. A smoothing spline was fit to the interpolated radial average and cone spacing was defined as the distance of the first peak away from zero in the fit. We validated the radial averaging by measuring the spacing of synthetic cone mosaics generated from Gaussian-convolved hexagonal grid patterns with known spacing, which yielded spacing ~3% smaller than the predetermined spacing. Thus, the radial averages of the cone mosaics may be marginally underestimated, though only when cones have a perfect hexagonal arrangement.

## Receptive field modelling
We used a validated biophysical light capture model to predict the size and shape of mapped receptive fields expected to result from 1, 2 or 3 cone receptive field centres. The modelling was based on a finite difference beam propagation method described in full detail previously32; we only outline relevant parameters here. The model solves the Helmholtz wave equation for a given volume system of refractive indices and an initial electric field, taking the electric field at one x-y plane and computing the field one step further in the propagated z direction. The voxel resolution was x = 0.1 μm, y = 0.1 μm and z = 0.2 μm. The initial electric field representing the light at z = 0 was calculated by the Fourier transform of the light at the pupil, having a truncated Gaussian profile with a 1/e width (1/e2 in intensity) of 4.75 mm and a beam diameter of 5.85 mm matching the AOSLO system. The eye axial length was assumed to be 18 mm74 and the ocular media had a refractive index of 1.33. By calculating the initial electric field in this manner, defocus could be easily introduced by using the Zernike polynomials to modify the phase of the light at the pupil.
Photoreceptor shape was defined volumetrically by 3 refractive index compartments, starting at the external limiting membrane (ELM) at z = 0, where light was initially assumed to be focused. The effects of defocus were investigated later, as described below. The model used different refractive indices for each compartment: extracellular space = 1.34, inner segment myoid = 1.3605, inner segment ellipsoid = 1.394 and the outer segment = 1.39 − κi, where the refractive index was a complex number which accounted for photon absorption, with κ in the imaginary part being the extinction coefficient. The value for κ was derived from a measurement of the absorption coefficient (α) of macaque M cones at 535 nm75. This absorption coefficient was converted to the extinction coefficient using κ = wαλ/4π, with w being a weighting based on normalized spectral sensitivity for L or M cones as appropriate56. We used κ = 7.22 × 10−4 for the 543 nm light (equivalent for L and M cones) and κ = 4.846 × 10−6 for the 710 nm light. When running simulations with the red light, we assumed only L cones contributed to the captured light, as the contribution from M cones would be ~19× less than L cones at this wavelength.
Cone dimensions were as follows: inner segment length = 34 μm (with myoid = 14 μm and ellipsoid = 20 μm), outer segment length = 50 μm. The photoreceptors at each x-y plane were circular, but the diameter narrowed with distance from the ELM. This gave inner segments a parabolic shape, while outer segments remained cylindrical. Seven identical photoreceptors were arranged in a hexagonal array. The cone spacing in this array also defines its diameter. Inner segment diameter at z = 0 was set to be a factor of 0.82 of the centre-to-centre cone spacing32,76. Outer segment diameter dos was also varied using the relationship dos = 0.12S + 0.35, where S is the photoreceptor spacing based on values given by Polyak67. Once the initial electric field and cone array have been set up in this manner, propagations were performed in the z direction down the length of the 7 cones and the amount of light absorbed in each outer segment was calculated. Examples of the cone shapes and propagations are shown in Fig. 4a.
To generate a simulated receptive field STA, it was assumed that the response of a neuron would be proportional to the amount of light absorbed by 1, 2 or 3 cones when the scanned stimulus beam was in a particular position relative to the cones. If multiple cones were being considered, they were assumed to contribute linearly. The raster scanning of the stimulus beam was mimicked by performing propagations starting at different x-y positions at z = 0, with a separation of 0.55 μm in a 22 × 22 μm grid centred over the central cone of the 7 cone array (matching a 400 pixels/° imaging resolution). This rastering was controlled by varying the tip and tilt Zernike coefficients at the pupil.
The resulting patterns of light capture were base representations of an STA containing the receptive field for the modelled cone system, where any combination of the 7 cones may be chosen. Examples are shown in Fig. 4b. However, this scenario is an idealised version that does not include other factors present in the experiment that alter the STA pattern. Four factors were implemented to better account for the experimental conditions: stimulus delivery jitter, the placement of the stimulus movie relative to the cone mosaic, downsampling and STA noise level.
First, we accounted for stimulus delivery jitter. Although stimuli were stabilised on the retina, there were still small random positional errors during the movie presentation. This error depends on stabilisation quality moment-by-moment during stimulus delivery and we estimated it to have a SD of 1.5 imaging pixels (~0.83 µm, see Supplementary Fig. 2 in ref. 48). Thus, we simulated light capture to be offset in a uniformly random direction with a magnitude determined by a normal distribution with a 1.5 imaging pixel SD. This offset was applied to the absorption maps by interpolation 100 times and an average was taken, resulting in a slight blurring of the STA. Second, because the cones driving the neural response were not known at the beginning of each recording, it was unlikely that placement of the movies had any stimulus pixel centred precisely on a cone. To incorporate this offset into the model, the jittered absorption maps were shifted by up to 0.5 cone spacing, in a uniformly random magnitude and direction, using interpolation. The resulting pattern of light capture with jitter and displacement can be seen in the middle row in Fig. 4b. Third, the fitting of elliptical 2D Gaussians to STAs will be substantially affected by spatial sampling. When a receptive field only contains a few stimulus pixels, the accuracy of any fit will be compromised by undersampling. As the modelled STAs were computed at the imaging resolution of the AOSLO, we therefore downsampled the STAs by 4× to match the delivered stimulus pixel resolution. Finally, noise was added to the modelled STA and a 2D elliptical Gaussian fit was performed to extract the radii of the major and minor axes (see bottom row of Fig. 4b). This noise had a normal distribution with a mean of zero and the 2D Gaussian fit used the same function as used for the empirical results.
These 4 steps simulated the outcome expected for one possible set of conditions. To compare our experimental results to the light capture modelling, we used a Monte Carlo procedure with n = 500,000 simulations for each of the 1, 2 and 3 cone conditions and for both 543 and 710 nm stimuli. The base simulations described above were propagations creating idealised STAs with the photoreceptor spacing ranging from 2.5–7.5 μm in 1 μm steps. There were two reasons for using cone spacing rather than eccentricity from the fovea as the metric for changing cone size. First, while cone size and spacing varies with eccentricity, there is also considerable variation between individuals, eyes and direction from the fovea1,2. Secondly, we could measure cone spacing directly from our AOSLO images, but not cone diameters. Thus, in our Monte Carlo procedure, we first selected cone spacings at random from a uniform distribution between 2.5 and 7.5 μm. For each of these spacings, new STAs were computed (for 1, 2 and 3 cone cases) using interpolation to create STAs lying between the 1 μm steps in the base simulations. We next added jitter, shift and noise to each of these STAs, ensuring that the 1, 2 and 3 cone cases had the same values applied. For the added noise, the SD was picked randomly from a Gamma distribution fitted to the measured SNRs of the experimental results (median = 8.77). Finally, a 2D Gaussian fit was applied to each STA. Fit outliers were removed with the rmoutliers function in MATLAB, using the movemedian filter with a window of 16. This function was applied to the raw results and their reciprocals due to the data having a long tail in the distribution and being bounded at zero. The removed fits were typically associated with low SNRs, which would not have resulted in identifiable receptive fields if such data had been real.
The simulated fits were then plotted as 3D clusters representing 1, 2 or 3 cone receptive fields, with the axes being cone spacing, the major axis of the elliptical fits and the minor axis. To measure how close the experimental data were to these clusters, we discretized the data along the cone spacing axis into bins of 0.1 μm. For each experimental STA, we calculated the Euclidean distances relative to the simulated data for the 1, 2 and 3 cone cases in the corresponding cone spacing bin. Each empirical STA was then classified as comprising 1, 2 or 3 cones based on the shortest distance.
The robustness and sensitivity of these simulated receptive fields were examined for parameters that could differ from assumed values drawn from ex vivo retinal tissue, which include refractive indices, photopigment extinction coefficient and cone inner and outer segment diameters. Reducing the refractive indices inside the cones substantially by 0.0046 (the difference in refractive index between 543 and 710 nm in water) changed the mean STA diameter by less than 1%, which was negligible. Altering the photopigment extinction coefficient affected the amount of light absorbed, but only influenced the amplitude of the STA representing the receptive field, not its diameter. Because we estimated cone diameters based on cone spacing, it is possible that cone diameters were under- or overestimated. Consequently, we ran simulations with the diameter of the cone outer segment altered by ± 20%. This yielded shifts in STAs that were ~1/3 of the SD in the original distribution of widths. Analogously, we varied the inner segment diameter by ± 10% and this shifted the simulated STAs by ~1/4 of the SD in the original width distribution. These results indicate that varying cone shape will change the size of the STA, as assessed by the Gaussian fit, but the effect of these changes is modest. We are not likely to have errors in the measured cone sizes greater than 20% given the averaged cone size estimates computed in Supplementary Fig. 2, where the error is <10%. Finally, the robustness of the simulations will primarily be affected by the initial electric field distribution size, for which eye length is the main uncertain factor. We calculated that a 10% increase in eye length (a large difference) would widen the Airy disc by 10%, which was ~0.3 µm for 543 nm light. Since our stimulus pixel sizes were >7× larger, this means the Airy disc size increase would be hard to detect in our STAs, suggesting that eye length differences would not affect our results substantially.

## Estimating the effect of defocus
The effects of defocus and optical coupling on the modelled results were also investigated. The focal position will clearly affect the light capture behaviour for a given cone and the effect can be significant with focal positions that vary in increments <0.1 D, which is smaller than what is normally appreciable with manual refraction32. The optimal focal position relative to the ELM is also likely to depend on cone size32. Simulations with focus on the ELM show a potential increase in receptive field size with small cone sizes and spacing (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). As a second effect, optical coupling may occur between cones, as light can be transferred from one waveguide to another when they are in close proximity77. It has been suggested that this may happen in vertebrate and invertebrate rod photoreceptors due to their small size and close packing78. As foveal cones have a comparable diameter to rods1, optical coupling may affect receptive field sizes when the cone spacing is small.
Optical coupling was examined by computing propagations with a 543 nm beam on a single cone and a 7 cone array for three different cone spacings: 2.5, 4.5 and 6.5 μm. If coupling occurred, the absorption of the light in the central cone would be reduced by the presence of the surrounding ring of cones, which would have increased light capture (Supplementary Fig. 3c).
To investigate defocus, simulated STAs were made as described above with a Monte Carlo procedure of n = 500,000 for 2.5, 4.5 and 6.5 μm spaced cones with 543 and 710 nm light, with the focus varying from −0.24–0.12 D relative to the ELM in 0.035 D steps. Foci were randomly selected using a uniform distribution and interpolation was used to create ideal STAs before adding jitter, displacement and noise. For this Monte Carlo procedure, the cone arrays had 19 cones arranged in 2 rings around the central cone. The additional ring in the array was added because if coupling was occurring, cones in the first ring would also be coupled to cones in the second ring, so their presence was required for this analysis. A 2D histogram of the mean STA diameter versus defocus showed that the size of the STA varied with defocus as expected (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Best focus was defined as where the mean STA diameter was at a minimum. The minimum was found by fitting a 6th-order polynomial to the mean of this data across each defocus bin. The turning point of this polynomial, given by its derivative, yielded the best focus. The best focus diameters were then compared to the simulated results obtained when the focus was at the ELM (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). These simulations showed that optical coupling affected defocus only when cone spacing was less than 3 μm in both wavelengths. Accordingly, we created plots that included the effect of defocus (Supplementary Fig. 3c–f). Because only the 2.5 μm cone spacing simulations were sensitive to defocus, we substituted the ideal STAs focused at the ELM with ideal STAs at their best focus and then repeated the remaining steps in the Monte Carlo procedure to generate the plots in Fig. 4c–f.

## Construction of spatial tuning curves
Spatial tuning curves were generated for a subset of the LGN neurons exposed to drifting grating stimuli and that had recordings with clear single-unit features (responses of neurons within multiunit recordings cannot be disentangled). Tuning curves were generated from 4 parvocellular neurons with an appreciable response to the grating stimuli, as well as a single magnocellular neuron for comparison (Supplementary Fig. 4).
Calculating the spatial tuning curve for a single neuron began by creating a spike density function for the spike train associated with each 2 s trial of a single spatial frequency and orientation79. The Gaussian width used for the spike density function was the time of a single stimulus frame (34 ms), representing the SD of the spike probability. To control for any variation in the phase of the neuron’s response across trials, the neural response to each trial was taken to be the amplitude of the real component of the Fourier transform spike density function at the stimulus temporal frequency (2 cycles/s). For a given spatial frequency, we averaged the Fourier-transformed responses across all orientations.
To estimate the firing rate for stimuli with the lowest spatial frequency we could deliver (i.e. the width of a movie frame), we used the amplitude of the neuron’s frame-locked response to the noise stimulus movies. Uncorrelated noise movies roughly approximate the response that might be expected for full-frame flicker. We created spike density functions for 0.5 s segments across the noise movies and averaged >1500 segments to yield a single spike density function representing the lowest spatial frequency (~2 cycles/°). The neural response was then calculated by Fourier transform as described above, but at the movie frame rate (30 Hz).

## Statistics and reproducibility
Data are reported with means ± 1 SD. No statistical method was used to predetermine the number of cells recorded. Reproducibility was gained by recording from multiple LGN cells of the same class across all three animals studied. Some STA data were excluded, as detailed in the Receptive Field Identification and Quantification section above.

## Figure Fig. 1: AOSLO schematic, stimulus delivery and example neural responses.a Optical schematic of retinal imaging and microstimulation system delivering 3 wavelengths of light split from a laser source (not depicted). Each wavelength was independently controlled via acousto-optical modulators. The combined beam was raster scanned across the retina by a pair of scanning mirrors. Infrared light (842 nm) was used to image the retina, while also presenting random noise stimulus movies composed of red (710 nm) and green (543 nm) light refreshing at 30 Hz. A wavefront sensor measured the infrared optical wavefront reflected from the eye and adaptive optics corrections were made by the deformable mirror. Photomultiplier tubes collected reflected light to build retinal images from the raster scans. Extracellular recordings were made with a microelectrode advanced along a vertical path through the LGN. b An AOSLO image of a cone mosaic with a single stimulus movie frame superimposed to scale. Each stimulus pixel was the size of 4 × 4 imaging pixels. Yellow pixels indicate when red and green lights were on simultaneously. The example frame represents a stimulus with a spatial frequency of 59 cycles/°. c A sequence of 8 stimulus frames shown above a set of spike rasters for 400 consecutive frames recorded from Neuron 1, exhibiting the typical frame-locked response caused by raster-scanned AOSLO stimulation. d Average sequence of frames in which one red and one green pixel maximally drove Neuron 1, shown above 400 of the corresponding spike rasters. e Peristimulus spike histograms for Neuron 1 reveal increased firing to the two ON pixels shown in (d) (black line) relative to the baseline stimuli in (c) (grey shading = 95% confidence intervals). The increased activity began ~50 ms after the pixels turned on in the raster (blue dashed line) and was followed by suppression and recovery to baseline.

Individual cones appear as small bright spots in the AOSLO images. Examples of 4 recorded receptive field locations are indicated by red dots. c–e STA maps of single parvocellular LGN neurons showing receptive fields with different response properties. Neurons 1 and 2 are from the retinas shown and Neuron 3 was at 0.88° eccentricity from a different retina. STAs are shown in separate rows for the red and green stimuli.

## Figure Fig. 3: Receptive fields of parvocellular LGN neurons align with the cone photoreceptor mosaic.a Average spike waveforms (black lines; shading = ±1 SD) of 4 isolated parvocellular neurons, one multiunit parvocellular recording that yielded 3 receptive fields and a magnocellular neuron along with their corresponding waveform SNRs and eccentricities from the fovea. Columns 2 and 4 contain data from Neurons 1 and 2, shown in Fig. 2. b STA frame of peak activity for each waveform superimposed on the cone mosaic at the location where stimulus movies landed on the retina, after correcting for TCA offsets. White cross and box indicate the centre and bounds of the movie stimulus as targeted in the IR channel. Receptive fields were fit with a 2D Gaussian (white ellipses; 1 SD level) to quantify receptive field size. c Red and green outlines of STAs from (b) are drawn over the cone mosaics to reveal the position of each receptive field. When a cell responded to red and green stimuli, both STAs are depicted. d Magnified views of images from c to help identify the cones likely corresponding to each receptive field centre. Degree scale bar equivalents: 0.1° ≈ 23.6 μm; 0.03° ≈ 7.08 μm.

## Figure Fig. 4: Light capture modelling of receptive fields and comparison with measured STA sizes.a A model array of 7 cone photoreceptors showing how the electric field for a point source of 543 nm light focused at the external limiting membrane (dashed line) propagates into the outer segments. Refractive indices of cone compartments are colour-coded. Example propagations are shown with the beam centred on the array or displaced by 1.56 μm or 3.30 μm. b Step-wise process for simulating receptive fields with 710 nm light and 5.5 μm cone spacing, showing parameters taken into account that reflect empirical sources of noise. Cone arrays mimicked 1, 2 or 3 cone receptive field centres, with absorption computed for a centred stimulus followed by stimulus jitter and shift. These data were then downsampled to the stimulus resolution, after which Gaussian noise was added (having an SNR of 8.77) and the simulated STAs were fit with a 2D Gaussian (red ovals). Plots of simulated receptive field minor (c, d) or major (e, f) axis diameters against cone spacing for 1 (red or green), 2 (black) or 3 cone centres (blue). Mapped LGN receptive fields are plotted with black outlines, based on primary colour sensitivity. For reference, cone spacing from 2.5–7.5 μm approximates to cone inner segment diameters of 2.05–6.14 μm.
