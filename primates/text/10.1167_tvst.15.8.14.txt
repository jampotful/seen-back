Retinal Layer Changes With Aging and Cognitive Decline in Rhesus Macaques.

# Retinal Layer Changes With Aging and Cognitive Decline in Rhesus Macaques

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeNonhuman primates (NHPs) are powerful animal models of aging and neurodegeneration. We hypothesize that age-related retinal thinning measured by optical coherence tomography (OCT) may correlate with cognitive and behavioral decline. Here, we evaluate the relationship between neurocognitive function in rhesus macaques and retinal layer thickness on OCT.MethodsWe evaluated 110 older rhesus macaques (mean age, 19.5 ± 3.3 years; range, 11–30 years) with an array of behavioral assessments, including visual acuity, decision-making, working memory, spatial memory, problem-solving, and social learning. We collected spectral domain OCT scans and measured retinal layer thicknesses in macular and peripapillary areas using custom segmentation software. Multivariate linear regression with generalized estimating equations was used to assess the association between retinal layer thicknesses and cognitive function.ResultsOCT measurements of retinal layers were consistent with published data from humans and NHPs. Thinning of the macular nerve fiber layer (mNFL; P = 0.001) and photoreceptor segments (PSs; P = 0.014) were associated with slower decision-making, whereas thinner inner nuclear layer (INL) was linked to slower problem-solving and less problem-solving flexibility (P = 0.023). Thinner outer plexiform layer (OPL; P = 0.022) and PSs (P = 0.001) were associated with better visual prediction, and thinning of the mNFL (P = 0.038), OPL (P = 0.039), total retinal thickness (P = 0.015), and peripapillary NFL (P = 0.002) were associated with better social learning.ConclusionsOlder rhesus macaques demonstrated measurable changes in behavior and retinal anatomy, but the relationship between retinal thinning and cognitive performance was modest, suggesting a unique relationship between retinal thinning and executive function, or a reflection of age-related decline and compensatory mechanisms.Translational RelevanceIdentifying noninvasive measures of age-related cognitive and behavioral decline using OCT measurements of retinal anatomy in NHPs may help accelerate future preclinical testing of novel treatments for neurodegenerative diseases in these animal models.Keywords: retinal layers, cognitive behavior, optical coherence tomography, nonhuman primates

## Introduction
Aging is associated with progressive structural and functional changes throughout the central nervous system and is the primary risk factor for many neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias.1,2 Age-associated cognitive decline can be difficult to detect in early stages, even though timely recognition is important for supportive interventions and emerging therapies.3–5 As the population ages, there is increasing interest in identifying non-invasive biomarkers that reflect neural aging and neurodegenerative processes before overt clinical decline becomes apparent.6–8
Because the retina shares developmental and neuroanatomical features with the brain, retinal imaging using high-resolution optical coherence tomography (OCT) has emerged as a promising non-invasive approach for studying aging and neurodegeneration.9,10 Retinal layer thinning has been reported in healthy aging, as well as neurodegenerative conditions such as AD, where retrograde transsynaptic degeneration has been proposed as a cause.11 AD patients demonstrate thinning of the nerve fiber layer (NFL),11–18 ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (GCIPL),17,19 and photoreceptor layers.20,21 Retinal layer thinning has also been observed in patients with Parkinson's disease,18,22 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis,23 frontotemporal dementia,16 or Lewy body dementia,22 as well as mild cognitive impairment.16,24,25 These imaging changes may thus serve as valuable endpoints in preclinical animal testing of AD treatments.
However, few animal models of AD recapitulate the long lifespan and cognitive decline of human patients.26,27 Aging is the primary risk factor for AD, and understanding the neurobiology of the aging central nervous system is critical for understanding its vulnerability to AD.28,29 Compared to other laboratory animals, nonhuman primates (NHPs) such as rhesus macaques have a relatively long lifespan, capacity for behavioral testing, and neuroanatomy resembling that of humans. NHPs also have a macula and retinal layers similar to those in human eyes, which can be longitudinally imaged and non-invasively measured using OCT.30–32 NHP models of retinal diseases, including inherited conditions such as cone dystrophy33 or Bardet–Biedel syndrome,34 and acquired conditions, including myopic foveoschisis35 and congenital Zika syndrome,36 have been described. Rhesus macaques have also been used to study corneal anatomy,37 glaucoma,38,39 and ocular drug and viral vector delivery.40–44
In this study, we performed behavioral testing and high-resolution OCT imaging in a cohort of older adult rhesus macaques at the National Biomedical Research Institute at UC Davis to explore the relationship between cognitive function and retinal layer thicknesses. We hypothesize that retinal thinning observed on OCT may be associated with age-related cognitive and behavioral decline in aging macaques, supporting the use of retinal imaging as a biomarker platform for aging and neurodegeneration research.

## Methods

## Animals
We performed ophthalmic examinations and ocular imaging on adult rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) from the National Biomedical Research Institute (NBRI) that had undergone behavioral testing as part of an ongoing study of healthy aging in NHPs. The animals were mostly housed indoors with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, but a subset of animals lived outdoors in large social groups and were exposed to the seasonal and weather conditions of the central valley region of California. All animals were provided ad libitum access to water and chow. The animal protocols followed the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research, complied with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and were approved by the UC Davis Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The cohort included 110 adult rhesus macaques who underwent biobehavioral testing and OCT imaging. The animals had a mean age of 19.5 ± 3.3 years (range, 11–30), corresponding approximately to 30 to 90 years of age in humans based on a 3:1 ratio of biological aging between rhesus macaques and humans.45 This age range therefore spanned middle-age to elderly adulthood in humans. Most animals were female (86%), reflecting, at least in part, the longer lifespan of female macaques within the colony (Supplementary Fig. S1).

## Behavioral Testing
Animals underwent one of two standardized biobehavioral assessments at NBRI. Animals were tested during a 25-hour relocation to a novel testing room (two animals per testing cohort) or during a 2-hour rapid version of the assessment within their indoor living cage. Statistical analyses accounted for the two testing conditions used. During assessments, animals participated in a series of tests designed to evaluate mobility, emotion, sensory acuity, social motivation, and cognition. For the current study, we focused on visual- and cognition-related tests, including visual acuity, decision-making, working memory, sequence prediction, problem-solving, and social learning (Fig. 1A; Table). The testing environment was designed to encourage participation, and tests were ordered and timed to prevent fatigue or withdrawal. Testing was conducted by trained experimenters who reached 85% or better reliability on all tests.

For the 25-hour assessment, each animal was housed in two connected side-by-side top cages with access to both sides, although animals were usually restricted to one side or the other for a given test. For the 2-hour rapid version, animals remained in standard indoor home cages. Animals were tested in the presence of at least one other animal in the same room. Visual access to other animals was limited. A subset of tests was selected from a larger dataset because they capture a broad range of cognitive domains that may change with aging and to reduce the likelihood of type 1 errors by including the number of tests in the analysis.

## Visual Acuity (Visual Function)
Visual acuity was evaluated by presenting a visual stimulus (2-inch red ball on a clear 12-inch stick) at distances of 2 meters, 1 meter, and 0.33 meter from the animal (Fig. 1A). The experimenter moved the stimulus in a fixed arc in space (3 feet in length and 1 foot in height at the peak of the arc). Durations of time that the animal focused on, followed, and looked at the stimulus (between 0 and 2 seconds) were measured and summed across all three distances. Scores were normalized (z-scored) relative to the year of testing to account for potential inter-year variability in testing conditions and observers.

## Food Preference (Decision-Making)
Previous studies in humans and monkeys have shown that decision-making, reaction time, and predictability of choice between valued resources worsen with older age.46,47 Animals were offered food items with increasing choice numbers (two, three, or four items) and complexity, with zero to four preferred food items (high sugar or salt content, such as fruits or popcorn) next to zero to three non-preferred food items (low sugar content, such as vegetables or chow). Thirty-six trials were presented for each test. Reaction times to choose and retrieve the food item were recorded, and further choices were not allowed. Preference priorities were generated from selections during two- and three-choice tests, and choice predictability was tested during the four-choice test (Fig. 1A). Reaction times for simple and complex decisions, as well as interindividual variability in reaction time with interference, were also recorded.

## Visual Prediction (Spatial Memory)
The ability to predict outcomes from a pre-set sequence of events is associated with general intelligence, statistical learning, and language skills in human infants and is compromised in adults with mild cognitive impairment.48,49 Macaques were assessed for their ability to predict the spatial location of a visual stimulus following a pre-set sequence displayed by a human experimenter. Animals were presented with 10 “problems,” and their performance video was recorded and coded by a trained observer. The experimenter stood at 0.33 meter from the animal with a stimulus (a 2-inch red ball on a clear 12-inch stick) and presented problems in order from simple (three locations, proceeding in a contiguous spatial direction) to complex (four locations, proceeding in the opposite direction with a final position in a non-contiguous location). For each problem, the experimenter conducted two sequence familiarization trials, placing the red ball in a series of three or four locations in space. Then, for the third “probe” trial, the experimenter stopped short of the last position without movement or eye saccade to the final expected position (i.e., without signaling the “correct” location). The observer recorded from the video the animal's eye movements toward the final position within a 90° angle. The prediction range was calculated on a scale from 0% to 100%.

## Food Puzzle Task (Problem-Solving)
Animals were presented with a 12 × 12-inch puzzle with different-sized holes from which animals may remove foods that are larger than most holes and were placed in a standardized fashion. Approximately 30 small food items were placed in the puzzle. Latency to “solve” the puzzle (remove a treat) was recorded each time an animal successfully removed food from the puzzle. Animals used multiple strategies to retrieve food from the environment. Each occurrence of a different specific strategy was recorded. Shorter latencies to solve, a higher number of solves, and greater diversity of strategies (recorded as the proportion of strategies divided by the total number of attempts to retrieve a food item) were considered indicators of better performance in problem-solving.

## Bell Ring (Social Learning)
We presented animals with a series of five novel objects. Latency to interact with the object or retrieve a preferred food item in front of the object and durations of interactions were recorded as described previously.50 For imitation learning, the human experimenter demonstrated pressing a hotel call bell to produce a sound three times, then stepped back to observe the animal's interaction with the call bell. The latency, as well as the number of times the animal pressed the bell, was recorded.

## Ophthalmic Examinations and Imaging
For ophthalmic examinations and imaging, we sedated the animals with intramuscular ketamine hydrochloride (initial dose 10 mg/kg, with re-dosing up to a total of 30 mg/kg), midazolam (0.1 mg/kg), and dexmedetomidine (0.015 mg/kg, with re-dosing at 0.0075 mg/kg 1 hour later). Clinical examinations were performed by a board-certified ophthalmologist (GY) or veterinary ophthalmologist (SMT). We examined the anterior segment using a portable slit-lamp biomicroscope (SL-17, Kowa Company Ltd., Nagoya, Japan) and the retinal fundus using an indirect ophthalmoscope (OMEGA 500; HEINE Optotechnik, Gilching, Germany) after applying topical phenylephrine and tropicamide to promote pupillary dilation. We obtained color fundus photographs using a 55° digital fundus camera (TRC-NW400; Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), followed by spectral-domain OCT imaging using a SPECTRALIS HRA+OCT system (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). For OCT imaging, we captured 20° × 20° macular OCT volume scans centered on the fovea with 1024 A-scans per B-scan, 25-µm B-scan spacing, and 16 images averaged for each B-scan. Additionally, a 3.5-mm-diameter circular scan centered on the optic nerve head was collected to assess peripapillary nerve fiber layer (ppNFL) thickness. Image scaling was based on mean ocular biometry measurements from a large cohort of normal rhesus macaque as previously described.30 Ocular biometry were not measured from the animals in the current study.

## Retinal Layer Thickness Analyses
Macular OCT images were analyzed using the Duke Optical Coherence Tomography Retinal Analysis Program (DOCTRAP), a custom, graph-based segmentation software51–53 that has been calibrated to measure retinal layer thicknesses in rhesus macaques based on prior surveys of normal animals.54 Macular OCT scans were segmented to delineate the macular nerve fiber layer (mNFL), GCIPL, inner nuclear layer (INL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and photoreceptor segments (PSs) measured from the external limiting membrane to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/Bruch's membrane complex. The ellipsoid zone was additionally delineated to measure the photoreceptor inner segment (IS) and outer segment (OS) layers separately. Total retinal thickness was defined as the distance from the internal limiting membrane to Bruch's membrane. All thickness measurements were averaged across the central 1-mm, 3-mm, and 5-mm circular regions corresponding to the foveal, parafoveal, and perifoveal regions of the macula based on the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid. We also measured the ppNFL from four pre-specified sectors (nasal, superior, temporal, and inferior) using the manufacturer's software (Heidelberg Explorer 1.10.4.0).55,56

## Statistical Analyses
We employed linear regression models with generalized estimating equations (GEEs), which adjust for inter-eye correlations, to analyze continuous outcomes (e.g., visual acuity), and a Poisson regression model with GEEs to analyze count outcomes (e.g., bell ring), to assess changes in retinal layer thicknesses and behavioral performance with age. To determine the association between OCT measurements and behavioral performance, we employed univariate regression models with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 to identify potentially important factors, which were then included in a multivariable regression model to identify image changes associated with each assessment outcome after adjusting for other clinically meaningful covariates (age, visual acuity, and housing status). Data from the 25-hour and 2-hour tests were combined for analysis. Not all animals completed every behavioral assessment because some tests were available only within specific testing paradigms (e.g., 25-hour relocation testing). Therefore, sample sizes differed between behavioral outcomes as summarized in the Table. Animals without data for a given behavioral test were excluded only from analyses involving that specific outcome. Post hoc correlations were conducted on a subset of 11 animals that underwent OCT 1 to 4 years prior to cognitive testing to determine the predictive value of early testing for later cognitive decline. To account for multiple comparisons, we controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and R packages (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) were used to create visual representations of the data for the interpretation of trends and relationships observed in the study. Figure 1A was created using BioRender and Canva Premium.

## Results

## Age-Related Changes in Behavioral Performance
Visual function was mostly unchanged across the age range of the adult animals we evaluated (Fig. 1B), whereas decision-making measured by food choices showed shorter latencies with aging, suggesting faster decision-making with older age (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Spatial memory, measured by visual prediction recall rate, increased slightly with aging, indicating a better ability to recall visual target positions (Fig. 1D). For problem-solving with a food puzzle task, older animals showed longer retrieval latencies and fewer variations in strategy relative to total attempts, suggesting an age-related decline in problem-solving efficiency and strategy (Figs. 1E, 1F). Social learning, as measured by the number of successful bell-ring trials, appeared to decline slightly with age (Fig. 1G). Our results demonstrate possible age-related decline in biobehavioral performance in rhesus macaques, although no statistically significant association was observed (P = 0.09–0.83).

## Age-Related Changes in Retinal Thickness Measurements
To assess the impact of aging on retinal structure, we measured the thicknesses of individual retinal layers averaged across the entire central 5-mm circular region centered on the fovea (Fig. 2A), as well as the ppNFL (Fig. 2B). Our results revealed high interindividual variability in total retinal thickness (Fig. 2C) and across individual retinal layer measurements (Figs. 2D–K), with overall trends toward thinning of total retinal thickness, INL, PSs, RPE, and ppNFL with older age, although no statistically significant association was observed (P = 0.12–0.99). ## Association Between Behavioral Performance and Retinal Thickness
To determine if behavioral performance was associated with retinal thickness measurements, we performed regression analyses that adjusted for age, visual acuity, and housing status. In the food preference test, thinning of the mNFL, PS, and RPE was associated with prolonged decision-making (Figs. 3A, 3D, 3G). Additionally in the food puzzle test, ONL thinning was associated with longer latencies to solve the puzzle and more frequent use of the same strategies, both potentially reflecting poorer problem-solving ability (Figs. 3C, 3D, 3I). However, other behavioral tests showed associations that were opposite of our initial hypotheses. For the visual prediction task, OPL and PS thinning was associated with better prediction rates (Figs. 3E, 3H). Thinning of the mNFL, OPL, and ppNFL was also associated with more bell-ring successes, possibly indicating better social learning (Figs. 3B, 3F, 3J). None of the other retinal layers showed a clear statistical association with performance on other behavioral tests (Fig. 3K). After adjusting for multiple-hypothesis testing, only PS thickness was associated with visual prediction (q = 0.012), and ppNFL was associated with bell ring performance (q = 0.029) (Fig. 3L). A subset of animals (n = 12) underwent OCT testing 1 to 4 years prior to cognitive testing. We were underpowered to use GEE, but conducted post hoc Pearson's correlations. These relationships were as strong or stronger than contemporaneous retinal-cognition associations. Decision-making was slower in individuals with thinner mRNFL 1 to 4 years earlier (two-choice r = −0.587, P = 0.045; three-choice r = −0.703, P = 0 .011). Remarkably, the effect on sequence prediction was the reverse of the full dataset: Retinal thinning in the IPL was associated with worse visual prediction 1 to 4 years later (r = 0.609, P = 0.047). In eight cases where cognitive tests occurred 1 to 2 years before OCT, no predictive relationships were observed (all P > 0.05).
To further explore if behavioral performance may be linked to localized or regional changes in retinal thickness, we performed temporal–superior–nasal–inferior–temporal (TSNIT) analyses for each retinal layer. Overall, we found no significant associations between age and TSNIT segmental retinal layer thicknesses after correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S2). When we analyzed possible associations between behavioral performance and TSNIT retinal layer thicknesses, we found several significant associations after FDR correction and adjusting for age, visual acuity, and housing status (Supplementary Table S3), including thinner nasal mNFL thickness with faster decision making (β = −0.321, P < 0.001, q = 0.044), thinner inferior PSs (β = −0.015, P < 0.001, q = 0.037) and temporal PSs (β = −0.016, P = 0.001, q = 0.044) with better spatial memory, and thinner temporal mNFL (β = 23.288, P < 0.001, q = 0.030) and superior RPE (β = 7.321, P = 0.001, q = 0.044) was associated with faster problem solving speed. Other nominal associations (P < 0.05) using TSNIT sectoral measurements did not remain significant after FDR correction. Overall, we did not observe a clear anatomic pattern of specific quadrant or retinal layer thinning that correlated with poorer behavioral performance.

## Discussion
NHPs provide a unique animal model that has retinal anatomy resembling humans and complex behaviors that can be quantified to study aging-related retinal and neurobehavioral changes relevant to neurodegeneration. In this study of 110 older rhesus macaques, we found that high-resolution OCT imaging of NHPs enables accurate measurements of retinal layers comparable to human studies that correlate with performance on several behavioral tests. We observed modest and variable associations between retinal structure and behavioral performances. Our findings highlight heterogeneity in structure–function relationships.
High-resolution spectral-domain OCT images provide near-histological visualization of retinal layers and are used frequently in clinical practice for the management of patients with retinal diseases. Normative values for retinal layer measurements have been determined for human populations, but few studies have evaluated these measures in NHPs.31 A study in rhesus macaques that employed continuously averaged retinal thicknesses across the central 3-mm segment around the fovea on single cross-sectional OCT scans found excellent correlation with histological measures.30 Our current study improved upon these prior datasets by employing semi-automated segmentation of retinal layers across the entire macular region to measure average thicknesses at different eccentricities from the fovea. By integrating across the entire OCT volume scan and employing automation in image segmentation, our measures are less susceptible to human subjectivity or error.57–60 Our measures were also concordant with rNFL measurements from younger cohorts of rhesus macaques and cynomolgous macaques61,62 from different colonies in China, although macular measurements were not reported in these studies.
Population-based studies of human OCT images showed PS thinning with age, and RPE thickening after 57 years of age.63 In contrast, our study in rhesus macaques did not reflect this trend, with RPE thickness decreasing with older age. However, the RPE thickening in humans is associated with age-related macular degeneration (AMD), likely due to the accumulation of drusen and basal deposits between the RPE and Bruch's membrane.64 By comparison, the incidence of AMD-like features in rhesus macaques is less common,57–60 and the smaller size of our animal cohort likely limits our sensitivity for detecting small changes in retinal or RPE thickness. RPE measurements in macaques may also be impacted by the darker uveal pigmentation, which impacts the reflectivity of the underlying choriocapillaris in NHPs.54,65 Because directional OCT was not systematically performed in this cohort, variable visualization of Henle fiber layer reflectivity may have influenced automated segmentation and could confound ONL thickness measurements, an additional limitation of our study.
We observed several associations between retinal layer thinning and aspects of cognitive decline in our current study. For example, mNFL and PS thinning were both associated with slower reaction time in deciding between multiple food choices, which is consistent with the notion that retinal ganglion neurons and photoreceptors are involved with visual–motor coordination and task execution.66–69 Decision-making reflects an executive function that is commonly affected in AD and dementia.70 Our findings also align with monkey and mouse studies that support a connection between retinal health and executive functions, including cognitive flexibility.71,72 We also observed that retinal thinning was associated with poorer problem-solving abilities: longer latencies to solve a puzzle and lower strategy diversity while problem-solving. Taken together, these results implicate executive function impairment with retinal thinning in monkeys, Whether these associations are causally related or are a result of other variables such as neural decline remains to be determined in future longitudinal studies. In a small subset of animals that underwent OCT testing 1 to 4 years prior to cognitive testing (equivalent to 4–16 humans years), we observed associations between earlier retinal thickness and later cognition. We observed no predictive relationship of cognition 1 to 2 years before retinal measures, however, indicating that retinal thinning may be earlier in the neurocognitive sequalae. These data may point to retinal thinning as an early biomarker of cognitive decline, similar to human studies. However, more animals will be have to be re-assessed for cognition in future studies to bear this out.
Contrary to our hypothesis, we found poorer performance on tasks related to sequence prediction and social learning. These results could implicate that the aging process,66–68,73 was associated with thickening of some retinal layers. Interestingly, some clinical studies in humans have also reported paradoxical retinal thickening in early-stage AD,24,25 suggesting a biphasic trajectory characterized by an initial neuroinflammatory hypertrophy prior to the development of atrophy. Our findings may also be impacted by the smaller number of animals eligible for the social learning tests, which were only available during the 25-hour relocation testing and thus provided a lower dynamic range and fewer data points than other tests. Finally, some tests may reflect other emotional or behavioral effects, in addition to cognitive performance. For example, the number of times an animal repeated the experimenter's bell ring may reflect impulsivity rather than social learning. The behavioral tests in our study were conducted as part of a larger, ongoing assessment of age-related cognitive decline that did not include animals with confirmed neurodegeneration, although NHP models of AD are being developed.28,74,75 More optimized methodologies, defined benchmarks, and broader animal population with more diverse neurodegenerative changes could enhance our ability to define retinal changes in cognitive function and neurodegeneration.76 The discrepancy among behavioral metrics that tap different aspects of cognition highlights the challenges of linking retinal structure to complex behavioral phenotypes. Simple linear associations may be insufficient to capture the multifactorial nature of aging and neurodegeneration. It is also possible that retinal thinning and its neurocognitive correlates directly impact executive functions such as decision-making, problem-solving, and sequence prediction, with weaker or indirect links to social learning.
Prior human studies have suggested that neurodegenerative processes may preferentially affect specific retinal sectors. For example, AD patients may exhibit superior ppNFL thinning, suggesting that more localized retinal measurements may be more sensitive than global metrics for detecting neurodegenerative changes.77,78 In our cohort, however, TSNIT analyses did not identify a clear anatomic pattern of regional retinal layer associations with behavioral tests after correction for multiple testing, perhaps due to the limited size of our cohort or the possibility that the localized changes observed in humans do not generalize to NHPs.
In addition to neurodegenerative disease, aging rhesus macaques may also develop other systemic and retinal conditions that influence retinal structure, including spontaneous metabolic dysfunction and age-related retinal abnormalities.31 Recent studies have described diabetic retinal changes and age-associated retinal pathology in macaques, suggesting that retinal OCT findings in aging animals likely reflect multiple overlapping biological processes rather than isolated neurodegeneration alone.79 These broader aging-related factors should be considered in future longitudinal studies incorporating systemic metabolic and inflammatory characterization.
Our study is strengthened by the use of high-quality OCT imaging, precise segmentation of retinal layers, and dual-grader measurements with rigorous quality control in a large cohort of older rhesus macaques. Although global retinal measurements reduce dimensionality and the multiple-comparison burden in exploratory analyses, future studies incorporating sectoral retinal analyses may identify localized retinal changes with greater sensitivity for specific neurodegenerative processes.80–82 Our analyses also adjusted for key covariates, including age, visual acuity, and housing status. Despite the lack of a clear age-related decline in retinal anatomy or cognitive performance, assumptions of biological aging as a linear process have been challenged in recent years,83 and more sophisticated nonlinear modeling could enhance our sensitivity for identifying significant associations in the future. Further studies incorporating NHP models of AD with amyloid-beta28 or tau pathology74,75 would also provide a wider range of cognitive impairments to better validate the clinical relevance of our behavioral tests. Ongoing longitudinal follow-up of this cohort, with repeat behavioral testing and retinal imaging, may further clarify the temporal relationships between retinal structural changes and aging-related behavioral decline. Although differences in lifespan, neurobiology, and factors such as mood and motivation may impact direct translation to human patients, NHPs remain a powerful animal model for studying retinal anatomy and cognitive behavior and a potential tool for developing novel therapies for neurodegenerative diseases.

## Figure Figure 1.: Behavioral assessments of cognitive status with aging in rhesus macaques. (A) Outline of behavioral assessments for testing cognitive functions, including visual acuity test of visual function, food preference test for rational decision-making latency, visual prediction test for spatial memory, food puzzle test of problem-solving speed and strategy, and bell ring test of social learning. (B–G) Scatterplots with trend lines depicting the association of performance on visual acuity (B), food preference (C), visual prediction (D), food puzzle latency (E), food puzzle strategy (F), and bell ring testing with age (G). The y-axes are oriented to reflect better performance at the top of each plot. Regression slopes are reported as standardized β coefficients and displayed within each panel.

## Figure Figure 2.: OCT measurement of retinal layers with aging in rhesus macaques. (A, B) Representative OCT scans (right) corresponding to the green lines on the infrared reflectance images (left) of the macula (A) and optic nerve (B), with segmentation lines to delineate the mNFL, GCIPL, INL, OPL, ONL, PS layer, and RPE, as well as ppNFL. (C–K) Scatterplots with trend lines depicting association of total retinal (C), mNFL (D), GCIPL (E), INL (F), OPL (G), ONL (H), PS (I), RPE (J), and ppNFL (K) thicknesses with age. Scale bars: 200 µm. Regression slopes are reported as standardized β coefficients and displayed within each panel.

## Figure Figure 3.: Association of behavioral test performance with retinal thickness in rhesus macaques.
