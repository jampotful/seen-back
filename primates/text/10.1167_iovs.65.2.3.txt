# Choroidal Morphology and Photoreceptor Activity Are Related and Affected by Myopia Development

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeThe choroid is critical for the regulation of eye growth and is involved in the pathogenesis of myopia-associated ocular complications. This study explores the relationship among choroidal biometry, photoreceptor activity, and myopic growth in marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) with lens-induced myopia.MethodsA total of 34 common marmosets aged 92 to 273 days old were included in this study. Axial myopia was induced in 17 marmosets using negative soft contact lenses and 17 marmosets served as untreated controls. Cycloplegic refraction (RE) and vitreous chamber depth (VCD) were measured using autorefraction and A-scan ultrasonography, respectively. Choroidal scans were obtained using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography and binarized to calculate subfoveal choroidal thickness (ChT), total choroidal area (TCA), luminal area (LA), stromal area (SA), choroidal vascularity index (CVI), and LA/SA. To assess photoreceptor activity, the a-wave of the full-field electroretinogram was measured. Regression models were used to investigate the relationship between outcome measures.ResultsEyes induced with axial myopia (RE = −7.14 ± 4.03 diopters [D], VCD = 6.86 ± 0.39 mm) showed significant reductions (4.92–21.24%) in all choroidal parameters (ChT, TCA, LA, SA, CVI, and LA/SA) compared to controls (RE = −1.25 ± 0.60 D, VCD = 6.58 ± 0.26 mm, all P < 0.05), which changed as a function of refraction and vitreous elongation, and were associated with a decrease in the a-wave amplitude. Further, multiple regression showed that a combination of ChT and CVI could well predict RE and VCD.ConclusionsThis study reports the existence of significant alterations in choroidal morphology in non-human primate eyes induced with myopia. The changes in choroidal anatomy were associated with reduced light-adapted a-wave amplitude. These findings may represent early markers for reduced visual performance and chorioretinal complications known to occur in eyes with large degrees of myopia.Keywords: myopia, choroid, emmetropization, spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), electroretinography
The process of emmetropization and myopia development follows evolutionarily conserved mechanisms across animal species.1–7 The postnatal refractive error developed by an organism involves the complex interplay between genetics and the visual environment.8 Myopia is characterized by an increase in vitreous chamber depth and scleral tissue remodeling that results from retinal signals responding to myopiagenic stimuli.9,10 Some blinding complications of myopia, such as chorioretinal degeneration,11 glaucoma,12 retinal tear, and detachment,13 may be a byproduct of the effect that axial elongation has on the retina, choroid, and sclera.
The choroid is a highly vascularized and pigmented tissue located between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the sclera that performs functions critical to vision and ocular health: supply of oxygen and nutrients to the photoreceptors and RPE, thermoregulation, light absorption, and intraocular pressure modulation among others.14,15 The relationship among emmetropization, myopia development, and choroidal morphology is well documented. Myopiagenic stimuli, such as form deprivation, hyperopic defocus, or OFF-pathway stimulation, are known to result in choroidal thinning, whereas myopic defocus or ON stimulation lead to a thickening.9,16 These rapid compensatory choroidal responses to myopic and hyperopic defocus have been observed in chicks,9 guinea pigs,17 marmosets,18 rhesus macaque,19 and humans20 and can be predictive of long-term axial changes.21
However, not only the thickness of the choroid, but also its vasculature and stroma are altered by normal and myopic growth. After Niblack choroidal image binarization was introduced by Sonoda et al.,22,23 and subsequently modified by Agrawal et al.,24 the choroidal vascularity index (CVI; luminal area/total choroidal area) has emerged as an important metric to monitor and manage a myriad of ocular diseases, including pathological myopia.25–33 The CVI reflects the vasculature status of the choroid, has a robust reproducibility and repeatability, and it is cost efficient, because a single subfoveal CVI is highly representative of the macular CVI in non-localized choroidal diseases.22,24,34
The choroid has a high blood flow rate, low oxygen exchange, and a fenestrated capillary bed,35 all of which aim to meet the large metabolic demands of the outer retina,36 which is particularly vulnerable to ischemia because of its high oxygen demand and low vascularity of the inner layers.35,37 As such, any structural or functional choroidal perturbation may affect the choroid's ability to support the functioning of photoreceptors. The a-wave is the initial negative deflection of the full-field ERG and reflects the responses of rod and cone photoreceptors. Under photopic conditions, the a-wave from a vertebrate ERG is generated by cone photoreceptor with contribution from hyperpolarizing second-order neurons.38 Therefore, the a-wave can be used as an indirect measure of choroidal activity.
Unlike choroidal thickness, only a limited number of studies have described the choroidal vascularity index in human myopic eyes, and shown contrasting results.33,39–48 Wang et al.,49 Guler Alis and Alis,39 and Liu et al.42 have identified a lower CVI in myopes, whereas work by Gupta et al.43 and Yazdani et al.48 report an enhanced CVI, and Alshareef et al. found no differences.40 To date, it remains unknown how the CVI and related vascular parameters are altered in an experimental model of myopia. In this study, we describe the choroidal characteristics of common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) undergoing spontaneous emmetropization compared to animals with lens-induced myopia and assessed how their subfoveal choroidal anatomy relates to photoreceptor activity using the ERG’s a-wave. Marmosets are small-bodied new world monkeys that have become an attractive experimental model in neuroscience due to their evolutionary closeness to humans.50 By identifying the anatomical changes that take place in the choroid as a consequence of myopia development, and how they relate to photoreceptor activity, this study aims to study subfoveal choroidal OCT images to identify early biomarkers to help detect eyes at risk of compromised visual function and chorioretinal complications.

## Methods

## Experimental Design
Data from a total of 34 common marmosets (19 male subjects [M], 15 female subjects [F], 92 to 273 days old) were used in this study: 17 (9 M and 8 F) induced with axial myopia using binocular custom-made negative single vision soft contact lenses (contact lens material methafilcon A, 58% water content, oxygen permeability: 21; Capricornia Contact Lens, Pty Ltd., Queensland, Australia). The remaining 17 marmosets (10 M and 7 F) were untreated controls and were measured at ages that matched those of treated animals.
All lens-rearing procedures followed established protocols.51–53 Negative lens treatment started at approximately 2.5 months of age (68.61 ± 3.34 days) and continued for 22 weeks until the marmosets were approximately 8 months of age. Due to the effective operating range of lens compensation,54,55 marmosets in the treatment group were initially reared with −5 diopters (D) and gradually stepped up by −5 D (to a maximum of −15 D) when the degree of the compensation reached 60% of the imposed defocus.51–53 Corneal curvature was measured using a custom-made infrared video keratometer, which guided the selection of the contact lens base curve to use. Contact lenses were fitted 0.1 mm flatter than the average K. Ocular surface health was regularly evaluated throughout the experimental period. No ocular complications were detected.
Marmosets were raised in family groups in rooms with controlled temperature and humidity (22°C and 60% relative humidity) and experienced a 9-hour light (illuminance approximately 700 lux) / 15-hour dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum throughout the experimental period.51–53
All animal rearing, treatment, and experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the State University of New York College of Optometry Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and followed the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

## Outcome Measures
Cycloplegia and pupillary mydriasis were induced using 1 to 2 drops of 1% cyclopentolate hydrochloride (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA). On-axis cycloplegic refractive error was measured 20 to 30 minutes later in awake marmosets using the Nidek ARK-900 autorefractor (Nidek Co., Ltd., Gamagori, Japan). Five refractive measurements were obtained, averaged, and converted into spherical equivalent refractive error (RE). Ocular biometry (anterior chamber depth [ACD], lens thickness [LT], and vitreous chamber depth [VCD]) was measured in anesthetized marmosets (alfaxalone, 15 mg/kg, hind limb, IM) using a high frequency A-scan ultrasound (25 MHz, Panametrics; NDT, Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA). An average of eight individual A-scan traces represented the axial ocular dimension for each marmoset.
Following the ultrasound measures, while marmosets remained under anesthesia and their eyes were fully dilated, the choroid was imaged using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Bioptigen, Inc., Durham, NC, USA). Modifications to our published retinal scanning protocol were made to enhance the quality of the choroidal scans (Fig. 1A).51 The enhanced choroidal scan was a rectangular volume scan (12 × 5.395 × 12 mm3, 700 A-scans/B-scan × 40 B-scans, and 2048 voxels per A-scan) with 30 frames per B-scan. To prevent tear film evaporation and enhance the scan quality, marmosets wore a custom-made rigid gas permeable contact lens during imaging (3.75 mm base curve, 5 mm diameter, and 0.00 D refraction; Conforma Laboratories, Inc., Norfolk, VA, USA), and the corneal surface was lubricated with artificial tears (Refresh Plus; carboxymethylcellulose sodium 0.5%; Allergan, Inc., Madison, NJ, USA). Imaging was performed close to the zero-delay line and the frames/B-scan were averaged to reduce speckle noise.

## Subfoveal Choroidal Image Binarization and Quantification
Choroidal OCT scans were exported as Tag Image File Format (TIFF) files and the scan with the foveal pit with discernable choroidal−scleral interface was selected for binarization and quantification using the method described by Agrawal et al.24 Briefly, the subfoveal choroidal image was converted into 8-bit and binarized with Niblack's auto local threshold using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).56 Thresholding before selecting the choroidal area allowed the precise identification of the chorioscleral border. The total choroidal area (TCA) was calculated by analyzing the central 3 mm (1.5 mm nasal and temporal of the fovea). The upper and lower boundaries were the RPE-Bruch membrane interface and the chorioscleral border, respectively. The polygon tool was then used to demarcate the region of interest, the image converted back into an RGB image, and the color threshold applied to automatically calculate the TCA, luminal (vascular) area (LA; dark pixels), and stromal (interstitial) area (SA = TCA-LA; light pixels) (Figs. 1B, 1C). We further determined the subfoveal choroidal thickness (ChT) using the method described by Benavente-Pérez et al.57 A reference line through the fovea was drawn perpendicular to a horizontal line and the thickness was measured along this orthogonal line from the base of the hyper-reflective retinal pigment epithelium to the choroid-scleral interface (Fig. 1D).

## Photopic Full-Field Electroretinogram Recordings
The photopic ERG recordings were obtained within a week of ocular biometry, refraction, and SD-OCT measurements to assess photoreceptor function. Marmoset eyes were anesthetized (acepromazine, 2.5 mg/kg and ketamine, 40 mg/kg, IM) prior to recording their light-adapted full-field responses using the Espion electrodiagnostic system (Diagnosys LLC, Lowell, MA, USA). The ERG responses were recorded to brief (200 ms) white stimuli that ranged in intensity from 1.56 to 832 cd/m2 on a 75 cd/m2 white background. Vitals were monitored every 5 minutes throughout the ERG recordings until recovery. The a-wave amplitude was measured from the ERG baseline to the negative a-wave trough. The b-wave amplitude was measured from the a-wave trough (or ERG baseline if the a-wave was not present) to the b-wave peak. The d-wave was measured from light offset to the peak of the d-wave (Fig. 1E). The luminance–response data were fitted with a generalized Naka-Rushton equation,58 and estimated parameters of saturated amplitude, semi-saturation constant and slope were extracted. However, because no differences were found between the Naka Rushton parameters, the raw data was used in the analysis. The amplitude and implicit time for each intensity were reported.
All measurements were conducted between 9 AM and 11 AM to eliminate the confounding effect of diurnal variations in ocular biometry, choroidal thickness, and ERG measures.59 The cycloplegia achieved before choroidal imaging eliminated the effect of accommodation on choroidal thickness.60 While all marmosets underwent ERG recordings, not all of them reached full immobilization under anesthesia, and data could not be gathered. We were able to record ERGs in 10 control (9 M and 1 F) and 6 treated marmosets (5 M and 1 F).

## Statistical Analyses
Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and SigmaPlot (version 10.0; Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA), respectively. Data from one randomly selected eye in each marmoset was analyzed to avoid inter-eye correlations. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent t-tests were used to compare refraction, ocular biometry, choroidal parameters, and ERG parameters between control and treated marmosets. Linear regression analyses were performed to explore the relationship among the outcome measures: age, refraction, ocular biometry, choroidal parameters, and ERG parameters. A stepwise multiple regression model was used to determine if any of the choroidal parameters could predict the degree of refraction, axial elongation, or a-wave amplitude. P values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

## Effect of Lens Treatment on Refraction and Ocular Biometry
At the end of treatment, marmosets treated with negative contact lenses developed significant degrees of myopia (treated = −7.14 ± 4.03 and control = −1.25 ± 0.60 D, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A) and deeper VCDs (treated = 6.86 ± 0.39 and control = 6.58 ± 0.26 mm, P < 0.05; Fig. 2B) compared to age-matched controls. The degree of induced myopia correlated with the enlargement of the vitreous chamber (R2 = 0.60, P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). There were no significant differences in post-treatment corneal curvature (treated = 3.54 ± 0.05 and control = 3.56 ± 0.05 mm, P = 0.18), anterior chamber depth (treated = 1.72 ± 0.07 and control = 1.74 ± 0.11 mm, P = 0.50), or lens thickness (treated = 1.94 ± 0.09 and control = 1.93 ± 0.10 mm, P = 0.64), which confirms that the myopia developed in treated marmosets was axial in nature.

## Association Between Choroidal Characteristics and Axial Myopia Development
At the end of treatment, marmosets induced with myopia ranged in age from 121 to 244 days old, developed axial myopia (RE = −7.14 ± 4.03 D and VCD = 6.86 ± 0.39 mm), their choroids were thinner (19.56% reduction, P < 0.001; see Fig. 3A), and had a reduction in total choroidal area (17.69% reduction, P < 0.001; see Fig. 3B), luminal area (21.23% reduction, P < 0.001; see Fig. 3C), stromal area (9.80% reduction, P < 0.05; see Fig. 3D), choroidal vascularity index (treated = 0.65 ± 0.02 and control = 0.69 ± 0.03, P < 0.001; see Fig. 3E), and luminal to stromal area ratio compared to controls (treated = 1.91 ± 0.17 and control = 2.24 ± 0.28, P < 0.001; see Fig. 3F) at the end of the study.
Opposite to the age-related thickening observed in control marmosets, as treated marmosets got older, their choroids thinned (−0.12 µm/day, R2 = 0.21, P = 0.06). Increasing degrees of myopia and deeper vitreous chambers were also associated with a subfoveal choroidal thinning (RE = R2 = 0.40, P < 0.01; see Fig. 4A; and VCD = R2 = 0.29, P < 0.05; see Fig. 4B), decreasing CVI (RE = R2 = 0.34, P < 0.05; see Fig. 4C and VCD = R2 = 0.41, P < 0.01; see Fig. 4D), and decreasing LA/SA (RE = R2 = 0.33, P < 0.05; see Fig. 4E and VCD = R2 = 0.41, P < 0.01; see Fig. 4F). In myopic marmosets, a thinner choroid was associated with a reduction in the luminal (R2 = 0.47, P < 0.01) but not the stromal area (R2 = 0.05, P = 0.39).

## Choroidal Characteristics as Predictors of End of Treatment Refraction and Eye Size
Stepwise multiple linear regression models indicated that the combination of choroidal thickness (standardized estimate; βRE = 0.55 and βVCD = −0.32) and choroidal vascularity index (βRE = −0.32 and βVCD = 0.35) could predict the refraction (R2 = 0.59, P < 0.001) and vitreous chamber depth (R2 = 0.29, P < 0.01) of the study marmosets.

## Discussion
This study provides evidence of the relationship between choroidal morphology and photoreceptor activity in the eyes of marmosets, both of which were affected by myopic eye growth. Untreated control marmosets emmetropized normally and their choroids experienced a mild age-related thickening. However, the choroid of marmosets with lens-induced myopia thinned due to a reduction in the total choroidal, luminal, and stromal areas. Greater degrees of myopia and vitreous elongation were associated with a greater reduction in choroidal biometry. More importantly, these choroidal parameters were predictors of photoreceptor activity; the amplitude of the ERG a-wave decreased as the choroid got thinner and the choroidal vascularity index, total choroidal, luminal (vascular), and stromal (interstitial) areas decreased.
This study shows the feasibility of objectively assessing the vascular and stromal characteristics of the choroid in vivo in an experimental nonhuman primate model by adopting the image binarization technique recently developed by Sonoda et al.23 and Agrawal et al.24 Although choroidal thickness has been a parameter widely used to assess choroidal changes during refractive error development for the past decade, it provides little morphological information regarding the nature of the changes. The CVI and LA/SA, on the contrary, are reflections of choroidal vascularity, provide information on the relative proportion of the choroid occupied by vascular and interstitial components, and can help understand the morphological changes known to occur in myopia.
As untreated control marmosets grew and emmetropized, they showed a trend toward a gradual subfoveal choroidal thickening likely due to a concurrent thickening of the entire choroid as marmosets grew older. A similar trend was reported by Troilo et al.18 also in marmosets using high-frequency A-scan ultrasonography. These normal maturation changes in choroidal thickness occur irrespective of refractive state and are similar to those reported in rhesus monkeys,19 tree shrews,61 and human eyes.62 The average percentage of CVI and LA/SA identified in marmoset eyes in this study aligns with the typical 66% CVI and 1.92 LA/SA reported in healthy human eyes,22,24,39 which reinforces the translational value of the marmoset model in choroid and myopia research. In addition, the choroidal vascularity index has a small variability and is not affected by age, refractive error or vitreous elongation – unlike choroidal thickness, which is known to thicken with age in human and nonhuman primate eyes.24,63 This highlights the robustness of the choroidal vascularity index as a measure of aberrant choroidal development and strengthens its value as a reliable objective marker of early choroidal changes.
In eyes induced with myopia, both the subfoveal choroidal thickness and total choroidal area were reduced, and the changes were proportional to the degree of myopic growth induced. In fact, the choroidal changes observed in treated marmosets in this study were mostly due to changes in the luminal component, suggesting they were vascular in nature, as described in non-degenerative high myopic human eyes.39,45,64 The lacunae identified during the image binarization could be thought of as lymphatic and not blood vessels. However, unlike the choroid of avian eyes, the existence of lymph vessels in the choroid of primates is controversial.14,65,66 Therefore, the dark pixels observed in the choroid of the marmoset most likely do not represent lymphatic vessel lacunae but instead luminal or vascular areas. However, the subfoveal thinning known to occur in human myopes has also been attributed to a reduction of the stromal component.40 Therefore, we hypothesize that both the vascular and interstitial choroidal components may play a role in regulating choroidal morphology during myopia development.14 Likely due to differences in choroidal ultrastructure, changes in choroidal thickness during myopia development are considerably smaller in magnitude in marmoset and mammal than in avian eyes.67 Their existence in mammals, however, confirms the role the choroid plays in retinal focusing mechanisms to reduce error signals, and suggests that the choroid may be actively driving or passively experiencing the consequences eye growth.17–19,61 The thinning of the choroid may facilitate the diffusion of myopic growth signals released from itself,15 the neuroretina,68,69 and RPE15 and affect extracellular matrix remodeling in the sclera.18 Alternatively, these vascular changes contributing to choroidal thickness changes may be not the cause, but the consequence of myopic growth, or a reflection of metabolic changes taking place in the myopic growing eye.9,70 Therefore, we propose that the choroid is involved in both cause and consequence of myopic growth by being part of the signaling cascade and also experiencing the effects of myopic elongation.
This study identified a reduction in both vascular and stromal components in the eyes of marmosets with induced myopic growth, suggesting that both these components are directly affected by the mechanical stretch of myopic elongation. In addition, the greater reduction observed in the vascular compared to the stromal component suggests that choroidal blood flow and choroidal smooth muscles might be involved in the mechanism that respond to imposed defocus.14,68 Although clinical studies describe a consistent reduction in TCA, LA, and SA in myopic subjects, the effect of myopia on the CVI varies greatly; some studies have reported an unchanged CVI,40,44 whereas others describe an increase,43,48 or a reduction in CVI in myopic eyes.39,42,45,46 This lack of agreement is likely due to differences in the rates of vascular to stromal reduction at the different stages of myopia development and progression. For example, While Gupta et al.43 report of reduction in both the LA and SA, the greater reduction in SA than LA resulted in a greater CVI in high myopes compared to emmetropes.
The decreased magnitude in LA, CVI, and LA/SA, which were proportional to the degree of myopic growth, may also suggest altered choroidal hemodynamics in myopic marmosets. Reduced choroidal circulation has been extensively reported in healthy human myopes71 and the avian myopia model.72,73 Morphologically, myopic eyes have been described to have increased choriocapillaris flow voids, reduced choriocapillary endothelial fenestrations, density, and blood vessel lumen.45,46,73 If the vascular changes identified in myopic marmosets are understood as a reduction in vessel density or lumen, they may represent early changes that might eventually lead to an altered choroidal blood flow rate and increase the risk for choroidal complications known to occur in myopia.74,75 The potential role of CVI in identifying myopes at risk of visual impairment has been suggested in the past, because young high myopes with and without chorioretinal complications were found to have lower CVI in turn related to reduced best corrected visual acuities.76,77
The multiregression analyses performed indicate that changes in choroidal thickness, vascularity index, and luminal to stromal ratio can explain 29% of the variance in myopic elongation and 59% of the myopic refractive changes. In our laboratory, we have previously described how myopic elongation alters the age-related inner thickening of the retina.51 In marmosets, myopia has a greater effect on the retina than on the choroid, which may be due to the greater degree of resistance the choroid offers during myopic growth and mechanical stretch compared to the retina.78 Alternatively, the effect of myopia outside the central 3 mm-wide subfoveal choroidal region might be different, because myopic growth is known to be asymmetrical. Although these findings are in agreement with studies describing a negative correlation between CVI (and/or LA) and eye size,42,44–46 a positive43,48 or no association39,41,47 have also been reported in myopic human eyes. The range of refractive error and axial length of participants, whether they were progressing or not, the nature of their myopia (axial or refractive), and the image analysis used may explain the lack of consensus among these studies.
Treated myopic marmosets had an attenuated a-wave amplitude relative to age-matched controls at the highest light intensity. While the effect of myopia on outer retina function is mixed, the preponderance of the evidence in human and animal studies report decreased photopic a-wave amplitudes with no effect on implicit time in physiological myopia.79,80 A possible explanation may be the direct effect of myopia on photoreceptors, suggesting that myopic growth results in phototransduction and mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction,81 and substantial deleterious changes to photoreceptor morphology.82 It could also be argued that changes in electrical resistance may explain the differences observed in the a-wave amplitude. However, if this was the case, the thinner choroids of myopic marmosets would impose a lower degree of resistance, in turn leading to a relative increase in ERG voltage amplitude. In addition, if myopic marmosets with thinner choroids in fact exhibited a decrease in choroidal resistance, this effect should affect all ERG components equally, which differs from the localized decrease we identified in the a-wave but not b- or d-wave amplitudes.
Another explanation can be gleaned from myopia resulting in occlusion of large choroidal vessels and narrowing of choriocapillaris.73,82 This is supported to a lesser extent by our analysis, where photoreceptor activity showed a decrease in a-wave amplitudes as the choroid thinned and the total choroidal, luminal, stromal areas, and choroidal vascularity index decreased, suggesting that the altered choroidal anatomy in myopic eyes may affect photoreceptor activity. The choroid is intricately associated with rods and cones: provides them with oxygen and nutrients, and is in charge of their thermoregulation.14,83 Altered choroidal volume, vasculature, and stroma can lead to compromised choroidal blood flow, which may affect the choroid's ability to support photoreceptor function, and potentially lead to alterations in a-wave amplitude especially at higher intensities where the metabolic demand is high and any shortcoming in the choroid's ability can manifest as significant reduction in photoreceptor activity, as seen from this study's results. Although the choroidal blood flow rate was not assessed in this study, the CVI changes observed in myopic marmosets suggest that the altered choroidal vasculature of myopic eyes can lead to a reduction in photoreceptor activity. In fact, alterations to the choroidal vasculature is known to have adverse effects on photoreceptor health and function, as seen in ocular conditions, such as retinitis pigmentosa, Stargardt disease, cone–rod dystrophy, and age-related macular degeneration, all of which are characterized by a reduction in CVI,25,32 and photoreceptor activity.84–86
Despite the translational relevance of these findings from the marmoset model to human myopia, this study has limitations. As this was a cross-sectional study, a cause-effect relationship could not be established and we could only draw associations among variables. In addition, we could only make inferences to altered blood flow in myopic marmosets from reduction in LA and CVI, as we did not measure choroidal hemodynamics. Imaging and binarization to delineate vascular and stromal areas in the choroid have technical limitations (such as shadows from large retinal vessels) that may affect the reliability of CVI as a reflection of choroidal vascularity.63,87 Only the subfoveal choroid was analyzed and related to refraction, ocular biometry, and full-field ERGs that may not be representative of the entire choroid. Finally, diurnal variation on choroidal parameters was not explored by this study. This temporal effect may interact with choroidal parameters to accentuate or obscure the identified myopia-associated changes in choroidal morphology and/or photoreceptor activity. This warrants further investigation.
In conclusion, this study assessed the morphology of the choroid and photoreceptor function in a nonhuman primate model of myopia in vivo using OCT derived parameters and full-field ERGs. Myopic marmosets showed a significant reduction in choroidal thickness, total, vascular and interstitial areas, and vascularity index that was associated with myopic growth. More importantly, the myopia-related changes in choroidal parameters were associated with an attenuated photoreceptor activity. The relationship between an attenuated choroidal morphology and photoreceptor activity implies myopic growth has an effect on the health and functioning of the outer retina and may serve as an early biomarker for determining myopic subjects at an increased risk of developing chorioretinal sequelae.
