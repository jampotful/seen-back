# Translational insights into abiotic interactions: From Arabidopsis to crop plants

## Abstract
AbstractUnderstanding crop plants responses to abiotic stress is increasingly important in this changing climate. We asked experts how discoveries in Arabidopsis thaliana have translated into advancements in abiotic crop stress resilience. The theme is that core regulatory networks identified in Arabidopsis are conserved in crops, but the molecular regulation varies among species. For cold tolerance, the regulatory framework is conserved, but MAP Kinase signaling promotes degradation of the INDUCER OF DREB1 EXPRESSION transcription factor in Arabidopsis but inhibits it in rice. For hypoxia, manipulation of the oxygen sensing Arg/N-degron pathway discovered in Arabidopsis has improved waterlogging and flood tolerance in barley, maize, wheat, and soybean. For light signaling, overexpression of PHYTOCHROME B reduces shade avoidance, improving yield under dense planting in potato, soybean, and maize. In rice, understanding of nitrogen responsiveness, uptake, and transport in Arabidopsis has inspired engineering of the NRT1 nitrate transceptor to increase yield. Arabidopsis research has provided leads for genetic manipulations that may improve drought resilience in crop species. Growing plants in space generates a complex array of stresses, and Arabidopsis experiments in the space station prepare for future development of robust crops as integral components of the life support systems. For environmental regulation of flowering time, the role of the GIGANTEA - CONTANS - FLOWERING LOCUS T module elucidated in Arabidopsis is largely conserved in crop plants, although additional regulators modify short-day responsiveness in rice, soybean, chrysanthemum, and potato.

## Introduction
Abiotic stress is a major contributor to crop yield loss, with factors such as extreme temperatures, changes in light, drought prevalence, and nutrient deficiencies challenging the ability of plants to thrive (reviewed in work cited by Eckardt et al. 2023).
Because plants are often ill equipped to cope with stress conditions outside those they have evolved to endure, the growing unpredictability of climate patterns exacerbates the negative impact of these stresses on agricultural productivity (Ray et al. 2019). To address this, breeding plants that are more resilient to abiotic stress is crucial for securing food production (Cooper and Messina 2023). However, engineering plants for abiotic resilience, through genome editing and/or synthetic biology efforts, requires a comprehensive understanding of the genetic pathways that govern these stress responses.
Studies on plant adaptation to extreme environments, including space—where agricultural production may be essential in the future—have greatly benefited from the ease of cultivation and advanced genetic tools provided by the plant model species Arabidopsis thaliana (Yaschenko et al. 2025, in this Focus Issue), hereafter Arabidopsis. Even though different plant species have evolved in distinct environments, withstanding different abiotic stresses, work on Arabidopsis has been instrumental in uncovering the genes and molecular networks involved in abiotic stress signaling and adaptation, many of which are conserved in crops. While challenges translating what has been learned in Arabidopsis into crops do exist (highlighted in Uauy et al. 2025, also in this Focus Issue), this review explores how some of the insights gained from Arabidopsis have informed the development of crop plants with increased abiotic stress tolerance in relation to cold stress, hypoxia, light signaling, drought, nitrogen use efficiency, growth in space, and flowering under different daylengths, also highlighting future avenues for enhanced crop productivity under rapid environmental change.

## Cold/freezing tolerance
(Written by Yiting Shi and Shuhua Yang)
As sessile organisms, plants constantly face temperature fluctuations, with cold stress posing significant threats to different developmental stages such as germination, seedling growth, and reproduction. The increasing frequency of extreme low-temperature events due to climate change exacerbates crop vulnerability, threatening agricultural productivity. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying plant cold responses is essential for developing resilient crop varieties.
Plants have evolved sophisticated biochemical and molecular defense mechanisms to cope with cold stress. Temperate plants employ cold acclimation, a physiological and biochemical adjustment triggered by exposure to sublethal low temperatures, as a primary survival strategy (Thomashow 1999). Arabidopsis has been instrumental in elucidating this process, particularly through the characterization of the DREB1/CBF–COR regulatory signaling pathway (Thomashow 1999; Shi et al. 2018). The discovery of DEHYDRATION RESPONSIVE ELEMENT (DRE)-BINDING 1/C-REPEAT BINDING FACTOR (DREB1/CBF) transcription factor in the 1990s marked a major breakthrough in understanding cold stress responses (Jaglo-Ottosen et al. 1998; Liu et al. 1998). DREB1 genes are rapidly induced by cold stress, encoding transcription factors that regulate COLD-RESPONSIVE (COR) genes to mitigate cold-induced damage (Thomashow 1999; Song et al. 2021). DREB1 expression is tightly regulated by transcription factors, including the INDUCER OF DREB1 EXPRESSION (ICE), CALMODULIN-BINDING TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATOR (CAMTA), NIN-LIKE PROTEIN 7 (NLP7), and MYB15 (Ding et al. 2024). Plant hormone signaling also modulates DREB1: brassinosteroid and strigolactone enhance its expression, whereas the role of ethylene appears to be species dependent (Ding et al. 2024). Additionally, DREB1 expression is governed by light signaling and the circadian clock (Kidokoro et al. 2022; Ding et al. 2024). This intricate regulatory network (Fig. 1A) presents both challenges and opportunities, making the DREB1 pathway a potential target for genetic manipulation to optimize cold tolerance in crops.
Genetic regulation of cold tolerance in Arabidopsis and major crops. A) Overview of the current understanding of the DREB1 regulatory network in Arabidopsis. Both positive and negative regulators that modulate DREB1 expression and/or protein stability through various mechanisms are depicted. B) Key genetic components identified in various crops, including homologous genes, natural variations, and loci associated with cold adaptation. These elements represent promising targets for genetic manipulation aimed at mitigating the trade-offs between yield and stress tolerance in the development of cold-tolerant crop varieties.
Plants perceive cold signals via specialized cold sensors, triggering signal transduction pathways mediated by secondary messengers (Fig. 1B). Rice CHILLING TOLERANCE DIVERGENCE 1 (COLD1) acts as a cold sensor that enhances cold tolerance by activating Ca2⁺ signaling (Ma et al. 2015). This cold-induced Ca²⁺ signaling serves as a crucial secondary messenger, initiating a cascade of downstream responses that are essential for cold tolerance. In Arabidopsis, cold stress induces the activation of calcium-permeable transporters and channels, such as ANNEXINs and CYCLIC NUCLEOTIDE-GATED CHANNELS (CNGCs), generating calcium signals (Liu et al. 2021; Peng et al. 2024). Downstream of these signals, calcium-related protein kinases, including Ca2+-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) and CBL‐interacting protein kinases (CIPKs), have been reported to play a role in regulating cold tolerance in plants (Ding et al. 2022, 2024). Moreover, a recent study revealed that COLD6 mediates cold responses through 2′,3′-cAMP signaling in rice (Luo et al. 2024). Nonetheless, the full repertoire of cold sensors and the underlying molecular mechanisms require further investigation.
Cold stress responses involve both positive and negative regulatory kinases that fine-tune cold tolerance. OPEN STOMATA 1 (OST1), a key regulator in the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway (Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and Shinozaki 2006), is activated by cold stress in an ABA-independent manner and phosphorylates its targets, including ICE1 and the E3 ligases PUB25/26, increasing their stability and/or activity, which in turn enhances DREB1 expression and freezing tolerance (Ding et al. 2024). In contrast, COLD-RESPONSIVE PROTEIN KINASE 1 (CRPK1) and MPK3/6 negatively regulate freezing tolerance by destabilizing DREB1 and ICE1 proteins, respectively (Liu et al. 2017a; Chen et al. 2021). These findings reveal a complex and dynamic regulatory network to modulate plant cold stress responses (Fig. 1A).
Arabidopsis cold signaling research provides a basis for identifying homologous genes in diverse crop species. For example, the functions of CBF/DREB1 homologs have been characterized in many crops, such as rice, wheat, maize, barley, and rye (Nie et al. 2022). In rice, the OST1 homolog OsSAPK8 phosphorylates and activates OsCNGC9 (Wang et al. 2021a). These findings highlight the value of comparative genomics in elucidating evolutionarily conserved components of cold responses. However, emerging evidence also points functional divergence of homologous genes among species. For instance, while MPK3 promotes ICE1 protein degradation in Arabidopsis (Li et al. 2017), its rice homolog instead enhances the stability of OsICE1 through phosphorylation (Zhang et al. 2017a). Such differences highlight the need for species-specific strategies when translating insights from Arabidopsis to agricultural crops.
Extensive studies on natural genetic variation have revealed key alleles associated with cold tolerance in crops. In rice, polymorphisms in COLD1, COLD6, COLD11, COG1 (Chilling-tolerance in Gengdao/japonica rice 1), qLTG3-1 (Low-temperature germinability on chromosome 3), LTG1, and bZIP73 confer the divergence in seedling-stage cold tolerance among different varieties (Ma et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2018a; Li et al. 2023; Xia et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2024), while natural variants of COLD TOLERANCE AT BOOTING STAGE (CTB4a) and CTB5 enhance rice cold tolerance during the booting stage (Zhang et al. 2017b; Guo et al. 2025 ). In maize, natural alleles of ZmICE1, ZmHSF21, ZmTIP4;3, and ZmRR1 have been identified to improve cold tolerance among maize inbred lines (Fujino et al. 2008; Zeng et al. 2021; Jiang et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024). A natural variant of bZIP68 from teosinte, the wild ancestor of maize, also contributes to enhanced maize cold tolerance (Li et al. 2022a). These findings highlight the value of both cultivated and wild germplasm for identifying superior alleles for improving cold resilience in crops.
Population genetics studies have revealed evidence of cold-driven adaptive evolution in plants. Natural variation in CBF2 in Arabidopsis correlates with local adaptation across different ecotypes, while copy number variations at the HvCBF locus in barley are similarly linked to cold tolerance (Gehan et al. 2015; Francia et al. 2016). In wheat, variations in TaVRN1 and TaHY5-like genes play crucial roles in environmental adaptation (Kippes et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2020a). In rice, the selection of favorable alleles in genes such as natural variation of COLD1, COLD6, HAN1, COG1, CTB4a, CTB5, and bZIP73 has facilitated the adaptation of japonica rice to high-latitude or high-altitude regions (Ma et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2017b; Liu et al. 2018a; Mao et al. 2019; Xia et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025). A recent breakthrough study in maize uncovered that a natural variant of the COLD-RESPONSIVE OPERATION LOCUS 1 gene contributes to enhanced cold tolerance and high-latitude adaptation (Zeng et al. 2025). These evolutionary and population genomic insights offer promising directions for breeding cold-resilient crop varieties.
While key components of the cold signaling network show remarkable evolutionary conservation across plant species (Nie et al. 2022), their direct application in crop improvement often faces challenges due to growth-defense trade-offs (Zhang et al. 2020). To address these challenges, several strategies can be employed: 1) utilizing tissue-specific or stress-inducible promoters to achieve precise spatiotemporal regulation of key genes; 2) identifying key regulatory elements or amino acid residues within the cold regulatory networks as genome-editing targets to fine-tune activity; and 3) exploring natural variations to improve cold tolerance without sacrificing growth or yield (Fig. 1). Recent advances in artificial intelligence and genome editing offer powerful tools to accelerate the development of climate-smart crop varieties capable of thriving in increasingly unpredictable climates.

## Hypoxia
(Written by Mohamad Abbas and Rashmi Sasidharan)
Plant tissues experience hypoxia or sub-ambient oxygen conditions when oxygen demand exceeds availability. Hypoxic conditions imposed by abiotic factors include flooding events, winter ice encasement, and soil compaction (Fig. 2). Reduced oxygen availability impairs oxidative phosphorylation-mediated ATP generation and negatively impacts cellular energy status. Hypoxia can also occur naturally during development, in densely packed tissues with high respiratory demand such as seeds, pollen, meristems, and tubers, and are vital signals shaping developmental programs (Fig. 2). Whether in the developmental or environmental stress context, plants need to sense prevailing oxygen conditions and react appropriately to adjust developmental programs or trigger stress-acclimation/adaptive mechanisms. Arabidopsis research has been instrumental in progressing our understanding of how plants perceive, transduce, and respond to low oxygen signals in environmental and developmental settings.
Arabidopsis research has yielded groundbreaking insights into plant low-oxygen sensing, signaling, and response in both developmental and environmental contexts. ATE, arginyl transferase; MetAP, methionine amino-peptidase; PRT6, proteolysis 6; oxCys, oxidized cysteine. The oxygen and possible nitric oxide (NO) positions in the pathway are shown. Oxygen is used by PCOs to oxidize amino-terminal Cys of the substrates. B) The different aspects of hypoxia. Arabidopsis studies have been instrumental in our current understanding of how plants deal with environment-induced and developmental hypoxia. Stressful hypoxia (right side panel) occurs in the roots of waterlogged plants or shoots of plants during complete submergence in turbid flood waters. Impaired gas diffusion in water also causes a rapid rise in ethylene in flooded tissues. Oxygen and ethylene sensing and signaling pathways are part of a complex molecular network essential for plant responses to abiotic stress–induced hypoxia. Nonstressful hypoxia (left side panel) corresponds to hypoxic niches that affect plant development. Soil oxygen content can vary from 3% to 15% depending on soil type, depth, and compactness, whereas SAMs maintain 3% to 6% O2. Flowering plants grow on an altitudinal range from sea level (pO2 21%) up to 6,000 meters above sea level (pO2 10%).
The negative consequences of abiotic environment-induced hypoxia and how plants tolerate it have been extensively studied in the context of flooding. Submerged plant tissues experience impaired gas exchange due to diminished gas diffusion rates in water (Fig. 2). Historically, research in this area focused on tolerant species, investigating flood adaptive traits such as aerenchyma or adventitious roots. Studies in Arabidopsis were few but intensified following the sequencing of its genome. Facilitated by a genome sequence, facile transient and stable transformation, microarray technology, community resources/tools, and the relative ease of genetic investigations, these early studies provided the first detailed insights into hypoxia-activated signaling and identified regulatory components (Sedbrook et al. 1996; Peng et al. 2001; Baxter-Burrell et al. 2002; Hunt et al. 2002; Shiao et al. 2002; Tournaire-Roux et al. 2003; Gonzali et al. 2005; Liu et al. 2005; Mustroph et al. 2009). Despite this rich body of work implicating roles for transients in calcium, reactive oxygen species, and ATP, evidence of a direct oxygen-sensing mechanism in plants proved elusive. In 2011, 2 independent studies identified an Arg/N-degron pathway mediating oxygen sensing in Arabidopsis (Gibbs et al. 2011; Licausi et al. 2011). This involved oxygen-dependent targeted proteolysis of Group VII ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTORs (ERFVIIs) transcription factors mediating hypoxia-responsive gene expression. Both studies stemmed from intriguing observations of altered hypoxia responses in Arabidopsis mutants and transgenics. Arabidopsis has 5 ERFVIIs, all of which are substrates of the N-degron pathway. In the presence of oxygen, oxidation of a conserved N-terminal cysteine residue marks these ERFVIIs for recognition and degradation by the N-degron pathway machinery leading to proteasomal degradation (Fig. 2A).
The discovery of this molecular mechanism of oxygen sensing spurred extensive follow-up studies in Arabidopsis and crops. There was further characterization of the N-degron pathway components and greater scrutiny of the molecular features, regulation, and functions of the ERFVIIs (e.g. Weits et al. 2014; Gasch et al. 2016). The rice ERFVII SUBMERGENCE1 SUB1A-1 confers tolerance to complete submergence by restricting growth and limiting energy expenditure (the so-called quiescence survival strategy) and facilitating post-submergence recovery (Emerick and Ronald 2019). Despite possessing a similar N-terminal degron sequence and function in triggering hypoxia responses and mediating submergence tolerance, SUB1A was found not to be an N-degron substrate in vitro (Gibbs et al. 2011). Subsequent studies have shed light on regulatory interactions between ERFVIIs and rice hypoxia sensing and comparisons of the flooding-induced quiescence between rice and Arabidopsis (Lin et al. 2019; Reynoso et al. 2019).
Importantly, the Arabidopsis studies revealed the N-degron pathway, controlling ERFVII stability, to be an attractive target for altering flood tolerance. The oxygen-sensing PLANT CYSTEINE OXIDASEs (PCOs), catalyzing the N-terminal cysteine oxidation of ERFVIIs (Fig. 2A), are particularly promising in this respect. Efforts toward unraveling the biochemical features of these enzymes could be instrumental for conditional manipulation of PCO activity via engineering of their oxygen sensitivity or substrate specificity (Taylor-Kearney and Flashman 2022). The manipulation of N-degron E3 ligase PROTEOLYSIS 6 (PRT6) expression in waterlogging sensitive barley significantly improved plant performance and yields (Mendiondo et al. 2016). ERFVIIs have been successfully characterized and targeted to improve flood tolerance in other crops such as maize, wheat, and soybean (Loreti and Perata 2023). Refined knowledge of ERFVII target binding sites could be useful in promoter editing to tune the expression of key regulators and enzymes (Gasch et al. 2016; Lee and Bailey-Serres 2019; Reynoso et al. 2019).
Recent Arabidopsis work has established how flood sensing and signaling encompasses a complex network involving interactions between oxygen, hormones (Hartman et al. 2019), redox (Sasidharan et al. 2021), energy (Kunkowska et al. 2023), and nutrients (Shahzad et al. 2016) sensing pathways. Mechanisms unraveling hypoxia-mediated modulation of root architecture (Shukla et al. 2019; Eysholdt-Derzsó et al. 2024) and post-hypoxic injury (Yeung et al. 2018) have provided important knowledge on flood-resilient traits. Vital post-submergence recovery mechanisms shared by rice and Arabidopsis underscore further possibilities for translation. Arabidopsis hypoxia and flooding-induced molecular responses remain a key comparative benchmark for multi-species comparisons assessing how variation in hypoxia tolerance is determined at the molecular level (Reynoso et al. 2019; van Veen et al. 2024).
Plants do not always perceive hypoxia as stressful but as a developmental cue. Hypoxia occurring due to soil compaction impacts developmental processes such as seed germination, seedling establishment, and the transition to photomorphogenesis via the N-degron components PRT6 and ARGININE-TRNA PROTEIN TRANSFERASE 1 and 2 (ATE1,2) (ATEs) (Fig. 2) (Holman et al. 2009; Gibbs et al. 2011; Abbas et al. 2015). PRT6 and the ERFVIIs constitute a molecular mechanism responsible for adaptation to altitudes, where the partial pressure of oxygen naturally changes based on elevation (Abbas et al. 2022) (Fig. 2). Knowledge of the role of chronic hypoxia as a developmental regulator has greatly benefited from Arabidopsis research. Arabidopsis shoot apical meristems maintain 3% to 6% oxygen (Fig. 2), which is essential for the N-degron substrates VERNALIZATION2 (VRN2) and LITTLE ZIPPER 2 (ZPR2) to modulate flowering and meristematic activity (Gibbs et al. 2018; Weits et al. 2019). Hypoxic niches of advanced lateral root primordia (LRP) allow stabilization of ERFVIIs and their binding to AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 7 (ARF7). This inhibits LRP gene expression, mainly LOB DOMAIN-CONTAINING PROTEIN 16 and 18 (LBD16,18) and contributes to the optimal spacing between LRs and root branching (Shukla et al. 2019).
The discovery of the molecular sensing mechanism whereby plants sense oxygen and in parallel nitric oxide (NO) was a game changer. Oxygen-requiring pathways in plants are diverse, and oxygen is involved in many enzymatically catalyzed redox reactions. In theory, any enzyme that facilitates redox reactions could potentially function as an oxygen sensor, especially when oxygen levels drop below the enzyme's affinity constant for its substrate. Thus, while the PCO branch of the N-degron pathway is the current main oxygen sensor in plants, the discovery of new oxygen sensors will not be surprising.
Our understanding of the molecular mechanism of oxygen sensing is relatively new (<15 years ago). The rich knowledge base established since retains Arabidopsis's status as an important model to address new questions and existing knowledge gaps. This includes determining the site of NO action, possible substrates of the PCO branch of the N-degron pathway, and identification of as yet unknown oxygen sensors. How plants differentiate between developmental and environment-induced hypoxia remains an intriguing question. Understanding these mechanisms and their engineering can aid the improvement of crop resilience to hypoxia-related challenges, including yield loss from floods and post-harvest preservation. Arabidopsis still boasts an impressive toolbox in molecular biology and genetics. Combined with the publicly accessible 1001 Genomes collection, it remains a vital model for further comprehension of hypoxia stress and resilience and will be needed to ensure that more success stories like that of SUB1 rice will emerge.

## Light signaling
(Written by Marcelo J. Yanovsky and Jorge José Casal)
Plant tolerance to high population densities (crowding) and plant cycle duration are crucial agronomic traits that significantly impact crop resource utilization and stress tolerance. Crowding intensifies mutual shading within the canopy, prompting architectural adaptations in response to changes in light quality and quantity. These adaptations directly modulate crowding tolerance and ultimately determine the optimal planting density (Fig. 3). Sowing date and location along the latitudinal axis influence the photoperiod experienced by the crop, which, depending on plant photoperiod sensitivity, dictates flowering time, the length of the plant cycle, and, consequently, the optimal sowing window (Fig. 3). This section demonstrates how studying Arabidopsis mutants with altered seedling phenotypes under varying light and dark conditions has provided valuable insights into the genetic underpinnings of these critical crop responses.
Plant responses to light cues modified by crop management practices significantly influence key agronomic traits, and Arabidopsis research has been instrumental in identifying the crucial genetic players involved. In dense stands, shade perceived by photoreceptors (PHYB, CRY1, PHYA) triggers architectural changes that modulate crowding tolerance. The photo period, determined by location (latitude) and sowing date, influences flowering time and cycle length, depending on genes involved in light signaling and in some cases interacting with the circadian clock (PHYC, PRR7, GI, LNK2, ELF3). These changes in crowding tolerance and cycle length ultimately determine the optimal population density, sowing dates, and suitable locations for crop cultivation. The light signaling genes identified in Arabidopsis are highlighted in white boxes.
The reduced red/far-red light ratios and UVA-blue irradiance caused by light absorption and reflection from green leaves (canopy light cues) respectively diminish the activities of phytochrome B (PHYB) (Reed et al. 1993) and cryptochrome 1 (CRY1) (Ahmad and Cashmore 1993), the primary photoreceptors governing plant responses to shade (Casal and Fankhauser 2023). Accurately simulating the profound impact of dense canopies on these light cues without compromising photosynthetic light availability is extremely challenging. The identification and functional characterization of PHYB and CRY1 genes in Arabidopsis facilitated the finding of mutants in various crop species, revealing a deeper-than-expected influence of photomorphogenesis. For instance, ZmphyB and Zmcry1b mutants in maize exhibit reduced leaf expansion in field conditions, suggesting that the decreased leaf area per plant in high-density crops is a sensory response rather than solely a consequence of resource competition (Wies et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2024). Studies in Arabidopsis have also provided a valuable model system to understand the molecular mechanisms by which canopy shade regulates important agronomic traits. For example, low blue light reduces the protective effect of GmCRY1 on DELLA proteins in soybean, and this enhances the expression of the GmWRKY100 transcription factor, ultimately leading to basal leaf senescence (Li et al. 2024a).
Arabidopsis mutants revealed that, contrary to phytochrome (PHY) B, PHYA activity is maximal under far-red light (Quail et al. 1995), and phyA mutants fail to survive under deep shade, rich in far-red light (Yanovsky et al. 1995). In maize, high PHYA activity in the shade promotes the degradation of the transcription factor RAVL1 that enhances the expression of LEAF ANGLE ARCHITECTURE OF SMART CANOPY 1 (LAC1) (Tian et al. 2024). The maize lac mutant displays leaf angles that increase tolerance to high population densities, supporting a crucial role of PHYA in plant adjustment to crowded environments (Tian et al. 2024).
The notion that plants may overreact to canopy light cues, potentially sacrificing yield, has persisted for years. Consequently, attenuating shade-avoidance responses in crops could be advantageous. Ectopic expression of the Arabidopsis PHYB gene, which maintains higher levels of active PHYB and reduces shade sensitivity, has successfully increased potato tuber yield under high-density planting (Boccalandro et al. 2003). Similarly, soybean and maize overexpressing GmCRY1b and ZmCRY1b, respectively, demonstrated improved lodging resistance and yield under dense conditions (Lyu et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2024).
However, reducing plants’ sensitivity to neighbor cues has caveats. For instance, ectopic expression of PHYB hinders vegetative growth and light foraging capacity in potato (Boccalandro et al. 2003). Tomato plants overexpressing SlCRY1a exhibited increased carotenoid content per fruit but showed a dwarf and bushy phenotype and a significant reduction in overall yield (Liu et al. 2018b). Noteworthy, fruit-specific expression of a constitutively active tomato PHYB2Y252H was enough to enhance carotenoids, tocopherols, flavonoids, and ascorbate, highlighting a promising tool for fruit fortification (Alves et al. 2020) that could avoid the negative impact of whole plant overexpression on vegetative growth.
Plants can anticipate seasonal changes by sensing day length. This ability comes from a combination of photoreceptors, which distinguish light from dark periods, and the circadian clock that tracks the passage of time. Research in Arabidopsis has been instrumental to identify the photoreceptors that perceive daylength in crops. For instance, PHYC mediates natural variation in flowering time responses of Arabidopsis (Liu et al. 2011) and plays a major role in the long-day induction of flowering in wheat (Chen et al. 2014).
Furthermore, genetic variants of genes that operate at the interphase of light and clock signaling have allowed crops grown at different latitudes to adjust their growth and development for optimal yield (Maeda and Nakamichi 2022). For instance, the major locus determining photoperiodic sensitivity in cereals, Photoperiod-1 (Ppd-1), encodes orthologues of Arabidopsis PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR 7 (PRR7). Similarly, the E2 locus controlling photoperiodic sensitivity in soybean encodes a homolog of Arabidopsis GIGANTEA (GI). Furthermore, a deletion in the NIGHT LIGHT-INDUCIBLE AND CLOCK-REGULATED 2 (LNK2) gene uncovered in Arabidopsis was involved in the adaptation of tomato from growing in the equator to its cultivation in northern Europe (Maeda and Nakamichi 2022). Variations in the EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3) gene have been linked to domestication and breeding practices that maximize crop productivity at different latitudes in soybean, wheat, barley, rice, and other species (Faure et al. 2012; Zakhrabekova et al. 2012; Lu et al. 2014; Maeda and Nakamichi 2022; Wittern et al. 2023). All the genes mentioned above are transcriptional regulators that function within the complex circadian network and play a role in regulating light signaling.
For reasons of space, we only focus on ELF3. In Arabidopsis, the elf3 mutation disrupts flowering time and circadian rhythms, as well as light inhibition of hypocotyl growth (Hicks et al. 1996; Zagotta et al. 1996). ELF3 functions as a scaffold protein that interacts with other clock-regulated proteins to form the evening complex (EC) (Nusinow et al. 2011), which acts by repressing the expression of hundreds of genes, including the clock genes PRR9, PRR7, and GI. In addition, ELF3 interacts with PHYB (Liu et al. 2001) and other light signaling proteins to modulate light control of the circadian clock and plant growth and development in both EC-dependent and EC-independent manners. This suggests that ELF3 acts as a bridge between light signaling and the circadian clock, potentially affecting a plant's response to day length (photoperiodism) through either pathway. For more details on the mechanisms involving ELF3, see the section in this review on “Flowering response to rising temperature.”
Soybeans are short-day plants originally cultivated in the temperate regions of China. Until a few decades ago, their cultivation in more equatorial regions was not feasible due to very low yields associated with extremely early flowering. Genetic variants at the J locus, which encodes an ELF3 orthologue, enabled soybean plants to flower much later in Brazil (Lu et al. 2014), allowing this country to become the largest producer of soybeans in the world. In wheat, a long-day plant cultivated in temperate zones, genetic variants associated with orthologues of ELF3 lead to early flowering, allowing the crop to elude the hot and dry summers of the Mediterranean region. In Japanese wheat varieties, the early flowering resulting from mutations in ELF3 orthologues helps anticipate the rainy season, avoiding pre-harvest sprouting and reducing the risk of fungal infections.
In crop species, ELF3 variations can affect photoperiodism by altering the clock, as seen in barley (Faure et al. 2012; Zakhrabekova et al. 2012). However, studies in soybean with altered ELF3 function revealed no significant changes in daily gene expression patterns, suggesting the circadian clock remained largely unaffected (Yue et al. 2021). Similarly, while mutations in all 4 ELF3 homologs of wheat disrupt circadian rhythms, variations in individual genes still influence flowering time without affecting the clock (Wittern et al. 2023). These findings suggest that natural variations in ELF3 likely influence flowering time and maturity in crops primarily through its role in light signaling rather than through its role in the circadian clock.

## Nitrogen use efficiency
(Written by Sandrine Ruffel and Nicolaus von Wirén)
Nitrogen (N), representing 1% to 6% of dry matter, is one of the most limiting elements for plant growth. Often, massive use of fertilizers constitutes the main strategy to avoid any impact of N limitation on yield. Plants absorb less than 50% of applied N, so with high fertilizer doses a large amount of reactive N forms is lost, leading to water pollution and greenhouse gas emissions with consequences on climate change (Gong et al. 2024). Reducing environmental impacts of fertilizers requires optimizing agricultural practices but also nitrogen use efficiency (NUE; defined as grain yield or biomass relative to the amount of N applied) in crops. The most common and straightforward approach to improve NUE is genetic selection and classical breeding of N-efficient crop cultivars (Hawkesford and Griffiths 2019). Alternatively, genetic improvement of crops may profit from our advanced understanding of N responsiveness, uptake, transport, assimilation, and reallocation in the model plant Arabidopsis, in which a cascade of inter-linked metabolic and developmental processes provides already a deep and comprehensive picture of the N-related regulatory network (Shanks et al. 2024).
In Arabidopsis, the perception of nitrate (NO3−), the main inorganic form used in most agrosystems, has been shown to rely on the NO3− transceptor NITRATE TRANSPORTER1.1/NRT1,PTR FAMILY6.3/CHLORATE RESISTANT1 (NRT1.1/NPF6.3/CHL1) and on the NODULE INCEPTION-like protein (NLP) transcription factor family (mainly NLP7 and NLP2,4,5,6,8,9) (Ho et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2017b, 2022a). Interestingly, different ways of targeting NRT1s have been shown to improve NUE in rice. Based on AtNRT1.1 homology, 3 orthologs have been identified in the rice genome: OsNRT1.1A, OsNRT1.1B, and OsNRT1.1C (Plett et al. 2010). Initially, genetic variability between indica and japonica rice at the OsNRT1.1B locus, causing higher NO3− influx, was found to account on average for up to 10% higher grain yield and NUE (Hu et al. 2015). Then, the elite allele of indica OsNRT1.1B was introduced in japonica by gene replacement using an innovative CRISPR/Cas9 method based on homology-directed DNA repair (Li et al. 2018). Here, the previous characterization of AtNRT1.1 as transceptor at the plasma membrane (Ho et al. 2009) has been pivotal to define OsNRT1.1B as the functional ortholog and to decipher its broader impact on NO3− uptake, translocation, and signaling (Hu et al. 2015). Although OsNRT1.1A localizes to the tonoplast, shows ammonium-inducible expression, and shares even higher homology with AtNRT1.1 than OsNRT1.1B (Wang et al. 2020), it also stimulates the expression of genes involved in N metabolism and utilization. Indeed, overexpression of OsNRT1.1A in rice increases grain yield and NUE and reduces the time required to reach maturation due to its impact on N assimilation and flowering (Wang et al. 2018).
Combining fundamental understanding of NO3− transport and remobilization in Arabidopsis provided alternative strategies to improve NUE in crops. On the basis of the low-affinity NO3− transporter AtNRT1.2/NPF4.6, involved in NO3− reallocation from old to young leaves, Chen et al. (2020) constructed a chimeric and hyperactive NO3− transporter named NC4N. To do so, the 4 transmembrane domains 2 to 5 were replaced by those of AtNRT1.1, increasing the uptake level by 12-fold magnitude compared with NRT1.2. To target the activity of this hyperactive low-affinity transporter to source organs, NC4N was fused to the AtNRT1.7/NPF2.13 promoter, conferring high expression in source leaves. Expressing this transgene in the Arabidopsis nrt1.7 mutant enhanced NO3− remobilization from leaves, and ectopic expression in wild-type rice improved plant growth, NUE, and grain yield by 8% to 11% in field experiments (Chen et al. 2020). This approach illustrates how successful translational approaches including redesigned structure-function aspects can benefit from fundamental Arabidopsis knowledge.
Manipulating NO3− reduction, N assimilation, or N remobilization during leaf senescence and reproductive stage is also considered as a valuable strategy to improve NUE in crops. Our current understanding of the function of individual isoenzymes or organic N transporters in this N metabolism is based on a large number of in-depth studies in Arabidopsis (Coruzzi 2003; Havé et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2022b). These have inspired transgenic approaches, in which overexpression of individual N metabolism genes led to better growth on low N or higher NUE. For example, the overexpression of the GLUTAMINE-DEPENDENT ASPARAGINE SYNTHASE1 (OsASN1), encoding an asparagine synthetase, increases rice yield under N-limiting conditions (Lee et al. 2020a). Likewise, overexpressing initial steps in ammonium assimilation is a promising strategy. The concurrent activation of the AMMONIUM TRANSPORTER1;2 (OsAMT1;2), an NH4+ transporter, and the FERREDOXIN-DEPENDENT GLUTAMATE SYNTHASE1 (OsGOGAT1), a glutamate synthase, led to higher grain yield under N-limiting conditions in rice (Lee et al. 2020b). The understanding of autophagy processes, involved into the degradation of unnecessary constituents in lytic compartments, is largely benefited from studies in model organisms. In particular, the role of autophagy and ATG8 (AUTOPHAGY-RELATED8), a protein involved in the autophagosome functioning, for nutrient recycling and organic N remobilization from leaves to seeds has been shown in Arabidopsis (Guiboileau et al. 2012). In rice, under normal conditions, the number of tillers, the N content in grains, as well as the yield are increased with the overexpression of OsATG8a (Yu et al. 2019).
Successful improvement of crop yield can also be achieved by targeting central regulators, such as transcription factors, involved in the coordination of the different steps of N use (Kasemsap and Bloom 2022). Arabidopsis studies once again can play a significant role in the search for NUE improvement. Based on the central role of NLPs in NO3− perception in Arabidopsis, analyses on rice homologs have been undertaken to assess their role in NUE. The overexpression of OsNLP3 and OsNLP4, the 2 closest homologs to AtNLP7 in rice, led to yield and NUE increases from 10% to 30% under moderate or sufficient N levels, and transcriptome analysis showed that they indeed coordinate the regulation of genes related to N signaling and assimilation (Wu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022a). Supervised machine-learning algorithm combined with gene network analysis on a large set of genomic data in Arabidopsis was another approach to identify candidate coordinators of nutrition and growth. This way, the scaffold protein BT2 has been predicted to be a central node in a network of genes related to N-use. Experimentally, the agronomical NUE (i.e. seed amount per plant/quantity of N applied) was found increased in the Arabidopsis bt1/bt2 double mutant as well as in a rice Osbt mutant, specifically in N-limited conditions. In both species, the NUE improvement in mutants is likely due to a higher NO3− transport level depending on the NRT2 transporter family, defining these BTB family members as repressors of the N use (Araus et al. 2016). More recently, a study presented an evolutionarily informed machine learning approach that uses conserved transcriptome responses to predict phenotypic outcomes, specifically nitrogen use efficiency, across species like Arabidopsis and maize. By reducing feature dimensionality with biologically relevant genes, the approach enhances predictive power and reveals in maize the role of the NFYA3 transcription factor in NUE under low N conditions (Cheng et al. 2021). In Arabidopsis, the transcription factor TGA1 has been identified as a hub in a N-dependent network displaying a unique and interesting property to control plant growth in response to N dose (Swift et al. 2020). Evaluating the role of its homologs for NUE in crops provides an exciting perspective. Instead of being only a resource to identify candidate genes for NUE improvement, Arabidopsis has also been used for its simplicity to delineate the role of a gene isolated from crops. This was the case for the transcription factor Dof1. Initially, this regulator has been isolated and studied in maize (ZmDof1) in which it coordinates the expression of some genes involved in carbon metabolism. In particular, it regulates the synthesis of oxaloacetate, a precursor for the synthesis of 2-oxoglutarate, which is the carbon-skeleton required for N assimilation (Yanagisawa 2000). To test the hypothesis that by overexpressing DOF, the activity of the nitrogen metabolism should be stimulated to stay coordinated with carbon metabolism, Arabidopsis transgenic plants expressing ZmDOF1 have been produced and analyzed. In addition to the expected increase of the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase activity, the N assimilation was found enhanced as well as a better growth in low nitrogen conditions (Yanagisawa et al. 2004). Later, the same approach was performed in rice, giving similar results (Kurai et al. 2011).
Eventually, optimizing the root system architecture potentially belongs to targets that may improve the use of nutrient acquisition and yield (Den Herder et al. 2010). A tremendous and essential work has and still is being done to delineate the architectural phenotypes improving the capture of nitrogen, as well as water and other nutrients, studying crops in soil (Lynch et al. 2024). Even in this context, better knowledge and an optimization of phenotypic traits could be considered with model systems combined to genetic and structure-function approaches. Indeed, determining genetic networks underlying root structure characteristics would reveal possible tight coordination, favorable or unfavorable, with metabolism. It is noteworthy that among the genes presented above, some of them control root development. AtNRT1.1 is a perfect example of a central node for NO3− acquisition and root development (Krouk et al. 2010). Eventually, extensive research on the signaling pathways controlling root development in response to N availability in Arabidopsis identified allelic variation in several molecular targets (Jia et al. 2022). Exploiting allelic variation in genes for beneficial root traits identified in Arabidopsis for crop improvement by genome editing provides another promising avenue for translational research (Fig. 4).
Deciphering nitrogen (N) nutrition and signaling in Arabidopsis contributes to improve yield and NUE in crops. The main N sources in plant nutrition are the 2 inorganic forms nitrate (NO3−) and ammonium (NH4+). In Arabidopsis, molecular mechanisms associated with their perception, uptake, transport, remobilization, and assimilation have been deciphered. In rice, it is mainly the overexpression of homolog genes that led to increase yield and NUE. Regulators, such as transcription factors, are also promising targets as potential coordinators of the different steps of N journey in plants. Making root systems more efficient at capturing nutrients by optimizing the architecture is another interesting avenue.

## Drought
(Written by Sarah M. Assmann and Noah A. Kinscherf)
Plants respond to droughts in complex ways. In drought escape, plants achieve a measure of reproductive success through acceleration of the life cycle. In drought tolerance, plants withstand desiccation, but this is often with a penalty on seed production. For crop improvement, optimal approaches are likely to capitalize on drought avoidance, defined here as morphological, anatomical, or physiological changes in response to water limitation that allow plant water status and thus yield to be minimally impacted. Morphological changes include altered root and aboveground architecture; anatomical changes include reduced stomatal density and changes in cell wall properties that favor turgor maintenance; and physiological changes include osmotic adjustment, as well as stomatal closure and consequent reduction of transpirational water loss.
As for many aspects of plant biology, research on Arabidopsis has led the field in the identification of genes involved in drought response. As reviewed in Wang et al. (2021b), more than a dozen genes have been identified in Arabidopsis that confer drought tolerance when their orthologs are overexpressed in a crop species, yet none of these has been commercialized. In fact, transgenes commercialized for performance under drought are rare; they include a bacterial RNA chaperone, CspB, that is utilized in Bayer's DroughtGard maize (Castiglioni et al. 2008; Nemali et al. 2015), and a sunflower homeodomain-leucine zipper transcription factor gene, HaHD4, with its stress-responsive promoter, favorably evaluated in soybean (Ribichich et al. 2020) and commercialized in wheat (Gupta 2024). While the stress-protective attributes of heterologous expression of these 2 genes were evaluated in Arabidopsis (Dezar et al. 2005; Castiglioni et al. 2008), the paucity of commercial varieties developed based on leads from this model species (Nuccio et al. 2018) raises the question of its role in translational biology for improved crop performance under drought.
One possible explanation for this situation relates to the types of gene candidates identified from Arabidopsis: 1) transcription factors, especially those responsive to the desiccation-signaling hormone, ABA; 2) genes involved in metabolic responses to drought, such as those that regulate synthesis of compatible solutes; and 3) genes involved in protein synthesis, modification, or turnover. It is possible that overexpression of these types of genes is, paradoxically, too protective, conferring survival under drought at the expense of yield, and/or that their overexpression leads to yield drag under non–water-limited conditions. One example in support of this hypothesis is a study by Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and colleagues (Kudo et al. 2019). They observed that overexpression in Arabidopsis of DREB1A/CBF3, a transcription factor that promotes drought and freezing tolerance, also decreased growth. The growth phenotype was partially alleviated without compromising drought tolerance, upon coexpression of a GA5, a rate-limiting enzyme in GA biosynthesis.
Further challenges to translating from Arabidopsis to crops are that the genetic makeup of Arabidopsis and crop plants differs (Inzé and Nelissen 2022); this is particularly true over larger phylogenetic distances such as those between Arabidopsis and grain crops. Moreover, the conditions used to uncover drought-related genes in Arabidopsis may not always sufficiently mimic the conditions that crops actually experience in the field (Langstroff et al. 2022).
However, the above observations scarcely mean that Arabidopsis research is irrelevant to crop improvement. First, Arabidopsis historically has been the sine qua non system for research into plant developmental biology and its genetic underpinnings. As argued herein, our resultant knowledge of plant development has been underexploited toward improving crop performance under drought. Second, when candidate QTLs or genes are identified directly in a crop species, fundamental knowledge of gene function, most often garnered from Arabidopsis, is vital in prioritizing leads. Third, Arabidopsis is a hotbed of methods development, with such methods often subsequently deployed in crop species. Each of these topics is touched on below in the context of plant development and drought response (Fig. 5).
Approaches and targets toward improved drought resilience. Popular techniques for identifying and assessing candidate genes include GWAS, TWAS, inducible and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-targeted gene regulation, chemical genetics, and single-cell RNA sequencing. Advanced techniques for assessing drought response phenotypes include hyperspectral and thermal imaging and imaging rhizotrons. Gene targets for enhancing drought tolerance and/or avoidance in Arabidopsis are associated with xylem anatomy, stomatal kinetics and/or density, stress signaling pathways, and root architecture. Candidates indicated with OE (overexpression) or KO (knockout) were shown to confer improved drought phenotypes after that manipulation. Of the gene targets in this figure, only CspB from Bacillus subtilis and HaHD4 from sunflower have been incorporated into commercial crop lines; however, several candidate genes that regulate stomatal behavior or reduce stomatal density upon manipulation in Arabidopsis have produced favorable drought-related phenotypes upon manipulation of a related gene in crop species.
Consider the path that a water molecule takes through the plant. Water that is taken up by the root moves symplastically across the endodermis (Ranathunge and Schreiber 2011), transits the vasculature of the shoot xylem, enters shoot tissue across the living cells of the xylem parenchyma, hydrates mesophyll tissue, and ultimately is lost to the atmosphere through stomata via the transpiration stream. At each step along the way, plant development influences water movement. Starting with roots, it has been argued that under drought a “steep, cheap, and deep” root architecture (Lynch 2018) is favorable: fewer longer roots (Voothuluru et al. 2024) with enhanced aerenchyma production, accompanied by lateral roots that grow at a more acute angle and are differentially produced at deeper locations. This architecture is proposed to reflect a strengthening of the gravitropic response (Rellán-Álvarez et al. 2015; Feng et al. 2016) and has been characterized in Arabidopsis through the development of specialized imaging rhizotrons (Rellán-Álvarez et al. 2015). Hydrotropism, or preferential root growth toward water independent of gravity, has been studied primarily in Arabidopsis, with 4 regulatory mutants, nhr1, miz1, miz2, and ahr1 identified (reviewed in Feng et al. 2016) that arguably could be targets for biotechnology. Additionally, hydropatterning, whereby lateral root development is promoted by local water availability (Bao et al. 2014), is a favorable trait under drought, and genetic control of lateral root formation has been elucidated largely in Arabidopsis (Banda et al. 2019).
Classically, the Casparian strip had been described as a suberized ring around endodermal cells that restricts transit of water and solutes to the xylem to a symplastic route. It was research in Arabidopsis that determined that the Casparian strip is fundamentally a lignified zone, albeit endodermal cell surfaces are also heavily suberized (Naseer et al. 2012). Observation of drought-induced increases in endodermal suberization in rice have been interpreted as a mechanism to retain water in the xylem when soil water potential decreases (Henry et al. 2012). Many of the developmental genes governing formation of the endodermis (Cui et al. 2007), as well as multiple other root cell types (Cederholm et al. 2012), were first identified in Arabidopsis by the seminal work of Philip Benfey and colleagues. Exemplifying how functional annotations from Arabidopsis inform studies in crops, Zaman et al. (2024) built on this knowledge to assign putative functions to a number of candidate loci associated (via genome-wide association studies [GWAS] and transcriptome-wide association studies [TWAS]) with root morphology traits in spring wheat.
Xylem hydraulic efficiency, defined as hydraulic conductance for a given pressure/tension gradient, and xylem safety, defined as lack of susceptibility to embolism formation and spread (Gleason et al. 2016), have been studied primarily in tree species (Torres-Ruiz et al. 2024). However, studies on Arabidopsis accessions and the Arabidopsis woody soc1 ful double mutant have importantly revealed that the extent of xylem wall lignification and the thickness of pit membranes within the pits that connect xylem elements are positively correlated with improved xylem safety (Thonglim et al. 2021, 2023), providing potential anatomical and gene targets for manipulation. Related to this, a GWAS study in Arabidopsis identified a transcription factor, XYLEM NAC DOMAIN 1 (XND1), that negatively regulates both xylem vessel formation and drought tolerance (defined as vegetative biomass production) (Tang et al. 2018).
In addition, novel aspects of hydraulic control by the vascular parenchyma, through which water transits the plasma membrane via water-conducting channels named aquaporins, have been studied extensively in Arabidopsis (Maurel et al. 2015). Simple overexpression of aquaporins can have either positive or negative effects on drought response (reviewed in Tenorio Berrío et al. 2022); this makes sense given that aquaporins can facilitate both water entry into the root xylem and water loss to the substomatal cavity. Manipulation of aquaporin activity, as elucidated in Arabidopsis (Torne-Srivastava et al. 2024) rather than aquaporin expression levels per se, may hold particular promise for improved drought resilience.
Most of the water loss from mature leaves occurs through stomata. Dynamic stomatal closure in response to signals engendered by soil drought and high VPD (sometimes referred to as “atmospheric drought”) is central to protection of plant water potential and hydraulic safety. Because stomatal closure in response to changes in light intensity or CO2 concentration is slow compared with the rapid effects of these parameters on photosynthesis, there can be a period when the ratio of assimilation to transpiration (water use efficiency) is reduced. Shortening this period by manipulating stomatal kinetics could theoretically improve water conservation. Although studies in Arabidopsis have approached stomatal kinetics in many ways (Nguyen et al. 2023), the translation of these strategies into crop species is still lacking. However, there are some promising leads. For example, overexpression in wheat of the wheat ortholog (TaNHX2) of an Arabidopsis vacuolar cation/H+ antiporter (NHX)-type exchanger (AtNHX2) that mediates K+ accumulation in the guard cell vacuole (Andrés et al. 2014) resulted in smaller stomatal apertures and improved drought tolerance, as assessed by plant survival rates and foliar fresh weight (Li et al. 2024b). Several groups are also assessing optogenetic approaches that physically link guard cell ion transporters, such as K+ channels and H +-ATPases, to light-responsive proteins to alter stomatal kinetics in Arabidopsis (Nguyen et al. 2023; Horaruang et al. 2022), with potential for translation into crop species.
The density of stomata on a leaf surface can be a critical influence on transpiration and thus water use efficiency (Doheny-Adams et al. 2012; Bertolino et al. 2019). Indeed, Ferguson et al. (2024) note that “optimal” stomatal density will likely become progressively lower as atmospheric concentrations of CO2 continue to rise. Stomatal development is one area wherein manipulation of developmental genes first identified in Arabidopsis, while not always guaranteed to succeed (Schuler et al. 2018), has shown promise in crop species.
The EPIDERMAL PATTERNING FACTOR (EPF) family of genes first identified in Arabidopsis encodes secretory peptides involved in the regulation of stomatal spacing and density (Hara et al. 2007). This family is composed of EPF genes, which are negative regulators of stomatal development, and EPF-like (EPFL) genes, which include positive regulators of stomatal development (Karavolias et al. 2023). Multiple studies in Arabidopsis found that overexpressing EPF genes or knocking out EPFL genes decreased stomatal density (Hara et al. 2007; Hunt et al. 2010; Doheny-Adams et al. 2012). These results have been translated into grain crops, with overexpression of EPF1 orthologs OsEPF1 in rice (Caine et al. 2019 ) and HvEPF1 in barley (Hughes et al. 2017) achieving reduced stomatal density and improved performance under drought: at ∼50% reduction in stomatal density in rice, there was no penalty on either assimilation or yield at light intensities below 1,000 µmol. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout in rice of a paralog of OsEPFL9, OsEPFL10, caused a moderate decrease in stomatal density and improved water conservation without reducing photosynthesis or impacting thermoregulation under field-relevant light conditions (Karavolias et al. 2023).
It has been predicted that due to rising atmospheric levels of CO2, concentrations of intracellular CO2 in C4 plants may exceed those required to maximize photosynthesis, theoretically permitting decreased stomatal conductance without negatively impacting carbon gain (Leakey et al. 2019). In sorghum, a C4 crop, constitutive expression of an EPF gene fusion, SbEPF2syn, containing domains from SbEPF2 and SbEPF9, the sorghum homologs of AtEPF2 and AtEPF9, reduced stomatal density and stomatal conductance without penalty to photosynthesis. However, while water use efficiency in the SbEPF2syn lines was improved relative to wild type, pleiotropic defects in development of flowers and panicles resulted in reduced seed yield (Ferguson et al. 2024).
Similarly to EPF genes, in Arabidopsis the subtilase STOMATAL DENSITY AND DISTRIBUTION 1 (AtSDD1) regulates stomatal patterning, decreasing stomatal density when overexpressed (Von Groll et al. 2002). Overexpression in cultivated tomato, Solanum lycopersicum, of SchSDD1, an AtSDD1 ortholog from the stress-tolerant wild tomato Solanum chilense, reduced stomatal density and improved water retention in excised leaves (Morales-Navarro et al. 2018). Together with the results from OsEPF10 knockout in rice (Karavolias et al. 2023), these studies support the value of identifying critical genes regulating stomatal density and patterning in Arabidopsis so that researchers can take advantage of orthologs and paralogs in crop species and their wild relatives.
Early on in Arabidopsis research, thermal imaging was used to measure the cooling effect of transpiration on leaf temperature as a proxy for stomatal conductance (Plessis et al. 2011). Scaling up this approach and combining it with rapid stomatal density phenotyping via optical tomography interpreted by machine-learning (Ferguson et al. 2021) allowed Leakey and colleagues to identify QTLs related to these traits in Setaria italica × Setaria viridae recombinant inbred lines grown under well-watered vs water-limited conditions (Prakash et al. 2021). Similar high-throughput field phenotyping, coupled with GWAS and TWAS, has identified candidate genes in sorghum that include orthologs of Arabidopsis genes known for their roles in stomatal behavior or development (Ferguson et al. 2021; Pignon et al. 2021). Such results highlight the importance of Arabidopsis research in both technique development and biological understanding. Another example of this dual role is provided by identification of ABA receptors in Arabidopsis, followed by development of receptor agonists as antitranspirants (e.g. Okamoto et al. 2013; Vaidya et al. 2019; Roeder et al. 2023), heralding a new type of agrochemical that could be used to improve drought resilience.
In both model and crop species, a growing CRISPR toolkit, enabling editing of both gene regulatory regions and ORFs (Soyk et al. 2020; Li et al. 2024c), offers new approaches to dissect and manipulate quantitative traits such as drought avoidance. Inducible and reversible systems of gene regulation arising from such approaches could foster crop productivity under drought in the absence of a yield penalty. Moreover, single-cell RNA-seq in Arabidopsis, including studies of drought effects (Illouz-Eliaz et al. 2024), is providing new insights into exactly which cell types to target. Although the path that water takes through the plant is a continuum, the ability to target specific aspects of that continuum via these and other advanced molecular approaches foreshadows a new type of precision agriculture, holding promise that crops will flourish despite the growing threat of drought engendered by climate change.

## Plant stress responses to space
(Written by Arkadipta Bakshi, Burcu Alptekin, and Simon Gilroy)
The Earth is teeming with life, and millions of years of evolution have equipped organisms with a vast array of adaptations to our planet's many and varied environments. However, biology's voyage into space began less than 80 years ago. If all of life's history on the Earth were compressed to a single year, the longest human mission in space would have lasted about 10 milliseconds. How then does biology fare in these truly novel and alien conditions? Some of the stresses found in spaceflight are identical to those on Earth. Drought is still drought, even for a plant grown on the international space station. The sensing and response systems that help plants survive these kinds of challenges in a terrestrial setting are equally relevant in space. However, there are a suite of factors that impact plant physiology and development that are unique to spaceflight and where biology's evolutionary history may be of little help (Fig. 6).
The unique stresses of living in space. Spaceflight imposes a series of novel factors that biology must adapt to. These include the biological effects of reduced gravity (microgravity) on physiology and development (such as altered gravity-responsive growth) and microgravity's physical effects on the growth environment (such as loss of convection and increased impact of the adhesion of water) and increased radiation exposure. The space vehicle itself imposes other challenges such as constraints on physical space for plant growth and often an elevated CO2 level (that can reach several thousands of ppm from crew respiration) along with a potentially complex environment of volatiles arising from outgassing from the plastics and polymers of the vehicle's construction.
Why study these effects of space on plants? One of the goals of space plant biologists is to develop robust crops as integral components of the life support systems that can sustain a crew on long-duration missions. Although these plants are grown in the highly engineered, closed environment of a spacecraft, many of their most desirable features will be familiar to plant breeders on Earth. Alongside selecting for plants that can thrive despite the unique stressors found in spaceflight, space agriculture targets the development of crops with reduced stature, high yield and harvest index, strong resilience to biotic and abiotic stress, and critically, plants that the crew enjoy eating. A compact, high-yielding tomato plant that grows robustly in space is of little value if the crew members just do not like how it tastes. Arabidopsis has proven a critical model in these studies, not least because its small size and rapid generation time fit well with the often-significant physical constraints of a spaceflight mission. However, space also provides a unique laboratory to conduct experiments that are simply impossible to perform on Earth. How does gravity shape plant growth and development on Earth? A direct approach to answering this question is to remove gravity and ask what happens. The insights that are being drawn from how spaceflight impacts Arabidopsis physiology and development are expanding our basic understanding of plant biology and offering opportunities to translate this knowledge into practical applications on Earth, especially in the context of closed environment agriculture (Mortimer and Gilliham 2022).
Without the pervasive 1×gravity of Earth, plants show disrupted morphogenesis, although they do still grow and develop robustly, being capable of germinating, flowering, and setting seeds. For example, Arabidopsis grown in spaceflight develops a relatively normal-looking rosette and root system, but the roots exhibit randomized growth directions and an enhanced skewing that is likely related in part to disruption of the cytoskeleton (e.g. Millar et al. 2011; Nakashima et al. 2014; Califar et al. 2020). In addition, lacking the internal signals to develop robust support tissues that are normally generated by its own weight on Earth, space-grown Arabidopsis shows evidence of cell wall remodeling in its transcriptomic, proteomic, and glycomic profiles (e.g. Johnson et al. 2017; Choi et al. 2019; Kruse et al. 2020; Nakashima et al. 2023). These changes likely reflect a shift away from depositing the strengthening polymers needed at 1×gravity, offering an insight into how manipulating mechanical signals might be used to tailor cell wall characteristics to enhance, for example, biomanufacturing or biofuel feedstocks.
In addition to these direct effects on the plant's biology, the microgravity environment profoundly alters the physical world that plants inhabit in spaceflight. Absent gravity's pervasive effects, liquid movements become dominated by forces such as surface tension and capillarity (Jones and Or 1999). Under these conditions, water tends to adhere to, and then creep over, hydrophilic surfaces such as the plant's root and shoot systems. This process can result in a flooding-like stress for the plants even at watering levels determined to be optimal for growth on Earth. This effect is compounded by the altered behavior of gasses in space, where density-related phenomena, such as buoyancy-driven convection, are absent (Lappa 2004). On Earth, convection leads to a constant mixing of the atmosphere around the plant, and its loss allows for the formation of gradients of gasses that would normally be rapidly dissipated. This physical effect means that steep, stable oxygen depletion zones can form around rapidly respiring tissues (Liao et al. 2004). Indeed, many transcriptomic studies of Arabidopsis growing in spaceflight have revealed an underlying molecular signature of hypoxic stress (Barker et al. 2023). Fusarium oxysporum was found as an opportunistic pathogen that eventually killed several Zinia plants grown on the International Space Station, with the cause of the infection thought to be related to suppression of the plants' defenses due to flooding and hypoxic stress (Schuerger et al. 2021). Crewed spacecraft are far from sterile environments, offering ample opportunities for such plant-microbe interactions. For example, the International Space Station has been continuously inhabited for 25 years and, not surprisingly, has a rich and diverse microbiome, including a wide range of plant pathogens (Urbaniak et al. 2022).
The effect of altered gravity is just one of many new challenges biology encounters once it moves into space. The Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere shields our biology from the high levels of ionizing radiation, such as from galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events, that are an inherent factor for life in space. Most of our knowledge on how plants respond to ionizing radiation comes from radioactively contaminated sites such as Chernobyl and Fukushima, and a few studies of simulated ionizing radiation with heavy ions (Mousseau and Møller 2020). This research suggests plants have a much higher radiation tolerance than animals. While the lethal dose of ionizing radiation for humans is only 5 Gy, plants can easily survive up to 50 Gy (Gudkov et al. 2019). Although the precise reasons behind this higher tolerance remain to be discovered, elements such as plasticity in development; innate regenerative capabilities; lack of mobile, proliferative cells; and the distributed, modular nature of plant structures and functions likely all contribute to this remarkable resilience. However, despite this radiation resistance, plants are not immune to radiation damage, with exposure to simulated galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events leading to developmental abnormalities and widespread changes in patterns of gene expression, particularly those related to DNA repair and glucosinolate metabolism (Zhang et al. 2022b; Dixit et al. 2023). Molecular mechanisms leading to these physiological changes have yet to be intensively explored, although it is notable that oxidative damage is one key effect of radiation exposure, likely accounting for 70% to 80% of radiation-related DNA damage (Gudkov et al. 2019). Of note in this context, induction of molecular markers of oxidative stress is one of the most commonly reported characteristics of plants growing in the spaceflight environment (e.g. Zupanska et al. 2013; Sugimoto et al. 2014; Kwon et al. 2015; Johnson et al. 2017; Choi et al. 2019; Barker et al. 2023). The upregulation of telomerase activity seen in Arabidopsis grown on orbit has even been proposed as a response to protect the genome from oxidative damage (Barcenilla et al. 2023). Researchers have attempted to leverage the unique combination of radiation exposure and other spaceflight-related stresses to develop new agricultural characteristics in plants grown in space. Indeed, Chinese scientists have reported using a combination of spacecraft and high-altitude balloons to develop ∼200 new crop varieties (Mohanta et al. 2021).
There are a host of other effects reported for Arabidopsis growing in spaceflight from the induction of novel alternative splicing (Beisel et al. 2019) to shifts in the patterns of cellulose synthase production (Olanrewaju et al. 2023). Determining the chain of cause and effect for these responses is a key element of current research. This knowledge is crucial to help develop potential biology- and engineering-based mitigation strategies that can be applied to the broad range of plants targeted for future space agriculture (Johnson et al. 2021). To give just one example, the extensive molecular dissection of spaceflight response spearheaded by work in Arabidopsis has led to the realization that spaceflight induces significantly increased levels of oxidative stress. This understanding has then helped target growing crops high in antioxidants, such as the Outredgeous cultivar of lettuce, microgreens, and even the cultivation of the carotenoid-rich duck weeds as strategies to help protect both the plant and the crew that consumes them.
With all these stresses and challenges impacting on organisms in space, you might easily conclude that plants could not survive in the spaceflight environment. However, Arabidopsis can complete a full lifecycle from seed to seed in space (reviewed in Medina et al. 2022), a finding that has paved the way for experiments with crop plants. Indeed, the crew of the International Space Station have grown and eaten their own lettuce and pepper harvests. Plants are remarkable in their developmental plasticity and physiological adaptability, and, rather than looking to science fiction writers for clues as to the green extraterrestrials we may meet in the future, simply looking to our gardens might provide a much better view.

## Flowering response to rising temperature
(Written by Malleshaiah SharathKumar and Salomé Prat)
The circadian clock synchronizes flowering with seasonal changes in day length and temperature, ensuring reproductive transitions occur at an optimal time of year. Seasonal and daily variations in both environmental cues drove the evolution of shared molecular mechanisms for sensing these input signals, as exemplified by the phytochromes. Red light absorption induces a conformational change of these light receptors leading to the active Pfr form, while elevated temperatures accelerate the reversal of nuclear phyB Pfr to its inactive Pr form, oppositely affecting light signaling (reviewed in Casal et al. 2024). As such, rising global temperature due to climate change significantly affects flowering timing in several plants, decreasing crop productivity and shrinking habitats for wild species.
Day length follows a predictable seasonal pattern, unlike temperature, which fluctuates with weather, cloud cover, and regional climate. Hence, the timing of flowering strongly relies on the day length (a process known as photoperiodism), categorizing plants as long-day plants (LDPs), short-day plants (SDPs), or day-neutral plants in which flowering is independent of day length (Gendron and Staiger 2023). Temperate crops are typically LDPs flowering in spring, when light hours exceed a critical threshold, ensuring seed production before autumn frosts. Near the equator, changes in day length are modest throughout the year, with seasons marked by dry summers and moist autumns. Tropical and subtropical crops are predominantly SDPs, initiating flowering when the day length is shorter than a critical threshold, close to 12 h. This response is suppressed when long nights are interrupted by a brief exposure to light (night-break [NB]), indicating that these plants primarily measure the length of the night.
As global temperatures rise, understanding how this cue affects flowering time is crucial for selecting crop cultivars that better cope with climate change. Warm temperatures induce early flowering in LDPs as an adaptive strategy to escape heat stress. Contrastingly, elevated temperatures suppress FLOWERING LOCUS-T (FT) expression and delay flowering in SDPs such as soybean or chrysanthemum (Nakano et al. 2013; Tang et al. 2023) while inhibiting tuber formation in potato (Hancock et al. 2014; Koch et al. 2024) via a yet poorly understood mechanism. Homologs of CONSTANS (CO) play a conserved role in activating FT gene expression in these short-day crops. However, additional negative regulators such as the soybean E1 B3 domain factor (Lin et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2024) or the chrysanthemum CsAFT and potato StSP5G anti-florigens play a central role in suppressing FT expression or its inducing activity in non-inductive long days (LDs; Higuchi et al. 2013; Kloosterman et al. 2013; Abelenda et al. 2016). This section summarizes the day-length pathways controlling FT gene expression in these SDPs, drawing on lessons learned from Arabidopsis. We also discuss how warm temperatures during the night hours distinctly affect FT expression, making these SDPs particularly susceptible to climate change.
Genetic studies in Arabidopsis were pivotal to identification of a central regulatory module driving flowering in LDs (Wang et al. 2024). The B-box CCT domain CO factor has a central role in this pathway by activating expression of the FT gene in leaves. In LD conditions, CO transcription is elevated in the afternoon, and the light stabilized CO protein goes on to activate FT expression (reviewed in Song et al. 2015; Takagi et al. 2023). In short days (SDs), CO is expressed at night and the protein is degraded by CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1), which prevents FT upregulation (Jang et al. 2008). GIGANTEA (GI) links the circadian clock and photoperiodic flowering by regulating FT expression through both CO-dependent and CO-independent mechanisms (Mizoguchi et al. 2005; Sawa et al. 2007). In the CO-dependent pathway, GI interacts with FLAVIN-BINDING, KELCH REPEAT, F-BOX 1 (FKF1) in blue light and destabilizes the CO repressor CYCLING DOF FACTOR 1 (CDF1), therefore promoting CO-mediated FT activation (Sawa et al. 2007; Takagi et al. 2023). FT is expressed in leaf phloem companion cells and the protein transported through sieve elements to the shoot apical meristem (SAM) (Corbesier et al. 2007). In the SAM, FT interacts with the bZIP transcription factor FLOWERING LOCUS D (FD) and 14-3-3 proteins to form a florigen activation complex (FAC) that triggers flowering transition (Abe et al. 2005; Taoka et al. 2011). Similar ternary complexes induce potato tuber formation and bud set in perennial trees (Parmentier-Line and Coleman 2016; Teo et al. 2017), showing that these FT complexes are highly conserved in angiosperms while regulating other developmental processes beyond flowering (Fig. 7).
Overview of flowering regulation in Arabidopsis (LD model plant) and its parallels in SD crops: soybean (Glycine max), Chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum seticuspe), and the tuberization pathway in potato (Solanum tuberosum). In Arabidopsis, central flowering regulators include the circadian clock and the interplay of florigens and antiflorigens to regulate flowering. Under LD photoperiods, the CO transcription factor activates FT expression. The FT protein moves from the leaves to the shoot apex, where it forms the Flowering Activation Complex (FAC) to initiate flowering. These regulators are conserved in SD crops, where they play critical roles in photoperiodic flowering, temperature-responsive flowering, and tuberization pathways. SD plants have evolved additional negative or antiflorigenic regulators (GmFT1a/4a in soybean, CsAFT in chrysanthemum, and StSP5G in potato) that suppress flowering and tuberization signals under LD or NB conditions or warm temperature.
In Arabidopsis, flowering time is accelerated by warm temperatures, and a similar response is observed when temperatures are elevated in the day or during nighttime. Mild temperatures promote a faster elongation of the young seedlings' hypocotyl and the upward positioning of leaves in a characteristic response known as thermomorphogenesis. This adaptive response is aimed at cooling the leaves and protecting the meristem from the warm soil and is found to in part rely on an effect on the circadian clock (Kerbler and Wigge 2023). The effects of warm temperature differ from those of heat stress that causes irreversible plant damage; these responses are governed by distinct signaling pathways (reviewed in Sato et al. 2024). The light receptor phytochromes act as major temperature sensors. Elevated temperatures promote the faster reversion of nuclear phyB Pfr into the non-active Pr form, causing inactivation of these photoreceptors and the nuclear accumulation of PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs) (Casal et al. 2024). FT transcript levels are elevated at 28 °C due to the removal of histone H2A.Z from the FT promoter, which enables PIF4-mediated activation of the FT gene (Kumar and Wigge 2010; Kumar et al. 2012). Other proteins were also revealed to act as temperature sensors, including the evening complex (EC) protein EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), which at warm temperatures undergoes phase separation through a prion-like domain (Jung et al. 2020). ELF3 forms condensates that disrupt EC function and alleviate PIF4 repression, which indirectly promotes activation of FT (Nusinow et al. 2011; Jung et al. 2020). While elevated temperatures accelerate flowering in Arabidopsis, a flowering delay is observed in oilseed rape (Brassica napus), underscoring that this response varies among Brassicaceae species. Analyses of H2A.Z occupancy revealed H2A.Z histone levels were not increased in the BnaFTA2 locus at 28 °C (Abelenda et al. 2023). Likewise, BnARP6-RNAi plants, silenced for the ACTIN-RELATED PROTEIN6 (ARP6), a component of the H2A.Z-incorporating SWR1 complex, showed little H2A.Z accumulation but still delayed flowering in response to elevated temperatures, indicating that BnaFTA2 is repressed via additional mechanism (Abelenda et al. 2023). Temperature indeed affects function of the circadian clock by inactivating the Arabidopsis evening complex (EC), hence suppressing EC feedback inhibition of genes like PRR9, PRR7, GI, and LUX, in addition to leading to de-repressed expression of PIF4 (Mizuno et al. 2014). Therefore, thermal flowering induction may result from temperature effects on the EC, GI, and other upstream FT regulators, which offers multiple possible strategies to improve climate resilience of this model plant as well as other LD crops.
Studies in Arabidopsis accentuate a central role of the GI-CO-FT module in flowering control. The main function of this central module is largely conserved in crops, including SDPs. Throughout natural evolution and domestication, several additional regulators contributed to modifying LD activation of this module into a SD response. These changes relied on LD activation of negative regulators or antiflorigenic homologs, SDs thus being required to release flowering inhibition by these repressors. Core clock regulators such as GI and the EC play also a central role in flowering time regulation in SDPs, but defects in the central clock genes lead in these plants to an opposite flowering phenotype compared with Arabidopsis (Lu et al. 2014; Bu et al. 2021; Andrade et al. 2022). Unlike Arabidopsis, flowering is inhibited in SDPs by a NB or short exposure to light in the middle of the night. Red light is the most effective for NB interruption, while its inhibitory effects are reversed by an additional pulse of FR-light, implicating phytochromes in this response. NB pulses as short as 10 min suppress flowering in most SDPs, while they do not affect entrainment of the clock (Ishikawa et al. 2005). Likewise, NB treatments suppress flowering only when given around 8 to 10 h after dusk, and an identical gating is observed in non–24-h-light/-dark cycles, indicating that this inhibition relies on a clock-regulated factor whose phase expression is set at dusk (Hayama et al. 2007). NB effects were found in chrysanthemum to activate the CsAFT anti-florigen at night (Higuchi et al. 2013), emphasizing that insights obtained in Arabidopsis are always not directly transferable to SD crops.
Significant progress has been made in understanding the rice, soybean, chrysanthemum, or potato day-length pathways. Rice is a facultative SD plant. The CO homolog Heading date 1 (Hd1) has a central role in SD flowering and activation of the FT homolog Hd3a. OsGI enhances OsHd1 expression (Hayama et al. 2003), while identification of the Ehd1 locus revealed that this pathway is more complex than in Arabidopsis. The OsGI-Hd1-Hd3a/RFT1 segment of the pathway has in SD a similar function as the Arabidopsis GI-CO-FT module, while OsHd1 acts in LDs as a repressor of OsEhd1 and OsHd3a/RFT1 on heterodimerization with OsGhd7 and OsDHT8 (reviewed by Vicentini et al. 2023). OsHd1 abundance is not affected by light (Yang et al. 2015), while diurnal sensing depends on OsGhd7 that is upregulated in LDs and the protein stabilized by the OsPHYA and OsPHYB phytochromes. These photoreceptors interfere with OsGI-mediated degradation of OsGhd7, linking photoperiod sensing with the circadian clock (Zheng et al. 2019).
In soybean, flowering is regulated by the GmPHYA (E3, E4)-E1-FT pathway. Key loci include E1–E4, J (GmELF3), and GmFT2a/FT5a, where E1-E4 delay flowering in LDs (Xia et al. 2012; Dong et al. 2023 ). The B3 domain E1 factor suppresses GmFT2a and GmFT5a expression, while GmPHYA2 and GmPHYA3 serve as the primary photoreceptors of the GmphyA-LUX-E1-FT module (Zhao et al. 2022; Lin et al. 2024). In potato, StSP6A functions as FT in Arabidopsis, regulating in this crop tuber formation. In LDs, StSP6A is suppressed by the anti-florigen StSP5G, whose expression is induced by StCOL1 in a phyB-dependent manner. Under SDs, StCDF1 represses StCOL1 and suppresses StSP5G, enabling StSP6A activation, whereas stabilizing alleles of StCDF1 were critical for potato adaptation to higher latitudes (Kloosterman et al. 2013 ; Abelenda et al. 2016). In chrysanthemum SD flowering is promoted by CsFTL3 (florigen), while the anti-florigen CsAFT inhibits flowering under LDs and in response to a NB. CsphyB senses the NB, while CsGI extends the photosensitive phase for NB responsive CsAFT induction and flowering inhibition (Higuchi et al. 2013; Oda et al. 2020). Hence, the roles of GI, CO-like factors, and florigen/anti-florigen pairs are conserved in soybean, chrysanthemum, and potato, while Ehd1 (rice), E1 (soybean), and StCDF1 (potato) were specifically recruited to a SD-dependent control of these pathways.
SD plants, such as soybean, chrysanthemum, and potato are significantly affected by elevated temperatures at night, which strongly suppress flowering and tuberization. Inhibition of flowering or tuberization correlates with suppressed expression of the GmFT2a/5a, CsFTL3, and StSP6A inducing signals. Interestingly, response of the FT florigen and antiflorigen genes is not identical to that observed on interruption of long nights by an NB, suggesting that warm temperatures impair activity of a different regulator in the pathway than the NB. Light and temperature input signals are both integrated by the circadian clock, and thus it is possible that high temperatures desynchronize the endogenous diel, leading to defective regulation of the FT florigen genes.
In rice, key loci such as Hd1, PRR37, Ghd8, and Hd6 had a critical role in spreading its growth habitat and adapting flowering time to new geographical latitudes (Takahashi et al. 2001; Guo et al. 2020b). OsHd1 acts as a stronger LD repressor at cooler temperatures, while sensitivity to temperature is reduced in hd1 mutants (Luan et al. 2009; Nagalla et al. 2021). Similarly, flowering suppression by OsGhd7 is enhanced under cooler conditions, while OsPRR37 was found to repress flowering at low temperatures but promote heading when temperature is over a critical temperature. Indeed, phyB enhances OsGhd7 transcription and stabilizes this repressor, thereby enhancing OsHd1-repressive function in LDs. This control is more relevant in cool conditions, whereas warmth-induced phyB inactivation decreases OsGhd7 abundance, reversing OsGhd7-OsHd1 repression of OsEhd1, OsHd3a, and OsRFT1. OsGhd7 transcript levels are also reduced in warm conditions in a phyB-independent manner, which leads to activation of OsRFT1 and flowering induction (Nagalla et al. 2021).
In soybean, a moderate increase in temperature (30 °C) promotes flowering, while flowering transition is delayed at 35 °C. GmFT2a and GmFT5a are upregulated at 30 °C independently of E1 in contrast to the flowering delay at 35 °C, where suppression of GmFT2a and GmFT5a is mediated through the phyA-E1 photoperiodic pathway (Tang et al. 2023). The molecular mechanisms underlying this control, however, remain obscure.
In chrysanthemum, elevated temperatures (∼30 °C) suppress flowering in SDs due to the inhibition of CsFTL3 in leaves (Nakano et al. 2013). Warm temperatures impair CsFTL3 expression and affect its downstream activation cascade. Heat sensitivity is higher during nighttime and particularly during the second half of the night (ZT 12–16), consistent with this response relying on an output of the circadian clock (Nakano et al. 2015, 2020). phyB was also established to sense NB treatments, while a circadian clock-dependent gating mechanism defines a photosensitive NB phase that coincides with midnight (Higuchi et al. 2013). Overexpression of GI extends the critical dark length for flowering and lengthens this photosensitive phase, hence suggesting that GI plays a pivotal role in gating CsAFT activation (Oda et al. 2020).
Temperature acts as a positive or negative signal for flowering, depending on the LD or SD photoperiodic behavior of plants. The day-length flowering regulators that integrate this environmental cue still remain unknown. Future research will thus be crucial to gain a molecular understanding of the mechanisms involved at perceiving warm temperatures at night. Revealing which actors mediate this poorly understood process will help to explain the opposing effects of temperature in flowering time regulation in LD and SD plants, while paving the way to the design of novel strategies addressed to improving climate change resilience of SD crops.
