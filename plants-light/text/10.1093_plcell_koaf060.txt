The UV-B photoreceptor UVR8 interacts with the LOX1 enzyme to promote stomatal closure through the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway.

# The UV-B photoreceptor UVR8 interacts with the LOX1 enzyme to promote stomatal closure through the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway

## Abstract
AbstractUltraviolet-B (UV-B) light-induced stomatal closure requires the photoreceptor UV RESISTANCE LOCUS 8 (UVR8) and nitric oxide (NO). However, the signaling pathways by which UV-B light regulates stomatal closure remain elusive. Here, we reveal that UVR8 signaling in the epidermis mediates stomatal closure in a tissue-specific manner in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana). UV-B light promotes PHOSPHOLIPASE 1 (PLIP1)/PLIP3-mediated linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid accumulation and induces LIPOXYGENASE 1 (LOX1) expression. LOX1, which catabolizes linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid to produce oxylipin derivatives, acts downstream of UVR8 and upstream of the salicylic acid (SA) pathway associated with stomatal defense. Photoactivated UVR8 interacts with LOX1 and enhances its activity. Protein crystallography demonstrates that A. thaliana LOX1 and its ortholog in soybean (Glycine max) share overall structural similarity and conserved residues in the oxygen cavity, substrate cavity, and metal-binding site that are required for 9-LOX activity. The disruption of UVR8–LOX1 contact sites near the LOX1 oxygen and substrate cavities prevents UVR8-enhanced LOX1 activity and compromises stomatal closure upon UV-B exposure. Overall, our study uncovers a noncanonical UV-B signaling module, consisting of the UVR8 photoreceptor and the cytoplasmic lipoxygenase, that mediates stomatal responses to UV-B light.

## Introduction
Plants have evolved multiple photoreceptors that monitor the quality, quantity, duration, and direction of ambient light to optimize their development and environmental responses throughout their life cycle (de Wit et al. 2016). Phytochromes (phys) are red/far-red light photoreceptors. Cryptochromes (CRYs), phototropins (PHOTs), and ZEITLUPE (ZTL) family members are ultraviolet-A (UV-A)/blue light photoreceptors. UV RESISTANCE LOCUS 8 (UVR8) is responsible for ultraviolet-B (UV-B) light (280 to 315 nm) perception and is evolutionarily conserved among plant species (Rizzini et al. 2011). Unlike the other types of photoreceptors, which rely on specific covalently attached chromophore molecules to sense specific light wavelengths, UVR8 perceives UV-B light via its own tryptophan residues (Christie et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012). UV-B light activates UVR8 by inducing a conformational switch from a homodimer to monomer (Rizzini et al. 2011; Christie et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012). Monomeric UVR8 then interacts directly with CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1) (Favory et al. 2009) and represses the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of COP1 toward its target proteins, such as ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), the central transcription factor of light signaling (Lau et al. 2019). COP1 positively regulates the nuclear accumulation of UVR8, resulting in the initiation of UV-B signal transduction for photomorphogenic and acclimatory UV-B responses (Qian et al. 2016; Yin et al. 2016; Fang et al. 2022). REPRESSOR OF UV-B PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS 1 (RUP1) and RUP2 provide negative feedback regulation in UV-B signaling pathway by mediating HY5 degradation and UVR8 inactivation (Gruber et al. 2010; Heijde and Ulm 2013; Heilmann and Jenkins 2013; Ren et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2023).
UVR8 is expressed ubiquitously in different tissues of Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) seedlings and plants (Rizzini et al. 2011), potentially allowing UV-B light signal transduction in roots and shoots to coordinate the development of the entire plant (Podolec et al. 2021). In underground plant tissues, UVR8 signaling inhibits lateral root growth by antagonizing auxin signaling (Yang et al. 2020b). During seedling emergence from the soil, UVR8 signaling mediates the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation to promote seedling photomorphogenesis (Favory et al. 2009). In the aboveground plant tissues, UVR8 signaling promotes cotyledon expansion (Qian et al. 2020) and stomatal closure to adjust to UV-B irradiation (Tossi et al. 2014). Importantly, UV-B light induces flavonoid accumulation in roots and shoots (Peer et al. 2001; Wan et al. 2018; Qian et al. 2020), a key metabolic response in UV-B acclimation and stress tolerance (Shi and Liu 2021). However, the metabolic pathway involved in UV-B-induced stomatal response awaits investigation.
Stomata are epidermal pores flanked by pairs of guard cells that are responsible for gas exchange and water balance in plants (Lawson and Matthews 2020). Moreover, stomata play a substantial role in plant defense as a potential entry point for pathogens (Melotto et al. 2008). To perform these functions, stomatal opening and closure are precisely controlled by environmental and internal signals, such as light and phytohormones (Arnaud and Hwang 2015; Yang et al. 2020a). Earlier studies have reported that UV radiation influences stomatal responses (Negash and Björn 1986; Eisinger et al. 2000). Indeed, low-level UV-B light stimulates stomatal closure in a UVR8- and nitric oxide (NO)-dependent manner (Tossi et al. 2014). UVR8 also mediates UV-B-induced sinapate accumulation, thereby promoting plant resistance against the fungal pathogen Botrytis cinerea (Demkura and Ballaré 2012). Appropriate stomatal closure is controlled by a balance among the levels of sinapate esters, NO, and malate (Li et al. 2023). However, the molecular mechanism by which UVR8 signaling regulates stomatal closure is less understood.
Lipoxygenases (LOXs) are nonheme iron-containing dioxygenases ubiquitously found in eukaryotic organisms. They catalyze the oxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to produce a great number of oxylipins (Wasternack and Feussner 2018). In plant cells, the biosynthesis of oxylipins occurs under various stress conditions that activate stress-related signaling pathways (Deboever et al. 2020). Plant LOXs are categorized as 9- and 13-LOXs according to the position of the oxygen incorporated into their substrates during LOX catalysis (Feussner and Wasternack 2002). The primary 13-LOX-derived products are jasmonates (JAs), a group of extensively studied oxylipins regulating plant growth, development, and stress tolerance (Wasternack and Hause 2013; Howe et al. 2018). LOX may be a point of entry for UV-B light signaling for the induction of biotic defense responses in JA-dependent and -independent pathways (Caputo et al. 2006; Demkura et al. 2010; Demkura and Ballaré 2012). For instance, the UVR8–TEOSINTE BRANCHED1, CYCLOIDEA and PCF 4 (TCP4)–LOX2 (UVR8–TCP4–LOX2) module regulates UV-B tolerance in a JA signaling pathway-dependent manner in Arabidopsis (Li et al. 2024). However, the interaction between UV-B signaling and the 9-LOX pathway is relatively less investigated. 9-LOX-derived products have been shown to function in the modulation of plant defenses (Vicente et al. 2012), lateral root development (Vellosillo et al. 2007), and stomatal closure (Montillet et al. 2013). In Arabidopsis, LOX1 is expressed in guard cells and encodes a 9-LOX that mediates an early pathogen response to trigger stomatal immunity (Montillet et al. 2013). However, it remains unclear whether the LOX1-dependent pathway functionally interacts with UV-B signaling in the regulation of stomatal responses.
In this study, we report that epidermal UVR8 signaling specifically mediates UV-B-induced stomatal closure in Arabidopsis. UV-B light induces LOX1 expression and promotes linoleic acid (18:2) and α-linolenic acid (18:3) accumulation primarily mediated by PHOSPHOLIPASE 1 (PLIP1)/PLIP3. LOX1 positively regulates UVR8-dependent stomatal closure through the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway and shares structural similarity with its soybean (Glycine max) ortholog. In response to UV-B irradiation, photoactivated UVR8 directly interacts with the C-terminus of LOX1, thereby enhancing LOX1 activity and promoting stomatal closure in a manner at least partially dependent on salicylic acid (SA) biosynthesis and signaling pathways.

## Results

## Epidermal UVR8 signaling is responsible for UV-B-induced stomatal closure
To study how the UV-B light signal regulates stomatal closure, we exposed the cotyledons of 10-d-old Arabidopsis seedlings grown under continuous white light (100 μmol m−2 s−1) to white light alone or supplemented with UV-B irradiation from 10 to 100 μW cm−2 in intensity for 3 h. We determined that stomatal closure is induced by low-level UV-B light (10 μW cm−2) and is enhanced with increasing UV-B light intensity, reaching a maximum closure at 50 μW cm−2. We did not observe this response in uvr8-6, a uvr8 null mutant allele with a T-DNA insertion, but the expression of a construct encoding UVR8 fused to YELLOW FLUORESCENT PROTEIN (YFP) driven by the native UVR8 promoter in uvr8-6 fully rescued this defect (Fig. 1, A and B). When we examined the stomata of mature true leaves from adult 3- to 4-wk-old plants, we observed a similar UV-B-induced and UVR8-dependent response for stomatal closure (Supplementary Fig. S1A). These results suggest that UV-B signaling initiated by the UV-B photoreceptor UVR8 is required for UV-B-induced stomatal closure. We chose UV-B light at an intensity of 50 μW cm−2, the level eliciting the maximum response, for subsequent stomatal analysis.

UVR8 is expressed throughout the plant (Rizzini et al. 2011). Although UV-B light promotes the nuclear accumulation of UVR8 in the cells of cotyledons and hypocotyls (Kaiserli and Jenkins 2007; Qian et al. 2016; Yin et al. 2016), exposure to 3-h UV-B irradiation did not apparently affect the nucleocytoplasmic distribution of YFP-UVR8 in the guard cells (Supplementary Fig. S1, B and C), indicating that the regulation of subcellular localization of UVR8 may occur in a tissue-specific manner. To investigate how UVR8 signaling mediates UV-B-induced stomatal closure, we generated transgenic plants expressing YFP-UVR8 in the epidermis, cortex, or phloem using the tissue-specific promoters from MERISTEM LAYER 1 (proML1) (Sessions et al. 1999), At1g09750 (proC1) (Lee et al. 2006; Preuten et al. 2013), or SUCROSE-PROTON SYMPORTER 2 (proSUC2) (Truernit and Sauer 1995), respectively, in the uvr8-6 background (Supplementary Fig. S1, D to F). In terms of UV-B-induced photomorphogenesis, uvr8-6 was fully rescued by UVR8 expressed in the epidermis and cortex, as evidenced by the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation under UV-B light, but only partially rescued by UVR8 expressed in the phloem (Fig. 1, C and D). By contrast, only UVR8 expressed in the epidermis fully rescued the defect of UV-B-induced stomatal closure typical of uvr8-6 (Fig. 1, E and F), demonstrating that epidermal UVR8 signaling is responsible for UV-B-induced stomatal closure.

## UV-B light promotes linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid accumulation and induces stomatal closure via LOX1
It is interesting that, in addition to flavonoid accumulation, a well-known UV-B-regulated metabolic process associated with stress tolerance, lipid accumulation is also affected by UV-B light in plants (Tsurumoto et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2023). However, how lipid accumulation contributes to stomatal response to UV-B light is unclear. To investigate the relationship between UV-B-regulated lipid metabolism and biological process, we performed liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to assess the consequences of a short UV-B irradiation on the cotyledon lipidome (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Quantification of total lipids showed that UV-B light does not affect the total amount of lipids following a 3-h irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Among the top 8 most abundant lipid subclasses (Supplementary Fig. S2C), the contents of free fatty acids (FFAs) rose upon UV-B treatment (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S2C), while the contents of other lipids were not affected or even decreased (Supplementary Fig. S2C; Supplementary Datasets S1 and S2). In particular, 2 types of PUFAs, linoleic acid (18:2) and linolenic acid (18:3), were more abundant than the other PUFAs, and their contents were elevated by a 3-h UV-B light treatment (Fig. 2A). We assessed the dynamic response of stomatal aperture during a time course of UV-B light exposure, as well as the changes in the contents of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Consistent with a previous study (Tossi et al. 2014), UV-B light induced stomatal closure within 1 h of irradiation, and this effect intensified with prolonged irradiation (Fig. 2B). At the same time, the levels of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid increased after 1 h of UV-B irradiation and peaked after 2 h before decreasing (Fig. 2, C and D).

To study the mechanism of UV-B-induced accumulation of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid, we first examined whether UV-B light regulated the expression of typical phospholipases that function upstream of LOX and cleave phospholipids in biological membranes to release PUFAs. PHOSPHOLIPASE1 (PLIP1), PLIP2, and PLIP3 are 3 key plastidial lipases negatively regulating stomatal aperture (Wang et al. 2017, 2018; Chang et al. 2023). We found that PLIP1 was induced, while PLIP2 and PLIP3 were repressed by UV-B light in a UVR8-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Then, we measured the stomatal aperture of plip1-2, plip2-2, and plip3 mutants (Alonso et al. 2003; Wang et al. 2017, 2018) and found that plip1-2 and plip3 but not plip2-2 failed to close their stomata in response to UV-B light (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Fig. S2E). GC-MS analyses showed that plip1-2 and plip3 but not plip2-2 failed to accumulate linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid after UV-B irradiation (Fig. 2, F and G). These results suggest that rather than PLIP2, PLIP1 and PLIP3 may play major roles in UV-B-induced stomatal closure and the accumulation of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid.
Furthermore, to identify putative regulatory factors involved in the UV-B-induced accumulation of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid, we performed a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using a list of previously identified UVR8-dependent UV-B-responsive genes (Favory et al. 2009), of which 278 genes were differentially expressed in response to 6 and 96 h of UV-B irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S3A; Supplementary Data Set 3). These genes were enriched in processes related to α-linolenic acid metabolism and linoleic acid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Specifically, ALLENE OXIDE CYCLASE 1 (AOC1), AOC2, and 3-KETO-ACYL-COENZYME A THIOLASE 5 (KAT5) were associated with α-linolenic acid metabolism, while LOX1 was the only gene associated with linoleic acid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S3C and Table S1). We asked whether these genes contributed to UV-B-induced stomatal closure by examining the stomatal response of their respective mutants. Like the wild-type Col-0, the aoc1, aoc2, and kat5 mutants showed normal UV-B-induced stomatal closure, whereas the lox1-1 mutant failed to close its stomata in response to UV-B light (Supplementary Fig. S3, D and E). These results suggest that LOX1-dependent PUFA metabolism may play a major role in UV-B-induced stomatal closure.
The Arabidopsis genome encodes 6 LOXs (LOX1 to 6) (Feussner and Wasternack 2002). Among these, LOX1 and LOX5 have 9-LOX activity, while the remaining 4 have 13-LOX activity (Bannenberg et al. 2009). To explore a possible correlation between UV-B light signaling and LOXs, we checked whether UV-B light regulates the expression of LOX genes. LOX1 was the main UV-B-inducible member of the LOX family, and its expression was strongly induced by UV-B light in cotyledons and roots (Supplementary Fig. S4A). LOX1 expression rose within 1 h of UV-B irradiation, peaked at 3 h, and declined in cotyledons (Fig. 3A). To study whether the LOX1 expression is dependent on UV-B light signaling, we examined the effects of key regulators of UV-B signaling pathway including UVR8, COP1, HY5, and RUP1/RUP2 on LOX1 expression using their reported mutants. LOX1 transcript levels were lower in these mutants than in Col-0 under white light conditions. UV-B treatment still induced LOX1 expression in these mutants, to a lesser extent in uvr8-6 and cop1-4 whereas to a greater extent in hy5-215 and rup1-1 rup2-1 (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that the LOX1 expression is largely dependent on UVR8 signaling. We also examined the tissue-specific expression pattern of LOX1 by generating transgenic plants expressing the firefly LUCIFERASE (LUC) reporter gene driven by the native LOX1 promoter. In agreement with our RT-qPCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4A), UV-B treatment resulted in stronger LUC luminescence in the cotyledons and roots of 10-d-old seedlings (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Fig. S4B). At the protein level, UV-B light did not alter LOX1 abundance, as determined by immunoblot analysis using polyclonal antibodies raised against full-length recombinant His-tagged LOX1 (His-LOX1) (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, we found lower LOX1 protein levels in uvr8-6 than those in Col-0 under white light alone and white light supplemented with 3-h UV-B treatment (Fig. 3E). This result suggests that UVR8 positively regulates LOX1 protein abundance in a UV-B independent manner.

Considering the possible functional similarity among LOXs in UV-B-induced stomatal closure, we examined the stomatal phenotypes of several lox single mutants, which revealed normal stomatal closure upon UV-B exposure, except with lox1-1 (Supplementary Fig. S4, C and D). However, unlike the uvr8-6 mutant showing defects in UV-B-induced stomatal closure and inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, lox1-1 seedlings developed normal hypocotyls like Col-0 under UV-B light (Supplementary Fig. S5, A to C). The transcriptional levels of several marker genes in UV-B-induced photomorphogenesis, such as EARLY LIGHT-INDUCIBLE PROTEIN 1 (ELIP1), ELIP2, CHALCONE SYNTHASE (CHS), and UDP-GLYCOSYLTRANSFERASE 84A1 (UGT84A1), were comparable between Col-0 and lox1-1 (Supplementary Fig. S5D). Collectively, these results support the notion that LOX1 plays a predominant role specifically in UV-B-induced stomatal closure.
To confirm the function of LOX1, we generated transgenic plants overexpressing LOX1-YFP under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter in the lox1-1 background (Supplementary Fig. S5E). The defect of stomatal response to UV-B light seen in lox1-1 was completely rescued by the constitutive expression of LOX1-YFP (Fig. 3F). UV-B treatment did not affect the cytoplasmic localization of LOX1-YFP in guard cells (Supplementary Fig. S5F). We then assessed the genetic interaction between UVR8 and LOX1 by generating the uvr8-6 lox1-1 double mutant by crossing the 2 single mutants. We determined that uvr8-6 lox1-1 seedlings phenocopy uvr8-6 and lox1-1, displaying no stomatal closure in response to UV-B light; in addition, expressing UVR8 in the lox1-1 background (lox1-1 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8) or overexpressing LOX1 in the uvr8-6 background (uvr8-6 LOX1-GFP) showed the same lack of stomatal closure in response to UV-B light, like the lox1-1 and uvr8-6 mutants (Fig. 3G). These results suggest that in UV-B-induced stomatal closure, LOX1 function is dependent on UVR8-initiated UV-B signaling, and UVR8 functions upstream of and requires LOX1.

## The LOX-derived oxylipin pathway positively regulates UV-B-induced stomatal closure
To determine whether UVR8 and LOX1 regulate UV-B-induced stomatal closure through the oxylipin pathway, we first measured the levels of the LOX substrates, linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid, among Col-0, uvr8-6, and lox1-1 seedlings grown under continuous white light alone (−UV-B) or supplemented with UV-B light (+UV-B) for 3 h. We observed that the UV-B treatment significantly increased the levels of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid in Col-0, whereas the increase was abolished in uvr8-6 and lox1-1 mutants (Fig. 4, A and B; Supplementary Fig. S6, A and B). Then, we treated the cotyledons of Col-0, uvr8-6, and lox1-1 10-d-old seedlings with either PUFA alone or in combination with UV-B light. We observed that 100 nm linoleic acid without UV-B light treatment effectively induced the closure of Col-0 stomata to the same extent as UV-B light. The concomitant treatment of seedlings with UV-B light and linoleic acid did not further promote stomatal closure (Fig. 4C). We obtained similar results when cotyledons were treated with α-linolenic acid alone or together with UV-B light (Fig. 4D), suggesting that the UV-B light and these PUFAs mediate stomatal closure through the same pathway. Importantly, the uvr8-6 and lox1-1 mutants showed no stomatal closure when treated with UV-B and/or linoleic acid (Fig. 4C) and/or α-linolenic acid (Fig. 4D). This defect that was completely rescued in the uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8 and lox1-1 LOX1-YFP transgenic lines (Fig. 4C). These data suggest that UV-B-induced stomatal closure requires linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid and probably their metabolism in a UVR8- and LOX1-dependent manner.

It has been identified that 9-keto-10(E),12(Z),15(Z)-octadecatrienoic acid (9-KOTE) and 9(S)-hydroxy-10(E),12(Z),15(Z)-octadecatrienoic acid (9-HOTE) are 2 9-LOX-generated oxylipins (Vicente et al. 2012; Montillet et al. 2013). To validate the effect of UV-B light on PUFA metabolism, we collected epidermal strips of mature leaves for oxylipin quantification using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometer (LC-MS) analysis. We found that accompanied with the accumulation of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid (Supplementary Fig. S6, C and D), the levels of 9-KOTE and 9-HOTE increased during UV-B irradiation (Fig. 4, E and F). However, the increase of these oxylipins was abolished in uvr8-6 and lox1-1 mutants (Fig. 4, E and F). These results suggest that UV-B light promotes the production of oxylipins from linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid in a UVR8- and LOX1-dependent manner. Further, we treated the seedlings grown under continuous white light with 9-KOTE to test the effect of this PUFA metabolite on the stomatal aperture. We observed that 100 nm 9-KOTE effectively induced the closure of Col-0 stomata to the same extent as UV-B treatment, with no additive effect from the combination of 9-KOTE and UV-B treatment. Notably, the application of 9-KOTE rescued the stomatal closure defects of uvr8-6 and lox1-1 regardless of UV-B treatment (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that LOX-derived oxylipin is a potential signal conveying stomatal closure under UV-B light. Therefore, we examined the stomatal response of 9-LOX signaling mutants, the oxylipin-insensitive mutants nonresponding to oxylipins 2 (noxy2) and noxy22 (renamed as ethylene-overproducer 1, eto1) (Gingerich et al. 2005; López et al. 2011; Vellosillo et al. 2013). None of these 3 mutants (noxy2-2, eto1-11, eto1-12) closed their stomata in response to UV-B light (Supplementary Fig. S6, E and F). When treated with linoleic acid alone or together with UV-B light, these mutants did not undergo stomatal closure when treated with UV-B and/or linoleic acid (Fig. 4H). These results suggest that in addition to PUFA accumulation and LOX1 activity, the LOX-derived oxylipin signaling pathway is also required for UV-B-induced stomatal closure.

## Photoactivated UVR8 directly interacts with LOX1
Since UVR8 and LOX1 both regulate UV-B-induced stomatal closure (Fig. 3F), we speculated that UVR8 and LOX1 might function together via protein–protein interaction. We investigated their subcellular localization by expressing constructs encoding fluorescent proteins fused to UVR8 or LOX1 in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium (Agrobacterium tumefaciens)-mediated infiltration and in stable transgenic Arabidopsis lines. In N. benthamiana leaves, CFP-UVR8 (a fusion of cyan fluorescent protein to UVR8) localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus, primarily colocalizing with LOX1-YFP in the cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. S7A). In the stomata of transgenic Arabidopsis LOX1-GFP transgenic lines, endogenous UVR8, detected by immunofluorescence microscopy using specific polyclonal antibodies, colocalized with LOX1 in the cytoplasm, and more so following UV-B light treatment (Supplementary Fig. S7B).
We also tested the direct interaction of UVR8 and LOX1 in an in vitro glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay. The recombinant LOX1 was pulled down by GST-UVR8 but not by GST (Fig. 5A). To verify the UVR8–LOX1 association in planta, we performed luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays in N. benthamiana leaves and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in Arabidopsis transgenic lines. In LCI assays, UVR8 and LOX1 were respectively fused to split N-terminal and C-terminal luciferase (nLUC, cLUC). We detected an interaction between UVR8 and LOX1 from the reconstituted luciferase activity when UVR8-nLUC and cLUC-LOX1 were co-expressed, and the interaction was enhanced by UV-B light (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Fig. S7C). In another set of LCI assays, LOX1 preferentially interacted with UVR8R338A, the constitutively monomeric variant of UVR8 (Wu et al. 2012; Huang et al. 2014), rather than with UVR8W285F, the constitutively dimeric variant of UVR8 (Heijde et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2014) (Supplementary Fig. S7, D and E). In Co-IP assays, endogenous LOX1 was co-immunoprecipitated with FLAG-UVR8 from FLAG-UVR8 transgenic seedlings specifically following UV-B light treatment (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that LOX1 interacts with UVR8 in a UV-B-enhanced manner.

To define the specific domains of LOX1 that are critical for UVR8 interaction, we used its N-terminus, containing the PLAT domain (LOX1PLAT, residues 1 to 186), and the C-terminus, containing the C1–C4 domains (LOX1C1–C4, residues 187 to 859), to test their interactions with UVR8R338A. LOX1C1–C4 showed a much stronger interaction with UVR8R338A than LOX1PLAT (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Fig. S8A). To identify the domain(s) in the LOX1 C-terminus responsible for its interaction with the UVR8 monomer, we divided the LOX1 C-terminus into 4 subdomains (LOX1-C1 to LOX1-C4); LCI assays established that UVR8R338A preferentially interacts with LOX1-C1 (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Fig. S8B). In a similar search for the domain(s) of UVR8 critical for its interaction with LOX1, we determined that the C-terminus of UVR8R338A (UVR8R338A 262–440 aa, residues 262 to 440) displays a stronger interaction with LOX1 than the C-terminus (UVR8262–440 aa, residues 262 to 440) or N-terminus of UVR8 (UVR81–261 aa, residues 1 to 261) (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S8C). The C-terminus of UVR8 has been reported to be necessary for UVR8 function in UV-B signaling (Cloix et al. 2012; Yin et al. 2015). Therefore, we examined whether UVR8C60 (residues 381 to 440) contributed to the interaction with LOX1. Indeed, the interaction of UVR8C60 with LOX1 was stronger than that of UVR8ΔC60 or UVR8R338AΔC60 lacking these 60 residues (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S8D). Taken together, the C1 domain of LOX1 and the C60 domain UVR8 compose the contact interface to mediate their direct interaction.

## Critical residues in LOX1 mediate 9-LOX activity and its interaction with UVR8
To reveal the molecular basis of how the UVR8–LOX1 complex functions, we initially aimed to determine the structure of the UVR8–LOX1 complex. Unfortunately, we were not able to obtain a high-concentration and stable protein complex for crystallization. Since the UVR8 structure has been well studied (Christie et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2012), we sought to solve the fine structure of LOX1 first and then simulate complex structure via molecular docking. We purified recombinant LOX1 from Escherichia coli, produced crystals, and analyzed the crystals by X-ray diffraction to obtain structural data (Fig. 6A). The crystal structure of LOX1 (residues 16 to 859) was determined at a resolution of 3.26 Å (Supplementary Table S2); the N-terminal 15 residues could not be resolved, possibly because of flexibility in this region. Overall, the LOX1 molecule contained an N-terminal region; an N-terminal β-barrel, known as a PLAT (Polycystin-1, Lipoxygenase, Alpha-Toxin) domain (residues 21 to 161); and a much larger α-helical lipoxygenase domain that houses the catalytic iron atom (residues 164 to 859) (Fig. 6B). We compared the protein sequence and structure of Arabidopsis LOX1 (AtLOX1) with that previously determined for soybean (Glycine max) LOX1 (GmLOX1; Minor et al. 1996). These 2 proteins showed high pairwise sequence identity (53.9%) and overall structural similarity (root-mean-square deviation [RMSD], 0.715) (Supplementary Fig. S9, A and B). GmLOX1 contains 5 predicted functional domains (GmLOX1D1 to GmLOX1D5) (Minor et al. 1996). We divided AtLOX1 into 5 corresponding domains (AtLOX1D1 to AtLOX1D5), performed a comparative analysis, and noticed that the corresponding domains of AtLOX1 and GmLOX1 have highly conserved structures (Fig. 6C). However, a detailed superposition to calculate the RMSD of these domains indicated the potential functional diversification of domain D3 (Supplementary Fig. S9C). Furthermore, protein sequence alignment of GmLOX1 and AtLOX1 showed that the critical residues in the oxygen cavity (R203 in GmLOX1, R216 in AtLOX1), substrate cavity (L541 and L546 in GmLOX1, L561 and L566 in AtLOX1), and metal-binding site (H499, H504, H690, N694, and I839 in GmLOX1; H519, H524, H711, N715, and I859 in AtLOX1) are all conserved (Minor et al. 1996; Knapp et al. 2001; Ivanov et al. 2010) (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Structural analysis revealed that the above putative functional sites are mainly located around the iron atom (Fig. 6D).

To test whether these residues are indeed required for LOX1 activity, we introduced point mutations and assayed the activity of the resulting recombinant proteins in vitro. Accordingly, we prepared 2 mutant LOX1 proteins: LOX1L561F, L566F (LOX1LF) with a defective substrate cavity and LOX1H519Q, H524Q, H711Q (LOX1HQ) with a disrupted metal-binding site (Fig. 6, E and F). Either set of mutations resulted in a complete loss of LOX1 enzyme activity (Fig. 6G). These results demonstrate that these critical residues are conserved and necessary for LOX1 activity in Arabidopsis.
Based on UVR8 and LOX1 structures and the specific domains responsible for the UVR8–LOX1 interaction, we obtained a docked structure of the UVR8–LOX1 complex using ZDOCK SERVER (Pierce et al. 2014). We identified several predicted contact sites on the surface of LOX1: K239, E240, P242, Q243, Y204, G221, T222, and Q223 (Fig. 7A). When residues K239 and E240 were replaced with A (LOX1KEA), the resulting variant protein lost its ability to interact with UVR8R338A (Fig. 7B; Supplementary Fig. S10A). We asked whether the conserved functional sites of LOX1 contributed to the UVR8–LOX1 interaction by mutating R216 in the oxygen cavity to F (LOX1RF) and tested this LOX1 variant and 2 other variants described above (LOX1LF and LOX1HQ) for their interaction with UVR8R338A. Disrupting either the oxygen cavity (LOX1RF) or the substrate cavity (LOX1LF) abolished the interaction of LOX1 with UVR8R338A (Fig. 7, C and D; Supplementary Fig. S10, B and C), while mutations in the metal-binding site (LOX1HQ) did not (Fig. 7E; Supplementary Fig. S10D). These results indicate that the interaction interface between LOX1 and UVR8 is likely near the functional sites of LOX1 and particularly close to its oxygen cavity.

## UV-B-induced stomatal closure depends on UVR8-enhanced LOX1 activity
To investigate how its interaction with UVR8 affects LOX1 activity, we assayed LOX1 activity in vitro and determined that LOX1 activity increases in the presence of GST-UVR8R338A but not GST alone. However, LOX1KEA, which is impaired in its UVR8 interaction (Fig. 7B), displayed LOX1 activity similar to that of intact LOX1, but its activity did not increase when incubated with GST-UVR8R338A (Fig. 8A; Supplementary Fig. S11A), suggesting that the UVR8–LOX1 interaction is responsible for the enhancement of LOX1 activity. The activity of LOX1LF and LOX1HQ was compromised even when GST-UVR8R338A was added (Fig. 8A; Supplementary Fig. S11A), suggesting that a basal activity of LOX1 is required for the promotive effect of UVR8. To explore whether photoactivated UVR8-enhanced LOX1 activity in planta, we transiently expressed constructs encoding UVR8 and LOX1 variants fused to LUC fragments in N. benthamiana leaves for enzyme assays. Consistent with the in vitro results, UVR8R338A increased the activity of LOX1 but not that of LOX1KEA (Fig. 8B), while LOX1RF, LOX1LF, and LOX1HQ all had impaired LOX1 activity regardless of the presence of UVR8 (Fig. 8, C to E). To extend these results to Arabidopsis, we used transgenic Arabidopsis lines expressing UVR8R338A or UVR8W285F under the UVR8 promoter in the uvr8-6 background (Huang et al. 2013). The uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8R338A seedlings, but not those of uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8W285F, showed normal stomatal closure in response to UV-B light (Fig. 8F). In agreement with this finding, UV-B light induced LOX activity in Col-0, uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8, and uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8R338A seedlings, but not in uvr8-6, lox1-1, or uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8W285F seedlings (Fig. 8G). Compared with Col-0 and uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8, the stomatal closure and LOX activity of uvr8-6 proUVR8:YFP-UVR8R338A seedlings were both higher in the absence of UV-B light (Fig. 8, F and G). Taken together, these results demonstrate that photoactivated UVR8 enhances LOX1 activity through their direct interaction.

Further, to analyze how the UVR8–LOX1 association regulates stomatal response to UV-B light, we introduced the constructs encoding LOX1 variants (LOX1KEA, LOX1RF, LOX1LF, and LOX1HQ) fused to YFP into lox1-1 under the control of the 35S promoter (Supplementary Fig. S11B). The stomata of lox1-1 LOX1-YFP seedlings closed more quickly upon UV-B irradiation than those not exposed to UV-B irradiation (Fig. 9C). Without UV-B irradiation, the stomatal aperture of lox1-1 LOX1-YFP seedlings gradually decreased with increasing concentration of linoleic acid. With UV-B irradiation, stomatal closure was complete even without linoleic acid (Fig. 9E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the optimal stomatal response to UV-B light requires the basal activity of LOX1 and its activity modulation mediated by UVR8 interaction.

## The SA pathway is required for LOX1 function in UV-B-induced stomatal closure
It has been reported that stomatal defense is under the control of the defense signal molecule SA (Melotto et al. 2006), and that SA acts downstream of LOX-derived oxylipins in stomatal immunity (Montillet et al. 2013). This information inspired us to explore the role of SA in the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway in UV-B-induced stomatal closure. We analyzed the effect of UV-B light on endogenous SA level, but observed no significant effect upon a 3-h UV-B treatment in Col-0 or uvr8-6 (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Then, we analyzed whether UV-B light affected SA-related gene expression, based on the published UV-B and SA-responsive transcriptomes (Favory et al. 2009; Ding et al. 2018). 21.2% (59 out of 278) of UVR8-dependent UV-B-responsive genes are also responsive to SA (Supplementary Data Set 4), suggesting that there is a molecular connection between UV-B and SA signaling pathways. To test the involvement of SA pathway, we examined the stomatal response of Col-0, uvr8-6 and lox1-1 treated with SA alone or together with UV-B light. 100 μm SA effectively induced stomatal closure in Col-0 to the same extent as UV-B treatment, with no additive effect from the combination of SA and UV-B treatment. Notably, the application of SA rescued the stomatal closure defects of uvr8-6 and lox1-1 regardless of UV-B treatment (Fig. 10, A and B). In addition, we noticed decreased stomatal closure in 2 different mutant alleles of ISOCHORISMATE SYNTHASE 1 (ICS1, also reported as SALICYLIC-ACID-INDUCTION DEFICIENT 2 [SID2]) with lower SA levels (Devireddy et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022a), even after treatment with UV-B light or 9-KOTE alone or in combination (Fig. 10, C and D; Supplementary Fig. S12D). Among the mutants of SA receptor genes NON-EXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES1 (NPR1), NPR3, and NPR4 (Ding et al. 2018), npr1-0 (Ding et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2020) and npr3 but not npr4-2 (Liu et al. 2005) showed no stomatal closure in response to treatment with UV-B light or 9-KOTE alone or in combination (Fig. 10, E and F; Supplementary Fig. S12D). These results support the idea that SA biosynthesis and signaling pathways act downstream of the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway in UV-B-induced stomatal closure.

Since JAs is another group of oxylipins regulating plant growth and stress tolerance (Wasternack and Hause 2013; Howe et al. 2018), we also investigated whether the 13-LOX-derived JA pathway is involved in UV-B-induced stomatal closure, by testing the JA receptor mutant coronatine insensitive 1-2 (coi1-2) (Zhang et al. 2019; Han et al. 2020) and the 2 JA signaling mutants myc2 (Shen et al. 2016) and jasmonate-resistant 1 (jar1) (Wang et al. 2011). These mutants closed their stomata in response to UV-B light, though jar1 appeared to be slightly less responsive to UV-B light (Supplementary Fig. S12, B to D). Together with the normal stomatal closure phenotypes observed in the lox3-2, lox4-1, and lox6 mutants with decreased JA levels (Caldelari et al. 2011; Chauvin et al. 2013) (Supplementary Fig. S4, C and D), these results indicate that JA signaling and 13-LOX-mediated JA biosynthesis might not be critically required for stomatal closure in the early response to UV-B light.
It has been established that UV-B light induces stomatal closure in a NO-dependent manner (Tossi et al. 2014). To test whether LOX1 is also involved in this process, we examined NO generation in stomata treated with UV-B or linoleic acid using the fluorescent probe 4,5-diaminofluorescein diacetate (DAF-FM-DA). UV-B light induced NO accumulation in Col-0 stomata but not in those of uvr8-6, in agreement with previous studies (Tossi et al. 2014; Li et al. 2023). In addition, UV-B light did not induce NO accumulation in lox1-1 stomata. The application of linoleic acid induced NO accumulation in Col-0 stomata, but not in uvr8-6 or lox1-1 stomata (Supplementary Fig. S12, E and F). These data suggest that LOX1 contributes to UV-B-induced NO accumulation in the stomata of cotyledons.

## Discussion
Plants employ various photoreceptors to detect the changes in light quality, intensity, and direction that affect stomatal dynamics (Matthews et al. 2017). PHOTs and CRYs function synergistically to regulate stomatal opening under blue light (Kinoshita et al. 2001; Mao et al. 2005), while phyB mediates stomatal opening under red light (Wang et al. 2010). However, exaggerated stomatal opening may lead to excessive water loss and even death. Downstream of PHOTs, 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, an oxylipin metabolite of the 13-LOX pathway, negatively regulates stomatal opening to prevent water loss under white light that decreases the abundance of linolenic acids (Chang et al. 2023). UVR8 regulates stomatal closure in a NO-dependent manner (Tossi et al. 2014). Here, we highlight an alternative role for UVR8 in stomatal closure by elevating PUFA levels, interacting with LOX1 and modulating its lipoxygenase activity under UV-B light (Fig. 11). Overall, downstream of these photoreceptors, multiple light signaling pathways interact and are connected via light energy conversion, membrane ion transport, and regulation of metabolic activity (Shimazaki et al. 2007; Yang et al. 2020a), to control the dynamics of stomatal movements and modulate the tradeoff between growth and defense in natural light environments.
A proposed working model for the UVR8–LOX1 module promoting UV-B-induced stomatal closure. UV-B light promotes the accumulation of PUFAs, linoleic acid (18:2) and α-linolenic acid (18:3) primarily mediated by PLIP1/PLIP3, and induces the expression of LOX1, whose encoding enzyme catabolizes them for oxylipin production. UVR8 is photoactivated and monomerized to interact with LOX1, which in turn activates the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway through SA biosynthesis and signaling pathway and ultimately promotes stomatal closure. Mutating the UVR8-interacting sites of LOX1 (mutation of K239 and E240 in UVR8–LOX1 interacting sites to A, LOX1KEA) retains LOX1 activity but abolishes the interaction with UVR8, which attenuates the activation of the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway and UV-B-induced stomatal closure. Disruption of the metal-binding site (mutation of H519, H524, and H711 in metal-binding sites to Q, LOX1HQ) of LOX1 impairs LOX1 activity but retains its interaction with UVR8. The disruption of the oxygen cavity (mutation of R216 in the oxygen cavity to F, LOX1RF) or substrate cavity (mutation of L561 and L566 in the substrate cavity to F, LOX1LF) of LOX1 impairs LOX1 activity and abolishes UVR8 interaction, leading to no stomatal response to UV-B exposure.
PUFA metabolism is a complex biological process subjected to multiple layers of regulation (Bazinet and Layé 2014; Dyall et al. 2022; Manson et al. 2023). Solar UV-B irradiation promotes the expression of various genes involved in fatty acid and oxylipin metabolism in longflower tobacco (Nicotiana longiflora) (Izaguirre et al. 2003). Here, we reveal that UV-B light leads to a mixture of upregulation and downregulation of PUFA metabolism-related genes in Arabidopsis thaliana (Supplementary Fig. S2), similarly with the findings in Chrysanthemum morifolium Ramat (Yang et al. 2018a). The UV-B-responsive elevation of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid relies at least on UVR8, PLIP1, and PLIP3, providing increased substrate availability for enhanced 9-LOX activity of LOX1 to mediate stomatal closure (Fig. 2). These PUFAs and the oxylipins derived from them potentially act as a trigger to mediate the interaction of UVR8–LOX1 and downstream signaling events. The positive regulation of PUFA metabolism by UVR8 and LOX1 outlines the molecular framework of UVR8 signaling in UV-B-induced stomatal closure. It is interesting that blue light perceived by phototropins induces the expression of PLIP genes, and PLIP-initiated oxylipin catabolism negatively regulates stomatal opening (Chang et al. 2023). It is possible that PLIPs may act as integrators of blue and UV-B light signaling pathways to optimize stomatal response. Considering PLIP1 and PLIP3 exhibited opposite UV-B-responsive expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. S2), the exact regulatory mechanism might be clarified by examining their endogenous protein abundance upon UV-B treatment, and the stomatal response in double and triple mutants of this gene family.
UVR8 signaling appears to primarily occur in outer cell layers, in agreement with the limited penetration depth of UV-B light through plant tissues and the high expression of UVR8 in the epidermis (Depaepe et al. 2023). At the seedling stage, UVR8 expression in the epidermis plays a major role in hypocotyl growth and cotyledon expansion (Bernula et al. 2017; Vanhaelewyn et al. 2019a). In adult plants, UVR8 expression in mesophyll cells is important for UV-B acclimation and stress tolerance (Bernula et al. 2017). In addition, UV-B-induced inflorescence phototropism is mediated mainly by UVR8 expressed in the epidermis and cortex, although its expression in deeper tissues such as endodermis also contributes (Vanhaelewyn et al. 2019b). Here, we demonstrate that UV-B-induced stomatal closure is a tissue-specific regulatory response mediated by epidermal UVR8 signaling (Fig. 1). However, it remains unknown how the UV-B signal is transmitted from superficial to deeper tissues of a plant or whether UVR8 exhibits an activation gradient across tissues to initiate these physiological responses. Transcriptome, proteome, or metabolome analysis at a single-cell scale will elucidate the spatiotemporal regulation of UVR8 signaling in plant development and stress responses.
At the subcellular level, UVR8 in the nucleus interacts with various transcription factors to alter their activity in transcriptional regulation. Under UV-B light, UVR8 accumulates in the nucleus and orchestrates the physiological responses of roots and shoots by modulating the DNA-binding activity of specific transcription factors, including MYB DOMAIN PROTEIN 13 (MYB13), MYB73, MYB77, BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1), BES1-INTERACTING MYC-LIKE 1 (BIM1), WRKY DNA-BINDING PROTEIN 36 (WRKY36), and TCP4 (Liang et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2018b; Qian et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2020b; Li et al. 2024). However, UVR8 proteins appear to be abundant in the cytoplasm in Arabidopsis under UV-B light (Qian et al. 2016; Yin et al. 2016), with unknown regulatory roles to be identified. As a cytoplasmic UVR8 signaling module, the formation of the UVR8–LOX1 complex allows the direct involvement of the UV-B photoreceptor in PUFA accumulation and metabolism regulation (Figs. 4 and 5). This function is distinct from the role of nuclear UVR8 in gene expression regulation, but to achieve functional specialization of subcellular UVR8 signaling outside the nucleus.
AtLOX1 shares high similarity in overall protein structure with its soybean ortholog and exhibited 9-LOX activity when the conserved residues in the oxygen cavity, substrate cavity, and metal-binding site were intact (Fig. 6). However, the variation in the specific domain D3 suggests functional diversification between GmLOX1 and AtLOX1 (Supplementary Fig. S9), which should be extensively studied. Upon UV-B irradiation, UVR8 is photoactivated, which exposes its C-terminus for direct interaction with LOX1, primarily via the lipoxygenase domain of LOX1, forming an interface near the oxygen cavity of LOX1 (Figs. 5 to 7). Therefore, the UVR8–LOX1 interaction probably leads to an enlarged pocket for oxygen capture in LOX1, boosting its 9-LOX activity and optimizing stomatal closure. In agreement with this notion, the disruption of the oxygen cavity in LOX1 not only abolished its 9-LOX activity but also disrupted its interaction with UVR8 (Figs. 7 and 8). It is interesting that LOX1 expression was induced by UV-B light, while its protein levels remained relatively stable (Fig. 3), raising the possibility of multiple layers of regulation. At the transcriptional level, LOX1 is induced by UV-B light, in a manner that is partially dependent on UVR8. At the posttranscriptional level, LOX1 abundance is positively regulated by UVR8, in a manner that is independent of UV-B light. At the posttranslational level, LOX1 activity is modulated via its direction interaction with UVR8. The recently released cryo-electron microscopy structure of the UVR8–COP1WD40 and UVR8W285A–RUP2 complexes showed that 2 interfaces in UVR8 are crucial for its interaction with COP1 or RUP2: the C27 subregion containing the VP motif and the core domain of UVR8 (Wang et al. 2022b). Further determination of the fine structure of the UVR8–LOX1 complex will illuminate how UVR8 exactly acts in the regulation of LOX1 stability and activity.
LOX1 mediates UV-B-induced stomatal closure upstream of the SA pathway and participates in NO generation (Fig. 10), suggesting that the UVR8–LOX1 module is a molecular link between UV-B light signaling and defense signaling. 9-KOTE positively regulates the transcriptional response to SA and bacterial infection, and shows protective activity against Pst DC3000 infection, suggesting that 9-KOTE triggers SA-responsive gene expression for SA signaling (Vicente et al. 2012). Furthermore, 9-KOTE induced a set of typical UV-B-responsive genes, including EARLY LIGHT-INDUCIBLE PROTEIN 2 (ELIP2), PYR1-LIKE 6 (PYL6), PHLOEM PROTEIN 2 A5 (PP2A5), and EP3 (Vicente et al. 2012). It could be expected that a potential increase in 9-KOTE levels mediated by UVR8–LOX1 could be at least partially responsible for activation of the SA- and UV-B-dependent pathways. Besides the SA-dependent pathway, UV-B light mediates defense response via both JA-dependent and -independent pathways (A.-H.-Mackerness et al. 1999; Stratmann et al. 2000; Demkura et al. 2010; Demkura and Ballaré 2012; Li et al. 2024). Notably, treatment with UV-B light increases JA levels in Arabidopsis (A.-H.-Mackerness et al. 1999), whereas no effects of UV-B light on JA levels have been detected in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) (Stratmann et al. 2000). In coyote tobacco (Nicotiana attenuata), UV-B-elicited resistance to herbivory is compromised when NaLOX3 is silenced and JA biosynthesis is abolished (Demkura et al. 2010). However, in Arabidopsis, UV-B radiation increases resistance to the necrotrophic fungus Botrytis cinerea, while jar1 mutants impaired in JA signaling are as resistant as its wild-type to this pathogen (Demkura and Ballaré 2012). Here, we speculate that the JA pathway might not contribute in a meaningful way to stomatal closure in the early response to UV-B light, but we cannot exclude the possibility that linolenic acids may accumulate in anticipation of JA biosynthesis for successful defense responses. In addition, considering the role of LOX1 in the minimization of moisture loss under excessive light stress (Devireddy et al. 2018), it will also be of interest to examine whether UV-B signaling contributes to stomata-modulated prevention of water loss in natural light environments.

Arabidopsis seedlings were grown under continuous white light alone (3 μmol m−2 s−1, −UV-B) or supplemented with UV-B light (7 μW cm−2, equivalent to ∼1 μmol m−2 s−1, +UV-B) for 4 d.

The AflII/BglII fragment containing the 35S promoter and the BglII/SpeI fragment containing the full-length YFP coding sequence were cloned into the pEarleyGate 104-proUVR8:YFP-UVR8 vector; the full-length LOX1 coding sequence was then cloned by ligation-independent cloning to obtain pEarleyGate 104-35S:LOX1-YFP. All primers are listed in Supplementary Data Set 5, and all constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

## Lipid extraction
For LC-MS/MS analysis, lipid extraction was performed according to a previous study (Hummel et al. 2011). Lipids were extracted from the cotyledons of 10-d-old seedlings grown under continuous white light (100 μmol m−2 s−1, −UV-B) or supplemented with UV-B light (50 μW cm−2, ∼7.5 μmol m−2 s−1, +UV-B) for 3 h. For LC-MS analysis, oxylipins were extracted from detached epidermal peels of fully expanded leaves of 3- to 4-wk-old plants grown in white light under a 14-h-light/10-h-dark photoperiod and then treated with UV-B light (50 μW cm−2, ∼7.5 μmol m−2 s−1) for 2 h or 6 h (+UV-B) or maintained in white light alone (−UV-B) as control. In detail, 500 mg of freeze-dried cotyledon tissues or 20 mg of epidermal peels was homogenized in liquid nitrogen and extracted using 1 mL of a homogenous methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE):methanol (3:1, v/v) mixture. For extraction, each sample was vortexed for 30 min at 4 °C. After adding 300 μL ultra-pure water, the sample was vortexed for 1 min and incubated for 10 min at 4 °C. The sample was centrifuged at 14,000g for 3 min at 4 °C. The 400 μL supernatant was transferred to a new tube and concentrated until completely dry at 20 °C. For the quantification of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid, the dried lipid extracts were resuspended in 200 μL lipid complex solution (acetonitrile:isopropanol = 1:1, v/v), vortexed for 3 min, and centrifuged at 14,000g for 3 min at 4 °C. Finally, 120 μL supernatant was used for LC-MS/MS analysis. For the quantification of 9-KOTE and 9-HOTE, the lipids were resuspended in a solvent mixture totaling 300 µL. This solvent comprised 30 µL of a solubilizing agent (a 2:1 ratio of dichloromethane [CH2Cl2] to MeOH) and 270 µL of a diluent formulated with acetonitrile (ACN), isopropanol (IPA), and H2O in a proportion of 65:30:5. Each resuspended sample underwent vortexing for 30 s, followed by centrifugation. From the processed mixture, 270 µL of supernatant was subjected to LC-MS analysis. Three independent experiments were performed.
For GC-MS analysis, lipid extraction was performed with modifications from the published protocol of a previous study (Patel et al. 2018). Lipids were extracted from the cotyledons of 10-d-old seedlings grown under continuous white light (100 μmol m−2 s−1, −UV-B) or supplemented with UV-B light (50 μW cm−2, ∼7.5 μmol m−2 s−1, +UV-B) for indicated durations, or detached epidermal peels of fully expanded leaves of 3- to 4-wk-old plants grown in white light under a 14-h-light/10-h-dark photoperiod and then treated with UV-B light (50 μW cm−2, ∼7.5 μmol m−2 s−1). Briefly, freeze-dried cotyledon tissues or detached epidermal peels were homogenized in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle; the powder was transferred to a centrifuge tube. After adding precooled 5% methanolic H2SO4 solution (H2SO4:methanol = 1:20, v/v), the sample was vortexed vigorously for 3 min. The sample was centrifuged at 10,000g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a Pyrex culture tube and heated for 2 h at 80 °C in a water bath for conversion of lipids to fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) by transmethylation. FAMEs were extracted by adding precooled 0.9% (w/v) NaCl and hexane after cooling to room temperature, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 2,000g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, the upper phase was transferred to 2-mL clear GC vials. FAME extracts were dried under nitrogen flow and dissolved in hexane for GC-MS analysis. Three or 4 independent experiments were performed.

## High-performance liquid chromatography
The quantification of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid was analyzed using an LC-electrospray ionization (ESI)-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD, https://sciex.com.cn/; MS, QTRAP 6500+ System, https://sciex.com/). The analytical conditions were as follows: UPLC column, Thermo AccucoreC30 (2.6 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm i.d.); solvent system, A: acetonitrile/water (60/40, [v/v], 0.1% [v/v] formic acid, 10 mm ammonium formate), B: acetonitrile/isopropanol (10/90 [v/v], 0.1% [v/v] formic acid, 10 mm ammonium formate); gradient program, A/B (80:20, [v/v]) at 0 min, 70:30 (v/v) at 2 min, 40:60 (v/v) at 4 min, 15:85 (v/v) at 9 min, 10:90 (v/v) at 14 min, 5:95 (v/v) at 15.5 min, 5:95 (v/v) at 17.3 min, 80:20 (v/v) at 17.3 min, 80:20 (v/v) at 20 min; flow rate, 0.35 mL min−1; temperature, 45 °C; injection volume: 2 μL. The effluent was alternatively connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS. Plant lipid contents were detected by MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/) based on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform.
The quantification of 9-KOTE and 9-HOTE was analyzed using a Vanquish Flex UPLC system coupled with a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 240 system equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source. Samples were separated through a BEH C8 column (2.1 × 100 mm with 1.7 μm particle size, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with column temperature maintained at 55 °C and mobile phases consisting of 2 mm ammonium formate in mobile phase A (40% water and 60% acetonitrile) and mobile phase B (90% isopropanol and 10% acetonitrile). The gradient started with 1.5 min of isocratic elution with 32% B (and 68% A). B was increased to 85% over the next 15.5 min and then from 85% B to 97% B in only 0.1 min. Maintained at 97% B for 2.4 min. Rapidly, the mobile phase composition was returned to 32% B within 0.1 min and maintained 5 min for column post-equilibration. The flow rate for mobile phases was set at 0.26 mL min−1. The injection volume was 4 µL. The mass spectrometer was operated in negative modes using a full scan in the scan range m/z 100 to 1500 Da. Capillary voltage of 3,000 V for negative. Ion transfer tube temp: 320 °C, vaporizer temp: 350 °C, sheath gas: 40 Arb, aux gas: 10 Arb, sweep gas: 10 Arb. Orbitrap resolution was set 120,000 in MS1. The normalized collision energy (CE) type was selected. The internal mass calibration remains on the whole time. The MS data were captured and processed through Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd, San Jose, USA) and TraceFinder 5.2 General Quan software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Ltd, San Jose, USA) was used to quantify the target compound.

## GC-MS conditions
GC-MS was performed on a CP-Wax 52 CB column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25-μm film thickness) using an 8890A Agilent (Agilent Technologies, US) instrument fitted with an MS detector (7000D Agilent). The injector temperature was 260 °C. The column oven temperature was initially held at 50 °C for 3 min and increased first to 170 °C at the rate of 10 °C min−1 and then to 205 °C at a rate of 3 °C min−1, held for 20 min, and subsequently increased to 235 °C at a rate of 5 °C min−1 and to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1. The MSD transfer temperature was 280 °C. The MS quadrupole and source temperature were maintained at 150 and 230 °C, respectively. GC mass data were analyzed using Masshunter software from Agilent Corp.

## ESI-MS/MS conditions
Linear ion trap (LIT) and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans were acquired on a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer (QTRAP) (QTRAP 6500+ LC-MS/MS System) equipped with an ESI Turbo ion spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion modes and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: ion source, turbo spray; source temperature 500 °C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5,500 V (positive), −4,500 V (negative); ion source gas 1 (GS1), gas 2 (GS2), and curtain gas (CUR) were set at 45, 55, and 35 psi, respectively. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 μm polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. QQQ scans were acquired as multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to 5 psi. The declustering potential (DP) and CE for individual MRM transitions were done with further DP and CE optimization. Specific MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.

## Stomatal aperture measurement
Stomatal aperture analysis was performed as previously described (Shang et al. 2016). Cotyledons from 10-d-old seedlings grown under continuous white light (100 μmol m−2 s−1) or epidermal strips of the abaxial surface from fully expanded 3- to 4-wk-old plants grown in white light under a 14-h-light/10-h-dark photoperiod were incubated in stomatal opening buffer (10 mm MES and 10 mm KCl, pH 6.1) at 22 °C for 3 h under white light (100 μmol m−2 s−1) and then treated with UV-B light (50 μW cm−2, ∼7.5 μmol m−2 s−1) for 3 h (+UV-B) or maintained in white light alone (−UV-B) as control. The stock solutions of 10 mm linoleic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) or 10 mm α-linolenic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared in 100 mm sodium phosphate (pH 8.0) containing 0.3% (v/v) Tween 20 and 28 mm NaOH and were diluted to 100 nm in opening buffer. The mock solution was similar but without linoleic acid or α-linolenic acid. 9-KOTE (Larodan Fine Chemicals) was diluted to 100 nm in opening buffer, or the same volume of ethanol was added to the opening buffer as a control (mock). SA (Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted to 100 μm in opening buffer, or the same volume of ethanol was added to the opening buffer as a control (mock). Stomatal apertures were evaluated by observing the stomata under a microscope (Leica DM6B) and measuring their width in ImageJ (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/). For each replicate, at least 50 randomly selected apertures were measured, representing at least 150 stomatal aperture measurements across the 3 biological replicates as described (Chang et al. 2023).

## Hypocotyl length measurement
Hypocotyl length was measured as previously described (Huang et al. 2012). Arabidopsis seedlings were grown under continuous white light alone (3 μmol m−2 s−1, −UV-B) or supplemented with UV-B light (7 μW cm−2, ∼1 μmol m−2 s−1, +UV-B) for 4 d. Three biological replicates were performed, each consisting of at least 30 seedlings. The quantification of hypocotyl length was performed by ImageJ.

## RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted from the cotyledons or roots of 10-d-old Arabidopsis seedlings, or from 4-d-old whole seedlings, using an Eastep Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega). Reverse transcription was performed using GoScript Reverse Transcription Mix with Oligo (dT) (Promega). qPCR analysis was performed using iTaq universal SYBR Green supermix (Bio-Rad) with a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). ACTIN2 was used as reference transcript. Each experiment was performed with 3 independent samples, and qPCR reactions were performed in 3 technical replicates for each sample. The primers used in this study are summarized in Supplementary Data Set 5.

## Confocal microscopy
Confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed on a Zeiss LSM780 or Zeiss LSM980 + Airyscan2 system. GFP, YFP, CFP, mCherry, Alexa Fluor 555, and chlorophyll were excited at 488, 514, 433, 552, 543, and 488 nm, respectively. GFP emission was collected between 505 and 550 nm, YFP emission between 519 and 550 nm, CFP emission between 480 and 520 nm, mCherry emission between 600 and 650 nm, Alexa Fluor 555 emission between 545 and 727 nm, and chlorophyll emission between 650 and 740 nm. The master gain value was no more than 750, the digital gain value was set to 1, and the pinhole was set to 1 AU. The relative fluorescence intensity of YFP signals in the nucleus and cytoplasm was measured as previously described (Qian et al. 2016). Briefly, the subcellular localization of YFP was visualized under water with a 40× objective. The same laser settings were used in different light conditions for the same set of experiments. The YFP fluorescence intensity in one guard cell and its nucleus were measured, and the relative nuclear level was calculated as the ratio of nuclear fluorescence intensity to total fluorescence intensity. The ratio of total YFP fluorescence intensity in one guard cell was set to 1, and the relative cytoplasmic level was calculated by subtracting the relative nuclear level from total fluorescence intensity in one guard cell. The quantification of fluorescence signal was performed in ImageJ. The fluorescence intensity of at least 75 stomata was measured from 3 biological replicates. At least 25 stomata were analyzed under each light condition.

## Luciferase complementation imaging assay
LCI was performed in the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana plants as described previously (Chen et al. 2008). Briefly, Agrobacterium strain GV2260 or GV3101 cells separately transformed with the appropriate pairs of nLUC- or cLUC-fusion constructs were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves (10 mm MgCl2, 10 mm MES [pH 5.7], 200 µM acetosyringone). After infiltration, the N. benthamiana plants were grown for at least 2 d and infiltrated with D-luciferin (100 μm), followed by imaging using a Tanon 5200S Luminescent Imaging Workstation. Polyclonal anti-LUC antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich, L0159) were used to detect nLUC- and cLUC-fused proteins (Saur et al. 2019). Three biological replicates were analyzed for each interaction pair, and 3 technical replicates were assayed per experiment.

## Co-immunoprecipitation assay
Total proteins (1 to 3 mg) were extracted from 4-d-old Arabidopsis seedlings grown under continuous white light alone (−UV-B) or supplemented with +UV-B in protein extraction buffer containing 50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, 10 mm NaF, 2 mm Na3VO4, 50 mm β-glycerophosphate, 1 mm phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The extracts were incubated with 20 µL anti-FLAG Magnetic Beads (Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed 5 times with wash buffer containing 50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.35% (v/v) Tween 20 before elution with 2× SDS protein loading buffer. All eluted products were boiled at 95 °C for 10 min before separation by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting. Anti-FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804), anti-LOX1 (this study), and anti-RPN6 (Ren et al. 2019) antibodies were used for immunoblot analysis. Anti-LOX1 was generated by raising rabbit polyclonal antibodies against full-length recombinant His-LOX1. Three independent experiments were performed, and 1 representative result was presented.

## Protein expression and purification
Recombinant His6-SUMO-LOX1 was produced in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) (Novagen) cells that were induced with 60 µM isopropyl-1-thio-β-D-galactopyranoside (IPTG) when the culture had reached OD600 = 1.0, followed by growth for 12 h at 16 °C. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 1,500g for 20 min at 4 °C and washed in buffer A (20 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mm NaCl). The supernatant was incubated in batches with 5 mL Ni-IDA SuperFlow (GE Healthcare), which was then washed in buffer A with 20 to 30 mm imidazole before eluting the protein with buffer A containing 300 mm imidazole. LOX1 was released from the resin by incubation with ubiquitin-like protein 1 (Ulp1) protease at 4 °C for 4 h. The protein solution was passed through a Heparin column (GE Healthcare) to further remove untargeted protein and nucleic acids with buffer B (25 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mm NaCl). LOX1 was further purified by gel filtration on a Superdex 200 10/300 GL (GE Healthcare) with 25 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mm NaCl, and 3 mm DTT, and finally concentrated to 10 mg mL−1. The variants LOX1KEA, LOX1LF, and LOX1HQ were each prepared, produced, and purified according to the above procedure.
GST and GST-UVR8R338A were produced in E. coli strain Transetta (DE3) and purified with glutathione/Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare) as described previously (Qian et al. 2020).

## In vitro pull-down assays
For the GST pull-down assays, 1 μg of GST or GST-UVR8R338A was mixed with 1 μg of LOX1 and 15 μL of Glutathione Sepharose 4B. The mixture was incubated in binding buffer (50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mm NaCl, and 0.1% [v/v] Tween 20) at 4 °C for 3 h. After centrifugation at 1,500g, the pellets were washed 3 times, eluted in 2× SDS loading buffer, and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min before immunoblotting. Anti-GST (TransGen Biotech, HT601-01), anti-LOX1 (this study) antibodies were used for immunoblot analysis. For each in vitro pull-down assay, 3 independent repetitions were performed, and 1 representative result was presented.

## Immunofluorescence assay
Arabidopsis seedlings grown under continuous white light and then treated with UV-B light (50 μW cm−2, ∼7.5 μmol m−2 s−1) for 3 h (+UV-B) or maintained in white light alone (−UV-B) as control were fixed with 4% (w/v) formaldehyde for 20 min and rinsed 3 times with 1× Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST). Seedlings were blocked in 1× TBST containing 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin for 1 h, rinsed with 1× TBST, and incubated with anti-UVR8 antibodies (Huang et al. 2013) at 4 °C overnight. After rinsing again, a secondary antibody was added (1:200 in 1× TBST; Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L [Alexa Fluor 555], Abcam, ab150078) for 2 h at room temperature. Samples were rinsed 3 times with 1× TBST and subjected to confocal microscopy.

## Crystallization and structure determination
LOX1 crystals were obtained by the hanging drop vapor diffusion method against a reservoir solution at 18 °C. Drops contained 1 µL of a protein mixture at 7 mg mL−1, combined with 50 μm linoleic acid and 1 µL reservoir solution. LOX1 crystals were grown from 25% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 4000 (PEG 4K), 100 mm sodium cacodylate, pH 5.0. All crystals were cryoprotected using corresponding reservoir buffers containing 20% (v/v) glycerol and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction data were collected at 100 K at beamlines BL-02U1, BL-10U2, BL-18U1, and BL-19U1 at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) in China. The diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using the HKL3000 package (Otwinowski and Minor 1997). The structure was determined by molecular replacement using the structure of soybean (Glycine max) lipoxygenase-1 (PDB: 3PZW) as the search model (Chruszcz et al. 2008). Models were manually built and adjusted with COOT (Emsley et al. 2010) and refined with PHENIX (Adams et al. 2010). All data processing and structure refinement statistics for LOX1 are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The structure figures were prepared using PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org/).

## LOX1 activity assays
For LOX activity assays in vitro, each reaction contained 0.25 μg wild-type or mutated LOX1 recombinant protein, incubated in 1 mL of reaction buffer containing 30 μm linoleic acid and 100 mm sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0). The increase in absorbance at 234 nm (A234) was recorded at 25 °C for 20 min using an Eppendorf BioSpectrometer. To study the effect of GST-UVR8R338A on LOX1 activity, 0.5 μg GST or GST-UVR8R338A was incubated with 0.25 μg wild-type or mutated LOX1 at 4 °C for 60 min. Then, the protein mixture was added to the reaction buffer at 25 °C for 20 min prior to A234 measurement.
For LOX activity assays in vivo, protein extracts were extracted from the cotyledons of 10-d-old Arabidopsis seedlings grown under continuous white light or infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves in 100 mm sodium phosphate (pH 6.0) buffer containing 1 mm PMSF and 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. LOX-catalyzed hydroperoxidation of linoleic acid at 25 °C was measured as an increase in absorbance at 234 nm. The assay mixture included 100 mm sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), 100 μm (Arabidopsis) or 30 μm (Nicotiana benthamiana) linoleic acid, and ∼5 μg crude enzyme extract in a total volume of 1 mL. The reaction absorbance at 234 nm was recorded at 60-s intervals for 300 s. One unit of LOX activity was defined as an increase in absorbance of 0.01 at 234 nm min−1 mg−1 of protein under assay conditions.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses of all bar graphs were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 22) or Microsoft Excel (2021). Values were represented as mean ± Sd. Significant differences between the 2 groups were tested with Student's t-test by using Microsoft Excel 2021 software. Differences between 3 or more groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test with IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 22). Details of statistical analyses including analytical methods, N values, and significance levels (P-values) can be found in the figures, figure legends, or Supplementary Data Set 6.

## Figure Figure 11.: A proposed working model for the UVR8–LOX1 module promoting UV-B-induced stomatal closure. UV-B light promotes the accumulation of PUFAs, linoleic acid (18:2) and α-linolenic acid (18:3) primarily mediated by PLIP1/PLIP3, and induces the expression of LOX1, whose encoding enzyme catabolizes them for oxylipin production. UVR8 is photoactivated and monomerized to interact with LOX1, which in turn activates the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway through SA biosynthesis and signaling pathway and ultimately promotes stomatal closure. Mutating the UVR8-interacting sites of LOX1 (mutation of K239 and E240 in UVR8–LOX1 interacting sites to A, LOX1KEA) retains LOX1 activity but abolishes the interaction with UVR8, which attenuates the activation of the LOX-derived oxylipin pathway and UV-B-induced stomatal closure. Disruption of the metal-binding site (mutation of H519, H524, and H711 in metal-binding sites to Q, LOX1HQ) of LOX1 impairs LOX1 activity but retains its interaction with UVR8. The disruption of the oxygen cavity (mutation of R216 in the oxygen cavity to F, LOX1RF) or substrate cavity (mutation of L561 and L566 in the substrate cavity to F, LOX1LF) of LOX1 impairs LOX1 activity and abolishes UVR8 interaction, leading to no stomatal response to UV-B exposure.
