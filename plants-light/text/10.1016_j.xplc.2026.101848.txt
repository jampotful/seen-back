# Functional conservation and diversity of phytochrome B and its potential applications in crop improvement

## Abstract
Phytochrome, first identified in the 1950s as a photoreceptor that senses red and far-red light, plays a crucial role in various aspects of plant growth and development. Numerous recent studies have revealed that phytochrome B (phyB) functions as both a photoreceptor and a thermosensor, integrating environmental light and temperature signals with internal hormonal, developmental, and metabolic cues to coordinate diverse cellular responses. As a core regulator, phyB has become one of the most extensively studied genes in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. More recently, research on phyB has extended to major staple crops and vegetables, including maize, rice, wheat, tomato, and cucumber, in which it modulates critical agronomic traits such as plant architecture, flowering time, density tolerance, and yield. This review summarizes recent advances in phyB research, examines phyB conservation and diversity across species, and explores future prospects for using this information to develop innovative breeding strategies for crop improvement.

## Introduction
In nature, animals perceive external light signals through specialized organs such as eyes and photoreceptor cells connected to the optic nerve. By contrast, plants lack dedicated visual organs and instead rely on a diverse set of photoreceptors to monitor multiple aspects of light, including its quantity (photon number), quality (wavelength), intensity, and duration. The main classes of plant photoreceptors include phytochromes (phytochrome A [phyA]–phyE), which sense red (R; 600–700 nm) and far-red (FR; 700–750 nm) light; cryptochromes (cry1 and cry2); phototropins (phot1 and phot2); several light-oxygen-voltage-domain-containing proteins such as LIGHT-OXYGEN-VOLTAGE KELCH PROTEIN 2 (LKP2), ZEITLUPE (ZTL), and FLAVIN-BINDING KELCH REPEAT F-BOX 1 (FKF1), which perceive UV-A (315–400 nm) and blue (400–500 nm) light; and UV RESISTANCE LOCUS 8 (UVR8), which detects UV-B (280–315 nm) light (Li et al., 2011). These photoreceptors convert specific light signals into distinct cellular responses, ultimately regulating various aspects of plant growth and development.
Phytochromes were first identified as photoreceptors for R and FR light following their isolation from etiolated maize seedlings in the 1950s (Butler et al., 1959). In general, dicot plants such as Arabidopsis have five phytochromes (phyA–phyE), whereas monocots like maize have three (phyA–phyC) (Mathews and Sharrock, 1996). Although all phytochromes can perceive and respond to R and FR light at the biochemical and molecular levels, they have distinct physiological roles. In Arabidopsis, phyA is a light-labile type I phytochrome that primarily mediates responses to continuous FR light, very-low-fluence responses, low-fluence responses, and high-irradiance responses. By contrast, the type II phytochromes (phyB–phyE) are more light-stable and exhibit overlapping functions in response to continuous R and white light. Genetic and physiological evidence indicates that phyB is the predominant and most abundant phytochrome. It mediates responses to continuous R light, low-fluence R-light and white light (e.g., seed germination), and high-irradiance responses (e.g., inhibition of hypocotyl elongation), as well as responses to high-intensity white light, such as full sunlight (Li et al., 2011). Over the past few decades, numerous studies have demonstrated that phyB plays a crucial role throughout the plant life cycle, influencing key developmental events ranging from seed germination and photomorphogenesis to flowering time and seed production.
Research on phyB over the past few decades can be characterized by three main trends. First, functional studies have expanded from the model plant Arabidopsis to diverse species, including staple crops, revealing the evolutionary conservation, functional diversity, and agricultural relevance of phyB. Second, deeper mechanistic insights have been gained, with research emphasis shifting toward understanding how phyB perceives and transduces external light and temperature signals, focusing on protein–protein interactions, post-translational modifications, photobody phase behavior, and insights from high-resolution 3D structural analyses. Third, the translation of mechanistic research into agricultural improvements has gained momentum, with phyB research increasingly directed toward agronomic traits and the goal of translating basic insights into molecular breeding strategies that enhance crop productivity and sustainability. Although phyB also mediates responses to diverse abiotic (e.g., cold, salinity, and heavy metals) and biotic (e.g., rice blast and sheath blight) stresses (Jiang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025a), these stress-related responses are not discussed in detail here owing to space limitations. ## Physiological functions of phyB in model plants and crops

## Seed germination
Early research on seed germination revealed that R light promotes germination of lettuce seeds, whereas FR light inhibits it (Borthwick et al., 1952). Since then, light-regulated seed germination has become a classic topic in plant photobiology. In Arabidopsis, phyB acts as a positive regulator, whereas PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR 1 (PIF1), also known as PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR 3-LIKE 5 (PIL5), acts downstream of phyB to repress seed germination (Oh et al., 2004). phyB promotes seed germination primarily by regulating the stability of its interacting proteins, such as PIF1. Under dark conditions (e.g., when seeds are buried), inactive phyB (Pr) localizes to the cytoplasm, allowing PIF1 to accumulate in the nucleus. Upon exposure to light, even at very low intensities, active phyB (Pfr) translocates into the nucleus and triggers PIF1 degradation (Shen et al., 2005). As a basic helix–loop–helix-type transcription factor, PIF1 inhibits seed germination by activating abscisic acid (ABA)-related genes (NCED6, NCED9, ABI3, and ABI5), repressing gibberellin (GA)-related genes (GA3ox1 and GA3ox2), and promoting expression of REPRESSOR OF GA1-3 (RGA) and GA INSENSITIVE (GAI). This regulation occurs either directly or indirectly via intermediate targets, such as SOMNUS (SOM) and REVEILLE 1 (RVE1) (Oh et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2008; Yang et al., 2020).
In addition to PIF1, phyB also regulates seed germination through interactions with other proteins, including the transcription factors FAR-RED ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 3 (FHY3) and ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR 55/58 (ERF55/58). phyB stabilizes FHY3, which promotes GA biosynthesis and seed germination by activating SPATULA (SPT) and repressing RVE2 and RVE7 (Liu et al., 2021b). In addition, phyB interacts with ERF55 and ERF58, suppressing their promotion of PIF1 and SOM expression, thereby inhibiting ABA synthesis and promoting GA synthesis (Li et al., 2022c). phyB also enhances the accumulation of the non-coding RNA HIDDEN TREASURE 1 (HID1), which modulates chromatin histone modifications of ABA biosynthesis genes such as NCED9, thus suppressing ABA synthesis (Wang et al., 2023b). Furthermore, phyB promotes seed germination by stabilizing the KNOX transcription factor BREVIPEDICELLUS (BP; also known as KNAT1), which increases trimethylated histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) deposition at the promoters of NCED6 and NCED9, repressing their expression and consequently reducing ABA biosynthesis (Gu et al., 2025).
Although the regulatory mechanisms by which phyB mediates seed germination are well understood in model plants (Figure 2), research in crop species remains limited. Translating this understanding to crops represents a critical research frontier, as seed germination is a key agricultural trait that directly affects seedling uniformity and growth vigor. ## Photomorphogenesis
Photomorphogenesis is a light-triggered developmental process characterized by de-etiolation, including apical hook opening, cotyledon greening, and reduced hypocotyl elongation. The critical role of phyB in suppressing hypocotyl cell elongation was established in 1991 with the identification of the classical hy3 (long-hypocotyl 3) mutant, which was found to be deficient in phyB (Somers et al., 1991). Subsequently, mutations in phyB have been shown to cause elongated hypocotyls in various dicot species, including tomato, soybean, and cucumber (Weller et al., 2000; Liu et al., 2021a; Zhao et al., 2022a). In monocots, loss of phyB function in maize (phyB1 phyB2) and rice (phyB) leads to an elongated mesocotyl, a phenotype similar to the elongated hypocotyl observed in dicots (Takano et al., 2005; Sheehan et al., 2007). Together, these findings indicate that phyB plays a conserved role in suppressing elongation growth in both the hypocotyl of Arabidopsis and the mesocotyl of cereal crops.
Over the past few decades, Arabidopsis hypocotyl elongation has been widely used as a model system for investigating how light signaling is integrated with other pathways, greatly advancing our understanding of the key roles of phyB in this process. In Arabidopsis, phyB inhibits hypocotyl elongation primarily by promoting degradation of the transcription factors PIF4 and PIF5, preventing their activation of cell elongation-promoting genes such as YUCCA8 (YUC8) and AUXIN/INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID 19/29 (IAA19/29) (Shen et al., 2007; Sun et al., 2013). The conservation of this core phyB–PIF module is evident in other species, including tomato and cucumber, highlighting its fundamental role in the control of cell elongation across diverse species (Chen et al., 2025b; Kunta et al., 2025). As well as triggering PIF4 and PIF5 degradation, phyB also interacts with the AT-hook motif transcription factor AHL21, reinforcing repression of PIF4 and adding another layer of inhibition to cell elongation (Liu et al., 2025b).
In addition to the phyB–PIF module, phyB integrates light signaling with multiple hormone pathways to coordinately repress hypocotyl elongation. In the auxin pathway, phyB inhibits the expression of auxin-related genes by promoting the degradation of PIF4 and PIF5 and through direct interaction with AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 6 (ARF6) and ARF8, suppressing their DNA-binding activity and thus attenuating auxin-induced hypocotyl elongation (Mao et al., 2020). In addition, phyB also interacts with and stabilizes the Aux/IAA proteins IAA7 and IAA12, further repressing auxin signal transduction (Xu et al., 2018). In the brassinosteroid (BR) pathway, phyB interacts with the transcription factors BRASSINAZOLE-RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) and BRI1 EMS SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1), inhibiting their DNA-binding capacities and transcriptional activation of cell elongation-related genes such as DWARF4 (DWF4) and PACLOBUTRAZOL RESISTANCE 1 (PRE1) (Wu et al., 2019b; Dong et al., 2020). In the karrikin (KAR) pathway, phyB interacts with SUPPRESSOR OF MAX2 1 (SMAX1) and SMXL2, suppressing phyB-mediated degradation of PIF4 and PIF5 in the absence of KAR signaling. Upon KAR signal activation, SMAX1 and SMXL2 are degraded, releasing phyB to promote PIF degradation and repress hypocotyl cell elongation (Chang et al., 2024).
phyB also promotes photomorphogenesis through multiple layers of epigenetic regulation. First, phyB recruits the SWI2/SNF2-related 1 (SWR1) chromatin remodeling complex via its subunits SWC6 and ARP6 to deposit the histone variant H2A.Z at the YUC9 locus, repressing auxin biosynthesis and hypocotyl elongation (Wei et al., 2021). Second, phyB collaborates with VIN3-LIKE 1 (VIL1) to enrich H3K27me3 marks at target loci, such as AtHB2 and HFR1, silencing growth-promoting genes independently of PIF4 (Kim et al., 2021). Third, phyB stabilizes the H3K27me3 demethylase RELATIVE OF EARLY FLOWERING 6 (REF6), which promotes the transcription of genes involved in cell elongation, including DUF668 (Yan et al., 2025).
The greening of etiolated seedlings upon light exposure involves not only chlorophyll synthesis but also chloroplast biogenesis and the establishment of photosynthetic capacity. In Arabidopsis and other species such as rice, maize, cucumber, and tomato, mutations in phyB consistently result in a mildly pale-green phenotype, highlighting its conserved positive role in chlorophyll biosynthesis and greening (Weller et al., 2000; Takano et al., 2005; Sheehan et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2025a). Mechanistically, phyB promotes greening primarily by triggering degradation of the negative regulators PIF3 and EIN3 and by activating chlorophyll biosynthesis genes such as PROTOCHLOROPHYLL OXIDOREDUCTASE A (PORA) and PORB (Zhong et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2017). phyB also antagonizes the inhibitory effects of high temperature and jasmonic acid on chloroplast biogenesis by directly binding to and stabilizing JASMONATE ZIM-DOMAIN 1 (JAZ1) and JAZ3, as well as by activating the HY5 pathway (Qi et al., 2026). Interestingly, several components of the phyB photobody in the nucleus, including HEMERA (HMR) and REGULATOR OF CHLOROPLAST BIOGENESIS (RCB), also localize to chloroplasts, suggesting potential roles in phyB-mediated greening, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified (Chen et al., 2010; Qiu et al., 2015; Yoo et al., 2019). Following greening, phyB continues to facilitate subsequent cellular processes, such as photosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism, including starch synthesis (Shi et al., 2024a).
In Arabidopsis, phyB promotes apical hook opening by interacting with HOOKLESS1 (HLS1) to attenuate its activity (Lyu et al., 2019). However, because crop plants do not form an apical hook in darkness, photomorphogenesis research in crops has focused primarily on repression of mesocotyl elongation and promotion of greening. Although the mechanisms underlying phyB-mediated photomorphogenesis remain poorly characterized in crops, they are generally presumed to be functionally similar to those established in Arabidopsis. For example, multiple chlorophyll biosynthesis genes, such as PORA and GUN4, are upregulated by phyB in rice, promoting chlorophyll biosynthesis and chloroplast biogenesis (Zhao et al., 2013). Collectively, these advances have expanded our understanding of the critical role of phyB in light-regulated plant development and highlighted its potential to improve agronomic traits, such as chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthetic capacity, that ultimately influence overall crop yield.

## Thermomorphogenesis
Thermomorphogenesis refers to the morphological and physiological adaptations undergone by plants in response to non-stressful warm temperatures, such as the accelerated hypocotyl cell elongation and flowering observed in Arabidopsis at ∼27°C compared with 20°C. Among the photoreceptors, phyB uniquely functions as a temperature sensor through thermal reversion within the 12°C–27°C range (Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016). Elevated temperatures accelerate the conversion of phyB from the active Pfr form to the inactive Pr form, a mechanism conserved across species including Arabidopsis, potato, and maize (Burgie et al., 2021). The transcription factor PIF4 acts as a key regulator of phyB-dependent thermomorphogenesis, using molecular mechanisms highly analogous to those involved in photomorphogenesis. In Arabidopsis, elevated temperatures reduce phyB activity, leading to increased PIF4 stability, which promotes hypocotyl elongation by activating auxin biosynthesis genes (e.g., YUC8) and growth-promoting genes (e.g., ATHB2) (Kim et al., 2020). In addition, bZIP transcription factors associated with the unfolded protein response (e.g., bZIP17, bZIP28, and bZIP60) interact with PIF4 and counteract the inhibitory effect of phyB on PIF4 stability, promoting hypocotyl growth under warm temperatures (Zhang et al., 2025b).
Elevated temperatures convert phyB from its active Pfr form to the inactive Pr form, whereas PHOTOPERIODIC CONTROL OF HYPOCOTYL 1 (PCH1) and PCH1-LIKE (PCHL) directly suppress this conversion by promoting the formation of phyB-dependent photobodies that stabilize phyB in the Pfr form (Huang et al., 2019). In addition to PCH1, other components of phyB-dependent photobodies, such as EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3) and HMR, also play key roles in mediating thermal responses. At ambient temperature, BRASSINOSTEROID-INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2) phosphorylates phyB at Tyr787, enhancing the interaction between phyB and ELF3 and facilitating assembly of a phyB–ELF3–HMR complex that stabilizes phyB in its active Pfr form. By contrast, under warm temperatures, BIN2 undergoes oligomerization, resulting in a loss of kinase activity and reduced phyB phosphorylation and function, ultimately triggering rapid growth (Yang et al., 2025). Notably, ELF3 itself also acts as a temperature sensor to mediate accelerated growth under warm conditions (Jung et al., 2020).

## Plant architecture
Plant architecture is a fundamental determinant of crop performance, adaptability, productivity, and yield potential. In recent decades, phyB has emerged as a key regulator of numerous agricultural traits, including plant height, tillering, branching, and leaf morphology, acting through the integration of canopy light and temperature signals with internal developmental cues.

## Plant height
In Arabidopsis, disruption of phyB accelerates cell elongation in hypocotyls and inflorescences, resulting in significantly taller plants (Reed et al., 1993). This inhibition of phyB on plant height is conserved in other species such as maize, sorghum, tomato, and cotton (Husaineid et al., 2007; Rao et al., 2011). In maize, phyB1 and phyB1 phyB2 mutants exhibit increased elongation of the mesocotyl and internodes, producing taller plants during the seedling and vegetative growth stages; however, mature phyB1 phyB2 mutants are ultimately shorter than controls (Sheehan et al., 2007; Shi et al., 2024b). Similarly, sorghum phyB mutants display faster growth during the vegetative stage but slower growth during reproduction (Kebrom and Mullet, 2016). The reduced final height of maize and sorghum phyB mutants likely results from early flowering, which terminates stem and internode elongation earlier. By contrast, control plants continue to grow, leading to a taller mature height. Notably, rice and wheat are exceptions to this conserved pattern, as phyB mutations in these species result in reduced plant height (Gu et al., 2011; Pearce et al., 2016). In wheat, phyB disruption causes a pronounced delay in the transition to the jointing stage and flowering (Pearce et al., 2016). Thus, failure to initiate stem and internode elongation due to a delayed flowering transition is likely a major factor contributing to the reduced height of phyB mutants in cereal crops such as wheat.
Although the physiological role of phyB in repressing internode elongation and plant height is well established across species, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Given the conservation of phyB, we hypothesize that its repression of plant height parallels its inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, potentially involving interactions with key proteins such as PIFs and BBXs, as well as various hormone signaling pathways. Supporting this hypothesis, studies in maize have demonstrated that higher-order ZmPIF mutants exhibit shortened mesocotyls and internodes, resulting in reduced plant height (Wu et al., 2019a). In addition to PIFs, the BBX family member DBB2 positively regulates plant height in maize (Wang et al., 2025). Although a direct molecular link between phyB and PIFs or BBX proteins has not yet been established in crops, analogous mechanisms are well documented in Arabidopsis. Taken together, these findings suggest that phyB-mediated regulation of plant height likely integrates light and hormone pathways, providing a theoretical basis for its practical application in crop improvement.
PhyB not only suppresses stem and internode elongation but also promotes the formation of secondary cell walls in stem cells, enhancing mechanical strength and structural support for growth (Luo et al., 2022). These dual functions of phyB could be harnessed to reduce lodging risk by modulating phyB expression or activity to prevent excessive growth. In maize, continuous expression of phyB hyperactive variants (Y98F and Y359F) produces moderate reductions in plant height and improves lodging resistance (Zhao et al., 2022b). In tomato, overexpression of phyB2 produces a pronounced semi-dwarf phenotype, with a reduction of up to 50% in plant height under greenhouse conditions (Husaineid et al., 2007). Moreover, heterologous expression of Arabidopsis phyB also confers a stable semi-dwarf phenotype in cotton and potato (Thiele et al., 1999; Rao et al., 2011). Together, these findings indicate that enhanced phyB expression typically produces a moderate reduction in height rather than excessive dwarfing. This improves lodging resistance, particularly under high-density planting conditions, highlighting the potential of phyB2 in genetic crop improvement.

## Bud outgrowth and branch formation
The reduced lateral branching observed in Arabidopsis phyB mutants highlights the positive role of phyB in promoting bud outgrowth (Finlayson et al., 2010). This function appears to be evolutionarily conserved, as phyB has also been shown to promote branching or tillering in sorghum, rice, tomato, potato, and cucumber (Weng et al., 2014; Kebrom and Mullet, 2016; Dong et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025c) (Figure 3). It is widely accepted that phyB facilitates lateral bud outgrowth by suppressing apical dominance, although the precise molecular mechanism remains to be fully characterized. Genetic evidence identified TEOSINTE BRANCHED 1 (TB1) as a key regulator of branching, with increased TB1 expression driving the transition from a highly tillered ancestor to single-stalk modern maize during domestication (Doebley et al., 1997). A similar phenotype directly linked to phyB has been observed in sorghum, in which the phyB-1 (58M-ma3R) mutant exhibits reduced tiller number and enhanced apical dominance, a trait intensively selected during breeding (Kebrom et al., 2006). Molecular analyses reveal that mutations in phyB lead to an accumulation of TB1 in axillary buds, thus suppressing bud growth (Kebrom and Mullet, 2016). Notably, phyB mutants in sorghum eventually develop tillers comparable to those of control plants at the adult stage, coinciding with increased cytokinin and sugar signals in the buds during later stages. phyB also promotes tillering by repressing MAX2, which encodes an F-box protein involved in strigolactone signaling that induces axillary bud dormancy and inhibits bud outgrowth (Kebrom et al., 2010). ## Leaf angle
Leaf angle is a key agronomic trait that influences canopy architecture, planting density, and yield. In Arabidopsis, phyB mutants exhibit pronounced upward-curled leaves, whereas phyB overexpression causes a slight increase in leaf angle (Zhao et al., 2022b). Similarly, in maize, phyB1 phyB2 mutants display smaller leaf angles and a more compact architecture (Shi et al., 2024b). At the cellular level, maize phyB1 and phyB2 promote leaf angle expansion by stimulating cell division on the adaxial side of the ligular region while inhibiting cell elongation on the abaxial side. At the molecular level, phyB enlarges leaf angle by interacting with and stabilizing LIGULELESS 1 (LG1), a classical SPL-type transcription factor that positively regulates leaf angle in crops such as maize, rice, wheat, and sorghum (Shi et al., 2024b). By contrast, rice phyB mutants exhibit an increased leaf angle for unknown reasons (Takano et al., 2005). Although the role of phyB in regulating leaf angle varies among different crops, it is clear that phyB has a substantial influence on leaf angle and plays a key role in the formation of smart plant architecture.

## Flowering time regulation
Flowering at the appropriate developmental stage and season is essential for plant fitness and crop productivity. In Arabidopsis, phyB mutants flower early under both long-day (LD) and short-day (SD) conditions, demonstrating that phyB generally acts as a repressor of flowering (Reed et al., 1993). Similar early-flowering phenotypes have been reported in phyB mutants of maize, rice, sorghum, pea, and cucumber, reinforcing the conserved role of phyB as a flowering repressor across diverse species (Childs et al., 1997; Weller et al., 2001; Takano et al., 2005; Sheehan et al., 2007; Hu et al., 2023). By contrast, phyB promotes flowering in wheat and barley, where phyB mutations delay heading time under both LD and SD conditions (Hanumappa et al., 1999; Pearce et al., 2016). Despite these divergent phenotypes, these findings reveal that phyB is a conserved key regulator of flowering time that acts upstream of photoperiodic pathways across different species.
Mechanistically, phyB influences flowering time primarily by modulating the circadian clock and photoperiodic pathways. In Arabidopsis, phyB delays flowering by interacting with the E3 ubiquitin ligase HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 1 (HOS1), facilitating degradation of CONSTANS (CO), and suppressing expression of the florigen FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) under LD, white light, and high-R light conditions (Lazaro et al., 2012, 2015; Takagi et al., 2023). In addition to the CO–FT regulatory module, phyB stabilizes the F-box protein FOF2 and modulates expression of FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC), fine-tuning flowering time in response to light quality (Yasui et al., 2012; Qu et al., 2024). phyB also physically interacts with and stabilizes ELF3, a core component of the evening complex in the circadian clock, indirectly regulating the rhythmic expression of floral integrators such as CO and FT and ultimately repressing flowering (Liu et al., 2001; Zhao et al., 2021).
In rice, phyB-mediated flowering time regulation is complex and intricately controlled. Rice is an SD plant with two genetically independent floral promoters, HEADING DATE 1 (Hd1) and EARLY HEADING DATE 1 (Ehd1) (Doi et al., 2004). Hd1, an ortholog of CO, promotes flowering under SD conditions by activating HEADING DATE 3a (Hd3a; an ortholog of FT) but represses Hd3a and inhibits flowering under LD conditions. phyB regulation of Hd1-mediated suppression of Hd3a is a primary cause of LD- or night-break-induced flowering inhibition (Ishikawa et al., 2005, 2011). Rice phyB interacts with OsPIL15 to enhance Hd1 expression, which further represses Hd3a and RICE FLOWERING LOCUS T 1 (RFT1), inhibiting flowering (Xie et al., 2025). Rice phyB also interacts with the zinc-finger protein EARLY FLOWERING AT LONG DAY 1 (ELD1) and regulates CCA1 splicing, as well as the rhythmic expression of Hd1, thus contributing to delayed flowering (Cai et al., 2025).
In addition to the broadly conserved Hd1–Hd3a flowering pathway, rice phyB also functions in the monocot-specific Ghd7–Ehd1 flowering pathway. Rice phyB directly interacts with Ghd7 and prevents its interaction with GIGANTEA (GI), stabilizing Ghd7 and repressing Ehd1 expression (Zheng et al., 2019). phyB-mediated repression of Ehd1 is further regulated by OsCOL4, a flowering suppressor under both LD and SD conditions (Lee et al., 2010). In addition, phyB may influences the interaction between Ghd7 and Hd1, adding another layer of inhibition on Ehd1 (Osugi et al., 2011; Nemoto et al., 2016). phyB also induces modification of ELF3-1 to delay flowering by relieving repression of Ghd7 and PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR 37 (PRR37) (Andrade et al., 2022). Together, these findings reveal that rice phyB regulates flowering time through multiple integrated layers.
In addition to its well-established role in regulating flowering time in response to diverse light signals, phyB also modulates flowering time by sensing dynamic temperature changes. In rice, phyB modulates Ghd7 repressor activity through its role as a temperature sensor, inhibiting expression of Ehd1, Hd3a, and RFT1 (Nagalla et al., 2021). High temperatures accelerate flowering by converting phyB from the active Pfr form to the inactive Pr form, releasing PIF4 to activate FT expression and promote flowering (Kumar et al., 2012; Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016). In soybean, phyB mediates high-temperature-induced flowering by promoting degradation of PRR5a and PRR5b, thus relieving repression of FT2a and FT5b (Hou et al., 2026). Interestingly, seasonal growth regulation in trees (e.g., poplar) during cold temperatures at high latitudes and altitudes also relies on the phyB–PIF cascade (Ding et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025a). Low temperature enhances the phyB–PIF4 interaction and promotes PIF4 degradation, thus preventing premature growth cessation and bud set under cooler conditions (Zhang et al., 2025a). In Arabidopsis, low temperature induces interactions between PIF3 and C-REPEAT BINDING FACTORs (CBFs), key transcription factors that mediate cold acclimation, stabilizing phyB and promoting degradation of PIF1, PIF4, and PIF5 to enhance freezing tolerance (Jiang et al., 2020).
Appropriate flowering time is vital for maximizing crop yield. Although the detailed molecular mechanisms by which phyB regulates flowering time remain unresolved in wheat and maize, phyB clearly plays a key role in this process in these staple crops. In maize, phyB1 and phyB2 function redundantly, with phyB2 playing the primary role in flowering time regulation (Sheehan et al., 2007). Interestingly, natural variation in the ZmphyB2 promoter has contributed to early flowering and was selected during maize adaptation from tropical to temperate regions, as well as in modern breeding programs (Wang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023). Similarly, in rice, phyB contributes to flowering time variation across diverse genetic backgrounds (Li et al., 2018; Jadamba et al., 2022). These findings explain the strong selection for early-flowering phyB haplotypes during crop adaptation to high latitudes. ## Leaf senescence
phyB serves as a central integrator that perceives environmental light and temperature cues to modulate leaf senescence. In Arabidopsis, R light and high R:FR light delay senescence, whereas FR light promotes it (Lim et al., 2007). Under white light and high R:FR conditions, phyB mutants display premature senescence in Arabidopsis and other species such as maize and cucumber (Sakuraba et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2025a). phyB inhibits senescence primarily by modulating the stability of multiple key downstream transcription factors, including PIF4, PIF5, and FHY3, ultimately controlling the expression of senescence-associated genes and influencing longevity (Song et al., 2014). Acting downstream of phyB, PIF4 and PIF5 promote leaf senescence by directly activating the expression of NON-YELLOWING 1 (NYE1) and ORESARA 1 (ORE1) (Sakuraba et al., 2014; Song et al., 2014). By contrast, FHY3 integrates age and light signals to inhibit senescence by directly repressing WRKY28, PIF4, ORE1, and NYE1, thereby delaying chlorophyll degradation and extending leaf longevity (Tian et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023a). Although a direct molecular link between phyB and FHY3 in the regulation of leaf senescence has not been established, they coordinately regulate seed germination (Liu et al., 2021b), suggesting that a similar phyB–FHY3 regulatory cascade may also act to inhibit senescence.
Under low R:FR conditions, phyB may regulate senescence through WRKY6 (Lim et al., 2018). In darkness, reduced phyB activity increases PIF5 stability and upregulates downstream genes, ultimately accelerating senescence. Similarly, rice phyB mutants exhibit early leaf senescence during extended darkness. Although phyB has been shown to play an essential role in leaf senescence in Arabidopsis, its role in crop senescence remains poorly understood.

## Shade avoidance responses
Increased planting density is an effective strategy for boosting yield in staple crops such as maize. However, high planting density leads to strong canopy shading, which reduces both internal light intensity and the R:FR ratio, subsequently triggering shade avoidance responses (SARs), characterized by enhanced stem elongation, reduced branching, accelerated flowering, and premature senescence (Franklin, 2008; Casal, 2013). The negative regulatory role of phyB in SARs is well conserved across shade-avoidant species, including Arabidopsis, maize, rice, soybean, sorghum, and tomato (Robson et al., 1993; Finlayson et al., 2007; Sheehan et al., 2007; Weng et al., 2014; Hussain et al., 2019).
phyB suppresses SARs primarily by promoting the degradation of positive regulators such as PIF4, PIF5, and PIF7, which directly activate cell-growth-promoting genes, including YUCs and IAAs involved in auxin biosynthesis and signaling (Casal and Fankhauser, 2023). A recent study revealed that phyB can also positively regulate the methyltransferase activity of the m6A methyltransferase complex, thus participating in SAR regulation through epigenetic modification of RNA, an effect particularly evident in transcripts of genes related to auxin and ABA signaling pathways (such as SAUR15/19/20 and MYB20) (Li et al., 2026). Shade also activates multiple hormone pathways, including auxin, GA, and BR, which act synergistically with downstream transcription factors to promote elongation and other aspects of SARs. Detailed information on these key regulators involved in SARs is available in recent reviews (Liu et al., 2021c; Casal and Fankhauser, 2023; Han et al., 2024; Pereyra et al., 2026).
Prolonged shade inhibits axillary bud outgrowth, reducing branching and promoting the elongation of stems or stalks (Casal, 2013; Schneider et al., 2019). Under shade conditions, phyB regulates BRC1 expression primarily through two major pathways. First, reduced phyB activity leads to accumulation of PIFs, which alleviates miR156-mediated suppression of SPL, subsequently activating BRC1 expression (Wang and Wang, 2015; Xie et al., 2017). Second, impaired phyB function reduces the abundance of FHY3 and FAR1, causing strong induction of BRC1 and inhibition of branch formation (Xie et al., 2020a).
In addition to regulating branch formation, these pathways also influence shade-induced early flowering. Prolonged shade reduces levels of miR156 and FHY3/FAR1, relieving repression of SPL3/4/5, which subsequently activate flowering regulatory genes such as FRUITFULL (FUL) and APETALA1 (AP1), promoting flowering (Xie et al., 2020b). Notably, phyB inactivation under shade enhances CO expression and stability, inducing FT expression, which represents a major pathway for shade-induced early flowering (Valverde et al., 2004; Wollenberg et al., 2008).
In maize, phyB1 phyB2 mutants exhibit typical SARs together with a compact plant architecture, indicating that phyB is a key regulator of plasticity in response to canopy shade (Sheehan et al., 2007; Shi et al., 2024b). During this process, phyB directly interacts with the SPL-type transcription factor LG1, increasing its stability and promoting leaf angle expansion under low-density or high R:FR conditions while decreasing its stability and reducing leaf angle under high-density or low R:FR conditions. Shade rapidly induces the expression of HOMEOBOX-TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 53 (HB53), a positive regulator of SARs that is directly repressed by LG1; consequently, disruption of HB53 significantly reduces plant height, increases leaf angle, and enhances plant density tolerance (Shi et al., 2024b). Interestingly, phyA also plays a crucial role in regulating density tolerance. Under high-density canopy shade, phyA accumulates and interacts with the transcription factor RELATED TO ABI3/VP1-LIKE 1 (RAVL1), promoting its degradation and reducing the expression of LEAF ANGLE ARCHITECTURE OF SMART CANOPY 1 (LAC1), a BR C-22 hydroxylase that primarily controls BR biosynthesis in the upper leaf ligular region, ultimately reducing upper leaf angle under high-density conditions (Tian et al., 2024). These findings offer new insights and are expected to accelerate the development of high-yielding maize varieties better adapted to high-density planting, with improved light use efficiency and enhanced lodging resistance.

## Seed production and crop yield
Previous studies on phyB have focused primarily on mechanistic scientific research, whereas recent efforts have increasingly shifted toward agricultural applications, including strategies to enhance yield and quality. In maize, loss of function of phyB1 or both phyB1 and phyB2 results in an over 40% reduction in biomass and grain yield under field conditions, highlighting the primary role of phyB in growth, biomass, and seed production (Wies et al., 2019). Consistent with these findings, numerous studies have shown that continuous expression of phyB or its hyperactive variants, such as AtPHYBY276H, AtPHYB1Y104F, ZmPHYB1Y98F, ZmPHYB1Y359F, and OsPHYBY283H, significantly reduces plant height and increases yield under tested conditions. These effects have been demonstrated in multiple species, including maize, rice, tobacco, tomato, and potato (Thiele et al., 1999; Wies et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2022b).
In rice, phyB plays an important role in regulating grain size and chalk formation by modulating cell division and starch grain morphology (Li et al., 2022a). Interestingly, the effect of phyB on grain traits appears to depend on subspecies, cultivar, and growing season, as contradictory effects have been reported in different studies (Li et al., 2022a, 2024). Most studies indicate that rice phyB mutants exhibit reduced grain yield per plant, whereas overexpression of phyB often also leads to decreased yield, highlighting the complex role of phyB in yield regulation (Hu et al., 2020). It is generally accepted that phyB regulates grain yield at multiple levels, including tiller number, plant height, heading time, panicle branching, and grain number (Hu et al., 2020). Moreover, phyB mutations in rice increase grain chalkiness and reduce quality. By contrast, appropriate upregulation of phyB is expected to enhance yield and grain quality (Zheng et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022a). Enhanced expression of genes related to chlorophyll biosynthesis, photosynthesis, and starch metabolism by phyB has been observed in Arabidopsis (Shi et al., 2024b). Together, these findings demonstrate that phyB acts as a central hub integrating light signaling and photosynthetic capacity to promote carbohydrate accumulation, thereby facilitating biomass accumulation and overall yield.
In addition to cereal crops, phyB also exerts profound effects on fruit development and quality in horticultural species. In tomato, fruit-specific mutations of SlphyB1 and SlphyB2 disrupt carbohydrate metabolism and carotenoid biosynthesis, highlighting the critical role of phyB in regulating fruit development, nutritional quality, and ripening (Gupta et al., 2014; Bianchetti et al., 2018). By contrast, fruit-specific expression of the active phyB2Y252H variant significantly enhances the accumulation of lycopene, carotenoids, tocopherols, flavonoids, and ascorbic acid, demonstrating the potential of phyB manipulation to improve fruit nutritional quality (Alves et al., 2020). A recent study revealed that SlphyB2 regulates fruit development by activating the transcription of light signaling components (e.g., SlHY5), which subsequently upregulate genes associated with fruit metabolism and ripening, accelerating metabolic reprogramming (Zhang et al., 2026). In addition, SlphyB1 and SlphyB2 contribute to fruit cuticle formation by repressing the downstream transcription factors SlPIF3, SlPIF4, and SlBBX28, ultimately resulting in a thinner fruit cuticle (Fernandes et al., 2026). Thus, phyB represents a key target for improving both yield and quality. Modification of phyB offers a promising avenue for crop genetic improvement, and its precise regulation and deployment hold the potential to drive major advances in high-yield, high-quality, and resource-efficient cultivation.

## Functional diversity of natural variation in phyB
Because phyB is a central regulator of plant growth and development, its natural variation has been the subject of extensive research in multiple species. In Arabidopsis, several coding-region polymorphisms in phyB, including an N-terminal GGGR deletion and I143L, V980I, and L1072V amino acid substitutions, significantly affect seed germination, seedling de-etiolation, and hypocotyl elongation (Ruiz-Diaz et al., 2022). Natural variation in phyB is also closely linked to phosphorus uptake capacity; for example, the reduced phosphate uptake activity observed in accessions Lm-2 and CSHL-5 is attributed to a T89K substitution in phyB (Sakuraba et al., 2018).
Interestingly, similar functional diversification driven by natural variation in phyB has been observed in crops, where such variation is often associated with ecological adaptation and agronomic optimization. In sorghum, directional selection has favored hypofunctional phyB alleles (ma3) that enhance apical dominance, reduce non-productive tillering, and improve adaptation to diverse environments (Childs et al., 1997). In maize, certain inbred lines (e.g., phyB2-F2) harbor a 10-bp deletion at the translational start site, resulting in null alleles of phyB2 and early flowering; conversely, introduction of a functional phyB2 allele significantly delays flowering (Sheehan et al., 2007). Natural variation in the ZmphyB2 promoter also influences flowering time. For example, an “rpt_family” miniature inverted-repeat transposable element insertion in the promoter region reduces phyB2 expression and leads to early flowering (Zhao et al., 2014). Haplotype frequency analysis further revealed that one haplotype (Hap4) has undergone continuous directional selection in modern maize breeding in China (Wang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023).
In rice, genome-wide association studies have identified natural allelic variants at the phyB locus associated with variation in panicle architecture and flowering time. Specifically, the H1 haplotype, defined by a C-to-A substitution in the second exon, confers increased panicle branching and flowering time variation in African rice (Ntakirutimana et al., 2023). Structural variations in the promoter and coding regions of phyB also have important biological significance in vegetables. In cucumber, a ∼4-kb region with large deletions and highly divergent sequences in the phyB promoter and a 5.5-kb terminal repeat retrotransposon insertion in the first exon of phyB both reduce its expression and activity, leading to pronounced hypocotyl elongation and accelerated leaf senescence (Hu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024, 2025a). Collectively, these natural variations in phyB represent valuable genetic resources for crop improvement and enhanced environmental adaptability (Supplemental Table 2).

## The molecular basis of phyB function

## Nuclear import mechanism of phyB
Nuclear import of phyB is a critical early step in light signaling. Unlike phyA, which is strictly dependent on its interaction partners FHY1 and FHY1-LIKE (FHL) for nuclear translocation (Hiltbrunner et al., 2005), phyB uses a distinct regulatory mechanism. Early studies proposed that phyB undergoes autonomous nuclear import via a C-terminal nuclear localization signal (Chen et al., 2005). However, later studies revealed that its nuclear accumulation is also dependent on partner proteins that contain nuclear localization signals (Pfeiffer et al., 2012; Klose et al., 2015). A recent study revealed that perception of R light by phyB specifically induces a transient increase in cytosolic Ca2+ levels, which activates the calcium-dependent protein kinases CPK6 and CPK12 to phosphorylate phyB at Ser80 and Ser106, promoting its nuclear translocation (Zhao et al., 2023). Thus, nuclear import of phyB depends on a light-triggered, calcium-dependent, rapid post-translational modification that ensures precise and responsive light signaling (Zhao et al., 2023; Viczián and Nagy, 2024).

## Structural basis of phyB
phyB is a multidomain signaling protein that senses both light and temperature and undergoes conformational switching for signal transduction (Legris et al., 2016). Recent cryoelectron microscopy studies have resolved its previously elusive 3D architecture and directly visualized distinct conformations of phyB in the R-light-activated Pfr and FR/dark-reverted Pr states (Li et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2024). In darkness, the C-terminal domains PAS2 and histidine-kinase-related domains (HKRD) of one phyB (Pr) molecule interact with the N-terminal Per/Arnt/Sim (nPAS) and cGMP-specific phosphodiesterase/adenylyl cyclase/FhlA (GAF) domains of another phyB (Pr) molecule to form a “head-to-tail” dimeric architecture (Li et al., 2022b). Upon R-light irradiation, phyB undergoes a Pr-to-Pfr conversion, leading to conformational changes, including the transition of the PHY tongue signature from a β-sheet to an α-helix. This conformational change causes steric clashes with the PAS2 domain, disrupting the extensive PAS2-mediated intra- and inter-molecular interactions characteristic of the Pr state, breaking the head-to-tail architecture, and inducing phyB to reorganize into the head-to-head dimerized active Pfr form (Wang et al., 2024). In addition, the APB-β motif of PIF6 binds to the N-terminal extension, inducing the formation of three α-helices (α1–α3), which further stabilize Pfr through hydrophobic interactions and reinforce the α-helical conformation of the PHY tongue via hydrogen bonding, stabilizing the head-to-head phyB–Pfr dimer and increasing the Pr-to-Pfr photoconversion efficiency of phyB (Li et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025).

## Nuclear photobodies of phyB
One of the key cellular processes in phyB signal transduction is the formation of nuclear photobodies, also known as nuclear speckles, upon light perception, a phenomenon first reported in 1999 (Kircher et al., 1999; Chen and Chory, 2011). Increased R:FR ratios, higher light intensity, or elevated phytochrome levels promote the transition from numerous small phyB photobodies to fewer large ones. By contrast, reduced R light or a low R:FR ratio causes large photobodies to disperse into smaller speckles or diffuse throughout the nucleoplasm (Chen et al., 2003; Trupkin et al., 2014; Van Buskirk et al., 2014; Willige et al., 2021). External and internal cues such as high temperature, salt stress, and jasmonic acid signaling also modulate photobody stability (Legris et al., 2016; Hahm et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023).
Since the discovery of phyB photobodies, several key regulators of photobody formation have been identified through genetic screens. HMR was the first positive regulator discovered that directly interacts with phyB without affecting its abundance (Chen et al., 2010). Subsequent genetic screens for hmr-like mutants identified RCB and NUCLEAR CONTROL OF PEP ACTIVITY (NCP), which share partially overlapping functions with HMR in the promotion of photobody formation (Yang et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2019). PAP8/pTAC6, a subunit of the PEP (plastid-encoded RNA polymerase) complex, also influences photobody formation through its interaction with HMR, as pap8 mutants exhibit impaired assembly of large phyB photobodies (Liebers et al., 2020). Although the precise regulatory mechanisms remain unclear, photobody formation appears to share similarities with the assembly of the PEP holoenzyme, as suggested by the dual localization of RCB, NCP, and PAP8 to both the nucleus and the plastid. Notably, a recent study revealed that phyB can interact with Nup96, a component of the nuclear pore complex, at the nuclear envelope to facilitate photobody formation and enhance phyB activity, revealing an additional layer in the assembly and function of phyB photobodies (Wang et al., 2026).
Currently, photobodies are proposed to form via liquid–liquid phase separation and to act as hubs that concentrate signaling components and enhance biochemical specificity (Chen et al., 2022). phyB is thought to act as the scaffold protein within photobodies, and recent mass spectrometry analyses have further refined their molecular composition, revealing that they contain not only phyB but also numerous interacting proteins (Kim et al., 2023). These components are classified as “primary clients,” which directly bind phyB, and “secondary clients,” whose localization depends on the presence of primary clients. At least 13 positive regulators facilitate phyB-mediated light responses, including phyC and phyE; the transcriptional regulators ELF3, HMR, TZP, and BBX4; the Pfr stabilizers PCH1 and PCHL; the kinase SOS2; the phosphatase PAPP5; and the splicing factors SFPS and SWAP1. Negative regulators include the transcriptional repressors PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, PIF5, PIF7, TPL, and TPRs; the kinases PPK1, PPK2, and PPK3; the ubiquitin E3 ligase complex COP1/SPA1; and the splicing factor SMP2 (Kim et al., 2023). Owing to space limitations, only a brief overview of selected components is provided here; for comprehensive discussions, readers are referred to several excellent reviews and studies (Van Buskirk et al., 2012; Ronald and Davis, 2019; Pardi and Nusinow, 2021; Kwon et al., 2024; Willige et al., 2024).
In the nucleus, phyB photobodies dynamically regulate their composition to mediate light signal transduction at multiple layers. First, photobodies enhance the stability of the active phyB (Pfr) form, prolonging light signaling. This stabilization is achieved through binding of PCH1 and PCHL to the Pfr form of phyB, followed by heterodimerization of phyB with phyC or phyE (Huang et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2023). Second, photobodies facilitate degradation or inactivation of negative regulators of light signaling, relieving their suppression of photomorphogenesis. This process involves the phosphorylation of PIFs by SOS2 and PPKs, as well as PIF3 degradation mediated by Light-Response Bric-a-BracK/Tramtrack/Broad proteins (LRBs) (Ni et al., 2014, 2017; Ma et al., 2023). Third, phyB photobodies regulate gene transcription by promoting chromatin remodeling and histone modifications. For example, phyB interacts with SWC6/ARP6 to recruit the SWI/SNF complex to HY5 target genes such as YUC9, enhancing H2A.Z deposition and repressing gene expression (Wei et al., 2021). Finally, photobodies modulate alternative splicing to optimize gene-expression output (Shikata et al., 2014). For comprehensive reviews of phyB photobody function in light signal transduction, readers are referred to several excellent studies (Kwon et al., 2024; Willige et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2025).
Although phyB photobodies have been extensively characterized in Arabidopsis, direct evidence demonstrating the formation of endogenous phyB photobodies in crops is still lacking. However, expression of crop phyB homologs, such as ZmphyB1 and OsphyB, in epidermal cells of N. benthamiana has clearly shown that these proteins can form nuclear photobodies (Zheng et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2024b). Given the central role of phyB in regulating various aspects of plant growth, further studies are needed to confirm the existence and functional significance of endogenous phyB photobodies in crops. A practical approach would be to express GFP-tagged phyB in crop calli or protoplasts to monitor condensate formation, followed by stable genetic transformation and functional analysis in crops, complemented by GFP-based pull-down assays and mass spectrometry to identify associated components.

## Concluding remarks and perspectives
Numerous recent studies have reinforced the central role of phyB in various aspects of plant growth and development, highlighting its importance for crop improvement. Despite these advances, fundamental questions regarding its physiological and molecular functions remain unresolved. Future studies should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which phyB perceives and transduces dynamic light and temperature signals to regulate plant growth and adaptation. It will also be valuable to explore potential roles of phyB in darkness or in the cytoplasm in addition to its well-characterized nuclear function in light signaling. Moreover, the evolutionary diversity of phyB across species warrants deeper investigation, with particular emphasis on the conservation of functional domains and the adaptation of variants to diverse light intensities and temperature conditions. In addition, the influence of phyB on agronomic traits requires further exploration at both physiological and genetic levels. The application of advanced cellular, molecular, and genetic tools—including single-cell and spatial multi-omics, tissue-specific transgenic approaches, and precise gene-editing technologies—will facilitate the optimization of phyB-mediated agricultural traits, such as ideal plant architecture, robust stems/stalks with increased tolerance to high planting density, improved light use efficiency, and enhanced yield potential. Collectively, these efforts will be crucial for addressing the challenges of global food security and agricultural sustainability.
