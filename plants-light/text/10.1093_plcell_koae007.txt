# Genetic regulation of self-organizing azimuthal canopy orientations and their impacts on light interception in maize

## Abstract
AbstractThe efficiency of solar radiation interception contributes to the photosynthetic efficiency of crop plants. Light interception is a function of canopy architecture, including plant density; leaf number, length, width, and angle; and azimuthal canopy orientation. We report on the ability of some maize (Zea mays) genotypes to alter the orientations of their leaves during development in coordination with adjacent plants. Although the upper canopies of these genotypes retain the typical alternate-distichous phyllotaxy of maize, their leaves grow parallel to those of adjacent plants. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) on this parallel canopy trait identified candidate genes, many of which are associated with shade avoidance syndrome, including phytochromeC2. GWAS conducted on the fraction of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) intercepted by canopies also identified multiple candidate genes, including liguleless1 (lg1), previously defined by its role in ligule development. Under high plant densities, mutants of shade avoidance syndrome and liguleless genes (lg1, lg2, and Lg3) exhibit altered canopy patterns, viz, the numbers of interrow leaves are greatly reduced as compared to those of nonmutant controls, resulting in dramatically decreased PAR interception. In at least the case of lg2, this phenotype is not a consequence of abnormal ligule development. Instead, liguleless gene functions are required for normal light responses, including azimuth canopy re-orientation.

## IN A NUTSHELL
Background: The dramatically increased yields of commercial maize hybrids are largely a consequence of improved tolerance to high plant densities. At high plant densities, adjacent plants can shade each other, reducing light penetration into the canopy and yields. Dicots, such as tomato and Arabidopsis, respond to shading by producing leaves that are more upright. Maize does not respond to shading in this manner.
Question: At high plant densities, how do maize plants adjust their canopy architectures to tolerate shading from adjacent plants?
Findings: We report on the ability of some maize genotypes to alter the azimuthal orientations of their leaves during development in coordination with adjacent plants. Although the upper canopies of these genotypes retain the typical alternate-distichous phyllotaxy of maize, their leaves grow parallel to those of adjacent plants. A genome-wide association study conducted on this parallel canopy trait and on the fraction of intercepted photosynthetically active radiation identified candidate genes, many of which are associated with shade avoidance syndrome and ligule development, such as liguleless1 (lg1). Further, mutagenesis studies demonstrated that liguleless gene functions are required for normal light responses, including azimuthal canopy re-orientation.
Next steps: Conduct experiments to investigate the roles of auxin and blue light-induced phototropism in azimuthal canopy re-orientation in maize.

## Introduction
Maize (Zea mays) grain yields in the United States have increased over the past 70 yr, with an estimated yearly gain of 115 kg/ha (Duvick et al. 2004). In parallel, plant densities have increased from 30,000 plants/ha in the 1930s to 75,000 plants/ha or more in the 2000s (Duvick et al. 2004) and as high as 93,000 plants/ha by the mid-2010s (Assefa et al. 2018). Era hybrid studies, which compare hybrids released in different years, demonstrate that at current plant densities modern hybrids have lower per plant grain yields but an improved tolerance to high plant densities (Duvick and Cassman 1999; Duvick 2005; Assefa et al. 2018). Thus, the increased yields of modern hybrids are primarily due to higher plant densities. For an individual plant, shade from adjacent plants increases at high plant densities. Increased shade throughout the growing season can lead to yield loss (Earley et al. 1966; Reed et al. 1988). Thus, canopy architecture (see Table 1), which affects the amount of shade experienced by adjacent plants and therefore light penetration, becomes an important factor influencing individual plant yields at high plant densities (Lambert and Johnson 1978; Toler et al. 1999; Hammer et al. 2009).
Definitions of key terms
One factor affecting canopy architecture is the angle between the leaf blade and stalk. More erect leaves result in greater light penetration into the middle and lower canopy layers. Upon comparing hybrids released in the United States from 1930 to 1990, Duvick et al. (2004) noted more erect leaves in hybrids released after 1970. Similar changes have also been reported in Chinese and European hybrids (Ma et al. 2014; Perez et al. 2019). These changes in leaf angles over time have been hypothesized to be an indirect response to selection for tolerance to higher plant densities that mitigate reductions in light penetration as a result of increased shading (Duvick and Cassman 1999; Duvick 2005; Wei et al. 2018). This hypothesis is supported by studies that introgressed single gene mutants and regulatory elements, such as liguleless2 (lg2), upright plant architecture2 (upa2) and rav-like1 (ravl1), into inbreds or hybrids to generate more upright leaf architectures. At high plant densities, such introgression lines had higher grain yields relative to wild-type controls (Lambert and Johnson 1978; Tian et al. 2019). On the other hand, Ariyanayagam et al. (1974) reported that direct selection for more erect leaves did not significantly increase grain yield at higher plant densities, suggesting that factors other than leaf angle contribute to tolerance to high plant density.
The efficiency of light interception in plants is usually calculated by the fraction of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) intercepted by the canopy. Factors other than leaf angle that affect PAR interception include leaf area index (LAI) and the azimuthal canopy orientation (see Table 1), i.e. the distribution of leaves around the stem as viewed from above relative to the axis of planting (Maddonni and Otegui 1996; Drouet et al. 1999). Both simulation and field studies have shown that light interception increases as more leaves are oriented perpendicular to the row (Toler et al. 1999; Maddonni et al. 2001a; Drouet and Kiniry 2008). Perez et al. (2019) analyzed the leaf patterns of greenhouse-grown plants of 60 European maize hybrids released from 1950 to 2015. They reported that all 60 hybrids exhibited similar canopy patterns, i.e. their leaves were oriented toward the interrow space (see Table 1), suggesting the importance of azimuthal canopy orientation.
In maize, azimuthal canopy orientation has been reported to be affected by plant density. Girardin and Tollenaar (1994) reported that, in Pioneer Hybrid 3902, a higher percentage of leaves 12 to 16 were observed to be perpendicular to the row as plant density increased from 70,000 plants/ha to 100,000 plants/ha. Another study showed that the ability of plants to adjust their azimuthal canopy orientations to be perpendicular to the axis of planting varies across genetic backgrounds (Maddonni et al. 2001b). By blocking far-red light from north–south or east–west directions, Maddonni et al. (2002) showed that this re-orientation is associated with low-red to far-red light ratios (R/FR) in the interrow spaces. This suggests that increased plant densities led to more light interception by adult leaves and a reduction in the R/FR ratio, causing the plants with canopies to adjust their azimuthal orientations to maximize light interception.
In plants, the R/FR ratio has long been known as a key factor triggering multiple responses, which are collectively referred to as the shade avoidance syndrome (SAS) (Casal 2013; Ballaré and Pierik 2017). SAS is well studied in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and typical responses include more erect leaf angles, stem elongation, and increased plant height (Casal 2013; Ballaré and Pierik 2017). Whether SAS is associated with the ability of plants to reorient their azimuthal canopy orientations remains unclear. In maize, several studies have been conducted on gene families participating in SAS, such as phytochrome and phytochrome-interacting factors (pifs) (Kebrom et al. 2006; Dubois et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2019; Wies et al. 2019; Li et al. 2020). Studies on maize SAS genes have reported reduced plant heights and leaf areas, faster flowering times and fewer tillers, but have also not reported alterations to azimuthal canopy orientation. Therefore, whether azimuthal canopy re-orientation (see Table 1) and SAS are regulated by similar or distinct pathways in response to changes in R/FR ratios induced by shading remains unclear.
In this study, we analyzed the genetics of azimuthal canopy orientation and its impact on light interception. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) for the parallel canopy trait (see Table 2) and PAR interception identified multiple candidate genes associated with SAS, extending previous morphological studies that established that azimuthal canopy re-orientation can be trigged by the R/FR ratio. Mutants of some of the candidate genes exhibited altered abilities to undergo azimuthal canopy re-orientation. Unexpectedly, we discovered that azimuthal canopy orientation is also affected by genes that regulate ligule development. Subsequently, we demonstrated that each of the 3 liguleless genes is required for a normal light response, consistent with the observation that liguleless gene function is also required for normal azimuth canopy re-orientation under high plant densities. Finally, we provide a mechanistic model that involves both SAS and blue light-mediated phototropism to explain the ability of some maize genotypes to undergo azimuthal canopy re-orientation.

## Results

## Phenotypic variation for canopy patterns and PAR interception
To explore the diversity of canopy patterns in the shoot apical meristem (SAM) diversity panel (Leiboff et al. 2015), top-view videos were collected at 40 and 50 days after planting (DAP, Materials and methods) over 2 growing seasons. Analysis of individual images from these videos revealed substantial phenotypic variation in canopy patterns among the 356 phenotyped inbred lines. Variation was quantified as the fraction of plants within each row that exhibit mutually parallel azimuthal canopy orientations.
To properly analyze these data, the percentages of plants within a genotype that exhibited the mutually parallel phenotype were transformed to η values (Fig. 1A; see Materials and methods). η values exhibited a moderate entry-mean heritability (h2 = 0.35 ± 0.08) and within years exhibited weak positive correlations between 40 DAP and 50 DAP (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S1). Based on the values of η for a given inbred at 2 timepoints (40 and 50 DAP), inbreds could be divided into 6 clusters (Fig. 2; see Materials and methods). Cluster 3 includes inbreds whose parallel canopy trait values switched from 0% mutually parallel plants to >25% mutually parallel plants 10 d later; clusters 2 and 5 exhibited the opposite pattern. In contrast, clusters 1, 4, and 6 exhibited little or no change between the 2 timepoints (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Data Set 1). Those clusters that exhibit plasticity for the parallel canopy trait illustrate canopy re-orientation. To more fully characterize canopy architecture, we also determined from the 2017 and 2018 videos the fraction of plants within a row whose top 2 fully expanded leaves were orientated toward the interrow spaces (i.e. ≥30° angle to the axis of planting, see Materials and methods). Using these data, we categorized members of the SAM diversity panel into 3 groups: on-row parallel, random, and off-row parallel (Fig. 1B, see Materials and methods).

Because canopy architecture is expected to affect PAR interception, PAR was measured above and below canopies of the SAM diversity panel in each of 2 summers (Materials and Methods; Supplementary Fig. S2). Within years PAR interception (the difference between the above and below canopy measurements of PAR) exhibited high correlations across 3 timepoints within genotypes and years. In contrast, only weak correlations were observed in across-year comparisons (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Data Set 2), probably at least in part due to differences in experimental design between years. For this reason, we compared the estimated genotypic effects of PAR interception of the 3 canopy groups (on-row parallel, random, and off-row parallel, Fig. 1B) separately for the each of the 2 yr. Not surprisingly, the on-row parallel inbreds consistently exhibited lower levels of PAR interception than the other 2 canopy groups (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Data Set 2).

## GWAS on parallel and PAR interception
We conducted GWAS for the parallel canopy trait using FarmCPU (Liu et al. 2016; Kusmec and Schnable 2018). GWAS was conducted on each of the single timepoints (40 and 50 DAP over 2 yr) separately and on predicted genotypic effects based on inbred measurements at 2 timepoints over the 2 yr (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S5). The combined analysis identified 45 peaks. For each peak, the single SNP with the highest −log10(lfsr) was designated a trait-associated SNP (TAS). The 3 TASs with the highest −log10(lfsr) values were inspected first. The genes closest to 2 of these 3 TASs (on Chr. 2 and. 5) have orthologs in other plants that have been associated with SAS. The TAS on Chr. 2 is 1.9 kb upstream of basic helix-loop-helix157 (bhlh157), whose Arabidopsis ortholog is BES1-INTRACTING MYC-LIKE PROTEIN 2 (BIM2), which positively modulates SAS (Cifuentes-Esquivel et al. 2013). The TAS on Chr. 5 is 9.1 kb upstream of phytochrome C2 (phyC2), the homolog of which has been shown to be involved in far-red (FR) light perception in rice (Oryza sativa) and maize (Takano et al. 2005; Yang et al. 2014; Woods et al. 2014; Li et al. 2020). The stability of the PHYC protein requires a functional phyB allele (Takano et al. 2005), which is a key gene in SAS (Casal 2013; Ballaré and Pierik 2017).

Maize canopy re-orientation can be triggered by changes in the R/FR ratio (Maddonni et al. 2002). Given this and the initial GWAS results, we hypothesized that genes that regulate SAS may contribute to variation in the parallel canopy trait. To test this hypothesis, we identified maize orthologs of a set of Arabidopsis genes associated with SAS (Casal 2013; Kegge et al. 2015). The genomic positions of the resulting 39 maize orthologs were compared to the positions of the 42 remaining TASs from the GWAS for the parallel canopy trait (see Materials and methods). This process resulted in a collection of 8 candidate genes (Fig. 3), 5 of which have been reported to participate in light responses (Table 2), including phyA2 and a phyA suppressor (chr. 8; Table 2, Supplementary Data Set 3). Based on a permutation test (Materials and methods), this outcome is statistically unlikely to have occurred by chance (permutation test, P < 0.001), providing strong support for the hypothesis that canopy re-orientation is regulated by SAS.
Six candidate genes were identified via GWAS conducted on the 4 individual timepoint-year combinations (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. S5). Three of these candidate genes overlapped with those identified via the combined analysis of the parallel canopy trait (Table 2). The constitutive expressor of pathogenesis-related genes5/hypersenescence1 (cpr5/hys1) gene, the Arabidopsis ortholog of one of the other 3 candidate genes (GRMZM2G158811), participates in constitutive disease resistance and regulates leaf senescence under dark conditions and PHYTOCHROME B/CRYPTOCHROME 1 (PHYB/CRY1)-dependent light responses (Bowling et al. 1997; Yoshida et al. 2002; Gu et al. 2016). Hence, in combination, our GWAS results provide genetic support for the observation of Maddonni et al. (2002) that reduced R/FR ratios trigger canopy re-orientation.
We then conducted GWAS on PAR interception measured at each of 3 single timepoints within each of 2 yr, as well as combined analyses across the 3 timepoints within each of the 2 yr (Supplementary Fig. S6). In combination, these 8 GWAS identified 3 candidate genes (Table 2). Two of these candidate genes (from 3 TASs) have orthologs from other plant species that have been associated with light signaling responses or photosynthetic activity. The third candidate gene (Fig. 3C), liguleless1 (lg1) encodes a protein that affects ligule and auricle development resulting in more upright leaves and shorter blades in both maize and rice (Moreno et al. 1997; Foster et al. 2004; Lee et al. 2007; Lewis et al. 2014). The identification of this gene from this GWAS is not surprising given that PAR interception is affected by LAI, which is itself affected by leaf angle (Falster and Westoby 2003; Niinemets 2010). Interestingly, the LG1 protein belongs to the SQUAMOSA-PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN (SBP) family, which has been proposed to participate in SAS (Wei et al. 2018).

## Validation of candidate genes via reverse genetics
Several candidate genes, including those identified via GWAS for the parallel canopy trait (phyC2 and cpr5) or for PAR interception (lg1), were selected for mutant analysis. Two additional genes were selected for analysis based on their liguleless phenotypes (lg2 and lg3) even though they were not identified in the GWAS. Finally, a mutant of phyB2 was included because it is a key gene in SAS.
We confirmed and characterized 6 Mu transposon insertions in 4 of these candidate genes: phyC2, cpr5, lg2, and lg1 (Materials and Methods; Supplementary Fig. S7). In addition, we characterized 3 previously described mutants—phyB2-1256(W22), lg2(A632), and the dominant Lg3-O mutant in candidate genes (Table 3). The dominant Lg3-O mutant was available in 2 genetic backgrounds (Ky21 and W64), which were analyzed in parallel. Each of these 8 mutant alleles was grown in replicated 4-row plots at 2 locations during the summer of 2021 and characterized for changes in both azimuthal canopy orientation and PAR interception. We began our characterization of these mutants by recording the total number of leaves orientated toward the interrow space between a pair of rows in each of the 4-row plots at 3 timepoints (40, 50, and 60 DAP). We designate this trait as the “number of interrow leaves” (Materials and methods). As compared with the wild-type W22 control, mutants in 4 of the candidate genes, cpr5, lg1, lg2, and both alleles of phyC2, exhibited significantly fewer interrow leaves during at least 1 of the 3 timepoints (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figs. S8 and S9, Supplementary Data Set 4).

To test whether these reduced numbers of interrow leaves were caused by other alterations in the canopy architectures of the mutants, we determined leaf numbers and leaf angles, as well as several other canopy architecture traits (Fig. 5, Supplementary Data Set 2). The differences in numbers of interrow leaves cannot be explained by differences in total numbers of leaves because no significant differences were detected in the total leaf number between any of the mutants and their controls at any of the 3 timepoints (Supplementary Data Set 4). Similarly, all tested mutants, except for lg1-mu(W22), exhibited either nonsignificant differences in leaf angles or only small differences (i.e. less than 5°). We therefore conclude that the observed reductions in the number of interrow leaves in the mutants were caused by changes in the azimuthal orientations of the mutant canopies. Notably, in these mutants and their wild-type controls, the number of interrow leaves was highly correlated with PAR interception, providing strong support for the conclusion that azimuthal canopy orientations can affect light interception (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S10).

Forty percent to 70% of leaves in the control rows had azimuthal angles between 60 to 120° and 240 to 300°, i.e. were roughly perpendicular to the axis of planting (Fig. 7). Mutants of all candidate genes (with the exception of phyB2) exhibited altered azimuthal canopy orientations. The lg1 mutant exhibited the most dramatically altered canopy pattern; it had 50% fewer interrow leaves as compared to its wild-type W22 inbred control. Based on analyses of both side and top views, the leaves of lg1 mutants were primarily stacked on top of each other and aligned with the axis of planting, leaving the interrow space essentially empty (Supplementary Fig. S8). We are not aware of prior literature reporting this azimuthal canopy phenotype in this well-studied mutant. Because the other 2 liguleless genes (lg2 and lg3), like lg1, encode transcription factors involved in defining the blade-sheath boundary, we tested whether these genes also affected this azimuthal canopy phenotype. We did this using 2 existing alleles of lg2 (lg2(A632) and lg2-mu(W22)) and the dominant Lg3 mutant allele, Lg3-O, introgressed into 2 inbred genetic backgrounds, i.e. Lg3-O(Ky21) and Lg3-O(W64). Mutants of both of these liguleless genes exhibited reduced numbers of interrow leaves as compared to controls (Fig. 7, B and C, Supplementary Figs. S12 and S13), indicating that all 3 liguleless genes affect not only leaf angles but also azimuthal canopy orientations.
The standard lg2(A632) mutant lacks ligules and exhibits altered leaf angles throughout development (Walsh et al. 1998). In contrast, only the first 3 seedling leaves of the lg2-mu (W22) mutant lack ligules (Supplementary Fig. S14); adult plants homozygous for this mutant exhibit normal ligules and auricles (Fig. 9) and adult leaf angles similar to those of its wild-type control (Fig. 5). Importantly, adult lg2-mu(W22) mutant plants exhibit tassel architecture and azimuthal canopy orientation phenotypes similar to those of lg2(A632) (Fig. 7, A and C), despite having wild-type ligules and leaf angles. In addition, no significant differences were observed in measured leaf traits between the Lg3-O(Ky21) mutant and its wild-type allele controls (Supplementary Fig. S15, Supplementary Data Set 4). Even so, this mutant also exhibited altered azimuthal canopy orientations (Fig. 7B) and significantly fewer interrow leaves (Supplementary Fig. S9, Supplementary Data Set 4) than its wild-type control. These results establish that the alterations in azimuthal canopy orientations and reduced numbers of interrow leaves are not an indirect consequence of the more vertical leaf angles associated with the liguleless mutants. Instead, we hypothesize that the observed differences in azimuthal canopy orientation associated with the liguleless mutants are due to a reduced sensitivity to the presence of adjacent plants.

## Liguleless genes affect canopy responses to plant density
To test this hypothesis, lg1-mu(W22) and lg2(A632) were planted at 4 densities together with the appropriate W22 and A632 controls and 3 additional inbred lines (1 from each of the 3 canopy patterns described in Fig. 1). Among wild-type inbred lines, as plant densities increased, the number of interrow leaves per plant also increased (Fig. 10, Supplementary Fig. S16), in agreement with previous studies (Maddonni and Otegui 1996; Drouet et al. 1999). In contrast, at high plant densities, the liguleless mutants either failed to exhibit a significant increase in the number of interrow leaves (lg1-mu(W22) vs. W22 (P = 0.23)) or even exhibited a significant reduction in the number of interrow leaves (lg2(A632) vs. A632 (P = 0.006)) (Fig. 11A, Supplementary Fig. S17). Similarly, over 2 yr, the liguleless mutant canopies exhibited more substantial deviations from the perpendicular relative to the axis of planting as compared with wild-type canopies (Fig. 11B) at high, but not low, plant densities (Fig. 11, C to F, Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Fig. S18). These results indicate that the lg1 and lg2 genes contribute to canopy responses to plant density.

## Liguleless gene functions are required for normal light responses during germination
The altered canopy responses described above led us to hypothesize that the liguleless genes are involved in light responses within the plant. To test this hypothesis, liguleless mutant seeds were planted together with their appropriate wild-type controls and grown in complete darkness or in a normal light/dark cycle (see Materials and methods). At 10 DAP, all of the liguleless mutants grown in the dark exhibited significantly shorter mesocotyls than their wild-type controls, but not when grown under a normal light/dark cycle (Fig. 12, Supplementary Fig. S19, Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Data Set 4). These results support our hypothesis that liguleless genes are involved in plant responses to light.

## Discussion
By affecting light interception and therefore overall photosynthetic efficiency, variation in canopy architecture contributes to dry matter accumulation and yield (Winter and Ohlrogge 1973; Tollenaar and Bruulsema 1988). Canopy architecture has, therefore, been hypothesized to have been an indirect target of selection through its contribution to tolerance to high plant densities (Duvick et al. 2004; Ma et al. 2014; Perez et al. 2019). Consistently, at high plant densities, some maize genotypes orient themselves parallel to each other and perpendicular to the axis of planting (Girardin and Tollenaar 1994; Toler et al. 1999; Maddonni et al. 2001a). In a maize seedling, the orientation of the first leaves is determined by the orientation in which the corresponding kernel was planted (Drouet and Moulia 1997; Toler et al. 1999; Torres et al. 2011). Thus, the observation that, for some genotypes later in development, individual plants within a row are oriented parallel to each other implies that at least some plants in that row must have rotated during development (Drouet and Moulia 1997). We have confirmed that some, but not all, genotypes have the ability to alter their azimuthal orientations during development (Fig. 2), a process we term azimuthal canopy re-orientation, which affects the parallel canopy trait.
GWAS for the parallel canopy trait identified multiple TASs. Because azimuthal canopy re-orientation can be affected by altered R/FR ratios (Maddonni et al. 2002), and altered R/FR can trigger SAS, we decided to focus on candidate genes associated with SAS. Significantly, more of the TASs were closely linked to genes involved in SAS (Table 2) than expected by chance, supporting the view that azimuthal canopy re-orientation is indeed a component of SAS. Mutants of both SAS-related candidate genes (phyC2 and cpr5) selected for analysis produced fewer interrow leaves and their canopies exhibited corresponding decreases in PAR interception as compared to wild-type controls. Phyllotaxy refers to the sequence and arrangement of leaves on a plant stem that is established during organogenesis as leaf primordia arise from the flanks of the SAM (reviewed by Kuhlemeier 2007 ). A key finding of the current study is that in response to environmental factors such as plant density, maize has the ability to substantially alter its phyllotaxy (viz, azimuthal canopy orientation) post-organogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S16). We therefore refer to the genes identified in this study that are associated with this process as “phyllotaxy genes”.
In dicots such as Arabidopsis and tomato in which SAS has been more extensively studied than in maize, SAS typically results in changes in the vertical plane, including more erect leaves, increased height, branching, and petiole elongation (Casal 2013; Ballaré and Pierik 2017). In these 2 species, SAS has not been reported to result in changes in azimuthal canopy re-orientation. In contrast, when grown at high plant densities, the heads of sunflowers have the ability to track the sun during the day, but this process does not include azimuthal canopy re-orientation as observed in maize. In addition, the rotation of sunflower heads is a consequence of stem rotation rather than of leaves (Atamian et al. 2016; López Pereira et al. 2017). Our observations of liguleless mutants (Fig. 11) suggest that in maize, azimuthal canopy re-orientation is achieved at least in part by twisting or rotation of leaves (Fig. 13), but further investigations to test whether stem rotation might contribute to canopy re-orientation would be valuable.
Model for canopy re-orientation. Yellow elongated arrows indicate light from the interrow space intercepted by leaves, as observed from above. Red arrows mark directions of auxin transport. A) Plants within a high-density axis of planting where the horizontal black dashed line marks the axis of planting (left) and examples of lg1 and lg2 mutants exhibiting negative phototropism in which leaves (marked by white rectangles) turn away from the light in the interrow space (photos on right). B to G) An individual wild-type (panels B to D) or mutant (panels E to G) leaf from red box in panel A is shown. Although substantial amounts of light reach the leaf surface from above, light is also received from the sides. More light is received from the interrow space than from the intra-row space because of shading by adjacent plants. This differential illumination triggers positive phototropism in wild-type plants (panels B to D) and negative phototropism in leaves of lg1 and lg2 mutants (panels E to G).
Conceptually, azimuthal canopy re-orientation requires not only the ability to physically reorient the canopy but also the ability to perceive the existence and orientation of neighboring plants and transduce this information. In plants, light perception involves multiple photoreceptors including cryptochromes, phototropins, and phytochromes (Casal 2013). Among the 3 main phytochromes, PHYA, PHYB, and PHYC, PHYB plays the predominant role in SAS under low R:FR ratios (Casal 2013; Ballaré and Pierik 2017). The maize genome contains 2 phyB genes, phyB1 and phyB2, both of which encode proteins that contribute to R/FR responses (Sheehan et al. 2007; Whipple et al. 2011). Neither of these genes was detected in our GWAS, but because causative genes are not always detected in a GWAS (Pearson and Manolio 2008; Tibbs Cortes et al. 2021), we analyzed a mutant (phyB2-12058) of the phyB2 gene. Although we did not observe significant differences in the number of interrow leaves in this mutant (Supplementary Fig. S9), the limited seed supply reduced the statistical power of this characterization. Hence, additional characterizations of phyB2-12058, and similar characterizations of the phyB1 mutant as well as their double mutant, are warranted.
For the other 2 phytochromes, PHYA is critical for the de-etiolation of Arabidopsis seedlings under low light, while phyC mutants display increased hypocotyl elongation under red light (Franklin and Quail 2010). In rice, both PHYA and PHYC perceive far-red light, and PHYC is also involved in blue light-induced leaf blade declination (Takano et al. 2005). In maize, the PHYC protein is encoded by 2 genes. The maize phyC1 and phyC2 genes can both complement the elongated hypocotyl phenotype of the Arabidopsis phyC mutant under constant red-light conditions, suggesting that both maize PHYC proteins may be involved in SAS (Li et al. 2020). We found that both mutants of the phyC2 gene altered azimuthal canopy re-orientation, resulting in significant reductions in the numbers of interrow leaves per plant (Figs. 4 and 5, Supplementary Data Set 2). This suggests that PHYC2 plays a role in SAS via azimuthal canopy re-orientation. Although, our results provide strong genetic evidence for the role of SAS in azimuthal canopy re-orientation. It is, however, likely that pathways other than SAS may also contribute to this trait.
Our GWAS for PAR interception identified lg1 as a candidate gene. This gene and lg2 were originally defined by mutants having erect leaves that lacked normal ligules (Moreno et al. 1997; Walsh et al. 1998). Here, we demonstrated that both lg1 and lg2 mutants also exhibit altered azimuthal canopy orientations and implicate these genes in plant responses to their neighbors. Although some mutant alleles of lg2 affect ligules and auricles throughout development, adult leaves of plants homozygous for the lg2-mu allele are morphologically identical to those of the W22 control. Even though these lg2-mu mutant plants develop normal ligules and auricles, they exhibit altered azimuthal canopy orientations. Hence, this mutant's effect on azimuthal canopy orientation is not simply a consequence of structural abnormalities at the base of its leaves (i.e. relating to the ligule and auricle). Instead, we hypothesize that their altered canopy phenotype is due to malfunctions in the mutant's ability to sense and/or respond to light. If this mutant had simply lost the ability to sense its neighbors, transduce signals related to the existence or positions of neighbors, or more generally, initiate SAS, we would expect their azimuthal canopy orientations to exhibit random patterns, but this is not the case. Mutants in both lg1 and lg2 exhibit specific responses to increasingly dense planting densities.
The elongation of maize mesocotyls during germination is auxin- and brassinosteroid-dependent (Kutschera and Wang 2016) and this elongation is inhibited under red light (Vanderhoef and Briggs 1978). Hence, our observation that liguless mutant seedlings grown in complete darkness exhibit shorter mesocotyls than wild-type controls (Fig. 12) supports our hypothesis that the lg1 and lg2 genes function in light response via SAS. In addition, these germination studies also indicate that the light response functions of these genes begin early in plant development. In maize, mutations in both lg1 and lg2 genes result in the downregulation of genes associated with auxin biosynthesis and transport, such as PIN-formed (PIN) (Johnston et al. 2014; Xiao et al. 2022). In combination, these data suggest a mechanism by which the liguleless genes, in response to light signals could regulate canopy re-orientation toward the better lit interrow spaces via auxin biosynthesis and signaling.
In maize, canopy orientation is affected by planting density (Fig. 10). Below, we propose a model to explain this phenomenon (Fig. 13). In Arabidopsis, a blue light-mediated auxin gradient is essential for positive phototropism in hypocotyls and negative phototropism in roots (Vanneste and Friml 2009; Fankhauser and Christie 2015). In Arabidopsis, the auxin gradient in the hypocotyl is mediated by phototropin (PHOT) proteins, such as PHOT1 and PHOT2, and the expression of the phot1 and phot2 genes is upregulated under low levels of blue light. Within cells on the lit side of hypocotyl, blue light results in the self-phosphorylation of the PHOT1 and PHOT2 proteins and further cause relocation of PIN proteins to the shaded side of the cell (Łabuz et al. 2012; Liscum et al. 2014). This relocation results in the transport of auxin away from the lit side of the hypocotyl to the shaded side, triggering positive phototropism (Liscum et al. 2014).
We hypothesize that the products of the candidate genes tested in this study may contribute to a similar process during plant density-dependent canopy re-orientation in maize. At higher plant densities, individual maize plants receive less light due to increased levels of shading by neighbors. This shading results in both reduced R/FR ratios and reduced levels of blue light. Under current agronomic practices, as the density of maize plants increases within a field, spacing between plants within a row decreases, while interrow spacing remains constant. Hence, particularly prior to canopy closure, most of the increased shading due to increased plant density is contributed by intra-row neighbors. When they first emerge, most leaves are not perpendicular to the row direction. In such instances, 1 side of the leaf is farther from the better lit interrow space and is therefore exposed to lower R/FR ratios and lower levels of blue light than the opposite side of the leaf that is closer to the interrow space (Casal 2013).
In our model (Fig. 13, B to D), as in Arabidopsis, phytochrome in the leaf tip senses the reduced R/FR ratio, triggering the synthesis of auxin and its transport to the leaf base along the leaf veins. This auxin then upregulates the expression of the lg1 or lg2 gene, thereby stimulating additional auxin synthesis at the leaf base. Our model includes a similar phot1-mediated process in the base of maize leaves, allowing auxin to accumulate on the shaded side of leaf bases. This results in cell elongation and the gradual re-orientation of the leaf toward the better lit interrow space (Fig. 13, C and D).
Our data indicate that this canopy re-orientation is disrupted in mutants of the maize lg1 and lg2 genes (Fig. 13A). Surprisingly, rather than simply not exhibiting canopy re-orientation, such mutants exhibit negative phototropism (Fig. 13, E to G). Because a pilot RNA-seq analysis demonstrated that mutations in either the lg1 gene or the lg2 gene result in a ∼1.5 fold upregulation of phot1 (Supplementary Fig. S20, Materials and Methods), the regulation of phot1 in Arabidopsis provides a potential explanation for the negative phototropism of the lg1 and lg2 mutants. An Arabidopsis ortholog of the maize lg1, SPL8, is involved in the activation of APETALA1 (AP1), whose expression results in the downregulation of phot1 (Wang et al. 2009; Smaczniak et al. 2012; Winter et al. 2015). This is relevant because in Arabidopsis roots, the PHOT1 protein can mediate negative phototropism. In contrast to its behavior in hypocotyls, on the lit side of roots, the PHOT1 protein mediates the accumulation of PIN proteins on the upper sides of transition zone cells. Consequently, roots grow away from blue light (Mo et al. 2015). There is another possible mechanism to explain the negative phototropism associated with mutants of the lg1 and lg2 genes. This explanation relies upon the observation, as discussed above, that these mutants may result in the suppression of IAA biosynthetic genes and PIN genes at the leaf base. This suppression could in turn decrease auxin levels or auxin signaling. Our observation that mesocotyl elongation is reduced during germination of liguleless mutants in the dark provides support for this mechanism. This is relevant because in Arabidopsis negative phototropism can also be triggered when auxin levels are reduced or disrupted in the stem (Bilderback 1984; Sato et al. 2015). In rare genotypes, canopy re-orientation results in the maladaptive on-row parallel canopy phenotype (Fig. 1). We hypothesize that this maladaptive canopy re-orientation is also the result of negative phototropism. Hence, this study provides multiple testable hypotheses worthy of further investigation. Such as:
The perception of blue light is involved in the initiation of azimuthal canopy re-orientation (Fig. 13C).
The light signal necessary for azimuthal canopy re-orientation is transduced via auxin signaling pathway (Fig. 13B).
Low auxin level at the leaf base triggers negative phototropism in liguleless mutants (Fig. 13, F and G).
Although the exact relationships among the liguleless genes and those associated with SAS and phototropism require further study, our identification of multiple candidate genes related with these 2 pathways, including phyC2, phyA2, phot2, and cry4, provides support for our hypothesis that the ability of the canopy to re-orientate is largely a consequence of adaptive responses to light signals. In summary, this study sheds light on a poorly characterized mechanism that has contributed to the adaptation of maize to increased plant densities. Given that most of the substantial increases in per acre yields of maize achieved since the 1930s have been the result of increased plant densities, rather than via increases in per plant yields (Duvick 2005), this study has the potential to contribute to future yield increases in other grasses.

## Materials and methods

## Plant materials
A subset of maize (Z. mays) inbred lines (N = 356) from the SAM maize diversity panel (Leiboff et al. 2015) was grown at the Iowa State University Ag Engineering and Agronomy Research Farm in Boone, Iowa (summer 2017), and the Curtiss Farm in Ames, Iowa (summer 2018). Each genotype was planted in a single 1.27 m length row, with an average of ∼25.4 cm spacing between plant within rows. Rows were oriented east–west with interrow spacings of ∼76.2 cm (∼84,000 plants/ha) and ∼88.9 cm (∼74,000 plants/ha) for the 2017 and 2018 experiments, respectively. Due to low germination rates, it was not possible to score every genotype for parallel canopy trait every year. In 2017 and 2018, 324 and 337 inbreds were scored, respectively. A total of 356 inbreds were scored in at least 1 yr.
The same population was grown during the summers of 2019 and 2020 to phenotype PAR interception. The 2019 experiment was planted at the Curtiss Farm in Ames, Iowa in 2-row plots using ∼1.06 m row length and ∼15.24 cm plant spacing. To evaluate the effect of row direction, 1 field was planted in an east–west orientation (with interrow space of ∼88.9 cm, equivalent to ∼74,000 plants/ha) and the second field was planted in a north–south orientation (with an interrow space of ∼76.2 cm, equivalent to ∼84,000 plants/ha). The 2020 experiment was planted at the Iowa State University Bennet Farm in Ames, Iowa. Inbreds were planted in 4-row plots with 3.04 m row length, ∼15.24 cm within-row spacing, and ∼76.2 cm interrow spacing (∼84,000 plants/ha).
In this study, data were collected at 3 major timepoints, 40, 50 and 60 DAP, that represent different developmental stages. Fifty DAP is the timepoint at which early flowering genotypes in the diversity panel reached anthesis (i.e. 50% of plants in a row started shedding pollen). By 60 DAP, at least 50% of plants in ∼50% of genotypes in the diversity panel had either begun tassel emergence or anthesis.

## Phenotyping PAR interception
Two measurements of PAR were collected. Above-canopy PAR was collected using 2 Li-190R sensors attached to the top of a 1.27 m long selfie stick with a 3D printed mount part specifically for the 2 sensors (Supplementary Fig. S2A). A Li-191R line sensor was put on a utility cart (Gorilla GOR400 400 lb cart, 40.6″ × 18.3″ × 21.6″, wheel with side panels removed) to collect ground-level (i.e. below canopy) PAR in the interrow space (Supplementary Fig. S2B). The 3 sensors were connected to a Li-1500 data logger. The logger was set to manual mode to collect both above-canopy and ground-level (below canopy) PAR when the cart was placed in the middle of interrow space of a 2-row (2019) or 4-row (2020) plot (Supplementary Fig. S2B). We estimated the amount of PAR intercepted by the canopy by subtracting the measurement of ground-level PAR from the average of the 2 above-canopy measurements. The subtracted value was then divided by the average of the 2 above-canopy measurements as estimate of PAR interception (Supplementary Fig. S2A).
In 2019 and 2020, PAR measurements were collected during 3 consecutive calendar wk. The 3 dates of data collection for the east–west planted 2019 field and the 2020 field occurred at approximately 40, 50, and 60 DAP (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Dates of data collection were occasionally shifted due to rainy weather. To control for diurnal variation in solar radiation, each field was divided into 3 blocks. At each timepoint, PAR measurements were collected in three 3-d blocks between 10:45 AM and 1:00 PM on each day. For each 3-d block, field blocks were measured in different orders following a Latin square design. Data for the 2019 field with north–south rows were collected using the same sampling design beginning on the first day after the completion of data collection for the east–west row field (Supplementary Fig. S2C).
In 2019, data collection spanned July 15th to August 8th. PAR measurements were collected once for each genotype per collection day. ## GWAS and candidate gene screening
GWAS was conducted using the SNP data set described in Leiboff et al. (2015) using the FarmCPUpp (Liu et al. 2016; Kusmec and Schnable 2018) package in R (version 3.4.2). Optimization of population structure and bin selection were conducted as previously described (Zhou et al. 2021). Estimated effects and standard errors for each SNP were used to estimate the local false sign rate (lfsr) using the “ashr” package (https://github.com/stephens999/ashr) in R (Stephens 2017 ). SNPs with lfsr < 0.05 were declared statistically significant.
The identified TASs were compared with 39 maize genes (Supplementary Data Set 5), whose orthologs in Arabidopsis had been reported to be associated with SAS (Casal 2013; Kegge et al. 2015). Genes from this list were considered candidate genes if their physical positions were within a 160 kb window centered on a TAS (i.e. 80 kb upstream and 80 kb downstream). This window was selected based on averaged LD decay and our experience with this diversity panel (Zhou et al. 2021). TASs not adjacent to genes in this list but belonging to visually distinct GWAS peaks were sorted by lfsr, and the TAS with the lowest lfsr from each peak was used to screen for candidate genes. Any AGPv2 gene models (https://www.maizegdb.org/gbrowse/maize_v2) that were located within a 160 kb interval surrounding these TASs were then used for the next round of screening. Based on a literature review, such genes were considered candidates if orthologs in rice, sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), or Arabidopsis (https://maize-pangenome-ensembl.gramene.org/Zea_maysb73/) were reported to participate in plant development (Table 1). In the case of X4_178140330, which identified cpr5, the screening distance was extended to 200 kb because there were no functional genes within the 160 kb window.
For GWAS on parallel canopy trait, every GWAS that identified genes from the 39 listed SAS genes, a permutation test with 1,000 iterations was performed by randomly drawing the same number (n equal to number of significant GWAS peaks visualized in Fig. 3) of TASs from the genome-wide SNPs and screened for candidate genes using the same procedure.

## Phenotyping phyllotaxy mutants
Nine mutants of 6 candidate genes were phenotypically evaluated during the summer of 2021. Five Mu-insertion mutants (McCarty et al. 2013), lg1-mu(W22), lg2-mu(W22), cpr5-mu(W22), phyC2-mu1(W22), phyC2-mu2(W22) (Supplementary Fig. S7; Supplementary Data Set 6), together with lg2(A632) (backcrossed 5 times to A632 then selfed for 6 generations) were obtained from the maize stock center (http://maizecoop.cropsci.uiuc.edu/, Table 3) in 2018 and 2019. The Mu-insertion phyb2-12058 allele that had been introgressed 4 times into the inbred W22 (Sheehan et al. 2007; Qiao et al. 2020) was kindly provided by Dr. Michael J. Scanlon (Cornell University). For Mu-insertion alleles obtained from the maize genetics stock center putative homozygotes were confirmed by genotyping (Supplementary Fig. S5) and then selfed for 2 generations. The Lg3-O mutant (Maize Stock Center ID: 304G; Schnable Lab Accession No. 730) was obtained from the maize stock center in 1994. It was first crossed to the inbreds W64 and Ky21 and then phenotypically heterozygous Lg3-O plants were backcrossed to W64 or Ky21 for 6 generations.
In summer 2021, these 9 mutants were planted at the Ag Engineering and Agronomy Research Farm (“Agronomy Farm”) in Boone, Iowa, and the “Johnson Farm” in Ames, Iowa. At each location, mutants were planted in 2 replicated 4-row plots. Due to a limited number of seeds, the lg2-mu stock was only planted in 1 replicate at each location, and the empty plot in the other replicate was filled with W22 R1-r as a control for the UniformMu stocks. In addition, W22, W64, A632, and Ky21 were planted as wild-type controls in each replicate. The row length was ∼2.28 m and the interrow spacing ∼76 cm. All plots, except Lg3-O, were jab-planted on May 26, 2021 with 2 kernels per jab and thinned at 10 DAP to achieve a plant density of ∼84,000 plants/ha. The segregating BC6F1 populations of Lg3-O in the W64 and Ky21 backgrounds were first planted in the greenhouse on May 27, 2021. After phenotyping for the liguleless phenotype at the V3–V4 stage, a total of 512 homozygous lg3/lg3 wild-type and 512 Lg3-O/lg3 heterozygous phenotypically liguleless seedling plants were transplanted to the 2 locations on 10 June (Agronomy) and 11 June (Johnson) (128 plants per replicate per location). Transplanted seedlings were irrigated once immediately after transplanting and again 4 d after transplanting.
In the summer of 2022, 5 of the above mutants, lg1-mu(W22), lg2-mu(W22), cpr5-mu(W22), phyC2-mu1(W22), phyC2-mu2(W22), lg2(A632) and 1 additional allele of cpr5, cpr5-mu2(W22) (Supplementary Data Set 6), were planted following the same field design, involving 2 replicates at each of 2 locations.
Starting at 40 DAP and continuing for 3 consecutive wk, azimuthal canopy orientation and PAR interception measurements were collected. PAR interception was then calculated following the protocol described above for the diversity panel in 2020. For each genotype, 3 PAR measurements were collected from each replicate. To evaluate azimuthal canopy orientation in the field, rather than using a top-view imaging system as described above, we first selected 2 adjacent rows within a plot, typically the middle 2 rows, except in those rare cases in which 1 of the middle 2 rows had fewer than 10 plants (after planting 16 kernels planted per row) under which condition the first or last 2 rows in the plot were used. The rater stood in the middle of the interrow space and identified a bounding box ∼60 cm (24 in.) wide centered on the rater and 10 consecutive plants deep (i.e. 20 plants in total, 10 from each row). Any leaves, including the leaves that had not fully emerged but already exhibited clear orientations, from these 20 plants that entered the marked ∼60 cm wide space were counted as interrow leaves (Supplementary Fig. S21). If the number of germinated plants or their distribution within the row did not provide the intended density, the plot was not selected to collect azimuthal canopy orientation. As a result, 1 replicate of phyC2-mu1 at Agronomy and 1 replicate of cpr5 and phyC2-mu2 at Johnson were excluded. To check if plants were measured at similar developmental stages, at the same time as we collected the orientation trait, 6 plants (3 per row) were randomly selected from the 20 scored plants, and the total number of visible leaves on each plant was recorded and compared with their wild-type controls (Supplementary Data Set 2). This information was later used to estimate the effects of azimuthal canopy orientation on PAR interception as described below.
At 60 DAP, top-view images of genotypes described above were collected from both locations using a DJI (Shenzhen DJI Sciences and Technologies Ltd., Shenzhen, China, 518057) Phantom4 Pro UAV. In each image, angles of azimuthal canopy orientation were measured from 10 consecutive plants from each of the 2 middle rows of 4-row plots. These plants exhibited the typical alternate-distichous phyllotaxy of maize (Fig. 7). The top-most fully expanded leaf was selected from each side of each plant (Supplementary Fig. S22). Thus 20 plants, i.e. 40 leaves per genotype per replication, were measured, for a total of 40 plants per genotype per location. The only exception was lg2-mu, which due to a shortage of seed was only planted a single replicate per location (i.e. only 20 plants per location were measured). The 3 top-most leaves, the flag leaf and the 2 leaves below the flag leaf, were in most instances still upright and not fully expanded and thus were not measured. As a result, the measured leaves were typically the third and fourth leaves below the flag leaf. The only exceptions were genotypes in the Ky21 inbred background, which were not yet tasseling at 60 DAP. For these later-maturing genotypes, the leaves selected for measurement were mostly the fourth and fifth leaves below the top-most visible leaf. Beginning at the axis of planting, the counterclockwise angle between the axis of planting and the midrib (Supplementary Fig. S22) was measured for each selected leaf, using the angle measurement function of ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).
To test for differences between the angles of azimuthal canopy orientations between mutants and their wild-type inbred controls, the angles from ImageJ were subtracted from 90° if the angle was ≥0 and ≤180° or 270° if the angle was >180°. The absolute values then therefore represented the degree of deviation from being perpendicular to axis of planting. Hence, a high value indicates that the measured leaf is strongly deflected from the interrow space. To evaluate the impacts of variation in plant canopy architecture on PAR interception, multiple morphological traits were collected around 60 DAP. For each plot, 3 plants were randomly selected and their heights, angles of primary ear leaf, angles of leaf above primary ear, and the length and width of the leaf above the primary ear were collected. Because plants in the Ky21 background had not yet achieved anthesis at 60 DAP, the length, width, and angle of the top-most mature leaf and angle of the second top-most mature leaf were collected instead of that of the ear leaf. The mean phenotypic values of each morphological trait within each plot were used as predictors for PAR interception in a multiple linear regression. After model optimization, the full model contained all morphological traits plus location as explanatory variables. We used single term deletions to calculate the type III ANOVA sums of squares and test for a significant effect of each morphological trait on PAR interception.

## Density planting
lg1-mu(W22), lg2(A632), and 5 inbred lines (W22, A632, Mo18W, CML333, and Mo45) were jab-planted on June 22, 2021 at 4 densities at the Curtiss Farm in Ames, Iowa. Genotypes were planted in 4-row plots with ∼9.14 m row length and ∼89 cm space between 2 rows. Plants were spaced at ∼19 cm, ∼25.4 cm, ∼45.7 cm, and ∼76.2 cm, corresponding to plant densities of ∼59,280 plants/ha, ∼42,978 plants/ha, ∼23,712 plants/ha, and ∼14,326 plants/ha, respectively.
The numbers of interrow leaves for each genotype were collected when W22 started tasseling at 55 DAP. For each 4-row plot, plants in the middle 2 rows were used for data collection. Because the lowest density had only 10 plants per row, the trait was collected from 6 consecutive plants at the center of each row, resulting in 12 phenotyped plants per genotype-density combination. For each of 12 plants for each genotype, the deviation in degrees of azimuthal canopy orientation from the perpendicular was also collected from top-view images collected at the same day using the methods described in the previous section.
To validate results of 2021, additional experiments were conducted at the Ag Engineering and Agronomy Research Farm (“Agronomy Farm”) in Boone, Iowa, and the “Johnson Farm” in Ames, Iowa. lg1-mu(W22), lg2(A632), W22, and A632 were planted in multiple densities. For the Johnson farm, 4 densities were used: ∼83,980 plants/ha, ∼45,489 plants/ha, ∼31,149 plants/ha, and 19,677 plants/ha meanwhile 2 densities, ∼83,980 plants/ha and 19,677 plants/ha, were used at the Agronomy farm. All genotypes were planted in 4-row plots with row lengths of 3 m for the first 3 densities and ∼9.14 m for the lowest density. In all experiments, the interrow space was 76.2 cm. Phenotyping was conducted at 60 DAP following the same protocols as described for the 2021 experiment.

## Analyses of gene expression
In a pilot study, plants homozygous for 3 liguleless mutants, lg1-mu(W22), lg2-mu (W22), and lg2(A632) and their respective wild-type controls, W22 and A632, were planted during the summer of 2022 at Ames, Iowa. All genotypes were planted in replicated 2-row plots in 3 m rows with an interrow spacing of 76.2 cm at a density of ∼83,980 plants/ha. At the V8 stage (40 DAP), the 13th leaf of 6 randomly selected plants per genotype/rep was harvested. Three cm leaf sections, from the base, middle, and tip, were collected from each leaf. The positions of these sections are as indicated in Whipple et al. (2011). The tissue sections, following the procedure outlined by Li et al. (2010), from the 6 plants from each genotype/rep were pooled into a 50 mL tube, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen prior to RNA extraction. Because 1 replicate was damaged during the growing season, RNA-seq data could be collected from only a single replicate.
RNA was extracted from each pooled leaf sample using a Qiagen RNeasy kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. The samples were then sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 instrument in paired-end 150 bp mode. Quality trimming, alignment to the B73 reference genome (v2 from MaizeGDB) of the raw reads were conducted following a previously described pipeline (Leiboff et al. 2015). The raw read counts per gene were then transformed into RPKM for downstream analyses.

## Germination of liguleless mutants under 2 light regimes
Three homozygous liguleless mutants, lg2(A632), lg1-mu(W22), and lg2-mu(W22), along with their appropriate wild-type controls and a population segregating for the Lg3-O allele in a Ky21 genetic background were planted in 2 growth chambers. The first growth chamber was set for 14 h of light (6 AM to 10 PM) and 10 h of darkness. The other chamber was set for constant darkness. Both chambers were maintained at a constant 28 °C. Four replications of each genotype and controls were grown. Each replicate consisted of 6 kernels planted in a single 6″ pot. Pots were randomly arranged within replications. Ten or 11 DAP germinated seedlings were harvested for trait collection. At this time, seedlings from the population segregating for Lg3-O were also phenotyped for the liguleless phenotype to distinguish the Lg3-O/lg3 heterozygous mutants and their lg3/lg3 wild-type controls. After harvest, mesocotyl length and the distance from the first node to the first leaf collar were measured for all seedlings using a soft measuring tape placed alongside the mesocotyl and the stem. The resulting values of all seedlings within a genotype within a replicate were averaged and used for subsequent analyses and comparisons.
Initially, 2 replicates of lg2(A632) and lg1-mu(W22) and their controls, as well as the Lg3-O segregating population, were planted in each growth chamber. Seven days later, 2 additional replicates of each of these genotypes were planted in the same 2 chambers. After harvesting replications 1 and 2, 4 replicates of lg2-mu(W22) and its W22 wild-type control were planted in the 2 growth chambers and harvested 10 d later.

## Table Table 1.: Definitions of key terms
Canopy architecture | The spatial configuration of the above-ground plant organs of a group of plants, including their leaf angles, leaf widths, leaf lengths, leaf numbers, and azimuthal canopy orientation.
Azimuthal canopy orientation | The distribution of leaves around the stem of a single plant relative to the axis of planting, as viewed from above.
Azimuthal canopy re-orientation | The ability of some maize genotypes to alter the orientations of their leaves during development in coordination with adjacent plants.
Canopy pattern | The distribution of azimuthal canopy orientations of collections of plants along an axis of planting.
Parallel canopy trait | A special case of canopy pattern in which leaves of individual plants grow parallel to those of adjacent plants. This trait is measured quantitatively by the value of η or the percentage of plants that are mutually parallel.

## Figure Figure 1.: Canopy patterns. A) Calculation of parallel canopy trait (η). The value of η is obtained via a logit transformation of the ratio (π) between the number of plants parallel to neighboring plants and the total number of plants in a row. The 2 white poles in each side of the image represent the edge of a planting row. B) Classification of canopy patterns. Canopy patterns are defined based on 2 phenotypes: η (i.e. parallel vs. nonparallel) and the orientation of plants relative to the axis of planting (i.e. on-row vs. off-row). The orientations of the first leaves of a maize seedling are determined by the orientation in which the corresponding kernel was planted. Hence, because kernel orientations are nonuniform during planting, absent canopy re-orientation a nonparallel canopy pattern will result. In contrast, some genotypes exhibit adaptive canopy re-orientation resulting in the off-row parallel canopy phenotype. In rare genotypes, re-orientation results in the maladaptive on-row parallel canopy phenotype.
