Far-red light orchestrates antiviral defense in plants.
Gong Q, He L, Huang F, Wang Y, Xie J, Hong Y, Liu Y. (2025). Science advances. . doi:10.1126/sciadv.adz7663. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Far-red light orchestrates antiviral defense in plants

## Abstract
AbstractLight undergoes dramatic spectral shifts at dusk (sunset) and dawn (sunrise), enhancing insect activity and increasing the risk of insect-borne virus transmission. However, how plants respond to these light changes and associated viral threats remains unclear. Here, we show that far-red (FR) light, enriched in skylight during dusk and dawn, activates plant antiviral defense via phytochrome A (phyA). FR light-induced phyA activation promotes degradation of phytochrome interacting factor 1 (PIF1), which triggers the REVEILLE 7 (RVE7)–enhanced disease susceptibility 1 (EDS1) cascade to enhance salicylic acid (SA) signaling. Furthermore, multiple viruses use their RNA-dependent RNA polymerases to disrupt the phyA–far-red elongated hypocotyl 1 (FHY1) interaction to block phyA nuclear transport, impairing FR light–induced plant defense. Our findings uncover the molecular basis of FR light–mediated plant antiviral defense and reveal a previously unidentified viral counter-defense mechanism, highlighting how plants interpret environmental light cues to adapt to insect-borne viral infections.

## INTRODUCTION
Light, fundamental to plants as both an energy source and environmental signal, originates from solar electromagnetic radiation. It regulates numerous developmental processes, including germination, de-etiolation, stomatal development, circadian rhythms, and flowering (1). Plants perceive light through photoreceptors that use chromophores to absorb photons. This absorption induces conformational changes in the photoreceptor protein to trigger downstream responses (2). These photoreceptors include red/far-red (FR) light-sensing phytochromes (phyA–phyE), blue light–sensing cryptochromes (cry1–cry3) and phototropins (phot1 and phot2), Zeitlupe family members (ZTL, FKF1, and LKP2), and the ultraviolet B (UV-B) receptor UVR8 (3, 4).
Light is a dynamic component of the terrestrial environment, exhibiting marked diurnal variations in spectral irradiance. The transitions at dusk (sunset) and dawn (sunrise) are associated with the most dramatic color changes. These rapid spectral shifts trigger strong responses in plants and other organisms, including insects. Pests often swarm over fields during twilight and dawn, exploiting low light to evade predators while benefiting from optimal feeding temperatures (5–7). Within this complex ecosystem, plants, insects, and viruses form intricate interactions. Phloem-feeding insects, such as aphids, whiteflies, and thrips, rank among the most destructive agricultural pests globally, responsible for transmitting up to 80% of plant viral pathogens that devastate crops (8). More than 40% of plant viruses rely on aphids for transmission (8, 9). However, how plants adapt to diurnal light fluctuations and the resulting increases in insect activity and viral infection remains an unresolved question.
Regardless of season or location, FR light enrichment is a consistent feature during dusk and dawn (10–12). While FR light promotes necrotrophic pathogen Botrytis cinerea infection (13–16), it reduces the infection rate of biotrophic pathogen powdery mildew (Oidium lycopersicum) (17). The FR light receptor phyA synergizes with phyB to regulate plant immunity (18). However, whether and how FR light affects plant antiviral defense is still poorly understood.
In this study, we demonstrate that FR light irradiation activates the photoreceptor phyA to degrade phytochrome interacting factor 1 (PIF1), triggering the REVEILLE 7 (RVE7)–enhanced disease susceptibility 1 (EDS1) cascade to induce antiviral defense. Intriguingly, FR light irradiation not only limits virus infection but also induces aphid resistance through the salicylic acid (SA) signaling pathway, thereby suppressing virus transmission. Furthermore, we reveal that multiple viruses use RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) proteins to counter FR light–induced antiviral defense by disrupting the phyA–far-red elongated hypocotyl 1 (FHY1) interaction to block FR light–induced phyA nuclear transport. These findings indicate that plants adapt to environmental light fluctuations to mitigate subsequent insect attacks and virus infections, supporting the application of FR light light-emitting diode (LEDs) in greenhouse horticulture to enhance crop immunity against viruses and potentially other pathogens.

## RESULTS

## FR light irradiation enhances plant antiviral defense
To investigate whether FR light irradiation affects plant resistance to viral infection, we inoculated Nicotiana benthamiana (Nb) plants with either cucumber mosaic virus (CMV) or green fluorescent protein (GFP)–tagged potato virus Y (PVY-GFP) under two light regimes: standard white light (white group) and monochromatic FR light (FR group). In the FR group, infected plants were exposed to 4 hours of FR light daily before being returned to white light. Compared to the white group, CMV-infected plants in the FR group exhibited attenuated viral systemic symptoms (Fig. 1A), consistent with reduced viral RNA and coat protein (CP) accumulation (Fig. 1, B and C). Similar effects were observed for PVY-GFP. FR light–treated plants displayed weaker GFP fluorescence in systemic leaves (Fig. 1D), accompanied by significant decreases in both viral RNA and CP levels (Fig. 1, E and F). The specificity of the antiviral CP antibodies was confirmed by Western blot analysis using negative controls (fig. S1). These results suggest that FR light enhances plant antiviral defense.

Since phyA is the principal photoreceptor for FR light (19), we used tobacco rattle virus (TRV)–based virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology (20) to knock-down (KD) phyA expression and tested its role in FR light–mediated antiviral immunity. In CMV-infected plants under white light, phyA-KD plants showed more severe symptoms and higher levels of viral RNA and CP than TRV control plants (Fig. 2, A to D). Notably, FR light treatment failed to reduce virus accumulation or symptoms in phyA-KD plants (Fig. 2, A, C, and D). Comparable results were obtained with PVY-GFP infection (Fig. 2, E to H). The role of phyA in FR light–mediated antiviral immunity was further validated in Arabidopsis thaliana (At): CMV-infected Col-0 plants exposed to FR light showed attenuated systemic symptoms compared to white-light controls (fig. S2A), consistent with the finding that FR light reduced viral RNA and CP accumulation (fig. S2, B and C). In contrast, under white light, CMV-infected phyA-211 mutants showed more severe symptoms and higher viral RNA and CP levels than wild-type (WT) controls, and FR light did not mitigate viral accumulation or symptoms in phyA-211 (fig. S2). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that FR light promotes antiviral defense via a phyA-dependent pathway in both Nb and At.

## RVE7 is required for FR light–induced virus resistance
To elucidate how FR light induces phyA-dependent plant antiviral defense, we performed comparative bioinformatics analyses on phyA chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from WT and phyA mutant. In a previous study, Chen et al. integrated ChIP-seq (AtphyA) and RNA-seq analyses in Arabidopsis phyA mutant plants and identified 279 genes positively regulated by phyA (21). In parallel, another study analyzed RNA-seq data from phyA mutants of both At and tomato under light-onset conditions, revealing 23 conserved genes showing phyA-dependent positive regulation in both species (22). By integrating these two datasets, we selected overlapping candidate targets. Promoter analysis of the corresponding Nb orthologs for G-box cis-elements (the phyA-associated motif) identified RVE7, also known as early phytochrome–responsive1 (EPR1) in At (23), as a potential regulator of FR light–induced virus resistance. In the Nb genome database, two RVE7 homologs (RVE7.1 and RVE7.2) were identified, sharing >91% nucleotide identity and likely representing two alleles of one gene derived from different ancestries of allotetraploid Nb. Both genes showed a transient induction followed by a marked decrease after CMV infection, further suggesting that they have a similar role in plant antiviral defense. Given that RVE7.1 consistently displayed higher expression levels than RVE7.2 (fig. S3A), we used RVE7.1 (hereafter RVE7) for subsequent biochemical and overexpression experiments. Using VIGS, we confirmed that silencing RVE7 (including RVE7.1 and RVE7.2) compromised FR light–induced resistance to CMV (fig. S4), in line with previous reports that RVE7 expression in Arabidopsis is modulated by phyA (23), suggesting that RVE7 functions downstream of phyA in FR light–mediated antiviral defense.
We then generated CRISPR-Cas9–edited RVE7 knockout (KO) lines (designated as rve7) (fig. S3B). After infecting plants with CMV and then treating them with or without FR light, rve7 plants exhibited more severe virus symptoms and increased viral RNA and CP levels in systemic leaves compared to WT plants (Fig. 3, A to C). Notably, there were no differences in viral symptoms or viral accumulation between FR light–treated and untreated rve7 plants (Fig. 3, A to C). Similar effects of rve7 on FR light–induced plant defense against PVY-GFP infection were also observed (Fig. 3, D to F). In addition, we generated transgenic Nb plants overexpressing HA-RVE7 (HA-RVE7-OE), with line #27 accumulating the highest RVE7 protein level (fig. S5). Upon CMV or PVY infection, the HA-RVE7-OE plants exhibited significantly enhanced resistance (fig. S6). Moreover, under white light, phyA-KD plants exhibited increased susceptibility to viral infection and accumulated higher levels of viral RNA compared to TRV control plants. phyA-KD/HA-RVE7-OE plants showed viral symptoms, viral RNA accumulation, and CP levels comparable to those in TRV/HA-RVE7-OE controls, and both RVE7 overexpression lines accumulated less viral RNA than the TRV control plants (fig. S7). Together, these results demonstrate that RVE7 overexpression restores the compromised antiviral defense of phyA-KD plants and establish RVE7 as an essential component of phyA-mediated FR light–induced immunity.

## phyA regulates RVE7 expression by inducing PIF1 degradation
We investigated whether and how FR light regulates RVE7 transcription in Nb plants. RVE7 mRNA levels were significantly reduced in phyA-KD plants but increased in phyA-GFP transiently overexpressing plants (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, FR light up-regulated RVE7 mRNA level in TRV control plants but not in phyA-KD plants (Fig. 4, B and C). These results indicate that FR light activates phyA to up-regulate RVE7 transcription. We further investigated how phyA regulates RVE7 transcription. It is known that phyA affects the expression of downstream genes through multiple transcription factors (TFs), among which PIF1 shares the most target genes with phyA (21). Combining with the fact that RVE7 promoter contains a G-box motif, which is a known binding site for PIF1 (21, 24), we hypothesized that phyA regulates RVE7 transcription via PIF1. ChIP–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay showed that PIF1 was associated with G-box–containing RVE7 promoter fragments (Fig. 4D). Yeast one–hybrid data and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) confirmed that PIF1 directly binds to the RVE7 promoter at the G-box site (Fig. 4, E and F). In addition, coexpression of PIF1 reduced transcription of a luciferase reporter driven by the RVE7 promoter (RVE7pro) but not by the G-box mutant version (RVE7Mpro) (Fig. 4G). Consistently, RVE7 mRNA levels significantly increased in PIF1-KD but reduced in PIF1-MYC transiently overexpressing plants (Fig. 4, H to J). These data demonstrate that PIF1 binds to RVE7 promoter and suppresses RVE7 transcription. Then, we checked the role of PIF1 in FR light–induced plant antiviral defense. Under white light, CMV-infected PIF1-KD plants displayed milder virus symptoms and lower viral RNA and CP levels compared to TRV-control plants (fig. S8, A to D). Notably, FR light treatment did not further affect virus symptoms or accumulation in PIF1-KD plants (fig. S8, A, C, and D). Similar results were also obtained for PVY-GFP in PIF1-KD and TRV control plants, regardless of FR light treatment (fig. S8, E to H). These data indicate that PIF1 acts as a negative regulator in phyA-mediated FR light–induced antiviral defense.

Next, we examined how phyA regulates the PIF1 activity to influence RVE7 transcription. It is known that photoactivated phyA would move into the nucleus and phosphorylate PIF1, resulting in PIF1 degradation via the ubiquitin/26S proteasome pathway (25, 26). Therefore, we explored whether phyA regulates RVE7 expression by inducing PIF1 degradation. PIF1 protein accumulation was reduced after FR light treatment (Fig. 4K). Moreover, transient phyA expression enhanced PIF1 degradation by the 26S proteasome system after FR light treatment (Fig. 4K), suggesting that FR light activates phyA, which then induces PIF1 degradation in Nb. In addition, transient phyA expression up-regulated RVE7 mRNA levels in TRV control plants but not in PIF1-KD plants (Fig. 4, L and M). FR light up-regulated RVE7 mRNA level in TRV control plants but not in PIF1-KD plants (Fig. 4, N and O). Furthermore, under white light, PIF1-KD plants exhibited increased resistance to viral infection and accumulated lower levels of viral RNA compared to TRV control plants. PIF1-KD/rve7 plants exhibited no significant differences in viral symptoms, viral RNA accumulation, or CP levels compared to TRV/rve7 controls, and both rve7 lines accumulated more viral RNA than the TRV control plants (fig. S9), demonstrating that RVE7 KO abolishes the enhanced antiviral defense observed in PIF1-KD plant (fig. S9). These results indicate that photoactivated phyA induces PIF1 degradation to regulate RVE7 transcription and that the enhanced antiviral defense in PIF1-KD plants is largely dependent on RVE7.

## RVE7 activates EDS1 transcription to regulate plant immunity
RVE7 is known to be an MYB-like TF. This led us to identify plant immunity genes regulated by RVE7. We performed RNA-seq analysis on 2-week-old WT and rve7 seedlings under white light and identified 347 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 216 up-regulated and 131 down-regulated genes (fig. S10, A to C, and table S1). Using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis focused on the “plant-pathogen interaction” category, we uncovered seven genes (fig. S10D and table S2). Through promoter sequence analysis of these genes, we identified an EE motif in the EDS1 promoter, potentially a binding site for RVE7 (27). Consistent with this, EDS1 mRNA level was lower in rve7 than in WT plants (fig. S10E and table S1). We then quantified EDS1 transcripts in rve7, RVE7-MYC transiently overexpressing, and WT leaf tissues to determine whether RVE7 influences EDS1 expression. EDS1 mRNA level was reduced in rve7 but increased in RVE7-MYC transiently overexpressing plants (Fig. 5, A and B). In addition, EDS1 mRNA levels were elevated in phyA-overexpressing and PIF1-KD plants but reduced in phyA-KD and PIF1-overexpressing plants, respectively (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, luciferase reporter assays showed that RVE7 enhanced transcription of reporter genes driven by EDS1 promoter (EDS1pro) but not the EDS1 EE-mutated promoter (EDS1Mpro) (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that RVE7 modulates EDS1 transcription. Furthermore, ChIP-qPCR, yeast one–hybrid, and EMSA assays demonstrated that RVE7 directly binds to the EDS1 promoter at the EE motif site (Fig. 5, E to G). FR light treatment did not induce EDS1 transcription in phyA-KD or rve7 plants, indicating that FR light–induced EDS1 activation depends on the phyA-RVE7 pathway (Fig. 5, H to J).

Given that EDS1 is crucial for SA accumulation and SA-mediated immunity (28, 29), we further investigated whether EDS1 contributes to phyA-RVE7–mediated antiviral immunity by using EDS1-KO lines (eds1) (fig. S3C) (30) and RVE7-KD/eds1 plants. RVE7-KD/eds1 plants were generated by knocking down RVE7 in eds1 plants by VIGS. Like rve7 mutants, RVE7-KD plants (in WT Nb background) also showed normal growth and susceptibility to CMV and PVY-GFP (Fig. 3 and fig. S11), indicating that RVE7-KD plants could mimic RVE7-KO plants. As expected, all RVE7-KD, eds1, and RVE7-KD/eds1 plants showed similar degrees of CMV or PVY-GFP infection but were all more susceptible to these viruses than WT plants (fig. S11). Unlike WT plants, FR light failed to induce antiviral defense in RVE7-KD, eds1, and RVE7-KD/eds1 plants (fig. S11). These results, together with the failure of FR light to enhance antiviral defense in eds1 plants (fig. S12), indicate that phyA-RVE7–mediated antiviral immunity operates through EDS1.
Moreover, we examined whether phyA regulates the SA signaling pathway via RVE7-EDS1 cascade by treating rve7, eds1, and WT plants with FR light. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) assays showed that FR light treatment induced expression of SA-associated defense genes (PR1, PR2, and PR5) in WT but not in rve7 or eds1 plants (fig. S13, A to C). These findings confirm that activated phyA enhances the SA signaling pathway via the RVE7-EDS1 cascade to limit virus infection.
Furthermore, FR light significantly decreased aphid survival after 24-hour feeding on WT plants but not on rve7 or eds1 plants (fig. S13D). Similarly, overexpression of RVE7 also markedly reduced aphid survival (fig. S13E). These results suggest that activation of the phyA-RVE7-EDS1 cascade enhances aphid resistance via the SA signaling pathway, consistent with the established role of SA in not only suppressing viral infection but also promoting anti–aphid immunity (9).

## Viral RdRP proteins relocate phyA to combat FR light induced immunity
In general, plants rapidly activate immune responses upon viral infection. To promote their own infection, some viral proteins act to counteract and partially suppress these defenses. Although this suppression is not fully effective, it can facilitate viral propagation. Therefore, we supposed that viruses may interfere with FR light–driven immunity. We cloned viral proteins from CMV to assess their potential effects on phyA activity. Intriguingly, we found that the RdRP protein 2a from CMV prevented FR light–induced translocation of phyA to the nucleus, as shown by subcellular fluorescence imaging (fig. S14A). Similarly, RdRP protein NIb from PVY and 126kD protein from TMV also blocked FR light–induced phyA nuclear localization (fig. S14A), consistent with results from subcellular immunoblot analyses (Fig. 6A). Phytochromes are unique in that they exist in two interconvertible forms: Pr and Pfr. In darkness, phytochromes are synthesized in the cytoplasm as inactive Pr. Upon absorbing red or FR light, Pr is converted to the active Pfr form, which then translocates to the nucleus to initiate downstream signaling processes (31–33). Previous studies have shown that the nuclear import of activated phyA is promoted by FHY1 (34, 35), prompting us to examine whether viral proteins interfere with the phyA-FHY1 interaction. Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays revealed that both FHY1 and viral RdRP proteins interact with phyA (Fig. 6, B and C, and fig. S14, B to E). Notably, Co-IP and LCI experiments revealed that the presence of viral proteins disrupts the phyA-FHY1 interaction (Fig. 6, D and E). We then explored their effects on the phyA-regulated RVE7 expression and found that viral RdRPs suppressed phyA-mediated activation of the RVE7 promoter (Fig. 6F) and interfered with FR light–induced transcription of RVE7 (Fig. 6G). Together, these results suggest that several plant viruses may have evolved RdRPs as a common mechanism to interfere with the phyA-RVE7-EDS1 module by disrupting phyA nuclear translocation to counteract FR light–induced plant antiviral immunity.

## DISCUSSION
Light is a dynamic environmental signal characterized by marked fluctuations in spectral quality throughout the diurnal cycle. These rapid changes in light spectra elicit strong physiological responses in both plants and herbivorous insects. Notably, many virus-transmitting insects exhibit heightened feeding and dispersal at dusk and dawn (5–7), periods enriched in FR light (10–12), thereby elevating the risk of viral transmission. How plants simultaneously adapt to shifting light environments and defend against viruses spread by feeding insects during these critical periods remains a fundamental yet mechanistically unresolved question. Among photoreceptors, phyA is the primary photoreceptor for FR light. Unlike other photoreceptors such as phyB, whose roles in regulating plant development (32, 36–38) and immunity (13–16, 18) are well characterized, the function of phyA in immune regulation remains largely unexplored (17).
In this study, we elucidate a previously uncharacterized FR light–dependent immune signaling pathway that enhances plant defense to viral pathogens and their insect vectors. Our results show that FR light activates phyA, which in turn promotes the proteasome-mediated degradation of PIF1, a key negative regulator of light signaling. The degradation of PIF1 relieves its repression of the MYB-like TF RVE7, allowing RVE7 to accumulate and directly activate the expression of EDS1, a central regulator of both basal and effector-triggered immunity (Figs. 4 and 5). This phyA-RVE7-EDS1 signaling module initiates a robust SA-dependent defense response that restricts viral replication and concurrently enhances resistance to aphid infestation (figs. S11 to S13). These findings provide insights into photoreceptor-mediated immunity and extend the functional relevance of light signaling in antiviral and antiinsect defense. Notably, previous studies have shown that FR light exerts contrasting effects on plant responses to biotrophic and necrotrophic pathogens: While it enhances susceptibility to the necrotrophic fungus B. cinerea (13–16), it reduces infection by the biotrophic fungus O. lycopersicum (powdery mildew) (17). Consistently, SA signaling is well known to mediate resistance to biotrophic pathogens as well as some phloem-feeding herbivores such as aphids, while jasmonic acid (JA) induces resistance against necrotrophic pathogens and chewing herbivores. The phyA-RVE7-EDS1 module described here therefore exemplifies how FR light preferentially channels defense outputs into the SA branch of immunity (39, 40).
An unexpected insight from our study is the immune function of RVE7. Unlike other RVE family members that have been extensively characterized in circadian rhythm regulation, the biological role of RVE7 has remained largely uncharacterized, particularly in plant immunity (22, 23, 27). Our results demonstrate that RVE7 acts as a direct transcriptional activator of EDS1, positioning it as a critical node in inducible immune signaling. This discovery broadens the functional repertoire of circadian regulators and reveals a previously unrecognized link between light perception, circadian control, and immune responses. This integration likely enables plants to dynamically coordinate growth and defense by allocating immune resources in response to environmental light fluctuations.
Our molecular insights into FR light–mediated plant immunity raise important questions about whether, and by what mechanisms, viruses can counteract these defense responses. Little is currently known about how viruses target light-regulated immune pathways. To date, only a few studies have reported that viral-encoded proteins, such as βC1 from two distinct viruses, can interact with PIF4 and suppress its positive regulation of RNA interference by disrupting PIF4 dimerization, thereby attenuating near-infrared light–induced antiviral responses (41). However, the molecular mechanisms by which pathogens interfere with FR light–mediated immune signaling remain unknown. We uncover a sophisticated viral counter-defense strategy targeting FR light–induced immunity. RdRPs from diverse plant viruses—including CMV, PVY, and TMV—interfere with the nuclear import of activated phyA by competitively disrupting the interaction between phyA and the nuclear transport factor FHY1 (Fig. 6 and fig. S14). This interference blocks phyA nuclear translocation under FR light, thereby preventing downstream transcriptional reprogramming required for activation of the RVE7-EDS1 immune pathway. Consequently, the suppression of SA signaling weakens host antiviral defenses (Fig. 7). The convergence of multiple viruses on this strategy suggests that subverting FR light–mediated defense is an evolutionarily conserved mechanism, emphasizing the ecological and evolutionary significance of light spectrum perception in shaping plant-virus interactions.

## Proposed working model for FR light–mediated plant antiviral defense and viral counter defense.
Under FR light treatment, phyA interacts with FHY1 to enter the nucleus. Inside the nucleus, phyA and FHY1 dissociate, with FHY1 exiting to assist additional phyA molecules into the nucleus. Dissociated phyA activates the RVE7-EDS1 module by degrading PIF1, enhancing the SA signaling pathway against viruses and aphids. Viral RdRP proteins (for example, CMV 2a, PVY NIb, and TMV 126kD) interfere with phyA-FHY1 interaction, preventing FR light–induced phyA nuclear translocation and thereby suppressing FR light–mediated plant antiviral defense. Arrows indicate activation; the “T” symbol indicates suppression.
Our study reveals a viral strategy to counteract FR light–induced antiviral immunity, adding to the growing landscape of light-defense interplays. Beyond FR, other light qualities also regulate plant immunity. For example, UV-B enhances JA-dependent resistance against chewing insects across species (42–45), and UV-B/UV-A irritation negatively affects growth of pea aphids and whiteflies (46, 47). Green light potentiates JA signaling against pathogens and insects (48). Red light significantly influences fungi infection and induces systemic resistance against root-knot nematode (32, 49). Notably, some pathogens exploit light signaling to promote virulence, such as the bacterial effector AvrPtoB, which degrades HY5 in a light-dependent manner (50), or the viral βC1 protein that manipulates PIFs and MYC2 to suppress JA and light signaling (51). These observations, together with our findings that multiple viral RdRPs disrupt phyA nuclear import to suppress SA-dependent defense, underscore the evolutionary significance of light-mediated immunity and the convergent tactics pathogens used to overcome it.
Our study unveiled the role and mechanism of FR light in plant immunity, which advocates for such integration of spectral light management into crop protection strategies. Supplemental FR light may be optimized to simultaneously activate endogenous antiviral defenses. However, prolonged exposure to a high FR light environment might trigger shade avoidance responses, leading to undesired morphological changes and a potential reduction in biomass. Furthermore, since SA is generally associated with resistance to biotrophs and JA with resistance to necrotrophic pathogens, enhancing SA signaling could theoretically increase susceptibility to necrotrophic pathogens. This trade-off may be mitigated through precise spectral management. The key is to use FR light as a transient, programmable “defense eliciting signal” rather than a constant component of the growth spectrum. Our proposed strategy relies not on continuous, high-intensity FR light exposure but on its application as a precise, timed signal to activate defenses without incurring significant costs. In environments with high necrotroph risk, the FR treatment protocol would need careful adjustment or combination with measures that bolster JA-mediated defenses.
In summary, synchronizing FR light exposure with peak insect vector activity could further boost plant resistance, offering a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to chemical pesticides. Combined with pathogen surveillance and precision agriculture tools, FR light manipulation could form the basis of a programmable and adaptive approach to crop protection, contributing to sustainable agriculture and global food security.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant growth conditions and light treatments
WT, rve7, eds1 (30), and 35S:HA-RVE7 overexpression (HA-RVE7-OE) transgenic plants, all in the N. benthamiana (Nb) genetic background, were used in this study. All seeds were surface-sterilized and placed on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (Sigma-Aldrich). Plants were grown at 26°C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod. After 10 days, seedlings were transplanted into soil pots and grown in a greenhouse under the same photoperiod. Seeds of A. thaliana Col-0 and phyA-211 (52) were plated on half-strength MS medium, vernalized at 4°C in darkness for 3 days, and subsequently germinated at 22°C for 7 days. Following this, the seedlings were transferred to soil and grown in a greenhouse under a 12-hour light/12-hour dark photoperiod at 22°C. FR light (730 nm, 4 μmol m−2 s−1) was supplied using an LED light source. Light intensity was measured using a HiPoint (HR-350) illumination spectrum analyzer. For light treatment of virus-infected plants, CMV- (53) and PVY-GFP–inoculated (54) plants were maintained under two conditions: The white group was kept under normal white light, while the FR group received 4 hours of FR light treatment daily during the daytime before being returned to normal white light. The 4-hour FR light regime was chosen because it robustly enhances antiviral immunity while minimizing undesirable developmental phenotypes associated with prolonged FR exposure (41, 55, 56).

## Generation of transgenic plants
The CRISPR-Cas9 and 35S:HA-RVE7 plasmids were generated as previously described (57, 58). All constructs were introduced into Nb leaf discs via Agrobacterium GV2260. Leaf discs were placed on MS medium supplemented with 6-BA (1.0 mg/ml; IB0100, Solarbio), Timentin (0.2 mg/ml; JS9008, JSENB), naphthaleneacetic acid (0.1 mg/ml; N8010, Solarbio), and hygromycin B (0.025 mg/ml; 400052, Merck Millipore) for callus, shoot, and root regeneration. Homozygous transgenic lines were identified by PCR amplification of the target site using 2 × T5 Super PCR Mix (TSE005, TSINGKE).

## Firefly LCI assays
The full-length coding sequences of FHY1, CMV2a, PVYNIb, and TMV126kD were cloned into the nLUC vector, while the full-length coding sequence of phyA was cloned into the cLUC vector. These constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium and coinfiltrated into Nb leaves. After 48 hours, luciferin substrate was applied to the leaf surfaces, and luminescence images were captured using the PlantView100 assay system (BLT PHOTON TECHNOLOGY). To examine the effect of viral proteins on the interaction between FHY1 and phyA, we coinfiltrated GUS-HA, 2a-HA, NIb-HA, and 126kD-HA together with FHY1-nLUC/phyA-cLUC, and observed luciferase activity 48 hours postinfiltration. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## Co-IP assay
Constructs were coexpressed in Nb leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration. Protein extracts were collected 48 hours postinfiltration and subjected to immunoprecipitation using GFP-Trap beads (KTSM1301, AlpaLifeBio). The beads were washed five times with Co-IP buffer, denatured in SDS loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and separated on a 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel (M00664, GenScript) for immunoblotting. Primary antibodies used included anti-hemagglutinin (3724S, CST), anti-GFP (3h9, ChromoTek), and anti-MYC (M20019M, Abmart), all at a 1:3000 dilution. Signals were detected using the StarSignal Chemiluminescent Assay Kit (E171-01, GeneStar). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with consistent results.

## Subcellular fluorescence imaging
Agrobacterium strains carrying the indicated constructs were infiltrated into Nb leaves. After 30 hours of growth in darkness, the dark-adapted Nb plants were exposed to FR light for 4 hours before observation. GFP fluorescence signals in tobacco epidermal cells were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP8) with excitation at 488 nm.

## Cytoplasm and nuclear separation assays
Cytoplasm and nuclear separation assays were performed as described previously (59). The indicated constructs were transiently coexpressed in Nb leaves. Plants were kept in darkness for 30 hours and then either maintained in darkness or exposed to FR light for 4 hours. Approximately 0.5 g of leaf tissue was harvested and ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen under dim green light in a dark room. The powder was mixed with 2 ml of cold lysis buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 20 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 250 mM sucrose, 25% glycerol, 30 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and a Roche protease inhibitor cocktail]. The homogenate was filtered through a nylon mesh and centrifuged at 1500g for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000g for 20 min at 4°C. The clear supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. The pellet was resuspended in 3 ml of washing buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM EDTA] and centrifuged at 1500g for 10 min. After three washes, the final nuclear pellet was collected as the nuclear fraction. Both fractions were mixed with SDS loading buffer and resolved on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel for immunoblot analysis. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) was used as a cytoplasmic marker and histone as a nuclear marker. Antibodies used included anti-PEPC (AS09458, Agrisera), anti-histone (3638S, CST), and anti-GFP (3h9, ChromoTek), each at a 1:5000 dilution. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with consistent results.

## Yeast one–hybrid assay
Full-length cDNA sequences of PIF1 and RVE7 were cloned into the pGADT7-Rec2 vector (Clontech), which contains the GAL4 activation domain. These constructs were cotransformed with reporter plasmids (pHIS2-cis/promoter of RVE7 or EDS1) into Y187 yeast cells (Clontech). Empty pGADT7-Rec2 and pHIS2-cis promoter vectors served as negative controls. Transformed yeast strains were spotted on selective media (minus (−) His, Leu, and Trp) containing 5 or 10 mM 3-AT. Images were taken after 3 days of incubation at 28°C. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay
GST, GST-PIF1, and GST-RVE7 proteins were purified using Glutathione Sepharose 4B resin (GE Healthcare) and eluted with 10 mM glutathione (reduced form) followed by size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 10/300 column (GE Healthcare). Water used in all procedures was double-distilled using a Milli-Q water purification system (Merck). Biotin-labeled oligonucleotide probes were synthesized by RUIBIO Technology. EMSA was performed using a Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (GS009, Beyotime) according to a published protocol (58). Experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## Luciferase activity assay
Promoter fragments [1300 base pairs (bp)] of RVE7/RVE7M and EDS1/EDS1M were cloned into the luciferase reporter vector. Primer sequences used for construct generation are listed in table S3 and were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Promoter regions were amplified from genomic DNA using M5 Magic High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (MF740, Mei5Bio) and purified with a Gel Extraction Kit (CW2302, CoWin Biosciences). The constructs were transiently expressed in Nb leaves via Agrobacterium infiltration. After 30 hours, leaves were harvested and treated with luciferin. Luciferase activity was recorded using the PlantView100 assay system (BLT PHOTON TECHNOLOGY). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## RNA-seq analysis
RNA-seq was performed by Majorbio (Shanghai, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (2 bp–by–50 bp read length). Raw data were aligned to the Nb reference genome (https://solgenomics.net/). DEGs were identified on the basis of transcripts per million reads with log2(foldchange) ≥1 or ≤−1 and P ≤ 0.05. KEGG (http://genome.jp/kegg/) pathway enrichment analysis was also conducted to identify DEGs-associated pathways.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TransZol reagent (Transgen Biotech) as previously described (9) and stored in 1.5-ml sterile centrifuge tubes (HRK-150-TP, Beijing Huaruikang Technology Co. Ltd.). A total of 5 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (AT311-02, Transgen Biotech). qRT-PCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11201ES08, YEASEN). Relative expression levels were normalized to ACTIN using the cycle threshold (CT) 2−∆∆CT method (60). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Primers used in the qRT-PCR assays are listed in table S3.

## Virus infection and quantification
To examine the role of RVE7 and EDS1 in virus infection, 3- to 4-week-old HA-RVE7-OE, rve7, and eds1 plants were infected with CMV or PVY-GFP. For performing virus infection in silenced plants, 16-day-old WT, HA-RVE7-OE, rve7, or eds1 plants were first agroinfiltrated with TRV-phyA, TRV-PIF1, TRV-RVE7, or TRV vector. Fourteen days after VIGS treatment, upper leaves were infected with CMV or PVY-GFP. Viral symptoms were recorded photographically at 5 to 6 days postinoculation. To inoculate Arabidopsis, purified virions propagated in Nb plants were used as the inoculum. Three expanded leaves of 3-week-old seedlings were mechanically inoculated with CMV at a concentration of 10 ng/μl. Immunoblot analysis used anti-CMV (CMV21-A, Alpha Diagnostic International) or anti-PVY (POTY11-A, Alpha Diagnostic International) antibodies at a 1:5000 dilution.

## Aphid survival assay
To evaluate the effect of FR light on aphid fitness, plants were exposed to feeding by the same number of wingless adult Myzus persicae. Plants were then subjected to FR light for 4 hours, and surviving aphids were counted 24 hours later. To evaluate the impact of HA-RVE7-OE transgenic plants on aphid performance, an equal number of aphids was introduced onto each plant, and their survival rates were recorded after 24 hours. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## ChIP-qPCR assay
ChIP-qPCR was performed following a published protocol (61) with minor modifications. Chromatin was extracted from 2 g of frozen Nb leaf tissue expressing cLUC-MYC, PIF1-MYC, or RVE7-MYC and sonicated for 6 min using a Bioruptor sonicator. The SimpleChIP Plus Kit (9005S, CST) was used for subsequent steps. Chromatin was incubated with 50 μl anti-MYC magnetic agarose beads at 4°C for 3 hours. The beads were washed four times to remove nonspecific binding. DNA enrichment was determined by qRT-PCR using the % input method (62). The ACTIN2 promoter was used as the negative control. ChIP-qPCR primers are listed in table S3. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## In vivo protein degradation assay
To assess PIF1 degradation in vivo in a phyA-dependent manner, PIF1-MYC was coexpressed with either GUS-GFP or phyA-GFP for 24 hours. Plants were then kept in the dark for 20 hours, followed by a 2-hour FR light treatment. Eight hours before sampling, the agroinfiltrated leaves were treated with MG132 (100 μM, C2211, Sigma-Aldrich) or an equivalent volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a control. MG132 was dissolved in DMSO.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test for analyses involving three or more groups. All quantitative data are presented as means ± SD. Detailed statistical analyses are provided in figure legends, and P values are indicated in figures. All experiments included at least three biological replicates (n ≥ 3). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism v9.0. Immunoblot signals were quantified using ImageJ software.
