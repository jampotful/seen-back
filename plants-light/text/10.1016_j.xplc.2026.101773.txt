Nucleoporin Nup96 promotes plant photomorphogenesis by facilitating phyB photobody formation and HOS1-mediated PIF5 degradation in Arabidopsis.
Wang Y, Feng R, Hu W, Zhang Z, Jiang J. (2026). Plant communications. . doi:10.1016/j.xplc.2026.101773. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Nucleoporin Nup96 promotes plant photomorphogenesis by facilitating phyB photobody formation and HOS1-mediated PIF5 degradation in Arabidopsis

## Abstract
The initiation of light signaling involves the formation of phyB nuclear photobodies (PBs). The nuclear pore complex (NPC) is the sole gate for macromolecule transport between the cytoplasm and nucleus; however, its role in phyB PB formation and light signal transduction remains unclear. Here, we identify Nup96, a nucleoporin in the NPC subcomplex Nup107-160, as a key regulator that promotes plant photomorphogenesis. Nup96 interacts with phyB at the nuclear envelope to facilitate phyB PB formation and enhance phyB activity. Moreover, this interaction promotes phyB–PIF5 association, thereby facilitating PIF5 degradation. Notably, knocking out PIF5 fully rescues the hypocotyl phenotype of nup96-1. Further analyses identify an E3 ligase, HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 1 (HOS1), which interacts with PIF5 to promote its degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway. Collectively, our study elucidates a mechanism by which Nup96 promotes PIF5 degradation by enhancing phyB activity and stabilizing HOS1, thereby ensuring precise light-regulated growth in plants.

## Introduction
Light not only provides energy for plant growth but also serves as a crucial environmental signal that regulates the entire plant life cycle, from seed germination and seedling establishment to seasonal flowering and senescence (Quail, 2002; Oh et al., 2004). To accurately perceive changing light conditions, plants have evolved multiple photoreceptors that sense different wavelengths, including red/far-red-light photoreceptor phytochromes, blue-light photoreceptor cryptochromes, and the UV-light photoreceptor UVR8 (Nagy and Schäfer, 2002; Quail, 2002; Rizzini et al., 2011). Arabidopsis possesses five phytochromes, designated phytochrome A to phytochrome E (phyA–phyE), among which phyA is most abundant in darkness, whereas phyB predominates under light conditions. In the dark, phyB is synthesized in the cytoplasm and exists in the inactive red-light-absorbing Pr form. Upon light exposure, phyB is photoconverted to the active far-red-light-absorbing Pfr form and subsequently translocated into the nucleus (Quail et al., 1995), where it assembles into membraneless organelles termed phyB photobodies (PBs) and interacts with PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORs (PIFs). This interaction promotes PIF phosphorylation and degradation, thereby relieving repression of downstream light-responsive genes involved in plant photomorphogenesis (Chen and Chory, 2011; Li et al., 2011; Van Buskirk et al., 2012; Leivar and Monte, 2014; Shi and Zhong, 2023).
Promoting PIF degradation is a central mechanism of phyB signaling. During the transition from dark to light, PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5 are rapidly degraded in a phyB-dependent manner (Al-Sady et al., 2006; Pham et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2021). PhyB can directly bind to the transcriptional activation domain of PIF3 to suppress its transcriptional activity or regulate its alternative splicing to inhibit protein accumulation (Dong et al., 2020; Yoo et al., 2021). In addition, the assembly and disassembly of phyB PB play an important role in mediating PIF degradation and phyB-dependent hypocotyl elongation (Kim et al., 2024). In a study investigating proteins required for phyB localization to PBs, Chen et al. identified HEMERA (HMR), which when mutated abolishes phyB PB formation in the nucleus and prevents PIF3 protein degradation (Chen et al., 2010). They further demonstrated that HMR directly interacts with phyB to regulate phyB PB formation and PIF3 degradation, thereby suppressing photomorphogenesis (Galvão et al., 2012). More recently, Choi’s group isolated phyB PBs and identified PCH1 and TZP as additional PB components (Kim et al., 2023). Loss of PCH1 reduces the number and size of large phyB PBs, stabilizes PIF4 protein, and consequently promotes hypocotyl elongation (Murcia et al., 2021). TZP not only regulates phyB PB formation but also acts synergistically with PCH1 to modulate PIF4 protein abundance and phyB signaling (Fang et al., 2022). However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying phyB PB formation are still not fully understood.
In eukaryotes, the nucleus is enclosed by a nuclear envelope, within which the nuclear pore complex (NPC) resides and functions as a selective barrier that mediates efficient and orderly bidirectional transport of RNAs and proteins between the nucleus and the cytoplasm. Molecules with a molecular weight of less than 40 kDa can freely diffuse across the nuclear envelope, whereas larger macromolecules depend on NPC-mediated transport (Raveh et al., 2016; Lin and Hoelz, 2019; Tang et al., 2024). Despite significant advances in our understanding of human and yeast NPCs, research on plant NPCs remains at an early stage, with many constituent proteins not identified until 2010 (Tamura et al., 2010). Although the overall architecture of NPCs is conserved among humans, yeast, and plants, their composition varies across organisms. Plant NPCs are composed of approximately 30 nucleoporins organized into distinct subcomplexes, among which the Nup107–160 subcomplex is highly conserved across eukaryotes (Meier et al., 2017). This subcomplex comprises Nup43, Nup85, Nup96, Nup160, Nup107, Nup133, SECRETORY 13 (Sec13), and SEC13 HOMOLOG 1 (Seh1), and plays critical roles in regulating mRNA transport (Meier et al., 2017). Among these components, Nup96 was identified as a suppressor of npr1-1, constitutive 1 (snc1) and as SUPPRESSOR OF AUXIN RESISTANCE 3 (SAR3); mutation of Nup96 results in enhanced pathogen susceptibility and early flowering (Zhang and Li, 2005; Parry et al., 2006). Nup96 was subsequently shown to interact with the E3 ubiquitin ligase HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 1 (HOS1), stabilizing HOS1 and thereby reducing the accumulation of CONSTANS (CO) protein to delay flowering (Cheng et al., 2020). In addition, Nup96 regulates flowering by interacting with RNA polymerase II (RPB) to modulate the expression of FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC) (Huang et al., 2024).
Despite increasing research on the functions of Nup96 and other nucleoporins in plant development and responses to abiotic and biotic stresses, the role of NPCs, or of specific nucleoporins, in regulating plant light signaling pathways remains unclear. In this study, we show that the nup96-1 mutant exhibits longer hypocotyls than the wild type (WT) under blue, red, and white light conditions. We further demonstrate that Nup96 directly interacts with phyB to modulate its PB formation in the nucleus. In addition, both in vivo degradation and in vitro cell-free degradation assays reveal impaired PIF5 degradation in the nup96-1 mutant; genetic analyses confirm that the long-hypocotyl phenotype of nup96-1 is PIF5 dependent. Finally, we identify HOS1 as the E3 ligase that mediates PIF5 degradation, thereby uncovering a novel regulatory mechanism governing the key plant photomorphogenesis regulators PIF5 and phyB.

## Results

## Nup96 is a positive regulator of plant photomorphogenesis
The long-petiole and dwarfism phenotypes of nup96-1 plants suggest that Nup96 is involved in regulating cell elongation (Zhang and Li, 2005). Light is one of the most important environmental factors controlling cell elongation and plant architecture. To determine whether Nup96 participates in light signaling pathways, we analyzed hypocotyl lengths of WT and nup96-1 seedlings under both dark and light conditions. Interestingly, nup96-1 hypocotyls were significantly shorter than those of WT in the dark but became longer than WT under light conditions. To assess the specificity of Nup96 in regulating hypocotyl elongation, we examined hypocotyl phenotypes of WT and mutants of all members of the Nup107–160 subcomplex , to which Nup96 belongs, under dark and light conditions. Notably, the hypocotyl lengths of nup85, nup133, nup107-3, sec13, and seh1-1 mutants were indistinguishable from those of WT under light conditions, whereas nup160-3 hypocotyls were longer than those of WT, although to a lesser extent than observed in nup96-1 (Supplemental Figures 1A–1C). These results suggest that Nup96 plays a dominant role in regulating hypocotyl elongation. To further confirm this role, we generated a Nup96 CRISPR mutant (CR-Nup96) lacking a 571-bp fragment within the N-terminal region (Supplemental Figures 2A–2C), as well as complementation lines of nup96-1. We then measured hypocotyl lengths of WT, nup96-1, CR-Nup96, and complementation lines grown in the dark, under white light, and under various monochromatic light conditions. Consistent with our earlier observations, nup96 mutants (nup96-1 and CR-Nup96) displayed significantly shorter hypocotyls than WT in the dark, but significantly longer hypocotyls under continuous blue, continuous red, and white light conditions. No significant difference was observed between nup96 mutants and WT under continuous far-red light (Figures 1A and 1B). Introduction of Nup96 driven by its native promoter into nup96-1 complemented the hypocotyl phenotypes under all tested light conditions, indicating that loss of Nup96 function is responsible for the observed phenotypes (Figures 1A and 1B). ## Nup96 functions independently of its autoproteolytic cleavage, and its accumulation at the nuclear envelope is positively regulated by light
Because the maturation of Arabidopsis Nup96 requires cleavage of its N-terminal autoproteolytic processing domain, we examined whether this proteolytic cleavage is necessary for regulating plant photomorphogenesis. Specifically, hypocotyl phenotypes were analyzed in nup96-1 mutants expressing either WT Nup96 or a cleavage-deficient Nup96 variant (Nup96m), both driven by the native Nup96 promoter under dark and light conditions. The results showed that both versions fully rescued the nup96-1 hypocotyl phenotypes regardless of light condition (Figures 2A and 2B). We next examined Nup96 expression levels and Nup96–GFP signal intensities in Nup96pro:Nup96-GFP/nup96-1 and Nup96pro:Nup96m-GFP/nup96-1 lines. These analyses revealed higher levels of Nup96 than Nup96m in the nup96-1 background, indicating that the similar phenotypes we observed between the two lines cannot be attributed to elevated expression or protein accumulation of Nup96m compensating for reduced Nup96 activity (Supplemental Figures 3A–3C). Collectively, these data indicate that proteolytic cleavage of Nup96 is not required for its regulation of hypocotyl elongation. To investigate how light regulates Nup96, we examined Nup96 transcript levels in the hypocotyls of seedlings grown in darkness, under white light, or under various monochromatic light conditions. Nup96 expression was significantly higher in dark-grown seedlings than under any light treatment (Figure 2C). To visualize the spatial expression pattern of Nup96, we generated Nup96 promoter-GUS reporter lines. GUS staining results revealed that Nup96 was predominantly expressed in hypocotyls in darkness, whereas expression was mainly detected in cotyledons and root tips under light conditions (Figure 2D), indicating that light regulates the spatial expression of Nup96. We then examined Nup96 protein levels using 35S:Nup96-Myc transgenic plants and found that Nup96 protein accumulated to higher levels under all tested light conditions compared with darkness (Figure 2E and Supplemental Figure 3D). Subcellular localization analyses using Nup96pro:Nup96-GFP/nup96-1 transgenic plants showed that Nup96 localized to the nuclear envelope under both dark and light conditions; however, GFP signal intensity was markedly stronger under light conditions than in darkness (Figures 2F and 2G). ## Nup96 interacts with phyB at the nuclear envelope
To investigate how Nup96 enhances plant photomorphogenesis, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening between Nup96 and various photoreceptors. This analysis identified a specific interaction between Nup96 and the red/far-red light photoreceptor phyB. In contrast, no interaction was detected between Nup96 and the far-red light photoreceptor phyA, the UV-light photoreceptor UVR8, or the blue-light photoreceptors CRY1 and CRY2 (Figure 3A and Supplemental Figure 4). To map the interaction domains, truncated phyB variants were generated for Y2H assays. These experiments revealed that both the D1 domain (the N-terminal photosensory module) and the D2 domain (the nuclear localization PAS domain) of phyB interact with Nup96, with the D1 domain exhibiting a stronger interaction. Y2H analyses further showed that the D2 domain of Nup96 (the C-terminal Nup96 domain) interact with phyB (Figures 3B and 3C). To confirm this interaction in planta, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays were performed by transiently co-expressing Nup96-Myc and phyB-GFP in Nicotiana benthamiana (N. benthamiana) leaves. Using anti-GFP antibodies, Nup96-Myc was efficiently co-precipitated with phyB-GFP, but not with GFP alone (Figure 3D), confirming the interaction between Nup96 and phyB. We further performed firefly luciferase (LUC) complementation imaging (LCI) assays by transiently co-expressing phyB fused to the N-terminal fragment of LUC (phyB-nLUC) and Nup96 fused to the C-terminal fragment of LUC (Nup96-cLUC) in N. benthamiana leaves. Robust LUC activity was detected exclusively in leaf regions co-expressing phyB-nLUC and Nup96-cLUC, but not in regions expressing phyB-nLUC with cLUC or Nup96-cLUC with nLUC (Figure 3E). Finally, bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays further visualized the interaction between Nup96 and phyB at the nuclear envelope (Figure 3F). ## Nup96 increases phyB activity by facilitating phyB photobody formation
To investigate the regulatory role of Nup96 on phyB, we examined PHYB expression in WT and nup96-1 plants. RT–qPCR analysis and GUS histochemical staining of a PHYB promoter-GUS reporter revealed similar PHYB expression levels in both genotypes under dark and light conditions (Figures 4A and 4B; Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B), indicating that Nup96 does not regulate PHYB at the transcriptional level. To determine whether phyB protein abundance or subcellular localization is affected by Nup96, nuclear fractionation was performed in WT and nup96-1. Immunoblot analyses revealed comparable levels of total and nuclear phyB protein in both genotypes (Figure 4C). In the nucleus, phyB assembles into PBs, whose number and size are correlated with phyB signaling strength and phyB-mediated light responses, including hypocotyl elongation (Kim et al., 2024). Therefore, WT and nup96-1 transgenic plants with comparable PHYB expression and phyB protein levels were used to visualize phyB PBs (Supplemental Figures 5C and 5D). Notably, the number of phyB PBs in hypocotyl nuclei was significantly reduced in nup96-1 compared with WT (Figures 4D and 4E). Consistently, the ratio of PB number to nuclear area was markedly lower in nup96-1 than in WT (Figure 4F), supporting a role for Nup96 in promoting phyB PB formation. Similar reductions in phyB PB number were also observed in the cotyledons of nup96-1 plants (Supplemental Figures 5E and 5F). To assess whether reduced phyB PB formation in nup96-1 affects phyB activity, fluence rate response assays were performed using WT and nup96-1 seedlings grown under continuous red light. While the hypocotyl lengths of WT progressively decreased with increasing light intensity, nup96-1 hypocotyl lengths showed little response, indicating compromised phyB activity in the absence of Nup96 (Figure 4G). We next overexpressed PHYB in both WT and nup96-1 backgrounds. RT–qPCR and immunoblot analyses confirmed that phyB transcript and protein levels were comparable between phyB-OE/nup96-1 and phyB-OE lines (Supplemental Figures 5G and 5H). Hypocotyl length measurements of WT, nup96-1, phyB-OE/nup96-1, and phyB-OE seedlings showed that PHYB overexpression partially rescued the long-hypocotyl phenotype of nup96-1 (Figures 4H and 4I). ## Nup96 facilitates PIF5 degradation
To dissect downstream light signaling genes affected by compromised phyB activity in nup96-1, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using WT, nup96-1, and phyB-9 seedlings grown under dark and light conditions. In darkness, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in nup96-1 were enriched in pathways related to oxygen levels, whereas under light conditions, DEGs were predominantly enriched in the “response to light stimulus” category, supporting a role for Nup96 in light signaling (Supplemental Figures 6A–6C; Supplemental Table 2). We therefore compared DEGs between nup96-1 and phyB-9 and found that the commonly upregulated DEGs were enriched in “response to red or far-red light” (Supplemental Figures 6D and 6E; Supplemental Table 2), within which the key photomorphogenesis suppressor PIF5 was identified (Supplemental Figure 6F). The category “response to auxin” was also enriched, consistent with the reported role of Nup96 as SUPPRESSOR OF AUXIN RESISTANCE 3 (Parry et al., 2006). In contrast, commonly downregulated DEGs were enriched in “response to salicylic acid” and “immune response” (Supplemental Figures 6G and 6H). RT–qPCR analysis confirmed that PIF5 transcript levels were comparable between WT and nup96-1 in the dark but were significantly elevated in nup96-1 under light conditions (Figure 5A). Consistently, PIF5 protein abundance was markedly higher in nup96-1 than in WT (Figure 5B). To determine whether Nup96 also regulates other PIFs, we examined PIF4 transcript and protein levels. PIF4 transcript levels were significantly increased in nup96-1, whereas its protein abundance was slightly increased compared with WT (Supplemental Figures 7A–7C). We therefore focused subsequent analyses on PIF5. To assess whether PIF5 overaccumulation in nup96-1 results from increased protein synthesis or reduced protein degradation, WT and nup96-1 seedlings were treated with the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide. Notably, PIF5 protein levels declined rapidly in WT but remained substantially higher in nup96-1, suggesting that PIF5 overaccumulation in nup96-1 is not due to enhanced protein synthesis (Figure 5C; Supplemental Figure 7D). Next, cell-free degradation assays were performed to examine PIF5 degradation kinetics. Specifically, purified PIF5 protein was incubated with total protein extracts from WT or nup96-1, and PIF5 protein levels were monitored over a 30-min period. PIF5 degradation occurred significantly more slowly in nup96-1 extracts than in WT extracts, and addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 suppressed PIF5 degradation in both genotypes (Figures 5D and 5E). ## HOS1 promotes PIF5 degradation
Next, PIF5 protein levels were examined in WT, the hos1-3 mutant, and the hos1-3 complementation line under light conditions. Immunoblotting results showed higher PIF5 protein levels in the hos1-3 mutant compared with WT, whereas complementation of hos1-3 with HOS1 fused to its native promoter restored PIF5 levels to those of WT, suggesting that HOS1 is responsible for PIF5 degradation (Figures 6E and 6F). In addition, a cell-free degradation assay was performed by co-incubating purified PIF5 protein with total protein extracts from WT and the hos1-3 mutant. PIF5 protein levels were monitored over a 30-min period. As shown in Figure 6G, PIF5 protein levels progressively decreased in WT with increasing incubation time, and addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 significantly inhibited PIF5 degradation. However, in the hos1-3 mutant, PIF5 degradation was much slower than in WT, and MG132 inhibited PIF5 degradation less effectively, indicating a key role for HOS1 in mediating PIF5 degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway (Figures 6G and 6H). We further asked whether HOS1 also regulates the degradation of other PIFs by examining PIF4 protein levels in WT and the hos1-3 mutant. WT and hos1-3 exhibited similar PIF4 protein levels, indicating that HOS1-mediated regulation is specific to PIF5 (Supplemental Figures 8A and 8B). Notably, HOS1 interacts with PIF5 in the nucleus; therefore, HOS1 may regulate PIF5 degradation through an NPC-independent mechanism. To investigate the physiological effects of PIF5 overaccumulation in hos1-3, hypocotyl lengths of WT, hos1-3, and the complementation line were quantified. Hypocotyls of hos1-3 were significantly longer than those of WT under light conditions, whereas complementation of hos1-3 restored hos1-3 hypocotyl length to WT levels, consistent with PIF5 protein abundance (Figures 6E, 6F, 6I, and 6J). To test genetic epistasis between PIF5 and HOS1, we crossed pif5-3 with hos1-3 and examined hypocotyl lengths of WT, the hos1-3 and pif5-3 single mutants, as well as the hos1-3/pif5-3 double mutant. Rescue of the long-hypocotyl phenotype of hos1-3 by pif5-3 demonstrated PIF5-dependent regulation of hypocotyl elongation in hos1-3 (Figures 6K and 6L). Collectively, these results suggest that both phyB and HOS1 contribute to PIF5 degradation in nup96-1.

## Discussion
Light-triggered phyB PB formation is critical for light signaling. However, whether and how the NPC—which mediates bidirectional transport of RNAs and proteins between the cytoplasm and nucleus—participates in phyB PB formation and phyB-mediated signal transduction has remained unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that Nup96 promotes PIF5 protein degradation by interacting with phyB to modulate phyB PB formation, enhancing phyB–PIF5 interaction and stabilizing HOS1. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized role for NPC components in light signal transduction and identify Nup96 as a novel regulator of phyB PB formation and PIF5 degradation, offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying plant photomorphogenesis.
We observed that light represses Nup96 transcription but stabilizes Nup96 protein (Figures 2C–2E). This regulatory pattern likely represents a strategy to balance response speed with energy efficiency. Light-induced stabilization of the Nup96 protein provides a rapid means to maintain nuclear pore function without requiring de novo transcription and translation, thereby enabling an immediate response to light signals. In contrast, transcriptional repression of Nup96 may function as a longer-term feedback mechanism to prevent excessive protein accumulation and optimize resource utilization.
A previous study demonstrated that nuclear size is reduced in nup96-1 (Huang et al., 2024). To determine whether reduced nuclear size contributes to the photomorphogenic defects observed in nup96-1, we examined nuclear morphology in mutants of several components of the Nup107-160 subcomplex (nup85, nup96-1, nup107-3, nup133, and nup160-3). As shown in Supplemental Figure 9, nup85, nup96-1, nup107-3, and nup160-3 all exhibited similarly reduced nuclear size; however, only nup96-1 and nup160-3 displayed elongated hypocotyls under light conditions (Supplemental Figures 1A–1C, 9A, and 9B). This lack of a consistent correlation between reduced nuclear size and hypocotyl phenotype suggests that nuclear size reduction is not the primary cause of the elongated hypocotyl phenotype in nup96-1. Consistent with this interpretation, a recent study reported that reduced nuclear size does not underlie the flowering-time defects of nup96-1 (Huang et al., 2024). Notably, the Nup107–160 subcomplex has previously been implicated in the regulation of plant immunity and flowering (Zhang and Li, 2005; Zhang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024). Here, we extended its functional scope to the regulation of hypocotyl elongation. The early flowering phenotype shared by all tested nup mutants and the long-hypocotyl phenotype specific to nup96-1 and nup160-3 (Supplemental Figures 1B and 1C) suggest that distinct nucleoporins within the Nup107-160 subcomplex regulate flowering and hypocotyl elongation through different mechanisms.
The phyB–PIF module serves as a central hub for regulating hypocotyl elongation. Recent work showed that overexpression of PHYB leads to enlargement of phyB PBs, which exert dual effects on PIF5 degradation: slightly overexpression of PHYB promotes PIF5 degradation, whereas significantly overexpression of PHYB stabilizes PIF5 (Kim et al., 2024). Extending these findings, our results demonstrate that a reduced number of phyB PBs in nup96-1 impairs PIF5 degradation (Figures 4D–4F and 5B–5E), thereby establishing phyB PBs as critical platforms for mediating PIF5 degradation and underscoring their importance in phyB signaling. The reduction in phyB PB number without changes in nuclear phyB abundance suggests that Nup96 does not directly regulate the nucleocytoplasmic transport of phyB (Figure 4C). Notably, phyB PBs differ in their subnuclear localization: some associate with the nucleolus, whereas others do not, and the non-nucleolar-associated PBs are more sensitive to temperature changes (Hahm et al., 2020). Our data show that Nup96 interacts with phyB at the nuclear envelope, potentially recruiting phyB toward the nuclear periphery and promoting the formation of non-nucleolar-associated PBs, thereby fine-tuning plant responses to fluctuating light conditions. To test this hypothesis, we quantified phyB PBs in phyB-GFP and phyB-GFP/nup96-1 seedlings with comparable phyB protein levels. We observed an increased proportion of nucleolar-associated PBs and a corresponding decrease in non-nucleolar-associated PBs in nup96-1 compared with WT (Supplemental Figure 10). These findings support the hypothesis that phyB PBs may preferentially form at the nuclear periphery. In contrast to Nup96—which influences hypocotyl elongation by modulating phyB PB formation rather than directly affecting PHYB expression or PHYB subcellular localization (Figures 4A–4F)—Nup98a regulates hypocotyl elongation under shaded conditions by upregulating PHYB expression (Gallemi et al., 2016). The distinct roles of Nup98a and Nup96 in controlling hypocotyl elongation may stem from their association with different NPC subcomplexes. Each NPC subcomplex likely fulfills specialized functions in plant development to ensure precise regulation of RNA and protein transport in response to diverse environmental cues.
Emerging evidence indicates that, beyond facilitating molecular transport between the cytoplasm and nucleus, the NPC also plays important roles in regulating gene expression and chromatin dynamics (Jung et al., 2014; Tang, 2023). For example, under warm temperature conditions, Nup96 is required for the proper expression of high-temperature-responsive genes (Zhang et al., 2020). Similarly, during salt stress, Nup96 is essential for the expression of a substantial subset of salt-induced genes, as well as genes involved in ethylene signaling (Nie et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). In our study, we observed elevated PIF5 expression in the nup96-1 mutant, suggesting that Nup96 acts to suppress PIF5 transcription. Several lines of evidence support this hypothesis. First, DEGs in nup96-1 are significantly enriched for “transcription regulatory activity” (Supplemental Figure 11). Second, HOS1 has been reported to function as a chromatin remodeling factor (Jung et al., 2014), raising the possibility that Nup96 regulates PIF5 expression through HOS1. Consistent with this idea, PIF5 transcript levels are increased in the hos1-3 mutant (Huang et al., 2024). Although the precise mechanism by which Nup96 suppresses PIF5 expression remains unclear, these findings collectively support a regulatory role for Nup96 in modulating PIF5 transcription. A recent study further demonstrated that Nup96 interacts with RNA polymerase II (RPB) to promote RPB occupancy at FLC and to position FLC at the nuclear periphery, thereby enhancing FLC expression (Huang et al., 2024). This observation strongly supports a role for Nup96 in transcriptional regulation. The dual function of Nup96—promoting FLC expression while repressing PIF5—suggests that Nup96 can exert both positive and negative regulatory effects on gene expression. In addition to Nup96, plant nuclear envelope transmembrane protein 2 (PNET2) and nuclear envelope–associated proteins (NEAPs) have also been shown to regulate gene expression through chromatin association (Tang et al., 2022; Tang, 2023), indicating a broader involvement of nuclear envelope proteins in chromatin-based gene regulation.
PIF degradation is central to light signal transduction. Previous studies have identified several E3 ligases involved in PIF turnover, among which CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1) is the only E3 ligase previously reported to mediate PIF5 degradation. Specifically, the COP1–SPA complex stabilizes PIF5 in the dark and promotes its degradation upon light exposure through the 26S proteasome pathway (Pham et al., 2018). Here, we identify the RING finger E3 ligase HOS1 as a promoter of PIF5 degradation and further demonstrate that this regulatory mechanism does not extend to PIF4 (Figures 6E–6H; Supplemental Figures 8A and 8B). This finding is consistent with earlier reports showing that HOS1 does not regulate PIF4 protein stability (Kim et al., 2017). However, how HOS1 selectively targets PIF5 rather than PIF4 for degradation remains unclear.
In summary, our findings support a model in which, in WT plants, Nup96 promotes PIF5 degradation by interacting with phyB to facilitate PB formation and enhance phyB–PIF5 interaction, as well as by stabilizing HOS1. This coordinated regulation suppresses BBX18 and BBX19 expression, resulting in shorter hypocotyls. In contrast, in the nup96-1 mutant, reduced numbers of phyB PBs and destabilization of HOS1 collectively impair PIF5 degradation. The resulting accumulation of PIF5 elevates BBX18 and BBX19 expression, leading to elongated hypocotyls (Figure 7E).

## Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
Arabidopsis Col-0 was used as the WT in this study. nup96-1 (SALK_109959), nup160-3 (SALK_133728C), phyB-9, and pif5-3 were provided by Professor Katayoon Dehesh (University of California, Riverside). T-DNA insertion lines of Arabidopsis nup43 (SALK_095344), nup85 (SALK_086258), nup107-3 (SALK_057072), nup133 (SALK_092608), seh1-1 (SALK_022717), sec13a (SALK_202621C), sec13b-1 (SALK_045825), and hos1-3 (SALK_069312C) were obtained from AraShare (http://www.arashare.cn). Nup96pro:Nup96-GFP/nup96-1 and Nup96pro:Nup96m-GFP/nup96-1 were kindly provided by Professor Yong-Fu Fu (Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences). HOS1pro:HOS1-GFP/hos1-3 was obtained from Yingfang Zhu (Henan University). Transgenic lines 35S:Nup96-Myc, PHYBpro:phyB-GFP, Nup96pro:GUS, and PHYBpro:GUS were generated by Agrobacterium tumefaciens–mediated transformation. PHYBpro:GUS/nup96-1 was generated by crossing PHYBpro:GUS with nup96-1. The nup96-1/pif5-3 and hos1-3/pif5-3 double mutants were generated by crossing nup96-1 and hos1-3 with pif5-3, respectively. The CR-Nup96 line was generated using the CRISPR–Cas9 system.
Seeds were surface-sterilized and sown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (½MS) medium containing 0.7% (w/v) agar, stratified at 4 °C for three days, and then transferred to a growth chamber maintained at 22 °C under continuous blue, far-red, or red light, or under long-day conditions (LD; 16 h light/8 h dark). Unless otherwise specified, all samples were collected at Zeitgeber time 10 (ZT10). Light intensities are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

## Hypocotyl length measurement
Four-day-old seedlings grown in darkness or 7-day-old seedlings grown under continuous blue light, continuous far-red light, continuous red light, or LD conditions were photographed. Hypocotyl lengths were measured using ImageJ.

## Plasmid construction and generation of transgenic Arabidopsis plants
To generate the 35S:NUP96-Myc construct, the CDS of Nup96 was amplified and cloned into the pDONR207 vector using Gateway BP Clonase Ⅱ (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and subsequently recombined into the pGWB517 vector using Gateway LR Clonase Ⅱ (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
To generate the PHYBpro:phyB-GFP construct, the native PHYB promoter (2 kb upstream of the PHYB start codon) and the phyB coding sequence were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector.
To generate the Nup96pro:GUS and PHYBpro:GUS constructs, the Nup96 promoter (2.2 kb upstream of the Nup96 start codon) and the PHYB promoter (2 kb upstream of the start codon) were amplified and ligated into the pCX-GUS vector.
For Y2H assays, the CDSs of phyB, phyB-D1 (1–634 aa), phyB-D2 (615–908 aa), phyB-D3 (901–1172 aa), Nup96, Nup96-D1 (1–506 aa), and Nup96-D2 (501–1047 aa) were amplified and cloned into the pDONR207 vector, followed by recombination into the pGBKT7 or pGADT7 vectors (Clontech) to generate AD-phyB, AD-phyB-D1, AD-phyB-D2, AD-phyB-D3, BD-Nup96, BD-Nup96-D1, and BD-Nup96-D2, respectively. The CDSs of PIF5 and HOS1 were amplified, cloned into the pDONR207 vector, and subsequently recombined into pGBKT7 or pGADT7 to generate AD-PIF5 and BD-HOS1, respectively.
For split LUC complementation assays, the CDSs of Nup96 and phyB were amplified and cloned into the pDONR207 vector and then recombined into the pMK7-nLUC or pMK7-cLUC vectors. The CDSs of PIF5 and HOS1 were cloned into pCAMBIA1300-nLUC or pCAMBIA1300-cLUC to generate pCAMBIA1300-PIF5-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-HOS1-cLUC, respectively.
All transgenic plants were generated using the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 1998). Primers used in this study are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

## Y2H assays
Y2H assays were performed according to the Clontech Yeast Protocols Handbook. Briefly, bait vectors (pGBKT7) and prey vectors (pGADT7) carrying the indicated constructs were co-transformed into Y2H Gold yeast cells. Transformants were plated on selective medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD/−Leu/−Trp) and grown at 30 °C for 3 days. Colonies were then transferred to selective medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, histidine, and adenine (SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade) to assess protein–protein interactions.

## Split LUC complementation assay
phyB-nLUC and Nup96-cLUC, HOS1-cLUC and PIF5-nLUC, as well as the corresponding control combinations, were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h of incubation, the abaxial surface of the leaves was sprayed with 1 mM luciferin, and luminescence signals were detected using a CCD camera (Tanon 4800 Multi).

## BiFC assays
nYFP-Nup96 and cYFP-phyB, HOS1-nYFP and PIF5-cYFP, along with the corresponding controls, were co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. After 48 h of incubation, YFP fluorescence signals were detected using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM980).

## Co-IP assay
Tobacco leaves (0.4 g) co-expressing phyB-GFP and Nup96-Myc or HOS1-GFP and PIF5-Myc were homogenized in 2 ml of IP buffer (40 mM Tris–HCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, EDTA-free protease inhibitor, and 2% glycerol) and centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. An aliquot (100 μl) of the supernatant was retained as input. The remaining supernatant was incubated with GFP-Trap beads (ChromoTek) at 4 °C for 2–3 h, followed by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The beads were collected, washed three times with IP buffer, and eluted in 2× SDS loading buffer. Total and immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-GFP (1:5000 [v/v], catalog no. HT80, TransGen) and anti-Myc (1:5000 [v/v], catalog no. A00704, GenScript) antibodies.

## Histochemical GUS staining
Four-day-old homozygous transgenic Nup96pro:GUS, PHYBpro:GUS, and PHYBpro:GUS/nup96-1 seedlings grown under dark or light conditions were subjected to histochemical GUS staining. Seedlings were submerged in GUS staining solution (HUYUETANG, Beijing, China) overnight and subsequently incubated in 70% ethanol for 3–6 h to remove chlorophyll. Images were captured using a stereomicroscope equipped with an SZ2-ILST CCD camera (Olympus).

## Protein extraction and nuclear fractionation
Total proteins for PIF immunoblot analysis were extracted as described previously (Qiu et al., 2017). Briefly, 7-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were harvested and homogenized in extraction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA [pH 8.0], 5% [w/v] SDS, 20% [v/v] glycerol, 20 mM DTT, 40 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 2 mM PMSF, 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche], 80 μM MG132 [Sigma], 80 μM MG115 [Sigma], 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [Roche], and 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide). Samples were immediately boiled for 10 min and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Supernatants were collected for immunoblot analyses.
Nuclear fractionation was performed as described previously (Peng et al., 2022). Seven-day-old WT and nup96-1 seedlings grown under LD conditions were harvested and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.4], 25% [v/v] glycerol, 20 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM DTT, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). Homogenates were filtered twice through two layers of Miracloth (Merck Millipore) and retained as total protein extracts. The extracts were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and further centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the cytosolic fraction. The remaining pellet was washed four times with NRBT buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 25% (v/v) glycerol, 2.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100, followed by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 2 min at 4 °C each time. The pellet was then resuspended in 300 μL of NRBT2 buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 0.035% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Subsequently, NRBT3 buffer containing 1.7 M sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 2 mM MgCl2, 0.15% (v/v) Triton X-100, 0.035% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail was added. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C, the pellet was resuspended in 2× SDS loading buffer and collected as the nuclear protein fraction.
The primary antibodies used were anti-PIF4 (1:750 [v/v], catalog no. AS163955, Agrisera), anti-PIF5 (1:1000 [v/v], catalog no. AS122112, Agrisera), anti-histone H3 (1:5000 [v/v], catalog no. ab1791, Abcam), anti-actin (1:5000 [v/v], catalog no. D191048, Sangon Biotech), and anti-phyB (1:1000 [v/v], produced by Beijing Protein Innovation). The secondary antibodies used were goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000 [v/v], catalog no. A3562, Sigma), goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000 [v/v], catalog no. A9169, Sigma), and rabbit anti-goat IgG (1:10,000 [v/v], catalog no. 305-035-003, Jackson ImmunoResearch).

## Protein purification
Cells harboring the GST-PIF5 recombinant construct were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside at 16 °C overnight, harvested at 4000 rpm for 20 min, and resuspended in PBS buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4 and 1.8 mM KH2PO4). Cells were lysed by sonication and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C. The GST-PIF5 recombinant protein in the supernatant was purified using GST beads (GE Healthcare).

## Cell-free protein degradation assay
Total proteins were extracted from 7-day-old WT and nup96-1 or WT and hos1-3 seedlings grown under LD conditions using extraction buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 0.5 M sucrose, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 5 mM DTT. Cell-free protein degradation assays were performed as described previously (Kong et al., 2015). Briefly, GST-PIF5 protein was mixed with total protein extracts and incubated at 25 °C for the indicated times in the presence or absence of MG132. Samples were then analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti-GST antibody (1:5000 [v/v], catalog no. HT601, TransGen).

## RNA extraction and RT–qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from seedlings grown under dark and light conditions using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega). Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (TransGen). Gene-specific primers were designed using QuantPrime (http://www.quantprime.de) and are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

## Confocal microscopy
Four-day-old Nup96pro:Nup96-GFP/nup96-1 seedlings grown in darkness or under LD conditions were used for Nup96 subcellular localization analyses using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. Seven-day-old phyB-GFP and phyB-GFP/nup96-1 seedlings grown under LD conditions were fixed as previously described (Yoo et al., 2019). Images of individual nuclei from cotyledon epidermal cells or hypocotyls were captured. The number of phyB PBs was quantified using ZEN 3.8 software.

## Transcriptome analyses
RNA seq was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with three biological replicates. Reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis reference genome (TAIR10) using HISAT2. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (false discovery rate <0.05, fold change > 2). Venn diagrams were generated using Venn (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/home/report/reportvenn.html). GO enrichment analyses were conducted using the Gene Ontology database (http://www.geneontology.org/).

## Statistical analyses
All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined using Tukey’s HSD test or Student’s t-test, with p < 0.05 considered significant.
