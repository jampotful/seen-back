Abstract:

31

Light perception at dawn plays a key role in coordinating multiple molecular

32

processes and in entraining the plant circadian clock. An Arabidopsis mutant

33

lacking the main photoreceptors however still shows clock entrainment, indicating

34

that the integration of light into the morning transcriptome is not well understood.

35

We performed a high-resolution RNA-seq time series experiment, sampling every

36

two-minutes from dawn. In parallel experiments, we perturbed temperature, the

37

circadian clock, photoreceptor signalling and chloroplast-derived light

38

signalling. We used this data to infer a gene network that describes the gene

39

expression dynamics after light stimulus in the morning, and then we validated

40

key edges. By sampling time points at high density, we are able to identify three

41

light- and temperature-sensitive bursts of transcription factor activity, one of which

42

lasts for only about eight minutes. Phytochrome and cryptochrome mutants

43

cause a delay in the transcriptional bursts at dawn, and completely remove a

44

burst of expression in key photomorphogenesis genes (HY5 and BBX

45

family). Our complete network is available online (http://www-

46

users.york.ac.uk/~de656/dawnBurst/dawnBurst.html). Taken together, our

47

results show that phytochrome and cryptochrome signaling is required for fine-

48

tuning the dawn transcriptional response to light, but separate pathways can

49

robustly activate much of the programme in their absence.

50

Introduction

51

Light at dawn is a major cue enabling plants to entrain circadian gene expression

52

programmes, coordinate developmental processes such as elongation growth and

53

anticipate likely daytime stresses. Transcripts that are induced at dawn include genes

54

involved in responses to drought (Grundy et al., 2015), and heat (Dickinson et al.,

55

2018), as well as in anthocyanin biosynthesis (Seaton et al., 2018). In addition to abiotic

56

stresses, Arabidopsis is also less susceptible to biotic stresses such as certain fungal

57

pathogens in the morning, which is linked to jasmonic acid signalling (Ingle et al.,

58

2015). Phytohormone genes in general represent another set of dawn-expressed genes
2

59

and are essential for controlling the diurnal rhythm of growth, and many of these genes

60

feature a G-box motif in their promoter (Michael et al., 2008). Consistent with this, a

61

large set of genes with G-box promoter motifs that are expressed within one hour of

62

dawn including many that are involved in phytohormone signalling and in response to

63

metals (Ezer et al., 2017b).

64

Light at dawn plays a key role for entraining the circadian clock (Millar, 2004; P. A.

65

Salomé and McClung, 2005; Webb et al., 2019), a process that is further contributed to

66

by changes in temperature (Gil and Park, 2019) and even humidity (Mwimba et al.,

67

2018). Despite the physiological importance of these responses, how light at dawn

68

entrains the clock is not fully understood. Red- and blue-light sensors – including

69

phytochrome A (phyA), phyB and cryptochrome 1 (cry1) – are involved in

70

entrainment (Devlin and Kay, 2000; Hall et al., 2002; Salomé et al., 2002; Somers et al.,

71

1998), while blue-light receptors of the zeitlupe (ztl) family also have clock-associated

72

functions, but appear to be more relevant at later times during the day and at dusk

73

(Christie et al., 2015; Suetsugu and Wada, 2013). In addition to clock entrainment,

74

phytochromes and cryptochromes are also core regulators of photoperiodism and

75

photomorphogenesis (Franklin et al., 2005; Jackson, 2009; Kami et al., 2010). phyA in

76

particular has been reported as a key sensor of dawn and photoperiod and is essential

77

for the induction of the core circadian clock component PSEUDO RESPONSE

78

REGULATOR 9 (PRR9) (Seaton et al., 2018). PRR9 and PRR7 together with the MYB

79

transcription factors CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) and LATE

80

ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY) constitute the morning loop of the circadian clock

81

(Creux and Harmer, 2019).

82

Many higher order mutants of photoreceptor or clock genes disrupt the gating of

83

hypocotyl growth and photoperiodic regulation of flowering, but have limited effects on

84

entrainment: a phyA phyB cry1 cry2 mutant still displays a functional clock (Yanovsky et

85

al., 2000) while clock mutants such as cca1 lhy and prr7 prr9 retain rhythmicity in many

86

clock outputs under light:dark photocycles (Farré et al., 2005; Mizoguchi et al., 2002),

87

suggesting that other pathways contribute to the entrainment and dawn-induced gene

88

expression. Indeed, photosynthesis and sugar production can also entrain the circadian

3

89

clock, producing a second ‘metabolic dawn’ (Haydon et al., 2013) and a chloroplast-

90

derived light signal is known to gate the expression of thermotolerance genes that peak

91

after dawn via HEAT SHOCK FACTOR A1 (HSFA1) transcription factors (Dickinson et

92

al., 2018).

93

To understand the burst of gene expression in response to light in the morning, we

94

performed a highly resolved RNA-seq time series. We find five distinct transcriptional

95

waves within the first two hours of the morning and characterise how each wave of

96

expression responds to temperature elevation and light signals during the night and

97

subjective day. By analysing a photoreceptor mutant (phyA phyB cry1 cry2), a circadian

98

clock mutant (prr5 prr7 prr9), and a mutant impaired in chloroplast light/temperature

99

signalling (hsfa1QK), we reveal that the dawn burst is not globally controlled by a single

100

pathway, but that partially distinct subsets of dawn genes are perturbed in each of these

101

mutant backgrounds. Furthermore, we infer a gene regulatory network and validate

102

edges using DNA binding data. We find that ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) and

103

B-BOX PROTEIN 31 (BBX31) are among the TFs that are predicted to regulate multiple

104

expression waves, and that HY5 and multiple BBX genes constitute a dawn subnetwork

105

that is controlled by phytochromes and cryptochromes to finetune photomorphogenic

106

and photoperiodic responses in the early morning.

107

Results

108

There is a burst of gene expression of DNA binding proteins at dawn

109

Since many groups of genes have been shown to have a dawn peak, we investigated

110

how extensive this behaviour is, and the extent to which genes encoding transcriptional

111

regulators also exhibit a peak in expression (Table S1). In previous 24 h RNA-seq time

112

courses (Ezer et al., 2017a; Jung et al., 2016), we observe that 39% of all genes

113

encoding DNA binding proteins peak immediately before (ZT0) or an hour after dawn

114

(ZT1) (Figure S1A). Genes related to abiotic stresses (Grundy et al., 2015; Dickinson et

115

al., 2018) and the phytohormones abscisic acid (ABA), auxin and brassinosteroid (BR)

116

(Michael et al. (2008) were previously found to display increased expression in the

4

117

morning. Consistent with these studies, we found that there is a significant enrichment

118

for ABA and ethylene-linked DNA binding proteins that have maximal expression in this

119

time period compared to other DNA binding proteins (58% and 55%, p<0.005 in both

120

cases using a Fisher Exact test with Bonferroni correction), (Figure 1A i, ii). There was

121

also an enrichment for DNA-binding proteins that are associated with GO terms related

122

to light (53%, p<0.02) and stress (55%, p<0.002) (Figure 1A iii, iv). However, we

123

found no significant enrichment for auxin or BR in the morning, which differs from

124

observations by Michael et al. (2008), and neither were circadian clock-associated DNA

125

binding proteins enriched, which is expected since only a subset of circadian genes are

126

active in the morning (Figure S1A).

127

Intriguingly, while phytochromes are important for light entrainment (Somers et al.,

128

1998), light entrainment of most transcripts is not lost in the phyABCDE background.

129

DNA binding factors with a peak in gene expression in the hour after dawn still largely

130

show a peak in expression in phyABCDE, although these are somewhat perturbed and

131

several individual genes show a large fold-change of expression (Figure 1B). Since

132

genes with a peak in expression immediately before dawn show increased expression

133

in phyABCDE before and after dawn we conclude that phytochromes may play a more

134

important role in inhibiting nighttime genes than activating morning genes. We observe

135

the same trends in a mutant of the evening clock gene EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3),

136

which is consistent with both pathways mainly controlling nighttime gene

137

expression (Ezer et al., 2017a) (Figure S1B).

138

There are multiple waves of gene expression after light exposure at

139

dawn

140

While many regulatory genes have peak expression at the onset of light, we do not

141

know whether these genes are all expressed in one cluster, or whether there are

142

separate individually resolved waves of morning gene expression. To address this, we

143

performed a high-resolution time series experiment at the onset of light, with 25 time

144

points sampled at 22°C (Table S2, Figure S2).

5

145

To visualise these data, we clustered the gene expression values from the 22°C time

146

series, focusing on genes with GO terms associated with DNA binding (see Methods

147

and Table S3). The dawn burst consists of multiple coordinated transcript waves of

148

genes associated with DNA binding. While there are genes whose expression levels

149

decrease (clusters 1-2) or increase (clusters 3-4) over the time course, we observe

150

three distinct bursts of expression. For the purposes of describing these clusters in a

151

concise way, we will refer to them as the clusters that peak at 16-24 minutes (clusters 7-

152

8), 18-45 minutes (clusters 9-10), and 45-105 minutes (clusters 5-6) (Figure 2A). The

153

full gene expression profiles can be viewed as box plots in Figure S3.

154

These clusters contain many genes that are relevant to light signalling, temperature

155

response, and immunity. Clusters 7-8 include many genes that are related to

156

temperature, such as HEAT SHOCK FACTOR A1A (HSFA1A) and immunity genes

157

such as the WRKY transcription factor WRKY33 and BASIC LEUCINE ZIPPER 10

158

(bZIP10). This is consistent with other research that suggests that plants may be less

159

sensitive to biotic stress at dawn (Ingle et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2011). Interestingly,

160

clusters 5-6 include HEAT SHOCK FACTOR B2B (HSFB2B) that suppresses the heat

161

shock response and inhibits HSFA1A (Ikeda et al., 2011). The clusters of genes that

162

peak at 45-105 minutes include genes that appear to be downstream of red (RVE7,

163

HY5, JMJ22, PNT1) and blue (MYC2, MYC4, CIB2, CRY3) light signalling.

164

The dawn gene expression waves are sensitive to temperature and

165

light

166

Given that light and temperature strongly influence the circadian clock and that many

167

dawn burst genes are associated with both light and temperature responses, we

168

investigated the effect of temperature and light perturbations on dawn expression

169

waves. We repeated the high resolution RNA-seq experiment at 27°C (Table S2,

170

Figure S2), and found that many genes continue to peak at the same time, although we

171

detect an earlier rise in expression for clusters 7-8. In addition, we observe that the

172

earlier expressed genes (clusters 1-2 and 7-8) have elevated expression at 27°C

173

compared to 22°C while peak expression was lower for the later expressed genes

6

174

(clusters 3-6) at 27°C (Figure 2A). Please recall that the clusters were determined

175

using the 22°C time series only. These results are shown in a complementary way

176

using boxplots in Figure S4.

177

Previously, Rugnone et al. (2013) found sets of genes that were induced or repressed

178

by light, and moreover they identified genes whose sensitivity to light was dependent on

179

whether the light treatment occurred at night or during the subjective day (after an

180

extended night). Comparing their gene lists with ours (Figure 2B, Table S4), we find

181

significant enrichment for light-repressed genes among early expressed genes in

182

clusters 1-2 (p-value < 1e-14, based on Fisher exact test with Bonferroni correction). In

183

contrast, those genes that were expressed late in the time series or that peaked at 45-

184

105 minutes or 18-45 minutes tended to be light-induced. We identified a number of key

185

light signalling genes in clusters 5-6 of our high resolution time course and we find that

186

over a third of genes within this group are light-induced. Intriguingly, the clusters of

187

genes that includes HSFA1A (clusters 7-8) are not enriched for light-induced genes,

188

except for a slight enrichment for genes whose expression is induced by light at night

189

(p-value <0.037).

190

These results show that there are multiple bursts of gene expression at dawn that are

191

differentially regulated by temperature and light, and moreover many of the dawn-

192

expressed genes are regulated by light in a time-of-day dependent manner.

193

Late dawn gene expression waves are delayed in both phyA phyB

194

cry1 cry2 (light signalling) and prr5 prr7 prr9 (circadian) mutants

195

DNA binding proteins appear to have multiple coordinated bursts of expression at dawn

196

and these bursts are either light- or temperature-dependent or both. To characterise

197

the gene regulatory mechanisms that underlie these distinct bursts, we performed RNA-

198

seq on a series of mutant strains that perturbed red- and blue-light sensitivity (phyA

199

phyB cry1 cry2, Ler background), the circadian clock morning loop (prr5 prr7 prr9, Col-0

200

background) and chloroplast-derived light/temperature signals (hsfA1QK, mostly Col-0

201

background), all of which have been previously characterised (Nakamichi et al., 2005;

202

Yanovsky et al., 2000; Yoshida et al., 2011). These were sampled at the most

7

203

informative time points according to NITPicker (Ezer and Keir, 2019). We observe

204

similar patterns of expression in this subset of time points as we were able to see in the

205

original time series in Col-0 (Figure 2C). There was strong temporal autocorrelation

206

within each time series (Figure S2A), suggesting that gene expression varied smoothly

207

over time, and consistent gene expression patterns across Col-0 biological replicates

208

(Figure S2B), despite different sequencing protocols being used. To be able to detect

209

common patterns of regulation among these genes, we re-clustered each of the five

210

main gene groups separately (Figure S5A, Table S5). Of 760 transcription factor

211

genes retained in this analysis, we find that roughly 50% show similar trends in

212

expression changes in phyA phyB cry1 cry2 and prr5 prr7 prr9 compared to the

213

respective wild types, i.e. they either show up- or downregulation in both mutants (e.g.

214

the large sub-clusters 1-2-III and 5-6-IX). On the other hand, approximately 200 genes

215

show distinct expression changes in one of the two mutants, i.e. they are misregulated

216

in only one background or show opposite trends in the two mutants (e.g. sub-clusters 3-

217

4-IX and 5-6-X). Compared to the photoreceptor and clock mutants, fewer sub-clusters

218

show clear misexpression in hsfa1QK, which is consistent with HSFA1 regulating many

219

non-TF targets, in particular heat shock proteins (Dickinson et al., 2018). The mutant-

220

specific differences in gene expression are described in depth in the legend of Figure

221

S5A.

222

Thus, it appears that the circadian clock, classical light signalling via

223

phytochromes/cryptochromes and, to a lesser degree, chloroplast-derived light signals

224

via HSFA1 contribute to the coordination of TF gene expression in the early morning.

225

We also note that very few genes among the early expression bursts (clusters 7-10)

226

show reduced expression in any mutant background. This could be attributed to the fact

227

that multiple signalling routes need to be disrupted for clear reduction to occur;

228

however, it is likely that additional light input pathways exist that are not covered in our

229

analysis and contribute particularly to the control of the early dawn burst.

230

An early morning gene regulatory network

231

Next, we wished to identify possible regulatory links by inferring a gene regulatory

232

network. Since transcription and translation are distinct events, it follows that there will
8

233

be a time delay between the appearance of a transcript and its cognate protein

234

corresponding to the time required to translate the mRNA. We therefore applied a time

235

delay network inference method called dynGenie3 (Huynh-Thu and Geurts,

236

2018). Because all large-scale network inference algorithms have large false-positive

237

rates, we wished to confirm as many edges as possible using available DNA binding

238

data via DAP-seq from O’Malley et al. (2016).

239

Among TFs in our network with available binding data, we found that 34.3% of our high

240

confidence edges (top 1% highest scoring edges) were consistent with DAP-seq. In

241

contrast, we only observed a 12.6% overlap with DAP-seq data among edges in our

242

network that received a score of ‘0’ using dynGenie3 among TF with available DAP-seq

243

data. This suggests that dynGenie3 is successfully enriching for biologically plausible

244

edges.

245

From now on, we only consider the subset of the dynGenie3 network that is consistent

246

with DAP-seq data (Figure 3A): the network consistently forms a U-shape under a

247

force-directed layout, with early expressed genes primarily on the left side of the U and

248

late expressed genes primarily on the right side. These genes are enriched for

249

photoperiodism-related GO terms, including GO terms associated with light sensing,

250

heat response and circadian rhythms (Figure 3B).

251

A number of transcription factors that are known to play a role in light signalling and

252

photomorphogenesis sit at the base of the U, linking early and late expressed

253

genes; these include HY5, BBX31, CYCLING DOF FACTOR 5 (CDF5) and REVEILLE

254

1 (RVE1) (Figure 3A). These transcription factors are all involved in light-controlled

255

hypocotyl elongation (Chattopadhyay et al., 1998; Heng et al., 2019; Martín et al., 2018;

256

Rawat et al., 2009). While RVE1 and CDF5 expression is under strong circadian control

257

and peaks at dawn (Henriques et al., 2017; Rawat et al., 2009), HY5 and BBX31 are

258

light-induced genes (Heng et al., 2019; Oyama et al., 1997) and their transcript levels

259

display a strong peak post dawn in our time course data (Figure S5B). Based on the

260

timing of their expression and their position within our network, RVE1 and CDF5 may

261

promote expression of growth-related genes before dawn, while HY5 and BBX31

262

appear to coordinate the down-regulation of early dawn genes as well as the induction
9

263

of subsequent transcriptional waves. Moreover, betweenness (HY5 and BBX31 have

264

the 12th and 13th highest values) and page rank (HY5 and BBX31 have the 6th and 3rd

265

highest values) indicate the important position of HY5 and BBX31 within the network

266

structure (Table S2).

267

HY5 is one of the most thoroughly characterised components of the light signalling

268

pathway and, aside from its role in promoting photomorphogenesis, has been found to

269

affect the circadian clock, temperature responses, chlorophyll and anthocyanin

270

biosynthesis as well as nutrient uptake (Gangappa and Botto, 2016). In agreement with

271

its multifaceted roles, we find genes related to light signalling (PHYTOCHROME

272

INTERACTING FACTOR 4/PIF4, EARLY LIGHT INDUCIBLE PROTEIN

273

1/ELIP1, BBX16), multiple heat shock genes, the circadian clock genes TIMING OF

274

CAB EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1) and ALTERED SEED GERMINATION 4 (ASG4)/RVE3

275

as well as several UDP glucosyltransferase genes involved in flavonoid metabolism

276

among its predicted targets (Figure 3C). In contrast to HY5, BBX31 represents a

277

negative regulator of photomorphogenesis (Heng et al., 2019). Its targets do not show

278

strong enrichment for specific biological processes but include the related BBX

279

transcription factors BBX18 and BBX30 as well as HY5 itself (Figure 3D). It has

280

previously been shown that HY5 represses BBX31 transcription (Heng et al., 2019), but

281

based on our data a reciprocal regulation of HY5 by BBX31 may occur as well.

282
283

Phytochromes and cryptochromes control morning

284

photomorphogenesis pathways via BBX family proteins and HY5

285

A regulatory network of BBX transcription factors, HY5 and the related bZIP

286

transcription factor HY5 HOMOLOG (HYH) has been postulated to control a large

287

portion of the light-regulated transcriptome (Xu, 2019). We observe that multiple

288

members of this network feature strongly during the dawn burst: not only HY5

289

and BBX31, but also HYH, BBX16, BBX24/SALT TOLERANCE (STO), BBX25/SALT

290

TOLERANCE HOMOLOG (STH), BBX30 and BBX32 transcript levels peak within an

291

hour after dawn, while these peaks are completely abolished in the phyA phyB cry1 cry2

10

292

mutant background (Figure 4) but only partially reduced in phyA phyB and cry1 cry2

293

double mutants (Figure S6). For many of these genes, expression still peaked at dawn

294

in prr5 prr7 prr9, but was generally higher and persisted over a longer time, while their

295

expression remained largely unaffected in hsfa1QK (Figure S7). This implies that

296

phytochromes and cryptochromes act in parallel to induce HY5, HYH and the BBX

297

genes (hereafter referred to as BBX Set A). Another set of BBX genes (BBX Set B),

298

comprised of BBX20 and BBX21, does not exhibit a dawn burst in expression, but their

299

transcript levels are strongly upregulated in the phyA phyB cry1 cry2 mutant (Figure 4).

300

Interestingly, apart from BBX16 all transcription factors included in Set A have been

301

shown to negatively regulate photomorphogenesis (Gangappa et al., 2013, 2013; Heng

302

et al., 2019; Holtan et al., 2011; Indorf et al., 2007; Job et al., 2018), while HY5, HYH as

303

well as BBX20 and BBX21 represent photomorphogenesis-promoting transcription

304

factors (Bursch et al., 2020; Chattopadhyay et al., 1998; Fan et al., 2012; Holm et al.,

305

2002; Job et al., 2018).

306

We sought to further examine the BBX/HY5 transcriptional network and its light

307

regulation with a set of qPCR experiments and monitored gene expression (a) during an

308

extended night, (b) in phyA phyB cry1 cry2, prr5 prr7 prr9 and hsfa1QK mutants (to

309

confirm the RNA-seq results), and (c) in hy5 hyh and bbx30 bbx31 mutants as well as in

310

a BBX31 overexpressor (BBX31-Ox). We investigated the expression patterns of BBX

311

and HY5/HYH genes shown in Figure 4 as well as six putative downstream targets of

312

BBX31/HY5 predicted in our network – BBX27 and PIF4, which show a drop in

313

transcript levels after dawn, as well as BBX18, POPEYE (PYE), ASG4/RVE3 and COLD

314

CIRCADIAN RHYTHM AND RNA-BINDING 1 (CCR1).

315

Notably, dawn peaks in the transcripts of BBX set A, HY5 and HYH were completely

316

absent during an extended night as well as in the phyA phyB cry1 cry2 mutant (Figure

317

5A, B; S8A, B), while expression levels of BBX set B remained high under these

318

conditions (Figure 5C; S8C). Putative targets that display induced expression after

319

dawn mimicked the pattern observed for BBX set A, while putative targets with

320

decreasing expression after dawn (BBX27, PIF4) displayed a similar pattern to BBX set

321

B (Figure 5D, S8D). These observations confirm that it is a light stimulus, and not the

11

322

circadian clock, that tightly controls this part of the dawn burst, and it can largely be

323

accounted for by light sensing via phytochromes and cryptochromes.

324

Expression patterns in bbx30 bbx31 were largely indistinguishable from those in wild

325

type (Figure 5; S8), likely because of the high degree of redundancy among BBX

326

proteins and/or residual BBX30 function as we detected high levels of truncated BBX30

327

transcript in the mutant. However, many dawn-induced genes, including the putative

328

direct targets BBX18, ASG4 and CCR1, displayed substantially lower transcript levels in

329

both the hy5 hyh mutant and the BBX31-Ox line (Figure 5A, B, D; S8A, B, D),

330

indicating that HY5 and HYH act as activators of the dawn burst, while BBX31

331

represents a negative regulator. Only one exception to this pattern was found: PYE

332

expression was unchanged in BBX31-Ox, but reduced in both hy5 hyh and bbx30

333

bbx31, suggesting positive effects of both upstream regulators.

334

Taken together, our results suggest that BBX and HY5/HYH factors constitute a highly

335

orchestrated transcriptional network that controls photomorphogenic and photoperiodic

336

responses at dawn downstream of phytochrome and cryptochrome photoreceptors.

337

Discussion

338

A high-resolution time series is required to capture complex

339

transcriptional dynamics at dawn

340
341

While several previous reports indicated a burst of gene expression in the early

342

morning, here we were able to fully characterise the transcriptional dynamics in this time

343

period using an RNA-seq time course with very high sampling frequency. We were able

344

to identify multiple coordinated waves of gene expression. These included clusters of

345

early-expressed genes which were enriched for light-repressed genes (clusters 1-2) as

346

well as clusters enriched for light-induced genes (clusters 5-6), which peaked during the

347

2 hour window after dawn and also displayed decreased peak expression levels at high

348

ambient temperature of 27°C. We also identified gene sets that were not enriched for

349

light-regulated genes (as defined by Rugnone et al., 2013), but showed clearly elevated
12

350

expression at high temperature and included many genes involved in heat stress

351

response and thermotolerance (clusters 7-8).

352

In general, it is a useful strategy to conduct a few high resolution time course

353

experiments, and then use this data to select informative time point experiments for

354

future experiments. Following this approach we find that a large proportion of TFs

355

change their expression in the early morning, and the expression of many of these

356

genes is perturbed in similar ways in both the circadian clock and photoreceptor

357

mutants. Specifically, we observed (i) elevated levels of temperature-responsive TF

358

genes and (ii) a time delay in the expression in most clusters. These findings agree with

359

the notion that phytochromes and cryptochromes provide light input for circadian

360

entrainment (Devlin and Kay, 2000; Hall et al., 2002; Salomé et al., 2002; Somers et

361

al., 1998) and are thus tightly linked to the circadian clock morning loop, constituted by

362

CCA1, LHY and the PRRs. Phytochromes also act as bona fide temperature sensors

363

(Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016), while cryptochromes can dampen temperature

364

responses (Ma et al., 2016) and both PRR9 and PRR7 are essential for temperature

365

entrainment of the clock (Salomé and McClung, 2005).

366

Whereas many late dawn burst TF genes show abolished or delayed induction in phyA

367

phyB cry1 cry2 and prr5 prr7 prr9, early dawn burst genes are either unaffected or

368

display higher expression in these genetic backgrounds. Phytochromes, cryptochromes

369

and PRRs have all been found to act as transcriptional (co-)repressors (Chen et al.,

370

2014; Jung et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016; Pedmale et al., 2016), which agrees with their

371

direct targets being upregulated in the respective mutants. They may hence regulate

372

late dawn bursts indirectly, by repressing early expressed negative regulators or

373

through post-transcriptional mechanisms. Phytochromes and cryptochromes in

374

particular control many TFs post-translationally through inhibition of the

375

CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1)/SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA-101

376

(SPA) complex, a key light signalling repressor that promotes degradation of many TFs

377

involved in photomorphogenesis (Hoecker, 2017; Podolec and Ulm, 2018). This leaves

378

us with the question of how early dawn burst genes are induced upon light exposure.

379

Other blue light receptors are still active in phyA phyB cry1 cry2: while ztl proteins have

13

380

core roles in circadian processes, phototropins are not directly linked to entrainment, but

381

trigger stomatal opening in response to light (Christie et al., 2015; Suetsugu and Wada,

382

2013), which may in turn be perceived as a change in water potential within the plant.

383

Moreover, not all light-triggered retrograde signals from chloroplasts are disrupted in the

384

hsfa1QK mutant. While HSFA1 TFs act in response to light-induced changes in the

385

chloroplast’s redox state (Dickinson et al., 2018), other signals may bypass this route.

386

The increase in primary photosynthates after dawn for instance can be perceived by

387

energy sensors such as Snf1-RELATED KINASE 1 (SnRK1) and TARGET OF

388

RAPAMYCIN (TOR), which are also known to affect clock entrainment (Shin et al.,

389

2017; Zhang et al., 2019).

390
391

It is important to note that we have characterised gene expression changes that result

392

from the kind of sudden onset of light that occur in growth chambers, rather than those

393

found in natural conditions, which have for instance been investigated by Annunziata et

394

al. (2018). Nevertheless, there are a number of benefits of this approach. Firstly, this

395

allows us to more directly compare our results to other studies of biotic and abiotic

396

responses in the early morning that were also performed in artificial growth chambers

397

with sudden onset of light (Cortijo et al., 2019; Dickinson et al., 2018; Ezer et al., 2017b;

398

Grundy et al., 2015; Ingle et al., 2015; Michael et al., 2008; Seaton et al.,

399

2018). Secondly, this kind of study allows us to directly address how the transcriptome

400

responds to a light stimulus in the morning. Specifically, since we know the exact time

401

that plants were first able to detect light, we can measure the exact time delay between

402

this exposure and a change in gene expression. Finally, farming in artificial lights is

403

becoming common, and it is important to understand how crops respond to lighting

404

conditions that are reminiscent of growth chambers (Dutta Gupta and Agarwal, 2017;

405

Ibaraki, 2016; Olvera-Gonzalez et al., 2013).

406

14

407

Phytochromes and cryptochromes coordinate light responses in the

408

early morning via HY5 and BBX transcription factors

409

Our time course data allowed us to infer a high-confidence transcriptional network that

410

controls the dawn burst in gene expression. HY5 and BBX family proteins appear at the

411

core of this network downstream of phytochrome and cryptochrome photoreceptors and

412

are likely to coordinate the early and late waves of gene expression in the early

413

morning. Notably, while loss of phytochrome function on its own strongly affects the pre-

414

dawn peak in DNA binding genes, phytochromes and cryptochromes can compensate

415

for each other’s function in controlling the post-dawn HY5/BBX subnetwork, at least

416

under white light. They induce both positive (HY5, HYH) and negative (BBX Set A)

417

regulators of light responses, while they simultaneously repress other

418

photomorphogenesis-promoting factors (BBX Set B). HY5 and HYH are not only

419

induced by light at the transcriptional level, but the respective proteins are also

420

stabilised through light-induced inactivation of the COP1/SPA repressor complex (Holm

421

et al., 2002; Osterlund et al., 2000; Saijo et al., 2003). Boosting HY5 and HYH protein

422

levels via both mechanisms will trigger light responses in the early morning, but this

423

process may require fine-tuning through other factors including BBX proteins. BBX24,

424

BBX25 and BBX32 interfere with HY5 transcriptional activity through direct interaction

425

(Gangappa et al., 2013; Holtan et al., 2011; Job et al., 2018), while BBX30 and BBX31

426

act downstream of HY5 to promote elongation growth (Heng et al., 2019). BBX20 and

427

BBX21 on the other hand promote HY5 function by increasing HY5 transcript level and

428

post-translationally enhancing HY5 activity (Bursch et al., 2020; Job et al., 2018; Wei et

429

al., 2016; Xu et al., 2018), but these effects seem to be largely suppressed at dawn.

430

Another layer of complexity is added by the fact that the COP1/SPA complex also

431

promotes degradation of several BBX proteins (Indorf et al. 2007; Yan et al. 2011; Xu et

432

al. 2016; Gangappa et al. 2013).

433

HY5 and BBX proteins interdependently tune photomorphogenic responses such as the

434

reduction in elongation growth after dawn and may provide light input to the circadian

435

clock via TOC1 and ASG4, but may also exert additional functions independently of

436

each other. BBX proteins have not yet been implicated in other HY5-mediated
15

437

responses such as biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. On the other hand, BBX30,

438

BBX31 and BBX32 act as negative regulators of the floral transition independently of

439

HY5 through the repression of FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) (Graeff et al., 2016; Tripathi

440

et al., 2017). Strong induction of these genes in the early morning may be involved in

441

gating FT’s responsiveness to inductive signals in the photoperiodic control of flowering.

442

In summary, our results reveal a large transcriptional regulatory network that controls

443

the dawn burst of gene expression downstream of photoreceptor, circadian clock and

444

chloroplast-derived signals. Phytochrome and cryptochrome photoreceptors coordinate

445

a subset of this network to adjust photomorphogenesis, photoperiodism and clock

446

entrainment in accordance with the plant’s light environment.

Methods

448

Plant material and growth conditions

449

All Arabidopsis thaliana mutants used in this study have been described previously.

450

phyA-201 phyB-5 (Reed et al., 1998), phyA-201 phyB-5 cry1-201 cry2/fha-1 (Yanovsky

451

et al., 2000) and phyA-201 phyB-1 phyC-1 phyD-1 phyE-1 (phyABCDE) (Hu et al.,

452

2013) are in the Ler background, bbx30-1 bbx31-1 (Yadav et al., 2019), BBX31-Ox

453

(Graeff et al., 2016), elf3-1 (Zagotta et al., 1996), cry1-104 cry2-1 (Mao et al., 2005),

454

hy5-2 hyh (Zhang et al., 2017) and prr5-11 prr7-11 prr9-10 (Nakamichi et al., 2005) are

455

in the Col-0 background, and hsfA1a hsfA1b hsfA1d hsfA1e (hsfa1QK) (Liu et al., 2011)

456

is in a mixed Col-0/Ws background.

457

For all experiments, seeds were surface-sterilised and sown on ½ Murashige and

458

Skoog (MS) agar plates at pH5.7 without sucrose; the agar was topped with nylon mesh

459

that allowed quick removal of seedlings for sample collection. They were stratified for 3

460

days at 4°C in the dark and then allowed to germinate for 24 h at 22°C under cool-white

461

fluorescent light at 75-85 µmol m-2 s-1. Plates were then shifted into chambers at

462

constant 22°C or 27°C and cool-white fluorescent light at 90-100 µmol m-2 s-1 with short

463

photoperiods (8 h light, 16 h dark). The plates from which we collected were picked

464

randomly, not based on position. We also mixed-up plate positions in the chamber on

465

several days to reduce positional effects.

466

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

467

For expression analysis, 15-20 7-d-old seedlings were collected per sample by snap-

468

freezing in liquid nitrogen, disrupted using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen) and RNA was

469

extracted using the MagMaxTM-96 Total RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

470

The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA

471

Synthesis Kit (Roche). Transcript levels were determined by qPCR on a LightCycler®

472

480 II instrument (Roche) using LightCycler® 480 SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche).

473

Data were analysed by the ΔΔCT method and two to three biological replicates were

17

474

used. Primers used for qPCR are listed in Table S6.

475
476

RNA-seq: High resolution time series

477

For the high-resolution time couse, 15-20 7-d-old whole seedlings were collected per

478

sample. Samples were collected 10 min before dawn as well as in 2 min intervals for the

479

first half hour after dawn followed by 15 min intervals for the following 90 min. RNA was

480

extracted as described above. RNA quality and integrity was assessed on an Agilent

481

2200 TapeStation. Library preparation was performed with 1 µg of high integrity total

482

RNA using the NEBNext® UltraTM Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New

483

England Biolabs). The libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq® 500 (Illumina) using

484

paired-end sequencing of 75 bp with a NextSeq® 500/550 High Output v2 Kit for 150

485

cycles.

486

Quant-seq: Low resolution time series

487

For the low-resolution time course, 15-20 7-d-old whole seedlings were collected per

488

sample. Samples were collected 10 min before dawn as well as 10, 24, 30, 45, 60, 105

489

and 120 min after dawn. RNA was extracted as described above. RNA quality and

490

integrity was assessed on an Agilent 2200 TapeStation. Library preparation was

491

performed with 500 ng of high integrity total RNA using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq

492

Library Prep Kit FWD for Illumina® (Lexogen). The libraries were sequenced on a

493

NextSeq® 500 (Illumina) using single-end sequencing of 75 bp with a NextSeq®

494

500/550 High Output v2 Kit for 75 cycles.

495

Bioinformatics analysis of RNA-seq data

496

All data is available in SRA: PRJNA687210. The Illumina RNA-seq data was analysed

497

using the same bioinformatics pipeline used in Ezer et al. (2017b), using Trimmomatic-

498

0.32 to trim reads (Bolger et al., 2014), Tophat for mapping to the TAIR10 annotated

499

genome (Trapnell et al., 2009), HTseq-count to find the raw counts after duplicates were

500

removed (Anders et al., 2015), and Cufflinks to calculate Fragments Per Kilobase

501

Million (FPKM), which was then converted into Transcripts Per Million (TPM) (Trapnell
18

502

et al., 2013). The time points for the Lexogen Quant-seq experiment were selected

503

using NITPicker (Ezer and Keir, 2019).

504

The Lexogen Quant-seq data was analysed using the Integrated Data Analysis Pipeline

505

on Bluebee® platform, which maps the reads to the genome using the STAR

506

Aligner (Dobin et al., 2013) and counts reads using HTseq-count (Anders et al.,

507

2015). Quant-seq expression values are expressed as Reads per Million (RPM),

508

because Quant-seq does not require normalisation by gene length.

509

Clustering

510

Clustering of the 24-hour time series (Figure 1) were drawn using hierarchical clustering

511

(default parameters of hclust in R).

512

Clustering of the high resolution time gene expression time series data (in Figure 2) was

513

performed using the CLUST algorithm (Abu-Jamous and Kelly, 2018), using

514

recommended settings for RNA-seq TPM data as per the reference manual (i.e. log2, Z-

515

and quantile normalisation of TPM values).

516

The clustering in Figure 2A was performed only on the 22°C high resolution time series

517

data. The other gene expression time series in Figure 2A and 2B were drawn using the

518

same gene order. The z-scores were calculated across all samples within each row of

519

Figure 2A and Figure 2B.

520

Gene list curation for network inference

521

A number of criteria were used to generate a gene list for performing network

522

inference.

523

In the beginning, all lowly or non-expressing genes were not included in the network

524

inference analysis. The criteria for removal each sample were: [1] rowSum(RPMj,t) < 7 x

525

nj,t x 1.05 (RPMj,t is RPM value and nj,t the total number of time-points for gene j at

526

timepoint t) and [2] genes that had <5 time-points where the TPMj < 7. Genes with at

527

least one time-point containing TPM = 0 were removed (requirement of dynGENIE3

528

package).

19

529

The final gene list used for network inference was obtained from the following sources:

530

(1) GO categories, (2) consensus cluster and (3) DE analysis. A gene only needed to

531

meet one of these three criteria in order to be included in the analysis. Note that prior to

532

the analysis in each of the three approaches, only genes satisfying the mentioned

533

expression thresholds were included.

534

Firstly, all genes that had GO categories that were of biological interest to us were

535

initially included. Specifically, this referred to genes that had Biological Process GO

536

terms that included the words ’stress’, ‘light’, ‘auxin’, ‘abscisic’, ‘ethylene’, ‘circadian’, as

537

well as all genes that had the Molecular Function ‘DNA binding’.

538

Secondly, we selected for genes that had similar expression patterns as other genes in

539

the data set. The reason we chose this criteria was that we did not want to include lots

540

of genes that had extremely noisy patterns of expression. Clustering of the time-course

541

gene expression was performed for each sample Col-0, Ler, prr5 prr7 prr9, phyA phyB

542

cry1 cry2, hsfa1QK at 22°C using the CLUST algorithm using the recommended

543

settings (mentioned previously). Recall that the CLUST algorithm filters genes that do

544

not cluster well with any of the clusters. Any gene that appeared in any of the clusters

545

detected by the CLUST algorithm was included in the analysis.

546

Thirdly, differential expression analysis of gene was performed based on the time-

547

course expression using the odp method from the package edge (R

548

Bioconductor) (Storey et al. 2005). Significant genes were chosen based on having an

549

adjusted p-value (q-value) < 0.05. The following WT and mutant pairs at 22°C were

550

compared: Col-0 vs prr5 prr7 prr9, Col-0 vs hsfa1QK and Ler vs phyA phyB cry1 cry2.

551

This analysis produced a gene list that was too expansive for dynGenie3. The filtered

552

gene list was ranked based on decreasing CV (sd/mean) with the top 1500 chosen for

553

network inference. All the 1500 selected genes were confirmed to satisfy the expression

554

cutoffs outlined earlier.

555

Network inference and analysis

556

Network inference on the curated gene list was performed using dynGENIE3 R

557

package (Huynh-Thu and Geurts 2018). For each gene, the time-course gene
20

558

expression was supplied from the following samples: Col-0/Ler/prr5 prr7 prr9/phyA phyB

559

cry1 cry2/hsfa1QK at 22°C and Col-0/Ler/prr5 prr7 prr9/phyA phyB cry1 cry2 at 27°C.

560

For downstream analysis of network, only edges that had scores within the top 1% and

561

that were consistent with DAP-seq data (O’Malley et al., 2016) were included. GO term

562

enrichment within the network was determined using the PAFway package in

563

CRAN. Figures were drawn using the network visualisation tools developed for Ara-

564

BOX-cis (Ezer et al., 2017b), and is available here: http://www-

565

users.york.ac.uk/~de656/dawnBurst/dawnBurst.html
