Arabidopsis PIEZO Regulates Root Growth in Response to Magnetic Field and Blue Light Signaling

Physiologia Plantarum. https://doi.org/10.1111/ppl.70630. doi:10.1111/ppl.70630. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Arabidopsis PIEZO Regulates Root Growth in Response to Magnetic Field and Blue Light Signaling

## Abstract
ABSTRACTThe mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO plays an important role in root growth. Magnetic fields (MF) and blue light are both known to have broad influence on plant growth as physical environmental factors; however, whether the PIEZO gene responds to MF and blue light to regulate root growth remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that Arabidopsis PIEZO regulates root growth in response to both MF and blue light. Mutation of PIEZO led to a significantly shorter root length under MF and blue light. Moreover, PIEZO expression in root tips was up‐regulated by a leaf‐derived blue light signal in the presence of MF, and PIEZO affected calcium ion influx and auxin transport related to PIN‐FORMED3 (PIN3), PIN6, and PIN7 under combined MF and blue light conditions. Genetic analyses revealed that the blue light receptors CRYPTOCHROME 1 (CRY1), CRY2, and PIN3 were all essential for PIEZO to perceive MF and blue light signals sent from the leaves to the roots and regulate root growth, with PIEZO acting downstream of both CRY1 and CRY2. Moreover, PIEZO influences amyloplast content and distribution in root tips under MF and blue light, though independently of the starch synthesis gene PGM1. Transcriptomic profiling and integrated gene regulatory network analysis further indicated that PIEZO coordinates multiple signaling pathways involving phytohormones (auxin, gibberellin, ethylene), Ca2+ dynamics, mechanosensors, and microRNAs, including miR5648‐5p, which post‐transcriptionally represses PIEZO. Our findings establish PIEZO as a central regulator that responds to MF and blue light signals to control root growth through coordinated action with CRY1/2, PIN3‐mediated auxin transport, calcium flux, and amyloplast distribution. This study demonstrates that PIEZO responds to diverse signals to regulate root growth.Keywords: AtPIEZO, blue light, magnetic field, root

## Introduction
Magnetic fields (MFs) are a fundamental environmental factor essential for the survival and development of organisms. In plants, MFs play a critical role in growth regulation (Hafeez et al. 2023; Tapia‐Belmonte et al. 2023; Saletnik et al. 2022; da Silva and Dobránszki 2016), including root growth (González‐Vidal et al. 2022; Vashisth and Joshi 2017). MF strengths ≤ 89 mT have no detectable effect on Arabidopsis root growth, while intensities ≥ 103 mT negatively regulate root development (Shabrangy and Luschnig 2025). The functions of MF in root growth regulation involve different factors, including cryptochromes (CRYs), a class of blue light‐sensitive flavoproteins, the auxin signaling pathway (Jin et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2018, 2015), and calcium ion (Ca2+) homeostasis (Narayana et al. 2018; Belyavskaya 2004). For instance, Arabidopsis seedlings grown on the International Space Station, where the galactic MF is 0.1–1 nT (Belyavskaya 2004), exhibit altered expression of calcium‐related genes compared with Earth‐grown seedlings (Correll et al. 2013).
MF effects on plant growth are also linked to light responses, particularly responses to blue light, which enhances Ca2+ influx (Grinberg et al. 2022) and activates CRY‐dependent signaling pathways (Albaqami et al. 2020; Agliassa et al. 2018; Ahmad et al. 2007). Blue light‐induced root growth is mediated by CRYs (Canamero et al. 2006), and auxin transport is regulated by PIN‐FORMED3 (PIN3) in Arabidopsis (Zhai et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2013). Mechanical stress responses in shoot apical meristems (SAMs) involve transient changes in cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentrations, which are essential for establishing PIN1 polarity (Li et al. 2019) and nuclear translocation of the photoreceptor phyB (Zhao et al. 2023). Ca2+ also plays a central role in root development by modulating auxin signaling (Wang et al. 2022; Chakraborty et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020; Leitão et al. 2019; Vanneste and Friml 2013). For example, the Ca2+ signaling module CALMODULIN IQ‐MOTIF CONTAINING PROTEIN (CaM‐IQM) interacts with auxin signaling repressors, such as CaM6 bound to IAA19, to regulate root development (Zhang et al. 2022). However, despite these advances, the interplay among MF, blue light, phytohormones (including auxin), and Ca2+ in root growth regulation remains poorly understood.
PIEZO proteins are evolutionarily conserved mechanosensors in land plants (Ashraf 2021). They form mechanically activated cation channels (Coste et al. 2010) that convert physico‐mechanical stimuli into bioelectrical signals (Yang et al. 2022). In Arabidopsis, PIEZO localizes to the tonoplast and regulates vacuole morphology (Radin et al. 2021). AtPIEZO also functions as a Ca2+ channel during mechanical force perception, influencing root growth (Mousavi et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2021). The FER‐PIF3‐PIEZO pathway controls primary root penetration into compacted soil (Bello‐Bello and Herrera‐Estrella 2024; Xu et al. 2024), while the rice PIEZO gene contains cis‐acting elements responsive to light and phytohormones such as methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and gibberellins (GAs) (Hu et al. 2021). Furthermore, MFs have been demonstrated to regulate root development by affecting intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis and light signaling pathways (Grinberg et al. 2022; Jin et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2018, 2015; Narayana et al. 2018; Belyavskaya 2004). Therefore, we hypothesized that PIEZO might serve as a key regulator that responds to MF and blue light signaling to regulate root growth.
In this study, we demonstrate that Arabidopsis PIEZO regulates root growth in response to MF and blue light. We demonstrate that PIEZO regulation of root growth integrates multiple signaling pathways, including PIN3‐mediated auxin transport, phytohormones (auxin, GA, and ethylene), Ca2+ flux, mechanosensors, amylopast distribution, CRY1, and CRY2, under both blue light and MF conditions. These findings uncover a new mechanism by which PIEZO regulates root growth through its responses to MF and blue light, establishing PIEZO's role as an important regulator of diverse signals.

## Materials and Methods

## Source of Static MF and Strength Measurements
The static MF was generated using commercially acquired NdFeB magnets (Grade N52, Hangzhou Permanent Magnet Group Co. Ltd.). The MF strength was confirmed to be 50, 200, or 500 mT using a handheld digital teslameter (Model KT‐105, Wenzhou Kerte Measuring Instrument Co. Ltd.). The MF was maintained at 6 mm working distance from the growing plants with < 5% spatial variation.

## Plant Materials and Growth Conditions
The Arabidopsis lines used in this experiment were all of ecotype Columbia (WT) background and included piezo‐c1 and ProPIEZO::GUS (Fang et al. 2021), 35S::PIEZO lines (#4, #9, and #16), piezo‐T, and cry 1‐104 , cry 2‐1 , and cry1/2 lines (Li et al. 2021). The Arabidopsis piezo‐T mutant line (NASCode: N663516) was purchased from AraShare (www.arashare.cn). The 35S::PIEZO lines were generated at the Biorun Company (Wuhan BIORUN Bio‐Tech Co. Ltd.). The cry1‐104 and cry2‐1 mutants were obtained from Professor Shuhua Yang's lab (China Agricultural University). The phyA (Reed et al. 1994), phyB (Reed et al. 1993), phyA/B (Devireddy et al. 2020), and cry1/2 mutants were obtained from Professor Haodong Chen's lab (Tsinghua University). The pin3‐4 mutant (Rakusová et al. 2011) was obtained from Professor Zhaojun Ding's lab (Shandong University). Arabidopsis seeds were grown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (M5519; Sigma‐Aldrich) containing 0.8% agar, cold‐treated in the dark at 4°C for 2 days, and then transferred to white light at 22°C under 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod for 4 days. Subsequently, these 4‐day‐old seedlings (with primary roots approximately 1 cm in length) were transferred to fresh 1/2 MS medium and attached to the N and S poles of magnets with strengths of 50, 200, or 500 mT, where the direction of the magnetic field was perpendicular to the direction of the gravitational force. The seedlings were then subjected to MF treatment under either red (48 μmol m−2 s−1) or blue (96 μmol m−2 s−1) light for 6 days, after which primary root lengths were measured using the ImageJ 1.41 software.
To detect the effect of the direction of the MF on regulating root growth, seedlings in which the primary root length was about 1 cm were transferred to a new plate with 1/2 MS medium, and then the plate was placed on either the N pole or the S pole of a NdFeB magnet N52 of strength 500 mT, where the direction of the MF was either antiparallel or parallel to the direction of gravitational force. Seedlings were grown under MF treatment for 6 days under red or blue light.
To detect the effect of MF on the medium, the plate containing 1/2 MS medium was first attached to the N or S pole of an N52 NdFeB magnet (500 mT) and kept in the dark for 6 days. Four‐day‐old Arabidopsis seedlings (with the primary root length of about 1 cm) grown on 1/2 MS medium under white light were then transferred to the MF‐pre‐treated 1/2 MS medium, or seedlings were transferred to a micronutrient‐deficient 1/2 MS medium (Cat. # CN260469‐100 g; CHINOOK) with 500 MF treatment. Both groups of seedlings were grown under blue light for 6 days.

## Arabidopsis Seedling Root Growth Under the Following Treatments With Different Phytohormones and Ethylene Glycol‐Bis (β‐Aminoethyl Ether)‐N,N,N′,N′‐Tetraacetic Acid
Four‐day‐old seedlings in which the primary root length was about 1 cm were transferred to a plate containing 1/2 MS medium complemented with one of the following reagents: 5 μM of the auxin transport inhibitor 2,3,5‐triiodobenzoic acid (TIBA) (Zou et al. 2019), 0.01 μM 1‐naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) (Rahman et al. 2002), 0.1 μM gibberellin A4 (GA4) (Nohales and Kay 2019), 100 nM 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylic acid (ACC) (Feng et al. 2017), 0.1 μM methyl jasmonate (MeJA) (Zhu et al. 2006) or 0.01 μM of the calcium ion inhibitor ethylene glycol‐bis (β‐aminoethyl ether)‐N,N,N′,N′‐tetraacetic acid (EGTA). The ACC and EGTA were dissolved in sterilized water. The chemicals TIBA, NAA, GA4, and MeJA were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and seedlings were treated with an equivalent volume of DMSO as a control. The plates containing the seedlings were attached to either the N or the S pole of an NdFeB magnet N52 of strength 500 mT and grown under blue light for 6 days.

## Root Phenotype of Seedlings With Roots Shaded and Grown in Darkness
To observe the effect of blue light on the expression of the PIEZO gene, seedlings of wild‐type Columbia‐o, piezo‐cl, cry 1‐104 , cry 2‐1 , cry1/2, piezo‐cl/cry 1‐104 , piezo‐cl/cry 2‐1 , piezo‐cl/cry1/2 mutants, and a transgenic line expressing ProPIEZO::GUS were grown in a plate containing 1/2 MS medium. A black baffle was placed below the seeds at a distance of 1 cm, with a small space left between the surface of the medium and the baffle to ensure that the seedling roots were able to grow through the baffle. A piece of tinfoil was used to cover the plate above the baffle, creating a dark environment that prevented the roots from receiving any light. Additionally, a piece of tinfoil was used to cover the area on top of the seeds, preventing light from directly irradiating the space between the baffle and the medium. This allowed the leaves to respond to light while keeping the roots in the dark. The seeds were sown on a plate with a baffle, tinfoil, and 1/2 MS medium, and were treated for 2 days at 4°C in darkness. Then, the plates were transferred to blue light conditions, and the seedlings were grown for 4 days at 22°C. After 4 days, the plates together with the seedlings were transferred to an MF of strength 500 mT and grown for 6 days under blue light at 22°C.

## GUS Staining
Ten‐day‐old seedlings were collected following a previously published report (Mousavi et al. 2021) and seedlings were stained with GUS solution [50 mM Na2HPO4·12H2O, 50 mM Na2HPO4·2H2O, 10% (v/v) Triton X‐100, 2 mM K3[Fe(CN6)], 2 mM K4[Fe(CN6)]·3H20, 10 mM EDTA, 1.04 mg mL−1 X‐Gluc (R0851, Thermo Fisher Scientific)] at 37°C in darkness for 12 h. The collected seedlings were then decolorized using ethanol. All samples were treated successively with ethanol at 30%, 75%, 90%, and then again with 75% (v/v) ethanol over 1 h. Then, either the whole plant or just the root tip was examined and photographed using a Leica fluorescence microscope (DM2000, Leica).

## Calcium Ion Flux Measurement
The root caps of 6‐day‐old seedlings of wild‐type and piezo‐c1 mutant lines were used to measure Ca2+ flux using Non‐invasive Micro‐test Technology (NMT). First, a 100 mM CaCl2 filling solution was injected into the ion flow rate sensor (XY‐CGQ‐01) with a 4–5 μm aperture, approximately 1.5 cm in length. Next, the Ca2+‐LIX exchanger (XY‐SJ‐Ca‐10) was taken up by the tip of the LIX Holder (XY‐LIX‐01), and then the Ca2+‐LIX exchanger at the tip of the holder was injected into the tip of the ion flow rate sensor using a syringe (XY‐ZSQTZ‐01) under the microscope. Next, a silver wire was chlorinated in silver chloride (XY‐RY‐05) for 25 s until it turned an off‐white color. It was inserted into the ion flux rate sensor, and the mounted ion flow rate sensor was connected to the pre‐ion amplifier. The prepared Ca2+ flow rate sensors were also calibrated with the three‐point calibration method using calibration solution 1, calibration solution 2 (XY‐RY‐02), and 0.1 mM CaCl2 test solution (XY‐RY‐01) with a theoretical value of 27 ± 5 mV/decade. The roots of seedlings from wild‐type or piezo‐c1 lines were fixed to the bottom of a 35 mm petri dish (XY‐PYM‐35) using a resin block (XY‐SZK). 0.1 mM CaCl2 (XY‐RY‐01) was added to submerge and stabilize the samples for 15 min, and then the samples were placed on the NMT microscope stage, the reference electrode (YG‐CBDJ‐01) was inserted into a 0.1 mM CaCl2 solution (XY‐RY‐01), and the stage and preion amplifier were adjusted to position the Ca2+ flux rate sensor near the root cap of the primary root. Finally, the N or S pole of a magnetic rod of strength 120 mT (Hangzhou Permanent Magnet Group Co. Ltd.) was placed close to and above the root cap, and the blue filters of the NMT microscope were used as the blue light source (wavelength: 450 nm; intensity: 7 μmol m−2 s−1). These blue filters were exclusively used for Ca2+ flux measurements due to technical constraints. After root samples were stabilized in the test solution for 20 min, the Ca2+ flux assay was conducted over a time course of 10 min for each data collection. Flow rate data were collected directly using the imFluxes V3.0 software (Xuyue.net) in pm cm−2 s−1, with positive values indicating Ca2+ efflux and negative values indicating Ca2+ influx. At least 7 roots were tested in each experiment for both the wild‐type and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings. All the reagents and consumable materials used in the experiments were purchased from Beijing Xuyue Technology Co. Ltd.

## RNA Isolation and cDNA Synthesis
Total RNA was collected from the primary roots of 6‐day‐old wild‐type, piezo‐c1, cry 1‐104 , cry 2‐1 , or cry1‐104/2–1 mutant seedlings that had been subjected to a 500 mT MF under blue light or red light, and from the primary roots of 6‐day‐old wild‐type seedlings or seedlings expressing ProPIEZO::GUS subjected to 500 mT MF treatment for 6 days, and from the leaves of 21‐day‐old wild‐type and 35::PIEZO lines (#4, #9 and #16) grown in soil. The isolation of RNA from the primary roots and leaves, as well as cDNA biosynthesis, is referred to in a previously published report (Jiang et al. 2023).

## Real‐Time PCR Analysis
The relative quantitative gene expression levels were determined using an ABI QuantStudio 7 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The 10 μL PCR reaction mixture contained 5 μL NovoStart SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein Scientific lnc.), 1 μL of cDNA template, 0.2 μL ROX2 (Novoprotein Scientific lnc.), 3 μL RNase free H2O and 0.8 μL of a 10 μM solution containing one of the following primer pairs: Atpiezo‐FP and Atpiezo‐RP, AtGUSn2‐FP and AtGUSn2‐RP, AtmiR5648‐loop‐FP and AtmiR5648‐loop‐RP, AtPIN1‐FP and AtPIN1‐RP, AtPIN2‐FP and AtPIN2‐RP, AtPIN3‐FP and AtPIN3‐RP, AtPIN4‐FP and AtPIN4‐RP, AtPIN5‐FP and AtPIN5‐RP, AtPIN6‐FP and AtPIN6‐RP, AtPIN7‐FP and AtPIN7‐RP, AtPIN8‐FP and AtPIN8‐RP, AtRCA‐FP and AtRCA‐RP, AtLHCB6‐FP and AtLHCB6‐RP, AtCPK19‐FP and AtCPK19‐RP, AtYUC5‐FP and AtYUC5‐RP, AtCNGC4‐FP and AtCNGC4‐RP, or AtPGM1‐FP and AtPGM1‐RP. The primer pairs were used to amplify the genes AtPIEZO, GUS, ath‐miR5648‐5p, AtPIN1, AtPIN2, AtPIN3, AtPIN4, AtPIN5, AtPIN6, AtPIN7, AtPIN8, AtRCA, AtLHCB6, AtCPK19, AtYUC5, AtCNGC4, and AtPGM1, respectively (Table S1). The AtActin2 gene (AT3G18780) acted as the internal control and was amplified with the primer pair AtActin2‐FP and AtActin2‐RP (Table S1). PCR was performed under the following conditions: denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 45 s, 55°C–62°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 1 min. Three biological replicates were made. The relative gene expression levels were calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The software SPSS version 19.0 (IBM Inc.) was used to analyze differences in gene expression. *p < 0.05 was taken to indicate a statistical difference, **p < 0.01 indicated a statistically significant difference.

## Transcriptome Analysis
The raw RNA‐seq reads were subjected to quality control with FastQC (v0.12.1). Subsequently, transcriptome analysis was performed using the HISAT2 pipeline (Pertea et al. 2016). The Arabidopsis genome version used was TAIR10, downloaded from the phytozome v13 (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/). The differentially expressed genes were identified using GFOLD (Feng et al. 2012).

## Construction of the Integrated Gene Regulatory Network
The integrated gene regulatory network comprises three types of networks: the transcription factor regulatory network, with a score of more than 0.7 (Nature Plants, 2021); the protein–protein interaction network (https://string‐db.org/), with a score of more than 0.7; and the miRNA‐target gene regulatory network. The regulation of target genes by miRNAs was predicted with psRNATarget (Nucleic Acids Research 2018). The sub‐network was extracted using the CytoHubba tool and visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.9.0).

## Analysis of the AtPIEZO Gene Promoter
The Ensembl Plants website (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) was searched for the AtPIEZO (AT2G48060) sequence, and the 2000 bp upstream of the AtPIEZO gene was used to analyze the promoter structure using online software Plant Care (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent. be/webtools/plantcare/html/). A visual representation was constructed using TBtools (Chen et al. 2020).

## Amyloplast Observation
The primary roots were collected from 6‐day‐old wild‐type or piezo‐c1 mutant seedlings that had been subjected to a 500 mT MF for 6 d under blue light. Roots were immersed in an I2‐KI solution (0.33% (w/v) I2 and 0.67% (w/v) KI) for 5 min, then rinsed in chloral hydrate (40 g chloral hydrate dissolved in 10 mL glycerine and 20 mL sterilized water) for 5 min (Chen et al. 2025). The amyloplasts in the root tips were observed under a microscope.

## MicroRNAs‐Treated Arabidopsis Leaves
To assess the effect of miRNAs on the expression levels of AtPIEZO, the Arabidopsis leaves were treated with a miRNA solution following a previously published report (Zhao et al. 2025). 25‐day‐old WT seedlings were grown in a greenhouse at 22°C under 16 h light conditions. Twenty microliters of miRNA ath‐miR5648‐5p (20 ng μL−1) were dropped onto the surface of leaves, and 24 h later, the leaves treated with miRNA ath‐miR5648‐5p were collected for real‐time PCR analysis of AtPIEZO gene expression. The miRNA ath‐miR5648‐5p sequence was 5′‐UUUGGAAAUAUUUGGCUUGACU‐3′, and a randomly synthesized miRNA A101228‐0005 (5′‐UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTTACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT‐3′, Sangon Biotech) was used as a negative control.

## Statistical Analysis
The ImageJ 1.41 software was used to measure root length, root growth angle, GUS level, and the content and reorientation angle of amyloplast. All images were processed in the Photoshop software. Data were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA or two‐tailed Student's t‐tests, and Origin 2022 and Photoshop were used to create and plot images.

## Results

## Arabidopsis PIEZO Responds to MF Direction in a Blue Light‐Dependent Manner to Regulate Root Growth
To investigate whether the PIEZO protein responds to an MF, we first examined its role in root growth under a 500 mT MF, with the MF direction perpendicular to the direction of gravity, in combination with either red or blue light (Figure 1a). When seedlings were grown under red light (Figure 1b–g), compared with those seedlings not subjected to MF treatment (Figure 1b,e), the primary root lengths of the wild‐type (WT) Col‐0 (Figure 1c,d) and piezo‐cl mutant (Figure 1f,g) seedlings subjected to a MF were significantly shorter (Figure 1n) and showed a decreased root growth angle (Figure 1p,q). However, when the seedlings were grown under blue light and subjected to a MF (Figure 1h–m), compared with the control (Figure 1h,k), the primary root lengths of WT seedlings did not show any obvious change in the presence of a MF (Figure 1o), while the root lengths of piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were significantly shorter (Figure 1o). The root growth angle did not show any obvious change in the WT, but increased in the piezo‐cl mutants when seedlings were grown in an MF under blue light (Figure 1r). The primary root length and root growth angle of a T‐DNA insertion mutant piezo‐T grown under red or blue light and subjected to MF treatment showed the same phenotype as the piezo‐cl mutant seedlings (Figure 1a–r and Figure S1).

When we examined the expression levels of the PIEZO gene after different treatment times (15 min, 30 min, 72 h, and 144 h), we found that the levels of PIEZO transcripts increased in WT seedlings grown under blue light in a 500 mT MF (Figure 1s). When a transgenic Arabidopsis expressing proPIEZO::GUS was grown under blue light and with a 500 mT MF, the levels of the GUS gene also increased compared with the control without MF treatment (Figure S2), confirming that the PIEZO responds to MF.
When seedlings overexpressing the PIEZO gene were grown under red or blue light in the presence of a MF (Figure S4a–z), compared with the control without MF treatment, the WT and PIEZO‐overexpressing lines #4, #9 and #16 showed reduced root length (Figure S4aa) and root growth angle (Figure S4ac) under red light, however, the root length (Figure S4ab) and root growth angle (Figure S4ad) did not show significant differences in these lines when grown under blue light.
To assess the effect of MF polarity relative to the direction of gravity, WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were exposed to a 500 mT MF, where the direction of the MF was either parallel (Figure S5a) or anti‐parallel (Figure S5b) to the direction of gravity, under either red or blue light. Compared with the seedlings not subjected to MF treatment, the root lengths of both WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were significantly reduced when the polarity of the MF was parallel to the direction of gravity under both red (Figure S5c–f,s) and blue light (Figure S5g–j,t). However, the root length was not significantly different in the WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings when the polarity of the MF was anti‐parallel to the direction of gravity under red light (Figure S5k–n,u) or blue light (Figure S5o–r,v). Compared with the control, the root length of WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings grown under blue light was significantly reduced (Figure S5t) or showed no difference (Figure S5v) when the polarity of the MF was parallel or anti‐parallel to the direction of gravity. The root lengths of the WT were unchanged, but the root length of piezo‐cl mutants were significantly reduced when seedlings were grown under blue light and subjected to a MF with polarity perpendicular to the direction of gravity (Figure 1o). These data indicate that PIEZO is sensitive to the polarity of MF under blue light when regulating root growth.
To check the effect of a MF on growth medium, which may then affect seedling root growth, 1/2 MS medium was subjected to a 500 mT MF for 6 days (Figure S6a). Compared with seedlings grown in the 1/2 MS medium that was not subjected to a MF (Figure S6b,e), the root lengths of both the WT (Figure S6c,d) and piezo‐cl mutant (Figure S6f,g) seedlings grown on the 1/2 MS medium previously subjected to a MF did not show significant differences under blue light (Figure S6h). To investigate the effect of trace elements in the medium on root growth under MF, WT, and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were grown on micronutrient‐deficient 1/2 MS medium under blue light with MF treatment (Figure S6i). Compared with the non‐MF controls (Figure S6j,m), the root length of MF‐treated WT seedlings showed no significant differences (Figure S6k,l,p). In contrast, the root length of MF‐treated piezo‐cl mutant seedlings was significantly shorter (Figure S6n–p). We next checked whether piezo mutant seedling responses to MF regulation of root growth were dependent on the strength of the MF (Figure S7a). Compared with the seedlings not subjected to a MF (Figure S7b,e,h,k), the root length of WT (Figure S7c,d) and piezo‐cl mutant (Figure S7f,g) seedlings did not show any significant differences when grown in a 50 mT MF under blue light (Figure S7n). However, the root lengths were unchanged in the WT (Figure S7i,j) but significantly reduced in the piezo‐cl mutant seedlings (Figure S7l,m) when grown in a 200 mT MF under blue light (Figure S7o). These data demonstrate that the PIEZO gene regulates root growth in a manner dependent on MF strength under blue light.

## PIEZO Perceives Blue Light Signal Transduction From Leaves to Roots in the Presence of a MF
The PIEZO promoter contains light‐responsive elements (Figure S8 and Table S2). To determine whether PIEZO perceives transduction of blue light signals from the leaves to the roots, seedlings expressing ProPIEZO::GUS were grown with leaves exposed to blue light and roots in darkness under a 500 mT MF (Figure 2a). Under these conditions, GUS expression was detected in the cotyledon, hypocotyl, vascular tissue and root tip (Figure 2b,c). Compared with the control seedlings not subjected to a MF (Figure 2d), the levels of GUS in the root tips (Figure 2e,f) were higher (Figure 2g). To further investigate the role of PIEZO in responding to the leaf‐to‐root transmission of blue light signaling, we analyzed PIEZO expression in the roots of WT seedlings subjected to root‐shading (but not leaf‐shading) and MF treatment under red light (Figure S9a). MF treatment significantly decreased root PIEZO expression compared with the non‐MF‐treated control (Figure S9b). In contrast, PIEZO expression increased when plants were grown under blue light (Figure 2h). We further examined the root lengths of WT and piezo‐cl seedlings grown in the dark and subjected to MF treatment (Figure S10a). Compared with the non‐MF‐treated controls (Figure S10b,e), there were no significant differences in root length observed in either the MF‐treated WT (Figure S10c,d) or piezo‐cl (Figure S10f,g) seedlings (Figure S10h). We then examined the root length of WT and piezo‐cl seedlings grown under blue light with leaves shaded (but roots exposed) and subjected to MF treatment (Figure S11a). Compared with the non‐MF‐treated controls (Figure S11b,e), the root lengths of both the MF‐treated WT (Figure S11c,d) and piezo‐cl (Figure S11f,g) seedlings were significantly shorter (Figure S11h). This demonstrates that the PIEZO gene perceives the blue light signal transduction from the leaves to roots in the presence of a MF.

## PIEZO Regulates Ca2+ Flux Under MF and Blue Light
The PIEZO gene is known to regulate intracellular Ca2+ transport in response to mechanical forces (Mousavi et al. 2021). Root length assays under 200 mT MF (Figure S7) show identical phenotypic trends to 500 mT treatments, MF intensities ≥ 103 mT can regulate root development (Shabrangy and Luschnig 2025), confirming effect consistency across intensities. To investigate whether PIEZO regulation of root growth is related to Ca2+ flux, the real‐time Ca2+ flux in WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings grown under blue light in a 120 mT MF was investigated (Figure S12). Under blue light without MF treatment, the roots of WT seedlings exhibited an influx of Ca2+ (Figure 3a). Whereas the roots of the piezo‐cl mutant showed a net efflux under the same conditions (Figure 3b). However, upon MF treatment, compared with the untreated roots, the roots of the WT seedlings displayed no significant changes in Ca2+ flux (Figure 3a), but a significant influx was observed in the piezo‐cl mutant roots (Figure 3b). The above data correlated with root length phenotypes of WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings subjected to a 500 mT MF under blue light (Figure 1o). To further investigate the effect of MF and blue light on the regulation of Ca2+ flux, WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS medium (Figure 3c–i) or on medium supplemented with 0.01 μM of the Ca2+ inhibitor EGTA (Figure 3c,j–o) in a 500 mT MF under blue light. The root lengths of WT seedlings grown on 1/2 MS medium with 500 mT MF treatment under blue light did not show any differences compared with the seedlings grown in 1/2 MS without a MF treatment (Figure 3d–f,p). However, the root lengths of the piezo‐cl mutant treated with 500 mT MF under blue light were shorter than the control seedlings grown without MF treatment (Figure 3g–i,p). Compared with the WT (Figure 3j) and piezo‐cl mutant (Figure 3m) seedlings that grew on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 0.01 μM EGTA but without MF treatment under blue light, the primary root lengths of WT (Figure 3k,l) and piezo‐cl mutant (Figure 3n,o) seedlings grown on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 0.01 μM EGTA with a MF and blue light treatment did not show any significant differences (Figure 3p). These results indicate that PIEZO regulates Ca2+ dynamics to regulate root growth under MF and blue light.

## PIEZO Regulates Different Phytohormone Pathways Involved in Root Growth
The root growth angles of piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were altered when seedlings were subjected to a MF under blue light (Figure 1r). To detect whether the phytohormone auxin has a role in the MF regulation of root growth in the piezo‐cl mutant, WT and piezo‐cl mutant seedlings were treated with the auxin transport inhibitor TIBA. The root lengths of WT seedlings grown on 1/2 MS medium complemented with DMSO as a control and subjected to a 500 mT MF under blue light (Figure 3c) were not different from those in seedlings grown without a MF (Figure 3q–s,ai), while root lengths in the piezo‐cl mutant were shorter (Figure 3t–v,ai). However, when seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS medium complemented with 5 μM TIBA in a MF under blue light, compared with the control grown without a MF, the primary root length remained unchanged in both the WT and the piezo‐cl mutant (Figure 3w–ab,ai). When the seedlings were grown on medium supplemented with 0.01 μM NAA and subjected to a MF under blue light, compared with the control grown without a MF, the primary root length in the WT was unchanged (Figure 3ac–ae,ai), while it was increased in the piezo‐cl mutant (Figure 3af–ah,ai).
We then checked the expression levels of the auxin efflux carrier genes PIN‐FORMED (PIN) 1–8 when seedlings were grown in a 500 mT MF under blue light (Figure 3aj–al and Figure S13). Compared with the WT, the levels of PIN3 (Figure 3aj) and PIN7 (Figure 3al) were increased in the mutant, but the expression of PIN6 (Figure 3ak) was decreased in the roots of piezo‐cl mutant seedlings subjected to a MF under blue light. This shows that PIEZO is able to regulate the intracellular auxin transport mediated by PIN3, 6, and 7 to control root growth in a MF under blue light. We further analyzed the root lengths of pin3‐4 and piezo‐cl/pin3‐4 mutants grown under blue light with MF treatment. Compared with the non‐MF controls (Figure 3am,ap,as,av), the root length of MF‐treated pin3‐4 mutants was significantly shorter (Figure 3at,au,ay). This is similar to the shortened root length observed in piezo‐cl mutants under blue light and MF treatment (Figure 3aq,ar,ay). In contrast, the root length of piezo‐cl/pin3‐4 double mutants under the same conditions showed no significant change (Figure 3aw,ax,ay), which is consistent with the unchanged root length phenotype of WT plants (Figure 3an,ao,ay). These results indicate that PIEZO regulates root length in a PIN3‐dependent manner under blue light and MF treatment.
The function of PIEZO is connected to ethylene (Xu et al. 2024). Because a MF close to null decreases GA levels (Xu et al. 2016) and because the PIEZO promoter contains GA‐ and MeJA‐responsive elements (Figure S8), we then examined the effects of GA4, ethylene and MeJA on the regulation of root growth in piezo‐cl mutant seedlings subjected to a 500 mT MF under blue light (Figure S14a). When seedlings were treated with 0.1 μM ACC, which is a precursor of ethylene biosynthesis, compared with control seedlings grown on normal 1/2 MS medium and without MF treatment under blue light, no significant differences were detected in the primary root lengths of WT seedlings, while root lengths were obviously reduced in the piezo‐cl mutant seedlings subjected to a MF under blue light (Figure S14b–g,af). However, the primary root lengths of WT and piezo‐cl seedlings did not show significant differences compared with the control grown without a MF when seedlings were treated with ACC and MF together under blue light (Figure S14h–m,af). Treatment with 0.1 μM GA4 also produced the same root phenotype as ACC (Figure S14n–y,ag). However, the primary root lengths of WT and piezo‐cl seedlings treated with 0.1 μM MeJA and grown in a MF were similar to those of the control treated with DMSO and grown in a MF under blue light (Figure S15n–s,z–ae,ag).

## CRY1 and 2 Can Respond to a MF to Regulate Root Growth
The PIEZO gene is able to respond to a MF to regulate root growth in the presence of blue light (Figure 1a–o). The blue light receptors CRY1 and 2 are also known to respond to a MF (Solov'yov et al. 2007; Agliassa et al. 2018) and affect auxin transport in root growth (Xu et al. 2018; Jin et al. 2019). To investigate whether CRY1 and 2 are involved in MF‐mediated root growth under blue light, the cry 1‐104 and cry 2‐1 single mutants and cry1/2 double mutant were subjected to a 500 mT MF under blue light (Figure 4a–m). Compared with the seedlings not subjected to a MF, the primary root lengths of WT seedlings grown in a MF under blue light were unchanged (Figure 4b–d,n), but root lengths were significantly reduced compared with the control in the cry 1‐104 (Figure 4e–g,n) and cry 2‐1 mutants (Figure 4h–j,n). However, the primary root lengths of the cry1/2 double mutant (Figure 4k–n) did not show any obvious changes compared with the control. This suggests that the blue light signaling pathway is involved in the MF‐mediation of root growth and that the functions of CRY1 and 2 in response to MF are complementary.

We further investigated the PIEZO gene expression in the roots of WT, cry1‐104, cry2‐1 and cry1/2 mutants under red and blue light with MF treatment. Under red light, compared with the non‐MF controls, MF treatment caused no significant change in PIEZO expression in WT roots, significantly reduced expression in cry1‐104 and cry1/2 mutants, but significantly increased expression in cry2‐1 roots (Figure S15a). When pif3 mutant seedlings were grown in a 500 mT MF under blue light, similar to the piezo‐cl mutant (Figure 1o), the primary root length of the pif3 mutant was significantly reduced compared with the control grown without a MF (Figure S16).

## PIEZO Regulates Amyloplast Content and Distribution in Root Tips Grown Under Blue Light and MF
In seedlings grown under blue light and a MF, the PIEZO gene influences root growth (Figure 1a–r). To detect whether PIEZO‐mediated regulation of root growth under these conditions is associated with root tip amyloplasts, we stained amyloplasts in the root tips of WT and piezo‐cl mutants treated with blue light and a MF (Figure 7a). Compared with the untreated controls (Figure 7b,e), MF‐treated WT root tips exhibited a significant decrease in amyloplast content (Figure 7c,d,h,i) but showed no significant difference in amyloplast deviation angle (Figure 7c,d,j,k). In contrast, the piezo‐cl mutant displayed no significant change in amyloplast content (Figure 7f,g,h,i), but a significant increase in amyloplast deviation angle was observed (Figure 7f,g,j,k). These results indicate that PIEZO negatively regulates amyloplast distribution under blue light and MF treatment. We further examined the expression of the amyloplast synthesis gene PGM1 (Chen et al. 2023) in WT and piezo‐cl roots under blue light and MF treatment (Figure S20a). Compared with untreated controls, PGM1 expression was significantly upregulated in both WT and piezo‐cl roots upon MF treatment (Figure S20b), suggesting that PGM1 is not involved in the PIEZO‐mediated negative regulation of amyloplast content under blue light and MF.

## PIEZO Regulates Root Growth via an Integrated Gene Regulatory Network
To investigate the mechanism of PIEZO regulation of root growth in a MF and under blue light, we analyzed WT and piezo‐cl seedling root transcriptomes. A total of 1415 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found in the root transcriptomes (Table S1 and Figure S21). We found that these DEGs exhibited different expression patterns (Figure S22), and a total of 1357 genes that specifically responded to blue light were identified in the piezo‐cl mutant. To investigate the mechanism by which PIEZO responds to a MF and blue light, we first constructed an integrated gene regulatory network (iGRN) involving 237 genes, by combining transcriptome data from this study, including protein–protein interaction data, miRNA regulation data, and transcription factor regulation data (Figure S23). Next, we extracted a sub‐network closely associated with PIEZO (Figure 8a). Genes from the WT and piezo‐cl root transcriptomes that had direct or indirect regulatory relationships with PIEZO, along with a microRNA miR5648‐5p, were identified as being involved in seven distinct biological processes, including auxin signaling, blue light signaling, calcium ion signaling, ethylene signaling, GA signaling, gravitropism, and mechanical pressure (Figure 8a,b). To further validate the reliability of the dynamic expression changes of gene members in the sub‐network, we next checked expression levels of DEGs in the sub‐network using real‐time PCR. The data showed that expression trends of 8 DEGs (Figure 8c) were consistent with those of the transcriptome sequencing (Figure 8b). Additionally, we examined the expression changes of the miR5648‐5p shown in the sub‐network (Figure 8a), and found that the levels of miR5648‐5p were significantly decreased in the roots of WT seedlings grown in a 500 mT MF under blue light (Figure 8d), which showed the opposite expression patterns of the PIEZO gene (Figure 1s). To further check whether PIEZO is a target gene of miR5648‐5p, synthetic miR5648‐5p was dropped on WT leaves, and the seedlings were grown under blue light (Figure S24a). Compared with control leaves spotted with water, miR5648‐5p treatment significantly reduced PIEZO expression levels in the leaves (Figure S24b). This suggests that this microRNA is able to regulate PIEZO expression, affecting root growth in a MF and under blue light. Based on the iGRN, we propose that PIEZO regulation of plant root growth in response to a MF and blue light involves certain phytohormones, including auxin, ethylene, and gibberellin, as well as calcium ion flux, gravity, mechanical pressure and miRNAs.

## Discussion
The mechanosensitive protein PIEZO plays an important role in plant development. In this study, we demonstrate that the PIEZO gene can respond to a MF and blue light to regulate root growth in Arabidopsis. PIEZO senses the MF polarity relative to gravity, and the role of PIEZO is regulated by CRY1 and CRY2‐mediated signal transduction from the leaves to the roots. Under blue light and MF conditions, PIEZO modulates Ca2+ flux, phytohormones including auxin transport, ethylene and gibberellin biosynthesis and signaling pathways, as well as miRNA.
MFs influence plant development, and PIEZO‐mediated root growth regulation depends on MF polarity: whether perpendicular, parallel, or anti‐parallel to gravity (Figure 1a and Figure S5). Although PIEZO localizes to the vacuole membrane (Radin et al. 2021), the mechanism by which it responds to MF polarity remains unclear. The PIEZO gene regulates root growth dependent on blue light (Figure 1b–o), and the blue light receptors CRY1 and 2 also respond to the MF to regulate root growth (Figure 4). These findings suggest that PIEZO functions within the blue light signaling pathway, as previously proposed (Bello‐Bello and Herrera‐Estrella 2024; Xu et al. 2024), and that either CRY1 or 2 is necessary for the plant to perceive a MF. Our genetic analyses reveal that PIEZO acts downstream of both CRY1 and CRY2 in regulating root length and is required for CRY‐mediated leaf‐to‐root signal transduction under MF and blue light. This establishes a clear genetic hierarchy in MF signaling, contrasting with previous models that proposed CRYs as primary magnetosensors without identified downstream effectors in growth regulation (Solov'yov et al. 2007; Ahmad et al. 2007).
MFs are known to affect auxin transport to regulate root growth (Jin et al. 2019), and the regulation of root growth by the PIEZO gene is associated with Ca2+ flux (Fang et al. 2021). Here, we demonstrate that PIEZO influences Ca 2+ efflux and auxin transport mediated by PIN3, PIN6, and PIN7 (Figure 3) under MF and blue light conditions. Notably, we found that PIEZO regulates auxin transport specifically through a PIN3‐dependent pathway, as genetic interaction studies showed that the piezo‐cl/pin3‐4 double mutant exhibited no significant change in root length under MF and blue light (Figure 3ay), similar to the WT response. This identifies a specific auxin transport component downstream of PIEZO in MF signaling.
PIEZO localizes to the vascular tissue and the root cap (Figure 2b–f), overlapping with the expression patterns of PIN3, PIN6, and PIN7 (Friml et al. 2002; Blilou et al. 2005; Benková et al. 2003). The iGRN (Figure 8) includes LAZY2 and LAZY4, which are involved in gravitropic responses (Chen et al. 2023), suggesting that the root caps respond to MF and gravity signals. Beyond auxin transport, PIEZO also affects amyloplast content and distribution in root tips under blue light and MF (Figure 7), though the starch synthesis gene PGM1 is not involved in this regulation (Figure S20). This suggests that PIEZO influences amyloplast dynamics through mechanisms independent of PGM1‐mediated starch biosynthesis, potentially affecting statolith function in gravitropism. Ca2+ flux, which is closely linked to auxin‐mediated root development (Wang et al. 2022; Leitão et al. 2019; Vanneste and Friml 2013), may be directly or indirectly regulated by the mechanosensors such as MCA1 and MCA2 or the calcium‐related protein RCA in the root of piezo‐cl mutant seedlings.
The PIEZO protein regulates plant development through interactions with multiple phytohormones (Hu et al. 2021). We found that GA and ethylene are involved in PIEZO‐mediated root growth regulation under MF and blue light (Figure S14). PIEZO did not directly regulate or interact with the GA‐related genes, except for ethylene‐related genes (Figure 8a). Although near‐null MFs alter the GA‐related gene expression (Xu et al. 2016), no differential expression of the GA‐related genes was observed in the piezo‐cl mutants (Figure 8a). The effect of GA4 on root growth in the piezo‐cl mutant under MF and blue light (Figure S14ag) may involve an unidentified pathway or may reflect MF‐induced changes in GA‐related gene expression linked to the PIEZO mutation. Ethylene may regulate root growth in the piezo‐cl mutant via the PIF3, as previously reported (Xu et al. 2024). Notably, the miRNA miR5648‐5p expression was also regulated by MF and could negatively regulate the PIEZO expression (Figure 8a,d and Figure S24). This post‐transcriptional regulation provides a homeostatic regulation of the CRY1–CRY2–PIEZO signaling pathway.
Solar activities (e.g., solar flares and coronal mass ejections) are key drivers of space weather hazards (Kong et al. 2017). These phenomena can severely disturb the geomagnetic field, triggering geomagnetic storms during which local MF intensity may sharply increase to hundreds of μT or even higher (Tulasi Ram et al. 2024; Kappenman 1996). The duration of such geomagnetic disturbances ranges from several hours to days (Akasofu and Chapman 1972). In this study, we demonstrated that PIEZO regulates Arabidopsis root growth under a 500 mT MF and blue light, which establishes key growth parameters for future efforts to promote plant growth in space or extreme magnetic environments. Future studies should focus on elucidating how PIEZO detects MF polarity and its connection to CRY1 and CRY2. Additionally, the mechanism by which miRNAs sense MFs and regulate PIEZO expression requires further investigation. Moreover, the relationship between PIEZO‐mediated Ca2+ fluxes and PIN3‐mediated auxin distribution remains unresearched. The integration of MF signaling with gravitational responses through amyloplasts warrants further investigation. This study reveals a new mechanism by which PIEZO responds to MF and blue light signals to regulate root growth, providing new insights into how the Earth's MF and light environment collectively shape plant development.

## Conclusions
In this study, we demonstrate that the mechanosensory protein PIEZO regulates Arabidopsis root growth in response to both blue light and a MF. PIEZO functions downstream of CRY1 and CRY2 to mediate root growth responses to MF. The signaling pathway involves PIEZO‐mediated regulation of calcium fluxes, PIN3‐mediated auxin transport and amyloplast dynamics, with PIEZO expression itself being fine‐tuned by miR5648‐5p. This study establishes a molecular framework in which PIEZO functions as a key regulator of MF and blue light signals, directing root growth.
