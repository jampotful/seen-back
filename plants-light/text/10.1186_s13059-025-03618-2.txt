Differential epigenetic regulation by blue and UV-A light reveals the key role of CsSDG36-mediated H3K4 methylation in leaf development and secondary metabolism in Camellia sinensis.

# Differential epigenetic regulation by blue and UV-A light reveals the key role of CsSDG36-mediated H3K4 methylation in leaf development and secondary metabolism in Camellia sinensis

## Abstract
BackgroundThere is a Chinese proverb that good tea comes from high mountains with clouds and mists, suggesting the important impact of environmental factors on the development and secondary metabolism in tea plants. However, the epigenetic mechanism involved is still unclear.ResultsHigh altitude results in light enhancement with a higher retention of short-wavelength light in cloudy conditions, suggesting the key role of short-wavelength light in the quality formation of tea plants. Thus, we focus on the representative short-wavelength light, blue and UV-A, and characterize plant responses in epigenome, transcriptome, leaf development, and metabolome. We profile six histone modifications under different light wavelengths and link these to leaf development and secondary metabolism, including changes in gene expression during flavonoid, theanine, caffeine, and β-carotene biosynthesis. There is higher stomatal density and thicker mesophyll tissues under blue light, with higher levels of chlorophyll components under UV-A light. The epigenome results in differential changes of stomatal density and quality components in different light conditions. We further identify crucial histone modifications in leaf development and secondary metabolism. Functional analyses suggest diverse regulations mediated by cryptochrome and phototropin in light adaptation, and we confirm the important role of CsSDG36-mediated histone H3K4 methylation.ConclusionOur results not only reveal the landscape of histone modifications, transcripts, leaf development, and metabolites from different lights in tea plants, but also provide insight into the roles of photoreceptors and epigenetic mechanisms involved in leaf development and secondary metabolism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03618-2.

## Background
Given excellent flavor and bio-active compounds, tea has emerged as one of the three non-alcoholic beverages worldwide. Tea is characterized by nutritional value and health benefits, such as eliminating free radicals, reducing inflammation, and preventing allergic reactions [1]. These effects are attributed to the abundant secondary metabolites in tea leaves, including flavonoids, free amino acids, alkaloids, and volatiles [2]. There is a Chinese proverb that good tea originates from high mountains with clouds and mists, emphasizing the important impact of environmental factors on secondary metabolism in tea plants. Clouds in the sky form rich diffuse light on the high mountains, which is highly correlated with the formation of flavor compounds in tea plants [3]. According to the wavelengths, the solar spectrum can divide into ultraviolet light (wavelength < 380 nm), visible light (380–720 nm, followed by blue, cyan, green, yellow, orange, and red lights), and infrared light (wavelength > 720 nm) [4]. Green light increases the content of proanthocyanidins and ascorbic acid [5], while red light enhances the activities of phenylalanine ammonialyase (PAL) and phenylacetaldehyde synthase (PAAS), resulting in the increase of volatile phenylpropanoids in tea plants [6]. UV-B light induces the accumulation of flavonoids through UV resistance LOCUS8 (UVR8) signal transduction pathway [7]. These bio-active metabolites in leaves form an important foundation of tea quality and flavors. Moreover, different light spectrum induces the changes of leaf pigment compounds as well, affecting the color of dried tea and tea infusion. Red and blue lights respectively regulate the biosynthesis of chlorophyll and carotenoids [8]. Except for metabolism synthesis, light spectrum regulates numerous physiological processes in plants, including stomatal opening [9], root growth [10], and leaf photosynthesis [11]. The growth and metabolism regulated by red and UV-B lights in tea plants have been extensively studied in the past decade, but little is known on the effects of blue and UV-A lights.
Light spectrums are sensed by specific photoreceptors in plants. Phytochromes and UVR8 perceive red/far-red light and UV-B light, respectively [12]. Phototroins absorb blue light, while cryptochromes absorb blue light and UV-A light [13]. Since the absorption spectrums of pigments differ, the light spectrums could diversely affect secondary metabolism and development in plants. The expression of phytochrome A (CsPHYA), phytochrome B (CsPHYB), cryptochrome 1 (CsCRY1), and cryptochrome 2 (CsCRY2) regulate the biosynthesis of chlorophyll and carotenoids in tea plants [8]. The downregulation of CsCRY2 and CsPHOT2 is closely related to the increased contents of proanthocyanidins and ascorbic acid [5]. Primary photoreceptors PHYB and CRY2 are involved in the light-dependent phenylpropanoid accumulation, and the transcription factor HY5 is required for the response in Arabidopsis [14]. Phototropins phosphorylate BLUS1 kinase, mediating stomatal opening and blue light-specific promotion of stomatal conductance [15]. UV-B photoreceptor UVR8 regulates auxin response and lateral root development by interacting with MYB73 [16]. Despite the signaling pathways mediated by photoreceptors in plants have been studied for decades, the epigenetic mechanisms involved are rarely reported. Under blue light, CRY1 physically associates with SWI2/SNF2-RELATED 1 chromatin remodeling complex (SWR1-C) to catalyze H2 A.Z incorporation into the chromatin [17], while CRY2 recruits MTA to regulate epitranscriptome [18]. Blue light affects the H3K9 acetylation levels of light-responsive genes [19]. UV light has been associated with DNA methylome and histone acetylation. In Arabidopsis, UV-B triggers DNA hypomethylation [20] and induces histone acetylation [21], while UV-C reduces the number of H3K9 me2-enriched regions [22]. Limited studies are far from sufficient for the epigenetic mechanisms mediated by photoreceptors. For tea plants, photoreceptor-mediated developmental and metabolic changes are hotspots, but the underlying epigenetic mechanism involved remains unclear.
Epigenetic modifications mediate gene expression by changing the chromatin states, including DNA methylation and histone modification [23]. In plants, epigenetic modifications play crucial roles in many biological processes, such as drought tolerance [24], flowering [25], and secondary metabolism [26]. Recent findings have highlighted the epigenetic regulation on growth and development in tea plants. DNA methylation is identified to be related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis and cold acclimation [27]. The post-translational modifications on histone N-terminal tails regulate the chromatin dynamics and transcription process, including acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation [23]. It is reported that histone modifications have a broad impact on plant responses to extreme temperature, salt, drought, and light. Histone deacetylase CsHD2 C negatively regulates H4ac on RD29 A and RD29B genes, resulting in decreased gene expression and lower tolerance to salt and abscisic acid (ABA) [28]. H4ac on CsABA4, CsNCED3-1, and CsNCED3-2 genes promotes gene expression, positively regulating the biosynthesis and accumulation of ABA during the post-harvest process [29]. CsHDA6 negatively regulates H3ac and H4ac on the CsAFS gene by interacting with the transcription factor CsMYC2, resulting in decreased gene expression and lower α-farnesene content [30]. The levels of H3K9me2 and DNA methylation on the promoter inhibit the expression of CsTSB2, resulting in lower indole content [31]. Besides, CsHDA8 involves in the formation of indole by binding to the promoter region of CsTSB2 and influencing the level of H3ac and H4ac [32]. CsHDA2 and CsMYC2 form a transcriptional regulatory module to decrease the H4ac level on CsNES promoter, resulting in the downregulation of CsNES expression and the decrease of (E)-nerolidol content [33]. These studies suggest the important role of epigenetic modifications in the adversity adaptability of tea plants. However, the function of histone modifications and regulatory mechanisms in light adaption remains largely unclear in tea plants.
In this study, we conducted comparative research to analyze the changes in epigenome and transcriptome from different lights. Then, we characterized leaf development and photosynthetic physiology responding to different lights, and highlighted the complicated regulation of histone modifications involved. Further functional analysis verified the important roles of photoreceptors and histone H3K4 methylations on leaf development and chlorophyll biosynthesis in tea plants, and confirmed the genes modulated by the histone methyltransferase CsSDG36. Finally, we characterized metabolome in response to different lights, and focused on the integrated regulation of histone modifications involved. Further functional analysis confirmed the key roles of photoreceptors and histone H3K4 methylations on quality component formation in tea plants, and identified the enzyme genes modulated by the histone methyltransferase CsSDG36. This novel information enhances our understanding on the epigenetic mechanisms involved in leaf development and secondary metabolisms in tea plants.

## Results

## The landscape of histone modifications reveals complicated epigenetic regulations on the adaptation to different lights in C. sinensis
To characterize the landscape of histone modifications, we obtained the genome-wide enriched regions in white light (W)-, blue light (B)-, and UV-A light (U)-treated tea leaves using antibodies respectively against H3K4ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K9me2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Approximately 70% of ChIP-seq reads were mapped to unique positions in the reference genome (Additional file 2: Table S1). Histone modification markers H3K4ac, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K9ac, and H3K9me2 were enriched across the whole gene body with higher abundance close to the transcription start site (TSS), while H3K4me1 was highly enriched in the transcribed region (Fig. 1a, Additional file 1: Fig. S2a). The most highly expressed genes were evenly distributed upstream and downstream of TSS. Interestingly, we found that the H3K4me1 marker of moderately high expressed genes was distributed both upstream and downstream of TSS in UV-A light, which was distinguished from the pattern observed in blue and white lights, where it mainly occupied gene body regions (Fig. 1b, Additional file 1: Fig. S2b-c). We obtained peaks of all samples (Additional file 3: Table S2) and visualized the distribution of peaks across the genome (Fig. 1c, Additional file 1: Fig. S2 d-e). Except for the H3K4me1 marker, five histone modifications were predominantly enriched in the intergenic regions, followed by the promoter region (Fig. 1d, Additional file 1: Fig. S2f). It was noticed that the H3K4me1 marker was primarily accumulated in the promoter region in blue and white light, while it was located in the intergenic region in UV-A light. The differentially modified genes (DMGs) of histone markers showed that H3K9ac tended to increase in abundance around genes, while H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 tended to decrease after treatment (Fig. 1e). Based on the GO enrichment analysis, DMGs of different comparisons exhibited enrichment diversity in biological processes. In U vs. W, processes related to photosynthesis were enriched in specific histone modifications. In B vs. W, processes related to acyltransferase and lyase processes were enriched in specific histone modifications (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Based on the KEGG enrichment analysis, DMGs of different comparisons exhibited enrichment diversity in metabolism pathways (Additional file 1: Fig. S4, Additional file 4: Table S3). In U vs. W, pathways related to amino acid metabolism and degradation, carotenoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis were enriched in specific histone modifications. In B vs. W, it was noticed that pathways related to nitrogen metabolism, caffeine metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis and metabolism were enriched in specific histone modifications. These results revealed the landscape of histone modifications from different lights, and further indicated the participation of histone modifications in development and secondary metabolism. To better understand the role of histone modifications in gene expression, we integrated the epigenome and transcriptome data from different lights. As shown in Fig. 2a, UV-A and blue lights similarly increased the H3K4me3 level, but decreased H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 levels. The levels of H3K4ac, H3K9ac, and H3K9me2 exhibited diverse changes in UV-A and blue lights (Fig. 2a). In different lights, 430 and 94 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were respectively up- and downregulated in B vs. W, while 686 and 442 DEGs were up- and downregulated in U vs. W (Additional file 1: Fig. S5a). A total of 171 common DEGs were identified in two groups, only 11.55% of total DEGs (Additional file 1: Fig. S5b). Enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed plant development-related processes (cell periphery, external encapsulating structure, cell wall, extracellular region, and apoplast) in blue light and numerous downregulated metabolic pathways in UV-A light (Additional file 1: Fig. S5c-e). Additionally, we clustered all DEGs into five clusters (C1 to C5) based on their expression patterns (Fig. 2b, Additional file 5: Table S4). Further correlation analysis was performed between gene expression and modification abundance near TSS with DEGs to identify candidate histone modifications for each cluster (Fig. 2c). DEGs in C1 showed high expression in blue and UV-A lights, which were enriched in “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” and “photosynthesis-antenna proteins” pathways (Fig. 2b). The expression of DEGs in C1 was positively related to H3K4ac, H3K4me3, and H3K9ac, but negatively correlated with H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 (Fig. 2c, Additional file 6: Table S5). DEGs in C2 exhibited high expression in UV-A light, which were enriched in the “porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism” pathway (Fig. 2b). The expression of DEGs in C2 was positively correlated with H3K4ac, H3K4me3, and H3K9ac as well, but negatively related to H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 (Fig. 2c, Additional file 6: Table S5). DEGs in C3 revealed high expression in white light, which were enriched in “flavonoid biosynthesis” and “starch and sucrose metabolism” pathways (Fig. 2b). The expression of DEGs in C3 was positively related to H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 (Fig. 2c, Additional file 6: Table S5). DEGs in C4 showed high expression in white and blue lights, which were enriched in “circadian rhythm—plant” and “starch and sucrose metabolism” pathways (Fig. 2b). The expression of DEGs in C4 was positively correlated with H3K4me1 and H3K4me2, but negatively correlated with H3K4ac, H3K9ac, H3K4me3, and H3K9me2 (Fig. 2c, Additional file 6: Table S5). DEGs in C5 exhibited high expression in blue light, which were enriched in pathways related to “amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism,” “galactose metabolism,” and “starch and sucrose metabolism” (Fig. 2b). The expression of DEGs in C5 was positively related to H3K4ac, H3K4me1, and H3K4me2 (Fig. 2c, Additional file 6: Table S5). Except for H3K9me2, histone modifications detected in this study were active markers for gene expression. Considering the function of each histone modification on gene expression, candidate key histone modifications in each cluster displayed a significant differentiation. H3K4ac, H3K9ac, and H3K4me3 were identified as key regulators in clusters 1 and 2. In clusters 3, 4, and 5, H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 served as common key regulators, with H3K9me2 in cluster 4 and H3K4ac in cluster 5. Furthermore, DEGs matching the changes of at least one histone modification in each cluster were respectively chosen for upset analysis (Fig. 2d–h, Additional file 7–8: Table S6-7). The results showed that most modification-related DEGs in each cluster were accumulated in single histone modification, suggesting the diversity of epigenetic regulations on gene expression. Compared with white light, DEGs in C1 and C4 showed similar changes in blue and UV-A lights with two light-regulated pathways (“circadian rhythm” and “photosynthesis”) involved (Fig. 2b), suggesting the similar effects of blue and UV-A lights on light-regulated pathways at transcription level in tea plants. However, none of the photosynthesis-related genes in cluster 1 was found in detected histone modifications (Fig. 2d), but two circadian rhythm-related genes of cluster 4 (CsFKF1 and CsPIF3) were identified in single histone modification (H3K4me3) (Fig. 2g). The “starch and sucrose metabolism” pathway was shared among C3, C4, and C5 (Fig. 2b), indicating the key role of this pathway in light adaptation and complicated regulatory mechanism at the transcription level. CsISA3 and CsCEL1 in C3 were respectively accumulated in H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 (Fig. 2f), while CsBAM3 in C4 was enriched in H3K4me3 (Fig. 2g). ## Integrated analyses from different lights reveal the diverse regulation of epigenetic modifications on leaf development in C. sinensis
To analyze the epigenetic mechanism of lights regulating plant development, we systematically evaluated their effects on the physiological and developmental traits in tea plants. UV-A light significantly increased SOD and POD enzyme activities in leaves, while blue light upregulated POD enzyme activity (Fig. 3a–c). In roots, UV-A light significantly reduced SOD enzyme activity, but increased CAT enzyme activity (Additional file 1: Fig. S6a-c). Blue light downregulated SOD and POD enzyme activities in roots, but upregulated CAT enzyme activity (Additional file 1: Fig. S6a-c). In addition, UV-A and blue lights caused minimal differences in chlorophyll fluorescence (Fig. 3d). These results suggested that tea plants adapted to different lights with little stress. Interestingly, only blue light significantly increased net photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate, but reduced intercellular CO2 concentration (Fig. 3e–h). The developmental results revealed increased stomatal density and thicker leaf, accompanied by enhanced palisade and spongy tissues in blue light as well (Fig. 3i–m). These data revealed the adaptation of tea plants to blue light through developmental adjustment. The expression patterns of regulatory genes involved in stomatal development were further studied in this study. The positive regulator CsEPFL9 was upregulated in blue light, resulting in the downregulation of many negative regulatory genes, including CsMPK6, CsEPF2b, CsSDD1, and CsYODAa (Fig. 3n, Additional file 9: Table S8). The genomic track showed the involvement of H3K9ac and H3K4me3 in the CsEPFL9 gene expression (Fig. 3o). Since few changes in developmental traits under UV-A light, the photosynthesis-related chlorophyll contents were further evaluated to analyze the adaptation of tea plants. Interestingly, UV-A light significantly enhanced chlorophyll content (Fig. 3p). UV-A light induced the significant accumulation of chlorophyll a (chl a) and chlorophyll b (chl b), while blue light only increased the content of chl b. Correspondingly, a series of genes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis were upregulated in UV-A light, including Glu-tRNA reductase (HEMA2), magnesium chelatase (CHLH), Mg-proto IX monomethyl ester cyclase (CRD), pchlide oxidoreductase (POR), and chlide a oxygenase (CAO) (Fig. 3q, Additional file 9: Table S8, Additional file 1: Fig. S6 d-e). Further analysis showed that H3K4ac was related to the changes of CsHEMA2 expression and chlorophyll (Additional file 1: Fig. S6f). H3K4me3 and H3K9ac were associated with the changes of CsPORC expression and chlorophyll (Additional file 1: Fig. S6 g). ## CsSDG36-mediated histone H3K4 methylations regulate the stomatal development and chlorophyll biosynthesis via the expression of CsEPFL9, CsPORC, and CsHEMA2
It was noteworthy that histone methylations exhibited specific regulations on adaptation to different lights, particularly H3K4me3. The methylation level of histone H3K4 around genes was positively correlated with gene expression, especially the H3K4me3 level [34]. To investigate the effect of H3K4 methylations on stomatal development and chlorophyll biosynthesis, tea leaves were treated with the histone H3K4 demethylation inhibitor CPI455 [35]. The expressions of CsHEMA2 were significantly upregulated after 10-day treatment, and stomatal density of treated plants was increased as well (Fig. 4a,b, Additional file 1: Fig. S7a). It was reported that CsSDG36, which is the homology of AtATX4, catalyzes H3K4 methylations in C. sinensis [36]. Histone H3K4 methyltransferase gene CsSDG36 was subsequently overexpressed in tea leaves (Additional file 1: Fig. S7b). The results revealed that CsSDG36 overexpression could increase the CsEPFL9 expression, resulting in higher stomatal density (Fig. 4c,d, Additional file 1: Fig. S7c). In addition, the expression of chlorophyll biosynthesis genes CsHEMA2 and CsPORC was upregulated as well (Fig. 4c). To further clarify the roles of photoreceptors in gene expression changes, CsPHOT2, CsCRY1a, and CsCRY1b were respectively overexpressed in tea leaves (Additional file 1: Fig. S7 d-f). The results showed that the CsEPFL9 expression and stomatal density of OE-CsPHOT2 plants were significantly increased (Fig. 4e,f, Additional file 1: Fig. S7 g), while the expressions of CsHEMA2 and CsPORC were significantly upregulated in the OE-CsCRY1a and OE-CsCRY1b plants, respectively (Fig. 4g,h, Additional file 1: Fig. S7 h-m). ChIP–qPCR analysis revealed that the H3K4me3 levels on CsHEMA2, CsPORC, and CsEPFL9 genes were substantially increased in the OE-CsSDG36 leaves (Fig. 4i). Moreover, we detected a significant enrichment of GFP-CsSDG36 on CsHEMA2, CsPORC, and CsEPFL9 genes in the OE-CsSDG36 leaves (Fig. 4j), suggesting that CsSDG36 directly binds to these genes and causes H3K4me3 deposition. Given that histone modification H3K9ac also involved in the expression of CsEPFL9, histone acetyltransferase gene CsGCN5 was further overexpressed in tea leaves (Fig. S7n). The stomatal density was increased in OE-CsGCN5 leaves, suggesting the involvement of histone acetylations in stomatal development (Fig. S7o-p). ## Integrated analyses from different lights reveal the diverse regulation of epigenetic modifications on secondary metabolisms in C. sinensis
To explore the effect of lights on the metabolites in tea leaves, we grew seedlings from different lights. After cultivation, a total of 2003 metabolites were detected using the UPLC-MS/MS system (Additional file 10: Table S9). Figure 5a shows that the metabolomic profile among white, blue, and UV-A light groups could be distinguished clearly by principal component analysis (PCA). We then identified the DAMs based on the standard of Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1, | log2 (Fold Change) |≥ 1, and P value < 0.05. A total of 44 DAMs (16 upregulated and 28 downregulated) were filtered in B vs. W (Fig. 5b), and 67 DAMs (8 upregulated and 59 downregulated) were filtered in U vs. W (Fig. 5c). The heatmap of total DAMs was generated to classify the overview of metabolomic profiling (Fig. 5d). DAMs were highly accumulated in flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic acids (Fig. 5e), with only 17 common DAMs identified in B vs. W and U vs. W (Fig. 5f). The top 20 DAMs in B vs. W included 6 upregulated and 14 downregulated metabolites with fold change values exceeding 1.2 (Fig. 5g), while the top 20 DAMs in U vs. W included 1 upregulated and 19 downregulated metabolites with fold change values exceeding 1.6 (Fig. 5h). To deeply understand the metabolism regulation in different lights, the KEGG enrichment analysis of DAMs was performed. In B vs. W, the metabolism pathways associated with caffeine metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism were enriched (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). In U vs. W, the metabolism pathways associated with carotenoid biosynthesis, biosynthesis of amino acids, and caffeine metabolism were enriched (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). ## CsSDG36-mediated histone H3K4 methylations regulate secondary metabolisms via the expression of CsF3’Hd and Cs7NMTd
Histone methylations exhibited specific regulation on multiple biosynthesis and metabolic pathways, especially H3K4me1, H3K4me2, and H3K4me3. To determine the roles of H3K4 methylations in regulating flavonoids, caffeine, and pigment levels in C. sinensis, we used CPI455 to increase H3K4me3 and H3K4me2 levels. qRT-PCR revealed that the expression of flavonoid, caffeine, and carotenoid biosynthesis-related genes was increased in treated leaves, including CsF3Ha, CsF3’Hc, CsF3’Hd, Cs7NMTd, Cs3NMT, CsHDS, and CsDXS3 (Fig. 8a). Transient overexpression of CsSDG36 was further conducted to identify its target genes involved in metabolism pathways (Additional file 1: Fig. S7b). The expression of flavonoid, caffeine, and carotenoid biosynthesis-related genes was substantially increased in OE-CsSDG36 leaves, including CsF3’Hd, Cs7NMTd, Cs3NMT, and CsHDS (Fig. 8b). The contents of naringenin and eriodictyol significantly decreased in OE-CsSDG36 leaves (Fig. 8c,d), while the leucocyanidin and dihydroquercetin exhibited no significant change (Fig. 8e, Additional file 1: Fig. S13a). In addition, the downstream products of flavonoid and caffeine biosynthesis pathway were accumulated in OE-CsSDG36 leaves, including catechin, caffeine, and theobromine (Fig. 8f–h). To further clarify the roles of photoreceptors in gene expression changes, we determined the expression of candidate genes respectively in OE-CsPHOT2, OE-CsCRY1a, OE-CsCRY1b, and OE-CsCRY2 leaves (Additional file 1: Fig. S13b). The results showed that candidate enzyme genes were significantly upregulated in OE-CsCRY2 leaves, including CsF3Ha, CsF3’Hd, Cs3NMT, and CsDXS3 (Fig. 8i). The expression of CsF3’Hc was significantly upregulated in the OE-CsCRY1a plants, while the expression of CsF3Ha, CsF3’Hc, CsHDS, and CsDXS3 was significantly upregulated in the OE-CsCRY1b plants (Additional file 1: Fig. S13c-d). Moreover, the Cs7NMTd expression was significantly upregulated in OE-CsPHOT2 plants (Fig. 8j). Further ChIP–qPCR analysis revealed that the H3K4me3 levels on CsF3’Hd and Cs7NMTd genes were substantially increased in the OE-CsSDG36 leaves (Fig. 8k), and CsSDG36 directly binds to the H3K4me3-enriched regions on CsF3’Hd and Cs7NMTd genes (Fig. 8l). Our results demonstrated that photoreceptors and CsSDG36-mediated H3K4 methylations collaboratively regulated the expression of many enzyme genes involved in the secondary metabolisms (Fig. 8m).

## Discussion
Previous studies have illuminated the regulation of epigenetic modifications on developmental and metabolic processes, including chromatin accessibility [39], DNA methylation [27], microRNA [40], and histone modifications [32]. In recent years, several high-quality reference genomes of tea plants have been assembled [41–43], and genome profiles of chromatin accessibility [39] and DNA methylation [27] have been published. Nevertheless, the genome profiles of histone markers remain rarely reported in Camellia sinensis. In this study, we provided the genome-wide landscapes of histone modifications from different lights, including the distribution patterns of six histone markers (Fig. 1). The epigenome data serve as the foundation for further epigenetic research. Except for H3K4me1, five histone modifications were predominantly enriched in the intergenic regions (Fig. 1d), which was probably related to the large genome size of tea plants resulting from burst events [44]. Similar distribution patterns were observed in other species with large genome as well, including Triticum aestivum [25] and Helianthus annuus [45]. The spatial distributions of H3K4 methylations are similar to those observed in Arabidopsis [46], with H3K4me1 located on gene body and H3K4me2/3 around TSS (Fig. 1a). H3K4me3 peaks distribute slightly upstream of H3K4me2, suggesting the potential function in proximal promoter activation and the early stage of transcription elongation [47]. In contrast, the enrichment of H3K4me1 in the transcribed region might be positively related to the gene elongation [47]. In addition, histone acetylations and H3K9me2 distribute around TSS (Fig. 1a), respectively promoting and inhibiting gene transcription via chromatin structure, including charge neutralization and recruitment of chromatin regulators [48]. Blue and UV-A lights are two spectrums recognized by a type of plant photoreceptors, including cryptochrome and phototropin [49]. Cryptochrome senses blue light and UV-A [50], while phototropin specifically senses blue light [51]. In this study, blue and UV-A lights caused significant differences in histone modifications at the genomic level (Fig. 2a). H3K4me1 served as a precursor for the formation of H3K4me2 and H3K4me3, and distinct H3K4 methyltransferase may contribute to the differential accumulation of H3K4me1, H3K4me2, and H3K4me3 [52]. Both UV-A and blue lights increased the H3K4me3 level with the decrease of H3K4me1 and H3K4me2 levels (Fig. 2a), suggesting that the preference for H3K4 sites toward trimethylation catalyzing by specific methyltransferases under UV-A and blue lights. The levels of H3K9ac and H3K9me2 showed opposite trends (Fig. 2a), implying the dynamic balance between them, which might be critical for maintaining gene activating or silencing [53]. Besides, a previous study reported the H3K4me1 and H3K9me2 coordinately regulate gene expression on gene body [54], which was similar to the histone modification states on the CsF3’Hd gene (Fig. 6f). DEGs with different expression patterns showed diverse histone modification levels (Fig. 2b–h), suggestting the diverse epigenetic regulations in different lights.
Tea plants have developed two strategies respectively in adaptation to blue and UV-A lights: regulating leaf development in blue light and altering secondary metabolisms in UV-A light (Figs. 3 and 5). Interestingly, histone H3K4 methylations regulated developmental and metabolic traits induced by different lights (Figs. 4 and 8), revealing the extensive involvement of epigenetic modifications in diverse signaling pathways. UV-A light, a representative short-wavelength spectrum, is specifically sensed by cryptochromes [55]. Previous studies revealed that UV-A causes developmental and metabolic changes in plants [56]. In this study, tea plants did not alter developmental traits, but changed metabolic flux in adaptation to UV-A light (Figs. 3 and 5), which probably attributed to the species difference. Among DAMs, 59 metabolites were significantly downregulated in UV-A light, accounting for 88.06%. The enriched pathways of DAMs in UV-A light included many featured metabolic pathways in tea plants, such as carotenoid biosynthesis, caffeine metabolism, and biosynthesis of amino acids (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). In UV-A light, more DAM-related DEGs were identified compared to blue light (Figs. 6 and 7), and many DEGs (CsHEMA2, CsPORC, CsF3Ha, CsF3’Hc, CsF3’Hd, Cs3NMTd, CsHDS, and CsDXS3) involved were further proved to be regulated by cryptochromes (Figs. 4 and 8). These results indicate that cryptochrome plays a key role in regulating secondary metabolisms under UV-A light in tea plants. Blue light is sensed by phototropins and cryptochromes, and phototropins separate the signaling pathway in blue light from that in UV-A light [55]. In adaptation to blue light, plants increase stomatal density and mesophyll tissue to enhance photosynthesis [57, 58]. These developmental adjustments have been observed in tea plants as well (Fig. 3), indicating the conservation of blue light adaptability among plant species. As the core metabolites of amino acid biosynthesis, glutamic acid could be transformed into Pro and Arg [59]. Arg is a potential precursor of proline, while Pro is considered to be a metabolite with protective functions for stress adaptation, recovery, and signaling [59]. The Pro and Arg contents increased under blue light (Additional file 1: Fig. S11a), reflecting the requirement of these substances to exert their stress-resistant function. A recent study reported that the predominant forms of amino acids in shoot tips were glutamate, theanine, glutamine, and arginine [60]. We found that tea leaves mainly accumulated glutamic acid under blue light (Additional file 1: Fig. S11a), suggesting a preference for the conversion to glutamic acid in blue light. Except for flavor compounds, blue light could notably increase the content of chlorophyll in tea plants [61]. Similarly, our data revealed that chlorophyll b was accumulated under blue light, resulting in the increase of total chlorophyll. Many DEGs in blue light were further proved to be regulated by phototropins, including CsEPFL9 and Cs7NMTd (Figs. 4 and 8). These data suggest that phototropin plays a crucial role in the regulation of developmental traits responding to blue light in tea plants.
Post-translational modifications of histones have been confirmed to participate in the formation of volatile metabolites. A series of histone deacetylases have been reported to be involved in the biosynthesis of α-farnesene, indole, and (E)-nerolidol, containing CsHDA2, CsHDA6, and CsHDA8 [30, 32, 33]. However, there are few studies on the regulation of histone methylations in metabolic processes of tea plants. In the present study, we confirmed that the expressions of enzyme genes in secondary metabolism pathways were closely related to H3K4 methylations (Fig. 8a). The SDG family has been systematically identified in Camellia sinensis, among which TRX subfamily consists of 11 members catalyzing histone H3K4 methylations [62]. We further verified that CsSDG36 positively regulated the transcription of developmental and metabolic genes, including CsEPFL9, CsPORC, CsHEMA2, CsF3’Hd, and Cs7NMTd (Fig. 4k, Fig. 8m). MYB transcription factors have been identified to be extensively involved in secondary metabolite biosynthesis and leaf development of the tea plant [63]. Considering that H3K4 methyltransferase can directly interact with specific transcription factor to regulate gene expression, including MYB [64], we hypothesize that CsSDG36 may interact with different transcription factors to deposit the H3K4me3 at specific sites and promote the gene expression. These findings provide novel gene and epigenetic targets for the quality breeding of tea plants, as well as the theoretical basis for UV-A light supplementation by LEDs in different stages of growth and tea production to improve tea quality. The molecular mechanism of how histone methyltransferases diversely regulate these traits from different lights needs further exploration.

## Conclusions
In this study, we integrated epigenome, transcriptome, leaf development, and metabolome and reported that photoreceptor and various histone modifications coordinately regulated gene expression during leaf development and secondary metabolism from different lights, resulting in developmental and metabolic changes. In addition, we verified the crucial role of CsSDG36 mediated-H3K4 methylations on leaf development and secondary metabolism in tea plants.

## Methods

## Plant materials and treatment
Given a previous study reported the enrichment of blue and ultraviolet lights in the high mountains [65], the spectra and light intensity were measured on the Wugong Mountain in Jiangxi Province at different altitudes under sunny and cloudy conditions using Lighting Passport (Asensetek, China). Our statistics showed that high altitude regions have a stronger light intensity than low altitude regions. Furthermore, we found that cloudy condition has a higher retention of short-wavelength lights compared to sunny condition at different altitudes (Additional file 1: Fig. S1a, Additional file 11: Table S10). To determine whether the changing pattern of light spectrum factors at different altitudes is the key factor affecting the development and secondary metabolites of tea plants in high mountains with clouds and mists, we conducted indoor simulation experiments with different lights. The 1-year-old C. sinensis seedlings (cv. Echa 10) were pre-cultivated using white light (photoperiod 12/12-h light/dark photoperiod and 200 μmol m−2 s−1 light density) at 22–25 °C for 10 days. Light spectrum treatment was performed as follows: Half of the 200 μmol m−2 s−1 white lamps were replaced with 100 μmol m−2 s−1 designated wavelength lamps, including white, blue, and UV-A light (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). Meanwhile, the light intensity and spectra at the height of 2nd tea leaf were measured by Lighting Passport (Asensetek, China) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1c, Additional file 12: Table S11). After cultivation of 14 days, the 2nd leaf blades were used to measure the leaf physiological parameters (stomatal density, anatomical analysis, photosynthetic characteristics, and chlorophyll fluorescence) or immediately fixed in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for further determination of metabolome and transcriptome. Both 2nd leaf blades and absorptive roots were sampled for activity determination of enzyme. For ChIP-seq experiment, the 2nd leaf blades were immediately cross-linking and fixed in liquid nitrogen after treatment of 14 days.
For inhibitor treatment, the second leaf of C. sinensis branches (cv. Echa 10) was submerged in inhibitor solution (6.25 μM CPI-455, 0.125% DMSO) under vacuum for 10 min, while 0.125% DMSO solution was set as the control. After infiltration, the branches were cultured in Hoagland’s nutrient solution for 3 days to determine metabolites and enzyme gene expression. The branches were cultured for 10 days to determine the stomatal phenotype and developmental gene expression.

## Metabolite profiling
Metabolite detection used UPLC-MS/MS system. Fifty micrograms powder of each frozen dried sample was weighed and extracted using 1.2 mL of extracted buffer (70% methanolic with 1 mg/L 2-chlorophenylalanine as the internal standard). After centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the sample was filtered using 0.22-μm needle filter and the injection volume was 2 μL. The water with 0.1% formic acid as phase A and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid as phase B. Separation of samples was performed on an Agilent SB-C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm). The initial analysis was separated on a gradient of 5–95% B for 9 min, 95% B for 1 min, 95 to 5% B for 1.1 min, and kept 5% B for 14 min, with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The column temperature was kept at 40 °C. The ESI source operated under the following parameters: source temperature 550 °C; ion spray voltage + 5.5 kV/− 4.5 kV; ion source gas I, gas II, and curtain gas were set at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively. Multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) was used as the monitoring mode to identify the metabolites, and the peak areas of the chromatographic peaks were integrated into quantification. VIP values were extracted from OPLS-DA results, which also contained score plots and permutation plots, and were generated using R package MetaboAnalystR. The DAMs were determined based on criteria of VIP > 1, | log2 (Fold Change) |≥ 1, and P value < 0.05. Three biological replicates were employed in this study. For analyzing the metabolite accumulation pattern of pathways, the peak area was normalized after removing outliers and filling with the mean value.
For targeted metabolic analysis, 150 mg of frozen sample powder was extracted in 1.5 mL of extracted buffer (75% methanolic with 7.5 μg/L acetaminophen-D4 as the internal standard) for 12 h at 4 °C. After filtering, the extracted samples were detected and analyzed using triple-quadrupole MS (TSQ Quantis; Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) with the C18 chromatographic column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm. Agilent, California, USA). Chromatographic conditions were as follows: mobile phase A, 0.1% (v/v) formic acid aqueous solution; mobile phase B, methanol; elution procedures: 0–2 min, B 1%; 2–4 min, B 1 to 15%; 4–12 min, B 15 to 32%; 12–8 min, B 32 to 95%; 18–23 min, B 95%; 23–24 min, B 95 to 1%; 24–25 min, B 1%; injection volume: 2 μL; flowing rate: 0.3 mL/min; column oven: 40 °C. The mass spectrometer voltage was set to + 3.8 kV/− 3.2 kV, capillary temperature at 300 °C, sheath gas to 40 arb, aux gas to 10 arb, AUX gas heater temperature at 300 °C, and S-lens RF level to 55. scan range: m/z 75–1100; resolution: 70,000 (MS1) and 17,500 (MS2); stepped normalized collision energy (NCE): 20 eV. Three biological replicates were employed in this study.

## ChIP assays
The cross-linking and chromatin isolation steps were conducted as described previously [66]. After resuspending the pellet using nuclei lysis buffer, the chromatin was fragmented into 100–500 bp by sonication using a Bioruptor (Diagenode) and the supernatant was obtained. ChIP was performed using antibodies as follows: H3K4ac (ABclonal, A16078), H3K4me1 (ABclonal, A2355), H3K4me2 (ABclonal, A2356), H3K4me3 (ABclonal, A2357), H3K4me3 (ABclonal, A22146), H3K9me2 (ABclonal, A2359), H3K9ac (ABclonal, A7255). Five microliters antibody was incubated with beads for 6 h on a rotator at 4 °C to form antibody-bead complexes. After washing, the chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C. Anti-GFP (ABclonal, AE079) or anti-IgG (ABclonal, AC044 N) antibody magnetic beads were used for immunoprecipitation of the fragmented chromatin following the manufacturer’s protocol. The immunoprecipitated chromatin was washed in a gradient manner, eluted, and reversed cross-link as previously published [54]. The ChIP DNA was purified, precipitated, washed, and resuspended in double distilled water for constructing the library or ChIP-qPCR analysis. Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform was used to sequence the ChIP-seq library. Two biological replicates were employed for ChIP-seq analyses. The primers used for ChIP–qPCR are listed in Additional file 13: Table S12.

## ChIP-seq analyses
Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA mem (v 0.7.12). The signals of 3 kb upstream and downstream of TSS and transcription end sites (TES) were calculated using the “computeMatrix” module of deepTools software. Narrow-peak calling was performed by MACS2 (version 2.1.0) with the threshold of p-value < 1 × e−3. Peak-related genes were identified and the function regions of the peak were annotated by ChIPseeker. Different peak analysis was based on the fold enrichment of peaks of different experiments. The differential peak was determined by the standard of | log2 (Fold Change) |≥ 1 and P-value ≤ 0.05. For analysis of H3K4me1, the different peak was determined using strict thresholds in B vs. W (P-value ≤ 1 × e−2) and in U vs. W (q value < 1 × e−3). To visualize the data, the.bw files were uploaded on the IGB genome browser.

## RNA-seq analyses
The total RNA was extracted using the rapid RNA isolation kit (Beijing Huayueyang Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). After detecting the RNA quality, libraries were constructed and submitted to Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). Clean reads were mapped on the tea plant reference genome to acquire the gene expression information. The expressed gene was counted by featureCounts and standardized with FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads). The standard of | log2 (Fold Change) |≥ 1 and padj ≤ 0.05 was used to identify the DEGs. The clusters were identified according to the expression pattern of DEGs, and the ChIP-seq reads of 2 kb upstream and downstream of the TSS were calculated as the RPM (reads per million mapped reads) value.

## Evaluation of leaf development-related phenotypes
The leaf development-related phenotypes were performed as previously described [67], including stomatal density, and anatomical analysis. Each experiment employed at least three biological replications.

## Analyses of photosynthetic characteristics and chlorophyll fluorescence
The photosynthetic parameters were performed as previously described [67], including transpiration rate, net photosynthetic rate, intercellular CO2 concentration, and stomatal conductance. For chlorophyll fluorescence determination, the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) of the leaves was determined using chlorophyll fluorescence imaging system IMAGINE-PAM/L (WALZ, Germany) after adaptation for 30 min in the dark. Each experiment employed at least five biological replications.

## Western blotting
The total protein was extracted and detected as previously published [36]. The same antibodies used in ChIP assays were employed to determine the histone modification level, while histone H3protein served as the control. Three biological replicates were employed in this study.

## Activity determination of enzyme
Total protein was extracted using NP-40 buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40, 1 × protein inhibitors cocktail, 50 μM MG132, 1 mM PMSF). The same amount of protein was used to determine the enzyme activity of PAL using ELISA Kit. Three biological replicates were employed in this experiment.
For anti-oxidant enzyme activity determination, 200 mg powder of each frozen sample was weighed and extracted using normal saline. The supernatant was used to determine the enzyme activity using assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), including superoxide disproportion (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) assay kit. Three biological replicates were employed in this experiment.

## Measurement of pigments
The leaves sampled after cultivation for 14 days were used to measure the content of pigment. The pigment was extracted and detected as described previously [68]. Three biological replicates were employed in this experiment.

## Transient overexpression
Transient overexpression assays were conducted based on previously published [69]. The coding regions of CsSDG36, CsPHOT2, CsCRY1a, CsCRY1b, CsCRY2, and CsGCN5 were cloned into pMDC43 plasmid for over-expression. The recombinant vector was transformed in Agrobacterium, followed by re-suspending in the liquid injection medium after cultivation. The second leaf of C. sinensis branches (cv. Echa 10) was submerged in injection medium suspension under vacuum for 10 min. After infiltration, the branches were cultured in Hoagland’s nutrient solution. Three biological replicates were employed in this experiment.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction
The RNA extraction, cDNA reverse transcription reaction, and PCR were performed as previously published [36]. GAPDH was selected as an internal control, and the relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each reaction was repeated three times in this experiment. All primers used are listed in Additional file 13: Table S12.
