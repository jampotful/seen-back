The small-molecule pifithrin-α deactivates ETR1 to repress shade avoidance in <i>Arabidopsis</i>.
Liang W, Huang S, Chen X, Jiang K, Gong X, Guo H, Li L. (2025). Science advances. . doi:10.1126/sciadv.adw9241. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# The small-molecule pifithrin-α deactivates ETR1 to repress shade avoidance in Arabidopsis

## Abstract
AbstractShade avoidance syndrome (SAS) commonly occurs in plants sensing shading and the proximity of neighbors, leading to morphological and physiological changes that are detrimental to plant health and consequently crop yield. Controlling SAS could be beneficial for sustainable agriculture. Here, we performed a phenotype-based chemical screening in Arabidopsis and identified pifithrin-α (PFT-α) as a compound that suppresses shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and gene expression. Through genetic, biochemical, and molecular approaches, we demonstrated that PFT-α mimics ethylene by deactivating the ethylene receptor 1 (ETR1). Accumulated ethylene-insensitive 3 (EIN3) influences the expression of phytochrome-interacting factors (PIF4 and PIF7) and subsequently inhibits shade-induced hypocotyl elongation. Our findings not only provide molecular insights into SAS but also suggest that PFT-α, acting as a potent ethylene mimic, plays a crucial role in hormone-regulated plant development and holds potential for modulating SAS in agricultural practices.

## INTRODUCTION
Plants are sessile organisms that are unable to escape from their neighboring competitors under high-density planting conditions. To overcome the shade conditions, many plant species have evolved a suite of adaptive responses, known as shade avoidance syndrome (SAS), including stem and petiole elongation, hyponastic leaf growth, reduced leaf development, and ultimately accelerated senescence and earlier flowering (1, 2). While SAS helps some plants compete for light, it also compromises their structural integrity, immunity, and, ultimately, crop yield (3, 4). Because of the considerable ecological and economic impacts of SAS, understanding its underlying mechanisms and finding ways to modulate it are crucial and urgently needed.
Over the past few decades, substantial advancements have been made in identifying the various light signaling components involved in regulating SAS and in elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms, mostly derived from extensive molecular studies in Arabidopsis. Under shade conditions, the inactivated phytochrome B (phyB) allows nuclear accumulation of the dephosphorylated form of phytochrome-interacting factor 7 (PIF7) (5, 6), stabilizes PIF4 and PIF5 (7), and enables their binding to and activation of downstream targets, predominantly auxin biosynthetic genes and cell wall–associated genes that promote stem elongation. Besides auxin, gibberellins (GAs) (8, 9) and brassinosteroids (BRs) (10, 11) are also involved in stem elongation. These mechanism studies provide effective targets for genetic engineering and breeding of lodging-resistant, high-yield cultivars suited for high-density planting. For example, overexpression of PHYA improves shade tolerance in turf grass (12, 13). However, the overexpression of PHYA in tomato leads to darker fruit pigmentation that can also alter fruit quality characteristics (12, 14). Thus, ideal targets for the attenuation of SAS in crop species would be those that have minimal pleiotropic effects (15).
Plant chemical biology may address these challenges using bioactive chemicals to modulate biological processes in a highly controlled manner, with precise regulation of dose and treatment duration. Shade responses vary depending on the organ, the stage of development, and/or the species (16). Small organic molecules can be applied to any plant tissue at appropriate concentrations at various stages of the plant life cycle and provide the advantage of circumventing complications associated with gene redundancy and lethal mutations (17). In rice, GA biosynthesis inhibitors have a potential to dampen SAS and form compact seedlings (18). A chemical, 3-bromo-7-nitroindazole, functions as an inhibitor of blue light signaling by direct binding to cryptochrome 1 (CRY1) (19). Over the past decade, emerging analogs, agonists, and inhibitors of plant hormone biosynthesis/signaling, pathogenesis, cell wall biosynthesis, endomembrane trafficking, and so on have been identified. The application of these compounds in basic research and agricultural practice has been well documented (20). Therefore, using a phenotype or assay-based chemical biology approach to identify small molecules that regulate SAS is certainly a promising strategy.
In the current study, we performed a phenotype-based screening with a MedChemExpress (MCE) chemical library comprising 880 natural compounds and drug-like compounds. The small-molecule pifithrin-α (PFT-α) was identified as an inhibitor of shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and gene expression. PFT-α was previously recognized as a specific p53 inhibitor using p53-dependent LacZ/β-galactosidase reporter assay from screening of chemical library (21). PFT-α has been reported to protect mice from the harmful effects of gamma irradiation, to protect mouse neurons from death, and to enhance the recovery of subventricular zone of mice after brain stroke (22, 23). The exact mechanism of how PFT-α suppresses p53 activity remains elusive.
Here, we demonstrated that PFT-α exhibits ethylene-like bioactivities. We proposed that PFT-α directly interferes with the ethylene receptor ethylene response 1 (ETR1), as evidenced by the effects of ethylene biosynthesis inhibitors on PFT-α responses, ethylene production levels, PFT-α’s response in ethylene signaling mutants, and transcriptional profiling following PFT-α treatment. The directly interaction between PFT-α and ETR1 was validated through nanodifferential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays. Furthermore, we found that PFT-α repressed PIF4/7 expressions though accumulated ethylene-insensitive 3 (EIN3). Given that shade increases ethylene emission, this negative feedback was involved in deep shade–induced hypocotyl elongation. We confirmed the effect of PFT-α during ripening, senescence, and stress resistance. The discovery of PFT-α elucidates the molecular mechanisms by which ethylene disrupts the shade signaling pathway, explores the internal negative feedback loop that ensures the appropriate SAS, and also provides a set of potential candidates for controlling the SAS through exogenous application.

## RESULTS

## PFT-α represses shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and PIL1 expression
To identify potential plant growth regulators affecting the shade avoidance response, we performed a chemical screen (using a building block containing 880 chemicals from MCE) by observing the hypocotyl elongation and luciferase (LUC) expression of pPIL1::LUC transgenic lines under shade conditions (Fig. 1A). From this, 68 compounds were identified that affected hypocotyl elongation under shade conditions, with 19 promoting growth and 49 inhibiting growth in the first-round screening (fig. S1, A and B). Among these, 37 compounds inhibited both hypocotyl elongation and PIL1 expression (Fig. 1B). We chose seven compounds for further verification (fig. S1C) and confirmed that the compound PFT-α effectively inhibited hypocotyl elongation in a dosage-dependent manner (Fig. 1, C and D). The repression of PFT-α on hypocotyl elongation was reflected in the reduced elongation of epidermal cells (Fig. 1E). Using a seedling phenotyping platform (DynaPlant), we found that PFT-α mainly influenced phase 4 of hypocotyl elongation in response to shade (Fig. 1F). Given the effect of PFT-α on firefly luciferase activity (24), we also used reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and a pPIL1::GUS transgenic line to confirm the repression of PFT-α on the expression of PIL1 (Fig. 1, G and H).

It has been reported that PFT-α is unstable in tissue culture medium and is rapidly converted to its condensation product PFT-β (fig. S1D) (25). Under our experimental conditions, PFT-β showed weak repression of hypocotyl elongation and did not enhance inhibition with increased dosage (fig. S1E). We also obtained four other chemicals that partially share common 4-methylacetophenone or 2-aminobenzothiazoles structure with PFT-α designated as analogs C1 to C5 and evaluated their bioactivities (fig. S1, D to F). We observed no phenotypic changes in seedlings treated by analogs C1 and C5. C2 and C3 displayed weak bioactivity. So far, PFT-α remains the most promising candidate for influencing the shade response and warrants further mechanistic investigation.

## PFT-α induces an ethylene-like response without affecting ethylene production
Unexpectedly, seedlings treated with PFT-α exhibited ethylene-like phenotypes, including reduced hypocotyl elongation and exaggerated curvature of the apical hook under dark conditions (Fig. 2A and fig. S2A). Similar to PFT-α, the ethylene precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) also reduced the shade-induced hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 2B), which is consistent with previous reports (26, 27). In addition, PFT-α and ACC showed similar inhibitory effects on hypocotyl elongation induced by high ambient temperatures (fig. S2B) (28).

We wondered whether PFT-α promoted ethylene biosynthesis. In higher plants, ethylene is synthesized from methionine → S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) → ACC → ethylene (29, 30). Ethylene production can be inhibited by chemicals that block synthesis and/or activity of ACC synthase (ACS) or ACC oxidase (ACO) (Fig. 2C). α-Aminooxyacetic acid (AOA) inhibits ACS activity (31). Analogs of ACC, such as α-aminoisobutyric acid (AIB), are documented to inhibit ethylene formation by competitively targeting ACO (32). Pyrazinamide (PZA) is reported to suppress the activity of ACO (33). We found that neither AOA nor AIB/PZA could rescue the phenotype induced by PFT-α under dark conditions (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S2, C to E). As expected, AIB was able to reverse the phenotype induced by ACC due to its competitive binding to ACO. Moreover, we measured ethylene production in etiolated seedlings treated with PFT-α and ACC. Unlike ACC, PFT-α did not increase ethylene production (Fig. 2F). These results indicate that PFT-α induces ethylene-like responses not by promoting ethylene biosynthesis.

## PFT-α activity depends on the ethylene signaling pathway
We further investigated the effects of PFT-α treatment on known ethylene signaling mutants. Ethylene mutants including etr1-1, ctr1-1, ein2-5, and ein3eil1 showed resistance to PFT-α (Fig. 3, A and B, and fig. S3, A and B). However, unlike the ethylene mutants showing insensitivity to ACC, they still exhibited some response to PFT-α, suggesting that PFT-α may act partially through the ethylene signaling pathway. In addition, the application of ethylene perception inhibitors, such as AgNO3 and 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP), blocked the most of the effects of PFT-α on seedlings (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S3, C and D), indicating that PFT-α likely acts on or upstream of the ethylene receptors in the ethylene signaling network.

To determine whether PFT-α acts as a copper ion chelator to perturb ethylene signaling, we examined the effects of copper ions and copper ion chelator, bathocuproinedisulfonic acid (BCS), on PFT-α responses. The addition of BCS could not enhance ethylene-like response induced by PFT-α (fig. S3E), and excess Cu2+(CuSO4) did not reverse the effects of PFT-α on plants (fig. S3F). These results suggest that PFT-α may not affect copper ion concentration.
Previous studies have shown that the copper ion transporter responsive-to-antagonist 1 (RAN1) is involved in the biogenesis of ethylene receptors (34). In addition, antioxidant protein 1 (ATX1) and copper chaperone (CCH) are other copper ion chaperones essential for copper ion homeostasis in Arabidopsis (35). We observed that PFT-α–induced the ethylene-like response in ran1-1, atx1, cch, and atx1cch mutants, similar to those in columbia-0 (Col-0) (fig. S3G), suggesting that PFT-α does not affect copper ion transport.

## PFT-α triggers ethylene-responsive genes’ expression
To better understand the effects of PFT-α on plants, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to compare the genome-wide expression pattern of 4-day-old etiolated seedlings treated by PFT-α for 4 hours. With P < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 1.2 cutoff, 853 significantly differentially expressed genes were identified as PFT-α–regulated genes, in which “reponses to wounding” and “cellular response to hypoxia” terms were enriched (Fig. 3E and data S1 and S2). Taking advantage of webtool Plant Regulomics (http://bioinfo.cemps.ac.cn/plant-regulomics/) (36), we compared the genome-wide expression profiles of PFT-α–treated seedlings with those treated with various hormones including auxin, BRs, GA, ethylene (ETH), cytokinin (CK), jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), abscisic acid (ABA), and strigolactones (SLs). Among them, the most significant correlation was found between PFT-α and ethylene treatments (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, we identified genes that were commonly regulated by both PFT-α and ACC (Fig. 3G and data S3 and S4). The expression levels of these overlapped genes (458 genes) displayed a clear positive correlation (Fig. 3H), supporting the similar effects of PFT-α and ethylene/ACC on gene expression patterns.

## PFT-α interacts with ethylene receptor ETR1
Genetic and gene expression analyses led us to hypothesize that PFT-α might activate ethylene signaling through ethylene receptors. Members of the ethylene receptor family feature transmembrane helices at the N terminus, followed by a GAF (cGMP-specific phosphodiesterases, Adenylyl cyclases, and FhlA) domain, kinase domain, and receiver domain at the C terminus (Fig. 4A) (37). We predicted potential PFT-α binding pockets of full-length ETR1 protein via CASTpFOLD webtool (https://cfold.bme.uic.edu/castpfold/) (38), and the top five predicted pockets are located at the C terminus (data S5). Therefore, we purified Flag-ETR1115–738aa-Strep, Flag-ETR1156–738aa-Strep, and Flag-ETR1306–738aa-Strep from human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293F suspension cells to test the binding of PFT-α and ETR1 (fig. S4, A and B). We first performed nanoDSF to test the interaction between PFT-α and truncated ETR1 proteins. Compared to the analog C1, PFT-α affected the thermal stability of ETR1115–738aa (Tm = 52.7° ± 0.1°C and ΔTm = 0.7° ± 0.1°C), ETR1156–738aa (Tm = 50.0° ± 0.2°C and ΔTm = 1.0° ± 0.2°C), and ETR1306–738aa (Tm = 53.3° ± 0.1°C and ΔTm = 1.8° ± 0.1°C), indicating the direct binding to ETR1 (Fig. 4, B to D).

The molecular modeling reveals that PFT-α docked with ETR1407-589aa [Protein Data Bank (PDB) entry ID: 4PL9] as similar binding pose as ligand adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP) (Fig. 4E). The 2-aminobenzothiazoles group of PFT-α forms π-π stacking interaction with Phe474 and establishes a hydrogen bond with Asn470. Besides, the benzothiazoles ring structure participates in hydrophobic interactions with both Phe474 and Ile518. The 4-methylacetophenone group forms hydrophobic interactions with Leu548. On the basis of these docking results, five amino acids—Asn470, Phe474, Ile518, Ile526, and Leu548—were identified suitable candidates for mutation (fig. S4, B to D). Mutating these amino acids to Ala decreased the thermal shift induced by PFT-α, especially in Asn470Ala (Tm = 50.0° ± 0.1°C and ΔTm = 0.4° ± 0.1°C), Phe474Ala (Tm = 50.0° ± 0.2°C and ΔTm = 0.5° ± 0.2°C), and Ile526Ala (Tm = 50.0° ± 0.2°C and ΔTm = 0.5° ± 0.2°C), indicating weak binding between ETR1 mutants and PFT-α (Fig. 4, F to I, and fig. S4, E to G), and these residues are involved in the interaction between ETR1 and PFT-α.
To further confirm results of interaction, we used SPR to quantify the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) for the Flag-ETR1156–738aa-Strep and PFT-α complex. The dose-response curve yielded a KD of 7.85 μM for this interaction (Fig. 4J). Together, we conclude that PFT-α interacts with ethylene receptor ETR1.

## PFT-α represses the expression of PIF4/7 through accumulated EIN3
As we expected, the inhibition effect of PFT-α on shade-induced hypocotyl elongation was absent in ethylene signaling mutants (etr1-1, ctr1-1, ein2-5, and ein3eil1). In addition, we also found that the impact of PFT-α and ACC was reduced in pif7-1 and pif4pif7 mutants (Fig. 5A and fig. S5A). Similar to ACC, PFT-α promoted the accumulation of EIN3 (Fig. 5B and fig. S5B).

To figure out the role of PIFs in PFT-α–mediated inhibition of shade-induced hypocotyl elongation, we analyzed the expressions of PIF4 and PIF7 in PFT-α–treated seedlings under shade conditions. PFT-α repressed the expressions of PIF4 and PIF7, and this repression attenuated in ein3eil1 mutants (Fig. 5C). Several published RNA-seq data also showed that ethylene or ACC treatments inhibit PIF4 and PIF7 expressions under dark or white light conditions, while the expression levels of PIF4 and PIF7 were higher in the ein3eil1 mutant, supporting that EIN3 negatively regulates the transcriptions of PIF4 and PIF7 (fig. S5C) (39, 40). Then, taking advantage of native PIF4 antibody and pPIF7::PIF7-Myc/pif7-1 transgenic line, PFT-α and ACC markedly reduced the protein levels of PIF4 and PIF7 (Fig. 5, D and E, and fig. S5, D and E). The reduction of PIF4 was less pronounced in ein3eil1 mutants (Fig. 5D and fig. S5D). EIN3 binding motif and EIN3 repress motif (41) were present in the promoters of PIF4 and PIF7 (fig. S5F), and EIN3 binding peaks were detected in the promoters of PIF4 and PIF7 by chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing assay (GSE220957), indicating direct regulation of EIN3 on the expression of PIF4/7.
Moreover, the expressions levels of PIL1, HFR1, and YUCCA8, the downstream genes of PIFs, decreased in PFT-α–treated Col-0 plants. In addition, this effect was fainted in ein3eil1 and pif4pif7 mutants (Fig. 5F). These results suggested that PFT-α repressed shade-induced hypocotyl elongation through the EIN3-mediated regulation on the expressions of PIF4/7.
The molecular mechanism of PFT-α found that ethylene functions as a negative regulator of hypocotyl elongation under shade condition. It has been reported that ethylene emissions were elevated under deep shade conditions, with decreased photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) and lower red to far-red light ratio (R:FR) (42). We found that the ein3eil1 and ein2-5 mutants exhibit longer hypocotyls under deep shade conditions (fig. S5, G and H), suggesting that an endogenous ethylene burst limits excessive hypocotyl elongation. We propose that the extent of hypocotyl elongation is likely the outcome of the interplay between shade-induced accumulation of PIF protein and EIN3-mediated repression of PIF mRNA levels. This balance is mediated by hormone/hormone analogs, such as ACC, PFT-α, and environmental conditions, such as deep shade condition (Fig. 5G).

## PFT-α regulates various ethylene-induced physiological processes
We propose that PFT-α deactivates ETR1, suggesting that it functions similarly to an ethylene analog. Because of the high conservation of ETR1 (fig. S6, A and B), we applied PFT-α across different species. As we expected, PFT-α inhibited the growth of shade-induced stem elongation in the tomato cultivar Ailsa Craig (fig. S6C). PFT-α promotes leaf senescence, leading to yellowing of detached Arabidopsis leaves (fig. S6D). Spraying PFT-α or ACC on Prunus salicina cultivars triggers changes in fruit color, and 1-MCP can relieve the function of PFT-α (fig. S6E). Ethylene also aids plants in coping with biotic and abiotic stresses (43, 44). We observed that application of PFT-α does increase the survival rates of Arabidopsis under 200 mM NaCl (fig. S6F). These results indicated that PFT-α could be harnessed to manipulate ethylene responses to improve crop productivity, postharvest handling, and increase stress tolerance in agriculture.

## DISCUSSION
SAS affects numerous agronomic traits including reduced grain yield and plant biomass (3). Previous studies on the molecular mechanisms of SAS have provided potential targets for genetic breeding aimed at improving yield under high-density planting conditions. In this study, we used a phenotype-based screening approach with a chemical library containing 880 diverse generic drugs. This screening led to the identification of PFT-α, a small compound that regulates hypocotyl elongation and gene expressions under shade conditions.
PFT-α was initially identified as a specific inhibitor of the tumor suppressor protein p53-mediated gene activation and apoptosis through an unknown directly binding targets (21). It has been effectively used both in vitro and in vivo to protect normal cells from lethal genotoxic stress caused by gamma radiation and chemotherapy, suggesting its potential clinical use to mitigate side effects associated with antitumor therapies (45). PFT-α has also been shown to be a potent agonist of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), evidenced by its ability to bind the AhR, induce the formation of its DNA binding complex, and up-regulate the classic AhR target gene CYP1A1 (46). However, there is no AhR homologous gene in plants based on amino acid BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool). Here, we found that PFT-α directly binds to the ethylene receptor ETR1. Because of the structure of full ETR1 has remained elusive (47), much of our understanding of PFT-α’s binding to receptors comes from biochemical analyses and structure prediction (Fig. 4). Docking simulations suggested that PFT-α binds to sites similar to ADP (fig. S4D), indicating that PFT-α might interfere with ADP binding, thereby affecting autophosphorylation or interaction with other downstream components. Through sequence alignment and on the basis of structural similarity (PDB entry ID: 4PL9), the C terminus of ETR1 may have similarities with the human pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isozyme family and the adenosine triphosphatase domain of heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) chaperone/DNA topoisomerase II. Further verification of these interactions in animal systems would be valuable. Our findings in Arabidopsis could help identify targets of PFT-α and reveal potential mechanisms for its application in human therapy.
Although we showed that PFT-α exhibits ethylene-like behaviors, we believe that it may interact with multiple targets and participate in additional signaling pathways. It has been reported that PIF3 has a p53-like transcription activation domain (AD) characterized by a hydrophobic activator motif flanked by acidic residues (48). In addition, PIF7 also contains a p53-like AD region. There is the possibility that PFT-α inhibits p53-like AD of PIF7 through other unknown targets. It is also possible that PFT-α influences other transcription factors containing p53 AD. The gene expression profiles induced by PFT-α and ACC treatment do not completely overlap (Fig. 3G), suggesting that PFT-α may have additional targets beyond ETR1.
We found that PFT-α regulates the transcription levels of PIF4/7 through EIN3, thereby inhibiting hypocotyl elongation (Fig. 5). It has been reported that EIN3 and PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5 have a synergistic or transcriptional regulatory relationship in the development of apical hooks and chloroplasts (49). In addition, EIN3 directly interacts with the PIF3 protein and binds to the promoter of the light-harvesting protein family gene (40). Under white light conditions, EIN3 promotes hypocotyl elongation by activating the expression of PIF3 (50). Under high-temperature conditions, EIN3 overexpression can inhibit PIF4-mediated hypocotyl elongation (28). These results indicate that EIN3 regulates the activity of different PIFs through diverse mechanisms, including transcriptional regulation, protein interactions, and possibly others, depending on environmental cues and developmental stages.
Our studies demonstrate that PFT-α directly binds to the ethylene receptor ETR1, leading to the accumulation of EIN3 and disruption of PIF functions. This attenuates shade-induced hypocotyl elongation, highlighting ethylene as a negative regulator of this process. Shade conditions have been reported to enhance ethylene biosynthesis across various species (51)[e.g. barley (Hordeum vulgare) (52), Helianthus annuus (53), and Sorghum bicolor (54)], probably due to the up-regulation of ACS4/8/9 through the stabilization of PIFs (55). Moreover, ethylene production is elevated with increased planting density, particularly under deep shade or canopy shade conditions (56–58). In crops such as Pisum sativum and Brassica napus, shade- or phyAphyB- triggered ethylene release rate (level of nanomoles per gram per hour) is higher than that in Arabidopsis (level of picomoles per gram per hour) (59, 60), suggesting that high-density planting of those crops may elevate ethylene levels to an inhibitory threshold. This threshold could modulate the expressions of PIF4/7, forming a negative feedback loop where ethylene acts as an endogenous signal to constrain excessive hypocotyl growth under shade conditions.
PFT-α can be used as an ethylene analog in laboratory and for other proposes to affect ethylene-related developmental processes (fig. S6). Our data also indicate that PFT-α attenuates hypocotyl thermomorphogenesis (fig. S2B). Compared to the strong inhibitory effect of ACC on root elongation, PFT-α shows a milder effect and confers greater salt resistance (fig. S6F). This is advantageous for maintaining root growth and improving tolerance to abiotic stress under agriculture conditions. However, PFT-α is less stable than ACC due to its rapid cyclization at room temperature (25). Thus, structure-activity relationship analysis and chemical optimization are needed to improve its stability without compromising its pharmacophore. Our results suggest that the 4-methylacetophenone group is involved in PFT-α function (fig. S1F), indicating that future modifications should retain this moiety. Overall, PFT-α is a promising lead compound for improving crop resilience under challenging environmental conditions.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant material and growth conditions
All Arabidopsis thaliana plants used in this study were of the Columbia-0 ecotype. The mutants related to the ethylene signaling pathway (etr1-1, ctr1-1, ein3-1eil1-1, and ein2-5) and copper chaperone mutants (ran1-1, atx1, cch, and atx1cch) were obtained from C.-K. Wen’ s laboratory. The pif4 (SALK_140393C), pif7-1, pif4pif7, pPIL1::LUC, and pPIL1::GUS mutants were described previously (5, 61). The promoters (2500–base pair upstream of the PIF7 start codon) and genomic DNA of PIF7 were amplified by PCR and cloned into p1302-Myc vectors to created pPIF7::PIF7-Myc/pif7-1.
For phenotypic analysis under shade condition, the plates were incubated in growth chambers under continuous white light conditions (red, ~20 μmol m−2 s−1; blue, ~20 μmol m−2 s−1; and far-red, ~5 μmol m−2 s−1) for 4 days at 22°C. The plates were either left under white light condition or transferred to shade conditions (red, ~20 μmol m−2 s−1; blue, ~20 μmol m−2 s−1; and far-red, ~60 μmol m−2 s−1) for 4 days. Unless otherwise mentioned, the “shade” mentioned in this article refers to this conditions. In the deep shade experiment, the plates were transferred to deep shade conditions (red, ~10 μmol m−2 s−1; blue, ~5 μmol m−2 s−1; and far-red, ~60 μmol m−2 s−1) for 6 days. For phenotypic analysis under dark conditions, the plates were incubated under white light conditions for 6 hours and then turned to dark conditions at 22°C for 4 days. For phenotypic analysis under high ambient temperature, the plates were incubated in growth chambers under continuous white light for 4 days at 22°C. The plates were either left in 22°C or transferred to 29°C for another 3 days. For measurement of dynamic hypocotyl growth rate by DynaPlant platform (Microlens Technologies), we referred to methods in the literature (62). After growth for 4 days under continuous light-emitting diode white light, the plates were transferred to 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 2% phytagel complemented with PFT-α or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for another 1 day, and then the light conditions was automatically switched to shade at the predefined time point. Images of individual hypocotyls were captured by a macrolens module every 15 min during the task.

## Plant chemical genetics screenings
The MCE 880 compounds library was used for screening regulator of shade avoidance in Arabidopsis. Three-day-old pPIL1::LUC seedlings grown in 1/2 MS medium under continuous white light were transferred to 96-well white plates (Sangon, China) containing 200 μl of 1/2 MS liquid medium with 10 μM MCE compounds or 1% DMSO and then turned to shade conditions for another 5 days. Subsequently, d-luciferin (GoldBio, China) was added to the medium and incubated under white light overnight. PIL1 promoter–driven luciferase activity was firstly detected by Cytation3 Plate Reader and Imager (BioTek, USA) under white light. The plates were turned to shade light conditions for 2 hours, and the luciferase activity under shade conditions was detected. Response values for PIL1 expression were obtained by dividing data from shade light by data from white light. The elongation of the hypocotyl was used to calculate the elongation ratio: [(compound − mock) / mock] × 100%.

## Chemical treatments
For inhibition of ethylene biosynthesis, 1 mM AIB (Macklin, China), 100 μM AOA hemihydrochloride (MCE, China), and 50 μM PZA (TargetMol, China) were added to medium referring to literatures (33, 63–65). For inhibition of ethylene receptor, 1-MCP [60 parts per million (ppm); Aladdin, China], 1 and 50 μM AgNO3 were added to medium. For chelated copper ion, 500 μM BCS (Shifeng Biology, China) was added to medium.

## Measurement of ethylene production
About 50 three-day-old etiolated seedlings of Col-0 were sown in 4-ml vials (CNW, China) containing 1/2 MS medium supplemented with indicated concentration of chemical. The vial was sealed with a rubber stopper for 24 hours at 22°C before ethylene sampling and then injected into a gas chromatograph (6890NGC, Agilent, USA) (66).

## RNA-seq analysis
Four-day-old dark-grown seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS liquid medium complemented with 30 μM PFT-α, 10 μM ACC, or 1 per mil (‰) DMSO (v/v) for 4 hours, and whole seedlings were collected. We sent 0.1 to 0.2 g of fresh plant material to Majorbio (www.majorbio.com/) to perform standard RNA-seq library preparation and construction. To identify differential expression genes (DEGs) between two different samples, the expression level of each gene was calculated according to the transcripts per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads method. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (67), and DEGs with fold change ≥ 1.2 and P ≤ 0.05 were considered to be significantly differentially expressed genes.
Gene Ontology enrichment analysis and comparison with several hormones were performed by the website Plant Regulomics (http://bioinfo.cemps.ac.cn/plant-regulomics/) (36). The intersection between two sets of genes was determined using Venny2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) and the P-value was computed by Fisher’s exact test, which was used to examine the significance of association.

## Recombinant protein expression and purification
ETR1 truncated forms and mutated forms were cloned from Arabidopsis cDNA by primers mentioned in table S1. The cDNA of ETR1 (residues 115 to 738, 156 to 738, and 306 to 738) was cloned into the pCAG vector with an N-terminal FLAG tag and a C-terminal Strep-tag II. ETR1115–738aa, ETR1156–738aa, and ETR1306–738aa, and all of mutated ETR1156–738aa were overexpressed in HEK293F suspension cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
To obtain a series of truncated ETR1 proteins, the HEK293F suspension cells (Invitrogen) were transiently transfected with the expression plasmids and polyethyleneimines (PEIs; Polysciences). Approximately 1 mg of plasmids were premixed with 3 mg of PEIs in 50 ml of medium for 16 min. Then, 50 ml of mixture was added to 1 liter of cell culture and incubated at 37°C for 12 hours. Ten millimolars of sodium butyrate was added to the culture, and the culture was incubated at 37°C for 48 hours before harvest. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 4000g for 10 min at 4°C and resuspended in lysis buffer [25 mM tris (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktails]. The cell resuspension solution was sonicated on ice, followed by harvesting by centrifugation at 13,000g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected and applied to anti-Flag M2 affinity resin (Sigma-Aldrich). The resin was rinsed with wash buffer plus 10 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate. The protein was eluted with wash buffer plus 200 μM Flag peptide. Protein samples were loaded onto a gel filtration column (Superdex 200 increase, Cytiva), equilibrated with size exclusion chromatography buffer containing 25 mM tris (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol. Fractions of the main peak containing pure protein were analyzed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), pooled, and concentrated.

## Nanodifferential scanning fluorimetry
Measurements of ETR1 truncated proteins binding with PFT-α was performed using Prometheus NT.48 (NanoTemper, Germany). ETR1 proteins purified in vitro was mixed with PFT-α and analog C1 for 15 min at room temperature. Intrinsic fluorescence at 330 and 380 nm were detected from 25° to 90°C at the speed of 3°C min−1. The data were retrieved and analyzed with the PR.ThermControl software. Three technical replicates per sample were used for nanoDSF assays.

## Surface plasmon resonance
SPR experiments were performed using a Biacore T200 instrument (GE Healthcare) by MCE (China). The protein ETR1156–738aa-Flag-Strep was coupled onto a CM5 chip (BR-1005-30, Cytiva). After incubation, PFT-α was injected at increasing concentrations of 0.078125, 0.3125, 0.625, 1.25, 5, and 10 μM to the protein-CM5 system. Processed data were fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir interaction model using the Biacore T200 control software (v.2.0, GE Healthcare).

## Molecular docking simulations
Docking simulation was performed using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE). The structure of the catalytic domain of ETR1 from A. thaliana was obtained from PDB entry ID: 4PL9 (resolution, 1.90 Å). The structure of PFT-α was prepared by wash module and conformational search before submitting to the docking simulation. The noncovalent interaction between ETR1407–589aa and PFT-α was predicted by Proteins PLUS (https://proteins.plus/).

## Quantitative real-time RT-PCR analysis
In Figs. 1G and 5 (C and F), seedlings grown for 8 days with white light were transferred to indicated chemicals plates for another 2 days and then transferred to shade conditions or remained in white light for 1 hour (Fig. 1G) and 4 hours (Fig. 5). Approximately 100 mg of seedlings grown on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 1% agar under different light conditions were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground to a fine powder. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Two micrograms of total RNA were used for reverse transcription using a First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Sharebio, China). The cDNAs were then subjected to real-time qPCR using the CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA) and SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Sharebio, China). Three biological replicates per sample were used for RT-qPCR analysis. The level of AT2G39960 mRNA was detected in parallel and used for normalization. Primer sequences are provided in table S1.

## Immunoblotting analysis
For extraction of total proteins, seedlings were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and boiled at 95°C for 10 min in 2× SDS loading buffer. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, the super-natant containing total proteins was separated on 8% SDS-PAGE gels and immunoblotted with anti-EIN3 (AS194273, Agrisera), anti-PIF4 (R2534-4, Abiocode) antibodies, anti-Myc (GNI4110-MC, GNI) and anti–β-tubulin (M30109M, Abmart). Three biological replicates per sample were used for immunoblotting analysis.

## Statistical analyses
The significant differences were calculated by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test as indicated in figure legends. GraphPad Prism was used for the statistical analysis. Error bars indicate SEM in DynaPlant analysis, RT-qPCR, and Western blot assay. Error bars indicate SD in phenotypic data and nanoDSF assay.
