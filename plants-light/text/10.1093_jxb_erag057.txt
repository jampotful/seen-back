Red-light-responsive metabolome reveals the crucial role of phytochrome A- and B-dependent secondary metabolism in Arabidopsis seed germination.
Li C, Zhu Y, Zhang D, Qian W, Ma S, Tang H, Yao H. (2026). Journal of experimental botany. . doi:10.1093/jxb/erag057. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Red-light-responsive metabolome reveals the crucial role of phytochrome A- and B-dependent secondary metabolism in Arabidopsis seed germination

## Abstract
AbstractPhytochrome A (phyA) and phyB are red and far-red photoreceptors that play essential roles in modulating seed germination. Despite extensive research on the roles of hormones and light signaling, the metabolic characteristics of phytochromes during red-light-mediated seed germination remain largely unknown. Using high-throughput non-targeted metabolomics, this study profiled Arabidopsis seeds including the wild-type (Col-0), single mutants (phyA-211 and phyB-9), the double mutant phyAB, and the pifq quadruple mutant during red-light-induced germination, and identified a total of 164 metabolites linked to primary and secondary metabolism. Comparative analysis of the metabolome revealed that the levels of flavonols including quercetin, kaempferol, and relevant glycosides were increased in phyA-211, decreased in Col-0 and phyB-9, and exhibited less change in phyAB, demonstrating that phyA and phyB antagonistically regulate flavonoid biosynthesis. Flavonoid biosynthesis was up-regulated in pifq under red light. Exogenous quercetin and kaempferol treatment modestly inhibited Col-0 and pifq seed germination. Taken together, these results reveal the comprehensive metabolic networks, highlight flavonoid metabolism under red light, and elucidate the synergistic and antagonistic roles of phyA and phyB in regulating seed metabolism. This study provides novel insight into the functional mechanisms of flavonoid metabolism and light signaling in plant development.

## Introduction
Seed germination is a critical initial stage in the plant life cycle, marking the transition from a quiescent to a metabolically active state (Chandrasekaran et al., 2022). The process involves several key stages, including seed imbibition, enzyme activation, respiration, and the formation of the root and shoot, all of which are driven by coordinated metabolic activities (Fait et al., 2006; Allen et al., 2010; Silva et al., 2017; Kozuka et al., 2020; Farooq et al., 2022). Among the environmental cues that regulate seed germination, light is one of the most significant factors (Sullivan and Deng, 2003; Qi et al., 2022). Light influences various aspects of plant physiology, such as photomorphogenesis, photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, and other biochemical processes, through its intensity, quality, and duration. Higher plants rely on photoreceptors to perceive environmental light signals, including intensity, quality, direction, and photoperiod. These signals are then integrated to regulate multiple stages of plant growth and development, with seed germination being a key process directly governed by light-mediated signaling (Kami et al., 2010).
In Arabidopsis, the phytochrome family comprises five members (phyA–phyE), of which phyA and phyB are essential for red/far-red light signaling, whereas phyC–phyE act cooperatively with phyB to enhance and broaden its functions (Franklin and Quail, 2010; Hu and Lagarias, 2025). Phytochromes exist in two photoconvertible forms: the inactive red-light-absorbing form (Pr) and the active far-red-light-absorbing form (Pfr). This photoconversion enables plants to monitor changes in the red to far-red light ratio. phyA reaches its highest abundance in the dark but undergoes rapid degradation upon illumination, while phyB is predominant under light conditions (Li et al., 2011). In plant light signaling, a subset of basic helix−loop−helix (bHLH) transcription factors called phytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs) function downstream of the phytochromes. Upon red light irradiation, active phytochromes translocate to the nucleus and interact with PIFs, which induces the degradation of PIFs (Leivar et al., 2008). As negative regulators of photomorphogenesis, PIFs redundantly control key developmental processes, including light-dependent seed germination, chloroplast development, and hypocotyl elongation (Huq et al., 2004; Oh et al., 2004; Leivar et al., 2008; Shin et al., 2009). Among the PIF family, PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5 have been the primary focus of extensive research. The pif1 pif3 pif4 pif5 (pifq) quadruple mutant displays constitutive photomorphogenesis even in darkness, making it a valuable tool for understanding the role of PIFs in light signaling (Shin et al., 2009). Notably, the negative roles of PIF1 in germination are partly attributed to their regulation of the metabolism of abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellin (GA) in a phytochrome-dependent manner (Oh et al., 2007).
Light signaling plays diverse roles in regulating plant metabolism. Previous studies demonstrated that phytochrome depletion altered core metabolism and led to the accumulation of tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) intermediates, sugars, and amino acids (Yang et al., 2016). Targeted metabolite profiling has shown that the metabolite pools of Arabidopsis seeds are distinct under continuous far-red and red light treatments (Ghassemian et al., 2006). In Arabidopsis leaves, phyB-9 mutants showed reduced starch accumulation under red light irradiation, implying that phyB acts as the critical positive regulator of starch biosynthesis in response to red light (Shi et al., 2024). The metabolite and gene expression profiling of wild-type and phyA-211 seedlings grown under far-red light has revealed that phyA plays a role in regulating polyamine biosynthesis (Jumtee et al., 2008). In addition, metabolomics analysis of wild-type and phyAB seeds in response to far-red and red light also showed that the levels of primary and secondary metabolites altered tremendously during de-etiolation, marked by a phytochrome-dependent reduction in sugar levels (Kozuka et al., 2020). However, the differential regulation of primary and secondary metabolism by phyA and phyB during red-light-induced seed germination remains largely unknown.
Flavonoids, a group of polyphenolic compounds including flavanols, anthocyanidins, flavanones, flavonols, isoflavones, flavones, and chalcones, are important secondary metabolites involved in seed development and germination (Rajjou and Debeaujon, 2008; Shen et al., 2022). Flavonoid biosynthesis is tightly regulated by environmental signals, including high light and nitrogen deficiency, which converge on the transcriptional control of biosynthetic gene expression by MYB−bHLH−WDR (MBW) complexes (Xu et al., 2015). Furthermore, plant hormones are involved in these regulatory processes: ABA and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) inhibit seed germination and activate flavonoid biosynthesis, whereas GA promotes germination and represses flavonoid biosynthesis (Loreti et al., 2008; Lewis et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020b). Previous studies have shown that the mutation of enzyme-encoding genes in flavonoid biosynthesis such as chalcone synthase (CHS) lead to low levels of proanthocyanidins and reduced permeability in the seed coat, which are associated with low dormancy and fast germination (Debeaujon et al., 2000; MacGregor et al., 2015). Moreover, the application of exogenous proanthocyanidins delayed seed germination by promoting de novo ABA biosynthesis in Arabidopsis, whereas proanthocyanidin-deficient mutant seeds exhibited lower ABA levels compared with wild-type seeds during germination (Jia et al., 2012). Flavonoids were also reported to regulate ABA signaling through their antioxidant activity in countering ABA-dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, and were involved in modulating IAA transport and signaling (Santelia et al., 2008; Watkins et al., 2014; Kuhn et al., 2016). However, the specific roles of flavonoids in red-light-induced seed germination remain poorly understood.
This study aims to provide an integrated view of the metabolic responses in both wild-type Arabidopsis and well-characterized phyA/phyB mutants during red-light-mediated seed germination. Using a high-throughput untargeted metabolomics approach, we characterized the red-light-responsive metabolome of wild-type (Col-0), phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq mutants. By identifying differential metabolites of wild-type and mutant seeds under red light irradiation, we revealed the comprehensive metabolic networks and the potentially antagonistic role of phyA and phyB in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis, shedding light on the functional mechanism of flavonoid metabolism in seed germination.

## Materials and methods

## Plant materials and seed germination
The phyA-211, phyB-9, the phyA-211 and phyB-9 double mutant (phyAB), and the quadruple mutant pifq have been described previously (Reed et al., 1993, 1994; Leivar et al., 2008; Su et al., 2015). All mutants were produced in the Col-0 background. Seeds of Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq were surface-sterilized, and then sown on plates containing 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium, 0.05% (w/v) MES, and 0.8% (w/v) agar (pH 5.8) within 1 h. The light treatments were adapted from previous studies (Shinomura et al., 1994; Song and Choi, 2019). Briefly, the seeds were subjected to two light treatments: a far-red light pulse (3.2 μmol m−2 s−1) for 5 min (FR, condition a), or the same far-red pulse followed by a red light pulse (20 μmol m−2 s−1) for another 5 min (FR/R, condition b). Far-red light was applied to inhibit germination by converting the active Pfr form of phytochromes into the inactive Pr form. Subsequent red light converted phytochromes back to the active Pfr form, thereby inducing seed germination. These seeds were incubated at 22 °C for 5 d in the dark. As a control, seeds were incubated at 22 °C for 5 d under continuous white light (WLc, 100 μmol m−2 s−1). For the exogenous flavonol treatment, specific amounts of kaempferol and quercetin were added to the MS medium to a final concentration of 100 μM, and all subsequent steps followed condition b. Germination percentage was measured after 5 d, and seeds with radicle protrusion were considered germinated. The samples from both condition a and b were then frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

## Sample preparation
Total metabolites from 50 mg samples were extracted with methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and methanol (MeOH), following a previous study with some modifications (Qian et al., 2023). Briefly, 50 mg of plant material was mixed with 225 μl of pre-cooled MeOH and homogenized three times. The homogenate was then sonicated in an ice bath for 10 min. Then, 750 μl of pre-cooled MTBE was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and then incubated on ice for 1 h. After the addition of 188 μl of deionized water, the samples were vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 14 000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. All steps were performed on ice. The lower phase (200 μl) was transferred to new tubes. The above extraction steps were repeated twice. The three extractions were combined and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. The dried extract was reconstituted in methanol:water (1:1, v/v) for metabolite analysis.

## UPLC-QTOF/MS-based metabolomics analysis
Non-targeted metabolomics was performed to analyze global metabolite profiles using LC-MS in both positive and negative ion modes (Supplementary Fig. S1). Briefly, the Shimadzu Nexera UHPLC system coupled to a 7600 QTOF mass spectrometer (AB Sciex) was used for untargeted metabolomics analysis. The 1 μl aliquots of sample were separated on a Waters Acquity UPLC T3 column (100 mm×2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 45 °C with gradient elution. Mobile phase A was deionized water with 5 mM ammonium acetate (NH4OAc) and 0.02% formic acid, and phase B was acetonitrile:water (95:5, v/v) with 5 mM NH4OAc and 0.02% formic acid. The flow rate was 0.3 ml min–1. The ion source parameters were set as follows: scan range, 50–1200 Da; source temperature, 300 °C; curtain gas, 35; collision gas, medium; two ion source gases, 60; ion spray voltage, 5500 V (positive ion mode)/–4500 V (negative ion mode); DP, 60; CE, 35±15.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time reverse transcription–PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from Arabidopsis seeds or seedlings using the RealPure®Plant Seed RNA Extraction Kit (Real-Times). Total RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time reverse transcription–PCR (RT–qPCR) was performed on an Applied Biosystems® QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The PCR conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Each reaction was performed in triplicate. The ACTIN2 and UBQ10 gene were used as reference genes. The primer sequences of the reference genes and the phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis pathway genes are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The expression levels of target genes across five genotypes, normalized to ACTIN2 and UBQ10 and calculated as the 2−ΔCT value, are summarized in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively. All data are presented as the mean±SEM.

## Data processing and statistical analysis
The raw metabolomics data were pre-processed by MetDNA2 (Metabolite identification and Dysregulated Network Analysis, http://metdna.zhulab.cn/), and metabolites were annotated according to the database (Zhou et al., 2022). The relative abundance of each metabolite was quantified based on its peak intensity. For RT–qPCR analysis, the mRNA levels of target genes were normalized to the reference genes ACTIN2 or UBQ10. The 2−ΔCT value under condition b was then divided by that under condition a to calculate 2−ΔΔCT, which represents the fold change in gene expression. Student’s t-test (normal distribution) or Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test (non-normal distribution) was used for differential analysis of metabolite abundance and relative expression of target genes, with a significance cut-off of P<0.05.
VENNY tool (version 2.1.0, http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) was used to compare differential metabolites of corresponding groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the metabolite profiles and metabolic function enrichment analysis were performed on the Metware Cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). Heatmap analysis was performed with the ‘pheatmap’ package in R (version 4.0.3). GraphPad Prism 10 was used to visualize metabolite abundance or gene expression changes across different groups.

## Results

## Global profile of metabolites in Arabidopsis seeds in response to red light
The experimental design incorporated five genotypes: Col-0 (wild-type) possessing functional phyA and phyB photoreceptors for red light perception; phyA-211, a phyA-deficient mutant with intact phyB; phyB-9, a phyB-deficient mutant retaining phyA activity; and phyAB, a dual photoreceptor-deficient mutant. Given that the pifq mutant displays constitutive photomorphogenesis even in darkness, it was included to confirm optimal germination conditions.
The seeds germinated when irradiated by far-red light for 5 min (FR) followed by red light for 5 min (FR/R), and germination percentage was ∼87, 53, and 28% in pifq, Col-0, and phyA-211, respectively; phyB-9 and phyAB seeds failed to germinate (Fig. 1A, B). Under FR treatment, seeds of Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, and phyAB did not germinate, whereas the pifq mutant achieved 83% germination (Fig. 1B). These differences in germination among Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, and phyAB seeds indicated that phyB was the major red light photoreceptor responsible for promoting seed germination under these experimental conditions, which was consistent with previous studies (Shinomura et al., 1994; Oh et al., 2004). In contrast, pifq seeds germinated irrespective of red or far-red light irradiation, consistent with the role of PIFs as key negative regulators of seed germination (Leivar et al., 2008). Non-targeted metabolomics was then performed to analyze global metabolite profiling. The MetDNA2 platform was subsequently used to annotate and quantify metabolites. This analysis identified 164 metabolites associated with primary and secondary metabolism, spanning eight distinct classes: 22 alkaloids, 25 amino acids and derivatives, 14 carbohydrates and derivatives, 10 lipids, 21 nucleotides and derivatives, 28 organic acids, 32 shikimates and phenylpropanoids, and 12 unclassified compounds (Fig. 1C). Complete annotations of 164 metabolites are provided in Supplementary Table S4. PCA demonstrated a clear separation between FR-treated and FR/R-treated samples across all genotypes, namely Col-0 (wild-type) and the mutants phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq (Fig. 1D). This distinct clustering suggests that red light induces significant metabolic reprogramming in Arabidopsis seeds.

## Differential metabolites in wild-type Arabidopsis seeds in response to red light
To depict the red light-responsive metabolome, differential analysis of metabolite abundance was performed in Col-0 seeds. A total of 103 metabolites exhibited significant alterations under FR/R conditions relative to FR conditions (P<0.05; the full dataset of fold change and P-value is available in Supplementary Table S5). Hierarchical clustering analysis further delineated the expression patterns of these differential metabolites, with prominent variations observed in six major classes: alkaloids, amino acids and derivatives, carbohydrates and derivatives, nucleotides and derivatives, organic acids, and shikimates and phenylpropanoids (Fig. 2). Five alkaloids exhibited pronounced accumulation under red light, with N8-acetylspermidine showing the most dramatic increase (27.9-fold), followed by agmatine, thiamine, 7-O-acetylsalutaridinol, and tyramine. Conversely, eight alkaloids were significantly down-regulated, including several indole-3-ethanol derivatives, among which indole-3-ethanol itself displayed the strongest reduction (3.6-fold).

Notably, red light induced significant up-regulation of free amino acids, including glutamate, histidine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, norleucine, phenylalanine, proline, and tryptophan, suggesting enhanced amino acid biosynthesis. In addition, two dipeptides, glutamyl-phenylalanine and glycyl-leucine, exhibited a marked accumulation under red light conditions. Similarly, levels of adenosine, cytidine, guanosine, and GMP also increased significantly. However, carbohydrates showed a divergent pattern: the levels of mannose and UDP-D-galactose increased, whereas those of sucrose, stachyose, and several phosphohexoses (e.g. fructose-6-phosphate) decreased. Among the 17 differentially regulated organic acids, hydroxypyruvate exhibited the most striking up-regulation (21-fold).
Importantly, as many as 25 shikimates and phenylpropanoids were found to be differentially regulated, and the majority of them showed down-regulation in response to red light. Among them, flavonoids displayed coordinated suppression, including flavonols (kaempferol, quercetin, and rutin), relevant glycosides (afzelin, kaempferol 3-O-glucoside, and quercetin 3-O-glucoside), and derivatives (quercimeritrin and quercitrin). This systemic attenuation of flavonoid biosynthesis in Col-0 seeds implies a regulatory role for flavonoids in phytochrome-mediated germination in response to red light.

## Comparison of differential metabolites between wild-type and phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq mutant seeds
Shikimate pathway derivatives provide the carbon skeletons for the aromatic amino acids including tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine, which in turn serve as precursors for a wide array of aromatic secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, alkaloids, and lignins. To study the metabolic characteristics of phytochromes and PIF mutant seeds in response to red light, we conducted differential analysis of metabolite abundance in phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq mutants (FR/R versus FR, P<0.05; the full dataset of fold change and P-value is available in Supplementary Tables S6–S9). The number of differential metabolites revealed genotype-specific metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 3A). Among the metabolic classes, amino acids and nucleotides showed pronounced accumulation, whereas carbohydrates and phenylpropanoids displayed coordinated depletion in Col-0 seeds (Fig. 3B).

Under red light conditions, 72 metabolites accumulated significantly while only six metabolites were reduced in phyA-211 seeds. In contrast, 86 metabolites were markedly depleted and 21 metabolites accumulated in phyB-9 seeds (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, phyA-211 and phyB-9 mutants displayed divergent metabolic profiles for organic acids, nucleotides, phenylpropanoids, and alkaloids. Notably, key secondary metabolites including flavonoids demonstrated reciprocal regulation, the levels of which were enhanced in phyA-211 but were suppressed in phyB-9 (Fig. 3C, D). This diametrically opposed pattern implies that phyA and phyB may have differential regulatory roles in red-light-mediated metabolic reprogramming. However, phyAB mutants exhibited minimal metabolic changes and lacked a clear trend when compared with the single mutant phyA-211 or phyB-9 (Fig. 3A, E). This attenuated response could be attributed to the absence of functional phyA and phyB required for red light perception. Unexpectedly, 70 metabolites were significantly changed in the pifq mutant under red light, with the levels of amino acids, organic acids, nucleotides, phenylpropanoids, and alkaloids showing an increasing trend (Fig. 3A, F). Given that pifq mutants display constitutive photomorphogenesis in darkness, these metabolic shifts may involve light signaling components beyond canonical PIF1/3/4/5. These results demonstrated genotype-specific metabolic reprogramming in the wild type and phytochrome-deficient mutants in response to red light.

## KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites in response to red light
To further identify the metabolic pathways associated with differential metabolites across genotypes, we performed enrichment analysis using the KEGG pathway database with a significance threshold of P<0.05. The top 10 enriched metabolic pathways across five genotypes were identified (Fig. 4), spanning four functional categories, namely carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, organic acid metabolism, and secondary metabolism. For Col-0 seeds, significantly enriched primary metabolic pathways mainly included galactose metabolism, aromatic and branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism. The significantly enriched secondary metabolism mainly included phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis. As the primary metabolites including phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan are critical precursors for phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, the significant enrichment of these pathways may underlie the coordinated metabolic changes responsible for the reduced flavonoid pool in Col-0 seeds in response to red light. The phyA-211 and phyB-9 mutant seeds shared several enriched metabolic pathways, including arginine biosynthesis, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis, and valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, indicating photoreceptor-specific regulation of metabolic networks. In contrast to the other four genotypes, the phyAB mutant exhibited fewer enriched metabolic pathways and a lack of rewiring in aromatic amino acid biosynthesis, branched-chain amino acid degradation, or phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, which was consistent with its attenuated red-light-sensing capacity. Additionally, differential metabolites of pifq mutant seeds were mainly mapped to amino acid metabolism, organic acid metabolism, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. These results of enrichment pathway analysis revealed that phyA and phyB signaling modulated both primary and secondary metabolism in response to red light in seed germination.

## phyA and phyB orchestrated the metabolic activities of Arabidopsis seed germination in response to red light
Previous studies have shown that phyA and phyB signaling is essential for the light-mediated control of primary metabolism in Arabidopsis leaves (Han et al., 2017). In Arabidopsis, phyA and phyB orchestrate seedling de-etiolation through both synergistic and antagonistic crosstalk within multifactorial light signaling networks (Su et al., 2015). To delineate photoreceptor-specific metabolic regulation in Arabidopsis seeds, we performed comparative analysis of differential metabolites in single and double mutants to identify metabolic signatures unique to phyA and phyB. As indicated by the Venn diagrams, 19 metabolites exhibited unique accumulation and 74 metabolites showed unique reduction in phyB-9, suggesting potential phyA-regulated metabolites (Fig. 5A). In contrast, 57 metabolites were uniquely up-regulated and two metabolites were uniquely down-regulated in phyA-211 mutants, representing potential phyB-regulated metabolites (Fig. 5B). This asymmetric distribution of metabolic perturbations indicated that phyA and phyB might function differently in regulating metabolic pathways of Arabidopsis seeds in response to red light. Furthermore, we conducted systematic comparison of potential phyA- and phyB-regulated metabolic profiles and identified 18 shared (16 up-regulated and 2 down-regulated) and 23 mutually exclusive metabolites (Fig. 5C), representing the synergistic and antagonistic metabolic regulation by phyA and phyB, respectively. Among the 18 shared metabolites, six free amino acids (arginine, histidine, isoleucine, norleucine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) exhibited consistent up-regulation in both phyA-211 and phyB-9 mutants, suggesting that phyA and phyB synergistically activate amino acid biosynthesis in response to red light (Fig. 5D). Notably, 11 of 23 oppositely regulated metabolites comprised shikimate pathway derivatives (including chorismate and p-coumaric acid) and flavonoids (such as afzelin, quercetin, quercimeritrin, and rutin). All 11 metabolites were up-regulated in phyA-211 and down-regulated in phyB-9 mutants (Fig. 5E). The inverse patterns indicate that phyA and phyB play antagonistic roles in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in response to red light.

The comparative analysis of differential metabolic profiles between wild-type Col-0 and phyAB seeds identified 85 exclusive metabolites in Col-0 (Supplementary Fig. S2A), comprising 36 up-regulated and 49 down-regulated metabolites. To dissect the roles of phyA and phyB signaling in metabolic regulation during red-light-induced seed germination, we compared these uniquely altered metabolites of Col-0 with those showing synergistic or antagonistic regulation by phytochromes (Fig. 5D, E; Supplementary Fig. S2B, C). These analyses showed that 13 differential metabolites of Col-0 were synergistically regulated by both phyA and phyB, five of which were free amino acids (histidine, isoleucine, norleucine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Additionally, 18 differential metabolites of Col-0 were antagonistically regulated by phyA and phyB. Ten of these comprised shikimate pathway derivatives (including chorismate and p-coumaric acid) as well as flavonoids (such as afzelin, quercetin, quercimeritrin, and rutin) (Supplementary Fig. S2E). Together, these results demonstrated the dual roles of phyA and phyB signaling in regulating the metabolic responses of Arabidopsis seeds to red light: their synergism in promoting amino acid biosynthesis and their antagonism in modulating flavonoid biosynthesis.
Previous studies revealed that two metabolic modules, namely ornithine–asparagine–polyamine and shikimic acid–tyrosine–tryptamine–phenylalanine–flavonoid, were closely related to seed germination in rice, and treatment with exogenous shikimic acids partially promoted the germination process, which underscored the importance of the shikimate pathway and subsequent synthesis of aromatic acids and flavonoids in affecting seed germination (Guo et al., 2021). Similarly, our findings also highlighted the significant changes in metabolism of shikimate–aromatic acids–flavonoids during the red-light-induced seed germination of Arabidopsis (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E). As the shikimate pathway acts as a bridge between primary and secondary metabolism, we infer that the sophisticated metabolic networks may interact with phytochrome-mediated light signaling to induce germination.

## Integrated metabolic networks of Arabidopsis seed germination in response to red light
To gain a comprehensive profile of primary metabolism during red-light-mediated Arabidopsis seed germination, we constructed an integrated metabolic network of differential metabolites associated with carbohydrate, amino acid, and organic acid metabolism across wild-type and mutant seeds (Supplementary Fig. S3). Six carbohydrates and their derivatives (mannose, sorbitol, mannitol, stachyose, sucrose, and UDP-D-galactose) were annotated to fructose and mannose metabolism, whereas four metabolites (fructose 6-phosphate, citrate, cis-aconitate, and malate) were mapped to glycolysis and the TCA cycle. Furthermore, six free amino acids (tryptophan, histidine, isoleucine, phenylalanine, lysine, and methionine) were mapped to amino acid biosynthesis; and seven nucleotides and their derivatives (adenosine, guanosine, AMP, GMP, dADP, 3′,5′-cAMP, and 3′,5′-cGMP) were correlated with purine metabolism. In addition, eight metabolites (glutamate, proline, N-acetylglutamate, N-acetylornithine, arginine, agmatine, 4-guanidinobutanal, and 4-guanidinobutanoate) were involved in arginine and proline metabolism. Importantly, the integrated primary metabolism network revealed that amino acid metabolism changed significantly in both Col-0 and mutant seeds. In contrast to the synergistic regulation by phyA and phyB, PIFs showed limited roles in regulating amino acid biosynthesis, as multiple free amino acids (histidine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) were up-regulated in both Col-0 and pifq (Supplementary Fig. S3). These results indicated that primary metabolism was coordinately regulated by photoreceptor signaling during Arabidopsis seed germination in response to red light.
Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, initiating with the conversion of phenylalanine to cinnamic acid, serves as a central pathway for producing diverse aromatic compounds with dual structure and signaling functions (Corso et al., 2020). In our models of Arabidopsis seed germination, metabolites involved in aromatic acid metabolism and downstream phenylpropanoid biosynthesis showed significant alterations in response to red light (Fig. 6). Three metabolites (3-dehydroquinate, 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate, and chorismate), which were mapped to the shikimate pathway for aromatic acid biosynthesis, showed consistently decreased levels in both Col-0 and phyB-9 seeds. Aromatic acids such as tryptophan and phenylalanine accumulated in all genotypes except phyAB seeds. Six tryptophan metabolism-associated metabolites were identified, with glucobrassicin, indole-3-acetaldoxime, and indole-3-acetaldehyde showing significant reductions in both Col-0 and phyB-9 seeds. Therein, indole-3-acetaldoxime and indole-3-acetaldehyde serve as precursors for IAA biosynthesis, implying that IAA signaling may be involved in seed germination in response to red light. The level of p-coumarate, a pivotal intermediate in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, was directly correlated with alterations in downstream metabolism, including flavonoid biosynthesis. In line with this, we observed that p-coumarate and nine downstream metabolites involved in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis accumulated in phyA-211 seeds and decreased in Col-0 and phyB-9 seeds (Fig. 6). These genotype-dependent modulation patterns revealed the antagonistic regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis by phyA and phyB. Moreover, the upstream and downstream metabolites of flavonoid biosynthesis were specifically down-regulated in Col-0 compared with pifq (Fig. 6), indicating the negative role of PIFs in regulating these processes. Taken together, our integrated analysis of primary and secondary metabolism highlights the crucial roles of phyA/phyB/PIF signaling in regulating amino acid metabolism and flavonoid metabolism in the response of Arabidopsis seeds to red light.

## The gene expression levels of key metabolic enzymes in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis
To link the observed perturbations in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid metabolism (Fig. 7A) with transcriptional regulation under red light, we conducted RT–qPCR analysis of key biosynthetic enzymes. For all genotypes, after normalization using ACTIN2 as control, the relative mRNA levels of FR/R were compared with FR conditions to calculate the relative fold change (Fig. 7B). Our results demonstrated that in Col-0 seeds, the expression of major biosynthetic enzymes involved in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis was generally down-regulated, indicating that red light suppressed these metabolic pathways. In contrast, pifq seeds exhibited widespread up-regulation of genes encoding key biosynthetic enzymes, suggesting the repressive roles of PIFs in regulating phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis. The observed changes in metabolite levels (Fig. 6) were highly consistent with the altered expression of genes encoding relevant biosynthetic enzymes (Fig. 7B) in both Col-0 and pifq. This consistency between transcriptional and metabolic regulation underscores a tightly coordinated response to red light, further emphasizing the intricate interplay between light signaling and metabolic reprogramming during seed germination.

Phenylalanine ammonia lyases (PALs) are the first and rate-limiting enzymes of phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis (Wang et al., 2021). In Col-0 seeds, PAL2 expression was significantly reduced, while PAL1 and PAL3 expression showed a declining and an increasing trend in response to red light, respectively (Fig. 7B). Differential regulation of PALs was observed in photoreceptor mutants: phyB-9 seeds maintained stable expression, whereas phyA-211 and phyAB exhibited significant PAL1 up-regulation and PAL2 down-regulation. In contrast, pifq mutants showed enhanced PAL activation (Fig. 7B). The similarity of phyA-211 and phyAB suggests a limited role for phyB in regulating the expression of PALs. As core enzymatic components bridging phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, the genes encoding cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H) and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase 1 (4CL1) were down-regulated in Col-0 and up-regulated in pifq seeds. Furthermore, C4H was also down-regulated in phyA-211, phyB-9, and phyAB seeds (Fig. 7B).
At the initiation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, the expression of 4-coumarate 3-hydroxylase (C3H) and p-coumaroyl-CoA 3′-hydroxylase (C3′H) genes showed widespread down-regulation in Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, and phyAB seeds, and was up-regulated in pifq seeds (Fig. 7B). In contrast, the expression of the caffeoyl-CoA 3-O-methyltransferase 1 (CCoAOMT1) gene displayed a broadly increasing trend across all genotypes, which was most pronounced in the phyA-211 mutant (Fig. 7B). This pattern suggests the activation of specific branches within the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. CHS and chalcone isomerase (CHI), whose products catalyze the early steps of flavonoid biosynthesis, were coordinately suppressed in Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, and phyAB seeds (Fig. 7B). Subsequent hydroxylation steps mediated by flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H) and flavonoid 3′-hydroxylase (F3′H) mirrored this pattern. The flavonol synthase 1 (FLS1) gene, encoding the key enzyme in flavonol biosynthesis, showed significant down-regulation in Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, and phyAB seeds, and up-regulation in pifq mutants (Fig. 7B). The relative expression of these genes was less affected by the other reference gene UBQ10 (Supplementary Fig. S4A).
In addition, we conducted hierarchical clustering analysis of gene expression patterns in these five genotypes. The results showed that Col-0 and phyB-9 formed a cohesive cluster, whereas phyA-211 exhibited relatively transcriptional proximity to pifq mutants (Fig. 7C; Supplementary Fig. S4B), a pattern that corresponded to the metabolites changes (Fig. 6). The transcriptional proximity of phyA-211 and pifq mutants might be contributed by the expression of CCoAOMT1 and CHS (Fig. 7C). Hence, our RT−qPCR and metabolomics analysis of flavonoid biosynthesis demonstrated a highly coordinated transcript−metabolite regulatory network in response to red light. To further investigate the functional role of flavonols in seed germination, we treated Col-0 and pifq seeds with either quercetin or kaempferol, and calculated the germination percentage 5 d after red light irradiation. The results showed that quercetin or kaempferol partially decreased the germination percentage of Col-0 and pifq seeds (Supplementary Fig. S5), suggesting an inhibitory role for flavonols in seed germination in response to red light.

## Discussion
Emerging evidence underscores phytochrome-mediated regulation of starch metabolism and other metabolic networks in response to different light intensity or spectral content (Ghassemian et al., 2006; Jumtee et al., 2008; Han et al., 2017; Kozuka et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2024). However, the differential regulation of primary and secondary metabolism by phyA and phyB and the specific roles of flavonoids during red-light-induced seed germination remain largely unknown. In this study, we performed metabolomic analysis of Col-0, phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq seeds under red light. Our results depict the comprehensive metabolic networks, demonstrate the synergistic and antagonistic roles of phyA and phyB in regulating seed metabolism, and highlight a crucial role for flavonoid metabolism during red-light-induced seed germination.
Considerable changes in metabolite profiles occurred during the transition from seeds to red-light-induced seedlings. Comparative metabolomics revealed rapid depletion of sucrose and stachyose levels in Col-0 seedlings when compared with non-germinated seeds (Fig. 2). This catabolic shift aligns with established phytochrome regulation of sucrose metabolism (Jumtee et al., 2008; Krahmer et al., 2018; Kozuka et al., 2020). Exogenous sucrose delays seed germination and enhances PIF5 protein levels in a phyB-independent way (Stewart et al., 2011). In our study, the decreased sucrose levels in Col-0 seeds may facilitate phytochrome signaling by inhibiting PIF5 and promote seed germination in response to red light. In contrast to the reduction of sucrose and stachyose levels, amino acid biosynthesis exhibited activation in Col-0 seeds, evidenced by the accumulation of glutamate, histidine, isoleucine, lysine, phenylalanine, proline, and tryptophan (Fig. 2). Furthermore, several biosynthesis pathways such as those of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan were significantly enriched in these differential amino acids (Fig. 4). These findings revealed the roles of phytochromes in regulating carbon reserve mobilization and amino acid biosynthesis. In addition, the free amino acids and nucleosides which accumulate dramatically during germination might serve as essential precursors for de novo biosynthesis of proteins and nucleic acids, while simultaneously functioning as a nitrogen source for systemic metabolic coordination (Zheng, 2009; Yang et al., 2020). As a pivotal TCA cycle intermediate, malate showed a 4-fold increase in Col-0 seedlings, indicating the interplay between carbon and nitrogen metabolism. Taken together, these metabolic alterations of Col-0 represented a key feature of seed germination in response to red light. A similar profile of primary metabolites was also observed in the pifq mutant, characterized by higher levels of amino acids, TCA intermediates, and nucleotides, and lower levels of sucrose and stachyose. Compared with Col-0, the common up-regulation of free amino acids (histidine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, norleucine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) in the pifq mutant indicates that PIFs have limited roles in regulating amino acid biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. S3).
The specific roles of phytochromes in orchestrating red-light-responsive metabolism are poorly characterized. Upon sensing far-red light or red light, phyA and phyB orchestrate diverse biological processes including seed germination and concomitant metabolic changes (Oh et al., 2004, 2020; Jumtee et al., 2008; Krahmer et al., 2018; Kozuka et al., 2020). Consistent with this functional specialization, red-light-induced germination assays demonstrated a striking phenotypic divergence: Col-0 and phyA-211 seeds exhibited modest germination, whereas phyB-9 and phyAB seeds showed severe germination arrest due to disrupted phyB signaling (Fig. 1A). Metabolomic profiling of phyA-211 and phyB-9 seeds revealed distinct metabolic patterns (Fig. 3C, D), underscoring the differential roles of phyA and phyB in regulating metabolic activities in response to red light. In contrast, phyAB mutants exhibited limited metabolic changes (Fig. 3E), possibly due to the activities of residual phytochromes C, D, and E for sensing red light (Ádám et al., 2013). Our results showed that phyA and phyB synergistically regulate amino acid metabolism and antagonistically regulate flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 5D, E). This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that phyA and phyB act synergistically and antagonistically to regulate seedling de-etiolation across several light signaling pathways (Su et al., 2015). A similar pattern was observed in Col-0 seeds (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E), characterized by generally elevated amino acids and reduced flavonoids, suggesting the existence of both synergistic and antagonistic roles for phyA and phyB. Regarding the light-dependent interactions between phytochromes and PIFs, phyA binds PIF1 and PIF3, while phyB interacts with a broader set, ranging from PIF1 to PIF8 (Pham et al., 2018). Therefore, differences in downstream light signaling might partially explain the distinct roles of phyA and phyB in regulating seed metabolism. Moreover, pifq mutants displayed widespread metabolic activation under red light, as evidenced by increased levels of 58 metabolites including amino acids, nucleotides, organic acids, and flavonoids (Fig. 3A, F), indicating the involvement of PIF signaling in response to red light.
Combined metabolomics and RT−qPCR analysis of flavonoid biosynthesis indicated a coordinated transcript−metabolite regulation in response to red light. In Col-0 and phyB-9 seeds, metabolomics analysis showed decreased levels of upstream and downstream metabolites in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. Consistent with the metabolic changes, we observed the concerted down-regulation of key flavonoid biosynthetic enzyme genes (CHS, CHI, F3H, and F3'H) in Col-0 and phyB-9 seeds (Fig. 7B). In addition, RT−qPCR demonstrated that the rate-limiting enzyme PAL2 was down-regulated in Col-0 seeds (Fig. 7B). This aligns with a previous study in Catharanthus roseus seeds, in which light treatment induced reduced levels of flavonols and down-regulation of PAL gene transcript levels during the apical hook opening phase (Yu et al., 2022). In contrast, the up-regulation of PAL1 in phyA-211 might provide more precursors for flavonoid biosynthesis. Previous studies have shown that PIF4 and PIF5 negatively regulate anthocyanin accumulation under red light, as pif4 and pif5 mutants accumulated anthocyanin and increased the expression of CHS and F3′H genes compared with the wild type (Liu et al., 2015). Consistent with previous studies, flavonoid biosynthetic enzymes were generally up-regulated in pifq mutants, resulting in flavonoid accumulation (Figs 6, 7B). The difference between Col-0 and pifq indicates that PIFs play negative roles in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in response to red light. Moreover, phyB has been reported to induce rapid degradation of PIF4 and PIF5 under red light (Shen et al., 2007; Lorrain et al., 2008). Based on these previous findings, our study hypothesizes that flavonoid biosynthesis is negatively regulated by the accumulation of PIF4 and PIF5 in phyB-9 mutants. Conversely, the phyB-induced rapid degradation of PIF4 and PIF5 in the phyA-211 mutant may partially lead to the accumulation of flavonoids. Our results also showed that some flavonoid biosynthesis genes were significantly altered in phyAB seeds (Fig. 7B), implying regulation by the remaining red-light-sensing phytochromes.
Flavonols may interact with phytohormone signaling to modulate seed germination. Previous studies have shown the differential regulation of key flavonoid biosynthetic genes by GA and ABA, with GA acting as a repressor and ABA as an activator (Loreti et al., 2008; Tan et al., 2019). Furthermore, endogenous ABA levels and the expression of ABA biosynthesis genes were decreased, while the expression of GA biosynthesis genes was increased in Arabidopsis seeds in response to red light (Seo et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2020a). Therefore, the low abundance of flavonoids in Col-0 seeds may be linked to the increased GA/ABA ratio. Moreover, IAA induces flavonoid accumulation primarily via MYB12, which activates the transcription of flavonoid biosynthetic genes such as CHS, CHI, and FLS (Lewis et al., 2011). Accordingly, the levels of indole-3-acetaldoxime and indole-3-acetaldehyde, two intermediates of IAA biosynthesis, were decreased in Col-0 and phyB-9. In contrast, the level of indole-3-acetaldehyde was increased in phyA-211 seeds (Fig. 6). These changes probably modulate IAA homeostasis and contribute to the genotype-specific changes in flavonoids (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S2E). In our study, flavonols are the main differential flavonoids in response to red light. Apart from affecting auxin transport and signaling, flavonols may regulate ABA signaling through their antioxidant activity in countering ABA-dependent ROS bursts (Santelia et al., 2008; Watkins et al., 2014; Kuhn et al., 2016). Given the potential decrease in IAA levels, we infer that the decreasing levels of flavonols may integrate IAA/ABA signaling to facilitate seed germination in Col-0 seeds. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that treatment with exogenous proanthocyanidins delays seed germination by promoting de novo biosynthesis of ABA in Arabidopsis (Jia et al., 2012). Interestingly, our results also showed that both quercetin and kaempferol partially inhibited germination in Col-0 and pifq seeds (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating an inhibitory role for flavonols in seed germination in response to red light. These studies provided important clues for further study of the regulatory mechanism of flavonols involved in IAA/ABA signaling during the red light response to seed germination. Furthermore, we carried out a comprehensive analysis of the metabolome and established a close link between phytochrome signaling and flavonoid metabolism during red-light-mediated seed germination. However, the regulatory mechanism of phytochrome signaling and flavonoid metabolism needs further exploration including the downstream signaling components responsible for specific metabolic characteristics of phyA and phyB. Although previous studies have shown that phytochromes orchestrate complex regulatory networks of light-responsive photomorphogenesis through two main signaling pathways, COP1–SPA and PIF pathways (Leivar and Quail, 2011; Lu et al., 2015; Sheerin et al., 2015), whether the COP1–SPA component is involved in the regulation of flavonoid metabolism during red-light-mediated seed germination requires further studies.
In conclusion, we performed metabolomic analysis of seeds of the Arabidopsis wild type (Col-0), and phyA-211, phyB-9, phyAB, and pifq mutants, and established a comprehensive profile of the red-light-responsive metabolome (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. S3). Our findings revealed the synergistic and antagonistic roles of phyA and phyB in regulating primary and secondary metabolism in response to red light. Notably, flavonoid biosynthesis emerged as a crucial metabolic pathway antagonistically regulated by phyA and phyB signaling. Furthermore, exogenous quercetin and kaempferol treatment modestly suppressed germination in both Col-0 and pifq seeds. These results establish a link between metabolites and seed germination in response to red light, providing clues to further study the regulatory mechanisms of specific secondary metabolites including flavonols on Arabidopsis seed germination.
