# Karrikin signalling: impacts on plant development and abiotic stress tolerance

## Abstract
AbstractPlants rely upon a diverse range of metabolites to control growth and development, and to overcome stress that results from suboptimal conditions. Karrikins (KARs) are a class of butenolide compounds found in smoke that stimulate seed germination and regulate various developmental processes in plants. KARs are perceived via a plant α/β-hydrolase called KARRIKIN INSENSITIVE2 (KAI2), which also functions as a receptor for a postulated phytohormone, provisionally termed KAI2 ligand (KL). Considered natural analogues of KL, KARs have been extensively studied for their effects on plant growth and their crosstalk with plant hormones. The perception and response pathway for KAR–KL signalling is closely related to that of strigolactones, another class of butenolides with numerous functions in regulating plant growth. KAR–KL signalling influences seed germination, seedling photomorphogenesis, root system architecture, abiotic stress responses, and arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis. Here, we summarize current knowledge of KAR–KL signalling, focusing on its role in plant development, its effects on stress tolerance, and its interaction with other signalling mechanisms.Keywords: Abiotic stress, butenolide, development, hormone, karrikin, photomorphogenesis, root architecture, signalling

## Introduction
Plants have evolved exquisite metabolic diversity as part of a sessile lifestyle. Beyond the classical plant hormones, numerous metabolites, such as jasmonates, salicylates, brassinosteroids (BRs), and, more recently, strigolactones (SLs), perform signalling and/or hormonal functions to coordinate plant growth, development, and stress tolerance. The discovery of these compounds has revolutionized plant science and agriculture, as hormone signalling underpins the development of high-yielding crop varieties. Further additions to the list of plant growth regulators are karrikins (KARs), first identified in the smoke of burning vegetation (Flematti et al., 2004).
Chemically classified as butenolides, KARs are perceived via a plant α/β-hydrolase called KARRIKIN INSENSITIVE2 (KAI2) (Sun and Ni, 2011; Waters et al., 2012). KAI2 is highly evolutionarily conserved: KAI2 homologues are present in all land plants and can be traced back to green algal ancestors (Bythell-Douglas et al., 2017). Considering also the limited ecological prevalence of fire, this suggests that the primary role of KAI2 is not as a receptor for KARs. Instead, it is likely that KAI2 and its downstream signalling pathway sense and respond to one or more unknown hormone-like metabolites, collectively termed ‘KAI2 ligand’ (KL), that regulate plant growth and development (Conn and Nelson, 2015; Conn et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2020). KAI2 has a paralogue in seed plants called DWARF14 (D14), the receptor for SL hormones that regulate shoot branching; both proteins are unusual receptor enzymes that hydrolyse their ligands, and belong to a family of α/β-hydrolases that probably originated in a bacterial ancestor (Arite et al., 2009; Yao et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022).
Several commonalities have been observed between KARs and SLs. Structurally, both KARs and SLs contain a butenolide moiety (or D ring) (Flematti et al., 2004, 2015). Functionally, both influence distinct but overlapping aspects of plant development, including seed germination (Cook et al., 1966; Nelson et al., 2012; Flematti et al., 2015), root architecture (Carbonnel et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2021; Sisaphaithong et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2021; Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2022), and mycorrhizal symbiosis (Akiyama et al., 2005; Gutjahr et al., 2015; Hull et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). Besides the paralogous receptor proteins, the signalling pathways of SL and KAR (Fig. 1) both exploit the F-box protein MORE AXILLARY BRANCHES2 (MAX2) (Nelson et al., 2011), which directs the proteasomal degradation of repressor proteins belonging to the SUPPRESSOR OF MAX2-LIKE (SMXL) family, namely SMAX1 and SMXL2 in Arabidopsis (Stanga et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2013; Khosla et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2020; Park et al., 2022). Because SMAX1 and SMXL2 act genetically as suppressors of both kai2 and max2 mutations, loss-of-function smax1 smxl2 double mutants have opposite phenotypes to kai2 and max2 loss-of-function alleles, and these phenotypes typically recapitulate a constitutive KAR–KL signalling response.
Signalling pathways for KAR–KL and SL feature shared and homologous components. The α/β-hydrolase KAI2 is the receptor for karrikins (or karrikin metabolites; KAR), and an assumed butenolide ligand (KL) of unknown structure (?). Its homologue, D14, is the receptor for strigolactones (SLs). Ligand binding induces a conformational change in the receptor, probably facilitating an interaction with MAX2, the F-box subunit of the SCF class of the E3 ubiquitin–protein ligase complex. The receptor and/or MAX2 interact with one or more specific members of the SMXL family of repressor proteins, which are then degraded. In part through changes in transcription of varied target genes, this degradation process activates downstream responses such as seed germination, modifications to root architecture, and stimulation of arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis.
To date, the nature and identity of endogenous KLs remain elusive. Given the ancient origins and consistent retention (and in some cases, expansion) of KAI2 over evolutionary time, the capacity to perceive KL may have been a pivotal innovation associated with the transition of green plants from aquatic to terrestrial habitats (Wang et al., 2022). Presumably, karrikins are KL mimics that can substitute for, or enhance, the effects of, KL. Observations of the actions of KARs might thus provide indirect insights into the effects of KLs. Given the chemical and mechanistic parallels between karrikins and SLs, it is reasonable to expect KLs to also have an apocarotenoid origin. There is a great deal of potential chemical diversity that can emerge from the oxidative cleavage of carotenoids, whether via enzymic or non-enzymic means, and searching this chemical space is gradually resulting in the discovery of new bioactive hormone-like compounds (Wang et al., 2019). However, to date, no definitive evidence has been found to indicate an apocarotenoid origin for KLs, nor have any clues on a biosynthetic route been uncovered via genetic approaches (Yao et al., 2018; Waters and Nelson, 2023). Indeed, rather than being a single compound, ‘KL’ may constitute several compounds of differing origins and chemical structure, unified by their capacity to signal through a KAI2-like α/β-hydrolase. One conceivable source for KL could be from outside the plant, perhaps from rhizospheric microorganisms such as arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, which rely on KAR–KL signalling to robustly colonize plants (Gutjahr et al., 2015; Choi et al., 2020).
Despite the conspicuous and frustrating absence of a known ligand for KAI2-mediated signalling, it is clear from molecular-genetic and physiological studies that this signalling pathway impacts many aspects of growth and development across land plants (Box 1). We refer to ‘KAR–KL signalling’ to describe the effects of signals that operate through the canonical KAI2–MAX2–SMAX1 signalling pathway. Such signals include KARs and synthetic butenolides, effects of which can be determined empirically, as well as unknown KL compounds, whose influence is inferred through mutant analysis. To complement other comprehensive reviews in this area (Varshney and Gutjahr, 2023; Waters and Nelson, 2023), this review will focus on the roles of KAR–KL signalling in plant growth responses and stress tolerance.

## Box 1. Functional diversity of KAI2 signalling among plants
KAI2-dependent signalling regulates a diverse set of plant developmental processes, as inferred from genetic studies in model plants. The core roles of promoting seed germination and seedling photomorphogenesis (e.g. hypocotyl elongation in dicots, or mesocotyl elongation in monocots) are widely described (Flematti et al., 2004; Nelson et al., 2010, 2011; Waters et al., 2012; Stanga et al., 2013; Kameoka and Kyozuka, 2015; Sun et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2020). KAI2 also influences below-ground development across the angiosperms, by promoting root hair growth and lateral root formation in Arabidopsis, Lotus japonicus, and Brachypodium. KAR–KL signalling has subtle effects on leaf shape and shoot growth, as kai2 mutants generally have a more elongated appearance that is reminiscent of impaired light signalling (Sun and Ni, 2011; Waters et al., 2012; Meng et al., 2021). There is also evidence that KAR–KL signalling affects flowering time, at least in Arabidopsis (Xu et al., 2022).
Beyond the angiosperms, KAI2-dependent signalling has been studied in the moss Physcomitrium patens and the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha. The P. patens genome encodes 13 KAI2-like proteins, making functional analysis by mutagenesis a complex matter. Some of these proteins have roles in detecting strigolactone-like quorum signals that regulate radial expansion, whereas others operate via MAX2 to regulate growth responses to light (Lopez-Obando et al., 2021). This latter function is analogous to the role of KAI2 in photomorphogenesis in angiosperms. In M. polymorpha, there are two KAI2 homologues, only one of which (MpKAI2a) has an obvious effect on growth and development in a MAX2-dependent manner. MpKAI2a is required for normal vegetative thallus growth in both the light and the dark, and also plays a significant role in asexual reproduction by controlling gemmae production (Mizuno et al., 2021; Komatsu et al., 2023).

## KAR–KL signalling in plant development
The exogenous application of KARs and synthetic KAI2 agonists such as GR24 and desmethyl-GR24 has been instrumental in probing the function of KAI2–KL signalling. Extensive analysis of KAR–KL signalling mutants—mainly kai2, max2, and smax1 in Arabidopsis thaliana, Lotus japonicus, Oryza sativa, and Brachypodium distachyon—has also provided crucial insights. In the following sections, we will explore the roles of KAR–KL signalling in several key aspects of plant development, highlighting the current understanding of the crosstalk with other signalling pathways.

## Seed germination
Plants rely on the timely control of germination to ensure successful establishment. KAR treatment enhances seed germination in many plants, with some species responding to concentrations as low as 1 nM (Flematti et al., 2004; Nelson et al., 2012). This germination-promoting effect can be attributed to the action of the KAR–KL signalling pathway: in A. thaliana, kai2 and max2 mutants exhibit increased dormancy, and this phenotype is counteracted by loss-of-function smax1 alleles (Nelson et al., 2011; Waters et al., 2012; Stanga et al., 2013).
Physiological seed dormancy is influenced by many factors and hormones that ultimately affect the balance of abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellin (GA) abundance or signalling activity. Previous studies have shown that KAR application could not recover germination of the GA-deficient Arabidopsis mutant ga1-3, although most transcriptional responses to KAR1 still occurred (Nelson et al., 2009, 2010). However, recent work shows that KAR–KL signalling can bypass the GA requirement for germination. Seeds of smax1 are highly resistant to the germination-inhibiting effects of the GA biosynthesis inhibitor, paclobutrazol (PAC) (Bunsick et al., 2020). However, the inverse is also true: PAC can partially suppress the enhanced germination of smax1 smxl2 double mutants (Xu et al., 2023). These findings suggest that relative flux through KAR–KL signalling and GA-dependent signalling pathways is integrated to influence the decision to germinate. Mechanistically, this may come about through both pathways converging upon DELLA growth repressor proteins (Xu et al., 2023) (see below). There is also evidence that, when GA biosynthesis is inhibited, the ability of KAR–KL signalling to trigger germination is dependent on a functional BR signalling pathway. This is potentially due to the antagonistic interaction of BR signalling on ABA signalling; loss of BR signalling may cause stronger ABA-imposed seed dormancy that over-rides germination promotion by KAR–KL (Bunsick and Lumba, 2021).
The seeds of positively photoblastic species require light to trigger germination. Although KARs do not replace this requirement for seed germination in Arabidopsis, they can enhance germination under light-limited conditions (Nelson et al., 2009). Consistent with this observation, kai2 and max2 mutants exhibit abnormally low rates of seed germination under limiting light, and this phenotype can be reversed by smax1 smxl2 mutations (Xu et al., 2023). Therefore, KAR–KL signalling cooperates with light signalling when regulating seed germination. The interplay between these two pathways is variable because in lettuce, unlike in Arabidopsis, KAR can overcome the light requirement for germination (Flematti et al., 2004; Martinez et al., 2022). This is consistent with the fact that lettuce seeds are inherently more sensitive to KAR than Arabidopsis (Flematti et al., 2004; Nelson et al., 2009). One route by which species-specific differences in sensitivity to KAR or other butenolide stimulants may come about is through the butenolide receptor itself. For example, the SL-responsive ShHTL7 from the parasitic weed Striga hermonthica can, when expressed in an Arabidopsis GA-deficient mutant, bypass the germination requirement for GA when supplied with exogenous SL analogue GR24 (Bunsick et al., 2020). Presumably, this outcome results from the exquisite affinity of ShHTL7 for its ligand, and/or an unusually strong capacity to direct turnover of SMAX1 and SMXL2. Accordingly, species-specific or even ecotype-specific differences in germination response to exogenous butenolides and light may result from the relative strength and direction of various inputs that all serve to influence germination.

## Hypocotyl growth and seedling light responses
Seedling photomorphogenesis is a critical aspect of early plant development that initiates phototrophic growth. KAR–KL signalling amplifies seedling responses to light, which results in reduced hypocotyl elongation, increased cotyledon expansion, and the up-regulation of light-responsive transcripts. For example, Arabidopsis kai2 seedlings exhibit a longer hypocotyl and smaller cotyledons compared with the wild type. However, these differences are not observed in continuous darkness, and KAR only inhibits hypocotyl elongation in light-grown Arabidopsis seedlings (Nelson et al., 2010; Sun and Ni, 2011; Waters et al., 2012). Conversely, smax1 smxl2 double mutants display considerably shorter hypocotyls and larger cotyledons than wild-type plants in the light, characteristics that resemble those of wild-type plants after KAR treatment (Stanga et al., 2016). Other species, including lettuce and Brassica tournefortii, also exhibit reduced hypocotyl elongation in the presence of KAR1 (Nelson et al., 2010; Sun et al., 2020), indicating that this is a highly conserved response.
In monocots such as rice, where the mesocotyl plays an analogous role to the hypocotyl to help the seedling reach the light, KAR inhibits mesocotyl elongation in a KAI2-dependent manner. This activity takes place in darkness, suggesting that KAR–KL signalling does not require light to regulate seedling growth in rice, unlike in Arabidopsis (Kameoka and Kyozuka, 2015; Zheng et al., 2020). However, Arabidopsis smax1 smxl2 seedlings are somewhat shorter than those of the wild type when grown in darkness, demonstrating that very strong activation of KAR–KL signalling can partially overcome the normal requirement for light in directing photomorphogenesis. In turn, these findings suggest that changes in the relative strength of light and KAR–KL signalling inputs can influence the developmental decision that a seedling will make. It would be informative to determine if different developmental strategies (e.g. epigeal versus hypogeal germination) come about through evolutionary changes in the relative strength of these two signalling pathways.
Transcriptome analysis of dormant Arabidopsis seeds treated with KAR1 has shown an up-regulation of light-responsive genes, including ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), which encodes a transcription factor that positively regulates responses to light (Nelson et al., 2010; Waters and Smith, 2013). hy5 seedlings show weak hypocotyl responses to KARs, but several transcriptional responses to KARs remain unchanged (Waters and Smith, 2013). The kai2-2 and hy5-1 mutations act additively in determining hypocotyl length, and KAI2 overexpression partially suppresses the hy5 long hypocotyl phenotype (Waters and Smith, 2013). These findings suggest that HY5 does not participate in the immediate perception or action of KARs, and that they define independent pathways that regulate hypocotyl growth. Nevertheless, the relative contribution of HY5 to seedling photomorphogenesis dominates over KAR–KL signalling. More recently, Bursch et al. (2021) have further elucidated the role of KAR–KL in promoting the activity of a transcriptional module comprising HY5, B-BOX DOMAIN PROTEIN 20 (BBX20), and BBX21. This module is crucial for the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation and anthocyanin accumulation, as evidenced by the insensitivity of the bbx20 bbx21 hypocotyls to KAR2, which is a phenotype similar to that of hy5 (Bursch et al., 2021).
Recent research suggests a leading role for SMAX1 in the regulation of hypocotyl elongation in response to elevated temperatures (thermomorphogenesis). Park et al. (2022) demonstrated that SMAX1-deficient mutants of Arabidopsis are largely insensitive to temperature-induced hypocotyl elongation. SMAX1 was found to interact with and presumably sequester phytochrome B (phyB), thereby limiting the ability of phyB to suppress the transcriptional activity of PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4). Because PIF4 is required for seedling thermomorphogenesis (Park et al., 2022), this suggests that the thermodynamic control of SMAX1 abundance could serve as a molecular gatekeeper by thermosensitizing PIF4-mediated hypocotyl morphogenesis (Park et al., 2022). Accordingly, it will be important to investigate the influence of temperature and other environmental factors on SMAX1 stability, which is subject to degradation through both KAI2-dependent and KAI2-independent mechanisms (Khosla et al., 2020). Regardless, given the well established role of phyB and PIF1 in seedling photomorphogenesis and seed germination, these findings also present a tantalizing mechanistic explanation for the enhancement of light responses by KAR–KL signalling.
As a general positive regulator of cell expansion, GA promotes hypocotyl elongation (Cowling and Harberd, 1999; Xiong et al., 2023). GA operates by degrading DELLA growth repressors: when DELLAs are removed through mutation or GA treatment, Arabidopsis hypocotyls elongate (Achard et al., 2007). In contrast, loss of SMAX1 through mutation or KAR treatment results in shortened hypocotyls. Investigating this counterplay, Kim et al. (2022) confirmed genetically and pharmacologically that these pathways counteract one another. Notably, they observed that light exposure leads to an accumulation of SMAX1 proteins, and this accumulation contributes to the enhanced degradation of DELLA proteins during the dark-to-light transition (Kim et al., 2022). They also demonstrated that SMAX1 could interact with two DELLA proteins (RGA and RGL2) in yeast, perhaps suggesting that DELLAs are sequestered and protected by SMAX1 in the light. This finding is consistent with the interaction between two more DELLA proteins (RGL1 and RGL3) and SMAX1 in planta, as reported by Xu et al. (2023); however, these authors could not find evidence for interaction between SMAX1 and RGL2. These findings suggest that SMAX1-mediated integration of light and KAR signals into DELLA-dependent pathways intricately modulates the process of seedling establishment, as it does for seed germination.
KAR–KL signalling also interacts with auxin transport in the hypocotyl. KAI2 facilitates the swift movement of auxin, synthesized at the shoot apex, towards the root meristem by fine-tuning the abundance of PIN auxin transport proteins (Hamon-Josse et al., 2022). The decrease in the levels of PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7 proteins in the hypocotyl region, coupled with an increase in PIN1, PIN3, and PIN7 abundance at the root meristem, is believed to impede hypocotyl elongation while promoting root growth. This rearrangement of PIN protein distribution is not observed in kai2 mutants, leading to the accumulation of auxin in the hypocotyl and subsequently promoting hypocotyl elongation (Hamon-Josse et al., 2022).

## Root system architecture and root hair growth
Plant roots constantly assimilate environmental information to make developmental decisions and enhance their growth for efficient nutrient absorption and anchoring. Investment in lateral roots and root hairs is a typical response to counteract nutrient scarcity in the soil by increasing the root surface area for nutrient uptake. Concurrently, directional growth becomes essential to cope with potential stressors such as salinity or to access moisture deep in the soil (Pierik and Testerink, 2014; Dietrich, 2018).
The KAR–KL pathway influences various aspects of root system architecture but, perhaps unsurprisingly, the response varies between species. For example, in Arabidopsis, KAI2 regulates lateral root density, but this role is additive with SL signalling via D14 (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2019, 2022; Swarbreck et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2021; Sepulveda et al., 2022). KAI2 and MAX2, but not D14, influence the vertical angle and straightness of root growth, albeit on the surface of agar plates, and this effect is mediated by SMAX1 and SMXL2 (Swarbreck et al., 2019; Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2019, 2022; Meng et al., 2021; Sepulveda et al., 2022). In Lotus japonicus, the density of post-embryonic roots is subtly stimulated by application of KAR1 and mediated by KAI2 (Carbonnel et al., 2020). In Brachypodium, kai2 mutants show a considerable increase in lateral root number and an increase in the distance between the root tip and zone of root hair initiation, but the degree to which these phenomena may be modulated by chemical means was not investigated (Meng et al., 2021).
An effect of KAR–KL signalling on root hair development perhaps tells a more consistent story. Exogenous butenolides that activate KAI2—in particular KAR2, rac-GR24, and GR24ent-5DS—stimulate root hair density and root hair elongation in a MAX2-dependent manner, demonstrating that KAR–KL signalling promotes root hair development (Ito et al., 2015; Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2019; Richmond et al., 2022). Further support comes from kai2 and max2 mutants of Arabidopsis and Brachypodium, which have shorter and less dense root hairs, and smax1 smxl2 and kuf1 mutants of Arabidopsis, which show the opposite phenotype of hyperactive root hair growth (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2019, 2022; Meng et al., 2021; Sepulveda et al., 2022). Lotus japonicus mutants deficient in SMAX1 similarly show increased root hair elongation (Carbonnel et al., 2020).
The effects of KAR–KL on root hair growth are mediated by ethylene and auxin: KAI2 activation (and subsequent SMAX1/SMXL2 degradation) releases the transcriptional inhibition of 1-AMINOCYCLOPROPANE-1-CARBOXYLATE SYNTHASE 7 (ACS7), thereby stimulating ethylene biosynthesis in both Arabidopsis and L. japonicus (Carbonnel et al., 2020; Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2022). In turn, increased ethylene signalling in roots initiates changes in auxin transport and distribution: for example, AUXIN TRANSPORTER PROTEIN1 (AUX1), a key auxin influx carrier, accumulates in the lateral root cap and the epidermal cells above it (Ruzicka et al., 2009). This facilitates increased auxin transport towards the region between the root elongation zone and the differentiation zone, where root hair elongation primarily occurs (Jones et al., 2009; Bhosale et al., 2018). AUX1 is necessary for the increased root hair length following treatment with KAR2; meanwhile, enhanced expression of AUX1 in the lateral root cap and superior epidermal cells is sufficient to phenocopy the long root hair phenotype of smax1 smxl2 (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2022).
Root hair development is acutely sensitive to available inorganic phosphate (Pi): typically, low Pi levels enhance root hair elongation and density (Müller and Schmidt, 2004; Bhosale et al., 2018). However, the root hairs of Arabidopsis kai2 and max2 mutants show defective elongation responses to changes in Pi availability, and although smax1 smxl2 root hairs do elongate as Pi levels fall, they are consistently longer than those of the wild type under identical conditions (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2022). Transcript levels for MAX2 and KAI2 increase in low Pi relative to high Pi conditions (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2022), which would presumably result in an intensified KL response in the roots. Under low Pi conditions, kai2 mutants fail to induce expression of AUX1, while smax1 smxl2 mutants accumulate AUX1 independently of Pi level (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2022). Therefore, Pi starvation triggers KAR–KL signalling, which initiates the ethylene–auxin cascade, and so induces the elongation of root hairs (Villaécija-Aguilar et al., 2019, 2022). Because root hairs are involved in water and mineral nutrient absorption as well as interactions with nutrient-capturing microbes, this effect may support a general role for KAR–KL signalling in nutrient acquisition. In plants that can accommodate AM fungi, an increase in KAR–KL signalling activity associated with Pi starvation would simultaneously help recruit the fungal symbiont to assist with Pi uptake (see below). This could result from increased exudation of SLs to act as a chemoattractant, as well as pre-conditioning the root to support colonization; both of these mechanisms are under KAR–KL control (Choi et al., 2020).

## KAR–KL signalling in arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis
AM fungi help plants by improving the acquisition of mineral nutrients from the soil, especially phosphate and nitrogen, for which the fungus receives carbohydrates and lipids in return (Roth and Paszkowski, 2017; Keymer and Gutjahr, 2018). In kai2 mutants of rice and Brachypodium, AM colonization fails at a very early stage (Gutjahr et al., 2015; Meng et al., 2021). In barley, kai2 mutants show a significant reduction in root colonization relative to the wild type, but this effect is less severe and more variable than reported for equivalent mutants in rice and Brachypodium (Li et al., 2022). Nevertheless, when fungal hyphae do successfully enter the roots in kai2 mutants, normal colonization structures are formed, including arbuscules (Gutjahr et al., 2015; Meng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). This observation indicates that it is the initiation of colonization that is inhibited, rather than the progression of colonization itself. Crucially, the rice loss-of-function smax1-1 mutant exhibits dramatically increased levels of fungal colonization compared with the wild type (Choi et al., 2020). In addition, smax1-1 fully suppresses the impaired colonization phenotype of d3, which lacks the rice homologue of MAX2 (Choi et al., 2020). SMAX1 also regulates the transcription of genes such as the transcription factor CYCLOPS and several kinases that are common symbiosis genes, as well as enzymes involved in SL biosynthesis (Choi et al., 2020). These findings are consistent with SMAX1 as a negative regulator that operates downstream of KAI2 and MAX2 in the regulation of AM colonization, as well as other aspects of KAI2-dependent signalling. Furthermore, the dramatic and opposite phenotypes of kai2 and smax1 mutants are broadly indicative of a signalling system that regulates the competency of the root to host AM fungi (Hull et al., 2021).
Although the role for KAI2 in AM colonization appears conserved among monocots, it is less comprehensively validated in dicots. In petunia, there are five KAI2 homologues, of which one, PhKAI2a, is reliably expressed in roots (Liu et al., 2019). Loss-of-function Phkai2a mutants were completely resistant to mycorrhizal colonization, and also showed a substantial reduction in numbers of non-suberized hypodermal passage cells (HPCs) (Liu et al., 2019). HPCs are exclusive entry points for AM fungal hyphae in several species (Sharda and Koide, 2008). As Phkai2a did not completely abolish formation of HPCs, the lack of AM colonization cannot result simply from a physical inaccessibility of the root cortex to the fungus. In Medicago truncatula, loss-of-function kai2 and max2 alleles conferred a moderate reduction (half that of the wild type) on AM colonization (Li et al., 2022). Therefore, in dicots, as with monocots, it is likely that KAR–KL signalling is necessary for conditioning the roots into a physiological state that is fully permissive for AM colonization. As a very low level of AM colonization is possible in rice and Brachypodium kai2 mutants, ‘permissiveness’ may be subject to other independent factors, which may differ in relative strength between taxonomic groups and environmental conditions. More intricate models for the role of KAR–KL signalling in AM colonization await detailed characterization in other dicot species, such as L. japonicus (Carbonnel et al., 2020).
Recently, a possible role has emerged for another α/β-hydrolase, DLK2, in the regulation of AM symbiosis. DLK2 is a paralogue of KAI2 and D14 that is transcriptionally up-regulated in response to JARs, SLs, and synthetic butenolide analogues in multiple species (Waters et al., 2012; Meng et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2021; Martinez et al., 2022). In Arabidopsis, DLK2 expression is subject to regulation by SMAX1 and SMXL2, rather than other SMXL members (Wang et al., 2020). To date, no clear function for DLK2 has emerged, as Arabidopsis dlk2 mutants show no obvious change in phenotype, and DLK2 itself shows poor hydrolytic activity towards GR24, but nevertheless retains enzymatic activity as a hydrolase (Waters et al., 2012; Végh et al., 2017; Bürger et al., 2022). Furthermore, DLK2 and closely related homologues seem to have lost the highly conserved residues present in KAI2 and D14 that are thought to form part of the interaction interface with MAX2 (Bythell-Douglas et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2018), which makes it unlikely that DLK2 has a receptor function akin to that of KAI2 and D14. In Brachypodium and rice, DLK2 transcripts are strongly induced in roots that are colonized by AM fungi (Kobae et al., 2018; Meng et al., 2021), while DLK2 promoter activity is stronger in arbuscule-containing cells in rice roots (Sisaphaithong et al., 2021). Some compelling functional data for DLK2 emerged from a recent study in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), in which DLK2 was targeted for down-regulation by RNAi (Ho-Plágaro et al., 2021). In DLK2 RNAi transgenics, mycorrhization was slightly—but significantly—enhanced, with a greater proportion of cells with arbuscules relative to empty vector control transgenics. Meanwhile, DLK2-overexpressing plants had the opposite phenotype, with reduced frequency of arbuscules and a significantly reduced level of mycorrhization overall (Ho-Plágaro et al., 2021). Speculatively, DLK2 might form part of a negative feedback loop to regulate the ‘permissive’ state of the root (Hull et al., 2021). This feedback could provide one of many possible mechanisms that plants could use for controlling the extent of AM symbiosis, perhaps in response to environmental influences such as nutrient status.

## KAR–KL signalling and abiotic stress tolerance
Plants encounter various abiotic and biotic stresses, and their ability to respond and adapt is critical for survival (Zhu, 2016). KAR–KL signalling has been implicated in mediating plant responses to abiotic stresses through the expression of stress-responsive genes and the activation of defence mechanisms (Li et al., 2017; Abdelrahman et al., 2022; Mostofa et al., 2023). Numerous studies have investigated the effects of KARs on plant growth performance, with a particular focus on abiotic stress tolerance (Table 1). Because relevant mutants are only available in a few species, the majority of such studies are phenomenological in nature; however, in general, KAR treatment improves the capacity of plants to withstand abiotic stresses, some of which we discuss in more detail below.
Evidence for the role of KAR–KL signalling in alleviating abiotic stress

## Drought
KAR–KL signalling has been implicated in drought stress tolerance (Li et al., 2017; Tian et al., 2022). In Arabidopsis, loss of KAI2 leads to a number of drought-related deficiencies, including increased rates of water loss from leaves, impaired stomatal responses to ABA, and reduced survival following water withdrawal (Fig. 2) (Li et al., 2017). These defects are consistent with similar phenotypes associated with max2 (Bu et al., 2014; Van Ha et al., 2014). At least part of the drought sensitivity of kai2 results from impaired formation of the cuticle proper, a wax-rich outer layer: in kai2, this layer is thinner and more permeable to solvents than in the wild type (Li et al., 2017). Overexpression of KAI2 results in a thicker and less permeable cuticle (Li et al., 2017), but there does not appear to be any effect on rates of water loss (Meng et al., 2021), which may suggest that other routes of water transmission (e.g. via stomata) become more relevant to overall water loss once the cuticle is beyond a certain thickness. To date, there is also no direct evidence that activation of KAI2 by chemical means (e.g. by application of KAR or other butenolides) results in increased drought tolerance, although rac-GR24 application can boost drought tolerance of wild-type Arabidopsis (Van Ha et al., 2014). Nevertheless, smax1 smxl2 mutants show an ~5-fold increase in drought survivability, suggesting that SMAX1 and SMXL2 are joint negative regulators of drought responses (Feng et al., 2023). Likewise, KUF1 also negatively regulates drought response through multiple mechanisms (Tian et al., 2022).
Schematic model of KAR–KL signalling in response to drought stress. Under water-limited conditions, KL production is tentatively elevated, and levels of activated KAI2 increase. This triggers the formation of the SCFMAX2–KAI2–SMAX1 (or SMXL2) complex. SMAX1 is then polyubiquitinated and degraded, activating KAR–KL downstream responses that include a reduction in stomatal aperture. This effect may come about through a transcriptionally mediated increase in ABA biosynthesis (Li et al. 2017). This short-term mechanism regulates drought responses by controlling water loss through transpiration. In addition to this immediate response, longer term KAR–KL mediated adaptations to drought conditions include an increase in cuticular deposition.
Early studies involving SL-deficient max1, max3, and max4 mutants of Arabidopsis disagreed on the involvement of SLs in drought tolerance (Bu et al., 2014; Van Ha et al., 2014). The basis for this ambiguity is not obvious, but it implies that SLs have a relatively mild effect on water relations that is not fully consistent between different experimental conditions. As d14 became available to replace max2 as an improved SL-insensitive mutant, this subtlety appears to have been borne out. Compared with kai2, the negative effect of d14 on drought tolerance is much reduced, but not to zero; pertinently, a d14 kai2 double mutant has an ~2.5-fold poorer survival rate after drought treatment when compared with a kai2 single mutant (Li et al., 2020). Although KAI2 mediates the majority of the effect, both pathways contribute to cell membrane integrity, cuticular permeability, control of stomatal aperture, and overall water loss (Li et al., 2020). One exception to this rule is that D14 contributes more to the accumulation of anthocyanin pigment during drought than KAI2, although again both contribute to this response (Li et al., 2020). Consistent with this involvement of D14 in drought response, smxl678 triple mutants have a dramatically improved drought survival rate, with most of this effect contributed by smxl6 and smxl7 (Yang et al., 2020). This smxl678 phenotype is accompanied by a thicker cuticle, increased sensitivity to ABA, and constitutive activation of many stress response genes (Yang et al., 2020). Thus, there is a considerable impact upon responses to drought when SMXL6, SMXL7, and SMXL8 are removed genetically, but the relative effect compared with the loss of SMAX1 and SMXL2 remains to be determined. Ultimately, targeting one pathway or the other in different species will require empirical observations, while taking pleiotropy into consideration.
The above conclusions about the roles of KAI2 and D14 in drought tolerance are derived solely from studies on Arabidopsis. In Brachypodium, strong kai2 alleles conferred only a very slight (~10%) increase in rates of water loss from leaves relative to the wild type, which is considerably less severe than the ~40% loss observed in null kai2 alleles of Arabidopsis (Meng et al., 2021). Genes involved in wax and fatty acid biosynthesis were differentially expressed in kai2 of Arabidopsis (Li et al., 2017) and were KAR inducible in Brachypodium (Meng et al., 2021), which suggests that the KAI2-dependent control of cuticle development is largely conserved in angiosperms. Some of the difference in the degree of water loss may be ascribed to differing cuticular wax composition or cuticle thickness between the two species. A thorough analysis of existing mutants in L. japonicus, pea, and rice, and others as they emerge, will help to establish how much scope there is to exploit KAI2-dependent signalling for engineering drought tolerance in species of agricultural relevance.

## Salinity and osmotic stress
KAR–KL signalling plays an important role in plant adaptation to salinity stress, a common environmental stressor that can significantly hamper plant growth and productivity (Shah et al., 2020, 2021a; Sharifi and Shirani Bidabadi, 2020; Hayat et al., 2021; Mostofa et al., 2023). In Arabidopsis, kai2 mutants are hypersensitive to salinity and osmotic stress in terms of impaired seed germination, suggesting that KAR–KL signalling helps to overcome such stress (Mostofa et al., 2023). KAI2 improves the ability of the plant to regulate shoot Na+ homeostasis, possibly by positive regulation of ion transporter genes such as SALT OVERLY SENSITIVE and the high-affinity potassium transporter gene HKT1. Compared with kai2, wild-type plants have higher levels of glutathione, improved glutathione-related redox status, and increased antioxidant enzyme activities, which implies that KAR–KL signalling is important for oxidative stress tolerance in general (Mostofa et al., 2023).
KAR1 has been found to alleviate salt stress in wheat by enhancing seed germination and promoting root and shoot growth under salt stress conditions. Specifically, KAR1 application was found to regulate the expression of genes involved in GA biosynthesis and ABA signalling, and to maintain redox and K+/Na+ homeostasis, thereby reducing oxidative damage and enhancing the plant’s tolerance to salt stress (Shah et al., 2021a). In another study, Shah et al. (2020) demonstrated that KAR1 could significantly alleviate both drought and salt stress in Sapium sebiferum, an important oil plant. KAR1 supplementation improved seedling biomass, taproot length, and the number of lateral roots. KAR1-treated seedlings also showed higher activities of key antioxidative enzymes, leading to a lower level of hydrogen peroxide, malondialdehyde, and electrolyte leakage (Shah et al., 2020). Nigella sativa, when seed-primed with 10 µM KAR1 and treated with foliar calcium chloride, showed a salinity-resistant phenotype with a higher gas exchange level, improved ascorbate–glutathione cycle, and increased total unsaturated fatty acids (Sharifi and Shirani Bidabadi, 2020). Hayat et al. (2021) demonstrated that plant-derived smoke (PDS) could alleviate salt stress in wheat. They found that PDS application enhanced seed germination, root/shoot length, and seedling biomass under salt stress conditions (Hayat et al., 2021). These studies assume that PDS contains KARs, which has been demonstrated for a range of combustible materials (Flematti et al., 2004, 2009; van Staden et al., 2004). It is thus reasonable to expect that all PDS contains KARs, even if not directly tested or quantified. However, it is highly likely that PDS contains other bioactive compounds that are not KARs, such as cyanohydrins or hydroquinone (Flematti et al., 2011; Kamran et al., 2017), meaning that we should be cautious when inferring roles for KAR–KL based on studies using PDS. Nevertheless, KAR–KL signalling appears to enhance plant tolerance to salinity stress through a variety of mechanisms, including the maintenance of ionic and osmotic balance, mitigation of oxidative stress, and modulation of stress-responsive hormone signalling. To what degree these broad physiological effects of KARs are mediated by the canonical KAI2–SMAX1 pathway, or through some other route, remains to be established.

## Shade avoidance
Because green leaves absorb red light but are generally transparent to far-red light, plants interpret a decrease in the ratio of red to far-red light (R:FR) as shading from encroaching vegetation. Plants measure R:FR using phytochrome light receptor proteins, and shade-avoiders such as Arabidopsis undergo hypocotyl and stem elongation as R:FR decreases (Franklin and Whitelam, 2005). In this regard, the elongated hypocotyl and leaf petiole phenotypes of kai2 under non-shade conditions are consistent with a constitutive shade response, or increased sensitivity to far-red light. However, kai2 retains the capacity to elongate further in response to simulated shade (Xu et al., 2022) and shows increased hypocotyl length relative to the wild type in monochromatic red light (Waters et al., 2012), so perhaps a more appropriate interpretation of these phenotypes is a reduced sensitivity of kai2 to red light. Satisfyingly, addition of KAR2 to Arabidopsis seedlings attenuates the shade avoidance response in a dose-dependent manner (Xu et al., 2022). This is consistent with prior conclusions that KAR enhances sensitivity to light (Nelson et al., 2010). In effect, the seedling experiences a subjectively higher R:FR ratio when treated with KAR or when the pathway is constitutively activated (i.e. in smax1 smxl2 mutants), and a subjectively lower R:FR ratio when KAR–KL signalling is absent (i.e. in kai2 or max2 mutants).
Changes in the levels and distribution of auxin are central to the shade avoidance response (Ma and Li, 2019). Treatment with l-kynurenine (an auxin biosynthesis inhibitor) partially restores elongation of kai2 hypocotyls under simulated shade, and kai2 mutants express higher levels of PIN7 auxin transporter relative to the wild type, especially under simulated shade (Xu et al., 2022). These findings suggest that aberrant auxin accumulation and polar auxin transport contribute to the hypocotyl phenotype of kai2, which agrees with other research indicating that KAI2 regulates auxin abundance in the hypocotyl during the dark-to-light transition (Hamon-Josse et al., 2022). As might be expected for a repressor of KAR–KL signalling, constitutive overexpression of SMAX1 enhances multiple aspects of the shade avoidance response, and also affects the expression of genes related to auxin transport and signalling (Xu et al., 2022). However, smax1 loss-of-function mutants retain the ability to respond to simulated shade, so butenolide signalling via KAI2–SMAX1 is only a modulator of the shade avoidance response, rather than an essential element.

## Temperature stress
Temperature serves as a crucial environmental signal that influences the distribution and behaviour of plants worldwide (Kerbler and Wigge, 2023). When temperatures are outside the physiological optimal range, plants experience heat or cold stress, leading to adverse and irreversible consequences for their growth, development, and yield (Huang et al., 2023). KAR–KL signalling has been shown to mitigate temperature stress in plants (Abdelrahman et al., 2022; Park et al., 2022). This is evident in both heat and cold stress conditions, with kai2 mutants exhibiting distinct responses compared with wild-type plants (Shah et al., 2021b; Abdelrahman et al., 2022).
In Arabidopsis, when seeds were exposed to elevated temperature sufficient to inhibit germination (30 °C) and subsequently returned to more optimal conditions (20°C), wild-type seeds germinated promptly, eventually reaching germination levels no different from controls grown at constant 20 °C (Wang et al., 2018). In contrast, similarly treated kai2 seeds could not attain germination levels observed in the 20 °C controls. Likewise, at a temperature that is inhibitory but nevertheless permissive for germination (27 °C), kai2 seeds were significantly more negatively affected than those of the wild type (Wang et al., 2018), implying that KAR–KL signalling is important for coping with high temperatures. Interestingly, KAR2 application slightly inhibited seed germination at elevated temperature in the wild type, suggesting that KAR–KL signalling promotes seed germination in general, but may prevent it when the conditions are unfavourable (Wang et al., 2018).
When exposed to heat stress conditions at a later vegetative stage, Arabidopsis kai2 mutants exhibit hypersensitivity, characterized by increased leaf surface temperature and cell membrane damage relative to the wild type (Abdelrahman et al., 2022). Transcriptome analyses revealed distinct profiles in heat-stressed mutants relative to the wild type; kai2 mutants showed impaired induction of transcripts related to the binding of misfolded proteins and heat shock-related transcription factors. This suggests that KAI2 signalling plays a crucial role in the regulation of heat stress responses, and its manipulation could potentially enhance heat tolerance in plants (Abdelrahman et al., 2022).
In the oil crop Sapium sebiferum (Euphorbiaceae), KAI2 was reported to be induced by cold shock, salinity, and osmotic stress (Shah et al., 2021b). Overexpression of this S. sebiferum KAI2 homologue (SsKAI2) in an Arabidopsis wild-type background significantly improved plant recovery after cold stress, which was accompanied by increased levels of proline, total soluble sugars, and total soluble protein (Shah et al., 2021b). SsKAI2 overexpression increased levels of transcripts related to cold stress acclimation. It also conferred an increased sensitivity to ABA, suggesting a potential interaction between KAI2 and ABA signalling under cold stress (Shah et al., 2021b). Although SsKAI2 might have particularly high affinity for KL relative to other homologues, it is unlikely that overexpressing this KAI2 homologue would uniquely confer tolerance to cold stress. Instead, given the patterns in stress tolerance described above, this outcome resulting from overexpression of the receptor probably reflects an improvement in abiotic stress tolerance in general. A tentative link with ABA signalling would benefit from further investigation of double mutants of kai2 and those deficient in ABA perception or biosynthesis.

## Outlook
Over the last two decades, we have progressed from the discovery of KARs, through the elucidation of the molecular signalling pathway, and now towards an increasing understanding of the biological processes that KAR–KL signalling influences. Some molecular aspects of the signalling mechanisms are barely explored; for instance, seed responses to SL in Phelipanche ramosa are governed by ABA-independent DNA methylation (Lechat et al., 2015), while a role is emerging for histone deacetylases in regulating the MAX2-dependent germination process in A. thaliana (Temmerman et al., 2023). These discoveries suggest that there may be further unexplored effects of KARs on epigenomes, warranting more systematic investigation. In general, the underlying molecular mechanisms by which KAR–KL signalling confers stress tolerance, and the specific targets required for this process, are not fully understood. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the intricate signalling networks and downstream effectors. Naturally, a practical question is whether we can manipulate KAR–KL signalling to improve stress tolerance in plants. Understanding the potential for genetic or chemical manipulation of this signalling pathway to enhance stress resilience is of great importance for sustainable agriculture, and no doubt the specific applications will vary from crop to crop. Future studies should explore the feasibility and efficacy of manipulating KAR–KL signalling to develop crop varieties with improved stress tolerance.
