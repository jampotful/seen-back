Biogenic retrograde signaling via GUN1 ensures thermotolerant chloroplast biogenesis during seedling establishment in Arabidopsis thaliana.
Qi S, Cui C, Xia J, Li M, Li M, Jeon JS, Kim C. (2026). Journal of integrative plant biology. . doi:10.1111/jipb.70127. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Biogenic retrograde signaling via GUN1 ensures thermotolerant chloroplast biogenesis during seedling establishment in Arabidopsis thaliana

## Abstract
ABSTRACTPlants must coordinate chloroplast biogenesis with environmental conditions during seedling establishment, as failure to do so results in impaired phototrophic growth. Despite the biological importance of this early developmental stage, the influence of environmental factors on chloroplast biogenesis remains poorly understood. Here, we reveal a crucial role for GENOMES UNCOUPLED1 (GUN1)‐mediated biogenic retrograde signaling in safeguarding chloroplast development and supporting seedling growth under heat stress. Loss of GUN1 causes severe bleaching and impaired photomorphogenesis at elevated temperatures. Genetic interaction analyses show that EXECUTER1 (EX1) and EXECUTER2 (EX2), key components of chloroplast ROS‐associated operational retrograde signaling, modulate the heat‐sensitive phenotype of gun1 mutants, indicating crosstalk between biogenic and operational retrograde pathways. We further demonstrate that the de‐repressed expression of photosynthesis‐associated nuclear genes, that is, genomes uncoupled expression, is a major contributor to the heat sensitivity and failed chloroplast biogenesis in gun1 seedlings under heat stress. These findings extend the current understanding of GUN1 function by showing its contribution to chloroplast development and thermotolerance through biogenic retrograde signaling during early seedling growth.Keywords: EXECUTER1, EXECUTER2, GUN1, retrograde signaling, thermotolerance

## INTRODUCTION
Endosymbiosis, a process in which one organism lives inside another, is fundamental to the evolution of eukaryotic cells, which contain endosymbiotic organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts (Timmis et al., 2004; Archibald, 2009). According to the endosymbiotic theory, these organelles originated from engulfed bacteria that gradually established a stable, mutualistic relationship with their host over evolutionary time. Cyanobacteria played a pivotal role in the evolution of chloroplasts. Gene transfer from cyanobacteria to the host nucleus occurred during endosymbiosis, resulting in approximately 100 genes being retained in the cyanobacterial descendants (Kleine et al., 2009) and giving rise to around 3,000 nuclear genes encoding plastid proteins (Christian et al., 2020). This process likely facilitates the development of bilateral communication pathways, encompassing both anterograde (nucleus‐to‐plastid) and retrograde (plastid‐to‐nucleus) signaling, which are crucial for coordinating timely plastid biogenesis and function (Chan et al., 2016; Kleine and Leister, 2016; Li and Kim, 2022; Lee and Kim, 2024).
Retrograde signaling is further classified into biogenic and operational signaling. The former is associated with chloroplast biogenesis, while the latter mainly involves plant stress responses. Stressed chloroplasts rapidly produce and utilize diverse signaling molecules to trigger operational retrograde signaling, responding to environmental changes (Jiang and Dehesh, 2021). This highlights the role of chloroplasts as dynamic environmental sensors that fine‐tune plant adaptation to changing conditions such as high temperature (Kleine and Leister, 2016; D'Alessandro et al., 2020; Jiang and Dehesh, 2021; Li and Kim, 2022). Heat stress directly impairs key components of the photosynthetic machinery, including photosystems I and II (PSI, PSII), the cytochrome b 6 f complex (Cyt b 6 f), and Rubisco, leading to severe photosynthetic dysfunction (Havaux, 1993a, 1993b; Wise et al., 2004; Ruppel et al., 2013; Zheng et al., 2016; Perdomo et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018). As the metabolic hub of photosynthesis, chloroplasts might thus act as central sensors of heat stress and orchestrate adaptive physiological responses through operational retrograde signaling networks (Yu et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2017; Dickinson et al., 2018; Pollastri et al., 2021).
An earlier genetic screen in Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) identified six genomes uncoupled (gun) mutants (gun1 to gun6) that show impaired biogenic retrograde signaling (Susek et al., 1993; Mochizuki et al., 2001; Larkin et al., 2003; Woodson et al., 2011; Richter et al., 2023). In these gun mutants, the expression of photosynthesis‐associated nuclear genes (PhANGs) is derepressed when chloroplast biogenesis is impaired, which indicates a disruption in the normal signaling pathway that coordinates nuclear gene expression with chloroplast development. In contrast, in wild‐type (WT) seedlings, the expression of PhANGs is repressed under the same conditions, highlighting the role of the GUN proteins in transcriptionally repressing PhANGs when chloroplast biogenesis is impaired. To disrupt chloroplast biogenesis, chemical inhibitors like norflurazon (NF) and lincomycin (LIN) are commonly used. NF blocks carotenoid biosynthesis, while LIN inhibits plastidial translation (Chamovitz et al., 1991; Mulo et al., 2003). Interestingly, the gun1 mutant shows a genome uncoupled phenotype when grown on either NF or LIN, in contrast to other gun mutants, which display the phenotype only in the presence of NF (Koussevitzky et al., 2007). The impairment of chloroplast biogenesis by these chemicals triggers GUN1 to orchestrate biogenic retrograde signaling, resulting in the repression of target genes, such as GOLDEN2‐LIKE transcription factors 1 (GLK1) and GLK2, through an unknown mechanism (Kakizaki et al., 2009; Pesaresi and Kim, 2019; Lee et al., 2023; Richter et al., 2023). GLK1 and GLK2 (hereafter referred to as GLK1/2) are positive regulators of PhANGs (Fitter et al., 2002; Waters et al., 2009; Tu et al., 2022), and their transcriptional repression leads to the downregulation of PhANGs. In contrast, overexpression of GLK1 or GLK2 results in a gun phenotype characterized by upregulation of PhANGs relative to WT in the presence of LIN or NF (Leister and Kleine, 2016; Martín et al., 2016).
Remarkably, except for gun1, causal mutations in the remaining gun mutants have been identified in proteins involved in tetrapyrrole biosynthesis (TBS), highlighting the connection between TBS activity and biogenic retrograde signaling (Mochizuki et al., 2001; Larkin et al., 2003; Rodermel and Park, 2003; Strand, 2004; Fölsche et al., 2022; Richter et al., 2023). gun1 has a causal mutation in a nuclear gene encoding a chloroplast pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) protein with the C‐terminal SMR (Small MutS‐Related) domain (Koussevitzky et al., 2007). PPR proteins are a family of RNA‐binding proteins that generally play a crucial role in RNA metabolism, particularly in post‐transcriptional processes (e.g., RNA editing, splicing, stability, and translation) within mitochondria and chloroplasts (Barkan and Small, 2014). PPR proteins are characterized by repeated motifs of approximately 35 amino acids, known as PPR motifs. These motifs form alpha‐helical structures, creating a unique RNA‐binding surface. The C‐terminal SMR domain is named after the MutS‐related protein, and its structural fold is similar to the DNA‐binding domain found in MutS, a protein involved in DNA mismatch repair (Moreira and Philippe, 1999; Fukui et al., 2007). While the MutS protein is not an endonuclease, certain SMR domain‐containing proteins show endonuclease activity (Fukui et al., 2007; Wu et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2017). Recent research suggests that GUN1 may also regulate TBS through interactions with tetrapyrroles and TBS proteins (Shimizu et al., 2019; Shimizu and Masuda, 2021). Besides, GUN1 interacts with cpHSC70‐1, an ATP‐dependent chaperone (Shi and Theg, 2010; Su and Li, 2010; Liu et al., 2014), to facilitate chloroplast protein import under conditions that induce biogenic retrograde signaling to repress the expression of PhANGs (Wu et al., 2019; Tadini et al., 2020).
Despite extensive research on GUN1, the biological significance of the coupled or uncoupled expression of PhANGs with dysfunctional plastids remains largely unexplored. Our recent findings illustrated that the salicylic acid (SA, a phytohormone involved in stress responses)‐driven uncoupled expression of PhANGs and photosynthesis‐associated plastid genes (PhAPGs) contributes to the lesion‐mimic phenotype of the lesion‐simulating disease 1 (lsd1) mutant (Lv et al., 2019). The loss of the transcription coregulator LSD1, a zinc finger family protein, appears to upregulate SA in a light‐dependent manner, leading to the development of uncontrolled foliar cell death, known as runaway cell death (RCD) (Dietrich et al., 1994, 1997). Before RCD occurs, SA‐induced SIGMA FACTOR BINDING PROTEIN1 (SIB1), targeted to both the nucleus and chloroplasts, drives the upregulation of PhANGs and downregulation of PhAPGs (Lai et al., 2011; Lv et al., 2019). The uncoupled accumulation of photosynthesis‐associated proteins eventually impairs the homeostasis of photosynthetic reactive oxygen species (ROS), including singlet oxygen (1O2). On reaching critical levels, 1O2 triggers EXECUTER1 (EX1)‐dependent operational retrograde signaling, which is pivotal in initiating RCD (Kim et al., 2012; Dogra et al., 2019, 2022; Lv et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2024). Interestingly, the loss of GUN1 signaling attenuates the RCD phenotype in lsd1 because derepressed GLK1/2 activate their downstream target transcription factors, WRKY18 and WRKY40, which antagonize SA‐dependent stress responses (Li et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023). Moreover, EX1‐ and GUN1‐mediated retrograde signaling pathways appear to contribute to the 1O2‐induced cell death phenotype in programmed cell death 8 (pcd8) RNAi mutants by enhancing salicylic acid and jasmonic acid biosynthesis (Geng et al., 2024). These recent findings strongly suggest that GUN1 and its downstream targets, GLK1/2, may also play a role in operational retrograde signaling.
This study reveals the essential role of GUN1‐mediated biogenic retrograde signaling in safeguarding chloroplast biogenesis and seedling viability during germination under heat stress conditions (hereafter HS). While previous studies reported temperature sensitivity in gun1 mutants (Miller et al., 2007; Marino et al., 2019; Honkanen and Small, 2022; Lasorella et al., 2022), we demonstrate that this sensitivity arises from disrupted coordination of photosynthesis‐associated nuclear gene expression and impaired plastid ROS homeostasis under HS. Our findings establish GUN1 as a central integrator of biogenic and operational retrograde signaling, essential for thermotolerance during early seedling development.

## RESULTS

## GUN1 is indispensable for conferring thermotolerance during seedling development
Recognizing GUN1's role as a central integrator in biogenic retrograde signaling, we questioned whether the gun1 mutant could achieve photoautotrophic growth during germination under abiotic stress conditions, a question that has not been previously addressed. To test this, we analyzed the phenotypes of WT and gun1 seedlings grown from the beginning on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar medium under high‐ (~30°C) or normal‐ (~22°C) temperature conditions. Although WT seedlings germinated and turned green under HS, their photosynthetic activity, measured as PSII quantum yield (Fv/Fm), was significantly diminished compared to seedlings grown under normal conditions (Figure 1A, B). In contrast, gun1 seedlings were bleached entirely, indicating that ~30°C imposes stress on both WT and gun1 seedlings, albeit the effect is less pronounced in WT (Figure 1A). It is worth noting that, as previously reported (Wu et al., 2018), a fraction of gun1 seedlings also develop white and pale green cotyledons under normal conditions (Figure 1B).

The expression of GUN1 with a C‐terminal GFP tag, driven by the endogenous promoter, rescued the heat‐sensitive phenotype of gun1 (Figure 1C). Consistent with the bleached cotyledon phenotype, immunoblot analyses revealed a significant reduction in photosynthesis‐associated proteins encoded by both PhANGs (e.g., light‐harvesting chlorophyll a/b‐binding proteins LHCB1‐4) and PhAPGs (e.g., D1, D2, and CP43) in the gun1 mutant (Figure 1D, E), further supporting its heat‐sensitive phenotype. It is noteworthy that the LHCB2 protein level was less affected in gun1 under HS (Figure 1D, E). This may be because LHCB2 expression is most strongly derepressed in gun1, as it has been used as a marker gene in studies of GUN1‐dependent retrograde signaling (Zhao et al., 2019; Honkanen and Small, 2022; Veciana et al., 2022), and thus its corresponding protein level was less affected compared to other LHCB proteins. Consistent with previous reports (Wu et al., 2018), GUN1‐GFP protein accumulated rapidly during early chloroplast biogenesis (Figure 1F, G). Although GUN1‐GFP showed similar early accumulation under both control and HS (Figure 1G), transcript levels were markedly reduced under heat stress (Figure 1F), indicating that the apparent increase in protein abundance is likely due to enhanced protein stability rather than transcriptional induction.
The heat‐bleached cotyledons of gun1 seedlings failed to recover after they were transferred to normal conditions (Figure S1A). Although the true leaves of gun1 seedlings kept under continuous 10‐d heat stress appeared larger than those subjected to a 7‐d recovery period, their Fv/Fm values were lower (Figure S1B). This finding suggests that GUN1 is required for the full recovery of photosynthetic activity in true leaves during the post‐stress phase.

## Overexpression of GUN1 enhances thermotolerance
In stark contrast to the effect of the GUN1 mutation, the overexpression of GUN1 improved thermotolerance during seedling development (Figure 2A). Four independent gun1 transgenic lines expressing GUN1‐GFP, two under the constitutive 35S promoter (Pro35S; oeGUN1#6 and oeGUN1#13) and two under the native GUN1 promoter (ProGUN1; GUN1#39 and GUN1#5), showed a clear GUN1 dose‐dependent enhancement of thermotolerance. A strong positive correlation was evident between the abundance of GUN1 protein and seedling thermotolerance, as indicated by Fv/Fm values and steady‐state levels of PSII core proteins D1 and D2 (Figure 2B–D). Additionally, transmission electron‐microscopic images of mesophyll cells from these heat‐stressed seedlings revealed the critical role of GUN1 in maintaining chloroplast biogenesis under HS. While the chloroplasts from WT and gun1 transgenic seedlings expressing GUN1‐GFP under different promoters showed grana stacks along with linear stroma lamellae, these structures were completely absent in gun1 mutant seedlings under HS (Figure 2E).

## Biogenic retrograde signaling is crucial for maintaining thermotolerance
What causes the failure of thermotolerance in gun1 during early seedling development? Is GUN1‐mediated biogenic retrograde signaling (hereafter GUN1‐bioRS) essential for acquiring thermotolerance? GUN1‐bioRS represses GLK1/2 expression and their downstream target genes, including those encoding LHCB proteins, in the presence of NF or LIN (Waters et al., 2009; Martín et al., 2016; Tokumaru et al., 2017). As such, we examined the transcript abundance of LHCB1.4 in WT and gun1 during germination. Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis revealed that the GUN1 mutation led to the significant upregulation of LHCB1.4 relative to WT, especially under HS (Figure 3A). These results suggest that heat‐stressed plastids activate GUN1‐bioRS to repress LHCB1.4 expression as early as 12 h after the onset of imbibition; although less pronounced, LHCB1.4 was also upregulated in gun1 compared to WT under normal conditions. To further validate this, we analyzed the mRNA transcriptome affected by the absence of GUN1 under normal and HS conditions. Genome‐wide RNA sequencing and top‐enriched KEGG analyses revealed significant impairment in controlling the expression of PhANGs in gun1. After 12 h of imbibition under HS, 57 genes were significantly upregulated in gun1 relative to WT, with a fold change greater than 2 (|Log2FC| > 1) and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Among these, 19 were identified as PhANGs (Figure 3B; Table S1). Subsequent RT‐qPCR analysis also confirmed the significant upregulation of GLK1 and its target genes, such as LHCBs, in gun1 compared to WT (Figure 3C). Moreover, the expression ratio (30°C/22°C) analyses between WT and gun1 from RT‐qPCR and the mRNA transcriptome also indicate that the GUN1 mutation led to more obvious de‐repression of PhANGs compared to WT (Figure S2A, B).

Given that the GUN1–GLK1 module orchestrates bioRS and that the GLK1/2 transcription factors function redundantly (Fitter et al., 2002), we investigated the roles of GLK1/2 in GUN1‐dependent thermotolerance using a gun1glk1glk2 triple‐knockout mutant. The ensuing heat‐stress experiment underlined the vital role of GUN1‐bioRS, as evidenced by the substantial rescue of thermotolerance in gun1glk1glk2 seedlings (Figure 3D, E). Notably, glk1glk2 seedlings showed a higher Fv/Fm than WT seedlings, suggesting that slightly reduced expression of PhANGs might improve PSII quantum yield under HS (Figure 3E). The RT‐qPCR analysis also confirmed decreased expression of LHCBs in gun1glk1glk2 compared to gun1 (Figure S3A). Unlike PhANGs, no significant changes of PhAPGs (such as psbA and psbD) were observed in gun1 compared to WT (Figure S3A). Subsequent immunoblot analysis showed partially restored levels of LHCB proteins in gun1glk1glk2, with notably elevated levels of the psbA‐encoded D1 and psbD‐encoded D2 proteins (Figure S3B). We speculate that reduced oxidative stress in this background stabilizes PSII core proteins, thereby increasing their steady‐state levels. It is also worth noting that higher levels of LHCBs were observed only at an earlier stage (36 h after imbibition), prior to the appearance of the heat‐sensitive phenotype. Once the phenotype becomes apparent, LHCB protein levels decline markedly, consistent with the heat‐sensitive phenotypes of gun1 seedlings. This stage‐dependent pattern highlights the crucial role of GUN1 in early chloroplast biogenesis under HS. Furthermore, RT‐qPCR revealed that loss of GUN1 resulted in an ~2.7‐fold upregulation of LHCB1.4 and a more substantial ~6.1‐fold increase in LHCB2.2 under HS (Figure 3C). Consistently, immunoblotting showed ~3.3‐fold higher LHCB1 protein in gun1 compared to WT, while LHCB2 accumulated ~7.2‐fold under HS (Figure S3B). These findings suggest that GUN1‐mediated retrograde signaling differentially regulates LHCB isoforms and may contribute to the observed uncoupling between mRNA and protein levels during heat stress.

## Impaired biogenic retrograde signaling leads to ROS accumulation
In our previous work, we demonstrated that the uncoupled expression of PhANGs, particularly the upregulation of LHCBs relative to PhAPGs, results in elevated levels of 1O2 in the chloroplasts of the Arabidopsis lsd1 mutant (Lv et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2023). SA‐induced upregulation of PhANGs, particularly those encoding PSII LHCB proteins, induces an increase in 1O2 levels in chloroplasts. This, in turn, activates EX1‐mediated operational retrograde signaling, contributing to foliar cell death. Building on this previous finding, we hypothesized that the enhanced expression of PhANGs, partially driven by the derepression of GLK1/2, may similarly induce ROS, potentially contributing to the heat‐sensitive phenotype of gun1 (Figures 1A, S3A). We then assessed cellular ROS levels, including superoxide anion (O2 −), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and 1O2. As anticipated, seedling development under HS led to higher ROS accumulation in gun1 seedlings than in WT seedlings (Figure 4A). Furthermore, elevated ROS levels were no longer observed in gun1 with the loss of GLK1/2 (Figure 4A). We also observed stronger chlorophyll autofluorescence in gun1 compared to WT, while the loss of GLK1/2 reduced this effect (Figure 4A). The increased chlorophyll autofluorescence could be because of the imbalanced assembly of photosynthetic apparatus, which results in reduced photochemical quenching of the absorbed light energy (Baker, 2008; Grieco et al., 2012; Murchie and Lawson, 2013; Nickelsen and Rengstl, 2013; Zivcak et al., 2014). As a result, the energy can be dissipated through chlorophyll fluorescence or converted into heat or ROS.

Heat shock proteins (HSP) 17.4 and HSP17.6 A are known to be strongly induced by oxidative stress and H2O2 treatment (Sewelam et al., 2019). We also confirmed the significant upregulation of HSP17.4 and HSP17.6A (hereafter HSPs) in gun1 (Table S1). The uncoupled genome‐induced ROS generation might be responsible for the induction of HSPs in gun1 under HS. Consistent with the notion, the inactivation of GLK1/2 significantly attenuated the expression of HSPs in gun1 (Figure 4B). These findings suggest that elevated ROS levels contribute to cotyledon bleaching and inhibit chloroplast biogenesis in gun1.

## EX1‐dependent operational retrograde signaling is activated in gun1 under HS
We then inactivated the chloroplast 1O2 sensor EX1, hypothesizing that disrupting EX1‐mediated operational retrograde signaling (EX1‐opRS) could alter the gun1 phenotype. Indeed, loss of EX1 partially alleviated the heat sensitivity of gun1 (Figure 5A, B), suggesting that other ROS, such as O2 − and H2O2, may also contribute to cotyledon bleaching (Figure 4A). To further validate the role of EX1‐opRS in the HS‐induced gun1 phenotype, we expressed GFP‐tagged EX1 or a truncated EX1 lacking the C‐terminal 1O2 sensor (SOS) domain, previously annotated as the domain of unknown function 3506 (DUF3506), under the control of Pro35S. It was previously shown that the SOS domain is crucial for EX1's thylakoid membrane association and the perception of the critical 1O2 level (Dogra et al., 2019). Notably, overexpression of EX1ΔSOS‐GFP (oeEX1ΔSOS) in gun1ex1 failed to restore the heat‐sensitive phenotype of gun1. In contrast, overexpression of full‐length EX1‐GFP (oeEX1) fully reinstated the defect in gun1ex1 (Figure 5A, B), highlighting the importance of 1O2‐triggered activation of EX1 signaling in mediating the gun1 heat‐sensitive response (Figure 5A, B). The impact of EX1 loss in gun1 became more pronounced after 10 d of extended heat exposure (Figure 5A). Furthermore, ex1 seedlings showed enhanced thermotolerance compared to WT seedlings, as evidenced by a significantly higher Fv/Fm value (Figure 5A, B).

A recent report indicated that EX1 relocates into the nucleus upon the release of 1O2 in plastids (Li et al., 2023). However, the nuclear EX1 signal was not detected in Arabidopsis plants treated with mycotoxin, known to hijack EX1 signaling to promote foliar pathogenicity (Shi et al., 2024). Moreover, our recent study demonstrated that chloroplast‐localized EX1 proteins do not relocate to the nucleus upon 1O2 generation, as confirmed by confocal microscopy and subcellular fractionation assays (Liu et al., 2024). Likewise, the nuclear GFP signal was not observed in gun1ex1oeEX1 and gun1ex1oeEX1∆SOS seedlings grown under HS (Figure 5C). Nevertheless, consistent with the reduced heat sensitivity, the expression of HSPs was less prominent in gun1ex1 than in gun1 (Figure 5D).

## EX2 loss exacerbates heat sensitivity in gun1, opposing the role of EX1
Given the role of EX1 in contributing to the heat‐sensitive phenotype of gun1, we next examined the function of the EX1‐like protein EX2, whose loss is known to enhance EX1‐dependent 1O2 signaling (Dogra et al., 2022). Strikingly, the loss of EX2 further exacerbated the heat sensitivity of gun1 and significantly increased the expression of HSPs compared to gun1 (Figure 6A–C). The contrasting heat sensitivities between gun1ex1 and gun1ex2 validate our previous finding that EX2 antagonizes EX1 signaling under excessive 1O2 production in chloroplasts (Dogra et al., 2022). Consistently, ex1 mutants displayed enhanced thermotolerance (Figure 5A, B), whereas ex2 mutants showed reduced thermotolerance relative to WT (Figure 6A, B), reflecting their opposing roles in 1O2 signaling. Under normal conditions, gun1ex2 seedlings showed a higher proportion of white or pale green cotyledons compared with gun1, whereas these phenotypes were fully reverted in gun1ex1 (Figure 6D). Similarly, inactivation of GLK1/2 resulted in higher Fv/Fm values than WT, and the aberrant cotyledon phenotypes of gun1 were largely suppressed in gun1glk1glk2 seedlings (Figure S4).

The occurrence of white or pale green cotyledons in gun1 may reflect elevated PhANG expression and consequent ROS accumulation, although our ROS detection method was insufficiently sensitive to detect differences between gun1 and WT under normal conditions (Figure 4A).

## EX1 and EX2 oppositely modulate chloroplast biogenesis in true leaves under HS
We next asked whether EX1 and EX2 influence thermotolerant chloroplast biogenesis in true leaves. To address this, we examined chloroplast ultrastructure at different stages following heat treatment using transmission electron microscopy. In cotyledons, the loss of GUN1 severely disrupted chloroplast organization (Figure S5). Consistent with the restored plant phenotypes (Figures 3D, 5A), additional mutations in GLK1/2 or EX1 partially ameliorated these thylakoid defects in the gun1 background, resulting in more densely stacked grana (Figure S5). In contrast, the loss of EX2 failed to suppress chloroplast defects in gun1; the gun1ex2 double mutant showed thylakoid membranes similarly disorganized (Figure S5).
A comparable pattern was observed in true leaves. Compared to WT seedlings, gun1 mutants contained more disordered grana stacks (Figure S5). This disruption was further aggravated by the ex2 mutation, which resulted in a near‐complete loss of the thylakoid membrane system (Figure S5). Conversely, and in agreement with observations in true leaves (Figures 3D, 5A), GLK1/2 or EX1 mutations in the gun1 background largely preserved thylakoid architecture, maintaining intact and well‐defined grana stacks (Figure S5). Together, these findings indicate that the role of GUN1 in regulating chloroplast biogenesis under heat stress is consistent between cotyledons and true leaves, and that EX1 and EX2 exert opposing effects on this process.

## Upregulation of PhANGs in gun1 compared to WT under normal conditions
Given the substantial impact of EX1 and EX2 (Figures 5A, B, 6A, B) as well as on the emergence of white or pale green cotyledons in a fraction of gun1 seedlings under normal growth conditions (Figure 6D), it is reasonable to infer that the gun1 phenotype under normal conditions stems from the uncoupled expression of photosynthesis‐associated nuclear and plastid genes. To investigate this further, we performed a genome‐wide transcriptome analysis to compare the expression levels of PhANGs and PhAPGs in WT and gun1 after 24 h of imbibition under normal conditions. This time point was chosen based on the RT‐qPCR results in Figure 3A, which revealed the most pronounced difference in LHCB1.4 expression levels between the WT and gun1. As predicted, we detected significant upregulation of 42 PhANGs in gun1 compared to WT (Figure S6A; Table S2). Genes with at least a twofold change in expression and an FDR of less than 0.05 were considered to be significantly differentially expressed. However, no apparent changes in PhAPG expression were observed (Figure S6B; Table S2). These results suggest that the uncoupled expression of upregulated PhANGs and unchanged PhAPGs may impede chloroplast biogenesis following germination in gun1.

## GLK overexpression augments gun1 phenotypes through enhanced PhANG expression
Noting that reduced expression of PhANGs partially alleviates the heat sensitivity of gun1, we hypothesized that further increasing PhANG expression might exacerbate the gun1 phenotype under HS. To this end, we generated gun1glk1glk2 transgenic seedlings overexpressing GLK1‐GFP or GLK2‐GFP under Pro35S and assessed their effects on the gun1 phenotype. As previously reported, overexpression of these GLK transcription factors resulted in root greening (Figure S7A, B; Kobayashi et al., 2012). Subsequent RT‐qPCR and immunoblot analyses confirmed that the overexpression of GLK1‐GFP or GLK2‐GFP significantly increased the steady‐state levels of PhANGs and the corresponding protein abundance compared to both WT and gun1 (Figures 7A–C, S8A, B). In agreement with our hypothesis, these transgenic seedlings showed augmented cotyledon photobleaching under normal and HS (Figures 7D–F, S8C). In addition, either of their overexpression in the gun1glk1glk2 background resulted in elevated expression of HSPs (Figures 7G, S8D), contrasting with the reduced expression of HSPs seen in gun1glk1glk2 compared to gun1 (Figure 4B). Collectively, our findings indicate that the uncoupled expression of photosynthesis‐related genes (either due to impaired GUN1‐bioRS or GLK1/2 overexpression in gun1) negatively impacts chloroplast biogenesis. These changes are partially attributable to disrupted ROS homeostasis and activation of EX1/2‐dependent opRS.

## GUN1 overexpression may enhance thermotolerance in gun1 by suppressing PhANG expression
The cumulative findings of this study suggest that the heat sensitivity observed in gun1 mutants is closely associated with elevated levels of PhANGs, highlighting the necessity of GUN1‐bioRS in maintaining chloroplast biogenesis under HS by appropriately repressing PhANG expression. Supporting this, GUN1 overexpression results in a dose‐dependent, significant reduction in GLK1 and LHCB expression compared to WT and gun1 (Figure S9). Additionally, the inverse correlation between LHCB transcript levels and Fv/Fm values in WT, gun1, and two independent GUN1 overexpression lines (Figures 2B, S9) further supports the notion that heightened expression of PhANGs relative to PhAPGs drives the photobleaching of gun1 under HS. This highlights the importance of coordinated expression of photosynthesis‐associated nuclear and plastid genes as a central nexus linking biogenic and operational retrograde signaling under HS during early seedling development.

## No apparent defects in RNA editing in gun1 under HS
The role of GUN1 in RNA editing under conditions that interfere with RS prompted us to investigate whether this function is also required for thermotolerance, considering that chloroplast RNA editing is highly sensitive to heat (Zhao et al., 2019; Chu and Wei, 2020; Sun et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). We examined the known RNA‐editing sites of GUN1 under normal and HS (Zhao et al., 2019) and did not observe consistent significant alterations in the RNA‐editing efficiency of plastid genes in gun1 under HS (Figure S10). Therefore, whether GUN1‐mediated chloroplast RNA metabolism contributes to heat tolerance as a PPR protein remains to be investigated.

## DISCUSSION
Our study emphasizes the biological significance of genomes coupled expression, a concept introduced over three decades ago with the discovery of gun mutants by Professor Joanne Chory's group (Susek et al., 1993). Although the related biogenic retrograde signaling pathway has been extensively studied, to our surprise, the significance of genomes coupled expression under normal or stress conditions remains largely unexplored.
Among gun mutants, gun1 has been studied extensively due to its genomes' uncoupled expression phenotype on NF‐ or LIN‐supplemented media (Koussevitzky et al., 2007). GUN1 accumulates during early chloroplast biogenesis, and some gun1 seedlings show white or pale green cotyledons, suggesting that a threshold level of GUN1 is required for timely chloroplast development (Wu et al., 2018). Although GUN1 is constitutively transcribed, it undergoes continuous turnover via interaction with the chloroplast chaperone ClpC1, a component of the Clp protease system, promoting rapid degradation under non‐stress conditions. During early chloroplast biogenesis, however, GUN1 is stabilized. Consistently, we observed GUN1 stabilization under HS (Figure 1F, G), highlighting the importance of post‐translational regulation in facilitating chloroplast development. Furthermore, gun1 seedlings with white or pale green cotyledons show broad alterations in plastid transcripts, implicating GUN1 in plastid RNA metabolism (Tang et al., 2024).
Beyond its role in bioRS under LIN or NF conditions, our study demonstrates that GUN1 is critical for early chloroplast biogenesis under HS (Figure 1A). Photobleaching of cotyledons under HS likely results from impaired bioRS, as supported by the gun1glk1glk2 mutant, which lacks the key downstream transcription factors GLK1/2. Loss of GLK1/2 compromised PhANG expression and partially rescued the gun1 phenotype under HS (Figures 3D, E, S3A), underscoring GUN1's role in orchestrating nuclear–plastid gene expression during stress.
Our study did not clarify the mechanism by which GUN1‐bioRS mediates the transcriptional repression of GLK1/2. Recent studies have found that GUN1 is involved in the import of nuclear‐encoded chloroplast proteins, including ClpD, in the presence of NF or LIN (Wu et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2025). Consequently, the absence of GUN1 leads to the accumulation of non‐imported precursor proteins, triggering chloroplast precursor overaccumulation stress (cPOS) responses, which are associated with the downregulation of GLK1 and GLK2, as well as their downstream PhANGs (Waters et al., 2009; Hong et al., 2025). However, overexpression of ClpD and its accumulation as a precursor form in the cytosol were shown to increase ROS levels, contrasting with our finding that suppression of GLK1 and GLK2 alleviates the elevated ROS phenotype in gun1. Thus, it remains unclear whether the cPOS pathway contributes to the heat‐sensitive phenotype observed in this study.
The uncoupled expression of photosynthesis‐associated genes led to the activation of EX1/2‐dependent operational retrograde signaling, driven by an elevated level of 1O2 (Figures 4A, 5A, and 6A). We previously highlighted the critical role of EX1‐Trp‐643 oxidation within the C‐terminal SOS domain in initiating 1O2 signaling (Dogra et al., 2019, 2022). Experimental evidence includes substitutions of Trp‐643 with a 1O2‐insensitive residue or the complete deletion of the SOS domain, both of which lead to the abrogation of 1O2 signaling. While recent reports proposed the nuclear re‐localization of EX1 and its interaction with WRKY18/40 transcription factors following the release of 1O2 in plastids as essential for regulating the expression of 1O2‐responsive nuclear genes (Li et al., 2023), we could detect neither any nuclear signal from the EX1‐GFP fusion protein under HS (Figure 5C) nor its interaction with WRKY18/40 transcription factors (Liu et al., 2024). This observation persisted even when Arabidopsis mature plants were treated with a mycotoxin TeA that induces a 1O2 burst in chloroplasts and activates the EX1‐mediated retrograde signaling (Shi et al., 2024). Consequently, the suggested nuclear re‐localization of EX1 requires further validation.
The significant role of EX1‐mediated signaling in cotyledon bleaching suggests that the GUN1–GLK module is crucial for maintaining plastid ROS homeostasis during early seedling development. Recently, BBX16, a member of the BBX family, was identified as a novel GLK1 target, influencing photomorphogenesis and serving as a pivotal point in the activation of retrograde signaling (Veciana et al., 2022). As a downstream target of GLK1, BBX16 optimizes seedling de‐etiolation by ensuring effective photoprotection. Another study elucidates the role of GUN1‐mediated retrograde signaling in restricting chloroplast development during skotomorphogenesis and the early light response, thereby safeguarding seedling emergence from darkness (Hernández‐Verdeja et al., 2022). Additionally, BPG4 (Brz‐insensitive, pale green), whose expression is induced by brassinosteroid deficiency and light, interacts with GLK1/2 to suppress their activity (Tachibana et al., 2024). Hence, the loss of BPG4 reinforces the expression of PhANGs, accompanied by ROS bursts and reduced Fv/Fm. However, given the established role of GLK in LHCII biogenesis (Waters et al., 2009), we cannot exclude an alternative explanation for the elevated Fv/Fm in glk1glk2 under normal conditions—namely, that reduced LHCII accumulation, while the PSII core remains unaffected, could independently increase Fv/Fm regardless of ROS levels. This possibility warrants further investigation.
As independent studies elucidate the pivotal role of GUN1 in cold acclimation (Marino et al., 2019; Honkanen and Small, 2022), it is plausible to consider GUN1 as a potential temperature sensor, directing the coordinated expression of PhANGs in response to temperature variations. This idea challenges the widely accepted view of GUN1 as merely a master integrator of biogenic retrograde signals, suggesting instead that GUN1 may serve as a critical mediator linking chloroplast development with adaptive stress responses, thereby bridging biogenic and operational retrograde signaling pathways (Figure 8). Recent findings uncovering the diverse functions of GUN1 in tetrapyrrole biosynthesis (Shimizu et al., 2019), proteostasis (Marino et al., 2019), and protein import (Wu et al., 2019) also raise questions about their potential role in regulating the expression of PhANGs during early chloroplast biogenesis under both normal and stress conditions. Moving forward, further research is needed to investigate how these processes interact with GUN1's function in safeguarding chloroplast biogenesis under various environmental conditions.
Proposed model of GUN1‐dependent biogenic signaling in heat stress tolerance during early chloroplast biogenesis
Loss of GUN1 leads to de‐repression of GLK1/2, key transcription factors that promote chloroplast biogenesis, even under heat stress. This results in the uncoupled expression of PhANGs in response to heat‐stressed plastids, leading to the accumulation of chloroplast‐derived ROS, including 1O2, and ultimately the failure of chloroplast biogenesis. The nuclear‐encoded chloroplast protein EX1, a 1O2 sensor, detects elevated levels of 1O2 via its C‐terminal SOS domain and activates operational retrograde signaling to induce stress‐related gene expression. Whether this stress gene upregulation directly inhibits chloroplast biogenesis remains to be determined. Meanwhile, the EX1‐like protein EX2 antagonizes EX1, attenuating EX1‐mediated 1O2 signaling in gun1. This antagonistic interaction between EX1 and EX2 is consistent with the findings of Dogra et al. (2022).

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant materials and growth conditions
All Arabidopsis thaliana seeds used in this study were derived from the Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) ecotype and were harvested from plants grown under continuous light (CL) conditions (100 μmol m−2 s−1 at 22°C ± 2°C). The Arabidopsis mutant seeds of gun1 (SAIL_290_D09), glk1glk2 (Atglk1.1; Atglk2.1) (Fitter et al., 2002), ex1 (SALK_002088) (Lee et al., 2007), and ex2 (SALK_012127) (Lee et al., 2007), and the transgenic seeds of glk1glk2Pro35S:GLK1‐GFP and glk1glk2Pro35S:GLK2‐GFP were obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC) (Waters et al., 2008). The ex1Pro35S:EX1‐GFP line has been previously described (Wang et al., 2016). To generate the gun1glk1glk2, gun1ex1, gun1ex2, and the gun1glk1glk2 transgenic lines overexpressing GLK1‐GFP or GLK2‐GFP, glk1glk2, ex1, ex2, glk1glk2Pro35S:GLK1‐GFP, and glk1glk2Pro35S:GLK2‐GFP transgenic lines were crossed with the gun1 mutant. To create the gun1ex1 mutant overexpressing EX1‐GFP, the ex1Pro35S:EX1‐GFP line was crossed with the gun1ex1 mutant. The genotypes of all mutants were confirmed by PCR‐based analyses using the relevant primers listed in Table S3. Seeds were sterilized in a 1.6% (v/v) hypochlorite solution for 5 min, washed five times with sterilized water, and then sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands) supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) sucrose and 0.75% (w/v) Plant agar, pH 5.75. After 2 d of stratification at 4°C in the dark, seeds were germinated and grown under CL conditions.

## RNA extraction and RT‐qPCR analyses
Total RNA from three independent biological replicates of the indicated genotypes was extracted using the Universal Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Spin‐column) (BioTeke, Beijing, China). According to the manufacturer's recommendation, 1 μg of total RNAs was reverse‐transcribed with the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The RT‐qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech) on the CFX96 real‐time PCR detection system (Bio‐RAD, Hercules, CA, USA). Relative transcript levels of the selected genes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001) and normalized to ACTIN2 (At3g18780) transcript levels. The primer sequences used in this study are provided in Table S3.

## Vector construction and generation of transgenic plants
We amplified the cDNA of stop‐codon‐less GUN1 and EX1ΔSOS by a PCR‐based approach using the primers listed in Table S3. The amplified products were cloned into the pDONR221 Gateway vector (Invitrogen) by the Gateway BP reaction (Invitrogen). After DNA sequencing verification, the entry vector was recombined into the Gateway‐compatible plant binary vector pGWB505 for C‐terminal fusion with EGFP (enhanced green fluorescent protein) using a Gateway LR reaction (Invitrogen). To generate the native promoter‐controlled GUN1‐GFP construct, we performed cloning of the 1,053 bp native promoter fused to the GUN1 coding sequence (without the stop codon) into the pGWB504 vector using the primers listed in Table S3. The generated vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 by electroporation. Arabidopsis stable transgenic lines were generated using a floral dip transformation procedure (Clough and Bent, 1998), and homozygous transgenic plants were selected on MS medium containing 25 mg L−1 hygromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

## Protein extraction and immunoblot analysis
Total protein was extracted with homogenization buffer (0.0625 M Tris‐HCl (pH 6.8), 1% (w/v) SDS, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.01% (v/v) 2‐mercaptoethanol), quantified using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), separated on 10%–12% SDS‐PAGE gels, and transferred to an Immuno‐Blot polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio‐Rad). For D1, we used a 12% SDS‐PAGE gel to achieve clear separation of the precursor and mature forms, as per the company's instructions. GFP‐tagged proteins, LHCB1, LHCB2, LHCB3, LHCB4, D1, D2, and CP43, were immunochemically detected with mouse anti‐GFP (1:10,000; Roche), rabbit anti‐LHCB1 (1:4,000; Agrisera), rabbit anti‐LHCB2 (1:7,000; Agrisera), rabbit anti‐LHCB3 (1:4,000; Agrisera), rabbit anti‐LHCB4 (1:7,000; Agrisera), rabbit anti‐D1 (1:10,000; Agrisera), rabbit anti‐D2 (1:10,000; Agrisera), and rabbit anti‐CP43 (1:5,000; Agrisera), respectively. The rabbit anti‐H3 (1:5,000; Agrisera) antibody was used as a loading control.

## ROS determination
1O2, O2 −, and H2O2 were detected using singlet oxygen sensor green (SOSG), nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), and 3,3’‐diaminobenzidine (DAB), respectively. For 1O2 detection, seedlings were immersed in a solution of 50 mM SOSG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Molecular Probes) in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), vacuum‐infiltrated for 2 min, and incubated in the dark for 4 h. Imaging was performed using a GFP filter with a TCS SP8 (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany). The 1O2− activated SOSG was visualized by excitation at 488 nm and emission at 530 nm. All the images were obtained and processed using Leica LAS AF Lite software, version 2.6.3 (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany). For O2 − detection, the seedlings were submerged in NBT staining solution (0.1 mg mL−1 NBT in 25 mM Hepes buffer, pH 7.6) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 12 h. After staining, seedlings were transferred to 80% ethanol and heated at 70°C for 30 min to remove chlorophyll. Once cleared, the ethanol was replaced with 50% glycerol for imaging. For H2O2 detection, the seedlings were stained with DAB solution (0.1 mg mL−1 DAB in 50 mM Tris‐acetate buffer, pH 5.0) under vacuum for 2 min, and then incubated in darkness at room temperature for 30 h. Following incubation, seedlings were bleached, stored, and imaged as with the NBT staining.

## Determining photochemical efficiency
The photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm) was determined using a FluorCam system (FC800‐C/1010GFP; Photon Systems Instruments), which contains a CCD camera and an irradiation system, according to the instrument manufacturer's instructions.

## Chlorophyll determination
Root samples crushed into powder in liquid nitrogen were homogenized in 1 mL of 96% EtOH and heated to 80°C for 20 min. The debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 648 nm and 664 nm. The concentration of chlorophyll a/b was measured using the following formulas:
Chlorophyll a (μg mL−1) = 13.36A664 − 5.19A648
Chlorophyll b (μg mL−1) = 27.43A648 − 8.12A664
A664 and A648 are the absorbances at 664 nm and 648 nm, respectively. The chlorophyll content (chlorophyll a + b; μg g−1) was calculated based on the chlorophyll concentration and fresh weight.

## RNA‐Seq library construction and data analysis

## For nuclear transcriptome
Total RNA was extracted from WT and the gun1 mutant after 12 h of seed imbibition under normal (100 μmol m−2 s−1 at 22°C ± 2°C) or HS (100 μmol m−2 s−1 at 30°C ± 1°C) conditions. The Universal Total RNA Extraction Kit (BioTeke, Beijing, China) was used to extract the total RNA. OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) conducted the transcriptome sequencing and analysis. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq. 6000 platform. 150 bp paired‐end reads were generated. Approximately 45–52 million raw reads per sample were generated. Raw reads in FASTQ format were processed using fastp to remove low‐quality reads, resulting in clean reads. Around 43–51 million clean reads per sample were retained for subsequent analyses. The clean reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015). FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments) of each gene was calculated (Roberts et al., 2011), and the read counts of each gene were obtained using the HTSeq‐count (Anders et al., 2015). PCA analysis was performed using R (v 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological replication of samples. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq. 2. A q‐value < 0.05 and a fold change > 2 or < 0.5 were set as the thresholds for identifying significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

## For whole transcriptome
For RNA extraction, three biological replicates of WT and gun1 mutant after 24 h of imbibition under normal conditions (100 μmol m−2 s−1 at 22°C ± 2°C) were used. Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Waltham, MA, USA), and the concentration, quality, and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared from 2 μg of total RNA with the following modifications: Ribosomal RNA was removed using the Epicentre Ribo‐Zero™ rRNA Removal Kit (Human/Mouse/Rat). RNA fragmentation was performed using divalent cations at elevated temperatures in an Illumina proprietary fragmentation buffer. The first strand of cDNA was synthesized using RNA as a template and random oligonucleotide primers. RNase H was then used to degrade the RNA strand, followed by second‐strand cDNA synthesis using a DNA polymerase I system with dNTPs and dUTP instead of dTTP. The double‐stranded cDNA was purified, and double‐end repair was performed, introducing an “A” base at the 3’ end and ligating sequencing adapters. USER enzyme (NEB, USA) was added to degrade the second strand of cDNA containing uracil. To select cDNA fragments of 400–500 bp in length, library fragments were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA). DNA fragments with adapters ligated on both ends were enriched using a 15‐cycle PCR reaction using Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail. The products were purified (AMPure XP system) and quantified using the Agilent high‐sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The sequencing library was then sequenced on a NovaSeq. 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. FASTP (v0.22.0) software was used to filter the sequencing data, producing high‐quality sequences (clean data) for further analysis. The filtered reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis Columbia‐0 reference genome (TAIR10) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Genes with at least a twofold change in expression and an FDR of less than 0.05 were deemed to be differentially expressed. DEG were analyzed for KEGG pathway enrichment using ClusterProfiler (v4.6.0), focusing on significant pathways with P‐values.

## Subcellular localization and confocal laser‐scanning microscopy
For subcellular localization of EX1, cotyledons from gun1ex1 transgenic seedlings overexpressing EX1‐GFP or EX1▵SOS‐GFP were analyzed. GFP, chlorophyll, and DAPI fluorescence signals were detected using a TCS SP8 confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany) following a 10‐min infiltration in a 1 μg mL−1 DAPI solution (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). GFP was excited with an argon laser at 488 nm, and its fluorescence was collected at 500–550 nm. DAPI was excited by a violet (405 nm) laser line, and its emission was collected at 430–480 nm. Chlorophyll autofluorescence was detected between 650 nm and 750 nm with excitation at 488 nm. All the images were acquired and processed using Leica LAS AF Lite software, version 2.6.3 (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany).

## Transmission electron microscopy
Cotyledons from 5‐d‐old seedlings were excised and immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde containing 25% glutaraldehyde and 0.2 M phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.2). The samples were subjected to vacuum infiltration (four cycles) to ensure complete fixative penetration. The samples were then dehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions (30% to 100%) and embedded in Spurr Epoxy Resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA). Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut using a Leica UC7 Ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany) with an ultradry diamond knife (Diatome, Biel/Bienne, Switzerland) and then stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Transmission electron microscopy was performed on a Hitachi HT7700 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

## Statistical analyses
The statistical analyses were performed using web‐based software (https://astatsa.com/OneWay_Anova_with_TukeyHSD/) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, La Jolla, CA, USA), with detailed results provided in Table S4. ## Figure Figure 8: Proposed model of GUN1‐dependent biogenic signaling in heat stress tolerance during early chloroplast biogenesis Loss of GUN1 leads to de‐repression of GLK1/2, key transcription factors that promote chloroplast biogenesis, even under heat stress. This results in the uncoupled expression of PhANGs in response to heat‐stressed plastids, leading to the accumulation of chloroplast‐derived ROS, including 1O2, and ultimately the failure of chloroplast biogenesis. The nuclear‐encoded chloroplast protein EX1, a 1O2 sensor, detects elevated levels of 1O2 via its C‐terminal SOS domain and activates operational retrograde signaling to induce stress‐related gene expression. Whether this stress gene upregulation directly inhibits chloroplast biogenesis remains to be determined. Meanwhile, the EX1‐like protein EX2 antagonizes EX1, attenuating EX1‐mediated 1O2 signaling in gun1. This antagonistic interaction between EX1 and EX2 is consistent with the findings of Dogra et al. (2022).
