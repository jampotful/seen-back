Genome-wide characterization of the PHY gene family in octoploid strawberry reveals the critical role of FxaPHY15 in far-red light signaling and fruit ripening.

# Genome-wide characterization of the PHY gene family in octoploid strawberry reveals the critical role of FxaPHY15 in far-red light signaling and fruit ripening

## Abstract
BackgroundPhytochrome (PHY) genes are crucial for the appropriate growth, development, and environmental adaptation of plants. Nevertheless, the composition of the PHY gene family in octoploid strawberry and its underlying light - signaling mechanisms remain unclear.ResultsThis study identified 18 FxaPHY members distributed across the subgenomes, categorized into four distinct evolutionary clades. Notably, FxaPHY15 emerged as a key candidate due to its profound responsiveness to far-red (FR) light and its specific transcriptional peak during the fruit turning-color transition. Subcellular assays and protein interaction networks further confirmed that FxaPHY15 localizes to the nucleus and forms a high-confidence regulatory module with PIF3 (phytochrome-interacting factors 3), potentially orchestrating the transition from vegetative growth to fruit maturation.ConclusionsIn summary, this study provides the first comprehensive overview of the PHY gene family in octoploid strawberry and establishes FxaPHY15 as a potential important candidate gene mediating far-red light responses and fruit development, laying the foundation for light signal transduction research in strawberry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08649-w.

## Background
Light is a key environmental factor influencing plant growth and development. It serves as the energy source for photosynthesis and acts as a signaling molecule regulating plant morphogenesis, physiological metabolism, and quality formation [1–3]. The effects of light on plant growth are primarily manifested in three aspects: light quality, light intensity, and photoperiod [4]. Over the course of long-term evolution, plants have developed various photoreceptors to perceive light signals. The major recognized photoreceptor categories include phytochromes (PHY), cryptochromes (CRYs), phototropins (PHOT), UV-B receptors, and zeitlupe (ZTL) family proteins [5]. Among these, the PHY gene family plays the most prominent role. PHYs perceive and transmit light signals through conformational changes between Pr (the red-light-absorbing form) and Pfr (the far-red-light-absorbing form), thereby regulating multiple aspects of plant growth and development, and play a significant role in postharvest management [6, 7].
Red light can promote flower bud differentiation in fruit trees such as strawberries, grapes, and bayberries, thereby accelerating flowering process [8–10]. Similarly, far-red (FR) light can induce flower bud formation in certain photoperiod-sensitive fruits, such as pitayas [11]. Additionally, supplementary FR light significantly improves the quality and root development of grafted watermelon seedlings [12]. In tomatoes, nocturnal red light augments leaf number at the flowering stage, whereas diurnal FR light treatment reduces it [13]. Moreover, supplementary red light irradiation accelerates tomato ripening [14]. For grapes, red light irradiation extends their postharvest shelf life [15]. Red light also accelerates the ripening of fruits such as mangoes and pears by promoting chlorophyll degradation, carotenoid synthesis, and advancing the respiratory climacteric. However, in non-climacteric fruits like kiwifruit and melons, red light does not significantly promote ripening [16]. Furthermore, the red light/far-red light ratio (R/FR) is a critical environmental signal regulating plant growth and development [17]. In modern agriculture, precise regulation of the R/FR ratio can optimize crop architecture and improve crop quality, serving as an important technical support for efficient agricultural models such as protected cultivation and plant factories. For example, a lower R/FR ratio (< 1) promotes stem elongation, leaf expansion, and early flowering, thereby increasing the yield of crops like tomatoes and strawberries [18]. Conversely, a higher R/FR ratio (> 1) enhances robust plant growth, inhibits excessive elongation, and improves fruit quality and stress resistance [3, 19].
All PHY proteins contain three domains: PHY (PF00360), PAS (PF00989) and GAF (PF01590), and the PAS and GAF domains form the light-sensing core, which regulates chromophore assembly and modifies the spectral properties of mutant proteins [20]. The PHY domain is critical for fine-tuning the conformational stability of the Pfr form of phytochromes, ensuring it has appropriate spectral properties, nuclear localization, and kinase activity [21, 22]. Generally, the initial step in PHY signal transduction involves PHYA perceiving FR light or PHYB-PHYE perceiving red light, leading to conformational changes between Pr and Pfr, upon photoactivation, phytochromes translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus [23], where they interact with PIFs, triggering the phosphorylation and subsequent 26 S proteasome-mediated degradation of PIFs. Thus, phytochromes mediate photomorphogenesis by degrading PIFs, which act as negative regulators of photomorphogenesis [24].
Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa Duch.), a member of the genus Fragaria in the Rosaceae family, is one of the most economically significant fruit crops worldwide, valued for its unique flavor and high nutritional content. It exhibits significant sensitivity of seedling growth, flowering timing, and fruit ripening to light quality [25]. In modern horticulture, especially in greenhouse cultivation, supplemental lighting is a critical strategy to enhance yield and off-season production. However, the physiological responses of strawberries to different light spectra are complex and variety-specific, necessitating a deeper understanding of their light-sensing machinery. However, the composition of the PHY gene family in octoploid strawberry and its underlying light-signaling mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we performed a genome-wide identification and molecular characterization of PHY genes in strawberries. Our analysis encompassed multiple aspects, including gene family classification, gene structure, conserved protein domains, chromosomal localization, gene duplication, collinearity relationships, and cis-acting elements in the promoter regions. Additionally, we conducted in-depth analyses of the tissue-specific expression of PHY genes, the growth and development of strawberry plants under different light qualities, and the expression patterns of these genes. This study aims to provide a theoretical foundation for further investigation into the molecular mechanisms by which the PHY gene family mediates strawberry responses to varying light qualities.

## Materials and methods

## Identification of the FxaPHY gene in strawberry
The amino acid sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana PHY proteins were retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). The whole-genome coding sequences and amino acid sequences of strawberry were obtained from the GDR database (https://www.rosaceae.org/Analysis/18085091). A local BLAST database was constructed using BioEdit software, followed by a BLASTp search (e-value ≤ 1e-5) [26]. Candidate PHY protein sequences were then validated using the online tool CD-Search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi), and sequences with incomplete conserved domains were removed, resulting in the identification of PHY family members in strawberry.

## Physicochemical property analysis of the FxaPHY gene
The physicochemical properties of the FxaPHY protein, including molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were predicted using ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/). The subcellular localization was predicted using the Cell-PLoc 2.0 web server (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/).

## Construction of the phylogenetic tree of the FxaPHY gene
The full-length amino acid sequences of five species-namely Fragaria × ananassa (strawberry) FxaPHY, A.thaliana AtPHYs, Oryza sativa (rice) OsPHYs, Camellia sinensis (tea plant) CsPHYs and Brassica napus (rape) BraPHYs, were multi-sequence aligned using the ClustalW program in MEGA 7. Phylogenetic relationships were constructed using the neighbor-joining method, with clade support assessed via 1,000 bootstrap replicates. EvolView (https://www.evolgenius.info/) was used for further visualization of the phylogenetic tree [27].

## Analysis of the structure and protein conserved motifs of the FxaPHY gene
To delineate the structural architecture of the FxaPHY proteins, we employed the MEME (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) to identify conserved motifs with the following parameters: maximum number of motifs = 10, minimum number of motifs = 3, maximum motif width = 50, and an e-value threshold of < 1e-30. Subsequently, the structure and conserved motif map of the FxaPHY gene were visualized using TBtools-II [28].

## Chromosomal localization, genetic linkage, collinearity and Ka/Ks analysis
To examine gene duplication events across the four subgenomes, genomic synteny and chromosomal distribution were mapped using the TBtools-II platform. Additionally, the collinearity between A.thaliana AtPHY genes and strawberry FxaPHY genes was examined, and the Ka/Ks values of the syntenic gene pairs were calculated using TBtools-II software.

## Analysis of cis-acting elements in the promoters of the FxaPHY gene
The 2,000 bp sequence upstream of the start codon of the FxaPHY gene was extracted using TBtools-II software and designated as the promoter region. Cis-acting elements were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), and the results were visualized with TBtools-II.

## Tissue-specific expression analysis of the FxaPHY gene
The octoploid strawberry cultivar ‘Benihoppe’ was selected experimental material because it is the most widely cultivated strawberry in East Asia with high sensitivity to light quality. Importantly, its high-quality, T2T-level genome assembly [29] provides a precise foundation for the genome-wide identification and evolutionary analysis of the PHY gene family. The seedlings were planted in a greenhouse at the Zhengzhou Fruit Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Healthy plants with uniform growth and free from pests and diseases were selected. Different tissue organs and fruits at various developmental stages were collected as follows: roots (healthy middle sections of the main roots were selected and rinsed with sterile water); stems (robust middle stem segments were selected, with leaves and epidermal hairs removed); leaves (fully expanded functional leaves from the middle part of the plant were selected, with petioles removed); and flowers (healthy, fully open flowers at full bloom were selected, with sepals and pedicels removed). Fruits were collected at four key developmental stages: young fruit stage (7 days after flowering, YF), expansion fruit stage (14 days after flowering, EF), turning-color fruit stage (21 days after flowering, TF), and mature fruit stage (28 days after flowering, MF). For each stage, uniformly sized fruits from the middle and lower parts of the plant were selected, with surface impurities and pedicels removed. All collected samples were quickly cut into 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm pieces using sterile scissors, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 10 min, and then transferred to a -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer for storage. Total RNA extraction was subsequently performed, with three biological replicates for each tissue and fruit developmental stage.

## Analysis of the expression level of the FxaPHY gene under different light qualities
Seedlings of cultivar ‘Benihoppe’ with three true leaves and one heart leaf, showing health and consistent growth, were used in this study. The seedlings were pre-adapted in a controlled artificial climate chamber for 7 days under the following conditions: temperature 25 °C/22°C (day/night), light intensity of 80 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, photoperiod 16 h per day, and relative humidity of 60%-70% [30, 31]. After the adaptation period, the seedlings were randomly divided into four groups for different light quality treatments, with three biological replicates per group, each containing 10 seedlings. The specific conditions for each light quality treatment group were as follows: - White light group (CK): White LED light source (LED light boards 120 cm × 50 cm, Ningbo Yanghui Instrument Co., Ltd, China) with an intensity of 80 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. - Red light group (R): Red LED light source (wavelength 660 ± 10 nm) with an intensity of 80 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. - FR light group: FR LED light source (wavelength 730 ± 10 nm) with an intensity of 80 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. Red: Far-red 3:7 group (R: FR = 3:7): A combination of red (660 ± 10 nm) and FR (730 ± 10 nm) LED light sources, with a light intensity ratio of 3:7 and a total intensity of 80 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. After 10 days of light quality treatment, functional leaves (the third leaf) from each treatment group were collected. One leaf per seedling was sampled, and leaves from three seedlings per biological replicate were pooled to form one sample. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until RNA extraction.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis
Total RNA was extracted with the Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Bioman, BIO9209–50). The integrity and purity of the RNA were assessed through 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometry, respectively. Subsequently, high - quality RNA samples were reverse - transcribed into cDNA using the THERMOscript 1st Strand kit (Bioman, BIO9295–100) and stored at − 20 °C for subsequent analysis.
Based on the FxaPHY gene sequence, TBtools-II was used to batch-design qRT-PCR-specific primers for the FxaPHY gene (Supplementary Table S1). The Actin gene served as the internal reference gene (Actin forward primer: 5’-TGGGTTTGCTGGAGATGAT-3’, Actin reverse primer: 5’-CAGTTAGGAGAACTGGGTGC-3’) [32]. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Bioman, BIO9508-L) on a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Roche, LightCycler 480). The system was performed in a 20 µL volume containing 10 µL of 2 × SYBR Green mix, 1.0 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 2 µL of cDNA template. The amplification program was set as follows: denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 58 °C for 30 s. A final melt curve analysis was conducted to verify the specificity of the amplification. All reactions were carried out in technical triplicate.

## Functional analysis of the FxaPHY15 gene
Using the protein database of A.thaliana as the background, the online software STRING (http://stringdb.org) was employed to construct a functional protein association network related to the FxaPHY15 gene. A high-confidence threshold of 0.9 was applied as the minimum interaction score.
Specific primers (Forward primer: 5’-ATGTCGTCTTCAAGACCCAGTC-3’, Reverse primer: 5’-GGACCTGATCATATGTGCAGC-3’) were designed based on the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of the FxaPHY15 gene, excluding the stop codon. PCR amplification was performed using cDNA from ‘Benihoppe’ as the template. The target sequence was inserted into the pRI101-GFP vector via homologous recombination to construct the recombinant overexpression plasmid vector pRI101-FxaPHY15-GFP.
The recombinant plasmid and empty pRI101-GFP vector were introduced into Agrobacterium GV3101 via heat shock method, with positive clones selected on YEP plates containing rifampicin and kanamycin. Confirmed clones were grown in YEP liquid medium (28℃, 200 rpm) to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. Cells were then harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, and 200 µM acetosyringone, pH 5.6) to a final OD600 of 0.5. Healthy leaves of Nicotiana tabacum were selected, and the Agrobacterium infiltration solution was injected into the lower epidermis using a needleless syringe. Infiltrated leaves were first maintained in darkness for 24 h and then transferred to standard growth conditions (16-h light/8-h dark) for 24–48 h. The treated leaves were examined under a laser scanning confocal microscopy (STELLARIS, Leica) with GFP fluorescence signals detected using 488 nm excitation light. Fluorescence distribution was analyzed by overlaying with the nuclear localization marker mCherry. The empty pRI101-GFP-transformed material served as the negative control.

## Statistical analysis
The relative expression levels of the strawberry FxaPHY gene in different tissues and under different light qualities treatments were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [33]. Heat maps of FxaPHY gene expression across different tissues were generated using TBtools-II, while bar charts illustrating relative gene expression levels and the significance of differences were created with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

## Results

## Physicochemical properties of the FxaPHY gene family
The physicochemical properties of the FxaPHY gene family were analyzed through homologous sequence alignment with the AtPHY proteins of A. thaliana. A total of 18 PHY genes were identified in cultivar ‘Benihoppe’ and designated as FxaPHY1 to FxaPHY18 based on their chromosomal locations. Predictions were made regarding the lengths of their coding sequences, the sizes of the encoded proteins, and various physicochemical properties, including relative molecular weights, isoelectric points, and GRAVY values. Notable differences in physicochemical characteristics were observed among the encoded proteins of the FxaPHY gene family. The open reading frame (ORF) lengths of FxaPHY genes ranged from 1,329 bp to 6,105 bp, with amino acid counts varying from 442 to 2,034 residues. Molecular weights ranged from 48.93 kDa to 226.69 kDa, while isoelectric points ranged from 5.33 to 6.49, all below 7. Except for FxaPHY2, the GRAVY values of the remaining 17 members were negative, indicating that they are predominantly acidic and hydrophilic proteins. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that all FxaPHY proteins are localized within the nucleus (Table 1).

## Evolutionary analysis of the FxaPHY gene family
To investigate the evolutionary relationships of FxaPHYs, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using five AtPHY members from A.thaliana, four OsPHYs from rice, six CsPHYs from tea plant, five BraPHYs from rape, and 18 FxaPHY family genes from strawberry (Fig. 1). The 18 FxaPHYs were classified into four subfamilies: PHYA, PHYB, PHYC and PHYE. Specifically, members FxaPHY11/13/15/17 and FxaPHY7–10 clustered within the PHYA and PHYB subfamilies respectively. Meanwhile, the PHYC and PHYE subfamilies were composed of FxaPHY12/14/16/18 and FxaPHY1–6. Additionally, FxaPHYs had closer genetic relationships with AtPHYs, CsPHYs, and BraPHYs than with OsPHYs, possibly because the former are dicotyledonous plants, whereas the latter is a monocotyledonous plant, which indicates evolutionary divergence following the monocot - dicot split.

## Analysis of structure, protein domain and motif of the FxaPHY gene
The FxaPHY gene family exhibited diverse gene structures across its various subfamilies (Fig. 2A and B). Gene structure analysis revealed that a majority of FxaPHYs (13/18; 72.2%) exhibited a conserved arrangement of four CDSs. In contrast, significant structural divergence was observed in several members: FxaPHY7 and FxaPHY9 possessed highly fragmented structures containing 17 and 19 CDSs, respectively, while FxaPHY13 and FxaPHY17 harbored only two. Beyond CDS variation, FxaPHY2, FxaPHY13, and FxaPHY17 lacked both 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs). Truncated UTRs were also noted in FxaPHY6/11 (missing 5’ UTR) and FxaPHY9 (missing 3’ UTR). Particularly, FxaPHY18 was uniquely characterized by the presence of two complete UTRs at each of its two termini, suggesting a potentially sophisticated regulatory mechanism governing its role in strawberry development.

Motif and domain analysis showed that motifs 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, and 10 are highly conserved across all FxaPHY members. While 14 of the 18 proteins harboedr the full complement of ten motifs, structural truncation was observed in several members: FxaPHY2 and FxaPHY6 lack motifs 3, 4, 7, and 8, while FxaPHY13 and FxaPHY17 specifically lack motif 8 (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the N - terminal structure of all FxaPHYs includes the characteristic PAS_2, GAF, and PHY domains (Supplementary Fig. S1). The mapping of these motifs onto known domains indicated that motifs 6, 10, and 5 correspond to the PAS_2, GAF, and PHY regions respectively (Fig. 2D), which validates their identity as typical phytochromes.

## Analysis of chromosomal location, genetic linkage and collinearity
Chromosomal mapping revealed that FxaPHYs were distributed across 14 chromosomes, predominantly near the ends (Fig. 3A). Single genes were mapped to 10 chromosomes (s1A/B/D, 3A/B/C, and 4 A/B/C/D), while subgroup 6 chromosomes (6 A–D) featured two genes each. Although no duplication events were found, 20 linked gene pairs—distributed across PHYA, B, C, and E subfamilies—were identified (Fig. 3B). Syntenic analysis between strawberry and Arabidopsis yielded 16 gene pairs (11 FxaPHYs and 5 AtPHYs; Fig. 3C). The evolutionary constraint on these genes was high, as evidenced by Ka/Ks ratios significantly lower than 1 (average 0.1454), reflecting the conserved nature of FxaPHY genes across eudicots (Supplementary Table S2).

## Analysis of cis-acting elements of the FxaPHYs gene promoter
To elucidate the potential functional roles of the genes within the FxaPHY family, cis-acting elements in their promoter regions were analyzed using the PlantCARE database. This analysis identified a total of 64 distinct types of cis-acting elements (Supplementary Table S3), predominantly associated with responses to light, hormonal regulation, and various stress conditions.
The FxaPHY promoters were enriched with motifs governing light and hormonal cues. Light - responsive elements, such as the G - box, ACE, and TCT - motifs, were highly prevalent. Moreover, a diverse array of hormone - sensitive elements was detected, specifically the ABRE (abscisic acid), TGA - element/AuxRR - core (auxin), ERE (ethylene), TCTGTTG (gibberellin), TGACG - motif (MeJA), and TCA - element (salicylic acid). This indicates complex cross - talk in FxaPHY transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, elements involved in defense and stress responses—such as ARE, DRE core, GC-motif, LTR, STRE, TC-rich repeats, W box, WRE3, and WUN-motif—were identified, along with those related to growth and development, including as-1, AAGAA-motif, O2-site, CCGTCC-motif, and cat-box. Additionally, binding sites for MYB and MYC transcription factors were observed (Fig. 4A).

A total of 787 cis-acting elements were identified across the FxaPHY gene family. Notably, the FxaPHY10 gene contained the highest number of responsive elements (67), including the greatest number of light-responsive elements (22), whereas FxaPHY16 exhibited the fewest responsive elements (16) and the lowest number of light-responsive elements (4) (Fig. 4B). Quantitative analysis revealed that light-responsive elements comprised the largest proportion (22.4%) of the identified cis-elements. These findings suggested that the expression of FxaPHY genes may be predominantly regulated by light signals, highlighting their significant role in photomorphogenic processes.

## Tissue-specific expression analysis of the FxaPHY gene
To characterize the spatiotemporal expression profiles of the FxaPHY family during growth and development, an expression atlas was constructed for 18 members including roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and multiple fruit developmental stages (Fig. 5). The results revealed significant divergence in spatiotemporal expression patterns among FxaPHY genes. Specifically, FxaPHY8, FxaPHY10, FxaPHY12 and FxaPHY16 maintained consistently high transcription levels across all examined tissues, reflecting a constitutive expression pattern. Conversely, FxaPHY3 and FxaPHY9 exhibited distinct tissue preferences for the stem, with expression levels reaching 4.78 and 4.55, respectively. In root tissues, FxaPHY11 displayed the highest transcriptional abundance (5.92), suggesting its potential involvement in subterranean light perception.

Notably, FxaPHY15 demonstrated a highly representative tissue- and developmental stage-specific expression profile. Beyond its significant transcriptional activity in roots (4.10), FxaPHY15 was uniquely characterized by a developmental induction pattern during fruit maturation. Its expression increased progressively alongside fruit development, peaking at the turning-color fruit stage (5.31) and establishing it as the most abundant family member during this phase. This dual expression dominance in both roots and ripening fruits—distinguishing it from members such as FxaPHY1 and FxaPHY3 which were downregulated during maturation—strongly suggests that FxaPHY15 acts as a core regulator in coordinating root photomorphogenesis and the non-climacteric ripening process in octoploid strawberry.

## Expression levels of the FxaPHY gene under various light quality conditions
In order to further validate the reactions of the FxaPHY gene family to different light quality. Eleven of the 18 FxaPHY genes showed patterns of differential expression, according to qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 6). The FxaPHY family displayed divergent expression profiles in response to R and FR light signals. While FxaPHY1 and FxaPHY3 were down-regulated by R light, members such as FxaPHY7, 8, and 11–18 were significantly up-regulated. Under FR light and a 3:7 R/FR ratio, a similar inhibitory effect was observed for FxaPHY1, 3, 16, and 18, while other family members were induced. Notably, FxaPHY7, 8, 15, and 17 were constitutively up-regulated regardless of the light quality. FxaPHY15, in particular, showed a striking dose-dependent-like induction, reaching 1.21-, 2.79-, and 3.22-fold of CK levels under R, FR, and 3:7 R/FR treatments. These results identify FxaPHY15 as a key integrator of R/FR light signaling in strawberry.

## Functional analysis of FxaPHY15
In order to predict the potential nuclear interaction partners of FxaPHY15—a PHYA subfamily member—an analysis was carried out utilizing the STRING database. The results identified several candidate interactors involved in light signaling, including the transcription factor PIF3, photoreceptors CRY1 and CRY2, and signaling components COP1 and FHL (Fig. 7A). Notably, the FxaPHY15–PIF3 interaction exhibited the highest confidence level (STRING score: 0.999), indicating a highly specific and robust binding affinity. This strongly supports a stable and selective molecular interaction between FxaPHY15 and PIF3. Consistent with its predicted role in transcriptional regulation, subcellular localization assays demonstrated that FxaPHY15 overlaps precisely with nuclear-specific markers (Fig. 7B). This nuclear-localized distribution establishes the necessary spatial framework for its function as a light-signaling mediator.

## Discussion
The copy number of PHY gene family members varies significantly across plant species, a diversity that is tightly linked to genome size, ploidy level, and evolutionary trajectory. Among diploid species, Brassica oleracea (6), Brassica rapa (5), Setaria italica (4), Camellia sinensis (6), and Magnolia spp. (5) exhibit distinct subfamily distribution patterns. Polyploid species typically possess expanded PHY repertoires; for instance, hexaploid wheat harbors 8–9 genes, while tetraploid Arachis hypogaea, Brassica juncea, and Brassica napus contain 8, 10, and 9 members, respectively.In this study, bioinformatic analysis identified 18 FxaPHY genes in cultivated strawberry, a count that substantially exceeds those reported in other polyploids. Such expansion is consistent with the polyploidization history of strawberry, specifically its octoploid background ($2n = 8x = 56$), which inherently multiplies the genomic repertoire through its four constituent subgenomes. While the model plant A.thaliana possesses five PHY genes (PHYA–PHYE) representing five unique subfamilies [34], the 18 FxaPHY genes were categorized into only four subfamilies. This categorization aligns with observations in cotton but contrasts with gramineous species (e.g., Oryza sativa, Zea mays, and wheat), which maintain only three subfamilies (PHYA, PHYB, and PHYC) [35, 36]. Such discrepancies support the notion that certain subfamilies, particularly PHYD, are frequently dispensable or have been lost across diverse evolutionary lineages [36].
The gene structure of PHY members varies across lineages; while species like tea [37] and cotton [38] typically harbor 4–6 CDS, FxaPHYs in strawberry display a much higher degree of structural complexity (1–19 CDS), similar to the architecture found in foxtail millet [39]. Spatially, the biased distribution of FxaPHYs toward chromosomal termini mirrors the genomic localization seen in cotton and Magnolia [38, 40]. Central to understanding their evolutionary trajectory is the Ka/Ks ratio. Given that Ka/Ks < 1 indicates strong purifying selection and sequence conservation, our results—showing ratios consistently below 1 for FxaPHY/AtPHY pairs—confirm that these genes are under intense functional constraint. This evolutionary conservation is a shared trait among Brassica and cotton PHY families [38, 41], reflecting the critical role these photoreceptors play in plant development. This selective pressure is vital for maintaining the functional and structural coherence of the indispensable PAS, GAF, and PHY motifs [20]. Even when peripheral structural modifications occur, such as the addition of 5’ introns, the preservation of these core motifs ensures functional stability, reinforcing the conclusion that FxaPHYs have been maintained by purifying selection to safeguard their sensory functions.
In this study, FxaPHY7, 8, 15, and 17 were induced by both R and FR light. While this broad-spectrum responsiveness deviates from the specialized R/FR sensitivity of typical PHYA and PHYB, it likely reflects functional differentiation and synergistic action within the strawberry phytochrome family. A similar co-regulatory pattern has been documented for PHYA and PHYB2 in tomato [42]. Our observation that FxaPHY15 exhibits a 2.79-fold induction under FR light suggests it has retained, or perhaps intensified, the ancestral FR-sensing function of the PHYA clade. This is further supported by our phylogenetic analysis, where FxaPHY15 clustered with PHYA orthologs from Arabidopsis, rice, and tea plant. Functional conservation across species is well-documented; for instance, PHYA overexpression in rice, tobacco, and turfgrass enhances FR sensitivity, leading to altered phenotypes such as dwarfing or improved shade tolerance [43–45]. Our findings suggest that FxaPHY15 shares core functional roles in regulating photomorphogenesis and development in strawberry. The observed nuclear localization of FxaPHY15 implies that it activates downstream signaling via a conserved nuclear transport pathway. Intriguingly, the high-confidence interaction between FxaPHY15 and PIF3 (STRING score: 0.999) aligns with the classical model of phytochrome-mediated degradation of negative regulators. In model plants, FR-activated PHYA inhibits hypocotyl elongation by binding its C-terminal output module (phyOPM) to the APA motif of PIFs [24, 46, 47]. Notably, the interaction between FxaPHY15 and FHL—a key facilitator of PHYA nuclear import—suggests that FxaPHY15 follows the ancestral nuclear trafficking route to exert its regulatory influence. The high-confidence FxaPHY15–PIF3 interaction, coupled with potential links to COP1 and CRY1/2, positions FxaPHY15 as a central hub for integrating FR signals with other environmental cues, such as blue light and endogenous hormonal pathways [48, 49].
Furthermore, the potential associations of FxaPHY15 with CRY1/2 and COP1 suggest its involvement in integrating multiple light signals and modulating protein stability, a regulatory pattern well-characterized in A. thaliana [50, 51]. Given that PHYA is the sole phytochrome dedicated to FR sensing, the high-confidence FxaPHY15–PIF3 interaction highlights its specialized role in the FR response. Such associations potentially enable molecular crosstalk with JA-mediated defense or the endogenous clock, allowing the plant to strategically navigate the competing demands of growth and the shade-avoidance syndrome [46, 51]. Collectively, these predicted interactions verify the functional conservation of FxaPHY15 and provide a robust foundation for future research into strawberry adaptation to complex light environments. The identification of FxaPHY15 as a key FR sensor provides a promising molecular target for ‘Light-Recipes’ in facility horticulture, where supplemental FR lighting could be used to precisely regulate strawberry architecture and fruit quality.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study provides a comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the PHY gene family in strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa), identifying 18 members across four evolutionary subfamilies. Our findings highlight FxaPHY15 might as a central mediator of light signaling, evidenced by its significant induction under FR light and its specific expression pattern during the fruit turning-color fruit stage. The nuclear localization of FxaPHY15 and its high-confidence interaction with PIF3 suggest a conserved yet specialized molecular mechanism in strawberry. These results not only broaden our understanding of phytochrome-mediated light perception in complex polyploid species but also identify FxaPHY15 as a promising genetic target for optimizing fruit ripening and quality through light environment manipulation in protected horticulture.
