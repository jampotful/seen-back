Integrated omics revealed regulatory mechanisms governing potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) seedling growth during shade avoidance syndrome.

# Integrated omics revealed regulatory mechanisms governing potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) seedling growth during shade avoidance syndrome

## Abstract
BackgroundPotato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is the fourth largest food crop worldwide with significant economic value and importance for food security. Shade avoidance syndrome (SAS) considerably affects crop architecture and productivity in high-density planting systems; however, its molecular mechanisms in potato remain poorly understood. MethodsPotato seedlings were subjected to four light treatments: white light (control, WL), low blue light (LBL, simulating blue light attenuation by plant canopies), low red: far-red ratio (WL + FR, simulating far-red reflection from neighboring plants), and their combination (LBL + FR, simulating complete plant shade environment). An integrated analysis including morphological characterization, leaf anatomical observations, hormone quantification, transcriptome sequencing, and metabolite profiling was performed to investigate plant responses to these conditions.ResultsMorphological analysis revealed that WL + FR primarily induced internode elongation (+ 20.0%) and leaf hyponasty, while LBL promoted stem elongation through increased node production (+ 36.3%). When combined, these signals (LBL + FR) synergistically enhanced stem elongation by 79.3%. Anatomical examination showed that LBL-treated leaves formed thickened palisade tissue layers (176.34 μm) with 6–7 layers of spongy tissue, whereas WL + FR resulted in thinner leaves (151.22 μm). Hormone profiling revealed that LBL increased gibberellic acid 3 (GA₃) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) levels, while WL + FR further elevated IAA. LBL + FR markedly increased zeatin riboside (ZR) level, highlighting intricate hormonal crosstalk underlying shade-avoidance responses. Transcriptomic analysis identified 6,057 differentially expressed genes enriched in photosynthesis, hormone signaling, and carbohydrate metabolism pathways. A total of 1,168 differentially accumulated metabolites were detected across treatments, particularly organic acids and lipids associated with TCA (tricarboxylic acid) cycle and starch-sucrose metabolism. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified PHYTOCHROME A (PHYA) as the central hub gene coordinating SAS responses under combined light stress, contrasting with the PHYB-centric model established in Arabidopsis.ConclusionsThese findings provide comprehensive insights into potato-specific SAS regulatory networks and contribute to understanding crop-specific light sensing strategies, with potential applications for breeding shade-tolerant varieties and applications for optimizing plant architecture in high-density planting systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08687-4.

## Introduction
Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is the fourth major food crop worldwide, playing a critical role in global food security [1, 2]. Driven by the need to maximize land-use efficiency, modern agricultural systems are increasingly shifting toward high-density planting and intercropping [3]. While potato has been characterized as a relatively shade-tolerant crop suitable for agroforestry [4], excessive or prolonged shading in high-density systems imposes substantial light-quality stress. More critically, global climate change is exacerbating the environmental impacts and ecological vulnerability of these intensive agricultural systems [5, 6]. The increasing frequency of extreme climatic events interacts with altered light environments, creating complex physiological challenges that severely compromise both crop growth and sustainable development goals [7].
In dense canopies, plants experience a spectral shift characterized by a decreased red-to-far-red (R: FR) ratio and the attenuation of blue light (low blue light, LBL). These signals trigger the shade avoidance syndrome (SAS), manifested as stem elongation, reduced branching, and accelerated senescence [8–10]. While SAS aids light competition in natural ecosystems [11], it is detrimental in agriculture as it diverts photoassimilates from storage organs to stem growth. In potato, shading has been shown to inhibit stolon formation and significantly reduce tuber numbers, thereby limiting the sink capacity [12]. Furthermore, simulated shade increases susceptibility to the necrotrophic pathogen Botrytis cinerea, revealing a conserved trade-off between growth and immunity [13].
Plants perceive light signals through diverse photoreceptors. Phytochromes (PHYs) detect R: FR changes [14, 15], while cryptochromes (CRYs) sense blue/UV-A light [16, 17]. Additionally, the circadian sensor ZEITLUPE (ZTL) [18] and phototropins (PHOTs) also contribute to light monitoring [19]. Notably, potato phototropins (StPHOTs) have been shown to interact with StNRL-9 to regulate blight resistance [20]. Under high R: FR conditions, phytochrome B (PHYB) exists predominantly in its biologically active Pfr state, which represses Phytochrome-Interacting Factors (PIFs). Canopy shading lowers the R: FR ratio, triggering the photoconversion of PHYB to its inactive Pr state. This inactivation stabilizes PIF proteins, which subsequently activate downstream auxin biosynthesis to drive elongation [21–25]. Simultaneously, the attenuation of blue light under SAS-inducing conditions prevents CRYs from photo-dependent homodimerization into physiologically active photobodies [17]. The absence of this photoactivation relieves the CRY-mediated repression of the E3 ubiquitin ligase COP1, subsequently altering the stability of downstream targets including PIFs [26]. Crucially, while both signals converge on PIFs to promote elongation, they recruit distinct downstream modules: the low R: FR response is predominantly auxin-driven, whereas LBL signaling often engages gibberellin (GA) metabolism and direct cell wall loosening mechanisms [27, 28].Unlike model species such as Arabidopsis, potato faces a unique developmental challenge: it requires highly coordinated resource allocation between aboveground photosynthetic source tissues and belowground storage sinks. Under high-density cultivation, an excessive SAS response disrupts this source-sink balance, reducing potato yield by 20–35% [12]. Thus, understanding the molecular regulation of SAS in potato has attracted considerable research interest. Early transgenic studies demonstrated that overexpressing PHYB suppresses shade-induced elongation and increases tuber yield by improving canopy photosynthetic rates [29, 30]. Recently, Gómez-Ocampo et al. [13] provided the first comprehensive transcriptomic and physiological characterization of SAS in wild-type potato (S. tuberosum var. Spunta), identifying the shade-induced upregulation of FT, PAR1b, and WRKY-like genes alongside the downregulation of photosynthetic transcripts. Building on this, Han et al. [31] functionally characterized StPIF3 as a positive SAS regulator, providing the first direct evidence of PIF-mediated shade responses in potato. Despite these substantial advances in understanding low R: FR signaling, the functional role of CRYs in mediating the LBL response remains largely uncharacterized in potato. It remains to be determined whether the CRY–GA signaling module identified in soybean [27] is evolutionarily conserved in tuber crops. While targeted photoreceptor engineering has successfully improved shade tolerance in various crops—such as PHYB overexpression in rice [32] and GmCRY1s manipulation in soybean [27]—similar CRY-based breeding strategies remain largely unexplored in potato. This represents a critical knowledge gap that remains to be addressed to optimize potato architecture for dense planting.
In this study, we employed an integrative multi-omics approach to investigate how low R: FR, LBL, and their combination affect potato seedling development. Given the gap in understanding CRY function in this crop, we specifically aimed to: (i) characterize physiological responses to each spectral component; (ii) identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differential metabolites (DIMs); and (iii) construct a regulatory network of light-quality signaling. This study provides critical insights into potato-specific light signal transduction and establish a molecular framework for breeding climate-smart, shade-tolerant varieties, thereby contributing to sustainable food security.

## Materials and methods

## Plant materials and experimental design
The experiment utilized virus-free tissue culture plantlets of potato (S. tuberosum L. cv. Atlantic), originally provided by the Agricultural Biotechnology Research Center, Ningxia Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences. This cultivar was selected for its vigorous growth characteristics and broad agricultural adaptability. To ensure a uniform genetic background and a pathogen-free status, all experimental materials were clonally propagated in vitro from a single initial virus-free plantlet and subcultured for three consecutive generations prior to the experiment.
After 20 days of growth in the final in vitro subculture cycle, uniform plantlets were carefully selected and transferred to individual pots containing soil. The plants were then grown for 14 days under controlled conditions: 16 h light/8 h dark, 60% relative humidity, and 23–25 °C. Four distinct light treatments were established using adjustable LED panels (Biorun Bio, Wuhan, China). These included white light (WL), LBL, low R: FR ratio (WL + FR), and a combination of LBL and WL + FR (LBL + FR). For the WL treatment, full-spectrum white LEDs with peak wavelengths at 450 nm and 570 nm were used to simulate natural daylight. The LBL treatment was achieved by filtering the white light through two layers of yellow filter (#101, Lee Filters, CA, USA) as previously described [33, 34]. This filtration reduced blue light intensity from 9.8 to 1.5 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. For the WL + FR treatment, white light was supplemented with far-red light (730 nm) to achieve an R: FR ratio of 0.41, substantially lower than 9.15 in WL. The LBL + FR treatment combined the lighting conditions of both LBL and WL + FR. Each treatment group comprised 18 plants.
Photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) was kept constant at 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ across all treatments to isolate the effects of light spectral composition rather than differences in light intensity [27]. Light quality and intensity were measured using a HiPoint HR-350 spectrometer (380–780 nm, ± 2 nm). R: FR ratios were calculated as previously described by Franklin [9]. Detailed spectral parameters, including photon flux densities for blue (400–500 nm), red (600–700 nm), and far-red (730 nm) regions, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

## Morphological characterization
To evaluate the effects of different light treatments on plant architecture, key morphological traits were assessed on the 14th day of treatment. Ten biological replicates were evaluated per treatment (n = 10). Plant height, defined as the vertical distance from the soil surface to the apical meristem of the main stem, was recorded for each plant, along with the number of internodes along the main stem. The length of individual internode was measured, and mean internode length was calculated. Branch number and petiole length were also measured. The leaf angle of the fourth fully expanded leaf, counting downwards from the stem apex, was measured using ImageJ software.

## Leaf anatomical analysis
To examine light-induced anatomical alterations, leaf tissue samples were collected from the terminal leaflet of the third fully expanded leaf from the stem apex. From these selected leaflets, approximately 5 × 5 mm segments were excised from the mid-lamina region, avoiding the midrib to minimize variability in tissue thickness. Immediately after excision, samples were fixed in FAA solution (formalin-acetic acid-alcohol, 5:5:90, v/v/v) for 24 h, dehydrated through an ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Transverse sections, 8 μm thick, were prepared using a rotary microtome and stained with Safranin-Fast Green. Sections were mounted on glass slides and examined under an optical microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan). For each section, three randomly selected regions were analyzed to determine the thickness of the entire leaf blade, upper epidermis, lower epidermis, palisade tissue, and spongy tissue. All thickness measurements were recorded in micrometers (µm) [35]. Six independent plants were sampled per treatment (n = 6).

## Measurement of photosynthetic pigments and anthocyanin content
To assess the effects of light treatments on photosynthetic capacity and stress response, the content of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and anthocyanin was measured. The terminal leaflet of the third fully expanded leaf from the stem apex was collected from three independent plants per treatment (n = 3).
For chlorophyll determination, approximately 0.1 g of fresh leaf tissue was finely ground with 1 mL of 80% acetone and a small amount of quartz sand in dim light. The homogenate was transferred to a 10 mL volumetric flask, and the mortar was rinsed with 80% acetone to ensure complete transfer, bringing the final volume to 10 mL. After dark extraction for 3 h until the residue turned white, the supernatant was collected. Absorbance values at 663 nm and 645 nm were measured using a microplate reader (Plant chlorophyll test kit; Mengxi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, Suzhou, China). Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll content were calculated using established empirical formulas.
For anthocyanin content, approximately 0.1 g of oven-dried leaf tissue (60 °C to constant weight) was used. Samples were ground and homogenized with 1 mL of extraction solution, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 2 h. After centrifugation at 8000×g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant was collected. Anthocyanin content was determined using the pH differential method with a microplate reader (Plant total anthocyanin test kit; Mengxi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), measuring absorbance at 530 nm and 700 nm in solutions adjusted to pH 1.0 and pH 4.5. The content was calculated based on the absorbance difference and formula provided in the kit.

## Quantification of endogenous hormones
To assess the effects of different light qualities on plant hormones, the contents of gibberellic acid 3 (GA3), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and zeatin riboside (ZR) were determined. Terminal leaflet of the third fully expanded leaf from the stem apex was collected from three independent plants per treatment (n = 3), immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until extraction. Hormone quantification was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (Shimadzu LC-30AD, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with mass spectrometry (AB Sciex Qtrap 6500; MA, USA) (HPLC–MS/MS). Standards for GA3, IAA, and ZR were procured from Yuanye Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Chromatographic grade methanol and acetonitrile were obtained from Merck (Germany).

## RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis
Terminal leaflet of the third fully expanded leaf was collected, with three biological replicates per treatment. Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of fresh tissue using TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). RNA integrity was rigorously assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN > 8.0). Sequencing libraries were generated using 2 µg of total RNA per sample and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (paired-end, 150 bp). Raw data were filtered using the Fastx-Toolkit software. Through featureCounts (v2.0.3), raw counts for each gene were quantified. Gene expression levels were quantified as reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM). Differential expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package (v. 4.8.2). Genes with |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and padj < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment of DEGs were performed using Blast2GO [36].
Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA R package [37]. An adjacency matrix was constructed and transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Gene modules were identified using a dynamic tree cut algorithm with a minimum module size of 30 genes, and similar modules were merged at a module eigengene distance threshold of 0.25. Modules showing module–trait correlations > 0.5 with specific light treatments were considered treatment-associated. Resulting networks were visualized in Cytoscape, and hub genes were defined based on their intramodular connectivity.

## Metabolite extraction and metabolomic analysis
Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on the terminal leaflet of the third fully expanded leaf from six independent plants per treatment (n = 6). Leaf tissue samples (80 mg) were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder. Metabolites were extracted with 1000 µL of methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v/v), centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min, vacuum-dried, and reconstituted in 100 µL of acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) solution. The mixture was then centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min before injection.
Metabolic profiling was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Agilent 1290 Infinity LC, CA, USA) coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600, MA, USA) (UHPLC–QTOF–MS). For hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) separation, a 2.1 mm × 100 mm ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide 1.7 μm column was used. In both positive and negative ESI modes, mobile phase A consisted of 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide in water, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5–7 min, 95–65% B; 7–8 min, 65–40% B; 8–9 min, 40% B; 9–9.1 min, 40–95% B; and 9.1–12.1 min, 95% B for re-equilibration. The ESI source conditions were: Ion Source Gas1 and Gas2, 60; curtain gas, 30; ion source temperature, 600 °C; and IonSpray voltage, + 5500 V.
Metabolite annotation was performed using the in-house database of Shanghai Applied Protein Technology, which contains a subset of metabolites validated with authentic standards, together with four public MS/MS spectral databases, including MassBank, METLIN, HMDB, and MoNA. To improve annotation reliability, metabolite annotation was based on three complementary criteria: accurate mass matching between experimental features and reference entries with a mass error < 25 ppm, MS/MS fragmentation pattern matching, and retention time (RT) matching when available. This integrated strategy reduced false-positive annotations and improved metabolite annotation accuracy in complex biological samples. Based on these criteria, most metabolites included in downstream analyses were annotated at Level 2 confidence, whereas metabolites supported by authentic standards in the in-house database were assigned a higher confidence level.
DIMs were identified based on a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score > 1 and a Fold Change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using the R package MetaboAnalystR (v. 4.0) with data log2-transformed and mean-centered. In addition, 200 permutation tests were conducted to assess model robustness and avoid overfitting.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis
Total RNA was extracted as described in 2.6. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan). The qRT-PCR reaction was performed in a 20 µL total volume containing 1 µL of diluted cDNA (1:10), 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, and 0.4 µM of each primer. The reactions were conducted on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. The cycling conditions were: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed from 60 °C to 95 °C to verify primer specificity.
The potato elongation factor 1-alpha gene (StEf1α) was used as an internal reference to calculate relative gene expression using the 2 − ΔΔCt method [35]. Candidate genes were key regulators involved in zeatin biosynthesis and plant hormone signal transduction. These included two glucosyltransferases (PGSC0003DMG400008137 and PGSC0003DMG400029490), an indole-3-acetic acid-amido synthetase GH3.6 (PGSC0003DMG400019274), a zeatin O-glucosyltransferase (PGSC0003DMG400028331), and a zeatin O-xylosyltransferase (PGSC0003DMG400029491). All gene sequences and corresponding IDs were retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/) for S. tuberosum under the Potato Genome Sequencing Consortium (PGSC) nomenclature. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Each group included three biological replicates, with three technical replicates per biological replicate.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. For data meeting assumptions, one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was conducted. For non-normal data, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction was applied. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

## Results

## Different light treatments led to distinct morphological changes in potato seedlings
To investigate SAS in potato, we established three controlled light treatments mimicking canopy shade, with WL serving as the control (Fig. 1A). Potato plants exhibited pronounced SAS phenotypes under both LBL and WL + FR conditions (Fig. 1B). Compared with WL, LBL and WL + FR triggered stem elongation by 20.0% and 36.3%, respectively, whereas the combined treatment (LBL + FR) produced a synergistic enhancement by 79.3% (Fig. 1C). Internode analysis revealed that low R: FR and LBL + FR promoted elongation primarily by increasing internode spacing rather than internode numbers, whereas LBL induced elongation through increased internode numbers. Notably, LBL-induced elongation was not attributable to changes in internode length or number individually (Fig. 1D-E), suggesting a cumulative morphological effect. Furthermore, light treatments significantly remodeled leaf architecture. Specifically, the combined LBL + FR treatment markedly suppressed plant branching (Fig. 1F). All simulated shade treatments significantly reduced petiole length compared with the WL control (Fig. 1G). LBL decreased leaf angles, resulting in more upright leaves, whereas low R: FR induced hyponasty, leading to increased leaf angles (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Table S3). These findings suggest that potato SAS may integrate distinct light-quality signals through both additive and synergistic interactions, with specific morphological outcomes depending on the spectral alterations.

## Simulated shade treatments induced changes in anatomical structure and photosynthetic pigments in potato leaves
To explore the effects of light quality on potato leaf anatomy and photosynthetic pigments, structural and pigment alterations were analyzed under different light treatments. Leaf anatomical structure exhibited significant alterations among treatments. All three shade treatments reduced intercellular space compared with WL. Leaf thickness differed markedly, with LBL-treated leaves being the thickest and WL + FR-treated leaves the thinnest. Layer-specific analysis showed that the upper epidermis was thickest under WL + FR and thinnest under LBL, while the lower epidermis was thickest under WL and thinnest under LBL + FR. In addition, LBL + FR treatment produced the greatest palisade and spongy tissue thicknesses, whereas the thinnest spongy tissue was observed under WL (Fig. 2A-D). Overall, LBL and LBL + FR treatments resulted in thicker leaves, with enlarged palisade cells and increased spongy tissue layers (Supplementary Table S4), suggesting adaptive responses to altered light quality.

Photosynthetic pigment analysis also revealed significant variation across treatments. Chlorophyll a/b ratio peaked under WL (2.44) and was lowest under LBL (2.38), indicating that light quality affected the relative abundance of photosystem I and II components (Fig. 2E). Total chlorophyll content was highest under LBL + FR (1.52 mg/g FW), moderate under WL (1.25 mg/g FW), and lowest under LBL (0.86 mg/g FW) (Fig. 2F). In contrast, LBL exposure triggered a massive accumulation of anthocyanins (17.72 µg g⁻¹ FW), representing a 7.7-fold increase compared with WL controls (2.29 µg g⁻¹ FW) (Fig. 2G). These results suggest that LBL conditions promote structural adjustments for improved light capture while simultaneously inducing anthocyanin accumulation that may contribute to photoprotection.

## Simulated shade treatments altered endogenous hormone levels in potato leaves
Key endogenous hormones were quantified in potato leaves under different light treatments. GA₃ level was highest under LBL (0.90 µg/g), but declined under WL + FR (0.73 µg/g) and LBL + FR (0.60 µg/g) (Fig. 3A). IAA level increased from 20.53 ng/g under WL to 26.21 ng/g under LBL, peaking at 38.88 ng/g under WL + FR, with LBL + FR showing an intermediate level (28.61 ng/g) (Fig. 3B). In contrast, ZR level was significantly elevated under LBL + FR (1.44 µg/g) compared with WL (0.33 µg/g) and LBL (0.21 µg/g) (Fig. 3C). These shifts may suggest intricate hormonal crosstalk under shade treatments.

## Transcriptomic profiling revealed light quality-dependent signaling pathways
To investigate transcriptomic responses of potato leaves under SAS-associated light treatments, RNA-sequencing was performed. High-quality sequencing data (≥ 6 Gb per sample, Q20 ≥ 97.72%, Q30 ≥ 93.38%, mapping rate 86.67%-86.80%) were obtained from 12 samples (three biological replicates per treatment). PCA revealed a clear separation of gene expression profiles among treatments (Fig. 4A). A total of 6,057 DEGs were identified (|log2 fold change| ≥ 1, padj < 0.05). Compared with WL, WL + FR, LBL + FR, and LBL resulted in 2,057, 3,090, and 4,212 DEGs, respectively. Notably, LBL induced the most substantial transcriptional changes, highlighting its strong impact on gene expression (Fig. 4B).

GO enrichment analysis showed that DEGs between LBL and WL were enriched in photosynthesis-related pathways, while those between LBL + FR and WL were associated with photosynthesis-light harvesting (Fig. S1). Venn diagram analysis showed that LBL induced the highest number of DEGs compared with other treatments. Further KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs in the three SAS-inducing groups were associated with carbohydrate metabolism and plant hormone signal transduction, consistent with the hormonal changes observed in Sect. 3.3 (Fig. 4C). Cluster analysis grouped DEGs into six expression patterns, with WL + FR- and LBL-induced DEGs enriched in hormone signal transduction pathways, and LBL + FR-induced DEGs enriched in the TCA cycle (Fig. S2).

## Expression patterns of key pathway genes under different light quality treatments in potato
To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying potato responses to shade-associated light treatments, we examined the relative expression patterns of genes involved in key pathways identified by KEGG enrichment analysis, including photosynthesis-antenna proteins (Fig. 5A), photosynthesis (Fig. 5B), starch and sucrose metabolism (Fig. 5C), and plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. 5D). Most DEGs in the photosynthesis-antenna proteins pathway were downregulated in WL + FR and LBL + FR, whereas LBL induced only mild suppression. Notably, PGSC0003DMG400004301, which encodes the chlorophyll a/b-binding protein type I (a photosystem II 10 kDa protein), was upregulated under LBL treatment, potentially contributing to the differential photosynthetic efficiency observed across treatments (Sect. 3.2). In contrast, two chlorophyll a/b-binding protein genes (PGSC0003DMG400008564 and PGSC0003DMG400019248) were strongly suppressed under LBL + FR treatment, indicating a severely compromised light-harvesting capacity (Fig. 5A).

In the photosynthesis pathway, LBL and LBL + FR triggered extensive downregulation of DEGs, whereas WL + FR induced a mixed pattern of gene expression alterations (Fig. 5B). Genes involved in starch and sucrose metabolism exhibited treatment-specific expression patterns. Under LBL treatment, genes encoding β-D-glucan hydrolase (PGSC0003DMG400003013), hexokinase 6 (PGSC0003DMG400013187), β-glucosidase (PGSC0003DMG400025239), and fructokinase (PGSC0003DMG400026916) were upregulated, with fold changes ranging from 1.8 to 2.3. In contrast, LBL + FR specifically induced the expression of trehalose-6-phosphate synthase and glucose-1-phosphate adenylyltransferase genes (PGSC0003DMG400028467, PGSC0003DMG400046891), with an average increase of 2.7-fold (Fig. 5C). These expression changes may suggest a shift in carbon allocation to support shade avoidance responses.
Plant hormone signal transduction pathways showed distinct expression patterns between WL + FR and LBL (Fig. 5D). In the auxin signaling pathway, LBL significantly upregulated 21 DEGs, including auxin response factors (ARFs), GH3s, auxin/indole-3-acetic acids (AUX/IAAs), and small auxin up-regulated RNA (SAUR), while WL + FR downregulated most of these genes, except for six auxin-related genes, with an average fold change of 1.9. In the gibberellin signaling pathway, the DELLA protein gene GAI (PGSC0003DMG400019212) was upregulated under WL + FR by 2.1-fold. These transcriptomic trends aligned with the endogenous hormone levels (Sect. 3.3) and may underlie the observed shade avoidance phenotypes. Expression patterns of hormone- and glucose metabolism-related genes were validated using qRT-PCR, consistent with RNA-seq data (Fig. S3).
In the circadian clock and photoreceptor regulation pathway, five key circadian rhythm-related genes exhibited light-quality-dependent expression patterns. For instance, Cryptochrome 1b (CRY1b, PGSC0003DMG400008545) was upregulated under WL (1.7-fold), but downregulated under LBL (2.2-fold); CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1, PGSC0003DMG400013334) was suppressed under both LBL and LBL + FR treatments (1.9–2.3-fold); Long Hypocotyl 5 (HY5, PGSC0003DMG401020784) was strongly induced under WL + FR (2.5-fold); and Phytochrome A (PHYA, PGSC0003DMG400015766) showed marked upregulation under LBL + FR (3.1-fold). These results highlight how potato perceives light quality via photoreceptors to initiate shade avoidance responses (Fig. 5E).

## Metabolomic profiling of potato leaves under different light quality treatments
PCA of metabolomic data demonstrated high reproducibility across biological replicates, with clear separation of treatment groups along PC1 (91.9% variance). Notably, samples from WL and LBL + FR were positioned furthest apart along the PC1 axis, while LBL and WL + FR occupied intermediate positions (Fig. 6A). These results reflected distinct metabolite accumulation patterns under different light quality conditions.

A total of 1,168 metabolites were identified, encompassing organic acids and derivatives (257), lipids and lipid-like molecules (224), organoheterocyclic compounds (146), and benzenoids (113) (Fig. 6B;Supplementary Table S8). Using thresholds of VIP > 1 and fold change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67, we detected 186 DIMs between LBL and WL, 260 DIMs between LBL + FR and WL, and 210 DIMs between WL + FR and WL. Organic acids and derivatives represented the most substantial changes across comparisons (Fig. 6C). Hierarchical clustering further revealed contrasting accumulation trends between WL and LBL + FR (Fig. 6D), underscoring the strong metabolic reprograming triggered by combined metabolic divergence induced by LBL + FR.
KEGG enrichment analysis of DIMs identified nine significantly altered amino acid metabolism pathways, including alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism; arginine and proline metabolism; arginine biosynthesis; D-amino acid metabolism, etc. These pathways were markedly affected under LBL + FR and WL + FR, with less disruption observed under LBL alone, suggesting that a low R: FR ratio may drive pronounced shifts in amino acid homeostasis, particularly in organic acid derivatives (Fig. S4). Additionally, five carbohydrate metabolism pathways were enriched, including citrate cycle (TCA cycle), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway, pyruvate metabolism, and starch and sucrose metabolism. These findings aligned with transcriptomic data (Sect. 3.4), implicating energy mobilization may serve as a key adaptive response to shade signals. The upregulation of these pathways under LBL + FR correlates with the observed stem elongation (Sect. 3.1) and likely supports rapid growth during SAS .

## Light quality-associated co-expression networks in potato revealed by WGCNA
To explore the gene co-expression networks associated with distinct light quality treatments, WGCNA was performed using transcriptomic data. To construct a scale-free network, a series of soft-thresholding powers were tested. A soft-threshold power of β = 19 was selected because it resulted in a scale-free topology fit index (R² = 0.965) with a slope of − 1.60, while maintaining adequate network connectivity (mean connectivity = 31.4) (Supplementary Table S5). Based on this threshold, a weighted adjacency matrix was constructed and genes were grouped into modules using hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting. This analysis revealed five co-expression modules, four of which exhibited significant correlations with specific light conditions. Among these, the yellow and green modules showed positive associations with the LBL + FR treatment, suggesting their involvement in mediating potato responses to combined LBL + FR signaling. The turquoise module was positively correlated with LBL alone, likely representing a gene set specialized in LBL perception. In contrast, the brown module displayed a negative correlation with LBL + FR (Fig. 7A). Functional annotation of hub genes within each module further supported their potential regulatory roles (Supplementary Table S6).

The top 500 gene pairs in each module were visualized based on their weight values, and the five genes with the highest node degree were designated as hub genes. In the green module, two prominent hub genes were identified: PHYA and a 67-kDa chloroplastic RNA-binding protein (PGSC0003DMG400013362). Both were markedly upregulated under LBL + FR (Fig. 7B-E). The central role of PHYA in SAS implies its contribution to the regulation of hormone signaling pathways, consistent with the changes in hormone levels observed under LBL + FR (Sect. 3.3). In the yellow module, hub genes included a trigger factor (PGSC0003DMG400022300), a uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis (PGSC0003DMG400027602), a ribosomal protein subunit binding protein β subunit (PGSC0003DMG400002537), and a chloroplast-localized 28-kDa ribonucleoprotein (PGSC0003DMG400015155) (Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that this module may play a role in chloroplast function and translation regulation under LBL + FR.
In the turquoise module, several hub genes were induced by LBL, including a lactoylglutathione lyase (PGSC0003DMG400016448), a transcription factor (PGSC0003DMG400003736), and a protein tyrosine phosphatase 1 (PTP-1, PGSC0003DMG400030842) (Fig. 7D). This suggests their involvement in blue light perception and downstream signaling, consistent with phenotypic observations (Sect. 3.1), where LBL treatment promoted stem elongation without altering leaf angle, indicating a regulatory mechanism distinct from that of LBL + FR-induced SAS.

## Discussion
SAS represents a critical adaptive strategy that enables heliophytic plants to compete for limited light resources through morphological plasticity, such as stem elongation and leaf repositioning [38, 39]. However, excessive shade avoidance responses in crop species can negatively affect plant architecture, lead to lodging, reduced photosynthetic efficiency, and yield reduction [40]. This issue is particularly relevant for potato, a species increasingly cultivated under high-density planting systems where canopy shading frequently occurs [41]. While SAS is classically induced by a low R: FR ratio [42–44], LBL triggers distinct phenotypic adjustments [45, 46], suggesting the involvement of alternative signaling pathways. Our study unveils a PHYA-centered regulatory paradigm for SAS in potato, contrasting with the PHYB-dominated model in Arabidopsis [47–51]. Under combined LBL + FR stress, PHYA emerged as a hub gene (Fig. 7E), potentially compensating for PHYB inactivation—a mechanism previously observed in other plant species [42, 52]. These findings suggest that potato may rely more on PHYA-mediated signaling to cope with complex canopy shade environments.

## Divergent hormonal strategies for stem elongation
The contrasting regulation of GA3 and IAA under LBL and WL + FR (Fig. 3) reveals distinct hormonal strategies underlying stem elongation in potato. WL + FR treatment significantly increased IAA levels (38.88 ng/g), consistent with the well-established role of auxin in promoting cell elongation during shade avoidance in dicot species [21, 22, 53, 54]. In contrast, LBL specifically stimulated GA3 (0.90 µg/g), suggesting that GAs play a more prominent role in potato responses to blue-light deficiency [55]. Notably, GA3 in potato appeared to promote node formation rather than internode elongation (Fig. 1D-E), suggesting that potato prioritizes structural reinforcement over vertical growth under blue light deficiency, possibly to prevent lodging in high-density planting. This pattern contrasts with Arabidopsis and monocots such as rice and maize, where GA primarily promotes internode elongation [56, 57], underscoring the need for crop-specific SAS models. This potato-specific strategy likely reflects a trade-off between investing resources in competitive shoot growth and preserving carbon reserves for tuber development—a metabolic priority unique to storage crop species.
A similar growth–allocation trade-off has been observed in other storage crops. In sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas), shade conditions promote vine elongation while simultaneously reducing storage root formation due to the diversion of assimilates toward shoot growth [58, 59]. Likewise, in cassava (Manihot esculenta), shading significantly decreases root biomass while stimulating stem elongation and leaf expansion [60, 61]. These studies indicate that storage crops often experience a more pronounced source–sink conflict during shade responses than grain crops. Our findings suggest that potato may employ a modified hormonal strategy to alleviate this trade-off, potentially allowing moderate shoot elongation without markedly compromising structural integrity or tuber development.

## Photoreceptor crosstalk and metabolic costs
Photoreceptor interactions play a key role in coordinating plant responses to fluctuating light environments. In our study, the downregulation of CRY1b under LBL conditions was accompanied by a marked increase in anthocyanin accumulation, indicating enhanced oxidative stress responses [62]. In Arabidopsis, CRY1 inactivation under LBL increases reactive oxygen species (ROS), necessitating anthocyanin-mediated ROS scavenging [63, 64]. However, potato accumulated ~ 8-fold more anthocyanin under LBL than WL + FR (Fig. 2F), suggesting stronger oxidative stress sensitivity—a trait possibly inherited from its shade-tolerant wild relatives [46, 65, 66]. Similar stress-induced metabolic responses have been reported in sweet potato and cassava, where shading alters flavonoid biosynthesis and antioxidant metabolism [67–69]. These protective mechanisms may help maintain cellular homeostasis when photosynthetic efficiency is compromised under suboptimal light conditions. Conversely, WL + FR-treated plants prioritized starch-to-sucrose conversion (Fig. 7C), likely to support rapid internode elongation at the expense of tuber carbohydrate reserves [70]. Such carbohydrate remobilization during shade avoidance has also been observed in sweet potato, where exposure of storage roots to light reduced starch content, indicating that carbon allocation in storage organs can be dynamically altered under non‑optimal light conditions [71]. These distinct metabolic strategies emphasize the need to optimize SAS in potato by balancing shoot elongation with tuber sink strength, an aspect often overlooked in model species.

## Toward shade-resilient potato varieties
The co-expression of PHYA and chloroplastic RNA-binding protein under LBL + FR (Fig. 7B, E) may suggest the involvement of retrograde signaling between plastids and nuclei in SAS regulation [72]. In multiple plant species, chloroplast-derived signals have been shown to influence nuclear gene expression, thereby coordinating photosynthetic efficiency and stress responses [73, 74]. Furthermore, our transcriptomic analysis highlights trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) as a potential mediator of carbon partitioning (Fig. 5C). T6P is widely recognized as a key signaling molecule linking sucrose status with plant growth and development. In cereal crops such as wheat, manipulation of the T6P pathway has been shown to improve carbohydrate allocation and yield stability under stress conditions [75]. A comparable regulatory role may exist in potato, where T6P could coordinate the balance between shade-induced shoot growth and tuber filling. Field trials modulating R: FR ratios during tuber bulking phase could validate these candidates, as demonstrated in maize [75]. From a breeding perspective, genes such as PHYA and T6P-related regulators represent promising targets for improving shade tolerance in potato. Genome-editing approaches, including CRISPR/Cas-based modification of light signaling pathways, may allow the development of cultivars that maintain optimal plant architecture while preserving tuber yield under dense planting systems.

## Limitations and future directions
While our controlled-environment study provides insights into potential molecular mechanisms underlying SAS, field validation under natural canopy shade—where dynamic light fluctuations occur—is essential. Additionally, spatiotemporal hormone profiling (e.g., cell-type-specific IAA/GA3 gradients) would help elucidate how potato coordinates organ-level growth responses. Comparative analyses with shade-tolerant Andean wild potato species may uncover novel alleles associated with SAS, offering promising targets for breeding shade-resilient cultivars.
