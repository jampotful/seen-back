Phytochrome B and the methyltransferase complex coordinately regulate N6-methyladenosine RNA modification and shade avoidance responses in Arabidopsis
Fengquan Li, Yué Zeng, Jiayu Wang, Lin Li (2026). Plant Communications. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xplc.2026.101789. doi:10.1016/j.xplc.2026.101789. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Phytochrome B and the methyltransferase complex coordinately regulate N6-methyladenosine RNA modification and shade avoidance responses in Arabidopsis

## Abstract
AbstractShade avoidance is a critical adaptive strategy that plants employ to respond to competition. N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant and dynamic mRNA modification, has emerged as a key epigenetic mechanism regulating adaptive responses. Here, we demonstrate that shade significantly reduces global m6A levels in a manner dependent on phytochrome B (phyB) and the m6A methyltransferase complex (MTC). Shade attenuates the interaction between phyB and the MTC, impairing both the RNA-binding capacity and methyltransferase activity of the complex. Transcriptome-wide m6A profiling revealed that shade treatment and phyB mutation similarly reduce m6A modification, particularly on transcripts involved in auxin and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling. Phenotypic analyses further showed that the MTC is required for shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and ABA hypersensitivity. Mechanistically, shade-induced m6A reduction enhances the stability of SAUR15/19/20 transcripts while decreasing the translation efficiency of ABA signaling repressors SCHNARCHZAPFEN, MYB20, and PROPEP3. Our findings uncover a mechanism by which phyB modulates m6A deposition to fine-tune plant elongation growth and stress adaptation under shade.Key words: shade avoidance, phyB, N6-methyladenosine, m6A methyltransferase complex

## Introduction
Under dense planting conditions, plants absorb red and blue light for photosynthesis while reflecting far-red (FR) light, enabling neighboring plants to perceive reductions in the red-to-far-red (R/FR) light ratio. Changes in the light environment caused by surrounding vegetation are rapidly perceived and trigger morphogenetic adjustments in plants. Shade-avoiding plants exhibit a series of adaptive responses to escape shading, including hypocotyl and petiole elongation, increased leaf hyponasty, accelerated flowering, and other developmental adjustments. These responses are collectively termed the shade avoidance syndrome (SAS) (Casal, 2012, 2013). In agricultural systems, SAS in shade-intolerant crops restricts planting density and ultimately limits yield (Ballaré and Pierik, 2017).
In Arabidopsis, phytochrome B (phyB) functions as the major red/far-red light photoreceptor, interconverting between the red-light-absorbing form Pr and the far-red-light-absorbing form Pfr (Rockwell and Lagarias, 2020). The phyB mutant exhibits a constitutive shade-avoidance phenotype, indicating that phyB acts as a key negative regulator of SAS (Reed et al., 1993). Under white light (WL) conditions, photoactivated phyB translocates to the nucleus and forms speckle-like photobodies, where it recruits interacting proteins such as phytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs) to regulate their activity (Chen et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023). Under low R/FR conditions, dispersal of phyB condensates releases PIFs, enabling them to activate the transcription of shade-responsive genes and thereby promote shade-avoidance responses (Willige et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2023). Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating the transcriptional regulation of SAS over the past two decades, the contribution of post-transcriptional regulation—particularly RNA modifications—remains largely unclear.
To date, more than 160 types of RNA modifications have been identified, which influence RNA function and regulate cellular processes (Boccaletto et al., 2022). Among these, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most abundant internal post-transcriptional modification in mRNA. In Arabidopsis, m6A is deposited by methyltransferase complexes (MTCs; writers) comprising the core subunits mRNA adenosine methylase A (MTA) and MTB, together with several accessory subunits, including FKBP12 INTERACTING PROTEIN 37 (FIP37), VIRILIZER (VIR), and HAKAI (Shen, 2023). Knockdown or mutation of these genes reduces m6A levels by 44%–88% and is associated with developmental defects (Wong et al., 2023). More recently, FIONA1 has been identified as an m6A methyltransferase, and mutations in this gene lead to a 14%–20% reduction in m6A levels (Wang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023). Conversely, m6A modifications can be reversibly removed by demethylases (erasers) (Duan et al., 2017; Martinez-Perez et al., 2017). m6A-modified RNAs are recognized by a range of m6A-binding proteins (readers), which mediate the biological functions of this modification (Arribas-Hernández et al., 2018; Song et al., 2021; Amara et al., 2023).
The m6A modification plays broad roles in regulating RNA metabolism, including RNA stability and translation (Yang et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2023). For example, the Arabidopsis m6A readers ECT1 and ECT8 recruit the decapping factor DECAPPING 5, directing m6A-modified mRNAs to processing bodies and thereby facilitating mRNA decay (Tang et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024). The mta mutation significantly decreases translational efficiency, suggesting a correlation between m6A abundance and translational status (Wang et al., 2024).
Several studies have reported light-dependent regulation of m6A modification. The blue-light receptor cryptochrome (CRY) interacts with the writer complex to enhance m6A deposition on CCA1 and PIF3/4/5, thereby regulating their RNA metabolism and controlling circadian rhythms and photomorphogenesis in Arabidopsis (Wang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023). Blue light also enhances the enzymatic activity of FIONA1 via CRY2, increasing m6A deposition on chloroplast-related transcripts and improving their translation to regulate chlorophyll homeostasis (Jiang et al., 2023). High light intensity (1000 μmol·m-2·s-1) also induces m6A deposition on numerous photosynthesis-related transcripts in a VIR-dependent manner, thereby maintaining their stability and translation efficiency under high-light stress (Zhang et al., 2022b). In soybean, GmMTA regulates the shade avoidance response induced by low blue light (Zhang et al., 2024). However, our understanding of how a reduced R/FR light ratio influences the m6A epitranscriptome remains limited, and the underlying regulatory mechanisms are not well characterized.
In this study, we reveal that shade suppresses global m6A levels through phyB and the MTC. Shade induces the dissociation of phyB from the MTC, thereby impairing its RNA-binding capacity and methyltransferase activity. As a result, m6A marks are reduced on transcripts involved in auxin and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling under shade conditions. The MTC mediates shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and ABA hypersensitivity. Shade treatment or mutation of methyltransferase components stabilizes the growth-promoting transcripts SAUR15, SAUR19, and SAUR20 while reducing the translation of ABA signaling repressors SCHNARCHZAPFEN (SNZ), MYB20, and PROPEP3. Our findings establish an epigenetic mechanism by which phyB modulates m6A deposition to coordinate plant elongation growth and stress responses under shade.

## Results

## Shade reduces m6A levels through phyB and the MTC
Using a commercial m6A quantification kit, we confirmed reduced m6A abundance in methyltransferase mutants (including mta, MTB-RNAi, vir-1, and hakai-1) (Supplemental Figure 1A), consistent with previous reports (Hu et al., 2021). To assess the effect of shade on m6A levels, 7-day-old white-light-grown Columbia-0 (Col-0), phyB-9, and mta seedlings were exposed to either WL (red [R]: 20 μmol·m−2·s−1, blue [B]: 20 μmol·m−2·s−1, FR: 5 μmol·m−2·s−1, and R/FR = 4) or shade (R: 20 μmol·m−2·s−1, B: 20 μmol·m−2·s−1, FR: 60 μmol·m−2·s−1, and R/FR = 0.33) conditions (Figure 1A) for 1 and 8 h. Using the same m6A quantification kit, we observed that prolonged shade exposure significantly decreased m6A levels (Figure 1B; Supplemental Figure 1B). In contrast, phyB-9 and mta mutants exhibited lower m6A levels under WL and showed no further reduction upon shade treatment (Figure 1B). Dot blot assays using an anti-m6A antibody further confirmed that shade significantly reduced mRNA m6A levels in Col-0 but not in phyB-9 or mta seedlings (Figure 1C; Supplemental Figure 1C). Additionally, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis verified that both m6A abundance and the m6A/A ratio were significantly reduced in shade-treated Col-0 and WL-grown phyB-9 seedlings (Figure 1D). Together, these results demonstrate that shade significantly reduces m6A levels in a manner dependent on phyB and the MTC.

## Shade attenuates the interaction between phyB and the MTC
We next investigated how shade and phyB regulate m6A levels. Analysis of published RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets indicated that neither shade treatment nor phyB mutation alters the transcript levels of m6A methyltransferases or demethylases (Supplemental Figure 2A). The results show that protein levels of MTA-GFP, MTB-GFP, FLAG-GFP-FIP37, HAKAI-GFP, and GFP-VIR remained stable after shade exposure (0–24 h) in transgenic lines ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta (Zhang et al., 2022a), ProMTB::MTB-GFP/mtb (Shen, 2023), ProACT2::FLAG-GFP-FIP37 (Wang et al., 2021), ProHAKAI::HAKAI-GFP/hakai (Zhang et al., 2022a), and ProVIR::GFP-VIR/vir-1 (Růžička et al., 2017) (Supplemental Figure 2B).
We then examined the interaction between phyB and the MTC. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments using WL-grown ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta seedlings showed that phyB co-immunoprecipitated with MTA-GFP. However, this interaction was markedly reduced in seedlings subjected to 8 h of shade treatment (Figure 2A; Supplemental Figure 2C), suggesting that shade weakens the association between phyB and the MTC. To determine whether this association depends on the Pfr form of phyB, we transferred ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta transgenic seedlings from dark conditions to red light (120 μmol·m-2·s-1) for 8 h. As expected, more phyB was co-precipitated with MTA-GFP, indicating that their association is light-dependent (Figure 2B; Supplemental Figure 2D). Similarly, the association between phyB and FIP37 was diminished under shade and enhanced upon transfer from dark to red light in ProACT2::FLAG-GFP-FIP37 transgenic seedlings (Figures 2C and 2D; Supplemental Figures 2E and 2F).

Previous studies have shown that phyB forms photobodies under WL but disperses under shade conditions (Xie et al., 2023). MTA, MTB, and VIR have also been reported to form nuclear bodies (Zhong et al., 2008; Růžička et al., 2017; Bai et al., 2024), and the formation of MTA nuclear bodies is dynamically regulated by blue light (Wang et al., 2021). We therefore hypothesized that shade may affect the nuclear body organization of the MTC. Using confocal microscopy, we observed that phyB-GFP formed distinct nuclear bodies under WL but became diffuse after 8 h of shade treatment (Figure 2E; Supplemental Figure 2G). Similarly, MTA-GFP and FLAG-GFP-FIP37 assembled into nuclear bodies under WL but became barely detectable under shade conditions (Figure 2E; Supplemental Figure 2G). When ProMTA::MTA-GFP was introduced into the phyB-9 background, MTA-GFP nuclear bodies were hardly observed even under WL (Figure 2E; Supplemental Figure 2G), indicating that their formation depends on phyB. Furthermore, immunostaining of phyB in ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta seedlings revealed that phyB and MTA-GFP colocalized in nuclear speckles under WL, whereas both signals became diffuse under shade conditions (Figure 2F; Supplemental Figure 2H; Supplemental Table 1).
These results suggest that under WL conditions, phyB recruits the m6A MTC into nuclear speckles, whereas shade treatment triggers the dispersal of the complex.

## Shade decreases the methyltransferase activity of the MTC
Previous studies have shown that the enzymatic activity of m6A methyltransferases can be modulated by interacting proteins (Jiang et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024). To evaluate the effects of shade and phyB on m6A methyltransferase activity, we isolated the MTC from transgenic lines using antibody-conjugated agarose beads. A synthetic 15-nt single-stranded RNA containing the m6A consensus motif (UGUAY) was used as the substrate. Comparable amounts of m6A methyltransferase purified from WL-grown ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta seedlings exhibited significantly higher activity than those isolated from shade-treated seedlings (Figure 3A; Supplemental Figure 3A). No such difference was observed in control samples enriched using Protein A beads (Figure 3A). Similarly, the MTC isolated from WL-grown ProACT2::FLAG-GFP-FIP37 plants showed higher activity than that from shade-treated seedlings (Figure 3B; Supplemental Figure 3B). In contrast, the complex purified from ProMTA::MTA-GFP/phyB-9 seedlings displayed markedly reduced activity compared with that from the wild type (Figure 3C; Supplemental Figure 3C). These results indicate that shade impairs the catalytic activity of the MTC in a phyB-dependent manner.

To determine whether phyB influences the RNA-binding capacity of the MTC, we first used AlphaFold to predict the structure of the MTA–MTB–RNA complex. The predicted model indicated that the catalytic center of MTA–MTB directly contacts the RNA (Supplemental Figure 3D). In the PHYB–PHYB–MTA–MTB–RNA complex, the catalytic center of MTA–MTB appeared to associate more tightly with the RNA (Supplemental Figure 3E). Interface analysis further suggested an increased number of RNA-contacting residues in the presence of PHYB (Supplemental Figure 3F). To validate this prediction, we performed RNA pull-down assays using biotin-labeled RNA, with unlabeled RNA serving as a negative control. Shade treatment markedly reduced the RNA-binding ability of MTA-GFP (Figure 3D; Supplemental Figure 3G). A similar reduction was observed for FLAG-GFP-FIP37 under shade conditions (Figure 3E; Supplemental Figure 3H). Moreover, RNA pull-down assays using ProMTA::MTA-GFP/phyB-9 seedlings showed decreased RNA binding of MTA-GFP compared with the wild type (Figure 3F; Supplemental Figure 3I). To further assess the RNA-binding activity of the MTC in vivo, we conducted RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays. The results showed that both MTA-GFP and FLAG-GFP-FIP37 exhibited reduced RNA association in shade-treated plants (Figures 3G and 3H; Supplemental Figures 3J and 3K), and MTA-GFP in the phyB-9 mutant background also showed reduced RNA-binding capacity (Figure 3I; Supplemental Figure 3L).
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that shade attenuates both the RNA-binding capacity and catalytic activity of the MTC in a phyB-dependent manner.

## Shade mediates transcriptome-wide reductions in m6A modification
To identify shade-induced and phyB-dependent m6A modifications, we conducted methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) using 7-day-old seedlings: Col-0 grown under WL, Col-0 treated with shade for 8 h, and the phyB-9 mutant grown under WL. m6A peaks were identified by comparing immunoprecipitated RNA with input RNA, using thresholds of fold enrichment > 2 and p < 10⁻⁵. We detected 31,321 peaks (corresponding to 18,028 genes) in WL-grown Col-0, 29,268 peaks (17,608 genes) in shade-treated Col-0, and 29,389 peaks (17,658 genes) in the phyB-9 mutant (Supplemental Figure 4A; Supplemental Table 2). The average number of peaks per transcript remained largely unchanged (Supplemental Figure 4B). In addition, m6A peaks were predominantly enriched near the stop codon (within a 200-nt window), followed by the start codon (within a 200-nt window), coding sequence, 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), and 5′ UTR. This m6A distribution pattern is consistent with that in Arabidopsis (Duan et al., 2017) and was not altered by shade treatment or phyB mutation (Figure 4A; Supplemental Figure 4C). Motif analysis further confirmed significant enrichment of the canonical RRACH (R = A or G; H = A, C, or U) motif and the plant-specific UGUAY (Y = C or U) motif across all samples (Figure 4B; Supplemental Figure 4D), in agreement with previous observations (Tang et al., 2023).

For quantitative comparison of the m6A epitranscriptome across samples, we generated a union peak set from all datasets using DiffBind and calculated relative m6A intensities for each peak with exomePeak2 (Supplemental Table 3). Comparison of m6A intensities showed that data points were predominantly distributed below the diagonal when comparing Col-0/WL with either Col-0/shade or phyB-9/WL, indicating reduced m6A levels under shade conditions or in the phyB mutant (Supplemental Figure 4E). In contrast, data points clustered closely along the diagonal in the comparison between Col-0/shade and phyB-9/WL, suggesting similar m6A levels between shade-treated Col-0 plants and phyB-9 mutants grown under WL (Supplemental Figure 4E). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that m6A levels were reduced in both shade-treated Col-0 and the phyB-9 mutant compared with WL-grown Col-0 (Figure 4C). Notably, the reduction was particularly pronounced around the start and stop codons (Figure 4D).
Using thresholds of fold change > 1.5 and p< 0.05, we identified 1,929 and 2,206 hypomethylated peaks corresponding to 1,768 and 1,974 transcripts in shade-treated seedlings and phyB-9, respectively (Figures 4E and 4F). Shade- and phyB-mediated m6A regulation exhibited a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.33) (Figure 4G). In total, 720 m6A peaks (686 genes) showed concordant decreases in methylation levels, whereas 479 m6A peaks (474 genes) showed concordant increases (Figure 4G; Supplemental Table 4), supporting the conclusion that both shade treatment and loss of phyB lead to reduced m6A modification.
Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that hypomethylated genes were enriched in functions related to abiotic stress responses, ABA response, auxin response, and light responses (Figure 4H), whereas hypermethylated genes were primarily associated with mRNA processing and vesicle-mediated transport (Figure 4I). Given the widespread decline in m6A levels under shade conditions and in the phyB mutant, we focused subsequent analyses on hypomethylated genes and their potential biological roles.

## m6A modification participates in shade-induced hypocotyl elongation
Given the established role of m6A modification in regulating RNA stability, we integrated the MeRIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets to examine the relationship between changes in m6A and transcript abundance. This analysis revealed a weak correlation (R2 = 0.01) between differential m6A peaks and changes in mRNA levels (Figure 5A; Supplemental Table 4). However, among transcripts with reduced m6A levels, 102 hypomethylated genes exhibited increased transcript abundance, whereas only 22 were downregulated (Figure 5A), suggesting that shade-induced m6A reduction tends to promote transcript accumulation. GO analysis further indicated that hypomethylated genes with elevated expression were enriched in responses to auxin and light (Figure 5B). In addition, 575 hypomethylated genes displayed stable transcript levels and were associated with abiotic stress-related processes, including salt stress and ABA response (Supplemental Figure 5A). The distribution patterns of m6A along transcripts were largely similar between genes with increased mRNA levels and those with unchanged expression (Supplemental Figures 5B and 5C).

To assess the functional relevance of m6A modification in SAS, we examined the phenotypes of several m6A writer mutants and knockdown lines, including mta, MTB-RNAi, hakai-1, and vir-1. These seedlings exhibited enhanced hypocotyl elongation under shade conditions and reduced sensitivity to continuous red light, FR light, blue light, and WL (Figure 5C; Supplemental Figure 5D).
Several SMALL AUXIN UPREGULATED RNA (SAUR) genes, known positive regulators of hypocotyl elongation (Ren and Gray, 2015; Pereyra et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), displayed reduced m6A modification and increased transcript abundance under shade treatment (Figure 5D). Using m6A–IP–qPCR, we confirmed that shade significantly decreased m6A modification of SAUR15/19/20 in a manner dependent on both phyB and MTA (Figure 5E). Consistently, RT–qPCR analysis revealed increased expression of SAUR15/19/20 in phyB-9 and mta mutants (Figure 5F). The higher expression levels observed in phyB-9 are likely attributable to stabilized PIFs that directly activate SAUR transcription (Sun et al., 2016). To exclude the influence of transcriptional regulation and determine whether shade affects the stability of SAUR15/19/20 transcripts, we inhibited transcription using 1 mM cordycepin and monitored RNA decay. Cordycepin effectively suppressed SAUR15/19/20 transcription, and the decay rates of SAUR15/19/20 transcripts were significantly slower in shade-treated Col-0 plants as well as in phyB-9 and mta mutants (Figure 5G). We further assessed the translation efficiency of SAUR15/19/20 and detected no significant differences (Supplemental Figure 5E). Together, these results indicate that shade reduces m6A modification on hypocotyl-elongation-related transcripts, such as SAUR15/19/20, thereby enhancing their mRNA stability.

## m6A modification contributes to shade-induced ABA hypersensitivity
In addition to its role in regulating RNA stability, m6A modification also modulates translational efficiency; reduced m6A abundance and translational output have been reported in the mta mutant (Wang et al., 2023, 2024). In our dataset, many transcripts exhibiting decreased m6A levels under shade conditions showed no significant changes in mRNA abundance (Figure 5A), suggesting that m6A likely modulates shade responses through translational regulation. To explore the relationship between changes in shade-regulated m6A and MTA-dependent translation, we integrated our MeRIP-seq dataset with a published translatome dataset from the amiR-mta mutant (GEO: GSE206292) (Figure 6A; Supplemental Table 4). Among genes showing reduced m6A levels under shade, we identified 217 transcripts whose translational efficiency was decreased in the amiR-mta mutant, compared with only 59 transcripts showing increased translational efficiency. These results suggest that the loss of m6A under shade conditions is primarily associated with reduced translational efficiency (Figure 6A). The distribution pattern of m6A across these 217 transcripts closely resembled the transcriptome-wide profile (Supplemental Figure 6A). GO analysis further revealed that these 217 genes were significantly enriched in stress-related processes, including responses to salt stress and ABA (Figure 6B).

To determine whether m6A modification contributes to shade-modulated abiotic stress responses, we assessed ABA sensitivity by measuring the primary root length of seedlings grown under WL or shade conditions. In the absence of ABA, primary root length did not differ significantly between WL and shade treatments (Figure 6C). However, in the presence of ABA, seedlings grown under shade exhibited significantly shorter primary roots than those grown under WL, indicating enhanced ABA sensitivity under shade conditions (Figure 6C). Consistent with this observation, the phyB-9 mutant displayed increased ABA sensitivity even under WL (Figure 6C), consistent with a previous report (Li et al., 2023). Moreover, m6A writer mutants and knockdown lines also exhibited enhanced ABA sensitivity, with a reduced differential response between WL and shade conditions (Figure 6C). These results suggest that the shade-induced reduction in m6A modification is associated with enhanced ABA sensitivity.
The transcripts of several negative regulators of ABA signaling, including SNZ, MYB20, and PROPEP3, showed reduced m6A levels under shade and decreased translational efficiency in mta (Figure 6D). m6A–IP–qPCR further confirmed that shade significantly reduced m6A modification of these transcripts in a manner dependent on both phyB and MTA (Figure 6E). Notably, the abundance and decay rates of SNZ, MYB20, and PROPEP3 transcripts remained largely unchanged under shade conditions or in phyB-9 and mta mutants (Figure 6F; Supplemental Figure 6B), whereas their translational efficiency was markedly reduced (Figure 6G).
The transcription factor SNZ negatively regulates ABA signaling by repressing the expression of ABI3 and ABI5, and the snz mutant exhibits hypersensitivity to ABA (Zou et al., 2013; Li et al., 2016). Similarly, the transcription factor MYB20 represses the expression of ABI1 and AHG3—positive regulators of ABA signaling—and its overexpression leads to reduced ABA sensitivity (Cui et al., 2013). The small peptide PROPEP3 is induced by salt stress, and its knockdown lines exhibit increased sensitivity to salt stress (Nakaminami et al., 2018). Phenotypic analysis of the snz-1, myb20, and propep3 mutants revealed that the snz-1 and myb20 mutants exhibited enhanced ABA sensitivity, accompanied by an attenuated shade response (Supplemental Figures 6C and 6D).
These results suggest that shade, acting through phyB and the MTC, reduces m6A modification on transcripts associated with abiotic stress responses, such as SNZ, MYB20, and PROPEP3, thereby decreasing their translational efficiency.

## Discussion
Previous studies of phytochromes have largely focused on their roles in transcriptional regulation. For instance, phyB interacts with and promotes the phosphorylation of PIF transcription factors, which are subsequently ubiquitinated and targeted for degradation (Ni et al., 2017; Paik et al., 2019). phyB also modulates the activity of protein phosphatase PP2A to dephosphorylate bZIP transcription factors, thereby facilitating their nuclear localization and transcriptional activity (Li et al., 2024). In contrast, our study reveals that phyB regulates the activity of the m6A methyltransferase complex, thereby influencing m6A deposition and fine-tuning mRNA stability and translation. This mechanism contributes to shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and ABA hypersensitivity (Figure 7). These findings uncover a previously unrecognized post-transcriptional function of phytochromes in modulating plant elongation growth and stress adaptation through m6A-mediated RNA regulation.
Proposed model illustrating how phyB-dependent modulation of m6A deposition regulates shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and ABA hypersensitivity.
Under WL conditions, photoactivated phyB interacts with the MTC, enhancing its RNA-binding capacity and methyltransferase activity, thereby promoting m6A deposition. Under shade conditions, however, reduced phyB–MTC interaction leads to decreased m6A levels. The loss of m6A marks stabilizes the transcripts of growth-promoting genes, such as SAUR15/19/20, while reducing the translation of abiotic stress-related transcripts, including SNZ, MYB20, and PROPEP3.
The interaction between full-length CRY2 and MTA does not exhibit a clear light response, although blue light modulates the interactions between MTA and CRY2 fragments containing either the C-terminal extension (CCE) or the photolyase homologous region (PHR) domain (Wang et al., 2021). In contrast to this largely light-independent interaction, our results show that phyB associates with the MTC in a light-dependent manner (Figure 2). Light-induced conformational changes in phyB regulate its nuclear localization and consequently its interaction with the MTC. Binding of the active phyB (Pfr) form enhances both the enzymatic activity and RNA-binding capacity of the MTC (Figure 3), potentially by altering the conformation of the catalytic center to facilitate tighter RNA association. In addition to this direct regulatory effect, phyB may also promote liquid–liquid phase separation, thereby increasing substrate availability and further enhancing MTC activity.
Dynamic and reversible m6A modifications play essential roles in regulating RNA metabolism. In this study, we focused specifically on the decrease in m6A modification induced by shade treatment or observed in the phyB-9 mutant. Integrative analyses combining shade-regulated m6A profiling with transcriptome and translatome datasets revealed that transcripts with reduced m6A levels under shade were primarily associated with either increased transcript abundance or decreased translational efficiency (Figures 5A and 6A). The functions of m6A are largely mediated by reader proteins, and modifications at different regions of a transcript can recruit distinct readers, thereby producing diverse regulatory outcomes (Fernandez Rodriguez et al., 2022; Cai et al., 2025). In our study, however, shade did not alter the overall distribution of m6A sites along transcripts (Figures 4A and 4D), and transcripts showing altered abundance or translational efficiency did not show differences in the positional distribution of m6A signals (Supplemental Figures 5B and 6A). Thus, the modification site itself is not the sole determinant of m6A function. Instead, the regulatory outcome is likely further shaped by the repertoire of available reader proteins, the cellular environment, and intrinsic features of the transcript (Tang et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2025). Identification of shade-responsive m6A reader proteins may therefore provide important mechanistic insights. Beyond its effects on mRNA stability and translation, dynamic changes in m6A modification may also influence additional aspects of RNA metabolism, including splicing and RNA transport.
m6A modification participates in diverse biological processes in plants. Under shade conditions, mutants of the m6A methyltransferase display elongated hypocotyls (Figure 5C), underscoring the importance of m6A in regulating hypocotyl elongation. Under WL conditions, however, m6A writer mutants show no obvious hypocotyl phenotype, likely because elongation-related genes are expressed at low levels, thereby masking the function of m6A. Overexpression lines or the use of specific chemical activators of methyltransferases may help further validate the roles of these enzymes. Previous studies have shown that m6A influences hypocotyl elongation by modulating the stability of PIF3/4/5 transcripts (Yang et al., 2023) or by facilitating the translation of HYH and PRR9 transcripts (Song et al., 2024). Here, we found that shade reduces m6A modification on SAUR15/19/20 transcripts while enhancing their stability (Figure 5). Shade also alters m6A modification on a large number of transcripts. These changes may either promote or suppress elongation-related processes. However, the combined effect of these m6A-mediated regulatory changes in hypocotyl elongation-related transcripts ultimately promotes hypocotyl elongation in response to shade.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that shade reduces m6A modification and the translation efficiency of abiotic stress-related transcripts, including SNZ, MYB20, and PROPEP3, and that mutants of the m6A writer exhibit ABA hypersensitivity under both WL and shade conditions (Figure 6). Complementing these findings, previous studies have shown that m6A modification stabilizes transcripts of the ABA signaling regulators RPN10 and FYVE1 (Song et al., 2021), whereas the ABA receptor PYL7 is translationally regulated through m6A within ECT8-mediated phase-separated condensates (Wu et al., 2024). Together, these findings indicate that shade-triggered m6A remodeling fine-tunes ABA responses by coordinately targeting multiple regulatory factors at both the transcript stability and translational levels. Mutations that precisely mimic or prevent m6A deposition without altering gene function would enable unambiguous dissection of m6A-mediated effects on mRNA regulation and shade avoidance responses.
Collectively, our results reveal that phyB, a master regulator of the SAS, cooperates with m6A regulators to drive epitranscriptomic reprogramming. This mechanism coordinates hypocotyl elongation and ABA hypersensitivity under shade conditions, providing important insights into how environmental signals dynamically modulate m6A-dependent regulatory networks.

## Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
The model organism used in this study was Arabidopsis thaliana. All plants were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background. The mutants used included ABI3::MTA/mta (SALK_074069), hakai-1 (GABI_271A12), vir-1, MTB-RNAi, phyB-9, snz-1 (SALK_030031), myb20 (SALK_205432), and propep3 (WiscDsLoxHs141_10G). The transgenic lines used in this study included Pro35S::phyB-GFP, ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta, ProMTB::MTB-GFP/mtb, ProACT2::FLAG-GFP-FIP37, ProHAKAI::HAKAI-GFP/hakai, ProVIR::GFP-VIR/vir-1, and ProMTA::MTA-GFP/phyB-9.
Seeds were germinated on plates containing half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium supplemented with 1% agar and without sucrose. After stratification at 4 °C in darkness for 3 days, plates were transferred to growth chambers maintained at 22 °C under the indicated light conditions.
For hypocotyl measurements, 3-day-old seedlings grown under WL were either maintained under WL (R: 20 μmol·m-2·s-1, B: 20 μmol·m-2·s-1, FR: 5 μmol·m-2·s-1; R/FR = 4) or transferred to simulated shade (R: 20 μmol·m-2·s-1, B: 20 μmol·m-2·s-1, FR: 60 μmol·m-2·s-1; R/FR = 0.33) for 4 days. For primary root length measurements, 2-day-old WL-grown seedlings were either maintained under WL or transferred to shade for 4 days in the presence of ABA. Seedlings were scanned using an HP Scanjet 8270 scanner, and hypocotyl or primary root length was measured using NIH ImageJ software.

## Quantification of m6A levels using a methylation quantification kit
Global m6A levels in RNA were quantified using the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Epigentek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 200 ng of purified total RNA from each sample was added to the assay wells, where m6A residues were captured by a specific antibody. After incubation and washing, a detection antibody and enhancer solution were sequentially applied, followed by colorimetric development. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Relative m6A levels were calculated based on the standard curve provided with the kit. Each sample was analyzed with at least three biological replicates.

## Dot blot analysis of m6A levels
Poly(A)+ RNA was purified from total RNA using Oligo (dT)25 magnetic beads (New England Biolabs, USA). Briefly, total RNA was denatured at 65°C for 5 min and then incubated with pre-washed magnetic beads in binding buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 1 M LiCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 10 mM DTT) at room temperature. The beads were sequentially washed with wash buffer 1 (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 0.5 M LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT), wash buffer 2 (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 0.5 M LiCl, and 1 mM EDTA), and low-salt buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 200 mM LiCl, and 1 mM EDTA). Poly(A)+ RNA was then eluted at 50 °C using elution buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.0] and 1 mM EDTA).
The eluted mRNA samples were heated at 95 °C for 3 min to disrupt secondary structures and immediately chilled on ice. The denatured mRNA was then spotted onto nitrocellulose membranes and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST) and subsequently blocked with TBST containing 5% BSA. Membranes were incubated with an anti-m6A antibody (1:500; Synaptic Systems) at 4 °C overnight, followed by washing with TBST and incubation with a secondary antibody for 1 h. Signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

## Quantification of m6A levels in mRNA by LC–MS/MS
Poly(A)+ RNA was purified from total RNA using Oligo (dT)25 magnetic beads (New England Biolabs). A total of 600 ng of mRNA was digested with nuclease P1 (1 U; New England Biolabs) and snake venom phosphodiesterase (0.01 U; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C for 2 h to generate nucleotides. Subsequently, FastAP alkaline phosphatase (1 U; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for an additional 1 h to convert nucleotides into nucleosides. After digestion, proteins and other contaminants were removed by methanol and acetonitrile precipitation. The resulting solution was diluted fivefold and injected into a QTRAP 6500 LC–MS/MS system. Nucleoside concentrations were quantified based on standard curves generated using pure commercial nucleosides (MCE).

## Co-IP assay
For shade treatment, seedlings were grown for 7 days under continuous WL and either maintained under WL or transferred to simulated shade for 8 h. For red-light induction, seedlings grown under continuous WL for 6 days were first kept in darkness for 24 h and then exposed to red light for 8 h. Total protein was extracted from 7-day-old transgenic seedlings using extraction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 10% glycerol, and a protease inhibitor cocktail). The crude extracts were filtered through Miracloth and centrifuged at 16,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the input fraction. For IP, the extracts were incubated with anti-GFP affinity beads (Smart-Lifesciences) for 2 h at 4°C with gentle rotation. The beads were then washed three times with extraction buffer and resuspended in protein loading buffer. Both immunoprecipitated proteins and input samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-GFP and anti-PHYB antibodies.

## Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy
For immunolocalization, 7-day-old ProMTA::MTA-GFP/mta seedlings were fixed in FAA solution, washed with PBS, and enzymatically digested to release nuclei. Samples were permeabilized with an IGEPAL/DMSO solution and blocked with 5% BSA containing 50 mM glycine. Seedlings were incubated with an anti-PHYB antibody (1:200; PHYTOAB, USA) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with AF555-labeled donkey anti-mouse IgG (1:200; Alexa Fluor, USA). The nuclear localization of phyB was observed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope, and MTA-GFP fluorescence was directly imaged in the same seedlings.

## Methyltransferase activity measurement
The MTC was immunoprecipitated from transgenic seedlings using anti-GFP beads, and its activity was measured using the MTase-Glo Methyltransferase Assay Kit (Promega, USA). Briefly, 1 g of fresh plant tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1.5 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, 10% glycerol, and a protease inhibitor cocktail). Anti-GFP beads (Smart-Lifesciences) were incubated with clarified protein extracts for 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were then washed three times with lysis buffer and resuspended in reaction buffer. For activity assays, 4 μl of bead suspension was incubated with 20 μM SAM and RNA substrates (10 μM–0 nM) for 30 min at 25°C. Following incubation, 2 μl of MTase-Glo reagent (10×) was added to convert S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH) to ADP, followed by the addition of 22 μl of MTase-Glo detection solution to convert ADP to ATP. The reactions were transferred to a white 96-well plate, and luminescence was measured using a BioTek Synergy Neo2 microplate reader. Reactions without RNA substrate were included as background controls, and their luminescence values were subtracted from those of reactions containing RNA substrate. Protein input for the enzymatic assays was analyzed by SDS–PAGE followed by immunoblotting.

## RNA pull-down assay
Approximately 1 g of fresh plant tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1.5 ml lysis buffer (40 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and RNase inhibitor and a protease inhibitor cocktail). The extract was filtered and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected (100 μl was reserved as input). Ten micrograms of biotin-labeled RNA was incubated with 1 ml of protein extract at 4°C for 1 h, with additional RNase inhibitor added midway through the incubation. Thirty microliters of streptavidin-agarose G beads (Invitrogen, USA) were pre-washed three times with lysis buffer and then incubated with the RNA–protein mixtures for 1 h at 4°C. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with lysis buffer. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 2× SDS loading buffer and analyzed by western blotting.

## In vivo RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)
RIP was conducted as previously described with minor modifications (Yang et al., 2023). Approximately 1 g of fresh plant tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and RNase inhibitor and a protease inhibitor cocktail). The homogenate was filtered twice and centrifuged at 12 000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for IP. Anti-GFP affinity beads (Smart-Lifesciences) were washed three times with lysis buffer and incubated with 1 ml of protein extract at 4°C for 1 h. The beads were then washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, and RNase inhibitor and a protease inhibitor cocktail). Ninety percent of the beads were used for RNA extraction using the TRIzol method, whereas the remaining 10% were resuspended in 2× SDS loading buffer for immunoblot analysis. The purified RNA was analyzed by 1.5% denaturing agarose–formaldehyde gel electrophoresis and stained with SYBR Gold (Invitrogen).

## Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq)
Two independent biological replicates were performed for each experiment. Total RNA was extracted and fragmented into ∼100-nt fragments. One portion was used as the input control (RNA-seq), and the other was subjected to immunoprecipitation with an anti-m6A antibody (MeRIP-seq). The enriched RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using random primers, followed by end repair and ligation of Illumina adapters. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapter sequences, low-quality reads, poly(A) reads, and reads containing >10% unknown nucleotides. Clean reads were first aligned to rRNA sequences using Bowtie2, and rRNA-derived reads were discarded. The remaining reads were then aligned to the reference genome.
m6A-enriched regions (peaks) were identified using MACS2, and peaks detected in biological replicates were retained for further analysis. Peak-associated genes were annotated based on genomic location, and their distribution across the start codon, stop codon, coding sequence, and 5′ and 3′ UTRs was analyzed. Sequence motifs within peak regions were identified using MEME Suite and DREME. Peaks across samples were merged using DiffBind, and relative methylation levels were calculated by comparing MeRIP and input data. Differentially methylated peaks between groups were identified using the criteria log2|FC| ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05. Peak positional distribution was analyzed using the computeMatrix tool, and peaks were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). GO enrichment analysis was performed using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov).

## MeRIP–qPCR
MeRIP–qPCR was performed based on previously reported protocols with some modifications (Zhang et al., 2022b). Total RNA was extracted from Arabidopsis seedlings using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). Poly(A)+ RNA was enriched using oligo (dT)25 magnetic beads (New England Biolabs). Purified mRNA was fragmented at 94 °C for 3 min in fragmentation buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5] and 100 mM ZnCl2) and immediately neutralized with 50 mM EDTA. The fragmented RNA was precipitated with ethanol and recovered for subsequent m6A IP. For IP, RNA was incubated with an anti-m6A antibody (1:300; Synaptic Systems) in IP buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.4], 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% IGEPAL CA-630, and RNase inhibitor) at 4°C for 2 h, followed by incubation with Protein A beads. After washing with IP buffer, m6A-enriched RNA was eluted, precipitated, and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Input RNA and IP samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA using a First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA was analyzed by real-time qPCR using a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Gene-specific primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplemental Table 5.

## RNA decay assay
Seven-day-old seedlings were grown under WL or treated with shade for 8 h. Seedlings were first incubated in vacuum buffer (15 mM sucrose, 1 mM KCl, 1 mM PIPES [pH 6.25], and 1 mM sodium citrate) for 15 min. To inhibit transcription, seedlings were then treated with vacuum buffer containing 1 mM cordycepin and subjected to vacuum infiltration for 2 min, which was defined as time zero. Subsequent samples were collected at 3, 10, and 60 min. Total RNA was extracted from each sample and reverse transcribed for qPCR analysis to assess RNA decay.

## Translation efficiency assay
The translation efficiency assay was performed as previously described (Zhou et al., 2021). Briefly, approximately 2 g of seedlings were ground in liquid nitrogen and lysed in 4 ml of extraction buffer (200 mM Tris–HCl [pH 9.0], 35 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl, 25 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 1% IGEPAL CA-630, 1 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and RNase inhibitor and a protease inhibitor cocktail) at 4 °C. The lysate was filtered and centrifuged at maximum speed for 20 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 3 ml of the clarified extract was carefully layered onto 10 ml of sucrose buffer (1.75 M sucrose, 400 mM Tris–HCl [pH 9.0], 35 mM MgCl2, 200 mM KCl, 5 mM EGTA, 5 mM DTT, 50 μM chloramphenicol, and 50 μM cycloheximide). Samples were subjected to ultracentrifugation at 200,000g for 4 h at 4 °C using a Beckman Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge equipped with an SW 41 Ti rotor. The resulting polysomal pellet was gently resuspended in 300 μl of DEPC-treated water to obtain polysomal RNA. Total RNA and polysomal RNA were extracted using the TRIzol method and reverse transcribed into cDNA. qPCR was then performed to quantify transcript abundance in the total and polysomal fractions, allowing the assessment of translational efficiency.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
In this study, data are presented as mean ± SD, and the number of samples or biological replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s test for multiple-group comparisons. Significant differences are indicated by asterisks or different letters (p < 0.05).

## Figure Figure 7: Proposed model illustrating how phyB-dependent modulation of m6A deposition regulates shade-induced hypocotyl elongation and ABA hypersensitivity.Under WL conditions, photoactivated phyB interacts with the MTC, enhancing its RNA-binding capacity and methyltransferase activity, thereby promoting m6A deposition. Under shade conditions, however, reduced phyB–MTC interaction leads to decreased m6A levels. The loss of m6A marks stabilizes the transcripts of growth-promoting genes, such as SAUR15/19/20, while reducing the translation of abiotic stress-related transcripts, including SNZ, MYB20, and PROPEP3.
