# NPR1 promotes blue light–induced plant photomorphogenesis by ubiquitinating and degrading PIF4

## Abstract
SignificanceNONEXPRESSER OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES 1 (NPR1) is pivotal in plant immunity by co-activating antimicrobial PR genes and promoting cell survival through the ubiquitination and degradation of immune regulators within cytoplasmic condensates. Although research on the role of salicylic acid in photomorphogenesis remains limited, our study identified a role for NPR1 in this process. Specifically, NPR1 mediates the post-translational regulation of PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) under blue light by serving as substrate adaptor for the CULLIN3-based E3 ligase, enabling PIF4 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation via the 26S proteasome in the nucleus. This reduces PIF4 abundance and inhibits hypocotyl elongation in response to blue light. Additionally, cryptochromes enhance this process by promoting the interaction and polyubiquitination of PIF4 by NPR1.
Light is an important environmental signal that plays a fundamental role in regulating plant growth and development. To ensure the successful growth of young seedlings immediately after germination, plants have evolved distinct developmental strategies. In darkness, plants undergo skotomorphogenesis, which is characterized by elongated hypocotyls, an apical hook, and closed cotyledons, which shield the delicate shoot tip during emergence from the soil. Upon exposure to light, plants transition to photomorphogenesis, featuring shorter hypocotyls, the absence of an apical hook, and open, expanded, green cotyledons. This growth pattern optimizes light capture for photosynthesis and enables seedlings to sustain themselves. The transition from dark to light conditions is critical for seedling survival. The activation of photomorphogenesis in response to light is critically dependent on the repression of several PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORs (PIFs), which are basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors that physically interact with phytochromes (1, 2). A total of eight PIFs have been identified in Arabidopsis, most of which function as repressors of photomorphogenesis (3–5). Genetic studies have highlighted the role of PIF3 and PIF4 in seedling development, PIF1 in the regulation of seed germination, and PIF7 in promoting shade avoidance responses (4, 6–10).
In response to light, PIFs are typically phosphorylated, leading to their inactivation through either through ubiquitination and degradation or exclusion from the nucleus (11–15). Understanding the ubiquitin E3 ligases involved in the regulation of PIF stability in response to light has therefore received considerable attention in recent years, leading to the identification of the ubiquitin E3 ligases acting on PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, PIF5, and PIF8. PIF3 was the first PIF for which E3 ubiquitin ligases were identified; it is now known to be ubiquitinated by both CUL1- and CUL3-based complexes, involving the E3 ligase substrate adapters EIN3-BINDING F BOX PROTEINs (EBFs, EBF1/2) (16) and LIGHT-RESPONSE BRIC-A-BRAC/TRAMTRACK/BROAD (LRBs) (17), respectively. PIF3 is ubiquitinated by a CUL3LRB complex in response to strong light, while its ubiquitination in response to both weak and strong light requires CUL1EBF1/2 (16, 17). The CUL4-based E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, which includes SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA-105 (SPAs, SPA1–4) and/or CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1) as its adapter protein, promotes the degradation of PIF1, PIF5, and PIF8. However, although these proteins are all commonly ubiquitinated by CUL4COP1/SPA, the specific light conditions under which this E3 ligase acts on each of them are different: CUL4COP1/SPA appears to ubiquitinate PIF1 specifically following a dark-to-light transition, ubiquitinates PIF5 more broadly in response to light, and preferentially targets PIF8 in darkness (18–20). These findings suggest that specific E3 ligase complexes may control the degradation of different PIF proteins and that the E3 ligase may target the same PIF protein differently under different light conditions.
Of all the known PIFs, PIF4 has been identified as a key regulator of hypocotyl elongation in response to light, shade, and high temperature (7, 21, 22). Continuous activation of PIF4 could lead to a physiological imbalance characterized by excessive hypocotyl elongation. To counteract this imbalance, plants have evolved several regulatory mechanisms, including the suppression of PIF4’s transcriptional activity and the destabilization of its protein. Studies have shown that two Arabidopsis blue light receptors—cryptochrome 1 (CRY1) and 2 (CRY2)—interact directly with PIF4 and inhibit its transcriptional activity under low blue light (LBL) conditions, thereby regulating genes involved in shade avoidance responses (23, 24). In addition, similar to PIF3, PIF4 undergoes ubiquitination mediated by a CUL3-based E3 ligase that uses the BTB domain-containing proteins BLADE-ON-PETIOLE 1 (BOP1) and BOP2 as substrate adapters, which are crucial for its degradation in response to red light (25). However, a conclusive demonstration of the light-dependent effect of BOPs on PIF4’s ubiquitination has not yet been achieved. While light-induced ubiquitination of PIF4 is reduced in the bop2 mutant, this mutant also exhibits reduced residual ubiquitination of PIF4 in darkness. Furthermore, the E3 ligases responsible for controlling PIF4 degradation under other light conditions, such as blue light, remain unknown.
Salicylic acid (SA), a key phytohormone in plant immunity, plays a central role in multiple immune responses (26, 27). Unlike typical plant hormones, SA is sensed by two distinct classes of receptors: NONEXPRESSER OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES 1 (NPR1) and NPR1-LIKE PROTEIN 3 (NPR3)/4(NPR4). Remarkably, these receptors exert opposite effects on the expression of defense genes (28–31). NPR1 has emerged as a crucial transcriptional coactivator, essential for the expression of antimicrobial PR genes, thereby conferring broad-spectrum disease resistance (28, 32–34). Conversely, NPR3 and NPR4 are negative regulators of plant defense (30, 35), acting as redundant transcriptional repressors (29). The NPR proteins share a common N-terminal broad complex, tram track, and bric-a-brac/poxvirus, zinc finger (BTB/POZ) domain, which is commonly found in the substrate adaptor subunits of the CULLIN3-RBX1 ubiquitin ligase complexes (CUL3s) (36). Consistent with their inhibitory role in SA signaling, NPR3 and NPR4 function as CUL3 substrate receptors, orchestrating the polyubiquitination and subsequent degradation of NPR1 (30). Recent studies have revealed the involvement of NPR1 in a CUL3-based ubiquitin E3 ligase system that facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of immune regulators within cytoplasmic biomolecular condensates induced by SA (37). In addition, NPR1 interacts with the gibberellin (GA) receptor GA INSENSITIVE DWARF1 (GID1), promoting the polyubiquitination and degradation of GID1, thereby modulating the balance between growth and defense (38). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which NPR1 regulates protein ubiquitination and stability in photomorphogenesis remain unexplored.
Plant hormones serve as central intrinsic regulators that influence photomorphogenesis. Among these, PIFs are increasingly recognized as central orchestrators integrating signals from different hormone pathways throughout plant growth and development, including GA, abscisic acid, brassinosteroid, jasmonate, ethylene (ET), auxins, and cytokinin (3, 6, 39–41). However, research on the role of SA in photomorphogenesis has been limited. This study sheds light on the facilitative role of SA in Arabidopsis seedling photomorphogenesis, operating through a PIF-dependent mechanism. Notably, NPR1 emerges as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis under blue light conditions, orchestrating the light-induced polyubiquitination and subsequent degradation of PIF4. NPR1 functions as a substrate adaptor for the CUL3-based E3 ligase, targeting PIF4 for degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway. Furthermore, the NPR1-mediated polyubiquitination of PIF4 in response to blue light is mediated by cryptochromes. Our findings not only reveal a role for NPR1 in plant photomorphogenesis, but also elucidate a mechanism governing the posttranslational regulation of PIF4 in response to blue light.

## Results

## SA Inhibits Hypocotyl Elongation in a PIF-Dependent Manner.
To evaluate the effects of SA on plant growth, we performed hypocotyl elongation assays using Arabidopsis seedlings. Columbia-0 (Col-0, wild type) Arabidopsis was grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing different concentrations of SA or without SA. As shown in Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1, seedlings grown on SA-containing medium had shorter hypocotyls and larger cotyledon area than those grown without SA; in addition, hypocotyl length decreased with increasing SA concentration. Notably, we observed that the SA-inhibited hypocotyl length was only evident in the light, but not in the dark.

Given that PIFs play a crucial role in regulating hypocotyl elongation in plant photomorphogenesis (3–5, 7, 21), we investigated the influence of SA-mediated growth inhibition on PIFs. As shown in Fig. 1C, the growth of the pifq and pif4-2 mutants was insensitive to SA treatment. Among the four PIF mutants, only the pif3 mutant displayed sensitivity to the SA-induced inhibition of hypocotyl length, suggesting that PIF3 is not involved in the regulation of SA-inhibited hypocotyl length (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Since NPR1 is a key regulator of SA signaling, we further investigated whether SA-mediated growth inhibition depends on NPR1. As expected, the growth of the npr1-1 mutant was insensitive to SA treatment. Introduction of NPR1-GFP under the control of the 35S promoter successfully restored the sensitivity of the npr1-1 mutant to the SA-induced inhibition of hypocotyl length and enlarged cotyledons, indicating that the mutant phenotype was indeed caused by loss of NPR1 function (Fig. 1D).

## NPR1 Acts as a Positive Regulator of Photomorphogenesis under Blue Light in a PIF4-Dependent Manner.
To investigate whether NPR1 plays a role in the regulation of photomorphogenesis, we cultured wild type (Col-0) and npr1-1 mutant seedlings in the dark or under continuous white, blue, red, or far-red light for 4 d and then measured their hypocotyl lengths. The npr1-1 mutant showed longer hypocotyls than the wild type in white and blue light, but not in red and far-red light. Additionally, the npr1 mutant showed smaller cotyledon area under white and blue light (Fig. 2B), indicating that NPR1 acts as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis, specifically in response to blue light (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Since NPR3, NPR4, and NPR1 belong to the same protein family and all function as SA receptors (28–31, 35, 42), we also investigated whether NPR3 and NPR4 play a role in the regulation of photomorphogenesis. Our results showed that the npr3, npr4, and npr3 npr4 mutants had no discernible effect on photomorphogenesis either in darkness or under continuous white and blue light conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Taken together, these observations suggest that NPR1 is specifically involved in the regulation of plant photomorphogenesis under blue light.

Since SA inhibits hypocotyl elongation in a PIF-dependent manner, we hypothesized that NPR1 is involved in the regulation of photomorphogenesis by PIFs. Therefore, we first examined the role of PIFs in photomorphogenesis under blue light and found that all four PIFs were involved in regulating photomorphogenesis under white light. However, we noted that the absence of PIF3 did not affect the regulation of photomorphogenesis under blue light (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Furthermore, to determine the genetic relationships between NPR1 and different PIFs, we generated quintuple and double mutants of npr1-1 crossed with the pif1-1, pif3-3, pif4-2, and pif5-3 mutants, and used the CRISPR-Cas9 system (43) to knock down NPR1 in the pifq, pif1-1, pif3-3, pif4-2, and pif5-3 mutants. Real-time PCR and immunoblot data indicated that the NPR1 and PIF4 loci were mutated in the npr1-crispr pif4-2-9 and npr1-crispr pifq-12 mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Subsequently, the npr1-crispr pif4-2-9 and npr1-crispr pifq-12 mutants were grown in the dark or under continuous white and blue light for 4 d; interestingly, we observed that the hypocotyl lengths of the pif4-2 npr1 and npr1-crispr pifq-12 mutant seedlings were significantly shorter than those of npr1-1 or the NPR1 CRISPR line (npr1-crispr-5), and were similar to those of the pif4-2 and pifq mutants under continuous white and blue light (Fig. 2 C–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Notably, the hypocotyl lengths of the pif1-1 npr1-1 and pif5-3 npr1-1 double mutant seedlings were intermediate between those of the npr1-1 and the corresponding pif mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). These data suggest that PIF4 contributes to the long hypocotyl phenotypes of npr1 mutants under blue light.

## NPR1 Interacts with PIF4.
To investigate the mechanisms by which NPR1 functions as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis under blue light in a PIF4-dependent manner, we examined the potential interaction between NPR1 and PIF4. Using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) (Fig. 3A) and split-luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays (Fig. 3B), we identified an interaction between NPR1 and PIF4 in Nicotiana benthamiana. Furthermore, we transiently expressed cLuc-NPR1 and nLuc-PIF4 proteins in Arabidopsis protoplasts and performed LCI assays, which also revealed the interaction between NPR1 and PIF4 (Fig. 3C). In addition, we performed Co-IP assays using the 35S::PIF4-MYC transgenic Arabidopsis line in both the 35S::NPR1-GFP and npr1-1 backgrounds. Consistent with the results shown in Fig. 3 A–C, the NPR1-GFP protein coimmunoprecipitated with PIF4-MYC (Fig. 3D). In parallel, we conducted Co-IP assays using transiently expressed PIF4-MYC and NPR1-mCherry in Arabidopsis protoplasts and observed that the NPR1-mCherry proteins coimmunoprecipitated with PIF4-MYC (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, in conjunction with the previously reported 35S::PIF4-MYC transgenic Arabidopsis line, the endogenous NPR1 protein coimmunoprecipitated with PIF4-MYC under both dark and blue light conditions, but not in the wild type (Col-0) control. Interestingly, our observations revealed an enhanced coimmunoprecipitation of endogenous NPR1 protein with PIF4-MYC in response to blue light (Fig. 3F), indicating that blue light promotes the interaction between NPR1 and PIF4. To test whether the interaction is direct or indirect, we performed pull-down assays. The Glutathione S transferase (GST)-PIF4 and His-NPR1 proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli and subsequently purified using affinity resins. As shown in Fig. 3G, His-NPR1 was specifically pulled down by GST-PIF4, but not by the GST control. Similarly, GST-PIF4 was specifically pulled down by His-NPR1-MYC (Fig. 3H).

## The CUL3-NPR1 Complex Mediates the Polyubiquitination of PIF4.
The above data demonstrated that NPR1 interacts with PIF4. Since NPR1 has been shown to participate in the formation of a ubiquitin E3 ligase with CUL3 to facilitate protein degradation, these results collectively implicate NPR1 in the potential promotion of polyubiquitination of PIF4. To investigate whether NPR1 directly mediates the polyubiquitination of PIF4, we performed in vitro ubiquitination assays using purified MBP-NPR1-MYC, GST-PIF4/His-PIF4, His-CUL3A, His-RBX1, His-E1 (GI: 136632), His-E2 (UBCh5b) and His-UBQ proteins. As shown in Fig. 4A, the levels of polyubiquitination were significantly higher in reactions containing all components compared to those lacking any single component. To further demonstrate the function of NPR1 in the polyubiquitination of PIF4, we compared the polyubiquitination levels of PIF4 in the presence of MBP or MBP-NPR1-MYC. Our results showed significantly higher polyubiquitination levels of PIF4 with MBP-NPR1-MYC than with MBP. Additionally, we conducted in vitro ubiquitination assays with purified MBP-npr1. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S8, the polyubiquitination of GST-PIF4/His-PIF4 with MBP-NPR1-MYC was significantly higher than that with MBP-npr1. These results suggest that NPR1 directly mediates the polyubiquitination of PIF4. Previous work by Zavaliev et al. (37) showed that the BTB domain of NPR1 is essential for interaction with CUL3 in Arabidopsis. To confirm that the enhancement of PIF4 ubiquitination relies on the formation of an E3 ligase complex, we generated MBP-NPR1(ΔBTB)-MYC, a recombinant protein that is unable to interact with CUL3. The results confirmed that NPR1 lacking the BTB domain is indeed unable to ubiquitinate PIF4 in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A).

Furthermore, semi-in vitro ubiquitination assays were conducted using the total protein extracts of the PIF4-overexpressing line (35S::PIF4-MYC). Recombinant GST-NPR1 proteins coupled to resins were incubated with the total protein extracts. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, the polyubiquitination of PIF4-MYC was significantly higher in the sample with GST-NPR1 than in the control sample with GST, suggesting that prokaryotically expressed NPR1 promotes the polyubiquitination of Arabidopsis PIF4. To further investigate the role of NPR1 in the polyubiquitination of PIF4 in vivo, ubiquitination assays were performed in Arabidopsis protoplasts. PIF4-MYC was coexpressed with mCherry or NPR1-mCherry in the Col-0 protoplasts. The PIF4-MYC protein immunoprecipitated by nano-MYC beads was subjected to western blotting using an anti-Flag antibody. Consistent with the results of the semi-in vitro ubiquitination assays, NPR1-mCherry expression significantly increased the polyubiquitination levels of PIF4 (Fig. 4C), indicating that NPR1 mediates the polyubiquitination of PIF4 in vivo. Furthermore, in vivo ubiquitination assays with NPR1-mCherry or NPR1(ΔBTB)-mCherry in Arabidopsis protoplasts showed that NPR1 can ubiquitinate PIF4, whereas NPR1(ΔBTB) lacks this ability (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). To confirm the role of NPR1 in the polyubiquitination of PIF4 in stably transformed plants, we examined the polyubiquitination levels of PIF4 in the presence of the 35S::NPR1-GFP transgene or in the npr1-1 background. The ubiquitinated PIF4 proteins were immunoprecipitated with anti-MYC beads, followed by western blotting analysis with the anti-ubiquitin antibody. As shown in Fig. 4D, the polyubiquitination levels of PIF4 were significantly increased in the NPR1-GFP background but decreased in the npr1-1 background. These results strongly suggest that NPR1 mediates the polyubiquitination of PIF4 in Arabidopsis.
To investigate whether SA regulates the interaction between NPR1 and PIF4, we performed split-LCI assays in N. benthamiana and Arabidopsis protoplasts, which showed that SA significantly enhances this interaction (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). In vivo ubiquitination assays with mCherry-NPR1 and PIF4-MYC further showed that PIF4 ubiquitination is significantly increased in the presence of SA (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). Consistent with the SA-induced enhancement of NPR1-PIF4 interaction and ubiquitination, we observed that SA enhanced PIF4 protein degradation under blue light in wild type plants. However, this SA-enhanced degradation of PIF4 was not detectable in the npr1-1 mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D).

## NPR1 Ubiquitinates PIF4 at Lys129, Lys252, and Lys428.
To identify the PIF4 ubiquitination sites, we performed LC-MS/MS analysis of the in vitro ubiquitination samples. Peptides showing fragment ions with preserved diglycine modifications after trypsin proteolysis were detected at Lys129, Lys252, and Lys428 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). To determine whether Lys129, Lys252, and Lys428 are ubiquitination sites, we substituted them with Arg to generate PIF4 (K129R/K252R/K428R, 3KR). These site-directed mutations did not alter the interaction between PIF4 and NPR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). We then performed in vitro ubiquitination assays using purified GST-PIF4 and GST-PIF4 (3KR). As shown in Fig. 4E, the polyubiquitination levels of GST-PIF4 (3KR) were significantly lower than those of GST-PIF4 in the presence of MBP-NPR1-MYC. Furthermore, semi-in vitro ubiquitination assays were performed on total protein extracts from Col-0 and the npr1-1 mutant. Recombinant GST-PIF4 and GST-PIF4 (3KR) proteins coupled to resins were incubated with the total protein extracts. As shown in Fig. 4F, the polyubiquitination of GST-PIF4 (3KR) was significantly lower than that of GST-PIF4 when incubated with the total protein extracts from both Col-0 and the npr1-1 mutant. Consistent with the results of the semi-in vitro ubiquitination assay, NPR1-mCherry expression significantly increased the polyubiquitination levels of PIF4 in Arabidopsis protoplasts, but failed to ubiquitinate PIF4 (3KR) (Fig. 4G). These results clearly demonstrate that NPR1 ubiquitinates PIF4 at Lys129, Lys252, and Lys428.
Consequently, we conducted semi-in vitro degradation assays to evaluate the degradation of purified recombinant GST-PIF4 and GST-PIF4 (3KR) proteins using Arabidopsis protein extracts from Col-0 and the npr1-1 mutant. The results showed that GST-PIF4 (3KR), when coincubated with plant proteins from Col-0 or the npr1-1 mutant, exhibited a significantly slower degradation rate compared to GST-PIF4. Furthermore, this degradation was prominently inhibited by the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Additionally, we investigated the effect of NPR1 on PIF4 degradation by transiently expressing NPR1-Flag and PIF4-MYC in N. benthamiana. As shown in Fig. 5B, PIF4-MYC was rapidly degraded in the presence of NPR1-Flag, and this degradation was completely inhibited by the 26S proteasome inhibitor MG132. We also found that PIF4 (3KR) was completely resistant to degradation by NPR1-Flag. These findings clearly demonstrate that NPR1 promotes the degradation of PIF4 by ubiquitinating its Lys129, Lys252, and Lys428 sites.

To further confirm this finding, we generated transgenic plants expressing PIF4pro:PIF4 (3KR)-MYC in the pif4-2 background. Our results showed that the levels of PIF4(3KR)-MYC were significantly lower than those of PIF4-MYC (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). The results showed that the hypocotyl lengths of PIF4pro:PIF4-MYC significantly shortened with increasing SA concentration. However, hypocotyl elongation in PIF4pro:PIF4(3KR)-MYC seedlings was insensitive to SA compared to PIF4pro:PIF4-MYC (Fig. 5D). Additionally, we examined the degradation of these two proteins in seedlings transferred from dark to blue light. The results showed that PIF4(3KR)-MYC was indeed more stable than PIF4-MYC when the dark-grown seedlings were exposed to blue light (Fig. 5E). Taken together, these results suggest that NPR1 facilitates the ubiquitination of PIF4 at Lys129, Lys252, and Lys428, thereby promoting photomorphogenesis in response to blue light.

## Cryptochromes Mediate the NPR1-Induced Ubiquitination and Degradation of PIF4 in Response to Blue Light.
Given the hypothesis that NPR1 stimulates photomorphogenesis under blue light by promoting PIF4 degradation, we examined endogenous PIF4 protein levels in both wild type and npr1-1 mutants under dark-to-blue and continuous blue light conditions. Our results showed that PIF4 undergoes degradation in response to blue light, with NPR1 mutations leading to significantly higher PIF4 accumulation under both dark-to-blue and continuous blue light conditions. Although PIF4 was also degraded under dark-to-red and far-red light conditions, NPR1 mutations did not affect its degradation under these conditions (Fig. 5F). These results indicate that NPR1 specifically regulates PIF4 protein levels in response to blue light.
To further confirm the role of NPR1 in the 26S proteasome-mediated degradation of PIF4, we performed an in vivo degradation assay using wild type and npr1-1 seedlings treated with CHX and MG132 (Fig. 5G). Western blot analysis revealed that PIF4 degradation was slower in the npr1-1 mutant than in wild type, indicating NPR1-dependent degradation under blue light. Furthermore, PIF4 degradation was significantly slower in npr1-1 under CHX treatment, aligning with real-time PCR data (SI Appendix, Fig. S15), suggesting that NPR1 does not affect PIF4 transcript levels under blue light. In N. benthamiana, NPR1-mediated PIF4 degradation in response to blue light was completely blocked by MG132, confirming the involvement of the 26S proteasome pathway (Fig. 5G). Taken together, these results demonstrate that NPR1 facilitates PIF4 degradation via the 26S proteasome under blue light. We also examined the in vivo ubiquitination levels of PIF4 in stably transformed plants under dark and dark-to-blue light conditions. As shown in Fig. 5H, PIF4 polyubiquitination significantly increased in the NPR1-GFP background, but decreased in the npr1-1 background during the dark-to-blue light transition. Notably, no ubiquitination was observed under darkness, even in the NPR1-GFP background.
Furthermore, we found that the blue light receptors CRY1 and CRY2 significantly enhanced the interaction between NPR1 and PIF4 in both N. benthamiana and Arabidopsis protoplasts (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Further analysis revealed that in N. benthamiana, CRY1 specifically enhanced the NPR1-mediated degradation of PIF4. In contrast, PIF4(3KR) remained stable and was not degraded by the CRY1-NPR1 complex (Fig. 6C). The in vivo ubiquitination assay demonstrated that PIF4 ubiquitination was significantly increased in 35S::NPR1-GFP seedlings when exposed to blue light compared to darkness (Fig. 5H). To confirm that CRYs enhance PIF4 ubiquitination by NPR1, we performed semi-in vitro ubiquitination assays. The results showed that polyubiquitination of GST-PIF4 was barely detectable in the cry1cry2 double mutant, but was significantly increased in 35S::GFP-CRY1 cry1cry2 and 35S::GFP-CRY2 cry1cry2 (Fig. 6D). The enhancement of the NPR1-PIF4 interaction by CRY1 and CRY2 and the subsequent degradation of PIF4 underscores the critical role of cryptochromes in modulating this pathway. These results strongly suggest that NPR1 mediates the polyubiquitination of PIF4 in response to blue light, a process that is significantly enhanced by cryptochromes.

## NPR1 Modulates Expression of Light-Signaling Genes under Blue Light in a PIF4-Dependent Manner.
To investigate how NPR1 modulates the expression of light-signaling genes in response to blue light in a PIF4-dependent manner, we performed a transcriptomic analysis using Col-0, npr1-1, pif4-2, and pif4 npr1-1 seedlings under continuous blue/red light for 4 d. Under blue light, we identified 1,359 and 459 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) specifically regulated by NPR1 and PIF4, respectively. A comparison of the DEGs individually regulated by NPR1 and PIF4 revealed that approximately 61% (282 out of 459) of the PIF4-regulated DEGs were shared between the npr1-1 and pif4-2 mutants (Fig. 6E). Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that genes involved in shoot system development, response to light stimulus, response to auxin, and gibberellin were enriched in the NPR1 and PIF4 coregulated DEGs (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). Heatmap analysis revealed that the expression patterns of the NPR1-regulated DEGs were almost completely opposite when comparing npr1-1 and pif4-2. Furthermore, the expression pattern of the NPR1-regulated DEGs in the pif4-2 npr1-1 double mutant was similar to that of pif4-2, but different from that of npr1-1 (Fig. 6F). GO enrichment was then performed using the web-based agriGO v2.0 tool (44). The results showed that the NPR1- and PIF4-regulated DEGs were enriched in several common gene categories, including auxin response, light, and temperature stimulus (Fig. 6G), suggesting that NPR1 and PIF4 respond to similar stimuli and regulate cell growth. qRT-PCR analysis of selected growth-promoting genes, including gibberellic acid-stimulated Arabidopsis 9 (45, 46), epithionitrile specifier protein (47), and MYB47 (48), revealed expression patterns consistent with those observed in the genome-wide analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Taken together, our data demonstrate epistatic effects of pif4-2 mutants on gene expression and hypocotyl growth under blue light. This result is consistent with the role of NPR1 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase of PIF4, which facilitates its degradation and positively regulates photomorphogenesis.
Conversely, under red light, we identified a total of 976 and 434 DEGs specifically regulated by NPR1 and PIF4, respectively. Compared to the number of DEGs coregulated by NPR1 and PIF4 under blue light, the number was significantly reduced under red light, accounting for only 22% (96 out of 434) of the PIF4-regulated genes (Fig. 6 E and H). Heatmap analysis revealed that the expression patterns of the NPR1-regulated DEGs in the npr1-1 pif4-2 double mutant differed from those of both npr1-1 and pif4-2 (Fig. 6I). GO enrichment analysis of the NPR1-regulated DEGs showed that genes regulated by NPR1 under red light were less enriched than those under blue light in light stimulus, auxin, and GA response (Fig. 6J), which is consistent with npr1-1 having a long hypocotyl phenotype under blue light, but not under red light (Fig. 2A). Therefore, the reduced number of NPR1–PIF4 coregulated genes and the different gene expression patterns between single (npr1-1 and pif4-2) and double (npr1-1 pif4-2) mutants suggest that NPR1 is not involved in PIF4 regulation under red light.
Based on our biochemical and genetic data, we propose a refined working model to elucidate how NPR1 facilitates blue light–induced plant photomorphogenesis by repressing PIF4 (Fig. 6K). In wild type plants, NPR1 acts as an adaptor for the CUL3-based ubiquitin E3 ligase and promotes the polyubiquitination of PIF4, leading to its degradation via the 26S proteasome. As a result, the stimulation of downstream PIF4-activated growth-promoting genes is significantly attenuated, leading to hypocotyl shortening and facilitating plant photomorphogenesis. Additionally, the NPR1-mediated polyubiquitination of PIF4 in response to blue light may be facilitated by cryptochromes. In the npr1-1 mutant, the level of PIF4 polyubiquitination is significantly reduced, resulting in the accumulation of PIF4 proteins and the subsequent stimulation of the expression of growth-promoting genes, thereby inhibiting plant photomorphogenesis.

## Discussion
NPR1, mediated by SA, acts as the key controller of the immune response and is one of the top five most studied Arabidopsis proteins. Despite these efforts, the biochemical functions of NPR1 in response to abiotic stresses remain largely unknown (49), its potential involvement in the regulation of photomorphogenesis has not been reported. Our findings reveal a role for NPR1 in plant photomorphogenesis. Furthermore, while research on NPR1 has predominantly focused on its role as a transcriptional coactivator (29, 33, 34), our study reveals NPR1’s dual functionality as a mediator of ubiquitin E3 ligase, thereby facilitating PIF4 protein degradation, in agreement with two recent studies (37, 38). In these studies, the authors observed that NPR1 forms a cytoplasmic condensate enriched in immune proteins, thereby promoting their degradation (37). In addition, NPR1 interacts with the GA receptor GID1, facilitating the polyubiquitination and subsequent degradation of GID1 in the nucleus, thereby modulating growth (38). In summary, NPR1 has been shown to target regulators of diverse pathways in both the cytoplasm and nucleus. The exploration of additional NPR1 substrates promises to shed light on the functional scope of NPR1 as a ubiquitin E3 ligase.
It has been reported that NPR1 exists in both an inactive oligomeric form and an active monomeric form accompanied by nuclear translocation, and that SA treatment facilitates the transition from oligomer to monomer (50–52). Our study showed that NPR1 can interact with PIF4 and polyubiquitinated PIF4 in the nucleus even in the absence of SA. There are two possible explanations for this phenomenon: First, in the absence of SA, although the majority of NPR1 proteins are in the oligomeric form, some are in the monomeric form, which may be located in the nucleus. Second, previous studies have demonstrated that cold conditions can induce the nuclear localization of NPR1 (53); therefore, it is possible that blue light exposure may similarly enhance the nuclear localization of NPR1, resulting in increased targeting of PIF4 by active NPR1 proteins. Further investigation is warranted to determine if and how blue light influences the oligomeric/monomeric ratio of NPR1 and its nuclear translocation.
NPR1, a member of the BTB-ankyrin protein family along with NPR2/3/4/5(BOP1)/6(BOP2), exhibits distinct functions in plant development (35, 54–57). Specifically, BOP1 and BOP2 act as adaptors for CUL3-based E3 complexes that facilitate PIF4 ubiquitination in response to red light (25). Interestingly, our study reveals that the regulation of NPR1-mediated PIF4 ubiquitination occurs under blue light. In particular, the bop1 bop2 double mutant shows red light–specific photomorphogenic phenotypes (25), whereas npr1-1 shows only blue light–induced photomorphogenic phenotypes (Fig. 2A), which implies precise regulation of PIF4 by different E3 ligases under different light conditions. Since NPR1 and BOP1/2, homologous proteins of the same family, can ubiquitinate PIF4 under different light conditions, they are likely to be regulated by photoreceptors such as phytochromes and cryptochromes upstream of PIF4. We hypothesize that the activation of different photoreceptors by different wavelengths of light may influence the interactions and ubiquitination levels of NPR1-PIF4 and BOP1/2-PIF4, respectively, thereby contributing to the precise posttranslational regulation of PIF4. The effect of different photoreceptors on the NPR1/BOP-mediated posttranslational modification of PIF4 deserves further investigation. In addition to its involvement in photomorphogenesis, PIF4 also plays a critical role in thermomorphogenesis at elevated temperatures (22). Further investigation is required to determine whether the regulation of PIF4 by NPR1 also affects thermomorphogenesis in plants, thereby regulating PIF4 in response to both light and temperature.
Although both PIF3 and PIF4 are ubiquitinated by a CUL3-based E3 ligase that uses BTB domain-containing proteins as substrate adaptors (16, 17, 25), the patterns of the light-induced changes differ between the PIF3 and PIF4 proteins. Light triggers a sustained degradation of PIF3, whereas light induces a rapid degradation followed by a gradual recovery of PIF4 (25, 58, 59). PIF4 protein levels in seedlings grown under continuous light are demonstrably higher than those observed in etiolated seedlings (58). Maintaining elevated PIF4 levels under continuous light may prepare seedlings for growth after de-etiolation, especially when rapid growth is required to adapt to environmental changes such as shade and high temperatures (22, 23). Light induces the phosphorylation of PIFs, leading to their inactivation either by ubiquitination followed by protein degradation or by exclusion from the nucleus (11, 12, 14, 15). In particular, light-induced phosphorylation is crucial for the proteasomal degradation of PIF3 (13). In contrast, our in vitro results using recombinant, unphosphorylated E. coli-produced proteins, along with previous findings on BOPs-mediated PIF4 degradation (25), suggest that PIF4 phosphorylation is not necessary for NPR1-mediated PIF4 degradation (Fig. 4A). The ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of nonphosphorylated PIF4 by CUL3NPR1 and CUL3BOP1/2 may play a pivotal role in the accumulation of PIF4 under continuous light conditions.
As a key regulator of light and temperature responses, the transcriptional activity of PIF4 is intricately controlled by environmental signals. The cryptochromes CRY1 and CRY2 interact with PIF4 and PIF5, modulating their activities to promote growth under LBL conditions (23). Additionally, CRY1 directly interacts with PIF4, inhibiting its transcriptional activity and suppressing high temperature-induced hypocotyl elongation (24). Specifically, our study further illustrates how cryptochromes help to integrate light signals into developmental responses by modulating the stability of key transcription factors like PIF4. The ability of CRY1 and CRY2 to enhance the NPR1-PIF4 interaction and subsequent degradation of PIF4 is critical for maintaining proper growth and adaptation in varying light environments, demonstrating the sophisticated nature of light signaling in plants.
Notably, NPR1 is widely recognized as a transcriptional cofactor alongside TGA transcription factors, promoting PR gene expression and enhancing pathogen resistance (54, 55, 60). Interestingly, NPR1 interacts with the transcription factors MYC, ETHYLENE INSENSITIVE3 (EIN3), and EIN3-LIKE1 as part of the SA-mediated crosstalk with jasmonic acid and ethylene, respectively (61, 62). Our study demonstrates that NPR1 facilitates blue light–induced photomorphogenesis by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of PIF4. Transient reporter gene expression assays revealed that PIF4 enhances the activity of the LONG HYPOCOTYL IN FAR-RED 1 and YUCCA8 promoters, which is counteracted by cotransfection with NPR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). However, the precise role of NPR1 as a transcriptional activator in the regulation of blue light–mediated photomorphogenesis and as a modulator of PIF4 transcriptional activity requires further investigation.
