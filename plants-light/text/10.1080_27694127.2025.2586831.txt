# Illuminating the regulatory link between blue light and autophagy in photomorphogenesis

## Abstract
ABSTRACTCryptochrome 1 (CRY1) promotes photomorphogenesis primarily by inhibiting Constituttive photomorphogenic 1 (COP1)/Suppressor of PHYA-105 1 (SPA1)-mediated degradation of HY5 via the 26S proteasome degradation pathway. However, it remained unknown whether autophagy, a conserved vacuolar recycling process induced by nutrient starvation, also participates in blue light signaling. Our latest study reveals that in Arabidopsis thaliana, under nutrient starvation, Autophagy-related 8 (ATG8) binds and targets Elongated hypocotyl 5 (HY5) for vacuolar degradation in darkness, thereby promoting skotomorphogenesis. Upon blue-light activation, however, CRY1 binds to ATG8 and blocks its interaction with HY5, which in turn inhibits the autophagic degradation of HY5 and promotes photomorphogenesis. Our findings thus establish a direct photoreceptor – autophagy functional connection that integrates light and nutrient cues to govern developmental transitions in plants.KEYWORDS: Autophagy, ATG8, CRY1, HY5, photomorphogenesis, skotomorphogenesis
In our recent study[1], we addressed a critical knowledge gap in understanding how the blue light photoreceptor CRY1 cross-talks with autophagy to regulate photomorphogenesis, which is notably characterized by reduced elongation of hypocotyls, unfolded and expanded cotyledons, and chlorophyll accumulation, which allows them to carry out photosynthesis and begin autotrophic growth. While it is well established that CRY1 signaling stabilizes the transcription factor HY5 by suppressing its proteasomal degradation mediated by COP1/SPA1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex to promote photomorphogenesis, whether autophagy also mediates HY5 degradation and photomorphogenesis under nutrient starvation remained unknown. Our findings reveal a direct, blue light-dependent physical interaction between CRY1 and ATG8, an interaction that serves as a molecular switch, finely tuning the autophagic turnover of HY5 and thereby orchestrating seedling development under nutrient starvation.
The core of this regulatory mechanism hinges on a blue light-dependent physical interaction between CRY1 and ATG8, a core autophagy protein. In the dark, ATG8 interacts with HY5 in the nucleus, facilitating its nuclear export and subsequent sequestration within autophagosomes for vacuolar degradation. This process maintains low HY5 levels to promote skotomorphogenesis, which is characterized by elongated hypocotyls and folded and closed cotyledons. These adaptations enable the seedling to emerge from the soil before its stored nutrients run out, so it can then capture the sunlight, undergo photomorphogenesis, and carry out photosynthesis (Figure 1). Upon blue light exposure, photoexcited CRY1 undergoes a conformational change that allows its directly binding to ATG8. The CRY1-ATG8 interaction acts as a competitive molecular switch: it sequesters ATG8 thereby preventing its association with HY5. As a result, the autophagic degradation of HY5 is inhibited, leading to its accumulation in the nucleus and promoting photomorphogenesis (Figure 1). Genetic analyses firmly position the core autophagy components ATG5, ATG7, and ATG8 downstream of CRY1, yet upstream of HY5 in regulating hypocotyl elongation. Notably, the physiological significance of this pathway is heightened under nutrient starvation, a condition that induces autophagy. Autophagy-deficient mutants such as atg5 atg7, atg8n exhibit enhanced photomorphogenesis phenotype under blue light combined with nutrient starvation, highlighting the critical role of autophagy in photomorphogenesis.
A model for CRY1-regulated selective autophagy of HY5. (A) In darkness, nuclear ATG8 binds and directs HY5 to autophagosomes for vacuolar degradation, promoting skotomorphogenesis. (B) Under blue light, photoexcited CRY1 binds and sequesters ATG8, preventing its interaction with HY5 and turnover, leading to HY5 accumulation and thus photomorphogenesis.
Overall, our study establishes the CRY1-ATG8 interaction as a central regulatory node that enables plants to integrate light and nutrient starvation signals. This finding opens the door to several compelling speculations. First, the competitive binding between CRY1 and HY5 for ATG8 represents a sophisticated and rapid post-translational mechanism for controlling the stability of a master transcription factor. This finding prompts a broader speculation: whether diverse signaling pathways, from environmental sensing (e.g., via other photoreceptors or thermosensors) to endogenous hormone signaling (e.g., via Gibberellin [GA]), might converge on ATG8 through direct interactions to regulate the turnover of specific proteins.
Second, our finding that the CRY1-ATG8 switch enables a “cost–benefit” analysis, prioritizing skotomorphogenesis in the dark to escape soil and photomorphogenesis in light to initiate photosynthesis, makes wondering whether plants possess a more universal “resource allocation decision-making system.” We speculate that under combined stresses, such as shade and nutrient limitation, analogous integration mechanisms, potentially involving phytochromes and autophagy, could help plants make optimal trade-offs, for instance, between vertical growth to compete for light and maintaining a low-consumption state to await better opportunities.
Finally, the observation that CRY1 also suppresses general autophagosome formation in the cytosol points to a possible dual functionality. Beyond specifically protecting HY5, CRY1 may modulate the autophagic flux under blue light. This could reflect a global mechanism to recalibrate cellular recycling and anabolic processes in response to light, potentially influencing a wider spectrum of physiological outcomes beyond development, such as stress adaptation.
Future research should aim to elucidate how plants allocate resources under combined stress. A key objective is to determine whether diverse environmental cues and hormonal signals converge on ATG8 as a central hub to directly control the turnover of key proteins. Understanding this mechanism will reveal universal principles of proteostasis that enable precise biological regulation across molecular to ecological scales.
