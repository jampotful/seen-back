TCP21 negatively regulates photomorphogenesis by integrating phyB- and HY5-mediated signaling pathways in Arabidopsis.

# TCP21 negatively regulates photomorphogenesis by integrating phyB‐ and HY5‐mediated signaling pathways in Arabidopsis

## Abstract
SUMMARYThe phytochrome B (phyB)‐transcription factor signaling module plays a critical role in regulating photomorphogenesis. In this study, TCP21, a member of the Class I TCP transcription factor family, was identified as a direct interacting partner of phyB. TCP21 negatively regulates photomorphogenesis through downregulating HY5 expression and repressing the transcriptional activity of HY5. Moreover, phyB physically interacts with TCP21 and inhibits its transcriptional activity. Notably, phyB outcompetes HY5 for binding to TCP21, thereby attenuating the interaction between TCP21 and HY5 and alleviating TCP21‐mediated repression of HY5 DNA‐binding ability. Collectively, these findings establish TCP21 as a key component within the phyB‐HY5 signaling module, contributing to plant adaptation to dynamic light environments.Keywords: Arabidopsis, HY5, photomorphogenesis, phyB, TCP21

## INTRODUCTION
Plants, as sessile organisms anchored in the soil, must respond to internal and external stimuli to adapt to constantly changing environments and optimize their growth and fitness. Light, the energy source required for photosynthesis, also serves as a vital environmental signal that regulates various physiological processes across the plant life cycle, ranging from germination to ripening (Paik & Huq, 2019; Zhou & Deng, 2025). Arabidopsis seedlings have evolved two distinct developmental programs to adapt to changes in light conditions. Upon germination in soil without light, dark‐grown seedlings undergo skotomorphogenesis characterized by elongated hypocotyls and closed cotyledons with an apical hook (Zhou & Deng, 2025). In contrast to skotomorphogenesis, Arabidopsis seedlings adopt the developmental program of photomorphogenesis when exposed to light. The hypocotyl elongation is inhibited in light‐grown seedlings, while the cotyledons open, expand, and green with well‐developed chloroplasts (Zhou & Deng, 2025).
So far, at least 14 photoreceptors in Arabidopsis, such as phytochromes and cryptochromes, have been identified to sense and transduce light signals (Liu et al., 2025). Among these photoreceptors, PHYTOCHROME B (phyB) functions as the predominant red light photoreceptor and has been extensively studied in Arabidopsis (Cheng et al., 2021; Viczián & Nagy, 2024). In company with the transition from darkness to red light, phyB undergoes light‐induced conformational remodeling from the inactive red light‐absorbing Pr form to the active far‐red light‐absorbing Pfr form (Cai & Huq, 2024). Subsequently, light‐activated phyB can translocate from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, where it assembles into photobodies and exerts its biological functions (Cai & Huq, 2024). phyB exists as an asymmetric dimeric chromoprotein, with each monomer consisting of an N‐terminal photosensory module (PSM) and a C‐terminal output module (OPM) (Burgie & Vierstra, 2014; Cheng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). The PSM is responsible for phytochromobilin binding and light perception, whereas the OPM is primarily involved in dimerization, nuclear localization, photobody assembly, and signal transduction to downstream effectors (Burgie & Vierstra, 2014; Cheng et al., 2021).
ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL5 (HY5), a member of the bZIP transcription factor family, was originally identified as the first COP1 substrate (Osterlund et al., 2000; Xiao et al., 2022). Now, HY5 has been widely recognized as a key positive regulator of photomorphogenesis (Xiao et al., 2022). By directly binding to a set of cis‐regulatory elements including T/G‐box (CACGTT), E‐box (CAATTG), GATA‐box (GATGATA), ACE‐box (ACGT), Z‐box (ATACGGT), C‐box (GTCANN), and hybrid C/G‐(G) and C/A‐boxes, HY5 orchestrates the expression of downstream light‐responsive factors and ultimately promotes photomorphogenic development (Gangappa & Botto, 2016; Xiao et al., 2022). It is estimated that approximately one‐third of the genes in Arabidopsis genome are directly or indirectly regulated by HY5 (Gangappa & Botto, 2016; Xiao et al., 2022). A previous study has revealed that 60% of the early‐induced genes and 27% of the late‐induced genes under red light are HY5 binding targets, indicating a hierarchical role of HY5 in red light‐dependent photomorphogenic development (Lee et al., 2007). Additionally, HY5 itself is subject to complex and precise regulations in plants. The expression of HY5 is under the control of diverse transcription factors including BBX11/14/15/16/21, WRKY32/36, TCP2, and HY5 itself, whereas the activity of HY5 is regulated by a series of co‐factors, such as BBX21/22/23/24/25/28/32, COR27/28 (Abbas et al., 2014; He et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020; Nasim et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2022; Xu, 2020; Yang et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2020).
TCP proteins are a class of plant‐specific transcription factors, characterized by a conserved TCP domain that confers both DNA‐ and protein‐binding capabilities (Wang et al., 2025). The Arabidopsis genome encodes a total of 24 TCPs (Wang et al., 2025). Based on the sequence difference of the TCP domain, these TCPs are classified into two subfamilies, Class I (TCP‐P) and Class II (TCP‐C), and Class II can be further divided into CYC and CIN clades (Liu et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022, 2025). TCP proteins have been reported to be involved in a wide range of biological processes, including photomorphogenesis (Viola et al., 2023). TCP2 acts as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis by promoting HY5/HYH expression (He et al., 2016). However, overexpression of TCP5, TCP13, and TCP17 significantly promotes hypocotyl elongation (Han et al., 2019). Additionally, TCP3 is identified as a COP1 substrate and regulates anthocyanin accumulation and flowering time (Tao et al., 2025). TCP10 in concert with GLK1 promotes cotyledon de‐etiolation in Arabidopsis (Alem et al., 2025).
In this study, we have identified TCP21, a member of the TCP transcription factor family, as a phyB‐interacting protein. TCP21 functions as a negative regulator of photomorphogenesis. TCP21 directly binds to the HY5 promoter and represses its expression, while the transcriptional activity of TCP21 can be inhibited by phyB. Moreover, TCP21 physically interacts with HY5 and represses its transcriptional activity. phyB outcompetes HY5 and interacts with TCP21, thus attenuating the interaction between TCP21 and HY5. Taken together, our results imply that TCP21 is involved in both phyB‐ and HY5‐mediated signaling pathways to mediate photomorphogenic development.

## RESULTS

## TCP21 physically interacts with phyB
In our previous work, we conducted a yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screen using the C‐terminus of phyB as the bait and successfully identified a series of potential phyB‐interacting factors (Liu et al., 2025). TCP21 was among these candidate proteins, and its interaction with phyB was further validated by Y2H assays (Figure 1A,B). Domain‐mapping assays revealed that the C‐terminal region of TCP21, but not the N‐terminal region, is responsible for mediating the interaction with phyB‐C (Figure S1). Next, in vitro pull‐down assays were performed to confirm the interaction between TCP21 and phyB. As shown in Figure 1(C), the recombinant MBP‐phyB‐C protein, but not MBP protein alone, could specifically pull down His‐TCP21 (Figure 1C).

Subsequently, we performed the firefly luciferase (LUC) complementation imaging (LCI) and in vivo co‐IP assays to examine whether TCP21 associates with phyB in planta. In LCI assays, cLUC‐TCP21 and phyB‐nLUC were transiently co‐expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, which generated a strong LUC signal compared with the negative controls (Figure 1D). Additionally, in vivo co‐IP assays were adopted to assess whether the interaction between TCP21 and phyB is light‐dependent. EGFP‐TCP21 Col‐0 overexpression plants were grown in the dark for 4 days and then either maintained in the dark (D) or exposed to red light (R) for 0.5 h before harvesting. The results showed that endogenous phyB could be specifically co‐immunoprecipitated by EGFP‐TCP21 under red light conditions, demonstrating that the TCP21‐phyB interaction is light‐dependent (Figure 1E). Taken together, all these data imply that TCP21 directly interacts with phyB likely in a light‐dependent manner.

## TCP21 encodes a negative regulator of photomorphogenesis
In order to determine whether TCP21 is involved in the light signaling pathway, we acquired a T‐DNA line tcp21‐1, which is a knockdown mutant, and generated two independent knockout mutants, tcp21‐2 and tcp21‐3, through CRISPR‐Cas9 technology (Figure S2) (Zhang et al., 2019). Next, the phenotypes of all these three tcp21 mutants were investigated under various light conditions. Compared with Col‐0, all three tcp21 mutants displayed significantly shortened hypocotyls under all tested light conditions (white, red, far‐red, and blue light), indicating that TCP21 is a negative regulator of photomorphogenesis (Figure 2A–H). However, all tcp21 mutants exhibited an indistinguishable phenotype with Col‐0 in the dark (Figure 2I,J).

To confirm that the mutations in TCP21 cause the shortened hypocotyl phenotype of tcp21 mutants, we performed genomic complementation experiments. The construction TCP21pro:TCP21‐Flag was transformed into tcp21‐2 background, and subsequently two independent transgenic lines were isolated. TCP21pro:TCP21‐Flag tcp21‐2 #4 completely restored the hypocotyl phenotype of tcp21‐2, whereas TCP21pro:TCP21‐Flag tcp21‐2 #9 partially complemented the hypocotyl phenotype of tcp21‐2 under white, blue, and far‐red light conditions (Figure S3).
Next, we generated two independent TCP21 overexpression lines and analyzed their phenotypes under all tested light conditions (Figures S4 and S5). The hypocotyl length of both TCP21 overexpression lines was significantly longer than that of Col‐0 under all light conditions, but comparable to that of Col‐0 in darkness (Figure S5). These results confirm that TCP21 negatively regulates photomorphogenesis.

## TCP21 directly binds to the promoter region of HY5 and represses its expression
A previous study has revealed that TCP2 acts as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis through binding to the chromatins of HY5/HYH and promoting their expression (He et al., 2016). Therefore, we investigated whether TCP21 also regulates the gene expression of HY5. Real‐time PCR analysis revealed that HY5 expression was significantly elevated in tcp21 mutants, whereas it was reduced in TCP21 overexpression lines, suggesting that HY5 is a potential downstream target of TCP21 (Figure 3A,B).

To examine this possibility, yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays were conducted, which revealed that TCP21 directly binds to the HY5 promoter in yeast cells (Figure 3C). It has been previously reported that TCP transcription factors recognize cis‐regulatory elements with the consensus sequences GTGGGNCC (complementary sequence: GGNCCCAC) or GTGGNCCC (complementary sequence: GGGNCCAC) (González‐Grandío & Cubas, 2016; Zhang et al., 2022). Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) further confirmed TCP21 specifically binds to the HY5 promoter region containing the GGGCCCAC motif, but not to the mutated sequence TTTAAACA (Figure 3D,E). Subsequently, transient expression assays using a dual‐LUC reporter system (DLR) in Arabidopsis protoplasts showed that TCP21 significantly represses the transcriptional activity of HY5pro:LUC (Figure 3F,G). Additionally, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)‐qPCR assays revealed that TCP21 binds to the HY5 promoter in vivo (Figure 3H). Collectively, these findings indicate that TCP21 functions as a transcriptional repressor of HY5.

## TCP21 directly interacts with HY5 and inhibits its transcriptional activity
To further investigate whether TCP21 physically interacts with HY5, we conducted a series of interaction assays. First, Y2H assays demonstrated that TCP21 indeed interacts with HY5 in yeast cells (Figure 4A). Domain‐mapping assays revealed that the C‐terminal region of TCP21, but not the N‐terminal region, is responsible for binding to HY5 (Figure S6). Further, we found that the N‐terminal region of HY5 was required for its interaction with TCP21 (Figure S6). Next, in vitro pull‐down assays were performed using purified recombinant proteins, including GST, GST‐TCP21, and His‐HY5. Immunoblot analysis revealed that GST‐TCP21, but not GST alone, specifically pulled down His‐HY5 (Figure 4B). To validate this interaction in planta, LCI assays were performed, which displayed a strong LUC signal when HY5‐nLUC and cLUC‐TCP21 were transiently co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves (Figure 4C). Furthermore, semi‐in vivo pull‐down assays were performed using recombinant His‐HY5 protein and cell extracts from the transgenic seedlings expressing EGFP‐TCP21. Immunoblot analysis revealed that EGFP‐TCP21 pulled down His‐HY5 (Figure 4D). These data collectively suggest that TCP21 interacts with HY5 both in vivo and in vitro.

Given the observed physical interaction between TCP21 and HY5, we next sought to examine their functional interplay. HY5 acts as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis by directly or indirectly mediating the expression of approximately one‐third of the genes in the Arabidopsis genome (Gangappa & Botto, 2016; Xiao et al., 2022). Therefore, we selected the promoter of IAA19, a known target of HY5, to test whether TCP21 affects the DNA‐binding ability of HY5 (Jing et al., 2013). In Y1H assays, activation domain (AD)‐fused HY5 was able to bind to the IAA19 promoter and activated the IAA19pro:LacZ reporter (Figure 4E). Notably, co‐expression of ΔAD‐TCP21 significantly reduced AD‐HY5‐activated IAA19pro:LacZ in yeast cells (Figure 4E). EMSA assays showed that purified His‐HY5, but not His‐TCP21, could directly bind to the IAA19 promoter. However, the binding capacity of His‐HY5 to the IAA19 promoter was progressively diminished with the increasing amounts of His‐TCP21 in the reactions (Figure 4F). Furthermore, DLR assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts revealed that HY5 represses the transcription of IAA19pro:LUC (Figure 4G,H). However, this repression was significantly attenuated in the presence of TCP21 (Figure 4G,H). Additionally, ChIP‐qPCR assays revealed that HY5 binds to the promoter of IAA19 in vivo (Figure 4I). Notably, the association of HY5 with the IAA19 promoter was significantly increased in tcp21‐2 seedlings compared with Col‐0 plants, implying that TCP21 inhibits the DNA‐binding ability of HY5 (Figure 4I). Taken together, these findings strongly suggest that TCP21 inhibits the transcriptional activity of HY5, probably by impairing its ability to bind to the promoters of its target genes.
To support the conclusion that TCP21 negatively controls HY5‐regulated gene expression, we performed qRT‐PCR analysis to assess IAA19 expression level in tcp21 mutants. As shown in Figure 4(J), the transcript level of IAA19 was drastically reduced in the tcp21‐2 and tcp21‐3 mutants compared with Col‐0 (Figure 4J). Given that IAA19 is also a target of PIF proteins, we examined the transcript and protein levels of PIFs in tcp21 mutants (Sun et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2019). PIF expression in tcp21 mutants was comparable to that in Col‐0, implying that the repression of IAA19 expression in tcp21 mutants is unlikely caused by PIFs (Figure S7).
In addition, the expression of six HY5‐regulated genes, IAA19, EXP2, PME16, CHS, LDOX, and PRX26, was examined in Col‐0, tcp21‐2, hy5‐215, and hy5‐215 tcp21‐2. The expression of these genes in hy5‐215 tcp21‐2 was close to those in hy5‐215, suggesting that TCP21 regulates gene expression, at least in part, in an HY5‐dependent manner (Figure 4K).

## TCP21 genetically interacts with HY5
To investigate the genetic relationship between TCP21 and HY5 in regulating photomorphogenesis, the hy5‐215 tcp21‐2 double mutant was generated by crossing hy5‐215 with tcp21‐2. Under all tested light conditions, hy5‐215 exhibited longer hypocotyls compared with Col‐0, whereas tcp21‐2 showed shorter hypocotyls (Figure S8). Notably, the hypocotyl length of hy5‐215 tcp21‐2 was longer than that of both Col‐0 and tcp21‐2, but significantly shorter than those of hy5‐215 (Figure S8). Taken together, these results, coupled with the observation that TCP21 represses HY5 expression and inhibits its transcriptional activity, demonstrate that HY5 might function partially downstream of TCP21 to inhibit hypocotyl elongation.

## phyB represses the transcriptional activity of TCP21
Given the physical interaction identified between phyB and TCP21 (Figure 1), we further explored the regulatory impact of phyB on TCP21's transcriptional activity. Y1H assays revealed that co‐expression of ΔAD‐phyB‐C significantly diminished the activation of HY5pro:LacZ reporter by AD‐TCP21 in yeast cells (Figure 5A). Additionally, EMSA assays showed that in vitro purified MBP‐phyB‐C was unable to bind to HY5 promoter directly. However, MBP‐phyB‐C, not MBP alone, markedly impaired the binding capacity of His‐TCP21 to HY5 promoter (Figure 5B). These findings were further validated in planta using DLR assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts. As shown in Figure 5(C,D), TCP21 repressed the transcription of HY5pro:LUC, while this repressive effect was significantly attenuated upon co‐expression of phyB (Figure 5C,D). Taken together, these findings strongly support the conclusion that phyB inhibits TCP21's transcriptional activity, likely by interfering with TCP21's capacity to bind to the promoters of its target genes.

## phyB attenuates the interaction between TCP21 and HY5
Domain‐mapping assays revealed that both phyB and HY5 interact with TCP21 via its C‐terminal region, suggesting a potential for competitive binding to TCP21 (Figures S1 and S6). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) analysis demonstrated that HY5 binds to TCP21 with KD = 4.12E‐5 m, whereas phyB exhibits a significantly stronger interaction, with KD <1.0E‐12 m, indicating that phyB has a substantially higher binding affinity for TCP21 compared with HY5 (Figure 6A). To further investigate the influence of phyB on the TCP21‐HY5 interaction, LCI assays were conducted under both red and far‐red light conditions. Co‐expression of cLUC‐TCP21 and HY5‐nLUC in N. benthamiana leaves generated robust LUC signals under both light conditions. However, the addition of phyB under red light, but not far‐red light, led to a reduction in signal intensity (Figure 6B–E). Collectively, these data indicate that the active phyB form could attenuate the interaction between TCP21 and HY5, likely through outcompeting HY5 for binding to TCP21.

## Genetic relationship between phyB and TCP21
To investigate the genetic relationship between phyB and TCP21, we generated phyB‐9 tcp21‐2 double mutant and analyzed its hypocotyl phenotype. Phenotype analysis revealed that the hypocotyl length of the phyB‐9 tcp21‐2 double mutant was intermediate between that of phyB‐9 and tcp21‐2 single mutants, partially restoring the hypocotyl phenotype of phyB‐9 (Figure S9). These genetic findings suggest that TCP21 functions in the phyB‐mediated signaling pathway.

## DISCUSSION
Among phytochromes, phyB plays a dominant role in the light‐grown plants. Upon red light illumination, phyB triggers photomorphogenic development via multilevel regulatory mechanisms, including transcriptional control (Cheng et al., 2021; Hernando et al., 2021). Gene expression profiling has revealed that up to 4857 differentially expressed genes are identified in phyB‐9 mutant, indicating that gene expression control plays a fundamental role in phyB‐mediated responses (Yan et al., 2025).
It is well established that light‐activated phyB drives transcriptome reprogramming through multiple mechanisms. First, phyB directly interacts with transcription factors and promotes their degradation, thereby altering downstream gene expression (Liu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2023). Second, phyB can regulate transcription factor activity via direct physical interaction (Legris et al., 2019; Park et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2018). A canonical example is the phyB‐PIF interaction, which directly blocks the DNA‐binding capacity of PIF proteins (Legris et al., 2019; Park et al., 2012). Consistent with this regulatory paradigm, our study demonstrates that phyB directly interacts with TCP21 and represses its DNA‐binding capacity (Figure 5). Furthermore, phyB can influence the interaction between TF and its co‐factors to reshape transcriptional outputs. For instance, phyB directly interacts with both AHL21 and PIF4 and acts as a molecular glue to strengthen the AHL21‐PIF4 interaction, which in turn promotes AHL21‐mediated repression of PIF4 transcriptional activation ability (Liu et al., 2025). However, in this study, we uncovered that phyB outcompetes TCP21 for binding to HY5, thus promoting HY5 activity through releasing TCP21‐mediated repression of HY5 transcriptional ability (Figures 4 and 6). Collectively, the phyB‐TF signaling module is central to the precise regulation of photomorphogenesis.
TCP proteins are a class of plant‐specific transcription factors comprising 24 members (Wang et al., 2025). To date, TCP proteins have been reported to be widely involved in mediating plant photomorphogenic development (Alem et al., 2025; Han et al., 2019; He et al., 2016; Tao et al., 2025). For instance, TCP2, belonging to the Class II subfamily of the TCP family, acts as a positive regulator of photomorphogenesis. CRY1 directly interacts with TCP2 and promotes its stability, enabling TCP2 to bind to HY5 and HYH chromatins and promote their expression (He et al., 2016). In this study, we show that the Class I TCP member TCP21 participates in phyB‐dependent signaling pathway and negatively regulates photomorphogenesis (Figure S10). phyB directly interacts with TCP21 and inhibits its transcriptional activity (Figures 1 and 5). Moreover, phyB outcompetes TCP21 for binding to HY5, thereby alleviating TCP21‐mediated repression of HY5 DNA‐binding ability (Figures 4 and 6). Taken together, these data imply that the TCP proteins have evolved divergent functions in photomorphogenesis and likely function through photoreceptor–TCP regulatory modules.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant materials and growth conditions
The Arabidopsis thaliana mutants phyB‐9 (Reed et al., 1993) and hy5‐215 (Oyama et al., 1997) have been described previously. The T‐DNA mutant tcp21‐1 (Salk_143403) was kindly provided by Dr. Cui (Zhang et al., 2019). tcp21‐2 and tcp21‐3 mutants were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Yang et al., 2015). phyB‐9 tcp21‐2 and hy5‐215 tcp21‐2 double mutants were produced via genetic crossing.
Arabidopsis seeds were surface‐sterilized in 15% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite containing 0.05% Triton X‐100 for 5 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with sterile ddH2O, and then sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (PhytoTech, pH 5.7, Lenexa, KS, USA) supplemented with 1% (w/v) sucrose and 0.8% (w/v) agar. Following cold stratification at 4°C in the dark for 3 days, the seeds were exposed to continuous white light (100 μmol m−2 sec−1) at 22°C for 12 h to synchronize germination, after which they were transferred to either dark or specified light conditions at 22°C for further treatments as indicated.

## Generation of CRISPR/Cas9‐induced tcp21 knockout mutants and TCP21 transgenic plants
The tcp21‐2 and tcp21‐3 mutants were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Two sgRNA target sites within the TCP21 exon were designed using CRISPR‐P (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR/). The sgRNA sequences were then cloned into the vector pHEE401E, which was subsequently transformed into wild‐type Col‐0 plants via Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101‐mediated floral dip. The Cas9 transgenic‐positive plants were selected on MS medium containing 50 mg L−1 hygromycin and further genotyped by DNA sequencing. The T‐DNA insertions carrying CRISPR/Cas9 in tcp21‐2 and tcp21‐3 mutants were removed by crossing with Col‐0.
For construction of TCP21pro:TCP21‐Flag tcp21‐2 transgenic plants, a 2008 bp genomic fragment upstream of the TCP21 start codon, along with the full‐length TCP21 coding sequence (CDS), was amplified and fused in‐frame to a 3 × Flag epitope tag. This sequence was subsequently cloned into the EcoRI/BamHI sites of the binary vector pCAMBIA1300. Subsequently, this construction was introduced into GV3101 and transformed into the tcp21‐2 mutant background. To generate EGFP‐TCP21 Col‐0 transgenic lines, the EGFP sequence was fused in‐frame with the TCP21 CDS and the native 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of TCP21. This assembly was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300‐UBQ vector at the EcoRI and BamHI sites, followed by transformation into Col‐0. Transgenic plants were selected on MS medium containing 50 mg L−1 hygromycin.

## Quantitative real‐time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from Arabidopsis seedlings using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). For cDNA synthesis, 0.5 μg of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed using the SupRealQ Purple Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (U+) (Vazyme) and conducted on the ABI QuantStudio 6 Pro System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Relative gene transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to PP2A expression. Primer sequences used for RT‐qPCR are provided in Table S1.

## Western blot and antibodies
Plant tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground into a fine powder, and lysed in an extraction buffer containing 8 m urea, 100 mM NaH2PO4, 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Basel‐Stadt, Switzerland). The resulting lysates were clarified by two consecutive centrifugations at 13 000 g for 10 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations were quantified using the Bradford assay (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Equal amounts of total protein were mixed with SDS loading buffer, denatured at 100°C for 10 min, and separated by SDS‐PAGE. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, which were then blocked with 5% (w/v) non‐fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies and detected using ECL reagent (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA).
The following primary antibodies were used: anti‐His (Sigma‐Aldrich, 1:1000, St. Louis, MO, USA), anti‐MBP (New England Biolabs, 1:5000, Ipswich, MA, USA), anti‐GST (Abclonal, 1:2000, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti‐phyB (PhytoAB, 1:1000, San Jose, CA, USA), anti‐GFP (Abmart, 1:2000, Shanghai, China), anti‐HSP82 (Beijing Protein Innovation, 1:5000, Beijing, China), anti‐Flag (Abmart, 1:2000), anti‐PIF1 (Abiocode, 1:1000, Agoura Hills, CA, USA), anti‐PIF3 (Agrisera, 1:1000, Vännäs, Sweden), anti‐PIF4 (Agrisera, 1:1000), and anti‐PIF5 (Agrisera, 1:1000).

## Measurement of hypocotyl length
Seedlings grown on MS medium were carefully transferred and arranged flat on 1% agar plates. Images were captured using a digital camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan), and hypocotyl lengths were quantified with ImageJ. Data were subsequently analyzed using GraphPad Prism.

## Y1H and Y2H assays
The full‐length CDS, the N‐terminal region (1–86 aa), and the C‐terminal region (87–239 aa) of TCP21, as well as the full‐length CDS, the N‐terminal region (1–77 aa), and the C‐terminal region (78–168 aa) of HY5, were individually cloned into the EcoRI/XhoI sites of pB42AD to generate AD constructs. The full‐length CDS, the N‐terminal region (1–86 aa), and the C‐terminal region (87–239 aa) of TCP21, as well as the N‐terminal region (1–651 aa) and C‐terminal region (652–1172 aa) of phyB, were individually cloned into the EcoRI/XhoI sites of pLexA to generate BD constructs. To construct pGADT7 (ΔAD), the GAL4 AD was excised from pGADT7 via KpnI/XhoI digestion, followed by assembly of a 136 bp target fragment into the digested vector. The CDS of TCP21 and the C‐terminal fragment of phyB were subsequently cloned into the KpnI/BglII sites of pGADT7 (ΔAD) to generate pGADT7 (ΔAD)‐TCP21 and pGADT7 (ΔAD)‐phyB‐C. Promoter fragments of HY5 (−567 to −1 bp) and IAA19 (−1500 to −1 bp) were cloned into pLacZ2μ to generate reporter plasmids.
For Y2H assays, various combinations of AD and BD plasmids were co‐transformed into EGY48 (p8op‐LacZ) yeast strain according to the Yeast Protocols Handbook (ClonTech, Mountain View, CA, USA). Transformants were selected on SD/‐His‐Trp‐Ura dropout plates and grown on SD/‐His‐Trp‐Ura medium supplemented with X‐gal for blue color development. For Y1H assays, yeast strain EGY48 was used. Transformants were selected on SD/‐Trp‐Ura or SD/‐Leu‐Trp‐Ura medium. β‐Galactosidase activity was quantified by liquid culture assays using ONPG as the substrate.

## In vitro pull‐down assay
The full‐length CDS of TCP21 and HY5 was individually cloned into the EcoRI/XhoI sites of pET‐28a to generate His‐tagged fusion proteins. The TCP21 CDS was also inserted into the EcoRI/XhoI sites of pGEX‐4T‐1 to express a GST‐tagged fusion protein. Additionally, the C‐terminal fragment of phyB was cloned into the EcoRI/SalI sites of pMAL‐c2X to produce an MBP‐tagged protein. All recombinant constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 for protein expression at 16°C with induction by 1 mM IPTG.
To assess the interaction between phyB‐C and TCP21, purified MBP or MBP‐phyB‐C proteins were incubated with His‐TCP21 in 1 ml binding buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP‐40) at 4°C for 2 h, respectively. Subsequently, 20 μl of pre‐equilibrated amylose resin (New England Biolabs) was added and incubated for an additional 2 h with gentle rotation. After three washes with wash buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP‐40), bound proteins were eluted with 1× SDS loading buffer, separated by SDS‐PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti‐MBP and anti‐His antibodies. For evaluation of HY5‐TCP21 interaction, GST‐HY5 and His‐TCP21 were similarly co‐incubated, followed by pull‐down using Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (Cytiva). The wash buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP‐40, and 10% glycerol was used. Proteins were detected by immunoblotting with anti‐GST and anti‐His antibodies.

## Luciferase complementation imaging assay
The full‐length CDS of phyB, HY5, and TCP21 was cloned into the KpnI/SalI sites of pCAMBIA1300‐nLUC or pCAMBIA1300‐cLUC to generate the fusion constructs pCAMBIA1300‐phyB‐nLUC, pCAMBIA1300‐HY5‐nLUC, and pCAMBIA1300‐cLUC‐TCP21. In addition, the CDS of phyB was amplified and inserted into the SalI/BamHI sites of pCAMBIA1300‐35Spro:GFP to produce pCAMBIA1300‐35Spro:phyB‐GFP. All the constructs were transformed into GV3101 and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves in the indicated combinations. Following infiltration, plants were kept in darkness for 24 h, then exposed to continuous white light for 48 h. Finally, LUC activity was measured using either a NightShade LB 985 (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany) or DynaPlant Desktop (Microlens, Beijing, China) system after applying 1 mM D‐luciferin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

## Co‐immunoprecipitation assay
Arabidopsis seedlings were grown in darkness for 4 days, and then either subjected to 0.5 h of red light or maintained in darkness. Approximately, 1 g of fresh plant tissue was harvested and homogenized in lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 100 μM MG132, 0.1% NP‐40, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation and incubated with 20 μl GFP beads (AlpalifeBio, Shezhen, China) under light or dark conditions for 2 h at 4°C. After three washes with lysis buffer, the immunoprecipitates were eluted with 1× SDS loading buffer and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti‐GFP, anti‐phyB, and anti‐HSP82 antibodies.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
ChIP assays were performed as previously described with minor modifications (Zhou et al., 2022). Seven‐day‐old white light‐grown seedlings (~2 g) were vacuum‐infiltrated with 1% formaldehyde for cross‐linking, and the reaction was quenched with 0.125 m glycine. After nuclei isolation, chromatin was sheared by sonication into fragments ranging from 200 to 500 bp. The resulting extracts were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti‐GFP or anti‐HY5 antibodies, and the immune complexes were captured using protein A/G agarose beads over a 4 h incubation. Following extensive washing, the bound chromatin was eluted and reverse cross‐linked at 65°C overnight. The DNA was recovered by ethanol precipitation and analyzed by qPCR. ChIP enrichment was calculated as a percentage of the input DNA (% of input) and further normalized to the Col‐0 control. Primer sequences used for RT‐qPCR are listed in Table S1.

## Biolayer interferometry
BLI analysis was performed using an Octet RED384 system (ForteBio, Fremont, CA, USA). Briefly, NTA biosensors (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) were equilibrated in PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) buffer for 60 sec to establish a baseline, followed by a 100 sec of His‐TCP21 loading onto the biosensor surface and a 60 sec wash with PBS. The His‐TCP21‐loaded sensors were then exposed to either GST‐HY5 or MBP‐phyB‐C for 100 sec to monitor association, and subsequently transferred to PBS for 100 sec to record dissociation. Binding responses were baseline‐corrected, interstep‐aligned, and analyzed using ForteBio Data Analysis Software (version 12.1). Kinetic parameters were derived from the association and dissociation phases by fitting the data to a 1:1 binding model.

## Dual‐luciferase reporter assay
Promoter fragments of HY5 (−567 to −1 bp) and IAA19 (−1500 to −1 bp) were individually cloned into the KpnI/NcoI sites of pGreenII 0800‐LUC to generate pGreenII 0800‐HY5pro:LUC and pGreenII 0800‐IAA19pro:LUC reporters. The CDS of HY5 and TCP21 were cloned into pGreenII 62‐SK at the XbaI/KpnI sites to create pGreenII 62‐SK‐35Spro:HY5 and pGreenII 62‐SK‐35Spro:TCP21 effector constructs. A 3× Flag fragment was inserted into pCAMBIA1300‐UBQ10 at the EcoRI/KpnI sites to generate vector pCAMBIA1300‐UBQ10pro:Flag. The CDS of TCP21 and phyB were then amplified into the KpnI/BamHI sites of pCAMBIA1300‐UBQ10pro:Flag to generate pCAMBIA1300‐UBQ10pro:TCP21 and pCAMBIA1300‐UBQ10pro:phyB effector constructs.
The indicated combinations of plasmids were introduced into Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasts as described previously (Yoo et al., 2007). After 14 h of dark incubation, protoplasts were collected, and firefly LUC and renilla luciferase (REN) activities were measured sequentially using the DLR assay system (Promega) on a single‐sample luminometers (YPH‐bio, Beijing, China).

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay
DNA probes were biotin‐labeled using the EMSA Probe Biotin Labeling Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). EMSA was performed using the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Scientific). Briefly, purified proteins were incubated with biotin‐labeled DNA probes at room temperature for 20 min. The resulting DNA–protein complexes were separated on a 5% (v/v) native polyacrylamide gel and electro‐transferred onto a nylon membrane in an ice‐water bath. Then the transferred DNA probes were UV‐cross‐linked and detected following the manufacturer's instructions.
