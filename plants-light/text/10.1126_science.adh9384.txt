Summary

29

In light-limiting conditions, aerial organs of most plants reorient their growth towards the light to

30

improve photosynthesis, through a process known as phototropism1-3. The blue light receptors

31

phototropin control phototropic responses through light-induced protein kinase activity4. Current

32

models posit that asymmetric activation of these sensory receptors across a unilaterally illuminated

33

organ leads to asymmetric distribution of the growth hormone auxin ultimately leading to growth re-

34

orientation4,5. However, the tissue properties required to generate a light gradient across the stem

35

triggering phototropism remain unclear1. Here we show that inter-cellular air channels6,7 are required

36

for an efficient phototropic response. These channels enhance light scattering (refraction and

37

reflection) in Arabidopsis hypocotyls thereby enhancing the light gradient across the photo-stimulated

38

organ. We identify an embryonically expressed ABC transporter that is required to keep air in inter-

39

cellular spaces in seedlings and for efficient phototropism. Our work suggests that this transporter

40

shapes cell wall properties to maintain air between cells. Moreover, we establish the functional

41

importance of inter-cellular air channels in the hypocotyl for phototropism.

Main text

57

In flowering plants light direction is sensed by the blue-light (BL) absorbing phototropin photoreceptors

58

(phot1 and phot2 in Arabidopsis)5. This typically leads to growth towards the light or positive

59

phototropism in aerial organs such as hypocotyls and stems4. This response is believed to contribute

60

to the optimization of photosynthesis particularly in limiting light conditions2,3,8. In dicotyledons like

61

Arabidopsis thaliana, the upper hypocotyl is both the site of light perception and the site of differential

62

growth ultimately leading to organ repositioning9,10. Upon unilateral BL irradiation differential phot

63

activation between the lit and the shaded side of the seedling is considered as the first step triggering

64

phototropism5,11. Substantial progress was made in elucidating the downstream steps linking phot

65

activation and the differential growth response5. However, the optical features of light-sensing tissues

66

enabling the formation of a light gradient that are required for a phototropic response remain poorly

67

characterized1.

68

Transparency of hypocotyls causes phototropic defects.

69

In a screen for Arabidopsis seedlings with reduced phototropism we identified a mutant with

70

transparent hypocotyls (Fig. 1). The causal gene was mapped to ATP-BINDING CASETTE G5

71

(ABCG5), which was confirmed by comparing the phenotype of multiple alleles and by

72

complementation (Extended data Fig. 1). We hypothesized that the phototropic defect in the mutant

73

was caused by enhanced light transmission and a shallower light gradient in the upper part of the

74

hypocotyl. To test this hypothesis, we compared abcg5-5 (from now on abcg5) and two previously

75

identified cristal mutants (cri7 and cri8), which also have transparent hypocotyls12. The defective gene

76

in these mutants is not known12, but they are not allelic12 and we found that the ABCG5 gene was

77

unaltered (see Supplementary Methods). The three mutants had similarly enhanced light transmission

78

in the hypocotyl (Fig. 1a). In response to low unilateral BL abcg5, cri7 and cri8 growth orientation was

79

random, while the wild type (WT) aligned with the light source and phot1 was unresponsive (Fig. 1b).

80

To determine whether these mutants have a specific hypocotyl tropism defect, we analyzed their

81

gravitropic response, which also relies on asymmetric growth caused by redistribution of the growth

82

hormone auxin 13. An auxin transporter mutant deficient in three PIN-FORMED genes (pin3pin4pin7)

83

showed the expected inability to re-orient its hypocotyl upon gravistimulation (Fig. 1c). In contrast, the

84

response of abcg5 and cri7 was similar to the WT, while cri8 showed a reduced response to gravity

85

(Fig. 1c). Both phototropism and gravitropism depend on growth; we therefore measured hypocotyl

86

elongation during the phototropic experiment and found that while abcg5 hypocotyls grew like the WT,

87

cristal mutants showed a growth defect (Extended data Fig. 2). We conclude that having a transparent

88

hypocotyl correlates with an altered ability to respond to light direction and pursued our study with the

89

abcg5 mutant due to the pleiotropic nature of cristal mutants12.

If the abcg5 phototropic defect were due to impaired light direction sensing, we would expect that it

91

requires active phototropins. Consistent with this hypothesis, the phot1abcg5 double mutant behaved

92

like a phot1 mutant in response to low BL (Extended data Fig. 3a), while the abcg5 mutants showed

93

many seedlings growing in the opposite direction (Fig. 1, Extended data Fig. 3a). These experiments

94

were performed with seedlings growing on vertical plates in contact with the media. Given that agar

95

scatters light and that plants were growing at the media-air interface, the light environment in this

96

experimental setup was probably relatively complex. Thus, the following experiments were performed

97

in free standing etiolated seedlings to obtain a simpler directional light cue (see Supplementary

98

Methods). To better characterize the phototropic phenotype of the abcg5 mutant, we analyzed pulse-

99

induced first positive phototropism, which corresponds to the conditions where light fluence (µmol m) is proportional to the phototropic response14. We irradiated etiolated seedlings with a 1-minute BL

100

2

101

pulse of various intensities (1.7, 0.17, 0.017, and 0.0017 µmol m-2 s-1). In agreement with previous

102

reports15,16, WT plants showed a bell-shaped fluence-response curve, whereas abcg5 mutants

103

showed a severe phototropic impairment (Extended data Fig. 3b). Next, we examined time-dependent

104

phototropism14, by irradiating etiolated seedlings continuously with different BL fluences (0.025,

105

0.125, and 2.5 µmol m-2 s-1) and recorded growth re-orientation over 6 hours. We found that WT plants

took longer to reach maximum curvature with increasing light intensity, confirming earlier studies16.

107

Strikingly, the abcg5 mutant took much longer than the WT to reach maximum curvature at all tested

108

light intensities (Extended data Fig. 3c). We used a bi-directional light treatment to further test the

109

ability of seedlings to respond to complex light environments. In such conditions WT plants grow

110

towards the stronger light source1. In our test area seedlings received a similar light intensity but

111

different light gradients depending on their position (P) (steep (P1, P6), medium (P2, P5) and shallow

112

(P3, P4)) (Extended data Fig. 3d). The ability of abcg5 mutants to grow towards stronger light was

113

reduced particularly when the gradient was shallow (Extended data Fig. 3e). Moreover, their response

114

was slower in this situation (Extended data Fig. 3f). Collectively our phototropism experiments

115

indicated that abcg5 mutants showed a phototropic defect in all tested conditions. The phenotype was

116

particularly pronounced in response to very low light fluences, high fluence rates, and in complex light

117

environments as on the surface of plates or with bi-directional irradiation.

118

Despite these obvious phototropic defects, we found that phot1-mediated phosphorylation events

119

occurring within minutes of light perception were not altered in the abcg5 mutant (Extended data Fig.

120

4). Indeed, the BL-induced mobility shifts of phot1 and its targets NPH3 and PKS4 observed on SDS-

121

PAGE gels were normal in abcg5. This is consistent with the abcg5 phototropic defect being due to a

122

reduced ability to establish a light gradient across the hypocotyl rather than in downstream phot

123

signaling (Extended data Fig. 4)17-19. Moreover, the defective BL response of abcg5 was specific to

124

phototropism as BL-induced inhibition of hypocotyl elongation was unaltered in abcg5 (Extended data

125

Fig. 5a). Transparency of the abcg5 mutant was restricted to the embryonic phase, while true leaves

126

and other plant organs developed similarly to the WT (Extended data Fig. 5b). This allowed us to test

127

the phototropic response in petioles. Interestingly, abcg5 mutant petioles showed a WT response

128

(Extended data Fig. 5c), hence we conclude that the phototropic defect of the mutant is restricted to

129

transparent organs. Analysis of ABCG5 gene expression showed that the gene was particularly

130

strongly expressed in developing embryos, with strongest expression in the cortex (Extended data

131

Fig. 5d), while we did not observe expression with a reporter line in seedlings. Of note, while we could

132

complement the mutant by expressing the GFP-ABCG5 transgene from the ABCG5 promoter,

133

complementation was unsuccessful when the construct was driven by the viral 35S promoter

134

(Extended data Fig. 1c). Given that the 35S promoter does not drive gene expression during the early

135

stages of embryogenesis in other species20,21, our complementation assays suggest that embryonic

136

expression of ABCG5 is functionally important. This may also explain the seedling-specific phenotype

137

of abcg5 mutants, while the mutant showed no obvious phenotype later in development (Extended

138

data Fig. 5). We conclude that abcg5 has a specific phototropic defect in seedlings that is most likely

139

due to hypocotyl transparency.

ABCG5 is required for inter-cellular air channels formation.

142

Light absorbing pigments were proposed to contribute to light gradient formation across photo-

143

stimulated plant tissues thereby enabling phototropism 22,23. We therefore analyzed the absorption

144

spectrum of soluble crude extracts from etiolated seedlings and found that abcg5 extracts showed an

145

absorption spectrum comparable to the WT (Extended data Fig. 6). A recent study showed that

146

ABCG5 is required for cuticle development in cotyledons with the mutant showing higher permeability

147

of cotyledons in light-grown seedlings24. Thus, we evaluated the hypothesis that a defect in cuticle

148

development in the hypocotyl explains the phototropic defect and hypocotyl transparency in etiolated

149

seedlings. First, we assessed the cuticle structure by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). We

150

did not find large differences between WT and abcg5, although as reported for the cotyledons, the

151

cuticle in abcg5 was slightly less compact than in the WT (Extended data Fig. 7a). However it was

152

still functional, since the toluidine blue cuticle permeability test showed that abcg5 and WT had a

153

similar permeability, which contrasted with a severe cuticular deficient mutant (long-chain acyl-CoA

154

synthetase2, lacs2) 25, (Extended data Fig. 7b). Moreover, despite cuticular defects, hypocotyls of

155

lacs2 mutants were not transparent and had a robust phototropic response indicating that hypocotyl

156

transparency and the phototropic defect in abcg5 are presumably unrelated to the cuticle (Extended

157

data Fig. 7c). Light-grown abcg5 seedlings sink in water suggesting that they contain less air than the

158

WT24. We performed a floating assay with dark-grown seedlings and found that dissected roots and

159

hypocotyls as well as whole seedlings of the abcg5 mutant sank more frequently than the WT,

160

indicative of reduced air content in the abcg5 mutant (Fig. 2a). Air channels were previously observed

161

in embryos and hypocotyls of several species6,7. They are present at the tricellular junctions between

162

cortex cells or between cortex and epidermal cells6. Thus, we analyzed transverse cuts of etiolated

163

hypocotyls using cryo-scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM) and TEM. Both in WT and abcg5

164

hypocotyls we observed intercellular spaces at the tricellular junctions formed by epidermal and cortex

165

cells. However, those spaces looked empty in the WT, while they were filled in the abcg5 mutant (Fig.

166

2b, Fig. 2c, Extended data Fig. 8). Moreover, in TEM images the WT showed a well-defined electron

167

dense layer in the outer side of the cell wall surrounding the intercellular spaces. Interestingly, in

168

abcg5 mutants this layer was diffuse, heterogeneous, and sometimes absent (Fig. 2b, Extended data

169

Fig. 8). This structure may correspond to the “splitting layer”26, that was hypothesized to contain

170

hydrophobic material sealing the intercellular space and allowing the formation of air channels. To

171

further investigate whether the difference between WT and abcg5 was the presence of air in

172

intercellular spaces, we used 3D-non-destructive X-ray microtomography7,27. We detected air

173

channels in the longitudinal direction in the WT but not in abcg5 mutants (Fig. 2d). Collectively, our

174

data suggest that the difference in light transmission between the WT and abcg5 may be explained

To visualize the light gradient across an etiolated hypocotyl we used a pPHOT1:PHOT1-GFP line

197

either in phot1phot2 29 (ABCG5) or in a phot1phot2abcg5 (abcg5) background. We made Z stacks

198

across the entire width of the hypocotyl using a confocal microscope equipped with a BL laser. As

199

observed previously9,29, the GFP signal was strongest in cortex cells due to the pPHOT1 expression

200

pattern (Fig. 3c,d). In the ABCG5 background we noticed GFP signal gaps along the plasma-

201

membrane, which correspond to the position of intercellular air spaces formed between the epidermis

202

and cortex or cortex-cortex junctions (Fig. 3c, Extended data Fig. 9b, c). These fluorescence gaps

203

were not observed in the abcg5 background (Fig. 3c, d, Extended data Fig. 9b). Similar gaps were

204

observed when using a line expressing 35S promoter-driven plasma-membrane associated GFP

205

(35S:myri-GFP) in the WT, indicating that the effect of air channels on fluorescence visualization is

206

not specific to PHOT1 (Extended data Fig. 9c). To determine the effect of light scattering on the light

207

gradient across the hypocotyl, we compared the PHOT1-GFP fluorescent signal on the lit versus

208

shaded sides of the hypocotyl (Fig. 3d). Our quantifications showed that the light gradient was

209

significantly steeper in the WT than in the abcg5 mutant or infiltrated seedlings (Fig. 3d, Extended

210

data Fig. 9d). Taken together our results show that air channels present in the WT enhance light

211

scattering thereby leading to a stronger light gradient across a unilaterally illuminated hypocotyl. The

212

characterization of the abcg5 mutant, which lacks these air channels, shows that this is functionally

213

relevant for the detection of directional light cues.

Conclusion

215

The photoproduct-gradient model of phototropism states that the difference in the levels of

216

photoproduct between the lit and shaded side regulates phototropic bending1. Some experimental

217

evidence supports the importance of light absorbing pigments in the establishment of a light

218

gradient22,30. However, it was also noted that light scattering (in the sense of light diffusion by refraction

preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

219

and reflection) is likely to be important given that the light path inside plant tissues traverses media

220

with different refractive indices (RI, air RI= 1, cell wall RI= 1.42, and cellular fluid RI=1.33)28. Our data

221

using the abcg5 mutant and water-infiltrated WT hypocotyls is consistent with earlier work in other

222

species in showing that air channels in hypocotyls contribute to such a light gradient (Fig. 3)28. The

223

shallower gradient in abcg5 presumably explains why the mutant has more difficulty growing towards

224

the light maximum in complex light environments and shows a delay in responding to unilateral light

225

(Fig. 1, Extended data Fig. 3). The residual phototropic response in the mutant can be explained by

226

the presence of a shallow gradient (Fig. 3). This gradient is presumably due to the difference in

227

refractive indices between cellular fluids and the cell wall, as it could be largely eliminated by

228

infiltrating sunflower hypocotyls with cedarwood oil, which created a medium with a homogeneous RI

229

28

230

of several species6,7,26,27. These channels form following the separation of a poorly characterized cell

231

wall layer called the “splitting layer”26. How this process occurs is poorly understood but interestingly

232

it was proposed that this layer comprises a lipophilic film analogous to suberin or cutin26. ABCG5 is

233

an ABC transporter related to transporters implicated in delivering precursors of the cuticular layer to

234

the extracellular space 31. Strong expression of ABCG5 in embryonic cortex cells (Extended data Fig.

235

5d) and its localization at the plasma-membrane24 suggest that it may be implicated in the deposition

236

of lipidic precursors required to seal air channels in embryonic tissues. Consistent with this idea, we

237

described an electron-dense layer visible by TEM surrounding the intercellular space in the WT, which

238

is reduced or absent in abcg5 mutants (Fig. 2b, Extended data Fig. 8). Finally, our work shows that

239

these air channels which play a role in gas exchange 6,7,27 also shape the optical properties of

240

translucent tissues enabling them to provide directional light information to plants.
