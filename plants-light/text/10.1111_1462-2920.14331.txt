Abstract
Pseudomonas syringae foliar pathogens are exposed to light changing
conditions during their epyphitic phase on plant surfaces. Eventually, bacteria
enter the apoplast, mainly through stomata, progressing inside the leaf tissue.
Stomatal opening and plant defenses are regulated by light and circadian
rhythms. Pathogens might have evolved to use light signals to optimize leaf
invasion and evade plant defenses. In this work a transcriptomic analysis has
been performed to evaluate the impact of light perception on P.syringae pv
tomato DC3000 (PsPto) gene expression. Light induces genetic reprogramming
in PsPto that entails significant changes in stress tolerance and virulence
related traits. White and blue light treatment up-regulate the expression of
genes involved in environmental stress resistance. While white light downregulates flagella synthesis and motility genes, blue light responsive categories
mainly include functions related with PsPto virulence comprising the upregulation of T3Es genes. Red light treatment exerts a global repression effect
over gene expression including almost all the PsPto coronatine biosynthetic
genes. This genetic reprogramming is accompanied by changes in virulence
output. We observed that PsPto virulence traits are primed during darkness
optimizing bacterial entry through stomata at dawn. Functional analysis of
photoreceptor mutants demonstrated the role of light perception during the early
stages of the infection primarily at the point of bacterial entry. Collectively these
results support a model in which PsPto exploits diurnal changes in light to
maximize survival, entry and virulence on plants.
Significance
Foliar bacterial pathogens perceive light during their life cycle. The extent to
which pathogens exploit light signals to promote virulence and plant infection is
poorly understood. Global gene expression analysis of Pseudomonas syringae
reveals the high effect of light on expression reprogramming. Bacteria
orchestrate the adaptation to environmental conditions and the onset of
infection using light cues to optimize the leaf invasion. Our results reveal that P.
syringae preferentially invades plants at dawn after a dark period in which
virulence traits crucial for entry through stomata are primed. These findings
provide new information regarding the environmental influences on disease
development, which, could help us to make a rational use of tools to fight
against plant bacterial diseases.

Introduction
Foliar bacteria live as epiphytes. To cause disease, however, all foliar
pathogens must enter the leaf tissues. Although wounds can enable pathogen
entry, stomata are the primary natural entry point for most pathogens of leaves,
with stomatal entry providing access to the network of intercellular spaces
known as the apoplast. The probability of bacterial entry into the apoplast is
greatly increased when a pathogen can prevent or reduce pathogen-triggered
stomatal closure, which is an important component of plant innate immunity (1).
This entry also requires active bacterial motility (2-5). Once in the apoplast,
bacterial expression of virulence traits, including the introduction of effector
proteins that suppress additional innate immune defenses, promotes the
eventual induction of disease symptoms. Pathogen survival and growth on leaf
surfaces and in the apoplast requires adaptations that confer tolerance to a
highly dynamic abiotic environment resulting from continuous changes in
temperature, water availability and light intensity (6, 7). Therefore, survival and
infection strategies are central elements dictating foliar disease outcomes.
Light and circadian rhythms are integral to the physiology of plants, regulating
stomatal movement as well as plant defense (8, 9). Light has a particularly large
role in plant defenses that involve salicylic acid signaling, as was illustrated by
the severe attenuation of defense gene expression, hypersensitive cell death,
systemic acquired resistance, and accumulated salicylic acid following
inoculation of avirulent Pseudomonas syringae into Arabidopsis and incubation
in the dark (10-11). The circadian clock also influences plant defenses based, in
part, on circadian regulation of defense genes (12) and pathogen-triggered
immunity, namely defenses induced by pathogen-associated molecular patterns
(PAMPs) (13). Thus, daytime stomatal opening, which optimizes carbon dioxide
uptake when sunlight is available for photosynthesis, may be coordinated with
robust PAMP-triggered defenses to compensate for the associated vulnerability
to pathogen invasion (13, 14). Diurnal regulation of plant defenses suggests
opportunities for pathogens to exploit light signals to counteract or evade these
defenses. Pathogens have evolved numerous mechanisms for counteracting or
evading plant defenses, as illustrated by the secretion of compounds by some
P. syringae and Xanthomonas campestris pathovars that interfere with PAMPinduced stomatal closure (1, 15, 16). However, the extent to which pathogens
exploit light signals to promote virulence and plant infection is poorly
understood.
Photosynthetic and non-photosynthetic bacteria are able to sense light and
regulate various physiological processes in response to light (17, 18, 19). These
responses include protection against phototoxicity-associated stresses (20) and
lifestyle choices, such as between host- and non-host-associated life stages or
between motile single-cells and non-motile multicellular assemblages (21). Like
plants, bacteria possess diverse photoreceptor proteins that detect visible light,

including members of the blue light-sensing light-oxygen-and-voltage (LOV)domain family, blue-light-sensing-using-FAD (BLUF)-domain family,
photoactive-yellow-protein (PYP) family, the cryptochrome/photolyase family,
proteorhodopsins, and the red light-sensing bacteriophytochrome family.
Although the photochemical properties of various photoreceptors of animal and
plant pathogens are known (22-30), the role of these photoreceptors in
regulating host colonization or infection traits has been examined in only a few
plant pathogens. In particular, photoreceptors are known to influence motility in
P. syringae pv. syringae (Pss) B728a (31), plasmid conjugation in
Agrobacterium tumefaciens (32), and motility, adhesion and virulence in
Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. citri (33) and Xanthomonas campestris pv.
campestris (29).
Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (PsPto) is the causal agent of
bacterial speck of tomato, which is characterized by the development of
chlorotic or necrotic symptoms in leaves, stems and fruits. PsPto is a
hemibiotrophic pathogen that can multiply on leaf surfaces and in the leaf
apoplast. After entering the leaf, PsPto uses a type three secretion system
(T3SS) to inject effector proteins (T3Es) into plant cells. These T3Es have been
widely investigated for their ability to subvert plant cell processes; subversion
strategies include modulating the host ubiquitin-proteasome system, host
cytoskeleton or MAPK cascades to disrupt pathogen recognition and receptor
signaling, hormone signaling and vesicle trafficking (34-36). In addition to these
T3Es, PsPto produces coronatine, a chlorosis-inducing phytotoxin that functions
in part by mimicking a jasmonic acid conjugate and activating a signaling
cascade that inhibits salicylic acid accumulation (37, 38). Coronatine contributes
to bacterial growth and lesion induction in plants (39) and, importantly,
antagonizes PAMP-induced stomatal closure to promote bacterial entry (16,
40).
The PsPto genome encodes a single blue light-sensing LOV-domain protein
and two red light-sensing bacteriophytochromes. Our previous studies
demonstrated that PsPto light perception affects motility, attachment to plant
leaves and virulence, and these phenotypes require the LOV photoreceptor (5).
Interestingly, light impacted these plant-based pathogen behaviors when the
pathogen was exposed to the light signal for only 10 minutes prior to inoculation
onto tomato leaves (5). This discovery illustrates that light can evoke changes in
pathogen traits or behavior that are detectable after the initial light stimulus is
gone. Such enduring environmental reprogramming of bacterial behavior is
generally a result of changes in global gene-expression patterns.
Here, we explored the interplay among light-mediated impacts on plant host
physiology, PsPto global gene expression patterns, PsPto photoreceptors, and
PsPto virulence. We carried out microarray hybridization experiments to explore
the effect of light perception on PsPto at a global level, demonstrating that light

induces genetic reprogramming in PsPto that involves large-scale changes in
stress tolerance- and virulence-related traits. These changes were
accompanied by changes in virulence output that varied depending on the
wavelength to which the bacteria were exposed before inoculation and the
physiological stage of the plants with respect to the diurnal cycle. We
discovered that PsPto expresses selected virulence-related traits in the
absence of light, and provide evidence that this nocturnal priming optimizes
bacterial entry through the stomata at dawn. The behavior of mutants altered in
the PsPto photoreceptors corroborates the role of light perception during the
early stages of infection, likely at the point of bacterial entry. Collectively, these
results support a model in which PsPto optimizes leaf invasion and subsequent
growth in the apoplast by exploiting cues based on the presence, absence, and
quality of light.
Results and discussion
Light regulates major fitness and virulence genes in PsPto. Previous
studies indicate that light influences motility, attachment and virulence in PsPto,
with white and blue light inhibiting motility but promoting bacterial attachment to
leaves (5). Here we performed global transcriptome analyses to analyze the
response of PsPto to light. We used custom-designed microarrays (Agilent
Technologies) with probes isolated from either PsPto cells after a 10-min
treatment with 70 μE/m2s white light, or PsPto cells exposed to only darkness.
As a complement to white light, which contains a profile of distinct wavelengths
across the visible spectrum (Fig. S1), we also performed treatments in which
PsPto cells were exposed to a 10-min treatment with either red or blue light (20
μE/m2s). The short duration of the light treatments were selected based on the
rapid kinetics of the photocycle for the PsPto blue light photoreceptor
(nanoseconds) and red light photoreceptor (milliseconds) (41, 42), and to
minimize growth and light stress (20) during the treatments. Genes were
identified as differentially expressed based on a ≥2-fold change between the
control and experimental conditions and a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.01.
The results were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information
(NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and are accessible through GEO
series accession nºxxxxx.
We identified 326 genes that were differentially expressed in response to the
white light treatment, with 85% up-regulated and 15% down-regulated. The blue
light treatment changed the transcript levels of 607 genes, with 64% upregulated and 36% down-regulated. The red light treatment changed the
expression of 341 genes, with 68% down-regulated and only 32% up-regulated.
These results indicate a strong and distinct bias of the red light treatment
toward gene repression, in contrast to the primary influence of white and blue
light toward gene induction. Among the differentially expressed genes, only 10
were shared among all three light treatments (Fig. 1A). Despite the broad

spectrum of the white light, there were more genes shared between the blueand red-light treatments (161) than between the blue- and white-light treatments
(109) or red- and white-light treatments (17) (Fig. 1B).
An analysis of the functional categories that were overrepresented among the
differentially-expressed genes, identified 9 categories that were
overrepresented among genes responding to the white light treatment (Fig. 2,
Table S1). The flagellar synthesis and motility category was dominated by
down-regulated genes; this down-regulation was consistent with our previous
findings that white light inhibits PsPto swarming motility, a flagellum-dependent
movement (5). Most of the other functional categories overrepresented after
white light treatment are dominated by up-regulated genes and relate to
tolerating water limitation and osmotic stress, including polysaccharide
synthesis and regulation, compatible solute synthesis, oxidative stress tolerance
and quaternary ammonium compound (e.g., betaine and choline) transport. We
previously reported the up-regulation of transcripts encoding proteins involved
in alginate biosynthesis and regulation by white light and correlated this with
increased attachment to leaves (5). Alginate production is associated with
tolerance to water limitation (43, 44) and PsPto biofilm formation (45). Similarly,
the biosynthesis of two major compatible solutes, N-acetylglutaminylglutamine
amide and trehalose, is associated with water stress tolerance by PsPto and
Pss B728a in culture (44), and genes for the biosynthetic enzymes are induced
in B728a in planta (7). Among the genes within the oxidative stress tolerance
category, katB was particularly highly induced. KatB is the major inducible
catalase in PsPto that responds to hydrogen peroxide (46), and it is required for
full virulence of PsPto in Arabidopsis thaliana (46). In fact, the katB gene was
induced by white, blue and red light. This induction by light and oxidative stress
(46), which are both dominant features of aerial leaf habitats, suggests a
possible adaptive advantage of genetic co-regulation of katB in PsPto by these
distinct environmental signals. Collectively, these findings suggest a coassociation among PsPto responses to white light, water limitation, and
oxidative stress, including responses that enhance stress tolerance and reduce
flagellar-dependent motility. This co-association may reflect the co-occurrence
of these stresses on aerial leaves over the diurnal cycle of a day, with water
limitation, light, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-associated oxidative stress
all potentially peaking at mid-day.
We found that the functional categories that were overrepresented among the
differentially-expressed genes responding to blue light were distinct from those
responding to white light. Most notably, major blue light-responsive categories
included functions related to PsPto virulence. A large number of genes
encoding proteins comprising the T3SS, including T3Es, were up-regulated.
The T3SS and its associated T3Es are major determinants of PsPto
pathogenesis (47), and these include effectors that induce an aqueous
environment in the apoplast that promotes PsPto growth (48). Moreover, some

P. syringae T3Es, at least in B728a, contribute to epiphytic fitness (49). The
functional category containing genes involved in γ-amino butyric acid (GABA)
metabolism and transport was also significantly overrepresented among blue
light-responsive genes, and like the T3SS genes, these genes primarily
exhibited up-regulation. These genes include gabP, which encodes a permease
for GABA uptake, and gabD-2, which encodes an enzyme implicated in GABA
catabolism. GABA is the most abundant amino acid in the tomato apoplast and
increases in abundance under abiotic and biotic stress conditions (50). GABA
can serve as a nitrogen source for PsPto (50) and as a signal that represses the
virulence genes hrpL and avrPto and induces the Arabidopsis defense gene
PR1 (50, 51). The GABA uptake and catabolism genes were up-regulated in
Pss B728a during growth in planta (7), supporting the prediction that GABA
uptake and catabolism may prevent GABA-mediated virulence repression (7,
50). Blue light-mediated activation of these virulence-related genes strongly
indicates that P. syringae has evolved mechanisms of using light signals to
enhance pathogenesis.
Like the overrepresented functional categories responding to white light, those
responding to blue light also include categories related to stress responses (Fig.
2, Table S1). The oxidative stress tolerance and cold shock protein categories
were overrepresented in response to white and blue light. However, the
chaperones/heat shock proteins, polyamine metabolism and transport, and
stress resistance categories were overrepresented specifically in response to
blue light. This may reflect the particularly damaging effects of blue light due to
its relatively short wavelength and thus high-energy status.
Among the functional categories containing genes that were differentially
expressed by the red light treatment, eight functional categories were
overrepresented (Fig. 2, Table S1). Notably, most of the genes contributing to
coronatine synthesis, transport and regulation were down-regulated. Coronatine
is a phytotoxin produced by PsPto that acts both prior to and during lesion
development. In the pre-invasive stage, coronatine suppresses stomatal
defense and thus facilitates pathogen entry into the apoplast (52). Once PsPto
is in the apoplast, coronatine promotes growth, at least in part, by modulating
plant defense responses, and this growth contributes to eventual disease
symptoms (52). Red light-mediated down-regulation of almost all of the PsPto
coronatine biosynthetic genes suggests that PsPto perceives red light as a
signal to remain in a non-invasive stage. Another functional category that was
overrepresented among differentially expressed genes was flagellar synthesis
and motility (Fig. 2). Among the genes in this category, 34% were downregulated in PsPto, which is consistent with a report of red light-mediated
repression of swarming motility in Pss B728a (31). Invasion of the plant
apoplast requires active movement (5), including movement toward open
stomata (40), and potentially even chemotaxis given that PsPto do not move

toward open stomata when the stomata are closed (40). The chemosensing and
chemotaxis category was also overrepresented among genes expressed after
the red light treatment, and like the flagellar genes, this reflected exclusively
down-regulation of genes. Together, these findings suggest that red light
negatively regulates PsPto movement, including possible movement toward
stomata, and thus could reduce its probability of invasion.
Although red and blue light similarly affect genes in the GABA metabolism and
transport category (Fig. 2, Table S1), in contrast to blue light, red light represses
genes in several overrepresented functional categories related to virulence and
stress tolerance. In particular, within the oxidative stress tolerance category, red
light down-regulates katB, a gene known to contribute to PsPto virulence (46).
Red light also down-regulates many genes in the chaperones/heat-shock
proteins, proteases and signal transduction mechanisms categories, with the
latter generally involved in responding to environmental signals (53).
Collectively, our results indicate that light influences the expression of many
genes involved in PsPto fitness and plant infection. Based on global
transcriptome data, many of the PsPto traits regulated by light in vitro are
similar to the Pss B728a traits differentially expressed during growth in epiphytic
sites, such as the down-regulation of flagellar motility, or during growth in the
apoplast, such as up-regulation of GABA transport and catabolism (7).
Moreover, genes involved in responding to environmental stresses were
strongly influenced by light and by growth in planta in these P. syringae strains.
These results therefore support a role for light as a prevalent signal during the
P. syringae lifecycle and as a regulator of the pathogenic behavior of this
bacterium.
The light conditions at the time of inoculation significantly affect virulence
outcomes. We postulate that PsPto uses light cues to ensure stomatal entry at
a time favorable for multiplication in the apoplast. To test this hypothesis, we
performed an experiment in which PsPto cells were treated for 10 min with
white, red or blue light, or were maintained in the darkness, and then were
inoculated into tomato plants at either a simulated dawn or simulated dusk.
Inoculation at a simulated, or subjective, dawn involved inoculation at the end of
the night cycle and then plant transfer to the light. Inoculation at a subjective
dusk involved inoculation at the end of the day cycle followed by plant transfer
to the dark. Plants were inoculated by dipping in the inoculum to enable natural
bacterial entry into the apoplast through stomata. At 6 days post-inoculation,
PsPto populations were higher in plants infected at subjective dawn than at
subjective dusk when the PsPto cells had been pre-treated with white light, blue
light or no light (Fig. 3A). These leaves also exhibited more severe symptoms
(Fig. 3B). The PsPto cells treated with red light established populations similar

to the other light treatments when inoculated at subjective dusk, but did not
establish larger populations or more severe symptoms when infected at
subjective dawn (Fig.3).
Stomata are the main entry points for PsPto into the plant apoplast, and this
entry is a critical step for PsPto pathogenesis. Plants regulate stomatal aperture
based on environmental conditions and circadian control, with most species
opening stomata as morning begins, maintaining open stomata through midday,
and closing stomata long before dusk. Some C3 plants may open stomata predawn (54, 55). Importantly, plants close stomata after the detection of
pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), even under bright light, and
this closure helps restrict pathogen invasion (56). Coronatine counteracts
PAMP-closed stomata (40), and as has been recently published, coronatine is
also able to open dark-closed stomata in Arabidopsis leaves (16). In this
context, the relative PsPto population sizes shown in Fig. 3 may primarily reflect
differences in stomatal entry. In particular, when plants are infected at
subjective dawn, the coronatine production and motility of dark-treated cells
may have enabled them to maximally exploit stomatal entry, thereby allowing
them to establish large populations; when infected at subjective dusk, the darkinduced stomatal closure may have attenuated this entry (Fig. 3A). The reduced
populations of the light-treated cells following infection at subjective dawn may
therefore reflect reduced motility (white light) and/or greatly reduced coronatine
production (red light), with the greater reductions in populations in the plants
infected at subjective dusk than subjective dawn, which commensurate with
these bacterial phenotypes in combination with dark-induced stomatal closure
(Fig. 3A). The similarity in the population sizes established by the red-lighttreated cells at subjective dawn and subjective dusk is consistent with a
complete deficiency in coronatine-mediated stomatal re-opening following
PsPto PAMP exposure at subjective dawn. In accordance with our results, A.
thaliana Col-0 plants were observed to be more resistant when spray-infected
with PsPto at night than at dawn (14, 57).
Pathogen exposure to darkness primes virulence. Exposing cells to
darkness prior to inoculation under subjective dawn resembles natural
conditions for infection by epiphytic P. syringae cells. Our finding of maximal
virulence with dark-treated cells inoculated at subjective dawn (Fig. 3) suggests
a priming effect of night during the infection process. To investigate whether
gene expression indicative of motility and coronatine production phenotypes
were associated with dark-primed virulence, we evaluated the impacts of the
light treatments on the expression of key genes for these phenotypes using
qRT-PCR with the RNA isolated for the microarray analysis. We observed that
the expression of fliC, the gene encoding flagellin, was higher in dark-treated
bacteria than in white-light-treated bacteria, as in our previous study (5); it was
also higher than in blue- and red-light-treated cells, consistent with attenuated
motility contributing to the reduced virulence of light-treated cells in plants

exposed to subjective dawn (Fig. 3). Recognition of flagellin by the FLS2
Pattern Recognition Receptor (PRR) activates plant immune responses during
the early stages of infection (58), suggesting that low levels of flagellin could
help PsPto evade FLS2-mediated immunity. However, this prediction is
contradicted by our findings that dark-treated P. syringae cells exhibit strong
fliC expression (Fig. 4), motility (5, 31) and virulence (Fig. 3), whereas lighttreated P. syringae cells exhibit lower fliC expression (Fig. 4), little or no motility
(5, 31) and virulence (Fig. 3). A previous report shows that a flagellin deficiency
was similarly correlated with low virulence, negating the potential benefit from
the evasion of FLS2-triggered immunity (59).
We also examined the expression of genes involved in coronatine biosynthesis.
The coronatine biosynthetic genes cmaA and cfa5, which encode coronamic
synthetase and coronafacic acid synthetase, respectively, were strongly
repressed by red light (Fig. 4), as was highlighted in the functional category
analysis above (Fig. 2). They were also repressed by white and blue light, a
finding that was not highlighted by the more general functional category
analysis, but one that reflects a general role of dark in activating, or light of
various wavelengths repressing, coronatine biosynthesis genes. This
observation is consistent with a recent report that PsPto coronatine production
at night opens dark-closed stomata and promotes infection (16).
We previously documented impacts of light on PsPto adhesion to the leaf
surface (5); therefore, we also analyzed the expression of algD, which encodes
a GDP mannose dehydrogenase that is critical to the synthesis of the
exopolysaccharide alginate. algD expression was significantly up-regulated by
white light (Fig. 4). Microarray data also revealed that the expression of other
alginate biosynthesis related genes was up-regulated after the red and blue
light treatments. A transcriptome study with P. syringae B728a showed
significant up-regulation of algD and most of the alginate biosynthesis genes in
response to blue, red, far-red and white light as compared to in the dark
(Janssen and Beattie, unpublished data), supporting increased alginate
production as a P. syringae response to light.
PsPto photoreceptor mutants exhibit impaired virulence and an inability
to prime virulence in the dark. To investigate the effect of light perception on
PsPto infection, we evaluated the virulence of mutants inactivated in each of the
PsPto photosensory proteins. We had previously constructed a mutant
insertionally inactivated in the blue light-sensing LOV protein (5), and here we
constructed mutants that were inactivated in each of the two red/far-red lightsensing bacteriophytochromes in PsPto. We examined the virulence of all three
of these mutants following exposure to a 10-min light treatment or constant
darkness with subsequent inoculation onto tomato plants, as described above,
and again assessed virulence based on the population sizes after 6 days of
light/dark cycles. When tomato plants were inoculated at subjective dawn and

dusk with strains that had been maintained in the dark, the PsPto photoreceptor
mutants were less virulent than the wild-type strain (Fig. 5A). The observation
that they showed reduced virulence when inoculated at subjective dawn and
subjective dusk indicates that the photoreceptor-mediated benefit was not a
function of the 12 h of light following inoculation at subjective dawn. We also
observed that the mutants showed reduced virulence when pre-exposed to dark
conditions (Fig. 5A) but not white or blue light conditions (Fig. 5B, 5C) following
inoculation at subjective dusk, which indicates that the photoreceptor-mediated
benefit was not realized during the 6 days of day/night growth, since this
light/dark cycle was common to all treatments. Thus, the inability of the
photoreceptor mutants to attain the full virulence advantage associated with the
dark pre-treatment indicates that the photoreceptors were required for nocturnal
priming. Although photoreceptors can help cells sense light, they can also help
sense the absence of light (60). This concept was advanced in studies with the
phytochrome FphA of Aspergillus nidulans, where the kinase activity of FphA
that initiates signal transduction was shown to increase under dark conditions
(60). These data suggest a similar phenomenon in the case of the PsPto
photoreceptors, and are consistent with the photoreceptors affecting traits in the
dark that subsequently benefit virulence. These results thus collectively provide
evidence for a role for the PsPto photoreceptors in dark-priming of virulence,
and highlight the importance of photoreceptor-mediated dark-priming during the
initial stages of infection.
When PsPto cells were exposed to white or blue light and then inoculated at
subjective dawn, the photoreceptor mutants were less virulent than the wildtype strain (Fig. 5B, 5C). This difference supports a role for the photoreceptors
in sensing light as well as sensing the absence of light, and likely involves lightmediated modulation of gene expression based on our microarray analyses.
The photoreceptors mutants were similar in virulence to the wild-type when preexposed to white or blue light and inoculated at subjective dusk (Fig. 5B, 5C),
which is a time when the stomata are generally closed. This similarity may be
due, at least in part, to the pre-treatment with light reducing wild-type coronatine
production (Fig. 4) to a level that was insufficient to re-open stomata on leaves
in the dark (16).
The distinct photoreceptor mutants were qualitatively similar to one another in
behavior following the dark, white-light and blue-light treatments, possibly due
to crosstalk among the photoreceptors during light perception (31, 61).
However, exposure of the PsPto strains to red light prior to inoculation at
subjective dusk showed that mutants lacking bphP1 or bphP2 were more
virulent than the wild type and lov mutant (Fig. 5D). Based on this finding and
the negative effect of red light on coronatine production genes (Fig. 2), we
propose that PsPto bacteriophytochromes mediate particularly strong
repression of coronatine production under red light. This would reduce the
ability of wild-type PsPto cells to re-open the stomata that close following

subjective dusk, thus reducing entry into the leaves; moreover, the lack of
bphP1 or bphP2 would prevent this repression, thus resulting in greater
coronatine production, greater entry, and larger populations (Fig. 5D). This
prediction is supported by the significantly greater expression of the coronatine
biosynthesis genes cmaA and cfa5 in the bphP mutants than in the wild type
after red light treatment (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, when we analyzed the average
stomatal aperture on tomato leaves that had been inoculated with the red lighttreated bphP mutants, we observed a significantly enhanced ability of the
mutants to reopen the stomata at night (Fig. 6B). In plants inoculated at
subjective dawn, the stomata were initially open and this effect was not
observed (data not shown). These results are consistent with the ability of
PsPto to reopen stomata at night (16), and with the involvement of a
Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris phytochrome in the down-regulation of
virulence factors including xanthan exopolysacharide production and biofilm
formation (29).
Overall, these results illustrate a potent influence of the diurnal cycle on both
plant and pathogen physiology and a consequent impact on virulence
outcomes. PsPto may be especially cued into light signals, as it is a poorer
epiphyte than other P. syringae pathogens, such as strain B728a, and therefore
likely depends more on entry into the apoplast for effective leaf colonization
(44).
Swarming motility in PsPto is regulated by light quality through the
photosensory proteins. Active motility is required for PsPto to enter the
apoplast (5) and is therefore important to establishing infections. Swarming
motility is a form of surface motility that requires flagella and surfactant
production (62-64). We previously demonstrated that swarming motility in PsPto
(5) and P. syringae B728a (31) depends on light quality and that flagellar gene
expression is down-regulated under white and red light (Fig. 2). Measuring
swarming motility can be challenging due to its extreme sensitivity to relative
humidity and temperature (65, 66). Since our previous report (5), we have
optimized our assay for measuring PsPto swarming motility for more robust
reproducibility. When this assay was used to evaluate the impact of distinct light
wavelengths on swarming motility, we observed that PsPto exhibited greater
swarming in the dark than under any of the wavelengths tested (Fig. 7). We
similarly observed white- and blue-light inhibition of PsPto swarming motility in
an earlier study (5), although PsPto showed greater swarming under the
conditions tested here. However, we previously observed red-light
enhancement (5) rather than red-light inhibition of PsPto wild-type swarming
motility (Fig. 7). Collectively, these results indicate that all of the wavelengths
tested reduced swarming motility.
Loss of the LOV photoreceptor reduced PsPto swarming motility under blue and
red light, indicating that this photoreceptor positively regulates motility and is

involved in the response to both blue and red wavelengths. The findings with
blue light are consistent with our previous results with P. syringae B728a, which
provided evidence that LOV negatively regulates swarming motility in blue light
by attenuating bacteriophytochrome-mediated repression of swarming motility
(31). Unlike B728a, however, PsPto also showed a role for LOV in the presence
of red light. Although LOV is widely known for sensing blue light (67), studies
with a LOV-histidine kinase in the picoalga Ostreococcus tauri (61) provided a
precedent for the involvement of a LOV photoreceptor in regulation under red
light.
Whereas red light inhibits swarming motility of the wild type, potentially due to
down-regulation of flagellar synthesis by red light (Figs. 2 and 4), the bphP1
mutant exhibited increased motility under blue, red and white light conditions
but not in the dark. These results illustrate that this photoreceptor functions as a
repressor of motility under light conditions, as was previously reported in P.
syringae B728a (31). The increased virulence of bphP1 after red light treatment
and inoculation at subjective dusk (Fig. 5) may have resulted, at least in part,
from the loss of repression of swarming motility, and thus increased entry into
the plant. Motility assays with the bphP2 mutant showed no difference in
swarming motility from the wild type (Fig. 7). Studies with P. syringae B728a
similarly found that loss of the bacteriophytochrome BphP2 did not detectably
alter swarming motility (31).
Conclusion
These studies on the impacts of light on PsPto gene expression, virulence and
swarming motility together support a model in which PsPto responds to diurnal
changes in light to optimize invasion of leaves (Fig. 8). In particular, PsPto
motility genes are maximally expressed at night, thus promoting active
colonization of the leaf surface when the stomata are closed. Nocturnal priming
of virulence optimizes the infection process at dawn. Nocturnal priming involves
the expression of selected virulence-related traits in the absence of light. These
traits include the production of coronatine, which ensures a successful infection
at dawn when stomatal opening is tempered by PAMP-induced stomatal closure
(16, 40) and the apoplast humidity is high, and motility, which is necessary to
enter the plant apoplast. Blue, white and red light all reduce the expression of
coronatine biosynthesis genes, but red light has the strongest repressive effect.
Although the biological significance of this sensitivity to red light is not clear, red
light dominates the light spectrum at dawn and thus may ensure a reduction in
coronatine production. It has been described that coronatine induces ROS in a
light-dependent manner and that coronatine-inducible ROS is necessary for
necrotic cell death in bacterial speck disease (68). It could be hypothesized that
red prevalent light during the first stages of infection at dawn, is used as a
signal to avoid early coronatine mediated ROS generation, which could function

as a too early warming in the plant; alternatively, the greater penetrating effect
of red/far-red light over blue light in the leaf tissue may reduce coronatine
production within the apoplast.
In our model, the increased quantity of light of all wavelengths by mid-day would
ensure the up-regulation of genes associated with environmental stress
tolerance, including genes for the production of alginate, a polysaccharide that
provides hydration and promotes adhesion to leaf surfaces, anti-oxidative
enzymes such as catalase, and compatible solutes for tolerating water
limitation. Our results are consistent with PsPto prioritizing the need to cope
with light-associated stressful conditions over active stomatal entry during the
day, despite that stomata are open. Interestingly, the expression of the PsPto
T3SS was strongest in the presence of blue light. The amount of blue light that
reaches the earth’s surface increases at mid-day, and although the intensity of
blue light reaching the apoplast would be attenuated via absorption by
chlorophyll, the intensity of blue light would be greatest at mid-day even in the
apoplast. Bacterial expression of the T3SS at mid-day would be commensurate
with PsPto-plant interactions that suppress basal defenses and support PsPto
multiplication in the apoplast. By dusk when the stomata are mostly closed, the
long period of light-mediated repression of coronatine synthesis and motility
would have greatly lowered the probability of stomatal entry, with the red lightenrichment associated with dusk further ensuring that coronatine synthesis is
repressed.
Our results with the photoreceptor mutants are consistent with this model. They
illustrate an active role for the photoreceptors in nocturnal virulence priming and
provide evidence for dark-mediated photoreceptor activities. They also illustrate
a role for white and blue light-mediated photoreceptor activities in promoting
virulence following inoculation at subjective dawn, and for red light-mediated
photoreceptor activities in reducing virulence following inoculation at subjective
dusk. Our experiments demonstrated that the latter was due, at least in part, to
the key role of a bacteriophytochrome in repressing coronatine production in
response to red light.
Altogether these results illustrate that PsPto has the ability to integrate distinct
monochromatic light signals over the course of a day, and by regulating the
expression of large numbers of genes, PsPto exploits diurnal changes in light to
maximize its virulence. Thus, just as plants exploit light cues to maximize their
resistance to pathogens (69-70), bacteria can exploit light cues to maximize
their virulence and survival on plants.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Microbiological methods. Bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are
listed in Table S2. Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato strain DC3000 (PsPto)
derivatives were grown at 28°C in King’s B (KB) medium (71). Escherichia coli

derivatives were grown at 37°C in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (72). When
appropriate, antibiotics were added to the medium at the following
concentrations (µg/ml): rifampicin, 25; streptomycin, 20; kanamycin, 50;
nitrofurantoin, 5.
Illumination conditions. White light was provided using fluorescent tubes
(Osram, Germany) at an intensity of 70 μE/m2s. Red and blue lights were
provided using GreenPower LED HF red (660 nm) and GreenPower LED HF
blue (458 nm) lights (Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands), respectively, each
at an intensity of 20 μE/m2s. To maintain dark conditions, plates and flasks were
covered with two layers of aluminum foil.
For the determination of white light spectrum, a portable fiber-fed spectrometer
(JAZ EL200-XR1 Extended-Range UV-VIS Spectrometer, Ocean Optics, Inc.,
Dunedin, FL) was used. The intensities of 10 exposures were averaged to avoid
possible blinking of the lamps. Light intensity was determined using two
photometers, a Sekonic C-700-U SpectroMaster Color Meter (Sekonic
Corporation, Japan) and a LI-COR LI-250A Light Meter (LI-COR Biosciences,
Lincoln, NE USA). The photometers were placed side-by-side with the optic
fiber to verify appropriate calibration. The portable spectrograph was calibrated
in flux using a standard tungsten lamp as reference. The wavelength
measurements were calibrated based on the spectra of thorium-neon and
thorium-argon lamps using standard procedures.
Molecular biology techniques. Bacterial growth was monitored based on
optical density measured at 600 nm (OD600) using a Jenway 6300
spectrophotometer. Plasmid DNA was isolated with the NucleoSpin Plasmid Kit
(Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). DNA restriction, dephosphorylation,
ligation and electrophoresis were performed using standard protocols (71) and
manufacturer instructions. DNA fragments were recovered from agarose gels
using the GenJet Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).
Competent cells were prepared and transformations were carried out using
standard procedures (73). KAPA HiFi DNA polymerase (Kapa Biosystems, Inc.,
Wilmington, MA, USA) was used for the amplification of DNA fragments.
Construction of PsPto bphP1 and bphP2 mutants. To generate the bphP1
mutant, an internal 442-bp fragment of PSPTO_1902 (corresponding to
nucleotides 44-485 of the coding sequence) was amplified by PCR from the
PsPto genome using primers 1902XbaF (5’ATTGTCTAGATGAGCCCATTCAG-3’) and 1902EcoRIR (5’CTCTGAATTCCACGGACACTG-3’) and cloned into pCHESI-Km (74). For the
construction of the bphP2 mutant, an internal 618-bp sequence of
PSPTO_2652 (corresponding to nucleotides 35-652 of the coding sequence)
was amplified by PCR from the PsPto genome using primers 2652EcoRIF (5’GAATCGAATTCAGGCGATACA-3’) and 2652XbaR (5’-

CAGCTCTAGAAGCAGCGCTTG-3’) and cloned into pCHESI-Km. The
resulting plasmids were introduced into PsPto by triparental mating (75) using
E. coli CC118λpir (for bphP1) or E. coli DH5α (for bphP2) as donors and E. coli
HB101 (pRK600) as a helper strain. As pCHESI-Km cannot replicate in PsPto,
single crossover cointegrates were selected based on resistance to kanamycin.
Nitrofurantoin was added to the selection plates to counter-select the donor
strain. Plasmid integration was confirmed using PCR and a Southern Blot.
RNA extraction. Bacterial cells grown overnight in the dark at 28°C on KB agar
were resuspended in 40 ml of liquid KB medium and adjusted to an OD 600 of
0.05. These bacterial suspensions were incubated in the dark at 28°C with
shaking at 150 rpm to an OD600 of 0.5. Cells placed in flasks were then treated
for 10 min under white light (70 μE/m2s), red light (20 μE/m2s), blue light (20
μE/m2s) or maintained in the darkness. These treatments were replicated four
times. Total RNA was extracted separately from each biological replicate from
each treatment using the TRI Reagent (Ambion) as recommended by the
manufacturer. The RNA samples were pre-treated with RNase-free DNase I
(Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and RNaseOUT Recombinant Ribonuclease
Inhibitor (Invitrogen Corp., Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by purification with
RNeasy columns (Qiagen Co., Hilden, Germany). The RNA samples were
quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Inc.,
Wilmington, DE, USA), their integrity was assessed by agarose gel
electrophoresis, and their quality was evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyser
2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).
Microarray hybridization experiments. Total RNA was extracted as described
above and the quality of the samples was checked using an Agilent Bioanalyser
2100 (Agilent Technologies).
For each treatment, the four biological replicates were independently hybridized
onto custom-designed microarrays containing the complete PsPto genome
(Agilent PsPto Oligo Microarrays 8x15K; #042459, Agilent Technologies).
Fluorescently-labeled cDNA for microarray hybridization was obtained using the
SuperScript Indirect cDNA Labeling System (Invitrogen Corp.). In brief, 15 μg of
total RNA were converted into cDNA using Superscript III reverse transcriptase
and random hexamers as primers, and including aminoallyl-modified
nucleotides in the reaction mixture. After cDNA purification, the Cy3 fluorescent
dyes (Amersham Biosciences Corp, Piscataway, NJ, USA) were coupled to the
amino-modified first-strand cDNA, and the labelling efficiencies were assessed
using a NanoDrop ND1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDropTechnologies).
Hybridization of these probes to the microarray was performed as described
(One-Color Microarray Based Gene Expression Analysis Manual Ver. 6.5,
Agilent Technologies). Briefly, for each hybridization, 600 ng of the Cy3 probes
were combined with 5 μl of 10X blocking agent and nuclease-free water in a 25
μl reaction, which was then combined with 25 μl of 2X GExHybridization buffer

and placed on ice, and then loaded onto a microarray and allowed to hybridize
at 65ºC for 17 h. The microarrays were then washed in GE wash buffer 1 at
room temperature for 1 min and in GE wash buffer 2 at 37ºC for 1 min.
Images from the Cy3 channel were equilibrated and captured with a HighResolution Microarray Scanner (Agilent Technologies) and the fluorescence in
each spot was quantified using Feature Extraction Software (Agilent Genomics).
Background correction and normalization of expression data were performed
using LIMMA (76, 77). LIMMA is part of Bioconductor, an R language project
(78). For local background correction and normalization, the methods
"normexp" and loess in LIMMA were used, respectively (77). To have similar
distribution across arrays and to achieve consistency among the arrays, the logratio values were scaled using the median-absolute-value as a scale estimator
(76). Differentially-expressed genes were determined using methods for linear
models. Each probe was tested for changes in expression by using an empirical
Bayes moderated t-statistic (77). To control the false discovery rate (FDR), pvalues were corrected using the method of Benjamani and Hochberg (79). The
expected FDR was controlled to be less than 1%; thus, genes were declared to
be differentially expressed if they exhibited a fold-change ≥ 2 and an FDR ≤
0.01. Hybridizations and statistical analyses were performed by the Genomics
Facility at Centro Nacional de Biotecnologia. Hierarchical clustering was
performed using the average linkage method and Pearson’s correlation
coefficient as a measure of similarity in the Multiexperiment Viewer (MeV) v.s.
4.3 software (www.mev.tm4.org), thus grouping the samples based on the
degree of similarity in gene expression.
Assignment and analysis of gene representation in functional categories.
The PsPto genes were assigned to 69 functional categories (Table S3),
following the procedure described by Yu and colleagues (7). Each gene was
assigned to a single functional category or to no category if it could be placed
into multiple categories or its function was unknown. For each functional
category, we computed the number of differentially-expressed and nondifferentially-expressed genes in that category, as well as the number of
differentially-expressed and non-differentially-expressed genes not in that
category. Genes that were enriched in a given functional category were
identified by performing a hypergeometric test (p-value ≤ 0.05) using the R
function ‘phyper’. The whole analysis was performed using R version 3.3.1 (R
Core Team, 2014; http://www.R- project.org/) with ad hoc scripts (available
upon request).
Quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR). Total RNA isolated for the microarray
analyses described above, was converted to cDNA using the High-Capacity
cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).
Primers were designed to amplify fragments of approximately 100 bp for all
genes of interest (Table S4), including rpoD, which was used as an internal

control for normalization. Real-time PCR amplifications were carried out on a
ABI PRISM 7300 RT PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using a SYBR Green
PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Thermal cycling conditions were as
follows: one cycle at 95°C for 10 min; 50 cycles at 95°C for 15 s and 65°C for 1
min; and a final cycle at 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, 95°C for 15 s and 60°C
for 15 s. The relative gene expression ratio was calculated using the
comparative critical threshold (∆∆Ct) method (80, 81).
Tomato virulence assays. PsPto strains were grown at 28°C for 24 h on KB
agar under dark conditions. Cells were resuspended in 10 mM MgCl 2 and
diluted to 108 cfu/ml with all activities performed under dark conditions (less than
0.05 μE/m2s). Prior to inoculation, the bacterial suspensions placed in flasks
were either subjected to a light treatment (white, red or blue light) for 10 min or
maintained in the darkness. Silwet L-77 was added to the bacterial suspensions
at a final concentration of 0.02% (vol/vol) immediately before inoculation.
Three-week-old tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum cv Moneymaker) were
collected either at the end of a 12-h night cycle or at the end of a 12-h day
cycle; inoculations of these plants corresponded to inoculation at the subjective
dawn or subjective dusk, respectively. Plants were inoculated by immersing all
of the leaves in a bacterial suspension (108 cfu/ml) for 30 s. Plants were
incubated in a growth chamber at 25°C and 60% RH with a daily light period
(100 μE/m2s) of 12 h. Six days after inoculation, the leaf symptoms were
recorded and bacterial populations from three plants were enumerated by
sampling five 1-cm leaf disks per plant. Plant material was homogenized in 10
mM MgCl2, and drop plated on KB agar supplemented with the appropriate
antibiotics. The average number of bacteria per cm2 isolated from five infected
tomato leaves was determined based on log-transformed data, and the
treatments were compared using Fisher’s least-significant-difference (LSD) post
hoc test for multiple comparisons (p-value ≤ 0.05), performed using the
statistical software package SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).
Stomatal assays. Tomato plants were collected at the subjective dusk and
inoculated as described above. Individual leaves were detached and placed
abaxial side down on a 300-µL drop of bacterial suspension (10 8 cfu/ml) on a
microscope slide; this was to ensure bacterial contact. The leaves were cut to
ensure that they remained flat on the slide. The leaves were then incubated in
the dark in a growth chamber for 3 h. We measured stomatal aperture as
described previously (16, 40, 82), except that leaves were directly analyzed
without propidium iodide staining. In particular, we imaged the abaxial surface
of the leaves using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems,
Wetzlar, Germany). Stomatal apertures were measured using the open-source
software ImageJ, specifically the Fiji distribution (83). For each treatment, we
imaged three independent leaves and measured 65 stomata on each leaf.

Significant differences were determined based on a Student’s t-test (p-value
≤0.05), performed using the statistical software package SPSS 22.0.
Swarming motility assays. PsPto strains were grown at 28°C for 24 h on KB
agar under dark conditions. Cells were resuspended in KB medium to an OD 600
of 1. Five microliters of the bacterial suspension were spotted onto soft KB agar
(0.5% agar, wt/vol). Plates were incubated for 16 h at 28°C and 80% RH under
white light (70 μE/m2s), red light (20 μE/m2s), blue light (20 μE/m2s) or dark
conditions. Three replicate plates were prepared for each treatment, and three
replicate experiments were performed. Swarm colonies were photographed, the
surface area of each colony was quantified using the area selection tool of
Adobe Photoshop software with readings in pixels, and treatment comparisons
were made using Fisher’s LSD test (p-value ≤ 0.05).

LSD test).
