Histone deacetylase 9 modulates the acetylation dynamics of phototropin 1 to fine-tune phototropic responses in plants.

# Histone deacetylase 9 modulates the acetylation dynamics of phototropin 1 to fine-tune phototropic responses in plants

## Abstract
AbstractPhototropism is essential for optimizing plant growth and development, with the blue light receptor phototropin 1 (phot1) acting as the primary photoreceptor. Although downstream components of phot1-mediated phototropic signaling have been studied extensively, the upstream regulatory mechanisms that control phot1 activity remain to be clarified. Here, we demonstrate that lysine acetylation dynamically modifies phot1 under both dark and light conditions. Site-directed mutagenesis of acetylated lysines revealed that acetylation regulates the light-induced autophosphorylation and kinase activity of phot1. Genetic screening of histone deacetylase (HDAC) mutants identified HDA9 as a key regulator of phototropism that physically interacts with phot1, modulating its acetylation and phosphorylation levels in response to light. We pinpointed K636 as the critical acetylation site targeted by HDA9, linking deacetylation to phot1 activation. Our findings reveal a regulatory paradigm in which HDA9-mediated deacetylation fine-tunes the phosphorylation dynamics of phot1 to control phototropic responses. This acetylation–phosphorylation crosstalk appears to be evolutionarily conserved, underscoring its broad significance in light signaling. Our study provides insight into the mechanisms by which antagonistic post-translational modifications precisely regulate photoreceptor sensitivity and signal transduction in plants.Key words: phot1, HDA9, phototropism, acetylation, phosphorylation, light signaling

## Introduction
Organisms have evolved and adapted to their ambient environment in various ways. For example, green plants use the directionality, quantity, and quality of light to optimize their survival. One such adaptive response is phototropism, or the reorientation of growth toward a light source, which can help optimize photosynthetic efficiency and promote development (Chen et al., 2004; Christie, 2007); this phenomenon was first noticed by Charles Darwin (Holland et al., 2009). Advantages of phototropism in plants include the maximization of photosynthetic light capture by aerial organs and water acquisition via roots (Pedmale et al., 2010). Phototropins are photoreceptors associated with phototropism that enable plants to direct their bodies, thus enhancing the efficacy of light capture (Khurana and Poff, 1989; Huala et al., 1997). Phototropins are found in land plants and green algae. Most plants possess multiple phototropin genes, and their physiological functions are conserved (Lariguet and Dunand, 2005). The model dicot Arabidopsis thaliana contains two phototropins (phot1 and phot2) that have partially overlapping functions but exhibit distinct photosensitivities (Christie, 2007). phot1 is a unique photoreceptor that regulates the phototropic responses of etiolated hypocotyls over a broad range of blue-light intensity from 10−5 to 102 μmol m−2 s−1, whereas the response of phot2 is elicited only by higher fluence rates (>10 μmol m−2 s−1) (Huala et al., 1997; Haga et al., 2015).
Phototropins are kinases, and several studies have shown strong correlations between light-induced phosphorylation and their phototropic activity (Short and Briggs, 1990; de Carbonnel et al., 2010). A phototropin molecule consists of a C-terminal Ser/Thr kinase domain and an N-terminal light-sensing domain composed of two light, oxygen, and voltage (LOV) domains (LOV1 and LOV2), each of which binds non-covalently to a flavin mononucleotide (FMN) as a chromophore (Christie et al., 1999; Christie, 2007). Blue-light photoactivation of LOV2 leads to conformational changes in the phototropin protein and relieves the repression of its kinase activity, thereby initiating autophosphorylation and a subsequent phototropic signaling cascade (Kimura and Kagawa, 2006). Phototropins localize on the inner surface of the plasma membrane and mediate responses to light, such as phototropism, chloroplast photorelocation, chloroplast accumulation, stomatal opening, and leaf flattening and positioning (Huala et al., 1997; Kagawa et al., 2001; Kinoshita et al., 2001). The initial signaling step in the phototropic response involves the perception of blue light by phototropins and the subsequent asymmetric distribution of auxin, which results in differential growth of tissues and phototropic bending (Briggs, 2014; Fankhauser and Christie, 2015). However, whether regulators are present between light signals and phototropins remains unknown.
Lysine acetylation is a reversible post-translational modification (PTM) that neutralizes the positive charge of the amino acid and changes protein function in different ways, regulating protein stability (Xu et al., 2017), enzymatic activity (Hasan et al., 2001; Su et al., 2017), subcellular localization (Sundaresan et al., 2011), crosstalk with other PTMs (Li et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2017), and protein–protein (Ikenoue et al., 2008) or protein–DNA interactions (Tang et al., 2013; Narita et al., 2019). Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are responsible for removing acetyl groups from lysine sites of both histone and non-histone proteins. Non-histone protein acetylation is involved in key cellular processes relevant to physiology and disease, such as gene transcription (Soutoglou et al., 2000), DNA-damage repair (Sun et al., 2005; Fan and Luo, 2010), cell division (Fan and Luo, 2010), signal transduction (Li et al., 2008; Glidden et al., 2012), autophagy (Lin et al., 2012; Wan et al., 2017), and metabolism (Anderson et al., 2017; Narita et al., 2019). In humans, the protein deleted in breast cancer 1 (DBC1) negatively regulates the enzymatic activity of sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), leading to increased acetylation and stabilization of p53 (Zhao et al., 2008). The acetylation of 14-3-3 proteins impairs their ability to bind phosphopeptides, confirming the existence of crosstalk between acetylation and phosphorylation, as the triple-acetylation mutant loses the ability to interact with phosphopeptides (Choudhary et al., 2009). The Arabidopsis HDAC protein HDA14 deacetylates various chloroplast proteins related to photosynthesis (Hartl et al., 2017). The histone acetyltransferase GCN5 and histone deacetylase HDA6 regulate jasmonate signaling by reversible acetylation of the transcriptional co-repressor TOPLESS in Arabidopsis (An et al., 2022). Although thousands of non-histone proteins are acetylated in plants, it is not clear how acetylation of the lysine (Kac) site on non-histone proteins is regulated in response to environmental signals such as light.
In A. thaliana, 18 HDACs are categorized into three families: the Reduced Potassium Dependences 3 (RPD3/HDA1-like) family, the plant-specific HD2-type family, and the NAD-dependent Silent Information Regulator 2 (SIR2) family (Liu et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2020). The RPD3/HDA1 member HDAC HISTONE DEACETYLASE 9 (HDA9) plays a key role in regulating various plant traits and responses (de Rooij et al., 2020). By catalyzing the removal of acetyl groups from lysine residues on histone protein tails, HDA9 negatively regulates gene expression, often through interactions with other proteins such as POWERDRESS (PWR) (Chen et al., 2004; Khan et al., 2020), HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 15 (HOS15) (Park et al., 2019), WRKY53 (Zheng et al., 2020), ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) (Yang et al., 2020, 2023), ABA-INSENSITIVE 4 (ABI4) (Baek et al., 2020; Khan et al., 2020), and EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3) (Lee et al., 2019; Park et al., 2019). At the phenotypic level, HDA9 regulates multiple developmental and physiological processes, including seed dormancy (van Zanten et al., 2014; Baek et al., 2020), flowering time (Kim et al., 2013; Lim et al., 2022), leaf senescence (Chen et al., 2016; Zareen et al., 2022), cell proliferation (Suzuki et al., 2018), transduction of heat signals to plant cells (Niu et al., 2022), hypocotyl elongation (Nguyen et al., 2023), disease resistance (Zhao et al., 2023), and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling (Park et al., 2023). HDA9 can also mediate responses to environmental signals such as salt stress, drought (Khan et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2020), and high temperatures (Shen et al., 2019; van der Woude et al., 2019). However, to date, no study has investigated the non-histone photoreceptor substrate of HDA9 or its potential involvement in light signaling.
In this study, we examined the post-translational acetylation of phot1 by HDA9 and its role in the regulation of phototropism in Arabidopsis. A protein acetylome analysis revealed that four lysine sites on phot1 underwent acetylation in etiolated Arabidopsis seedlings. Substitution of the K636 acetylation site with glutamine (Q) inhibited light-induced phosphorylation of phot1, thus weakening the phototropic response in plants. We found that HDA9 interacted with and deacetylated phot1 in vivo. Light attenuated the HDA9–phot1 interaction, leading to an increase in phot1 acetylation, which may attenuagte its light-dependent phosphorylation. The K636Q substitution in phot1 also disrupted the HDA9–phot1 interaction. Our findings suggested that HDA9 acts as a molecular regulator that maintains the normal homeostasis of phot1 acetylation to control phot1 light-induced phosphorylation, with acetylated K636 as a recognition and target site in phototropism. These results provides several new insights into phototropic signaling: (1) there is a direct link between the activation of a photoreceptor as a cytoplasmic protein and HDA9-mediated deacetylation; (2) homeostasis of acetylation is strongly correlated with the dynamics of phot1 kinase activity; and (3) the histone deacetylase HDA9 directly deacetylates phototropin as a non-histone substrate, thus fine-tuning plant phototropic growth.

## Results

## Acetylation of phot1 affects its role in phototropic responses
To evaluate the function of protein acetylation during the switch from skotomorphogenesis to photomorphogenesis, we identified lysine-acetylated proteins in 7-day-old etiolated Arabidopsis seedlings grown in the dark or exposed to red light for 3 h. Acetylproteomic data revealed the presence of four acetylated lysine sites (K129, K404, K603, and K636) in the blue-light photoreceptor phot1 (Figure 1A and Supplemental Figure 1A–1E), prompting us to investigate the potential functions of these phot1 Kac sites in photomorphogenesis.

To assess the functional significance of the phot1 acetylation sites, we substituted all four lysine (K) residues with arginine (R) (to inhibit acetylation) or glutamine (Q) (to mimic acetylation), i.e., PHOT1:PHOT1RRRR-GFP and PHOT1:PHOT1QQQQ-GFP, using PHOT1:PHOT1-GFP as controls, and introduced these constructs into the phot1,2 double mutant to generate complementation lines (Figure 1A). We assessed the effects of the amino acid substitutions by examining phot1-mediated cotyledon position and hypocotyl phototropic curvature. PHOT1:PHOT1-GFP and PHOT1:PHOT1RRRR-GFP transgenic lines had cotyledon angles and hypocotyl curvatures comparable to those of wild-type Columbia-0 (Col), whereas the angles and curvatures of the PHOT1QQQQ lines were similar to those of the phot1,2 double mutant (Figure 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 2A–2D). These findings indicated that the K–Q substitutions in the phot1 protein eliminated its phototropic function.

## Mutation of acetylation sites on phot1 disrupts its light-induced phosphorylation
We next investigated how mimicking acetylation would affect the biochemical functions of phot1. Because phot1 is a kinase that can be autophosphorylated and activated by illumination, we hypothesized that light-mediated acetylation might influence its phosphorylation. Previous studies had shown that phosphorylated phot1 migrates more slowly than non-phosphorylated phot1 in SDS–PAGE gels (Kimura et al., 2020). We therefore examined two phot1 phosphorylation mutants to determine whether their migration would be affected by blue-light irradiation: a kinase-dead mutant containing a D806N substitution in the Mg2+-ATP binding site, and a mutant with an S851A substitution in the activation loop that also compromised phosphorylation (Inoue et al., 2008). Immunoblot assays showed that neither the PHOT1D806N-FLAG nor the PHOT1S851A-Myc bands shifted upward upon blue-light irradiation (Supplemental Figure 3A and 3B). Subsequent liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) showed that the PHOT1-fusion protein in the higher band was highly phosphorylated (Supplemental Figure 3C and 3D). These findings confirmed that the higher, slower-migrating band was the phosphorylated form of phot1. Consistent with this result, the phosphorylation level of phot1 increased under light induction, and its protein level increased rapidly within 1 h and decreased over 2 h after illumination (Figure 1D). After exposure to blue light for 45 min, the etiolated seedlings were transferred back to dark conditions; this caused a decrease in phot1 protein content and phosphorylation within 2 h (Figure 1E), confirming that the phosphorylation state of phot1 was controlled by the light signal. The K–Q substitution mutant of phot1 lost its light-mediated phosphorylation activity, as demonstrated by comparison of the Arabidopsis transgenic lines PHOT1QQQQ-GFP and PHOT1-GFP (Figure 1F; Supplemental Figure 4A–4E), and this result was confirmed by transient expression of the PHOT1QQQQ-GFP fusion protein in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana), using PHOT1RRRR-GFP and PHOT1-GFP as controls (Figure 1G). The defective kinase activity of PHOT1QQQQ was consistent with the loss of phototropism observed in the transgenic plants (Figure 1B and 1C). By contrast, the K–R mutation did not affect phototropic bending growth or light-mediated phosphorylation of phot1 (Figure 1B, 1C, and 1G).
Because the Arabidopsis plants were grown under white light at 100 μmol m−2 s−1 and the light-induced phosphorylation of phot1 was assessed under blue light at 30 μmol m−2 s−1, we examined the effects of different light qualities on phot1 phosphorylation. We found that phot1 phosphorylation increased to a similar level upon irradiation with white light or blue light. However, green, red, and far-red light did not affect phot1 autophosphorylation (Figure 1H). Overall, white light and blue light had similar effects on phot1, consistent with previous findings (Kimura et al., 2020). K–Q substitution of the four identified lysine acetylation sites led to a loss of phot1 phosphorylation and function under both types of light in both Arabidopsis and tobacco plants.

## One key acetylation site regulates phot1 phosphorylation and acetylation in phototropism
To identify which of the four substitution sites was crucial for phot1 and phot1-mediated phototropism, we individually substituted the K residue at each potential phot1 acetylation site with Q, generating four phot1 variant proteins (PHOT1K129Q, PHOT1K404Q, PHOT1K603Q, and PHOT1K636Q). In phototropic growth assays, phot1 complementation lines with the K636Q substitution had cotyledonary petiole angles and cotyledon angles similar to those of the phot1,2 double mutant, whereas lines with other site-directed point mutations (K129Q, K404Q, and K603Q) were similar to the Col ecotype (Figure 2A and Supplemental Figure 5A–5C). Hypocotyl curvature in response to unilateral light in lines with the K636Q mutation was similar to that in the phot1,2 double mutant, whereas lines with the K129Q, K404Q, and K603Q substitutions had hypocotyl curvatures similar to that of Col (Figure 2B). These findings suggested that the deficiency in phot1 regulation of phototropism was mainly due to mutation of K636, a critical lysine residue located between the Jα helices and the Ser/Thr kinase domain.

To evaluate whether the K636Q substitution was sufficient to affect light-induced phosphorylation of phot1, we performed immunoblotting and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays using tobacco transiently expressing the PHOT1-GFP or PHOT1K636Q-GFP fusion gene (Figure 2C). The results showed that the K636Q substitution significantly reduced the autophosphorylation of phot1 in response to light. We next examined whether K636Q affected the acetylation of phot1 after light irradiation. The acetylation level of PHOT1-GFP increased after illumination, and the K636Q substitution of phot1 showed a considerably higher acetylation level than the native phot1 protein under dark and light conditions. These findings suggested that the hyperacetylation of phot1 induced by K636Q mutation may inhibit its light-mediated phosphorylation and activation. These results were consistent with the finding that K636Q substitution greatly reduced the phototropic sensitivity of the plants (Figure 2A). Overall, these results indicated that the regulation of acetylation at K636 is important for light-induced phosphorylation of phot1 in phototropic signaling.

## HDA9 positively regulates phototropism and light-dependent phot1 phosphorylation
On the basis of the aforementioned results, we speculated that there may be a lysine deacetylase that adjusts the acetylation level of phot1 in phototropism. We screened HDAC mutants and transgenic lines by phenotyping (Supplemental Figure 6) and found that hda9-1 and hda9-2, two knockout mutants of HDA9 (van Zanten et al., 2014), showed a smaller cotyledonary petiole angle and a greater hypocotyl curvature toward light, similar to those of the phot1,2 double mutant (Figure 3A and 3B). An immunoblotting assay showed that endogenous phot1 had a lower light-induced phosphorylation level in the hda9-1 mutant than in Col, whereas phot1 phosphorylation was higher in plants overexpressing HDA9 than in Col in response to 30 μmol m−2 s−1 of blue-light (Figure 3C). We next tested the effect of different light intensities on phot1 and found that hda9-1 consistently showed much lower phosphorylation levels at 3, 10, and 18 μmol m−2 s−1 of blue light, whereas 35S-HDA9-GFP showed a slightly higher phot1 phosphorylation level compared with the Col ecotype (Figure 3D). In addition, overexpression of HDA9 appeared to increase the phot1 protein level after illumination, whereas the absence of HDA9 reduced phot1 accumulation. Although phosphorylation of phot1 is most significant for its activation, the increase in protein level further enhances this effect. Overall, we found that HDA9 positively regulates phototropic growth in plants, probably via light-mediated phosphorylation of phot1.

## HDA9 deacetylates and regulates the kinase activity of phot1
We hypothesized that HDA9 might influence phototropic signaling by interacting with phot1. To test this hypothesis, we co-transformed nYFP-HDA9 (N terminus of YFP) and cYFP-PHOT1 (C terminus of YFP) into Arabidopsis protoplasts and performed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay (Figure 4A). We found complementary fluorescence on the protoplast membrane, which suggested that phot1 and HDA9 interacted on the plasma membrane. Similarly, when we fused HDA9 with GFP and phot1 with mCherry to examine their subcellular localization, we found that both were present at high levels individually and together on the membrane (Figure 4B). Furthermore, our observations also revealed nuclear signal aggregation in cells expressing HDA9-GFP, suggesting additional epigenetic regulatory functions beyond this established role. We then used a firefly luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay to assess the phot1–HDA9 interaction. The results showed that PHOT1-nLUC (N terminus of luciferase) interacted with cLUC-HDA9 (C terminus of luciferase) (Figure 4C and 4E). By contrast, the interaction between PHOT1K636Q-nLUC and cLUC-HDA9 was barely detectable (Figure 4D and 4F), suggesting that PHOT1K636Q-GFP showed a higher acetylation level because it did not interact with HDA9 (Figure 2C). The results of co-IP analysis showed that PHOT1-GFP could pull down HDA9-Myc under both light and dark conditions in Agrobacterium-infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves, although the interaction decreased after illumination (Figure 4G). However, this physical association disappeared when K636 was substituted with glutamine in phot1, suggesting that K636 is an important acetylation site for recruitment of HDA9, enabling the deacetylation of phot1 in the light signaling pathway (Figure 4H).

On the basis of these results, we speculated that HDA9 may deacetylate phot1 as a non-histone substrate to promote phototropic growth. To test this hypothesis, we first investigated whether phot1 can be deacetylated by HDA9 in vivo. Total proteins were extracted from N. benthamiana leaves transiently expressing PHOT1-GFP and HDA9-Myc or PHOT1-GFP alone as a control. The PHOT1-GFP protein complexes were immunoprecipitated with GFP-trap beads and then probed with anti-acetyl-lysine antibodies. The acetylation level of phot1 under dark and light conditions was lower in the presence of HDA9 than when PHOT1-GFP was expressed alone (Figure 5A). This finding suggested that the PHOT1-GFP fusion protein underwent acetylation and that HDA9 deacetylated phot1 in vivo. These results agreed with the co-IP results, which indicated that HDA9 interacted with phot1 under both illumination conditions in vivo (Figure 4G). Consistent with the above findings, the acetylation level of phot1 increased slightly and reproducibly after illumination in plants expressing PHOT1-GFP and HDA9-Myc or PHOT1-GFP alone (Figure 4, Figure 5G and Figure 4, Figure 5A). In parallel experiments, we found that the acetylation level of PHOT1K636Q-GFP was similar with or without HDA9-Myc co-expression (Figure 5B). This indicated that the K636Q substitution substantially impaired HDA9-mediated deacetylation of phot1. Notably, phot1 acetylation levels were consistently lower in Col plants than in the hda9-1 mutant under both light and dark conditions (Figure 5C). Also, the light signal probably influenced the affinity of phot1–HDA9, given that the protein level and subcellular localization of HDA9 were not regulated by light (Supplemental Figure 7A and 7B). Nevertheless, the enzyme activity of HDA9 was increased in the light (Supplemental Figure 7C). These results suggested that HDA9 plays a key role in the deacetylation of phot1 in vivo and that K636 of phot1 might be involved in the recognition of HDA9 or might be one of the deacetylation targets of HDA9. Therefore, HDA9 could not deacetylate the mutant phot1 protein.

Because HDA9 can influence phot1 phosphorylation by regulating the acetylation dynamics of phot1, we next hypothesized that HDA9 may directly affect the kinase activity of phot1, as phot1 is capable of autophosphorylation. We used transgenic Arabidopsis mutants and transgenic tobacco plants to detect endogenous phot1 kinase activity. The results showed that deletion of HDA9 resulted in phot1 kinase activity that was half that of the wild type, whereas phot1 activity in the HDA9-GFP overexpression line was approximately 1.4-fold that of the Col wild type (Figure 5D). Similarly, in a tobacco transient expression system, PHOT1K636Q-Myc had a kinase activity similar to that of kinase-dead PHOT1S852A-Myc, both of which were about 25% that of wild-type PHOT1-Myc (Figure 5E). The results of kinase activity assays were consistent with those of previous phosphorylation experiments. In summary, HDA9 can regulate the light-induced kinase activity of phot1 and participate in the phototropic signaling pathway by affecting the normal acetylation dynamics of phot1, with K636 of phot1 as a key recognition site.

## HDA9 positively regulates phototropic growth via phot1
We next investigated the genetic interactions between HDA9 and phot1 during phototropic signaling. The phot1,2 double mutant and hda9-1 showed a smaller cotyledonary petiole angle than the Col ecotype. This phenotype was also found after overexpression of HDA9-GFP in the phot1,2 mutant and the hda9 phot1,2 triple mutant, suggesting that overexpression of HDA9-GFP could not rescue the phototropic deficiency of phot1,2 (Figure 6A and Supplemental Figure 8A–8C). Overexpression of PHOT1-FLAG and HDA9-GFP separately in Col or overexpression of PHOT1-FLAG in hda9-1 resulted in a cotyledonary petiole angle similar to that of Col (Figure 6A; Supplemental Figure 9A and 9B). Similarly, the phototropic bending angle of the hypocotyl in the phot1,2 mutant overexpressing HDA9-GFP or the hda9 phot1,2 triple mutant was similar to that of the phot1,2 double mutant. By contrast, the hypocotyl curvatures of Col seedlings overexpressing PHOT1-FLAG or HDA9-GFP or hda9-1 seedlings overexpressing PHOT1-FLAG were similar to that of Col, consistent with the positive roles of HDA9 and phot1 in phototropic signaling (Figure 6B and Supplemental Figure 9C). Overall, these results suggested that HDA9 can regulate plant phototropism through phot1.

## HDA9 regulates phot1 through a key acetylated lysine in a conserved light-signaling pathway
We investigated 25 of the most important economic and food crops in the world and identified a conserved phototropic signaling pathway by which plant cells transduce a light signal through a critical acetylated lysine residue of phot1. K636 of AtPHOT1 was found to be a conserved lysine residue in phototropins of plants such as oats, oilseed rape, sunflower, rose, strawberry, apple, pear, peanut, cocoa, musk, pumpkin, cucumber, camellia, grape, sesame, eggplant, maize, green pepper, tobacco, barley, tomato, and fern (Supplemental Figure 10). Light induction increased the autophosphorylation of SlPHOT1, NtPHOT1, and AhPHOT1, whereas the light-mediated kinase activity of phot1 diminished when the conserved lysine sites were substituted with glutamine (Figure 7A). Our findings suggest that this light-signal transduction pathway mediated by phot1 acetylation–phosphorylation may be a conserved light response among different plant species.

(B) A model demonstrating how acetylation dynamically modulates phot1 activity to control phototropic responses. The acetylation level of phot1 is reversibly regulated by HDA9 and unknown acetyltransferases (ATs). In the dark, the histone deacetylase HDA9 specifically recognizes and binds to phot1, maintaining it in a low-acetylation state that primes the photoreceptor for light activation. Consistent with this scenario, the hda9 mutant exhibits hyperacetylation of phot1. Upon light perception, phot1 undergoes rapid phosphorylation to initiate signaling, and its interaction with HDA9 is concurrently weakened. The resulting accumulation of acetylated phot1—mediated by yet-unidentified acetyltransferases—acts as a negative feedback loop to attenuate phot1 phosphorylation, thereby preventing signal overamplification. In the hda9 mutant, hyperacetylation of phot1 may sterically impede phosphorylation at nearby sites, suppressing its light-induced activation. This HDA9-mediated acetylation–deacetylation cycle functions as a tunable braking system that dynamically fine-tunes phototropic signaling cascades, ensuring an optimal phototropic response.

## Discussion
Although phot1 and its downstream interactors are known to be important for phototropic growth, the regulatory mechanism that influences the link between phot1 and the light signal is not clear. In this study, we revealed an acetylation-dependent mechanism that regulates the kinase activity of phot1 in phototropic signaling. Our findings demonstrate how the interaction between HDA9 and phot1 actively switches the sensitivity of phot1 between acetylation and phosphorylation rapidly and reversibly. Our study reveals a novel regulatory mechanism whereby phot1 activity is dynamically modulated by acetylation to control phototropic responses. In the absence of light, the histone deacetylase HDA9 specifically interacts with membrane-associated phot1 and targets lysine 636 (K636) for recognition, maintaining phot1 in a low-acetylation state that is primed for light activation. Upon light perception, phot1 undergoes rapid phosphorylation, which not only activates its signaling function but also reduces its affinity for HDA9. The subsequent accumulation of acetylated phot1—mediated by yet-to-be-identified acetyltransferases—forms a negative feedback loop that attenuates phot1 phosphorylation, thereby preventing excessive signal amplification. This cyclic acetylation–deacetylation operates as a molecular braking system that precisely calibrates phototropic signal transduction, ensuring an optimal growth response. Crucially, we demonstrated that phot1 acetylation does not serve as an activation switch but rather functions as a tunable rheostat to maintain phototropic homeostasis (Figure 7B).
Our results showed that four lysine sites of phot1 were modified by acetylation (Figure 1A), and the K636Q substitution inhibited light-induced autophosphorylation of phot1 and decreased the phototropic response (Figure 2A–2C; Supplemental Figure 5A and 5B). These findings indicate that acetylation of K636 may play an essential role in modulating the function of phot1 in phototropism. By analyzing HDAC mutants and transgenic lines that overexpressed HDACs, we found that both hda9 mutants exhibited phototropism defects similar to those in phot1,2 double mutants (Figure 3A and 3B). We also found that HDA9 interacted with phot1 at the plasma membrane under light and dark conditions and that the intensity of this interaction was greater in the dark (Figure 4A–4C and 4G). These findings suggest that HDA9 plays a positive role in phototropism, possibly through its interaction with phot1. Future investigations should focus on characterizing the shared genetic network by which phot1 and HDA9 regulate photomorphogenesis, in particular by identifying their common downstream targets and characterizing the functional significance of these transcriptional outputs. A critical unanswered question is whether HDA9 exerts its regulatory effects through direct epigenetic modification of these target loci, as this would establish a novel mechanistic link between photoreceptor signaling and chromatin-based regulation. Addressing these questions will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how light perception and epigenetic regulation converge to orchestrate photomorphogenic responses.
We also found that HDA9 deacetylated phot1, and the acetylation level of phot1 was higher in the light than in the dark (Figures 2C, 5A, and 5C), indicating that HDA9 was closely involved in regulating dynamic changes in phot1 acetylation during the dark–light transition. The HDA9–phot1 interaction was suppressed by the K636Q mutation in phot1 (Figure 4D–4F and 4H). Moreover, the K636Q substitution caused a reduction in light-triggered phot1 phosphorylation and an increase in phot1 acetylation, consistent with the finding that the K636Q substitution weakened the HDA9–phot1 interaction (Figures 2C, 4D, 4F, and 4H). These results indicated that HDA9 may modulate the autophosphorylation of phot1 through the K636 residue and that the K636Q mutation may inhibit the recruitment of HDA9 to phot1 for phot1 deacetylation. Alternatively, acetylation of phot1 at K636 may lead to changes in protein conformation, which in turn may affect its binding affinity to HDA9. We found that normal acetylation of K636 is key for recruitment of HDA9 to deacetylate phot1 in the light, enabling its light-induced autophosphorylation and enzymatic activity (Figure 5D and 5E). In summary, the HDA9–phot1 module regulates dynamic phot1 acetylation during phototropism. Similar to our findings, another study showed that the acetylation of 14-3-3 proteins can impair their ability to bind phosphopeptides, which in turn can affect their phosphorylation. These interactions indicated that crosstalk occurs between acetylation and phosphorylation (Choudhary et al., 2009).
Many studies have shown that K-to-R substitutions can block acetylation without affecting the positive charge on the residues, whereas K-to-Q substitutions can mimic acetylation (Barlev et al., 2001). We substituted the K residue at each potential phot1 acetylation site with R or Q, generating six phot1 variant proteins (PHOT1RRRR, PHOT1QQQQ, PHOT1K129Q, PHOT1K404Q, PHOT1K603Q, and PHOT1K636Q). Phenotyping assays showed that K-to-R substitutions did not affect phot1 function (Figure 1B and 1C). In addition, when the K636Q mutant version of phot1 barely interacted with HDA9, the phot1 protein retained its acetylation (Figures 2C and 5B). Because the HDA9-defective plant showed a weak phototropic response but not a complete absence of phototropic growth, as observed in the phot1,2 double mutant (Figure 3A and 3B), we speculated that the regulation of phototropism in plants requires the complete phosphorylation and kinase activity of phot1 mediated by HDA9. In addition, mutation of the four K residues in PHOT1 to R had no significant effect on the phototactic response of plants (Figure 1B and 1C) or on the light-induced phosphorylation activity of PHOT1 (Figure 1G). These findings suggest that PHOT1RRRR retains its ability to interact with HDA9 without perturbing the overall acetylation dynamics mediated by HDA9. This hypothesis is supported by co-IP assays demonstrating unaltered physical interaction between PHOT1RRRR and HDA9 (Supplemental Figure 11). Importantly, we propose that HDA9 likely regulates additional acetylation sites on phot1 in addition to the four lysine residues studied here. The results of immunoprecipitation followed by LC–MS/MS confirmed the presence of novel acetylation sites on phot1 (Supplemental Figure 12), and it will be important to determine the complete repertoire of phot1 lysine residues regulated by HDA9 in future work, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of this acetylation-dependent regulatory mechanism.
It will also be important to determine whether and how HDA9-mediated phot1 deacetylation regulates other phot1-interacting proteins, given that phot1 interacts with several important kinases during light signaling, including NPH3 (Barlev et al., 2001; Sullivan et al., 2021), RPT2 (Haga et al., 2015), PKS4 (Demarsy et al., 2012), and FER (Li et al., 2022). In addition, researchers have reported that HDA9, together with several interacting proteins, plays various epigenetic roles in the regulation of multiple plant traits and environmental responses (de Rooij et al., 2020). In this study, we showed that HDA9 possesses non-histone-protein deacetylase activity (Figures 4 and 5A) and that HDA9-mediated phot1 deacetylation plays a positive role in phototropic regulation (Figures 3 and 6) in what may be an evolutionarily conserved phototropism signaling pathway (Figure 7A and Supplemental Figure 10). Given that histone acetyltransferases (HATs) catalyze the deposition of acetyl groups, whereas HDACs remove these marks (Liu et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2020), the specific HATs that promote the addition of acetyl groups to phot1 to achieve acetylation homeostasis during phototropic regulation need to be identified, since the acetylation level of phot1 increases after exposure to light (Figure 2C). Overall, our results show that phot1 and its regulators, such as HDACs and HATs, are critical elements in phototropic plant growth. A combination of genetic, molecular, biochemical, and structural studies may provide further insight into how the specificity of the role of phot1 acetylation is determined and how changes in phot1 acetylation are modulated.
Post-translational modifications provide a reliable way to determine protein function, and the acetylation of histone and non-histone proteins plays a role in various cellular processes and human diseases. Acetylation is also associated with cancer, as the tumor suppressor p53 has been found to undergo acetylation (Gu and Roeder, 1997). Many studies have related HDACs to cancer and other diseases, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (Howie et al., 2019), leukemia (Mullighan et al., 2011), and nervous-system-related diseases (D’Mello, 2019). According to statistics at Plant PTM viewer (https://www.psb.ugent.be/webtools/ptm-viewer/index.php), 334 255 PTMs matching 128 920 proteins have been found in eight plant species; these PTMs include 24 820 acetylated sites in 11 630 proteins. Although protein acetylation is an important process, information on its underlying mechanisms is limited. In this study, we found that HDA9 controls the photosensitivity of phot1 through acetylation of its K636 site. The K636 site is therefore a promising target for modifying and regulating the activity of phot1 in phototropism-related plant architecture.

## Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
Most phot1 (Huala et al., 1997), phot2 (Kagawa et al., 2001), phot1,2 (Kinoshita et al., 2001), hda9-1 (van Zanten et al., 2014), and hda9-2 (van Zanten et al., 2014) mutants have been described previously. Other mutants are described in this study. The 35S:HDA9-GFP transgenic line was reported previously (Yang et al., 2020). The hda9 phot1,2 triple mutant was generated by crossing hda9-1 with phot1,2. Homozygous plants were selected by genotyping. For functional analysis, the constructs PHOT1:PHOT1-GFP, PHOT1:PHOT1K129/404/603/636R-GFP, PHOT1:PHOT1K129/404/603/636Q-GFP, PHOT1:PHOT1K129Q-GFP, PHOT1:PHOT1K404Q-GFP, PHOT1:PHOT1K603Q-GFP, and PHOT1:PHOT1K636Q-GFP were cloned into pCambia1302 vectors to generate transgenic plants in the phot1,2 background expressing a PHOT1:PHOT1s-GFP fusion (GFP-tagged PHOT1 driven by a 2-kb PHOT1 promoter). The 35S:PHOT1-FLAG construct was cloned into the pCambia1302 vector and introduced into Col or hda9-1 to generate 35S:PHOT1-FLAG and 35S:PHOT1-FLAG hda9 transgenic plants, respectively. The 35S:HDA9-GFP construct was cloned into the pCambia1302 vector and introduced into Col or phot1,2 to generate 35S:HDA9-GFP and 35S:HDA9-GFP phot1,2 transgenic plants. The UBQ10:PHOT1-GFP, UBQ10:PHOT1K129/404/603/636R-GFP, UBQ10:PHOT1K129/404/603/636Q-GFP, UBQ10:PHOT1K636Q-GFP, UBQ10:PHOT1-FLAG, UBQ10:PHOT1D806N-FLAG, UBQ10:PHOT1-Myc, UBQ10:PHOT1S851A-Myc, 35S:HDA9-Myc, lPHOT1-GFP, UBQ10:NtPHOT1-GFP, and UBQ10:AhPHOT1-GFP constructs were cloned into pCambia1300 and introduced into N. benthamiana for transient expression. The vector with the UBQ10 promoter is a modified vector with enhanced expression of exogenous proteins (Zhang et al., 2025).
The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, which was used to transform Arabidopsis plants by the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 1998). Transformants were selected on the basis of their hygromycin resistance. T3, T4, or T5 homozygous lines were used for subsequent experiments. The PHOT1K129/404/603/636R, PHOT1K129/404/603/636Q, PHOT1K129Q, PHOT1K404Q, PHOT1K603Q, and PHOT1K636Q substitutions were created by site-directed mutagenesis of the PHOT1 coding sequence.
For cotyledon angle phenotyping, seeds were stratified for 3 days at 4°C and transferred to soil at 22°C under a long-day photoperiod (16-h light/8-h dark) for 10 days. For phototropism phenotyping, the seeds were cultured on half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium (containing 0.3% sucrose and 1.5% agar) and stratified for 5 days at 4°C. The seeds were then transferred to a growth chamber and left in darkness at 22°C for 3 days before being irradiated with unilateral white light at 180 μmol m−2 s−1 for 48 h. For immunoblotting, LCI, and co-IP assays with N. benthamiana, the seeds were planted in soil at 22°C under a long-day photoperiod for 4 weeks. Agroinfiltration was then performed, and the plants were kept in darkness overnight, then transferred to long-day conditions for another 3 days. Before exposure to different light conditions, the plants were kept in darkness overnight. For immunoblotting, RNA-sequencing, and RT–qPCR assays of Arabidopsis, seeds were planted on half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium, stratified for 3 days at 4°C, transferred to a growth chamber, and left in darkness at 22°C for 4 days before being exposed to the indicated light environments.

## Identification of global acetylation sites by LC–MS/MS
Seven-day-old etiolated Arabidopsis seedlings were either kept in darkness or exposed to red light for 3 h. Each harvested sample was ground into cell powder in liquid nitrogen and transferred to a 5-ml centrifuge tube. Four volumes of lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail) were added to the cell powder, and the mixture was sonicated three times on ice using a high-intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz). For PTM experiments, inhibitors were also added to the lysis buffer (e.g., 3 μM trichostatin A and 50 mM nicotinamide for acetylation). The remaining debris was removed by centrifugation at 20 000 g and 4°C for 10 min. Finally, the protein was precipitated with cold 20% trichloroacetic acid for 2 h at −20°C. After centrifugation at 12 000 g and 4°C for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded. The remaining precipitate was washed with cold acetone three times. The protein was redissolved in 8 M urea, and the protein concentration was determined with a kit from BCA according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
For digestion, the protein solution was reduced with 5 mM DTT for 30 min at 56°C and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. The protein sample was then diluted by adding 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) to a urea concentration less than 2 M. Finally, trypsin was added at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for an initial overnight digestion and at a 1:100 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for a second 4-h digestion. After trypsin digestion, peptides were desalted using a Strata X C18 SPE column (Phenomenex) and vacuum dried. Peptides were reconstituted in 0.5 M TEAB and processed according to the protocol of the TMT kit/iTRAQ kit (AB SCIEX, BOS, USA). In brief, one unit of TMT/iTRAQ reagent was thawed and reconstituted in acetonitrile. The peptide mixtures were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature before being pooled, desalted, and dried by vacuum centrifugation.
The tryptic peptides were fractionated by high-pH reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography using a Thermo Betasil C18 column (5-μm particles, 10 mm internal diameter [i.d.], 250 mm length). In brief, peptides were first separated into 60 fractions using a gradient of 8%–32% acetonitrile (pH 9.0) over 60 min. The peptides were then combined into fractions and dried by vacuum centrifugation. To enrich acetylation-modified peptides, tryptic peptides dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris–HCl, and 0.5% NP-40 [pH 8.0]) were incubated with prewashed pan-anti-acetyl-lysine antibody beads at 4°C overnight with gentle shaking. The beads were then washed four times with NETN buffer and twice with H2O. The bound peptides were eluted from the beads with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Finally, the eluted fractions were combined and vacuum dried. For LC–MS/MS analysis, the peptides were desalted with C18 ZipTips (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
The tryptic peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and directly loaded onto a home-made reversed-phase analytical column (15 cm length, 75 μm i.d.). The gradient comprised an increase from 6% to 23% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 98% acetonitrile) over 26 min, an increase from 23% to 35% over 8 min, and an increase to 80% over 3 min, followed by holding at 80% for the last 3 min, all at a constant flow rate of 400 nl min−1 on an EASY-nLC 1000 UPLC system. The peptides were subjected to a nanospray ion (NSI) source followed by MS/MS on a Q Exactive Plus instrument (Thermo) coupled online to the UPLC. The electrospray voltage was 2.0 kV. The m/z scan range was 350–1800 for full scan, and intact peptides were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 70 000. Peptides were then selected for MS/MS using a normalized collision energy setting of 28, and the fragments were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 17 500. The data-dependent procedure alternated between one MS scan followed by 20 MS/MS scans with 15.0-s dynamic exclusion. Automatic gain control (AGC) was set to 5E4. Fixed first mass was set to 100 m/z.
The resulting MS/MS data were processed using the MaxQuant search engine (v.1.5.2.8). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the Arabidopsis proteome (TAIR10) database concatenated with a reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P was specified as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to 4 missing cleavages. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set to 20 ppm in First search and 5 ppm in Main search, and the mass tolerance for fragment ions was set to 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as a fixed modification and acetylation modification and oxidation on Met were specified as variable modifications. The false discovery rate was adjusted to <1%, and the minimum score for modified peptides was set to >40.

## Immunoblot analysis
Total proteins were extracted using 200 μl of liquid-nitrogen-ground powder from Arabidopsis seedlings or Agrobacterium-infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves. The powder was dissolved in 200 μl of 2× SDS sample buffer, boiled for 5 min, centrifuged twice at 12 000 rpm for 15 min, and analyzed using a 6% (for phot1 or PHOT1-GFP) or 10% (all other proteins) SDS–PAGE gel. The proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad) and detected with anti-PHOT1 (1:5000; Agrisera, AS10720), anti-actin (1:2000; ABclonal, AC009), anti-GFP (1:1000; Santa Cruz, sc-9996), anti-ac-Lys (1:500; CST, #9441), and anti-Myc (1:1000; Santa Cruz, sc-40) antibodies. Blots were developed using horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and detected with a Clarity Western ECL Substrate System (Bio-Rad).

## Co-IP assays
A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 containing the indicated constructs was co-infiltrated into 4-week-old fully expanded leaves of N. benthamiana plants. After incubation for 3 days, the infiltrated leaves were harvested according to the indicated light condition and homogenized in protein extraction buffer (0.5% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM EDTA-Na2 [pH 8.0], 10 mM DTT, 0.5 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF], and Roche protease inhibitor cocktail). The extract was centrifuged twice at 12 000 rpm for 15 min. After protein extraction, 15 μl of anti-GFP antibody-bound magnetic beads (KT Health, KTSM1311) were added to 2 ml of sample and rotated at 4°C for 3 h. The beads were washed twice with PBST (PBS with 0.05% Tween-20), and the proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 2× SDS protein buffer for 5 min. All protein samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-Myc antibody (1:1000; Santa Cruz, sc-40).

## Subcellular localization and BiFC assays
Using the MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix (ABclonal, RK21020), genes encoding GFP and mCherry were fused to the C termini of the HDA9 and PHOT1 coding regions, with expression of the fusion protein driven by the CaMV 35S RNA promoter. HDA9 and PHOT1 were cloned into pBiFC vectors and purified with the HiPure Plasmid EF Mini Kit (Magen, P1112-02). Protoplasts were isolated from leaves of 3-week-old, long-day-grown Arabidopsis Col plants as described previously (Yoo et al., 2007). After incubation for 16 h at 22°C in darkness, the protoplasts were examined under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 700).

## LCI assays
PHOT1 and HDA9 were cloned into the pCambia1300-nLUC and pCambia1300-cLUC vectors, respectively. After incubation for 3 days, the infiltrated leaves were harvested according to the indicated light condition and sprayed with 1 mM luciferin (Sigma, L9504) in darkness for 3 min before analysis of luminescence. Images of luminescence were captured using a low-light-cooled CCD imaging apparatus (LB985 NightSHADE, Berthold Technologies). All images shown in the figures are representative of at least five tobacco leaves.

## Phototropism

## Cotyledon positioning

## Figure Figure 7: HDA9 positively regulates phot1 modification in plant phototropism.(A) Light-induced phosphorylation of SlPHOT1s, NtPHOT1s, and AhPHOT1s. Immunoblot assays in transiently transformed N. benthamiana leaves. Leaves were grown overnight in darkness after agroinfiltration for 3 days, then exposed to 100 μmol m−2 s−1 of white light for 0, 1, or 2 h. SlPHOT1, NtPHOT1, and AhPHOT1 were cloned using cotyledon cDNA from tomato, tobacco, and peanut as templates. The double asterisk (∗∗) indicates phosphorylated PHOT1-GFP proteins, and the single asterisk (∗) indicates non-phosphorylated PHOT1-GFP proteins.(B) A model demonstrating how acetylation dynamically modulates phot1 activity to control phototropic responses. The acetylation level of phot1 is reversibly regulated by HDA9 and unknown acetyltransferases (ATs). In the dark, the histone deacetylase HDA9 specifically recognizes and binds to phot1, maintaining it in a low-acetylation state that primes the photoreceptor for light activation. Consistent with this scenario, the hda9 mutant exhibits hyperacetylation of phot1. Upon light perception, phot1 undergoes rapid phosphorylation to initiate signaling, and its interaction with HDA9 is concurrently weakened. The resulting accumulation of acetylated phot1—mediated by yet-unidentified acetyltransferases—acts as a negative feedback loop to attenuate phot1 phosphorylation, thereby preventing signal overamplification. In the hda9 mutant, hyperacetylation of phot1 may sterically impede phosphorylation at nearby sites, suppressing its light-induced activation. This HDA9-mediated acetylation–deacetylation cycle functions as a tunable braking system that dynamically fine-tunes phototropic signaling cascades, ensuring an optimal phototropic response.
