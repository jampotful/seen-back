# Behavioral and transcriptomic responses of wild least horseshoe bats to artificial light

## Abstract
SummaryArtificial light has altered nighttime environments, jeopardizing the fitness of organisms. While many nocturnal animals avoid artificial light, the molecular mechanisms behind this phenomenon remain elusive. This study aimed to explore the behavioral and transcriptomic responses of least horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus pusillus) to artificial light. We manipulated three lighting conditions (i.e., dark control, white light, and green light) inside bat roost. We assessed behavioral responses and gene expressions in the adrenal gland-kidney complexes and brains of bats. Our analyses showed that white and green light caused a decline in bat flight activity and the number of echolocation vocalizations per pass. White and green light activated gene expressions responsible for glucocorticoid synthesis and metabolism. The flight activity of bats was negatively associated with expression levels of ADCY8 and SERPINA6 genes. These findings imply that gene expressions involved in glucocorticoid signaling pathways affect light aversion in wild bats.Subject areas: Zoology, Evolutionary biology, Evolutionary ecology, Transcriptomics

## Introduction
The extensive use of artificial lighting has altered the natural light-dark cycles in most ecosystems, leading to the emergence of “ecological light pollution.”1,2,3 Satellite remote sensing data indicate that over 20% of the world’s land surfaces have suffered from light pollution.4 This issue is expected to be exacerbated in future decades due to the growing use of bright and broad-spectrum light-emitting diodes (LEDs).5 Increasing studies have documented the adverse impacts of artificial light at night (ALAN) on a variety of organisms,6 yet the mechanisms underlying the effects of light pollution remain an open question.
Although certain insects display positive phototaxis, many nocturnal animals avoid ALAN.7,8,9 From an ultimate perspective, this aversion to light may be attributed to the increased environmental risk caused by ALAN, since visually oriented predators are more adept at detecting prey in illuminated environments.10 From a proximate perspective, ALAN may act as an environmental stressor that triggers the expression of genes linked to glucocorticoid production and metabolism via the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis.11,12 Further, ALAN can interfere with gene expression related to circadian rhythms of animals, potentially leading to alternations in nightly activity.13,14 Evidence of circadian entrainment induced by ALAN has been observed in the cricket (Gryllus bimaculatus),15 subterranean tuco-tuco (Ctenomys aff. knighti),16 and mice.17 Nevertheless, the molecular basis of light avoidance behavior in nocturnal animals remains unknown, as the association between light aversion and expression profiles of stress- and clock-related genes has not be established.
Bats constitute an ideal animal model for exploring the underlying mechanisms of light pollution. They can perceive visible light through their eyes, and some species possess ultraviolet vision.18,19,20 The visual system of bats is characterized by a higher rod/cone ratio specialized for nocturnal niche, making them susceptible to ALAN.20,21 Prior studies have shown that the impacts of ALAN on bats depend on foraging guilds and light characteristics.22,23,24,25,26,27,28,29 In general, slower-flying bats avoid ALAN due to light-dependent environmental risk, while faster-flying bats can tolerate ALAN and gain foraging benefit due to insect concentration around light sources.21,30 Most slower-flying bats avoid white, green, and yellow light, but blue and red light appear to have minimal detrimental effects on their activities.26,27,31,32 So far, it remains unclear whether stress- and clock-related genes mediate light avoidance in bats, particularly in their natural environments.
The goal of this study was to investigate the behavioral and transcriptomic responses of wild least horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus pusillus) to artificial light. These bats inhabit natural or man-made caves and seek foraging after sunset. They are sensitive to white light during roost departure, irrespective of the presence of potential predators.29 We manipulated three lighting conditions at the bat roost, namely the dark control, white light, and green light (Figures 1 and S1). Using synchronized video and audio recordings, we monitored behavioral responses of Rhinolophus pusillus under each lighting condition. We used transcriptomic sequencing to determine gene expression in the adrenal gland-kidney complexes and brains of captured bats. We hypothesized that the expression of genes involved in glucocorticoid signaling and circadian entrainment pathways shaped light avoidance behavior in least horseshoe bats. We made the following predictions: (1) LED light would lead to a reduction in flight activity and the emission of echolocation pulses per pass among the bats; (2) LED light would trigger the expression of genes responsible for glucocorticoid secretion, glucocorticoid metabolism, and circadian entrainment; and (3) the expression levels of key genes involved in glucocorticoid signaling and circadian entrainment pathways would be negatively related to bat flight activity.

The emergence time of bats flying out was predicted by treatment type (linear mixed model [LMM]: estimate = 423.38, t = 6.62, p < 0.0001). Bats delayed their roost departure during light exposure, with average delays ranging from 20.11 to 21.60 min (white light, estimate = 921.99, z = 22.01, p < 0.0001; green light, estimate = 856.07, z = 20.58, p < 0.0001; Figure 2C). The emergence time of bats flying out showed no significant difference under white and green light (estimate = −65.92, z = −1.60, p = 0.25; Figure 2C).
The maximum number of echolocation pulses emitted by bats during each pass was dependent on treatment type (GLMM: estimate = −0.14, z = −2.85, p = 0.0044). When compared with the dark control, the maximum number of bat echolocation pulses per pass declined by 26.54% under white light (estimate = −0.25, z = −2.81, p = 0.014; Figure 2D) and by 30.94% under green light (estimate = −0.25, z = −2.66, p = 0.021; Figure 2D). The maximum number of bat echolocation vocalizations per pass showed no pronounced difference in the presence of white and green light (estimate = −0.005, z = −0.05, p = 0.99; Figure 2D).
The number of flights in caged bats was not influenced by treatment type (GLMM: estimate = −0.37, z = −1.10, p = 0.27) after controlling for experimental date and the order of cages. Despite the limited effects of green light (estimate = −0.73, z = −1.15, p = 0.48), the number of flights in caged bats was reduced under white light versus the dark control (estimate = −1.66, z = −2.59, p = 0.026; Figure 2E; Videos S4 and S5).

## Transcriptomic responses of bats
RNA sequencing of the adrenal gland-kidney complexes and brains from nine caged bats yielded 114.53 Gb of transcriptome data. The clean reads were assembled into 267,974 transcripts through de novo transcriptome assembly, which were then consolidated into 208,214 unigenes. The lengths of these unigenes were 301–21,780 bp. In total, 158,339 (76.05%) unigenes were annotated by at least one annotation database (Table S1). We found that 72%–84% of clean reads from each sample were successfully aligned with the reference genome of R. pusillus. Quantitative real-time PCR (real-time qPCR) confirmed that two key genes (i.e., ADCY8 and SERPINA6) related to glucocorticoid signaling, along with ten randomly selected genes, exhibited expression patterns consistent with those identified through RNA sequencing (Figure S2).
The reference-free and reference-based transcriptome analyses (RFTA and RBTA) indicated that genes expressed in the adrenal gland-kidney complexes showed significant enrichment across 15–226 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (Tables S2, S3, S4, S5, S6, and S7) and 1–135 Gene Ontology (GO) terms (Tables S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, and S13) when contrasting illuminated groups with dark control group. Specifically, the pathways of cortisol production (RFTA: ko04927, false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.0001, Figures 3A and 3B; RBTA: rfq04927, FDR < 0.0001; Figures 3H and 3I) and Cushing syndrome (RFTA: ko04934, FDR < 0.0001, Figures 3A and 3B; RBTA: rfq04934, FDR = 0.0002; Figures 3H and 3I) were significantly enriched by active genes from illuminated groups compared to dark control group. The glucocorticoid synthesis (RFTA: GO:0006704, FDR = 0.041, Figure 3C; RBTA: GO:0006704, FDR = 0.015; Figure 3J) and metabolism terms (RFTA: GO:0008211, FDR = 0.0006, Figure 3C; RBTA: GO:0008211, FDR = 0.0023; Figure 3J) were remarkably enriched by active genes from white light group compared to dark control group. The terms related to glucocorticoid synthesis (RFTA: GO:0006704, FDR = 0.33, Figure 3D; RBTA: GO:0006704, FDR = 0.011; Figure 3K) and metabolism (RFTA: GO:0008211, FDR = 0.092, Figure 3D; RBTA: GO:0008211, FDR = 0.003; Figure 3K) showed an upregulated tendency in green light group versus dark control group. The glucocorticoid signaling pathways or terms were not significantly enriched by active genes from green light group versus white light group (RFTA & RBTA: all FDR > 0.10; Tables S4, S7, S10, and S13).

## Relationship between bat behavior and transcriptomic responses
Both RFTA and RBTA verified that the ADCY8 gene from the adrenal gland-kidney complexes was enriched in the cortisol synthesis and secretion pathway (Figures 4A and 5A). The SERPINA6 gene from the adrenal gland-kidney complexes was involved in the glucocorticoid metabolism term when assessed through RFTA (Figure 4A). A significant negative correlation was observed between the number of flights in caged bats and expression level of the ADCY8 gene, as determined by RFTA (n = 9, rPearson = −0.63, p = 0.034; GLMM, estimate = −15.81, z = −6.54, p < 0.0001; Figures 4A−4C) and RBTA (n = 9, rPearson = −0.63, p = 0.033; GLMM, estimate = −2.79, z = −2.08, p = 0.038; Figures 5A−5C). The number of flights in caged bats was also predicted by expression level of the SERPINA6 gene (n = 9, rPearson = −0.60, p = 0.045; GLMM: estimate = −4.49, z = −6.56, p < 0.0001; Figures 4A, 4B, and 4D).

## Discussion
In this study, we investigated the behavioral and transcriptomic responses of wild R. pusillus to artificial light. Our controlled experiments demonstrated that LED light disturbed evening emergence in R. pusillus, as evidenced by decreased flight activity, fewer echolocation pulses per flight, and a lower proportion of bats flying out. Both reference-free and reference-based transcriptome analyses of the adrenal gland-kidney complexes in R. pusillus indicated that LED light activated gene expressions related to glucocorticoid production and metabolism. Notably, the flight activity of captured bats was inversely correlated with expression levels of the ADCY8 and SERPINA6 genes within glucocorticoid signaling pathways. These results support the hypothesis of light-induced stress,11,12,13 providing novel insights into the role of gene expression in influencing light aversion behavior in wild bats.
The nocturnal activity of R. pusillus was disrupted by white and green light, corroborating the findings of previous investigations. For example, lesser horseshoe bats reduced their movement along hedgerows following the introduction of white LED, orange high-pressure sodium, and green induction lamps.22,23,26 Myotis and Plecotus bats avoided illuminated sites in the presence of white and green light.27 White and green light at cave entrances inhibited evening emergence in some slow-flying Rhinolophus, Hipposideros, Myotis, and Miniopterus species.31,32 The similar detrimental effects of white light on nocturnal behavior have also been reported in wetas,33 treefrogs,34 and Mongolian five-toed jerboa.35
One potential explanation for the light avoidance behavior observed in R. pusillus is attributed to stress-inducing effects of artificial light.11,12 This hypothesis is supported by transcriptomic analyses of bat adrenal gland-kidney complexes, which showed a consistent upregulation of active genes associated with glucocorticoid production and metabolism when exposed to white or green light. The flight activity of caged bats declined with elevated expression levels of the ADCY8 and SERPINA6 genes that encode proteins essential for cortisol synthesis, secretion, and transportation.36,37 This phenomenon has not been previously identified in bats or other wildlife. One recent study indicated that red LED light enhanced the expression of cortisol-synthesis genes Cyp21a2 and Cyp11c1 in the head kidney of largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) larvae under laboratory conditions.38 Prolonged exposure to white light induced the release of corticosterone in the tadpoles of Rio Grande leopard frog (Rana berlandieri) and Gulf Coast toad (Bufo valliceps).39 White LED light led to increased levels of corticosterone in the blood and feathers of adult and juvenile great tits (Parus major).40,41 These findings suggest that artificial light can act as an environmental stressor, eliciting stress-related gene expression and hormonal secretion.
Previous studies have shown that artificial illumination at night can enhance the expression of core clock genes (i.e., Per, Clock, Cry, and Bmal) in the brains or suprachiasmatic nuclei of some animals, including the cricket (G. bimaculatus),15 Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar),42 and mice.43 However, this study did not reveal significant elevation in expression levels of these core clock genes in the brains from illuminated groups versus dark control group (Figures S3 and S4). The core clock genes in R. pusillus brains were not enriched in the circadian entrainment pathway, although this pathway was remarkably enriched by other active genes when assessed through the reference-free transcriptome analysis (Tables S14, S15, and S16). Similarly, Touzot et al. (2022) reported that white LED light did not elevate the expression levels of the Per, Clock, Cry, and Bmal genes in the heads and bodies of common toad (Bufo bufo) tadpoles.44 Alaasam et al. (2021) showed that 1.5 lux of 5,000 K LED light had negligible effects on the expression of core clock genes in the brains of zebra finches (Taeniopygia guttata).45 The discrepancies among these studies may be attributed to species-specific tolerance thresholds to artificial light and different lighting conditions. Further research is warranted to explore the effects of artificial light with different spectra and intensities on the expression of core clock genes in light-sensitive animals.
In summary, our behavioral experiment demonstrated that R. pusillus avoided white and green light during roost departure. The observed aversion to light in R. pusillus was tightly linked to expression levels of the ADCY8 and SERPINA6 genes, which were implicated in the pathways of glucocorticoid synthesis and metabolism. These findings provide evidence indicating that gene expressions involved in glucocorticoid signaling pathways shape light-averse behavior in wild bats. This study represents a significant advancement in our understanding of the molecular basis of light aversion in wild bats.

## Limitations of the study
This study is subjected to three limitations. First, to minimize the potential effects of artificial light on the reproductive activity of female R. pusillus, light manipulation was exclusively performed at the roost of the male colony, rather than at the roosts of both male and maternal colonies. Second, we did not expose caged bats to prolonged periods of darkness or LED light over several weeks, as acclimatization of R. pusillus to the temporary laboratory environment is challenging. Consequently, these bats were merely exposed to darkness or LED light for a duration of 60 min within their roosting chamber. This acute exposure to light may explain the absence of differentially expressed genes related to glucocorticoid signaling and circadian rhythm in R. pusillus. Third, we conducted transcriptomic sequencing on the whole bat brains instead of focusing on specific brain regions involved in stress response and circadian regulation, which may affect the resolution of the transcriptomic data.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Animals
Field experiments were conducted at the roost of a male R. pusillus colony in Fangshan National Geology Park, Nanjing, China, from May to July 2023. These bats inhabited an abandoned man-made cave, which contained two entrances, a 200 m passage, and four compartments. A maternal colony of R. pusillus dwelled in another man-made cave. To prevent potential negative effects on female reproductive behavior, the maternal colony was excluded from the manipulative experiments. Experimental protocols adhered to the relevant laws governing vertebrate research and the guidelines established by the ASAB/ABS for the use of animals in research. All the experimental procedures were approved by the Committee on the Use and Care of Animals of the China West Normal University (Approval No. CWNU2022D001). R. pusillus is not included in the list of key protected wild animals in China. The killing of this species outside protected areas for scientific purposes is permitted without requiring the authorization from governmental authorities.

## Method details

## Light manipulation at the roost exit
The experimental set-up for light manipulation was designed following one prior study.29 In particular, we obstructed one entrance of the cave with a soft cloth 1 h before sunset, restricting the bats to using one exit during light manipulation. To monitor bats’ flight activity, we placed a high-speed infrared thermal camera (T640; FLIR Systems, Inc., Portland, OR, USA) inside the roost at a distance of 6 m from the designated exit. A four-channel ultrasonic detector (UltrasoundGate 416H; Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin, Germany) was used for recording echolocation vocalizations of bats (Figure S1). Video and audio recording systems under the control of a laptop computer were mounted on a tripod 1.5 m above the ground. The focal passage (6 × 1.2 × 2.1 m) contained a corner connecting to the main passage. The infrared thermal camera had a minimal focus distance of 25 mm and a field of view of 25° × 19°, allowing it to record flight activities of bats as they emerged from the focal passage. Four CM16/CMPA microphones (Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin, Germany) were positioned in a triangular setup, angled at 45° towards the focal exit. To allow bats to acclimate to the experimental setup, video and audio recording systems were not activated during the first week. The obstructed cave entrance was restored at the end of each nightly trial.
Following the acclimation phase, we installed solar-powered 50-W LED lamps (17 × 16 cm, Pengke Technology Lighting Co., Ltd, China) in white or green color on the right side of the cave wall at a distance of 3 m from the focal exit. We used LED lamps to generate the dark control, white light (peak wavelength, 454 nm; wavelength range, 380–780 nm), and green light (peak wavelength, 523 nm; wavelength range, 460–780 nm). Each light color was adjusted to nearly 49 lux using a remote control, aligning with the intensity range of local streetlights (n = 10, 40–52 lux). The LED lamp was deactivated during the dark control. The illuminance levels of experimental LED lamps and streetlights were assessed using a TA632B illuminance meter (Suzhou Te'ansi Electronic Industry Co., Ltd., China), which was placed at a height of 1.7 m and a distance of 0.6 m from the light sources.
Video and audio recordings were manually initiated upon the emergence of the first bat after sunset. The infrared thermal camera was operated at a frame rate of 30 frames per second with an emissivity rate of 0.81. The sampling rate of ultrasonic recording system was configured as 375 kHz at 16 bits per sample, maintaining consistent recording gain throughout the trials. The laptop screen was turned off to minimize the effect of ambient light emitted by the laptop. Three different treatments were randomly performed on separate nights. Considering the foraging requirements of R. pusillus, each trial lasted for 60 min, with only one treatment administered per night. Each treatment was repeated eight times across different nights. In addition to the acclimation period, we conducted light manipulations at the roost exit for 31 nights without rain and strong wind. R. pusillus departed from their roost within 40 min of sunset under dark conditions.29 A significant rise in insect abundance was recorded between 30 and 90 min after sunset in their foraging habitats.29 Consequently, R. pusillus was able to continue foraging after a 60-min exposure to light.

## Light manipulation at the roost compartment
After finishing the experiments at the roost exit, we conducted light manipulation within a compartment (6 × 2.5 × 2.2 m) of the bat roost over three nights. This compartment was isolated from the main passage by a door, allowing us to observe bat behavior without interference from external factors. Three cotton yarn cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm) were positioned on a large cardboard box at a height of 0.7 m above the ground. To replicate lighting conditions at the roost exit, a white or green LED lamp was mounted on a tripod at a distance of 1.2 m from the cages (Figure S1). Each light color was calibrated to approximately 49 lux using a remote control, and the LED lamp was switched off to generate the dark control. Three adult male R. pusillus were captured using a hand net and allowed to acclimate in respective cages for 60 min prior to sunset. To reduce the potential interactions among the bats, two foam boards were placed between the cages. After a 60-min acclimation, these three bats were exposed to only one of three treatments (dark control, white light, or green light) for an additional 60 min. The 60-min light exposure for caged bats was aligned with experimental conditions at the roost exit and has been shown to induce gene expression in the brains and internal organs of small mammals.43,59 An infrared thermal camera positioned 2 m behind the cages was utilized to monitor flight activities of bats. In total, nine adult males were captured for light manipulations within the roost compartment.

## Sample collection and transcriptomic experiment
Following a 60-min exposure to either darkness or LED light, nine caged bats were euthanized for collecting their adrenal glands, kidneys, and brains, which are directly linked to glucocorticoid signaling and circadian entrainment.11,14 The adrenal glands and kidneys were not individually separated for each bat, as these organs play a role in the secretion and excretion of glucocorticoid hormones, respectively. The biological samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and then preserved at −80°C. We extracted RNA from each sample using the TRIzol method (Invitrogen, CA, USA). We assessed the quality and purity of RNA using agarose gels and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Thermo Scientific, DE, USA). Equal amounts (1.5 μg) of RNA from each sample were used for the conversion of mRNA to complementary DNA (cDNA). cDNA libraries were constructed at an equimolar ratio for transcriptomic sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform.

## Quantification and statistical analysis

## Behavioral analysis
We analyzed bat videos and audios using FLIR Tools + (FLIR Systems, Inc., Portland, OR, USA) and Avisoft-SASLab Pro (Avisoft Bioacoustics). To quantify behavioral responses of free-flying bats, we recorded the number of bat passes, proportion of bats flying out, emergence time of bats flying out, and maximum number of echolocation pulses per pass. A bat was categorized as a “pass” when it flew toward the focal exit. The emergence time of bats flying out was quantified after correcting for sunset time per night. The maximum number of bat echolocation pulses per pass was determined by analyzing the synchronized video and audio data. The number of flights in caged bats was also obtained through video analysis. To reduce potential bias from the presence of researchers, data of the first free-flying bat and caged bats during the initial 5 min were eliminated. The size of male colony was estimated by calculating the total number of bats flying out and staying in the roost. Nightly temperature and moon phase were obtained from a meteorological station located approximately 8 km from the bat roost, using packages RNCEP48 and lunar.49 Considering that ambient temperature (ANOVA: df = 2, F = 0.30, p = 0.74) and moon phase (Kruskal-Wallis test: df = 2, χ2 = 0.36, p = 0.84) showed no significant differences across the treatments, we eliminate these variables to simplify the models.

## Transcriptomic analysis
We filtered the raw sequencing reads by removal of reads containing adaptor sequences, ambiguous "N" bases, and those with over 50% of low-quality bases. We used high-quality cleaned reads for de novo transcriptome assembly via Trinity 2.4.0.47 The assembled transcripts with a minimal length of 300 bp were used for further analyses. We applied CD-HIT v4.8.1 to minimize sequence redundancy across the transcripts.50 To characterize gene expression profiles, we aligned the clean reads to assembled transcripts using Bowtie2.60 The unigene annotation was conducted among seven databases, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), NCBI non-redundant protein sequences (Nr), NCBI nucleotide sequences (Nt), Swiss-Prot, Protein families (Pfam), and Clusters of Orthologous Groups of Proteins (COG/KOG). Considering that the reference-free transcriptome assembly may result in higher false-positive rates, we also mapped the clean reads to the reference genome of R. pusillus46 using Hisat2.51 Only the clean reads exhibiting an exact match or a single mismatch were used for annotation against the reference genome. FeatureCounts v1.5.0p3 was used to determine the number of clean reads mapped to each gene.53
We assessed expression levels (fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads; FPKM) of genes based on their transcript length and read counts mapped to the genes using RSEM52 and featureCounts v1.5.0p3.53 The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified through DESeq2 when |log2 (fold-change)| > 1.0 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05.54 We found 12–142 DEGs in the adrenal gland-kidney complexes and 13–165 DEGs in the brains between illuminated groups and dark control group using reference-free and reference-based methods (Figure S5; Tables S26, S27, S28, S29, S30, S31, S32, S33, S34, S35, S36, and S37). Given the lack of enriched pathways and GO terms related to glucocorticoid signaling and circadian entrainment based on DEGs, we used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to assess KEGG and GO enrichments via clusterProfiler.55 KEGG pathways and GO terms were considered significantly enriched when FDR < 0.05.61,62 The use of GSEA allowed for examination of subtle variation in gene expression at the biological level and reduced the likelihood of neglecting cumulative effects of gene sets on specific pathways.61

## Quantitative real-time PCR validation
We selected 12 genes expressed in the adrenal gland-kidney complexes and brains of bats to assess the reliability of the transcriptomic data through quantitative real-time PCR analysis, using GAPDH as a reference gene. These genes included ADCY8 and SERPINA6, which were negatively related to the number of bat flights. Moreover, we randomly selected ten other genes that exhibited over a two-fold change in expression levels when comparing the illuminated groups to dark control group. The gene primers (Table S38) were designed with Primer Premier 6.0. cDNAs were synthesized from RNA samples used for RNA-Seq analysis, with 1 μg of total RNA reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). PCR was conducted on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) in 20 μl reaction volume, comprising 2 μl of diluted cDNA, 10 μl of 2×UltraSYBR Mixture (Cowin Biotech Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China), 7 μl of ddH2O, and 1 μl of forward and reverse primers. PCR was achieved using the following steps: an initial pre-denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles consisting of 10 s at 95°C and 60 s at 60°C, and concluded with a melting curve analysis. The expression level of target genes was quantified relative to GAPDH using 2−ΔΔCt method.63 All the samples were amplified in triplicate, and the average values were used to calculate expression levels of target genes. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to evaluate the correlation between expression data obtained from qPCR and RNA sequencing.

## Statistical test
General and generalized linear mixed models (LMM and GLMM), combined with post-hoc Tukey’s test with Bonferroni correction, were applied to examine the effects of LED light on bat behavior using packages lme4,56 nlme,57 and multcomp.58 Each behavioral index of free-flying bats was designated as the dependent variable, with treatment type as a fixed variable, while experimental date and colony size were designated as random variables. For caged bats, the number of flights was classified as the dependent variable, with treatment type as a fixed variable, and experimental date and cage codes were classified as random variables. We employed GLMM with a negative binomial distribution to assess the effects of treatment type on the number of bat passes, maximum number of echolocation pulses per pass, and the number of bat flights, as these count variables exhibited pronounced overdispersion. GLMM with a binomial distribution were used to determine whether treatment type affected the proportion of bats flying out. LMM was employed to evaluate the impact of treatment type on bat emergence time, which was quantified as a continuous variable. Prior to fitting the LMM, bat emergence time was Box-Cox transformed to meet the normality. LMM was fitted after accounting for within-group heteroscedasticity, as the emergence time data showed unequal variances across treatment types. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to identify the key genes that were significantly associated with the number of bat flights. The negative binomial GLMM was also used to analyze the effects of key gene expressions on the number of bat flights, while accounting for experimental date and cage codes. We conducted all the statistics in R 3.6.1.
