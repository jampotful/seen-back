Caudal auricular muscle variations and the evolution of echolocation behavior in pteropodid bats

# Caudal auricular muscle variations and the evolution of echolocation behavior in pteropodid bats

## Abstract
Among bats, rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans, but not pteropodids, exhibit laryngeal echolocation. Although Rousettus has been regarded as the only pteropodid capable of echolocation using tongue clicks, recent evidence suggests that other species of pteropodids are also capable of echolocation using wing clicks. Studies on laryngeal echolocators suggest that delicate ear movements are essential for the echolocation behavior of bats and that the cervicoauricularis muscles play a critical role in such ear movements. In this study, we observed the gross anatomy of cervicoauricularis muscles in three species of pteropodids (Cynopterus sphinx, Eonycteris spelaea, and Rousettus leschenaultii) to examine whether ear muscle anatomy varies among pteropodids with different echolocation types and between pteropodids and laryngeal echolocating bats. We found that M. cervicoauricularis profundus originates from the nuchal crest in tongue-click echolocators (R. leschenaultii) and from the midline in wing-click echolocators (C. sphinx and E. spelaea). In general, tongue-click echolocation using high click rates is considered to be more sophisticated in terms of sonar performance than wing-click echolocation. M. cervicoauricularis profundus originating from the nuchal crest (CPNC) is not common in non-bat laurasiatherian mammals, but can be found in laryngeal echolocating bats. As it pulls the ear pinna caudally in the horizontal plane and increases the access to sound, CPNC found in R. leschenaultii and laryngeal echolocating bats may be a key characteristic of the sophisticated active echolocation behavior of bats.
Extant bat species are behaviorally categorized into two major types: 1) echolocating species involving members of the suborder, Yangochiroptera (yangochiropterans), and the superfamily, Rhinolophoidae (rhinolophoids), emitting calls produced by the larynx (laryngeal echolocators) and 2) non-echolocating species represented by family Pteropodidae (pteropodids), excluding genus Rousettus, which can use primitive echolocation based on “tongue clicks” (lingual echolocators) to navigate caves [11, 12, 27]. Growing evidence suggests that bats of non-lingual echolocating pteropodids, such as Eonycteris, Cynopterus, and Macroglossus, can use a primitive echolocation form based on wing clicks (wing-click echolocators) [2, 10]. Echolocation performance (inferred from click rates) decreases from Eonycteris to Cynopterus and Macroglossus [2] and appears to be higher in cave dwellers [2, 10, 14, 21]. Boonman et al. [2] reported that Rousettus aegyptiacus uses wing clicks in addition to lingual echolocation. These findings indicate the complex evolutionary history of click-based primitive echolocation in pteropodids, highlighting the necessity of evaluating the genetic and morphological basis of echolocation to understand the origin and evolution of echolocation in bats [2].
Ear motion plays a crucial role in bat echolocation behavior [15, 16, 20, 26] and differs considerably among bat species, possibly reflecting their divergent echolocation strategies. For instance, Rhinolophus (rhinolophids) and Hipposideros (hipposiderids), which are members of rhinolophoids, actively create Doppler shifts with their fast-moving pinnae to encode additional sensory information [25]. Moreover, the four cervicoauricularis muscles trigger ear movement, critically affecting their echolocation performance [17, 20, 25]. Although pteropodids are treated as echolocators, the specific roles of ear movements and basic anatomy of the cervicoauricularis muscles in pteropodids remain largely unexplored. Chi et al. [5] reported that the greater short-nosed fruit bat (Cynopterus sphinx) has three cervicoauricularis muscles similar to other laurasiatherian mammals but different from rhinolophoid bats with four muscles. Whether this divergence in cervicoauricularis muscles between rhinolophoids (laryngeal echolocators) and Cynopterus (non-laryngeal echolocator) are associated with different echolocation strategies and/or performance remains unclear [5]. Furthermore, how ear muscle anatomy varies among pteropodids with different echolocation performances remains unknown.
In this study, we compared the number and attachment of the cervicoauricularis muscles among three pteropodid species: C. sphinx, Eonycteris spelaea (cave nectar bat), and R. leschenaultii (Leschenault’s rousette). Cynopterus sphinx is a frugivorous bat common in tropical forests and areas where fruit crops are cultivated [21]. Rousettus leschenaultii is a frugivorous cave dweller that uses tongue and wing-click echolocation [23]. Eonycteris spelaea is a nectarivorous cave dweller that uses wing-click echolocation and travels long distances to feed on nectar and fruits [10, 13, 18, 22]. Here, we highlighted the diversity of muscle patterns among pteropodids with different echolocation types and discussed the possible association between variations in caudal auricular muscles and the echolocation behavior of bats.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
Chi et al. [5] reported the 3D reconstruction and macroscopic dissection techniques of the cervicoauricularis muscles of C. sphinx. In this study, we used the muscle nomenclature proposed by Chi et al. [5]. M. cervicoscutularis, which attaches to the scutiform cartilage, was omitted from this study because of its negligible role in the ear movements of bats [19, 20]. We studied the caudal auricular muscles directly attached to the caudal part of the pinna (M. cervicoauricularis) in the adult greater short-nosed fruit bat (C. sphinx), adult cave nectar bat (E. spelaea), and adult Leschenault’s rousette (R. leschenaultii) curated at the Institute of Ecology and Biological Resources, Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology (Hanoi, Vietnam). Bats were obtained with the relevant capture permit and ethical approval from the Bac Kan Provincial People’s Committee (dispatch no. 7796/UBND-VXNV dated November 21, 2022) and Institute of Ecology and Biological Resource (dispatch no. 1676/STTNSV dated November 8, 2022). Bats were euthanized by inhaling 5% isoflurane, fixed, and preserved in 70% ethanol. One sample was used for scanning, and an additional sample was dissected and observed macroscopically for each species. The maturity of the samples was confirmed by the presence of fully erupted and occluded dentition. Image enhancement techniques adopted from a previous study [9] were used to visualize the muscular anatomy of the bats. Samples were stained with 1% iodine in 100% ethanol for 14 d, followed by scanning using a micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system (inspeXio SMX-90CT Plus; Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) with a 90 KV source voltage and 100 μA source current. Voxel size of the image was 39 μm for C. sphinx, 18 μm for E. spelaea, and 20 μm for R. leschenaultii. We manually segmented and reconstructed the micro-CT scans using Amira 5.2 (Visage Imaging, San Diego, CA, USA). Additional adult samples of E. spelaea and R. leschenaultii were macroscopically dissected, fixed in Carnoy’s solution, and preserved in 70% ethanol to confirm their 3D reconstructions.

## M. cervicoauricularis superficialis
Eonycteris spelaea and R. leschenaultii had M. cervicoauricularissuperficialis as the most rostral muscle, which originated from the midline of the skull and inserted into the upper dorsal part of the ear pinna (Figs. 1A and 2A). In contrast, M. cervicoauricularissuperficialis of C. sphinx was wider than that of E. spelaea and R. leschenaultii, originated from M. cervicoauricularis medius, and inserted into the upper dorsal part of the ear pinna (Fig. 3A).

## M. cervicoauricularis medius
In E. spelaea and R. leschenaultii, M. cervicoauricularis medius originated from the midline of the skull adjacent to M. cervicoauricularis superficialis and inserted into the middle dorsal part of the ear pinna (Figs. 1A and 2A). In C. sphinx, the muscle originated from the midline of the skull beneath M. cervicoauricularis superficialis (Fig. 3A).

In E. spelaea, M. cervicoauricularis profundus, the most caudal muscle, originated from the dorsal side, slightly lateral to the midline of the skull adjacent to M. cervicoauricularis medius, and inserted into the lower medial part of the ear pinna (Figs. 1A and 4AFig. 4.Comparison of M. cervicoauricularis profundus topologies in left lateral (left column) and dorsal (right column) views. ## DISCUSSION
In this study, we found that E. spelaea, R. leschenaultii, and C. sphinx have three components in their cervicoauricularis muscles (Figs. 1, 2, and 3). We previously reported three cervicoauricularis muscles in the common ancestor of laurasiatherians [5]. Other studies have reported four muscles in rhinolophoid bats [19, 20]. As C. sphinx and yangochiropteran bats have three muscles, we proposed that the ancestral condition of bats is three and that the four-muscle condition is only a derived condition [5]. As only one pteropodid species has been studied so far [5], further investigation of other pteropodids is necessary to confirm this tentative hypothesis.
The three-muscle condition observed in E. spelaea and R. leschenaultii supports our hypothesis that it is the ancestral pattern of pteropodids and the entire clade of extant bats. In E. spelaea and C. sphinx, all three cervicoauricularis muscles (M. cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis medius, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus) originate from the midline (Figs. 1, 3, 4A, and 4C). However, M. cervicoauricularis profundus in R. leschenaultii does not originate from the midline but from the nuchal crest (Figs. 2 and 4B). The degree of separation of each cervicoauricularis muscle was the major difference among the three species. In E. spelaea, M. cervicoauricularis superficialis does not cover M. cervicoauricularis medius, but M. cervicoauricularis medius slightly covers the rostral region of M. cervicoauricularis profundus (Fig. 1). In contrast, the three cervicoauricularis muscles are clearly separated in R. leschenaultii (Fig. 2). Moreover, M. cervicoauricularis profundus does not originate from the midline, but from the nuchal crest in R. leschenaultii (Fig. 4B). C. sphinx had a low degree of separation between each M. cervicoauricularis. Its M. cervicoauricularis superficialis covers most of the muscle bundles of M. cervicoauricularis medius and slightly covers the rostral region of M. cervicoauricularis profundus (Fig. 3). In general, separate and independent muscles allow the fine control of movements [8]. Based on click rates, Boonman et al. [2] reported that the click-based echolocation ability of Cynopterus is one of the lowest among pteropodids. Forest, where the environment is not completely dark, is the major habitat of Cynopterus [6]; therefore, members of this genus do not rely heavily on echolocation. In contrast, Eonycteris and Rousettus are cave dwellers [10, 14] flying in complete darkness, where echolocation abilities are advantageous. Therefore, separation of cervicoauricularis muscles may reflect the degree of echolocation ability, facilitating delicate and sophisticated ear movements during echolocation in bats.
M. cervicoauricularis profundus originating from the nuchal crest (CPNC) is uncommon among non-bat laurasiatherian mammals but can be found in various laryngeal echolocating bats [19, 20]. Schneider [19, 20] reported that laryngeal echolocating rhinolophoids have different numbers of cervicoauricularis muscle components compared to non-bat laurasiatherians. The most distinctive features of rhinolophoids are that their cervicoauricularis muscles have four components and that their two caudal muscles, M. cervicoauricularis profundus major and profundus minor, originate from the nuchal crest. In the greater horseshoe bat (Rhinolophus ferrumequinum), in addition to M. cervicoauricularis profundus major and profundus minor, M. cervicoauricularis superficialis minor also originates from the nuchal crest. Its most rostral cervicoauricularis muscle, M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major, originates from the midline. In the trident bat (Asellia tridens), M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major and superficialis minor originate from the midline, while M. cervicoauricularis profundus major and profundus minor originate from the nuchal crest. Mouse-eared bat (Myotis myotis), a yangochiropteran, has only three muscles (M. cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis medius, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus), as reported by Schneider [19] (based on the nomenclature proposed by Chi et al. [5]). In My. myotis, the two rostral muscles, M. cervicoauricularis superficialis and M. cervicoauricularis medius, originate from the midline, whereas the most caudal M. cervicoauricularis profundus originates from the nuchal crest. These results indicate that the most caudal and two caudal cervicoauricularis muscles originate from the nuchal crest instead of the skull midline in laryngeal echolocating bats (Rh. ferrumequinum, A. tridens, and My. myotis).
CPNC should perform differently compared to the cases originating from the midline. In general, the key role of M. cervicoauricularis is to raise the ear pinna in common laurasiatherians, such as dogs (Canis lupus), horses (Equus caballus), and cattle (Bos taurus) [3, 4, 7, 11]. However, rhinolophids and hipposiderids with two components of CPNC actively create Doppler shifts with their fast-moving pinnae to encode additional sensory information. Their cervicoauricularis muscles exercise alternating ear motions, with one ear moving rostrally and the other moving caudally [25]. CPNC pulls the ear pinna caudally in the horizontal plane. Horizontal ear movements are widely observed in bats during echolocation. Rousettus is capable of multi-angle ear movements, facilitating quick localization of the finest sounds [14]. The big brown bat (Eptesicus fuscus), a yangochiropteran, increases interpinna separation (caudal movement of the pinnae in the horizontal plane) as an echolocated target approach [24]. These reports suggest that the horizontal movement of the ear increases the access to sound information, which may be related to the active echolocation ability of the species. Phylogenetically, Eonycteris is more closely related to Rousettus than to Cynopterus [1]. However, M. cervicoauricularis profundus originates from the midline of the skull in E. spelaea and C. sphinx (Fig. 4A and 4C) but from the nuchal crest in R. leschenaultii (Fig. 4B) and other laryngeal echolocating bats (Rh. ferrumequinum, A. tridens, and My. myotis) [19, 20]. Rousettus uses tongue- and wing-click echolocation [2, 27]. Based on click rates, the click-based echolocation ability of Rousettus is arguably higher than that of Eonycteris and C. sphinx [2]. CPNC, which is independently acquired by laryngeal echolocating bats and Rousettus, may be a key characteristic of sophisticated active echolocation behavior in bats.
In this study, we confirmed that the three-muscle condition of cervicoauricularis muscles is universal in pteropodids and ancestral in extant bats. In contrast, the four-muscle condition is unique to rhinolophoids, the sister group of pteropodids, and a derived condition for bats. However, homology among the four muscles and their functional significance in echolocation remain ambiguous, highlighting the need for further embryological and anatomical studies of caudal auricular muscles in various rhinolophoids to shed light on these issues.
