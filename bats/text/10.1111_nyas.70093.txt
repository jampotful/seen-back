# Laminar Distribution of Spectrotemporal Receptive Field Subtypes in the Primary Auditory Cortex of Echolocating Bats

## Abstract
ABSTRACTThe primary auditory cortex (A1) is crucial for processing sound features and integrating multidimensional information. Evidence shows that cortical microcircuits in Layers 2/3 of A1 selectively integrate spectrotemporal information to create networks that respond to complex sounds in a species‐specific manner, but many fundamental details about this neurocomputational process remain unknown. The bat auditory system is primarily adapted to analyze the echoes of their own calls for information about the identity and location of targets. Assuming bat A1 follows typical mammalian organization, we hypothesized that compared to Layer 4, neurons in Layers 2 and 3 would exhibit greater selectivity for complex features used in biosonar target discrimination, namely rapid amplitude modulations embedded in frequency‐modulated sweeps. To test this hypothesis, we used microelectrode arrays to examine the laminar distribution of spectrotemporal receptive fields (STRFs) in A1 of adult Mexican free‐tailed bats. Layer 4 neurons displayed downward frequency‐modulated STRFs resembling biosonar pulses, while neurons in more superficial layers showed multipeaked STRFs resembling spectral echoes of preferred prey. We suggest that cortical microcircuits in bat A1 transform discrete spectral cues arriving in Layer 4 into broadband analyses of echo spectral envelope in Layers 2/3 that favor naturalistic sound features important for foraging bats.

## Introduction
A central goal of auditory neuroscience is to understand how the auditory cortex (AC) contributes to the encoding and representation of natural sounds. The primary auditory cortex (A1) encodes complex sound patterns, but questions remain about its functional organization. There is a growing consensus that acoustic information arriving in the thalamorecipient Layer 4 is integrated by microcircuits in supragranular Layers 2/3, with high specificity for behaviorally relevant stimuli like species‐specific communication sounds [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The auditory system of echolocating bats exploits time delays between outgoing pulses and returning echoes to perform target ranging [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Bats also extract information about ensonified objects from the amplitude and spectral envelope of returning echoes [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. When a call reflects off an irregular surface, overlapping echoes create a complex acoustic interference pattern unique to each target, enabling object discrimination [15, 16, 17, 18]. Although some complex response properties such as duration tuning and FM selectivity are inherited from earlier processing stages [11, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], additional features of the spectrotemporal envelope may be extracted and encoded by the canonical columnar networks of A1 [4, 5, 26, 27, 28]. If bat A1 follows the general mammalian plan, neurons in Thalamorecipient Layer 4 should prefer simpler acoustic information, while neurons in the supragranular layers should integrate more complex feature sets [4, 5, 26, 27, 28].
Spectrotemporal receptive fields (STRFs) provide a useful strategy for quantifying the complex response properties of auditory neurons [29]. STRFs are constructed by plotting spike‐triggered averages (STAs) of neural responses at a range of frequencies and poststimulus latencies, and offer a quantitative linear description of a neuron's selectivity to specific stimulus features [3]. STRFs are powerful tools for reconstructing neural circuit dynamics and have been used to explore the functional microarchitecture of the AC in many species [3, 27, 30]. STRFs show diversity in their features (e.g., number of peaks, frequency selectivity). Mammalian AC shows anatomical segregation of single‐peak and multipeak STRFs, but specific locations of these neurons vary across species [4, 5, 27, 31]. Despite the usefulness of STRFs for comparing the functional organization of the auditory system across species, no prior studies have described STRFs from the bat AC. Cortical neurons of echolocating bats show a superior auditory signal‐to‐noise ratio compared to other mammals [32], which may produce clearer STRF patterns than in other animal models. Comparing STRFs across layers of bat A1 could thus test hypotheses about hierarchical sound representation in the ascending auditory system [33] and help distinguish between conserved and derived cortical microcircuits performing similar functions in different species.
To address this, we used a dynamic moving ripple (DMR) stimulus tailored to the bat auditory system to derive STRFs at different cortical depths throughout the A1 tonotopic axis in Mexican free‐tailed bats (Tadarida brasiliensis). Based on work in other species, we predicted that neurons in Layer 4 would display relatively simple STRFs and that neurons in Layers 2/3 would show more complex STRFs. Our results supported our predictions, with many STRFs in Layers 2 and 3 featuring stacked excitatory and inhibitory receptive fields, reflecting selectivity for echo spectral glint patterns. These findings offer new insights into how mammalian cortical microcircuits may enable auditory object encoding.

## Materials and Methods

## Animals
We performed electrophysiological recordings in the A1 of seven adult Mexican free‐tailed bats. Bats were locally wild‐caught and socially housed indoors in a custom‐built vivarium (4 m wide × 8 m long × 3 m high) with a reversed light cycle (14 h on/10 h off) with the lights going off at 12:00 noon. Temperature and humidity within the vivarium were automatically regulated to simulate local natural conditions. Bats were fed ad libitum a diet of mealworms (Tenebrio larvae) supplemented with essential vitamins and minerals. All animal experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC Animal Use Protocol #2017‐0163D).

## Surgical Procedures
The surgical procedures for this study have been described previously [34]. Briefly, bats received a prophylactic injection of buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) and were anesthetized with isoflurane. Periodic tail or ear pinches were used to ensure the animals remained in a surgical plane of anesthesia. Once anesthetized, the bat was placed in a custom stereotaxic apparatus and immobilized via a custom bite bar. We applied topical bupivacaine and then reflected the skin and muscles over the skull to allow a headpost to be affixed to the skull with dental cement and tissue adhesive (Vetbond, 3M). After attaching the headpost, the skin was repositioned and sealed around the post and the animals were allowed to recover for at least 24 h. Buprenorphine and topical bupivacaine were re‐administered prior to and every 8 h and as needed to prevent the animals from regaining consciousness. On the day of experimentation, bats again received prophylactic buprenorphine and were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane to allow placement into the stereotaxic apparatus. We then performed a 0.5‐mm diameter craniotomy over the left AC while bats were still fully anesthetized with isoflurane. Animals were then allowed to awaken in the head‐fixed position, after which the experimental procedures began.

## Acoustic Stimuli
All acoustic stimuli were constructed and delivered using custom scripts and graphical user interfaces developed in MATLAB v. 2022a (MathWorks; Natick, MA, USA). Stimuli were generated at either 200 kHz (DMR) or 250 kHz (all others) using a multifunction I/O device USB‐6356 (National Instruments; Austin, TX, USA). Stimuli were amplified using a Sony STR‐DE197 audio amplifier (Inwood, KY, USA) and broadcast to bats via a calibrated Foundtek RD1.0 Ribbon Tweeter speaker (Jiaxing, Zhejiang, China) positioned 10 cm from the bats’ ears. Speaker calibration was performed using a Brüel and Kjær sound recording system (Brüel and Kjær; Naerum, Denmark) that included a ¼ in microphone (type 4135) connected to a conditioning microphone amplifier (Nexus 2690). The calibrated speaker response was flat (± 5 dB) from 10 to 80 kHz. Tone pips were used to generate tuning curves. Each tone pip was 5 ms, and pips were pseudo‐randomly varied in 5 kHz steps, while amplitudes were varied in 10 dB steps, 10 trials per combination, delivered with an interstimulus interval of 300 ms. Tones had rise/fall times of 0.3 ms and ranged in frequency from 10 to 80 kHz and in amplitude from 20 to 80 dB SPL.
To derive cortical STRFs, we employed a DMR stimulus. The dynamic ripple stimulus was a temporally varying broadband sound (5000–80,000 Hz) composed of 316 sinusoidal carriers, equally spaced on a log‐frequency axis per octave and each with a randomized phase [35]. The carrier magnitude at any time was modulated by the spectrotemporal envelope, consisting of sinusoidal amplitude peaks (ripples) on a logarithmic frequency axis (Figure 1A). The exact shape of the ripple was controlled by spectral modulation rate or ripple density (RD(t)), measured in spectral peaks per octave, and temporal modulation rate (TM(t)), measured in Hz. Both parameters varied such that at any given time, the spectrotemporal envelope of the DMR approximated a two‐dimensional (2D) sinusoidal grating given by the following formula: Ex,t=A×sin2×π×RDt+RPt Where RP(t) is the ripple phase calculated from the temporal integration of the temporal modulation TM(t). For the present study, the modulation depth, equal to 2 × A, was set to 40 dB. RD(t) and TM(t) varied randomly and independently during the stimulus, with RD(t) uniformly distributed between zero and four cycles per octave and TM(t) uniformly distributed between −40 Hz (upward sweep) and 40 Hz (downward sweep). The time variation of both parameters was gradual so that RD had a maximum rate of change of one cycle per octave per second, and the temporal modulation rate had a maximum rate of change of 3 Hz per second. As a result, the long‐term correlation structure of the DMR stimulus is nearly spectrotemporally independent, at least within the specified bounds for spectral and temporal modulation. Details regarding the DMR stimulus design and its rationale have been described previously [3, 29, 35, 36]. Our rationale for using this stimulus is that (1) the stimulus profile includes broadband, multi‐harmonic frequency modulations such as those that comprise the bats echolocation pulses, (2) DMRs have been widely applied to AC in many different species, and (3) a prior study in free‐tailed bats used a series of fixed‐rate ripple stimuli to calculate STRFs in the inferior colliculus [11]. Thus, the DMR stimulus was predicted to reliably evoke cortical neuron activity and supports comparisons between species and across successive stages of auditory processing.

Each DMR stimulus was presented continuously for 15 min, and the DMR was only run once at each electrode penetration site because of difficulties in obtaining continuous and stable recordings of single units for the full stimulus duration.

## Electrophysiological Recordings
All recordings were performed in a custom‐built sound‐attenuating room. Neural activity was recorded using silicon 16‐channel 2 × 8 acute microelectrode arrays, with 250 µm between shanks and 50 µm between contact sites (NeuroNexus; Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Electrode resistances ranged between 0.95 and 1.72 MΩ. Probes were positioned perpendicular to the pial surface based upon landmarks and stereotaxic coordinates and then inserted slowly into the brain through the intact dura mater to a depth of approximately 900 ± 50 µm at the deepest contact point using an MX7600R micromanipulator system (Siskiyou; Grants Pass, OR, USA). Activity was recorded for offline analysis using the Omniplex Neural Data Acquisition System (Plexon; Dallas, TX, USA) with a sampling rate of 40 kHz per channel at 16‐bit precision. Neural recordings were synchronized to the onset of each acoustic stimulus using a TTL pulse output to the Plexon data acquisition system passed from a National Instruments soundcard.

## Analysis
The electrophysiological recordings were analyzed offline using custom‐made MATLAB scripts and spike‐sorted to isolate single units following methods outlined by Quiroga et al. [37], and exported spike times were used for all subsequent analysis. The raw signal was digitally bandpass‐filtered offline (elliptic, second order) between 500 and 3000 Hz to obtain the spike activity. Recordings with spike amplitudes lower than four times the amplitude of the recording background noise were not included in the data analysis. Thus, the signal‐to‐noise ratio of the analyzed spikes was ≥ 12 dB. We then used a threshold of 2.5 times the standard deviation of the baseline noise for spike detection. Spike sorting was performed using the Plexon Offline Sorter. After thresholding, detected spikes were clustered in feature space based upon peak, valley, and width at half‐maximum measurements using a semiautomated PCA. Wavelet transformation and superparamagnetic clustering resulted in isolation of single‐unit extracellular potentials that matched with qualitative assessments of spike waveforms and estimates of single‐unit isolation based on spike refractory periods.

## Derivation of STRF Parameters
To derive STRFs, we followed the approach of Paninski [38], fitting a linear–nonlinear–Poisson cascade model of neural responses to our spiking data. Using 2‐ms bins, we calculated the DMR stimulus envelope at 81 frequencies equally spaced on a log scale between 5 and 80 kHz (Figure 1A). The STRF was defined as a set of linear weightings at these 81 frequencies and 26 time lags ranging from 0 to 50 ms (Figure 1B top, Figure 1C,D, blue line). The stimulus input for a given time bin was equal to the STRF‐weighted linear sum of the DMR stimulus before that time bin. The model also contained a 51‐bin‐long (100 ms) spike history filter that was triggered at each recorded spike and summed (Figure 1B bottom, Figure 1C,D, red line). The stimulus input (g) plus the spike history (h) was passed through a nonlinear rate function p(g + h) to produce a predicted spike probability for each time bin (Figure 1E). The rate function p(u) = P3 + P2 × max(0,u + P1) where P1 is a bias (negative threshold), P2 is the gain, and P3 is the background firing probability.
To derive the STRF, we fit the model to the experimental data by finding parameters that minimized the negative log‐likelihood of generating the recorded spike trains. To prevent numerical overfitting, we regularized STRF parameters by adding penalty terms that restricted the model's ability to contort itself to fit particular values. We used second‐order local smoothing for both time and frequency plus an L1 or Lasso regularization term that tends to drive parameters to 0, leading to sparse parameters fits. The minimized objective function was as follows: −∑isilogpi−pi+∑t,fλt−kt−1,f+2kt,f−kt+1,f2+λf−kt,f−1+2kt,f−kt,f+12+λ1kt,f where s is the vector representing the recorded binary spike train, p is the vector of estimated spike probabilities, and kt,f is the value of the STRF kernel at time‐frequency bin t,f. The regularization parameters λ f = 10, λ t = 10, and λ1 = 0.01 were set by hand based on an exploratory first/second half cross‐validation analysis performed on several units. The STRF structure was generally robust to changes in meta‐parameter values. Since our goal was to compare STRFs across units, we used the same values for all units in the dataset.
Spike history parameters were not subject to regularization but were reduced in dimensionality. The entire 100 ms (51 bin) spike history filter was converted to a log scale where 10 positive lobe cosine‐shaped windows were placed with 50% overlap. The vector of window values at bin centers for each window served as a basis set for the spike history, with optimization applied to the 10 basis coefficients.
Since scaling all STRF parameters is equivalent to changing the transfer function gain (P2), we fixed the gain during our gradient‐based optimization. More specifically, we used the STA of the stimulus as an initial filter and scaled that to yield a convolved filter output with a standard deviation of 1. We found the optimal fit for the three transfer function parameters P1, P2, and P3, with a fixed filter and no spike history to find the values. These served as the initial state for the full optimization of the model, including P1 and P3 (but not P2) as free parameters. After this optimization, we fixed the filter and spike history parameters and performed a final optimization of the transfer function. Since we expect the model's ability to extract STRF structure to be limited by the precision in the stimulus, we smoothed the final STRF filter with a 2D smoothing kernel that was the outer product of positive lobe cosine vectors along the time and frequency axes with frequencies of 40 Hz and 4 cycles/octave (the temporal and spectral modulation frequency cutoffs used to construct the DMR stimulus).
All optimization was performed using custom scripts written in MATLAB v. 2024b (MathWorks; Natick, MA, USA), based closely on the Nonlinear Input Model (NIM) toolbox [39]. Documentation can be found at github.com/TroyerLab/TroyerLab_LNfit.

## Analyzing the Model Fit
To maximize our sampling of the spectral and temporal modulation space, we recorded spikes in response to a single 15‐min‐long DMR stimulus. Without repeated stimuli, there is no firing rate estimate that can be compared to our model's prediction. As an alternative approach, we divided the distribution of summed input values (g + h above) and divided the data into 98 one‐percentile bins, excluding the top and bottom percentiles. For each of these bins, we computed the firing rate as the fraction of bins containing a spike and compared these to the predicted values and calculated the match between values using R 2 (Figure 1E). Across units, the R 2 typically exceeded 0.8 (mean = 0.96, Figure 1F).
The model's spike nonlinearity consists of a linear rectified component on top of a background firing rate. If the linear–nonlinear cascade model explained all spikes, then the background component should be very low. Conversely, if the STRF and spike history inputs do not capture neural responses, then the fitted nonlinearity should be dominated by the uniform background firing rate component. Therefore, for each percentile bin, we subtracted the number of spikes expected from the background firing rate from the total number of spikes predicted and averaged to get the fraction of spiking accounted for by the spike nonlinearity. For overall units, the nonlinear component accounted for an average of 61% of the spikes (Figure 1G).

## Characterizing STRF Structure
From our modeled STRFs, we measured several spectral and temporal variables. Excitatory and inhibitory regions of the STRF were counted only if their firing rate value was 3 standard deviations or greater from the mean activity, following prior work [29, 40]. Some neurons appeared to display multiple excitatory and/or inhibitory peaks (see Figure 2). Excitatory peaks were considered separate if the firing rate between them fell below 50% of the highest firing rate. Inhibitory peaks were considered separate if the firing rate between them surpassed 50% of the lowest firing rate. Peak bandwidth and integration time were calculated as the difference between the maximum and minimum frequencies and between the maximum and minimum latencies, respectively. Because bat auditory neurons show a preference for log‐linear rather than linear frequency modulation seen in naturalistic FM sweeps [41, 42], all measures related to frequency (e.g., bandwidth, Euclidean distance) were examined based on both raw frequencies and log base 2 (log2) frequencies. To ensure a more comprehensive analysis of STRF activity patterns, we also calculated bandwidth and integration time for groups of excitatory peaks and groups of inhibitory peaks (referred to here as subfields), and for the combined excitatory and inhibitory activity areas of each unit's STRF.

We measured the directional tilt (DT) of significant regions of each STRF, which represents a shift in the latency of excitation or inhibition along the frequency axis. To calculate DT for excitatory and inhibitory peaks, we selected the dominant peak in terms of STA and then recorded frequency and latency values as 2D coordinates tracing along the spectrotemporal range of the peak's most extreme STA values. We then scaled frequency and latency values by dividing them by their respective variable's standard deviation across all peaks in the data, regressed frequency onto latency for each peak, and converted regression slopes for latency into radians and then to angle proportions representing DT using the following formula: DT=2×tan−1βπ where β equals the standardized slope of regressing log2‐frequency over latency. The range of values (theoretically 0.99 to −0.99) is similar to the directional selectivity measures used in prior work [11], but with fewer assumptions about which ranges of DT are highly directionally selective. In the present analyses, negative values represent a downward direction for convenience. For subfield DT, points were plotted from one peak to another within the same type of peaks (excitatory or inhibitory) as well as the nearest and next most extreme subthreshold peaks if they appeared to continue a larger trend in the direction of peaks across the range of the STRF. Subfield DT was again calculated based on the trigonometric transformation of regression slopes across these scaled 2D STRF coordinates. Lines with a slope of approximately 0 were considered flat, while lines with an undefined slope, no discernible pattern, or a DT with an absolute value close to 1 were judged to have no tilt.
The last STRF parameter we calculated was the degree of spectrotemporal stacking (or lack thereof) between excitatory and inhibitory regions in neurons with both excitatory and inhibitory peaks. For this, we identified median STRF coordinates of each peak or subfield, standardized the values, and then calculated the standardized Euclidean distance between them using the formula: Distance=Δlatency2+Δfrequency2
Smaller inter‐subfield or inter‐peak distance numbers signified more tight interleaving of significant excitatory and inhibitory subfields of the STRF.
Many STRF parameters had similar measures across activity types (i.e., excitatory, inhibitory) and scope of measurement (i.e., peak, subfield, unit), which could be collinear with one another and needlessly inflate type I error in multiple tests. To narrow down STRF variables for subsequent analyses, we conducted chi‐square analyses between related categorical STRF variables and tested Cohen's kappa for agreement, with alpha‐correction (Benjamini–Hochberg method, p = 0.05) to adjust for the number of tests. Subfield and unit measures that agreed significantly with their respective peak measures were excluded from later analyses. Inhibitory STRF measures were also excluded from later analyses if they agreed with their excitatory counterpart measures.

## STRF Categorization and Statistical Analysis
To explore how best to categorize STRFs, we entered various combinations of STRF parameters into clustering analyses to select the best‐fitting solutions for each clustering analysis type. Different clustering algorithms can produce different solutions from the same data, so we decided that using more than one clustering method would be useful. We first used the DBSCAN function in MATLAB to perform density‐based clustering [43] with combinations of standardized continuous STRF variables. Density‐based clustering is a nonparametric method that separates clusters based on relative gaps between contiguous regions of points. This method is more tolerant of non‐normal and discrete data than traditional clustering analyses are [43]. For all density‐based cluster analyses, we based cluster estimation on Minkowski distance between clusters and set the minimum number of neighboring units for each given unit in a cluster at k = number of variables + 1. Epsilon values were chosen according to best fit based on the average distance between each point and its nearest k neighbors, namely the highest value of density‐based cluster validation (DBCV [44]). For the sake of comparison, we also analyzed the STRF parameters as categorical variables using the network‐inspired machine learning technique hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA [45]) and the structural equation modeling method latent class analysis (LCA [46]). Continuous variables were converted to categorical variables based on a median split or else another reasonable distributional cutoff (e.g., a bimodal distribution with a large gap between halves). Cutoff criteria are summarized in Table 1. HCA and LCA model fits were evaluated based on the cubic clustering criterion (CCC) and −log likelihood, respectively. All two‐predictor combinations were tested iteratively, and then the top‐scoring two‐predictor combinations were tested for improvement in the model after adding additional variables, and so on, until no improvement could be had from further additions of variables. We chose the cluster structure and variable combination that best represented the diverse range of STRFs statistically across all three analysis types. Clusters were then compared to one another in terms of STRF parameters and rostrocaudal position of their respective neurons. We started with separate multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVAs) or multivariate regression analyses predicting individual STRF properties or sets of related STRF properties from the anatomical location of neurons (standardized rostrocaudal location controlling for depth or vice versa). STRF variables showing significant individual relationships with the neurons’ position in A1 were reentered into a full MANOVA model, and all partial effects that proved both nonsignificant and low effect size were removed.

## Results

## STRFs in Bat A1 Showed High Pattern Variability
We studied the responses to pure tones (10‐ms duration) of frequencies between 10 and 80 kHz, and DMR across the rostrocaudal axis of A1 of the Mexican free‐tailed bat (Figure 2A). Data presented here are from 114 neurons recorded in A1 at depths between 52 and 853 µm, corresponding to the six cortical layers [47]. Characteristic frequencies (CF, the stimulus frequency to which the neuron was most sensitive) measured in the tuning curves obtained from the responses to the pure tones span from 15 to 80 kHz (Figure 2B). To verify the validity of STRF excitatory regions, we compared tone‐evoked tuning curves of the 114 sample neurons with their corresponding STRFs. The CF was compared with the peak frequency of the STRF, the frequency on the spectral axis of the STRF that had the highest peak STA. In almost every neuron, the CF was in close agreement with the STRF frequency obtained from modeled responses to ripple stimuli, and the two variables were highly correlated (r = 0.90, p < 0.001). Compared to STRFs derived from STAs, modeled STRF parameters showed a higher number of peaks (19.1% more excitatory peaks, t(114) = 3.02, p < 0.001; 21.3% more inhibitory peaks, t(113) = 2.79, p = 0.003) but were not different across other STRF domains. As described previously [47, 48], the A1 of Tadarida is tonotopically organized with lower frequencies represented in more rostral positions and a continuous increase in the caudal direction (Figure 2C). Figure 2D illustrates a common STRF shape obtained from a neuron recorded at 537 µm, corresponding to thalamorecipient input Layer 4 [46]. This STRF has a clear excitatory region that tilted downward in frequency, preceded by a prominent inhibitory region that was also tilted downward. Chi‐square agreement tests revealed that most STRF variables were redundant with one another across variations of the same general type of variable (i.e., excitatory vs. inhibitory vs. overall; peak vs. subfield vs. overall). The only thematically similar variables that were not redundant with one another were the number of peaks (no redundancy between excitatory peaks, inhibitory peaks, or total peaks), bandwidth (excitatory subfield bandwidth not redundant with unit bandwidth), and directional tilt (peak directional tilt not redundant with subfield directional tilt). Because of this, these variations of the variables were retained for subsequent analyses. Euclidean distance and integration time were only analyzed at the peak level, and no other inhibitory peak or subfield measures were tested in cluster analysis.

However, a consideration of the clustering results and the variety of neurons usually found in the mammalian cortex suggested that a hierarchical model with categories based on the number of excitatory peaks, log‐adjusted subfield bandwidth, and peak Euclidean distance would produce the most clearly delineated STRF groups. The resulting 10 clusters were assigned arbitrary numbers for convenience (Table 4). Clusters 1 and 2 showed multipeak, broadly tuned neurons without stacked inhibition. Most neurons (78%) in Clusters 3 and 5 had downward‐tilted and slightly overlapping excitatory and inhibitory subfields. Clusters 4 and 10 showed a pattern of tightly interleaved narrowband excitatory and inhibitory peaks, often with more than one inhibitory peak. Cluster 6 and 7 neurons had upright broadband receptive fields and usually had absent or non‐stacked inhibition. STRFs in Cluster 8 consisted of narrowband and non‐interleaved single excitatory and inhibitory peaks. Lastly, Cluster 9 consisted of narrow bandwidth STRFs with no inhibition. Combining clusters that were statistically most similar to one another in terms of the important STRF variables mentioned earlier reduced the cluster solution to 5 categories: Interleaved (29 neurons); Broad (22 neurons); Multipeak, narrow (19 neurons); Staggered (7 neurons); and Single peak, narrow (37 neurons) (Figure 5).

## Cortical Organization of STRFs
Preliminary logistic regression results predicting neuronal location from STRF variables revealed that the number of total peaks varied by cortical depth (R 2 = 0.03, χ 2(6) = 12.65, p = 0.049), while none of the STRF variables varied by rostrocaudal location. ANOVA results showed that the five clusters varied in distribution across cortical depth (R 2 = 0.08, F(4,109) = 2.47, p = 0.049). Analyzing these differences further in chi‐square contrasts by cluster between Layers 2/3 and 4, interleaved and staggered STRFs were 1.92 times more likely to be found in Layers 2 and 3 than broad STRFs (R 2 = 0.05; χ 2(1) = 3.85; p = 0.047). These findings supported our hypothesis that AC neurons perform increasingly more complex computations in more superficial layers of AC. ## Discussion
The goal of this work was to understand how auditory cortical microcircuits are functionally organized to encode complex sounds. The bat auditory system illustrates how the standard mammalian neuroarchitecture may be adapted to perform conserved functions in a specialized context, namely biosonar performance. STRFs are an excellent tool for deconstructing the functional architecture of sensory cortices, but no prior study had examined STRFs in bat AC. Filling this gap in knowledge facilitates interspecific comparisons that may reveal fundamental principles of cortical microarchitecture. Prior investigation of STRFs in bats was carried out in the inferior colliculus of the same species used here [11], which allows for complementary evaluation of how acoustic information is processed across ascending auditory stages. Furthermore, our main analyses suggest that STRFs (and by extension, neural responses to the DMR) are at least partly separable into types based on the number of excitatory peaks, frequency bandwidth, stacked inhibition, spectral directional tilt, rostrocaudal location, and cortical depth. Neurons in Layers 2 and 3 were more likely to have multiple peaks or stacked inhibition, which may represent a more fine‐tuned coactivation of excitatory and inhibitory activity necessary for detailed object processing. A downward frequency‐modulated (dFM) sweep should span both excitatory and inhibitory peaks of a stacked STRF, consequently suppressing the response to unmodulated FM echoes. Instead, these units may respond best to an amplitude‐modulated echo spectral envelope at the unit's preferred spectral modulation rate. We could not directly test this theory because it was only possible to derive STRFs post hoc. However, the overrepresentation of STRFs with multiple peaks (68%), stacked inhibition (42%), and/or narrow bandwidths (74%) in superficial layers supports the assumption that these neurons perform more complex species‐specific signal processing functions. Additionally, the presence of a small number of these complex STRF types in deeper layers may reflect either the transmission of complex tuning features from the superficial layers to deep layers or the possibility that deep cortical layers may inherit some complex response features from earlier stages in the ascending auditory system, as seen in prior work studying delay‐tuning and FM selectivity [11, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

## Spectral Interference Patterns in Biosonar Echoes
Most bats emit pulses consisting of steep dFM sweeps when performing echolocation [18, 49]. Amplitude and spectral modulations from dFM pulses deliver critical information for detecting, ranging, and classifying targets on a millisecond timescale [16, 18]. When a biosonar pulse reflects from an irregular surface, multiple overlapping echoes separated by very short time delays convolve to generate a sound percept with narrowband (< 10 kHz) spectral peaks and notches embedded within dFM. Behavioral studies have shown that bats possess the ability to gauge target shapes and textures based upon these rapid spectral envelope modulations [9, 13, 16, 17, 50, 51, 52].
Simmons et al. proposed a computational model to explain how the bat auditory system processes FM echoes, called the spectrogram correlation and transformation (SCAT) model [9, 51, 52]. The model proposes three processing blocks—cochlear, temporal, and spectral—where the final step simplifies the reconstruction of a target's shape based on the interference patterns in the echo spectral envelope. Single‐peaked dFM STRFs would likely correspond to principal neurons in the model (i.e., from thalamocortical input Layer 4), and the multipeaked STRFs could represent the final spectral block, which acts to vote (via ensemble coding) on the presence or absence of discrete glint patterns at specific delays. The higher proportion of Layers 2/3 neurons with stacked STRFs is consistent with and may represent the cellular basis for the hypothesized final stages of spectral envelope analyses in SCAT [52]. Stacked excitatory and inhibitory activity may thus play a vital role in auditory object recognition for echolocating bats.

## External Validity of the DMR Stimulus
Tonal stimuli elicited responses at less than half of recording sites, and in many instances, recordings from single channels failed to remain stable throughout the DMR stimulus. Consequently, only a subset of the sampled units produced meaningful STRFs, and thus our results are not a comprehensive survey of all neuronal subtypes in A1. The design of the DMR stimulus includes FMs that mimic the acoustic patterns of natural biosonar but may not effectively stimulate neurons tuned to other species‐relevant sound patterns like social calls and pulse‐echo pairings. These results also may not generalize to bat species that do not rely primarily on dFM, such as constant‐frequency bats. However, the DMR stimulus proved suitable for evoking consistent and stable responses from many neurons in Layer 4, and these STRFs strongly resembled STRFs previously reported in the inferior colliculus [11]. The bandwidth, temporal dynamics, and spectral motion sensitivity revealed by these STRFs matched predictions based on previous studies, indicating that the STRFs were valid.

## Multipeak STRFs
Several prior studies in other mammals have reported the presence of multipeaked tuning curves for STRFs [5, 27, 28, 29, 31, 36, 53]. Some complex STRFs contain multiple irregularly shaped excitatory and inhibitory fields, purportedly involved in processing communication calls [4, 54]. Similarly, in cats, multipeaked tuning curves were found in the dorsal fringe of A1, with a subset showing inhibitory fields separating the excitatory fields [31]. Meanwhile, in mice, guinea pigs, and marmosets, the multipeaked STRFs were found embedded within the more superficial layers (2–4) of A1 [4, 5, 27]. Here, 58% of STRFs were multipeaked or stacked, making it tempting to conclude that the prevalence of this STRF pattern reveals the ecological significance of spectral envelope coding for echolocating bats. On the other hand, one study in mice found that the number of multipeaked STRFs detected rose dramatically when switching methods from spike analyses to deriving STRFs from subthreshold postsynaptic potentials [27]. Thus, the high percentage of multipeaked STRFs reported here may reveal a difference in the cellular or synaptic transduction processes at work in bat A1, making it easier to detect multi‐frequency tuning patterns with spikes in this species.

## Statistical Considerations
Clustering results were not perfectly consistent across all three types of analyses used, especially when comparing parametric analysis with categorical variables to nonparametric analysis with continuous and count variables. This underscores the importance of using more than one appropriate type of clustering analysis for complex data like these, as different analyses may provide complementary information. It is also highly probable that the conversion of continuous variables to binary or trinary categorical variables for parametric analyses reduced the power of those analyses. Moreover, the addition of variables in clustering analyses has a sharp diminishing of returns in improvement of model fit because additional variables in the model cause the sample to be divided into more clusters. The number of clusters is thus bounded by the number of subjects in the sample. Larger sample sizes (>3000) could help us circumvent some of these issues, but it was not feasible to sample from that many neurons in the present study. Future studies may benefit from using larger samples or more sophisticated methods to analyze STRF features.

## Conclusion
This study provided a survey of common STRF categories displayed by neurons at different layers of an FM bat's primary AC. The abundance of neurons with multipeaked STRFs and complex inhibition patterns in the superficial cortical layers supports predictions of how the bat auditory system encodes and analyzes behaviorally relevant biosonar echoes while navigating and foraging for flying insects. Importantly, each of the STRF types described here has previously been seen in other mammals, which reinforces that the bat AC, while specialized, still closely follows the standard mammalian plan. The primary differences appear to be the extent of FM filtering already present in thalamorecipient neurons and the higher prevalence of multipeaked tuning curves in the supragranular layers compared to other mammals (although see [27]). Based on these observations, the bat auditory system may have required only modest adaptations and neuroanatomical reorganizations in support of their echolocation and pursuit of prey.
