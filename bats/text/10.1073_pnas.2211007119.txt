Sustainable pest control inspired by prey-predator ultrasound interactions.

# Sustainable pest control inspired by prey–predator ultrasound interactions

## Abstract
SignificanceGlobal warming probably increases occurrence and migration levels of agricultural pests (e.g., armyworms and desert locusts), which are typically regulated with chemical insecticides; however, minimizing the unwanted effects of such chemicals on nontarget organisms and the environment is a priority for sustainable crop production. Here, we show the implementation of synthetic ultrasounds in plant protection against moth pests, which have evolved ultrasound-sensitive ears and evade predatory bats that emit ultrasonic echolocation calls. The intrusion of eared moths into crop-producing fields was suppressed where ultrasound-equipment repelling major moth pests without leading to auditory adaptation was active. Synthetic ultrasounds reduce the need to apply insecticides to target moths, which contribute to mitigate damage to agroecosystems and development of insecticide-resistant pests.
“Like moths to a flame,” many nocturnal flying insects are attracted to streetlights at night in summer. Large numbers of these insects are consumed by predacious bats that generate ultrasounds to hunt via echolocation. Insectivorous bats locate insect prey using ultrasonic echoes that bounce from their targets, which they pursue and capture with great accuracy (1). An echolocating bat can consume insects at a rate of >25% their own body weight in one night (2). For example, when a single moth-eating bat, such as the eastern bent-wing bat (Miniopterus fuliginosus; weighing 13 g) or the greater horseshoe bat (Rhinolophus ferrumequinum; weighing 24 g), hunts adult moths weighing ∼0.1 g, it is assumed that they can consume around 30 to 60 or more moths per night. Therefore, crop damage caused by the larvae of moths is expected to be reduced when bats feed on moth pests in such large quantities (3). Consequently, bats clearly provide an important agroecological service; in the United States, the annual economic benefit of insectivorous bats is estimated to be more than US$3.7 billion (4).
For eared insects, however, ultrasound can act as a cue with which to perceive the presence/approach of bats and thereby avoid predation. About 85% of the ∼115,000 currently described species of macromoths have ears that consist of tympanal organs or a similar structure (5, 6). Such moths, including agricultural pests (e.g., noctuid, erebid, geometrid, pyralid, and crambid moths), can detect the ultrasounds emitted by bats. Fossil analysis and recent molecular biological techniques indicate that ultrasound-emitting insectivorous bats emerged on Earth about 50 to 65 million years ago (7). Although most ancestors of the eared moths had evolved hearing ability to possibly detect environment noise before the emergence of bats (6), their evasive reactions to ultrasound are likely to have been driven by strong predation pressure from echolocating bats (7).
Noctuoid moths, excluding Notodontidae, have two auditory cells in each tympanal organ (mainly composed of a thin membrane and air sacs) situated behind a depression in the cuticle near the base of the hindwing (Fig. 1A). When they detect the ultrasounds of bats, flying moths flee from the sound source by suddenly flying in a looping or spiraling pattern or by ending their flight and falling/diving to the ground, whereas resting moths become motionless. Because these bat-avoidance responses can also be induced by synthetic ultrasound, emitting such ultrasound is considered a useful technique for preventing moth pests from flying into agricultural farms and consequently for protecting crops (8–10). Since the 1960s, controlling agricultural pests, such as the tobacco moth, cabbage looper moth, Indian meal moth, and corn borer moth, with man-made ultrasonic pulses has been tested in fields (9, 11–13). However, the use of ultrasound, which reaches into narrower areas because of higher atmospheric attenuation and directionality when compared with sounds that are audible to humans in the air, is not yet considered suitable in large open fields. In addition, because only small numbers of the temporal parameters of ultrasonic pulses have been tested limitedly in the past studies, the efficacy of this method for reducing crop damage has yet to be stably and robustly demonstrated in field trials. Thus, the key structures of ultrasounds for repelling moth pests have still been uncertain for over 60 y.

Moths from the genus Spodoptera (Noctuidae) are known as seriously harmful agricultural pests because they have extended emergence areas, emergence periods, and migration distances, as well as a large number of host crop species. As to the fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda) that is one of the most destructive agricultural pests in the world, the annual economic cost is estimated to be more than US$6.2 billion for maize production in only Africa (14). As well as S. frugiperda, some other species of Spodoptera moths have been known to have critical negative impacts on crop yields. In Asia the common cutworm (Spodoptera litura), beet armyworm (Spodoptera exigua), and fall armyworm (S. frugiperda) are broadly distributed and monitored as notable pests (with the latter species added to the list in 2018). These Spodoptera species see to migrate to Japan from mainland China and the surrounding regions every year, and their abundance is particularly high after midsummer (15, 16). Although most cultivated vegetables are treated with chemical insecticides that decrease pest larvae numbers and reduce crop damage, the development of alternative plant protection methods is required to mitigate the negative impact of insecticides on the environment and to prevent accelerated acquisition of insecticide resistance in some pest populations. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to develop a behavioral regulation technique using synthetic ultrasounds to suppress the intrusion of Spodoptera moths into crop fields and thereby protect agricultural products from Spodoptera larvae.

## Results and Discussion
First, we determined auditory thresholds of which criteria are the minimum sound pressure causing one or two neural spikes from the most sensitive auditory neuron, A1 cell. Extracellular recordings of auditory nerves showed that S. litura and S. exigua have the lowest auditory thresholds of 37.1 dB peSPL (peak equivalent sound pressure level in decibels; relative to (re.). 20 µPa) at 24.2 kHz (3 dB bandwidth, Q3 dB = 16.7 to 39.3 kHz) and 39.3 dB peSPL at 27.3 kHz (Q3 dB = 20.9 to 35.0 kHz), respectively (Fig. 1B). To cover auditory tuning curves, including the different sensitive frequencies among moth species, we used broadband ultrasounds, i.e., ultrasonic white noise with a 10-dB bandwidth of 10 to 70 kHz (Fig. 1C). The hearing thresholds to ultrasonic white noise did not differ between the two tested Spodoptera species (likelihood ratio test [LRT] in a generalized linear model (GLM) with gamma error distribution, n = 8 for S. litura and n = 9 for S. exigua, χ21 = 0.014, P = 0.11) and the values were comparable to those of pure tone pulses (Fig. 1D).
In subsequent tethered-flight experiments, we specified the temporal parameters, i.e., pulse duration and pulse repetition rate (the number of pulses per second), and sound pressure levels of the ultrasonic white noises that cause frequent cessation of wing flapping (flight stop) in S. litura, S. exigua, and S. frugiperda. For each of these species, occurrence and type of evasive responses were different between temporal parameters (LRT in cumulative link mixed models [CLMMs], n = 40 for S. litura, n = 13 for S. exigua, and n = 12 for S. frugiperda, LR statistics = 109.16 to 224.71, P < 0.0001). Ultrasounds with a pulse duration of 5 ms and a 10-Hz cycle as the repetition rate (10 pulses per second), which were presented at 100 dB peSPL, ended the flight of most individual moths under tethered conditions (percentage of flights ended: 79.2% in S. litura, 84.6% in S. exigua, and 83.3% in S. frugiperda; Fig. 2 A–C and Movie S1 and S2). Further screening of the time domain for evasive ultrasounds showed that a 6-ms pulse duration with a 10-Hz cycle was most effective at stopping wing flapping in S. litura (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). These ultrasonic pulses made Spodoptera moths initiate evasive action in which they turned away when the sound stimuli were broadcast at >58 dB peSPL at the moth’s tympanic membrane position (Fig. 2 D–F). Compared with the temporal characteristics of echolocation calls from a dominant insectivorous bat, namely the Japanese house bat (Pipistrellus abramus; Chiroptera, Vespertilionoidea), which is distributed in most agricultural areas of Japan (17), the acoustic parameters of the repellent ultrasonic pulse specified in this study, i.e., a 6-ms pulse duration with a 10-Hz cycle, were approximately included in the search phase of the bat calls (n = 12 call sequences; Fig. 2G). Eared insects in their natural habitats would most often be encountered by bats during the search phase of their echolocation calls. Therefore, the finding that the most repellent ultrasound for S. litura corresponds to echolocation calls emitted by bats in search mode suggests the possible outcome of adaptation under natural selection that has increased the survival rate of insect prey against bat predators (18, 19).

The repulsive effects of synthetic ultrasounds used for pest control are presumed to be affected by auditory adaptation in eared pests (20). In moths undergoing auditory adaptation to a single intense sound pulse (including tone) with a long duration or to consecutive intense sound pulses with a high repetition rate, the neural spike periods between the onset of a spike to the sound stimulus in the auditory A1 cell and that of the following spike have been found to lengthen within a short period (<100 ms), i.e., the spike period of the adapting A1 cell exceeds 2.6 ms (21–24). Here, for S. litura (Fig. 3) and S. exigua (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), ultrasonic white noise with a 6-ms pulse duration presented with a 10-Hz cycle at 100 dB peSPL for 2 s seemed not to cause auditory adaptation in the moths. Spike latencies (Fig. 3 A and D), the number of spikes per pulse stimulus (Fig. 3 B and E), and spike periods (Fig. 3 C and F) in the A1 and A2 cells of S. litura did not differ between the 1st, 5th, 10th, 15th, and 20th pulse stimuli (LRT in generalized additive mixed models [GAMMs] with gamma error distributions for spike latency and period and Poisson error distribution for spike number, n = 9 for A1 spike and n = 9 for A2 spike, χ22 = 0.93 to 4.32, P = 0.12 to 0.63). Auditory responses in S. exigua were similar to those of S. litura (SI Appendix, Fig. S2; LRT in generalized linear mixed models [GLMMs] with gamma error distributions for spike latency and period and Poisson error distribution for spike number, n = 6 for A1 spike and n = 6 for A2 spike, χ21 = 0.0019 to 1.86, P = 0.17 to 0.97). In these species, the mean spike periods in both auditory cells for the 1st to 20th pulse stimuli, i.e., the time between an elicited spike and the following spike, never exceeded 2.6 ms (Fig. 3 C and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and F), indicating that the auditory cells did not adapt to the experimental ultrasonic pulses for at least 2 s.

Preventing gravid female moths from intruding into cultivated fields for oviposition would be one of the useful applications of ultrasound technology in agricultural pest control. In indoor flight-tunnel experiments conducted for 10 min, the orientations toward host plants of mated S. litura (Fig. 4A), S. exigua (Fig. 4B), and S. frugiperda (Fig. 4C) females were significantly suppressed by ultrasonic white noise with a 6-ms duration and 10-Hz cycle (P < 0.0072). Because this suppression of orientation lasted for 10 min at minimum, the ultrasonic pulse tested here seems to cause no or little behavioral habituation in Spodoptera moths.

Next, we conducted field tests using ultrasound emitters capable of delivering the repulsive broadband ultrasonic pulses for the purpose of plant protection. In these field tests, we used a custom-made of ultrasound generator with organic flexible piezoelectric film, which is lightweight and has an easily modified shape (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). This ultrasound emitter can efficiently propagate ultrasounds with frequency components consisting of a broad bandwidth applying 40 V via an amplifier: 20.6 to 51.2 kHz at >90 dB peSPL for 1 m (with most energy at 97.7 dB peSPL and 30.2 kHz) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Due to the shape of the film wrapped around the cylindrical case with 34-mm outer diameter, the emitter can generate ultrasounds in horizontal and vertical directions, at 360° around the central axis of the cylinder-like ultrasound emitter (the horizontal azimuth and vertical elevation of >90 dB peSPL were ±80° and −10° to 20° from the film surface, respectively; SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D). An example of the installation of emitters and the propagation of ultrasounds in a cultivation facility with a single house parallel to each other is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4. Ultrasounds at ≥60 dB peSPL roughly travel horizontally to cover a circle with a radius of 24.2 m at maximum around the emitter. For a flying noctuid moth, the rate of escape elicited by ultrasonic pulses presented at 58 and 64 dB peSPL was >54.5% and 100%, respectively (Fig. 2 D–F). It means that four ultrasonic emitters are sufficient to cover a 50-m × 50-m field although a single amplifier (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A, Top) can connect to up to eight emitters (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A, Bottom).
For S. litura, we tested the effectiveness of the ultrasonic pulses in a grower’s field consisting of five greenhouses, in which strawberries were planted every September after midsummer when feral adult moths of this species are highly abundant in the field until late autumn. At the medium height of the side windows (∼150 cm above ground level), two ultrasonic emitters were hung from pipes on the ceiling of one greenhouse at each end of the east and west sides (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). The ultrasonic emitters and device were set up shortly before planting or immediately after planting in the field, from the beginning to the end of September. Ultrasonic pulses were emitted from just before sunset to just after sunrise, which includes the active period of S. litura. Field surveys were conducted for 3 y, from 2016 to 2018, and the number of egg masses of S. litura laid in the cultivation facility was counted at 1- to 2-wk intervals in 2016, when the ultrasonic emitters were not set up, and in 2017 and 2018, under conditions where the equipment was operational. The number of egg masses decreased significantly when the ultrasonic equipment was active: 18.5 egg masses were found per 100 m2 in 28 d in 2016, whereas 0.82 and 0.23 egg masses were found in 41 d in 2017 and 35 d in 2018, respectively (Fig. 4D; P < 0.0001). Because synthetic ultrasound did not inhibit the egg-laying behavior of moths during laboratory observations, we inferred that the decreased number of egg masses in 2017 and 2018 was due to the synthetic ultrasound suppressing the flight of mated female moths into the cultivation facility. Considering the number of S. litura that occurred in the field, which was measured by counting the number of male moths captured using sex pheromone traps in a nearby area (where the ultrasound from the emitters was not active), we could attribute the low number of egg masses in the surveyed fields in 2017 and 2018 to the effects of synthetic ultrasound (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B; P < 0.0001). During the survey period in these field trials, the farmer applied an insecticide for protecting the strawberries against S. litura larvae hatched from a few egg masses only once (chlorantraniliprole) in the ultrasound-treated field. In the control field where the ultrasound equipment was not installed, and all other parameters except the number of insecticide applications against S. litura were the same as the ultrasound-treated field, however, the farmer needed to use insecticides against S. litura three or four times (chlorantraniliprole, emamectin benzoate, flubendiamide, lufenuron, or pyridalyl). Because the occurrences of other pests, such as spider mites (Tetranychus urticae) and thrips (Frankliniella intonsa), were not reduced by ultrasound, the farmer was required to apply pesticides (e.g., acequinocyl, bifenazate, cyenopyrafen, fenpyroximate, flonicamid, milbemectin, pyrifluquinazon, spinosad, and spirotetramat) to decrease these pests. We observed no disadvantageous effects on the pollination efficacy of honey bees or the growth of strawberry flowers and fruits in the ultrasound-treated field compared to the control field.
For S. exigua, we set a pair of ultrasound emitters on steel rods inserted obliquely into the ground in four corners of an agricultural open field containing Japanese bunching onion (Welsh onion) in late July 2019, 2 wk after plantation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D). To propagate ultrasonic pulses diagonally above the ground where mated female S. exigua moths might fly over the field, two ultrasound emitters were combined at an angle of 30°. In this field trial, the number of S. exigua egg masses was assessed at weekly intervals in September and October in two closely situated Japanese bunching onion fields with and without the ultrasound equipment running during the night (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). The number of egg masses found on Japanese bunching onion leaves in the control field without ultrasound was significantly higher than that in the field with running ultrasound equipment: 176.5 egg masses were found per 100 m2 in 36 d in the control field, whereas 49.7 egg masses were found in the field with running ultrasound equipment (Fig. 4E; P < 0.0001). Similar experiments were performed in long green onion fields in summer and autumn 2019. In summer, 3.1 to 21.6% of long green onions were infested with S. exigua larvae in the control fields; however, damaged plants were not detected in the field with active ultrasound equipment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In autumn, the infestation rate in the control fields was 48.0 to 71.2%, whereas the infestation rate in the field with active ultrasound was <12.0% (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Thus, the synthetic ultrasounds significantly suppressed the infestation of long green onions by S. exigua larvae (P = 0.00066). Consequently, in the field with active ultrasound, the farmer did not apply any insecticide against S. exigua in summer and did apply an insecticide only once (spinetoram) to mitigate the leaf damage caused by S. exigua larvae in autumn; however, the farmer in the control field required three insecticide applications (tolfenpyrad [twice] and cypermethrin) in summer and nine insecticide applications (methomyl [twice], chlorantraniliprole, cypermethrin, metaflumizone, pyridalyl, phenthoate, spinetoram, and spinosad) in autumn. In both fields of Japanese bunching onion and long green onion, synthetic ultrasound did not reduce infestation by thrips (Thrips tabaci) and leafminer flies (Liriomyza chinensis): The farmers needed to apply insecticides (e.g., flonicamid, flometoquin, pyrifluquinazon, spinetoram, and spinosad) against these pests.
Applying physical plant-protection strategies against insect pests, such as the use of ultrasound technology, could notably decrease the risk of pests acquiring insecticide resistance because the use of insecticides could be reduced. Thus, the use of such methods is expected to contribute to sustainable agriculture, including environmental conservation and the preservation of natural enemies such as beneficial predatory bugs, although the potential negative effects caused by ultrasound to natural enemies were not determined in the present study (25). The use of ultrasound as an antipest technique exploits the defense strategy of eared moths against echolocating bats. Therefore, moths are not likely to distinguish the synthetic ultrasonic pulses from bat echolocation calls in both the short and long term because of their need to avoid actual hunting bats. Even if these ultrasound treatments represent potential anthropogenic noise pollution to echolocating bats in fields where ultrasound equipment is running (26), such bats would hunt flying insects at the periphery of the noisy area, which suggests that the ultrasound equipment and bat predators would be compatible and combine to provide improved plant protection.

## Materials and Methods

## Insects.
In this study, we focused on three Spodoptera species, the common cutworm (S. litura), the beet armyworm (S. exigua), and the fall armyworm (S. frugiperda). Laboratory strains were established from wild populations: for S. litura, egg masses and larvae were collected from taro and strawberry leaves at Nankoku, Kochi, Japan and Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan in August and September 2016 to 2018; for S. exigua, egg masses and larvae were collected from the Japanese bunching onion leaves at Kumagaya, Saitama, Japan and Matsudo, Chiba, Japan in September and October 2017 to 2019; for S. frugiperda, larvae were collected from dent corn and sweetcorn leaves at Koshi, Kumamoto, Japan and Itoman, Okinawa, Japan in March–June 2020. Larvae derived from gravid females were reared on artificial diets (Insecta LFM and Insecta LFS; Nosan Corp., Kanagawa, Japan) and kept at a constant temperature of 24 °C under a 16:8 h light:dark cycle until they were used in the experiment. Male and female moths were sexed on the day of emergence (0 d old) and housed in 30-cm × 30-cm × 30-cm nylon mesh cages with a 10% honey solution supply. We used 2- to 4-d-old moths, which were wild-caught insects and their offspring from the first to fifth generations, to avoid colony artifact and inbreeding influence. Ethical approval was not required for this study.

## Auditory Threshold.
Auditory threshold curves for sound frequency in S. litura and S. exigua were produced by recording their auditory nerve responses to pure-tone pulses at 5 to 100 kHz. Following the procedure described in our previous studies (19, 27), the moths were placed ventral-side up on a block of dental wax to enable dissection of the right meso- and metathoracic coxas and to record the extracellular action potentials from nerve branch IIIN1b, which contains the axons of two auditory cells, A1 and A2 cells, in each tympanal organ. A PFA-coated silver wire recording electrode (#785500 [bare diameter; 76.2 µm]; AM Systems) was placed on the nerve branch, and an indifferent reference electrode was inserted into the metathorax. Neural signals were amplified (Neuron SpikerBox; Backyard Brains) and digitized [USB-1604HS A/D D/A converter (320-kHz sampling, 16 bits); Measurement Computing) using the software DASYLab Basic version 12.0 (Measurement Computing) after eliminating 50-Hz noise (HumBug Noise Eliminator; Digitimer Ltd.).
An electrostatic speaker (ES1 speaker connected to an ED1 speaker driver; Tucker-Davis Technologies) was placed 10 cm from the moth, at the same height, facing the right tympanic membrane. Pure tones were presented through the A/D D/A converter (320 kHz sampling). The pulse duration was 20 ms (1-ms rise/fall time), delivered at two pulses per second (= 480-ms interpulse interval) and with amplitude varying in 1-dB increments. The sound frequency of the pure tone pulse was randomly chosen in 5-kHz steps. Sound stimuli were recorded with a microphone (type 4939 1/4-inch microphone [grid off] connected to a type 2670 preamplifier and type 2690 Nexus conditioning amplifier [20 Hz to 140 kHz band-pass filter]; Brüel & Kjaer) placed <1 mm from the moth’s left ear and simultaneously digitized with the nerve responses. All the electrophysiological recordings were run on the vibration isolator (DT-4048M-C; Herz Co., Ltd.) in a Faraday cage (100 × 70 × 70 cm) with sound-absorbing acoustic foam (SONEX; Pinta Acoustic, Inc.) under rearing conditions.
Sound and neural data were saved as separate .asc files on a laptop computer, subsequently converted to a single stereo .wav file, and then analyzed using BatSound 4.03 software (Pettersson Elektronik). We examined the minimum sound level measured as the dB peSPL (re. 20 µPa) (28), with reference to a known peak signal voltage from a sound calibrator (type 4231 [94 dB SPL at 1 kHz]; Brüel & Kjaer) of a pulse stimulus that evoked one to two action potentials in the most sensitive auditory cell (i.e., the A1 cell). We statistically derived the mean and 95% confidence intervals of the auditory threshold curves using a GAMM with a random effect of individual ID, which was run in the “gamm4” package (29) in R 4.0.3 (30).
To determine the auditory thresholds to ultrasonic white noise (Fig. 1C) with 20-ms duration pulse delivered at 2 Hz cycle in S. litura and S. exigua, we recorded neural signals using the same procedures described above. We compared the thresholds to ultrasonic white noise in the two Spodoptera species by using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) in a generalized linear model (GLM) with Gamma error distribution in R.

## Auditory Adaptation.
To test whether pulsed ultrasonic white noise causes auditory adaptation in S. litura and S. exigua, we presented the moths with an ultrasonic noise had a 6-ms pulse-duration with a 10-Hz cycle on 100 dB peSPL for 2 s, i.e., 20 pulses in total. The spike latencies, the numbers of spikes, and spike periods of auditory A1 and A2 cells were then measured and compared between the first, fifth,10th, 15th, and 20th pulse stimuli for S. litura and between the first and 20th pulse stimuli for S. exigua.
In statistical analyses, we calculated the mean and 95% confidence interval using the GAMM (Gamma error distribution for spike latency and spike period; Poisson error distribution for spike numbers) with a random effect of individual identification number (ID) for S. litura. For S. exigua, we derived the mean and 95% confidence interval using a GLMM (gamma error distribution for spike latency and spike period; Poisson error distribution for spike numbers) with a random effect of individual ID, which was run in the “lme4” package (31) in R.

## Behavioral Screening of Repellent Ultrasonic Pulses.
We examined the incidence and mode of avoidance maneuvers exhibited by the three Spodoptera species in response to ultrasound stimuli (see below). For individual moths, a tiny grabber-type IC clip (C-126 B; Teishin Electric Corp.) was used to pinch their dorsal surface cuticle at the second or third abdominal segment, and they were suspended from a metal rod attached to a magnet stand (model ML-MX; Lightest Corp.) in the soundproof Faraday cage. These tethered moths generally started to fly when a small piece of paper that served as a platform was removed from beneath their legs or a subtle air puff was directed toward moths without a platform. Moths that did not exhibit continuous flight were removed from the experiment. Immediately after stable flight lasting for at least 5 s had been confirmed, the ultrasound stimuli were presented to each tethered flying moth in random order, with different stimuli being broadcast to the same moth at approximately 5-min intervals to avoid behavioral fatigue. Moth behavior was then directly observed or recorded using a digital high-definition camcorder in infrared recording mode (HDR-PJ760V; Sony). All behavioral experiments were conducted in the dark phase under a dim red light, and the behavioral responses that were observed during the stimulus presentation were analyzed blindly.
To determine the pulse structure of ultrasounds that cause evasive maneuvers in the three Spodoptera species, we synthesized 35 types of sound stimuli using Audacity 12.1.0 software (192-kHz sampling, 16 bits, .wav file format; Audacity Team). These pulses were composed of ultrasonic white noise (Fig. 1C) and included temporal parameters that are similar to those of echolocation calls generated by insectivorous bats of low- and high-duty cycle species (Fig. 2G) (18). The temporal structure of the 35-type stimuli was 34-type pulses, which were combined into 1-, 5-, 10-, 20-, 40-, 80-, and 160-ms duration pulses with 1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 pulse-repetition rates (pulses/s) and one continuous tone. Each ultrasound stimulus was broadcast for 2 s at 100 dB peSPL from the electrostatic speaker (ES1 speaker connected to an ED1 speaker driver) positioned 10 cm away from a tethered flying moth. The stimuli sound levels were confirmed with the recording system (type 4939 microphone [grid off] connected to a type 2670 preamplifier and type 2690 Nexus conditioning amplifier) by referring to a known signal voltage from the sound calibrator (type 4231).
For S. litura, we further analyzed behavioral responses to additional 34-type pulses, which were combinations of 1- to 10-ms duration pulses and 5.6 to 50.0 pulse-repetition rates. These pulses showed temporal structures close to those of the most repellent pulse (Fig. 2A); we held one parameter out of the three parameters, i.e., 5-ms duration pulse, 10-Hz cycle, and 95-ms interpulse interval.
The rate of occurrence of evasive responses under the tethered condition was analyzed using a LRT in CLMMs, which were run in the “ordinal” package (32) in R. In these models, three representative escape behaviors of the flying insects to echolocating bats (33–35) were included as an ordered response variable [“no response” = 0, “turning-away response” = 1 (Movie S1), and “flight-stop response” = 2 (Movie S2)], whereas moth individual ID was included as a random effect. The 35-type stimuli or 34-type pulses and genders were incorporated into the statistical models as fixed effects. The deviance between the full model and each model that lacked one of the explanatory variables outlined above was analyzed.
To determine the stimulus sound levels that cause evasive behaviors (turning-away and flight-stop responses) in the three Spodoptera species, we broadcast 6-ms duration pulses at a 10-Hz cycle (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) for 2 s to a tethered moth. Different sound levels of 10 type ranging from 46 to 100 dB peSPL were presented with 6-dB steps incrementally. The experimental procedures were the same as those described for the behavioral tests where the 35-type stimuli were used. Statistical analysis was also conducted as described above, except for the fixed effects of the 10-type sound levels and genders.

## Flight Tunnel Experiment.
We examined the repellent effects of pulsed ultrasonic white noise with a 6-ms duration pulse at a 10-Hz cycle on host-finding flight in mated females of the three Spodoptera species. These orientation flights were induced by host plants, i.e., strawberry leaves for S. litura, Japanese bunching onion leaves for S. exigua, and sweetcorn leaves for S. frugiperda, placed upwind in an acrylic flight tunnel (11.5-cm internal diameter, 66-cm in length, and a wind speed of 0.25 cm/s). In the preliminary experiments, we confirmed that the first 1 h of scotophase was the timing of flights for oviposition in mated females of the three species. We used gravid females that had mated a day prior in the mesh cage. Host plants were not made available to these females before the experiment. We placed a square sticky board (10 cm × 10 cm; Sumitomo Chemical) at the most upwind part of the tunnel, with screen steel mesh fitted at each end to delimit the movement of moths and host plant leaves outside of the flight tunnel. After setting this sticky trap, we introduced mated females into the downwind end of the tunnel. The number of female moths captured on the sticky board was counted 10 min after their release. We changed the sticky board and host plant leaves for every trial.
Under conditions in which females demonstrated orientation flights toward their host plants for oviposition, we played a sound stimulus during the flight tunnel trial. As a negative control to the repellent ultrasonic pulse, we continuously presented background room noise recorded with the recording system (type 4939 microphone [grid off] connected to type 2670 preamplifier and type 2690 Nexus conditioning amplifier; USB-1604HS A/D D/A converter [192-kHz sampling, 16 bits]). At a distance of 10 cm from the ES1 speaker membrane, the room noise and ultrasonic pulse were broadcast at 22 dB peSPL (the actual sound level of the background room noise) and 100 dB peSPL, respectively. We placed the speaker at the upwind mesh screen of the tunnel and directed the speaker membrane downwind. In each trial, the stimulus was looped for 10 min. To analyze the effect of the ultrasonic pulse on the mated females’ approach to host plants, we performed LRT using a GLM with binomial error distribution in R.

## Bat Echolocation Calls.
In late May and August 2017, we manually recorded the echolocation calls of the Japanese house bat (P. abramus), which is a predominant bat species in the agricultural fields of Japan (17). Recordings were made using an ultrasonic MEMS microphone (Ultramic UM200K; DODOTORONIC, Castel Gandolfo, RM) connected to a laptop computer via USB (192-kHz sampling rate, 16 bits, .wav file format; Audacity 12.1.0). Before manually recording the calls, we monitored bat activity using a portable bat detector (D240X, Pettersson Elektronik) in heterodyne mode. We then measured the temporal characteristics of the pulse duration and interpulse interval (the duration of silence between pulses) in the search, approach, and terminal phases of the echolocation call sequences, according to a previously defined predation sequence (18). The start and end points of a pulse were determined by discriminating sounds >1 dB from the background noise using an oscillogram and spectrogram with BatSound software. We statistically derived the mean and 95% confidence intervals of the relationship between pulse duration and pulse rate using a GAMM with a random effect of individual ID in R.

## Pest Control Test in Greenhouses Cultivating Strawberry.
Strawberry is a known host plant of S. litura. In the middle of September to the beginning of November 2016 to 2018, we ran field experiments using synthetic repellent ultrasonic white noise, which was expected to suppress the intrusion of S. litura into strawberry greenhouses. We selected five greenhouses for strawberry cultivation at Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan. After a strawberry grower planted young strawberries in the greenhouses in the middle of September or later, we counted the number of egg masses of S. litura in all greenhouses every 1 or 2 wk. The ultrasound equipment (for more details, see the main text and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) was not set up in 2016, but it was set up and active in 2017 and 2018, during which time synthetic repellent ultrasonic pulses of 6 ms in duration with 10-Hz cycles were emitted by the equipment during the night (from sunset [approximately 17:00] to sunrise [approximately 5:30]). To account for the influence of the abundance of the S. litura wild population on the number of egg masses counted, we compared the number of male moths caught in a pheromone trap placed in the neighboring city (Ryuugasaki, Ibaraki, Japan; 15.5 km away from the experimental greenhouses) among the 3 y. These trap data were obtained from open data at https://www.pref.ibaraki.jp/nourinsuisan/nosose/byobo/boujosidou/yosatsujoho/yosatsujoho.html (in Japanese). To analyze the effect of the repellent ultrasonic pulse on the number of egg masses in the greenhouses with and without normalization based on the population abundance, we conducted LR tests using a GAMM with Poisson error distribution in R.

## Pest Control Test in Open Fields Cultivating Green Onion.
Larvae of S. exigua very often infest green onion plants, including the Japanese bunching onion and long green onion in Asia. Thus, we investigated the effect of the repellent ultrasonic pulse on the intrusion of S. exigua into open fields of Japanese bunching onion and long green onion.
In two open fields of Japanese bunching onion at Matsudo, Chiba, Japan, we counted the number of egg masses of S. exigua from early September to the middle of October 2019, during which time the occurrence of wild S. exigua moths increases. In one field, we placed the ultrasound equipment; four pairs of two ultrasound emitters combined at 30° were set on the edge of each steel rod; these were inserted into the ground at the four corners of the field (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D) 2 wk after a grower planted young green onions in the middle of July. The synthetic ultrasonic pulses were emitted during the night, and 2,120 plants of Japanese bunching onion were surveyed. As a control, the ultrasound equipment was not set up in the other field (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), and 1,720 plants were surveyed under these conditions. To analyze the effect of the repellent ultrasonic pulse, we performed LR tests using a GAMM with Poisson error distribution in R.
For the long green onion, we set up the ultrasound equipment (two pairs of two ultrasound emitters combined at an angle of 30°; SI Appendix, Fig. S4C) in one open field (approximately 10 m × 50 m) but did not set up this equipment in two other open fields at Kyoto, Kyoto, Japan. Insecticides were applied according to the degree of damage caused by S. exigua larvae and other pests such as thrips and leafminer flies. Instead of the number of egg masses, we examined the number of plants damaged by S. exigua in each field in early July to early August 2019 and throughout September 2019 by observing 25 to 225 plants per field. Resultant data were analyzed in R using LR tests in a GAMM with a binomial error distribution; data were pooled because of the small number of surveys in summer and autumn seasons.
