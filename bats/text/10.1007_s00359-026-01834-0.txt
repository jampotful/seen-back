# Beamforming as a mechanism for azimuthal localization of targets by FM-echolocating big brown bats

## Abstract
Echolocating big brown bats broadcast downward-sweeping, frequency-modulated (FM) biosonar sounds and process the returning echoes to track and capture flying insects. Neurons in the inferior colliculus are tuned to different frequencies along the FM sweep and form a representation of target azimuth largely on the basis of sensitivity to interaural differences in intensity, with canonical interaural time difference cues by themselves less reliable. Here, we reanalyze neurophysiological data on binaural sensitivity of inferior colliculus neurons consistent with a signal-processing model used in engineered sonar for generating directional beams. Model inputs are the times-of-occurrence of phasic-on responses to FM sweeps presented at a range of interaural intensities and azimuths. Tuning curves for interaural intensity were computed, and the slopes of these tuning curves plotted against neuronal best frequency and sound azimuth. The resulting aggregate plots suggest that the bat forms two distinct beams, one encompassing the entire FM sweep for a narrow zone of azimuths straight ahead and the other restricted to lower frequencies and offside to the left and the right. When interpreted with respect to psychophysical data, these analyses suggest that the forward beam allows for acute target perception, while the offside beams allow for perception of multiple objects that constitute clutter. The distribution of azimuths further suggests the formation of numerous detailed offside beams pointing in different directions to accommodate the width of the spatial arrangement of the clutter.

## Introduction
Localizing sound sources in azimuth is a key auditory skill that relies on comparing signals received at the left and right ears. Across various species, animals have developed a remarkable array of methods to make this comparison (Brown and May 2005; Grothe et al. 2010; Grothe and Pecka 2014). Frogs, lizards, and birds determine azimuth by use of internally-coupled ears that receive airborne sound directly on an external tympanic membrane and indirectly from within the oral cavity (van Hemmen et al. 2016). Peter Narins has been at the forefront of elucidating the operation of internally-coupled ears in frogs (Wang and Narins 1996; Ho and Narins 2006; Shen et al. 2008; Narins 2016). His work connects acoustic theory with biology, exemplifying the power of a comparative and integrative approach. Echolocation is another system where acoustic theory applies to practical biology. Echolocating bats navigate their environment using sonar, leveraging detailed knowledge of their emitted waveforms to build an internal map of nearby objects from returning echoes (Simmons 2012, 2014). Here, we suggest a new model, inspired by signal processing methods used in engineered sonar, for how bats can localize echoes in azimuth.
Following the initial experiments on the ability of bats to avoid obstacles using echolocation (Griffin 1958), subsequent studies (Griffin 1953; Griffin et al. 1960) revealed that several bat species (the big brown bat Eptesicus fuscus; the little brown bat Myotis lucifugus) also echolocate to locate and intercept flying insect prey (Grinnell et al. 2016). These experiments raised serious questions about how echoes are localized in space. Analysis of the aim of the bat’s head in relation to the location of targets from high-speed cinematography of aerial interceptions showed localization accuracy to be roughly 5º (Webster and Brazier 1965). Further experiments found that the bat’s tracking accuracy is even more precise. Psychophysical measurements with stationary big brown bats indicate an accuracy of about 1º in azimuth (Simmons et al. 1983; Masters et al. 1985) and 3–4º in elevation (Lawrence and Simmons 1982). Flying big brown bats show a tracking acuity of about 3º in azimuth (Ghose and Moss 2003). Target localization is a key aspect of echolocation as a perceptual mode, but the specific acoustic features and neural bases of this sharp behavioral tracking remain less clear.

## Directionality of echolocation
Both broadcast emission and echo reception are directional. The big brown bat emits its broadcasts through the open mouth (Fig. 1a). The broadcasts are frequency-modulated (FM), sweeping downward in two prominent harmonics—FM1 from about 50 to 23 kHz, FM2 from 100 to 45 kHz (Fig. 1b). The duration and frequency structure of broadcasts vary with the distance to the target, which thus affects the slope or rate of the FM sweeps (Surlykke and Moss 2000; Saillant et al. 2007; Hiryu et al. 2010). Not only do bats change these broadcast parameters, but also they shorten the time intervals between individual broadcasts according to target distance (Simmons et al. 1979; Surlykke and Moss 2000; Schnitzler et al. 2003; Saillant et al. 2007). The bat’s mouth produces a wide beam to ensonify the surroundings (Hartley and Suthers 1989; Ghose and Moss 2003) (Fig. 1d). The beam’s momentary width depends on frequency, which in turn depends on the high-to-low FM sweep. The emitted beam narrows continuously from covering a smaller segment of ± 25º at 80 kHz to covering nearly the entire frontal hemisphere of ± 90º at 25 kHz. In broadcasts, the occurrence of lower frequencies is several milliseconds later than the occurrence of higher frequencies, and they spread more widely to impinge on offside targets more strongly than higher frequencies. The strongest ensonification of offside targets thus occurs later than for targets located straight ahead. The dynamics of target registration across frequencies therefore include different effects for on-axis objects than offside objects. Furthermore, offside objects return proportionately weaker echoes, which affects neural registration of echo delay. The external ears (Fig. 1a) receive the echoes, but unlike symmetric broadcast beaming (Fig. 1d), the shape of each external ear creates asymmetric receiving beams pointed to the left or right (Fig. 1e). Each ear’s beams are oriented about 15–20º toward the ipsilateral side. They become narrower with increasing frequency while the beam axis shifts progressively more to the ipsilateral side (Jen and Chen 1988; Wotton et al. 1995, 1996). Aside from the two broad 25 kHz beams (red on the left, faded pink on the right in Fig. 1e), narrowing occurs mainly on the outer or ipsilateral side, while the beam aim remains much the same on the contralateral side. On the contralateral margin, the receiving beam patterns overlap, except for the 25 kHz beam, which remains wide on both sides. This lowest frequency, being both broadcast and picked up most widely, allows for echo detection regardless of target azimuth. In fact, when the bat is cruising and searching for prey in open areas, only the low frequency 25–30 kHz band is emitted. Results from jamming experiments show that big brown bats defend the narrow low frequency window for echo detection (Bates et al. 2008; Simmons 2017).

The two offset acoustic axes for directional reception at the ears (Fig. 1e) create echo interaural intensity differences (IIDs) between the two ears that can be used to determine the azimuth of a moving target. At an azimuth of 45º to one side, IIDs are 5 to 20 dB (Haresign et al. 1996). Interaural time differences (ITDs) depend on the 1.4 cm separation between the left and right ears. At an azimuth of 45º to one side, ITDs are about 35–40 µs (Haresign et al. 1996). Tracking the target by maintaining the IID and ITD near zero will keep the sonar beam locked onto the target’s azimuth as it moves relative to the bat. This process, called “monopulse” in radar and sonar (Skolnik 1980; Denny 2007), involves steering the beam toward the area in space to minimize the IID values across frequencies. It utilizes two receivers with different directional axes to decipher target direction from the echo of each broadcast (hence monopulse). (With only one receiving transducer, direction must be determined by scanning over successive broadcasts, not just one pulse.) The bat’s version of monopulse is based on the acoustic directionality of the left and right external ears, which is projected up the central auditory pathways, manifesting, for example, as directional selectivity of neurons in the inferior colliculus (IC; Fig. 1f).

## Behavioral and acoustic challenges
Video (thermal infrared) observations of big brown bats capturing insects in various environments highlight the versatility of the bat’s echolocation for target detection (Simmons et al. 2001; Simmons 2005). The bat faces two diametrically opposed challenges—finding and intercepting a target in an open space free from obstacles versus guiding flight through cluttered spaces surrounded by obstacles. The first challenge is to find, track, and capture flying insects in open spaces as well as in the presence of clutter having varying degrees of proximity to clutter. During aerial interceptions, the action usually is in an open area where the target is isolated from background objects and its echoes arrive earlier than echoes from more-distant clutter. Here, the bat aims its broadcasts at the insect, and echoes from the target are easily tracked.
The second challenge arises when big brown bats perform what amounts to the opposite task—to locate a region of space with no objects in it while in the presence of clutter immediately surrounding the open space – whether or not they are pursuing prey. A task involving flight through an opening in a net to approach and capture a mealworm on the other side shows how the bat aims its beam first at the edge of the net surrounding the opening and then at the mealworm itself (Moss and Surlykke 2010). These are not multiple scanning motions back and forth between the net and the insect, then back to the net, but focused on one part of the scene at a time. The bat’s aiming responses are precise and appear meant to isolate one element at a time in the scene. During aerial hunting in the presence of multiple flying insects, Japanese pipistrelle bats (Pipistrellus abramus) aim their sonar broadcasts at individual insects during approach, sometimes shifting their attention briefly to a different insect before returning their sonar aim to complete the ongoing interception (Fujioka et al. 2014). Detecting and paying attention to one target in the presence of multiple targets and distinguishing it from nearby objects of similar size while still being aware of the locations of these other, nonattended objects is therefore within the capabilities of FM bat echolocation.
Tests in which big brown bats fly along corridors between rows of vertically-hanging plastic chains further illustrate the problem posed by extraneous echoes (Petrites et al. 2009; Hiryu et al. 2010; Knowles et al. 2015; Wheeler et al. 2016; Simmons et al. 2020; Tuninetti et al. 2021). The rows of chains on the bat’s left and right give the bat an open path to fly along. Because both the emitting and receiving beams are broad (Fig. 1d, e), echoes from multiple chains on the left and right are received by both ears. The chains in the rows reflect numerous echoes from both sides (Fig. 2a, b) which reach the bat’s ears simultaneously from both directions. When the chains are aligned with the bat in the center of the corridor, each pair of chains—one on the left and one on the right—returns their echoes within about 150 µs of each other depending on the bat’s momentary position around the center line of the corridor. Additionally, there is not merely one echo from each chain. The individual chains are complex sources of reflections; each link reflects its own multipart echo, and multiple links within each chain lining the corridor send back multiple reflections to the flying bat (Knowles et al. 2015). If an obstacle blocks the corridor, its echoes will arrive at delays surrounded by echoes from the chains, some of which may be effectively simultaneous or overlapping with those from the obstacle. In these tests, big brown bats keep their head and sonar beam aimed along the corridor, leading the aim slightly into any upcoming turn, with variability no more than about ± 5º (Knowles et al. 2015; Wheeler et al. 2016; Tuninetti et al. 2021). They can detect and avoid a fine net blocking the chain corridor even when its echoes are 20–25 dB weaker than the echoes from the surrounding chains (Knowles et al. 2015). The sonar scene task (visualized with an acoustic beamforming camera in Fig. 2a) involves detecting whether the upcoming flight path contains no collision hazards while simultaneously following the corridor walls, even when the corridor turns or changes direction abruptly (Wheeler et al. 2016; Tuninetti et al. 2021). The corridor can be as narrow as 40 cm, similar to the bat’s wingspan, and the bat can still successfully navigate along it (Wheeler et al. 2016). Furthermore, the bat perceives adjacent chains on each side individually, as separated by a gap, because it sometimes exits the corridor by flying through the open space between two adjacent chains (Simmons et al. 2020; Tuninetti et al. 2021). Echolocation is not restricted to registering just one target out of several; it is limited to attending to one target at a time (as judged by the aim of the sonar beam), but other elements of the scene are registered well enough to drive momentary shifts in attention without having to initiate a new search for them.

## Beamforming
For efficient orientation, obstacle avoidance, and prey interception, the bat must distinguish the direction of potential echoes, whether coming from within an otherwise empty corridor, to the front because of the aim of the beams, or from the walls (off to the sides). The challenge of identifying the strategies the bat uses can be addressed by borrowing signal processing techniques for generating directional beams in engineered sonar (‘beamforming’; Johnson and Dudgeon 1992). Engineered sonar receivers often use an array of multiple transducers that face forward, capturing each echo at slightly different times based on the echo’s direction of arrival. By comparing the amplitudes, phases, or arrival times of echoes at selected pairs of transducers in the array, the direction of the target can be identified. The calculations produce a set of different potential directions, or beams, that keep echoes separated according to their direction of origin. Due to the presence of numerous transducers in the array, these beams exist simultaneously, pointing in various directions to distinguish echoes arriving from different angles, even if they have overlapping delays. Beamforming involves complex calculations that require multiple receiving elements arranged in a grid, along with carefully designed processing methods that use outputs from multiple subgroups of those receivers (Johnson and Dudgeon 1992) — not just two ears. Of course, bats have only two ears, but the need for numerous receivers can be shifted from the spatial domain, which involves amplitude, timing, and phase of signals at each array element, to the frequency domain. In the frequency domain, a different set of computations can generate multiple beams. Images produced by ensonification of a chain corridor with a beamforming camera suggest that the bat may use beamforming based on response properties of multiple, binaurally-sensitive neurons.

## Directional responses in the bat auditory system
Neurons tuned to ultrasonic frequencies that respond selectively to the azimuth of sound sources have been identified in the midbrain (IC and superior colliculus) and auditory cortex of several bat species (Grinnell 1963; Fuzessery and Pollak 1984; Wong 1984; Jen et al. 1984, 1987; Wenstrup et al. 1988; Pollak and Casseday 1989; Shen et al. 1997; Valentine and Moss 1997; Razak and Fuzessery 2000; Razak 2012; Greiter and Frizlaff 2017; Kothari et al. 2018; Wohlgemuth et al. 2016). These neural responses are affected by sound amplitude, timing, spectral shape around the tuned frequency, and interaural differences in these features. Because bats have small heads [ear-to-ear distance is 1.4 cm in big brown bats (Fig. 1a), which is only about one wavelength at the lowest frequency used for echolocation (f = 25 kHz; λ = 1.4 cm)], it has been argued that they rely more on IID cues than ITD cues for determining azimuth (Koay et al. 1998; Heffner et al. 2010).
In the first study of binaural processing in echolocating bats (the FM species M. lucifugus and Plecotus townsendii), Grinnell (1963) demonstrated that sound azimuth strongly affected the threshold of neural responses in the IC; sounds arriving from the contralateral side typically evoked stronger responses than sounds arriving from the ipsilateral side. Although both IID and ITD cues were available because sounds were presented free-field, Grinnell (1963) argued that IID cues were more important, due to the bats’ small head size. A similar pattern of directional sensitivity based on IID cues was observed in subsequent experiments with big brown bats (Jen and Sun 1984; Jen et al. 1987). Results (Fig. 1f) show that directional tuning depends in part on the directional properties of the external ears (Fig. 1e); however, the distribution of neural best azimuths is sharper than the acoustic directionality, due to excitatory and inhibitory interactions along the ascending auditory pathway (Grothe and Pecka 2014), and is concentrated on the contralateral side. This directional tuning still yields only a coarse representation of the direction of a sonar target. Moreover, the bat’s behavioral acuity is sharper still (Webster and Brazier 1965; Simmons et al. 1983; Masters et al. 1985).
Acoustic considerations suggest that ITDs, either as onset time or ongoing phase disparities, do not operate in the ultrasonic frequency range used for echolocation. Wenstrup et al. (1988) observed no neurons responsive to ITDs in the IC of the constant-frequency mustache bat (Pteronotus parnellii). Haqqee et al. (2021) recorded responses of neurons in the IC of big brown bats to short duration tones presented at different ITDs. Neurons sensitive to ITDs had lower best frequencies (mean 37.4 kHz) than those that were sensitive to IIDs (mean 43.4 kHz). Because a relatively larger proportion of neurons were sensitive to IIDs over the bat’s entire ultrasonic range, Haqqee et al. (2021) argued that ITD by itself functions only as a “weak” binaural cue for azimuthal localization.
Seminal research on the IC of Mexican freetail bats (Tadarida brasiliensis) argued that the two dimensions of IID and ITD are not entirely orthogonal but instead are linked by the critical effect of amplitude-latency trading (Pollak 1988). Amplitude-latency trading occurs when a sound decreases in amplitude and the neural responses it elicits are retarded in latency (Brugge et al. 1969). That is, changes in sound level are transformed into changes in response latency, such that responses to more intense sounds have shorter latencies. The maximum ITD in the freetail bat is only 30 µs at 90º due to the small size of its head. The concurrent IIDs depend in part on the directionality of the external ears, rising to 40 dB for an azimuth of ± 40º and then declining to 30 dB at ± 90º. The (contralateral) side of the IC receiving the more intense sound fires earlier, so that the neural latency difference adds to the acoustic time difference. The average neural time delay increases by 47 µs for every 1-dB decrease in sound intensity (Pollak 1988), thus magnifying the acoustic ITD. Pollak (1988) proposed that, due to the phasic on-responses and long latency ranges of IC neurons, amplitude-latency trading operates to magnify timing differences across the entire range of biologically-relevant ultrasonic frequencies. Amplitude-latency trading can be ambiguous: the directionality of the ears could produce a range of intensity differences across frequencies, so that a range of latency shifts (rather than one discrete shift) could occur in the contralateral and ipsilateral pathways.
The slightly larger head of big brown bats (Fig. 1a) yields ITDs of about 60 µs at 90º left or right. Behavioral and neurophysiological experiments report amplitude-latency trading ratios in the range of 15–30 µs/dB (Simmons et al. 1990; Haqqee et al. 2021; Warnecke et al. 2021). For example, if an echo arrives from an azimuth of 20º contralaterally, the ITD itself is just 14 µs, but the associated IID is near 10 dB, so the effects of amplitude-latency trading add approximately an additional 200 µs, a significant magnification of the ITD alone. The important point here is that the nominally distinct ITD and IID cues appear to be merged into a single representation that converges both parameters onto the same phasic neural responses. It remains to be seen, however, how reliable this single representation is for explaining the bat’s perception.

## Interactive representation of sound azimuth for beamforming
To further examine the neural code for binaural processing in the echolocation range, Haresign et al. (1996) recorded responses of single neurons from the IC of awake big brown bats using glass micropipettes (NaCl filled, 5–15 MΩ; glass/carbon-fiber 0.25–1 MΩ). Stimuli (tones for identifying neuronal best frequency, FM sweeps for analysis of binaural properties; 2–5 ms duration, rise-fall 0.5 ms, 4 Hz repetition rate) were presented dichotically at varying azimuths using Bruel & Kjaer ¼-inch condenser microphones as earphones. Detailed techniques are available in Ferragamo et al. (1998). Data were collected from 56 binaurally-sensitive (EI) neurons, located both dorsally and ventrally in the IC (best frequencies to tones in the range of ~ 15–80 kHz).
Figure 3 shows a representative tuning curve of neural response strength (proportion of spikes) as a function of IID (contralateral sound pressure level relative to ipsilateral sound pressure level). The IID tuning curve has two primary descriptors: The first is the dynamic range from full response for contralateral stronger to no response at ipsilateral stronger (15 dB in Fig. 3), and the second is the midpoint (50%) on the falling ipsilateral slope. We call this midpoint the “inflection point” (IP; at −3 dB in Fig. 3). To obtain these values, the contralateral monaural level was set slightly above threshold (dashed horizontal line in Fig. 3) and the level at the ipsilateral ear was adjusted to “titrate” the response strength and find the IP. Each IP marks the location of a neuron’s response falling from one-spike phasic on the contralateral side to no response on the ipsilateral side. Over a sample of 56 neurons (Fig. 4), IP indices span from about 0 dB to ± 15 dB for differences equivalent to left or right up to about 75º, but are distributed across neuron best frequencies uniformly. A modest plurality of IPs for neurons align around 0 dB (i.e. straight ahead in azimuth), and these cover all frequencies in the FM sweep, including the lower frequencies of ~ 15–20 kHz, which occur during the terminal stage of prey interception (the “buzz”; Simmons et al. 1979; Surlykke and Moss 2000; Saillant et al. 2007). But the IPs for the other neurons with values further away from 0 dB (from ± 5 to ± 15 dB), also cover all frequencies. The data in Fig. 4 indicate no obvious relation between values for IPs at different IIDs and neuronal best frequencies. The slopes of the responses around the IPs for neurons tuned to different frequencies are as steep as in the example of Fig. 3. This interacts with the phasic single-spike character of the responses to register the moment when the IID’s IP occurs in the FM sweep.

When IPs are now plotted as approximate azimuths associated with different IIDs across frequencies from the external ear’s receiving gain (Fig. 1e), a distinctive pattern emerges (Fig. 5). The previously-mentioned plurality of IPs that are spread over all frequencies in both FM1 and FM2 are aligned to straight ahead within a narrow range of ± 5º. Another group of IPs covers only frequencies in FM1. These are oriented offside from about 10º to 75º left or right. They look to the sides in different directions and seem ideal for locating the multiple parts of offside clutter. Thus, on a large scale, two groups of IP response distributions in azimuth emerge in Fig. 5: One corresponds to tightly-grouped forward-looking IIDs with wide FM1 and FM2 bandwidth coverage, while the other corresponds to a widespread offside-looking IIDs that cover only FM1.

Inserting the polar plot from Fig. 5 into a diagram (Fig. 6) of the bat’s flight track along a corridor between rows of vertical chains illustrates how the bat might segregate echoes from an object obstructing the path. Determining the distance and direction of an obstacle situated in the upcoming path requires precise localization and the full broadcast bandwidth to achieve the necessary degree of acuity. Because the bat’s head is aimed along the upcoming corridor, neurons tuned to the wide band of frequencies in FM1 and FM2 associated with small IIDs provide a forward-looking sonar view that isolates echoes from any object located within the upcoming path along the corridor. Low frequencies in FM1 emitted in the terminal buzz stage (Simmons et al. 1979; Surlykke and Moss 2000; Saillant et al. 2007) are represented in the frontal region because the target insect must be actively and precisely tracked for final interception. Simultaneously, neurons tuned only to FM1 with offside IID azimuths produce an offside sonar view of the rows of chains of the left and right. Registering the offside echoes returned by the chains to the left and right is reliable using frequencies in FM1, but frequencies in FM2 are less reliable because the frequency dependence of the broadcasts weakens them to a greater extent depending on the size of the offside azimuth.

As mentioned above, the remaining point to address is conversion of the full-contralateral response that occurs when the IID is more contralateral than the IP itself to a closed directionally-tuned neural beam with both contralateral and ipsilateral skirts. The EI neurons in Fig. 5 act as “edge-detectors” for the location of the IPs when neural responses are driven by dichotic stimulation. However, neurons in the IC respond to free-field sounds with full directional tuning on only one best azimuth (Jen et al. 1987).

## Discussion
Our analyses suggest that directionality of neurons in the big brown bat’s IC can be described by the operation of two beams formed by the time-of-occurrence of the IID, as represented by the slope rather than the peak of IID tuning curves. These analyses differ from those previously used to quantify binaural sensitivity in the bat IC by first identifying the IP (50% slope on the IID tuning curve) and plotting these IPs against azimuth, rather than against IID itself. The pattern of results suggests the presence of two beams, one centered in forward-looking space representing all frequencies in the bat’s FM sweep, and the other looking off-side, representing only frequencies in FM1. Within each half of the IC, the progression of IPs looks like a thermometer-like code for contralateral azimuths (Pollak and Casseday 1989). That is, responses are driven from the azimuth of the IP to all other azimuths located more contralaterally—which is one-half of a true beam. The two halves of the IC communicate bilaterally, which could form the other, ipsilateral half required to segregate clutter located in different azimuths. The range of azimuths covered by the left and right offside beams imply that they are elements of a finer-scale beamforming system manifested for the left and right sides in the contralateral right and left IC.
The separation between those beams representing both FM1 and FM2 for the full broadcast band and those representing the band only of FM1 is congruent with prior findings about the relation between FM1 and FM2 for biosonar perception (Simmons 2012, 2014; Warnecke and Simmons 2016). In psychophysical tests, big brown bats perceive target features with high acuity only when the entire broadcast band is contained in echoes, which means that the bat’s head and sonar beam must be closely aimed at the target; otherwise, the higher broadcast frequencies in FM2 will be weakened by the target’s off-axis location (Bates et al. 2011; Warnecke and Simmons 2016). Acute perception of target features thus emerges interactively from the bat’s accurate tracking of target direction, which keeps echoes within the desired wide-bandwidth regime. The mechanism involves detection of coherence in time (response latency) across all emitted frequencies in echoes. Very small decreases in amplitude at high frequencies cause amplitude-latency trading to disrupt this coherence, with disproportionate effects on perceptual acuity for targets (Simmons 2014). Objects located off to the sides yield echoes with weaker FM2 frequencies, which drives the loss of coherence in neural responses and reduces perceptual acuity in a manner that benefits perception through a cognitive process that manifests itself across the frequencies in echoes to achieve an effect more often achieved from echo reception with a spatially-distributed multiple-transducer array (Ming et al. 2021; Simmons et al. 2026).
Our analyses expand on earlier ideas about binaural localization of ultrasonic broadcasts and echoes. Grinnell and Grinnell (1965) already proposed that the bat’s brain could overcome ambiguities in localization by comparing relative intensities of different frequencies at the two ears, with ITDs as canonically defined relatively unimportant. Pollak (1988) suggested that binaurally-sensitive IC neurons, because of their phasic on-responding, can take advantage of latency differences between contralateral and ipsilateral activation to magnify acoustic ITDs to form expanded neural ITDs. He acknowledged, however, that amplitude-latency trading alone may not be precise enough to disambiguate cues introduced by ear directionality. We suggest that neurons use amplitude-latency trading to estimate the time-of-occurrence of contralateral vs. ipsilateral input at each individual frequency in the FM sweep. Further, amplitude-latency trading combined with IID sensitivity (slope of the tuning curve) operates to uncover the formation of two beams that separate out frontal from off-side azimuthal locations. Overall, our data suggest that the time-of-occurrence of particular IID IPs are represented as a unitary code, not just as the convergence of time and intensity differences but their collapse into one neural time-frequency response space.
The results presented here are drawn from the wider range of neural response features that characterize the big brown bat’s IC. Frequency tuning, phasic responses, and amplitude-latency trading effects are present in most IC neurons (Ferragamo et al. 1998). Most binaurally-sensitive neurons are of the EI type. The beamforming analysis suggests that the three critical directions—front-looking, left-looking, side-looking—are represented by different EI neurons. Alternatively, the same population may participate in the formation of multiple beams; this possibility can be tackled by means of computational modeling (Simmons et al. 2026). Taking into account earlier data (Ferragamo et al. 1998; Simmons 2014), we propose that population neural response profiles together might be better described as organized around a time-frequency, spectrogram-like representation that incorporates echo-delay information into its expanded latency dimension (Ming et al. 2021), while also including interaural differences associated with target direction (Simmons et al. 2026). We further suggest that big brown bats might employ array computations in the frequency domain even though they have only two ears.
There are several limitations to our analyses. First, data were collected using earphones, thus ignoring the effects of ear directionality (Simmons et al. 2026). A further model of azimuthal localization must take the frequency-dependent ear directionality into account. Second, our analyses are limited to localization in azimuth and do not address the problem of localization in elevation, which is also affected by ear directionality. The two dimensions of azimuth and elevation could be conceived as separate or even orthogonal; however, the acoustics (Wotton et al. 1995; Haresign et al. 1996; Aytekin et al. 2004), the neurophysiology (Grinnell and Grinnell 1965; Haresign et al. 1996; Ferragamo et al. 1998), and most importantly the psychophysics (Wotton et al. 1996; Simmons et al. 2002) confirm that these dimensions are intertwined. Further modeling efforts are needed to uncover the mechanisms of this intertwining (Simmons et al. 2026).
Finally, these results have practical implications. Due to amplitude-latency trading, the limited width of the bat’s two-ear horizontal array for determining azimuth is magnified to become effectively wider as a function of concurrent IIDs while still using the basic neural representation of IIDs and their IPs across frequency. A sonar-guided vehicle in air could have an array width of only 1.5 cm and yet achieve an effective array width of more than 7 cm if the receiver were to incorporate bat-like echo processing, which converges IIDs and ITDs onto a single azimuthal scale.
