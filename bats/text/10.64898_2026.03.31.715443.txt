Abstract

16

Intraspecific variation is a prerequisite for natural selection and can manifest in various phenotypic

17

traits, including vocal signals. However, classifying individuals based on their vocalizations, or

18

acoustic individual identification (AIID), remains a significant challenge. This is particularly true for

19

species that use rapidly varying echolocation calls for orientation. Here, we demonstrate that deep

20

learning can overcome the limitation of traditional methods and reveal persistent individual

21

signatures within bat echolocation calls. We recorded echolocation calls from 34 individuals of the

22

greater leaf-nosed bat (Hipposideros armiger) under controlled laboratory conditions, with 19

23

individuals recorded repeatedly over three months. We show that a convolutional neural network

24

(CNN) dramatically outperforms a traditional method, achieving an average identification accuracy

25

of 84% for single calls and 91% for call sequences. In contrast, the traditional Discriminant

26

Functional Analysis method achieved accuracies of only 39% and 47%, respectively. Through

27

systematically altering the temporal structure of echolocation calls in input sequences, we found that

28

temporal patterning enhances individual classification accuracy, suggesting it contributes to the

29

encoding of individual-specific information. This study revealed that echolocation calls of H.

1 INTRODUCTION

36

Intraspecific variation in genetic traits can provide individuals with adaptive advantages to drive

37

natural selection 1. Individual identification serves as the cornerstone of ecological and evolutionary

38

research, providing critical insights into population dynamics, social structures, and movement

39

ecology 2–4. To date, individual identification and tracking in the wild have relied heavily on

40

conventional techniques like mark-recapture and telemetry 5–7. However, these methods suffer from

41

significant limitations: they are labor-intensive, invasive, and susceptible to observer bias or

42

behavioral alterations in the study subjects 8–10.

43

Bioacoustic signals represent a promising alternative approach for individual identification, given

44

that vocalizations encode individual-specific features through genetic, environmental, and cultural

45

influences 11–14. Acoustic individual identification (AIID), which involves identifying individuals by

46

their calls, eliminates the need for making physical contact with the individual, allows efficient

47

long-term monitoring, and reduces personnel costs. Early AIID implementations using traditional

48

machine learning, including support vector machines, Hidden Markov Models, or Discriminant

49

Function Analysis (DFA) 15–17, demonstrated conceptual promise but suffered critical constraints:

50

reliance on expert knowledge and experience, vulnerability to ambient noise contamination and

51

inter-class spectral overlap, constrained generalizability under small-sample conditions, and

52

performance degradation caused by low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) 18,19. These limitations critically

53

impair identification fidelity, often resulting in classification accuracy that falls below 50%, which is

54

insufficient for robust ecological inference and restricts researchers to studying individuals only in

55

specific contexts.

56

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), offers

57

paradigm-shifting capabilities for pattern recognition, garnering increased attention from ecologists

58

20–22

59

CNNs autonomously extract discriminative features through hierarchical learning of spectrotemporal

. Unlike traditional machine learning approaches that rely on hand-crafted feature extraction,

60

patterns, bypassing error-prone manual feature selection while maintaining robustness to acoustic

61

variability 23. Such tools can automate the analysis of various types of data, ranging from species

62

abundance to behavioral patterns and derived from multiple data sources such as images or audio

63

recordings, which are widely used by ecologists for automated species identification, environmental

64

monitoring, ecological modelling or behavioral studies 24,25. Moreover, the use of deep learning

65

methods for individual identification has also become a subject of heightened research involving

66

diverse taxa, such as humans, primates, and birds 26–29. However, most existing applications focus on

67

image-based individual identification, while the use of acoustic signals for individual identification

68

remains underdeveloped. A key unresolved question is whether deep learning is suitable for AIID,

69

considering the pronounced intra-individual variability in vocal signals in many taxa 20.

70

Bat echolocation calls are well-known for their remarkable plasticity, which vary across behavioral

71

tasks, environments, and time 30–33, thus representing a highly challenging case for robust AIID. The

72

high flexibility of their calls has led to enduring scientific controversy over whether bat echolocation

73

calls truly encode individual signatures 34,35. Additionally, the physical and ecological demands of

74

echolocation may impose evolutionary constraints on vocal inter-individual variability. Because

75

echolocation calls must maintain acoustic features optimized for target detection and spatial

76

navigation, the extent of inter-individual variability may be more limited than in many

77

non-echolocating species, where variability in vocal signatures is important for individual

78

recognition. Determining whether stable individual signatures can still be extracted under such

79

constraints represents a key challenge for acoustic individual identification in echolocating mammals.

80

Notably, stability does not imply invariance of acoustic signals, but rather the persistence of

81

individual-specific information despite substantial within-individual variability across time and

82

context. Addressing this question would help to reconcile the debate whether individual signatures in

83

echolocation calls exist, and if so, it can facilitate an application of AIID as a powerful tool for the

84

behavior and ecology in a wide range of vocally active species.

85

Here, we first conducted a systematic literature search of peer-reviewed studies applying deep

86

learning to the acoustic identification of species and individuals, establishing baselines to evaluate

87

the performance of our models. We demonstrate that, comparable to species-level classification, deep

88

learning is a powerful method for AIID under controlled laboratory conditions, achieving up to 91%

89

accuracy even when applied to the challenging domain of bat echolocation calls.

2.1 Literature survey

93

We first investigated whether deep learning has been successfully applied to acoustic identification

94

of species and individuals across taxa, and if so, with what accuracy. We conducted a systematic

95

literature search of peer-reviewed studies that applied deep learning to species and individual

96

identification based on acoustic signals. The literature search was performed using the Web of

97

Science "all databases" on February 28, 2025, with the following search terms: TS = “individual

98

identification” AND TS = “deep learn” or TS = “species identification” AND TS = “deep learn”.

99

100

101

102

103

2.3 Call sequence segmentation

141

This study utilized two distinct datasets derived from labeled audio recordings. The first was

142

composed of standard 5-second call sequences, referred to as sequence-level identification. The

143

second dataset consisted of single calls, which were uniformly zero-padded to 1 second to maintain

144

dimensional consistency, referred to as call-level identification. The single calls were obtained by

145

processing call sequences through an Endpoint Detection Algorithm. Briefly, the call sequences

146

firstly underwent several preprocessing steps, including the removal of the DC component and

147

amplitude normalization, which collectively serve to enhance the stability and robustness of

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.31.715443; this version posted April 2, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

148

subsequent signal analysis and feature extraction. A Hamming window (length = 200 points) was

149

employed for audio framing, with adjacent frames overlapping by 120 points (60% overlap ratio) to

150

maintain signal continuity. Following the framing process, energy-entropy ratio features were

151

extracted, with high and low thresholds set to 0.1 times and 0.05 times the dynamic range,

152

respectively. To mitigate transient noise, a 10-point median filtering technique was employed.

153

Furthermore, dynamic thresholds were established by analyzing a leading 0.25-second silent segment,

154

combined with constraints of a minimum valid segment (5 frames) and maximum silent interval (8

155

frames), to precisely extract individual vocalization clips from each sequence.

2.4 Discriminant function analysis

157

Discriminant function analysis (DFA), a statistical classification method designed to identify optimal

158

linear combinations for distinguishing between two or more predefined groups, was implemented for

159

individual identification. We selected DFA due to its historical prevalence in bat acoustic studies,

160

allowing for a direct reference to prior literature. Two distinct models for bat individual identification

161

were constructed, utilizing call sequence datasets and single call datasets, respectively. For call-level

162

identification, the frequency spectrum surrounding the dominant second harmonic of the

163

echolocation calls (60–80 kHz) was firstly transformed to the Mel scale via a Discrete Fourier

164

Transform (DFT), which provide a nonlinear resampling of the frequency axis and a compact

165

representation of spectral information for machine-learning analysis. Mel-frequency representations

166

were chosen due to their widespread application in the machine learning community. Although the

167

Mel scale is essentially a logarithmic transformation that de-emphasizes higher frequencies,

168

restricting the frequency range to 60–80 kHz effectively preserves the spectral detail relevant to these

169

echolocation calls. Then, feature extraction employed a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) to derive

170

Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), Zero Crossing Rate (ZCR), and Spectral Centroid

171

(SC). Mean and standard deviation values of these features were calculated to generate

172

44-dimensional feature vectors (comprising 40 dimensions from 20 MFCCs, 2 dimensions from ZCR,

173

and 2 dimensions from SC), ensuring stability for subsequent model training and classification. For

174

individual identification, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was employed using Python’s librosa

175

library with the following parameters: no pre-emphasis, n_mfcc=20, frame size=2048 samples, hop

176

length=512 samples, and Hann window spectral smoothing. This pipeline maintained dimensional

consistency across training and test sets while capturing stable acoustic characteristics for

178

classification. During analysis, the total dataset was partitioned with 10% allocated as the test dataset,

179

while the remaining 90% was divided into training and validation datasets through 5-fold

180

cross-validation. The same methodology was applied to the sequence-level identification.

2.5 Deep learning

182

The experiment uses the same datasets as the DFA model for comparative analysis, with

183

experimental conditions grouped into two main experiments: call- and sequence-level identification.

184

To investigate whether deep learning-based individual identification models rely on spectral features

185

and temporal cues. At the call-level, the following conditions were included: single call (original

186

single call datasets), multiple consecutive calls (sequences of 2 to 23 calls were extracted in their

187

natural, original order from the call sequences dataset), and multiple randomly selected calls (2 to 23

188

calls randomly sampled from different positions within the call sequences dataset, with no regard to

189

their original order). For the sequence-level, the conditions included: 5-second call sequences

190

(original single call datasets), 5-second time-reversal sequences (reversing the order of calls within

191

the sequences), 5-second position-random sequences (calls within the sequences randomly reordered,

192

disrupting natural temporal structure), and call-swapping sequences (hybrid sequences constructed

193

by replacing the spectral content of one individual’s calls with that of another, while preserving the

194

original temporal structure). By comparing classification performance across these conditions, we

195

tested three hypotheses. First, if temporal cues contribute to encode individual identity, disrupting

196

their natural order of calls should reduce classification accuracy. Second, if spectral features are the

197

primary carriers of identity information, preserving an individual's spectral content while altering its

198

temporal structure should still yield high accuracy. Conversely, if temporal patterns dominate,

199

sequences with preserved temporal structure but foreign spectral content should show better

200

performance. Third, the call-swapping experiment was designed to directly evaluate the relative

201

contribution of spectral and temporal features to individual identification. For all conditions except

202

single calls and 5-second call sequences, we employed endpoint detection using a ratio method to

203

identify the calls in each audio file, obtaining the starting and ending frames for each call. We

204

performed echo filtering and saved each call individually, when the interval between the previous

205

call and the current call is less than 0.01 seconds and the maximum amplitude of the previous call is

206

more than three times that of the current call.

For individual identification, each condition firstly was partitioned with 10% allocated as the test

208

209

cross-validation. Each audio segment underwent Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with a

210

4096-point Fourier window and 2048-point frame shift to compute time-frequency spectrograms. A

211

bandpass filter (55 to 85 kHz) was applied to retain frequency bands containing the dominant second

212

harmonic component. Concurrently, the spectrograms were converted to log-Mel spectrograms using

213

128 Mel filter banks for the 60-80 kHz frequency range to achieve dimensionality reduction 40. The

214

ResNest50d network was selected as the backbone for feature extraction and classification 41. This

215

choice was motivated by three main considerations 42,43. First, CNN architectures with integrated

216

attention mechanisms have been shown to substantially improve classification performance in

217

bioacoustic contexts, where features are subtle and background noise is complex. However, it is

218

worth noting that the split-attention mechanism in ResNeSt operates on grouped features along the

219

channel dimension rather than on spatial positions within the input spectrogram. As a result, the

220

attention weights do not directly indicate which time–frequency regions are most influential, limiting

221

the direct interpretability of the model's decisions in bioacoustic terms. Second, empirical evidence

222

indicates that ResNeSt50 outperforms other mainstream CNN architectures when processing

223

complex animal vocalization spectrograms, demonstrating its strong ability to capture intricate

224

time–frequency patterns. Third, the ResNeSt50d variant incorporates architectural enhancements

225

from the ResNet-D design, enabling the model to reduce feature map dimensionality while

226

preserving the structural integrity of acoustic signals. This helps minimize the loss of key

227

discriminative information, which is crucial for individual-level identification in bat echolocation

228

calls. Nevertheless, we also selected EfficientNet-B0 as the backbone network for feature extraction

229

and classification to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. During the training process, the

230

model parameters are iteratively updated using a Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer with

231

momentum, with an initial learning rate set to 0.01, a minimum learning rate of 0.0001. A cosine

232

annealing learning rate scheduling strategy was implemented, where the annealing cycle Tmax was set

233

to 100, matching the total training epochs. To explore the acoustic basis of individual signatures, we

234

separated each echolocation call into its constant frequency (CF) and frequency-modulated (FM)

235

components and performed individual recognition on each component independently. The CF and

236

FM components were separated by estimating the end of the CF component and the start of the FM

2.6 Statistical analysis

240

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (v 2022.10.0+353, R version 4.2.2) and

241

MATLAB (R2021b, MathWorks, USA). Principal component analysis was used to examine the

242

variability of individual calls using the FactoMineR package based on acoustic parameters, including

243

peak frequency, call duration, bandwidth, and pulse interval. We employed the Bhattacharyya

244

coefficient to quantify the degree of overlap between individuals' call distributions in the reduced

245

feature space. A higher coefficient value indicates greater overlap, meaning that individuals are

246

difficult to distinguish based solely on these linearly transformed feature. We also employed

247

Mann-Whitney U test (as data normality was rejected according to Shapiro-Wilk test in the package

248

stats) to compare the differences in average accuracy between groups.

3 RESULTS

251

3.1 Accuracy for acoustic identification of animal species by deep learning

252

We conducted a comprehensive review of 110 studies that evaluated acoustic identification of

253

species based on deep learning. These studies encompassed a broad range of species, from

254

well-studied taxa such as birds (67.29%) and bats (20.56%) to less frequently studied amphibian,

255

marine mammals, and invertebrates (Figure 1A; Table S1). Regarding the classification

256

performance, the overall average identification accuracy for all taxa was 0.89 ± 0.08 (mean ± SD),

257

with the highest average accuracy for marine mammals at 0.92 ± 0.03 (mean ± SD). For bats, the

258

average species-level identification accuracy was also high at 0.89 ± 0.08, and the lowest for

259

invertebrates at 0.86 and was derived from only a single study (Figure 1A). Additionally, we

260

systematically reviewed 17 studies focusing on the bats acoustic individual identification based on

261

traditional methods. We found the identification accuracy for individual bats based on traditional

262

methods was much lower, at 0.48 ± 0.22 (mean ± SD), with the normalized accuracy of 0.46 ± 0.23

263

(mean ± SD; Figure 1B; Table S2).

264

3.3 AIID by the traditional DFA

276

We first employed traditional methods to investigate AIID of bats. The results of the DFA showed

277

that the average classification accuracy based on single call for the 34 individuals was 0.39 ± 0.21.

278

Furthermore, no significant difference in classification accuracy was observed between the CCNU

279

and NENU sites (Mann-Whitney U test: P = 0.19; Figure 3A). For 5-second call sequences

280

recognition, the average classification accuracy improved to 0.47 ± 0.26, with classification accuracy

281

at the CCNU site significantly higher than that at NENU (Mann-Whitney U test: P = 0.02).

282

3.4 AIID by deep learning

283

In contrast, deep learning achieved markedly higher performance. The model showed stable

284

convergence throughout training, with classification accuracy consistently increasing and

285

286

287

288

289

significantly higher than at NENU (0.90 vs. 0.77: Mann-Whitney U test: P = 0.002). Furthermore,

290

we observed a progressive increase in classification accuracy with the number of calls incorporated

291

into the analysis. Multiple consecutive calls consistently outperformed those constructed from

292

multiple randomly selected calls (Figure 3D), suggesting temporal sequence information may help

293

to enhance classification performance.

294

295

5-second call sequence paradigm, culminating in an overall mean accuracy of 0.91 ± 0.12 (mean ±

296

SD) and the normalized accuracy of 0.91 (Figure 3C). The classification accuracy at CCNU was

Besides, Figure 4C displays the schematic representation of call-swapping sequences. We found that

308

when synthesizing novel call profiles through recombination of spectral features and temporal

309

sequence information, individuals identified based on spectral features (feature dominated) exhibited

310

significantly higher accuracy than those identified from temporal sequence information (position

311

dominated) (Figure 4D). This indicates that the importance of spectral features in recognition is

312

greater than that of temporal sequence information. However, when each call was decomposed into

313

its CF and FM components and evaluated independently, the classification accuracy deteriorated

314

markedly (CF-only: 0.34 ± 0.28; FM-only: 0.30 ± 0.39; Figure S3).

4 DISCUSSION

317

In this study, we successfully applied deep learning to AIID under controlled laboratory conditions

318

and achieved an unprecedented recognition accuracy of up to 0.91, which is comparable to the

319

benchmark for species identification (0.89 on average), despite the inherent challenge of discerning

320

minute individual vocal differences. This achievement is particularly striking given that individual

321

identification demands resolving acoustic variations orders of magnitude smaller than

322

between-species distinctions. In contrast, traditional methods, such as discriminant function analysis

323

(DFA), achieved accuracy approximately two times lower than that of the deep learning,

324

underscoring deep learning’s capacity to decode nonlinear spectrotemporal interactions impervious

325

to manual feature engineering. Our dataset, comprising 34 bats monitored over three months and

326

17,584 calls, demonstrates the natural range of vocal plasticity in bat echolocation calls. This

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.31.715443; this version posted April 2, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

327

variability, driven by factors such as environmental noise 46, behavioral states 47, and ontogenetic

328

changes 48, and social factors 49, has historically posed challenges for conventional methods 34,50.

329

The primary objective of this study was to establish a proof-of-concept under controlled conditions,

330

specifically to evaluate whether deep learning is suitable for AIID. Our results suggest that deep

331

neural networks effectively leverage hierarchical feature abstraction to isolate stable ’vocal

332

fingerprints’, even in groups with excessive intra-individual variability in acoustic signals, achieving

333

robustness where traditional methods tend to struggle 51. Although individual call features such as

334

frequency or duration vary substantially across time, the relative configuration and joint distribution

335

of these features appear to form a robust identity signature. Deep learning models are particularly

336

well suited to capture such distributed representations, enabling reliable identification even when

337

individual acoustic components are highly variable. In this context, our use of “reveals” refers to the

338

model’s ability to detect and utilize identity-relevant information, rather than to explicitly identify the

339

precise acoustic features involved. However, the noticeably higher classification accuracy at CCNU

340

compared to NENU suggests that site-specific factors may have influenced model performance,

341

potentially arising from acoustic artifacts introduced by differences in recording environments and

342

equipment, coupled with a lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the NENU recordings.

343

Our research highlights the crucial role of spectral features in individual identification, providing

344

compelling evidence for the encoding of individual information in the echolocation calls of bats.

345

Spectral features, such as frequency and bandwidth of echolocation calls, serve as distinctive

346

biological markers that allow researchers to differentiate between individuals within a species 52–54.

347

Studies have shown that variations in the frequency components of echolocation calls can reflect

348

individual identity, with certain bats exhibiting unique call characteristics that can be consistently

349

recognized across different contexts 55,56. Furthermore, the application of advanced bioacoustic

350

analysis techniques 30,38,57 has enabled researchers to automate the identification process by

351

analyzing these spectral features, thereby improving the efficiency and accuracy of individual

352

recognition. The encoding of individual labels through spectral characteristics is not merely an

353

analytical tool, but a critical aspect in the broader context of understanding bat ecology and behavior.

354

To further investigate the acoustic basis of individual signatures, we conducted a preliminary

355

analysis by separating each echolocation call into its CF and FM components and performing

356

recognition on each independently. Both CF-only and FM-only models yielded markedly lower

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.31.715443; this version posted April 2, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

357

accuracy (with averages below 35%), far below the 91% achieved with full-spectrum calls. This

358

suggests that individual identity is not encoded in isolated acoustic components, but rather

359

distributed across the full spectrotemporal structure. This may also explain the poor performance of

360

traditional approaches such as DFA, which rely on manually extracted features. However, we believe

361

this result should not be taken as direct evidence that FM bat echolocation calls cannot encode

362

individual signatures, for several reasons. First, we did not specifically fine-tune the model to

363

optimize performance; therefore, these results likely represent an underestimation of the potential

364

accuracy. Second, the FM component in CF-FM bats appears more stereotyped than in purely FM

365

bats. For instance, both the duration and bandwidth of the FM component in H. armiger exhibit a

366

much narrower dynamic range compared to those of many FM bats, such as the big brown bat 38,58.

367

Third, there is solid evidence that the echolocation calls of FM bats contain strong individual

368

signatures 52,54, although the number of individuals involved in previous analyses has typically been

369

limited. Furthermore, it is critical to interpret these findings within the constraints of the

370

methodology. The deep learning model identifies a robust acoustic signature that could facilitate

371

individual identification. This demonstrates the information encoding capacity of the calls but does

372

not constitute evidence that bats perceptually discriminate or cognitively utilize these cues. The

373

fundamental question of whether bats use these specific, model-derived features for social

374

recognition remains unanswered.

375

In addition to spectral features, when both spectral and temporal features are integrated, the

376

classification performance improves significantly, highlighting the value of temporal sequence

377

information in individual recognition tasks. This temporal pattern provides context and relational

378

dynamics that are essential for distinguishing between individuals, particularly in species where

379

vocalizations are complex and varied 59,60. Thus, the encoding of individual labels through

380

time-based characteristics is not just an auxiliary feature; it is a fundamental element that shapes how

381

organisms interact and communicate within their species 61. By incorporating temporal pattern into

382

identification frameworks, such as Recurrent Neural Networks 62, researchers can better understand

383

social structures, mating systems, and communication strategies in various species, lending insight

384

into behavioral ecology and evolution. However, it is worth noting that this study is that all

385

recordings were obtained from perching individuals. In many bat species, the timing of echolocation

386

pulses during flight is partially coupled with the wingbeat cycle, which can influence call emission

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.31.715443; this version posted April 2, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

387

patterns and temporal structure 63. Consequently, the importance of pulse interval as a discriminative

388

feature identified in our analysis may differ under flight conditions.

389

This study establishes bats as model organisms for advancing AIID development, where by

390

surmounting traditional methods limitations through deep learning, our framework enables

391

non-invasive individual studies at under controlled laboratory conditions. These results show great

392

promise for extrapolating to other acoustically active taxa. Echolocation signals are primarily under

393

stabilizing selection for reliable echo detection, which should theoretically minimize individual

394

variation. In contrast, many other animal vocalizations are under diversifying selection to encode

395

information such as individual identity, group membership, or reproductive status. With appropriate

396

adaptation to species-specific acoustic characteristics, such methods may be extended to other

397

vocally complex systems (e.g., cetaceans and songbirds), enabling novel insights into adaptive signal

398

evolution, dynamic social network topologies, host-pathogen coevolution, and anthropogenic impact

399

assessments 4.

400

Nevertheless, application of AIID to freely-ranging wild animals still faces considerable challenges.

401

First, our experiments were conducted on recordings of single bats in a resting state under controlled

402

laboratory conditions. In real ecological environments, bats often fly and move in groups. In such

403

cases, the complexity of acoustic data will significantly increase, potentially leading to a decrease in

404

identification accuracy. Moreover, echolocation calls produced across different behavioral contexts,

405

such as flight, foraging, or prey capture, may exhibit context-dependent acoustic variation driven by

406

task-specific sensorimotor demands, which could further influence the performance of CNN-based

407

individual identification. In addition, environmental complexities such as reflections and

408

reverberation in cluttered habitats and calls from conspecific and heterospecific individuals can

409

introduce spectral-temporal distortions, which require advanced algorithms for separating

410

overlapping calls64. A further limitation is that the present study employed a closed-set design, in

411

which all individuals included in the test set were also represented in the training set. In contrast,

412

real-world applications, such as long-term acoustic monitoring of wild animals, will inevitably

413

involve open-set scenarios, where calls from previously unobserved individuals must either be

414

identified as unknown or incorporated into the recognition system. The closed-set framework

415

adopted here therefore represents an initial step toward validating the feasibility of deep

416

learning-based individual identification under controlled conditions. Extending this approach to

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.31.715443; this version posted April 2, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

417

open-set recognition will be an important direction for future research, potentially involving

418

strategies such as novelty detection or incremental learning to enable robust performance in more

419

realistic ecological settings. Future research should focus on optimizing algorithms, expanding

420

datasets, and enhancing the robustness of models to evaluate whether individual signatures remain

421

detectable across diverse behavioral contexts and ecological environments, a prerequisite for

422

applying AIID to freely behaving wild populations.
