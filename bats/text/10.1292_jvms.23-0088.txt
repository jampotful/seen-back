Running Head: EAR MUSCLES IN FRUIT BAT

Abstract
Bats can be phylogenetically classified into three major groups: pteropodids, rhinolophoids,
and yangochiropterans. While rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans are capable of laryngeal
echolocation, pteropodids lack this ability. Delicate ear movements are essential for
echolocation behavior in bats with laryngeal echolocation. Caudal auricular muscles, especially
the cervicoauricularis group, play a critical role in such ear movements. Previously, caudal
auricular muscles were studied in three species of bats with laryngeal echolocation, but to our
knowledge, there have been no studies on non-laryngeal echolocators, the pteropodids. Here,
we describe the gross anatomy of the cervicoauricularis muscles and their innervation in
Cynopterus sphinx by using diffusible iodine-based contrast-enhanced computed tomography
and 3D reconstructions of immunohistochemically stained serial sections. A previous study on
bats with laryngeal echolocation reported that rhinolophoids have four cervicoauricularis
muscles and yangochiropterans have three. We observed three cervicoauricularis muscles in
the pteropodid C. sphinx. The number of cervicoauricularis muscles and their innervation
pattern were comparable to those of non-bat boreoeutherian mammals and yangochiropterans,
suggesting that pteropodids, and yangochiropterans maintain the general condition of
boreoeutherian mammals and that rhinolophoids have a derived condition. The unique
nomenclature had been previously applied to the cervicoauricularis muscles of bats with
laryngeal echolocation, but given the commonality between non-bat laurasiatherians and bats,
with the exception of rhinolophoids, maintaining the conventional nomenclature (i.e., M.
cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis medius, and M. cervicoauricularis
profundus) is proposed for bats.

Keywords: comparative anatomy, echolocation, evolution, skull

Introduction
Bats (order Chiroptera) have undergone one of the greatest adaptive radiations in
mammalian history, driven by the acquisition of powered flight and echolocation ability [14].
In the present account, three major groups, the pteropodids, rhinolophoids, and
yangochiropterans, comprise the extant bats [27]. Among these, rhinolophoids and
yangochiropterans are capable of laryngeal echolocation, while the pteropodids lack such
ability [11, 32]. Although pteropodids are not equipped with the sophisticated biosonar ability
to perform laryngeal echolocation, many of them are able to conduct primitive types of
echolocation such as tongue-click echolocation and wing-beat echolocation [2]. Echolocation
strategies are also highly diverse among bats with laryngeal echolocation [14].
Experimental evidence has shown that ear motions have an impact on echolocation
ability in bats [19–21, 25, 31]. Rhinolophids and hipposiderids actively create Doppler shifts
with their fast-moving pinnae to encode additional sensory information [30]. The
cervicoauricularis muscles play a critical role in triggering ear movement, and inhibition of
these muscles is reported to decrease echolocation performance [22, 30]. However, the diversity
of ear movements and their link to the variation in echolocation behavior in bats remains largely
unclear.
The caudal auricular muscles, innervated by the facial nerve, generally originate
from the dorsal cervical region and insert into the auricle in mammals [4, 5, 9]. To date, caudal
auricular muscles have been studied for two rhinolophoids (Rhinolophus ferrumequinum and
Asellia tridens) [24, 25] and one yangochiropteran (Myotis myotis) [24], but there have been no
reports on pteropodids. Here, we describe the caudal auricular muscles attached directly to the
pinna (cervicoauricularis muscles) and their innervation pattern in the greater short-nosed fruit
bat (Cynopterus sphinx). To our knowledge, this is the first report of its kind for the pteropodids.
Cynopterus sphinx is widely distributed from Pakistan, India, and Sri Lanka to southern China

and Vietnam, the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, Sulawesi, Timor, and smaller
islands in the Malay Archipelago [1, 3, 7, 16]. It is common in tropical forests and areas where
fruit crops are cultivated [26]. We first examined the number of muscles, attachments, and
innervation of the cervicoauricularis muscles and then compared them with those of other bats
and non-bat mammals. Observations on 3D reconstructed fetal samples were conducted to
understand the detailed innervation to the cervicoauricularis muscles and nearby structures.
This study resolves the confused nomenclature for the cervicoauricularis muscles and provides
a unifying framework to understand the evolution and variation of the cervicoauricularis
muscles in bats.

Materials and Methods
We studied the caudal auricular muscles that are directly attached to the caudal part
of the pinna (M. cervicoauricularis) in C. sphinx. The M. cervicoscutularis, which attaches to
the scutiform cartilage, was omitted from this study. Two adult C. sphinx from the collection
of the Institute of Ecology and Biological Resources, Vietnam Academy of Science and
Technology (IEBR; Hanoi, Vietnam) were used in this study. They were euthanized by 5%
isoflurane inhalation and then fixed and preserved in 70% ethanol. To enhance the contrast of
the CT images, we followed the image enhancement techniques used in a previous study
(diceCT) [10] and stained one specimen with 1% iodine in 100% ethanol for 14 days before
scanning. Then it was scanned using a microCT system (inspeXio SMX-90CT Plus, Shimadzu
Corp., Kyoto, Japan) with 90KV source voltage and 100 uA source current; the voxel size of
the images was 39 μm. We reconstructed the microCT scan of the fruit bat in the cranial part
using Amira 5.2 software (Visage Imaging, San Diego, CA, USA). Another specimen was
observed macroscopically under a light microscope (BX60, Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan)
equipped with a digital camera (DP70, Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

A late-stage fetus (Stage 22) of C. sphinx was used for serial sectioning and 3D
reconstructions. Staging was conducted based on the embryonic development of the Egyptian
fruit Bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus) [15]. The cranium of the fetal sample was dehydrated with a
graded series of ethanol (70–100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The
embedded sample was cut into 9 μm-thick sections using a rotary microtome HM355S and
Section Transfer System (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) and then placed on glass slides (CRE-04,
Matsunami Glass Ind., Osaka, Japan). Deparaffinized sections were stained with hematoxylin
and eosin (H & E) (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan) and underwent immunohistochemical
staining with a mouse anti-acetylated tubulin monoclonal antibody (1: 2000, Sigma-Aldrich,
no. T7451). The staining was processed with biotinylated anti-mouse IgG (1:200, BA-2000,
Vector Laboratories Inc., Newark, CA, USA) followed by Avidin–biotin complex (Vectastain
ABC Kit, Peroxidase (standard) PK-4000, Vector Laboratories) and Takara DAB Substrate
(MK210, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). After staining, the sections were dehydrated in a graded
series of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and covered with cover glass (NEO, Matsunami Glass Ind.,
Osaka, Japan). Images of serial sections were captured using a light microscope (SMZ-18,
Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera (DS-Ri2, Nikon Corp., Tokyo,
Japan) connected to a computer. The 3D reconstruction was conducted using Avizo 6.3
(Visualization Sciences Group, Hillsboro, OR, USA).
An embryonic day 14.5 fetus (E14.5) of wild-type laboratory mouse (Mus musculus)
(C57BL/6J) was studied as an outgroup. A pregnant mouse was euthanized by cervical
dislocation, and the collected embryo was fixed in modified Serra's fixative (4% PFA
containing ethanol and acetic acid), dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections were
cut at a thickness of 6 μm. We used anti-acetylated tubulin (1:2000, no. T7451, clone 6-11B-1,
Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and HRP-conjugated polyclonal goat anti-mouse (no.
P0447, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) to visualize the nerve axons. Next, the sections were stained
with Alcian blue and H & E, following standard protocols. Images of serial sections were

captured using a light microscope (BX60, Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a
digital camera (DP70, Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan) connected to a computer. The 3D
reconstruction was performed using Amira 2022.2

Results
The 3D reconstruction from the diceCT scan of the adult C. sphinx clarified the
shape and topology of the cervicoauricularis muscles (Fig. 1A, B). Based on C. lupus [5, 26],
we tentatively identified the cervicoauricularis muscles directly attached to the auricle as M.
cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis medius, and M. cervicoauricularis
profundus. The most rostral muscle originated from the midline of the skull and inserted into
the upper part of the pinna (Fig. 1B). The second muscle originated from the midline of the
skull but ran underneath the most rostral muscle and inserted into the middle level of the pinna
(Fig. 1B). The third muscle, located most rostrally among the three muscles, originated from
the midline of the skull and inserted into the lower part of the pinna (Fig. 1B). A wing-related
muscle, the M. occipitopollicalis, which is peculiar to bats, was also found to originate from
the caudal midline region of the skull and insert into the propatagium (Fig. 1B).
The 3D reconstruction of serial sections from the fetal specimen clarified the shape,
topology, and innervation of the cervicoauricularis muscles and nearby muscles innervated by
the facial nerve (Fig. 2A). All three cervicoauricularis muscles were present at this stage,
already showing the basic topological patterns observed in the adult specimen (Figs. 1, 2). In
this fetal specimen, we were also able to observe the peripheral morphology of the facial nerve
(Fig. 2A, B). The facial nerve had five branches (Fig. 2B). The N. retroauricularis supplied all
three cervicoauricularis muscles. The neighboring M. occipitopollicalis was supplied by the N.
retroauricularis inferior, which was the first subbranch from the N. retroauricularis. The M.
platysma was found to be supplied by the N. facialis ramus colli. The N. digastricus, which

supplied the posterior belly of the M. digastricus, and the N. facialis ramus buccalis ventralis,
which was the thickest branch extending to buccal nerve and mandibular nerve, were also
identified (Fig. 2B).
The 3D reconstruction of serial sections from the E14.5 fetal M. musculus specimen
clarified the shape, topology, and innervation of the cervicoauricularis muscles (Fig. 3A). All
three cervicoauricularis muscles were present at this stage. In this fetal specimen, we were able
to observe the peripheral morphology of the facial nerve. The N. retroauricularis supplied all
three cervicoauricularis muscles (M. cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis
medius, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus), as shown in Figure 3B. The M.
cervicoauricularis medius was found to run under the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis and
M. cervicoauricularis profundus. The insertion of the M. cervicoauricularis medius was found
to be ventral to the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis. The insertion of M. cervicoauricularis
superficialis was found to be the most rostral among the three muscles. The insertion of M.
cervicoauricularis profundus was found to be the most caudal among the three muscles.

Discussion
Our results showed all three cervicoauricularis muscles in C. sphinx originate from
the midline of the skull (Fig. 1). Previous studies by Schneider [24, 25] reported that the
laryngeal echolocating rhinolophoids have different numbers of cervicoauricularis muscles
compared to non-bat laurasiatherians. The most distinctive features of the muscles of the
rhinolophoids have four cervicoauricularis muscles. The two caudal muscles, the M.
cervicoauricularis profundus major, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus minor, do not
originate from the midline but from the nuchal crest. In the horseshoe bat (R. ferrumequinum),
in addition to the M. cervicoauricularis profundus major and profundus minor, the M.
cervicoauricularis superficialis minor also originates from the nuchal crest. Its most rostral

cervicoauricularis muscle, the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major, originates from the
midline. In the trident bat (A. tridens), the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major and minor
originate from the midline, and the M. cervicoauricularis profundus major and minor originate
from the nuchal crest. The mouse-eared bat (M. myotis), which belongs to the Yangochiroptera,
was reported by Schneider [24] to have only three muscles (the M. cervicoauricularis
superficialis major, M. cervicoauricularis superficialis minor, and M. cervicoauricularis
profundus minor) (but note that inconsistent muscle names are used between his figures and
some parts of the text, which is possibly merely a typo). In M. myotis, the two rostral muscles,
the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major and M. cervicoauricularis superficialis minor,
originate from the midline, whereas the most caudal M. cervicoauricularis profundus minor
originates from the nuchal crest [24, 25]. While the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis minor,
M. cervicoauricularis profundus minor and M. cervicoauricularis profundus major of R.
ferrumequinum, the M. cervicoauricularis profundus minor and M. cervicoauricularis
profundus major of A. tridens, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus minor of M. myotis
originate from the nuchal crest [24, 25], all three cervicoauricularis muscles originate from the
midline in C. sphinx (Fig. 1).
Phylogenetically, the yinpterochiropteran C. sphinx is more closely related to R.
ferrumequinum and A. tridens than to the yangochiropteran M. myotis. R. ferrumequinum, A.
tridens, and M. myotis are capable of laryngeal echolocation while C. sphinx lacks such ability.
Whether C. sphinx or laryngeal echolocating bats retains the ancestral condition for ear muscle
attachment pattern can be inferred by comparing them with other non-bat laurasiatherians.
Terrestrial eulipotyphlans (the common shrew Sorex araneus and European hedgehog
Erinaceus europaeus) have three components of the cervicoauricularis muscles [6, 18]. The
origin of the muscles is also from the midline; however, the origin is not from the skull as in
bats but from the midline of the dorsal side of the neck [6, 18]. A subterranean eulipotyphlan,
the hairy-tailed mole (Parascalops breweri), lacks the pinna but possesses two

cervicoauricularis muscles. The M. cervicoauricularis superficialis originates from the dorsal
midline of the neck and inserts into the ear canal, while the M. cervicoauricularis profundus
originates under the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis from the midline and inserts into the
forelimb [28]. These reports suggest that the cervicoauricularis muscles commonly originate
from the dorsal midline of the neck in eulipotyphlans. In carnivorans, felids (domestic cat Felis
catus, tiger Panthera tigris, and serval Leptailurus serval) show a cervicoauricularis muscle
pattern that is comparable to that of C. sphinx, with the same three muscles originating from
the caudal region of the midline of the skull (Fig. 1) [8, 23]. The dog (Canis lupus) also has
three muscle components of the cervicoauricularis muscles. The cervicoauricularis muscles do
not originate from the skull but from the nuchal ligament [9, 12, 13]. The nuchal ligament is
located in the midline; thus, C. lupus still follows the “midline rule” for cervicoauricularis
muscle origins. In perissodactyls, the horse (Equus caballus) and the South American tapir
(Tapirus terrestris) have three components of the cervicoauricularis muscles, all of which
originate from the insertion site of the nuchal ligament that is again in the midline [5, 29]. The
cattle (Bos taurus) also has three cervicoauricularis muscles, and the sites of the origin of these
muscles are similar to that found in perissodactyls [4].
Taken these together, the ancestral laurasiatherian condition of muscle number of
the cervicoauricularis muscle is postulated to be three. The number of cervicoauricularis
muscles of laurasiatherians is summarized in Table 1. The three-muscle condition seems to be
widely conserved among laurasiatherians, and the two-muscle condition found in the talpids is
likely to be a derived condition. We suggest that C. sphinx and M. myotis retains the ancestral
condition of laurasiatherians, i.e. the three-muscle condition, and that the four-muscle condition
found in rhinolophoids is a derived condition for laurasiatherian mammals. Our wide survey of
laurasiatherians also suggests that all of them follow the “midline rule” for the origin of the
cervicoauricularis muscles, although the origin site vary from the head or neck.
Our observations on fetal anatomy allowed us to address the homologies of the

cervicoauricularis muscles among boreoeutherian mammals. Detailed information on
innervation to the cervicoauricularis muscles is still largely lacking, but in C. lupus [9, 12, 13],
E. caballus [5], B. taurus [4], and M. musculus, three cervicoauricularis muscles (M.
cervicoauricularis superficialis, medius, and profundus) are innervated by the N.
retroauricularis [4, 5, 9]. E14.5 fetus of M. musculus in this study showed three components
of the cervicoauricularis muscles, which agreed with observations on various adult rodents
(Laonastes aenigmamus, Ctenodactylus gundi, and Chinchilla lanigera) by Zherebtsova [33].
According to Zherebtsova, in adult rodents, the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis is dorsal to
the M. cervicoauricularis medius, and its insertion is the most rostral among the three muscles.
The insertion of the M. cervicoauricularis profundus is the most caudal among the three
muscles. The insertion of the M. cervicoauricularis medius is located between those of the M.
cervicoauricularis superficialis and M. cervicoauricularis profundus. The origins of all three
muscles are from the midline. In E14.5 fetus of M. musculus, the muscles were not fully
developed. Thus, their origins were not yet from the midline, but the relative topologies of the
three muscles were comparable to those reported by Zherebtsova and also to those of M. myotis
[24] and C. sphinx. Our observation of the fetus revealed that all three cervicoauricularis
muscles of C. sphinx are innervated by the N. retroauricularis. Conventionally, there has been
no specific nomenclature for the subbranches of N. retroauricularis that innervate the three
cervicoauricularis muscles; therefore, we term the subbranch of N. retroauricularis innervating
the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis as the N. retroauricularis ramus cervicoauricularis
superficialis, the subbranch innervating the M. cervicoauricularis medius as the N.
retroauricularis ramus cervicoauricularis medius, and the subbranch innervating the M.
cervicoauricularis profundus as the N. retroauricularis ramus cervicoauricularis profundus,
respectively (Fig. 3). Given that the branch innervating the M. occipitopollicalis in bats (R.
ferrumequinum, A. tridens, and M. myotis) was referred to as the N. retroauricularis inferior
by Schneider and his co-worker [24, 25], we term the other branch innervating the M.

cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis medius, and M. cervicoauricularis
profundus as the N. retroauricularis superior (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). To our knowledge, the relative
topologies of the rami of N. retroauricularis innervating the three muscles are largely
undescribed even among domestic animals. Thus available references are poor, but our detailed
observations found that the innervation pattern of the three cervicoauricularis muscles in M.
musculus is comparable to those in C. sphinx and M. myotis (Fig. 4). Given the homogeneity of
the cervicoauricularis muscle numbers and innervation patterns among the three species, the
three muscles in C. sphinx should be considered as the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis, M.
cervicoauricularis medius, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus, as in M. musculus (Figs. 2, 3,
and 4). Schneider referred to the three muscles in M. myotis as the “M. cervicoauricularis
superficialis major,” “M. cervicoauricularis superficialis minor,” and “M. cervicoauricularis
profundus minor,” but based on our homological examination, we propose to rather use “M.
cervicoauricularis superficialis,” “M. cervicoauricularis medius,” and “M. cervicoauricularis
profundus,” respectively, following the nomenclature widely used in other mammals.
We found that the muscle number and the three-muscle condition of the
cervicoauricularis is widely conserved among therian mammals. Innervation patterns are
comparable between C. sphinx, M. myotis and M. musculus (Fig. 4), but ambiguity remains for
the rhinolophoids (R. ferrumequinum and A. tridens), which have four cervicoauricular muscles
(M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major, M. cervicoauricularis superficialis minor, M.
cervicoauricularis profundus minor, and M. cervicoauricularis profundus major) [24, 25].
Innervation to the “M. cervicoauricularis superficialis major,” “M. cervicoauricularis
superficialis minor,” and “M. cervicoauricularis profundus minor” [24, 25] appears to be
comparable to the M. cervicoauricularis superficialis, M. cervicoauricularis medius, and M.
cervicoauricularis profundus in C. sphinx, M. myotis, and M. musculus, respectively; therefore,
these should be treated as homologous. We postulate that the “M. cervicoauricularis profundus
major” of rhinolophoids, which is innervated by the N. retroauricularis inferior, should be

further studied and renamed in the future. Rhinolophoids that conduct constant-frequency
echolocation show conspicuous pinna motions as part of their biosonar behavior, which are not
found in frequency-modulated echolocating bats (the majority of yangochiropterans) or nonlaryngeal echolocating bats (all pteropodids) [31]. It is possible that the greater number of
cervicoauricularis muscles reflects their rapid and delicate pinna motions. In addition, the most
caudal or two caudal cervicoauricularis muscles originate from the nuchal crest in laryngeal
echolocators (R. ferrumequinum, A. tridens, and M. myotis), whereas all three muscles originate
from the midline in terrestrial non-bat boreoeutherian mammals and in C. sphinx. Changes in
muscle attachment site generally influences the action of muscles [17]. Therefore, we suspect
that the position of the M. cervicoauricularis profundus may be evolutionary linked to
echolocation function in bats. Further studies on ear muscles in various bats are expected to
shed further light on the function, homology, and evolution of these muscles in bats.
