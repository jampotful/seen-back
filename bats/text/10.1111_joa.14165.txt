Prenatal growth patterns of the upper jaw complex with implications for laryngeal echolocation in bats.

# Prenatal growth patterns of the upper jaw complex with implications for laryngeal echolocation in bats

## Abstract
AbstractCraniofacial morphology is extremely diversified within bat phylogeny, however growth and development of the palate in bats remains unstudied. The formation of both midline and bilateral orofacial clefts in laryngeally echolocating bats, morphologically similar to the syndromic and non‐syndromic cleft palate in humans, are not well understood. Developmental series of prenatal samples (n = 128) and adults (n = 10) of eight bat species (two pteropodids, four rhinolophoids, and two yangochiropterans), and two non‐bat mammals (Mus musculus and Erinaceus amurensis), were CT‐scanned and cranial bones forming the upper jaw complex were three‐dimensionally visualised to assess whether differences in palate development can be observed across bat phylogeny. Volumetric data of bones composing the upper jaw complex were measured to quantify palate growth. The premaxilla is relatively reduced in bats compared to other mammals and its shape is heterogeneous depending on the presence and type of orofacial cleft across bat phylogeny. The palatine process of premaxillary bones is lacking in pteropodids and yangochiropterans, whereas the premaxilla is a mobile structure which is only in contact caudally with the maxilla by a fibrous membrane or suture in rhinolophoids. In all bats, maxillary bones progressively extend caudally and palatine bones, in some cases split into three branches, extend caudally so that they are completely fused to another one medially prior to the birth. Ossification of the vomer and fusion of the maxillary and palatine bones occur earlier in rhinolophoids than in pteropodids and yangochiropterans. The vomer ossifies bilaterally from two different ossification centres in yangochiropterans, which is uncommon in other bats and non‐bat mammals. Analysis of ontogenetic allometric trajectories of the upper jaw complex revealed faster development of maxillary, vomer, and palatine bones in yangochiropterans compared to other bats, especially rhinolophoids. Ancestral state reconstruction revealed that yangochiropterans have a higher magnitude of change in ossification rate compared to other bats and E. amurensis a lower magnitude compared to M. musculus and bats. This study provides new evidence of heterochronic shifts in craniofacial development and growth across bat phylogeny that can improve understanding of the developmental differences characterising nasal and oral emission strategies.

## INTRODUCTION
Bats (Chiroptera) are divided into two major groups: yinpterochiropterans and yangochiropterans (Teeling et al., 2000, 2002, 2005; Van Den Bussche & Hoofer, 2004). Yinpterochiropterans are represented by pteropodids and rhinolophoids. Both rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans have evolved laryngeal echolocation by producing high‐frequency pulses from their larynx. Two different types (nasal vs. oral) of emission of echolocation calls have been observed in bats. Pteropodids are not able to produce such complex laryngeal echolocation calls, but several pteropodid species can use tongue clicking. Bats experience great diversity in cranial morphology which is associated with their variability in feeding and/or echolocation strategies (Arbour et al., 2019, 2021; Dumont, 2004; Freeman, 1988; Leiser‐Miller & Santana, 2020; Pedersen & Timm, 2012; Santana & Lofgren, 2013; Van Cakenberghe et al., 2002; Wilson et al., 2016). Many laryngeally echolocating bats have evolved orofacial cleft, which has been considered to resemble the congenital cleft palate observed in humans and other mammals (Frick, 1954; Giannini & Simmons, 2007b; Orr et al., 2016; Usui & Tokita, 2018; Wible & Bhatnagar, 1996). Both bilateral and midline orofacial clefts have been observed in laryngeally echolocating bats, whereas no similar bony discontinuities have been described in pteropodids. There is no clear association between the occurrence of orofacial cleft in bats and the type of echolocation emission (i.e., nasal vs. oral) or the presence of other cranial structures (e.g., vomeronasal organ, noseleaf) (Orr et al., 2016). Although the functional significance of orofacial clefting in bats is not well understood, the presence of clefting could notably enhance the interference reduction of the returning echolocation signal and the modulation of nasal acoustic emissions, reduce the overall weight of the cranium, or help with capture of larger prey (Orr et al., 2016). To date, syndromic congenital cleft palate in bats has only been reported in Pteropus conspicillatus (a pteropodid species) juveniles from a north‐eastern Australian colony (McMichael et al., 2023).
In mammals, the primary palate is formed during embryogenesis from the fusion of the paired maxillary prominences (MxP) and medial paired nasal processes (MNP) which are derived from neural crest cells (Diewert & Wang, 1992). The premaxillary bones subsequently develop representing the main bony structure of the primary palate. The secondary palate is composed of the palatine process of maxillary bones and the horizontal lamina of palatine bones. In earlier stages, both left and right maxillary bones grow as vertical palatal shelves on each side of the tongue. These palatal shelves progressively extend horizontally above the tongue and finally fuse with each other and the nasal septum to form the secondary palate, separating the oral cavity and the nasal chambers (Abramyan & Richman, 2015; Bush & Jiang, 2012; Ferguson, 1981, 1987; Moxham, 2003). Abnormal craniofacial development during palatogenesis can result in a cleft palate, a congenital deformity of the soft and/or hard palate which can be associated with a cleft lip. Non‐syndromic and syndromic cleft palate have been observed in humans and other mammals, such as rodents, carnivorans, artiodactyls, and non‐human primates (Amin et al., 1994; Davies, 2011; Fainstat, 1954; Goldschmidt et al., 2010; Leslie & Marazita, 2013; Loevy & Fenyes, 1968; Panter et al., 2000; Shaw et al., 2015; Shupe et al., 1968; Swindler & Merrill, 1971). Both genetic and environmental (e.g., maternal alcohol or drug consumption) factors can contribute to the occurrence of cleft palate in humans (Bell et al., 2014; Lan et al., 2016; Leslie & Marazita, 2013; Panter et al., 2000). Ontogenetic mechanisms and abnormalities of genes underlying syndromic and non‐syndromic cleft palates have been identified (Bush & Jiang, 2012; Carinci et al., 2003; Dixon et al., 2011; Ferguson, 1981, 1987; Greene & Pratt, 1976; Juriloff & Harris, 2008; Kohli & Kohli, 2012; Leslie & Marazita, 2013; Lesmana & Auerkari, 2016; Meng et al., 2009; Stanier & Moore, 2004). For example, particular defects in signalling of sonic hedgehog (SHH), Bmp4, and FGF genes, which play a key role in development of secondary palate during palatogenesis, have been considered as potential causes of cleft palate (Cobourne & Green, 2012; Gong & Guo, 2003; Hu & Helms, 1999; Riley et al., 2007).
Despite the potential functional significance of clefting in bats, and its possible relevance for biomedical research, little attention has been devoted to quantifying the magnitude and patterning of variation in development of the upper jaw complex (premaxilla, maxilla, vomer, and palatine) among bats. Such data may assist in interpreting the diversity in emission of laryngeal echolocation calls within bats and may further reveal insights into the debated origins and evolutionary history of bat echolocation. To date, it has not been possible to distinguish between two evolutionary scenarios: first, the common ancestor of all bats already evolved laryngeal echolocation, which was subsequently lost in pteropodids, and second, laryngeal echolocation independently evolved in both rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans (Carter & Adams, 2016; Davies et al., 2013; Hand et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Maltby et al., 2010; Nojiri, Fukui et al., 2021; Parker et al., 2013; Springer, 2013; Teeling, 2009; Teeling et al., 2000; Veselka et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2017). There has been much recent interest in the extent to which the evolution and development of cranial features linked to echolocation, such as the inner ear and larynx, may inform this debate (Brualla et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2017; Nojiri et al., 2018; Nojiri, Fukui et al., 2021a; Nojiri, Wlison et al., 2021b; Nojiri et al., 2022, 2024; Ito et al., 2021; Brualla et al., 2024; Usui et al., 2024).
Prenatal development plays a key role in the impressive morphological diversification in mammals. Changes in timing and/or rate of developmental events, known as heterochronic shifts, are considered one source of adaptive (e.g., sensory, dietary, and ecological) innovations (Bininda‐Emonds et al., 2003; Dobreva et al., 2022; Goswami, 2007; Haeckel, 1866; Hall & Hanken, 2023; Koyabu et al., 2014; Kyomen et al., 2023; McKinney & McNamara, 2013; McNamara, 2012; Webster & Zelditch, 2005). Ontogenetic allometry, the change of shape with size over the course of ontogeny, has been cited as an additional major source of morphological variation in mammals (Cardini, 2019; Goswami et al., 2012; Goswami & Prochel, 2007; López‐Aguirre et al., 2019; White et al., 2023; Wilson, 2011). Interspecific variation in ossification heterochrony (Goswami, 2007; Hautier et al., 2011; Koyabu et al., 2011, 2014; Nojiri et al., 2022; Sánchez‐Villagra et al., 2008) and in ontogenetic allometry (Cardini, 2019; Goswami et al., 2012; Goswami & Prochel, 2007; Le Verger et al., 2024; López‐Aguirre et al., 2019; Mitchell et al., 2024; White et al., 2023; Wilson, 2011; Zelditch & Swiderski, 2023). of the skull has been observed among mammalian species. Elucidating the developmental mechanisms underlying observed morphological variation has long received attention from evolutionary developmental biologists (Alberch, 1980; Arthur, 2002; Fostowicz‐Frelik & Tseng, 2023; Goswami, 2007; Goswami et al., 2022; Haeckel, 1866; Hall, 2012; Koyabu et al., 2014; Usui & Tokita, 2018; von Baer, 1828), however, interspecific variation in prenatal development is comparably less studied than in postnatal development (Carter & Adams, 2016; Geiger et al., 2014; Jin et al., 2012; Porto et al., 2013; Smith, 2006; White et al., 2023; Wilson, 2018). The molecular mechanisms underlying the prenatal development of the palate are relatively well understood in mice and humans (Bush & Jiang, 2012; Deshpande & Goudy, 2019; Li et al., 2017), but further investigations are necessary to detail palate formation in bats.
The aim of this study is to provide further insights into the prenatal development of the upper jaw complex within bat phylogeny. Specifically, crania of prenatal bat samples were imaged using micro computed tomography (microCT), and bones of the upper jaw complex were semi‐automatically segmented and visualised in 3D. We compared ontogenetic allometric trajectories of volumetric growth for each bone, to identify specific ontogenetic patterns and to assess whether differences in ossification rate occur between laryngeally echolocating (LE) and non‐laryngeally echolocating (non‐LE) bats. We reconstructed the ancestral state of ossification rate of the upper jaw complex and discussed differences in growth patterns between midline and bilateral orofacial clefts to infer the evolutionary history of echolocation strategies in bats. We hypothesise that, if laryngeal echolocation evolved in the common ancestor of all bats, ossification rate of the upper jaw complex would be similar between pteropodids, rhinolophoids, and yangochiropterans but different to non‐bat mammals. On the contrary, if laryngeal echolocation convergently evolved within the bat lineage, laryngeally echolocating bats would have a slower ossification rate of the upper jaw complex than both pteropodids and non‐bat mammals and differences in ossification rate would be observed between oral‐emitting yangochiropterans and nasal‐emitting rhinolophoids.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Data acquisition and 3D visualization
A total of 128 foetuses or newborn samples and 10 adult specimens, representing eight bat species and two non‐bat mammals, (Mus musculus and Erinaceus amurensis), are included in this study (Table S1). Bat species consist of two pteropodids (Cynopterus sphinx and Rousettus leschenaultii), four rhinolophoids (Aselliscus dongbacanus and Hipposideros larvatus, Rhinolophus pusillus and Rhinolophus thomasi), and two yangochiropterans (Pipistrellus abramus and Vespertilio sinensis). Aselliscus dongbacanus and H. larvatus are hipposiderids and Rh. pusillus and Rh. thomasi are rhinolophids. Pipistrellus abramus and V. sinensis are vespertilionids. Rhinolophus pusillus and V. sinensis were collected in Japan, approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba (Approval numbers #21–151, #22–448). Other bats were collected in Vietnam, approved by the Research Committee of the Institute of Ecology and Biological Resources, Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology (Approval numbers 352/STTNSV, 624/STTNSV, and 983/STTNSV). Since prenatal bat specimens were collected in the wild, information on gestation days is missing. The collected samples were fixed in Carnoy's fixative and subsequently preserved in 70% ethanol. Erinaceus amurensis prenatal specimens were loaned from collections at the Tokyo University of Agriculture (Koyabu et al., 2011). The adult specimen of Rh. thomasi was loaned from the National Science Museum, Thailand. The adult specimen of E. amurensis was loaned from the Kanagawa Prefectural Museum of Natural History, Japan. Mus musculus (C57BL/6 strain) specimens were loaned from the collections at the University Museum of the University of Tokyo, Japan. Each specimen was scanned using a microfocal X‐ray computed tomography (microCT) system (inspeXio SMX‐90CT Plus, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) at the University of Tsukuba with a 70 kV source voltage and 100 mA source current. The resolution of 3D scans varies from 9.6 μm to 38 μm in bat species, while the resolution was between 35.7 and 104.4 μm in E. amurensis, and between 10.3 and 24.8 μm in M. musculus (Table S1). Crania of both embryonic and adult specimens were three‐dimensionally reconstructed, and cranial bones forming the upper jaw complex (premaxilla, maxilla, vomer, and palatine) were individually semi‐automatically segmented using both threshold and brush tools in Amira 3D v2023.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Developmental series of the upper jaw complex in non‐bat mammals, pteropodids, rhinolophoids, and yangochiropterans were visualised in rostral, lateral, and ventral views (Figures 1, 2, 3). Based on the taxonomic sampling coverage, pteropodids were represented by Ro. leschenaultii, while Rh. pusillus was selected as delegate for rhinolophoids and P. abramus for yangochiropterans. A specific colour was assigned to the mask for each bone, and visualisation was aided by applying transparency (20%) to the other bony elements composing the cranium. Developmental series of the upper jaw complex of bat species not represented in Figures 1, 2, 3 are illustrated in Figures S1–S, 3 in the Supplementary Data.

## Ontogenetic trajectory analysis
Bat prenatal samples were staged based on visualization of their external morphology following the Cretekos Staging (CS) system (Cretekos et al., 2005), and M. musculus foetuses following Theiler (1989). Due to the lack of staging system for hedgehogs, crown‐rump lengths were used as an alternative for staging assessment of E. amurensis prenatal samples. The differences in ossification rate of the upper jaw complex were described by comparing allometric slope of each species. To assess changes in interspecific growth patterns, the volume of premaxillary, maxillary, vomer, and palatine bones (hard tissue) in foetuses and adults was measured from 3D meshes using the automated volume measurement tool in Amira (Table S2). The dentary length, corresponding to the distance from the most anterior edge to the most posterior portion of the condyloid process, was used as a proxy of overall cranial size (Table S2). For standardization purpose, both the bone volume and dentary length of prenatal samples were divided by those of adult specimens. Then, the volume of cranial bones was log‐ and cube root‐transformed, while the dentary length was log‐transformed to reduce the size effect between species. Ontogenetic allometric trajectories of each species were plotted and compared for all four studied bones with the R function scatterplot from the package car v3.1–2 (Fox & Weisberg, 2018). Reduced major axis regression of each bone volume ~ dentary length was performed for each species separately using the R package smatr v. 3.4–8 (Warton et al., 2012) and the function sma. The function multcomp was applied for interspecific pairwise comparisons on ontogenetic slope coefficients (Table S3) and p values (Table S4) were extracted to evaluate whether shifts in ossification rates could be detected across bat lineages.

## Ancestral state reconstruction
From Upham et al.'s maximum likelihood species‐level mammalian phylogeny (Upham et al., 2019), a phylogenetic tree matching our sampled species was created using the “cutting” function in Mesquite v3.81 and edited in FigTree. v1.4.4, retaining branch lengths (File S1). Slope coefficients from the reduced major axis regression of each bone were extracted for each species, separately, to create an allometric space using phylogenetic principal component (phy‐PCA) analysis. To estimate the number of significant principal components, a broken stick model was undertaken by generating a theoretical distribution of variance across components and comparing this distribution with the actual eigenvalues from the principal component analysis. The principal components are considered as significant when their eigenvalues are higher than the expected eigenvalues from the broken stick model (Figure S4). An allometric space is a virtual space where taxa are ordinated depending on the allometric patterns of their ontogenetic allometric trajectories (Gerber et al., 2008). A phylomorphospace was generated by projecting the phylogenetic tree into the allometric space using the function phylomorphospace from the package phytools v2.1–1 (Revell, 2024). The presence of phylogenetic signal was tested on the allometric space using the Kmult (K‐) statistic with the function phylosig (Revell, 2024). The K‐statistic is a multivariate test estimating the similitude between observed measurements across a phylogenetic tree and a Brownian motion (BM) evolution model. Slope coefficients were mapped onto the phylogenetic tree to assess the evolution of ontogenetic allometric trajectories of the upper jaw complex within bat phylogeny using the maximum likelihood ancestral state reconstruction with the function contMap (Revell, 2024). To compare the allometric trajectories of each species with the isometry, we integrated the isometric reference into the allometric space by adding a matrix (0.5,0.5,0.5,0.5) to the principal component analysis, where each vector is equal to p^(−1/2) with p corresponding to the number of variables (here, p = 4).

## RESULTS

## Anatomical description
In C. sphinx, the ossification centres of premaxillary and maxillary bones are visible from CS17, while they can only be observed from CS19 in Ro. leschenaultii (Figures 1, 2, 3). Ossification of the palatine starts in CS18 in C. sphinx and CS19 in Ro. leschenaultii. The vomer begins to ossify from a single element on the sagittal plane in CS21 in C. sphinx and in CS20 in Ro. leschenaultii. Premaxillary bones adopt a V‐shape in the ventral view since left and right ossification centres are closer ventrally than dorsally. The palatine processes of premaxilla are lacking even in adults. In both pteropodids, the palatal shelves first enlarge as they get closer to one another before taking a relatively more posterior position from CS20–21 to CS24. In contrast, the horizontal lamina of palatine bones expands rostrally. Three different extended branches of the horizontal lamina can be observed rostrally in CS20 in Ro. leschenaultii (see Figure 3). The most medial branches fuse with each other before joining the most lateral branch later in CS20. A similar pattern is visible on the rostral part of the horizontal lamina of palatine bones in C. sphinx (specimen VN17‐214). Both left and right premaxillary bones are in contact in CS24 closing ventrally the heart‐shaped incisive foramen, which is located rostrally to the palatal shelves. In both pteropodids, left and right maxillary bones, such as left and right palatine bones, fuse on the sagittal plane in CS24. The premaxilla and maxilla are in contact from CS24. Maxilla has caudally expanded in C. sphinx and Ro. leschenaultii adults but has not entirely covered laterally the horizontal lamina of palatine bones. The perpendicular lamina of palatine bones is slightly dorsally curved inward. The distance between the rostral margin of the palatal shelves of maxilla and the caudal edge of the interpalatine suture (MPL) is 14.27 mm long in C. sphinx and 18.77 mm long in Ro. leschenaultii. First tooth buds ossify from CS21 in Ro. leschenaultii and CS23 in C. sphinx.
The premaxilla and maxilla have started to ossify in CS16 in Rh. thomasi, in CS17 in Rh. pusillus, and in CS18 in H. larvatus. In A. dongbacanus, the maxilla ossifies earlier (CS18) than the premaxilla (CS19). Rhinolophus thomasi and H. larvatus exhibit palatine and vomer from CS18, whereas these bones are visible from CS19 in A. dongbacanus. The palatine ossifies earlier (CS18) than vomer (CS19) in Rh. pusillus. In all rhinolophoids, the premaxilla first ossifies in two distinct ossification centres which are later joined by a fibrous suture or fused, taking an arrow‐like shape. However, this junction or fusion occurs in different stages depending on the species. Both left and right premaxillary bones are fused in CS18 in H. larvatus, in CS20 in Rh. pusillus, and in CS22 in A. dongbacanus. The premaxilla in Rh. thomasi is characterised by the absence of fusion on the sagittal plane but left and right premaxillary bones are in contact by a fibrous suture from CS19. The palatal shelves of maxilla are in contact in CS22 in Rh. pusillus and A. dongbacanus. They are still not in contact in CS24 in both H. larvatus and Rh. thomasi. In all rhinolophoids, the left and right palatine bones are in contact from CS22. The caudal portion of the palatine process of the premaxilla is wider than its rostral portion except for A. dongbacanus. This process is relatively long in both Rhinolophus species and short in A. dongbacanus and H. larvatus. Laterally, the premaxilla is not in contact with the maxilla in adults forming a bilateral (also named paramedian) cleft. Ventrally, the rostral edge of maxilla is forming a U‐shape. The premaxilla and maxilla are caudally articulated via a sutural joint in A. dongbacanus and H. larvatus but are not in contact in Rh. pusillus and Rh. thomasi. The perpendicular lamina of palatine bones is dorsally curved inward. Incisive foramina are continuous with the bilateral cleft. MPL is relatively short, varying between 1.64 mm in A. dongbacanus and 3.70 mm in H. larvatus. Tooth buds start to ossify in different stages depending on the species. They can be observed from stage CS18 in H. larvatus, stage CS19 in Rh. thomasi, stage CS20 in Rh. pusillus, and stage CS21 in A. dongbacanus.
In P. abramus, premaxillary, maxillary, and palatine bones have started to ossify in CS19 while premaxillary and maxillary bones can be observed from CS17 and palatine bones from CS18 in V. sinensis. In both yangochiropterans, vomer ossifies in CS21 from two bilateral elements, which later fuse on the sagittal plane in CS22. Left and right premaxillary bones will independently develop and remain separated in adults forming a midline cleft. The incisive fissure is continuous to the cleft. The palatal shelves of maxillary bones are still not in contact in CS24 but are fused in adults. The rostral portion of the palatal shelves of maxillary bones is U‐shaped. Premaxilla and maxilla are latero‐caudally in contact from CS22. In both yangochiropterans, the interpalatine suture is closing in CS24, initially more rostrally, then the suture is completely closed all along the sagittal plane in adults. The perpendicular lamina of the palatine bones is slightly dorsally curved inward. Maxilla has latero‐caudally extended in adults but has not completely covered laterally the horizontal lamina of palatine bones. MPL is 4.74 mm long in P. abramus and 6.43 mm long in V. sinensis. Tooth buds are visible from CS22.
The premaxilla and maxilla represent the two first cranial bones to ossify shortly after the dentary in all bat groups, however, variation in ossification timing can be observed both between and within the different bat groups (pteropodids, rhinolophoids, and yangochiropterans). The palatine bones ossify either at the same stage as the premaxilla and maxilla or slightly later. The vomer constitutes the last bone to ossify, especially in pteropodids and yangochiropterans. In M. musculus, the maxilla ossifies slightly earlier than the premaxilla and palatine in E15.5 and the paired vomer bones ossify in E16.5. In E. amurensis, the maxilla and palatine are the first cranial bones to ossify. Ossification of the premaxillary and vomer bones has started prior to the crown‐rump length reaching 33.26 mm. It can be assumed that the premaxilla has started to ossify over a considerable amount of time since the maxilla is in contact with the premaxilla and palatine, and both nasal and palatine processes of the premaxilla are developed when CRL is 33.26 mm. The palatal shelves of the maxilla fuse on the sagittal plane earlier (CS22) in A. dongbacanus and Rh. pusillus than in H. larvatus, Rh. thomasi, pteropodids and yangochiropterans (CS24). The horizontal laminae of the palatine fuse earlier (CS22) in rhinolophoids than in pteropodids and yangochiropterans (CS24). In M. musculus, the palatal shelves of the maxilla and the horizontal laminae of the palatine are still not fused on the sagittal plane in newborns (P1), while the horizontal laminae of the palatine are fused when CRL is 33.26 mm, and the palatal shelves of the maxilla are nearly completely fused when CRL is 50.83 in E. amurensis. Despite its different morphology in bat lineages, the premaxilla first ossifies in two distinct ossification centres in all bats but also in both non‐bat mammals. The two ossification centres of the premaxilla fuse earlier (CS18–22) in rhinolophoids than in pteropodids (CS24), while they don't fuse in yangochiropterans. The right and left premaxillary bones fuse in E17.5 in M. musculus and when CRL is 33.26 in E. amurensis. Each premaxilla carries one incisor in rhinolophoids and two incisors in pteropodids and yangochiropterans. Mus musculus has a pair of developed incisors, while E. amurensis has three pairs of incisors on the premaxilla. The vomer ossifies in a unique ossification centre in M. musculus, E. amurensis, and all bats except for yangochiropterans in which the vomer ossifies in two symmetrical ossification centres. The interdigitation of the maxilloincisive suture is ventrally more complex in M. musculus than in E. amurensis and all bats. The right and left incisive foramina are separated in non‐bat mammals, while they are connected in pteropodids or rostrally opened in rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans forming a cleft palate. Both M. musculus and E. amurensis have anterior palatine foramina, contrary to all bats. In M. musculus, the palatine processes of the premaxilla and maxilla are fused with vomer separating both anterior palatine foramina. In E. amurensis, the anterior palatine foramina are situated on the ventral portion of the maxilla‐palatine suture and do not include the middle palatine foramina, which are more rostrally located.

## Ontogenetic allometry
Pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences in ontogenetic allometric trajectories for each bone between species (Figure 4; Table S4). In particular, V. sinensis, E. amurensis, H. larvatus, Rh. thomasi, and P. abramus, showed the greatest numbers of significant pairwise differences in growth among species in our sample (Table S4). Growth of the premaxilla (Figure 4(a)) in H. larvatus, Rh. thomasi, and E. amurensis is significantly slower than in other bats and M. musculus (p <0.0009). However, there is no significant difference in premaxilla growth between these three species (p >0.05). Both yangochiropterans, V. sinensis and P. abramus, exhibit a relatively faster ossification rate of the maxilla (Figure 4(b)). Indeed, V. sinensis is significantly different from other species (p <0.041), except for P. abramus (p >0.05), and P. abramus is significantly different from Rh. pusillus (p = 0.017), Rh. thomasi (p = 0.024), Ro. leschenaultii (p = 0.003), and E. amurensis (p = 0.0001). ## Evolution of ontogenetic allometric trajectories
Ontogenetic allometry is illustrated in a phylomorphospace from a principal component analysis (Figure 5). Comparisons between eigenvalues of the four principal components and the broken stick model show that only the two first principal components, comprising 86.6% of the sample variance, are significant (Figure S4). The first two principal components accounted for 61.7% and 25.1% of variance in the allometric space, respectively. The positive scores of PC1 are correlated with a faster growth of all four bones: premaxilla (31.6% of variance), vomer (28.6%), palatine (20.0%), and maxilla (12.1%). The positive scores of PC2 are correlated with a faster growth of premaxilla (22.10% of variance) but a slower growth of maxilla (5.34%), palatine (1.67%), and vomer (21.0%). The multivariate test of phylogenetic signal in allometric space yielded a K‐statistic of 0.471 (p = 0.218). In the allometric space, E. amurensis represents the closest point and V. sinensis the most distant point from the isometric reference (Figure S5). Erinaceus amurensis is characterised by slower growth of the premaxilla, maxilla, and vomer in PC1 and slower growth of the vomer in PC2. Hipposideros larvatus and Rh. thomasi are defined by slower growth of the premaxilla in both PC1 and PC2. Vespertilio sinensis is characterised by a faster growth of the maxilla, vomer, and palatine in PC1. Other species have a relatively average growth rate of the upper jaw complex. The ancestral state reconstruction (Figure 6) reveals that, for PC1, yangochiropterans exhibit a higher magnitude of change in ossification rate (0.922 for V. sinensis and 0.301 for P. abramus), compared to yinpterochiropterans or non‐bat mammals. Erinaceus amurensis has a lower magnitude of change in ossification rate (−0.843) in comparison with all other species. Among bats, H. larvatus and Rh. thomasi have a lower magnitude of change in ossification rate than other bats (−0.3 and − 0.712, respectively). For PC2, V. sinensis (−0.536) and H. larvatus (−0.515) have a lower magnitude of change in ossification rate. However, as the positive scores of PC2 are correlated with slower growth, this result indicates that the vomer grows faster in these species than the others. Mus musculus (0.323), Ro. leschenaultii (0.352), and A. dongbacanus (0.327) have a higher magnitude of change in ossification rate for PC2.

## DISCUSSION
This study provides a comprehensive overview of prenatal development of the bones composing the upper jaw complex across bat lineages. Our results show that bones composing the upper jaw complex ossify in the same order in bats and non‐bat mammals, although the premaxilla ossifies slightly later (CRL = 33.26) than palatine (CRL = 15.28) in E. amurensis (Figures 1, 2, 3). Growth patterns of the upper jaw complex are relatively similar in pteropodids and E. amurensis, however, the palatine process of premaxillary bones is absent in pteropodids since the premaxilla and maxilla are not fused to each other on the sagittal plane. Anatomical descriptions revealed several similarities in ossification timing of the upper jaw complex in bats. Premaxillary and maxillary bones are the first bones of the upper jaw complex to ossify (from CS16 to CS19) despite some differences of ossification timing between bat species (Figure 7), which is consistent with the general ossification sequence in mammals (Koyabu et al., 2014). Palatine ossification timing is relatively similar (CS18‐19) across bat phylogeny (Figure 7). The palatine shelves of maxillary bones are progressively extending caudally and the horizontal lamina of palatine bones extending rostrally, later fusing to each other and forming the secondary palate, which separates the oral cavity and nasal chambers. Bats do not exhibit palatine foramina in contrary to the non‐bat mammals M. musculus and E. amurensis. The horizontal lamina extends rostrally into three different branches in pteropodids. Analysing the first phases of palatine growth (between CS18 and CS21) of further prenatal individuals in both rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans could confirm whether this pattern is specific to this group or common to all bats as horizontal laminae have considerably grown between the first and second earlier stages in which palatine appears in these groups. The complete closure of the secondary palate occurs relatively late (CS24) on the sagittal plane where both intermaxillary and interpalatine sutures make contact. Although our sampling of stage 24 in H. larvatus and Rh. thomasi does not illustrate the fusion of the palatal shelves of the maxilla, we hypothesise that the maxillary bones should still reach midline and be fused prior to birth. A better sampling of this stage or the addition of newborn specimens could provide more details and confirm our hypothesis.
Ossification timing of the bones composing the upper jaw complex in bats. Timing of fusion of the paired maxillary and palatine bones is also indicated. Stages are based on the Creketos staging system (CS) in bats (Cretekos et al., 2005).
In parallel, different strategies in timing of the first appearance of the palate ossification centre can be observed between rhinolophoids and both pteropodids and yangochiropterans. A particular feature observed in yangochiropterans is the bilateral ossification of the vomer which was also reported in some mammals (e.g., cats, dogs, boars, opossums, and humans), but not yet in other bats (Terry, 1909; Beer, 1937). Generally, as it is visible in other bats, M. musculus, and E. amurensis, the vomer ossifies as a unique element visible in the sagittal plane (Beer, 1937; Goswami & Prochel, 2007). This feature is associated with a slower ossification rate of vomer in V. sinensis, but not in P. abramus. Additional time could be required to form the vomer if the ossification centres are originally unfused compared with the case when the vomer arises from a single element. In terms of overall developmental stage of bats, ossification of the vomer occurs during earlier stages in rhinolophoids (CS18‐19) than in pteropodids and yangochiropterans (CS20‐21; see Figure 7). However, the vomer ossifies around a similar size range (dentary length between 3.22 and 5.39 mm in rhinolophoids, between 4.54 and 6.74 mm in pteropodids, and between 2.97 and 4.06 mm in yangochiropterans). Palatal shelves of maxillary bones in Rh. pusillus and A. dongbacanus fuse in CS22, while they are fused in CS24 at the earliest in other bats present in this study (Figure 7). The left and right portions of the horizontal lamina of the palatine bones are fused earlier in rhinolophoids (CS22) compared with in pteropodids and yangochiropterans (CS24). These heterochronic shifts in ossification timing in rhinolophoids may be associated with the evolutionary shortening of the palatal length. Indeed, rhinolophoids are characterised by a shorter distance between the rostral margin of the palatal shelves of maxilla and the caudal edge of the interpalatine suture compared to pteropodids and yangochiropterans. Morphological differences in prenatal development between taxa influence morphological variations in later stages (Werneburg & Geiger, 2017). Relations between early developmental timing and a larger size of bones in adults have been demonstrated in squamates and domestic carnivores (Werneburg et al., 2015; Werneburg & Geiger, 2017). Heterochronic shifts of ontogenetic allometric trajectories have already been documented for the petrosal bone and postcranium in bats suggesting two independent origins of laryngeal echolocation within bat phylogeny (Hockman et al., 2009; Koyabu & Son, 2014; López‐Aguirre et al., 2019; Nojiri, Fukui et al., 2021, Nojiri et al., 2022, Nojiri et al., 2023). However, heterochronic shifts are challenging to interpret because they can yield conflicting or different results depending on the question and the sampling of the study. Foot bones in bats develop earlier than carpal ones yet remain proportionally much smaller in adults compared with other mammals (Hockman et al., 2009; Koyabu & Son, 2014; Nojiri et al., 2023). On the other hand, petrosal bones of laryngeally echolocating bats initiate their ossification earlier and are enlarged in comparison with non‐laryngeally echolocating mammals (Nojiri, Fukui et al., 2021). Morphological differences of the palate, especially palatal width, in earlier stages of development (CS≤18) in phyllostomids contribute to the great diversity of palatal shape observed in adult specimens in this group (Sears, 2014). Ossification rate of palate width and length does not significantly differ in phyllostomids except for Artibeus jamaicensis (Sears, 2014). Laryngeal echolocation may have constrained craniofacial morphology especially the upper jaw and the nasal chambers. The morphology of the palate plays a crucial role in both oral and nasal emission since the palate separates the oral cavity and the nasal chambers (Pedersen, 1993, 1998). Thus, allometric variation in palatal growth could provide clues about morphological differences in adults between nasal and oral emitters. Being nasal emitters, rhinolophoids could benefit from increased airflow volume passing through their nasal chambers to emit sound pulses from the larynx to the nostrils because of their short palate. The nasal chambers are interpreted as “resonators”, assisted by ventroflexion of the skull during flight for a better control of the directionality of echolocation calls with the mobile premaxilla and nose‐leaves, in contrast with oral emitters, such as yangochiropterans, in which the skull is dorsoflexed (Gessinger et al., 2021; Pedersen, 1993, 1995, 1998; Pedersen & Müller, 2013). Size differences of snout and nose‐leaves, as well as overall volume of nasal chambers, are thought to be related to echolocation functions, especially echolocation call frequency (Armstrong, 2002; Armstrong & Coles, 2007). Frequency of echolocation calls from fossil bat specimens have been estimated based on palatal length, which correlated with the harmonic filtering (Hand & Archer, 2005; Pedersen, 2000; Suthers et al., 1988; Wilson et al., 2016). The anatomy of nasal turbinates situated in the nasal chambers can also affect the acoustic peculiarity of the echolocation pulse (Eiting et al., 2014; Eiting et al., 2015; Ito et al., 2021). In most of rhinolophoids, the premaxilla is “moveable” since it is only in contact with the maxilla by a small fibrous caudal suture. This “moveable” premaxilla is interpreted as capable of modulating nasal emission of echolocation calls in association with nose‐leaves (Orr et al., 2016). Our study shows oral emitters experience a faster growth of the palate compared to nasal emitters. Nasal emitters need more time to form their shorter but more complex palate than oral emitters. However, palatal growth of phyllostomids and nycterids, which are nasal emitter yangochiropterans, should be analysed to confirm whether differences in palatal growth between yangochiropterans and rhinolophoids result of their different echolocation strategies or their phylogenetic position. Finally, studying postnatal growth of the palate could provide more information on the development of echolocation capabilities. This is because newborn bats are only able to produce isolated calls and barely hear echolocation echoes, and only later develop more sophisticated calls until reaching adulthood (Brown & Grinnell, 1980; Hiryu & Riquimaroux, 2011; Monroy et al., 2011; Vater et al., 2003). The cranium, but also the larynx, morphologically differs between bat neonates and adults and craniofacial features can still form or become more complex during postnatal development which could impact the emission of echolocation calls explaining the shift from isolated calls to more sophisticated calls (Nojiri et al., 2024).
It is generally considered that the premaxilla ossifies from a pair of bilateral ossification centres in mammals during the early foetal period (O'Rahilly & Gardner, 1972; Parker, 1997a, 1997b; Woo, 1949). However, the homological understanding of the premaxilla was recently questioned (Higashiyama et al., 2021; Higashiyama et al., 2023). Higashiyama et al. (2021), and later Higashiyama et al. (2023), alternatively suggested that the premaxilla is formed from two pairs of ossification centres (a pair of lateral bodies and a pair of palatine processes) in mammals. Our study shows that premaxilla ossifies from a unique pair of ossification centres in bats. The premaxilla is greatly reduced in bats compared to other mammals with the exception of a few special cases such as glyptodonts (Giannini & Simmons, 2007b; Wible & Novacek, 1988). The morphology of the premaxilla is highly diverse within bat lineages. Variation in premaxilla morphology in bats mainly results in the absence of an interincisive suture between premaxillary bones or fusion between the premaxilla and maxilla forming either midline or bilateral patterns of orofacial cleft (Giannini & Simmons, 2007b). According to our study, the palatine process of the premaxilla is lacking in both pteropodids and yangochiropterans (Figure 3). Premaxillary bones are not in contact in yangochiropterans forming a midline cleft. In rhinolophoids, the lateral body of the premaxilla are lacking and the premaxilla is not laterally in contact with the maxilla forming a bilateral cleft. The faster ossification rate of the premaxilla in bats, except in Rh. thomasi and H. larvatus, compared to E. amurensis could be induced by the absence of the palatine or lateral body of the premaxilla in bats. The morphology of the premaxilla is extremely diversified across bat lineages (Giannini & Simmons, 2007b). Our results show that the premaxilla, with the vomer, represents the most variable bone of the upper jaw complex in ossification rate in bats. This incredible variation of premaxilla morphology in bats is used for taxonomic comparisons at species level (Giannini & Simmons, 2007b; Miller, 1907) and can be explained by shifts in ossification rate between species from the same group, especially in rhinolophoids. Differences in ossification rate can contribute to variations in bone morphological complexity. Indeed, a slower ossification rate, as observed in Rh. thomasi and H. larvatus, would be necessary to form a premaxilla with a more complex shape. For example, the medial flange of the palatine process of the premaxilla is proportionally wider in H. larvatus than in other rhinolophoids. In addition, the lateral flanges of the palatine processes are short in Rh. pusillus, whereas they are relatively elongated in Rh. thomasi.
In both clefting patterns, the inner space of the cleft is filled with a translucent and fibrous membrane (Orr et al., 2016). A fibrous membrane requires less time to form than a bony element. For example, a premaxilla completely fused laterally with the maxilla, as in pteropodids, would grow slowly than a bat premaxilla exhibiting an orofacial cleft with a fibrous membrane, such as in rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans. However, no significant distinction of ossification rate of the premaxilla was reported between these groups. Rhinolophus thomasi and H. larvatus do not have particular features concerning the fibrous membrane of the cleft that could explain the slower ossification rate of their premaxilla. This fibrous membrane is not present in the syndromic bilateral or midline cleft palate in humans (Orr et al., 2016). Orofacial clefts are rostrally situated to the incisive foramina in bats contrary to non‐bat mammals in which cleft palate extends caudally to the incisive foramina on the sagittal plane between both palatal shelves (Amin et al., 1994; Davies, 2011; Fainstat, 1954; Goldschmidt et al., 2010; Leslie & Marazita, 2013; Loevy & Fenyes, 1968; Panter et al., 2000; Shaw et al., 2015; Shupe et al., 1968; Swindler & Merrill, 1971), however, this clefting condition can be observed in humans (Dixon et al., 2011; Kernahan & Stark, 1958; Orr et al., 2016). The bat premaxilla supports a smaller number of incisors than in E. amurensis which has three incisors on each premaxilla. Generally, the premaxilla carries two incisors in pteropodids and yangochiropterans and only one extremely reduced incisor in rhinolophoids. However, this does not correspond to a general pattern, as exceptions exist in each group, such as megadermatids, which are characterised by an edentulous premaxilla, or the vespertilionid Nycticeius, which has only one incisor on each premaxilla (Giannini & Simmons, 2007a; Nowak, 1999; Watkins, 1972). The morphology and number of teeth on the maxilla are also relatively variable between bat species, which depend on different dietary strategies. The timing of tooth calcification does not seem to follow a specific pattern in bats since the stage in which tooth buds are first visible is heterogeneous in each group (Figures 1, 2, 3). We consider that the number of incisors does not influence the ossification rate of the premaxilla in bats since incisors represent a limited portion of the overall volume of premaxilla and no significant differences in ossification rate of the premaxilla were reported, firstly, between Rh. pusillus and A. dongbacanus, then between pteropodids and yangochiropterans which exhibit different number of incisors on the premaxilla.
Ontogenetic allometric trajectories of the upper jaw complex highlighted that yangochiropterans, P. abramus and V. sinensis, exhibit a faster ossification rate of the maxillary, vomer, and palatine bones compared to pteropodids, rhinolophoids, and non‐bat mammals. Pteropodids and rhinolophoids, both representing yinpterochiropterans, share similar ossification rate of bones composing the upper jaw complex despite their morphological differences and the presence of bilateral cleft in rhinolophoids. The slower premaxilla growth of Rh. thomasi and H. larvatus constitutes the only significant variation in yinpterochiropteran palate ossification rate. The growth of the upper jaw complex is heterogeneous between rhinolophoids, especially between Rh. pusillus and Rh. thomasi, which are closely related. Rhinolophus thomasi has a relatively high magnitude of change in ossification rate compared to other rhinolophoids. Both Rh. thomasi and H. larvatus are separated with Rh. pusillus and A. dongbacanus in the phylomorphospace, which is mainly explained by their significantly lower ossification rate of premaxillary bones. Their magnitude of change in ossification rate is relatively higher compared to other bats. The growth of the upper jaw complex of E. amurensis is significantly slower, especially the premaxilla, maxilla, and vomer, compared to M. musculus and all bats present in this study. The ancestral state reconstruction concluded the slow growth of the upper jaw complex in E. amurensis does not represent the condition of the ancestor of all bats but seems to be particular to this species.
The molecular and genetic mechanisms behind the craniofacial morphology in bats were studied by Usui and Tokita (2018). They considered that heterochronic events in skull ossification would contribute to small‐scale changes, such as midline clefts in yangochiropterans, in craniofacial morphology during mammalian evolution while large scale changes, such as bilateral clefts in rhinolophoids, are related to modification of growth pattern of the facial primordium during the earlier phase of embryogenesis (Usui & Tokita, 2018). Our results revealed that heterochronic shifts in ossification timing or rate of premaxilla does not greatly contribute to the formation of bilateral cleft since premaxilla ossification rate is similar in pteropodids and rhinolophoids (except for Rh. thomasi and H. larvatus), and M. musculus. These results are consistent with Usui and Tokita's (2018) hypothesis concerning the bilateral cleft. However, these authors considered midline clefts to result from shorter and/or delayed ossification of the premaxilla compared to the common ancestor, which was not observed in this study. No heterochronic shifts were noticed in ossification timing or rate of premaxilla in bats exhibiting a midline cleft. In contrary, heterochronic shifts in timing or rate were observed in the other bones composing the upper jaw complex (maxilla, palatine, and vomer). Differences in the gene Pax9 and Bmp expression have contributed to the radiation of craniofacial morphological diversity in bats (Camacho et al., 2021; Phillips et al., 2013) and were noticed in palatogenesis in mammals (Baek et al., 2011; Li et al., 2019). In addition, a greater repetition of glutamine and alanine amino acids (Q/A ratio) within RUNX2 gene, which also contribute to craniofacial development, is associated with a shorter face in phyllostomid bats (Ferraz et al., 2018), which contrasts with other mammals, such as carnivorans or primates, in which Q/A ratio is positively correlated with the lengthening of the face (Ferraz et al., 2018; Kashi & King, 2006; Sears et al., 2007; Usui & Tokita, 2018). However, phyllostomid bats with higher Q/A ratio appear to have a larger palate (Ferraz et al., 2018). Variation in rate and duration of cell proliferation during craniofacial development has been examined in phyllostomid bats (Camacho, 2020; Camacho et al., 2020). It appears to be an important mechanism of evolutionary diversification of craniofacial morphology in this group as heterochronic shifts were observed between short‐ and long‐faced species. Indeed, short‐faced bats experience a faster cell proliferation than long‐faced bats.
To conclude, this study documents different developmental strategies between rhinolophoids and yangochiropterans in both ossification timing and rate of the upper jaw complex and provides some of the first evidence of heterochronic shifts in palatal development within bat phylogeny. No significant difference in growth patterns of the palate was observed between laryngeally echolocating and non‐laryngeally echolocating bats. This study could serve as a stepping stone to future research to understand developmental mechanisms related to the diversity of craniofacial morphology and the evolution of laryngeal echolocation and diet in bats. The function of the palate in emission of echolocation calls and the genetic and molecular mechanisms behind the palatal development in bats were not analysed in this study. A more complete staging coverage and a larger bat species sample could refine our results and thus, along with genetic and molecular data, serve to address the function of the palate in emission of echolocation. Yangochiropterans are only represented by two members of the vespertilionid family. Studying the prenatal development of the upper jaw complex in members of other families (e.g. miniopterids, mormoopids, and phyllostomids which notably possess a vomeronasal organ) within yangochiropterans could provide more evidence confirming whether ontogenetic patterns observed in P. abramus and V. sinensis can be attributed to all yangochiropteran representatives or are only restricted to vespertilionids.
