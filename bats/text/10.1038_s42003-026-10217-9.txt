Horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon) suppress clutter noise through echolocation frequency control to detect prey.

# Horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon) suppress clutter noise through echolocation frequency control to detect prey

## Abstract
AbstractEfficient information acquisition is essential for survival, and the adaptive adjustment of self-generated signals in active sensing species offers profound insights into how animals solve sensory challenges. Horseshoe bats lower the frequency of their echolocation calls during flight so that echoes remain stable at a reference frequency (fref) despite Doppler shifts. Here we reveal a previously unrecognized function: it not only aligns echoes with the most sensitive frequency range but also suppresses background clutter noise to enhance prey detection. Using phantom echo playbacks and on-board recordings, we show that bats compensate for the highest-frequency echoes rather than the strongest ones. This shifts clutter echoes below fref, leaving a “silent spectral window” above it. Furthermore, recordings during prey capture and noise playback experiments show that spectral glints from fluttering moth wings appear in this window and are exploited for prey detection, illustrating how sensory systems are shaped for reliable information extraction in cluttered environments.Subject terms: Behavioural ecology, Navigation

## Introduction
Acquiring information efficiently through sensory inputs is an essential adaptation for animal survival. Animals with active sensory systems that emit their own signals often adaptively optimize the design and use of these signals according to the context. For example, bats and dolphins are well known to increase the emission rate of their echolocation calls in the final phase of prey capture, thereby improving localization accuracy and update rate1–3.
Some bat species forage in acoustically cluttered environments, such forest margins or near vegetation. Among them, High Duty Cycle (HDC) bats have evolved specialized echolocation for detecting prey fluttering4. They emit long-duration calls with short silent intervals, combining constant frequency (CF) and frequency-modulated (FM) components5,6. When a CF signal reflects from a moving object, it induces a Doppler shift, making the echo especially informative about prey flutter or object motion7. HDC bats are adapted to detect such shifts in weak echoes, with auditory sensitivity sharply tuned to a narrow frequency range—known as the acoustic fovea—centered around the CF component of their calls8. During flight, however, Doppler shift also arise from bat’s own movement. To counter this, HDC bats lower the frequency of their emitted pulses so that returning echoes remain constant at a reference frequency, fref, which corresponds to the center frequency of the acoustic fovea. This behavior, Doppler shift compensation (DSC), is one of the most iconic and extensively investigated phenomena in bat research and widely regarded as a hallmark of their remarkable biosonar sophistication9–14.
This neural-behavioral specialization provides a powerful model for studying efficient information-acquisition strategies in animals. However, despite extensive research, some aspects of DSC remain unresolved, particularly because previous work on DSC during prey pursuit highlighted a paradox. Although DSC is generally interpreted as a strategy to stabilize returning echoes within the acoustic fovea, studies examining prey-attack situations—where prey echoes are reasonably considered the most relevant signals—have reported a notable finding. By comparing the bats’ relative velocities with prey and background walls and the amount of the compensated frequency shifts, these studies inferred which echoes served as the targets of Doppler shift compensation. The results demonstrated that bats do not compensate for Doppler shifts in prey echoes but instead compensate for echoes originating from stationary background objects such as walls7,15,16. If DSC merely served to control returning echo frequency within the fixed region of peak sensory sensitivity, it should target the echoes of prey—the most relevant signals. Yet, this has not been observed and could be interpreted as paradoxical in the context of DSC. We therefore considered that DSC may serve a broader strategic role and investigated its functional significance to gain a deeper understanding of DSC. Specifically, we asked whether DSC might have an additional, previously overlooked function beyond maintaining echoes within the auditory fovea. Our results suggest that, by performing DSC, bats suppress background echoes originating from their own emitted pulses below a certain frequency range, potentially functioning as a physical strategy that secures a clutter-free, effectively silent spectral band for detecting prey-induced glints. These findings provide direct evidence for an overlooked function of DSC in substantially improving the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) by exploiting the physical properties of echoes. Thus, HDC bats appear to use DSC not only as a neural specialization but also in a manner that illustrates how active sensing systems can transcend physiological limits by leveraging physical principles, with implications that extend beyond bats.

## Results

## Clarifying the echo selection criteria in DSC behavior
To address the central question of why bats do not compensate for prey echoes, we examined the echo selection rules underlying DSC. In natural flight, bats encounter numerous echoes of varying intensity and Doppler shift, arriving from different directions and distances, and must choose one for DSC. We therefore asked which acoustic feature bats prioritize for DSC when selecting the target echo. Based on previous studies showing that DSC is often referenced to echoes from stationary background objects such as walls15, we first considered the possibility that bats rely on the strongest-intensity echo, typically provide the most reliable sensory input. Alternatively, from the perspective of Doppler processing, the most strongly Doppler-shifted echo—namely, the echo with the highest frequency—may represent the most informative signal. We therefore hypothesized that bats base DSC either on the strongest intensity echo or on the echo with the highest frequency. To test this, we performed a real-time playback experiment in four individuals. The bats’ calls were captured, processed to manipulate intensity and frequency, and played back as phantom echoes through tweeters (Fig. 1A). We first examined two control conditions: a constant Doppler shift and a periodic Doppler shift. Under the constant shift condition, the playback stimulus was processed with a fixed +1.5 kHz frequency shift, and bats consistently lowered their call frequency, demonstrating a clear DSC response. Under the periodic shift condition, where the frequency shift varied sinusoidally between 0 and +1.5 kHz over a 5 s cycle, the bats exhibited a corresponding periodic modulation in their call frequency. Although DSC is known to exhibit a finite and relatively slow response dynamic17, and the temporal stability of Doppler shifts could therefore influence target selection, these results indicate that a 5 s modulation is sufficiently stable to be tracked by the DSC system and thus remains a valid target. Having confirmed that the bats showed stable DSC responses to the control stimuli, we then introduced the multiple Doppler shift condition to test how they select target echoes. This stimulus consisted of five virtual echo streams that differed in intensity and frequency shift. The maximum-intensity echo stream varied sinusoidally between 0 and +1.5 kHz with a 5 s period, matching the periodic Doppler-shift control condition described above. In addition, four lower-intensity echo streams ( − 20 dB relative to the maximum-intensity stream) were superimposed. These streams also varied sinusoidally between 0 and + 1.5 kHz over a 5 s cycle, but their phases were offset such that the +1.5 kHz peak occurred sequentially at 1 s intervals (i.e., phase offsets of 360°/5 = 72°). This configuration generated a composite echo pattern in which the Doppler shift continuously changed across the five streams. When referenced to the maximum-intensity echo stream, the Doppler shift varied periodically with a 5 s cycle. In contrast, when referenced to the highest-frequency echo (20 dB weaker), one of the five streams always remained near +1.5 kHz, effectively approximating a constant-shift condition. This stimulus design enable us to test which echo characteristic governs DSC behavior. If DSC is intensity-driven, compensation should track the maximum-intensity echoes and vary periodically. Conversely, if DSC is frequency-driven, compensation should follow the highest-frequency echoes and remain stable. The outcome was clear. Instead of modulating their calls with the sinusoidal shift, bats consistently lowered their call frequency (Fig. 1B, see also Supplementary Fig 1). Thus, bats compensate not for the strongest intensity echoes, but for those with the highest frequency.

## On-board microphones recording of actual echoes perceived by bats
Although phantom echo experiments allow highly flexible stimulus design, they are constrained by artificial conditions. We therefore conducted free-flight experiments using the same four individuals to measure the echoes bats actually receive, in order to validate the plausibility of the findings obtained in the phantom echo experiments. When the sound reflectivity of all walls in the flight chamber is uniform, echo intensity depends primarily on pulse emission direction, which generally aligns with the flight path producing the largest Doppler shifts. Consequently, maximum-intensity echoes and highest-frequency echoes often coincide. To disrupt this relationship and reproduce conditions similar to the playback experiments, in which maximum-intensity echoes do not necessarily correspond to the highest-frequency echoes, two acoustically distinct wall types, “echoic walls” and “quiet walls,” were arranged alternately within the flight chamber (Fig. 2A). The echoic walls were positioned inside the flight area and left reflective, with sound-absorbing foam removed. In contrast, the quiet walls were covered with sound-absorbing foam and placed outside the flight space, separated by the flight nets. Under this configuration, sounds passing through the nets underwent additional propagation distance relative to reflections from the echoic walls, resulting in attenuation, and were further reduced upon reflection by the absorbing surface of the foams. Echoes originating from the nets were considered sufficiently weak compared with those from the reflective walls. When bats flew within the square flight space, they typically followed trajectories approximating circular flight. Consequently, bats sequentially oriented toward alternating echoic-wall and quiet-wall regions, generating high-intensity echoes from the echoic walls and low-intensity echoes from the quiet walls in an alternating manner. This design aimed to independently manipulate echo intensity and Doppler-shifted frequency, creating conditions where maximum-intensity echoes did not necessarily coincide with highest-frequency echoes. To measure the echoes actually perceived by bats while flying in the chamber under this configuration, a custom-built on-board microphone was mounted on the bats’ back during circular flight. These recordings confirmed that we successfully obtained echo sequences in which the maximum-intensity echoes did not necessarily coincide with the highest-frequency echoes, and further revealed that bats consistently compensated for the highest-frequency echoes, even when those echoes were weak (Fig. 2B, see also Supplementary Fig 2). This on-board recordings, therefore, confirmed that the DSC strategy observed in real-time playback also occurs under naturalistic flight conditions. Because the largest Doppler shifts typically correspond to targets located in the forward direction, the results of the free-flight experiments alone might also permit an alternative interpretation—that bats preferentially compensated for frontal echoes rather than for the highest-frequency echoes per se. However, in the playback experiments, binaural intensity and timing cues were controlled at same level across all phantom echo stimuli, eliminating directional (left–right or frontal) information. Taken together, these two experimental approaches therefore support the interpretation that DSC is primarily guided by the highest-frequency echoes. Notably, high-quality recordings revealed a distinct spectral structure: the frequencies above the reference frequency fref were markedly quiet, in contrast to the many prominent echoes below fref. This spectral silence arises as a direct physical consequence of compensating for the highest-frequency echoes. The result was a sharply defined “silent spectral window”, suggesting that bats may use DSC to actively create such a band for prey detection. To address this question, we conducted further experiments examining DSC behavior in the context of prey capture.

## Measurements of DSC behavior in the context of prey capturing
Two newly captured wild bats were equipped with the same custom-built on-board microphones as in the previous experiment, and we recorded the echoes bats received while attacking tethered noctuid moth (Fig. 3A). To mimic natural fly-catching, moths were presented to perching bats, which readily took off to attack. Bats performed attacks on the moth while carrying the on-board microphone, and we successfully observed clear spectral glints in the recordings immediately before prey capture (Fig. 3B, see also Supplementary Fig 3). These glints—periodic modulations in amplitude and frequency generated when the CF component of a call reflects from fluttering moth wings— are known to be critical cues for detecting and identifying prey18 (see also Supplementary Fig 4). As evident from the spectrograms, even when spectral glints produced high-frequency peaks, they did not serve as targets for DSC. This observation is not fully consistent with the results of the two preceding experiments, which showed that DSC is guided by the highest-frequency echoes, and therefore indicates that prey-induced glints represent an exception. A plausible explanation is that glints are not suitable anchors for DSC because they are temporally brief and spectrally unstable. In the present study, the measured glints exhibited frequency shifts of approximately 1 kHz but lasted only about 5 ms. Rapid frequency adjustments required to track such transient events are likely to be limited by biomechanical or production-related constraints. Indeed, DSC responses are known to be relatively slow, typically requiring more than 200 ms to complete a 1 kHz compensation17. More importantly, careful inspection of the spectrograms revealed that by ignoring the instantaneous Doppler shifts associated with glints and maintaining DSC on the highest-frequency background echoes, a silent spectral window was consistently preserved, within which glints emerged with a conspicuously high signal-to-noise ratio. These results suggest that DSC does more than simply place echoes into a sensitive band. Instead, it may contribute to the suppression of background clutter and carve out a silent spectral channel for prey detection. In other words, by stabilizing the frequency of returning echoes, bats effectively create a clutter-free spectral window in which prey-generated motion cues are most salient, thereby enhancing the detection of prey-derived spectral glints.

## Narrow-band noise playback experiments for prey detection
Finally, we performed a narrow-band noise playback experiment to assess the functional importance of the “silent spectral window”. Six newly captured wild bats were used in this test. Moths were presented while a narrow band of Gaussian noise 2 kHz above the resting frequency was played (above fref jamming), testing whether bats could still detect and attack prey. As controls, we used a no jamming condition and a below fref jamming condition in which Gaussian noise 2 kHz below the resting frequency was presented -this band corresponds to the range where most echoes typically return during flight (Fig. 4A).

Each of the six bats was tested across all three noise playback conditions, with four trials per condition. The effect was clear. Whereas bats attacked in 100% of trials under both the no jamming and a below fref jamming conditions, the attack rate dropped to 37.5% (95% CI: 21–58%) when above fref noise playback jammed the quiet spectral window (Fig. 4B, see also Supplementary Movie 1). These findings demonstrate that bats rely on the silent spectral window to detect prey-associated spectral glints and that keeping this band free of clutter is essential for successful prey detection.

## Discussion
Our phantom echo playbacks experiment and on-board microphone recordings show that horseshoe bats compensate for the highest-frequency echoes during DSC, thereby creating a remarkably silent spectral window above fref. Recordings and noise playback interference in the context of prey capture revealed that spectral glints from fluttering moth wings fall within this silent spectral window and are exploit for prey detection. These results provide direct evidence for the overlooked function of DSC as S/N improving strategy, showing that HDC bats use DSC not only as a neural specialization but also as a physical strategy to suppress background echoes of their own emitted pulses to frequencies below a certain range, thereby securing a clutter-free silent spectral window for prey detection. This finding illustrates how active sensing systems can transcend physiological limits by leveraging physical principles, with implications that extend beyond bats.
Our findings also align with previous reports that auditory sensitivity in HDC bats can extend slightly above fref, with relatively shallow decline19–21, consistent with the frequency range we identified as the silent window (Supplementary Experiments and Results, and Supplementary Fig 5). Together, these results suggest that the auditory fovea and DSC act in concert: the fovea provides heightened sensitivity around and above fref, while DSC eliminates competing echoes from this range.
The discovery that bats combine neural tuning with a physical strategy to create an acoustic “quiet zone” highlights an evolutionary solution to the limits of active sensing. By leveraging physics as well as physiology, bats achieve a functional improvement in signal-to-noise ratio that enhances prey detection under natural clutter. To further evaluate this interpretation, comparative examination of DSC under conditions with and without prey may provide valuable insights. Although our results indicate that bats compensate toward the highest-frequency echoes even in the absence of prey, the precision of this compensation was not quantitatively assessed, and this remains an important topic for future work. Furthermore, while the present experiments focused on two candidate factors governing DSC target selection—maximum frequency and maximum intensity—other variables, such as the temporal stability and time scale of frequency modulation, may also contribute. Although our experimental design neither confirms nor excludes the influence of these factors, future studies addressing these possibilities would help to refine and extend the hypothesis proposed here. While proximate mechanisms of DSC have been well studied, our work provides a clear explanation of its ultimate function and evolutionary significance, establishing DSC as an adaptive strategy for active sensing in complex environments.

## Methods

## Animals
In this study, 11 greater Japanese horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon; 9 males and 2 females) were used. The exact ages of the bats were unknown because they were all wild caught. These bats were captured from a cave located in Fukui Prefecture, Japan. We were licensed to collect bats, and we complied with all Japanese laws (permits from Fukui Prefecture in 2022 and 2023). The bats were housed in a room (3 × 2 × 2 m) at Doshisha University in Kyoto, Japan. The temperature and humidity in the room were monitored and controlled to mimic the cave environment, with temperatures around 20 degrees Celsius and humidity above 80% all year round. The room was cleaned, and mealworms and water were placed every 2–3 days to allow the bats to eat and drink ad libitum. The experiments were conducted under the permission of the Animal Experiment Committee of Doshisha University (Permission number: A22015, A23017). All experiments were conducted between 2022 and 2023. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. No surgical procedures were performed at any stage of the study, and no anaesthesia, analgesia, or euthanasia was administered.

## Experiments to elucidate the factors that determine the target echo during DSC
Bats in flight receive multiple echoes with varying intensity and frequency, one of which is selected as the target echo for Doppler shift compensation (DSC). However, it remains unclear what rules bats follow when selecting target echoes from multiple echoes. We hypothesized that bats select either the echo with the maximum-intensity or the one with the highest-frequency as their target among multiple echoes. To test these hypotheses, two experiments were conducted: real-time playback experiment with phantom echoes and a free-flight experiment. Both experiments were conducted in an experimental chamber (9 × 4.5 × 2.4 m). Acoustic foams are put on the walls to minimize echoes from the walls. During the experiments, red LED lighting (wavelengths 650 nm) was used to decrease the possibility of bats using visual information.

## Real-time playback experiments for clarifying the echo selection criteria in DSC behavior
In real-time playback experiments, combinations of multiple phantom echoes with different sound intensities and time-varying frequency shifts were created as stimuli and presented to the stationary bats in real time to test which echoes the bats selected for DSC. Following a protocol of our previous research22, real-time playback of phantom echoes was achieved by capturing the bat’s emitted pulses with a ultrasonic microphone (Avisoft, CM16) positioned approximately 20 cm away from the bat (Note that the bats were held by the experimenter to ensure they remained in front of the microphone throughout the experiment). The signals were then sent through a microphone amplifier (TDT, MA3) to a digital signal processor (TDT, RX6, 200 kHz sampling rate) for frequency modulation processing (Doppler shift) using a double heterodyne method. The details of the double heterodyne processing in TDT RX6 correspond to the protocol of our previous study23. Since actual echoes, other than the phantom echo signals, are unnecessary as a stimulus for bats, we designed an earphone-like system based on a previous setup24, with couplers connected to the tweeter (TDT, ES1) via a speaker driver (TDT, ED1), ensuring that only the playback phantom echoes are heard by the bats (Fig. 1A). The frequency response of the playback system was within ± 7 dB between 50 and 70 kHz, corresponding to the echolocation call frequency of the greater Japanese horseshoe bats. The bats’ emitted pulses and playback phantom echoes were recorded using a DAQ (NI, USB-6356, 500 kHz sampling rate). The signal processing delay by TDT RX6 was less than 1 ms, which is sufficiently short.
In this experiment, three different patterns of time-varying frequency shifts were presented for each four bats, processed. The first two patterns served as positive controls, while the last one was the main test stimulus (Fig. 1B). The first is a stimulus pattern with a constant +1.5 kHz Doppler shifts from the emitted pulse frequency. The 1.5 kHz Doppler shift corresponds to a typical flight speed of 3–4 m/s with a CF2 frequency of 68 kHz under laboratory conditions10. The second stimulus pattern consists of a periodic change in Doppler shifts from 0 to 1.5 kHz with a period of 5 s. The last main stimulus consisted of five phantom echo streams, one strong phantom echo and four weak phantom echoes with differed in frequency shift ( − 20 dB compared to the strong one). Although the absolute playback level depended on the bat’s emitted pulse SPL and was therefore not constant as is also the case during natural flight, the relative frequency and intensity relationship between the strong and weak echoes in the multiple Doppler shifts stimulus was strictly controlled. The carrier signals for double-heterodyne were generated and combined in MATLAB prior to multiplication with the bat’s emitted calls, ensuring an exact −20 dB intensity difference. The processing time was less than 1 ms for all stimuli. This was designed so that if the bat focused on the maximum-intensity echo as the target echo for DSC, the frequency pattern of the stimulus would be perceived as varying sinusoidally from 0 to 1.5 kHz over a 5-second period. In contrast, if the bat focused on the highest-frequency echo for DSC, the stimulus pattern would remain constant at 1.5 kHz without changing over time. Thus, if the bat modulates the CF2 of its emitted pulses sinusoidally over a 5-second period, we can conclude that it is compensating for the echoes with the maximum-intensity. On the other hand, if the bats continuously decrease the frequency of its emitted pulses by the same amount, we can conclude that they are compensating for the highest-frequency echoes.

## Free-flight experiment using on-board microphones to record the actual echoes perceived by bats
For bats in free flight, a more natural behavior, we investigated whether they compensate for the highest-frequency echoes or the maximum-intensity echoes at the reference frequency, fref when performing DSC. A square flight space (4.5 × 4.5 m) was created in the center of the experimental chamber (9 × 4.5 m) by dividing it with two nets (Fig. 2A). Sound-absorbing material (attenuation of about −25 dB in the ultrasound range) was installed on the outer walls beyond the nets, creating a contrast in echo intensity between these net-separated walls (‘quiet walls’) and the unobstructed inner walls (‘echoic walls’) within the flight space. In this way, bats flying in circles within the flight space alternated between ‘quiet walls’ and the ‘echoic walls’, creating an environment where the maximum-intensity echoes reaching the bats were not necessarily the highest-frequency echoes. Experiments were conducted on four individual bats.
A custom-made on-board microphone (based on Knowles SPU0410LR5H, logger type, design by Girlie) was attached to the bat to directly measure the echoes from these walls that reach the bats during flight. The on-board microphone was attached to the bat’s back using double-sided adhesive tape and was gently removed immediately after the experiment. This procedure did not involve any surgical manipulation and was entirely non-invasive. The weight of the on-board microphone is 2.5 g, including batteries, which is about 10% of the body weight of greater Japanese horseshoe bats. The sampling rate is 288 kHz, the sensitivity is within ±4 dB between 50 and 70 kHz, and the dynamic range is approximately 70 dB. In particular, within the 65–70 kHz range, which corresponds to the frequency of the CF2 component of the emitted pulse, important for this measurement, the microphone exhibits a ± 2.5 dB response and a very low self-noise level equivalent to 20 dB SPL in terms of sound pressure.

## Measurements of behavior during prey capturing
We investigated the DSC behavior performed by bats while they headed towards the flattering prey moths for predation, as well as the echoes reaching them. Furthermore, we examined the contribution of the target-echo determinants identified in Section 1.1 and 1.2 to predatory behavior.

## On-board recordings during tethered moth presentations
The sequence of emitted pulses and echoes received by bats while flying toward a fluttering moth during predation was measured using the same on-board microphone described in Section 1.2 above. The prey moth, Goniocraspidum pryeri, was captured in a cave. There are records of bats consuming this moth in the wild25. The moth was tethered to the ceiling by a thin wire hooked around its abdomen and a thread (90 cm of #30 cotton yarn, Fig. 3A). Without fatally damage, it fluttered vigorously for a few minutes and flew freely within the range of the thread. The tethered moth was gently held by the experimenter and released from their hand after confirming that the bat was stably perched on their perch. The bats equipped with on-board microphone were able to take off from the perches at any time and attack the moths. When a bat successfully caught a moth, it was removed from the wire, allowing the bat to feed on it. Experiments were conducted on five individual bats. Of these, three individuals accepted the on-board microphone, and two successfully attacked tethered moths, providing clear recordings of prey-capture events.

## Narrow-band noise playback experiments for prey detection
Next, to confirm the frequency range crucial for prey detection, narrow-band Gaussian noise was played at 2 kHz below and above fref, respectively. This was done to determine whether the frequency band used by bats for predatory behavior was below or above fref. The former is the band where DSC gathers a large number of echoes from the surroundings (Fig. 2B, S2), while the latter is the quiet band where no echoes from the surroundings are returned, and only glints from fluttering prey are observed (Fig. 3B, S3). The largest spectral glints from G. pryeri, are approximately ± 1.5 kHz. The playback of 2 kHz band noise completely masks the glints from the moths in the respective bands. Since fref varied across individuals, the resting frequency (frest) of each individual was measured the day before the experiment, and narrow-band Gaussian noise was prepared in advance according to each individual’s frest. The band noise was output from a DAQ (NI USB-6356, sampling at 500 kHz) and was emitted from a tweeter (Pioneer, PT-R7III) through an audio amplifier (Pioneer, A-D3). Following the same procedure as the previous experiment, the bat was perched on the perch, but the tweeter was positioned 1.5 m behind the moth, directed toward both the bat and the moth (Fig. 4A). The sound pressure levels of the playback noise and prey-generated glints at the bat’s perch position were evaluated using a MEMS microphone (Knowles, SPU0410LR5H-QB) calibrated against a Brüel & Kjær 1/8-inch reference microphone. The playback noise was measured at approximately 60 dB SPL. In contrast, glints reflected from the moth were weak and temporally unstable, often approaching the measurement threshold and were only occasionally detectable. Because glint intensity depends strongly on the bat’s emitted pulse level and orientation, precise SPL quantification was challenging. However, given the relatively low microphone self-noise level ( ≤ 20 dB SPL equivalent), glint levels at the perch were estimated to be on the order of approximately 20 dB SPL. Based on these measurements, the playback noise was considered sufficiently higher in level than the glints at the perch position. The tethered moth was gently held by the experimenter and released from their hand after confirming that the bat was stably perched on the perch, and after the noise playback had begun. There were three conditions: two controls (one with no noise and one with band noise in the 2 kHz below fref) and a main condition with noise in the 2 kHz band above fref. Each noise stimulus was pre-generated in MATLAB according to the fref of each individual measured on the previous day. Three trials, in which the three stimulus conditions were presented in randomized order, were defined as one set. The order of stimulus conditions was determined by an experimenter who was not involved in handling the bats. The experimenter handling the bats was blinded to the stimulus conditions during stimulus presentation. Each bat completed four sets, resulting in a total of 12 trials per individual. We classified trials in which bats took off from the perch immediately after moth presentation as attack behavior. Trials in which bats remained on the perch despite moth fluttering were classified as no-attack behavior. Although bats occasionally hovered after takeoff and sometimes failed to capture the moth, distinguishing hovering from non-hovering flights was not straightforward and risked introducing subjective bias. In addition, unsuccessful capture attempts could arise from experimental variability (e.g., variability in the attachment strength of the moth to the tether). Therefore, we adopted a simple and objective classification criterion based solely on whether the bat took off from the perch.

## Statistics and Reproducibility

## Statistical analysis for narrow-band noise playback experiments
The analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2). Attack behavior was modeled as a binary response variable (attack vs. no attack). Because two conditions produced complete separation (100% attack rate), we used Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression, which provides stable, bias-reduced estimates under separation. Condition was included as a categorical predictor. Statistical significance was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests. Parameter estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals derived from the profile penalized likelihood. Analyses were conducted using the logistf package.

## Reproducibility
A total of 11 bats were used in this study. In the real-time playback experiments designed to examine the acoustic features of target echoes underlying DSC, four bats were tested, each exposed once to three experimental conditions. The same four individuals were used in the free-flight experiments, each tested once. For supplementary auditory experiments, three bats were used, including two of the original four individuals and one additional bat. For behavioral measurements during prey capture, six newly captured bats were used. Of these, two individuals were used in the on-board microphone experiments. In the noise playback experiments, all six bats were tested, with each individual exposed to three conditions repeated four times. Given the use of wild animals, the number of individuals was kept to the minimum required to obtain reliable and interpretable results while avoiding unnecessary use of animals.

## Figure Fig. 1: Real-time playback experiment for clarifying the echo selection criteria in DSC behavior.A Schematic diagram of the experimental setup. The echolocation pulse were captured using a microphone, processed in real-time, and played back through tweeters as phantom echoes. B Representative examples of playback stimuli (red) and bat responses (blue). Under constant Doppler shift conditions, the playback stimulus was processed with a 1.5 kHz frequency shift, and in response, the bats showed a DSC response by consistently lowering their call frequency. Under periodic Doppler shift conditions, where the frequency shift varied sinusoidally between 0 and 1.5 kHz with a 5-second cycle, the bats exhibited a corresponding periodic modulation in their call frequency. Finally, under the multiple Doppler shift condition, where echoes of different intensities were combined such that the maximum-intensity echoes exhibited a frequency shift that modulated periodically while the highest-frequency echoes, though lower in intensity ( − 20 dB), consistently maintained shift, the bats respond by consistently lowering their call frequency. See also Supplementary Fig 1.

## Figure Fig. 2: Free flight experiment for echo measurement with on-board microphone.A Experimental setup and on-board microphone. The flight area was created by nets within a chamber and sound-absorbing material was installed on the walls beyond the nets (quiet walls), while the inner walls remained reflective (echoic walls). This makes bats flying in circles to alternately encounter quiet and echoic wall. B Representative examples of frequency patterns of pulses and returning echoes recorded by on-board microphones during free flight. A condition was successfully created in which high- and low-intensity echoes alternated, and the maximum-intensity echoes did not necessarily correspond to the highest-frequency ones. The highest-frequency echoes consistently maintained a fixed frequency, confirming that DSC targeted the highest-frequency echoes, not the maximum-intensity echoes, which is consistent with the real-time playback experiment. Furthermore, notably silent spectral window was observed above the fref, whereas numerous echoes were observed below the fref. See also Supplementary Fig 2.

## Figure Fig. 3: Prey capturing experiment for echo measurement with on-board microphone.A Experimental setup for prey capturing experiment. A tethered moth was presented, and bats equipped with onboard microphones were released to attack. B Representative examples of frequency patterns of pulses and returning echoes recorded by on-board microphones at the time of prey capture. Just before the attack, spectral glints caused by the fluttering of moth wings was detected. These glints were observed within the same frequency range as the quiet band identified in the free-flight experiment. See also Supplementary Fig 3.

## Figure Fig. 4: Prey detection test under noise playback in “silent spectral windows”.A Experimental setup for noise playback experiment. The bats were placed on a perch, and moths were presented in front of a tweeter while noise was played back from the tweeter. Three conditions were tested: two controls (no jamming noise and noise 2 kHz below fref) and a main condition with noise 2 kHz above fref. B Presence or absence of predatory behavior under each noise condition. Table showing whether bats took off from the perch to capture the moth (○) or remained on the perch (×) after the moth was presented. The order of symbols within each stimulus reflects the set number. Because stimulus order was randomized within each set, the relative order across stimuli is not meaningful. The attack rate under the above fref jamming condition was 37.5% (95% CI: 21–58%), whereas attack rate was 100% in the no jamming condition and the below fref jamming conditions, and the effect of Condition was significant (likelihood ratio test: χ² = 36.03, df = 2, p = 1.5 × 10−8). See also Supplementary Movie 1.
