Abstract
A preliminary study of the odonate fauna of Wayanad Wildlife Sanctuary, Kerala, southern India was
carried out from August 2020 to November 2020. Ponds, streams, and swamps in the sanctuary were
sampled using Visual Encounter Surveys (VES). A total of 84 species (49 dragonflies and 35 damselflies)
from 11 families were recorded, out of which 13 are endemic to the Western Ghats. Orthetrum pruinosum
was found to be the most common species in the sanctuary. Indolestes pulcherrimus, an endemic species
with no recent records, is being reported for the first time with photographs from the wild. The highest
number of species was recorded in the ponds (56), followed by streams (46) and swamps (33). However,
the number of endemic species was highest in the streams (8), followed by swamps (3) and ponds (2).

INTRODUCTION
Odonata are helpful indicators of freshwater as well as terrestrial habitat conditions. Several studies have
shown changes in odonate species richness and/or community composition as responses to
deforestation, suggesting their potential as bioassessment tools in the tropics (Šigutová et al. 2019).
Odonate larvae are highly specific to particular aquatic habitats and this makes them an ideal model
system to address questions in ecology, evolutionary biology, and biogeography, and for monitoring the
health of freshwater ecosystems (Subramanian and Babu 2020). Odonates are ubiquitous in all
freshwater ecosystems, but in the first global assessment of this insect order, it was found that one in 10
species of dragonflies and damselflies is threatened with extinction (Clausnitzer et al. 2009). Though the
Indian odonate fauna is well described in terms of adult taxonomy, their ecology is poorly known
(Subramanian 2005). The impact of landscape changes happening in peninsular India for the past fifty
or more years on odonate distribution and status is not known. This knowledge gap can only be filled by
more current field surveys to assess the changes in the conservation status and distribution of the
species (Subramanian 2009).
Currently, 6392 species of Odonata are recognized worldwide (Paulson et al. 2023). In India, 493 species
and 27 subspecies in 154 genera and 18 families have been identified (Subramanian and Babu 2020).
The Western Ghats hosts 193 species (Subramanian et al. 2018) of which, 178 have been currently
recorded from Kerala (Society for Odonate Studies 2023). The highest diversity and endemism of
Odonata in the Western Ghats has been reported from the Nilgiri–Wayanad–Kodagu complex and
Anaimalai Hills (Subramanian et al. 2018). In a systematic study of the odonates of the southern Western
Ghats by the Zoological Survey of India, extremely rare specimens such as Chlorogomphus campioni and
Idionyx saffronata were collected from Wayanad (Emiliyamma 2014). Another independent study
recorded 59 species from various locations in the district (Susanth and Anooj 2020). In addition, threeday Odonata surveys conducted by Malabar Natural History Society and Kerala Forests and Wildlife
Department with public participation in 2014 and 2016 in the Wayanad Wildlife Sanctuary (WWS)
recorded 68 and 75 species of odonates respectively. However, there has been no intensive effort made to
document the Odonata diversity of WWS till now.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area
Wayanad district in Kerala, India is located in the Western Ghats at altitudes ranging from 300 to 2200
meters. WWS (11°35’- 11° 51’ N & 76° 02’-76° 27’ E) is a part of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve and has an
extent of 344.44 km2 across four ranges. The sanctuary is contiguous with Nagarhole and Bandipur Tiger
Reserves of Karnataka to the northeast and Mudumalai Wildlife Sanctuary of Tamil Nadu to the
southeast. It is also contiguous with the South and the North Wayanad Forest Divisions. The major
vegetation types of the sanctuary include the west coast tropical semi-evergreen forests, southern moist
mixed deciduous forest, southern dry mixed deciduous forest and plantations of Eucalyptus spp.,

Tectona grandis, Bombax spp. and Gmelina arborea (Champion and Seth 1968). The sanctuary lies on a
plateau, with an average altitude of 900 m. The highest peak is Karottimala at 1158 m (Sunilkumar
2012).
The WWS has several microhabitats that are suitable for supporting Odonata. The sanctuary is within a
significant part of the catchment area of the Kabani River which flows into Karnataka. The Nulpuzha and
Mavinahalla streams confluence in the southern portion of the sanctuary to become the Nuguhole river.
Manjalthodu and other small streams in the sanctuary become dry during peak summer season.
Nulpuzha is the main perennial stream in the study area. Natural swampy areas locally called ‘vayals’
occur mostly amidst moist deciduous forests and plantations. In addition, the forest department has
excavated numerous waterholes (ponds) for the wild animals (Easa 1999). These waterholes are
regularly maintained by the department.

Methods
Fieldwork was carried out from August to November 2020, a period which falls within the most
appropriate time to document adult odonates in India (Subramanian 2005; Nair 2011). Considering
accessibility and spatial coverage within the study area, 33 ponds, 28 stream stretches and 12 swamps
were randomly selected for sampling (Fig. 1). Timed visual encounter surveys (VES) were conducted to
record species richness and abundance. In each selected habitat, the observer surveyed Odonata along
the edge of the waterbody for 20 minutes. The species encountered were identified and enumerated, and
photographed using a Canon 600D camera with 250 mm lens. The identities of the species were
confirmed by referring to taxonomic monographs (Fraser 1933, 1934 & 1936) and field guides
(Subramanian 2005, 2009; Kiran and Raju 2013; Jose and Chandran 2020). A checklist was prepared
using systematic arrangement and taxonomy according to Kalkman et al. 2020. The identified Odonata
species were categorized into five relative frequency classes based on the number of habitats in which
they occurred. The categories include- very common (> 45%), common (30%-45%), occasional (15%-30%),
rare (5%-15%), and very rare (< 5%). Species sighted opportunistically from the sanctuary were also added
to the checklist and included in the ‘very rare’ relative frequency class. Shannon Wiener diversity index (H)
was calculated for each habitat and the values were Box-Cox transformed. Normality was tested using
Page 3/16

Shapiro-Wilk test (W = 0.96, p > 0.05) and one-way ANOVA was used to test if the diversity differed
significantly between ponds, streams and swamps.

RESULTS
A total of 84 species of Odonates (49 dragonflies and 35 damselflies) were recorded from the sanctuary,
of which 13 are endemic to the Western Ghats (Table 1). Species richness was the highest in the ponds
(56), followed by streams (46) and swamps (33). However, the number of endemic species recorded was
the highest in the streams (8), followed by swamps (3) and ponds (2) (Fig. 2). Species diversity as
measured by H, differed significantly between the three types of waterbodies (F = 20.4, p < 0.01). On an
average, ponds had higher H, followed by streams and swamps (Fig. 3). Eight species, including six
endemics were sighted opportunistically within the territory of the sanctuary. Orthetrum pruinosum was
the most common species, recorded from nearly 80% of the sampled waterbodies. The largest proportion
of species (36%) came under the ‘very rare’ relative frequency class and only 6% were ‘very common’
(Fig. 4).

Comments on significant records of Odonata
The following odonates observed from WWS are significant records considering their current known
geographical distributions.
Page 8/16

1. Indolestes pulcherrimus (Fraser 1924)
This endemic species has a very restricted range and was earlier reported only from swamps in the hills
of Karnataka (Subramanian et al. 2018). In the present study, multiple colonies were found in the Sulthan
Bathery Range, sometimes at a considerable distance from water. The male of this species was identified
based on characteristics such as a turquoise blue thorax, a dark metallic green dorsum with a narrow
blue mid-dorsal carina, brown legs, a turquoise blue abdomen marked with black, cerci blue at bases
turning into dirty white and finally brown at apices and blackish brown paraprocts about half the length
of cerci (Image 1). The female is pale olivaceous brown with black markings as in the male and a few
pairs in copula reaffirmed the identity of the species (Image 2). This is the first photographic record of
this species from the wild.
2. Amphiallagma parvum (Selys, 1876)
Though this species is widely distributed in most South Asian countries and has been reported from
many states in India (Subramanian et al. 2018), it is known from Kerala only through sparse
photographic records (Anonymous 2021a). A male was photographed from a pond in the Muthanga
Range and it was identified based on the characters such as azure blue thorax with black dorsum, azure
blue antehumeral stripes bordered with black, pale blue abdomen marked with black on dorsum up to
segment 7 and a narrow black mid dorsal stripe on segment 10 (Image 3).
3. Ceriagrion olivaceum aurantiacum Fraser, 1924
This taxon was described by Fraser in 1923 as a South Indian species, Ceriagrion aurantiacum occurring
in “Nilgiri Waynad and Coorg” but later, the same author (1933) associated it with C. olivaceum and
treated it as a “race” of the latter. C. o. olivaceum is considered to be widespread in India and parts of
mainland Southeast Asia (Kalkman et al. 2020). C. o. aurantiacum is slightly smaller than C. o. olivaceum
and its subapical tooth of cerci has a hook which is replaced by a black ridge in C. o. olivaceum (Asahina
1967). Clearly, this damselfly merits a taxonomic revisit. It was recorded from one stream in Kurichiyat
Range and one pond in Tholpetty Range, and identified referring to the characters like dull mahogany red
thorax (versus dull green thorax of C. o. olivaceum), reddish brown abdomen that turns darker towards
the last segments and yellow legs with all femora having a black stripe running along the extensor
surface (Image 4).
4. Indothemis limbata (Selys, 1891)
This dragonfly has two subspecies, I. limbata limbata occurring in Myanmar and southeastern Asia and I.
limbata sita in Sri Lanka and parts of India. In India, it has been reported from West Bengal, Karnataka,
Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Orissa, Arunachal Pradesh and Assam (Dow 2011; Anonymous 2021b).
From Kerala, there is a single photographic record of this species from the northernmost district of
Kasaragod by Muhammed Haneef (Dragonflies of Kerala 2020). In the present study, it was recorded
from nine ponds spread across all the four ranges of WWS. All the observed individuals were males
which were identified referring to characters like dark black eyes turning brown below, purplish frons,

Page 9/16

uniformly black prothorax, thorax and legs, bases of hindwings with extensive dark brown marking and
black anal appendages (Image 5).

DISCUSSION
This was the first intensive study to explore the odonate diversity of WWS. With 84 species, this study has
recorded more odonate species compared to other similar studies from Thattekkad Bird Sanctuary (82
species, Varghese et al. 2014) and Chinnar Wildlife Sanctuary (48 species, Adarsh et al. 2015) in the
same state. As some odonate species are highly seasonal and can be observed on the wing for only a
short period of the year, it is likely that the present study has inadvertently failed to record some species.
Only a year-round study can reveal the complete odonate diversity of the sanctuary. The ‘ponds’ in the
sanctuary, which recorded the highest species richness and diversity are, in most cases, waterholes dug
for larger wild animals. It is evident that odonates have largely benefitted from the presence of these
human-made habitats. However, it must be noted that only a few endemic species are supported by this
habitat type. It is important to maintain the integrity of streams in the sanctuary, which host at least eight
odonate species endemic to the Western Ghats. Activities which prevent the free flow of water such as
dam construction could be detrimental to these species. The naturally occurring swamps (‘vayals’) were
difficult to sample which could explain their relatively low odonate diversity in the present study.
Moreover, they had very low water levels; a condition not favoured by most odonate species. However, the
swamps play an important ecological role by maintaining a high water table in the region (Nair 1998) and
therefore, indirectly contributed to the high odonate diversity in the ponds and streams.
Even though it is a protected area, WWS faces numerous anthropogenic pressures in the form of manmade fires, invasive species, road kills and unsustainable harvesting of non-timber forest products
(Anoop and Ganesh 2020). Studies have shown that anthropogenic disturbances can have detrimental
effects on odonate species (Martins 2009; Villanueva and Mohagan 2010) or may drastically affect the
species assemblage (Dinakaran and Anbalagan 2007; Reece and Mcintyre 2009; Kulkarni and
Subramanian 2013). Many endemic species have a narrow distribution across the Western Ghats,
occurring in only small patches of suitable habitats (Subramanian et al. 2011; Koparde et al. 2014). To
ensure the conservation of such species, it is imperative to protect their microhabitats.
