Abstract
Damselflies (Odonata: Zygoptera) have evolved highly developed visual systems for mate
recognition, flight and prey detection. Wing Interference Patterns (WIPs), which are colors generated by thin-film interference in the wing membrane, could function as visual
signals and play a role in sexual selection. Because the color of these signals depends on
membrane thickness, a potential conflict exists between changes in wing area and the need
to maintain a consistent signal. We examined how wing membrane thickness scales with
wing area in two damselfly species, Ischnura elegans and Enallagma cyathigerum. Using
hyperspectral imaging, we found that while wing area was highly variable, membrane
thickness showed low intraspecific variability. We found no evidence for any significant
correlation between wing thickness and wing area or any support for sexual selection
operating on these traits. Low intraspecific variation of the membrane thickness results
in a consistent Wing Interference Signal (WIS), which is useful for species identification
with remote sensing approaches.

Introduction
Background
While insect wings are primarily adapted for flight performance, recent studies on specular
reflexes have revealed that they may also be crucial for mate choice (Butterworth et al.
2021; Hawkes et al. 2019; Shevtsova and Hansson 2011). Given these dual roles, the order
Odonata presents an ideal study system to investigate the extent to which sexual selection shapes these traits. Odonata comprises Anisoptera (dragonflies), and Zygoptera (damselflies). According to fossil and molecular evidence, they are one of the oldest clades of
winged insects (Pterygota) that evolved several hundreds of millions of years ago (Kohli et
al. 2021). Odonata are characterized by a semi-aquatic life cycle where their aquatic nymph
stage is followed by the terrestrial adult stage. Adult Odonata are some of the most efficient
aerial predators (Combes et al. 2013; Usherwood and Lehmann 2008) among insects. Some
species of Odonata had up to 70 cm wide wingspans (Cannell 2018) in the Paleozoic era,
which is thought to have been enabled by high oxygen levels that peaked at 35% during
that period (Harrison et al. 2010). Odonata are active in daytime and identify their prey
(small flying insects) by their contrast against the bright sky, which they attack from below
(Bomphrey et al. 2016; Combes 2015; Duong et al. 2017). Their wingbeat frequencies range
between 20 and 50 Hz (Rüppell 1989; Wakeling and Ellington 1997).

Odonate vision
Apart from their high flight capabilities that include hovering, upside-down and backwards
flights, the visual system of Odonata is extraordinary making them strong candidates for
perceiving and discriminating visual signals from other insect wings. They have the largest
compound eyes among insects with as many as 30,000 ommatidia (Nation Sr 2022). This
feature provides them with the highest spatial resolution among insects. Finally, in daylight
their temporal resolution or flicker fusion frequency (FFF) reaches 300 Hz (Ruck 1961), a
capability that grants them exceptional visual acuity for hunting and navigating, and potentially mate recognition. In addition, some species of Odonata have been demonstrated to
have vision that includes polarization sensitivity and six different spectral bands, including
one broad band and one deep red narrow band (Brydegaard et al. 2018; Cezário et al. 2025).
Insect vision and associated neural network complexity comes with an energy cost (Vuong
et al. 2023) but it is unknown what spectral features in their natural environment could
motivate both their high number of spectral receptors and their possession of the narrowest spectral receptor recorded in the animal kingdom (Cronin et al. 2014). Discrimination
against the dull spectral features of sky, waterbodies, vegetation, soil and prey could be
differentiated with far fewer spectral bands. A previous review (Brydegaard et al. 2018) suggests that Odonata vision meets all requirements for being able to remotely assess spectral
interference fringes from insect wings. Their visual systems thus meet the requirements to
enable them to perceive the wings of potential mates when adjacent as well as detect and
resolve light flashes from flying conspecifics at a distance.

Wing interference
Wing interference patterns (Shevtsova and Hansson 2011) (WIPs) and wing interference
signals (Li et al. 2023a, b; Müller et al. 2023) (WISs) have recently gained increased interest
in evolutionary and sensory biology (Hawkes et al. 2019; Katayama et al. 2014). We refer
to WIPs as the chromatic patterns across insect wings which could be mediated between
insects in close proximity or in tandem and we refer to WISs as the spatially averaged interference over wings which can be mediated via flashes over some distance in the environment. The phenomena arise due to thin-film interference between the reflected light wave
from the first air-chitin interface of the wing membrane and the second chitin-air interface
(Leertouwer et al. 2011). Consequently, resonant reflectance occurs at certain wavelengths,
depending on the membrane thickness and observation angle according to the following
equation (Li et al. 2023a, b; Yin et al. 2006):

Allometry
The dual role of flight and sexual selection in wings may give rise to conflicting selection pressures between different functions. The chromaticity of the WIPs and WISs is tied
to membrane thickness and may be under stabilizing selection. However, we also expect
allometric scaling (the scaling of biological traits with organism size) between wing thickness and remaining dimensions (Huxley et al. 1993). Allometry is important in the context
of WIP and WIS evolution for at least three reasons. First, structural integrity generally
requires wings to thicken as they grow larger (Ribak et al. 2025). Thus, a trade-off would
emerge if visual signaling would require a constant thickness to preserve a specific interference color. Second, isometry as a trait keeping the same proportions as the animal grows
larger, serves as a null expectation, and any departure from it points to a change in shape
with size. Because thickness is a length, but wing area is a length squared, isometry here
predicts thickness to scale with the square root of area rather than with area directly. In the
area-versus-thickness form we fit, that corresponds to area scaling with the square of thickness (an exponent of two), and a thickness that grows more or less steeply than this departs
from isometry. Third, interspecific (cross-species) comparisons of some insect families have
revealed that wing thickness scales with wing area, with correlations around 70% (Li et al.
2023a, b). The slope of this relationship differs between insect families (Li et al. 2023a, b;
Müller et al. 2023). Across 30 hover fly species (family Syrphidae) the two sexes of each
species deviated jointly from the general allometric trend (Li et al. 2023a, b). Such evolutionary allometric relationships could arise either from developmental constraints arising
from allometry within populations (static allometry) or from specific size-trait relationships

maintained through adaptation (Voje et al. 2014). So far, no studies have systematically
investigated the static allometry between wing thickness and size in insects.

Sexual selection
Here we investigated the intraspecific relationship between wing area and membrane thickness within two damselfly species of the family Coenagrionidae (“pond damselflies”). In
contrast to previous broad, multi-species comparisons, no study, to our knowledge, has
yet investigated the intraspecific allometric correlation between wing area and thickness.
Mounting evidence now suggests that WIPs are targets of sexual selection in several insect
species (Hawkes et al. 2019; Shevtsova and Hansson 2011). High species specificity of
WIPs has been proposed to function as an accurate species recognition apparatus (Hawkes
et al. 2019) while subtle within-species variation may serve as a target of mate choice (Butterworth et al. 2021; Hawkes et al. 2019; Katayama et al. 2014). In insects, it has previously
been shown that mating success in fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster) is a target of directional sexual selection with respect to chromatic saturation of WIPs but under stabilizing
selection for a specific hue (De Lisle et al. 2025). For Odonata, there is no evidence for
any such role of WIPs in either dragonflies or damselflies, although other types of wing
optical properties have been found to be targets of both sexual and natural selection in
the genus Calopteryx (Kuchta and Svensson 2014; Svensson et al. 2010; Svensson and
Waller 2013). Although WIPs are often considered weak under diffuse dark field illumination (Katayama et al. 2014), they become the strongest visual signal in the environment
under specular geometries, exceeding the brightness of the sky, water surface and vegetation
(Brydegaard et al. 2018; Müller et al. 2023). This high contrast allows WISs to be mediated
over distance for day-active species in open, sun-exposed habitats. Crucially, the effectiveness of these highly species-specific signals also depends on the wing’s structural integrity.
A well performing individual would have fewer collisions with the environment, fewer
close encounters with predators and therefore intact wings with fewer flaws (Combes et
al. 2010; Mountcastle and Combes 2014). Since thin-film interference relies on a uniform,
smooth surface, any physical damage or age-related wear deteriorates the coherent scattering, reducing the brightness and chromaticity of the signal. In analogy, specular interference
is also the preferred approach to evaluate surface quality of optical elements in photonics
(Hong 2010). In evolutionary ecology, WIPs and WISs could serve as honest signals of
physical condition, wing integrity and flight performance. However, emitting such vivid
signals is also prone to carry evolutionary costs. Just as Odonates likely perceive these highcontrast signals (Brydegaard et al. 2018), it is conceivable that predators with acute vision
could also detect them (Outomuro and Johansson 2015).

Spectral features from structural versus absorption
The perception of WIPs and WISs resulting from the macroscopic wing thickness is governed by the quantum absorption bands of the observer’s visual opsins (Fig. 1). The perceived chromaticity of the WISs therefore raises fundamental questions regarding the
evolution and constraints of the wing structure (Shevtsova et al. 2011). A change as small
as ± 100 nm in wing thickness can entirely flip the chromatic impression of WIPs and WISs
based on the properties of the visual system of these Odonates (Fig. 1). Thus, a relative

The mode locking hypothesis
Hypothetically, stabilizing sexual selection for a specific wing thickness could arise from
interference resonance and the molecular absorption bands of the female opsins. If that
held true, stabilizing selection could act to mode-lock the thickness to preserve the specific chromatic signal. In this study we test one prediction based on this hypothesis, that
reproductively successful individuals (those captured in copula) will display significantly
reduced phenotypic variance in wing thickness compared to the general population, reflecting the removal of deviations from the optimal signal. Should this hold true, it would be an
example of mode-locking (Svelto and Hanna 2010), and would imply that a specific mode,
m in Eq. 1, would be locked in respect to opsin absorption bands in Fig. 1 through sexual
selection.
In laser physics this mechanism is well-known (Svelto and Hanna 2010). In laser cavities, a pumped gain medium, with an inverted electron population, constitutes available
resources in the intracavity environment. Slightly more successful photons with advantages in terms of; (1) propagation along the cavity, (2) constructive resonance wavelengths
between cavity mirror or (3) polarization preferences in respect to Brewster windows
quickly outcompete and extinguish less competitive photons. In other words, lasers are a
case of light speed evolution. This physical analogy illustrates the core mechanism we propose for damselflies: evolutionary mode-locking. Just as the certain photons excel in respect
to the macroscopic length of a laser cavity and quantum resonance of the gain medium, so
could certain individuals outcompete others according to their wing thickness in relation to
the molecular absorption bands of their opsins.

Aim
To investigate if there is evidence consistent with stabilizing sexual selection acting on
wing thickness, knowledge on how wing thickness scales with size within species, and how
wing size and thickness are related to mating success in their natural habitat is needed. We
therefore set out to determine: (1) whether wing membrane thickness is a fixed trait with low
intraspecific variability; (2) if membrane thickness scales with wing area or is constrained;
and (3) whether there is evidence of sexual selection acting on wing thickness among the
examined species. To examine these relationships, we investigated the allometric relationship between wing area and membrane thickness in two species of damselfly, Ischnura elegans and Enallagma cyathigerum. These two species belong to the family Coenagrionidae
(pond damselflies), a speciose family which is characterized by high population densities
and strong male-male competition for females, resulting in sexual selection on wing size,
wing shape, and body size (Gosden and Svensson 2009; Gupta et al. 2025). Investigating
the role of WIPs and WISs - if any - on mating success in natural populations of these
species - would clarify if sexual selection operates also on these wing coloration traits as
well, in addition to the already well-established role of wing size, wing shape and body
size. We used shortwave infrared hyperspectral imaging to create high-resolution maps of
wing membrane thickness and compared these measurements to wing area. Furthermore, by
evaluating mating success in wild populations, we explored whether wing thickness is a trait
currently under sexual selection in these species.

Materials and methods
Specimen collection and preparation
Specimens used in this study are a subset of samples reported in Gupta et al. (2025). A
total of 214 specimens, including 108 I. elegans (60 males and 48 females) and 106 E.
cyathigerum (63 males and 43 females), were used for this study. They were collected in
13 small ponds surrounded by open grass fields located around Lund in southern Sweden.
Sampling was carried out between May and July of 2020 for I. elegans and between May
and July of 2021 for E. cyathigerum. For further details regarding geographic coordinates,
sampling strategy, and natural history of the two species, see Gupta et al. (2025). Copulation status was assessed by noting whether individuals were captured in the mating posture
specific to Odonata (e.g., “mating wheel”, (Corbet 1999). Because I. elegans always forms
a mating wheel when we capture them, the copula means genital contact and likely sperm
transfer. In E. cyathigerum, we captured couples in both mating wheel and tandem positions.
In the former case, it is strictly speaking not possible to verify whether copulation has taken
place but we defined both as copula because no further information was available through
our sampling. The majority of I. elegans were captured in copula, with 45 males and all 48
females collected while mating, whereas the E. cyathigerum sample included both mating pairs (23 males and 28 females) and single individuals. Upon capture, sexual maturity
was assessed based on body color and wing stiffness, and only sexually mature individuals
were used in this study. Individuals were sacrificed through an exposure to low temperature
(− 4 ℃). No collection permits were required, as sampling was conducted outside nature
reserves and neither Ischnura elegans nor Enallagma cyathigerum is threatened; both are
classified as Least Concern (LC) by the IUCN. Our sampling is part of a long-term population survey since 2000, and the data suggests no sign of our sampling affecting the population size (Gómez-Llano et al. 2020). For the subsequent hyperspectral scanning (Fig. 2), all
four wings of each individual were detached and mounted. However, wings were excluded
from the final analysis if they were cropped by the camera’s limited field of view (Fig. 2d)

Hyperspectral imaging system
A polarimetric short-wave infrared (SWIR) push broom hyperspectral camera (HySpex
SWIR-384, Norsk Elektro Optikk AS, Norway (HySpex; Li et al. 2022) was used to capture
the spectral profiles of wings of damselflies. This imaging system operated across a wavelength range of 0.95–2.5 μm, offering a spatial resolution of 240 μm per pixel and capturing
data across 288 spectral bands. Illumination was provided by a 150 W halogen–tungsten
lamp. The camera and light source were aligned at ± 56° relative to the wing surface, a configuration near the Brewster angle of chitin to optimize the detection of interference fringes
(Hong 2010). While damselfly wings naturally exhibit variation in orientations of the local
surface normals (Jongerius and Lentink 2010) due to venation, the angular acceptance of
both the illumination source and the camera objective is sufficiently broad to accommodate
these deviations. This optical tolerance ensures that the specular condition is maintained
across the vast majority of the membrane surface, despite its non-planar topography (see
Fig. 2a). Two ultra-broadband polarizers were incorporated to measure co-polarized reflectance. The hyperspectral camera included a lens with a 20 mm aperture and a scanning width
of 40 mm, allowing multiple wings to be imaged in a single exposure. All hyperspectral signals were calibrated using a Lambertian gray reference standard with 50% reflectance. Due
to the highly reflective nature of damselfly wings, reflectance values occasionally exceeded
100% when compared to the diffuse calibration standard. Damselfly wings were spread
flat and mounted 20 mm above the black neoprene to minimize background reflections. A
motorized translation stage was then used to move the mounted damselfly wings across the
camera’s line sensor to capture the hyperspectral image.

Wing thickness calculation
Wing thickness was determined from the captured spectral fringes. It is important to clarify
the relationship between the visual pattern, the spectral data, and the physical trait. We
use the term WIP to describe the spatially chromatic patterns observed in reflected light
(Figs. 2b and d and 3). In contrast, spectral fringe refers to the reflectance observed within
the 288 spectral bands captured by the hyperspectral camera (Fig. 2c). While each pixel
yields a spectral profile corresponding to local thickness, the effective wing thickness is the
thickness obtained from the spatially integrated spectral profile of the whole membrane.
The same thin-film interference governs the visible wing interference pattern, so the SWIRderived effective thickness predicts the visible signal an observer would perceive. The correlation coefficient C and quality parameter Q (Eqs. 5–6) were used to match the measured
and modelled fringes for both the per-pixel thickness and the effective (whole-membrane)
thickness. The only thresholding step was a basic background removal to separate the wing
from its surroundings; veins regions were not explicitly masked, but they scatter light and

Confidence interval calculation
To assess the reliability and variability of our statistical moment estimates for effective wing
thickness and wing area, we computed 95% confidence intervals for measures such as the
mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, skewness, kurtosis, median, interquartile
range, and relative spread.
We achieved this using the Bootstrap resampling technique. This method works by
repeatedly drawing samples from the original dataset with replacement, meaning that once
an observation is drawn, it is put back, making it eligible to be selected again. Confidence
intervals for the descriptive statistics (Tables 1 and 2, S1–S2) were obtained by bootstrap
resampling (100,000 resamples; 2.5th–97.5th percentiles). All analyses were performed
in MATLAB. From this distribution, we calculated the 95% confidence interval for our
original statistics by taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. This provides a non-parametric
estimation of the uncertainty for each reported value. It should be noted that this bootstrapping procedure was applied only to the population statistics in Tables 1 and 2, S1 and
S2. Confidence intervals for the fitted fringe-model parameters, the effective and per-pixel
wing thickness obtained by trust-region nonlinear fitting of the interference model (Fig. 2)
were calculated using the Delta method (asymptotic normal approximation based on the
Jacobian). For the allometric analysis (Fig. 4), wing area was modelled as a power law of
effective thickness, A = a·db, fitted in log-log form as a linear mixed-effects model (fitlme,
MATLAB) with individual identity as a random effect to account for the inclusion of multiple wings per individual. The scaling exponent b and its 95% confidence interval were
taken from the fixed-effects estimates.

Statistical analysis
To characterize the variation of wing traits, we computed a set of descriptive statistics,
including the mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range, skewness, and kurtosis. Kurtosis was specifically analyzed to assess the peakedness of the distributions and
to detect potential Gaussian or bimodal deviations that could indicate the presence of different groups. Additionally, to enable direct comparisons of variability between traits with
disparate physical dimensions (wing area in mm2 versus thickness in µm), we calculated
the relative spread (defined as the interquartile range divided by the median). This normalized metric allows for a robust assessment of whether wing thickness exhibits lower relative variability than wing area. To evaluate whether wing thickness traits in damselflies are
subject to selection, we compared wing thickness traits between mated and unmated males
upon capture in the field (Lande and Arnold 1983). Males that were captured in tandem or
in copula were classified as ‘coupled’, while males that were captured solitary were classified as ‘single’. This cross-sectional estimate is an established method of estimating male
mating success and sexual selection in damselflies (Gosden and Svensson 2008). We com-

Results
Wing membrane thickness distribution
The wings shown in Fig. 3 represent the most common thickness patterns observed for each
species and sex. The species differed in the degree of sexual dimorphism: in I. elegans,
females have larger wings (Villalobos-Jiménez and Hassall 2019) but comparable thickness
to males, whereas in E. cyathigerum, males and females show minimal differences both in
wing thickness and size. In both species forewings had more extensive thickened regions
near the leading edge but also larger thin regions near the trailing edge compared to hindwings. This variation in wing thickness is responsible for the local interference patterns of
the wings.

Scaling of thickness with wing area
The relationship between wing area A and effective wing thickness d differs among males
and females of I. elegans and E. cyathigerum (Fig. 4, for copula and singles plotted in
separated plot, see Fig. S1−2). This effective fringe was obtained by spatially integrating
the spectral profiles of all wing pixels across the XY plane representing the wings surface,
where the Z-plane corresponds to the wings thickness. Power-law fits (A = a·db) applied to
the wing area A and effective wing thickness d scatter plot consistently yielded slopes b
with confidence intervals that generally included zero, indicating near-zero or only weakly
negative/positive trends across species and sexes. Including individual identity as a random
effect, to account for the non-independence of multiple wings per individual, did not change
this result: under the mixed-effects model all slopes remained non-significant, with 95%
confidence intervals for b including zero in every group (Fig. 4, Fig. S6).

Statistical distributions of wing thickness and area
We found that while the area displays a relative spread (defined as the interquartile range
divided by the median) of 36%, the wing thickness only displays a relative spread of 10%.
Membrane thickness remains remarkably uniform within each sex and species group
(Fig. 4; Tables 1 and 2; Tables S1 and S2). The median values closely mirror the means,
indicating minimal influence from outliers. We also checked the distribution for bimodality
by calculating the kurtosis (a Gaussian distribution has a kurtosis of 3, while a flat top-hat
distribution has 1.8). All groups, except for the I. elegans male hindwing (mated) and the

13

48 Page 16 of 21

Evolutionary Ecology (2026) 40:48

E. cyathigerum male hindwing (single), indicated normal distributions of wing thickness
(Tables S1-S2, Fig. S3-4). Hindwings were consistently thicker than forewings. Low dispersion in membrane thickness is also supported by the small standard deviations (approximately 50 to 80 nm, representing 5–9% of the mean) and IQR-to-median ratios (5–13%),
which suggest limited relative variability. In contrast, wing area showed considerably higher
variability. Mean values ranged from approximately 63 to 83 mm², with standard deviations
typically ranging from 8.5 to 20 mm² (ca. 13–25% of the mean). The IQR-to-median ratios
for area also reflect a notably larger relative spread (21–39%).
We tested the hypothesis that individuals with high mating success that were found in
copula would exhibit a narrower range of wing thicknesses, which would be indicative of
stabilizing selection on this trait. To formally test for differences based on mating status, we
compared wing traits between individuals found in copula versus those caught as singles
(see Tables 1 and 2 for all p-values). Across all traits, comparisons between coupled and
single individuals were non-significant, with the single exception of the variance in wing
area for I. elegans male hindwings (p = 0.043 per wing; p = 0.023 per individual), which
was not significant after Bonferroni correction. A few other comparisons showed weak,
non-significant trends — most notably the median wing thickness of E. cyathigerum female
hindwings, while the majority of tests showed no trend at all (p > 0.4). Per-trait p-values are
reported in Tables 1 and 2.

Discussion
Our study found that wing membrane thickness in the examined local populations of the
damselflies Ischnura elegans and Enallagma cyathigerum does not scale with wing area.
We show that membrane thickness shows low variability in these two species, and that it
does not follow the expected allometric scaling. Importantly, this conclusion is not merely
based on a lack of statistical significance. The 95% confidence intervals for the allometric
scaling exponents are narrow and consistently include zero (Fig. 4), implying no detectable thickness scaling with area despite a ~ 30% relative variance in wing area. We express
area as a power law of thickness, A = a·db; since both are geometric traits, this relation is
algebraically invertible and implies no causal or temporal ordering between them. Under
isometry this model would predict an exponent of two, but our exponents are close to zero,
so thickness does not scale with wing area across the size range sampled. Because field of
view limitations and wing damage prevented us from restricting measurements to a single
side, multiple wings per individual were included. To account for this non-independence,
individual identity was included as a random effect in the allometric models (Fig. 4), and the
coupled-versus-single comparisons were repeated on per-individual means (Tables 1 and 2);
neither changed our conclusions. An individual’s left and right wings also yield highly consistent thickness estimates (Table S3, Fig. S5), independently confirming that our thickness
measurements are reproducible. The corrected and uncorrected fits are compared in Fig. S6.
The observed invariance in wing thickness stands in stark contrast to the variation in
wing area. Potentially, wing thickness could be constrained by a selective pressure that
overrides typical developmental scaling. This near-constant thickness is likely necessary
for maintaining a consistent and effective optical signal. While our extensive sampling of
519 wings provides high confidence for the lack of allometry in these damselfly species,

13

Evolutionary Ecology (2026) 40:48

Page 17 of 21 48

the relationship between wing size and thickness appears to be taxon dependent. This result
contrasts with findings from other insect orders, such as Diptera and Hymenoptera, where
wing thickness and area are positively correlated across species (Li et al. 2023a, b). To
determine if this thickness invariance is a widespread phenomenon or a unique evolutionary
strategy of Odonata, similar high-resolution allometric examinations must be extended to
other insect families and species. However, we restrict our comparative analysis to studies
utilizing quantitative hyperspectral spectroscopy (Li et al. 2022, 2023a, b; Müller et al.
2023). Quantitative metrics and interpretations of WIPs studies based on widespread RGB
imagery is challenging since the interpretation of RGB chromaticity by Newton rings often
provides misleading results. Local variations in pigmentation (melanization) also alter the
refractive index via the Kramers-Kronig relations. High absorption by melanin significantly
increases the local refractive index, which effectively increases the optical path length even
if the physical thickness remains constant. Failing to account for this optical coupling can
lead to systematic artifacts, such as inverted thickness gradients where the trailing edge
appears thicker than the leading edge (Garg et al. 2024), that contradict established aerodynamic profiles. Furthermore, the examined wings lack melanin in these regions, so we are
not affected by refractive index variations, allowing us to disregard thickness from pigmentation artifacts.
Our finding suggests that a phenotypic trait can be decoupled from allometric scaling
that governs other morphological traits, potentially because it serves as a critical signaling
function. Furthermore, because wing integrity is essential for this signal, damage to their
wings from sources such as predation attempts could make WIPs indicators of adult age and
quality in insects (Kemp 2002; Sasaki et al. 2017).
We tested the hypothesis that reproductively successful individuals would exhibit a narrower range of wing thicknesses due to stabilizing selection; we also tested whether their
mean wing thickness differed, which would indicate directional selection. In particular, if
strong stabilizing selection was operating, we would expect the variance in wing thickness
to be significantly lower in the group caught in copula (representing a sample of successful individuals), compared to the more heterogeneous group of single males. However, our
results supported neither: we found no statistical difference in either the mean or the variance of wing thickness between the two groups. This may suggest that either stabilizing
sexual selection acting on wing thickness is absent in these species, or that it is weak, or
that our assessment of mating success was too noisy to detect such effects, given the small
effect size and the modest sample size in this study. Further support for sexual selection not
operating on WIPs in these damselflies, or is weak, is the fact that they inhabit open and
light habitats (Willink et al. 2025) where such colours are likely to be less visible, compared
to insects living in darker forest habitats (Shevtsova et al. 2011) and that sexual dimorphism
in WIPs in both the species we investigated here is limited (Fig. 3). Nonetheless, the high
intraspecific consistency of membrane thickness, despite large variation in wing size, suggests that some selective pressure, possibly stabilizing natural selection, maintains a specific
thickness.
While the lack of evidence for sexual selection on wing thickness and WIPs may harbor
biological insights, we also acknowledge that our sampling strategy is limited in various
ways to test this hypothesis properly. First, our sample size (n = 108 for I. elegans, n = 106
for E. cyathigerum across multiple populations) is low for estimating selection in natural
populations (Kingsolver et al. 2001). Second, females of I. elegans exhibit three distinct

13

48 Page 18 of 21

Evolutionary Ecology (2026) 40:48

color morphs that may contribute to different mating dynamics, which may have contributed
to reduce the statistical power in our analyses. Third, the two damselfly species studied here
can mate multiple times during a single mating season, meaning that our estimate of selection is noisy, even under ideal conditions. While the present method for estimating sexual
selection is established in many species of Odonata including the two focal species in this
research (Steele et al. 2011; Svensson et al. 2006), the inherent noisiness in our estimate
may suggest that null result should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence for the lack of
sexual selection on wing thickness. Obtaining greater sample size, controlling for female
morphs and spatiotemporal variation in selection on wing thickness would be an important
future direction to further elucidate the role of WIPs in sexual selection of damselflies.
The invariant wing thickness in relation to wing area observed in this study yields a key
opportunity for remote insect species identification. Wing thickness seems to be a stable
intraspecies characteristic that is independent of wing size, making it a potentially powerful biometric for remote species identification using technologies like hyperspectral lidar
(Müller et al. 2023). Just as specular interference is used to assess the quality of optical
components (Hong 2010), the integrity of an insect’s WIP could also indicate its age and
flight performance.
This study underscores that systematic hyperspectral imaging from a range of different taxa will facilitate WIS based inference of taxonomic origin. Hyperspectral imaging is
a powerful approach for quantifying wing-membrane thickness, but the optimal spectral
window depends on the species. Because the spacing between interference fringes scales
with the square of wavelength and inversely with membrane thickness, the fringes of a
very thin wing become broad and sparsely spaced at the long wavelengths; for the thinnestwinged insects, such as mosquitoes, fewer than one full oscillation may fall within the
NIR and SWIR window (Li et al. 2025), limiting the precision of the thickness estimate.
Visible-range hyperspectral imaging, with its shorter wavelengths and correspondingly finer
fringe spacing, is therefore better suited to thin, small clear-winged insects (Stavenga 2014),
whereas the NIR and SWIR range is well suited to thicker, larger-winged species such as the
damselflies studied here. A further advantage of the NIR and SWIR ranges is that melanin
absorption is weak at these wavelengths, so thickness retrieval is less confounded by the
pigmentation-induced refractive-index variation that complicates measurements in the visible. Systematic scans will capture species specific thicknesses and expected optical signals,
improving the ability to identify taxa in lidar data from optical WIS.
