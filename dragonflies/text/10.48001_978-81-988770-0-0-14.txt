Abstract
Ecocriticism, in recent years, has migrated to locations other than the terrestrial grounds.
It has taken into account the ecology of the ocean, giving rise to Blue Cultural Studies and
Blue Humanities. In this interdisciplinary approach, literary and cultural texts that depict
ocean and waters have been analyzed to comment upon the cultural practices that respect
and celebrate water bodies. At the core this approach develops an interdisciplinary analytical model to examine the relationship between human beings and oceans and highlights the
cultural, spiritual, and emotional connections that people have with water. As a mode of
enquiry, it overlaps with History, Literature and Cultural Studies and tries to emphasize
the environmental dimensions of human-water relationship. A literary work that is rooted

1 Introduction
Sea Fiction, also known as Oceanic Fiction or Nautical Fiction has the sea as the setting
and has a water body that connects and separates people and places. This genre is
considered significant in postcolonial literature as sea is often depicted as the link for
trade, human mobility, imperialism and exposure to the world (Kerstin Knopf). Oceanic
novel often tells the story of adventure, exploration, cross-culturalism and survival. In
such a novel, ocean is not merely a setting but rather an agency which can interact with
human beings and shape their destinies (Dilip Menon et al.). Rich symbolism is another
aspect of oceanic novel where sea, voyage, ship and other nautical images become symbols
of freedom, slavery, escape, connection, isolation and so on. Voyages in such novels often
stand for the journey of self-discovery which becomes more nuanced if the protagonist is
a colonized subject or a woman. Emma Staniland argues that the presence of water in
fiction can be considered as a global theme and women in fictional waters and women
writing about water can be considered as a peculiar cultural codification that stands for
the mapping of a fluid terrain. She maintains that water and water bodies are metaphors of
liquidity, apart from providing thematic routes. She also finds a deep connection between
women and water, linking the fluidity with amniotic origin. Furthermore, she traces a
genealogical and geographical connection between women and water. In “Hydrofeminism:
Or, On Becoming a Body of Water,” Astrida Neimanis explains that all humans are both
materially and metaphysically linked via water. Likewise, Leighan Renaud, in her section
on Nalo Hopkinson’s The Salt Roads, demonstrates how Ezili’s spirit connects Black women
across different times and places. This lineage of liquid femininity illustrates how ancestry
moves through water, fostering new types of relational identity and resistance. These
aquatic lineages operate not only in spiritual ways but also politically—are vitalizing
forgotten stories and communities: “We are all bodies of water, in the constitutional, the
genealogical, and the geographical sense” (3).

2 Women of the Sea
Edward Rowe Snow in her work Women of the Sea highlights the frequently overlooked
maritime narratives of women. Rather than being mere passengers or victims, the women
featured in this study actively influence, endure, and at times control the vast oceanic
environments they occupy. In a maritime realm led by male perspectives, Snow reclaims
and amplifies stories that portray women as sailors, survivors, pirates, navigators, lighthouse keepers, and legendary symbols of defiance. From the determined Susanna Haswell
Rowson, who was shipwrecked in her youth and later emerged as an educational reformer
and literary figure, to Hsi Kai Ching Yih, the powerful leader of the largest pirate fleet
ever, these women exemplify various ways that gender, geography, and maritime experience converge. Snowframes these narratives not as anomalies but as essential elements of
maritime history. Edward Rowe Snow’s work challenges the idea of the ocean as solely
a male domain. Rather, it shows how the ocean serves as a catalyst for women’s empowerment. She traces the journey of Susanna from a shipwrecked girl to an empowered
woman:
Susanna published, under the patronage of the Duchess of Devonshire, her first work
entitled Victoria: A Novel in Two Volumes. Printed SUSANNA in Covent Garden, the
book consists of a series of letters interspersed with poetry. The plot is weak, and not until
1790, when she published Charlotte Temple, did her literary efforts succeed. Susanna now
went on to great heights in the literary world, her Charlotte Temple outdistancing another
great favorite, Horace Walpole’s Castle of Otranto. She had a brief, but important, career
on the stage, and then she established a girls’ school in Greater Boston which is still
operating today (12-13) .

In Women of the Sea, Snow reveals that the ocean frequently acts as a spiritual force—
serving to unveil, cleanse, or begin. Oceanic spiritual beliefs are prominently depicted
in the tale of Mademoiselle de Bourk, a young French noblewoman who was captured
and enslaved following a shipwreck on the Barbary Coast. Her tale is rich with prayers,
metaphorical tempests, and wondrous salvations. The sea in this place is transitional—
a threshold between countries, existences, and spiritual realms. These shifts resonate
with age-old religious and mythological frameworks: saltwater baptism, divine trials via
suffering, and life’s renewal through ocean journeys: “We are dying of hunger and subject
to every kind of ill treatment... I implore you instantly to take compassion on our misery”
(25).
Edward Rowe Snow’s female characters frequently embody transitional identities influenced by diaspora, exile, and mixed cultural aﬀiliations. Their oceanic encounters remove
them from established national, social, or gender roles. For instance, Snow describes,
Joanna Colcord, who was born on a vessel, grows up completely on the ocean. Her marine
upbringing influences her scholarly identity as a thinker and change-maker. She represents a subject that is detached from specific territories—neither tied to a particular land
nor a nation. Likewise, Mei Ying, a Chinese woman taken by pirates and subsequently
celebrated in poetry, emerges as a spiritual icon. Her identity shifts from individual to
legend: “Chen ke kin seaou hee, Chuy sze chung soo meen... Ying lee shang pei hwuy”
(34). Translated as ”Although her life was tumultuous, her goodness shone,” the poem
portrays her as both a victim and a moral model. These women represent ‘oceanic identity,’ a concept explored in diaspora studies and Blue Humanities— identities shaped not
by land and permanence, but through movement, disruption, and interconnections. Their
perspectives are dynamic, diverse, and defy patriarchal rigidity.

3 Blue Culture Studies
Blue Cultural Studies signifies a pivotal shift in environmental humanities, transitioning
from the ‘green’ concentration on land-based environments and ecological management
to the ‘blue’ focus on seas, oceans, and water-based knowledge systems. Traditional ecocritical perspectives emphasize rootedness, boundaries, and biocentric land stories, while
Blue Humanities highlight mobility, fluidity, relationality, and interconnectedness across
aquatic landscapes. Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor’s The Dragonfly Sea illustrates this change,
not only thematically but also in structure and spirit. The story unfolds in the Indian
Ocean islands, especially Pate Island, and focuses on Ayaana, whose existence from childhood to adulthood is spiritually and culturally connected to the sea. Her identity does
not stem from a specific national or tribal origin; instead, it traverses maritime pathways
linking East Africa to China, Oman, and further locations. In this voyage, the ocean serves
not merely as a symbol of potential but as a core awareness that reflects, confronts, and

ocean’s dynamic, elusive character. Such fluidity not only challenges patriarchal and territorial authority, but also creates possibility for new feminine epistemologies and healing
practices based on relationships instead of control. Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor’s work offers
a literary growth, an oceanic reimagining of feminine identity.
Blue Humanities views the ocean as a reservoir of cultural memory, trauma, trade,
spirituality, and migration, rather than as an empty place or a geographical barrier. In
The Dragonfly Sea, Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor transforms the Indian Ocean into a layered
record of hidden histories. Ayaana’s life has always been affected by a feeling of mysticism
around the water. Her mother, Munira, tells her stories about individuals who come from
the sea—ghosts, lovers, traders, and ancestors—and Ayaana incorporates these sea-tales
into her emotional and cultural life. During her childhood, Ayaana seeks sanctuary in the
rhythms of the water:
Time-dissolving floating. Solitude and wordlessness, and everything traveled toward
an unknown beckoning. Even she did. But underwater she did not need to worry about
labeling things in order to contain them. Feeling, sensing, experiencing—that was enough
for knowledge. The sea had many eyes, and, now hers were another pair. A passing
fish stared. A human looked back. She drifted with the currents, with the things of the
current. She drifted until it was necessary to surface for air (20) .
Through the above extract Owuor unveils the profound connection Ayaana has with
the ocean. It serves as both an audience and storyteller—a contrast to the quietness
of the adult humans around her. The ocean carries tales the earth is hesitant to share.
It murmurs not truths, but heritage recollections and spiritual realities. In ecofeminist
terms, Ayaana’s intuitive understanding of the ocean signifies a non-patriarchal way of
knowing—based on care, attentiveness, and non-verbal bonds.

4 The Portryal of Sea and Temporal Space
The sea is portrayed as maternal in the narrative, yet it challenges the societal constructs
of motherhood and legitimacy. While Munira, her birth mother, is plagued by societal
criticism and distances herself from Ayaana’s feelings, the ocean welcomes Ayaana’s obliviousness. It does not need responses. This oceanic maternity, which is not anthropocentric, relies on emotional resonance rather than blood ties. Notably, prior to her formal
schooling or journeys, Ayaana cultivates a physical instinct regarding tidal movements
and oceanic patterns. She aligns herself with the sea’s ‘whimsical language,’ understanding its emotions and cues in ways that anticipate her future spiritual growth. This early

This additionally illustrates Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor’s dedication to a tangible maritime poetics. Ayaana’s understanding comes not from literature or wise figures but from
physical awareness. Her thoughts, feelings, and self-awareness are aligned with a nonhuman realm, making her oceanic identity both spiritual and ecological. In ecofeminist
theory, this stage of Ayaana’s existence illustrates an essential concept: that healing and
identity arise not from external forces, but from experienced relationships with the earth—
or, in this scenario, the ocean. The young girl’s talent for positioning herself within oceanic
patterns instead of land-based structures is her initial expression of defiance. She isn’t
required to be validated by her father’s identity or her mother’s background; the sea provides her with a more timeless and vast heritage. This moment in the story is crucial as it
reveals that Ayaana’s quest is not about discovering a father or a sense of national identity.
It involves making herself understandable through the language of waves, tides, and ancestral movements. As she transitions into the later phases of her life—where this balance
is shattered by treachery, banishment, and alienation—the difference from her childhood
connection to the sea will highlight how profoundly the ocean shapes her fundamental
identity.
On land, Ayaana is forever a daughter, a burden, a source of scandal for someone.
Her father is missing, her mother is viewed as inappropriate, and she endures the weight
of Pate’s societal scrutiny. Owuor subverts these narratives by positioning the sea as
Ayaana’s genuine parent, guide, and confidant. From the beginning of the novel, Ayaana
senses her uniqueness compared to her peers—not solely due to her uncertain heritage but
also because of her profound inner experiences. She discovers connection not in individuals
but in marine animals, ocean tides, and the stars shining over the water:
The sea was liquid charcoal spattered with the innards of moonlight. She tumbled
into the seduction of the deep, craving its feel. Pulsing water. No gaps in the ocean, no
distances between beings. She sank deeper, losing the sense of up or down. The sea. Its
iridescent layers. A creature burning an inner fire floated past (137).

Ayaana’s selection as a student of Chinese descent is a key moment in the novel that
merges the lines between biology, myth, and recollection. The government-backed effort
to identify descendants of Zheng He’s 15th-century fleet highlights a symbolic recovery
of Indian Ocean links—while also revealing the problematic and oversimplifying aspects
of genetic essentialism. Owuor challenges the notion that identity and belonging can be
quantified or validated via bloodline testing, proposing that the ocean provides a much
broader and more accurate account of kinship. Regardless of the formal language surrounding DNA testing and diplomatic goodwill, Ayaana perceives her inclusion in the
program as random—even to her. Her community views the project with scepticism and
confusion, and Ayaana herself is uncertain about her link to the Chinese admiral. However, on a more profound emotional level, her existence is deeply connected to the ocean’s
narratives of movement and interaction. Owuor clarifies this directly: “Even me. I have
Chinese blood—you see my eyes? Can you see?” She pulled at her eyelids” (172).
As Ayaana moves into her teenage years, her once close and instinctive bond with the
sea faces challenges from societal expectations and gendered scrutiny. Her encounters with
betrayal—both in love and within her community—signify a crucial turning point in the
story, where the land transforms into an adversary and the sea becomes her sole refuge.
The evaluation and regulation of her body and decisions reflect the patriarchal standards
that ecofeminism condemns as harmful to women and the environment. On Pate Island,
the land environment becomes stifling, ruled by regulations, rumours, and embarrassment.
The betrayal happens when a love interest—whose feelings Ayaana thinks are genuine—
publicly shames and dehumanizes her. The treachery is both personal and symbolic.
Ayaana is reduced to a source of controversy. Her emotional landscape is devastated, and
her hitherto uncertain position in her community crumbles completely: “An exchange
of furies in one glance. Ayaana clenched her fists, angry and hopeless. Mama Suleiman
smirked. Suleiman guffawed. Ayaana shriveled. By her will alone, no tears fell. Suleiman’s
braying pummeled Ayaana’s heart” (160). The social harshness of terrestrial existence here
stands in stark contrast to the previous softness of the ocean. Ayaana is now perceived not
as an individual but as a container of disgrace. This treachery—of trust, of closeness, and
of respect—exposes the patriarchal frameworks that ecofeminism connects as inherently
tied to mechanisms of oppression and authority.
Ayaana looks to the water for restoration, rather than the community. Her return to
the ocean is motivated by survival rather than escapism. She kneels before the water,
shattered, wishing for transformation rather than erasure. Owuor depicts Ayaana finding
safety in the flowing embrace of the sea—not to disintegrate, but to reclaim the self that
existed before she was wounded. She also implies that Ayaana aspires to abandon the role
of the embarrassed girl and revert to a more basic, oceanic nature. In this performance,
the water serves as a feminine counterpoint to patriarchal earthly judgment. It is a holy

Despite her training as a maritime cadet—ironically, in a landlocked environment—
Ayaana persistently searches for the metaphysical ocean. The formal, bureaucratic knowledge she gains in China is rooted in accuracy and order, contrasting sharply with her
previous intuitive, physical connection to the sea. She learns to find her way with instruments, equations, and illustrations, but longs for the sounds and quietness of the actual
sea. Although Ayaana performs well in her studies, she feels lost spiritually. This disconnection emphasizes a key principle of ecofeminism: that genuine understanding comes
from relationships, not from viewing things as objects. The sea, once a shared educator and spirit partner, is now merely information in the Chinese classroom: “She would
have to relinquish her feeling for water to the power of numbers, navigational compasses,
Napier’s Rules, coordinates, and geopolitics. She watched her lecturer. Could she propose
that the sea sweats differently depending on the time and flavor of day and night? …
moon” (322)
Ayaana also continues to exist on the outskirts culturally. Her heritage designation,
which allowed her entry, turns into a point of examination. Despite being selected for
her alleged Chinese heritage, she continues to be regarded as an outsider. The historical
memory used to justify her inclusion is not experienced but enforced. This institutional
discord reveals the vulnerability of identity when formed through bureaucratic and racialized structures. Her sense of belonging, once vast, is now conditional and divided.
Ayaana does not entirely lose herself. She starts to develop an inner ocean—a mental
reconstruction of her former environments, recollections, and insights. She creates maps
from recollection, enjoys tunes that evoke waves, and reflects on her past bond with the
ocean. The reemergence of these components in her awareness indicates the strength of
her oceanic identity. Owuor demonstrates the spiritual resilience of oceanic awareness.
Even in its lack, the ocean stays vibrant within her—recollection serving as a means of
defiance against the dulling impacts of relocation.

Ayaana’s exchanges with Lai Jin, the Chinese artist and fellow sailor, are also significant during this stage. While their relationship is mixed—part friendship, part guidance,
and part romance—it aids Ayaana in starting to reconstruct her life. By reminiscing about
the ocean and talking about restoration, they discover mutual understanding. Their discussions focus less on technical expertise and more on significance. In a striking moment,
they discuss shattered pottery, resulting in a deep metaphor for restoration. This moment
brings to mind the Japanese technique of kintsugi, which applies gold to fix shattered ceramics, thus honouring flaws. This philosophy profoundly resonates with Ayaana. Her
self—hurt by betrayal, loss, and exile—is not diminished in value, but transformed. In
ecofeminist language, the metaphor suggests that feminine identity is not straightforward
or unblemished, but rather cyclical and marked by scars, with wholeness encompassing
brokenness: “Her voice cracked. “You can make it whole?” Lai Jin answered, “I will try.”
“It will be scarred.” “Yes” (441).
Ayaana’s stay in China thus transforms into a contradictory period: it is a spiritual
wasteland that directs her to her inner ocean. She comes out of it more centred, more
intricate, and more acutely conscious of the constraints of externally defined identities.
This development prepares her eventual return to Pate—not as a girl in search of approval,
but as a woman moulded by vast resilience. Ayaana’s return to Pate is characterized by
stillness, modesty, and a feeling of serene harmony. She does not come back as a victorious
heroine. There is no celebration, no reinstatement of social rank, no ultimate disclosure
about her parentage. Instead, Owuor presents the readers with a return influenced by
the sea’s principles: circular, tidal, contemplative, and limitless. Ayaana has stopped
looking for answers from others—she has discovered how to embrace uncertainty, to hear
without anticipation, to adapt. This indicates Ayaana’s arrival into what ecofeminism
identifies as developed relationality. She does not aim to control or be saved by her native
land. Nor does she seek to regain her position in a social structure that previously cast
her out. Rather, she discovers her balance in the elements—in water, salt, and breeze.
Her experiences have rendered her both more isolated and more intertwined, not with
individuals, specifically, but with the broader network of existence and recollection:
It was close to a new morning when the last of Ayaana’s welcomers left. She then
settled into Pate’s particular lonelinesses, among its drifting primordial ghosts. To these
she could show the contours of a densely populated inner topography, the portions of
China she had brought back to her island. Reaching through clouds, grasping at…nothing
(473). This reunification with the ocean is both heartfelt and spiritually significant, as well

A significant symbolic act is Ayaana’s participation in a surreal sea-ritual, where she
steps into the water, not to flee but to connect. This action recalls earlier instances in the
novel when she had found comfort in the sea, yet her method has changed now. She does
not negotiate. She makes no request. She presents herself in harmony, not in urgency. This
action embodies a fundamental ecofeminist understanding: that restoration arises not from
control or finality, but from mutual exchange with nature. Ayaana does not dominate the
ocean; she integrates into its rhythm. She does not rise above her past; she incorporates
it into a broader, oceanic identity. Her identity is now infused with the ethics of water—
gentleness, power, flow, and renewal. Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor’s ending of the narrative
avoids emotional catharsis. Instead, she grounds Ayaana in a sense of spiritual maturity.
Ayaana does not take control of her life; rather, she obtains the insight to completely
embrace it. Her development is distinguished by surrender rather than conquest—not to
fate, but to the rhythm of existence, which ebbs and flows like the sea. The Dragonfly Sea
does not conclude with assurance. It concludes with breath—with potential. In a society
rife with dislocation and border-drawing, Yvonne Adhiambo Owuor’s depiction of Ayaana
proposes a new kind of belonging—one based on a tale of quiet, grief, and the glint of love.
Ayaana is not done; she is still in motion, much like the sea that gave life to her spirit:
Ayaana could not move as fine essences seeped into her bones and transmuted the
past. Munira would arrive from Pemba just in time to adorn her daughter’s body with
henna, her finest work. Weeks later, a delicately made-up and bejeweled Ayaana emerged
in a flowing gown of ivory silk and lace. She floated across infinite thresholds, this rose—
scented Ayaana, Jamal’s betrothed (547) .
Thus, in Owuor’s narrative Ayaana and the sea have interchangeable identities. While
Ayaana imbibes ocean’s vastness, mystery and fluidity, the ocean imbibes from her vivacity, femininity and continuity. Furthermore, Ayaana is a product of the tidalectics—the
dialectic made of the ebb and flow of water and land and solid and liquid.

5 Conclusion
In Owuor’s narrative Ayaana and the sea possess interchangeable identities that dissolve
the boundary between human and nature. Ayaana embodies the ocean’s vastness, mystery, and fluidity, while the sea, in turn, reflects her vivacity, femininity, and continuity.
Their relationship is not one of mere metaphor but of mutual becoming, where both sustain and reshape each other’s essence. This fusion illustrates the tidalectic—a rhythm
of ebb and flow between water and land, solidity and liquidity—that structures not only
Ayaana’s identity but also the narrative’s larger cosmology. By aligning Ayaana with the
sea’s movements, Owuor underscores a vision of existence that is cyclical, interdependent,
and rooted in the fluid negotiations between human life and the natural world. Ayaana,
therefore, emerges as both child and custodian of the sea, symbolizing the inseparability
of the feminine spirit from the elemental forces that shape being and belonging.
