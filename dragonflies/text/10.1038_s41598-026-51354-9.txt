Generative adversarial networks enable biomimetic topology fusion with balanced mechanical performance and aesthetic quality

# Generative adversarial networks enable biomimetic topology fusion with balanced mechanical performance and aesthetic quality

## Abstract
AbstractConventional structural design studies often prioritize mechanical metrics, yet lack a unified narrative that renders the aesthetic expression of form both quantifiable and verifiable. To address this gap, we develop a GAN-based framework for biomimetic topology fusion generation, leveraging Cycle-Consistent GANs (CycleGAN) to learn bidirectional mappings and morphological translations between two classes of natural prototypes under unpaired supervision: performance-oriented morphologies (e.g., dragonfly wing venation and leaf venation), which exhibit high structural efficiency but comparatively weak visual order, and aesthetics-oriented patterns (e.g., honeycomb cells and pinecone spirals), which display pronounced geometric regularity and proportional structure but limited load-bearing capacity. Through cross-domain translation and fusion, the model synthesizes hybrid topological textures that simultaneously encode cues of structural robustness and ordered geometric features. These synthesized morphologies are subsequently validated via flexural (bending) testing in terms of load-carrying capacity and energy absorption efficiency, and are objectively characterized by a multi-metric aesthetic quantification scheme—computed on binary, vectorized structural maps—covering symmetry, complexity, and order. Across multiple morphology-pair settings, the fusion-generated structures exhibit a more balanced overall profile in both mechanical response and aesthetic metrics, indicating effective synergy between engineering usability and visual expression. In addition, we provide an application example in conceptual form design for orthopedic exoskeletal products, illustrating the cross-domain potential of the proposed approach at the interface of engineering design and aesthetic design.Keywords: Generative adversarial networks, Topological morphology fusion, Biomimetic design, Quantitative aesthetic evaluation, Structural performance assessmentSubject terms: Biomaterials, Theory and computation, Mechanical engineering, Machine learning

## Introduction
The synergistic design of topological morphologies at the interface of mechanical response and visual expression has become an increasingly important topic in materials and structural engineering1,2. Historically, topology-driven morphology design has been largely guided by mechanical criteria—such as strength, stiffness, and energy absorption—where load-bearing and protective functions are achieved through the control of geometry and material distribution3. With the growing demand in product design, architecture, and human-centered engineering for an integrated “function–ornament” paradigm, however, geometric order, proportional relationships, and visual appeal are being systematically incorporated into the engineering design discourse4,5. For instance, in electromagneto-thermal functional structures, Lee et al. reported a microwave-transparent metallic metamaterial heater that achieves near-unity transmission in the W band (75–110 GHz) while maintaining extremely low sheet resistance and rapid heating under low voltage, exemplifying how structural parameterization can concurrently satisfy multiple physical performance requirements6. In terms of objective characterization of “materials aesthetics,” diffraction patterns of quasicrystalline or fivefold-symmetric structures—interpretable as Fourier-domain signatures of spatial organization—can reveal non-crystallographic fivefold symmetry and multiscale hierarchical order, thereby providing a basis for quantitative analysis of geometric regularity and visual structural features7. Furthermore, in multifunctional topological lattice research, a recently proposed hollow tetrahedral truss–plate (HTTP) hybrid lattice introduces resonant cavities within hollow struts and plate elements to markedly enhance acoustic absorption, while still retaining high specific modulus and specific energy absorption under quasi-static bending. This demonstrates the practical feasibility of jointly considering acoustic and mechanical metrics within a single topological morphology8.
Traditional structural topology design has long been dominated by explicit physical models and iterative solving techniques, with the most representative methods being the SIMP (Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization) method9 and the Level Set method10. The SIMP method introduces a pseudo-density variable on a fixed finite element mesh, and uses a penalization scheme to push the material distribution towards a “0/1” discretization. The typical stiffness-density relation is expressed as , where represents the material density, and is a penalty exponent, thereby iteratively updating the material layout under given loadings and constraints to achieve an efficient load-bearing path11. The Level Set method, on the other hand, represents the structural boundary using an implicit function , and the boundary evolution is modeled through a Hamilton–Jacobi-type front propagation equation, such as , allowing for iterative updates of the morphology through "moving boundaries12." While these engineering-driven solution paradigms are mature and reliable for mechanical performance, the resulting morphologies often exhibit strong engineering characteristics such as fragmented branches, local notching, or highly complex porous boundaries, limiting direct control over “visual order/aesthetic vocabulary13.” To address this gap, recent research has explored the explicit incorporation of aesthetic principles into the topology design process. For example, Loos et al. applied gestalt grouping principles from industrial design to topological structures, using manual post-processing to enhance visual coherence, and their user studies verified the improved visual appeal of such interventions, suggesting that aesthetic goals can be systematically integrated into structural design through rules-based morphological constraints14. Further, Zhong et al. proposed a text-guided style transfer approach, allowing designers to control the visual style of topological structures through textual descriptions, thus pushing the "function–appearance" interplay towards a more interactive generative paradigm15. Simultaneously, data-driven generative models, particularly GANs, have been widely applied to the rapid generation of structural forms and exploration of design space16. Oh et al. introduced the "Deep Generative Design" framework, which couples topology design results with generative models iteratively to enhance the ability and diversity of early-stage design generation17. Nie et al. proposed TopologyGAN, which uses multiple physical fields from the initial domain as conditional inputs to drive a conditional GAN (cGAN) to generate structural distributions, enhancing the model’s ability to generalize across varying boundary conditions and loading scenarios18. Moreover, GAN-based methods that learn latent similarities across different operating conditions have been suggested to address the pain point of frequently changing parameters in engineering practice, enabling rapid prediction of “near-optimal” topologies during early design stages to enhance design flexibility19. In summary, current research continues to advance engineering reliability along the path of physical constraint-based iterative solutions while simultaneously integrating aesthetic rules, text-guided techniques, and generative models like GANs. These innovations are progressively shaping a new structural design paradigm that simultaneously addresses "performance–aesthetics" joint representation and design exploration.
“Biomimetic inspiration” has become a widely adopted route for innovation in materials and structural engineering20. Bio-inspired design (BID) goes beyond superficial shape replication; instead, it extracts transferable principles from biological prototypes21—such as hierarchical organization, load-transfer architectures, and geometric order—and translates them into manufacturable structural configurations22, thereby offering design cues that can simultaneously enhance mechanical response and visual expression23,24. For instance, inspired by the spiral-lamellar (Bouligand-like) architecture of mantis shrimp appendages, Zhang et al. developed basalt-fiber laminates with coupled sinusoidal and helicoidal layups, achieving markedly improved impact toughness and demonstrating an engineering realization of the “hierarchical orientation–energy dissipation/stress homogenization” mechanism25. Likewise, the diving-bell wall of the water spider (Argyroneta aquatica)—a composite of a silk-fiber network and a protein hydrogel—forms a mesh system characterized by “anchoring fibers–interwoven microfilaments–gel bonding,” exhibiting robust functional stability and structural clarity, and offering insights into the synergy between network organization and interfacial materials26. The double-diagonal reinforced lattice skeleton of deep-sea glass sponges has also motivated biomimetic lattices; studies indicate that such reinforced topologies can exhibit stable auxetic behavior under compression while maintaining high stiffness and energy absorption, underscoring the direct relevance of biological “reinforced topology” for engineering compression resistance and dissipation27. Furthermore, biomimetic gradient and hierarchical lattice designs for energy-absorption applications suggest that introducing multi-feature hierarchical gradient units and coordinated collapse mechanisms can significantly improve energy-absorption efficiency and plateau stability while preserving controllable crushing, providing mechanistically grounded guidance for multiscale configuration design28. In the domains of continuum structures and composite layup design, Völkl et al. systematically examined the Computer-Aided Internal Optimisation (CAIO) method, which iteratively aligns fiber principal axes along principal stress trajectories to reduce shear stress and strengthen load paths, and they reported convergence criteria and applicability analyses for engineering deployment; related standards likewise categorize growth-inspired structural evolution tools such as SKO/CAO/CAIO as a class of computable biomimetic design methodologies29. Nevertheless, despite substantial progress from these diverse perspectives, comparatively few studies have unified, within a single closed-loop workflow, morphology generation/fusion, mechanical validation, and objective quantification of aesthetic characteristics—thereby limiting the systematic articulation of an interpretable design space under a two-dimensional “performance–aesthetics” trade-off30,31.
In nature, dragonfly wings exhibit exceptional functional properties resulting from the coupling and synergy of morphology, structure, and material, providing biological inspiration for the research and development of aerospace vehicles, biomimetic flapping-wing devices, and micro air vehicles32. Chen et al. developed a 3D-printed, self-similar carbon fiber reinforced composite sandwich structure inspired by honeycomb arrangements, significantly enhancing energy absorption and impact resistance in automotive crashworthiness and aerospace buffering applications, while simultaneously demonstrating an aesthetically harmonious geometric configuration33. The spiral phyllotactic arrangement within pinecones is often associated with geometric regularities such as the Fibonacci sequence. Its hierarchical textures and proportional relationships can produce a strong sense of visual order and perceptual salience34. However, existing studies have largely examined these structures from morphological and geometric perspectives, while systematic mechanical characterization under engineering-relevant loading conditions remains comparatively limited35. Yan et al. proposed a biomimetic fin configuration inspired by the branching structure and heat-transfer mechanisms of plant leaf veins to enhance the efficiency of solar thermal management systems36. Building on this, further analysis of the combinatory relationships between different natural forms and structures (e.g., dragonfly wings–honeycomb, leaf venation–pinecone) can facilitate the simultaneous introduction of "structural cues for load transfer/energy dissipation" and "geometric order/proportional hierarchy visual cues" within the same design context. This approach provides a more interpretable natural reference for the topology fusion generation of biomimetic composites and the analysis of the "performance–aesthetics" dual-dimension trade-off37,38.
Innovative biomimetic composite topology-morphology design can draw inspiration from these distinctive and exquisitely organized natural forms and structures39. Building on this premise, we propose a generative design framework oriented toward the two-dimensional performance–aesthetics trade-off40: leveraging CycleGAN to learn bidirectional mappings between two classes of natural prototypes under unpaired supervision, and synthesizing hybrid topological textures via cross-domain feature transfer and morphological fusion. This enables a single structure to concurrently encode “structural cues for load transfer/energy dissipation41,42” and “visual cues of geometric order/proportional hierarchy43.” The generated outcomes are subsequently subjected to joint characterization and comparative analysis through mechanical experiments and a quantitative aesthetic metric suite, thereby assessing the extent to which the proposed fusion-generation strategy can synergize engineering usability with visual expression.
In this study, we conducted a multi-scale analysis of morphological structures derived from dragonfly wings, honeycombs, pinecones, and leaf veins, integrating high-precision image acquisition with aesthetic computations to evaluate their structural order and functional performance. The results indicate that disordered structures, such as dragonfly wings and leaf veins, demonstrate superior mechanical properties, including efficient load transmission and fatigue resistance. Conversely, ordered structures, exemplified by pinecones, excel aesthetically through enhanced visual order and textural coherence. Honeycomb structures exhibit an intermediate balance, effectively combining stable mechanical support with harmonious visual appeal44.
Based on these insights, we employed CycleGAN to learn the intrinsic associations among these structural morphologies, enabling the integration of mechanical performance characteristics with ordered aesthetic features. Experimental results demonstrate that the resulting topological morphologies achieve verifiable engineering usability, exhibiting improved flexural (bending-dominated transverse) load-bearing capacity and energy absorption under quasi-static loading, while attaining higher geometric order and visual coherence according to the proposed multi-metric aesthetic evaluation scheme45.
Further experimental testing and numerical simulations have validated the effectiveness of the proposed method across dimensions such as load-bearing capacity, energy absorption, and failure modes. By transferring and fusing representative topological textures and geometric organizational features from natural prototypes such as dragonfly wings, honeycombs, pinecones, and leaf venation, we have developed a comprehensive topology generation and evaluation process for biomimetic composite materials. This approach simultaneously incorporates "structural cues for load transfer/energy dissipation" and "visual cues of geometric order/proportional hierarchy" within the same structure, resulting in novel topologies that balance both engineering usability and aesthetic appeal. The proposed framework offers a more interpretable interdisciplinary research perspective for design, materials science, and engineering, while laying a methodological foundation for the application of high-performance composite materials in real-world product concepts, such as exoskeletal structures46,47.

## Materials and methods

## CycleGAN-based morphology-fusion biomimetic design method

## Research design and technical workflow
CycleGAN (Cycle-Consistent Generative Adversarial Networks) is a generative adversarial network (GAN) architecture primarily utilized for unsupervised image-to-image translation. As depicted in Fig. 1a, the network architecture comprises two generators and two discriminators. A key advantage of CycleGAN is its ability to facilitate image translation between two distinct domains without requiring paired datasets; specifically, the training datasets consist of two separate classes of images, each characterized by unique features and potentially unrelated content48. Through machine learning, CycleGAN effectively learns to fuse these two distinct classes of images into integrated, novel morphological outcomes.

In this study, leveraging the unique capabilities of CycleGAN, we selected two distinct morphological classes from nature as our training datasets. The first class comprises performance-oriented morphologies characterized by excellent mechanical properties yet relatively poor aesthetic order, such as leaf venation patterns and dragonfly wing textures. The second class includes aesthetically-oriented morphologies exhibiting high visual order and beauty but weaker mechanical performance, exemplified by pinecone spirals and honeycomb lattices. By pairwise training of these two distinct image sets through CycleGAN, we obtained novel fused morphologies as illustrated within the red frames in Fig. 1b. These resultant morphologies represent innovative combinations of the original natural forms. Notably, the "dragonfly wing + pinecone" fused morphology demonstrates enhanced mechanical performance compared to the original pinecone structure while simultaneously exhibiting superior aesthetic order relative to the dragonfly wing pattern. As evident in Fig. 1b, the fused morphologies clearly show a substantial improvement in visual orderliness compared to their original performance-oriented inputs (dragonfly wings or leaf veins).
Figure 1a illustrates the proposed CycleGAN-based workflow for cross-domain transfer and fusion generation of biomimetic topological morphologies: under unpaired supervision, the model learns bidirectional mappings between an aesthetics-oriented domain and a performance-oriented domain, and synthesizes hybrid topological textures that incorporate morphological cues from both sources for subsequent 3D modeling and structural validation. To examine whether this generative mechanism yields a more balanced overall profile across the two dimensions of mechanical performance and aesthetic quality, we conduct systematic mechanical testing and quantitative aesthetic assessment at the generated-sample level. The results indicate that the fusion-generated morphologies achieve verifiable engineering usability in key responses such as flexural load-bearing capacity and energy absorption, while exhibiting higher geometric order and visual coherence under a multi-metric aesthetic evaluation scheme, thereby supporting our hypothesis of synergistic improvement along the performance–aesthetics dual dimensions.
To further extend this method, we also applied the fusion process to two performance-oriented morphologies. As shown in Fig. 1c, the dragonfly wing and leaf vein patterns were fused, resulting in the morphology displayed within the blue frame. Subsequent mechanical performance tests and aesthetic evaluations verified that the fused morphology exhibited superior metrics compared to the original individual forms.
The method proposed in this study integrates GAN technology with biomimetic design principles, achieving a performance–aesthetics dual-dimension trade-off in topological morphology at a conceptual level. The core concept lies in leveraging the image translation capability of CycleGAN to enable unsupervised migration and fusion between two fundamentally distinct natural structural morphologies. The architecture of CycleGAN consists of paired generators and discriminators, ensuring the reversibility of morphological transformation through the enforcement of cycle consistency constraints. Specifically, converting morphology A to morphology B and subsequently back to A can approximately reconstruct the original form.
This mechanism guarantees the retention of essential features from each source morphology during the fusion process: the generated hybrid morphology not only inherits mechanical structural characteristics from the performance-oriented morphology (e.g., branched networks and random voids conducive to load-bearing) but also incorporates ordered textures and visual patterns from the aesthetics-oriented morphology. In other words, CycleGAN functions as a morphological "translator," conveying the intrinsic structural content of one natural form through the visual language of another, resulting in a novel topological morphology that seamlessly blends the strengths of both.
Due to its ability to operate without paired training data, CycleGAN can flexibly learn the feature distributions of various structures from a biological morphology database, automatically exploring regions within the morphological space where both performance and aesthetics are optimized. This data-driven generative approach surpasses the limitations of traditional topological configuration design, enabling the algorithm to “learn” design strategies inherent to natural evolution and, consequently, produce novel topological structures that exceed human intuition.
During the morphological fusion process, the discriminators in CycleGAN ensure that the generated morphologies exhibit realistic distributions within their respective domains, thereby preventing incoherence caused by simple splicing. Simultaneously, the cycle consistency loss encourages the generated morphologies to retain critical structural features of the input shapes, thereby preserving their original functionality.
In this study, the training set comprises two distinct classes: one consists of biological structures with exceptional mechanical performance but low morphological order (e.g., dragonfly wings and leaf veins), while the other contains highly ordered but mechanically weaker aesthetic structures (e.g., honeycombs and pinecones). After learning the deep correspondence between these morphological classes, CycleGAN successfully fuses them into novel topological configurations. These fused biomimetic morphologies visually exhibit the ordered textures or symmetric features characteristic of aesthetic shapes, while internally maintaining the multi-scale support networks typical of performance-oriented structures.
This morphological integration is achieved through GAN-driven reconfiguration of high-dimensional feature distributions rather than through simple linear superposition, allowing the generated morphologies to simultaneously satisfy both mechanical and aesthetic requirements. Overall, the scientific mechanism underpinning the proposed method lies in leveraging CycleGAN’s adversarial training and cycle-consistency constraints to enable cross-domain transfer and morphological fusion of biomimetic design elements. This, in turn, yields topological structures that integrate complementary advantages from multiple sources and provides a reproducible pathway for biomimetic structure generation and joint evaluation under the performance–aesthetics dual-dimension trade-off.

## Dataset acquisition and selection criteria
This study established an image dataset comprising various natural structural morphologies for training the CycleGAN model. We collected four types of images from nature, including dragonfly wings, plant leaf veins, honeycomb structures, and pinecone textures, with each category containing 500 images, totaling 2000 images. All images underwent preprocessing, including cropping and resizing to 256 × 256 pixels, to ensure uniform resolution and scale. These four types of images can be classified into two distinct morphological categories: performance-oriented and aesthetic-oriented. Dragonfly wings and leaf vein textures exhibit excellent mechanical performance but relatively low visual orderliness, representing performance-oriented morphologies. In contrast, honeycomb and pinecone structures are characterized by high geometric order and aesthetic appeal but relatively weaker mechanical performance, classifying them as aesthetic-oriented morphologies. This image dataset provides a rich source of training samples for the subsequent unsupervised learning of the CycleGAN model.
To ensure the reliability and comparability of the dataset for GAN training, we established stringent criteria for image acquisition and screening. First, dragonfly-wing and leaf-vein textures were captured under a consistent orthographic view (i.e., a camera orientation approximately aligned with the surface normal of the specimen) to minimize perspective distortion and its interference with textural features. Whenever possible, imaging was performed under diffuse natural illumination or within a softbox setup to reduce specular highlights and shadows that could bias grayscale statistics. For geometric structures such as honeycomb and pinecone patterns, we ensured that each image contained complete representative units without notable occlusion; acquisition angles included frontal views as well as moderate oblique views (e.g., ± 15°) to capture the geometry of repeated units and hierarchical organization. In addition, all raw images had a minimum resolution of 2,000 × 2,000 pixels, and camera metadata (device model, focal length, and aperture) were recorded consistently to support standardized cropping and rescaling during preprocessing. The preprocessing pipeline further removed images that were blurred, over-/under-exposed, or contaminated by background noise; cropped the region of interest to center the primary structure; and standardized scale and rotation so that the 256 × 256 pixel inputs to CycleGAN remained comparable in geometric scale and orientation. Collectively, these criteria not only enforce consistency in texture, shape, and proportional features across the training set, but also ensure that the learned cross-domain mappings are driven primarily by structural differences rather than acquisition conditions or image-quality artifacts, thereby improving the stability and reproducibility of the generated results.

## Mechanical performance testing

## Image vectorization and biomimetic morphology modeling
To facilitate three-dimensional modeling, the fused morphological images generated by CycleGAN are converted from pixel matrix form to vector contours. We employ the Raster-to-Vector (R2V) algorithm to extract structural edge curves from the images, resulting in black-and-white binary vector graphics. Subsequently, these vector graphics are imported into Rhino modeling software. Using the Grasshopper parametric design tool and the Dendro voxelization plugin, the vector structures are transformed into three-dimensional solid models. Specifically, the vector curves are converted into tubular elements of a specified radius, which are then aggregated to form the corresponding mesh structure. This process effectively transforms the original two-dimensional textures into three-dimensional topological truss morphologies.
To ensure comparability of different morphological specimens in mechanical testing, we standardized the dimensions and mass of all models. First, each model was uniformly scaled to a square plate size with an outer envelope of 5 cm × 5 cm. Subsequently, the radii of the tubular elements were iteratively adjusted to maintain the theoretical weight of each digital model at the same value. As a result, the weight of each designed 3D-printed specimen was approximately 1 g, thereby ensuring material consistency across different topological morphology specimens.
In Rhino–Grasshopper, we established a unified parametric modeling and normalization workflow for all specimens. First, the vector curves obtained via raster-to-vector (R2V) conversion were geometrically cleaned in Rhino (removing self-intersections, discontinuities, and short spurious segments), and then re-parameterized and resampled in Grasshopper at a consistent step size to ensure that the subsequent strut generation yields comparable curvature and node distributions. Next, when converting the curve network into a strut system, we generated circular-section tubular members around each curve using the Pipe (curve-to-tube) operation with end caps. The Rhino Pipe command explicitly supports modes such as single-wall solid and thick-walled (hollow) tubes; to avoid introducing an additional wall-thickness variable and to keep material usage controllable, we adopted single-wall solid struts (non-hollow) as the default representation. Thereafter, to improve boolean robustness at complex junctions and to obtain printable watertight solids, we used Dendro to encapsulate the curve/mesh geometry into OpenVDB-based volumetric data and extracted an isosurface via “Volume to Mesh,” producing watertight meshes; this voxelization–isosurface reconstruction route substantially reduces boolean failure rates for complex geometries and improves repeatability.
For standardization, each model was first uniformly scaled to a common bounding size (5 cm × 5 cm). Strut radii were then controlled using a “global radius multiplier + trunk/branch hierarchy factor” scheme: based on the topological graph of the curve network (nodes as junctions and edges as curve segments), the longest primary path (or the path with the highest connectivity contribution) was defined as the “trunk,” while the remaining edges were treated as “branches.” A hierarchical radius relationship () was imposed to reflect the “primary vein/primary beam–secondary branch” organization commonly observed in natural morphologies. Meanwhile, the global radius multiplier was iteratively adjusted so that the theoretical mass (or volume) of each specimen remained consistent, thereby normalizing material usage while preserving the relative trunk–branch hierarchy.

Finally, regarding anisotropy considerations, we observe that natural structures like dragonfly wings exhibit significant material and structural differences between "veins (high strength, high modulus)" and "membranes (low modulus)," leading to directional dependence in their overall mechanical response. Therefore, before modeling and 3D printing the specimens, we align the models uniformly based on the principal direction of the curve network (e.g., the principal component direction based on the trunk direction or the tangent direction of the curve), ensuring that the trunk direction aligns with the specimen’s longitudinal (spanwise) axis under the quasi-static flexural (bending-dominated transverse) loading configuration. This alignment minimizes anisotropic deviations introduced by posture differences and ensures comparability between different groups (Real/Fake, different morphology categories).

## 3D printing of specimens
High-precision additive manufacturing equipment was employed to fabricate the aforementioned digital models into physical specimens. We used the Bambu Lab X1 Carbon fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printer to sequentially print the biomimetic structural specimens using polylactic acid (PLA) material. Each specimen had a printed size of approximately 5 cm × 5 cm and a single-piece weight of about 1 g, consistent with the designed model’s expected weight. The printing process utilized a 0.4 mm diameter nozzle and a 0.2 mm layer thickness, balancing precision and efficiency. After fabrication, all printed specimens underwent visual inspection to ensure the absence of apparent defects.

## Quasi-static flexural (bending) test setup and parameter specifications
Quasi-static mechanical tests under bending-dominated transverse loading were performed on the 3D-printed biomimetic topological specimens. Unlike conventional axial compression, the loading configuration (Fig. 4a) constrains the specimen in a horizontal orientation on a clamping fixture (with end conditions approximating fixed–fixed support), while the indenter applies a vertical displacement at mid-span and the force–displacement response is recorded. Because the specimen’s geometric axis (horizontal) is not collinear with the external load direction (vertical), the dominant internal stress state is characterized by a bending-governed response accompanied by a certain level of shear. Accordingly, the test is more appropriately described as a modified three-point bending configuration with clamped (fixed–fixed) ends, i.e., a transverse bending test whose logic is consistent with standard flexural testing of polymeric materials, wherein mid-span loading is used to evaluate load-carrying response and failure behavior under bending-dominated conditions.

## Quantitative aesthetic evaluation

## 28 Quantitative aesthetic evaluation indicators
Aesthetic evaluation is inherently subjective, making it difficult to quantify aesthetics objectively using any single criterion or variable51. Consequently, computation-based objective methods have increasingly become an important means for characterizing aesthetic properties of images52. In this study, focusing on 2D topological texture maps where “structure is aesthetics” (binary black-and-white vector images with the structure as foreground and the background as white), a suite of 28 quantitative aesthetic metrics is constructed to cover multiple dimensions—information complexity; contrast and edge content; geometric compactness and void ratio; symmetry and directional order; repetitiveness and rhythmicity; and topology/fractality—thereby providing a reproducible, objective description of morphological “orderliness, balance, and complexity” with minimal subjective intervention. Lower values typically indicate that structural information is more concentrated and the overall structure is more orderly55,56.
RMS Contrast (RMSC): Quantifies contrast as the standard deviation of pixel intensities (independent of spatial frequency), making it well suited for complex textures; higher contrast often enhances perceived visual clarity and salience57–59.
Edge Density (ED): The proportion of edge pixels reflects the density of contours/details. High ED typically indicates more complex textures and higher visual load (depending on your aesthetic preference, it can be seen as “complex” or “disordered”). In the 2D case, the Euler number is defined as the difference between the number of connected components and the number of holes in the binary image. In this study, we compute a linear weighted sum of the three normalized components (with equal weights or weights specified by prior considerations)82.

Accordingly, we define CogLd as a linear combination of normalized complexity and normalized contrast83,84.

The standard deviation of these densities represents the uniformity of the distribution: smaller values indicate greater uniformity and overall order85,86.

In this study, we approximate the strength of “repetitive rhythm” by the intensity of the primary peak in the 2D autocorrelation of the edge map at non-zero delays (relative to the zero delay)87,88.

## Results

## Mechanical performance measurement results
In this study, 3D-printed specimens were fabricated from the generated biomimetic fusion morphologies. The images produced by CycleGAN were first vectorized, followed by geometric modeling using Rhino software. To ensure consistency across all test specimens, the model dimensions were standardized, with each square-shaped specimen having an outer contour edge length of 5 cm. Additionally, the thickness of the cylindrical elements within the models was adjusted to maintain a uniform weight of 1 g per 3D-printed specimen. This standardization ensured consistency in material usage across all test samples, facilitating accurate comparative analysis.
After completing the 3D printing of all specimens, mechanical performance testing was conducted. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 4a. First, each specimen was horizontally fixed below the force gauge, with the gauge’s telescopic rod gently contacting the upper surface of the specimen without exerting pressure. At this initial stage, the force gauge displayed a reading of zero, and the scale on the left side of the gauge was set to zero, marking the initial state of the mechanical measurement. Next, the force gauge was activated to apply downward pressure at the center of the specimen through the telescopic rod. The loading was continued until the indenter displaced the specimen to structural failure, at which point the measured load dropped to approximately zero. Throughout the experiment, the variation in force measurements was recorded from start to failure, generating pressure variation curves. As shown in Fig. 4b–t depict the comparative pressure data curves for each set of fusion morphologies (Fake A or Fake B) against their input morphologies (Real A and Real B). In these comparative plots, the yellow curve represents the force variation for the fused biomimetic morphology, while the blue and green curves correspond to the force data of the input morphologies Real A and Real B, respectively. It can be observed that in most cases, the yellow curve exhibits superior mechanical performance. Specifically, in panels h and i, the yellow curve (Fake A) outperforms the green curve, representing the aesthetic-oriented morphology (Real B), but remains lower than the blue curve of the performance-oriented morphology (Real A). This outcome is consistent with the pre-experimental hypothesis, indicating that the generated morphology exhibits enhanced mechanical performance compared to its aesthetically oriented parent shape. In summary, the experimental results indicate that the fusion-generated biomimetic morphologies produced by CycleGAN are overall superior to the parent input morphologies in key mechanical metrics, thereby validating the effectiveness of the proposed fusion-generation strategy in improving mechanical performance.
Through prototype fabrication and testing, this study verified the effectiveness of the proposed morphological fusion strategy in terms of both mechanical performance and aesthetic characteristics. In the aspect of mechanical performance, the pressure testing results clearly indicate that the fused biomimetic structures exhibit superior load-bearing capacity and energy absorption compared to their original morphologies. In comparative tests involving various combinations (e.g., "dragonfly + honeycomb," "dragonfly + pinecone," "leaf vein + honeycomb"), the majority of fused morphology specimens demonstrated higher maximum compressive strength than either of the original morphological specimens. Even in cases where the fused structure did not entirely surpass the performance of the best individual parent morphology, its mechanical behavior significantly exceeded that of the aesthetically oriented parent structure. This indicates that the GAN-generated fused designs successfully achieve the integration of aesthetic elements without sacrificing high strength, thereby avoiding any pronounced trade-off between performance and aesthetics. On the contrary, we observed a synergistic enhancement: the fused structures typically exhibit more uniform stress distribution and multi-path load transmission channels. For instance, during mechanical performance testing, where specimens were subjected to central pressure loading, the fused topological network structures displayed a characteristic of multi-directional force dispersion, effectively absorbing and dissipating impact energy. This improvement in performance can be attributed to the combination of multi-scale and multi-orientation support elements within the fused morphology. The irregular frameworks derived from dragonfly wings or leaf veins provide resistance to local buckling and crack propagation, while the periodic units originating from honeycombs or pinecones impart structural stability and uniformity. Consequently, the resulting topological architecture is both robust and resilient, displaying mechanical advantages that significantly surpass those of the original natural morphologies.
In a small number of comparison groups, the peak load of the fusion morphology is slightly lower than that of the “performance-oriented” parent prototype. This does not necessarily indicate a failure of the fusion-generation strategy; rather, it is typically attributable to the introduction of periodic units/voids from the aesthetics-oriented domain and additional nodal connections. These additions can divert an otherwise more “direct” primary load path, leading to more pronounced stress concentrations at junctions and earlier triggers for local buckling. Moreover, because specimen mass was normalized in this study (i.e., constant material usage), the fused structures often “trade” richer texture and hierarchical detail for locally thinner struts or higher strut slenderness ratios, which partially weakens the advantage of the performance-oriented prototype’s strut system aligned with the principal load-bearing direction. Prior lattice/architected-material studies have shown that stress concentration in nodal regions is a major source of early damage and failure under compressive loading; improving node geometry can markedly enhance strength and energy absorption, underscoring that nodes are frequently critical weak links. In addition, lattice strength and stiffness are highly sensitive to strut slenderness and relative density (e.g., ); a moderate reduction in can substantially increase yielding and peak strength, implying that “thinner/more slender” primary load-bearing struts after fusion may reasonably lead to a modest decrease in peak load. Therefore, observing that the fusion morphology is “slightly inferior to the performance-oriented parent” under certain conditions is a mechanically plausible outcome. Nevertheless, because the fused designs still retain the parent prototype’s trunk–branch load pathways and multi-channel force-transfer skeleton, their load-bearing and energy-absorption capacities typically remain markedly higher than those of the aesthetics-oriented parent, yielding an overall more balanced performance–aesthetics two-dimensional trade-off.

The results demonstrate that, in most test groups depicted in Fig. 5, the aesthetic scores of the fused morphologies (Fake A) are higher than those of the input morphologies (Real A and Real B). In groups h and i, the aesthetic score of Fake A is lower than that of Real A but higher than that of Real B.
Through the quantitative evaluation and weighted scoring of 28 aesthetic metrics, it is evident that the majority of fusion-generated morphologies in Fig. 5 outperform the parent input morphologies in terms of aesthetic scores. These results demonstrate that the proposed method effectively enhances aesthetic quality across dimensions such as visual orderliness, symmetry, and complexity.
In summary, image analysis and quantitative evaluation reveal that compared to the disordered and chaotic performance-oriented morphologies, the fused structures exhibit significantly enhanced texture order and richer hierarchical complexity. These generated morphologies often incorporate geometric elements reminiscent of honeycomb or spiral patterns, making the overall design more structured, symmetric, and rhythmically aesthetic.
For instance, the “dragonfly + pinecone” fused morphology inherits the ordered beauty of the pinecone’s spiral arrangement while retaining the intricate details of the dragonfly wing network, thereby presenting a dual aesthetic characterized by both harmony and complexity. This enhancement in aesthetics is not only reflected in objective metrics (such as the statistical orderliness of texture distribution and fractal dimensions) but also perceptually manifests as a more recognizable and artistic structure when viewed by the human eye. The generated morphologies demonstrate a balanced integration of natural, evolution-like random variations with the symmetry and regularity typically associated with artificial design. In essence, the GAN-based fusion design endows the structures with an “evolved beauty,” achieving a high level of unity between functionality and form.

## Discussions

## Significance of the findings
This study addresses the synergistic demand for mechanical performance and aesthetic quality in topological structural design by proposing a CycleGAN-based biomimetic morphology-fusion approach, whose effectiveness and distinctive value are validated experimentally. The method extracts complementary cues from two classes of biological prototypes: one emphasizes structural organization conducive to load transfer and energy dissipation, whereas the other emphasizes a visual vocabulary of geometric order and proportional hierarchy. By leveraging CycleGAN to learn cross-domain mappings and cycle-consistency constraints under unpaired data, the approach enables cross-domain transfer and fusion generation of morphological elements, thereby producing topological morphologies that simultaneously exhibit more robust mechanical responses and more ordered visual structures. Compared with traditional iterative topology-solution paradigms driven by explicit mechanical objectives, this generative framework incorporates naturally ordered textural features at the generation stage, facilitating globally coherent patterns that balance order and richness in visual expression. Experimental evidence further indicates that the GAN-generated structures achieve substantial improvements over their parent prototypes in key metrics such as peak load capacity under bending-dominated transverse loading and energy absorption, while also exhibiting higher geometric order and visual coherence under the proposed multi-metric quantitative aesthetic evaluation scheme. Taken together, the proposed approach integrates AI-based generation with mechanism abstraction in biomimetic design, offering a generation-and-evaluation perspective for exploring the performance–aesthetics dual-dimensional trade-off at the interface of materials science and design studies. Future work may extend along directions including multi-scale structural representations, expansion to more complex morphologies, and further refinement of training and evaluation pipelines to enhance generalizability and engineering deployability.
At the microstructural level, the “performance–aesthetics two-dimensional trade-off mechanism” can be interpreted as a coupling between a load-transfer network and a visual-order network within an integrated topology. Performance-oriented prototypes typically correspond to a “trunk–branch” multi-path connected skeleton, whose key advantage stems from high nodal connectivity and triangulation constraints. These features bias struts toward axial load-bearing rather than bending-dominated deformation under external loads, thereby yielding higher specific stiffness/strength—an interpretation that can be further rationalized through the Maxwell stability criterion used to distinguish stretching-dominated from bending-dominated architectures. In contrast, aesthetics-oriented prototypes often exhibit more regular voids and more scale-consistent unit rhythms. Structurally, such “orderliness” manifests as a more uniform pore-size distribution and smoother local curvature variations. This pore organization not only produces perceptible symmetry/repetition and rhythmicity in visual appearance (humans show a general aesthetic preference for symmetry and ordered patterns, partly attributable to reduced image complexity and lower information-processing burden), but also mitigates local “weak-spot clustering” through more homogeneous geometric scales, promoting deformation sharing across multiple units rather than single-point instability.
When these two feature sets are integrated into a fused morphology, the resulting topology often adopts a composite configuration characterized by “continuous trunk load-carrying + redundant branch-level load redistribution + periodic-unit-constrained deformation modes.” Here, the trunk ensures continuous primary load paths along dominant loading directions; branches and multi-nodal connections provide redundancy and suppress the propagation of single-path failures; and periodic pores/units offer lateral constraints and geometric rhythm, which collectively lead to more uniform stress/strain fields and a more controllable progressive-failure process at the macroscopic scale. Notably, nodal junctions are both where coupling occurs (load splitting, redirection, and geometric transitions) and where potential weaknesses emerge. Extensive lattice studies indicate that stress concentration at nodes often governs compressive failure, and that node reinforcement or geometric smoothing can markedly shift failure locations and improve modulus/strength/energy absorption. Accordingly, node morphology and pore-gradient distribution jointly constitute “key microstructural knobs” within the dual-dimensional trade-off. From an aesthetic standpoint, the combination of “regular rhythm as a global baseline + branch-induced perturbations providing local variation” corresponds to a visual experience in which order coexists with richness: regular pores enhance symmetry, directional coherence, and rhythmicity, whereas branching networks introduce multiscale detail and moderate complexity, preventing excessive monotony. Therefore, micro-scale connectivity and pore distribution do not separately serve performance or aesthetics; rather, the synergy among “load paths–rhythmic units–nodal transitions” establishes a shared structural basis through which mechanical and aesthetic attributes co-vary.
Compared with existing biomimetic design studies, the present work exhibits several distinctive innovations. Conventional biomimicry has often relied on single-source inspirations—i.e., emulating a particular biological structure to improve a specific engineering attribute103. For example, designs inspired by mantis shrimp, avian bone, or plant venation typically target the enhancement of selected performance metrics104, while aesthetic considerations are treated as secondary or incidental105,106. In contrast, this study adopts a dual-prototype fusion paradigm, leveraging machine learning to integrate the advantageous traits of two (or even multiple) natural morphologies within a single structure. Such composite biomimicry is difficult to achieve through human intuition alone, yet can be realized efficiently via data-driven GAN-based generation.
Moreover, relative to biomimetic topology design approaches based on SKO and CAIO (e.g., Völkl et al.), which emphasize iterative solving of material distribution and fiber orientation along principal stress trajectories, the present approach places greater emphasis on direct cross-domain transfer of morphological features and the explicit articulation of global visual order. Prior SKO/CAIO-style methods primarily modulate material allocation by mimicking growth-like mechanisms to improve performance-related indicators (e.g., isotropy), while their resulting geometries typically remain strongly engineering-oriented in aesthetic expression. By contrast, the present framework introduces aesthetics-oriented morphologies directly in the image domain, enabling a tighter integration of functional cues and visual language in the generated outcomes. Collectively, these contrasts highlight a conceptual shift: rather than treating nature solely as a source of inspiration, natural morphologies are treated as “data” to be learned and recombined, thereby facilitating the synthesis of structural forms that would be unlikely to emerge from conventional, intuition-driven design processes.
CycleGAN-based unsupervised morphology fusion differs fundamentally from prior approaches that impose aesthetic constraints manually. For example, Loos et al. explicitly guided the visual coherence of topological structures using Gestalt-inspired principles (unity-in-variety)14, whereas Zhong et al. further introduced textual prompts to enable text-guided stylization of structural appearance15. In contrast, the present framework can automatically learn aesthetic attributes from biological patterns and fuse them with mechanically relevant structural features without manual intervention, offering higher automation and adaptability. Fang et al. combined GANs with evolutionary algorithms to solve for and generate complex architectural shell morphologies, thereby jointly accommodating structural efficiency and formal aesthetics107; Peng et al., leveraging machine learning, generated personalized microstructures for orthopedic implants, achieving a balance between mechanical performance and biocompatibility in biomedical applications40. Analogously, the proposed approach can be extended to generative design in architecture and industrial products: by tailoring the training data, designers can synthesize structural components that satisfy engineering constraints while maintaining aesthetic appeal. Such a fusion-oriented strategy is consistent with recent developments in mechanical metamaterials and additive manufacturing108,109.

## Application scenarios
In industrial product applications, beyond the two design concepts presented above, the outcomes of this study exhibit substantial interdisciplinary potential and can offer new insights for materials science, design research, and biomimetic engineering. From a materials-science perspective, fused biomimetic topological structures can be exploited to develop high-performance composites and lightweight load-bearing architectures. Conventional materials design often prioritizes mechanical properties alone; by contrast, the present approach provides a pathway to consider the aesthetic attributes of internal structural organization as well. For instance, in engineering foams, lattice scaffolds, or porous materials, introducing biomimetic fusion morphologies can enhance structural order while maintaining strength and stiffness, potentially reducing defect sensitivity and imparting distinctive visual identities. This is particularly valuable for materials and components where both functionality and appearance matter, such as exposed architectural structural elements or internal frames in consumer electronics. The design discipline can likewise benefit directly from the new design paradigm enabled by this method. With generative adversarial networks, designers can move beyond the limitations of human experience and automatically generate a large set of structural concept candidates that integrate aesthetic appeal with functional intent. In integrated form–structure design (e.g., furniture, vehicle frames, and wearable devices), algorithmically generated biomimetic textures can simultaneously serve as load-bearing frameworks and ornamental patterns, operationalizing the principle of “structure as ornament” and reducing redundant parts. Such a synchronized functional–aesthetic design approach aligns with contemporary industrial design demands for lightweighting, integration, and enhanced visual quality110.
In biomimetic engineering and other interdisciplinary domains, the present study likewise demonstrates promising application prospects. Biomimetic engineering spans a broad spectrum ranging from bio-inspired robotics to biomedical devices, and the proposed morphology-fusion strategy can be leveraged to advance design in these areas. For example, structural components in bio-inspired robots or exoskeletal systems may benefit from fused biomimetic topological morphologies to achieve higher strength-to-weight ratios, enabling robotic bodies that are both robust and lightweight while exhibiting more natural, fluent form languages—features that can be advantageous for locomotion performance and energy efficiency. As illustrated by the exoskeleton and helmet concepts discussed above, this represents a typical cross-domain application: through modular tiling and surface-mapping techniques, generated biomimetic units can be seamlessly deployed onto complex freeform surfaces, facilitating the fabrication of human-conforming equipment that is both aesthetically appealing and functionally practical (e.g., customized braces, protective gear, or prosthetic shells). Moreover, in architecture and civil engineering, the approach can inform integrated structure–ornament design. Building façades or load-bearing elements may adopt algorithmically generated biomimetic meshes as structural frameworks that not only provide mechanically advantageous internal topological organization, but also produce distinctive textural patterns, thereby introducing more organic aesthetic qualities into the built environment. In this way, architects and engineers can collaborate to create spatial structures with enhanced vitality and artistic expressiveness while meeting safety requirements. Overall, this study helps bridge the gap between engineering performance enhancement and aesthetic design, and the proposed technique can serve as a connective framework across disciplines: materials science emphasizes internal structural performance, design and art emphasize formal aesthetics, and biomimetics emphasizes nature-inspired principles. The fused topological morphologies developed herein lie at the intersection of these perspectives, underscoring the broader potential for cross-disciplinary innovation.

## Limitations and future directions
In addition, it should be noted that the proposed CycleGAN fusion model fundamentally learns and generates structural units in a two-dimensional topological texture space. Accordingly, its deployment in three-dimensional industrial-design scenarios follows a “2D generation → 3D mapping and solidification” workflow. Specifically, the 2D fused units are first tiled and extended on a plane; the resulting planar pattern is then morphed and attached to a target 3D surface via Rhino/Grasshopper surface-mapping operations (e.g., Rhino’s FlowAlongSrf or Grasshopper’s Surface Morph), using the surface UV parameter domain, after which printable mesh shells are obtained through thickening/offsetting. A key prerequisite for this approach is that the target surface admits relatively uniform parameterization; otherwise, the mapping may induce localized dilation and contraction (i.e., non-uniform scaling) and consequently distort unit scales. Therefore, the modeling pipeline includes surface reconstruction/subdivision to improve the UV parameter distribution, and, when necessary, density compensation on the mapped mesh (e.g., increasing local sampling density or adjusting strut radii in regions of high curvature or large parametric stretch) to maintain comparability in overall material usage and porosity. Rhino’s documentation explicitly notes that FlowAlongSrf relies on the UV directions of the base and target surfaces and requires “even parameterization” to avoid severe geometric stretching. Likewise, Grasshopper’s Surface Morph is a standard component for mapping geometry into a surface UVW coordinate system, making it suitable for translating 2D patterns into structural languages on 3D surfaces.
With respect to anisotropy, 2D structures—particularly trunk–branch textures—often exhibit pronounced directional dependence. Hence, during 3D attachment, the principal orientation of the texture is aligned with the principal direction of the product surface (e.g., the axial direction of a wrist orthosis, or the circumferential/meridional directions of a helmet), while prioritizing continuity of the “trunk” pathway along the dominant load-bearing direction to mitigate orientation-induced anisotropic bias that would arise from arbitrary rotations. It should be emphasized that the purpose of the 3D case studies is to demonstrate that the proposed 2D fused textures can be translated into shell/surface structures (e.g., orthoses and helmet shells) in an engineering-feasible manner. For truly volumetric 3D lattices or scenarios requiring rigorous impact/fatigue substantiation, future work should incorporate explicit dynamic simulations and/or corresponding dynamic experiments to provide more stringent engineering validation.

## Conclusion
This work addresses the generation of biomimetic topological morphologies under a two-dimensional performance–aesthetics trade-off by proposing a fusion-oriented generative framework centered on generative adversarial networks. Specifically, CycleGAN is leveraged to learn cross-domain mappings and enforce cycle-consistency constraints under unpaired data, enabling a controllable recombination of structural cues from performance-oriented and aesthetics-oriented natural prototypes within a unified topological texture. Methodologically, the study establishes a closed-loop pipeline spanning 2D texture fusion, 3D solidification modeling, quasi-static flexural (bending-dominated) validation, and 28-metric aesthetic quantification with attribute-space trade-off analysis, thereby allowing the coupling among “load-transfer pathways–pore/connection organization–visual order features” to be quantitatively characterized and interpreted in a comparative manner. Overall, the proposed approach provides a reproducible paradigm for cross-domain fusion and joint evaluation, and offers a transferable morphology-generation interface and methodological foundation for engineering product concepts integrating structure and ornament.

## Figure Fig. 6: Dual-objective attribute space and pareto front: occupied regions and expansion of bio-inspired morphology-fusion designs. This figure simultaneously maps the aesthetic and mechanical performance of the design candidates in a two-dimensional objective space. The x-axis denotes Aesthetic performance and the y-axis denotes Mechanical performance (both ↑ indicate better performance). Solid circles represent fused/generated samples (Fused/Generated), whereas open circles represent the corresponding baseline parent samples (RealA/RealB, annotated as A/B). Gray line segments connect each triplet, illustrating the direction and magnitude of the attribute shift induced by fusion. Colors indicate five fusion types (QF/QS/YF/YQ/YS), and the semi-transparent envelopes delineate each type’s region of occupation in the objective space; marginal density curves on the top and right summarize distributional differences along the two axes. The thick black curve and the gray dashed curve correspond to the Pareto fronts (non-dominated boundaries) of the generated and initial samples, respectively, characterizing the effective feasible set under the dual-objective trade-off. Notably, the generated samples expand toward the upper-right direction relative to the initial occupied region and its Pareto front, allowing some designs to enter and fill previously unoccupied or sparsely populated high-aesthetic–mid-to-high-mechanics regions, thereby providing a more explicit visualization of the expansion of the feasible “performance–aesthetics” solution space.
