# A survey of beneficial insects in Southeast USA sesame (Lamiales: Pedaliaceae) fields

## Abstract
AbstractWhile sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) is one of the oldest domesticated oilseed crops, it has only recently become a new and emerging crop for the southeast USA. As little is known about the insect fauna of sesame in the United States, it will be important to document insect communities now while management programs are still being developed. Here, we provide the first survey of beneficial insects, including pollinators and potential natural enemies, for sesame in the United States. Beneficial insects within sesame fields were surveyed in 2023 at 2 locations in North Carolina, United States using 2 sampling methods: visual identification and sticky cards. During visual surveys, insects were field identified, with up to 5 physical specimens collected per species for identification verification. Pollen was then removed, identified, and quantified from collected specimens. Potential natural enemies collected on sticky cards were categorized as stilt bugs (Hemiptera: Berytidae: Jalysus spinosus Say), dragonflies (Odonata: Anisoptera), lady beetles (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae), and wasps (Hymenoptera: Apocrita). We found 18 bee (Hymenoptera: Anthophila) species visiting sesame flowers, with honey bees collecting the most sesame pollen per specimen. Wasps were the most abundant natural enemy category, with over 100 wasps collected at some sampling events. The information from this study can be used to ensure beneficial insect communities are protected as sesame production is expanded in the United States.

## Introduction
Sesame Sesamum indicum L. was one of the first oilseed crops domesticated by man (Kinman 1953) and has been an important crop internationally since ancient Egypt (Langham 1934), but has only been regularly grown in some regions of the United States since the mid-1900s (Kinman 1953). Sesame is an annual, warm-season oilseed crop most often produced in Texas, Oklahoma, and Kansas, United States. Increasing demand and a need to diversify production regions to minimize climate-related loss has brought focus on the southeast USA as a new production region. Preliminary field trials in 2021 and 2022 found that sesame can be produced profitably in the southeast region (Chavez et al. 2024). These results led to approximately 16,000 acres of contracted sesame in the Southeast (North Carolina, South Carolina, Virginia, Georgia, and Alabama) in 2023.
Sesame is considered a pollinator-independent crop, meaning visitation from pollinators is not needed for successful seed set (Andrade et al. 2014). However, there is evidence that the crop will still benefit from pollinator visitation, resulting in increased seed set and seed weight (Stein et al. 2017); similar benefits of pollinator visitation has also been documented in other pollinator-independent crops (Esquivel et al. 2021, Levenson et al. 2022). As sesame has floral pollen, floral nectar, and extrafloral nectaries (Andrade et al. 2014) it can be highly attractive to pollinators (Kamel et al. 2013); however, there is no information on the pollinator communities of sesame in the United States. Also of interest, while some pests of concern have been noted in other sesame growing regions in the United States and internationally (Kinman 1953, Akinyemi et al. 2015), there are currently no documented economically damaging pests of sesame in the southeast USA. However, it is anticipated that aphids, corn earworms, and stink bugs could cause issues in this region. Thus, it is important to document potential natural enemies present in southeast USA sesame fields now, so that if pest concerns do emerge, growers can make more informed management decisions.
To address these knowledge gaps our objectives were to (i) survey pollinators visiting sesame fields in the southeast USA, with a particular focus on bees (Hymenoptera: Anthophila), and (ii) document potential natural enemies for sesame.
For our first objective, we focused on bee pollinators (Hymenoptera: Anthophila) as they are considered the most efficient insect pollinator group. For our second objective, we grouped natural enemies into 4 categories: stilt bugs (Hemiptera: Berytidae: Jalysus spinosus Say), dragonflies (Odonata: Anisoptera), lady beetles (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae), and wasps (Hymenoptera: Apocrita). We selected these groups based on their documented ability to provide natural enemy services in other systems. Both dragonfly nymphs and adults are predatory; while nymphs are aquatic, adults will eat small insects (Triplehorn and Johnson 2005). Lady beetles will eat insect eggs, aphids, and other soft-bodied insects (Altieri et al. 2014). Stilt bugs are significant predators of caterpillars, aphids, and other soft-bodied insects, which are economically important pests in other crop systems (Nelson and Burrack 2017). Finally, wasps eat caterpillars and small insects (Altieri et al. 2014) while parasitoids kill their prey during development (Marshall 2006).
It will be important to better understand the insect fauna of sesame now, particularly the beneficial insect community, as planted sesame acreage expands in the southeast but before pest management programs are fully developed.

## Materials and Methods

## Site Selection
Samples were collected in August and September 2023 (Levenson et al. 2025) at 2 North Carolina Department of Agriculture & Consumer Services and North Carolina State University Experimental Agricultural Research Stations (hereafter referred to as research stations): United States: North Carolina: Clinton: Horticulture Crops Research Station (35.0217310, −78.2831780); United States: North Carolina: Oxford: Oxford Tobacco Research Station (36.3032210, −78.6121390). Fields in both locations were approximately 2 acres in size and planted for observational purposes. The variety S39 (Sesaco Corporation, Austin, Texas) was planted at approximately 150,000 plants/ac (1 lb/ac) using a planter with 30″ row spacing.
To document the beneficial insect communities in the sesame fields, we utilized 2 main sampling methods during peak crop bloom: visual surveys and sticky cards. Both methods were repeated 3 times per site (see dataset in Dryad, Levenson et al. 2025).

## Insect Surveys
We conducted timed visual surveys for 30 min, once per week, for 3 wk; as sesame bloom lasts for roughly 1 mo, we timed our surveys for the middle to end of peak bloom. During surveys, one observer walked 3 transects per field: 2 along the outer rows at the field edges and one through the middle row of the field. During visual surveys, only active floral visitors were documented. Insects were visually identified in the field down to the lowest taxonomic level possible. When needed, and possible, a physical specimen was collected with a hand net for identification confirmation. The timer was stopped for any interruptions or specimen handling during the survey period. After the survey period was completed, 3 to 5 voucher specimens of each insect species were collected with a hand net, avoiding the collection of bumble bee queens, to act as an identification verification collection. Again, only active floral visitors were collected.

## Pollen Identification From Physical Specimens
When a physical specimen was collected, it was placed in an individual 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube and stored on ice while transported back to North Carolina State University. There, specimens were stored at −20 °C until further processing. Bee specimens were identified to the lowest taxonomic level possible using Levenson and Youngsteadt (2019) and Ascher and Pickering (2016). Non-bee specimens were identified to the lowest taxonomic level possible using Marshall (2006) and Triplehorn and Johnson (2005). Specimen vouchers will be deposited in the North Carolina State University Insect Collection under accession numbers NCSU_ENT00329600–NCSU_ENT00329699.
As we were interested in understanding which insect species were collecting resources from sesame flowers, we performed pollen identification from the physical specimens. We created pollen slides using Fuchsin jelly cut into 2 mm cuboidal portions and following recommendations outlined in Kearns and Inouye (1993). Using forceps, each specimen was swabbed with the jelly to collect a sample of any pollen present onto 2 slides. One slide was prepared from swabbing the head and mouthparts of each specimen and a second slide was prepared from swabbing the legs (for non-bees) and the pollen carrying structures (for bees) of each specimen. Such structures on bee specimens included the corbicular pollen balls, scopa on the hind legs, or scopa on the underside of the abdomen, depending on the bee species. For simplicity, we refer to these 2 slide types as “head” and “leg” hereafter. Between each sample, forceps were cleaned with a 70% ethanol rinse, followed by a tap water rinse, to prevent cross-contamination. A small reference library was also created by collecting a flower head from each blooming flower species present at each sampling event. A sesame flower was also collected from both sites and included in the reference library. Pollen slides were created by swabbing the stamen of each flower head; forceps were again cleaned between each flower species.
Following modified methodology outlined in Levenson et al. (2022), pollen slides were manually counted under a compound light microscope at 400× magnification. Five fields of view (FOV) were counted per slide; FOV were randomly selected, working in a clockwise direction around the gel and ending in the middle. Pollen grains were quantified in each FOV separately, with pollen types classified as sesame pollen or non-sesame pollen.

## Sticky Cards
On the same day as when insect surveys were conducted, sticky card traps were placed around the perimeter of the field to act as a passive sampling tool. On each of the 4 sides of the field, 2 sticky cards were placed on stakes, 1 red sticky card and 1 yellow sticky card, facing parallel to the field edge; both colors were used to sample as much of the insect community as possible. Sticky cards measured approximately 25.5 cm × 12 mm and 33 cm × 14.5, respectively, and were sticky on both sides. Sticky cards remained in the field for 1 wk when they were then collected and replaced. When collected, sticky cards were sealed with plastic wrap, stored on ice during transportation back to North Carolina State University, and stored at −20 °C until further processing. Both sides of each card were inspected under a stereomicroscope for potential natural enemies; these insects were sorted into 4 categories: stilt bugs, dragonflies, lady beetles, and wasps and parasitoids (hereafter referred to as wasps).

## Statistical Analysis
Analysis conducted in RStudio (version 4.2.3, R Core Team 2024). We used 1-way ANOVAs in base R to compare insect abundance, insect species richness, bee abundance, and bee species richness between the 2 sites. A 𝜒2 analysis with a Bonferroni correction was conducted in base R to compare bee genera occurrences between the 2 sites.
To analyze pollen counts, data were summed across FOV for each sample. We used a generalized linear model with a negative binomial distribution, using the MASS package (Venables and Ripley 2002), and included the total number of sesame pollen grains and the total number of non-sesame pollen grains as dependent variables with pollen location (head or leg), site, week, and insect type as independent variables, in separate models. In each model, we also included the total number of pollen grains per insect, log transformed, as an offset, as outlined in Levenson et al. (2022). To preserve statistical rigor, we did not include any random effects. We used the emmeans (Lenth 2024), multcomp (Hothorn et al. 2008), and multcompView (Graves et al. 2024) to conduct a Tukey’s post-hoc analysis.
To analyze insect data collected by sticky cards, we used a generalized linear model with a negative binomial distribution. We included the total number of beneficial insects as the dependent variable and site, week, and card color as independent variables, in separate models. Due to sample size, we only analyzed the number of stilt bugs and wasps further. We used 1-way ANOVAs with stilt bugs and wasps as the dependent variables and card color as the independent variable, in separate models. To preserve statistical rigor, we did not include any random effects. We conducted a Tukey’s post-hoc analysis in base R where appropriate.
To visualize results, we used the iNEXT (Hsieh et al. 2025), ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), dplyr (Wickham et al. 2023), and tidyr (Wickham et al. 2024) packages. To create the extrapolated rarefaction curves and estimated sample coverage, we only used bee specimens (both visually observed and collected) that were identified to species (see Table 1 and Levenson et al. 2025), as this was the most robust data in our dataset.

## Results

## Insect Surveys
From both sites, a total of 756 bees and 92 non-bee insects were documented, 732 from visual surveys and 116 from specimens (Table 1 and Table 2). At Oxford Tobacco Research Station (hereafter referred to as Oxford), 341 bees were documented, 282 from visual surveys and 59 from specimens. Additionally, 10 non-bees were documented, 4 from visual surveys and 6 from specimens. At Horticulture Crops Research Station at Clinton (hereafter referred to as Clinton) 415 bees were documented, 375 from visual surveys and 40 from specimens. Additionally, 82 non-bees were documented, with 71 from visual surveys and 11 from specimens. Out of all insects collected, the order Hymenoptera, and more specifically bees, were the most frequent sesame visitors.
In total, we found 18 different bee species visiting sesame flowers. While the rarefaction curves do not yet asymptote, the extrapolation levels off quickly (Fig. 1). Additionally, the sample coverage estimate for both stations was 0.99. Together this demonstrates that while we likely did not sample all bee species present in the sesame fields, we had high levels of sample completeness. Bumble bees (Hymenoptera: Apidae: Bombus) were the most abundant bee group (399), followed by carpenter bees (Xylocopa; 153), and honey bees (Apis mellifera L.; 107). Most notably, there were many Bombus pensylvanicus (De Geer) visiting sesame (89), including B. pensylvanicus queens (Fig. 2A and B). This species is of particular interest as it is currently under review to be added to the endangered species list by the United States Fish and Wildlife Service at the time of writing this paper (Endangered and Threatened Wildlife and Plants 2021). Bombus impatiens was the most commonly documented visitor (307); while this species is commercially available as a managed species, we assume that the individuals in this study are wild as the research stations do not typically purchase colonies of this species. We also noted hummingbirds visiting sesame flowers at Oxford. While the majority of insect activity was visiting flowers, we also note that Lasioglossum (Hymenoptera: Halictidae), stilt bugs, dragonflies, and wasps were found visiting extrafloral nectaries. Although sesame is an attractive crop for insects (see Table 1), we note that these insects, particularly bees, still visited blooming weeds within the fields, while sesame was in bloom. We also note that new sesame blooms were closed around 900 h, with most blooms open by 1000 h; there was a noticeable, anecdotal increase in insect activity during visual surveys once blooms opened. Previous literature has documented that sesame blooms may only stay open for a few hours (Andrade et al. 2014); while we did not track length of bloom duration, we were able to document insect visitation as late as 1500 h. Additionally, the parasitic sharp-tailed bee (Hymenoptera: Megachilidae: Coelioxys) was present at Clinton, though it was not found visiting flowers.

## Pollen Identification From Physical Specimens
We created 2 pollen slides each (1 head and 1 leg pollen slide) from 116 specimens. During analysis, we removed 15 and 6 specimens that had zero pollen grains documented when processing the head and leg pollen slides, respectively. Specimens that had no pollen on the head slide included Apis mellifera (1), Lasioglossum (5), Ceratina (Hymenoptera: Apidae; 1), Augochlora pura Say (Hymenoptera: Halictidae; 1), stilt bugs (2), wasps (3), and moths (3). Specimens that had no pollen on the leg slide included Lasioglossum (3), Ceratina (1), and moths (3). Within each slide, we quantified the number of pollen grains from 5 FOV for a total of 1200 FOV. Neither, the total number of sesame pollen grains (z = 0.35, P = 0.73, 0.04 ± SE 0.12) nor the total number of non-sesame pollen grains (z = −0.30, P = 0.77, −0.06 ± SE 0.22) were significantly different when comparing between the head or the leg; however, since there were numerically more sesame pollen grains on insect leg pollen slides, we used the data from leg for further analysis.
Neither the total number of sesame pollen grains (z = 0.11, P = 0.91; 0.02 ± SE 0.16) nor the total number of non-sesame pollen grains (z = −0.16, P = 0.88; −0.05 ± SE 0.30) were significantly different between the 2 sites. The total number of sesame pollen grains was not significantly different across weeks with week 1 as the reference level (week 2: z = −0.80, P = 0.42, −0.15 ± SE 0.19; week 3: z = 1.59, P = 0.11, 0.29 ± SE 0.18). However, the total number of non-sesame pollen grains was significantly different across weeks with week 1 (3.38 ± SE 0.23 [B]) as the reference level (week 2: z = 0.58, P = 0.56, 3.59 ± SE 0.27 [B]; week 3: z = −2.98, P = 0.00286, 2.36 ± SE 0.26 [A]).
There was a significant difference in the number of total sesame pollen grains across insect types (Fig. 3). With the bee genus Agapostemon (Hymenoptera: Halictidae) as the reference level, we found that the bee genera Apis (z = 4.36, P = 0.00001, 3.22 ± SE 0.74), Bombus (z = 4.17, P = 0.00003, 3.06 ± SE 0.73), Lasioglossum (z = 4.21, P = 0.00003, 3.16 ± SE 0.75), and Melissodes (z = 4.14, P = 0.00004, 3.06 ± SE 0.74) had significantly more sesame pollen grains. Megachile (Hymenoptera: Megachilidae; z = 1.92, P = 0.054, 2.62 ± SE 1.36), Xylocopa (z = 1.82, P = 0.07, 1.39 ± SE 0.77), Ceratina (z = 1.77, P = 0.08), Halictus (Hymenoptera: Halictidae; z = 0.00, P = 1.00, −30.38 ± SE 6270000), and Augochlora (z = 0.00, P = 1.00, −30.09 ± SE 6270000) did not have a significantly different number of sesame pollen grains. We found that Lepidopteran (z = 3.38, P = 0.0007, 2.80 ± SE 0.83) and wasps (z = 2.37, P = 0.02, 2.02 ± SE 0.85) had significantly more sesame pollen grains, but Jalysus (z = 0.84, P = 0.40, 1.09 ± SE 1.29) did not have a significantly different number of sesame pollen grains. With the bee genus Agapostemon as the reference level again, Apis (z = −2.97, P = 0.003, −2.92 ± SE 0.99) and Lasioglossum (z = −1.97, P = 0.05, −2.00 ± SE 1.01) had significantly less non-sesame pollen grains; all other insect types did not have a significantly different number of non-sesame pollen grains (all P values > 0.12, all estimates < 0.05, all SE < 1.67).

## Sticky Cards
There was a combined total of 2,726 beneficial insects identified on the sticky cards. At Oxford research station 1,480 beneficial insects were identified: 1,395 wasps and parasitoids, 73 stilt bugs, 11 lady beetles, and 1 dragonfly. Horticulture Crops Research Station had 1,246 insects identified: 1,204 wasps and parasitoids, 37 stilt bugs, 3 lady beetles, and 2 dragonflies. We note, anecdotally, that dragonflies were more abundant at the sites than represented by the sticky card collections. The number of beneficials on sticky cards was not significantly different across sites (z = 0.80, P = 0.42). The number of beneficial insects significantly decreased across weeks, with week 1 (3.54 ± 0.14 [B]) as the reference level (week 2: 3.46 ± 0.13 [AB], z = −0.45, P = 0.65; week 3: 3.05 ± 0.14 [A], z = −2.57, P = 0.01). Yellow sticky cards had more stilt bugs (F1,92 = 19.8, P = 0.00002) and wasps (F1,92 = 92.94, P < 0.0001) compared to red sticky cards.

## Discussion
Here, we provide the first survey of beneficial insects in southeast USA sesame fields. We found bees to be the most prominent pollinator group for sesame, with particular emphasis on the genera Apis and Bombus and the species of concern Bombus pensylvanicus. Our findings are consistent with research from Egypt and India (Kamel et al. 2013, Kavadana et al. 2022). Since these species were documented to have collected sesame pollen through our pollen identification, it is likely that these species are responsible for the majority of cross pollination in sesame. While sesame is generally regarded as a self-pollinating crop, cross pollination is beneficial for maintaining genetic variety, increasing seed yield and reducing inbreeding depression effects and yield gap (Stein et al. 2017). However, many questions remain in regard to pollinator communities of sesame in southeast USA. For example, previous work in Brazil has demonstrated that sesame flowers open at 700 h and are receptive until midday (Andrade et al. 2014), with bloom occurring in November and December. We noted sesame flowers opening later in the day, with bloom occurring in August and September, which may change how long they are receptive. Another area to explore further is the role floral and extrafloral nectaries play with insect visitation. It has been noted that during pathogen infections, sesame extrafloral nectaries are reverted to flower-like structures (Singh et al. 2022); how this may affect insect visitation, and in turn yield, is unknown.
Combining the findings across our sampling methods, we demonstrate there are large numbers of wasps and parasitoids in and around the sesame fields, which act as natural predators to many harmful insect species (Marshall 2006). There is potential for stimulation of parasitoid presence in other crop fields from planting sesame nearby. However, it has been shown that placing sesame alongside other crops could reduce sesame production mainly due to pollinators preferring other flowers over sesame (Ashri and Singh 2006). Future work should explore how much pest control these natural enemy communities can provide in sesame or if they are more likely to be passersby.
Moving forward, as commercial sesame acreage grows in the United States, it is likely that pest and other management problems will begin to emerge. The information presented here will aid in developing successful pest management programs for farmers growing this new and emerging crop while protecting important beneficial insects—such as pollinators and natural enemies. While our study provides an important, first survey of beneficial insects in southeast USA sesame fields, our study is limited by the sample size, sampling duration, and site number. Future, more in depth, research is needed to further understand the beneficial insect populations and the services they provide. For example, previous work has noted that dipterans do visit sesame (Kamel et al. 2013); however, in our study we did not document any dipteran visitors. More research will help determine if this is due to study design or geography as the lack of dipteran visitors has been previously noted in other locations (Langham 1934).
