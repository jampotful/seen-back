Abstract
Insect coloration evolves under multiple selective pressures, and in dragonflies (Odonata: Anisoptera)
body color likely plays roles in visual signaling and thermoregulation. Melanin-based dark body
coloration in particular may aid muscle warming for flight under the thermal melanism hypothesis (TMH)
and enhance immune protection in humid environments (Gloger’s rule). Recent work has shown
phenology and climate jointly impact melanism at the assemblage level, but understanding processes
that generate these patterns requires explicit consideration of intraspecific variation. Here we use a
computer-vision pipeline to quantify threshold-based darkness metrics from ~ 75,000 iNaturalist images,
representing 159 species. We also introduce new methods to account for lighting bias, key for best use
of field images. Focusing on the conterminous United States, we test whether temperature, solar
radiation, and humidity interact with phenology to shape melanism within and between species. We find
phenology strongly mediates temperature effects: the darkest individuals are among the earliest fliers in
the coldest climates. We also find support for a solar-radiation component of TMH and a nonlinear
humidity–melanism relationship consistent with Gloger’s rule. Our results demonstrate that dragonfly
body coloration reflects multiple interacting ecological pressures. We also provide scalable tools and
methods broadly applicable in studies of color biology.

Introduction
Dark colors resulting from embedded melanin pigments are among the most widely studied functional
traits in animals (1,2). Such colors are often linked to broad ecogeographical “rules”, the best-known of
which are the thermal melanism hypothesis (TMH) and Gloger’s rule (3,4). The TMH predicts that
organisms in colder environments will be darker to improve solar heat absorption and thermoregulation.
Gloger's rule, on the other hand, predicts darker coloration in warmer and more humid environments,
where melanin's direct or pleiotropic associations with pathogen resistance may be advantageous in
pathogen-rich climates (1). In insects, evidence for both “rules” has been variable. This variability likely
reflects a mix of methodological inconsistencies, such as differences in rule definition or scope,
environmental gradients examined, and the genuine and dramatic variability in the ecology of different
insect groups (4). Studied groups often differ in their thermoregulatory needs and strategies (5), use of
microhabitat to reduce thermal pressure (6), function of color for mate signaling (7) , desiccation
tolerance (8), and other factors. Additionally, the needs and pressures experienced within a species can
vary and exhibit trade-offs contingent upon environmental conditions (9).
Such variation in the relevance of climatic selective pressures is both spatial (across the geographic
range of species), and temporal (across the course of the year and among years). Phenology – the
seasonal timing of key biotic events such as emergence or peak flight activity— is thus a strong
determinant of the climatic conditions an organism experiences. Given this, it has been hypothesized
that phenology could mediate climate-based selection, or conversely, that climate could mediate
selection on phenological traits (10–13). For example, hatching phenology was one of the strongest
predictors of how alpine Orthoptera species responded to microclimate and elevation (13). Spring
Page 2/21

temperature was a key predictor of how the flowering time of a perennial herb responded to variation in
grazing intensity across years (12). In plants more generally, it has long been known that flower color
within populations and even individuals can shift over the season to best attract different sets of
pollinators (14). And recent work has revealed interactions between phenology, climate, and color in
insects: a study of dragonfly assemblages in Great Britain found that species active during the early or
late parts of the season, when solar radiation is lower, tend to exhibit darker body coloration (15). There,
the improved predictive power of solar radiation relative to temperature supports the relevance of solar
radiation in the context of thermal melanism. Taken together, these results highlight the importance of
seasonal flight timing when testing ecogeographical color rules.
In addition to bright colors from pigments and structural elements, dragonflies (Odonata: Anisoptera)
display a variety of dark melanistic color patterns on their bodies and wings. On the body, dark coloration
ranges from stripes and spots to coverage of almost the entire body (16). On odonate wings, black or
brown melanistic spots implicated in sexual and territorial signaling are present but do not generally aid
in warming (17). Studies have largely supported the TMH for body color in dragonflies, but shown
weaker or more inconsistent support for Gloger’s rule (18,19). Body melanism studies have primarily
looked at assemblages with color data from field guide images, and have yet to incorporate intraspecific
color data. A main reason for the paucity of studies is that color fades in dragonfly museum specimens,
which are typically a primary source of color data. Dragonflies with bright colors in life can become dark
weeks to months after death due to pigment and structural color degradation, especially when proper
preservation via acetone drying is not applied (20). This uncertainty about the colors we observe in old
specimens complicates melanistic color quantification below the species level.
We set out to test whether climate-melanism-phenology relationships hold within and across dragonfly
species, focusing on a broad spatiotemporal gradient in the conterminus USA. Our work explicitly
attempts to disentangle the many possible drivers of melanistic color, including temperature, solar
radiation, and humidity. Key to this effort is the use of “in-situ” field images of dragonflies, where color
has not faded. In particular, we utilize field photographs with Research Grade designation on iNaturalist.
iNaturalist provides a vast source of in-life color and recent studies have begun to take advantage of its
richness while accounting for its heavy noise (21). We developed a computer vision pipeline that uses
artificial intelligence approaches to derive color data, leveraging hundreds of thousands of images to
obtain metrics for how dragonfly species vary spatially in melanistic color while reducing the influence of
confounders such as observation bias. We also take measures to reduce noise when capturing metrics
of dark coloration, given the unstandardized nature of iNaturalist images.
Having obtained this data, we test the core hypothesis that climate variables (temperature, solar
radiation, and humidity) interact with emergence phenology to drive patterns in dark body coloration
consistent with the TMH and Gloger's rule (Fig. 1). Our statistical approach enables disentangling
potential climate predictors, while incorporating flight onset timing as ecological context, to uncover the
extrinsic determinants of dragonfly body coloration. More broadly, our pipeline and results demonstrate
the value of iNaturalist data in investigating traits across broad spatial and temporal scales.

Methods
Data acquisition
We began data collection by downloading a GBIF archive of all ~1.4 million dragonfly (Odonata:
Anisoptera) iNaturalist observations globally (DOI: 10.15468/dl.rbnm5e). Filtering to those in the
conterminus USA reduced the data set to 800,000. To reduce computational burden while maintaining
taxonomic breadth, we randomly sampled a maximum of 2,000 observations per species. We kept all
observations if the number was already below 2,000, but only kept species where the number was at
least 500, a number we deemed necessary to yield the spatial and temporal resolution needed. Given
that well-sampled species hold a disproportionate number of observations, this process reduced the
total number of observations to ~180,000. We downloaded the “medium” quality images for these
~180,000 using links in the GBIF archive and our custom multithreaded downloader then proceeded to
segmentation and filtering. We note that in addition to field photographs, iNaturalist includes an
extremely small number (<1%) of preserved specimen photographs and we did not attempt to
differentiate these. While specimen images do have different color and lighting conditions than field
images we do not expect these extremely rare images to introduce bias into our results. At this stage we
also removed three Corduliidae genera (Dorocordulia, Cordulia, Somatochlora) from the data because
they frequently exhibit dark metallic structural coloration which our threshold metrics (see Melanistic
color quantification) cannot distinguish from the true melanism that is the target of our hypotheses.

Segmenting insects from background
Our two-stage approach for segmentation, or removing insects from their background, starts with a
widely used foundational machine learning approach called groundedSAM (22) (Fig. 2). GroundedSAM
first passes images through the groundingDINO zero-shot object detector to obtain a bounding box
around the insect (23), then sends that box to SegmentAnything to determine which pixels in the box are
those of the insect (24). These models are expected to excel on the majority of iNaturalist images
relative to other models because their broad training makes them effective at finding image subjects in
the face of highly variable targets and backgrounds (24). All groundingDINO versions use the Swin-B
backbone, and we used the one trained on the CoG and COCO datasets. GroundingDINO takes a natural
language prompt that it uses to find its target. Of the prompts “subject”, “insect”, and “dragonfly” all
showed very similar error rates for a test set of 100 random images that included poor quality images
unlikely to return good segments, with 30/100, 28/100, and 28/100 erroneous. Given this, we chose
“insect” for being a middle ground of specificity. Box threshold (the required confidence score for
GroundingDINO to propose a bounding box) and text threshold (the required confidence that a proposed
box matches the word prompt) were both set to the relatively permissive value of 0.25 given that our
primary filtering happens later on. We kept at most one box per image - the box of highest confidence and processed it with SegmentAnything to generate a binary mask. We used the largest variant of SAM,
ViT-H (Vision Transformer - Huge), to maximize segmentation accuracy. To simplify downstream steps

Page 4/21

and because SAM occasionally creates piecemeal masks, we kept only the largest mask in the image
and removed small masks separate from the primary segment.
We opted to remove wings from our dragonfly segments because our aim in this study is to capture
melanistic body coloration. Further, given that wings are often at least partially transparent, it is nearly
impossible to avoid capturing background coloration; for example, green vegetation in the background
often causes erroneous “green” wings. Since groundedSAM was unable to separate wings from body, we
trained an additional UNet++ model, a cutting-edge segmentation architecture that excels at generalizing
from small datasets (25), using a dataset we created containing 500 groundedSAM-extracted segments
with wings and corresponding masks without wings. In this way groundedSAM does the heavy lifting by
handling challenging background variation, then our custom model fine-tunes the segments for our use
case of obtaining dragonfly bodies without wings. To normalize the output segments which have
different sizes and subject angles, we created a custom non-AI computer vision method that finds
candidate vertical lines using a Hough lines transform applied to a skeletonized segment, picks the line
of best symmetry through the segment, then transforms the segment such that the line would become
vertical. This process “straightens out” the segmented dragonfly such that the dragonfly is directly
upright with the head always at the top.

Segment filtering
While segmentation yields reasonable segments for the majority of images, erroneous segments arise
when groundedSAM makes a mistake, our wing removal UNet++ makes a mistake, or most commonly,
when the original image was of low quality due to low resolution, an awkward angle, or an obscured
organism. Images of the nymph life stage are also undesirable, and while those labeled as such in the
metadata are removed, those that are not are retained. To filter against both of these cases, we first
passed every segment through a ResNet18 pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset to extract features from
the penultimate fully-connected layer, which captures high-level abstract representations of the image.
Next we clustered the images using these features with gaussian-mixture clustering (specifying ten
clusters), resulting in clusters where segments in each cluster generally appear similar to one another.
Because noisy “blobs” and partial dragonflies have distinctly different image characteristics than our
desired whole dorsal dragonfly bodies, our wanted and unwanted images generally fall into separate
clusters from one another. For each cluster we manually reviewed 100 random images, identified
clusters with more than 5% (1 in 20) segments being poor or nymphs, and removed those clusters in
their entirety from the pool while keeping all images from the remaining “good” clusters. We considered
a segment poor if it was not an adult dragonfly body or did not retain at least ~90% of the dragonfly body
area when judged by eye. The clustering stage was performed in six batches of 30,000 images per batch
to reduce the memory overhead associated with clustering large numbers of images, seeded for each
batch for reproducibility.

Melanistic color quantification

Page 5/21

We quantified the dark color of each segment as the percent of body pixels below a lightness threshold
and obtained this metric for thresholds of 0.2 and 0.3. For example with the 0.2 threshold, the metric
value for a body segment would be 0.25 if 25% of all pixels in the body segment were as dark or darker
than 0.2 HSL lightness. We selected 0.2 as our primary threshold because it appeared to capture
reasonable estimates of dark coloration in line with human perception but also calculated the more
permissive 0.3 in order to use it in sensitivity analysis. It is necessary to account for noise in these
metrics given the presence of uncontrolled lightning and camera variation in citizen science datasets.
Ambient lighting can brighten or darken the pixel colors of the dragonfly, and diffuse reflections from the
environment—such as light bouncing off vegetation—could also slightly shift pixel values. Differences in
camera sensor sensitivity, color filter arrays, flash setting, and in-camera processing (e.g., automatic
white balance or tone mapping) can further alter recorded brightness and color balance, adding variation
to our measurements. While thresholds are not fully isolated from this noise, a restrictive threshold is
less sensitive to this noise than mean lightness, where any lighting or camera variation directly alters the
mean value. By contrast, using a threshold reduces some of this noise since only those pixels that are
pushed above or below that threshold due to camera or external lighting conditions will have an impact.
The exact code used for segmentation, filtering, and color and lighting quantification can be found in the
project GItHub repository, and the reusable functions we called in that repository can be found in the
repository for an in-development software package: both repositories are listed in the Data Availability
Statement.

Data processing
To account for spatial biases in sampling density and reduce noise, we aggregated individual
observation body segment records into species–grid cell combinations using a 250 km equal-area grid
over the conterminus USA (Fig. 3). Each unique species–cell pair was retained only if it contained at
least 10 records with valid values for the day-of-year of observation. We then estimated the phenological
onset of summer emergence for each species cell using the 10th percentile of a Weibull distribution
generated using the observed day-of-year values, approximating the start of seasonal activity. Because
species-cells with more observations are more likely to pick up the early and late tails of the distribution,
we applied a correction by regressing onset on the number of observations and adjusting residuals to
retain the original mean. This approach removes systematic trends in onset timing associated with
sampling effort. To each species-cell we also added the average percent dark color of all observations
belonging to it. In this way, our transformed data captures how each dragonfly species varies spatially in
both color and emergence timing. Finally, to each cell we added the mean annual climate values for
temperature (“tavg”), solar radiation (“srad”), and vapor pressure (“vapr”) across the spatial range of that
cell from WorldClim (26). We consider vapor pressure (absolute atmospheric moisture) to be the most
direct metric for testing the effect of humidity on color, especially useful for our purposes because it is
independent of temperature (in contrast to temperature-dependent metrics like vapor pressure deficit or
relative humidity). For clarity and brevity, we use “humidity” to refer to vapor pressure throughout the
paper. We used annual climate means instead of seasonal values because phenological onset already
Page 6/21

reflects the seasonal climate that species are exposed to in a given grid cell. Structuring the variation
this way allows us to more directly test our hypothesis that onset mediates the effect of climate. All
predictor variables were scaled before analysis to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one,
enabling more direct comparison across predictors and improving model convergence.

Quantifying systemic lighting bias
Noise is most problematic when it is correlated with a response variable, for example if well-lit
observations occur more often in the north than the south or vice versa. Because lighting and camera
conditions are inseparable without detailed camera information, we aimed to capture them together in
one metric. Our metric is based on the assumptions that 1) biological variation in lightness below the
species-cell level should produce roughly symmetric distributions around the true species-cell mean; 2)
the lighting and camera regime of a species-cell (which could vary due to geography, habitat, or observer
behavior) introduces asymmetric (rather than uniform) measurement error. When this regime is
integrated with the symmetric biological signal to yield the data we observe, the magnitude and direction
of the skew in the distribution of that data should be dependent on the magnitude and direction (dark or
light) of the lighting regime bias. Based on this logic and our two assumptions, we use the skewness of
the distribution of the mean lightness of segments within species-cells as a proxy for the directional
lighting noise regime (Fig. 4). In this way our metric treats the shape of the distribution itself as a
diagnostic: a roughly symmetric distribution indicates balanced lighting conditions (metric ≈ 0), negative
skew (metric < 0) indicates an excess of underexposed images relative to overexposed ones, and
positive skew (metric > 0) indicates an excess of overexposed images. Assumption 1 is consistent with
our modeling approach which already aggregates to species-cell and thus does account for speciesspecific within-cell variation. To test whether lighting bias is a confounder for latitudinal or climate
effects, we test for correlations between it and the climate variables as well as incorporating it directly
into our model selection framework as a covariate. We calculate skewness using the skewness() method
from the R package moments.

Selection of climate variable models
We investigated three climatic variables – temperature, solar radiation, and humidity – using a
systematic stepwise model selection approach with the R package buildmer (27). Because the three
climate predictors were highly collinear, inclusion of all three in the same model led to prohibitively high
variance inflation factors (VIFs). As no standard transformation or regularization approach allowed for
interpretable models with all variables retained, we instead proceeded with stepwise selection for three
separate models, each including phenological onset and a different pair of climate variables.
Approaches like this enable assessing the effect of each climate variable while accounting for its
covariance with the other, and is commonly used in ecological studies facing strong collinearity among
environmental predictors when interpretability is a priority (28). For each pair of climate variables we
started with a full model including all main effects and interactions up to the second order among
phenological onset and the climate variables using natural spline terms (df = 2). Models were fit as
Page 7/21

generalized linear mixed models using glmmTMB with species and grid cell as random intercepts and
Gaussian error distributions. Backward stepwise elimination proceeded by removing terms that reduced
AIC, with the constraint that both basis functions of each natural spline were retained or removed
together.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses
We created three additional groups of paired climate models to increase confidence in our selected dark
color threshold, account for the possibility of phylogenetic confounding, and assess whether bias’ mean
shift is responsible for observed effects. The first additional model group uses a more permissive HSL
lightness threshold for dark coloration of 0.3 instead of 0.2. As glmmTMB does not support custom
covariance structures, the second model group uses the R package phyr and a tree of North American
species (17) to create phylogenetically corrected versions of our final model formulas. Given our
argument that skew represents bias, the third model group uses data where within each species-cell we
both removed the most skewed data points using IQR and used the median black threshold value
instead of the mean - qualitatively similar results from such a model would confirm that our main model
effects are not the result of lighting bias’ effect on the mean.

Results
Summary of data filtering steps
Our filtering removed ~105k images (58%) of our original dataset of ~180k, leading to a final set of ~75k
images (42% of the original dataset) representing 159 of the ~330 species of dragonflies known from
North America, including 76 Libellulidae, 41 Gomphidae, 22 Aeshnidae, 11 Corduliidae, 4
Cordulegastridae, 3 Macromiidae, and 2 Petaluridae. Our approach removing image clusters with >5% of
segments being poor quality (see “Segment filtering” in Methods) ensured that the rate of poor
segments in images used in analysis is below 5%. We considered this error rate to be acceptable for
downstream analyses, especially given that the few erroneous images remaining should introduce noise
rather than systemic bias.

Testing systemic lighting bias
Our proxy for systemic lighting bias of species-cells, CIELAB lightness skew (Fig. 4), was dropped in
every model when performing model selection and showed negligible correlations with most predictors
variables (latitude: r = 0.008, p = 0.73; temperature: r = -0.036, p = 0.13; solar radiation: r = -0.013, p =
0.58) and only weak correlations with longitude (r = 0.052, p = 0.025) and humidity (r = -0.049, p = 0.040).
Interestingly, skew shows a small positive correlation with our body darkness threshold metric (r=0.20, p
< 0.01); darker species-cells are more positively skewed (have worse overexposure).

Climate modeling results

Page 8/21

To investigate what climate variables influence dark body color and whether their effects are contingent
on phenology or one another, we modeled the percent of body area covered by dark color as a function
of phenological onset and two out of three climate variables. Thus we created an onset-temperaturesolar radiation model, an onset-temperature-humidity model, and an onset-solar radiation-humidity
model, with model formulas reached using stepwise model selection. Stepwise selection was highly
decisive for all models, with the chosen term to drop always outperforming the next best alternative by
at least 50 ΔAIC. In this and subsequent results we report and visualize analysis of the 0.2 HSL darkness
threshold color metric for brevity.
In the temperature–solar radiation model (subsequently shortened to “temperature-srad model”), darker
coloration was associated with lower solar radiation (population-level effect estimates for spline terms =
–0.11 and –0.06, both p < 0.001), while the interaction between onset and the first temperature spline
was positive (p < 0.001). The effect of temperature on darkness is strongest in species that onset early,
and temperature has weak to no effect for those onsetting late.
In the temperature–humidity model, the first humidity spline term was positive (p < 0.001), indicating
darker coloration in more humid environments. The temperature effects were broadly similar to the
temperature-solar radiation model, showing an interaction with onset, but the slope was greater and a
negative relationship persisted even in the late-onset species-cells. In the solar radiation–humidity
model, darker coloration was associated with lower solar radiation (spline terms = –0.10 and –0.05, both
p < 0.001). While humidity effects on their own were weak and non-significant, a small but significant
interaction was present between onset and the first humidity spline (p = 0.006) where darkness was
more sensitive to humidity for later onset species-cells. All models include a significant negative
association between later onset and dark coloration that was independent from climate, with lateremerging species–cells generally exhibiting lighter body color.

Nonlinearity in climate models
We anticipated the possibility of non-linear fits across gradients, especially exponential fits where trends
tend to “level out” or show diminishing returns. We found that indeed non-linear terms were retained
across our models fitting climate, species body darkness and onset phenology. The strongest nonlinear
relationships existed for temperature in the temperature-srad and temperature-humidity models, and
humidity in the temperature-humidity model only (Fig. 5). In the temperature-srad model, visualizing the
interaction effect of temperature and onset shows that low temperatures strongly increase body
darkness of early onset species but this effect rapidly levels out in warmer environments. Only low to
intermediate temperatures are associated with changes in melanism and once intermediate
temperatures are exceeded increasing temperature further has little to no effect. By contrast, we do not
see such non-linearity for solar radiation, which has a roughly equal effect on darkness at high and low
values. In the temperature-humidity model, the effect of humidity levels out similarly to temperature in
the temperature-srad model; when humidity exceeds intermediate values, it no longer impacts melanism.

Discussion
Using more than 75,000 iNaturalist images of dragonflies representing 159 species from the
conterminus USA and a semi-automated computer vision pipeline that isolates dragonfly bodies, filters
out erroneous images, and applies a threshold to obtain dark body coverage, we tested the prediction
that phenological onset timing mediates the relationship between climate and melanism in dragonflies.
Our data includes 48% of the ~330 species of dragonflies known from North America, mainly comprising
Libellulidae (47%), Gomphidae (26%), Aeshnidae (13%), and Corduliidae (7%). To our knowledge, we are
the first to derive and analyze quantitative animal color data from iNaturalist at this taxonomic and
spatial scale, and we expand on previous dragonfly melanism work using a broad climatic gradient,
expansive intraspecific color data, and explicit decoupling of climate predictors and phenology. After
aggregating our records to species-grid-cell combinations and calculating the phenological onset of
summer emergence as the 10th percentile of observation dates, we modeled how onset, temperature,
solar radiation, and humidity influence melanism across 159 species. In addition to testing our core
prediction that phenology and climate jointly shape dragonfly body melanism, we evaluated support for
the TMH and Gloger’s rule and found that both are supported, depending on context (29). These results
align with a growing body of literature suggesting the importance of a multidimensional context
dependency rather than a single “rule” predominating (29). Below, we discuss more about the critical
importance of phenology mediating these responses, along with prospects and next steps for broadscale assembly of color information from community science field images.
The effect of temperature on melanism depends on adult flight phenology
Our core prediction is that melanism coverage on the body would be highest in dragonflies that emerge
the earliest in the spring and dwell in areas with low annual temperatures or low solar radiation. In
particular, populations with very early onsets are exposed to the most challenging thermal windows,
where darker bodies could convey critical warming of wing muscles for flight, while the thermal windows
of late-emergers, during peak summer conditions, may pose far less of a challenge (30,31). Our top
models include a strong, significant interaction between onset and temperature, supporting this
hypothesis that temperature’s effect on melanism is contingent on phenology. At low temperatures,
early-onset dragonflies are markedly darker, but this temperature effect shrinks to zero for late-onset
dragonflies.
Decoupling the effects of temperature, humidity, and solar radiation poses a challenge to ecologists due
to the strong geographic correlations between all three variables. These correlations make it statistically
difficult to determine which variable is driving observed patterns in melanism: the effect attributed to one
predictor may partially or wholly reflect the influence of correlated variables. However, our environmental
Page 10/21

gradient across the conterminus USA captures regions where similar thermal regimes coincide with
contrasting humidity levels (e.g., humid subtropical Southeast vs. arid Southwest vs. temperate
Northeast), providing sufficient leverage for our two-variable-at-a-time models to reveal that solar
radiation and humidity also shape melanism. Solar radiation shows a linear negative effect with
melanism while humidity shows a markedly non-linear effect. Lighter bodies are more common in
regions where humidity is low, but intermediate and high humidity yield similarly darker phenotypes,
suggesting a threshold or saturation effect. Once cuticle darkness (or correlated immune function)
reaches a level sufficient to protect dragonflies in humid conditions, further darkening appears to provide
no additional advantage. We note that while the temperature-onset effect holds in both temperature
models, the apparent humidity-onset relationship in the solar–humidity model is likely an artifact of the
very collinearity challenges described above, with humidity partially absorbing temperature’s effect when
temperature is excluded.
Our finding that phenology onset interacts with temperature, but not solar radiation, has intriguing
implications for dragonfly thermal ecology. This asymmetry could reflect the lower and less
physiologically challenging interannual variability in solar radiation seasonality compared to temperature
seasonality. Solar radiation's dependence on orbital geometry makes it more predictable than
temperature, which is buffered and destabilized by atmospheric dynamics. Thus, early-emergers at high
latitudes likely face unpredictable cold snaps, which may be a strong selective pressure for darker
bodies as a buffer against this thermal uncertainty.
Beyond its integration with climate effects, later onset also showed a significant direct negative
association with melanism in all three models: later emerging dragonflies are lighter in color. This
suggests that effects beyond climate also influence color variation across the flight season, such as
signaling needs for light or dark color that vary across the season and are largely decoupled from
climate-based physiological needs. However, our analysis cannot rule out other explanations for this
climate-decoupled effect, such as unmeasured climate variables, specimen age, or a specific effect of
pruinosity signaling from Libellulidae, which we discuss in more detail below. In general, the diverging
patterns of our three climate variables with respect to onset suggest that dragonflies face a mosaic of
pressures: temperature imposes phenology-dependent selection, solar radiation and humidity exert
phenology-independent pressures, while phenology itself has a net effect. While it has been tempting to
only test one or the other ecogeographic rule, this work provides strong support for a multidimensional,
context dependent framework (4) that critically integrates both the temporal (phenological) and spatial
(geographic) dimensions of species' thermal niches (32).
Assemblage-based vs. intraspecific hierarchical modeling
Our findings complement recent work by Novella-Fernandez et al (2023), who demonstrated seasonal
and climate-based color variation in dragonfly assemblages across Great Britain, providing the
inspiration for our study of the conterminus USA. They show both that assemblage color is darker when
radiation is lower during the year and that assemblages are also darker early and late in the season,
Page 11/21

suggesting a link between the two. Our approach expands upon this by decoupling phenology from
annual climate, treating these variables as independent predictors of intraspecific and inter-specific
melanistic color that could interact. Our multilevel hierarchical modeling structure allows us to answer a
fundamentally different question: not whether assemblage color tracks seasonal climate fluctuations,
but whether populations with earlier versus later emergence timing respond differently using color to the
same annual climate regime, tested across >150 species. Our results suggest that this is indeed the
case for temperature but not solar radiation, which diverges from results in Novella-Fernandez et al
(2023), who found that assemblage composition shifts toward darker species on days with lower solar
radiation. We argue that their solar radiation result, reflecting between-species associations of body
color with seasonal timing, and our solar radiation result revealing that within species populations
respond to annual radiation similarly regardless of when they emerge, are compatible and may indicate
that assemblage-level phenological associations and species-level climate adaptation operate through
distinct mechanisms. The increased or contextually different climate variation between the USA and
Great Britain could also be responsible for these differences. While assemblage approaches excel at
revealing community-level patterns of seasonal niche partitioning, hierarchical frameworks that capture
intraspecific patterns across a wide range of species that themselves live in multiple different climatic
conditions are necessary to begin to disentangle the phenology-mediated and context-dependent nature
of climate-based selection within and ultimately across species.
Leveraging citizen science and AI for large-scale trait analysis
Our study demonstrates the power of merging large-scale image data with AI-driven computer vision to
quantify morphological traits at scale, usable for deriving new insights across space, time, and taxa. The
most similar work to date involved quantifying bird color from manual ROIs and was intended primarily
for methods validation through comparison to measured color spectra. Recent advances in foundational
image detection and segmentation, such as groundedSAM, enable such work by automating the process
of background removal necessary for color quantification - such segmentation is not feasible to do
manually for hundreds of thousands of images. Our approach is especially relevant for odonate color
quantification, allowing us to bypass the limitations of both (a) odonate museum specimen color, where
degradation of pigments, which is impacted by preservation method, makes it challenging to take dark
color at face value, and (b) species colors derived from field guide images, which are often used as an
alternative to museum specimens but lack any intraspecific color information. We leveraged this data to
address emerging hypotheses about phenology–climate–trait interactions using a dataset spanning
hundreds of species and much of the multidimensional climatic gradient of the conterminus USA. We
highlight the usefulness and vigor of keeping a “human-in-the-loop” in our filtering step, in which a
human observer picks image clusters to keep and drop based on predefined criteria for erroneous
images and error rates. Other benefits of our filtering approach include reproducibility using clustering
seeds and not having to train a specialized model. We also note the value of facing bias and noise
challenges by quantifying specific sources of noise and statistically evaluating their potential for
systemic confounding of results, as we did via measuring potential lighting and camera noise based on
the skew of lightness distributions within species.
Page 12/21

Limitations of color quantification and the challenge of lighting
Despite its advantages, our approach to analyzing color at scale has multiple avenues for improvement
and extension. A significant limitation in our models and citizen science data in general is the absence of
consistent and robust annotations. Of greatest concern is our inability to account for sex when many
dragonflies exhibit strong sexual dimorphism in color and pattern, resulting in differences in melanism
coverage. In this scenario it is possible that our results could be driven by one sex alone, especially for
groups with the strongest dimorphism in melanism, such as Libellula, or species where the sexes have
different thermoregulatory needs. Such intraspecific color could also be problematic for our lighting bias
quantification if our skew metric is capturing dimorphism (distributions with multiple peaks) more than it
does genuine skew. Pruniosity, a UV-reflective waxy coating that appears white or pale-blue, is an
especially relevant case of dimorphism because it increases across the flight season and is most
pronounced in males (33). While this temporal increase in lighter color could be thermoregulatory by
reflecting summer heat (and thus in line with our non-pruinose color data), it could also confound our
phenology-climate interactions if pruinosity is primarily used for signaling rather than thermoregulation.
These issues involving color dimorphism could be addressed in future research by either inductively
identifying species with color morphs by detecting instances where color or pattern features fall into
clean intraspecific clusters, which is non-laborious but a new methodological challenge. Color-coding for
sex by hand is simpler and likely accurate, but very laborious for human observers and thus difficult to
scale. A potential middle ground is developing a machine learning model for classifying dragonfly sex
using a supervised approach, but creating a model with sufficient and unbiased accuracy across the
whole suborder would be challenging.
A related consideration is that darker species-cells showed slightly more positive lightness skew, a
pattern consistent with our interpretation that skew reflects lighting artifacts: species near the lower
lightness bound have limited room for negative skew but substantial room for positive skew via
overexposure. This noise pattern would, on average, shift the darkest dragonflies slightly lighter,
potentially leading to minor underestimation of effect size in our models but not causing any changes in
effect direction.
More generally, we argue that the quality of our filtered images is sufficient for our dark color threshold
metric, especially given the use of species-grid-cell averages which helps smooth individual image
variability in lighting, camera, and viewing angle. However, the aforementioned image quality issues still
persist and could decrease the confidence of our estimates even when it doesn’t shift their direction.
And while our thresholding is more robust to lighting noise than color means, we could be missing subtle
but functional variation between dark brown and black colors.
Finally, while we presented a single method for quantifying lighting (our approach focusing on skew) we
also tried multiple other strategies which landed on the cutting room floor. This included clustering
image lightness histograms or backgrounds in an attempt to identify lighting regimes, searching for
specular highlights indicating overexposure, and leveraging the pixel lightness distributions of images.
Page 13/21

We broadly suggest that approaches that don’t take into account the similarity in color of images of the
same species are typically untenable, and resulting metrics pose a risk of being biased themselves.
Regardless of the approach taken, quantifying lighting noise in images of such immense variety hundreds of species, taken from a variety of angles, by a range of cameras, within a breadth of
environmental backgrounds - is a monumental challenge that will only become more important to
address, where possible, in the future. More work, potentially leveraging continued innovation in machine
learning, is still required to determine whether more consistent and “foolproof” methods exist for
addressing the perpetual challenge that lighting bias poses to image-based macroecology.
Future directions
Given our study is associational, an important next step would be experimental validation of melanism’s
thermal benefits across phenological gradients—for instance, testing whether dark early-emerging
individuals reach flight-ready temperatures faster than light individuals under equivalent cold conditions.
For dragonflies, it also remains unclear whether spatial variation in body melanism is primarily the result
of plasticity or regional adaptation, and common garden experiments are needed to help determine what
mechanisms drive climate-associated color. Some research suggests that diel (daily) activity patterns
could also be relevant in shaping thermal responses in insects, and iNaturalist data may be apt for
testing whether this could apply to dragonflies (34) (CITE our diel paper once it comes out). As a whole,
our findings in dragonflies suggest that phenology-mediated relationships between climate and color
could extend to other ectotherms with variation in their seasonal life cycles, highlighting the need to
incorporate phenology into trait-based ecology (31,35,36). As climate continued to warm, our results
imply that organismal responses could unfold across multiple dimensions simultaneously—spatial
distributions, phenological timing, and trait evolution—with thermal melanism and Gloger's rule operating
not as competing hypotheses but as context-dependent selective forces contingent on the spatial and
temporal conditions populations experience. Understanding such responses will be critical and the
convergence of citizen science, AI, and ecological theory offers a practical path forward for such global
questions in trait-based ecology.
