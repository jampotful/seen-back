Abstract
Plant-pollinator interactions are shaped not only by environmental conditions but also by biotic interactions such as predation. Predators influence prey through direct consumption and non-consumptive effects (NCEs) arising from perceived
predation risk altering prey traits such as foraging behaviour. Emergent freshwater macrophytes such as the yellow water
lily (Nuphar lutea) create a shared interface where predators and pollinators co-occur. Yet, NCEs of predation risk on
pollinators and their consequences for plant-pollinator interactions remain severely understudied, especially at aquaticterrestrial transition zones. Therefore, we experimentally tested how artificial dragonflies, as standardised visual predation cues, shape the foraging behaviour of pollinators on N. lutea and assessed whether these behaviours were related to
natural variation in odonate abundance. We quantified visitation rates and visit durations under artificial dragonfly, neutral
object, and control treatments, and related visit duration to natural odonate abundance. Our results showed that artificial
dragonfly-like cues reduced visitation rates by up to 50% and visit durations by up to 18 s per visit, across pollinator
taxa. Moreover, visit duration showed a negative association with natural odonate abundance. Structural equation models revealed that pollinator behaviour was indirectly associated with environmental conditions (i.e., wind and overcast
weather) via their negative effects on odonate abundance. Consequently, our findings demonstrate that predator-mediated
NCEs can play a substantial role in shaping pollinator foraging on N. lutea, highlighting that plant-pollinator interactions
at freshwater-land ecotones may depend on dynamic predation risks and underlying environmental drivers. These insights
are particularly important for understanding macrophyte-insect interactions because communities at aquatic-terrestrial
interfaces are especially vulnerable to shifts in environmental conditions on land and water.

themselves can function both as mutualists and as prey
(Benoit and Kalisz 2020). Predation on these pollinators

also their ecological roles (Romero et al. 2011; Benoit and
Kalisz 2020). Predator-prey interactions have often been
evaluated for direct, consumptive effects between predator
and prey species. However, over the last decades, the role
of “non-consumptive effects“ (NCE) have gained recognition in experimental studies (e.g., Romero et al. 2011).
Such effects arise from perceived predation risk, rather than
direct consumption events. NCEs include alterations in prey
phenotypes, such as changes in behaviour, morphology,
physiology, or life history (Werner and Peacor 2003; Sheriff
and Thaler 2014). By imposing fitness costs associated with
defensive strategies, NCEs can equal or even exceed the
population-level impacts of direct consumption (Preisser et

how prey perceive and respond to spatial heterogeneity in
predation risk.
Non-consumptive effects of predators are often mediated
through the “landscape of fear”, a conceptual framework
describing how spatial variation in perceived predation risk
shapes prey behaviour (Bleicher 2017; Gaynor et al. 2019).
Aspects in the physical landscape and spatial differences in
predation risk create zones of higher and lower perceived
danger (Gaynor et al. 2019). In a landscape of fear, prey
adjust their behaviour through changes in vigilance or
energy allocation due to the perception of direct and indirect
cues, learned behaviour, and the balance between the cost
and benefit of responses. For foraging prey species such as
many pollinators, a landscape of fear may cause shorter floral visits and avoidance of flowers posing high perceived
risk of predation, thus decreasing foraging efficiency (Preisser et al. 2005; Laundré et al. 2010). In fact, a recent study
showed that flower-visiting insects often face predation risk
while foraging on flowers, driving behavioral adaptations
that enable them to recognize and avoid predators or other
unfamiliar objects on flowers (Antiqueira et al. 2025). These
behavioural changes can have cascading effects on other species via trait-mediated-indirect-interactions (TMIIs) (Knight
et al. 2005). In TMIIs, a predator exerts indirect influence on
a third species by altering traits, such as pollination behaviour, of an intermediate species which then can indirectly
have negative consequences for interacting species such as
flowering plants (Gonçalves-Souza et al. 2008; Wirsing et
al. 2021). For instance, in three-way interactions within pollination networks, predators may disrupt the behaviour of
pollinating insects (intermediate species) through perceived
predation risk, thereby reducing the pollination success of
plants that depend on plant-pollinator interactions (Knight
et al. 2005). Although non-consumptive effects are increasingly recognised as important drivers of ecological interactions, their influence on pollination dynamics remain poorly
understood (Benoit and Kalisz 2020; Camurça et al. 2024).
Yellow water lilies (Nuphar lutea) exhibit a particularly
interesting ecology and pollination strategy (Ervik et al.
1995). As a freshwater macrophyte, most of its biomass is
submerged; however, through its floating leaves and emergent reproductive organs, it inhabits an aquatic-terrestrial
transitional zone that links flying insect pollinators with
freshwater habitats (Ervik et al. 1995). Underwater, N. lutea
provides habitat for numerous aquatic insect larvae, including predatory odonate larvae, which use macrophyte shoots
for shelter or hunting grounds (Hunger 1998; Brito et al.
2021; Sentis et al. 2022). At the water surface, its floating
leaves and emergent flowers further offer suitable habitats for larval emergence (Hunger 1998) and oviposition
(Röbelen and Schütte 2020). During spring and summer,
also pollinators benefit from the nectar and pollen of the

brightly coloured blossoms. N. lutea is mainly visited by
inconspicuous pollinators of the groups Diptera (including
syrphid-flies) and Hymenoptera (e.g., Apis mellifera and
Bombus terrestris). Fly species of the families Ephydridae
(e.g., Notiphila, Hydrellia), Scatophagidae (especially the
yellow-water-lily-fly Hydromyza livens), and related taxa
exhibit close associations with Nuphar species and contribute substantially to their pollination (Van der Velde and
Brock 1980, Lippok and Renner 1997). N. lutea displays
protogynous flower development, as the sexual phases of
the floral organs are temporally separated, which represents
an evolutionary adaptation to insect pollination and effectively reduces the likelihood of self-pollination (Lippok
and Renner 1997). Although widespread, N. lutea remains
an understudied freshwater macrophyte, which offers an
ideal system to investigate non-consumptive interactions
between predators and pollinators at the interface between
land and water.
Odonates (dragonflies and damselflies) are some of the
most effective insect predators, with exceptionally high predation success rates of up to 97% (Olberg et al. 2000). As
visually oriented sit-and-wait and patrol predators, they can
influence pollination not only through direct consumption
of pollinators (Benoit and Kalisz 2020; Sentis et al. 2022)
but potentially also by generating spatially structured predation risk that alters pollinator behaviour. Such risk effects
place odonates unequivocally within a landscape-of-fear
framework, where perceived predation risk can mediate
trait-based responses in pollinators, potentially leading to
indirect effects on plant-pollinator interactions (Bleicher
2017; Gavini and Quintero 2024). Through their complex
life cycle and dual role as aquatic larvae and terrestrial
adults, odonates also link freshwater and terrestrial ecosystems, making them particularly relevant for studying predator-mediated interactions at ecosystem boundaries (Knight
et al. 2005). Their presence around emergent aquatic macrophytes, such as N. lutea, creates a natural context in which
predation risk experienced by pollinators may cascade
across habitats. Despite this potential, non-consumptive
effects of odonate predation risk on pollinator behaviour,
and the resulting consequences for plant-pollinator interactions, particularly at aquatic-terrestrial interfaces, remain
poorly understood (Benoit and Kalisz 2020). This knowledge gap is especially relevant given that interfaces between
freshwater and terrestrial systems are highly sensitive to
environmental change, as they are affected by shifts in both
systems (McFadden et al. 2023; Vagheei and Boano 2025).
Here, we examined how visually perceived predation
risk from odonates shapes pollinator behaviour in an aquatic
plant-pollinator system. Using artificial dragonfly models as standardised visual predation cues, we tested their
effects on pollinator visitation rate and visit duration across

different pollinator taxa visiting N. lutea. To place these
experimental effects within the broader ecological context
of natural predation risk, we additionally quantified natural odonate abundance in the field. We then used a structural equation modelling (SEM) approach to evaluate how
abiotic conditions, including wind and weather, influenced
natural odonate abundance and how this variation indirectly
relates to pollinator visit duration. Specifically, we expected
that (i) artificial dragonflies would reduce both pollinator
visitation rate and visit duration relative to control conditions; and that (ii) higher natural odonate abundance would
be associated with shorter visit durations. By integrating
experimental manipulation with observational data, our
study examines non-consumptive predator effects within a
landscape-of-fear framework and assesses their potential to
generate trait-mediated indirect interactions (TMIIs) affecting plant-pollinator interactions. Together, these results
provide insight into non-trophic predator-prey interactions,
highlight the role of abiotic drivers in shaping perceived
predation risk, and improve our understanding of crossecosystem linkages and pollination dynamics at aquaticterrestrial boundaries, with potential consequences for the
reproductive ecology of N. lutea.

Materials and methods
Study site
We conducted the field experiment in an urban pond in
Hamburg, Germany (53°33’33.9"N 9°51’41.9"E). This
artificially created pond (maximum depth = 0.6 m) is surrounded by abundant riparian vegetation and harbours
various freshwater macrophyte species (e.g., Myriophyllum
spicatum, Ceratophyllum demersum, Nuphar lutea, Phragmites australis). There was a high abundance of floatingleaved plants, mainly N. lutea with a surface coverage of
90% during peak flowering season in June/July 2025. At the
interface between land and water, floating-leafed flowering
plants such as N. lutea function as ecological connectors
between aquatic and terrestrial systems. The pond harboured different species of fish, most notably of the genus
Cyprinus (carp), as well as water birds such as Gallinula
chloropus (common moorhen) and Anas platyrhynchos
(mallard). We characterised the water physicochemical conditions (HACH HQ Multiprobe) in June, July and August:
the water temperature ranged from 19 to 22 °C during the
day, pH ranged from 7.2 in August to 7.7 in June and July,
conductivity ranged between 408 and 428 µs/cm, and dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.4 to 8.2 mg/L which corresponded to 73–83% dissolved oxygen.

Study design
To investigate possible effects of artificial dragonflies on
pollinator behaviour (visitation rate, visit duration), we performed a field experiment using a randomised block design
(Fig. 1a). We observed a total of 22 one-hour blocks over
a span of 15 different days between June and August. This
corresponds to an observational period of 1320 min. We
selected nine yellow water lily flowers per block with an
average spacing of 1 m (max: 2 m, min: 0.5 m) between
flowers and observed visits by pollinating insects. Visit
duration was measured using a stopwatch. We assigned the
treatments randomly to individual flowers, resulting in three
flowers per treatment (artificial dragonfly, neutral object,
control). To examine a potential relationship between natural odonate abundance and visit duration, we recorded the
number of odonates active in the immediate vicinity of a
flower during each pollinator visit (individuals/m3). In total,
we recorded 176 odonates over 459 plant-pollinator interactions with a sum of 449 min of visitation. We considered
that N. lutea follows a protogynous flower development and
thus, initially undergo a female phase, followed by a male
phase lasting several days. We selected the flowers at the
same stage of development and at equal distances from each
other. Since individual yellow water lily flowers only bloom
for a few days before sinking and being replaced by new
flowers, it was not possible to ensure a perfectly even distribution of flowers over a predetermined area throughout the
entire flowering period. However, we minimised environmental differences between blocks as much as possible to
ensure the most independent distribution and observation of
the treatments. Wind and weather were recorded as binary
environmental variables with “wind” as ~ 2 m/s or higher or
“no wind” when calm. We recorded weather as either overcast or clear as these were the only conditions where pollinators of N. lutea were effectively active.

Visual predation cues
To test the effects of perceived dragonfly predation risk of
pollinators of N. lutea, we attached artificial dragonflies
with a length of 5.5 cm and a maximum width (wingspan)
of 6 cm and made from PVC plastic on the fully emerged
inflorescences of N. lutea (Fig. 1b). The artificial dragonflies display the most important morphological characteristics of Anisoptera and were comparable in dimensions to
four-spotted chasers (Libellula quadrimaculata), a commonly found dragonfly species at the study site during the
observational period. We attached the artificial dragonflies
to the side of the water lily flowers as field observations
have previously shown that the dragonflies found there
(mainly Libellula quadrimaculata) use the Nuphar flowers

Statistical analysis
We performed all statistical analyses using R Version 4.5.1
(R Core Team 2025). All data and code to fully reproduce
our analyses are publicly available under: ​h​t​t​​p​s​:​/​​/​d​o​​i​.​o​​r​g​/​1​0​
.​5​0​6​1​/​d​r​y​a​d​.​1​c​5​9​z​w​4​b​t​​​​.​​​
To assess how experimental treatments influenced visitation rates (visits per treatment per hour), we used generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) using the package lme4
(Bates et al. 2015). We constructed the separate models for
Diptera and Hymenoptera and with observational blocks as
random effect and with treatment as fixed effect. For each
treatment inside each block, we aggregated count data,
including zero visits. We constructed the models following
a Poisson and a negative binomial distribution and tested
them for overdispersion and zero inflation. Overdispersion was not present in the Diptera model and negligible in
the Hymenoptera model. We compared models with Poisson and with negative binomial distribution using AIC and
Likelihood Ratio Test, and because the models were similar
(Δ AIC < 2; LRT: X² = 0.0011, p = 0.97), we used Poisson
GLMMs. To assess the normality of error distributions and
homoscedasticity of the models, we visually inspected diagnostic plots such as residuals vs. fitted values. We assessed
contrasts among treatments using estimated marginal means
(EMMs) with the package emmeans and a Tukey correction for multiple comparisons (Lenth et al. 2025). Blocks in
which no Hymenoptera were observed throughout the sampling period were excluded from the Hymenoptera analyses
because they represented periods without any activity of the
focal pollinator group, and therefore contained no information on treatment effects. Hence they would have inflated
standard errors and lowered estimated marginal means,
without explaining effects due to our experimental manipulations (Diptera: 22 blocks, Hymenoptera: 16 blocks). To
compare treatment effects between pollinator groups, we
constructed models including both groups, comparing models with and without interaction between treatment effects
and pollinator group using Likelihood Ratio Tests.
To assess how different experimental treatments influenced visit durations, we also used linear mixed models
using the package lme4 (Bates et al. 2015). Again, we
constructed the models with observational blocks as random effect and with treatment and odonate abundance as
fixed effects. We compared models with and without interaction between treatment and odonate abundance using
Likelihood Ratio Tests. We obtained minimal adequate
models by excluding factors, as well as interactions without significance and by using AIC comparisons through a
top-down approach, starting with full models. We applied
a log10-transformation to the response variable (visit duration) prior to testing for normal distribution. We assessed

Page 5 of 13

the normality of errors and homoscedasticity of the models
visually using residuals vs. fitted values plots. We assessed
contrasts among treatments using estimated marginal means
(EMMs) with the package emmeans and a Tukey correction
for multiple comparisons (Lenth et al. 2025). To compare
treatment effects between pollinator groups, we constructed
a third model using data from both groups using the same
procedure, comparing models with and without interaction
using Likelihood Ratio Tests.
To examine a potential relationship between odonate
abundance and pollinator visit duration, we calculated
Pearson coefficients for each pollinator group using log10transformed data for visit duration. We then visualised possible interactions between treatment and odonate abundance
regarding visit duration using predicted values from the
LMMs.
To test indirect associations between visit duration and
environmental factors, we constructed structural equation
models (SEMs). We constructed the path models using the
package lavaan (Rosseel 2012). We included indirect effects
of both wind and weather mediated by odonate abundance.
Exogenous variables are allowed to correlate by default, so
wind and weather were modelled with a freely estimated
covariance. Consequently, each path coefficient reflects
the effect of a predictor while statistically controlling for
the other (Rosseel 2012). We transformed the data of the
response variable (visit duration) using log10 to achieve a
normal distribution. We used Most Likelihood as an estimator for the models. We constructed the path models separately for both dipteran and hymenopteran pollinators. We
evaluated model fit using multiple fit indices (X², RMSEA,
SRMR, CFI, TLI). Following recent recommendations, we
interpreted fit indices descriptively rather than using strict
cut-off values and considered theoretical expectations and
path coefficients (Groskurth et al. 2024). Detailed hypotheses for each included link are presented in Table S1.

Pollinators of Nuphar lutea
We found a diverse assemblage of insect taxa, with Diptera
(n = 281) representing the most frequent pollinator-group
on N. lutea. Dipterans included generalist taxa such as
hoverflies (Syrphidae), including Episyrphus balteatus, as
well as taxa associated with specialists of Nuphar, notably
Ephydridae (specifically Hydrelliinae) and Scathophagidae,
with individuals tentatively identified as Hydromyza livens.
Hymenopterans (n = 162) included the honeybee (Apis mellifera) and the buff-tailed bumblebee (Bombus terrestris).

Discussion

NCEs (Dukas 2001; Dukas and Morse 2003; Knight et al.
2005), disrupting plant-pollinator mutualisms with cascading effects on plant reproduction (Werner and Peacor
2003; Knight et al. 2005; Gavini and Quintero 2024). Our
results show that dragonfly-like visual cues of predation
risk, simulated by artificial dragonflies, elicited behavioural
responses consistent with non-consumptive predator effects
on pollinator behaviour on Nuphar lutea. To the best of our
knowledge, this is the first study observing the influence of

Together, these novel findings support the hypothesis that
odonates can exert NCEs on pollinators at aquatic-terrestrial
interfaces and imply the need to consider how environmental conditions indirectly influence these insect-macrophyte
dynamics.
The strong reduction in visitation rates under the artificial dragonfly treatment for both dipterans and hymenopterans indicate that dragonfly-like visual predator cues alone
can significantly deter floral visitors. Our results show that
artificial dragonflies reduced the visitation rate of pollinators by 50% (Diptera: Fig. 2a) and 46% (Hymenoptera:
Fig. 2b). These findings exceed the average 36% reduction
in pollinator visits under elevated predation risk, as reported
in a meta-analysis, and may potentially have severe consequences for plant fitness through TMIIs (Romero et al.
2011). While only restricted to one studied pond, our findings align with previous studies showing that pollinators are
highly responsive to visual signals associated with predation, such as crab spiders (Gonçalves-Souza et al. 2008).
Odonates employ a wide variety of hunting strategies that
differ among species. However, this behavioural trait diversity of odonates can also depend on environmental contexts,
such as land use (De Resende et al. 2021). While some species hunt as sit-and-wait predators from concealed perches
within riparian or emergent vegetation, others are highly
active aerial predators that capture prey almost continuously
while in flight (Sentis et al. 2022; De Resende et al. 2021).
During the experiment, we visually observed multiple
dragonfly species within the family Libellulidae, that used
the flowers of N. lutea as perching points. Libellulidae are
known to include numerous perching species (Olberg et al.
2000); a behavioural response by pollinators to the artificial
dragonflies may therefore plausibly reflect a common spatial avoidance pattern due to sit-and-wait predators. Interestingly, the neutral-object treatment also caused a moderate
reduction in dipteran visits, though not in hymenopterans.
This implies that dipterans may be more sensitive to structural disturbances or novel objects, whereas hymenopterans
appear to differentiate between biologically relevant and
irrelevant cues more effectively. Although such taxon-specific behavioural plasticity in response to visual stimuli has
been reported across pollinator groups (Romero et al. 2011),
we found no evidence for overall differences in responses

between pollinators of N. lutea, as artificial dragonflies
reduced visitation rates similarly across pollinator groups.
A more taxonomically precise quantification of pollinator
groups or individual species would likely have revealed
finer differences in behavioural patterns both within and
between Diptera and Hymenoptera. The non-significant
difference between artificial dragonfly and neutral-object
treatments in visitation rates suggests a partial contribution
of unspecific mechanical or visual disturbance (neophobia:
Antiqueira et al. 2025). However, the consistent and stronger reduction under artificial dragonflies suggests that most
of the response is attributable to risk perception rather than
an artefact of the study design.
Reduced visit durations on flowers with artificial dragonflies reinforce the idea that perceived predation risk affects
not only the decision to approach but also the time spent
foraging. Our results showed that artificial dragonflies
decreased the mean visit duration of pollinators by 38%
(Diptera: Fig. 3a) and 40% (Hymenoptera: Fig. 3b), comparable to the 51% average reduction in visit duration, as
reported by a meta-analysis (Romero et al. 2011). Given N.
lutea protogynous floral phases (Ervik et al. 1995), shorter
visit durations could have potentially reduced pollen transfer efficiency and thus reproductive success (Knight et al.
2005). Although we did not measure fitness directly, this
possibility is consistent with findings of a meta-analysis
showing that interference in plant-pollinator mutualisms
were associated with an average 17% reduction in plant fitness (Romero and Koricheva 2011). To better understand
the cascading effects of odonate predation risk on the reproductive success of N. lutea, we would need to quantify
the extend to which pollen limitation may occur. Similar
to visitation rates, the non-significant difference between
artificial dragonfly and neutral-object treatments suggests
a partial contribution of object-bound disturbance, though
the consistent and stronger reduction under artificial dragonflies highlights that most of the response is attributable to
risk perception. These findings parallel experimental work
showing that pollinators trade-off feeding time for safety
under elevated predation risk (Romero et al. 2011).
The shared aquatic-terrestrial interface created by N.
lutea supports diverse odonate assemblages, which impose
substantial predation risk on flower-visiting insects (Knight
et al. 2005). Given this consistently high predation pressure around N. lutea, pollinators may adjust their behaviour
in response to odonate abundance, relying on visual cues
and movement in their surroundings. Consistent with this,
visit duration showed a strongly negative relationship with
odonate abundance across both pollinator groups (Fig. 4).
Interestingly, the linear mixed models revealed a significant interaction between treatment and odonate abundance
only present in Diptera (Fig. 4a). The significant interaction

Non-consumptive effects of odonate predators on pollinators of water lilies

between treatment and odonate abundance (Fig. 4a) indicates that dipterans respond to static, dragonfly-like visual
cues but may be even more sensitive to other relevant stimuli like movement or olfactory cues (Romero et al. 2011).
These findings align with studies demonstrating that Drosophilid flies exhibit diverse sensitivities to visual predation cues (MacLeod and Luong 2024) and escape through
exceptionally fast motor escape skills (Card and Dickinson
2008). The spatial distribution of dipteran species that are
specialised pollinators of Nuphar (Lippok and Renner 1997)
is more restricted compared to other pollinator groups.
For these dipteran species, the trade-off between resource
acquisition and predator avoidance may be less pronounced
than for hymenopterans like bees, which forage across
larger spatial scales (Beekman and Ratnieks 2000; Huey
and Nieh 2017). Hymenopterans likely face higher costs
when reacting to predation risk, as avoiding certain high
reward flowers or patches may considerably reduce foraging efficiency (Gavini and Quintero 2024). Although visit
duration was also negatively correlated with natural odonate abundance in Hymenoptera, treatment effects remained
clearly distinguishable over rising odonate abundance. Due
to their larger body size, Honeybees (Apis mellifera) and
buff-tailed bumblebees (Bombus terrestris) are likely more
frequently preyed upon by large anisopterans (Olberg et al.
2005). Therefore, hymenopterans could react differently to
perceived predation risk posed by dragonflies compared to
damselflies. However, we could not disentangle possible
differences since odonate abundance was recorded only at a
low taxonomic resolution and a more detailed quantification
might reveal more nuanced predator-specific effects on different pollinator groups. Although we were able to examine
the relationship between odonate abundance and visit duration of pollinators, We were not able to draw similar conclusions about pollinator visitation rates. This would require a
different approach, in which odonate abundance is recorded
independently and at regular intervals throughout the observational blocks.
Our study not only shows direct effects of simulated predation risk on pollinators of N. lutea and associations with
live odonate abundance, but also revealed indirect relationships with environmental variables, mediated by odonate
abundance. These findings fit within the inherently dynamic
“landscape of fear” framework, in which shifts in predator distribution over time alter the spatial patterns of perceived predation risk (Laundré et al. 2010). Environmental
factors that positively influence odonate abundance may
therefore intensify this “landscape of fear,” exacerbating
trade-offs faced by pollinators (Gavini and Quintero 2024;
Gaynor et al. 2019). Trade-offs can, for example, include
the cost and benefits of increased vigilance, spatiotemporal
redistribution, social behaviours, or foraging (Gaynor et al.

Page 11 of 13

77

2019). Our findings imply that wind had a negative effect
on odonate abundance in the study area, thus indirectly
causing an elevation of pollinators foraging duration. As
primarily ectothermic organisms, the ability to thermoregulate differs among odonate species and is mostly dependent
on body size and hunting behaviour (Guerra et al. 2026).
Most perching species are “thermal conformers“ that rely
mostly on air temperature or heliothermic species that
become active when direct sunlight is available (De Marco
and Resende 2002). Many large “fliers“ are endothermic
and produce endogeneous heat with the ability to thermoregulate by controlling their haemolymph circulation (De
Marco and Resende 2002). Therefore, direct sunlight can
increase odonate activity and hunting behaviour, which is
especially relevant in a warming world (Guerra et al. 2026).
Our findings imply that clear weather has a positive effect
on odonate abundance, increasing their activity and indirectly decreasing pollinator visit duration. We observed that
small red-eyed damselflies became active almost immediately when clouds dispersed and direct sunlight was present.
This observation is consistent with reports indicating that
this species depends on warm conditions and exhibits an
almost immediate reduction in activity when direct sunlight
is obscured by cloud cover or during strong winds (Hunger
1998).
Many pollinators benefit from the same weather conditions (Miyashita et al. 2023) that also favour odonates (De
Marco and Resende 2002). On clear, calm days, both odonate abundance and pollinator activity may increase. This
conflict between favourable environmental conditions and
the need to avoid predation risk can further heighten the
trade-offs experienced by pollinators. Therefore, environmental variables can shape the non-consumptive effects
between odonates and pollinators, consequently amplifying
cascading effects on plant-pollinator mutualisms through
TMIIs. Simultaneous visits under high pollinator activity
may have reduced observational accuracy. The path models
(Fig. 5) explained 15% (Diptera) and 12% (Hymenoptera)
of the variance in visit duration. The relatively low variance
explained by the models may reflect the inherent complexity of pollinator behaviour, which is influenced by numerous
factors beyond predation (Mailly et al. 2025). Additionally,
species-specific differences, not captured due to low taxonomic resolution, may further contribute to unexplained
variation. As species and their interactions become increasingly affected by climate change, it is essential to account
for the extent to which environmental variables can affect
ecological interactions (Vagheei and Boano 2025). In addition to trophic interactions, it is especially important to also
consider such non-trophic interactions and their cascading
effects (e.g., TMIIs) (Benoit and Kalisz 2020; Romero et
al. 2011).

In conclusion, our findings suggest that visually perceived dragonfly-like cues can elicit behavioural responses
in N. lutea pollinators consistent with non-consumptive
responses to predation risk. Additionally, our data suggests
that odonates may not only influence the foraging behaviour of pollinators via direct effects, but also by mediating
indirect effects of environmental conditions on pollinator
behaviour. Therefore, future studies of NCEs and TMIIs
should additionally consider the possible indirect effects
of environmental factors. Our findings highlight relevant
NCEs at aquatic-terrestrial interfaces, impacting plant-pollinator interactions and thus possibly influencing the reproductive success of N. lutea through TMIIs. Understanding
how non-consumptive effects shape the pollination dynamics in freshwater-land ecotones could support conservational efforts aiming to preserve biodiversity on both land
and water.
