Abstract
1. Turbidity challenges the visual performance of aquatic animals. During development,
environments with limited visibility may affect the fine-tuning of visual systems and
thus the perception of, and response to, risk. While turbidity has frequently been used
to characterise permanent aquatic habitats, it has been an overlooked feature of
ephemeral ones.
2. Here, we use the natural diversity of ephemeral rearing sites (phytotelmata) in which
the tadpoles of two poison frog species are deposited and confined until metamorphosis
to explore the relationship between environments with limited visibility and response
to perceived risk.
3. We sampled wild tadpoles of Dendrobates tinctorius, a rearing-site generalist with
facultatively cannibalistic tadpoles, and Oophaga (formerly Dendrobates) pumilio, a
small-phytotelm specialist dependent on maternal food-provisioning, to investigate
how the visual environment in rearing sites influences tadpole behaviour. We
hypothesised that turbid rearing conditions negatively impact both species’ ability to
perceive risk, decreasing response strength to predatory visual stimuli. Using
experimental arenas, we measured tadpole activity and space first on a black and white
background, and then on either black or white backgrounds where tadpoles were
exposed to visual stimuli of (potentially cannibalistic) conspecifics or potential
predators.
4. When placed in a novel arena, the effects of rearing environment on D. tinctorius
tadpoles were clear: tadpoles from darker pools were less active than tadpoles from
brighter pools, and did not respond to either visual stimuli, whereas tadpoles from
brighter pools swam more when paired with conspecifics versus odonate larvae,
suggesting that tadpoles can visually discriminate between predators. For O. pumilio,
tadpoles were more active on experimental backgrounds that more closely matched the
luminosity of their rearing sites, but their responses to the two visual stimuli did not
differ.
5. Larval specialisation associated with species-specific microhabitat use may underlie the
observed responses to visual stimuli, which has implications for the stability of species
interactions and trophic dynamics in pool communities. Together, our findings
demonstrate that light availability of wild larval rearing conditions influences the
perception of risk in novel contexts, and provide insight into how visually guided
animals may respond to sudden environmental disturbances.
Keywords: larval vision, phytotelmata, poison frog, tadpole-rearing sites, predator-prey
interactions, sensory ecology, limited visibility

Introduction

92
93

Sensory recognition is crucial for the success of predators and the survival of prey. While

94

predators and prey are in an evolutionary arms race to refine their detection of each other, there

95

are factors outside of their control that can limit the accuracy of their perception (chemical

96

contamination: Weis and Candelmo 2012; light pollution: Minnaar et al. 2015). Nevertheless,

97

developmental plasticity associated with the growth of organs and tissues creates the

98

opportunity for bodily systems to optimise their function in a given environment. For example,

99

the optic tecta of the brain in both fishes and amphibians enlarges when developing in habitats

100

with higher conspecific densities (Gonda et al. 2009, Trokovic et al. 2011), while nutritionally

101

poor diets in mammals (Lee and Houston 1993) and birds (Savory and Gentle 1976) induce an

102

increase in gut size, optimising digestion. Sensory systems are particularly susceptible targets

103

of selection, as they are energetically expensive structures (Niven and Laughlin 2008) whose

104

performance is fundamental to individual survival and reproductive success (Streinzer et al.

105

2013). Bentho-pelagic elasmobranchs, for example, have denser and larger olfactory surfaces

106

than benthic species, which supposedly compensates for the scarcity of visual cues in the open

107

ocean (Schluessel et al. 2008). Male net-casting spiders (Deinopis spinosa) have enlarged

108

principal eyes (and a more developed integration centre to process their inputs) compared to

109

females and juveniles; the change in eye size in males is hypothesised to enhance predator

110

detection as a result of switching from a sedentary to a wandering lifestyle (Stafstrom et al.

111

2017). These examples illustrate how sensory organs can compensate to adjust sensory function

112

when faced with pressures from contrasting ecological niches (Schluessel et al. 2008).

113
114

Turbidity is a ubiquitous yet often underappreciated environmental pressure that shapes the

115

dynamics of predator-prey interactions in aquatic environments (Abrahams and Kattenfeld

116

1997, Horppila et al. 2004, Van de Meutter et al. 2005). In experimental tests on the impact of

117

turbidity, both background colour and the turbidity of rearing conditions have been found to

118

influence the perception of risk by prey, affecting both their activity (invertebrates: Van de

119

Meutter et al. 2005, fish: Leris et al. 2022) and space-use (invertebrates: Horppila et al. 2004),

120

while generally reducing capture rates by predators (reviewed by Ortega et al. 2020).

121

Laboratory studies have shown that the photic environment during development affects the

122

fine-tuning of visual systems (tadpoles: Bridges 1970, fishes: Kröger et al. 1999, Fuller et al.

123

2010). From a proximate perspective, one mechanism that has been shown to flexibly change

124

the spectral tuning of the eyes is the vitamin A1/A2 chromophore exchange system in the retina

125

(Bridges 1972, Reuter et al. 1971). In this system, a lower A1/A2 ratio generates a red-shifted

126

spectral sensitivity which can be advantageous in reddish environments (Corbo 2021) such as

127

freshwater (Lythgoe 1979) and turbid waters (Jerlov 1976). Ultimately, while environmental

128

conditions affect immediate (activational) individual responses, they also play a long-term

129

(organisational) role in shaping the development of sensory systems throughout an individual’s

130

lifetime (Snell-Rood 2013). Previous work on both killifish (Fuller et al. 2010) and guppies

131

(Ehlman et al. 2015) has linked the developmental impact of limited light and turbidity with

132

the spectral tuning of the visual system, which in turn has cascading effects on the behaviour

133

of fishes in novel contexts. These studies establish an important connection between proximate-

134

level restructuring of the visual system and ultimate-level changes in behaviour that can be

135

used to predict outcomes of predator-prey interactions.

136
137

Phytotelm-breeding amphibians provide an exciting vertebrate model to further explore the

138

relationship between environments with limited visibility and visually guided behaviours, as

139

parents of some species transport their tadpoles to isolated pools of water in the vegetation

140

where they are confined until metamorphosis. Phytotelmata are water-filled vegetal structures

141

such as treeholes, palm bracts, and seed capsules, which are inhabited by diverse communities

142

(Kitching 2000), and can appear as any combination of bright/dark and crystalline/turbid

143

(Fouilloux et al. 2022a). Tadpole deposition sites are a unique system, because the conditions

144

in lentic phytotelm environments can change suddenly and then persist for long periods of time

145

(Leris et al. 2022, Fouilloux et al. 2021). In turn, the ‘darkness’ can be a result of light

146

absorption by pigments and substances in the water itself, or the inner walls of the container,

147

or both. Despite this remarkable variation, tadpole vision has never been explored in the context

148

of the photic environment of rearing conditions. This is at least in part because earlier studies

149

suggest that tadpole vision is generally ‘poor’ (e.g. myopic: Hoff et al. 1999, Mathis et al. 1988;

150

low visual acuity expected on the basis of small eye size: Caves et al. 2018; Butler et al. 2022),

151

and it had been well established that tadpoles rely heavily on chemical cues to navigate and

152

assess their environment (Mathis and Vincent 2000, Weiss et al. 2021). More recently,

153

however, experiments that isolate and combine sensory modalities have uncovered an

154

important role of tadpole vision in conspecific communication and predator-prey interactions

155

(Hettyey et al. 2012, Stynoski and Noble 2012, Kumpulainen 2022), and even some extent of

156

illumination-dependent differential expression in vision related genes has been reported (Schott

4

157

et al. 2022). Previous work demonstrating plasticity in tadpole vision substantiates the

158

hypothesis that environments with selective light absorption may induce spectral shifts in their

159

visual system (Donner and Yovanovich 2020, Schott et al. 2022) versus eliciting other adaptive

160

responses, such as sensory compensation (Ehlman et al. 2015). Ultimately, although turbidity

161

is frequently used to characterise permanent aquatic habitats, it has been an overlooked feature

162

of ephemeral ones; as such, the impact of turbidity on the sensory development and visual

163

recognition in species evolved to use ephemeral habitats remains unknown.

164
165

In this study, we investigated how wild-caught larvae of two phytotelm-breeding poison frog

166

species (Dendrobates tinctorius and Oophaga pumilio), reared in pools with different optical

167

characteristics, respond to predator and conspecific visual stimuli in novel contexts.

168

Dendrobates tinctorius tadpoles are aggressive predators that will consume each other

169

facultatively (Rojas 2014, Rojas and Pašukonis 2019, Fouilloux et al. 2022b). They are

170

deposited in a wide range of microhabitat types and frequently cohabit pools with other

171

tadpoles and insect larvae that can be either predators (e.g., dragonflies: Odonata) or prey

172

(Rojas and Pašukonis 2019). In contrast, O. pumilio tadpoles depend on their mothers to deposit

173

unfertilized, nutritive eggs in their pools (Stynoski et al. 2009), which serve as their primary

174

food source throughout development (Brust 1993). In addition to being oophagous, O. pumilio

175

tadpoles are small-pool specialists, primarily using bromeliad and other plant axils as pools,

176

which they occupy singly; if multiple depositions occur, smaller tadpoles are cannibalised

177

within a couple of days (Brust 1993, Stynoski 2009).

178
179

Given the variability in light availability and visibility inside phytotelmata, we hypothesise that

180

the visual environment of rearing sites influences the development of sensory systems, and

181

thus, the behaviour of larvae in novel conditions. Predatory tadpoles such as D. tinctorius may

182

rely on vision (Kumpalainen 2022) and thus, low visibility during development could impact

183

the phenotype of D. tinctorius visual systems (e.g., by red-shifting spectral sensitivity,

184

Fouilloux et al. 2022a), which may mitigate the loss of acuity and improve predator

185

performance in turbid conditions. With this said, a red-shifted spectrum in tadpoles is not a

186

failsafe to overcoming the challenges of a visually limited environment. We predict that D.

187

tinctorius reared in habitats with poor visibility would be generally less active (reduced prey

188

response: Kimbell and Morrell 2015) and less discerning between visual stimuli (poor learning

189

in turbid conditions: Chivers et al. 2012) than tadpoles reared in clear habitats. In comparison,

5

190

given the restricted surface area of pools and high predation rates (67%, Maple 2002) in O.

191

pumilio rearing sites, tadpole responses to perceived risk are better contextualised in terms of

192

“seeking refuge and freezing” instead of the “escaping or attacking” response that more

193

accurately describes D. tinctorius’ behavioural repertoire. Thus, although O. pumilio tadpoles

194

have been shown to respond differently to positive (food-bearing mother) and negative

195

(predator) visual stimuli (Stynoski and Noble 2012), we expect a more generalised fear

196

response where we predict that experimental contexts that are perceived as safer (i.e., more

197

similar to rearing conditions) will elicit stronger behavioural responses (i.e., more activity)

198

from O. pumilio tadpoles.

Methods

202

Study species

203

Dendrobates tinctorius and Oophaga pumilio are Neotropical poison frog species with

204

intensive parental care. In D. tinctorius fathers care for the terrestrial clutches and transport

205

tadpoles from oviposition sites to phytotelmata. D. tinctorius pool use is especially flexible

206

compared to other phytotelm-breeding species, as these tadpoles occur in pools from a wide

207

range of substrates that can be vastly different sizes (Fig. 1, volume: 19mL to 270L, height =

208

0- >20m, Fouilloux et al. 2021). Despite being facultative cannibals, D. tinctorius tadpoles are

209

frequently placed in pools occupied by larger conspecifics (Rojas 2014). In these pools,

210

tadpoles also encounter heterospecific tadpoles (generally Allobates femoralis) and insects

211

(predatory Odonata larvae; Rojas 2014, Rojas and Pašukonis 2019, Fouilloux et al. 2021). For

212

this study, D. tinctorius tadpoles were taken from dead palm bracts, treeholes, and fallen trees

213

found around Camp Pararé, Les Nouragues Field Station, French Guiana from March to April

214

2022. Tadpoles were captured using small aquarium nets and spoons. Focal tadpoles ranged

215

from stages 25 to 41 (Gosner 1960) and weighed 0.05–0.88 g.

216
217

Unlike in D. tinctorius, O. pumilio mothers are the primary caregivers, transporting tadpoles

218

from terrestrial clutches to phytotelmata (Brust 1993). O. pumilio are much more specialised

219

in their pool use than D. tinctorius, generally opting for small bromeliads and other axil-

220

forming plants (volume: 10 to 100 mL, Stynoski unpublished data; Maple 2002). In addition

221

to using much smaller pools, O. pumilio mothers feed deposited tadpoles with nutritive eggs,

222

which serve as an obligate food source until metamorphosis (Brust 1993, Stynoski 2009).

Basic set-up

238

Background choice assay

239

All tadpoles (D. tinctorius, n = 20; O. pumilio, n = 25) were placed in the centre of an arena

240

with a 2x2 black/white chequered background (Fig 2). The activity and background choice of

241

tadpoles were quantified by scan-samples every 15 seconds for 15 minutes.

242
243

Open field tests

244

Following the background choice assay, we performed multiple open field tests. These

245

consisted of an arena with either an entirely black or white background used as extreme cases

246

of ‘crystalline dark’ and ‘crystalline bright’ visual environments, respectively (Fig. 1),

247

containing different visual stimuli in the arena centre, i.e., either a conspecific tadpole or a

248

known predator placed in an isolated container filled with rainwater. We recorded activity

249

(swimming or not swimming) and space use (zone occupancy) of the focal tadpole at 15 second

250

intervals for 15 minutes in each treatment. Zones were delineated by distances from the edge

251

of the stimulus container in the arena centre, where Zone 1 was up to 2 cm away from the

252

centre, Zone 2 was 2-5 cm away from the centre, and Zone 3 was beyond 5 cm.

Statistics

314

Reflectance values from RGB photos (for both D. tinctorius and O. pumilio) were assessed

315

using a principal components analysis (see Supplement S4 and Supp. Fig 3 for methods to

316

compare photography and spectrophotometer quantification of water appearance (S4)). For

317

both D. tinctorius and O. pumilio models the first component (PC1) explained more than 95%

318

of the variance and thus were used as the predictor to represent differences in phytotelm photic

319

environment (Fig 3, Supp. Fig 4). O. pumilio models were coded with either mean maximum

320

321

322

323

324

Discussion

456

Developmental conditions shape the way in which many organisms perceive risk. Throughout

457

our sampling of phytotelmata we found that both D. tinctorius and O. pumilio tadpoles occur

458

in a wide range of microhabitats that vary in their brightness and turbidity (Fig 1, 3) and that

459

these differences have measurable effects on the tadpoles’ behaviour when paired with relevant

460

visual stimuli.

461
462

The effect of photic environment on activity across species

463

Both species of tadpoles use vision to assess their surroundings. Visual environment has

464

different consequences on the behaviour of a tadpole predator versus one dependent on

465

maternal provisioning throughout development. When assessing an individual’s preference for

466

brightness, our choice experiments established a tadpole’s preference for black versus white

467

backgrounds. Tadpoles of both species spent at least 75 percent of their time on black

468

backgrounds where their dark brown bodies are considerably concealed. Similar results of

469

animals choosing concealing environments have been shown in killifish (Kjernsmo and

470

Merilaita 2012) and other amphibian larvae, all of which avoided non-concealing backgrounds

18

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.03.08.531703; this version posted March 10, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

471

(Eterovick et al. 2018) and had higher survival rates on concealing backgrounds when faced

472

with predators (Espanha et al. 2015). Having established this preference, we followed up by

473

introducing an additional risk component (predatory visual stimulus) on either entirely white

474

or black backgrounds.

475
476

For both species, the visibility (including, but not limited to turbidity) of rearing conditions

477

significantly influenced tadpole activity in a novel context. In D. tinctorius, tadpoles from

478

darker pools moved less irrespective of the experimental background colour or visual stimuli

479

(Fig 4, B) with which they were paired. This behavioural pattern has generally been established

480

across the animal kingdom, where animals that were reared in more turbid conditions are less

481

active and social in novel contexts (Fuller et al. 2010, but see work on damselfly responses

482

reported by Van de Meutter et al. 2005). The implications of darker pools on activity are worth

483

considering, as visually limited environments tend to negatively impact predation (Ortega et

484

al. 2020). Predatory species such as D. tinctorius can consume both con- and heterospecifics

485

(Rojas and Paškonis 2019); lower tadpole activity could have cascading effects on cannibalism

486

rates, diet-quality, and thus time to metamorphosis (CF unpublished data, Kupferberg 1997)

487

which may have negative implications for the survival of tadpoles, as pool stability is never

488

guaranteed in ephemeral habitats (Fouilloux et al. 2021). From a community perspective, a

489

shift in predator diet composition may affect the richness of the microhabitat, where species

490

able to use other modalities to navigate their environment may outcompete vision-dependent

491

species when faced with turbid conditions.

492
493

O. pumilio tadpoles were significantly more active on background conditions that more closely

494

matched their rearing conditions (Fig 4, D). A similar trend was also found in Trinidadian

495

guppies, where fish raised in clear water aquaria were more active in clear conditions compared

496

to turbid conditions (Ehlman et al. 2015). Ultimately, the visual landscape of phytotelmata can

497

drastically change depending on various biotic and abiotic factors, and it is not far-fetched to

498

imagine a crystalline pool suddenly becoming turbid (or vice versa), as pools regularly dry and

499

fill up again with clear rainwater and debris regularly falls from above (Romero et al. 2020).

500

Based on our results, we would hypothesise that an O. pumilio tadpole from a brighter pool

501

would consequently move less in dark conditions. This change in activity as a function of a

502

changing environment has important implications for O. pumilio tadpoles, and may impact

503

both the begging ritual between tadpoles and their mothers and the ability of tadpoles to detect

19

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.03.08.531703; this version posted March 10, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

504

and evade terrestrial predators entering their pools (Stynoski and Noble 2012, Stynoski et al.

505

2018). As most of O. pumilio tadpoles’ interactions occur at the air-water interface,

506

manipulating object size, distance, and colour from the edge of a turbid versus a crystalline

507

arena could disentangle the strength of tadpole responses to positive/negative stimuli and the

508

acuity of tadpole vision in visually limited environments.

509
510

While we cannot conclude the behavioural changes in either species to be a result of visual

511

restructuring, previous work has found lighting conditions of rearing environments (tea-stained

512

vs. clear) to affect both foraging behaviour and opsin expression in the eye (Fuller et al. 2010).

513

Ultimately, there exists a wealth of mechanistic oriented vision research, primarily in fish,

514

showing that environmental light and colour affect the expression of visual pigments during

515

development (Fuller et al. 2005, Shand et al. 2008). Thus, although we do not assess the visual

516

system from a proximate perspective, multiple studies have shown that tadpoles rely on vision

517

to assess risk (Hettyey et al. 2012, Szabo et al. 2021, this study), substantiating the role of

518

vision as an important modality in the larval umwelt. While the link between turbidity as a

519

particular case of ‘red-shifted’ environment (Jerlov 1976, Corbo 2021) and visual restructuring

520

remains to be experimentally tested in tadpoles, recent interest in this field (Donner and

521

Yovanovich 2020, Corbo 2021) is starting to yield exciting insights. It is well established that

522

the challenge in turbid waters is not light availability (and thus it cannot be straightforwardly

523

overcome with increased sensitivity) but rather distance-dependent contrast degradation due to

524

non-image forming scattered light (Lythgoe 1979). In this scenario, myopic eyes such as those

525

of tadpoles might not be so disadvantageous after all (Mitra et al 2022), as the image quality

526

of the visual scene is proportionately better when viewing objects close by. There are neither

527

theoretical nor empirical estimates of tadpoles’ visual acuity, however the recent confirmation

528

that they tend to have spherical lenses even in species in which the adult lenses are flattened

529

(Mitra et al 2022) opens up the possibility for tadpoles to present adaptations to maximise

530

(within the constraints imposed by eye size) their spatial resolution and thus have a better

531

chance to deal with turbid visual environments.

532
533

Ecology shapes species’ responses to perceived risk

534

For each species, only one variable influenced consistently both activity and space use: the

535

visual stimulus with which tadpoles were paired for D. tinctorius, and background colour for

20

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.03.08.531703; this version posted March 10, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

536

O. pumilio. The implication of these variables can be contextualised by comparing the biology

537

of a habitat generalist versus a habitat specialist.

538
539

The outcome of most of D. tinctorius tadpoles’ biological interactions is context-dependent, as

540

D. tinctorius tadpoles can readily cannibalise conspecifics (Rojas 2014), but can also co-exist

541

with them (Fouilloux et al. 2021); they can survive when odonate larvae are present, but can

542

easily become their prey when the naiads are large enough (Rojas and Pašukonis 2019). Within

543

the continuum of perceived risk for D. tinctorius, we find that in undesirable contexts (i.e.,

544

white background, an experimental environment tadpoles avoid when given the choice) a

545

tadpole’s behavioural response is to “engage” with a potential threat. D. tinctorius tadpoles

546

from clear pools swim more and spend more time in the arena centre where the stimulus is

547

placed, suggesting that tadpoles recognize and seek to interact with conspecifics, even when

548

provided only visual cues (similar to Kumpulainen 2022’s findings in a laboratory setting). In

549

comparison, when paired with an odonate larva, which was larger or the same size as focal

550

tadpoles 90 percent of the time, tadpoles from clear pools spend more time frozen at the edges

551

of the arena. Previous work using open field tests associates elevated stress levels and risk

552

aversion with arena centre avoidance (Champagne et al. 2010), suggesting that tadpoles might

553

occupy the arena edges in response to the potential risk posed by the naiad. While D. tinctorius

554

tadpoles from dark coloured pools seem to have adopted a “sit and freeze” strategy no matter

555

the condition, tadpoles from lighter pools are able to recognise and change strategies depending

556

on the visual stimulus with which they are faced. As a result of the flexibility of D. tinctorius

557

deposition strategies, tadpoles are more likely to encounter diverse heterospecifics (tadpoles,

558

dragonflies, mosquitoes) on varied backgrounds (dead bark, young plants, treeholes) and thus,

559

need to be able to distinguish different types of visual stimuli.

560
561

These findings do not suggest that D. tinctorius tadpoles are more visually oriented than O.

562

pumilio but instead highlight how the natural history of different species shapes their response

563

to perceived risk. For example, the effect of previous predator exposure generating stronger

564

responses in ensuing encounters has been shown before in tadpoles (via chemical cues, Fraker

565

2009). In D. tinctorius, focal tadpoles from pools containing more conspecifics spent less time

566

in the arena centre (Supp. Fig 8), suggesting that the strength of stimuli avoidance may be

567

informed by previous antagonistic experiences. In comparison, O. pumilio are obligately

568

oophagous tadpoles which, in most instances, develop singly and in particularly small water

21

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.03.08.531703; this version posted March 10, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

569

volumes. In the context of their natural history, a tadpole’s response to risk is to seek refuge.

570

In the wild, this can be done by diving down to the detritus at the bottom of a leaf axil, for

571

example. Previous work has established that O. pumilio tadpoles see and respond to diverse

572

visual stimuli (Stynoski and Noble, 2012, Stynoski et al. 2018); in the context of this study the

573

potential effect of a visual stimulus may have been overridden by the design of the experiment

574

itself, as tadpoles were in an arena larger than any of their natural deposition sites and with

575

nowhere to hide (800mL in this study vs. ~15 - 150mL in previous experiments; Stynoski et

576

al. 2018, Khazan et al. 2019). While this design was intentional to create a comparable context

577

to D. tinctorius, its role in contributing to a tadpole’s perceived risk should be acknowledged.

578

Overall, O. pumilio tadpoles spend less time in the arena centre when on a white background

579

compared to black backgrounds (even in the control instance when there is no stimulus, Fig 4,

580

C). For an O. pumilio tadpole, it could be that when on white backgrounds the potential

581

“refuge” provided by edge effects of the arena draws tadpoles away from the arena centre. In

582

a novel context, tadpole “freezing” appeared to be the general strategy adopted by tadpoles;

583

without a refuge to hide in there was no reason for individuals to modulate their activity in

584

response to the distinct risks of each stimulus.

585
586

Phytotelmata as models for environmental disturbance

587

Phytotelmata provide natural mesocosms to test and manipulate diverse habitat quality

588

indicators on the development of animals. The natural diversity in phytotelm turbidity can be

589

used as a small-scale model to predict how other aquatic animals may respond to sudden

590

changes in habitat quality (Busse et al. 2018, Romero et al. 2020). Ecologically, predator or

591

prey advantage in turbid conditions depends on the short-term adaptability of either player’s

592

detection/evasion systems (Abrahams and Kattenfeld 1997); ultimately, here two visually

593

guided species respond in ways that could negatively impact their survival in the form of

594

decreased foraging or hindered access to parental care. Although the plasticity of the visual

595

system may mitigate these effects to some degree, based on our data we would expect animals

596

dependent on other modalities to better succeed in visually limited environments. To our

597

knowledge, this is the first study to quantify phytotelm photic environment: we validate that

598

digital photography (with a colour standard) generates the same ranking of pool

599

brightness/turbidity as full spectral readings obtained with a spectrophotometer, demonstrating

600

the accuracy of photography as a quantification tool for microhabitat visual environment and

22

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.03.08.531703; this version posted March 10, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

601

opening the door for future studies to pursue other turbidity-related questions independently of

602

having access to expensive laboratory devices.

603
604

Conclusions

605

We compare the perception of risk of a flexible predatory species to a microhabitat specialist

606

with an adapted oophagous diet, testing both species’ responses to visual stimuli having

607

developed in varied photic environments. The perception of risk in animals is context-

608

dependent, and the light quality of rearing conditions affects the response to risk in novel

609

contexts. As animals face increasingly disturbed habitats, these results highlight how animals

610

with varied natural histories depend on vision and how they might respond to sudden

611

environmental disturbances.
