Multiple opsin expression in cubozoan ocelli indicates functional redundancy

# Multiple opsin expression in cubozoan ocelli indicates functional redundancy

## Abstract
Box jellyfish have a complex visual system comprising 24 eyes of four morphological types. The visual functions of the two large image-forming lens eyes are well-understood; however, far less is known about the two lateral pairs of smaller eyes, the pit eyes (PE) and slit eyes (SE). Here, we raised antibodies against five opsins identified from Tripedalia cystophora transcriptomic data for immunohistochemical studies. One opsin was expressed in the outer segments of the PE photoreceptors, suggesting this as the PE photopigment. Expression patterns also suggest that two other opsins are photopigments in the SE photoreceptor outer segments; remarkably, a different opsin and a putative photoisomerase were also identified in the SE in a prior study. This is the first time that multiple opsin expression has been localised to the outer segments of photoreceptors in a cnidarian eye - particularly intriguing given the unknown function of the SE. Multiple opsins expressed in the SE, and some opsins that are expressed in both the SE and the manubrium (thus potentially with both visual and non-visual functions) supports the hypothesis of functional redundancy in the many Tripedalia opsins, which phylogenetic analysis suggests are even more numerous than the 19 opsins currently known.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44915-5.

## Introduction
Light reception is found throughout Cnidaria, ranging from extraocular photoreceptors to image-forming eyes. Most of our knowledge of cnidarian photoreception stems from morphological and behavioural studies; however, few studies integrate these approaches, coupling visual behaviour with specific light receptive structures. While many scyphomedusae and hydromedusae have simple ocelli, their behavioural functions are only known in a couple of species through ablation experiments or responses to simple light stimuli1–5. Only the charismatic tropical and subtropical cubozoans (box jellyfish; Fig. 1A,D) have evolved true image-forming eyes in their medusa stage. All known box jellyfish have four rhopalia, each bearing two pairs of ocelli (slit eyes and pit eyes; SE and PE) and two camera-type eyes (upper and lower lens eyes; ULE and LLE) (Fig. 1B,E). In the Caribbean box jellyfish Tripedalia cystophora, these lens eyes have a coarse spatial resolution of 10–20°, a low temporal resolution with flicker-fusion frequencies of 6–7 Hz, and are colour-blind6–8, but nonetheless provide fundamental visual information for several key behavioural tasks. This small mangrove-dwelling species swims between the prop roots towards light shafts where their copepod prey is located, using the LLE to avoid damage from bumping into the roots9,10, and the ULE to navigate towards mangrove canopies within lagoons11. While the involvement of the ULE and LLE in these visually-guided behaviours are well-understood, the functions of the two lateral pairs of ocelli remain obscure.

Genomic and transcriptomic data have facilitated several studies into the molecular biology of cnidarian photoreception revealing that, like all other known animal eyes, (1) vision is opsin-based12–14, and (2) a G-protein transduction cascade is triggered by photoisomerization of the opsin bound 11-cis retinal to all-trans retinal14,15. Interestingly, genomic and transcriptomic data have also revealed an extraordinarily high number of opsin genes in some cnidarians, even within the eye-less hydrozoan Hydra vulgaris and anthozoan Nematostella vectensis. These two species have 45 and 29 opsin genes, respectively, putatively as a result of tandem duplications16,17. Furthermore, in situ hybridization studies showed that most tissues in the hydrozoan Cladonema radiatum expressed opsins, but also that several of the 18 opsins known from this species are associated with ocelli at the tentacular base18. In general, fewer opsin genes are found in scyphozoans; for example, only seven are known from the moon jellyfish, Aurelia aurita14. The available data points to a large variation in the number of opsins between the different species of the eye-bearing box jellyfish, but in T. cystophora 19 different opsins have been reported so far19–22.
These high numbers of opsin genes present in many cnidarians strongly suggest a large degree of redundancy in the visual system, and that not all these opsins are functionally distinct. However, limited expression studies have been conducted, and the degree of redundancy is unknown20,23. Furthermore, opsins parsed from transcriptomic data might not represent separate genes, but instead alleles of the same gene, especially if the RNA was extracted and pooled from several animals. Interestingly, our previous immunohistochemistry studies reveal unique expression patterns for five of the 19 T. cystophora opsins, suggesting functionally disparate genes19,20. These results also suggest that the three distinct eye types (lens eyes, pit eyes, and slit eyes) each use a different opsin as photopigment.
Here, we further explore the functional diversity of T. cystophora opsins to investigate how much of the found opsin diversity can be explained by alleles and/or redundancy and to gain additional knowledge of the box jellyfish visual system, especially the lesser known PE and SE. Novel custom-made antibodies against another five T. cystophora opsins were selected on the basis that phylogenetic analyses resolved these as sister opsins20, and thus with the putatively highest chance of being alleles of the same genes. The expression patterns were also sought verified using in situ hybridization. However, our results from immunohistochemistry experiments do not support the presence of several alleles from the same opsin genes, but instead suggest separate genes and functional redundancy of T. cystophora opsins. This is especially the case with respect to SE opsins and extraocular opsins expressed in the manubrium.

## Methods

## Custom-made opsin-antibodies
The five T. cystophora opsins investigated in this study were identified by Liegertová et al. (2015) from transcriptomic data: Tcop1, Tcop2, Tcop6, Tcop7 and Tcop11 (see Suppl. Mat. 1 for GenBank accession numbers, and for synonyms of opsin terminology used in prior studies). These opsins were selected out of the 14 T. cystophora opsins not yet investigated, after phylogenetic analyses resolved three pairs of sister opsins: pair 1, Tcop1 and Tcop11; pair 2, Tcop6 and Tcop7; pair 3, Tcop2 and Tcop19-SEO (the latter previously examined in another expression study; Garm et al., 2022). By targeting these opsin pairs, this study aimed to distinguish alleles from genes and to investigate functional redundancy. Note that Tcop19-SEO was recovered from transcriptomic data by Nielsen et al. (2019), but not by Liegertová et al. (2015). Custom-made antibodies against the five investigated opsins were produced in rabbits (Genosphere, Paris, France) using amino acid sequences of length 12–20 aa from their C-terminus (Suppl. Mat. 1).

## Sampling
Adult-sized T. cystophora medusae (bell diameter, BD: 6–9 mm) were collected from a mangrove lagoon in the Everglades, Florida (locality: 25.8205, − 81.4251). To verify that opsin expression patterns are identical in juvenile and adult medusae, juvenile medusae (BD: 1.5–2.5 mm) were obtained from cultures at the Institute of Physiology, Kiel University (see Fig. 1 for organization of the visual system in adult and juvenile medusae of T. cystophora). The juveniles were cultured in 250 L tanks with recycled seawater on a 12:12 h light: dark cycle, a temperature of 28–29 °C, and a salinity of 35–36, and were fed Selco-enriched Artemia sp. nauplii daily. All material used in experiments were fixed in 0.15 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS: 10 mM phosphate, 2.68 mM KCl and 140 mM NaCl, pH = 7.45, made from tablets, Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for an hour at room temperature post-dissection, and were subsequently stored in 0.15 M PBS until use. Experiments followed either single or sequential staining protocols, as described below. In all experiments, Tcop19-SEO was used as a positive control.

## Single staining protocol
Rhopalia were removed from 16 juvenile medusae post-fixation (i.e., three animals for each novel custom-made antibody tested, plus a positive control) and the remainder of the animal was dissected into halves/quarters, with each containing two/one pedalia/pedalium with tentacles and part of the manubrium. Here, medusae were starved for 24 h prior to fixation to prevent gut contents interfering with staining. Additionally, 11 rhopalia from four adults (i.e., two rhopalia for each novel custom-made antibody tested, plus a positive control) were dissected pre-fixation from medusae.
Samples were washed with blocking buffer (0.15 M PBS with 0.1% Triton X and 0.5% bovine serum albumin) eight times, twice after 0, 5, 15, and 60 min. After the last wash, three samples were kept in blocking buffer as a secondary antibody-only control to verify that staining with the custom-made antibodies was specific. This control experiment followed the general staining protocol outlined herein, with the exception that the primary antibodies were excluded from the protocol. Primary antibodies were added to the remaining material at a concentration of 1:1000 diluted in blocking buffer (0.15 M PBS with 0.1% Triton X and 0.5% BSA) and incubated for 72 h at 5 °C in darkness, followed by a second series of washes as before. The secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor™ 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG; Invitrogen, #A11034) was added to all samples at a concentration of 1:1000 diluted in blocking buffer and incubated for 24 h at 5 °C in darkness. Finally, the material was washed with 0.15 M PBS following the schedule above and mounted on glass slides. The material was mounted either in glycerol or DAPI in glycerol (concentration 100 µg/ml) using coverslips with wax “feet,” sealed with a nail polish (except where subsequent antibody elution was performed), and stored at 5 °C in darkness until scanned within three days after preparation. The preparations were all scanned on an Eclipse Ti2 microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc.; NII) using 10×/20× dry or 40×/60× oil immersion objectives. Fluorescent labelling was recorded using a 488 nm laser and FITC filter set up, and the transmitted light imaging mode was used to obtain a conventional contrast image, with maximum projections obtained from the scans via Fiji v. 1.54f24 or NIS-Elements AR v. 5.30.02 (NII) software used in illustrations.

## Sequential staining and eluting protocol
Following initial results, it was necessary to investigate in greater detail whether staining patterns for multiple opsins overlapped in the SE. A conventional double-staining protocol could not be used for this experiment, since all available primary antibodies were raised in rabbits, likely causing secondary antibody cross-reactions. Instead, a sequential staining and eluting protocol using 2-Mercaptoethanol/SDS (2-ME/SDS) was used, which previous studies have demonstrated as an effective tool for consistent antibody elution25,26. The 2-ME/SDS protocol used in this study allows for multiple staining rounds of different primary antibodies with the same secondary antibody, and additionally minimises the likelihood of cross-reactivity without degrading antigen integrity in the tissue25,26. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of such a protocol using vibratome sections specifically with gelatin, and we note that the heat and 2-ME/SDS applied to the samples did not impact the stability of the sections.
All rhopalia from three adults were embedded in gelatin in a petri dish, using a standard protocol and with one slit eye oriented with the longitudinal axis horizontally in the top part of the prep. The sample was cooled at 4 °C for 15 min, then covered with 10% PFA and kept at 4 °C for two days to harden. The gelatin was transferred to 0.15 M PBS and was trimmed into a block, keeping the orientation of the slit eye. A vibratome (VT1000S, Leica, Germany) was used to cut two section (40 μm thick) across the transverse plane of the first slit eye. The block was turned and re-trimmed to orient the second slit eye with its longitudinal axis perpendicular to direction of sectioning and sectioned at 20 μm, resulting in a total of four sections each containing half a slit eye. In the transverse plane of the slit eye, both the vitreous cells and the retina are visible, allowing confirmation of specific labelling of the photoreceptor outer segments and no unspecific staining in the vitreous cells (Fig. 1B,C,E). Note that the pit eyes are much smaller than the slit eyes (adult SE length, 90–110 μm; SE maximum width, 30–35 μm; PE outer diameter, 25–30 μm; Fig. 1B27; thus, sectioning of the PEs was less consistent. All rhopalia from three juveniles were also sectioned as above (sections were 30 μm thick); however, the orientation varied due to the small size of the slit eye (approximate juvenile SE length, 50 μm; SE maximum width, 25 μm; Fig. 1D,E).
Immunostaining was performed as above, with modified concentrations for primary and secondary antibodies (both 1:500) in case sections contained very little of the SE and/or PE retinas. One half of each slit eye was used for each of the three opsins exhibiting staining in the SE (Tcop1, Tcop2 and Tcop19-SEO) or PE (Tcop11) in initial or prior studies20. After scanning (as above), rhopalia sections were stripped of antibodies. The 2-ME/SDS solution comprised 20 ml 10% SDS, 12.5 ml 0.5 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 67.5 ml ultra-pure water, and 800 µl 2-ME (Sigma). Samples were incubated in the 2-ME/SDS solution at 56 °C for 30 min and subsequently washed with diH20 after 4 × 15 min. Samples were then washed with blocking buffer after 0, 5, 15, and 60 min prior to a new immunostaining round with a different primary antibody.
During this experiment, three additional rhopalia were sectioned as above for control preparations. Specificity of antibody staining was verified by omission of primary antibodies as in the single staining protocol. In an initial test, two sections were used to verify the effectiveness of the antibody stripping protocol by scanning the sections before and after the antibody elution. Secondly, we tested whether cross-reactivity was likely to occur by reapplying the same primary antibody to two sections and a different primary antibody to another two sections, comparing the expression patterns between the rounds. After sequential rounds of staining and eluting were completed for all four opsins, we used a fifth staining round to verify that the staining pattern and signal intensity for a given antibody had not markedly changed compared to the first round.

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization, FISH
To visualize expression of target opsin mRNAs, we employed a Hybridization Chain Reaction—Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (HCR-FISH) protocol using a fluorescent probe-based detection system. The procedure was adapted from previously established methods28 with minor modifications, and all reagents and buffers were obtained from Molecular Instruments (Los Angeles, CA, USA). For each opsin gene, 15–30 sequences distributed across the full-length transcript were used to design antisense probes targeting distinct regions of the mRNA to ensure robust transcript detection. In total 15 rhopalia and three manubria were dissected from medusae in seawater and fixed immediately in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature with gentle agitation. Following fixation, tissues were rinsed and washed twice in 0.15 M PBS for 10 min each, before undergoing a methanol dehydration series, as follows: 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 100% (10 min each wash). Samples were placed in fresh 100% methanol and stored at − 20 °C in for 2 months, until use. After a rehydration series with methanol on ice (90%, 70%, 50%, 30%), samples were washed three times for 10 min each in 0.15 M PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma, P1379) at room temperature. Permeabilization was performed at 37 °C for 30 min in a buffer containing 1% SDS, 0.5% Tween-20, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 150 mM NaCl (pH 7.5). After permeabilization, the buffer was removed, and warm (37 °C) hybridization buffer (Molecular Instruments, HCR Buffers Pack) was added for a 30-min pre-hybridization incubation. This was followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C with warm hybridization buffer containing 15 nM of each split-initiator probe: B3-tagged Tcop1 (combination 1) or Tcop11 (combination 2), B4-tagged Tcop2 (combination 1 and 2), and B5-tagged Tcop19-SEO (combination 1) or Tcop6 (combination 2) probes (Molecular Instruments). Combination 1 was used for six rhopalia and combination 2 for six rhopalia and the three manubria. After hybridization, samples were washed four times for 15 min each at 37 °C with pre-warmed probe wash buffer, followed by two 5-minute washes at room temperature in 5× SSCT (prepared from 20× SSC concentrate (Sigma, S6639) with 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma, P1379)). Tissues were then incubated in amplification buffer (Molecular Instruments, HCR Buffers Pack) for 40 min at room temperature. Fluorophore-labelled hairpins (120 nM each; Alexa Fluor 488-B3, Alexa Fluor 647-B4, and Alexa Fluor 546-B5) were added to fresh amplification buffer, and tissues were incubated overnight at room temperature in the dark. Following amplification, samples were washed five times for 10 min in 5× SSCT and then six times for 15 min each in 0.15 M PBS, all at room temperature. Processed tissues were then counter stained with DAPI for 1 h, rinsed several times in 0.15 M PBS, and mounted on poly-L-lysine–coated slides (Sigma, P8920) using ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, 36984), and sealed with a 0.12-mm spacer and a 0.1-mm glass coverslip. Confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 900 microscope equipped with airyscan 2 and with ZEN acquisition software. Negative control experiments were performed on three rhopalia by leaving out the probes but adding the respective amplifiers.

## Phylogenetic analysis of cubozoan opsins
All T. cystophora opsins that were annotated as such were included in phylogenetic analyses19,21,22,29 (see Suppl. Mat. 2 for accession numbers), along with additional data from T. cystophora and other cubozoan species parsed from NCBI using the following searches. (1) One annotated cubozoan opsin was found via blastp searches against each of the 19 known T. cystophora opsins (Carbydea rastonii)15. (2) Additional candidate cubozoan opsin genes were identified from the TSA (Transcriptome Shotgun Assembly) database by performing tblastn searches against each T. cystophora opsin for each of the species with sequence data available, including T. cystophora in order to check for additional putative opsins for this species (Alatina alata, Chironex yamaguchii, Copula sivickisi, Morbakka virulenta)21,30,31 (Suppl. Mat. 2). (3) The WGS (Whole Genome Shotgun) archive was used in tblastn searches for cubozoan opsins as above, for every species with available whole-genome data (A. alata, Carybdea cf. marsupialis, C. yamaguchii, M. virulenta, Tamoya ohboya)30,32; see Suppl. Mat. 2 for direct NCBI submissions used). When necessary, assembly of reads was performed via MAFFT v.733 with the L-INS-i algorithm and all other parameters kept as default, using an alignment of the 19 known T. cystophora genes as a reference. From these, congruent sequences were extracted, and candidate opsin genes were verified as such in initial phylogenetic analyses with both Go-opsin sequences from13 and lophotrochozoan xenopsins as outgroups14. Those that were not resolved as within the cnidops clade were discarded, as were all partial sequences less than half the length of T. cystophora opsins. For the final analysis, only the lophotrochozoan xenopsin outgroup was used20. Duplicate sequences in TSA datasets were removed; WGS scaffolds similar (but not identical) in sequence were kept, under the assumption that these may represent gene duplications. The final database represented 210 ingroup (cubozoan) sequences.
The final alignment was trimmed to remove the N- and C-terminal sequences, according to a previous study34 (Suppl. Mat. 2). IQ-TREE was used to test for homogeneity of character composition in the alignment (Suppl. Mat. 2), with all sequences that failed the initial composition chi-square test removed. The best amino acid substitution model was LG + G4, chosen via ModelTest-NG35 under the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc). Variable and phylogenetically informative sites were identified via IQ-TREE36. The aligned and trimmed dataset was used to construct a Maximum Likelihood analysis via IQ-TREE, using the ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) feature as it obtains less biased support values than RAxML rapid bootstrap36,37 to assess the robustness of the inferred topology. Note that ultrafast bootstrap approximation implemented by IQ-TREE has a different interpretation to the standard bootstrap; only nodes with support values exceeding 95% are considered ‘highly’ supported36,37. MEGA v. 10.2.638 was used to estimate the genetic distance of each major clade, using the p-distance model and all default settings.

## Results

## Single opsin expressed in the PE
The antibodies against Tcop11 displayed a consistently bright stain in the PE retina in single-stain experiments with whole mount rhopalia in both adults and juveniles (Fig. 2A–C). The stain was homogenous and only seen in the outer segments of the PE photoreceptors (Fig. 2; see prior work27 for PE morphology). Expression of Tcop11 was also present in sequential staining of vibratome sections of the PE, albeit varying in brightness depending on how much of the PE retina was included in each section (Fig. 2D). Finally, mRNA expression of Tcop11 in the PE photoreceptors was verified by a weak stain in their cell body region using HCR-FISH (Suppl. Mat. 3). No other probes marked the PE region and all the controls were blank. See Suppl. Mat. 4 for summary of opsin expression in the rhopalia of T. cystophora.

## Multiple opsins expressed in the SE
The antibodies against opsins Tcop1 and Tcop2, as well as the positive control Tcop19-SEO, all displayed a consistently bright stain in the SE retina (Figs. 3 and 4). Results from the whole mount single-stain experiments suggested that staining occurred throughout the retina, though patterns varied somewhat between individuals (e.g. Tcop1; Fig. 3A,B). In some whole-mount preparations, it was difficult to determine with any certainty whether the retina was stained, or if the antibodies had become caught in the vitreous cells, leading to unspecific staining (Fig. 1C, Suppl. Mat. 5). However, confocal images of transversely sectioned SE in sequential staining experiments verified that staining in the SE occurred in the photoreceptor outer segments only, and not as unspecific stain in vitreous cells (Fig. 4).

## Multiple extraocular opsin expression in the manubrium
Tcop2 and Tcop11 were expressed in the manubrium as well as the photoreceptor outer segments of the SE and PE, respectively (Fig. 5A,C–E). Tcop6 was also expressed in the manubrium (Fig. 5B). The stain with the antibodies against these three opsins was restricted to the very distal part of the manubrium and here the opsins appeared to be expressed unevenly throughout the cytoplasm of several epithelial cells (Fig. 5D). Note that an artefact seen in initial results was staining of the stylets and nematocyst thread of fired nematocytes, which likely resulted from antibodies being caught in these structures (Suppl. Mat. 5). Unfortunately, the HCR-FISH staining of the manubrium had a too high noise level to be functionally interpreted.

## Phylogenetic analysis
The inclusion of opsin data from other box jellyfish taxa suggests that genes closely related to T. cystophora opsins are found throughout Cubozoa. Assuming that two opsins from the same species are functionally distinct if they are separated in the topology by the inclusion of other species, then both C. cf. marsupialis and C. yamaguchii have 34 putative opsins (22 of which were found in more than one sample of C. yamaguchii). A. alata has 29 opsins, M. virulenta and C. sivickisi have 15 opsins, and T. ohboya has five opsins (Fig. 6). Furthermore, the analysis suggests an additional four putative opsins for T. cystophora from transcriptome data, leading to a total of 23 opsins (Fig. 6).

The analysis suggests that the clade containing Tcop13-LEO within Group 2 (as defined by a previous study20 has the lowest genetic distance (p-distance = 0.08), yet the highest diversity in terms of cubozoan species (Fig. 6). This is the only opsin clade containing all representatives of cubozoan species included in this study (A. alata, C. cf. marsupialis, C. rastonii, C. yamaguchii, C. sivickisi, M. virulenta, T. ohboya, and T. cystophora) (Fig. 6). Similarly, clades containing Tcop5-GEO and Tcop9-GCO (also within Group 2) include all cubozoan representatives except C. rastonii (lacking next-generation sequencing data) and/or T. ohboya (Fig. 6). Analyses resolve clades Group 1 A + (Group 1B + Group 2) (Fig. 6), contrary to previous studies which resolved either (1) Group 1B + (Group 1 A + Group 2) or (2) Group 2 + (Group 1 A + Group 1B)13,20,22,23, suggesting further research is needed in order to resolve deep cubozoan opsin relationships; however, this is beyond the scope of this study.

## Discussion
The box jellyfish visual system has long been of interest for its unusual complexity among cnidarians, with four morphologically distinct eye types of putatively diverse visual functions6,9,39 (Fig. 1B,E). Previous opsin expression results suggest that these visual functions are underpinned by different photopigments, with one opsin found to be expressed in the photoreceptor outer segments of the lens eyes (Tcop13-LEO) and another in the photoreceptor outer segments of the SE (Tcop19-SEO)20. Our current results from the pit eyes support such a system; however, our results from the slit eyes strongly suggest this is more complex.

## Multiple opsins expressed in the SE photoreceptor outer segments
The most interesting, yet perplexing, result from this study is that Tcop1, Tcop2 along with Tcop19-SEO were consistently expressed in the SE photoreceptor outer segments (Figs. 3 and 4). As was previously described for Tcop19-SEO, the expression patterns of Tcop1 and Tcop2 are only seen in the photoreceptor outer segments and appear homogenous, unlike the striated expression patterns of Tcop5-GEO20. This strongly suggests that Tcop1, Tcop2 and Tcop19-SEO are all photopigments in the SE photoreceptors, which is an unexpected high number of opsins in a cnidarian ocellus. Even more perplexing is the fact that the staining patterns from these three antibodies varied among specimens. With immunostaining, there is always the caveat of unspecific binding of the antibodies, or of several proteins having the target sequence of amino acids. However, we find both scenarios highly unlikely to have happened here, as all labelling in each sample is restricted to the same, limited regions of the rhopalium and manubrium. Furthermore, all labelling in the rhopalium is restricted to photoreceptor outer segments - the exact area where opsins are expressed in photoreceptors. It is extremely improbable that other proteins with the same target sequence as the opsins are expressed here, supported by the fact that BLAST searches of the antibody epitopes against the available transcriptomic datasets revealed no significant similarity to any other putative proteins. The results of the whole mount HCR-FISH staining also supported the expression of two of the three opsins (Tcop1 and Tcop19-SEO) in the SE photoreceptors, but the weak stain did not allow for evaluation of whether they were expressed in different photoreceptors or coexpressed in the same cells. We interpret the weak stain as result of low opsin turnover in the photoreceptors.
It is unexpected that an ocellus like SE express multiple opsins and we believe that the most likely explanation for this is redundancy. Here we define redundancy as a functional indifference as to which of the three opsins, or combinations of opsins, are expressed in the photoreceptor outer segments. This could be the case if they have similar λ-max (peak absorption wavelength) or if the behaviours they are involved in are at least to some degree wavelength indifferent. Unfortunately, spectral characterization of the SE photoreceptors has not been possible, and the behavioural tasks of SE are still unknown. It should also be noted that the opsins might differ in other ways than their spectral sensitivity, e.g. they could have different transduction cascades, which in turn could lead to differences in temporal properties or signal amplification. Nothing is known about this in the slit eye photoreceptors, though. The fact that 1920,22, or even 23 (current study), different opsin genes are expressed in Tripedalia supports an overall redundancy, since it is hard to imagine that this jellyfish has 19 (23) different light-influenced processes, each requiring its own specific opsin. Furthermore, opsin redundancy in cubozoans in general is also indicated by our opsin phylogeny (see later for detailed discussion).
Still, there are alternative functional explanations for having multiple opsins expressed in the SE photoreceptors. These include the possibilities that they form the basis for colour vision, broadband photoreceptors40,41, or dimers42- or a combination of these. Again, even though we cannot completely rule out these alternatives, we find them all highly unlikely, since in these cases we would expect the staining pattern to be consistent and with either no overlap (colour vision) or a consistent overlap (broadband and dimer receptors) between the three antibodies. By contrast, there is substantial variation in the staining of each antibody among single-stain preparations, and preparations from sequential staining experiments display significant, albeit inconsistent, overlap in expression patterns.
One of our goals was to explore the possibility that the opsin diversity in Tripedalia is at least partially due to some of the sequences not being separate genes but instead different alleles of the same gene. This could potentially explain the expression of both Tcop2 and Tcop19-SEO in the SE photoreceptors. Although these opsins were resolved as sister opsins and thus potentially alleles in prior phylogenetic analyses20, several cubozoan species (A. alata, C. cf. marsupialis and C. yamaguchii) with full genome data available possess opsins, which are closely related to both genes, suggesting an earlier divergence in cubozoan evolutionary history (Fig. 6). Furthermore, even though they are sister opsins their sequences, e.g. the c-terminus the antibodies were raised against, vary substantially more than expected for alleles.

## Tcop11 identified as a pit eye photopigment
Our previous studies found specific opsins as photopigments in SE and in LLE and ULE, and a lack of expression of both these opsins in the PE lead to the assumption that the PE expresses its own opsin20. This is supported by the present study, where we show strong indications of Tcop11 as a photopigment of the PE photoreceptors different from the opsins in the other eye types (Fig. 2) and with the specificity of the staining supported by the HCR-FISH preparations. All tested PE preparations were labelled by the Tcop11 antibody exclusively within the photoreceptor outer segments, where the active photopigment is located. Furthermore, the mass of photoreceptor outer segments appears homogenously stained, which corroborates our previous electron microscopy work that showed all PE photoreceptors as identical in morphology27. Whether Tcop11 is the sole visual pigment in the pit eye or more are expressed as in SE, remains unknown until the expression pattern of all Tripedalia opsins are examined. One other opsin, Tcop5-GEO, has been found in the photoreceptors of the PE, SE and lens eyes; however, this opsin was only expressed in the central part of the outer segments, which was used to support the argument that this opsin functions as a photoisomerase rather than a visual pigment20. Note that in a previous study20, phylogenetic tip labels for Tcop5-GEO and Tcop19-SEO were swapped in error, which nulls the argument that Tcop5-GEO is in a different evolutionary clade to other visual T. cystophora opsins.

## Extraocular expression of opsins
Three of the five opsins tested in this study (Tcop2, Tcop6 and Tcop11) were found to be expressed outside eyes and ocelli. Interestingly, these opsins were all expressed in a high number of cell bodies situated in the same distal part of the manubrium (Fig. 5). So far, there are no records of a light-mediated response in the manubrium; however, this is suggested by our results and warrants further investigation. The distal end of the manubrium is used to pick up prey items captured by the tentacles, and the most likely hypothesis for opsin-mediated light sensitivity in this region is that it is involved in feeding behaviour, since T. cystophora are only active diurnally43. Interestingly, a previous study found particularly high expression levels of cyclic nucleotide–gated channel-like (CNG-like) genes in both the rhopalia and manubrium44, which are necessary for phototransduction. Opsins are also expressed in the manubrium of the hydromedusae Clytia hemiscpherica and Cladonema radiatum; however, here opsins are believed to be involved in gamete maturation, as the gonads develop in the manubrium18,23.Opsin expression in the manubrium is supported by the study that originally annotated Tcop2, Tcop6, and Tcop11 performing tissue specific transcriptomics. Curiously, they found it to be different opsins from those found here, and Tcop2, Tcop6, and Tcop11 they found to be expressed in the rhopalia (all three opsins) and gonads (Tcop2 only)22. Regardless of the functional explanation, it is interesting that these three opsins have very similar expression patterns in the manubrium, which adds further support to the redundancy hypothesis given the unlikelihood of extraocular photoreception involved in colour discrimination or broadband reception. Unfortunately, the HCR-FISH preparation of manubria were too noisy to validate both the coexpression in the same cells and opsin identity. Still, the specificity of the opsin antibodies was validated by HCR-FISH staining in the rhopalia. Redundancy could be further investigated by testing the expression pattern of the different opsins in other life stages of T. cystophora, since a previous transcriptomic study indicated that several of the opsins are expressed in one or a combination of larvae, metamorphosing polyps and medusae22. In addition, this could also help reveal the function of Tcop7, which according to our data seems not to be expressed in the medusae stage.

## Opsin phylogeny supports redundancy
In Cnidaria, the study of functional diversity and redundancy of opsins is particularly within box jellyfish, given the importance vision has for their ecology and the well-described visually-guided behaviours of T. cystophora. Our analyses of publicly available next-generation sequencing (NGS) data suggest two cubozoan species, (T. ohboya and C. sivickisi, with whole genome and transcriptomic data available, respectively) lack genes closely related to Tcop11, identified here as a PE photopigment for T. cystophora (Fig. 6). Although C. sivickisi and T. cystophora have opposite patterns of diurnal activity, and thus different visual behaviours, the PE and other eyes are superficially nearly identical43. It is therefore likely that the PE is functional in C. sivickisi, which must therefore use a different opsin as photopigment in PE, supporting the hypothesis of functional redundancy. Similarly, only C. yamaguchii and C. cf. marsupialis possess opsins that are closely related to the four opsins that are expressed within the SE of T. cystophora (Tcop1, Tcop2, Tcop5-GEO, and Tcop19-SEO); C. sivickisi, M. virulenta and A. alata have two or three of these opsins, but not all four.
The fact that most opsins are not found in all the five species with their whole genome sequenced, suggests several opsin loss and gain events throughout the evolutionary history of the group. Nevertheless, the opsins most tightly associated with essential visual, feeding or mating functions are the most highly conserved: Tcop13-LEO, the ‘lens eye opsin’ found in T. cystophora ULE and LLE photoreceptors, and Tcop9-GCO, the ‘gonad and cnidocyte opsin’, both from opsin Group 220. This suggests that these opsins are more tightly controlled, with fewer gene duplications maintained. We suggest that the fact that all species of cubozoans lack one or more of the other opsins indicates mutational erosion of functionally redundant gene copies. Multiple lineages have retained opsin genes in several different opsin groups (Groups 1 A, 1B, and 2), which would suggest that each group has a unique function45. However, this is not reflected in opsin expression results, which show the overlap of Tcop2, Tcop19-SEO (both Group 1B) and Tcop1 (Group 1 A) in the SE. Phylogenetic results also show that T. ohboya lacks any opsin representative within Group 1B (Figs. 4 and 6), which could mean that the specific functions supported by this opsin clade are entirely lost in this species. It should also be noted that despite the available NGS data, it is possible that not all opsin genes were recovered in our analyses.
