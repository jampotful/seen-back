Abstract
Coral studies, including interactions, coral health and reef recovery, remain poorly documented in the Galápagos Islands. This gap is assessed at four
shallow coral-patch sites around Santa Cruz Island during April-May 2025 integrating three different approaches: 1. Characterizing coral-algal
interactions, using forty randomly selected photographs showing direct coral contact with turf algae or crustose coralline algae (CCA) were classified
as: no visible impact, reduced pigmentation at the contact zone, and algal overgrowth; 2. Characterizing benthic composition, from 120
photoquadrats distributed along twelve 20 m transects; and 3. Evaluating coral health status, using the CoralWatch method for colonies intercepted
within the quadrats. Our results showed that contacts with CCA showed no visible coral tissue damage, whereas turf contacts were frequently
associated with pigmentation loss or overgrowth. Interaction severity was significantly associated with macroalgal functional group (p = 0.019) and
sampling site (p = 0.036), but not with coral species (p = 0.223). In total, 30 macroalgal taxa were recorded. Live coral cover was low at all sites (0–
14%), whereas turf algae dominated three sites, reaching up to 81% cover. Coral health was significantly associated with the abundance of both turf
algae (p < 0.001) and CCA (p = 0.011). Turf abundance was generally associated with poorer visual coral health, whereas healthier-appearing colonies
occurred more frequently where turf was absent. These findings indicate that coral-algal interactions may influence coral conditions on Santa Cruz
Island and provide a baseline for future long-term monitoring of its coral patches.

Introduction
Coral reefs are among the most biodiverse ecosystems on the planet (Galand et al. 2023; Yuan et al. 2024). They support more than 25% of marine
life, including nearly 4,000 fish species and thousands of benthic invertebrates (Spalding et al. 2001). Increasing sea surface temperature, ocean
acidification, and deoxygenation associated with climate change constitute some of the most evident pressures faced by coral reefs (Pezner et al.
2023; Sani et al. 2024). These stressors, in addition to eutrophication and the overfishing of herbivores, can cause the proliferation of macroalgae,
representing another significant threat to coral reefs (McCook et al. 2001). In extreme cases, reefs can undergo a phase shift towards macroalgal
dominance (McManus and Polsenberg 2004; Díaz-Pulido et al. 2011). The competitive mechanisms of macroalgae produce various direct or indirect
effects on coral health (Box and Mumby 2007; Vermeij et al. 2009; Manikandan et al. 2021). Among these mechanisms, allelopathic compounds
stand out, causing negative changes in the coral microbiome, reducing coral growth, and affecting its resilience to stress events (Vega Thurber et al.
2012; Bonaldo and Hay 2014).
The Galápagos Archipelago, located in the eastern equatorial Pacific Ocean approximately 1,000 km from continental Ecuador, is globally recognized
for its biodiversity and unique marine ecosystems (Edgar et al. 2010). Coral reefs on these islands have shown a marked decline since the 1982–
1983 El Niño event, which caused more than 95% coral mortality (Glynn 1994; Glynn et al. 2018; Yuval et al. 2025). In Galápagos, systematic but
historical records on macroalgal composition exist (Taylor 1945; Dawson 1963; Garske 2002; Ruiz and Ziemmeck 2014, 2016a, 2016b), recent
information is limited (Tompkins and Wolff 2017; Gabrielson et al. 2024; Dalgo et al. 2025), and comprehensive knowledge on coral-algal interactions
remains scarce (Glynn et al. 2018). Characterizing these two factors is crucial for understanding the resilience status of corals in Galápagos because
macroalgae compete with corals for available substrate (McCook et al. 2001; Barott et al. 2012b) and may outcompete them after ecological
disturbances or stress periods (Rölfer et al. 2021; Adam et al. 2022; Kopecky et al. 2025). More recent work suggests that these ecosystems are
facing a rapid expansion of invasive macroalgae such as Caulerpa chemnitzia and Caulerpa racemosa, species that have displaced coral cover in sites
such as Wellington Reef, on Darwin Island in the Galápagos Archipelago (Glynn et al. 2018; Riegl et al. 2019; Keith et al. 2022). Moreover, studies
indicate that El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events are linked to increases in algal populations, suggesting that climate change could intensify
this problem (Keith et al. 2022).
The objective of the present study is to characterize the co-occurrence between macroalgae and corals on Santa Cruz Island, Galápagos Archipelago,
through in situ observations, to assess the corals' health status when present by different algal-functional groups. Understanding coral-algal
interactions helps managers identify the drivers and consequences of shifts from coral- to algal-dominated reef states, informing interventions that
maintain coral recruitment, recovery, and reef resilience.

Materials and Methods

Study site
The study was conducted during the warm-wet season (April–May 2025) at four sites (Borrero Bay, Conway Bay, Academy Bay, and Punta Estrada)
with coral patches located on Santa Cruz Island, Galápagos (Fig. 1). This island, the second largest in the archipelago, is influenced by several ocean
currents, including the Panama Current, the Humboldt Current, the Equatorial Undercurrent or Cromwell Current, and the South Equatorial Current
(Palacios 2004; Witman et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2014). During the sampling period, NOAA Coral Reef Watch satellite data for the Galápagos regional
station indicated an average sea surface temperature (SST) ranging from 25 to 28°C (NOAA Coral Reef Watch 2019).

Benthic cover
At each study site, spatially discrete coral communities composed of colonies growing on pre-existing substrates were identified and referred to as
“coral patches”, a configuration commonly observed across the Galápagos Archipelago. These differ from structural coral reefs, which are formed by
Page 2/19

the accumulation of coral-built carbonate frameworks, with Wellington Reef at Darwin Island representing the only remaining extensive example in the
archipelago (Glynn et al. 2018; Riegl et al. 2019). The coral patches included Pavona clavus, Pavona chiriquiensis, Psammocora stellata, and
Pocillopora sp., the latter identified only to genus level due to the difficulty of species-level identification associated with the high phenotypic plasticity
of the genus (Baums et al. 2014; Paz-García et al. 2015; Glynn et al. 2018). At Punta Estrada, an additional Psammocora was recorded and retained as
Psammocora sp. because it could not be confidently assigned to a species.
Benthic cover was assessed through SCUBA diving and snorkeling at depths between 1 and 7 m, following an adapted version of the methodology
described in the Subtidal Monitoring Manual by Banks et al. (2016). Three 20 m transects per site (12 transects in total) were placed parallel to the
coastline, with 0.5 × 0.5 m quadrats (area = 0.25 m²), divided every 5 cm and placed every 2 m along each transect. This methodology is also
consistent with established coral reef monitoring approaches based on transects and quadrats (Hill and Wilkinson 2004; Kuo et al. 2022).
Photographs of 10 quadrats were recorded along each transect, resulting in a total of 120 sampled quadrats (n = 120).
All benthic organisms within the quadrats were identified. Scleractinian corals were identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level. Macroalgae
were classified into functional groups following Liao et al. (2019), treating turf algae and crustose coralline algae (CCA) as separate functional
groups. For this study, turf algae were defined as multi-specific assemblages of filamentous red, brown, and green algae forming dense mats that can
trap sediment and reach approximately 2 cm in height, excluding cyanobacteria (Stuercke and McDermid 2004; Connell et al. 2014; Longo et al. 2015;
de Bakker et al. 2017; Liao et al. 2019). Crustose coralline algae (CCA) were defined as calcifying red algae that form calcium carbonate crusts over
hard substrates (McCoy and Kamenos 2015). No other benthic organisms were recorded within the sampled quadrats. Non-biological substrates
were classified as rock, sand, or dead coral. Macroalgal richness was calculated as the total number of taxa recorded at each site.
From each quadrat, qualitative abundance categories were established for turf algae and CCA according to the number of intersection points at which
their presence was recorded (n): absent (n = 0), low (n = 1–25), moderate (n = 26–50), abundant (n = 51–75), and dominant (n > 75). These categories
allowed the relationship between coral health status and the abundance of turf algae and CCA to be analyzed (see below). Representative
photographs of the study sites and field sampling activities are shown in Online Resource 1.

Coral-algal interactions
To characterize coral-algal interactions, a total of 40 randomly selected photographs showing evidence of contact between corals and macroalgae
were used, equivalent to 10 images from each of the four sites. Corals were classified according to Wild et al. (2014) into three categories: I: no
visible impact, II: reduced pigmentation of coral tissue in the contact zone, and III: algal overgrowth (Fig. 2; Online Resource 2). In addition, coral
health was assessed in the colonies intercepted by the transect within each quadrat, using the CoralWatch Coral Health Chart methodology (Siebeck
et al. 2006). Based on the recorded color values (from 1 to 6), qualitative categories were grouped into three broader coral health categories to
facilitate ecological interpretation and statistical analysis: bleached/pale (colors 1–2), intermediate pigmentation (colors 3–4), and pigmented/nonbleached (colors 5–6). Using a probabilistic decision tree, the percentage distribution of the different coral-algal interaction categories was
represented across the four study sites: Conway Bay, Borrero Bay, Academy Bay, and Punta Estrada.

Macroalgal sample collection and preservation
Macroalgal samples were selected and collected throughout the entire coral patch for taxonomic identification, including fleshy macroalgae that were
not recorded within the sampled quadrats or in direct contact with corals. The samples were stored in sealed bags or labeled tubes. They were
transported to the laboratory, rinsed with filtered seawater to remove sediments and associated fauna. Finally, one portion was pressed onto
herbarium sheets and labeled for preservation, while the other portion was used for identification.

Taxonomic identification of macroalgae
The preserved samples were separated and photographed alongside a metric scale using a TOMLOV digital stereoscope (Shenzhen, China).
Histological sections were then prepared to observe cellular structures, using a Euromex microscope (Arnhem, The Netherlands) coupled with
AmScope software.
Identification was performed through morphological comparison with reference specimens from the Marine Invertebrate Collection of the Charles
Darwin Research Station (MCCDRS), supported by specialized taxonomic literature (Taylor 1945; Müller-Gelinek Ycaza and Salazar Orquera 1996;
South and Skelton 2000; Santelices 2004; Pardo and Solé 2007; Won et al. 2009; Kim and Boo 2012; León Álvarez et al. 2012; León Álvarez and Núñez
Reséndiz 2012; Maneveldt and Keats 2014; Koh and Kim 2018; León Álvarez et al. 2019; Micael et al. 2020; Patarra et al. 2020; Nauer et al. 2022; Brito
et al. 2024; Quiroz-González et al. 2024; Brito et al. 2025), digital reference collections (Macro-Algae Portal 2026), and online taxonomic resources
(Anderson et al. 2016). Nomenclature and taxonomic status were verified using AlgaeBase (Guiry and Guiry 2026) and the World Register of Marine
Species (WoRMS Editorial Board 2026). The occurrence of identified taxa in the Galápagos Archipelago was additionally verified using the Galapagos
Species Database (Charles Darwin Foundation n.d.-a).

Statistical analyses
Benthic cover values are presented as mean percent cover ± standard error (SE), calculated across the three transects at each sampling site (n = 3;
Online Resource 3). Coral-algae interactions were analyzed using qualitative variable methods. Fisher’s Exact Test was applied to assess
associations among the following variables: site, macroalgal type, coral species, and coral-algal interaction category (Fisher 1922; Agresti 2012).
Similarly, this test was used to evaluate associations between coral health and categorical variables, including CCA abundance, turf abundance, coral
species, and study site, as well as species-specific associations between coral health and macroalgal abundance. Macroalgal richness was evaluated
for each sampling site, and the Jaccard similarity index was used to compare macroalgal species composition among sites based on
presence/absence data (Jaccard 1901; Legendre and Legendre 2012). Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was applied, allowing associations
among variables to be identified (Greenacre 2017). Finally, probabilistic decision trees were used to visualize the relationships between coral-algal
interaction categories and the combinations of different macroalgal types and coral species. All tests were performed at a significance level of α =
0.05 using R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025).

Results

Benthic cover
Across sites, low coral cover was documented (see Table 1). In Borrero Bay, rocky substrate was most dominant (67.5 ± 2.3%), with coral cover
represented by Pocillopora sp. (12.5 ± 4.7%) and P. stellata (1.1 ± 0.1%). Turf cover was relatively low (4.7 ± 3.0%), while CCA showed minimal values
(0.0 ± 0.0%). In Conway Bay, the dominant benthos was macroalgae, turf (56.3 ± 8.2%) and CCA (4.0 ± 1.6%) (the latter colonizing dead coral
structures), and this site presented the highest coral cover among the four evaluated sites, mainly Pocillopora sp. (13.7 ± 3.9%). In Academy Bay, turf
cover was high (56.0 ± 8.1%), accompanied by a sand proportion of (36.2 ± 9.2%), with no record of bare rocky substrate and low coral cover of P.
clavus (6.0 ± 1.7%). Finally, in Punta Estrada, turf algae showed the highest cover among all sites (81.2 ± 7.8%), accompanied by low proportions of
sand (10.9 ± 3.9%) and rock (7.9 ± 4.0%), with no live corals recorded within the quadrats.

Coral health
The coral health scores recorded at the sampling sites (Table 2) showed differences in the general condition of the colonies assessed. Borrero Bay
showed the best overall coral health, with the highest median score among sites (median = 5), corresponding to healthy colonies, and color scores
ranging from 2 to 5, based on 16 evaluated colonies (predominantly Pocillopora sp.). In contrast, Academy Bay showed the poorest coral health, with
the lowest median score among sites (median = 2), corresponding to poor health, and color scores ranging from 1 to 3, based on 18 evaluated
colonies (all P. clavus). Conway Bay presented an intermediate condition, with a median score of 3, corresponding to moderate health, and color
scores ranging from 2 to 5, based on 19 evaluated colonies (all Pocillopora sp.).
The patterns summarized in Table 2 also showed that bleached/pale and intermediate colonies were mostly recorded in quadrats with moderate to
abundant turf, whereas pigmented/non-bleached colonies were mostly recorded in quadrats where turf was absent.

Coral health was significantly associated with CCA abundance (p = 0.011) and turf abundance (p < 0.001). Coral health was also significantly
associated with coral species (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001) and study site (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001). However, species-specific analyses
showed that the association between coral health and macroalgal abundance was not consistent across all coral species. P. clavus showed no
significant association between coral health and CCA abundance (p = 0.216) or turf abundance (p = 0.407). In contrast, Pocillopora sp. showed
significant associations between coral health and both CCA abundance (p = 0.012) and turf abundance (p < 0.001). For P. stellata, the association with
CCA abundance could not be tested because CCA was absent in all records, while no significant association was found with turf abundance (p =
1.000).

Coral-algal interactions
The type of macroalgae (turf vs. CCA) was found to significantly influence the severity of the interaction with corals (p = 0.019), independently of coral
species (p = 0.610). In contrast, the sampling site was determined to influence the type of coral-algal interaction (p = 0.036). There were no significant
differences among coral species with respect to their vulnerability to the established interaction categories (p = 0.223). In addition, the spatial
distribution of corals varied significantly among sites (p < 0.001); however, no spatial preference was found in the distribution of macroalgal types (p
= 1).
Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) (Fig. 3) allowed associations among coral species, macroalgal functional groups, coral-algal interaction
categories, and sampling sites. The first dimension primarily separated interactions characterized by CCA and Category I from those associated with
turf algae and Categories II and III. In addition, Conway Bay was positioned closer to CCA and Category I, whereas Academy Bay was more closely
associated with turf algae and Categories II-III. The second dimension mainly reflected differences in the distribution of coral species among
sampling sites.
At each site, the effect of the different algal functional groups on the Scleractinian coral species present was visualized using a probabilistic decision
tree (Fig. 4). The observed interactions were classified into categories I, II, and III, according to the criteria established in the methodology used,
allowing patterns to be compared among sites and related to local ecological factors.
In Conway Bay, the results showed a favorable pattern in areas dominated by CCA, where Pocillopora sp. colonies were entirely classified as Category
I (100%). However, in areas dominated by turf, Pocillopora sp. showed a more heterogeneous color distribution: 44.4% in Category I, 33.3% in Category
II, and 22.2% in Category III.
On the other hand, corals at Borrero Bay showed a more contrasting dynamic between substrates. In areas dominated by CCA, Pocillopora sp. was
entirely classified as Category I (100%), similar to what was observed in Conway Bay. However, in areas with turf, Pocillopora sp. was distributed as
25% in Category II and 75% in Category III, while P. stellata was completely represented in Category III (100%).
In Academy Bay, the results showed a more unfavorable pattern for P. clavus. In areas dominated by turf, no colonies were found in Category I, while
40% of the colonies were in Category II and 60% in Category III.
Finally, in Punta Estrada (Fig. 4), three species were evaluated: P. chiriquiensis, Psammocora sp., and P. clavus. For all three species in contact with turf
algae, 50% of the colonies were classified as Category II and the remaining 50% as Category III.
Signs consistent with trematodiasis were observed in corals from Punta Estrada. Interactions with colonial ascidians were also recorded on this
same site, with ascidians colonizing portions of coral tissue (Online Resource 1).

Discussion

Coral-macroalgal relationships at the community level
In general terms, this study revealed low live coral cover, with a predominance of turf macroalgae at most of the evaluated sites, whereas crustose
CCA represented only a minor component of the benthic community in the surveyed coral patches on Santa Cruz Island. However, determining
whether the current coral cover is naturally low or reflecting a recent decline is challenging due to the lack of site-specific baseline information. The
low coral cover observed should also be interpreted considering that some coral species in the Galápagos exhibit relatively slow growth rates; for
example, P. clavus has been reported to grow at approximately 0.80 cm year⁻¹ in Santa Cruz (Glynn et al. 2018). Nevertheless, the combination of low
live coral cover and high turf cover recorded at Punta Estrada, Conway Bay, and Academy Bay is consistent with patterns that have been associated
with transitions toward algal-dominated reef communities, suggesting a possible tendency toward a phase shift (McManus and Polsenberg 2004;
Bruno et al. 2009). However, historical baseline data and long-term monitoring are required to determine whether such a transition is occurring in
these communities.

Coral-macroalgae interaction: Turf algae versus CCA
Colonies surrounded by moderate to abundant turf generally exhibited poor health, whereas healthier (pigmented/non-bleached) colonies were more
frequently associated with quadrats where turf was absent (Table 2). Nevertheless, species-specific analyses revealed that significant associations
were only detected for Pocillopora sp., whereas no significant associations were found for P. clavus or P. stellata. The observed species-specific
differences are consistent with the context-dependent nature of coral-macroalgal interactions reported in previous studies (McCook et al. 2001; Box
and Mumby 2007; Venera-Ponton et al. 2011; Swierts and Vermeij 2016; Rölfer et al. 2021). These results should be interpreted cautiously because
coral species were not evenly distributed among the study sites. The coral P. stellata was represented by a few colonies and occurred only at Borrero
Bay, while P. clavus and Pocillopora sp. were concentrated at different sites with contrasting benthic composition. Therefore, the apparent differences
among coral species and the abundance of turf algae and CCA may partly reflect site-related variation rather than intrinsic differences in their
response to macroalgal abundance.
The macroalgal functional group, turf or CCA, significantly influenced the visual health of corals in the coral-algal interaction, independently of coral
species, indicating that the identity of the competing macroalgae was a stronger predictor of interaction outcome than the coral taxa involved. In
most cases, where the coral species P. clavus, P. chiriquiensis, Pocillopora sp., P. stellata, and Psammocora sp. were in contact with turf algae, type II or
III effects on coral tissue were observed; that is, a decrease in tissue pigmentation or algal overgrowth, respectively. This is in alignment with findings
from previous studies that mention that turf algae exert negative effects on corals, from the inhibition of photosynthesis (Vermeij et al. 2010;
Clements and Hay 2023; Altman-Kurosaki et al. 2025) to the release of dissolved organic compounds that stimulate bacterial growth at the coral-algal
interface (Haas et al. 2011; Fong et al. 2020), generating hypoxic conditions that can lead to coral tissue death (Smith et al. 2006). Likewise, it has
been demonstrated that algal turfs inhibit larval settlement and the survival of coral recruits (Birrell et al. 2008; Barott and Rohwer 2012). Previous
studies conducted on reefs of the tropical Pacific, specifically in Mo’orea, French Polynesia, have also shown that macroalgae can modify the marine
Page 8/19

chemical environment through the release of exudates and metabolites that favor the development of copiotrophic or potentially pathogenic
microbial communities (Haas et al. 2013; Nelson et al. 2013; Pozas-Schacre et al. 2025), which could negatively affect coral health. While this study
cannot ascertain the cause of the negative interaction, it supports prior work that the presence of turf algae caused visual health impacts on corals.
In contrast, in all cases in which corals interacted with CCA, this interaction was category I. This agrees with prior studies showing that CCA generally
have neutral or even beneficial interactions with corals, producing considerably weaker competitive effects than turf algae and lacking many of the
negative associations that can cause coral stress, such as hypoxia, allelopathy, and the proliferation of harmful microbial communities (Barott et al.
2012a; Barott and Rohwer 2012; Wangpraseurt et al. 2012). Moreover, CCA are widely recognized as key facilitators of coral recruitment by emitting
chemical cues that induce larval settlement and metamorphosis (Heyward and Negri 1999; Harrington et al. 2004; Whitman et al. 2020; Jorissen et al.
2021; Lei et al. 2021), as well as by producing metabolites and harboring associated microbial communities that create a favorable
microenvironment, thereby enhancing coral settlement and early post-settlement survival (Gómez-Lemos et al. 2018).

Coral health across sites
Coral health varied among the surveyed sites, with corals appearing visually healthier in Borrero Bay, intermediate in Conway Bay, and the poorest in
Academy Bay. The unhealthier (bleached/pale) condition observed in Academy Bay may partly reflect its greater exposure to anthropogenic pressure
compared with the other sites. This urbanized coastal sector of Puerto Ayora has experienced water-quality degradation associated with dense
human settlement, insufficient wastewater treatment, population growth, tourism development, and vessel-related contamination (Mateus et al. 2019;
Vega-Mariño et al. 2024), all of which may threaten the persistence and recovery of the Galápagos coral communities (Glynn et al. 2018).

Taxonomy and competition mechanisms of macroalgae
A total of 30 macroalgal taxa were identified within the coral patches of Santa Cruz Island, either forming part of the turf or as individual macroalgae.
All macroalgae identified to species level are reported as native to the Galápagos Islands. In cases where identification only reached the genus level,
these have been reported for the islands.
Among the taxa recorded within turf assemblages, several genera have been associated with mechanisms that may negatively affect corals, either
through the collective physical properties of the turf or through species-specific chemical effects. Dense turf mats can overgrow or shade coral
tissue, cause abrasion, and trap sediments, thereby reducing the availability of suitable substrate for coral recruitment (Leong et al. 2018; GómezCubillos et al. 2020). In experimental conditions, the presence of Ulva fasciata increased the mortality of Montipora capitata planulae, effect that was
substantially reduced by antibiotics, suggesting that algal exudates may affect coral larvae indirectly by stimulating microbial activity or increasing
their susceptibility to microbial infection (Vermeij et al. 2009). Crude extracts of Bryopsis sp. also caused concentration-dependent mortality in
Pocillopora acuta larvae, providing evidence of allelopathic compounds within this genus, although the substances responsible were not identified
(Fong et al. 2019). Similarly, Pterocladiella caerulescens reduced the survival of M. capitata planulae, and no larvae settled in its presence, indicating
that species within this genus may interfere with coral recruitment (Vermeij et al. 2009). Nevertheless, these effects are not consistent across all turfforming algae; for example, extracts of Hypnea pannosa produced no detectable effect on the mortality or settlement of P. acuta larvae (Fong et al.
2019).
Other turf-associated genera recorded in this study may compete through their growth form rather than through demonstrated chemical effects.
Stoloniferous species of Herposiphonia and Polysiphonia growing at coral–algal boundaries have been shown to modify their morphology and
increase the formation of new ramets, responses that may facilitate the persistence of turf mats and the colonization of available substrate adjacent
to living coral tissue (Cetz-Navarro et al. 2015). However, because the species examined in that study differ from those recorded in Santa Cruz, these
mechanisms should be considered potential genus-level analogues rather than effects demonstrated for the local taxa.
Among the macroalgal taxa recorded, Porolithon sp. was the only CCA alga identified in all sites except for Punta Estrada, where no CCA was found.
Its occurrence may be favorable to corals, as species within this genus can promote early stages of coral recruitment (Whitman et al. 2020). For
example, Yang et al. (2021) found that Porolithon onkodes significantly enhanced settlement and metamorphosis in Pocillopora damicornis larvae,
with these responses being strongly associated with the composition of the algal-associated bacterial community. Nevertheless, the specimens
examined in the present study cannot be confirmed as P. onkodes because they were identified only to the genus level, although this is the only
species of Porolithon currently listed for the Galápagos Islands in the Galápagos Species Database (Charles Darwin Foundation n.d.-b).
Historical information on macroalgal composition across the Galápagos Archipelago remains limited and spatially uneven, restricting robust
assessments of long-term changes among islands and sites (Tompkins and Wolff 2017; Glynn et al. 2018). For Santa Cruz Island, most historical
observations relevant to the present study come from Academy Bay, where Kendrick (1988), comparing observations from 1987 with those of
Wellington (1975), reported a shift from bladed and branching algae to filamentous and crustose forms after the 1982–1983 ENSO event. This
pattern was subsequently described as a long-term shift toward filamentous and crustose forms (Tompkins and Wolff 2017). Several filamentous
genera reported after this shift, including Cladophora, Chaetomorpha, Ceramium, and Polysiphonia, were also recorded in the present study. However,
comparisons should be made cautiously because the historical surveys focused mainly on intertidal communities, whereas the present study
examined shallow subtidal coral patches.

Site macroalgae richness
Page 9/19

The spatial variation in macroalgal richness indicates heterogeneous environmental conditions among sites. Conway Bay exhibited the highest
richness (Table 3), possibly associated with the greater habitat heterogeneity and availability of microhabitats and refuges observed at this site. In
contrast, Punta Estrada presented the lowest richness, which may be explained by reduced water visibility and strong wave action observed during
the field survey, which promotes sediment resuspension. The resulting reduction and alteration of underwater light could affect macroalgal
productivity and potentially influence assemblage composition (Tait et al. 2014). These conditions may also partly explain the absence of live coral
within the surveyed quadrats, as reduced light can impair the photosynthetic performance of coral symbionts and contribute to bleaching or mortality
(Erftemeijer et al. 2012; Bessell-Browne et al. 2017), while sediment deposition may smother coral tissue and limit recruitment (Erftemeijer et al.
2012).
Future studies should establish long-term monitoring of benthic communities around Santa Cruz Island and include sampling during the cold-dry
season, which was not covered in the present study. This would allow changes in coral cover and health, as well as macroalgal cover and community
composition, to be detected over time. Future assessments should also incorporate key environmental variables, including nutrients, temperature,
herbivory, and sedimentation, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing coral-algal interactions. Molecular tools
should be included to improve macroalgal taxonomic identification and support a more accurate interpretation of the ecological roles of the identified
taxa in Galápagos coral communities. This monitoring should eventually be extended to other islands in the Galápagos Archipelago, helping to
distinguish seasonal variability from persistent ecological changes and assess spatial differences among locations.

Conclusion
On Santa Cruz Island, Galápagos Archipelago, coral-algal interaction outcomes differed between algal functional groups. Higher turf abundance was
associated with poorer coral health, while lower turf abundance was associated with healthier coral conditions. Different biological or
physicochemical factors not considered in this study could influence these interactions and should be considered in future studies. These findings
provide a baseline for assessing future changes in coral-algal interactions and coral health on Santa Cruz Island.

Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) shows the relationships among coral-macroalgal interaction categories, coral species, macroalgal
