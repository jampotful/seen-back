An ancient apical patterning system sets the position of the forebrain in chordates.
Gattoni G, Keitley D, Sawle A, Benito-Gutiérrez E. (2025). Science advances. . doi:10.1126/sciadv.adq4731. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# An ancient apical patterning system sets the position of the forebrain in chordates

## Abstract
AbstractThe evolutionary origin of the vertebrate brain remains a major subject of debate, as its development from a dorsal tubular neuroepithelium is unique to chordates. To shed light on the evolutionary emergence of the vertebrate brain, we compared anterior neuroectoderm development across deuterostome species, using available single-cell datasets from sea urchin, amphioxus, and zebrafish embryos. We identified a conserved gene co-expression module, comparable to the anterior gene regulatory network (aGRN) controlling apical organ development in ambulacrarians, and spatially mapped it by multiplexed in situ hybridization to the developing retina and hypothalamus of chordates. Using functional approaches, we show Wnt signaling regulating this co-expression module in amphioxus, like the aGRN in echinoderms, and that its overactivation suppresses forebrain identity. This suggests a previously undescribed role for Wnt signaling in amphioxus in determining the position of the forebrain. We propose this Wnt-regulated gene co-expression module as a possible mechanism by which the brain set antero-dorsally early in chordate evolution.

## INTRODUCTION
The vertebrate brain is one of the most complex structures in nature. Despite considerable advances in our understanding of brain anatomy and physiology, its evolutionary origin remains a major subject of debate. While complex brains have been described in protostome (annelids, arthropods, and mollusks) and deuterostome (chordates) taxa, they display substantial differences in architecture and development (1). Such differences sustain the debate regarding the evolutionary origins of these intricate systems. One prevailing hypothesis suggests a common ancestry for the brains of protostomes and deuterostomes within the bilaterian lineage, while other theories propose independent evolutionary pathways for these distinct groups (1–4). Comparing the central nervous system (CNS) of these distantly related groups has been particularly challenging as chordates are the only deuterostome group having a brain (4, 5). The other two deuterostome phyla, echinoderms and hemichordates (together forming the taxon Ambulacraria), instead lack a brain: Adult hemichordates have a diffuse nerve net with dorsal and ventral nerve cords, while echinoderms have pentaradial nerve cords linked by a nerve ring (6–8).
In contrast with morphological studies highlighting the differences of neural organization across phyla, developmental and molecular studies revealed that the patterning of the most anterior portion of the nervous system [here defined as anterior neuroectoderm (ANE)] shows remarkable similarities across animals (9–12). In most invertebrate deuterostomes, protostomes, and cnidarians studied to date, the formation of the anterior nervous system requires early, mutually repressive interactions between Wnt/β-catenin signaling, emanating from the future blastoporal side of the embryo (vegetal pole in Bilateria and oral pole in Cnidaria), and the transcription factors Six3/6 and FoxQ2 on the opposite side (animal pole in Bilateria and aboral pole in Cnidaria), in a domain previously defined as apical plate (9, 13–17). Knockdown experiments and pharmacological treatments in cnidarian, protostome, and ambulacrarian embryos have demonstrated that Wnt overactivation results in a loss of Six3/6 and FoxQ2 expression from the aboral/animal pole, followed by the loss of the ANE (13, 17–20). Conversely, Wnt inhibition leads to an expansion of the Six3/6 and FoxQ2 domain across the ectoderm, with a consequent expansion of the ANE (19, 21–25). These data support a model of apical/anterior patterning in which Six3/6 represses Wnt expression at the animal/aboral pole and acts together with FoxQ2 to specify the apical/aboral region (26, 27). After these broad domains have been established during cleavage, in ambulacrarians, noncanonical Wnt pathways progressively restrict Six3/6 and FoxQ2 anteriorly toward the animal pole via interactions with the Wnt receptor Frz5/8 (17, 22). At the animal pole, Wnt inhibitors of the sFRP and Dkk families prevent Six3/6 and FoxQ2 down-regulation by Wnt (17, 19, 28, 29). These interactions result in the correct positioning and temporal synchronization of a conserved set of downstream transcription factors that are crucial for ANE development (11, 30, 31).
Despite direct interactions between the co-expressed genes have been resolved in detail only in ambulacrarians, these results have recently led to the suggestion that a conserved anterior gene regulatory network (aGRN) specifying ANE identity might have appeared early during nervous system evolution, with the ancestor of Eumetazoa (Cnidaria + Bilateria) (11, 32, 33). Extensive data on ANE development from more taxa are needed to test this hypothesis and to define the composition of the aGRN. Moreover, these important discoveries leave open the question of how the complex and diverse neural architectures of metazoans evolved starting from this common patterning system, including the chordate brain if the aGRN is conserved in their lineage. While the adult nervous systems of eumetazoans are highly diverse, the larval ANE in several invertebrate lineages, including ambulacrarian deuterostomes, spiralian protostomes, and cnidarians, is characterized by the presence of an apical organ (AO). The AO is a sensory-neurosecretory structure that forms from the anteriormost part of the ectoderm in all these phyla, defined by the presence of serotonergic neurons and of a shared set of aGRN transcription factors expressed during AO development (13, 32, 34, 35). Among deuterostomes, both echinoderms and hemichordates have dipleurula-type larvae whose nervous system is characterized by an anterior serotonergic AO that is thought to control larval activity (although functional validation remains scarce), as well as neurons associated with the motile ciliary bands (35, 36). Because of the similarity in cell-type repertoire and molecular signature across distantly related taxa, AOs specified by an aGRN have been proposed as a conserved feature of Eumetazoan nervous systems (13, 30, 37).
AOs have also featured prominently in scenarios attempting to understand the evolutionary origin of the chordate CNS, ever since Walter Garstang proposed the AO and ciliary band of dipleurula larvae as the forerunners of the chordate nerve cord in his “Auricularia hypothesis” (38). This hypothesis was incompatible with Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire’s proposal of a dorsal-ventral inversion in vertebrates compared to protostomes, which gained experimental support from the work of Holley and collaborators, showing a conserved role of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)/Chordin axis in the specification of the CNS on opposite sides of the body in frogs and flies (39). On the basis of this evidence, Nielsen and Lacalli proposed alternative scenarios, adapting Garstang’s idea on the formation of the neural tube through fusion of the ciliary bands, but these hypotheses were divided on whether this fusion should be inclusive of the AO (40, 41). Lacalli reasoned that serotonergic cells are a hallmark of AOs, as we explained above, and a remarkable feature of the amphioxus frontal eye complex (42), suggesting that the AO might have been incorporated into the frontal eye complex of amphioxus and that, by extension, cells of the neural retina in vertebrates might be derivatives of the “primitive” AO. However, there is at present no clear molecular or developmental data across chordates directly confirming this scenario, and data coming from hemichordates and lophotrochozoans challenges the ciliary band fusion hypothesis and the concept of a centralized nervous system in the urbilaterian (7, 12, 43).
Furthermore, an important detail of the seminal work by Holley et al. (39) regarding the molecular support for the dorsal-ventral inversion scenario is that ectopically dorsalized neural axes in frogs (through sog injection) are devoid of head structures anterior to the hindbrain, suggesting that the specification of the anterior brain might depend on a different mechanism and that the dorsal-ventral inversion might have taken place in a step-wise manner. Additional evidence for this comes from the pattern of expression of Six3 in vertebrates. While it has been shown that mutually repressive interactions between Six3 and Wnt are also key to establish the identity of the “secondary prosencephalon” (telencephalon + retina + hypoyhalamus) in the forebrain (44), there are two key differences in this pathway between vertebrates and invertebrates: (i) Six3 expression in vertebrates is not influenced by FoxQ2, which has been lost in placental mammals (45); and (ii) Six3 in vertebrates is expressed after the start of gastrulation in cells already committed to a neuronal fate (46, 47), meaning there is an upstream mechanism that specifies this anterior territory as neurectoderm before Six3 is expressed. Although it is generally assumed that anterior ectodermal cells will acquire a neural identity following the specification of the ANE/ANB (anterior neural ectoderm border) in vertebrates, little is known about the mechanism that transforms these anterior ectodermal cells into neurectoderm and whether Six3 is involved in this process (48).
Amphioxus is the only chordate for which there is evidence of an anterior domain expressing Six3/6 and FoxQ2 early in gastrulation (49, 50). The domain occupied by FoxQ2 has been shown to be under the influence of Wnt signaling (51). However, a comprehensive investigation aimed at elucidating whether this represents an apical patterning system, like that described in cnidarian, protostome, and ambulacrarian embryos (13, 17–20), or relates to the vertebrate ANE is missing in amphioxus. Here, we address this question by analyzing the co-expression of genes in amphioxus that we identify as key across species for the specification of the ANE. We use these genes as a readout in pharmacological treatments paralleling those previously used to characterize the above-described aGRN in ambulacrarians (19, 22) and find that there is an apical patterning system in amphioxus with mixed invertebrate (AO) and vertebrate (ANE) characteristics.
We show this through comparative analysis of publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from sea urchin (52) and zebrafish (53) embryos, resulting in the identification of a conserved set of genes that are differentially expressed in specific cells belonging to the sea urchin AO and the zebrafish hypothalamus and retina. By combining single-cell transcriptomics, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and multiplexed in situ hybridization, we further find that, in amphioxus, these genes act coordinately to specify the ANE early in embryogenesis, in a manner similar to that shown for the sea urchin AO. Later in development, the same set of genes acts coordinately to specify retinal and hypothalamic cell types in amphioxus, as we predicted using single-cell transcriptomics data of the zebrafish forebrain. Using a functional approach, we discover in addition that the amphioxus ANE is controlled by mutually repressive interactions between vegetal Wnt/β-catenin signaling and anterior/animal Six3/6 and FoxQ2, indicating the conservation of an apical patterning system similar to the one driven by the ambulacrarian aGRN in chordates. When these mutually repressive interactions are inhibited in amphioxus, aGRN genes fail to be positioned in the anterior-dorsal side of the embryo, leading to the specific loss of forebrain identity but not of neural tissue. Together, our study offers the previously lacking experimental evidence that Wnt inhibition is crucial at the ANB for positioning the forebrain in basally branching chordates. In this regard, we propose a plausible mechanism through which the chordate forebrain came to be positioned at the antero-dorsal end of the body during evolution.

## RESULTS

## The AO in sea urchins is specified through biphasic co-expression of genes that, in zebrafish, operate collectively during the development of the hypothalamus
The animal pole of several invertebrate embryos is set by an aGRN directing the formation of the ANE (11, 33). This network has been extensively characterized in sea urchin development, with several studies using in situ hybridization, RNA-seq, and/or scRNA-seq to investigate the expression of aGRN genes and their function in the specification of the sea urchin larval AO (11, 17, 23, 29, 54–56). While vertebrates also have an ANE, they lack AOs. Instead, ANE precursors contribute to the development of the forebrain (11, 57). Given their ANE origin, we first investigated at the single-cell level whether a common set of genes is involved in the development of the ANE across deuterostomes. To this end, we comparatively analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from the sea urchin, (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, Echinodermata), where the ANE forms the AO (52), and zebrafish (Danio rerio, Chordata), where the ANE contributes to the development of the forebrain (53).
We started by analyzing a recently published scRNA-seq dataset of sea urchin development, with a particular focus on the ANE and the cell types that are derived from it. We re-annotated three of the clusters in the dataset as “AO” based on the ectodermal expression of 11 previously characterized marker genes (Fig. 1A; see Materials and Methods) (11). Hereafter, we define this set of genes as “aGRN genes.” When examining the expression patterns in this cluster across development, we noted that the expression of marker genes was associated with a clear temporal sequence of gene activation. Further computational analysis based on these marker genes divided the AO population into two groups, corresponding to early and late phases of apical plate development (Fig. 1A and fig. S1, A to C). The early phase is characterized by the expression of FoxQ2, Six3/6, Frz5/8, sFRP1/2/5, Dkk3, and Otx, while, in the late phase, Dkk1, Fezf, Rx, Nk2.1, and Lhx2/9 are activated (fig. S1C; see Supplementary Text). Within the AO megacluster (blue + green + yellow in Fig. 1Ai), cells follow a trajectory of maturation that recapitulates the biphasic pattern described above and can be divided into three groups, representing early and late phases of specification, and a phase of terminal differentiation (Fig. 1Aii). Cells that we define as having an “early apical plate” (EAP) identity are predominantly found in embryos between the morula and hatched blastula (B) stages and express early-phase genes. The cells that have a “late apical plate” (LAP) identity belong mostly to embryos between hatched B and late gastrula stages and add the expression of late phase markers to the EAP profile (Fig. 1A, i and ii, and fig. S1, B and C). Last, within the LAP cluster, we identify serotonergic neurons of the AO as a group of cells that start expressing Tph from the late gastrula stage (fig. S1, C and D). This cluster of serotonergic neurons is also marked by strong Fezf and Lhx2/9 expression (Fig. 1Aii) but has Six3/6 switched off and low levels of most early genes. To assess whether the 11 genes highlighted above are specific markers of EAP and LAP clusters, we performed differential gene expression (DGE) analyses between the ectoderm and EAP or LAP clusters. This revealed that, with the exception of Otx (in EAP) and Dkk1 (in LAP), the abovementioned early and late phase genes are the top differentially expressed markers in EAP and LAP cells respectively, further supporting their key role in AO development (fig. S2A; see Supplementary Text).

Next, we examined the expression patterns of forebrain cells during zebrafish development, using a time-course scRNA-seq dataset generated between 4 and 24 hours postfertilization (hpf) (53). Focusing on the set of 11 markers discussed above, we found that, with the exception of FoxQ2, at least one paralog for each orthologous gene (of Six3/6, Frz5/8, sFRP1/2/5, Fezf, Rx, Nkx2.1, Otx, and Lhx2/9) is spatially restricted to the forebrain/optic region of the force-directed graph embedding annotated in the original publication (Fig. 1B). To gain a better insight on the identity of these cells within the forebrain cluster, we used definitions given by the prosomeric model, which considers the hypothalamus as part of the secondary prosencephalon and separated from the diencephalon (58). In the original study, Wagner et al. (53) annotate telencephalon and diencephalon subclusters within the forebrain/optic region; however, no distinction was made between the hypothalamus and diencephalon. Therefore, we isolated the cells originally annotated as “diencephalon” and used the expression of key transcription factors to computationally divide them into hypothalamic and diencephalic populations (fig. S1, E and F; see Materials and Methods). We then plotted our set of aGRN transcription factors across the newly annotated forebrain dataset (Fig. 1C). This revealed that an aGRN signature is present within the hypothalamus and, to a lesser extent, the retina and telencephalon, while it is absent from the posterior diencephalon and the floor plate (Fig. 1Cii). To understand how specific this signature is in the zebrafish brain, we performed DGE analyses on ectodermally derived tissues and across brain regions belonging to the secondary prosencephalon. We found that, with the exception of the Otx and Dkk families, most of the orthologous genes identified in our scRNA-seq analysis are among the most differentially expressed in the hypothalamus, supporting their key role during the specification of this part of the zebrafish brain (fig. S2, B and Cii; see Supplementary Text).
Last, to strengthen our cross-species analysis, we used OrthoFinder (59) to identify any additional orthologous genes up-regulated in both the sea urchin apical plate and the zebrafish hypothalamus (see fig. S2 and Materials and Methods). Most of the aGRN markers, particularly those belonging to the LAP phase (sFRP1/2/5a, Nk2.1, Rx, Fezf, and Lhx2/9), appear among the top differentially expressed genes in both species, together with two additional genes: the retinoic acid degrader Cyp26b1 and the Fgf receptor Fgfrl1, again conserved in the sea urchin late apical plate (AP) and zebrafish hypothalamus (fig. S2C; see Supplementary Text). In sum, we find that the same co-expressing module, inclusive of aGRN genes active in the sea urchin apical plate that were previously shown to specify the AO in this species, is also present in cells fated to develop as the retino-hypothalamic region of the zebrafish forebrain.

## The aGRN in amphioxus involves the expression of the conserved set of genes specifying the sea urchin AO and the zebrafish hypothalamus
Having identified a co-expression module involved in the specification of AO and retino-hypothalamic cells in the sea urchin and zebrafish, respectively, we set out to characterize the expression patterns of the corresponding orthologous genes in amphioxus during development. Here, again, we first took a single-cell transcriptomics approach to pinpoint cells displaying similar expression profiles in a recently published single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset of Branchiostoma floridae development (Fig. 1D and fig. S3) (60). Given that sea urchin, zebrafish, and amphioxus exhibit stark differences in developmental timing, we profiled all genes across all time points sampled to unbiasedly identify those stages in which aGRN genes are expressed. By looking at the dynamics of expression in ectodermal cells, we detected a clear biphasic separation in amphioxus, similar to that observed in the sea urchin: with EAP genes such as FoxQ2, Six3/6, Frz5/8, and sFRP1/2/5 expressed early in amphioxus embryogenesis and LAP genes such as Fezf, Rx, Nk2.1, and Lhx2/9 expressed only at the beginning of neurulation. The same analysis in zebrafish showed that the sequential activation of this co-expression module occurs rapidly, after the formation of the neural plate (Fig. 1D), without the phased dynamics observed in the sea urchin and amphioxus. DGE analyses using publicly available bulk RNA-seq dataset across 14 developmental stages from Branchiostoma lanceolatum (61) further confirmed the phased sequential activation of the genes forming part of the co-expression module in amphioxus (fig. S4; see Supplementary Text).
Although single-cell transcriptomics is a powerful approach to resolve shared expression patterns among species, it does not offer insights into the precise location within the embryo where specific gene sets are co-expressed. This was key here to establish whether the aGRN genes in amphioxus are also spatially confined anteriorly/apically as observed in the sea urchin. To this aim, we spatially resolved the co-expression of the core aGRN genes at the developmental stages identified in our analysis by in situ hybridization using the chain reaction (HCR) approach, which allows the simultaneous detection of multiple genes per embryo (see fig. S5 and Supplementary Text for a detailed description of the developmental expression of each of the 24 genes considered) (62, 63). As predicted by our RNA-seq analysis (fig. S4) FoxQ2 and Wnt8 were the first genes to be expressed from the B stage to the mid-neurula stage. We co-profiled these two genes together with the broad early neural marker Neurogenin (Ngn) to control for neuronal tissue specification (Fig. 2A). FoxQ2 is initially expressed in the entire animal half of the embryo at the B stage (Fig. 2A and fig. S5). These FoxQ2-positive cells were bordered at the equator of the embryos by weak nuclear Wnt8. This complementary pattern persisted to the G3 stage (early gastrula), when the first Ngn-positive cells appeared in the dorsal neural tube within the FoxQ2 domain (Fig. 2A). During late gastrulation and neurulation, FoxQ2 restricts toward the anterior side of the embryo and, over time, disappears from the ventral side, while Wnt8 restricts to the lateral and ventral mesendoderm. On the dorsal side, FoxQ2 progressively restricts toward the tip of the neural tube, such that, by the 7-somite stage (7ss), it does not co-localize with Ngn and is only expressed in the anterior tip of the ectoderm (Fig. 2A and fig. S5). Both the relative volume and relative number of FoxQ2+ cells markedly decrease during this time (fig. S5), demonstrating that the initial expression of FoxQ2 across the animal half of the blastula and its restriction during gastrulation resemble the establishment of animal and vegetal fields and the early apical patterning of ambulacrarian larvae.

We next searched for EAP identities in amphioxus embryos by co-profiling the expression of four genes acting in the early phase of ANE specification in sea urchin (FoxQ2, Six3/6, Frz5/8, and sFRP1/2/5a) (Fig. 2B; see Supplementary Text for description of gene expression). All genes were expressed in the anterior-dorsal ectoderm at late gastrula (G4) stage. While FoxQ2, Six3/6, and Frz5/8 were co-expressed in a broad antero-dorsal ectodermal area, sFRP1/2/5a appeared dorsally restricted within this co-expression domain. During the first phases of neurulation, Six3/6 and Frz5/8 gradually restrict their domains of expression following a similar trend to that observed for FoxQ2, with sFRP1/2/5a remaining expressed at the boundary between the anterior neural tube and nonneural ectoderm (Fig. 2B). Unlike FoxQ2, however, Six3/6 and Frz5/8 transcripts are still located in the nervous system, and, at the 7ss, Six3/6 expression is divided into anterior and posterior domains separated by an intercalated Six3/6-negative region, as previously described (64). At the stages considered above, Dkk3 is also expressed in the anterior ectoderm, similar to sFRP1/2/5a (fig. S6). This reveals a timing and position congruent with a role of sFRP1/2/5a and Dkk3 in blocking Wnt signaling anteriorly/apically, as described in echinoderms (17, 65).
A distinctive feature of AO patterning in echinoderms is that the initial phase of specification, defined above by the expression of genes such as Six3/6, FoxQ2, and Frz5/8, occurs in progenitor cells that will give rise to both neural and nonneural ectoderm. It is only during the late phase that a subset of these ectodermal progenitors becomes specified as AO neurons. This contrasts with vertebrates, where we first detected the aGRN signature when the distinction between nonneural and neural ectoderm has been already made (Fig. 1). To investigate this in amphioxus, we co-profiled the amphioxus Six3/6 with the pan-neural marker Elav and the ciliated ectodermal cell marker FoxJ, which, during early neurulation, label neural and nonneural ectoderm, respectively (Fig. 2C). At these stages, we find that Six3/6 expressed both in anterior-dorsal Elav-positive cells and in the anteriorly abutting FoxJ-positive cells. As development progresses and the neural tube becomes morphologically apparent, Six3/6 restricts anteriorly but continues to be expressed in both neural and nonneural anterior ectoderm (fig. S6). Together, our results indicate that apical patterning in amphioxus is characterized by an initial phase of specification acting on anterior ectodermal cells, including those fated to neuronal and nonneural identities, and before any neuronal differentiation is observed. This strongly resembles the condition in ambulacrarians, suggesting that the early phase of apical plate and ANE specification entails a spatial restriction of aGRN genes in basally branching chordates as well.

## Late aGRN genes are expressed in the amphioxus brain
We next focused on identifying LAP identities in amphioxus embryos by co-profiling the expression of genes active in the late phase of AO specification in sea urchin (Fezf, Otx, Rx, Nk2.1, and Lhx2/9) along with Six3/6, which is active throughout the two phases (Fig. 3). Given the strong aGRN signal detected from the zebrafish hypothalamus, we included amphioxus embryos at later developmental stages, when the cerebral vesicle begins to regionalize. In general, during neurulation, LAP markers progressively appear in the anterior-dorsal ectoderm, within the region where EAP genes are expressed. Consequently, by the 12ss, all these markers are expressed in the amphioxus cerebral vesicle (Fig. 3, A and B). Fezf and Otx, for example, are localized in the anterior tip of the neural plate starting from the initiation of neurulation (N0 stage), while Rx, Nk2.1, Lhx2/9, and Dkk1 appear in the anterior neural plate by the 7ss (Fig. 3, A and B). By the end of neurulation, the expression of these markers becomes confined to individual cell types in both the anterior Six3/6-positive and the intercalated Six3/6-negative regions of the larval cerebral vesicle. In summary, our results show that genes acting in the late phase of AO specification in echinoderms are primarily expressed in the anterior ectoderm and the early developing brain in amphioxus.

A well-known feature of AOs in marine invertebrate larvae is the presence of serotonergic neurons. It has long been known that the frontal eye complex in amphioxus is equipped with serotonergic neurons (66, 67), but little is known about the molecular profile of these cells. We detected SerT expression in amphioxus at the 14ss together with two LAP genes that are present in sea urchin AO serotonergic neurons, Fezf and Lhx2/9 (see Fig. 1A), as well as other two aGRN markers Six3/6 and Otx (Fig. 3C). We found that serotonergic neurons in the amphioxus frontal eye complex are characterized by strong Fezf and weak Lhx2/9 expression, as in sea urchin, but are devoid of Otx as previously suggested (67) and rarely contain Six3/6 transcripts in their soma (Fig. 3C). Together, we show that amphioxus LAP genes are activated within the domain of EAP genes and follow a similar temporal sequence as in the sea urchin, but, in addition, they contribute to define the increasingly diverse cell-type repertoire of the larval brain.

## The specification of the amphioxus ANE is dependent on Wnt signaling
Several studies have demonstrated that the specification of the AO of invertebrate larvae and the forebrain of vertebrate embryos is regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin signaling (11, 13, 68). Whether this means that there is a common underlying mechanism is still an open question. In cnidarian, protostome, and deuterostome larvae, the expression of Six3/6 and FoxQ2 is negatively regulated by members of the Wnt family, particularly Wnt1 and Wnt8, such that ectopic activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling results in the loss of aGRN genes (13, 17–19, 21). In vertebrates, Six3 is essential to keep the prospective forebrain region free from inhibitory Wnt signaling, and ectopic activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling results in the loss of forebrain structures (44). Given this, we sought to investigate whether ANE development in amphioxus is also under the control of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. To this aim, we treated embryos with Azakenpaullone (AZA), a strong GSK-3 inhibitor commonly used to overactivate Wnt/β-catenin signaling (69). Developing embryos were treated with either 10 μM of AZA or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a control at two developmental time points: from the B stage (4.5 hpf) to the early N0 stage (12 hpf) and from the late gastrula (G5) until the 7ss (Fig. 4A).

Using RNA-seq, we first profiled all genes that we found as part of the amphioxus ANE signature (Fig. 4B). Our results show that, in early-treated embryos, all aGRN genes are markedly down-regulated after AZA treatment, except for sFRP1/2/5a, which appears up-regulated in response to the treatment (Fig. 4Bi). We next investigated the context in which these changes occurred by specifically examining the expression of neural, ectodermal, and mesodermal genes. We observed a down-regulation of early pan-neural markers upon Wnt overactivation, consistent with published in situ hybridization results (70, 71), while mesodermal markers were up-regulated (Fig. 4Bi and fig. S7A). Furthermore, genes marking the nonneural ectoderm at this stage decrease significantly in expression in treated embryos, whereas orthologs related to established vertebrate stem cells markers were up-regulated. These observations suggest that the neural tissue loss may not be linked to a failure of ectoderm progenitors transitioning specifically into neuroectoderm but may be attributed to the ectoderm persisting in an undifferentiated state in the treated embryos (fig. S7A). Supporting this notion, immunofluorescence for acetylated tubulin shows the absence of ectodermal cilia in treated embryos at the early neurula stage (fig. S7B). In late AZA treatments, most ANE genes were strongly down-regulated, akin to what was observed in early-treated embryos (Fig. 4Bii). The exception was again sFRP1/2/5a, accompanied this time by Otx. However, unlike the early AZA treatment, late-treated embryos did not show a significant decrease in neural or epidermal markers; nevertheless, mesodermal genes continued to show increased expression levels (Fig. 4Bii and fig. S7A). Accordingly, acetylated tubulin detection in late-treated embryos showed no loss of cilia in either the epidermis or the central canal of the neural tube (fig. S7B).
Using in situ HCR, we then aimed to spatially resolve the location of ANE genes differentially expressed between control and treated conditions (Fig. 4C). Our profiling revealed that early-treated embryos lack cells with an ANE identity, as demonstrated by the ectoderm-specific loss of Six3/6, Frz5/8, sFRP1/2/5a, and Otx, which instead persisted in the mesendoderm, along with the complete absence of FoxQ2, Fezf, and Dkk3 (Fig. 4Ci). In these embryos, we also see the neural marker Elav and the early floor plate marker Nk2.1 completely absent from the prospective dorsal neuroectoderm, indicating a loss of neural plate identity (Fig. 4Ci). To rule out an off-target effect of AZA on dorso-ventral specification, we co-profiled the dorsal determinant chordin (Chd) together with PSmad1/5/8, as a readout of ventral BMP activity, in normal and treated embryos. We found no prominent differences in their dorso-ventral localization following Wnt overactivation, showing that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has no effect on dorsal-ventral specification at these early stages (fig. S7B) and proving that the loss of neural plate identities in these embryos is not attributable to a loss of dorsal identity. Our in situ profiling in late-treated embryos further shows that the network specifying the ANE is lost upon Wnt overactivation, with all markers disappearing specifically from the anterior ectoderm (Fig. 4Cii). Again, sFRP1/2/5a and Otx remain strongly expressed in the mesendoderm, indicating a mesendoderm-specific gene program consistent with our RNA-seq data.
In contrast to the early-treatment group, late-treated embryos maintained Elav expression in the neural plate as anticipated from our RNA-seq profiling (Fig. 4Cii). After treatment, Nk2.1 expression persists in the floor plate, indicating that its identity might be established at late stages of gastrulation but is lost in the brain. Moreover, late-treated embryos have a continuous Elav domain with a rostral end coincident with the ANB, clearly contrasting with that of control embryos, where Elav has a segmented pattern and is absent anteriorly (Fig. 4Cii). To investigate this further, we used IMARIS software to segment the neural plate of late-treated and control embryos (Fig. 5A). Our analysis found no significant difference in the cell number (n = 12, Student t test, P = 0.607) or volume (n = 16, Student t test, P = 0.591) of the neural tissue in treated and control embryos (Fig. 5A). This suggests that the loss of ANE markers is unlikely to be attributable to a loss of anterior neural tissue. To explore alternative explanations, we examined the identity of cells in the anterior neural plate of late AZA-treated embryos. Specifically, we co-detected the expression of the anterior markers Otx and Pax4/6 together with Gbx (marking the posterior brain border at the level of the first somite boundary) and Hox1 (with expression beginning at the third somite, corresponding to the putative hindbrain region) (72) (Fig. 5B). Our results show that only a small subset of cells in the posterior Otx and Pax4/6 domain remains in treated embryos, localized within the posterior portion of the recently defined di-mesencephalic domain (72). These cells are, thus, located at the anterior boundary with Gbx, which shifts to the anterior tip of the neuroectoderm in response to Wnt overactivation. Gbx anterior shift is also accompanied by an anterior shift of Hox1 expression, indicating that the anterior brain, where aGRN genes are typically expressed in control embryos, loses its anterior identity in late AZA-treated embryos by acquiring a posterior identity.

Collectively, our data suggest that aGRN genes are negatively regulated by Wnt signaling in amphioxus. The overactivation of Wnt leads to the depletion of anterior ectodermal structures, encompassing both neural (ANE) and nonneural, mirroring previous observations in ambulacrarian larvae. In addition, our results suggest that, although prolonged Wnt overactivation does not affect the quantity of cells designated to form neurons in the neural tube, aGRN genes play a crucial role in steering neurons toward adopting an anterior brain fate in amphioxus.

## Apical patterning is essential to set the hypothalamic identity of neurons in the anterior brain of amphioxus
After establishing the critical role of a highly conserved interplay between Wnt signaling and aGRN genes in determining the ANE fates in amphioxus, we sought to investigate the specific identity of the anterior neurons affected by our functional manipulations. Our investigation pointed toward the hypothalamus as the brain region potentially most affected by the absence of aGRN genes. This inference was substantiated by two key findings. First, we found a strong signal for the aGRN to be operating during the specification of hypothalamic cells in the zebrafish brain (Fig. 1B). Second, we found that the loss of aGRN induced by Wnt overactivation in amphioxus led to the depletion of Nk2.1 and Fezf (see Fig. 4), well-established markers for early hypothalamic fate in vertebrates (fig. S1F) (72, 73). In normal development, the posterior limit of Fezf expression domain would meet the anterior limit of Nkx2.1 expression at the intercalated Six3/6-negative region, which we recently defined as hypothalamic as evidenced by co-expression of vertebrate hypothalamic markers FoxD and Otp (64). Given this, we reasoned that the loss of Six3/6 expression in AZA-treated embryos might imply a loss of the hypothalamic territory within the developing forebrain.
To test this hypothesis, we expanded upon our previously published dataset on Otp, FoxD, and Six3/6 (64) by co-analyzing the expression in early amphioxus larvae of additional key hypothalamic markers, Bsx (74) and Hmx (pro-orthologous of Hmx2 and Hmx3) (75), which are known to be expressed in the zebrafish hypothalamus (Fig. 6A and fig. S8A; see Supplementary Text for description of expression pattern). Our analysis revealed that at the 12ss and 14ss Bsx co-localizes with the two bilateral groups of Otp-positive cells in the pre-infundibular region of the brain, which lies in the Six3/6−/FoxD+/Otp+ domain that we define as hypothalamic (Fig. 6A). Noticeably, this Bsx domain is absent from embryos in which cell division has been arrested from the 7ss (fig. S7B), aligning with our previous finding of amphioxus hypothalamic development through intercalated growth during neurulation (64). Complementing this Bsx pattern, we detect Hmx expression in anterior pre-infundibular cells, again within the Six3/6−/FoxD+/Otp+ domain (Fig. 6Aii).

The vertebrate hypothalamus is also characterized by the expression of the microRNA miR-7, which is additionally associated with neurosecretory centers in both protostomes and deuterostomes (76). Therefore, we investigated the expression of miR-7 in amphioxus at the one–gill slit larval stage and found it co-localizing with the hypothalamic marker Otp in the intercalated Six3/6-negative region (Fig. 6B and fig. S8C; see Supplementary Text for description of expression pattern). Furthermore, our study identifies the presence of two amphioxus neuropeptides from the calcitonin/calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) family, previously named calcitonin family peptides (CTFPs) (77), —CTFP1 and CTFP3—in the hypothalamic, intercalated Six3/6-negative region (Fig. 6B; see Supplementary Text for description of expression pattern). Their expression appears highly dynamic: While transcripts of both neuropeptides can be detected in the cerebral vesicle at the 14ss, only CTFP3 remains expressed in one–gill slit larvae (Fig. 6B). Considering the association of miR-7 with neurosecretory regions of the brain and the expression pattern of two calcitonin-family peptides in the amphioxus larva, these results are consistent with the amphioxus hypothalamus including neurosecretory cell types.
Having found that the preinfundibular brain of amphioxus has a strong hypothalamic identity and neurosecretory capacity, we conducted further investigations to determine whether such identities are maintained in the absence of aGRN markers. To this aim, we co-profiled FoxD, Bsx, CTFP1, and CTFP2, already expressed at the 7ss, in late AZA-treated embryos, using both in situ and RNA-seq profiling (Fig. 6D and fig. S8D). Our results show no trace of hypothalamic or neurosecretory identities following Wnt overactivation, showing that the hypothalamic identity is lost upon depletion of the ANE molecular signature.

## DISCUSSION
The appearance of a dorsal anterior tubular brain is one of the major evolutionary transitions in natural history for which we have no explanation. While a wealth of information on CNS anatomy, physiology, and development has been revealed by comparative analysis, the diversity of neural organizations in modern animals has hindered our understanding of chordate nervous system evolution. Garstang’s Auricularia hypothesis first proposed that the deuterostome ancestor had bilateral rows of cilia, similar to those in echinoderm and enteropneust dipleurula larvae, and associated nerve cells, which would have converged to the dorsal midline in the chordate ancestor to form the CNS (38). This view was incompatible with Saint-Hilaire’s proposal of a dorsal-ventral inversion in vertebrates relative to protostomes, which assumed a preexisting centralized nervous system in the ancestor of deuterostomes (78). The work of Holley and collaborators, published in 1995, experimentally demonstrated Saint-Hilaire’s hypothesis, by showing that the Drosophila sog gene and the Xenopus chordin gene are functionally homologous and able to specify a neural axis in whatever part of the body that they are injected, and BMP has an anti-neuralizing effect on opposite body sides in these organisms (39). However, while those ectopic neural axes showed anteroposterior patterning, they would consistently miss the anterior portion of the brain, suggesting that anterior brain specification depended on another mechanism.
Over the years, this has led to numerous hypotheses that try to explain the evolutionary origin of the vertebrate brain. As Saint-Hilaire’s proposal gained support from new molecular data, Nielsen and Lacalli adapted Garstang’s scenario to explain the formation of the neural tube through fusion of ciliary bands in the context of the dorsal-ventral inversion. Opinions were divided here on whether this fusion should be inclusive of the AO. Nielsen suggested that the chordate CNS evolved through the lateral fusion of ventral parts of the circumoral ciliary band from an enteropneust-like ancestor (41). Nielsen did not include the AO in this internalized ciliary band of chordates, as it degenerates in many invertebrate larvae after metamorphosis (79). Instead, on the basis of primarily the presence of serotonergic neurons in both the AO and the amphioxus frontal eye complex, Lacalli proposed that, in line with Garstang’s and Nielsen’s ideas, the amphioxus CNS evolved from the fusion and internalization of ciliary bands, including their associated AO, through a rolling-up process. Lacalli’s perspective was that this rolling-up process could explain the formation of the neural tube itself and the consequent anterior-dorsal orientation of the ciliated apexes of the serotonergic cells in the amphioxus frontal eye (40). In both scenarios, the positioning of oral and apical structures in the context of the dorsal-ventral inversion is problematic, precisely because there is no evidence for these structures to be inverted in chordates, where the mouth, for example, lies opposite to the brain, unlike in protostomes. This has led to a substantial debate about the classical concept of protostomy and deuterostomy and to questioning whether the chordate mouth can be considered homologous to the protostome mouth [see (80, 81) for further discussion on the subject].
The alternative scenario arising in more recent years departs from the necessity of having a conserved dorsal-ventral patterning system across bilaterians and from having a centralized CNS in the urbilaterian (43, 82). This primarily comes from research in hemichordates showing that the expression of a large number of transcription factors across the antero-posterior axis in the hemichordates’ ectoderm is similar to the chordate CNS, including the presence of vertebrate-like brain organizers (12, 19, 83). Together with similar studies in insects, annelids (9, 76, 84), and, more recently, brachiopods, nemerteans, and xenacoelomorphs (43, 85–87), it seems to indicate an ancient origin of the anterior-posterior patterning of the nervous system, despite its morphological diversity across animals. Somehow, this brings AOs right back at the epicenter of the debate, as they are apical/anterior structures, abundant across metazoans with diverse adult neural architectures - ranging from nerve nets to ventrally centralized nerve cords- and very similar in molecular profile (9, 33, 36, 37, 86, 88–94). More recently, data on multiple arthropod species have shown a similar molecular signature to the one found in the AO in the anteriormost portion of the CNS (33, 90–92). Thus, considering that several components of the aGRN that specifies the anterior portion of the neuroectoderm (ANE) are highly conserved, it has been proposed that the anterior brain evolved in bilaterians by coalescence of two separated neuronal systems: an anterior “apical nervous system” (ANS) and a posterior “blastoporal nervous system” (BNS). This chimeric brain hypothesis (95), very similar to the earlier “animal-axial” hypothesis (96), proposes that the ANS corresponds to the AO of invertebrate larvae and part of the vertebrate forebrain, while the BNS corresponds to the remaining posterior nervous system. However, both hypotheses pose the same fundamental problem regarding the conflicting fate of ANE precursors in ambulacrarians and chordates, which give rise to the AO (11, 97) and the forebrain (68, 98) respectively. Our work solves this puzzle by showing specific retinal and hypothalamic cells in the amphioxus forebrain as derivatives of an ANE established at the time of gastrulation, using a conserved set of genes that, in the sea urchin, induce the formation of the AO.
Here, we harnessed the power of single-cell transcriptomics to quantify developmental differences and similarities in the profiles of individual cells contributing to anterior neural structures across three phylogenetically key species: sea urchin, amphioxus, and zebrafish. Unlike previous approaches that broadly compared gene expression patterns during development (11, 13, 30), our focus was on specific co-expression profiles shared by single cells, regardless of the species to which they belong. As a result, our analysis identified a co-expression module of 11 genes that can serve as a reference signature for identifying cells belonging to the ANE across species, as more single-cell atlases become available. This enabled us to precisely locate the identified signature in cells of the sea urchin AO, as well as in specific hypothalamic and retinal cells of the amphioxus and zebrafish forebrain. This finding challenges the previous scenario that claimed widespread expression of AO genes in the vertebrate brain, including the hindbrain (30).
In terms of regulation, all the genes in the co-expression module have been demonstrated in the sea urchin to be part of the same gene regulatory network (aGRN). While our study does not evaluate the one-to-one interactions between the genes in the co-expression module, our single-cell analysis throughout development reveals a clear sequence of activation in the three species studied, indicative of co-regulation. Intriguingly, this sequence of activation follows a biphasic pattern in both the sea urchin and amphioxus, but not in zebrafish, indicating a potentially similar underlying regulation in invertebrate deuterostomes at these early stages of specification.
Supporting this, in both ambulacrarians and cephalochordates, a broad and radial apical plate, defined by the expression of Six3/6 and FoxQ2, is established by the blastula stage on the animal side of the embryo. This region is delimited by Wnt8 at the equator of the amphioxus embryo, and, precisely above this boundary, the tissue is specified as neuroectoderm (as demonstrated by Ngn expression; see fig. S5). While Six3/6 and FoxQ2 restrict antero-dorsally, the neuroectoderm broadens at the expense of Wnt8, which retracts posteriorly, allowing the neuroectoderm to converge antero-dorsally (see fig. S5). This process, initiated at the B stage akin to the sea urchin, continues until the end of gastrulation in amphioxus, a stage at which we observe the genes of the co-expression module persisting in both the anterior-dorsal ectoderm and neuroectoderm. This distinction is substantial compared to zebrafish, where the genes in the co-expression module are activated after neuroectoderm segregation only, indicating that the early activation stage could be crucial for ectoderm neuroectoderm segregation, and, in vertebrates, such a segregation phase might occur too rapidly to be observed, thus explaining the loss of phasing in zebrafish.
On the other hand, the second phase of activation in amphioxus shares more similarities with that of zebrafish as it involves the specification of retinal and hypothalamic cell types. This discovery further reinforces our previously published proposal regarding the amphioxus hypothalamic region as a morphological continuation of the eye field based on co-expression profiles (64), adding to previous anatomical observations made by electronic microscopy (99, 100). In the late phase of specification, the cells with an active co-expression module lose the compact arrangement observed in the early phase of specification (compare N0 to 12ss in Fig. 3). This is likely due to intercalated growth starting in the amphioxus brain at the 7ss (64). As we previously showed, both serotonergic cells of the frontal eye and the hypothalamus arise from a localized burst of proliferation at the 7ss, intercalating among cells likely established in the neural plate during earlier developmental stages [see the glutamatergic cells of the frontal eye as an example of cell type established at earlier developmental stages in (64)]. Here, we further characterize amphioxus larval hypothalamic cell types, which express FoxD, Otp, Bsx, Hmx, and miR-7 as well as the calcitonin-family neuropeptide CTFP3. Moreover, our results suggest that these cells are exposed to interactions with cells that have an active aGRN co-expression module from birth, thereby influencing their cell fate choices. Given the parallel with zebrafish, these interactions might be key to set the identity manifested at later stages of development. In support of this, our current study shows that serotonergic cells acquire their identity through the down-regulation of proliferative Six3/6 and the up-regulation of Fezf, a key player in serotonergic development in the sea urchin AO (55) and in a specific type of amacrine cell in the vertebrate retina known as the ON starburst amacrine cells, which are responsive to changes in light intensity (101).
Unlike in the sea urchin (30), we find no involvement of the aGRN genes in neurogenesis but see its major contribution toward setting the forebrain in the right position. This is supported by our functional assays, which show that silencing of aGRN genes by Wnt overactivation in the amphioxus neural plate does not affect neurogenesis, as embryos form an antero-posteriorly patterned neural tube without neuronal loss, but it does impede the specification of forebrain identity (Fig. 5). These experiments in amphioxus reveal a phenotype similar to the one published by Holley and collaborators in vertebrates (39), suggesting that the conserved aGRN co-expression module described here, acting under regulation of Wnt, might be the mechanism by which the forebrain is set in chordates.
Consequently, our study also reveals a previously unidentified role for Wnt signaling in amphioxus, adding to the previously reported functions in the specification of dorsal and posterior fates (51, 70, 71). Here, we show that, while Wnt overactivation at B stages results in a complete loss of neuronal structures, the same perturbation at early gastrula stages selectively leads to a loss of anterior (forebrain) fates. These effects are not due to a loss of dorsal identity, as the distribution of Chordin and PSmad1/5/8 remains normal after Wnt overactivation (fig. S8). Thus, we propose that the main role of amphioxus early zygotic Wnt from the B stage is to restrict the size and position of the early aGRN to the anterior-dorsal side of the embryo through mutually repressive interactions, similarly to how the anterior/apical territory is set in ambulacrarians (17, 22, 97). As mentioned above, this is supported by a similar expression dynamics of aGRN genes in amphioxus and ambulacrarians, further suggesting that Wnt8 in amphioxus likely controls the restriction of FoxQ2, Six3/6, and Frz5/8 to the anterior/animal side of the embryo, while Six3/6, sFRP1/2/5a, and Dkk3 inhibit Wnt signaling anteriorly (65). We infer that this mutual inhibition restricts the aGRN molecular signature to the anterior portion of the developing neuroectoderm, defining two distinct territories: an anterior one, where aGRN genes are active, competent to give rise to the anteriormost portion of the brain (secondary prosencephalon); and a posterior one that specifies posterior identities inclusive of the hindbrain, as suggested by the anteriorized Gbx expression in AZA-treated embryos (Fig. 5).
By demonstrating the conservation of the ANE molecular signature in chordates, our results are compatible with the hypothetical contribution of the ancestral “apical brain” to the vertebrate brain proposed under the chimeric brain evolutionary scenario (95), highlighting the use of an ancestral aGRN co-expression module to set and prime the anterior neural plate as forebrain specifically in chordates. Although little is known about the mechanism by which the position of the brain is set in vertebrates, it has been observed that the rostral nonneural ectoderm is required to form the ANB, shown in zebrafish to be a source of secreted Frizzled related proteins (SFRPs) that act to repress posteriorizing Wnt signals, which are essential to form the forebrain (48, 68, 98, 102). Work from the Weinberg lab (103) has also shown that, in zebrafish, the AP patterning of the neuroectoderm is not dependent on the primary organizer (which is positioned by maternal β-catenin), but it is affected by zygotic Wnt signaling. This is consistent with our observations in amphioxus that, while the organizer-dependent Wnt signaling is key to form the posterior neural tube, the earlier organizer-independent Wnt is key to specify forebrain identity. This suggests that a similar apical patterning system as the one identified in amphioxus but devoid of FoxQ2 might be also operating in vertebrates to position the forebrain early in development.
In sum, putting together our experimental data and rigorous quantitative analyses in the context of the existing literature, we propose an evolutionary scenario that explains the origin of the chordate forebrain (Fig. 7). We propose that an aGRN ancestral to deuterostomes has a conserved function in instructing ANE-derived nervous tissue with a specific “anterior” sensory-neurosecretory identity, regardless of the organization of the nervous system or life cycle (Fig. 7A) (104). In the ambulacrarian lineage, the ancient network would have been used to specify the AO of the dipleurula-type larva, as shown by modern indirect-developing echinoderms and hemichordates, and the proboscis nervous system of enteropneust hemichordates, which was recently shown to contain a high concentration of neuropeptidergic neurons (7). In the chordate lineage, specifically, the early phase of activation of aGRN genes would have been key to dorsally segregate neuroectoderm from apical ectoderm via mutually repressive interactions between Wnt and Six3/6-FoxQ2. This would have allowed the displacement of the neural-competent tissue from an apical position to an anterior-dorsal position (Fig. 7B), probably induced by an active and inverted BMP/Chordin axis, as seen in modern amphioxus (105, 106). This would align the ANE with the revolutionary developmental innovation that represents a dorsal neural tube formed through neurulation. As such, this would result in the coalescence of “apically derived” and “blastoporal-derived” nervous systems on the dorsal side of the body. Through the late activation of the remaining aGRN genes, both anterior cells and newborn cells in this anterior domain would be primed to become the precursors of the secondary prosencephalon. Supporting this notion, we previously showed that this anterior region in amphioxus, lost upon Wnt overactivation, includes both gastrulation-derived glutamatergic neurons and neurulation-derived serotonergic and hypothalamic cells (64). Together with previous data showing the presence of telencephalic- and hypothalamic-like domains in Tunicates (107) and of telencephalic- and retina-like domains in amphioxus (67, 108), our present characterization of hypothalamic cell types in the amphioxus brain indicates that the secondary prosencephalon likely originated in the chordate ancestor. This scenario implies that the ambulacrarian AO and the vertebrate forebrain are not homologous at the organ level but are specified by a conserved co-expressed module of orthologous genes, giving rise to sensory-neurosecretory centers in both clades.

## Proposed evolutionary scenario for aGRN and ANE evolution in deuterostomes.
The last common ancestor of deuterostomes (regardless of his body plan) had an aGRN controlling the specification of the ANE on the anterior/apical portion of the body. This aGRN consisted of two phases. In the early phase, Wnt signaling from the vegetal pole controlled the anterior specification and restriction (see arrows) of an anterior apical plate, in which Six3/6, FoxQ2, Frz5/8, and Wnt inhibitors were expressed. In the late phase, late aGRN genes within the apical plate guided the specification of the ANE [(A), full box]. In the ambulacrarian lineage, the ANE develops into the AO of the larva. In the chordate lineage, neurogenesis was concentrated on the dorsal side of the embryo, but the aGRN remained active anteriorly, where Wnt inhibitors were expressed, and was still controlled by vegetal Wnt signaling. The aGRN therefore affects only the anterior portion of the neuroectoderm, which acquire a sensory-neurosecretory forebrain identity [(B), dashed box]. This condition is still observed in modern cephalochordates. In the vertebrate lineage, the early portion of the aGRN was lost, and the network was restricted to the neuroectoderm, where it specified retino-hypothalamic fate in the secondary prosencephalon [(C), dotted box]. Moreover, the main source of Wnt affecting the aGRN became the posterior portion of the forebrain. In all figures, the anterior side of the body is on the left. Dashed horizontal lines distinguish the areas of the embryo in which BMP (green) and Chd (magenta) are active and highlight the dorsoventral inversion in the cordate lineage.
The limited range of action of aGRN genes within the CNS is also compatible with the notion of modularity that we previously proposed on the basis of the specific proliferation pattern of the brain in amphioxus (64). Conceptually speaking, such underlying modularity would allow the brain to evolve at its own pace in different chordate lineages: In the Olfactores lineage (Tunicata + Vertebrata) leading to vertebrates, the early expression of FoxQ2 was lost and expression of aGRN genes became restricted to the neuroectoderm, possibly under the control of Sox2 genes as shown in vertebrates (Fig. 7C) (109). Because FoxQ2 is still present in most vertebrate genomes and is expressed in the zebrafish retina (45), it would be interesting to test how marked shifts in its expression may have affected the evolution of the aGRN. This also means that the true relationship between the ANE in different deuterostome phyla becomes apparent only when taking cephalochordates into consideration.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## scRNA-seq analysis

## Sea urchin developmental dataset
Normalized scRNA-seq data from the gastrulating S. purpuratus was obtained from (52) [Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), accession number GSE149221]. Batch correction was first performed using fastMNN (using 20 nearest neighbors), implemented in the batchelor R package (110) on the top 2000 highly variable genes. This gene set was obtained by using the “modelGeneVar” and “getTopHVGs” functions in the scran R package (111), which accounts for the mean-variance relationship of each gene. Samples were then integrated in reverse order of their developmental stage so that samples are merged with decreasing numbers of cells. The 50 corrected principal components were subsequently used to obtain a reduced dimensionality uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). This was performed using the UMAP implementation in Scanpy (112) using 75 nearest neighbors and a 0.9 minimum distance parameter.

## Reannotation
The Foster et al. (52) cell-type annotations were obtained by cross-referencing clusters in the raw Seurat object with labels given in the original publication. We then performed a finer annotation of AO cell types based on the distinct expression of 11 previously characterized marker genes (11, 17, 23, 54, 55, 97). By averaging the expression of apical patterning genes across developmental stages, we found a clear distinction in the genes expressed from the eight-cell to early B stages and from the hatched B to late gastrula stages (fig. S1C). This motivated the separation into an “early” and “late” apical plate cell type defined by those apical patterning genes expressed at early and late time points. To classify cells of the apical plate, the sea urchin dataset was iteratively clustered and filtered on the basis of the expression of these apical plate cell-type markers. Leiden clustering, implemented in Scanpy (113), was performed at various resolutions (resolution parameter ranging from 1 to 8) on the 50 corrected principal components. These clusters were then assigned a cell type by comparing with the expression profiles of apical patterning genes, the serotonergic neuron marker, Tph, as well as the developmental stages and original Foster et al. (52) annotations.

## Zebrafish developmental dataset
Raw counts and metadata were obtained from (53). These raw counts were normalized and log transformed using the “computeSumFactors” and “logNormCounts” functions from the scran R package. The two-dimensional, force-directed graph representation in Fig. 1 (Bi and Ci) was replicated from the original publication using the provided Matlab script (https://github.com/wagnerde/STITCH/) and graph visualization software Gephi using the ForceAtlas2 algorithm (114). In this setup, Wagner et al. (53) link cells across developmental stages using the STITCH graph construction algorithm. This graph is then visualized using the ForceAtlas2.

## Reannotation
Cells annotated by Wagner et al. (53) as “diencephalon” from stages 14, 18, and 24 hpf were isolated to obtain a more refined annotation according to the prosomeric model. As with the sea urchin, multiple rounds of Leiden clustering and filtering were performed. The STITCH graph was used for both Leiden clustering and ForceAtlas2 visualization with Scanpy (fig. S1, E and F). Hypothalamus cells were then separated from the diencephalon according to the expression of hypothalamus (fezf1, fezf2, nkx2.1, and rx3) and posterior diencephalon (barh2, irx3a, pitx2, and pitx3) markers, reported in previous studies.

## Amphioxus developmental dataset
Normalized single-nucleus RNA-seq data were obtained from (60) via links provided in the accompanying shiny app (https://lifeomics.shinyapps.io/shinyappmulti/). Cell type and stage annotations were provided in the downloaded Seurat object, as well as UMAP embedding coordinates. Annotations for each amphioxus gene ID were obtained from the original study (available through the link above and via https://github.com/XingyanLiu/AmphioxusAnalysis/tree/main/resources). However, the gene IDs used for “Dkk3” and “Dkk1” were confirmed using BLAST best alignment hits. UMAP expression plots were created using Scanpy.

## Stage heatmaps
The sea urchin, amphioxus, and zebrafish datasets were filtered to contain only ectoderm-related cell types. Specifically, cell types annotated as “SN,” “late AP,” “early AP,” “other ectoderm,” and “other neural” were retained in the Foster et al. (52) sea urchin dataset. The Ma et al. (60) amphioxus dataset was filtered on the basis of the lineage annotations provided in the original study. Specifically, “B_0,” “B_1,” “epithelial ectoderm,” and “neural ectoderm” cells were retained. Similarly, only “forebrain/optic,” “midbrain,” “hindbrain/spinal cord,” “neural crest,” and “epidermal” cell types were used from the Wagner et al. (53) zebrafish dataset. After filtering, normalized gene expression values were averaged across cells within each developmental stage and min-max normalized within each gene. This was then plotted using the ComplexHeatmap package.

## Zebrafish hypothalamic dataset
scRNA-seq data from (73) were also used to query later stages of zebrafish brain development. Processed Seurat objects from each developmental stage were downloaded from GEO with accession number GSE158142 and combined into a single SingleCellExperiment object. The counts data were renormalized using the “multibacthNorm” function from the batchelor R package, which removes systematic differences in coverage across batches. All batches in the combined dataset were then integrated together using fastMNN with 20 mutually nearest neighbors. Batches were merged in reverse order of developmental stage and in order of decreasing numbers of cells. As in the analysis of the Wagner et al. (53) dataset, the corrected principal components were used to compute a reduced dimensionality UMAP (15 nearest neighbors, minimum distance of 0.5). The dataset was then filtered to include only those cells annotated by the original authors as being related to the hypothalamus (labeled as “hypothalamus/telencephalon, gabaergic,” “hypothalamus,” “hypothalamus,” “ventral diencephalon/hypothalamus,” “hypothalamus neurons,” or “Hypothalamus”).

## Amphioxus husbandry, spawning, and embryo fixation
Adult individuals of European amphioxus B. lanceolatum were collected from the sand in Banyuls-sur-Mer, France, and transported to Cambridge, UK, where they were kept in a custom-made facility at the Department of Zoology. Here, amphioxus was maintained, and gametes were obtained as described in (115). After in vitro fertilization, embryos were raised in petri dishes in filtered artificial salt water (ASW) in an incubator at 21°C. One–gill slit larvae raised up to 4 days after fertilization were fed with algae from the 48-hpf stage as previously described (115). At the desired stage embryos were collected and fixed in ice-cold 3.7% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and Mops buffer for 12 hours, then washed in sodium phosphate buffer saline (NPBS), dehydrated, and stored in 100% methanol (MeOH) at −20°C. For RNA-seq, live embryos were transferred in a ribonuclease-free Eppendorf, fast-frozen with liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80°C before processing. The nomenclature for early stages is taken from (116), while, during neurulation, the number of somites is used to unbiasedly identify the embryonic stage.

## Drug treatments
Fertilized eggs were raised at 21°C. At 4.5 hpf (B stage), random pools (several hundreds) of embryos from different progenitors were transferred into small beakers with 10 ml of 10 μM AZA or DMSO (as a control) in filtered ASW and left to develop at 21°C up to 12 hpf (early neurula stage) or 21 hpf (7ss). AZA is an inhibitor of GSK3β and has been used in marine larvae in previous works (13, 69). At 12 hpf, the embryos were either fixed in 3.7% PFA in Mops buffer and stored in methanol at −20°C or frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. For all the experiments, three replicas for treatment and control were used.

## Amphioxus RNA-seq analysis

## Amphioxus developmental dataset
The B. lanceolatum transcriptome and genome described in (61) were downloaded via the link provided. In addition, the FASTA files for the mitochondrial B. lanceolatum genome and transcriptome sequences were retrieved from National Center for Biotechnology Information (AB19483). The four references were used to generate a reference index using Salmon (version 1.3.0) (117).
Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.9), and adapter contamination was removed by trimming the reads using Trimmomatic version 0.39. Transcript expression was quantified with Salmon (version 1.3.0). Transcript level expression values were imported into R and summarized to gene level using the Bioconductor package _tximport (118). Counts were then normalized for library size and composition bias using DESeq2 (119).

## DE of AZA-treated embryos
Control and treated embryos frozen in liquid nitrogen (three biological replicates per condition) were ground with an electric pestle in a 1.5-ml tube while thawing, and, then, total RNA was extracted using Norgen Total RNA Purification Plus Kit (catalog no. 48300). Purified RNA was stored in −80°C. RNA quantity and quality were checked using a BioAnalyzer. Samples were submitted for sequencing on a 96-well plate and arranged according to a block randomized order in the wells to avoid spatial biases on sample groups during library preparation. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (no. 20020595) following the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 system.
Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.9), and reads were trimmed using fastp version 0.21.0 to remove adapter contamination and poly-G tails. Transcript expression was quantified with Salmon (version 1.3.0). Transcript level expression values were imported into R and summarized to gene level using the Bioconductor package _tximport (118). DGE analysis between AZA treated and control groups was carried out using DESeq2 (119), the “local” fit method was used for dispersion estimates, and multiple testing correction was carried out using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (120). Datasets are available at GEO with accession number GSE239838.

## Probe synthesis and in situ HCR
Sequences for the genes used in this study (listed in table S1) were reconstructed through the Reciprocal Best Hits approach: Genes from other amphioxus and deuterostome species were used to identify candidate orthologs in the B. lanceolatum transcriptome (61); the top candidates were then compared with published transcriptomes using BLAST. Reconstructed sequences were then sent to Molecular Instruments Inc. to prepare probe pairs used in version 3 in situ HCR, performed on embryos as described in (63): Briefly, samples were rehydrated in NPBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 (NPBT) bleached for 30 min with a solution of 5% deionized formamide, 1.5% H2O2, and 0.2% saline sodium citrate (SSC) in nuclease-free water and permeabilized for 3 hours in NPBS, 1% DMSO, and 1% Triton X-100. The embryos were incubated in Hybridization Buffer (HB; Molecular Instruments) for 2 hours, and, then, the probes were added in HB overnight at 37°C. The following day, probe excess was removed with Wash Buffer (Molecular Instrument) followed by 5× SSC and 0.1% Triton X-100. The samples were then incubated in Amplification Buffer (AB; Molecular Instruments) for 30 min and then left overnight in the dark at room temperature in AB and 0.03 μM of each hairpin (Molecular Instruments). Embryos were washed in the dark in 5× SSC and 0.1% Triton X-100 and incubated overnight with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/ml) in NPBT, then washed in NPBT, and transferred in a glass-bottomed dish in 100% glycerol. For each transcript, HCRs were replicated in different combinations at least three times, and an average of three representative embryos from each experiment were imaged with an Olympus V3000 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope.

## Immunohistochemistry
Fixed embryos were processed for immunofluorescence as described in (64). Briefly, embryos were rehydrated and bleached as described above for in situ HCR and permeabilized overnight in NPBS, 1% DMSO, and 1% Triton X-100. The following day, samples were blocked in blocking solution (NPBS, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.1% bovine serum albumin, and 5% NGS) for 3 hours and then incubated overnight at 4°C in block with primary antibodies: mouse anti-acetylated tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T6793) at 1:250 or rabbit anti-phosphorylated Smad1/5/8 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9511S) at 1:100. The following day, embryos were thoroughly washed with NPBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 and incubated first in blocking solution for 3 hours and then overnight at 4°C in the dark in blocking solution with DAPI (1:500) and secondary antibodies: goat antimouse (Invitrogen, 84540) and goat antirabbit (Abcam, 150083), both at a dilution of 1:250. Samples were lastly washed in NPBS and 0.1% Triton X-100, mounted in 80% glycerol, and imaged with on an Olympus V3000 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope.

## Image analysis
Z stacks of 7ss embryos treated with AZA or DMSO and stained with HCR were imported in IMARIS (IMARIS 9.7.2, Bitplane, Oxford Instruments). The neural plate was manually segmented in 16 embryos (8 AZA treated and 8 controls) by drawing splines around the cell nuclei visible through the DAPI signal every four slices in transverse section. At this embryonic stage, the neural plate is clearly distinguishable from the rest of the ectoderm in cross section; in addition, Elav signal was used in half of the samples to validate the precision of the segmentation. In the segmented neural plate, cells were counted manually using the DAPI signal, while volumes were calculated automatically by IMARIS.

## Figure Fig. 7.: Proposed evolutionary scenario for aGRN and ANE evolution in deuterostomes.The last common ancestor of deuterostomes (regardless of his body plan) had an aGRN controlling the specification of the ANE on the anterior/apical portion of the body. This aGRN consisted of two phases. In the early phase, Wnt signaling from the vegetal pole controlled the anterior specification and restriction (see arrows) of an anterior apical plate, in which Six3/6, FoxQ2, Frz5/8, and Wnt inhibitors were expressed. In the late phase, late aGRN genes within the apical plate guided the specification of the ANE [(A), full box]. In the ambulacrarian lineage, the ANE develops into the AO of the larva. In the chordate lineage, neurogenesis was concentrated on the dorsal side of the embryo, but the aGRN remained active anteriorly, where Wnt inhibitors were expressed, and was still controlled by vegetal Wnt signaling. The aGRN therefore affects only the anterior portion of the neuroectoderm, which acquire a sensory-neurosecretory forebrain identity [(B), dashed box]. This condition is still observed in modern cephalochordates. In the vertebrate lineage, the early portion of the aGRN was lost, and the network was restricted to the neuroectoderm, where it specified retino-hypothalamic fate in the secondary prosencephalon [(C), dotted box]. Moreover, the main source of Wnt affecting the aGRN became the posterior portion of the forebrain. In all figures, the anterior side of the body is on the left. Dashed horizontal lines distinguish the areas of the embryo in which BMP (green) and Chd (magenta) are active and highlight the dorsoventral inversion in the cordate lineage.
