Successful goniosynechialysis for secondary angle-closure glaucoma after ocular jellyfish sting.

# Successful goniosynechialysis for secondary angle-closure glaucoma after ocular jellyfish sting

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeLittle is known about the ocular effects of jellyfish stings and their optimal management. We report the case of a 25-year-old fisherman that presented to the Emergency Department with a painful red eye, photophobia and blurred vision 24 hours after a jellyfish sting in his right eye (RE), that was projected from a fishing net.ObservationVisual acuity of the RE was 20/80. Examination of the RE revealed anterior uveitis, areas of iris stromal atrophy, 360° peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) and intraocular pressure (IOP) of 45 mmHg. Left eye was normal. Anterior segment inflammation resolved with topical anti-inflammatory therapy, but IOP remained uncontrolled despite maximal hypotensive therapy. Two months later, goniosynechialysis was performed and restored aqueous outflow, achieving long-term IOP control. The surgery was uneventful, and PAS were separated from the angle using micro-forceps.Conclusions and ImportanceThis case highlights important ocular findings – acute iris stromal atrophy, extensive PAS and severe ocular hypertension – secondary to jellyfish toxins, raising awareness to the rare entity of jellyfish stings. Early recognition and prompt surgical intervention may be sight-saving, in cases of ocular jellyfish stings, preventing the development of secondary chronic angle-closure glaucoma.Keywords: Ocular jellyfish sting, Ocular hypertension, Secondary angle-closure glaucoma, Peripheral anterior synechiae, Goniosynechialysis

## Introduction
Ocular jellyfish stings represent a rare ophthalmic emergency, which explains the scarcity of case reports in the medical literature, and consequently, our limited understanding of their presentation, optimal management, natural history, prognosis, and potential complications.
The jellyfish contact triggers the nematocysts, which are intracellular stinging organelles storing a filament that rapidly discharges, causing the injection and release of the venom into the target tissue within milliseconds, which produces the characteristic local and systemic reactions.1 Most injuries are reported as self-limited and resolved spontaneously within 24–48 hours. The first case report presenting an ocular jellyfish sting was published in 1944.2 Regarding ocular symptoms, ocular jellyfish stings usually produce self-limiting episodes of intense burning pain, tearing, and photophobia. Reported anterior segment findings include segment conjunctival and limbal injection with or without chemosis, punctate epithelial keratitis, corneal stromal edema, endothelial cell swelling, atypical severe iritis with mild anterior uveitis and focal peripheral anterior synechiae.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Transient increase in intraocular pressure had also been described after a sting.4
We report a case of extensive 360° peripheral anterior synechiae and secondary angle-closure glaucoma following a jellyfish sting, successfully treated with goniosynechialysis (GSL), ultimately preventing the development of chronic sequelae.

## Case description
A 25-year-old fisherman, with no relevant past medical or ocular history, presented at the emergency department of Hospital São João (Porto, Portugal), complaining of right eye (RE) pain, redness, photophobia and blurred vision, 24h following ocular contact with a jellyfish that was projected from a fishing net.
Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/80 (Snellen) in the RE and 20/20 (Snellen) in the left eye (LE). The RE pupil was slightly dilated and sluggishly reactive. Anterior segment slit lamp examination showed perilimbal injection, epithelial keratitis, deep anterior chamber with 2+ cells, and patchy iris stromal atrophy without posterior synechiae. Intraocular pressure was 45 mmHg RE and 18 mmHg LE. Gonioscopy revealed extensive PAS preventing visualization of trabecular meshwork (TM) in 360°, with no signs of trauma. Anterior segment OCT demonstrated the presence of angle closure with high PAS in 360° (Fig. 1). LE was normal. Fundoscopy was unremarkable bilaterally. Retinal multimodal imaging (optical coherence tomography, fluorescein and indocyanine green angiography) was also normal.

Gonioscopy revealing the presence of iridotrabecular synechiae. 1C. Anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT) image demonstrating the presence of high peripheral anterior synechiae (∗).
The patient was started on oral acetazolamide (250 mg every 6h), topical hypotensive therapy (timolol and brimonidine, every 12 hours), corticosteroids (topical dexamethasone 1 mg/ml, applied every 2 hours) and topical cyclopentolate (every 12 hours). Inflammation resolved within weeks, but intraocular pressure (IOP) remained uncontrolled despite therapy, so it was decided to proceed to a goniosynechialysis, which was performed through two small superior corneal incisions, using cohesive viscoelastic and micro-forceps, under direct view using a Swan Jacob gonioprism. The mechanical separation of PAS from the TM allowed for angle re-opening, thereby avoiding more invasive procedures. The procedure was uneventful.
Postoperatively, pilocarpine (q6h) and topical antibiotic therapy were maintained for 2 weeks, whereas topical corticosteroids were continued for 4 weeks with gradual taper. Intraocular pressure decreased progressively, allowing for stepwise withdrawal of the hypotensive medication, reaching IOP 10 mmHg at six weeks. Seven months after surgery, BCVA was 20/20 (Snellen), IOP remained stable in the low teens without hypotensive therapy and there were no signs of anterior segment inflammation. There was a mild superior corectopy due to the persistence of focal superior PAS, that were difficult to access between the two superior corneal incisions. TM was visible inferiorly, nasally and temporally with heavy pigmentation on gonioscopy (Fig. 2). The optic discs were healthy bilaterally, with no evidence of glaucomatous damage.

## Discussion
This case report evidences the occurrence of secondary angle-closure glaucoma with extensive PAS after the very uncommon event of an ocular jellyfish sting, followed by the prosecution of a minimally invasive management strategy that revealed itself to be successful in the treatment of the patient.
Jellyfish venom, composed of inflammatory mediators, polypeptides and enzymes, is thought to be injected in mucosa through nematocysts that are concentrated in the tentacles. Tentacles are elongated, flexible appendages extending from the margin of the jellyfish's bell, and they represent the primary structure used to capture prey and defend against predators. It can trigger a spectrum of neurotoxic and myotoxic inflammatory reactions, either as a direct effect of the venom or as host-mediated hypersensitivity responses. Myotoxic inflammatory reaction may involve the pupillary sphincter and dilator muscles, contributing to some of the pupillary changes observed in affected patients.2,5
Ocular findings described in the literature include pupillary changes, keratitis, anterior uveitis and ocular hypertension (usually attributed to a transient hypertensive phase secondary to inflammatory response).2 However, in our patient, the most likely mechanism for persistent ocular hypertension was angle obstruction from PAS. The development of secondary angle closure glaucoma in association with an ocular jellyfish sting was not described in prior reports.2,5 Zheng et al. reported optic atrophy, retinal vascular occlusion, retinal pigmentation and a macular scar as sequelae of ocular jellyfish stings.7 Our patient underwent a multimodal retinal evaluation, both at the diagnosis and after 7 months of follow-up, with no signs of posterior segment involvement.
The differential diagnosis considered were blunt trauma, chronic uveitis, and iridocorneal endothelial (ICE) syndrome. Blunt trauma, including the possibility of contusion related to the jellyfish projection, could explain anterior uveitis and transient IOP rise but not the acute onset of extensive PAS, and no signs of contusion or globe injury were present. Chronic intraocular inflammation was considered unlikely given the patient unremarkable ocular history and the absence of other signs of intraocular chronic inflammation. Furthermore, the observed 360° high PAS pattern is not characteristic of chronic anterior uveitis. ICE syndrome is a unilateral condition, typically occurring in young women, characterized by progressive PAS that often leads to elevated IOP, but it is usually accompanied by endothelial changes such as “hammered silver” appearance, corneal edema, polycoria, corectopia, ectropion uveae or iris nodules, none of which were observed in this case. Additionally, the extensive PAS and elevated IOP without glaucomatous optic neuropathy are inconsistent with the progressive, chronic synechial formation typical of ICE, supporting an acute PAS formation process caused by jellyfish-induced toxicity.
Immediate abundant washing with water or saline solution after ocular sting may be a determinant factor for decreasing the burden and time of contact between the venom and the ocular surface. The population should be educated regarding those general measures, specifically the high-risk groups involved in aquatic activities. Once an ocular jellyfish sting is suspected, topical corticosteroids and cycloplegics are the mainstay of initial treatment.5 IOP elevation is generally managed with topical hypotensive agents acutely, while chronic management must be tailored to the specific late changes observed.
This case report describes the rapid onset of inflammation, hypertension and extensive 360° PAS following ocular contact with a jellyfish sting. The secondary angle-closure and elevated IOP without glaucomatous optic neuropathy strongly supports the acute installation of these rare clinical findings caused by jellyfish-induced toxicity.
Regarding management, in our patient, persistent angle closure due to 360° PAS and IOP elevation posed a risk for long-term glaucomatous damage. Goniosynechialysis was chosen as a first minimally invasive surgery before considering a filtering procedure. By mechanically separating PAS from the angle, restoring aqueous humor access to the TM, GSL directly addressed the underlying mechanism. Shingleton BJ et al. demonstrated GSL efficacy in recent angle-closure glaucoma with synechiae of less than 6 months' duration, demonstrating a significant IOP reduction either stand-alone procedure or combined with other glaucoma surgeries.8,9 Successful IOP following PAS removal supports the acute nature of synechiae formation in our case. At 7 months, the patient remained with a quiet anterior chamber, BCVA of 20/20 and normal IOP without medication, underscoring GSL as a safe and effective procedure in cases of acute PAS formation with ocular hypertension following ocular jellyfish stings.
