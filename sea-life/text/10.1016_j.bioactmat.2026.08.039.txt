An oral coral-like resveratrol nanocomplex protects against dry eye disease through ocular and systemic redox homeostasis

# An oral coral-like resveratrol nanocomplex protects against dry eye disease through ocular and systemic redox homeostasis

## Abstract
Dry eye disease (DED) is a progressive ocular disorder in which chronic inflammation and oxidative stress synergistically damage ocular tissues and impair vision. While topical anti-inflammatory and artificial tears therapies are the standard treatment for DED, their long-term application is limited by adverse effects and poor patient compliance. Emerging evidence suggests that systemic redox dysregulation contributes to DED pathogenesis, highlighting the potential of oral antioxidant intervention as a disease-modifying strategy. However, the clinical translation of oral antioxidants has been constrained by poor bioavailability and rapid metabolic clearance. Herein, a coral-like resveratrol nanocomplex was fabricated with a composite natural carrier of sericin and dipotassium glycyrrhizinate through a simple pH-ultrasonic-shifting method in aqueous solution, enabling effective oral delivery of this potent polyphenolic antioxidant. This coral-like nanocomplex markedly enhanced gastrointestinal stability and systemic exposure of resveratrol, increasing oral bioavailability by approximately 6.62-fold and ocular accumulation by 2.49-fold. The nanocomplex exerted robust ocular and systemic antioxidant effects and protected against experimental DED in mice. Oral administration of this nanocomplex protected against benzalkonium chloride (BAC)-induced corneal epithelial damage, tear film dysfunction, and inflammatory responses. Mechanistically, the nanocomplex alleviated oxidative damage through ocular and systemic redox homeostasis. Rather than acting solely on the ocular surface, this orally nanomedicine may provide complementary therapeutic benefits through ocular and systemic redox regulation, while also being associated with modulation of gut microbiota and metabolic profiles. These findings support oral redox intervention as a promising complementary strategy for protecting against the DED, particularly for reducing the burden of prolonged topical treatment.

## Introduction
Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial ocular disorder with steadily increasing prevalence in recent years [1]. Patients commonly experience symptoms such as ocular dryness, foreign body sensation, and visual fatigue, while more severe cases may present with conjunctival hyperemia, pronounced discomfort, swelling, and burning sensations. If left untreated, DED can ultimately compromise visual function and quality of life. Among the various pathological factors involved, inflammation plays a central role in both the onset and progression of DED. It not only triggers disease development but also manifests prominently in affected patients. Consequently, the effective modulation of inflammatory responses is a key therapeutic objective in managing DED. Current drug therapies for DED primarily rely on cyclosporine A, tacrolimus, and corticosteroids. Although these agents are clinically effective, their long-term administration is often associated with undesirable side effects, including drug resistance development, higher cataract formation risk, and elevated intraocular pressure. These limitations highlight the urgent need for alternative and innovative therapeutic strategies to address the growing clinical burden of DED [2].
Oxidative stress is considered to play a substantial role in the onset and progression of DED; it originates from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant capacity, which ultimately leads to cellular apoptosis and nerve damage [3]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies that restore redox homeostasis at the systemic level may offer advantages over purely local interventions. Accordingly, in recent years, systemic antioxidant intervention through oral administration has been increasingly recognized as a potential alternative to long-term topical therapy for DED, particularly in the context of oxidative stress-mediated and inflammation-associated disease progression [4]. Oral administration of antioxidants, particularly polyphenols, represents a practical and patient-friendly approach for preventing and alleviating dry eye symptoms associated with oxidative stress [5]. By targeting systemic oxidative imbalance and its downstream inflammatory consequences, this approach extends therapeutic possibilities beyond conventional topical eye drop-based treatments [[6], [7], [8]]. Resveratrol (RES), a well-known polyphenolic antioxidant, has received considerable attention because of its diverse biological activities. Although the precise effects of RES on human health and disease progression are being actively investigated, accumulating evidence indicates that its antioxidant properties, along with associated anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, are beneficial to ocular tissues [9,10]. However, the clinical translation of oral antioxidant therapy is hindered by inadequate bioavailability [11,12]. For instance, orally administered RES suffers from poor aqueous solubility and extensive first-pass metabolism, leading to low systemic exposure and consequently limited antioxidant efficacy. Therefore, effective strategies to overcome these pharmacokinetic limitations are essential for enabling the clinical application of RES as a oral antioxidant therapy for ocular diseases [9,13,14].
Natural nanocarriers have recently demonstrated significant promise in the oral delivery of polyphenols, owing to their favorable biocompatibility and ability to protect labile bioactive compounds. Our previous studies have demonstrated that the amphiphilic sweetener dipotassium glycyrrhizinate (DG) can improve the gastrointestinal stability of RES through its pH-responsive in situ gelation. DG can also inhibit the activity of uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs), thereby attenuating first-pass metabolism and improving the oral bioavailability of RES [13,14]. Despite its advantages, DG alone exhibits limited delivery stability, and its strong inhibitory effect on metabolic enzymes at high concentrations may pose potential safety concerns. To overcome these limitations, sericin (SER), which constitutes approximately 25–30% of cocoon weight and has traditionally been considered a low-value byproduct of the silk industry, was introduced here as another natural nanocarrier [15]. Beyond its low-cost and sustainable origin, SER has attracted increasing attention because of its intrinsic antioxidant, antibacterial, and cell growth-promoting properties. More importantly, from a drug delivery perspective, SER can form supramolecular nanostructured matrices that may prolong intestinal residence time of polyphenols, making it a suitable complementary component for advanced oral delivery systems and pharmacological applications [16,17].
In the present study, RES was loaded using a composite SER–DG nanocarrier system. Compared with single-carrier formulations, the SER–DG composite nanocarrier exhibited smaller hydrodynamic diameter and improved stability and drug-loading capacity of RES (Scheme 1). Notably, the resulting RES@SER–DG (R@SD) nanocomplex exhibited a distinctive coral-like morphology, suggesting strong interactions between the carrier components rather than simple physical mixing. These enhancements in RES stability and drug-loading capacity contributed to its improved oral bioavailability, increased ocular tissue distribution, and ultimately, robust systemic and ocular antioxidant activity. Oral administration of the R@SD protected against benzalkonium chloride (BAC)-induced corneal epithelial damage, tear flim dysfunction, and inflammatory responses. This protective effect is likely associated with coordinated redox regulation across both ocular tissues and major organs. In addition, oral administration may partially remodel BAC-induced alterations in gut microbiota and metabolic profiles, further supporting the involvement of systemic biological regulation in ocular protection. Rather than relying solely on local ocular delivery, this orally deliverable nanomedicine represents a promising complementary strategy for DED by simultaneously ocular and systemic redox homeostasis. Collectively, these findings support the development of oral antioxidant nanomedicines as a complementary approach to conventional topical therapies.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the design, fabrication, and systemic therapeutic effects of orally delivered resveratrol-based nanocomplex for dry eye disease through enhanced oral RES absorption and biodistribution, and regulate ocular and systemic redox homeostasis (Created in BioRender.com).

## Methods

## Materials and animals
RES and SER (purity ≥ 99% crude protein content) were purchased from Macklin Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). DG (purity ≥ 98%) was sourced from Fujie Co., Ltd (Sanyuan, Shanxi, China), and all other reagents were of analytical grade. Male Sprague–Dawley rats and female C57BL/6 mice were used for the experiments. The Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University approved this study (Approval No. AEEI-2024-286). The animals were treated according to the Association Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

## Preparation and formulation optimization of R@SD nanocomplex
The R@SD nanocomplex and the corresponding single-carrier nanoformulations were prepared according to our previously developed time-saving pH-ultrasonic-shifting method [18]. Briefly, the components were mixed in water, adjusted to pH 12 with 1 M NaOH, and ultrasonicated (200 W, 3 min, 3 s on/3 s off) in an ice bath until a clear solution was obtained. The pH of this solution was then adjusted to 7.0 with HCl, and the solution was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min to remove unencapsulated RES.
The RES-loaded DG (R@D) nanoformulations and RES-loaded SER (R@S) nanoformulations were optimized based on encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading capacity (LC). The EE and LC of RES were determined by disrupting these nanoformulations in methanol; subsequently, the supernatants were analyzed by HPLC at 306 nm following a previously described protocol [19]. The details of these calculation and determination assays are available in Supporting Information. First, various concentrations of DG (15, 30, 45, 60, and 75 mg/mL) or SER (15, 30, 45, 60, and 75 mg/mL) were used to encapsulate the RES (fixed at 5 mg/mL concentration) in preparing the R@D or R@S nanoformulations. Different concentrations of SER were subsequently introduced as a complementary carrier together with DG to prepare R@SD nanoformulations. Additionally, the aqueous storage stability was included as a formulation screening parameter and was evaluated by measuring the retention of RES in the nanoformulations over a 40-day storage period at 4 °C.

## Physicochemical properties of R@SD nanocomplex
Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra, X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra, fluorescence spectra, and UV-vis absorption spectra of R@SD nanocomplex were determined using a Nicolet iS10 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), a D/max-2400 X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan), a F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan), and a U-3900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan), respectively [20]. The micro-morphology of R@SD nanocomplex was obtained from a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-1200EX; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for its aqueous state and a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS, Czechia) for its solid state [21,22].

## Oral bioavailability and biodistribution of R@SD nanocomplex
The in vitro release behavior and cellular uptake of the formulation were first evaluated using the dialysis bag method and Caco-2 cells, respectively. And cellular uptake was visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Leica STELLARIS, Wetzlar, Germany). Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supporting Information. In vivo, the oral bioavailability and tissue distribution of free RES (suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose) and R@SD nanocomplex were evaluated in rats at an equivalent RES dose of 50 mg/kg by following the established protocol [14]. Ocular distribution was assessed at 15, 30, and 120 min after oral administration, with the sampling time points selected based on preliminary bioavailability results. Gastrointestinal distribution was also examined at 15 min post-administration. Detailed procedures for biological sample collection and quantitative analysis are described in Supporting Information. To further visualize tissue distribution, an ex vivo fluorescence imaging study was performed using an IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA). Curcumin (CUR), a naturally fluorescent polyphenol structurally related to RES, was used as a fluorescent probe to fabricate CUR-loaded and RES-loaded SER–DG nanocomplexes (CUR-labeled R@SD) by utilizing a fabrication protocol similar to that described in Section 2.2 [23,24]. Female C57BL/6 mice were orally administered free CUR or CUR-labeled R@SD with matched fluorescence intensity and euthanized by CO2 at predetermined time points (15, 30, and 60 min). Harvested organs and gastrointestinal tissues were then collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging and quantitative analysis [23,25,26].

## In vitro safety profiles of R@SD nanocomplex
The in vitro/ex vivo safety profiles of R@SD nanocomplex were evaluated by cytotoxicity assay, Calcein-AM/PI assay (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd.), hen's egg test on the chorioallantoic membrane (HET-CAM) assay, and hemolysis assay. The details of these assays are available in Supporting Information.

## In vitro antioxidant profiles of R@SD nanocomplex
The in vitro antioxidant profiles of R@SD nanocomplex were evaluated using ferric ion reducing antioxidant potential (FRAP) and 2,2ʹ-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) assays with a series of concentrations and time points. Corneal epithelial cells (CECs), including human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and mouse corneal epithelial cells (MCECs), were induced by hyperosmolar medium as the in vitro dry eye model for further evaluating the in vitro antioxidant activities of R@SD nanocomplex. The cell viability and ROS level of CECs were analyzed by the CCK-8 and ROS assay kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) as reported previously [1]. To comprehensively evaluate the oxidative stress status, the gene expression levels of core antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD1, SOD2, and GPX1) and a key effector of ROS-associated inflammation (MMP9) were measured by qRT-PCR (Applied Biosystems 7500, Foster City, USA) [27]. The relative expression levels of genes were normalized using the nuclear 18S rRNA gene. Primer sequences are provided in Supporting Information (Table S2, Supporting Information). Intracellular Fe2+ levels and mitochondrial membrane potential were measured using the Fe2+ detection (Elabscience, E-BC-F101) and the tetramethylrhodamine (TMRM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) assay, respectively, according to the manufacturer's instructions [1].

## In vivo effect of R@SD nanocomplex on DED in mice
Fifty female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) without ocular surface abnormalities were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 10, per group), including PBS, BAC, RES, SD, and R@SD groups. Except for the PBS group, DED was induced by topical administration of 5 μL of 0.1% BAC to both eyes once daily for 14 consecutive days. During DED induction, mice received oral administration of normal phosphate buffer saline (PBS and BAC groups), free RES (suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose) (RES group), SD nanocarrier (SD group), or R@SD nanocomplex (R@SD group). For formulations given, the administered dose was standardized to an equivalent RES dose of 50 mg/kg/day. And the SD group received an equivalent SD carrier dose (including the 450 mg/kg/day SER and 450 mg/kg/day DG) to that contained in the R@SD formulation. Oral administration was initiated concurrently with DED induction and continued for 14 days.
After 14 days of DED induction and administration, tear volume and corneal epithelial integrity were determined using phenol red cotton thread and sodium fluorescein staining assays by an investigator who was blinded to treatment allocation (Z. L.). Following scarification and fixation, the eyeballs were embedded in paraffin and sliced into 4-μm-thick sections for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining. Immunohistochemical staining of corneal sections was performed to assess inflammatory-related markers (IL-1β, MMP9, TNF-α, and IL-10), corneal integrity-related proteins (CK3+12, ZO-1, and Occludin), and oxidative stress-associated markers (8-OHdG, NOX4, and SOD2). The details of the immunohistochemical staining method are provided in Supporting Information (Table S3).
To further benchmark the therapeutic performance of R@SD against current clinical management of DED, two clinically approved anti-inflammatory eye drops, cyclosporine A (CsA) (Zirun®; Shenyang Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) (twice daily) and lifitegrast (LiF) (Langyueming®, Chengdu Kanghong Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd.) (twice daily), together with sodium hyaluronate (HA) artificial tears (HYLO®; URSAPHARM Arzneimittel GmbH.) (three times daily), were included as positive controls in the BAC-induced DED model. After 14 days of DED induction and administration, tear volume and corneal epithelial integrity were determined using phenol red cotton thread and sodium fluorescein staining assays by an investigator who was blinded to treatment allocation (Z. L.). Following scarification and fixation, the eyeballs were embedded in paraffin and sliced into 4-μm-thick sections for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining.

## In vivo biocompatibility of R@SD nanocomplex
After 14 days of DED induction and administration, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were collected for H&E staining. Serum biochemical parameters, including aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), creatinine (CREA), and blood urea nitrogen (UREA), were analyzed to evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of R@SD nanocomplex. Furthermore, an additional 21-day repeated oral administration study was conducted to evaluate the preliminary biosafety of R@SD. In addition to the above-mentioned safety evaluations, body weight changes and daily food intake were monitored throughout the experimental period. Additional blood biochemical parameters, including creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase-2 (LDH2), as well as routine hematological parameters, including hemoglobin (HGB), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (NEUT), and platelet count (PLT), were further analyzed.

## Fecal sample collection and analysis
After 14 days of DED induction and administration, fecal samples were obtained from mice, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and maintained at −80 °C until further processing. Microbial community composition was analyzed by metagenomic sequencing, and untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry [28,29]. These data were analyzed on the free online platform of Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com). Detailed analysis procedures are provided in Supporting Information.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used to assess the normality of the data. Differences among groups were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v10.2 by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For comparisons among multiple groups, ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple-comparison test was used for all-pairwise comparisons, whereas Dunnett's multiple-comparison test was used when multiple treatment groups were compared with a designated control. P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference between groups.

## Results and discussion

## Optimization of the R@SD nanocomplex formulation
The poor aqueous solubility of polyphenols severely limits their oral bioavailability and subsequent in vivo efficacy. To address this issue, we previously used DG as a biosurfactant to construct polyphenol-loaded nanomicelles [30,31]. These studies demonstrated that DG possesses distinct advantages for encapsulating polyphenols in both ocular and oral delivery contexts. DG can enhance the aqueous solubility of polyphenols, thereby facilitating their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in topical ophthalmic applications. DG can also transform polyphenol from crystalline to amorphous state for enhancing its absorption in oral application. Notably, our previous work revealed that DG improves the gastrointestinal stability of RES through pH-responsive in situ gelation, while simultaneously inhibiting the activity of UGTs [13,14]. This dual mechanism attenuates first-pass metabolism and substantially enhances the oral bioavailability of RES. Therefore, to overcome the intrinsic pharmacokinetic limitations of polyphenols and enable effective oral intervention in DED, DG was selected again as the primary delivery carrier for RES in the present study (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1Design, optimization, and characterization of R@SD nanocomplex. The EE and LC of R@D in different formulations are shown in Fig. 1B. R@D 5:15 had the lowest EE of 10.46% ± 0.72% and LC of 3.37% ± 0.22%. Increasing the proportion of DG markedly improved EE, which reached 86.3% in the R@D 5:45 formulation. However, further increasing DG content only slightly enhanced EE while reducing LC, indicating a diminishing return in drug-loading performance at higher carrier ratios. Based on its favorable EE and LC profiles, R@D 5:45 was initially selected for further evaluation. However, long-term aqueous stability studies revealed a critical limitation. As shown in Fig. 1C, both R@D 5:45 and R@D 5:60 formulations were not stable in the 40-day test, whereas the R@D 5:75 formulation remained stable. Although the R@D 5:45 formulation had the largest EE and highest LC, it showed poor aqueous stability. Similarly, the R@D 5:75 formulation had the largest EE and good aqueous stability but displayed the lowest LC among these three formulations (P < 0.05). Although formulations with higher DG content exhibited improved stability over 40 days, they simultaneously showed reduced LC. In contrast, formulations with higher LC showed inferior aqueous stability (P < 0.05). Taken together, balancing the LC and aqueous stability is a challenging issue for the DG delivery system. Increasing DG content is a conventional strategy to improve aqueous stability; however, this inevitably compromises LC. More importantly, excessive DG may lead to marked inhibition of the activity of UGTs, potentially disturbing metabolic homeostasis. Therefore, in the present study, resolving the imbalance between high drug loading and adequate aqueous stability was the key challenge in obtaining the optimal formulation.
Prior to constructing the composite system, the encapsulation capacity of SER alone was evaluated (Fig. 1D). Regardless of the amount added, the EE remained below 10%, indicating that SER alone is insufficient for efficiently encapsulating poorly water-soluble RES. SER was subsequently introduced into the optimized R@D 5:45 formulation to construct the composite R@SD nanocomplex (Fig. 1A). Notably, the incorporation of SER markedly improved the aqueous stability of the system compared with R@D 5:45. As shown in Fig. 1E, the R@SD 5:45:45, R@SD 5:60:45, and R@SD 5:75:45 formulations showed higher aqueous stabilities than the R@SD 5:15:45 and R@SD 5:30:45 formulations (P < 0.05). Considering both stability enhancement and formulation economy, R@SD 5:45:45 was selected as the optimal composite formulation for subsequent studies. R@D and R@SD were then compared based on EE, aqueous stability, and particle size indicators (Fig. 1F). Comparative analysis revealed that the incorporation of SER further increased EE from 86.26% ± 4.39% to 94.59% ± 2.12%, indicating a synergistic improvement in drug encapsulation. After 60 days of storage, R@SD retained 87.07% ± 2.76% of RES, whereas R@D retained only 43.18% ± 8.39%, demonstrating a substantial enhancement in long-term aqueous stability. A noteworthy observation is that this improved aqueous stability of R@SD has higher clinical transformation prospects than our previously fabricated RES@SPI-DG and R-Q@P-D nanoparticles [13,14]. The DLS comparison revealed that the hydrodynamic diameters of SD, R@S, and R@SD were below 30 nm, indicating that these systems were smaller than the DG and R@D nanomicelles. This phenomenon suggests that strong intermolecular interactions between SER and DG remarkably modulate the microstructure of the SD composite system.

## Interaction mechanisms in R@SD nanocomplex
To determine the interaction forces between RES, SER, and DG, multiple spectroscopic analyses were performed. As shown in Fig. 1G, the characteristic absorption peaks of SER appeared at 1539.25 and 1643.47 cm−1, while the characteristic absorption peaks of DG appeared at 1038.32 and 1597.30 cm−1. Following SER and DG mixing, the characteristic absorption peaks of SD appeared at 1041.75, 1539.22, 1598.18, and 1644.31 cm−1, which displayed the characteristic peaks of SER and DG. Furthermore, compared with individual SER (3268.47 cm−1) and DG (3341.00 cm−1), the SER-DG complex exhibited a red shift of the O–H stretching band to 3246.08 cm−1, suggesting an altered hydrogen-bonding environment and the involvement of intermolecular interactions during supramolecular assembly. The characteristic absorption peaks of RES appeared at 672.97, 830.26, 1150.88, 1380.59, and 1580.61 cm−1. No new characteristic absorption peaks were observed in the FTIR spectrum of R@SD compared with those of RES, SD, and the physical mixture (R&S&D), suggesting that the R@SD assembly is driven by noncovalent interactions rather than chemical bond formation. XRD spectra showed the SER and DG had no crystallization peaks, whereas crystallization peaks were observed for RES at 6.59°, 16.34°, 19.18°, and 28.35° (Fig. 1H). Crystallization peaks of RES appeared in R&S&D but disappeared in R@D and R@SD, indicating that the SD carrier could transform the RES from a crystalline to amorphous state. This amorphization can enhance dissolution behavior and oral absorption of poorly water-soluble compounds. The additional diffraction peaks observed in R@D and R@SD were attributed to residual NaCl generated during the pH-ultrasound-shifting process, as confirmed by control experiments. The endogenous fluorescence intensity of SER, SD, and R@SD gradually decreased, indicating that RES and DG interacted with SER as the quenching agents (Fig. 1I). Quenching of the fluorescence intensity suggests the presence of hydrophobic interaction forces between the quenching agents and SER. UV–vis spectra confirmed the successful incorporation of RES into the R@SD nanocomplex, as evidenced by the retention of characteristic absorption features (Fig. 1J).

## Micro-morphology of R@SD nanocomplex
Micro-morphology critically influences the physicochemical and biological performance of nanocarriers [[32], [33], [34]]. Accordingly, we used a SEM and TEM to examine the microstructural characteristics of freeze-dried and freshly prepared R@SD, respectively. As observed in SEM images, the lyophilized RES appeared as a long rod structure (Fig. 1K). Both lyophilized DG and SER showed the spherical structure, whereas the structural observation of lyophilized SD was not clear. The structure of lyophilized RES, DG, and SER could be observed in the image of lyophilized R&S&D. Notably, the structure of lyophilized R@SD appeared as a worm-like short rod. Considering that the lyophilization process might influence the micro-morphology of samples, the aqueous state images were recorded by TEM, as shown in Fig. 1L. Because of the poor water solubility of RES, its TEM image could not be recorded. The freshly prepared SER showed silk-like nanostructure, while the freshly prepared DG showed spherical nanomicelle structure. Interestingly, the freshly prepared SD showed coral-like nanostructure that differentiated it from SER and DG. The TEM images of RES-loaded R@S, R@D, and R@SD showed similar micro-morphology compared with free SER, DG, and SD carriers. The coral-like nanostructure appeared again in the R@SD image, confirming that this shape did not occur accidently. This unique morphology is likely attributed to cooperative noncovalent interactions between SER and DG, resulting in supramolecular self-assembly.

## In vitro release behavior, in vitro cellular uptake, in vivo oral bioavailability, and biodistribution
In vitro release behavior and in vitro cellular uptake are widely used as the preliminary indicator to evaluate formulation performance and to predict potential in vivo pharmacokinetic outcomes. Under simulated gastrointestinal fluid conditions, R@SD exhibited a markedly enhanced release profile of RES compared with free RES. The cumulative release of RES from R@SD reached 53.74% ± 7.57% at 8 h, which was significantly higher than that of free RES (12.38% ± 1.45%) (Fig. 2A). In vitro cellular uptake results showed that the fluorescence intensity of fluorescence probe in R@D and R@SD increased significantly compared with free fluorescence probe (Figure S1). This result indicated the DG or SD nanocarrier increased the RES uptake efficiency by Caco-2. Moreover, the comparable fluorescence intensities observed for R@D and R@SD suggested that the two nanocarriers exhibited similar cellular uptake profiles in Caco-2 cells. These findings suggest that R@SD improves the dissolution and release behavior of RES under simulated gastrointestinal conditions and may potentially contribute to enhance oral bioavailability. The oral pharmacokinetics of R@SD was subsequently determined. As shown in Fig. 2B, the oral administration of R@SD resulted in a plasma maximum concentration (Cmax) of RES of 2.03 μg/mL at 15 min, representing a 12.68-fold increase compared with that for free RES (0.16 μg/mL) (Table S1). Moreover, the R@SD group exhibited a higher RES concentration versus time curve (AUC(0-t)) of 2.66 mg/L/h in the plasma, which was 6.62-fold that of free RES (0.40 mg/L/h) (Fig. 2C). To the best of our knowledge, these values and the extent of improvement of AUC(0-t) and Cmax were higher than those reported in some studies [13,35,36]. Additionally, to investigate whether the introduction of SER merely modified the formulation composition or fundamentally altered the delivery behavior, R@D was included as a control group. Unlike R@SD, R@D exhibited a spherical morphology and poor storage stability, becoming unstable within a few days. As shown in Figures S2 and S3, R@D showed markedly faster plasma clearance of RES than R@SD. Although the two formulations exhibited comparable Cmax values, the AUC(0–t) of R@D was significantly lower than that of R@SD (P = 0.0029). These findings suggest that the incorporation of SER not only changed the morphology of the nanocomplex but also improved its stability and sustained systemic exposure, thereby contributing to the superior oral delivery performance of R@SD. On the basis of our previous findings, the improved bioavailability of RES may be attributed to multiple factors. The solubilizing effect of DG together with the stabilizing property of SER likely enhanced the oral absorption of RES. DG may function as a reversible dual-target inhibitor of intestinal metabolic enzymes, thereby reducing the metabolic degradation of RES [14]. Most important of all, the introduction of SER appears to further stabilize the nanocomplex, resulting in the formation of a unique coral-like architecture with improved structural integrity and prolonged circulation behavior. Next, we assessed ocular distribution; the Tmax value observed in ocular tissues aligned well with that in plasma (Fig. 2E). This improvement appears slightly greater than those reported for other polyphenol-based delivery systems [7,8,37]. Additionally, R@SD nanocomplex was prepared through a simple pH-ultrasonic-shifting method in an aqueous solution without organic solvents; this approach may offer advantages in terms of manufacturing simplicity and clinical translation compared with other polyphenol-based delivery systems [7,8,37,38]. Furthermore, as shown in Table S1, the plasma half-life (t1/2) and mean residence time (MRT(0-t)) of R@SD were 0.87-fold and 0.69-fold those of free RES, respectively. Considering the overall distribution characteristics, these results suggest that R@SD may undergo rapid gastrointestinal absorption followed by systemic distribution to major tissues.
To further validate our speculation, CUR was employed as a surrogate fluorescent probe to label the polyphenol-based nanocomplex and visualize its in vivo distribution [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Following oral administration with free CUR and CUR-labeled R@SD, the presence of fluorescence could be observed in gastrointestinal tissues and major organs at different time points (Fig. 2G and H). The significantly higher intestinal fluorescence intensity at early time points (15 min) indicates accelerated mucosal permeation, which may be attributed to the nanoscale dimensions and coral-like surface architecture that increase interfacial contact and facilitate epithelial interaction (Fig. 2I). Enhanced signals in the liver and kidneys (15 and 30 min) further imply rapid systemic distribution of RES and physiological clearance pathways (Fig. 2J and K). Collectively, these findings establish a coherent in vitro–in vivo correlation and demonstrate that R@SD not only improves dissolution but also modulates absorption kinetics and tissue distribution of RES, thereby enhancing systemic therapeutic potential for DED.

## In vitro safety evaluation of R@SD nanocomplex
The in vitro safety profile of R@SD was evaluated through cell viability assays, including CCK-8 assay and Calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining. As shown in Fig. 3A, both HCECs and MCECs maintained high viability across the tested concentrations, with 1 μg/mL identified as a safe working concentration. To further exclude potential cytotoxicity originating from individual components, RES and SD were separately assessed at the same concentration. Neither component exhibited significant cytotoxic effects at 1 μg/mL (Fig. 3B), indicating that the observed cytocompatibility was intrinsic to the formulation constituents. Consistently, live/dead staining further confirmed the favorable cytocompatibility of R@SD, as evidenced by predominant green fluorescence and minimal PI-positive cells (Fig. 3C). The potential ocular irritation of R@SD was subsequently assessed by the HET-CAM assay. As illustrated in Fig. 3D, marked vascular hemorrhage was observed in the 0.1 M NaOH group (positive control), whereas no visible vascular damage occurred in the R@SD or component groups, comparable to the NaCl negative control group. The results of trypan blue staining further supported these findings, with the NaOH group exhibiting the highest dye weight. In hemolysis assay, visible hemolysis was observed only in the Triton and NaOH groups, whereas erythrocytes in the remaining groups precipitated at the bottom of the tubes (Fig. 3E). The pale-yellow coloration observed in the R@SD group was attributed to its intrinsic color rather than to hemoglobin release, which was further confirmed by quantitative hemolysis analysis. The calculated hemolysis percentages remained within the acceptable safety limit (lower than 5%). Collectively, these results demonstrate that R@SD exhibits favorable cytocompatibility, negligible irritation potential, and excellent hemocompatibility in vitro. The absence of acute toxicity may be attributed to the plant-derived nature of its components, which are recognized as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [14]. Importantly, R@SD was fabricated using a previously developed pH-ultrasonic-shifting strategy [18], which integrates pH-shifting and ultrasonic process in an aqueous environment without the use of any organic solvents or synthetic surfactants. Conventional nanoparticle fabrication methods often require organic solvents or surfactants that may introduce concerns related to residual toxicity and necessitate additional purification steps such as dialysis or rotary evaporation. By eliminating these steps, the present one-pot preparation approach simplifies the manufacturing process, potentially reducing production time and cost while improving translational feasibility. Excessive systemic ROS can enhance inflammatory signaling and contribute to ocular surface inflammation and epithelial dysfunction. Notably, although several ROS-scavenging eye drops have been developed for DED treatment [42,43], strategies for regulating systemic antioxidant levels remain largely unexplored. Therefore, we further examined whether the protective effects of R@SD were associated with systemic antioxidant regulation. Current clinical treatment of DED primarily relies on topical therapies targeting local inflammation. However, the present findings suggest that systemic oxidative imbalance may also play a critical role in disease progression, which was consistent with some latest clinical and fundamental research studies [4,38,41,44]. In this context, the robust antioxidant capacity achieved through the oral administration of R@SD may provide a complementary strategy to conventional topical treatments by maintaining ocular and systemic redox homeostasis.

## In vivo biosafety evaluation of R@SD
Because biosafety is a critical issue in the translational development of nanomedicines, the oral compatibility of R@SD was further investigated after the in vivo efficacy study. Histological examination of the major organs and retinal tissues revealed no apparent pathological abnormalities or tissue damage in the PBS, RES, SD, or R@SD groups (Figures S4A and S4B). Consistently, hematological biochemical analyses showed no significant differences among the PBS, RES, SD, and R@SD groups in the indicators of liver function (AST and ALT) or kidney function (CREA and UREA) (Figures S4C and S4D). These results indicate that the oral administration of this nanocomplex did not induce detectable hepatic or renal toxicity.

Previous studies have shown that DED models exhibit gut microbial dysbiosis, and interventions such as probiotics, prebiotics, and polyphenols can alleviate ocular inflammation by modulating gut microbiota composition and function, supporting the existence of a gut–eye axis [44,[46], [47], [48]]. To evaluate whether the oral administration of R@SD modulates the gut microbiota under BAC-induced DED, microbial diversity and community structure were comprehensively analyzed. These changes were consistent with the restoration of Shannon and Simpson indices, suggesting that R@SD does not simply enrich specific taxa but rather mitigates the overdominance of certain genera and promotes a more balanced microbial distribution [51].
Differential abundance analysis identified several genera with significant differences among the groups, including Muribaculum, Duncaniella, Lepagella, Ligilactobacillus, Allobaculum, Faecalibaculum, Ileibacterium, Acetatifactor, Eubacterium, and Hungatella (Fig. 10H). In the BAC-treated group, Muribaculum, Faecalibaculum, and Ileibacterium were notably enriched (28.44% ± 7.95%, 5.08% ± 5.06%, and 3.68% ± 3.58%, respectively), whereas their abundance was markedly reduced following R@SD intervention (12.81% ± 4.68%, 0.40% ± 0.16%, and 0.02% ± 0.01%, respectively). Members of the Muribaculaceae family produce short-chain fatty acids such as acetate, propionate, and succinate through polysaccharide fermentation, and their dysregulation is associated with enhanced oxidative stress and inflammatory conditions [51]. The excessive enrichment of Muribaculum in the BAC-treated group and its normalization following oral R@SD administration is consistent with a shift toward a microbial composition more closely resembling that of healthy mice. However, this modulation was not a complete restoration. For example, Allobaculum and Lepagella remained at relatively low levels in the R@SD group compared with those in the PBS group. These findings indicate that R@SD induces selective microbial remodeling rather than broadly restoring the microbiota to control levels. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that oral microbiota-targeted interventions often induce specific rather than universal microbial changes [47,52,53]. Overall, oral R@SD was associated with selective remodeling of the gut microbiota. It reduced the expansion of several BAC-enriched genera and increased the relative abundance of multiple bacterial taxa. Notably, the restoration of Lactobacillus-related genera, such as Ligilactobacillus, is consistent with previous reports on resveratrol-mediated microbiota modulation [54]. Furthermore, the recovery of short-chain fatty acid-producing genera (e.g., Acetatifactor, Eubacterium, and Hungatella) raising the possibility that gut-derived metabolites, including butyrate, may participate in gut–eye axis-associated biological regulation.

## Metabolomic profiling associated with R@SD intervention
To determine whether microbiota remodeling was accompanied by functional metabolic changes, untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed comparing the BAC and R@SD groups. A total of 2572 metabolic features were detected, among which 679 were annotated in KEGG databases. Multivariate analysis revealed clear metabolic differences between the two groups. In principal component analysis (PCA), PC1 and PC2 explained 38.00% and 17.00% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 11A). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) showed that the first two components explained 31.60% and 18.00% of the variance, and the ANOSIM analysis further confirmed significant group separation (R = 0.695, P = 0.001) (Fig. 11B). These findings indicate that R@SD intervention was associated with a distinct metabolic profile rather than isolated metabolite changes, which was consistent with previously reported metabolomic alterations in DED [55]. Volcano plot analysis identified 263 significantly altered metabolites (VIP > 1, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05, and corresponding to a ≥ 2-fold change), including 186 downregulated and 77 upregulated metabolites (Fig. 11C). The predominance of downregulated metabolites suggests that R@SD primarily reduces the accumulation of abnormal metabolites under BAC-induced stress, indicating broad metabolic alterations following oral R@SD administration. Angiotensin II analogs, 5′-deoxyadenosine, Asp-Val, steroid conjugates, and cholesterol-related derivatives were among the most significantly downregulated metabolites (Fig. 11D), indicating modulation of lipid inflammatory mediators, nucleotide metabolism, and peptide turnover. As shown previously, increased lipid inflammatory mediators (such as PGE2) are associated with Th17 cell activation and inflammatory progression in BAC-induced DED models, whereas antioxidant interventions suppress this process [56]; this finding is consistent with the observed downregulation trends in the present study. Notably, 3-methylindole was significantly reduced (log2FC = −5.5), suggesting the involvement of tryptophan-related metabolic pathways. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the annotated subset of the 263 differential metabolites identified using an OPLS-DA-derived variable importance in projection (FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05) (Fig. 11E). Among the pathways, the lowest P values were observed for phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis; phenylalanine metabolism; biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids; phenylpropanoid biosynthesis; and tryptophan metabolism. Nucleotide metabolism also ranked among the lower-P-value terms, showing that the differential set was not limited to aromatic amino acid-associated metabolites. The recurrence of phenylalanine- and tryptophan-related terms among these pathways indicates that aromatic amino acid metabolism differed between the R@SD and BAC groups. KEGG also assigned metabolites to phenylpropanoid and plant secondary-metabolite biosynthesis. These labels denote shared compound mappings rather than plant-specific biosynthetic activity. Because RES is a polyphenol subject to intestinal microbial biotransformation [58,59], the phenylpropanoid-related annotations are compatible with treatment-associated changes in polyphenol-derived compounds.
Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated between 20 differential bacterial genera and the 20 most abundant metabolites within the FDR-defined differential set across BAC and R@SD samples (Fig. 11F). The heatmap contained two opposing correlation blocks. Ligilactobacillus, Hungatella, Oscillibacter, Eubacterium, Acetatifactor, and Roseburia were predominantly negatively correlated with the block containing thymine, L-phenylalanine, adenine, and kynurenic acid, but positively correlated with the smaller block containing indole, indole-3-carboxylic acid, and indole-3-lactic acid. Lepagella, Muribaculum, and Allobaculum showed the reverse pattern. Indole-related metabolites showed the clearest reciprocal associations with the two genus clusters. Sverall, oral R@SD was associated with coordinated alterations in gut microbiota composition and metabolic profiles. These findings support the potential involvement of gut microbiota-related metabolic regulation in the therapeutic effects against DED. However, further studies are required to establish a causal relationship.

## Conclusion
In summary, we developed a coral-like RES nanocomplex using a composite natural carrier of SER and DG through a scalable, solvent-free pH-ultrasonic-shifting method. This nanocomplex markedly improved the gastrointestinal stability and oral bioavailability of RES, leading to enhanced systemic exposure and ocular accumulation. In a BAC-induced murine model of DED, oral administration of the nanocomplex effectively protected the ocular surface against epithelial injury, tear film dysfunction, and inflammatory responses. Collectively, our findings suggest that these therapeutic effects are associated with improved ocular and systemic redox homeostasis, accompanied by partial remodeling of BAC-induced gut microbiota and metabolic profiles. These findings support oral redox intervention as a promising complementary strategy for ocular protection and provide a therapeutic framework for the development of orally deliverable nanomedicines for DED.
