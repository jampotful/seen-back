# Bovine ocular microbiome: the next frontier in managing Pinkeye in cattle

## Abstract
Infectious bovine keratoconjunctivitis (IBK), or pinkeye, represents a significant economic challenge to dairy and beef cattle industries resulting in decreased productivity and increased treatment costs. The current IBK prevention and control strategies in cattle face challenges owing to the multifactorial nature of the disease, the rise of antibiotic resistance in IBK pathogens, and inconsistent efficacy of IBK vaccines. Recent efforts in metagenomic characterization of the eye microbiome in humans and animals, including cattle, have revealed that the ocular surface is colonized by relatively diverse and dynamic microbial community that is essential for maintaining ocular health and can be leveraged to enhance resistance against infectious ocular diseases. In this narrative review, we provide comprehensive insights into the ocular microbiota by summarizing the amplicon and metagenomic sequencing- and culture-based studies conducted in cattle, and by reviewing relevant findings from humans and other animal species. We also explore the potential of the ocular microbiome as a new frontier in managing IBK. Finally, we examine the gut-eye-microbiome axis and discuss its potential contribution in improving the resistance of cattle against IBK.

## Introduction
Infectious bovine keratoconjunctivitis (IBK), also known as pinkeye, is the most prevalent and economically important ocular disease affecting both dairy and beef cattle worldwide [1]. The disease is particularly detrimental to animal welfare and productivity, causing substantial losses in cattle industries through decreased weight gain, reduced milk yield, and increased treatment costs. For instance, calves that developed IBK were reported to have weaning weights 8.9 kg lower than healthy calves. This is likely due to the impact of IBK-related pain, impaired vision, and poor feed intake on growth performance [2–4]. In dairy cattle, decrease in milk production or loss of body condition scores may result from the discomfort, and loss of appetite associated with blindness [5]. The IBK is characterized by inflammation of the cornea and conjunctiva, manifesting with symptoms such as blepharospasm, epiphora, photophobia, corneal edema, opacity, vascularization, and ulceration, potentially corneal perforation and blindness [1, 2, 6, 7]. The clinical symptoms are primarily confined to the eyes, including conjunctivitis and/or keratitis with a notable percentage (10%) of cases leading to corneal ulcers [6]. Recently, it was reported that the average annual between-herd prevalence of IBK varies widely by species, age and geographical region. In Bos indicus cattle morbidity rates range from 0.6% in cows to 2% in calves while in Bos taurus, rates range from 3% in cows to 10.0% calves [2, 6]. Moreover, IBK is characterized by a rapid transmission, often spreading within 30 days of appearance of clinical manifestations [6]. The severity of IBK also varies widely, ranging from mild clinical presentations with transient discomfort to severe cases resulting in permanent loss of vision if left untreated [2, 6]. The IBK is still the most prevalent ocular disease in cattle ever since it was first identified in dairy cattle in Nebraska, USA, in 1889, where it was termed “keratitis contagiosa” [8]. The subsequent outbreaks were reported as “contagious ophthalmia” in England in 1897 and as “keratitis infectiosa” in the Netherlands in 1911 [6]. Currently, the global prevalence of IBK still varies widely, influenced by regional, seasonal, and herd-specific factors such as age and herd size [2]. For instance, in the USA, IBK within-herd incidence in yearling beef cattle ranged from 57 to 98%, compared to 10.6% in Australian cattle in 2018 [2]. The severity of IBK outbreaks also varies depending on the combination risk factors associated with disease occurrence, and the affected animals often remaining carriers for months post-recovery [1, 9–11].
The risk factors associated with IBK development constitute the epidemiological triad: (1) environmental factors such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation, dust, fomites and flies [2, 12, 13], (2) host factors such as breed (heritability ranges from 0.00 to 0.28 depending on the breed) [12, 14], age, immune status, concurrent illnesses, nutritional status, body condition score, and stressors [15, 16], and (3) presence of bacterial pathogens including Moraxella spp. [17] and Mycoplasma spp. [18]. Moraxella bovis, a gram-negative coccobacillus, is considered to be the primary cause of IBK [19–21], and this bacterial pathogen has remarkable adaptability to the ocular environment, thriving on ocular surfaces like conjunctival sac and corneal surface amidst predisposing factors such as ocular trauma and environmental stressors [1]. M. bovis cells can attach to corneal epithelial cells through pili and produce hemolysins and cytotoxins and can cause tissue necrosis and inflammation in the eye [22–24]. M. bovis is also highly contagious and can spread rapidly within a cattle herd by direct contact or environmental transmission [1]. Moraxella bovoculi is also considered an important agent associated with IBK [25, 26]. In addition, Mycoplasma bovis [18, 27], Neisseria ovis [28], and other opportunist bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus spp. have also been reported to be involved in IBK pathogenesis, even though a decisive causal association is yet to be determined [7, 29].
Despite several decades of research, the currently available preventive and therapeutic strategies for IBK remain inadequate. For instance, M. bovis and M. bovoculi, the primary pathogens of IBK, exhibit increasing multidrug resistance, with notable insensitivity to oxacillin, methicillin, and carbenicillin [30]. The biofilm forming ability of Moraxella further reduces antibiotic efficacy and contributes to its multidrug resistance highlighting the necessity for developing antibiotic alternatives to prevent and control IBK in cattle [31]. In addition, there is escalating threat of antibiotic resistance in both veterinary and human medicine which demands an urgent pursuit of effective antibiotic alternatives [32]. The effectiveness of the available commercial vaccines using M. bovis fimbriae is limited due to high antigenic diversity of IBK etiologic agents [33–35]. The commercial and autogenous vaccines for IBK have also demonstrated inconsistent effectiveness, as evidenced by a recent trial where the autogenous vaccine lowered IBK incidence, yet antibody levels did not align with protective outcomes [36]. In addition, the non-antibiotic approaches, such as fly control [37], non-antibiotic based topical applications [38–41], and the use of predatory bacterium Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus 109 J [42], remain in preliminary stages of development with limited efficacy [43].
Recent advancements in metagenomic sequencing have provided new insights into the ocular microbiota in humans [44] and animals, including cattle [7, 45, 46]. Studies based on metagenomic characterization of the ocular microbiota in cattle have revealed that the ocular surface is colonized by a relatively diverse and dynamic microbial community, and that the ocular microbiome is important in eye health with the potential to improve cattle resistance against IBK [7, 45, 46]. The objectives of the present narrative review are: (1) to summarize amplicon and metagenomic sequencing studies on the ocular microbiota in cattle, along with relevant findings from humans and other species; (2) to explore the potential of the ocular microbiome to as the next frontier for managing IBK; and (3) to review the gut-eye-microbiome axis and its potential for improving resistance of cattle against IBK.

## The ocular microbiota
Next-generation sequencing-based results have revealed that there is a relatively diverse and complex microbial community present in the ocular surface of humans and animals known as ocular microbiota [47–49]. The ocular microbiota encompasses the diverse community of microorganisms residing on the ocular surface of healthy cattle (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The ocular surface microbiota, while limited in biomass, has been documented in several healthy animal species [7, 46, 49–54] and also in association with human ocular diseases [55–57]. Various studies characterizing the ocular microbiota in different hosts including humans, cattle, horses, pigs and lab animals are summarized in Table 1. Human studies using 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing revealed that the ocular microbiota is primarily composed of bacterial phyla Proteobacteria, Bacillota and Actinobacteria with common genera within these phyla being Staphylococcus, Bacillus, Paracoccus, Acinobacter, Corynebacterium, Moraxella and Propionibacterium [47, 55, 58, 59]. Horses also have been reported to possess a diverse ocular microbiota with the phylum Proteobacteria and genera Pedobacter, Massilia, Pseudomonas, Sphingomonas, Suttonella and Verticia dominating the community [52]. In addition, pigs have also been reported to harbor a relatively rich microbial community in their ocular surface, which is dominated by Bacillota, Proteobacteria and Fusobacteria phyla, and by Streptococcus, Rothia, Moraxella, Staphylococcus, Lactobacillus and Actinobacillus genera [51]. The bacterial composition differs across ocular surface sites, with fewer taxa observed on the corneal surface compared to the conjunctiva [51]. Results from 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing-based characterization of ocular microbiota cats [49], and dogs [50] also revealed the presence of relatively diverse ocular microbiota in those companion animals. A 16 S rRNA sequencing-based study conducted on lab animals including mice, rabbits and guinea pigs have also showed that lab animals harbor distinct and unique ocular microbiota, dominated by phyla Proteobacteria, Bacillota and Deinococcota, and by Acinetobacter, Corynebacterium, and Hymenobacter genera [60]. Most of the studies reported that Moraxella spp. are also a part of healthy ocular microbiota of these animal species [7, 46].

The ocular microbiota of cattle differs significantly from that of humans and most other animals, characterized by unique species such as M. bovoculi and M. bovis [7, 61], though both share some microbial similarities, such as the presence of Proteobacteria and Bacillota, while also displaying notable differences [7, 61, 62]. Recently, Bartenslager and colleagues investigated the bovine ocular microbiome using 227 calves, with an average starting age of 65 days, and monitored their progress until weaning, which occurred around 204 days [46]. They identified a total of 5,820 unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) which were dominated by three phyla: Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria, accounting for 79% of total ocular microbiota. Meanwhile, at genus level, Pasteurella, Mycoplasma, Moraxella, Escherichia-Schigella, and Acinetobacter were the most prevalent. In another study, Gafen and colleagues collected 604 ocular samples from both beef and dairy cattle across 8 different states in the U.S. and individually classified 228 eyes as IBK infected and 376 eyes as normal (Less than 1 year old: 573 eyes, 1–5 years old: 24 eyes, Over 6 years old: 7 eyes) [7]. Using the 16 S rRNA sequencing, they detected a total of 21,879 ASVs and filtered it down to 20,191 ASVs for downstream analysis [7]. They were able to identify seven prominent bacterial phyla, which included Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Deferribacterota, Bacillota, Fusobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobiota [7]. The most relatively abundant genera included Corynebacterium, Moraxella, Pasteurella, Rothia, Staphylococcus, Mycoplasma, and Proteus [7]. Recently, our research group also conducted a study on ocular swab samples collected from 14 calves at approximately 30 h after birth using both qPCR and 16S rRNA gene sequencing [45]. We observed more than 420 ASVs in ocular microbiota which were dominated by Bacillota, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota and Bacteroidota [45]. At genus level, Streptococcus, Clostridium, Moraxella, Escherichia-Shigella, Corynebacterium, Acinebacter and Planococcus were the most relatively abundant in the eyes of these newborn calves [45, 62]. To date, most studies involving culture-based techniques have primarily focused on isolating pathogenic agents associated with IBK for diagnostic purposes, with limited attention given to the characterization of the broader ocular microbiome [6, 7, 18, 26]. Our group recently characterized the culturable fraction of the bovine ocular microbiota using extensive culturing [45, 62, 63]. We isolated 658 bacterial strains from the bovine ocular surface and identified Bacillus, Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, and Moraxella amongst the most dominant genera. These 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing -and culture-based studies together suggest that there is a relatively diverse and dynamic microbial community present in the bovine ocular surface.

## Potential roles of the ocular microbiota in maintaining ocular health and immune response
The ocular surface which encompasses the cornea, conjunctiva, and tear film, simultaneously supports vision and acts as an immunological unit of the eye [64]. The ocular microbiota may contribute to ocular innate immunity by providing rapid, nonspecific protection against corneal infections [64, 65]. It’s relatively well established that in healthy hosts, the ocular epithelial cells tolerate commensal bacteria without triggering adverse inflammatory reaction [66] suggesting the symbiotic relationship between the host innate immune response and commensal microbiota colonization in the eye [66]. Recently, we proposed that the ocular microbiota might play a vital role in maintaining ocular health and modulating immune responses within the eye by suppressing pathogens associated with IBK [62]. Competition for binding sites and nutrients is a well-known mechanism by which commensal microbes protect mucosal surfaces such as gut against pathogens [67]. However, given the paucibacterial nature of the ocular surface, the presence of ocular bacteria likely exerts additional immune impacts beyond preventing colonization by pathogens. These impacts may include triggering the local immune system and potentially involving microbes from other mucosal sites in modulating immune responses in the eye [65]. Generally, commensal microorganisms are known to contribute to immune tolerance and homeostasis by priming local immune cells [68], secreting antimicrobial peptides [69], and outcompeting pathogens [62, 70]. They also produce antimicrobial peptides and metabolites that inhibit opportunistic microbes, maintaining immune equilibrium at the ocular surface [62, 70].Commensal bacteria could also interact directly with the ocular immune system through influencing the activation of the local immune cells and production of cytokines [48]. Notably, ocular epithelial cells selectively produce cytokines in response to pathogens [71]. Conversely, they exhibit no reaction to non-pathogenic bacteria, thereby promoting the colonization of a bona fide microbiota [71].
The presence of ocular microbiota could be influenced by tear film which serves as lubrication and chemical barrier. The tear film, an 8 μm layer on the ocular surface, is crucial for vision and eye health by providing refractive power [72], nourishment, lubrication, and a chemical barrier while maintaining microbial balance [73]. Tears are composed of lipid, aqueous, and mucin layers which collectively stabilize and protect the ocular surface, supports hydration and immunity, and facilitate healing and defense against environmental and microbial threats [64]. Tears produced by the lacrimal glands contain water, electrolytes, and proteins such as lysozyme, lactoferrin, and tear-specific prealbumin (lipocalin), and beta-lysin [74–76], which are spread across the ocular surface and along the lid margins through continuous blinking of the eyelids. The antimicrobial action of lysozyme helps to regulate the growth of ocular bacteria by breaking the glycosidic bonds of the peptidoglycan thus preventing microbial imbalance that could lead to infection [77]. Meanwhile, lactoferrin guards eyes against oxidative damage as well as contributes to maintaining a healthy microbial environment on the ocular surface. On the other hands, lipocalins may aid in clearing harmful substances or facilitating lipid distribution, further supporting ocular surface integrity [78]. Therefore, it is possible that the imbalance in these protective mechanisms, or disruption of the tear film, can lead to an overgrowth of pathogenic microbes, potentially contributing to dysbiosis resulting in eye infections including IBK. The disruption of the ocular microbiome, due to insufficient antimicrobial proteins or an imbalance in the tear film components, may increase susceptibility to such infections, leading to the development of eye diseases including IBK [79]. Pathogens including M. bovis and M. bovoculi infections that can be exacerbated by a compromise of tear film and weakening of antimicrobial defenses [79].
Blinking distributes tears containing antimicrobial proteins and immunoglobulins across the ocular surface, clearing debris and microorganisms while providing hydration and nutrients [80]. In cattle, tears drain through the lacrimal system, which connects to the conjunctiva through the lacrimal puncta and canaliculi, and via the nasolacrimal duct into the nose. During this process, lacrimal duct-associated lymphoid tissue detects antigens in tears and activates immune cells to regulate the ocular microbiota [81]. This immune surveillance maintains microbial balance and possibly protects the ocular surface from pathogenic colonization [80] including those causing IBK. Hence disruptions in these mechanisms can potentially increase susceptibility to IBK and other ocular diseases.
In many mammals including cattle, eyelashes play a crucial role in protecting the ocular surface by trapping airborne particles and reducing tear evaporation, thereby preserving tear film stability [82]. This protection potentially helps maintain a balanced ocular microbiome by minimizing external microbial contamination and supporting the antimicrobial properties of the tear film. Optimal eyelash length also regulates airflow, reducing shear stress that could disturb the ocular environment [82]. The inadequate protection or altered airflow dynamics, may compromise the ocular microbiome and increase susceptibility to infections like IBK. Therefore, maintaining optimal eyelash function is essential for reducing microbial imbalances and preventing IBK development. Studies into eyelash-inspired breeding strategies could be a possible approach to protect animals against ocular diseases.
Another critical component of tears is secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) and IgG which bind to pathogens and blocks their adherence to ocular epithelial cells, providing an essential barrier against infections [83]. The corneal innate immune system, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and Langerhans cells, plays a crucial role in defending against microbial threats. Epithelial cells secrete cytokines like TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8 in response to infection, fibroblasts produce these cytokines and α-defensin to prevent pathogens while the Langerhans cells regulate adaptive immunity by modulating B and T lymphocytes [84, 85]. This immune response is essential for maintaining the balance of the ocular microbiome, preventing infection while coexisting with beneficial commensal bacteria.
Additionally, when Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on epithelial and immune cells recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), they trigger innate immune responses [85, 86]. The interaction between PAMPs and PRRs leads to cellular activation, triggering the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and interferons. These molecules act as alarm signals for the immune system, alerting it to the presence of an attack. This response helps recruit immune cells to the site of infection to eliminate pathogens [71]. At the same time, the ocular microenvironment regulates immune responses through anti-inflammatory cytokines like TGF-β and IL-10, which modulate macrophages and T-cells, preventing excessive inflammation [87]. For example, TLR4 responds to Gram-negative bacterial lipopolysaccharides [88], and TLR2 detects Gram-positive bacterial components [89, 90]. However, regulatory mechanisms, including minimal TLR4 expression and inhibitory molecules like Single Ig IL-1-related receptor, prevent excessive inflammation, which could disrupt the commensal microbiota [91].
Other ocular immune cells, including T cells, B cells, and macrophages, maintain delicate balance. T cells, B cells, myeloid cells (such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils), and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) all play distinct roles in defending against infection and regulating inflammation [92, 93]. Conjunctival T cells, including CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, γδ T cells, and unconventional T cells, are key in infection and inflammation [93]. B cells, while less prevalent, are important antigen-presenting cells that contribute to adaptive immune responses. Macrophages and dendritic cells, both specialized antigen-presenting cells, help recognize pathogens and mediate immune defense by initiating appropriate responses [93]. NK cells, a subset of ILCs, are more abundant in the conjunctiva and lacrimal gland and are involved in type 1 immune responses, defending against intracellular pathogens and maintaining immune homeostasis [94]. Owing to a large body of evidence suggesting the presence of self-sustaining ocular microbiome in harmony with well-established ocular immune system, it is reasonable to speculate that the ocular immune system tolerates commensal microbiota but mount response against IBK pathogens such as M. Bovis and other associated etiological agents. Therefore, understanding the interplay between commensal microbiota and the ocular immune system is pivotal for managing conditions like IBK and maintaining ocular health and homeostasis.

## The factors shaping the bovine ocular microbiota
The bovine ocular microbiota is a dynamic and diverse microbial community that can be influenced by host-specific factors, environmental conditions, and the complex interactions between commensal organisms and pathogens [2](Fig. 2).
Fig. 2Factors influencing the ocular microbiome and the epidemiological triad of Infectious Bovine Keratoconjunctivitis (IBK). Host factors include the ocular microbiome, breed differences, age, vaccination and immunity status, antibiotic and ointment use, nutritional status and body condition, and stress from transportation. Factors influencing the ocular microbiome and the epidemiological triad of Infectious Bovine Keratoconjunctivitis (IBK). Host factors include the ocular microbiome, breed differences, age, vaccination and immunity status, antibiotic and ointment use, nutritional status and body condition, and stress from transportation. Environmental factors include feeding strategy and pasture status, seasonal weather, and housing environments exposed to dust, plant debris, pollen, wind, and UV radiation. IBK transmission can be exacerbated by the presence of vectors (e.g., flies) and movement of fomites e.g., farm equipment, feed trucks, and machines between farms. The microbiome factor like dysbiosis of ocular microbiome with high abundance of IBK etiological agents like Moraxella bovis, Moraxella bovoculi, Mycoplasma spp., and Neisseria spp. possibly contributes to outbreak of IBK
Recent studies have demonstrated that host factors such as age, sex, breed, and immune status are critical determinants of bovine ocular microbiome which influence their susceptibility to IBK [7, 95]. These studies revealed that alpha diversity indices varied with age, breed, and disease status. In terms of microbial composition, M. binocular was significantly more prevalent in IBK eyes (25.9%) compared to normal eyes (4.4%). In contrast, Actinobacteriota and specific species like Corynebacterium variabile and Corynebacterium stationis were more abundant in normal eyes, while Rothia nasimurium was more prevalent in IBK eyes [7]. These findings highlight distinct microbial patterns based on cattle-specific factors which are linked to IBK, despite similarities in overall diversity. Notable breed-specific differences were also reported elsewhere [3], with Bos taurus breeds being more susceptible to IBK than Bos indicus [3]. For instance, it has been documented that IBK incidence ranges from 22.4% in Herefords which is highly susceptible to 1.3% in Pinzgauers, while crossbreeding Herefords with Bos indicus were shown to increase their resistance to IBK [3]. This could be explained by genetically influenced factors such as tear composition, eyelash length [82], and inherent immune status [96].
Age also plays a critical role in the colonization of the ocular microbiome, with younger cattle, particularly calves, being more prone to IBK [2]. Studies have shown that their underdeveloped immune systems increase their vulnerability to pathogenic colonization, making them more susceptible to infections like IBK [2]. This underscores the importance of age-related factors in the development and progression of ocular diseases in cattle. Host health and disease status could also influence the homeostasis of ocular microbial community [7, 97, 98]. Although development of effective vaccine against IBK still remains under investigation, existing knowledge suggests that vaccination practices supports herd health [99], and could reduce incidence of diseases [26, 33, 100]. Mechanisms linking the microbiota and immune response to vaccination has been documented [101]. Therefore, we argue that the design of IBK vaccines should account for the concept of interactions between the host and resident ocular microbiota for effective immune response [102]. Use of antibiotics in treatment of infections are known to disrupt the microbiome homeostasis in hosts [103, 104]. Furthermore, the nutritional status and body condition score of cattle are crucial determinants of their overall health and immunity, with well-nourished animals being better equipped to maintain a balanced microbiome [10, 105]. Furthermore, stress associated with handling animals such as transportation could potentially weaken immunity and disrupts microbiome balance [106].
On the other hand, environmental factors around the farms potentially impact the ocular microbiota and likelihood for IBK outbreak in the herd. For example, the housing environments with suboptimal standards increase the exposure of animals to dust, plant debris, pollen, wind, and UV radiation, all of which can disrupt the delicate ocular microbiota and may increase the risk of IBK outbreaks [107]. Flies like Musca autumnalis which are known to spread M. bovis between cattle multiply and thrive in dirty farm environments [108, 109]. The application of insecticides for fly control could also reduce the fly population but may also influence the host microbiome [103]. Another environmental factor that could influence the ocular microbiota are contaminated fomites, such as farm equipment and feed trucks, which serve as reservoirs for transmission of microbes including pathogens between farms, thus increasing the likelihood of microbial imbalance occurrence of IBK [110]. Thus improving housing conditions to reduce exposure to dust, debris, and other irritants, implementing effective fly control measures, and maintaining proper sanitation are critical to reducing IBK incidence [107, 111]. Furthermore, poor feeding strategies and pasture conditions not only compromise nutritional intake but also expose cattle to pathogens present in contaminated feed or ocular trauma in grazing areas [14, 110, 112]. Hence, optimal nutrition and high pasture quality promote efficient nutrient utilization which is essential for microbial stability and immunity [14, 112, 113]. Although IBK can affect cattle year-round, seasonal weather, particularly warmer periods, promotes fly reproduction and increases environmental stressors like strong winds which collectively increases the risk of IBK outbreak [2, 108, 111, 114].
The third component of the epidemiological triad is the pathogens [2]. Under stable health conditions, the bovine ocular microbiota maintains a state of eubiosis—a balanced microbial community that promotes ocular health and helps prevent disease. However, various disruptions such as environmental stressors, immune suppression, or physical injury can trigger dysbiosis [7]. This imbalance leads to a proliferation of opportunistic pathogens, including M. bovis and Mycoplasma spp., which capitalize on the altered microbial and immune landscape [7]. In this dysbiotic state, these pathogens thrive, contributing to the onset and progression of IBK. The transition from microbial harmony to imbalance highlights the critical role of the ocular microbiome in IBK pathogenesis as illustrated in Fig. 3. The judicious use of antibiotics and insecticides plays a pivotal role in managing IBK by enhancing immunity while minimizing disruptions to the ocular microbiome [103, 104]. However, excessive, or improper antibiotic use can inadvertently disturb microbial balance, leading to dysbiosis and altered inflammatory response [115] which could potentially exacerbate IBK progression. In addition, the rising incidence of antibiotic resistance in IBK pathogens including M. bovis, M. bovoculi and M. ovis [116–118] further underscores the urgency of adopting sustainable strategies for IBK management. This necessitates a shift toward approaches that not only control the disease but also preserves the integrity of the ocular microbiome and mitigates the risk of resistance development.

## Probiotic and their potential in ocular health
According to the World Health Organization, probiotics are “live microorganisms which, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host.” [119]. These live microorganisms, which include species like Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Saccharomyces, have shown promising effects in supporting various physiological systems beyond the gut. Although most research to date has focused extensively on the role of probiotics in gut health, where their mechanisms of action are well-established [120], recent studies have explored their potential in maintaining and enhancing ocular health [62, 63, 121]. Probiotics exert their beneficial effects through a range of mechanisms that impact the host health at both local and systemic levels. One of the primary mechanisms of action of probiotics is preventing harmful pathogens from colonizing mucosal surfaces by occupying adhesion sites on epithelial surfaces and outcompeting pathogens for nutrients [122, 123]. In addition to this colonization resistance, probiotics produce antimicrobial compounds, including organic acids, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and bacteriocins, which can inhibit the growth of pathogenic microorganisms [122]. Bacteriocins are antimicrobial peptides produced by probiotics, such as Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactococcus lactis, and Enterococcus faecalis, which act by disrupting the cell membranes, forming pores, or interfering with cell wall biosynthesis in pathogens [124–126]. Bifidobacterium spp. has also demonstrated the ability to inhibit the growth of pathogens like S. aureus and S. epidermidis by secreting antimicrobial substances, such as bacteriocins, and by producing lactic acid, which lowers the pH and creates an environment less favorable for bacterial growth [127]. This mechanism allows probiotics to not only inhibit growth of pathogens but also maintain microbial homeostasis by promoting the survival of beneficial microbes on the ocular surface. Thus when dysbiosis occurs during IBK infections, probiotics possibly contributes to restoration of eubiosis of the microbial environment by promoting the growth of beneficial species and suppressing pathogenic ones [122] as illustrated in Fig. 3. This rebalancing effect is due to the capability of the probiotics to modulate the immune system by stimulating cytokine production, reducing inflammation, and strengthening mucosal defenses [122, 128]. For example, probiotics like Lactobacillus Acidophilus can induce the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which help downregulate inflammatory cascades and reduce ocular inflammation [129]. Moreover, they can modulate immune responses by promoting a Th1 or regulatory T cell (Treg) phenotype, counterbalancing the Th2-mediated immune responses often associated with allergic and inflammatory ocular conditions [130]. Probiotics also have been shown to downregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory markers like ICAM-1 and TLR-4 on the conjunctival epithelium, which are critical in mediating inflammatory responses [129].
Although the research on probiotics in context of ocular health is still very limited, emerging body of evidence suggests its potential application in improving ocular health [62, 131]. Our research group recently examined the bovine ocular commensal bacteria for their in vitro antimicrobial activities against IBK pathogens [62]. We found that Weizmannia coagulans, Lentillactobacillus buchneri, and Paenibacillus polymyxa have ability to inhibit both M. bovis and M. bovoculi isolates [62]. This finding opens the door to developing a novel probiotic-based approach for mitigating IBK in cattle using commensal ocular bacteria. A recent study was conducted in a mice model where eye drops containing the cell-free supernatant of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum significantly reduced ocular inflammation by lowering levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ [131]. Additionally, the supernatant decreased markers of inflammation and oxidative stress, including IL-6, IL-8, lipid peroxidation, and nitric oxide, in retinal pigment epithelial cells [131]. Lactobacillus acidophilus has been reported to relieve vernal keratoconjunctivitis symptoms by reducing ocular inflammation [129], thus exploration of its application in IBK treatment presents a promising research avenue. Another study involving microbiological characterization of bacterial conjunctivitis reported that cell-free preparations of probiotics such as Lactobacillus acidophilus EMCC 1324, L. helveticus EMCC 1654, L. plantarum EMCC 1027, L. rhamnosus EMCC 1105, Bifidobacterium longum EMCC 1547, and B. bifidum EMCC 1334have exhibited significant antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus strains which were resistant to common antibiotics like oxacillin, penicillin G, and cephalexin [132]. This evidence offers promising potential for the use of probiotics as an alternative to antibiotics in the treatment of IBK, highlighting a sustainable and microbiome-friendly approach to managing the disease. Another study explored the direct antipathogenic effects of Lactobacillus spp. on Moraxella catarrhalis, a common respiratory pathogen that also causes eye infections in children [133]. They demonstrated that Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG significantly inhibits M. catarrhalis adhesion to human airway epithelial Calu-3 cells and reduces the expression of MUC5AC mucin and pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α [134]. Given the similarities between M. catarrhalis and M. bovis as well as M. bovoculi, we suggest that Lactobacillus probiotics hold potential as an effective prevention for IBK in cattle, highlighting the need for further research in this area. Therefore, current evidence suggests that probiotics may offer an alternative to antibiotics for managing IBK by inhibiting IBK pathogens, favoring the growth of beneficial bacteria and modulating ocular immune responses. While probiotics show promise for treating IBK, further research is required to fully understand its applicability especially characterization of effective candidate species, their modes of action, optimal routes of administration and efficacy.

## Phage therapy as an alternative for modulating ocular health
A bacteriophage is a virus that specifically targets and eliminates bacteria host by injecting its DNA into bacterial cells, taking over their machinery to replicate and ultimately killing the bacterial host [135]. Phage therapy predominantly utilizes strictly lytic phages with precise antimicrobial action against target bacterial pathogens and thus requires thorough purification to eliminate bacterial debris and endotoxins [136]. Lytic phages begin their infection by attaching to specific receptors on the bacterial host’s cell surface, which can be found on the cell walls of Gram-positive or Gram-negative bacteria, on polysaccharide capsules, or on structures like pili and flagella [137, 138]. And then inject their genetic material inside. Once inside the bacterial cells, the phage’s genetic material can either merge with the bacterial DNA and get passed down as the bacteria divides, or it can take over the bacterial replicating machinery to create new phages. When enough phage particles are produced, lytic proteins activate to break down the bacterial cell wall, releasing new phages to start the infection cycle again [139]. Phages have attracted attention for their potential in understanding microbial dynamics and as tools for controlling bacterial populations in various ecosystems including clinical infections [136]. With the growing threat of antibiotic resistance, phages are being explored as a viable alternative, and advances in genetic technologies now enable detailed characterization and bioengineering of phages for specialized purposes [140]. In the context of the ocular microbiota and IBK treatment, phages offer promising applications as targeted antimicrobials, biofilm disruptors, and diagnostic tools. Their ability to selectively target pathogens, possibly including M. bovis and M. bovoculi, positions them as feasible and safe options for preventing and treating IBK.
To date, no research has directly investigated the application of phage therapy against IBK associated bacterial pathogens. However, several studies on other bacterial pathogens have shown promising outcomes, highlighting the potential of phage therapy as an effective alternative to traditional antimicrobial treatments. For instance, the potential application of phage therapy in managing bacterial ocular infections have been proposed as alternative therapy against antibiotic resistant eye pathogens [141]. Phage therapy offers additional benefits like minimal disruption to the native ocular microbiota, adaptability to evolving bacterial resistance, and the ability to target biofilms [141]. Recently, a study was conducted using mouse model to examine the potential use of phage therapy for treatment of bacterial pathogens causing neonatal sepsis in children including on Moraxella catarrhalis [142]. In this study, mice were injected intraperitoneally with the minimum lethal dose of Moraxella catarrhalis and treated with varying concentrations of a specific phage after 45 min post-infection [142]. It was observed that phage concentrations of one MOI (multiplicity of infection) and 2 MOI successfully rescued 100% of the mice from bacteremia within 48 h, while a lower concentration of 0.01 MOI rescued 60–80% of the mice [142]. They also tested treatment with delays of 5, 14, 18, and 24 h post-infection, and all treated groups eventually recovered, further demonstrating the efficacy of the phage treatment even with delayed treatment interventions [142].
A recent study explored the use of an in-situ gel-forming ophthalmic formulation containing bacteriophages for the treatment of Pseudomonas aeruginosa keratoconjunctivitis [143]. This innovative Cystoviridae phage formulation was designed to transit from a liquid to a gel upon contact with the eye, enhancing its retention and therapeutic efficacy [143]. The histological analysis revealed that the treated group demonstrated re-epithelialization and a return to normal corneal structure whereas the untreated groups suffered significant corneal damage, including edema and stromal destruction [143]. These findings further underscore the potential of phage-based gel formulations as a promising alternative to antibiotics for combating resistant bacterial eye infections. In another study, the therapeutic potential of Bacillus phage lysin PlyB for treating ocular infections was investigated [144]. PlyB demonstrated rapid, group-specific bactericidal activity against Bacillus cereus vegetative cells in vitro and in ex vivo rabbit vitreous [144]. Moreover, after intravitreal administration in vivo, PlyB effectively eliminated B. cereus in models of endophthalmitis and keratitis, preventing ocular tissue damage [144]. Importantly, PlyB was shown to be safe, with no cytotoxic or hemolytic effects, making it a promising alternative for managing B. cereus eye infections resistant to conventional antibiotics [144]. It has also been reported that unlike broad-spectrum antibiotics which often struggle to penetrate biofilms, noted in the case of Vibrio cholerae and Yersinia pestis [145], phages effectively degrade biofilms using enzymes like EPS depolymerase, overcoming challenges faced by antibiotics [146]. This evidence strongly suggests the possibility of developing customized phage cocktails capable of effectively treating IBK infections by targeting a broader spectrum of its bacteria pathogens.

## Using essential oils to enhance ocular health
Essential oils (EOs) are natural volatile aromatic compounds derived from various plant parts such as roots, stems, leaves and flowers, with unique fragrance, flavors and bioactivity [147, 148]. The EOs are composed of secondary metabolites like alcohols, esters, phenols, and ketones, support plant growth and interactions with other organisms [149]. They are widely used for sterilization, antiviral, antifungal, antiparasitic, insecticidal, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic purposes [150]. Essential oil presents as an antimicrobial alternative with the potential ability to target IBK pathogens with little impact on the ocular microbiome [151]. Studies on the gut microbiome have shown that certain EOs can selectively inhibit pathogenic bacteria while promoting the growth of beneficial microbes such as Bacilli, Lactobacillales, Streptococcaceae, and Veillonellaceae [152, 153]. Our research group recently reported that EOs exhibit higher minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against commensal lactic acid-producing bacteria compared to bovine respiratory pathogens in the nasopharyngeal microbiota of healthy beef cattle, suggesting that EOs may selectively target pathogens while preserving beneficial bacteria [154, 155]. Although currently there is no such evidence for selective effects on the ocular microbiome, it is plausible that essential oils could be formulated to target IBK-associated pathogens while preserving beneficial ocular bacteria. Therefore, further research is needed to investigate the safety of essential oils on native ocular microbiota.
The EOs such as lavender, thyme, peppermint, cajuput, cinnamon, eucalyptus, sage and tea tree exhibit antibacterial properties against a wide range of bacterial pathogens [156–159]. For instance, thyme EO, rich in thymol and carvacrol, acts by forming hydrogen bonds with active sites of various bacterial enzymes and thereby inhibit pathogens [156, 160]. Our research group recently demonstrated that EOs, particularly ajowan, fennel, cinnamon leaf, and thyme effectively inhibit bovine respiratory disease (BRD) pathogens such as Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida, and Histophilus somni in vitro, while showing minimal impact on bovine nasopharyngeal commensal bacteria [155]. The thyme EO also exhibits biofilm-disrupting activity, which is crucial in managing bacterial infections that involve biofilm formation [161]. The key active components of thyme EO are thymol and carvacrol, and these components can disrupt bacterial cell membranes and disrupt biofilm structures [161]. Meanwhile, peppermint EO alone displayed a relatively weak antibacterial activity. However, when used in combination with other EOs, it shows antibacterial potential owing to its high methanol content [156, 162]. Cinnamon EO has also been shown to disrupt bacterial cell membranes and inhibit pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus because of its cinnamaldehyde content [163–165]. Eucalyptus EO can disrupt Gram-negative bacterial membranes through antimicrobial components like 1,8-cineole, p-cymene, cis-geraniol, and terpinolene [156, 166]. Sage EO has proven antibacterial effects against various pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi, and S. aureus. Its antimicrobial properties are mainly linked to the presence of camphor, thujone, and 1,8-cineole [167, 168]. Tea tree EO also inhibited the growth of S. aureus and E. coli and reduced the adhesion of Streptococcus mutans. This EO also displayed antibiofilm activity [169]. Recent research demonstrated that some EOs can alter the microbial community structure, composition, and diversity, while displaying limited antibacterial activity against beneficial commensal bacteria such as lactic acid-producing bacteria [154]. Essential oils like ajowan, thyme, and fennel have been shown to significantly reduce the growth of nasopharyngeal microbiota and alter microbial diversity by decreasing pathogenic bacteria such as Moraxellaceae while increasing beneficial bacteria like Enterobacteriaceae [170].
One investigation evaluated the cytotoxicity of 20 EOs on human eye conjunctiva cells in vitro and their antimicrobial activity with the goal of identifying safe and effective EOs agents for ocular use [171]. Interestingly, the tested EOs did not show cytotoxicity at low concentrations, with rosmarinus officinalis, melaleuca alternifolia, and thymus vulgaris L. red thyme geraniol EO has antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. It is possible that EOs could effectively target IBK pathogens without causing eye infection, but further studies are needed to evaluate their antimicrobial activity and safety in bovine ocular cells.
A separate investigation evaluated the antibacterial activity of Origanum vulgare L. and O. majorana L. EOs on coagulase-negative Staphylococcus aureus, Enterobacter spp., Proteus spp., Acinetobacter spp., and Klebsiella spp. isolated from the patients with conjunctivitis [172]. Both EOs displayed significant inhibition against all the bacterial strains tested, shown by a large zone of inhibition ranging from 15 to 32 mm [172]. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) for the Origanum vulgare L. and O. majorana L. were 5–20 µL/mL and 2.5–10 µL/mL, respectively. These concentrations significantly inhibited bacterial cell viability, eliminating the entire inoculum within 24 h of exposure [172]. A study conducted in India evaluated the antibacterial properties of acmella EO against Moraxella spp [173]. The results showed that the Moraxella spp. exhibits sensitivity to the EO, with a mean inhibition zone of 14 mm at a concentration of 5 µL/mL (v/v) and the MIC of 3.5 µL/mL (v/v). Scanning electron microscopy showed significant bacterial cell disruption by the EO, underscoring its potential as an effective antibacterial agent [173].
Another promising avenue for EOs in ocular microbiota modulation and IBK pathogen management is their potential application as microencapsulated EOs (MEO), for precise and sustained delivery of active ingredients to the eye. Microencapsulation can protect EOs from degradation, maintain their therapeutic properties [174], while reducing the risk of disrupting ocular microbiota. This makes it an appealing strategy for both treating and preventing IBK. Studies on MEO have demonstrated their effectiveness in improving growth performance and intestinal health in weaned piglets, with enhancements in intestinal morphology such as increased villus height and villus-to-crypt ratios [175]. This approach could be adapted for ocular application as it can ensure controlled release of therapeutic compounds [176] to the eye and potentially improve IBK management by targeting harmful pathogens like M. bovis and Mycoplasma spp. This method enhances diffusion by breaking through epithelial barriers, responding to environmental factors like temperature, solubility, and pH changes, or triggering release through capsule wall degradation, maximizing the therapeutic efficacy of EOs [176].
While EOs demonstrate promising antimicrobial and biofilm-disrupting properties, the possible cytotoxicity of EOs to bovine corneal epithelial cells remain largely unknown. Thus, in vitro cytotoxicity studies using bovine corneal epithelial cells will be essential for its safe application. Further studies are needed to identify the most effective active molecules in EOs and develop appropriate formulations, particularly through microencapsulation, to precisely inhibit IBK pathogens and enhance ocular microbiome-mediated eye health while minimizing harm to ocular tissues. Therefore, there are opportunities for future research in adapting EO-based treatments specifically for manipulation of bovine ocular microbiota and enhance cattle resistance against IBK pathogen infection.

## Potential application of nanotechnology and nanoparticles to modulate the ocular microbiota
Nanoparticles are materials with dimensions that fall within the nanoscale, typically less than 100 nm [177–180]. They have gained prominence in modern medicine, traditionally serving as contrast agents in imaging and carriers for drug and gene delivery to tumors [181]. Nanoparticles have also been explored for their potential applications in microbial control [182, 183]. For instance, a recent study evaluated the effectiveness of silver nanoparticles in preventing the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, S. aureus, and Acanthamoeba castellanii in contact lenses [183]. It was reported that when Etaficon A lenses were soaked in varying concentrations of silver nanoparticles, the bacterial viability and adhesion on the lenses, as well as in the surrounding solution, were significantly reduced compared to untreated lenses [183]. It has also been reported that hydrogel disks (a proxy for contact lenses) infused with silver nanoparticles exhibited significant antimicrobial effects, particularly against P. aeruginosa [184]. The study demonstrated that higher concentrations of silver nanoparticles were required to achieve sustained antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, with their effectiveness lasting between 6 and 72 h, depending on the concentration and bacterial species targeted [184]. Although the use of contact lenses in cattle may seem a far-fetched possibility, this highlights the potential of silver nanoparticle-impregnated materials as an innovative solution to managing ocular diseases. Interestingly, nanoparticles show potential efficacy against multidrug-resistant bacteria [185]. Further studies propose the use of antibiotic-conjugated polymeric nanoparticles as a promising strategy to combat multidrug-resistant microbes [186]. Composed of biocompatible polymers, these nanoparticles encapsulate and protect antibiotics, enabling targeted, controlled, and stimuli-triggered release, thereby enhancing the precision and effectiveness of antimicrobial therapies [187].
Lipid-based nanoparticles have been widely explored for enhancing therapeutic capabilities of antimicrobials [181, 188, 189]. Lipid-based nanostructures include liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid nanocarriers which offer a promising solution for delivering and controlling the release of antimicrobial substances [188, 189]. Lipid-based nanocarriers protect active compounds, enhance stability, and enable targeted, sustained release of natural antimicrobials, such as essential oils. For instance, fusogenic liposomes have gained significant attention due to their ability to fuse with bacterial cell membranes and there by enhancing the delivery and efficacy of bactericidal agents [189, 190]. This fusion mechanism not only facilitates the release of therapeutic content directly into bacterial cells but also contributes to the destruction of bacterial membranes [190].
In 2001, Alonso and colleagues pioneered the use of chitosan nanoparticles for drug delivery to the ocular surface [191] and conducted studies on their properties and applications [191–193]. These nanoparticles, synthesized with an average size in hundreds of nanometers and a stable zeta potential of + 35 to + 37 mV, exhibited resistance to aggregation [194, 195]. In vitro studies demonstrated their stability in tear fluid containing mucin and lysozyme, with minimal impact on mucin viscosity [194]. Chitosan nanoparticles showed no significant toxicity to conjunctival and epithelial cell lines at concentrations up to 2 mg/mL for 24 h [194]. In vivo studies in rabbits revealed that topically administered chitosan nanoparticles did not cause inflammation or damage to the corneal epithelial layer [196]. Fluorescein-labeled nanoparticles accumulated effectively in the cornea and conjunctiva, highlighting their potential for targeted ocular drug delivery [197]. Poly (alkyl cyanoacrylate) nanoparticles also exhibited a four-fold greater accumulation in inflamed rabbit eye tissues compared to healthy tissue, highlighting their targeted delivery capabilities [198]. In vivo studies on chitosan-based hybrid nanoparticles in rabbits showed sustained release of therapeutic agents like betamethasone sodium phosphate for up to 24 h, significantly exceeding the performance of traditional drug solutions [199].
However, despite these promising developments, further studies are needed especially regarding the stability of nanoparticles, its scalability for mass production, as well as the in vivo fate and long-term toxicity of nanoparticles especially in the context of bovine ocular microbiome and IBK management [200]. In addition, while current research has demonstrated effectiveness in small-scale studies, the scalability of nanoparticle production for widespread clinical use remains as a significant obstacle [201]. It is crucial to comprehensively understand the long-term effects and potential toxicity of these nanoparticles to ensure their safety for long-term use in ocular therapies in cattle [195], let al.one its potential impact on farmers handling it and consumers of livestock products. Nevertheless, owing to their ability to deliver active substances precisely to target sites [202], nanoparticles potentially offer a novel approach to modulating the ocular microbiota and treating IBK [203]. This targeted delivery makes them particularly effective for managing microbial imbalances and infections in the eye, a delicate anatomical structure. Nanoparticle-based systems could also present a promising avenue enhancing the bioavailability and efficacy of therapeutics of ocular diseases microbiome research, particularly in both human and animal models including IBK.

## The gut- eye- microbiota axis and its potential use for improved ocular health
Evidence shows the existence of several communication pathways linking the gut to other organs, such as the gut-testis axis, where gut microbial composition influences testicular function, fertility, and immune responses [204–206]. Similarly, the gut-brain axis highlights the role of gut microbes in regulating neurological functions and behavior [207–209], while the gut-lung axis demonstrates microbial influence over respiratory health and immune responses in the lungs [210, 211]. An emerging body of literature also highlights the interconnectedness of the gut and ocular microbiota through the gut-eye-microbiota axis, a pathway where microbial interactions influence systemic and ocular health [212–214]. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating immune responses, which extend to the eye, potentially impacting conditions such as uveitis, dry eye disease, and infectious keratitis [215]. Although this concept is still at infancy, emerging evidence suggests that disturbances in gut microbial communities can trigger inflammatory processes that affect the eyes [213–215]. Gut dysbiosis has been reported to influence the progression of several ocular diseases in human, such as uveitis, dry eye syndrome, macular degeneration, and glaucoma [216]. For instance, probiotic eye drops containing S. boulardii and L. rhamnosus improved vernal keratoconjunctivitis symptoms [216].Therefore, manipulating the gut microbiota shows a promise in influencing the trajectory of these eye conditions, offering a potential therapeutic avenue for managing both acute and chronic ocular disorders [217].
Gut homeostasis plays a vital role in supporting retinal health by regulating the immune system and producing anti-inflammatory compounds like SCFAs, bacteriocins, secondary bile acids, indoles, and polyamines [218]. However, disruptions in gut balance interferes with the interaction mechanisms between the gut and eye, often mediated by SCFAs and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) [115, 216]. Gut dysbiosis mostly favour pro-inflammatory bacteria over the anti-inflammatory microbes which disrupts the gut barrier resulting into metabolic endotoxemia, systemic inflammation, and potential eye disease [115, 219, 220]. Much as the exact causal link between gut microbiota changes and eye diseases is not fully understood, it is proposed that the gut microbial imbalance may provoke inflammation on ocular surface, contributing to optic nerve damage and the progression of eye conditions [212, 219].
Recent research highlights that gut-derived butyrate plays a crucial role in suppressing ocular surface inflammation by down regulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and maintaining corneal barrier integrity [221]. It was demonstrated in murine models where tributyrin, a prodrug from butyrate, reduced desiccating stress-induced ocular inflammation and prevented the loss of conjunctival goblet cells [221]. The mechanism of action involves the SCFA transporter SLC5A8, which is expressed on the ocular surface and facilitates the anti-inflammatory effect of butyrate [221]. A pilot study recently conducted by Chen and colleagues suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis in humans significantly influences the progression of ocular diseases like glaucoma, particularly through immune modulation [222]. It was observed that dysbiosis triggers macrophage-related pathways, leading to increased inflammation and immune dysfunction in the eye [221]. Genes such as NFKB1, IL18, and TLR9 have been identified as crucial regulators of these immune responses, and their role in activating pro-inflammatory processes is linked to glaucoma development [222]. Targeting gut microbiota to regulate these pathways offers promising potential for new diagnostic markers and therapeutic strategies for glaucoma [222]. Studies have shown that supplementation with SCFAs can mitigate the severity of immune-mediated diseases such as uveitis by suppressing the activity of pro-inflammatory T cells and enhancing the function of regulatory T cells, which are essential for controlling excessive immune reactions. A decrease in SCFA levels, as seen in dysbiosis, weakens the gut’s protective role, thereby exacerbating ocular inflammation [220]. Lipopolysaccharides (LPS), a component of the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria, plays a significant role in immune system activation when the gut barrier is compromised [115]. The LPS can enter the bloodstream and, through immune system pathways, reach the eye, where it triggers inflammation [115, 223]. This has been observed in cases where LPS contributed to the degeneration of neurons and axons in animal models [224]. These mechanisms highlight the crucial role that gut health plays in maintaining ocular health, suggesting that restoring gut microbiota balance may help in managing or preventing ocular conditions like uveitis and glaucoma.
The gut lining protects the host from external antigens like food particles, microbes, pathogens, and toxins while maintaining intestinal integrity and regulating immune responses [225, 226]. The gut associated lymphoid tissue encompass specialized immune structures, such as lymphoid follicles containing array of immune cells, including B cells, T cells, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, which are critical in modulating immune activity [227, 228]. The relationship between gut microbiota and autoimmune diseases is complex and multifaceted. Alterations in gut microbial composition can lead to the activation and differentiation of specific immune cells, such as Th17 and other T helper cells, which are implicated in causing immune-mediated inflammation in various tissues, including the eyes. These interactions demonstrate how gut health can directly influence immune responses in distant organs [229, 230]. In ocular diseases like uveitis, an abnormal immune response may lead to chronic inflammation or autoimmunity, where the body’s immune system mistakenly targets healthy ocular tissues [231]. Such aberrant immune activity is often associated with disruptions in the gut microbiome, reinforcing the critical role that gut health plays in the development and regulation of ocular conditions [232].
In ocular conditions like dry eye and blepharitis, there is growing evidence linking osmolarity alterations to gut microbiota dysregulation in human [216]. Manipulating the gut microbiota through probiotics and prebiotics or bacteriophages presents a promising approach in clinical practice, although further research is required to fully explore its potential in ocular health and IBK treatment. One potential approach could involve designing feeding strategies that modulate the rumen microbiota, enhancing the production of antimicrobial metabolites like SCFAs. These metabolites have antimicrobial properties and may indirectly benefit the ocular microbiome by reducing systemic inflammation or influencing mucosal microbial populations [233, 234]. This dietary intervention could be tailored towards strengthening systemic immunity through rumen microbiota manipulation to increase resistance against IBK infections. For example, research by Schären and colleagues has highlighted that diet-driven changes in the rumen microbiome can improve immune function, emphasizing the potential of immune-focused dietary interventions [235]. Despite promising connections between gut microbiota and eye, further studies are needed to fully understand the complexities of these interactions and how it can be leveraged to improve ocular health. Future innovations may lead to new treatments targeting the gut microbiota, providing alternative or adjunctive therapies for managing ocular diseases by modulating both the gut and eye microbiome in humans [216] and livestock including cattle.

## Conclusion
IBK is an important ocular disease caused by pathogens such as Moraxella bovis, Moraxella bovoculi, and Mycoplasma spp., which continues to pose significant health and economic challenges to both the beef and dairy cattle industries. Its epidemiology is complex and multifactorial triad encompassing multiple pathogens, the host factors, and environmental influences, which collectively determine IBK outbreak and progression. Recent research suggests that the ocular microbiome and its connection to the gut through the “microbiota-gut-eye axis” maybe involved in the development and progression of IBK. Therefore, exploring the ocular microbiota-targeted strategies including probiotics, bacteriophages, essential oils and nanotechnology are potential promising strategies for IBK management as alternatives to traditional antibiotics and vaccine therapies. These strategies could be tailored to restoration of the ocular microbial balance, strengthen host ocular immunity, and provide precise pathogen-specific treatments. Advancement in controlled-release therapy such as therapeutic contact lenses bring potential for sustainable and localized treatment, reducing disease recurrence and the need for antibiotics. Despite these promising developments, advancing microbiome-targeted therapies will be essential for creating sustainable solutions to manage IBK, enhance animal welfare, mitigate economic losses, and decrease reliance on antibiotics in cattle industry.
