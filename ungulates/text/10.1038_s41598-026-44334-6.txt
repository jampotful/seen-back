Species-level identification and differentiation of deer antlers using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and chemometrics.
Sharma CP, Bhatia D, Singh R. (2026). Scientific reports. . doi:10.1038/s41598-026-44334-6. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Species-level identification and differentiation of deer antlers using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and chemometrics

## Abstract
AbstractAntlers are the fastest-growing osseous tissue in mammals, undergoing periodic regeneration associated with the mating season. Their high strength and resistance to fracture have made them a valuable material for the manufacture of hunting tools, artifacts, furniture, and traditional medicines. The growing demand for antlers and derived products has fueled illegal trade, contributing to a significant decline in deer populations. Therefore, the present study aims to identify and differentiate antler samples from three deer species (spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer) using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy coupled with chemometrics. Initial visual comparison of antler spectra failed to distinguish species due to their similar chemical compositions. However, the PCA model achieved partial separation with an accuracy of 93.33%, limited by some spectral misclustering. To improve differentiation, a PLS-DA model was developed, which distinguished antler spectra with 100% accuracy. External validation and a blind test further confirmed the model’s predictive performance, yielding 100% accuracy. The findings demonstrate that ATR-FTIR spectroscopy combined with PLS-DA provides a reliable and efficient approach for species-level identification of antler samples. This approach will offer a valuable tool for wildlife forensic investigations and assist law enforcement agencies in monitoring and curbing the illegal antler trade.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44334-6.Keywords: Wildlife forensics, Cervidae, Antler, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, PLS-DASubject terms: Chemistry, Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

## Introduction
Historically, deer antlers have been a highly valued osseous material for the manufacture of hunting tools, owing to their superior ability to withstand flexural stresses compared to other bony materials1–3. They were also widely used for manufacturing artifacts, furniture, ornaments, and pistol butts, among other things3–5. Moreover, deer antlers have been widely used in traditional medicine across various Asian countries, including China and Korea, based on the belief that they can alleviate aging, stress, and inflammation while exhibiting phagocytic properties6–8. The global market for deer antlers is underscored by a TRAFFIC report of 1997, which estimated the annual trade value at approximately $500 million, with South Korea being the top importing country5. Furthermore, the global deer antler extract market was estimated at around USD 1238.9 million in 2004 and is anticipated to increase from approximately USD 1320.6 million in 2025 to nearly USD 2500 million by 2035, reflecting sustained demand for antler-derived products9. Seizure-based assessments further indicate that between 2015 and 2021, approximately 4,000 wildlife species were documented across nearly 140,000 seizure records, highlighting the extensive scale and taxonomic diversity of the illegal wildlife trade10.
Antlers are bony projections that emerge from the frontal bones of the skull and are covered with a soft vascular skin known as velvet during their rapid growth stage. Primarily, antlers consist of hydroxyapatite, collagen, non-collagenous protein, and water, similar to other bones but with a higher organic proportion and lower mineral content11. Antlers serve as a characteristic feature of male members of the family cervidae, except for the genus Rangifer, where females also possess antlers. India harbors a rich diversity of cervids, with seven species including Cervus hanglu hanglu (Kashmir stag), Rucervus duvaucelii (swamp deer), Rucervus eldii eldii (sangai), Muntiacus muntjak (barking deer), Axis axis (spotted deer), Axis porcinus (hog deer), and Rusa unicolor (sambar deer)4. For their conservation and protection, India has imposed stringent prohibitions on the trade of antlers under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 19724,12. This was further strengthened in 1998 with a comprehensive ban on the trade of shed/cast antlers and their derived products, to safeguard deer populations and curb illegal wildlife exploitation13.
Despite the stringent nationwide ban on antler trade, illegal poaching and trafficking persist, as evidenced by several reported cases. For instance, in 2014, two individuals were apprehended near the boundary of Rajaji National Park with 100 kg of antlers from swamp deer and sambar deer. That same year, authorities found 50 kg of antlers and horns of sambar, chital, and gaur that had been poached in Bandipur National Park, while five poachers were arrested in Oddanchathram (Tamil Nadu) in possession of three poached sambar antlers. Additionally, in 2015, officials seized 16 antlers belonging to reindeer, chital, and barking deer from a bungalow within the Biligiri Rangaswamy Temple Tiger Reserve of Bandipur13.
The number of reported antler seizures highlights the need for the development of a reliable technique to identify and differentiate antlers from various cervidae species. Several prior studies have pursued this goal. In 2006, Choi et al. successfully differentiated antlers of Cervus elaphus from three different countries (China, New Zealand, and Russia) using 1H NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) spectroscopy in combination with principal component analysis (PCA)6. Similarly, in 2007, Leonard et al. characterized C. elaphus antlers non-destructively using X-ray microtomography and image analysis2. In 2016, Lefebvre et al. reported a non-destructive approach for differentiating reindeer and red deer antlers using X-ray microtomography, along with the support vector machine (SVM) tool. Notably, this work represented the first application of such a non-destructive method for species-level differentiation of archaeological antlers1. In 2018, Guo et al. successfully developed a method to distinguish velvet antlers from reindeer, sika deer, and red deer based on protein profile, utilizing ultra-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-QTOF-MS) with PCA7. Recently, Singh et al. performed X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis to characterize the elemental composition of chital, sambar, hog deer, swamp deer, and barking deer antlers, enabling species differentiation through PCA of the elemental profile4.
The aforementioned techniques have yielded promising results during antler analysis. However, there remains a need for a method that can deliver rapid, reliable, non-destructive, eco-friendly, and cost-effective outcomes. To meet these requirements, the present study employs attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy for antler identification and differentiation. Additionally, this technique requires only a minimal amount of sample, with negligible sample preparation. Moreover, integrating multivariate statistical tools for spectral data analysis reduces bias, enhances objectivity, and adds statistical validity to the results14. This combined approach has proven effective in analyzing diverse wildlife-derived items, including claws15, hairs16, ivory17, feathers18, horns19, and blood stains20. Thus, the present study aims to differentiate antler samples from three Cervidae species, including spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer, as their antlers are widely traded4,21. Notably, the current approach is well-suited for the analysis of processed, furnished, or powdered samples that lack morphological features. Moreover, as a non-destructive approach, samples remain preserved for subsequent analyses, such as elemental and DNA analysis.

## Materials and methods

## Sample collection
Reference antler samples were obtained from the repository of the Wildlife Forensic & Conservation Genetics Cell at the Wildlife Institute of India (WII), Dehradun. In the present study, a total of 30 antler samples, representing 10 individuals each from the three deer species (spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer), were analyzed. Homogeneity of the samples was initially evaluated by analyzing samples from three different locations along the length, i.e., proximal, middle, and distal, as shown in Fig. 1. As no variations were observed among these samples, the middle portion was selected for subsequent analysis. An additional set of ten antler samples (X1-X10) from spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer was collected for a blind test, with their identities withheld from the analyst until final predictions were completed. No live animals were sampled in this study.
Representative locations of the sampling sites.

## Sample preparation
Sample preparation was performed by drilling the compact bone portion of the antler’s beam to obtain the powdered sample.

## Sample analysis
All reference antler samples were examined with a Bruker Alpha ATR-FTIR spectrometer (Billerica, MA) fitted with a Zinc Selenide (ZnSe) crystal and an air-cooled deuterated triglycine sulfate (DTGS) detector. About 50 mg of powder sample was placed onto the crystal surface, and the clamp was tightened to maintain uniform contact. Spectral acquisition was performed in the mid-infrared region (4000–600 cm−1) via OPUS software (version 8.2) in absorbance mode, employing 64 scans per spectrum at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Prior to each sample analysis, the crystal was cleaned with acetone (spectroscopic grade) and lint-free wipes, and a background spectrum was acquired with air. Repeatability was evaluated by analyzing each sample three times, while homogeneity was assessed by analyzing samples collected from three different locations within each species’ antler samples. To evaluate the variations among the individuals of the same species, antler samples from three individuals of each deer species were examined.
Triplicate analysis of each sample yielded a total of 90 spectra (3 spectra per sample). This dataset was split into a training dataset having 60 spectra (2 spectra per sample) and a validation dataset having 30 spectra (one spectrum per sample).

## Chemometrics
Chemometric methods are commonly employed to improve the efficiency of multivariate data analysis and incorporate statistical value into interpretations14. In this study, principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were applied following an initial visual comparison of the antler spectra. The Unscrambler® X software (ver. 10.5) developed by Camo Analytics was utilized for all the chemometric analyses. The training dataset, spanning the range of 4000–600 cm−1, was imported to the software in OPUS format. Prior to performing PCA and PLS-DA, the dataset was pre-processed to minimize noise and irrelevant variations in the dataset. Therefore, four pre-processing techniques were applied before these analyses.
Baseline offset: To correct the baseline drift.
Standard normal variate (SNV): To remove the scattering effect.
Deresolve: To reduce the noise.
Orthogonal signal correction (OSC) using the NIPALS (non-linear iterative partial least squares) algorithm: To remove irrelevant variations from the X-variable unrelated to responses.

## Principal component analysis (PCA)
PCA is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction tool that transforms a multivariate dataset into a smaller set of variables called principal components (PCs), while preserving the actual information within the dataset14. These PCs can then be employed to develop a PCA model to explore the similarities or dissimilarities within the dataset. In this study, the PCA model was constructed using the singular value decomposition (SVD) algorithm on a mean-centered, pre-processed training dataset spanning the 4000–600 cm−1 range.

## Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA)
PLS-DA is a supervised dimensionality reduction tool that extracts latent variables (LVs) from a multivariate dataset. These LVs are derived by prioritizing predictor variables that have a strong correlation with the response variables and are used for both sample classification and prediction. In PLS-DA, dummy numbers are assigned to the Y-variables to enable classification. Unknown samples are classified by comparing their predicted Y values with the assigned dummy values14. In this study, the PLS-DA was constructed using a a kernel algorithm and random cross-validation (CV) on a pre-processed training dataset covering the 4000–600 cm−1 range. The dummy values assigned were “1” for spotted deer antler, “2” for sambar deer antler, and “3” for swamp deer antler.

## Validation studies
While using chemometric tools, it is crucial to assess their accuracy and classification capability to ensure that the present approach yields reliable and consistent outcomes. In this study, external validation was conducted by projecting a validation dataset (other than the training dataset) onto the developed chemometric models. Furthermore, a blind test was conducted to classify ten unknown antler samples (X1-X10) from spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer. Throughout this test, the identities of unknown samples were withheld from the analyst until the final predictions were completed.
The performance of the developed chemometric models was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score for cross-validation, external validation, and blind test.

## Results and discussion

## Visual comparison of spectra
Initially, the ATR-FTIR spectra of antler samples from these three species were overlaid using OPUS software for comparative analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The spectra revealed similar peaks corresponding to hydroxyapatite and collagen. The peaks at 1636, 1538, and 1242 cm−1 are attributed to the CO stretching of the amide I band, CN stretching of the amide II band, and NH deformation of the amide III band, respectively. These amide bands indicate the presence of collagen protein in the samples. Similarly, the peaks at 1438 and 1412 cm−1 correspond to the asymmetrical stretching of CO32− (carbonate) in hydroxyapatite. However, hydroxyapatite also exhibits several other peaks at 1340, 1016, 866, and 619 cm−1, which are associated with CH3 wagging, asymmetrical stretching of PO43- (phosphate), CO32− (carbonate) bending, and asymmetrical bending of PO43− (phosphate), respectively17,22.
Overlaid ATR-FTIR spectra of spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer antler samples in the MIR range.
Repeatability, homogeneity, and variations among the individuals of the same species were assessed, as shown in Figs. S1, S2, and S3, respectively. The relative standard deviation (RSD) values were calculated as 0.23% for the repeatability test, 0.58% for the homogeneity test, and 0.44% for the variations among the individuals of the same species. These low RSD values across the tests indicate no significant variation among the analyzed antler spectra.

## PCA model
During PCA, seven PCs were examined, collectively accounting for 92% of the dataset’s total variation. PC1 explained 86% of this variation, while PC2 to PC7 each contributed 1%, as shown in Fig. 3. While constructing the 3D-PCA model, various combinations of PCs were evaluated, and a combination of PC1, PC2, and PC3 was found to provide the best representation of trends in the dataset. A 3D-PCA score plot was constructed using the PCs mentioned above, as shown in Fig. 4. This plot differentiated antler spectra of these species into three distinct clusters, with a few exceptions, including two sambar deer antler spectra clustered near swamp deer antler spectra, and two spotted deer antler spectra clustered near sambar deer antler spectra. The misclustering of antler spectra on the PCA score plot resulted in a decrease in accuracy to 93.33%.

## PLS-DA model
The PLS-DA model was developed to enhance the differentiation of the antler spectra with high accuracy. Five LVs or factors were selected for the PLS-DA model based on their classification performance during cross-validation. A 3D-PLS-DA score plot, incorporating the first three factors (factor 1, factor 2, and factor 3), is illustrated in Fig. 5. The model effectively differentiated the antler spectra of three deer species into distinct groups with 100% accuracy. During model calibration, a high R-square value of 0.99 and a low RMSE (root mean square error) value of 0.04 were obtained.
3D PLS-DA score plot to discriminate antler spectra of spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer.
Furthermore, random cross-validation was performed on the developed PLS-DA model to minimize the risk of overfitting. The model achieved a high R-square value of 0.97 and a low RMSE value of 0.13 during cross-validation. The high R-square values for both calibration and cross-validation reflect the strong performance of the model, while the low RMSE values indicate minimal prediction error rate. ## Blind test
Ten unknown antler samples (X1-X10) from spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer were employed for the blind test to evaluate the prediction performance of the PLS-DA model. The samples were processed and analyzed following the same experimental procedures described in the sample preparation and analysis sections. The class prediction of the unknown antler spectra was carried out using the PLS-DA model developed using the training dataset. Figure 8 demonstrates the prediction results, where the Y-axis represents the known sample classes and the X-axis denotes the unknown samples. As summarized in Table 3, each unknown sample was assigned to a specific class based on its predicted Y-score, which corresponded to or closely matched the dummy Y-values of the known class (class 1- spotted deer, class 2- sambar deer, and class 3- swamp deer). A comparison with previously reported studies demonstrated the relative strengths and limitations of existing and present approaches. Choi et al. achieved 100% accuracy in differentiating Cervus elaphus antlers by geographical origin using a PCA model derived from 1H NMR spectra, whereas Leonard et al. focused only on structural characterization without any comparative or predictive classification. Lefebvre et al. employed SVM modelling on X-ray microtomography data to differentiate reindeer and red deer antlers, achieving predictive accuracy of 82% for modern samples and 42% for archaeological samples. Guo et al. performed protein profiling-based differentiation of reindeer, sika deer, and red deer velvet antlers using PCA, and this approach showed limited discriminatory power between red deer and sika deer due to similarity in protein compositions. In contrast, Singh et al. reported 100% discrimination accuracy among five different deer species using XRY-based PCA model, although the analysis was restricted to samples obtained from single individuals. In comparison, the present work findings demonstrate that combining ATR-FTIR spectroscopy with PLS-DA provides a rapid, accurate, cost-effective, and non-destructive alternative to existing methods for the identification and differentiation of antler samples from spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer.

## Conclusion
Deer antlers are frequently encountered in wildlife seizures due to their high demand as an osseous material used in the manufacturing of artifacts, furniture, pistol butts, and traditional medicines. Consequently, the accurate identification of the species of origin of seized antlers or their derivatives is crucial in wildlife forensic investigations and plays a vital role in assisting law enforcement agencies in monitoring and curbing the illegal antler trade. In the present study, antlers from three deer species were identified and differentiated using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy combined with chemometric analysis. ATR-FTIR spectroscopy provides a rapid, reliable, and non-destructive approach for analyzing antler samples, while chemometric techniques facilitate objective interpretation of results by minimizing bias in the data evaluation. Additionally, this technique can produce reliable results from trace amounts of powder sample, while preserving the sample for further elemental and DNA profiling.
The mid-IR spectra of antler samples from spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer were first examined visually. Due to the similar chemical compositions of the antlers across these species, visual comparisons failed to differentiate between them. Thereafter, chemometric techniques, including PCA and PLS-DA, were applied to the pre-processed training dataset. The PCA model explained 92% of the total variation within the dataset using the first seven PCs and achieved a differentiation accuracy of 93.33% due to the misclustering of two sambar deer antler spectra and two spotted deer antler spectra. To enhance differentiation accuracy, a PLS-DA model was developed, which successfully differentiated antler spectra from the three deer species with 100% accuracy. The model exhibited high R-square values and low RMSE values during both calibration and cross-validation, demonstrating its robustness and prediction efficiency.
Validation studies, including external validation and a blind test, were performed to validate the prediction performance of the PLS-DA model. Both tests yielded 100% accuracy, correctly classifying the validation dataset and precisely identifying the unknown samples. These results demonstrate that combining ATR-FTIR spectroscopy with PLS-DA provides a rapid, accurate, cost-effective, and non-destructive alternative to existing methods for the identification and differentiation of antler samples from spotted deer, sambar deer, and swamp deer. However, further research with a large sample size and inclusion of additional species is required to explore the efficiency of the present approach comprehensively.
