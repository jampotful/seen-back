Exploring horses’ (Equus caballus) gaze and asymmetric ear position in relation to human attentional cues

# Exploring horses’ (Equus caballus) gaze and asymmetric ear position in relation to human attentional cues

## Abstract
AbstractStudies have shown that horses are sensitive to human attentional cues. Also, there is some evidence that they might be able to infer the knowledge state of a human and communicate intentionally with us. However, this ability is not fully characterized and certain behaviors, like gaze, asymmetric ears position or vocalizations, which could work as attention-getting behaviors, have been scarcely studied in this context. The aim of the present study was to assess whether horses’ gaze toward a person, asymmetric ears position and vocalizations are subject to audience effects and adjust to human attentional cues, which would suggest a communicative function. For this purpose, we adapted a protocol used with domestic dogs [Kaminski et al. 2017] and observed horses’ spontaneous behavior (gaze, asymmetric ears position and vocalizations) in the presence of a human holding and not holding food in an attentive position (facing the horse with open eyes) and in a non-attentive position (with her back turned towards the horse). We found significant evidence of horses being sensitive to human attentional cues (reflected in the horses’ gaze duration and asymmetric position of the ears), but not of intentional communication towards humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10071-024-01909-y.Keywords: Horse cognition, Attentional state, Intentional communication, Gaze, Asymmetric ears, Vocalizations

## Introduction
The ability to detect the attentional cues of others and respond accordingly has adaptive advantages, particularly for social species. For example, following others’ gaze allows to detect the presence of predators, competitors and allies and can help noticing significant objects and events (Castellano-Navarro et al. 2021; Proops et al. 2013). Being sensitive to others’ attentional cues is, in addition, a requisite for understanding their mental state, including their visual perspective, knowledge, beliefs and other aspects of Theory of Mind, which allows to interpret, predict, and manipulate their behavior better (Castellano-Navarro et al. 2020; Karg et al. 2015). Also, adjustment to others’ attentional cues is one of the operational criteria used to determine intentionality in animal communication (Leavens et al. 2005; Rodrigues and Fröhlich 2024; Townsend et al. 2017).
Evidence indicates that domesticated species with a close relationship with humans adapt their behavior to human attentional cues (e.g., Dogs: Call et al. 2003; Schwab and Huber 2006; Virányi et al. 2004; Goats: Nawroth et al. 2015; Pigs: Nawroth et al. 2013). Among them, dogs are the most studied, and it has been claimed that they might be able to discriminate human’s attentional state in a variety of contexts (e.g., Brauer et al., 2004; Call et al. 2003; Gácsi et al. 2004; Hare and Tomasello 2005; Kaminski et al. 2009; Schwab and Huber 2006; Virányi et al. 2004), as well as they might communicate intentionally with humans (e.g., Gaunet and Deputte 2011; Piotti and Kaminski 2016; Savalli et al. 2014; Wynne 2016). Although not as abundant, research on horse cognition has increased in recent years, allowing us to gain insight on this species’ social abilities (for reviews, see: Brubaker and Udell 2016; Hausberger et al. 2008; Merkies and Franzin 2021).
Some studies have shown that horses are sensitive to human attentional cues (Krueger and Flauger 2008; Krueger et al. 2011; Proops and McComb 2010; Proops et al. 2013; Ringhofer and Yamamoto 2017; Trösch et al. 2019). For instance, Proops and McComb (2010) observed that adult horses approached for food an attentive, more than an inattentive, person based on her body and head orientation as well as on eye cues (open/closed) (see also Proops et al. 2013).
Other studies have tried to assess whether horses can discriminate between an experimenter who has witnessed a food-hiding process and an experimenter who has not, which would suggest that horses are able to infer the knowledge state of a human towards a baiting event (although this would be arguable, see Penn and Povinelli 2007). Ringhofer and Yamamoto (2017) found that horses touched and gazed longer towards a human in the non-witness compared to the witness condition. The authors argued that the horses might have begged the witness for the food during the baiting process, without success, and could have therefore interpreted that this person had no intention to give them the food, which would explain why they directed more behaviors towards the non-witness during the test (thus rendering the results inconclusive). To preclude this possibility, Trösch et al. (2019) conducted a similar study in which both experimenters were simultaneously present during the baiting process. They found that the horses gazed and touched the witness significantly more than the non-witness. Besides the implications regarding Theory of Mind (the horses seemed to expect receiving food from a knowledgeable more than from an unknowledgeable person), the authors interpreted the horses’ gazing and touching behaviors as attention-getting, communicative behaviors that would serve to tell the experimenter about the presence of food and as a request.
More direct research on communication has also suggested that, in provisioning contexts, horses might perform communicative signs according to humans’ attentional cues. When presenting horses with an out-of-reach source of food and an experimenter who was either frontally oriented, backward oriented, walking away from the arena or frontally oriented with other people present in the arena, Malavasi and Huber (2016) observed a higher rate of gaze alternation in the frontal condition than in all other conditions. The authors interpreted this as the horses using referential gestures to manipulate the attention of the human and obtain the food. Although, in principle, this same result could be regarded as a consequence of both food and human faces attracting the horses’ attention (note that faces are relevant stimuli for mammals, especially for social species; see Leopold and Rhodes 2010 and Stone 2010), more in the absence of distractors, according to the authors, the horses elaborated further their communication by switching from a visual to a tactile cue and demonstrated perseverance in their communicative efforts. Similarly, Takimoto et al. (2016) observed that horses performed more visual behaviors (gaze alternation between the experimenter and the food) when the experimenter had her eyes open and more auditory and tactile behaviors (pawing on the ground, touching the experimenter) when she had her eyes closed. The authors also interpreted these as intentional, begging behaviors. Besides gaze, gaze alternation, pawing and touching, horses’ ears position (in particular, asymmetric ears position, with one ear oriented forwards and the other one backwards) and ears movement have also been proposed as possible communicative gestures towards humans in food-provisioning contexts. Alterisio and collaborators observed that these behaviors were more frequent, and their duration was longer, in an unsolvable task when a person was present compared to when no person was present or in a non-food condition (Alterisio et al. 2018). In contrast, horses did not use vocalizations as communicative signs towards humans in a similar context (Ringhofer and Yamamoto 2017), although it might be that they preferred to use visual over auditive signals in this case, since the person was facing them and, although in isolation, they were tested in an outdoor paddock, where making sounds could be perceived as implying the risk of attracting the attention of potential competitors (see Castellano-Navarro et al. 2021 for a similar finding with Japanese macaques).
Taking the above into account, and considering the scarcity of data on the topic, the main aim of our study was to investigate whether horses’ gaze toward a person, asymmetric ears position and vocalizations vary according to human attentional cues, and whether they have a communicative function. For this purpose, we adapted a protocol designed by Kaminski et al. (2017) that aimed to assess whether dogs’ facial expressions are subject to an audience effect and/or change in response to an arousing stimulus (food). In their study, Kaminski et al. (2017) found that dogs produced significantly more facial movements when the human was facing them with open eyes than when she had her back towards them, whereas whether she held food or not did not affect the dogs’ behavior. The authors concluded that dogs are sensitive to the human’s attentional state and proposed that facial expressions are not just involuntary displays of food-related emotional states, but rather potential attempts of communicative behavior. One could interpret these results as the dogs’ facial expressions being involuntary displays of socially-related emotional states, though. Moreover, the fact that the dogs did not produce more facial expressions when the human was attentive and with food in her hands speaks against the interpretation that they used facial expressions as intentional, communicative signals. Nevertheless, Kaminski et al.’s (2017) protocol can be useful to identify both subjects’ sensitivity to human attentional cues (if behaviors differ between the attentive and the non-attentive human conditions) and intentional communication (if more visual behaviors are produced in the attentive and more non-visual behaviors are produced in the non-attentive condition, particularly in the presence of food). Unfortunately, the experimental setup did not allow us to study pawing and touching. However, we provided the necessary conditions (horses tested in isolation in a familiar, indoor space and a human present but showing the back to the horse) to assess the horses’ use of vocalizations in this context.
In addition, we wanted to investigate whether gaze laterality (gaze with left eye, with right eye or binocular gaze) varied across conditions. Whereas binocular gaze has been said to reflect attention and stimulus anticipation in horses (Hintze et al. 2016; Rochais et al. 2017; Waring 2003), lateral gaze has been interpreted as showing emotions (de Boyer des Roches et al. 2008; Farmer et al. 2010; Larose et al. 2006). The evidence is, however, mixed. Thus, whereas some studies indicate that horses tend to look at neutral stimuli with the right eye (de Boyer des Roches et al. 2008; Austin and Rogers 2014), the left eye has been associated to the inspection of stimuli both with a positive valence (de Boyer des Roches et al. 2008) and a negative valence (Wathan et al. 2016). A preference for using the left eye has also been found in situations that required being vigilant, both in feral (Austin and Rogers 2012) and Przewalski horses (Austin and Rogers 2014), when observing a human (Farmer et al. 2010) and during the inspection of novel stimuli, particularly those eliciting more arousal (Larose et al. 2006), as well as in situations of social interaction (Farmer et al. 2010). Therefore, more research is needed to confirm the predominance of left, over right, eye gaze on arousing stimuli, and to clarify whether left and right eye gaze are contingent on the type of stimulus observed (appetitive or aversive). Similarly, some authors have proposed that horses’ ears position may reflect emotions. Specifically, it has been stated that ears forwards might reflect attention to a stimuli eliciting positive emotions (Gleerup et al. 2015; Smith et al. 2018; Stomp et al. 2018; Rochais et al. 2017), whereas ears backwards has been mainly related with negative experiences (pain, fear, discomfort, submission, avoidance or aggression context) (Dalla Costa et al. 2014; Gleerup et al. 2015; McDonnell 2003; Smith et al. 2018; Waring 2003) (but see Wathan et al. 2016). Only a few studies have evaluated asymmetrical ears in relation to emotions, though, and, as with gaze laterality, the results are mixed (Alterisio et al. 2018; Lansade et al. 2018; Smith et al. 2018; Wathan et al. 2016). Smith et al. (2018) observed, among other behaviors, the asymmetric position and movement of the ears of horses exposed to human vocalizations with different valences. They found that horses showed more asymmetric ears when exposed to positive vocalizations. However, other studies have found an association between asymmetrical ears and negative emotions in horses (Gleerup et al. 2015; Lansade et al. 2018; Wathan et al. 2016). For instance, Wathan et al. (2016) found that when observing pictures of conspecifics showing agonistic expressions, horses spent longer time with their ears in asymmetrical position compared to when observing conspecifics with relaxed expressions. Another interpretation of asymmetric ears is that they might reflect arousal states related to vigilance (Alterisio et al. 2018; Austin and Rogers 2012, 2014). Additional data would contribute to evaluate these alternative explanations for asymmetric ears in horses.
In sum, following Kaminski et al. 2017; we presented horses with four conditions: an attentive human holding food, an attentive human not holding food, a non-attentive human holding food and a non-attentive human not holding food. We measured the horses’ gaze duration, asymmetric ears duration, and vocalizations. If, as has been proposed in the literature, these behaviors are used by the horses as communicative cues towards humans to obtain food, we expected to find differences in these behaviors between the attentive and the non-attentive conditions, more in the condition with food than in the condition without food. Also, we expected to find different patterns in the visual compared to the auditory behaviors, with gaze and asymmetric ears occurring more in the attentive conditions and vocalizations occurring more in the non-attentive conditions. If an effect of human attention but not of food presence was found, this could be interpreted as horses being sensitive to human attention cues, as previous studies have found, but not as them using these behaviors as communicative signals. Finally, considering the previous mixed results, we had no specific predictions for the possibility that gaze laterality and ears asymmetry express emotions. However, we were interested in exploring whether these behaviors would show a pattern consistent with this kind of interpretation or, rather, with horses’ attention, food anticipation or alert state.

## Materials and methods

## Subjects
We assessed 18 domestic horses (15 females and 3 males; mean age = 13.61 ± 3.82 years) housed at the Veterinary Teaching and Research Farm of the Universidad Cardenal Herrera-CEU (Valencia, Spain). All the horses were habituated to interacting regularly with humans. During the study they were fed according to the normal daily routine and the test took place half an hour to 2 h before the second feeding, so no specific food deprivation was required. Water was available ad libitum by automatic drinkers.

## Materials
Each horse was tested individually in a family box (2.90 × 2.90 m) with a window through which the horse could observe the stimuli presented outside. Three cameras (Sony HDR-CX625) on tripods were used to record the testing. The main camera was located just behind the experimenter, who stood 1,5 m in front of the window, whereas the two auxiliary cameras were located 1,5 m from the window, one on each side (right and left) (Fig. 1). Two pieces of apple and one piece of carrot were used in each condition that required food.
Experimental Setting. Experimenter position in (a) Attentive (AF, ANF) and (b) Non-Attentive (NAF, NANF) conditions

## Procedure
We adapted the protocol designed by Kaminski et al. (2017) for assessing the ability of dogs to communicate intentionally based on human´s attentional states. The subject was allowed to move around the box freely during the test. Few minutes before the test, the experimenter, who was unknown to the horses, opened the window and interacted with the horse by petting and talking to it, without offering any food. This period, of 3 min approximately, allowed the animal to familiarize with the human and with the tripods and cameras that were located outside the box. After this familiarization period the test began. The test was always performed by the same female experimenter. Only the experimenter and the subject were present during testing. The protocol consisted of four conditions (Fig. 1) where the experimenter stood:
In an attentive position with food (Attentive-food: A-F) (facing the horse with the arms pointing towards the horse and the palms placed together displaying food).
In an attentive position without food (Attentive-no food: A-NF) (facing the horse in the same position as A-F condition but not displaying food).
In a non-attentive position with food (Non-attentive-food: NA-F) (with the back turned towards the horse and the arms behind the back, with the palms placed together displaying food).
In a non-attentive position without food (Non-attentive-non-food: NA-NF) (in the same position as NA-F condition but not displaying food).
Each condition lasted 2 min, during which the experimenter stood still and did not respond to the horse’s behavior, which was recorded by the cameras. Between conditions there was a brief interval (30 s) where the experimenter interacted (pet and talk) with the subject and prepared for the next condition. The horses did not receive the food shown in food conditions until the four conditions concluded. While not used, the food was kept outside of the subject’s view.
A within-subjects design was used. Each subject received all four conditions in a single session. The order of conditions was counterbalanced across horses, with the restriction that the test never started with condition NA-NF, to avoid demotivation.

## Data analyses
Behaviors were recorded using the open-source software BORIS (Behavioral Observation Research Interactive Software) V.7.4.14, (Friard and Gamba 2016). One independent observer analyzed all the measures in 100% of the video-taped material. A second coder, who was naïve to this study, coded 25% of the material (randomly selected). To test inter-observer reliability, we calculated Pearson’s coefficients of correlation for all the measures, which showed good reliability rs > 0.92, ps < 0.02, n = 5).
All the analyses were run with SPSS 23.0. All tests were two-tailed, α = 0.05. Data were analyzed using Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) with a Gamma distribution. Models were built using the Backward method, based on the gradual elimination of variables to find a reduced model that best explains the data. The distribution of each dependent variable [gaze, gaze laterality (direct, right, left gaze), asymmetrical ears] was related to the fixed factors. Because fifteen of the eighteen horses never produced vocalizations in any of the four conditions, this variable was excluded from the analysis.
For gaze, the initial model included Attentional state (attentive, not attentive), Food (present, not present), Gaze, Sex (female, male), Age and Order of conditions (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th ) as fixed factors. This model also included all possible interactions. Because sex, age and testing order as well as their interactions failed to reach significance, these factors were removed from the model. The final reduced model included Attentional state, Food and Gaze as fixed factors and their interactions (Gaze x Attentional state, Gaze x Food and Attentional state x Food). In addition, horses’ ID was included as a random variable accounting for the repeated measures within each horse.
For gaze laterality (gaze with both eyes, with right eye, and with left eye) the model included Attentional state, Food and Gaze laterality as fixed factors and their interactions (Gaze laterality x Attentional state, Gaze laterality x Food and Attentional state x Food). In addition, horses’ ID was included as a random variable accounting for the repeated measures within each horse.
For asymmetric ears position the initial model included Attentional state (attentive, not attentive), Food (present, not present), Sex (female, male), Age and Order of conditions (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th ) as fixed factors. This model also included all possible interactions. The horses’ ID was included as a random variable accounting for the repeated measures within each horse. Because sex, age and testing order as well as their interactions failed to reach significance, these factors were removed from the model. The final reduced model included Attentional state and Food as fixed factors and their interaction (Attentional state x Food). In addition, horses’ ID was included as a random variable accounting for the repeated measures within each horse.
In case of significance, the estimated coefficients, standard errors (SE), and confidence intervals (CI) (95%) are reported as an indication of the degree of change in the outcome. When required, additional comparisons were conducted using paired contrasts (sequential adjusted Bonferroni).

## Results

## Gaze
Horses’ gaze duration towards the experimenter differed across conditions (A-F condition: 38.95 ± 25.84 s; A-NF condition: 26.36 ± 25.88 s; NA-F: 21.68 ± 24.21 s; NA-NF: 21.79 ± 18.60 s). The analysis yielded a significant effect of Attentional state (F(1.15) = 9.59, p = .002) on Gaze duration. Subjects looked longer in those conditions where the experimenter was attentive (A-F, A-NF) (12.02 ± 11.91) compared to when she was not (NA-F, NA-NF) (8.05 ± 11.88) (SE = 1.28 CI.95 = 1.44, 6.51, t (145) = 3,10, p < .002). No principal effect of Food nor interactions were found (p > .05) (Fig. 3).
Average duration of horses’ gaze towards the experimenter during conditions A-F (attentive with food), A-NF (attentive without food), NA-F (non-attentive with food) and NA-NF (non-attentive without food). *P < .05. two-tailed tests
Moreover, the model showed a significant effect of Gaze laterality (F(2.16) = 7.89, p = .001). In general, horses gazed with both eyes (13.08 ± 11.05) more than with their left (8.02 ± 10.99) (SE = 1.56 CI.95 = 1.52, 8.60, t (155) = 3,24, p < .003) and right eye (6.79 ± 10.98) (SE = 1.58 CI.95 = 2.45, 10.11, t (155) = 3,97, p < .0001). No principal effect of Food was found (p > .05). However, the model yielded a significant Gaze laterality x Food interaction (F(2,16) = 5.23, p = .006). Paired contrasts revealed that, in the conditions where there was food present, horses gazed more with both eyes (16.69 ± 11.25 s) than with their left (6.59 ± 10.98 s) (SE = 2.67, CI.95 = 3.65, 16.55, t (155) = 3.79, p < .001) and right eye (6.46 ± 11.02 s) (SE = 2.81, CI.95 = 3.87, 16.60, t (155) = 3.64, p < .001). Other comparisons were not significant, p > .05 (Table 2).
Mean and standard deviation of the duration of both eyes, right eye and left eye gaze toward the experimenter across conditions

## Ears
Ears’ asymmetric position was not affected by Attentional state (p > .05). However, a principal effect of Food was found F(1.56) = 5.20, p = .026). Subjects spent more time with their ears in an asymmetrical position (specifically left ear back/right ear forward) in conditions where there was food present (0.28 ± 0.14 s) compared to conditions without food (0.18 ± 0.13 s) (SE = 0.04 CI.95 = 0.012, 0.19, t (56) = 2.81, p < .026). In addition, the model yielded a significant Attentional state x Food interaction (F(1,56) = 4.33, p = .042). In those conditions where food was present, the horses spent longer time with their ears in an asymmetrical position when the experimenter was inattentive (0.36 ± 0.15 s) compared to when she was attentive (0.20 ± 0.14 s) (b = 0.16, SE = 0.07, CI.95 = 0.01, 0.31, t (56) = 2.14, p < .036) (Fig. 4).
Mean rate of asymmetric ears position during conditions A-F (attentive with food), A-NF (attentive without food), NA-F (non-attentive with food) and NA-NF (non-attentive without food). *P < .05. two-tailed tests

## Vocalizations
Only three horses vocalized during this study. They did so 10 times overall, of which there were 6 whinnies (2 in condition A-F: attentive position with food; and 4 in condition A-NF: attentive position without food), 1 nicker (in condition A-NF) and 3 snorts (1 in condition A-F and 2 in condition A-NF).

## Discussion
Consistent with the idea that horses detect human attentional cues, the amount of time that horses looked at the experimenter when she was oriented towards them (attentive position) was higher in comparison to when she had her back turned to them (inattentive position). This was irrespective of the experimenter holding food or not, so our results do not support the interpretation that gaze has a begging function. This is in contrast with what has been concluded about gaze alternation, pawing and touching in previous studies with horses (e.g., Takimoto et al. 2016) and other species (e.g., Barrera et al. 2011; Bentosela et al. 2008), but coincides with the results obtained by Kaminski et al. (2017) with dogs. It could be argued that, even in the absence of visible food, the horses might use their gaze as a begging gesture to obtain (non-visible) food or other kind of appetitive stimulus, like petting. Future studies should investigate this possibility. However, the longer gaze towards an attentive human might just reflect the fact that faces are an important category of visual stimuli for vertebrates, particularly mammals (Leopold and Rhodes 2010) and social species, like horses, which have proved able to discriminate humans based on their face (e.g., Stone 2010). Moreover, domestic horses are probably influenced by the experience that humans usually face them before starting an interaction, so attention (Rochais et al. 2017) and stimulus anticipation (Hintze et al. 2016) could explain our results.
Horses preferred looking straight with both eyes, particularly when the experimenter held food, and we found no evidence of them gazing more with the left or the right eye. This supports the idea that horses’ binocular gaze shows attention, in this case towards a human but, mainly, towards food (Rochais et al. 2017). Also, the horses’ binocular gaze could show stimulus anticipation, since humans habitually provide them with food (or perhaps they expected to be able to grab the food by themselves, see below) (Hintze et al. 2016). Considering that horses’ binocular vision comprises only a narrow space at their front (Saslow 2002) and given that 3D vision allows to calculate distances better, it might be that the horses were trying to assess the distance to the experimenter and the food. As a prey species, however, it is likely that horses rely heavily on senses other than sight (Saslow 2002), so we must also consider the possibility that their straight position was related to the use of smell or hearing.
One of our main interests was assessing whether ears asymmetry might function as a communicative cue, as has been proposed in the literature (Alterisio et al. 2018). Our results showed a principal effect of food presence on the horses’ ears asymmetry. In particular, the horses showed more ears asymmetry when the experimenter held food compared to when she did not. In addition, we found that, in those conditions with food, the horses showed more ears asymmetry (left ear backwards/right ear forwards) when the experimenter was inattentive compared to when she was attentive. These results further support the idea that horses detect humans’ attention cues but contradict the hypothesis proposed by Alterisio et al. (2018) that ears asymmetry may have a communicative function. One possible explanation would be that, when the experimenter was non-attentive, the horses divided their attention between the food and other aspects of their environment, thus positioning their ears asymmetrically. Alternatively, this behavior could reflect alertness (Alterisio et al. 2018; Austin and Rogers 2012, 2014). In our study, when the experimenter was not attentive, the horses may have been more prone to try to grab the food by themselves. This would require extra vigilance, both because of the vulnerable posture of the horse when stretching its neck (Alterisio et al. 2018) and because of the potential negative reaction by the experimenter. Ears asymmetry, therefore, might reflect alert in this case. This explanation is based on the idea that ears asymmetry is especially suitable in situations requiring general, rather than focused, attention, since it allows to detect auditive stimuli coming from different directions.
As said in the data analyses and in the results sections, horses’ vocalizations were scarce in this study, so no statistical analyses were carried with this variable. However, it is worth noting that all vocalizations occurred in conditions where the experimenter was attentive. This fits the idea that horses identify humans’ attentional cues. Since, apparently, vocalizations occurred irrespective of food being present or not, future studies should investigate the possibility that they serve as a communicative cue towards humans other than begging. Also, it would be interesting to assess whether and how different types of vocalizations may have different functions in this and other contexts.
Finally, in our study, horses’ age and sex did not seem to affect the results. However, in our sample there were only three male horses, so it is possible that the results are not sufficiently representative of this sex. Future research should confirm this and consider analyzing other variables, like sociability, trainability, general temperament, different experiences during ontogeny and familiarity with the experimenter.
In conclusion, the present study offers new empirical data supporting the idea that horses detect human attentional cues. This appeared reflected in the horses’ gaze duration and asymmetric position of the ears, as well as it was suggested by the few vocalizations recorded. Unlike what has been proposed in the literature, however, we did not find evidence in favor of these variables being used as communicative signals by the horses. We interpret these behaviors as well as gaze laterality in terms of attention, stimulus anticipation and vigilance state, although we cannot rule out the possibility that they express emotions related to food and human presence. Hopefully, future studies will help clarify these points.
