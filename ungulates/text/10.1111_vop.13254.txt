Microphthalmia with multiple ocular abnormalities in a foal.

# Microphthalmia with multiple ocular abnormalities in a foal

## Abstract
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this report was to provide a clinical description and clinical, ultrasonographic, and histologic images of a foal with microphthalmia and multiple ocular abnormalities.Animals StudiedA 12‐h old Friesian‐American Paint Horse crossbred filly presented for blindness, microphthalmia and marked ventral strabismus in both eyes.ProcedureA complete ophthalmic examination was performed. Ultrasound biomicroscopy and B‐mode ultrasonography were performed. The globes were submitted for histopathology.ResultsUltrasound biomicroscopy demonstrated a hyperechoic cornea void of the typical epithelium, stroma, and Descemet's membrane layers. The anterior chamber was spanned by thick strands of hyperechoic tissue extending from the iris to the cornea. The lens was not visualized. B‐mode ultrasound showed aphakia and mild, mottled echogenicity within the vitreous with no evidence of retinal detachment. On histopathology, both globes were microphthalmic with poorly defined corneal tissue. The anterior chambers were poorly formed and contained lacrimal glandular tissue. Portions of iridal tissue were present, but no lenses were noted. The retinas were segmentally detached and markedly atrophied with areas of retina dysplasia noted.ConclusionsThis report provides a clinical, ultrasonographic and histologic description of a rare, congenital condition in a foal characterized by microphthalmia, aphakia, poorly defined corneal tissue, choristomatous differentiation of the anterior segment and retinal dysplasia.

## INTRODUCTION
This report describes a foal with bilateral microphthalmia with multiple ocular abnormalities, including microphthalmia, anterior segment dysgenesis, corneal meibomian gland choristomatous differentiation, uveal cysts and lacrimal gland and duct differentiation, aphakia, multifocal inner to full‐thickness retinal atrophy with retinal rosettes, optic nerve cupping, and optic nerve dysplasia. Fragola and Teixeira reported similar abnormalities in a series of 11 foals in an abstract summarizing histopathologic findings. 1 In that abstract, a genetic mutation was not identified. The current case report adds a clinical and ultrasound biomicroscopic description to morphologic descriptions of this syndrome, which may allow for easier identification of affected foals.

## CASE REPORT

## History and clinical examination
A 12‐hour old Friesian‐American Paint Horse crossbred filly presented to the Purdue Veterinary Hospital Ophthalmology service for “abnormal eyes”. The owner was not present at parturition and found the foal standing and nursing. The pregnancy was normal. However, the mare had a retained placenta that passed shortly after administration of oxytocin. There was no history of illness or potential toxin exposure during the pregnancy.
The physical exam was unremarkable besides grossly abnormal eyes. On ophthalmic examination, the foal had a negative menace response and dazzle reflex in both eyes (OU). The foal appeared completely blind as she tried to navigate the room and find the mare. On gross examination microphthalmia and marked ventral strabismus were noted OU. Schirmer tear test and fluorescein staining were not performed. Intraocular pressures as measured with a TonoVet Plus® (Icare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland) were 37 mmHg in the right eye (OD) and 32 mmHg in the left eye (OS). Slit‐lamp biomicroscopy (SL‐17, Kowa, Torrance, CA, USA) revealed moderate conjunctival hyperemia OU. A staphyloma was noted dorsonasally OD. The dorsal corneal region OU was opaque with dense pigmentation and structures that had the appearance of hair follicles (Figure 1). The follicle formation was more pronounced OD than OS. The ventral cornea could not be assessed OU due to the marked ventral strabismus. The anterior chamber, lens, and posterior segment OU were not visible due to the absence of clear cornea.

The dorsal corneal region was opaque with dense pigmentation and structures that had the appearance of hair follicles (asterisk). The follicle formation was more pronounced OD than OS. A staphyloma was noted dorsonasally OD (arrow). (B) Clinical image of OS that also presents with ventral strabismus, dense pigmentation, and hair follicle like structures similar to OD. ## Diagnostics

## Ultrasound biomicroscopy
Ultrasound biomicroscopy (Linear 50 mHz UBM probe; Aviso, Quantell Medical, Bozeman MT, USA) using a ClearScan® probe cover (ESI, Inc., Plymouth, MN, USA) was performed OU. UBM demonstrated a diffusely hyperechoic cornea missing the typical epithelial, stromal, and Descemet's membrane layers (Figure 2). The anterior chamber was spanned by thick strands of hyperechoic tissue extending from the iris to the cornea. The lens was not visualized. B‐mode ultrasound examination (10 mHz probe; Aviso, Quantell Medical, Bozeman MT, USA) was performed to assess the posterior segment. B‐mode ultrasound showed aphakia and mild, mottled echogenicity within the vitreous with no evidence of retinal detachment.
B‐mode ultrasonography of OD (A) and OS (B) that showed aphakia and mild, mottled echogenicity within the vitreous with no overt evidence of retinal detachment. Ultrasound biomicroscopy of OD (C) and OS (D) that showed the anterior chamber spanned by thick strands of hyperechoic tissue extending from the iris to the cornea.

## Histopathology
The owners elected to euthanize the foal due to the grave prognosis for vision. The owners donated the foal for necropsy, released all tissues for research purposes, and gave consent for publication. After euthanasia, the globes were enucleated (Figure 3). The globes measured 25 mm in diameter. The globes were then placed in 10% formalin. A complete general necropsy was performed by the Purdue University Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratory. A focally extensive, mild neutrophilic omphalophlebitis was noted. No signs of dysmaturity or congenital malformations besides the ocular lesions were noted.
The gross image of OD after enucleation (A) and cross‐section after fixation (C) are shown. The gross image of OS after enucleation (B) and cross‐section after fixation (D) are shown. On cross‐section, aphakia was present OU. The anterior uvea is seen replaced with broad sheet of iridal stroma with multiple cystic spaces that blend into the stroma of the posterior cornea.
The globes were sent to the Michigan State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory for histologic examination. The histologic diagnoses OU were microphthalmia, anterior segment dysgenesis, corneal meibomian gland choriostomatous differentiation, uveal cysts and lacrimal gland and duct differentiation, aphakia, multifocal inner to full‐thickness retinal atrophy with retinal rosettes, optic nerve cupping, and optic nerve dysplasia (Figure 4).
High magnification histologic features of bilateral microphthalmia and anterior segment dysgenesis in a foal. OU diagnoses are the same and histologic features are shown collectively. (A) Choriostomatous meibomian glands in the cornea. (B–D) Uveal cyst‐like spaces lined by cuboidal epithelium (B), stratified squamous epithelium (C), and pseudostratified ciliated epithelium (D) with underlying regions of lacrimal gland differentiation (asterisks). (E) Retinal atrophy with retinal rosettes (arrow). (F) Optic nerve dysplasia with disorganized nerve fascicles and cavitary spaces.
Representative sections from the sagittally bisected OD were stained using hematoxylin and eosin; changes were similar OS. Overall, the globe was small and there was severe malformation of the anterior segment. The cornea was thickened, characterized by fibrovascular tissue diffusely throughout the corneal stroma and multiple discrete, lobulated glands within the superficial corneal stroma composed of well differentiated, vacuolated sebocytes surrounding central keratinized squamous ducts, and a peripheral rim of basaloid reserve cells. These glands were contiguous with the overlying epithelium which was hyperplastic, followed by an undulating basement membrane, and contained large numbers of basal cells containing finely granular melanin pigment.
The anterior uvea was replaced by a broad sheet of dense iridal stroma that contained multiple cystic spaces and small numbers of interstitial neutrophils. There was no ciliary cleft, trabecular meshwork, or lens in examined sections. Descemet's membrane was diffusely inapparent and the iridal stroma blended into the stroma of the posterior cornea. Cystic spaces within the band of iridal stroma ranged in size from 1 to 10 mm and were variably lined by cuboidal epithelium, pseudostratified ciliated epithelium containing few goblet cells, and non‐keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. The iridal stroma underlying pseudostratified ciliated epithelium contained tubuloacinar glands as well as multiple small ducts lined by simple cuboidal to ciliated epithelium. Tubuloacinar glands were composed of polygonal epithelial cells that contained moderate amounts of vacuolated basophilic cytoplasm and had hyperchromatic basilary oriented nuclei. The posterior aspect of the band of iridal stroma was lined by a pigmented posterior epithelium.
The retina was segmentally detached, had segmental inner to full‐thickness atrophy, and very rare rosettes. Rosettes were composed of neurons from the outer nuclear layer, radially arranged around a lumen which was internally lined by the external limiting membrane and photoreceptor segments. Portions of the retina were also adhered to the posterior aspect of the band of iridal stroma. The optic disc was markedly cupped and nerve fascicles within the lamina cribrosa were disorganized and occasionally surrounded rare, unlined, empty cavitary spaces.

## DISCUSSION
The foal described in this paper had ocular lesions similar to those in 11 foals described in the abstract by Fragola and Teixeira. 1 Both globes were microphthalmic with poorly defined corneal tissue. The anterior chambers were poorly formed and contained lacrimal glandular tissue. Portions of iridal tissue were present, but no lenses were noted. The retinas were segmentally detached and markedly atrophied with areas of retina dysplasia noted.
The breed of the foal in this paper was a cross between a Paint and a Friesian. In the abstract by Fragola and Teixeira, only one foal (1/11) was listed as a Paint. There were no Friesians described; however, 3/11 foals were unknown breeds. 1 We hypothesize a major error in the formation of the optic vesicle that resulted in microphthalmia. Microphthalmia may result from deficient development of the optic vesicle or through delayed or incomplete closure of the optic fissure. 2 The abnormal optic vesicle formation would lead to a downstream effect on cell differentiation for each subsequent developmental step. The optic vesicle may have had either absent or incomplete contact with the overlying surface ectoderm, interfering with the “lens forming bias” signaling important for lens differentiation. 3 This would create an abnormal lens placode and consequently a lens vesicle that was unable to initiate formation of a lens, resulting in aphakia. 4
A gene mutation that critically effects ocular embryonic differentiation could be responsible for the ocular changes in the currently described foal. Interestingly, in humans, mutations in the FOXE3 gene have been documented in individuals with bilateral, congenital sclerocornea associated with aphakia, microphthalmia, and optic disc coloboma. 5 , 6 The FOXE3 gene encodes a transcription factor that is expressed during formation of the lens placode. The condition is inherited as an autosomal recessive trait. The presence of ocular anomalies in the heterozygote FOXE3 parents have varied: some are clinically normal while others have anterior segment anomalies. There appears to be variability in the penetrance of the mutant allele dependent on genetic background. 5 , 6
Absent or incomplete lens vesicle formation may have led to an incomplete separation from the surface ectoderm, resulting in anterior segment dysgenesis. 4 , 7 Erroneous lens vesicle formation likely interfered with appropriate development of the optic cup, more specifically the inner layer of the developing optic cup resulting in a dysplastic retina. This case also had segmental retinal detachments with inner to full‐thickness retinal atrophy with few rosettes. Interestingly, the segmental retinal detachments were not detected during ultrasound examination. This was likely because the segmental areas of atrophied retina could not be differentiated from the mottled echogenicity within the vitreous.
This case, along with the foals described by Fragola and Teixeira, 1 are the only reports of this set of ocular anomalies in equines. A similar presentation has been described in a 2 month old European red deer with bilateral aphakia, anterior segment dysgenesis, and retinal dysplasia. 8 Histologically, the deer also had choristomatous lesions with hyperplastic squamous epithelial cells with sebaceous gland differentiation within the corneal stroma. 8 A hereditary etiology was also hypothesized as the cause of the ocular abnormalities within the red deer.
To the authors' knowledge, there are no known reports of any infectious agents causing aphakia or retinal dysplasia in horses. Furthermore, the mare and foal were both systemically healthy upon presentation. Teratogens have been shown in animal models to induce anterior segment dysgenesis as they caused failure of the lens vesicle to separate from the ectoderm. 9 The owner was not aware of any potential teratogen exposure when asked about husbandry and environment. However, this does not discount teratogens as a potential cause as the owner may have been unable to witness or recognize potential exposure.
Future studies to identify specific gene mutations in affected equine patients with this clinical syndrome would be beneficial. Perhaps FOXE3 mutations are involved in these cases. Genetic material was sent to the UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory to facilitate such future studies.
