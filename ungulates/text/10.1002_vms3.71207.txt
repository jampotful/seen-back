# Precision Livestock Farming Technologies for Sheep Welfare in Extensive Systems: A Comprehensive Review

## Abstract
ABSTRACTBackgroundEnsuring animal welfare in extensive sheep production systems remains challenging due to large grazing areas, limited human supervision and the difficulty of detecting early signs of health or behavioural problems. Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) technologies have emerged as promising tools to enhance monitoring and welfare assessment in such environments.ObjectiveThis review examines recent advances in PLF technologies and their potential contribution to improving sheep welfare in extensive farming systems.MethodsA synthesis of more than 35 peer‐reviewed studies was conducted, focusing on the use of wearable sensors, computer vision, artificial intelligence and environmental monitoring technologies for behavioural and physiological assessment in sheep.ResultsThe reviewed literature indicates that wearable devices, including accelerometers and Global Positioning System (GPS) collars, can achieve up to 85% accuracy in detecting lameness and over 97% accuracy in identifying stress‐related physiological changes. Integration of remote sensing tools, drones and ground‐based sensors also improves monitoring of grazing patterns, social behaviour and environmental conditions. However, adoption remains limited due to economic costs, technical constraints and limited farmer awareness.ConclusionsAdvances in cost‐effective sensors, standardized welfare indicators and participatory technology design may facilitate wider adoption of PLF tools, supporting improved animal welfare, productivity and sustainability in extensive sheep production systems.

## Introduction
Animal welfare has emerged as a central concern in modern livestock production, reflecting both ethical imperatives and economic realities. Society's heightened awareness of how farm animals are raised, cared for and treated has elevated welfare into a priority alongside productivity and sustainability (Fraser 2008; Broom 2011; Berckmans 2017). This evolving landscape requires producers to embrace methods that not only optimize output but also ensure that animals live under conditions that meet their physiological and behavioural needs. Sheep farming, particularly in extensive production systems characterized by large pastures, rugged terrains and dispersed flocks, presents unique challenges to effective welfare management. Unlike intensive dairy or pig operations, where animals are housed in confined environments, monitoring individual sheep's behaviour, health status and environmental exposures across vast outdoor landscapes is difficult and labour‐intensive. Traditional welfare assessment, often reliant on periodic visual inspection or manual handling, is limited in scope and timeliness, leaving welfare issues underdetected or addressed only after problems become severe (Dwyer 2009; Munoz et al. 2019; Gómez et al. 2021; Plaza, Palacio et al. 2022; Plaza, Sánchez et al. 2022). Recent technological advancements, collectively referred to as Precision Livestock Farming (PLF), offer a transformative approach to these challenges by leveraging continuous, real‐time data collection at the individual‐animal level. PLF integrates wearable sensors, computer vision (CV) systems and environmental monitoring tools to provide detailed, objective insights into behaviours and health indicators previously difficult to capture remotely (Gómez et al. 2021; Papakonstantinou et al. 2024).
Extensive sheep systems are common worldwide, particularly in regions, such as the Mediterranean, Australia, the Americas and parts of Asia and Africa, where pastoral grazing is the norm (Bernabucci et al. 2025). In these systems, farmers often manage hundreds or thousands of animals over large, sometimes inaccessible terrain, complicating the timely identification of disease, injury or stress. For example, lameness, a major welfare and economic concern in sheep, is traditionally detected through close behavioural observation or adverse clinical signs, which often appear late (de Rancourt et al. 2006; Kaler and Green 2009; Bernués et al. 2011; Lewis et al. 2022). This delay prolongs animal suffering and reduces treatment efficacy (Kaler et al. 2020). Similarly, detecting parasitic burdens, nutritional deficits or social stress require extensive labour and expertise, limiting proactive management. The sparse human–animal interaction inherent in extensive settings further exacerbates these difficulties, as animals have less frequent contact with caretakers, thereby masking symptoms and limiting opportunities for intervention (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). As a result, welfare management in extensive sheep farming has historically lagged behind more controlled systems, such as dairy cattle, where technological solutions are better integrated. PLF offers the promise of overcoming these inherent challenges by providing continuous, automated and objective animal monitoring (Berckmans 2017; Bernabucci et al. 2025). Wearable devices, such as Global Positioning System (GPS) collars and accelerometers, record movement patterns and social interactions, enabling early detection of deviations that may be related to health or welfare concerns (Gómez et al. 2021; Ikurior et al. 2021; Almasi et al. 2022; Ermetin et al. 2022). CV technologies use camera systems and artificial intelligence (AI) to assess body condition, posture and behavioural cues without handling animals directly (Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). Environmental sensors complement animal‐focused data by tracking microclimatic parameters, including temperature, humidity and pasture quality, to contextualize welfare risks, such as heat stress, undernutrition or adverse weather exposure (Rose and Chilvers 2018; Bernabucci et al. 2025). Integration of these multidimensional data streams empowers farmers to respond proactively, preventing welfare deterioration rather than reacting after welfare has been compromised.
In diverse regions worldwide, PLF technologies have demonstrated significant benefits for sheep welfare. In Patagonia, the use of GPS and accelerometer sensors on Merino sheep enabled detailed monitoring of grazing behaviour, bite rates and movement patterns during drought‐induced nutritional stress. This real‐time data supported targeted management strategies that reduced mortality under extreme climatic conditions (Bailey et al. 2018; di Virgilio et al. 2018). Similarly, pilot programs in Scotland and Norway equipped sheep with proximity loggers and accelerometers to automatically detect lameness and social isolation, enabling farmers to intervene promptly and enhance flock welfare (SEFARI 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). In China, innovative multisensory collars capable of recording physiological parameters, such as heart rate and body temperature, achieved over 97% accuracy in identifying health and stress issues, allowing precise, timely interventions (Fuchs et al. 2019; dos Reis and White 2022; Reis et al. 2023). Beyond ethical considerations, PLF adoption in sheep production offers substantial economic benefits. Early disease detection and precision nutrition management reduce morbidity and mortality losses and optimize feed efficiency (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024; Bernabucci et al. 2025). By improving animal health and welfare, farms can enhance productivity and product quality while minimizing environmental footprints through smarter resource use. Moreover, PLF can contribute to sustainable livestock systems by enabling better pasture management and reducing unnecessary medical treatments, such as antibiotics, aligning with global efforts to combat antimicrobial resistance and environmental degradation (Rose and Chilvers 2018; Schillings et al. 2023). Although PLF technologies have gained significant ground in the cattle and pig sectors, their uptake in sheep farming remains comparatively limited. The extensive nature of sheep systems necessitates rugged, affordable and user‐friendly technologies that can operate in remote areas with limited connectivity (Tzanidakis et al. 2023). Additionally, a few existing solutions have undergone the broad external validation necessary to build confidence among farmers and industry stakeholders. Farmer attitudes towards technology adoption vary, with many being motivated by curiosity or labour savings but concerned about costs, complexity and data management challenges (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). Collaboration between researchers, technology developers and farmers is vital to create tailored PLF systems that meet real‐world needs and promote welfare outcomes. Maintaining and enhancing sheep welfare under extensive production systems remains a significant challenge that traditional methods struggle to address effectively. PLF represents a promising frontier by enabling continuous individual monitoring, early anomaly detection and data‐driven management decisions. By adopting and refining PLF technologies suited to extensive sheep farming conditions, the sector can advance both welfare and sustainability objectives, meeting ethical standards and market demands alike. Although several reviews have examined PLF technologies across livestock species or extensive production systems, few have specifically focused on their application to welfare assessment and management in extensively raised sheep. Therefore, this review provides a dedicated synthesis of recent advances in wearable sensors, CV, AI and environmental monitoring technologies, with particular emphasis on their welfare‐related applications, performance, limitations and future opportunities in extensive sheep systems.

## Review Methodology
The following research guided this comprehensive literature review question: ‘What is the role of Precision Livestock Farming technologies in supporting welfare assessment and management in extensive sheep production systems?’.
The literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science as the primary bibliographic databases. Google Scholar was additionally consulted as a complementary search tool to identify potentially relevant studies not captured through database searches. Although Google Scholar offers broad coverage of academic literature, including conference papers and other scholarly documents, its results may vary over time and are less reproducible than those obtained from structured bibliographic databases. Searches were performed using keywords, including ‘precision livestock farming’, ‘PLF sheep’, ‘wearable sensors sheep welfare’, ‘computer vision sheep’, ‘extensive sheep systems’ and ‘animal welfare monitoring’, combined with Boolean operators. A publication date restriction was applied, and only peer‐reviewed articles and conference proceedings published between 2010 and 2025 were included. This timeframe was selected to capture recent developments and technological advances in PLF relevant to sheep welfare assessment and management. Studies were included if they (1) focused on PLF technologies applied to sheep; (2) reported quantitative outcomes (e.g., accuracy metrics >80% for lameness detection via accelerometers); (3) addressed welfare in extensive systems; and (4) were empirical. Exclusion criteria eliminated non‐sheep studies, qualitative‐only reports or those on intensive housing. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts and full‐text articles for eligibility. Disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion and consensus after reviewing the original publication and applying the predefined eligibility criteria. This process ensured consistency and transparency in study selection (Figure 1). Although elements of structured literature identification and study selection were applied, the present study was designed as a comprehensive literature review rather than a formal systematic review and therefore, did not follow the full PRISMA 2020 reporting framework.

## PLF Technologies for Sheep Welfare

## Types and Functions of Wearable Sensors
Accelerometers and gyroscopes are widely used to monitor activity patterns, such as walking, resting, grazing and standing, and to detect early deviations in movement. Abnormal reductions in activity or gait changes can serve as early‐warning signs of lameness or systemic illness (Kaler et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2025). For example, tri‐axial accelerometers mounted on sheep necks in the United Kingdom detected lameness with up to 85% sensitivity (Kaler et al. 2020). GPS collars record spatial movements, mapping grazing routes, home ranges and distances travelled (Bailey et al. 2018; Lewis Baida et al. 2021). Such data are essential for detecting welfare risks, such as social isolation, excessive walking (linked to discomfort or limited resources) and changes in flock dynamics (Bailey et al. 2018; di Virgilio et al. 2018). Emerging wearable devices can now measure physiological parameters, such as heart rate, respiration and body temperature without invasive handling (Zhang et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2025). In China, multisensory collars combining these biometric readings achieved over 97% accuracy in identifying stress or disease, enabling faster and more targeted interventions (Zhang et al. 2020). In Patagonia, GPS and accelerometer data from Merino sheep revealed changes in bite rates and movement during drought‐induced nutritional stress. This allowed farmers to adjust management strategies rapidly, lowering mortality rates (di Virgilio et al. 2018). In many studies (Ding et al. 2025), proximity sensors combined with accelerometers automated the detection of lambing events and mother–lamb bonding behaviours, facilitating timely interventions for vulnerable ewes and lambs. Smart ear tags integrating accelerometer and gyroscope technology provided highly accurate early detection of lameness, reducing economic losses and animal suffering (Kaler et al. 2020).

## Integrative Advantages and Barriers
The adoption of wearable sensors in PLF is transforming flock management from a reactive, primarily visual‐inspection‐based approach to a proactive, data‐driven system capable of delivering real‐time alerts and detailed behavioural summaries at the individual‐animal level (Berckmans 2017; Gómez et al. 2021). Continuous monitoring of activity, location and physiological parameters enables early detection of health disorders, welfare impairments and reproductive events, often before clinical signs become visible to the farmer (Caja et al. 2016; Berckmans 2017). Aggregating and analysing sensor‐derived data across flocks and over time allows the identification of broader patterns, such as disease outbreaks, heat‐stress episodes or environmental constraints, that would be difficult or impossible to detect using traditional observation methods alone (Tullo et al. 2013; Gómez et al. 2021). These population‐level insights also support evidence‐based decision‐making and adaptive management strategies in extensive grazing systems. Despite these advantages, the widespread adoption of wearable technologies in sheep production remains hindered by several barriers. High device costs remain a major limitation for large‐scale implementation, particularly in extensive systems with low profit margins (Caja et al. 2016; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). In addition, extensive sheep farming requires devices that are durable, lightweight and capable of long‐term operation, with sufficient battery life and resistance to harsh environmental conditions, such as dust, humidity and temperature extremes (Tzanidakis et al. 2023). Another critical challenge lies in data integration and usability. Information generated by multiple sensor types must be combined into interoperable, user‐friendly platforms that present clear and actionable outputs rather than complex raw data, ensuring practical adoption by farmers (Tullo et al. 2013; Berckmans 2017). Overcoming these barriers will require the development of robust, affordable and scalable PLF systems, specifically designed for extensive livestock environments, as well as improved data analytics and decision‐support tools that translate sensor data into meaningful management actions (Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024).

## Core PLF Technologies Applied to Sheep Welfare
For clarity, the review is organized progressively from the description of the main PLF technologies to their practical welfare applications, followed by the principal challenges and future research directions associated with their implementation in extensive sheep production systems.

## Sensors and Wearables
Although the previous section examined wearable sensors in detail as a standalone category of PLF technologies, this section briefly revisits these tools within the broader context of integrated PLF systems alongside CV, AI and environmental monitoring technologies.
Wearable sensors form one of the core pillars of PLF applications in sheep production (Brown et al. 2012; Odintsov Vaintrub et al. 2021; Aquilani et al. 2022). These devices, most commonly designed as collars, ear tags or halters, enable continuous, real‐time monitoring of animal health, behaviour and activity. This capability is particularly valuable in extensive production systems, where direct visual inspection is often infrequent and logistically challenging (Gómez et al. 2021; Fan et al. 2022). Tri‐axial accelerometers are among the most widely adopted wearable technologies, capable of capturing movement in three‐dimensional space. These devices can distinguish between a range of activities, such as grazing, walking, ruminating and resting. For example, in a commercial Merino sheep farm in Victoria, Australia, 160 sheep were fitted with ActiGraph accelerometers, and their behaviours were classified using Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithms. Results demonstrated high consistency, with daily grazing time showing a repeatability of 0.70 and a heritability of 0.44, indicating that these sensors can reliably be used for selective breeding and welfare trait evaluation (Almasi et al. 2023). Other studies applying Random Forest and SVM classifiers have achieved accuracies and specificities above 90% for grazing, rumination and resting detection (Riaboff et al. 2020; Ikurior et al. 2021). Tri‐axial accelerometers mounted under the lower jaw have classified grazing, rumination and resting with >90% accuracy, sensitivity and specificity, which are considered gold standards for remote welfare monitoring (Ikurior et al. 2021). Research is moving towards integrating accelerometers with pressure and force sensors into composite devices, expanding functionality from basic activity tracking to assessing feeding efficiency and supporting health diagnostics (Ding et al. 2025). GPS tracking collars are essential for monitoring spatial movements, grazing routes and social interactions in open rangeland systems (Jin et al. 2022). They enable precise mapping of resource‐use patterns, detection of isolation behaviour and evaluation of how terrain influences activity distribution. For instance, in a 900‐ha organic pastoral system, GPS data collected every 5 min, combined with orthophotos and LiDAR digital elevation models, showed that slope, orientation and the availability of short pasture grass significantly shaped sheep habitat preferences. These insights helped improve grazing management and welfare outcomes (Ciliberti et al. 2025). New generations of wearable devices now incorporate biometric sensing capabilities to measure parameters, such as heart rate, UV exposure and body temperature. One notable innovation is a tetrapod zinc oxide‐based UV sensor embedded in a collar to assess UV exposure and its welfare implications in grazing sheep (Knoepfel et al. 2022). Although these technologies hold substantial promise, especially when integrated into flexible smart textiles or miniaturized sensor tags, further field validation in diverse sheep production systems is essential before they can be widely adopted (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024).

## CV and AI
CV combined with AI, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of models, is rapidly transforming PLF applications for sheep by enabling non‐contact, automated welfare assessment (Redmon et al. 2016; Gómez et al. 2021). These technologies leverage image and video analysis to quantify key welfare parameters without physical handling, thereby reducing stress for animals and labour demands for farmers (Tullo et al. 2013). YOLO and its improved variants (e.g., YOLOv8n_BiF_DSC) have demonstrated exceptionally high accuracy, achieving mAP50 scores above 96% in challenging real‐world conditions, for recognizing livestock postures, detecting movement patterns and identifying abnormal behaviours. Although much of the validation has been in cattle, early trials in sheep have shown promising results, with reliable detection of body condition changes and lameness from subtle gait or weight distribution variations (Sant'Ana et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023, 2024). Unlike manual scoring systems, which are labour‐intensive and time‐consuming, CV–AI platforms can automatically process data from large flocks, identify outliers and detect welfare concerns continuously (Berckmans 2017). This scalability is particularly valuable in extensive systems where regular close‐contact inspections are impractical (Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Gómez et al. 2021). Recent advances include the training of YOLO‐based models to simultaneously recognize and count sheep, classify behaviours (e.g., feeding vs. resting) and flag abnormal movement patterns, representing a breakthrough in remote, labour‐saving welfare assessment (Hu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). Additionally, CNN‐powered video analytics have been piloted on commercial farms to estimate liveweight and body condition scores with accuracy comparable to traditional manual scoring methods, but at a fraction of the time and labour cost (Gómez et al. 2021). By integrating these CV systems into broader PLF frameworks, sheep welfare monitoring can become more objective, continuous and scalable, enabling early detection of welfare issues and more informed management decisions.

## Environmental Monitoring
Environmental monitoring within PLF plays a critical role in linking microclimatic conditions and pasture quality with animal behaviour and welfare outcomes. By combining weather stations, Internet of Things (IoT) sensors, drones and satellite remote sensing, these systems allow for a comprehensive understanding of how environmental factors affect sheep well‐being and productivity (Figure 2) (Plaza, Palacio et al. 2022; Plaza, Sánchez et al. 2022; Gómez et al. 2021). Fixed and portable weather stations measure parameters, such as temperature, humidity, wind speed and precipitation in real time. This microclimate data is essential for assessing heat‐or cold‐stress risks and can be directly correlated with animal activity data collected from accelerometers to identify climate‐related welfare threats. Such integration enables informed decisions about shelter provision, grazing schedules and pasture management to mitigate environmental stressors (Norton et al. 2019; Losacco et al. 2025). Drones and satellite platforms, such as Sentinel‐1 and Sentinel‐2, are used to monitor vegetation cover, pasture biomass and terrain features (Vidal‐Cardos et al. 2025). In Spanish rangelands, remote sensing revealed that sheep preferentially selected north‐facing gentle slopes with short grass. These findings were validated by GPS tracking data, confirming a direct relationship between terrain characteristics, forage availability and movement patterns (Plaza, Palacio et al. 2022; Plaza, Sánchez et al. 2022). Combining environmental data with behavioural metrics enables proactive pasture and flock management. For example, integrating satellite‐derived vegetation maps with GPS tracking allowed farmers to identify overgrazed zones, adjust flock distribution and maintain optimal grazing pressure, thereby reducing risks, such as undernutrition and parasite infestations (Gómez et al. 2021). In the context of sheep PLF, core technologies converge to create a holistic monitoring ecosystem: wearable sensors for behaviour and health assessment, such as accelerometers for locomotion analysis, GPS trackers for spatial and social interaction mapping and emerging biometric sensors for physiological monitoring. CV and AI models (e.g., YOLO algorithms) for large‐scale, automated and non‐contact evaluation of body condition, posture and behavioural anomalies, reducing reliance on labour‐intensive manual observation. Environmental monitoring systems, using IoT sensors, weather stations and remote sensing, to integrate climate, landscape and forage data with welfare outcomes, enabling proactive, data‐driven decisions that safeguard health and optimize productivity (Figure 2).

CV and AI technologies offer a complementary, non‐invasive approach to welfare assessment by analysing body condition, posture and behavioural cues through image‐based systems. Although accuracy is generally described as high, Table 1 appropriately emphasizes that performance is highly context‐dependent. Lighting variability, camera placement, environmental complexity and data quality strongly influence system reliability (Berckmans 2017; Kaler et al. 2020; Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). This underscores that, despite their promise, CV systems are currently better suited to semi‐controlled environments or strategic locations (e.g., handling areas, water points) rather than fully open rangelands. In addition, the need for substantial data storage capacity and high initial investment limits accessibility for many sheep producers.
Environmental monitoring technologies, including weather stations, IoT sensors, drones and satellite remote sensing, play a crucial contextual role in welfare assessment by capturing microclimatic and landscape‐level variables. These systems deliver real‐time, high‐precision data on temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation and vegetation dynamics, all of which directly influence sheep behaviour, heat‐stress risk and nutritional status (Ermetin et al. 2022; Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024; Bernabucci et al. 2025). Although environmental sensors are generally robust and accurate, Table 1 correctly identifies data integration complexity and sensor maintenance as major limitations. On their own, these tools do not directly measure animal welfare but instead gain value when combined with animal‐based sensors and behavioural data streams.

## Applications of PLF for Sheep Welfare

## Health and Disease Detection
PLF plays a significant role in the early detection of health disorders in sheep, which is crucial for timely treatment and welfare improvement. Although Gómez et al. (2021) focused on pig production systems, the review highlighted broader PLF approaches for welfare monitoring that may also be relevant to sheep production when appropriately adapted. For example, wearable accelerometers and proximity loggers have been successfully utilized to monitor deviations in locomotion and social behaviours that signal disease onset before visible symptoms manifest, enabling farmers to intervene earlier and reduce animal suffering. Although biosensor‐based diagnostics (such as serological tests or automated blood analysis) have been well integrated into PLF platforms in cattle, their adoption in sheep farming remains limited. This gap is largely due to challenges in deploying such technologies in extensive sheep systems and a lack of validated integration protocols (SEFARI 2023). However, targeted use of diagnostics combined with behavioural monitoring shows promise. For instance, in Scotland, serological testing for sheep scab has been combined with PLF tools to effectively target treatment to affected flocks, reducing both disease prevalence and welfare compromise (SEFARI 2023). Future efforts are needed to integrate biosensor diagnostics into sheep PLF systems more broadly to enhance flock health surveillance.
Beyond enhancing animal welfare, improving disease detection and treatment through PLF could also contribute to a more judicious use of medication, helping to reduce routine prophylactic treatments. This approach may offer additional benefits in terms of environmental sustainability and public health, particularly by addressing concerns related to antimicrobial resistance.

## Behavioural and Emotional Welfare Assessment
PLF technologies enable continuous, objective monitoring of sheep behaviour, providing insights into both negative and positive welfare states. Behavioural time budgets, distribution of time across grazing, ruminating, resting and other activities serve as key indicators of animal welfare (Gómez et al. 2021). Deviations from normal time budgets can suggest welfare challenges, such as pain, stress or discomfort. Additionally, PLF tools quantify social behaviour metrics, such as ewe–lamb proximity and flock cohesion, which are vital emotional welfare indicators. For example, studies using GPS collars and proximity loggers demonstrate that changes in ewe–lamb distance correlate with stress or illness, providing an early welfare signal (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). Such fine‐grained social behavioural data are valuable not only to detect welfare deficits but also to assess positive states like maternal bonding and social play. Although most current PLF applications emphasize detecting negative welfare states, the assessment of positive emotional states remains a developing research frontier. Advanced sensor arrays, CV and acoustic monitoring are being explored to identify play behaviours, exploratory activity and vocalizations reflecting positive affective states, elements critical for comprehensive welfare evaluation (Gómez et al. 2021). In addition, the integration of physiological and environmental data with behavioural metrics is gaining traction as a way to enhance emotional state recognition. For instance, changes in heart rate or ambient stressors may reinforce or contextualize behavioural observations (Schillings et al. 2021a). Furthermore, the ability to monitor individual animals through radio frequency identification (RFID) or advanced image recognition adds granularity to welfare assessment, helping identify subtle anomalies in behaviour that may go unnoticed at the group level (Ren et al. 2020; Herlin et al. 2021). Emerging AI‐driven models, particularly those using machine learning algorithms, are being developed to analyse multisource data and detect complex emotional patterns in real time. As these technologies advance, ethical considerations regarding data use, animal autonomy and intervention thresholds must also be taken into account.

## Nutrition and Resource Management
Nutrition is foundational to sheep welfare, and PLF technologies provide tools for precision grazing and nutritional management. GPS tracking combined with pasture quality mapping allows the optimization of grazing patterns, ensuring sheep access to nutritious forage while avoiding overgrazing or undue competition that may induce stress (Gómez et al. 2021). Automated weighing systems and image‐based liveweight estimation enable frequent and stress‐free monitoring of individual body condition and growth. This supports timely adjustments to feeding regimes, supplementation or pasture movement to prevent malnutrition or excessive weight loss (Gómez et al. 2021). Such data‐driven management improves feed efficiency, enhances animal health and ultimately contributes to welfare improvement. For example, in extensive sheep grazing systems, automated liveweight estimation using video analysis has reduced animal handling stress and provided accurate growth tracking, enabling managers to intervene proactively in cases of nutritional deficiency or illness (Sant'Ana et al. 2021). Beyond monitoring body weight, systems that combine RFID tags with automated feeding stations allow for individual tracking of feed intake (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). This level of information is particularly useful in spotting early nutritional issues, illness or stress caused by competition within the group. It also makes it easier to provide targeted dietary support to animals with greater needs, such as pregnant ewes or fast‐growing lambs (Schillings et al. 2021b). Water intake, though often underestimated, plays an equally important role in animal welfare. Newer technologies include intelligent drinking stations that monitor usage and can alert farmers to unusual patterns, such as reduced water intake during periods of heat stress or illness, that may signal an underlying problem (Schillings et al. 2021a). Looking to the future, predictive tools that use behavioural, health and environmental data to estimate nutritional requirements could make feeding practices more precise and responsive. By adapting rations to actual needs, these tools would not only support better welfare outcomes but also help reduce feed waste and limit overgrazing, particularly in ecologically sensitive pasture systems.

## Challenges and Limitations in PLF Deployment

## Adoption Barriers
One of the foremost challenges stems from intrinsic factors related to the nature of extensive sheep farming. Large flock sizes spread over rugged, remote terrains make continuous monitoring and intervention difficult (Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). Limited digital connectivity in such environments hampers real‐time data transmission and integration. Moreover, many sheep farmers remain sceptical about adopting new technologies owing to concerns over cost, complexity and uncertain benefits (Tullo et al. 2013; Berckmans 2017). Farmers accustomed to traditional management may view PLF tools as disruptive or fear losing control of their animals and operations (Caja et al. 2016; Rose et al. 2016; Eastwood et al. 2019). This hesitance can result from insufficient knowledge, lack of technical support or a perception that PLF systems are designed primarily for more intensive systems, such as dairy cattle rather than sheep (Tzanidakis et al. 2023; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). For example, a study in Northern Europe identified that a lack of user‐friendly interfaces and inadequate farmer training severely limited PLF uptake despite its potential utility (Rose et al. 2016; Eastwood et al. 2019).

## Economic Constraints
The high upfront cost of PLF technologies, including sensor hardware, data infrastructure, software platforms and maintenance, is a major barrier for many producers, especially smallholders and those operating in low‐margin extensive systems (Caja et al. 2016; Berckmans 2017; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024). The unclear or delayed economic return on investment (ROI) further discourages purchase. For example, GPS and accelerometer collars may cost hundreds of dollars each, which becomes prohibitive when scaling to large flocks. Likewise, data management subscriptions and technical support add ongoing expenses (Reeves et al. 2025). Without clear, quantifiable benefits tied to productivity gains, health improvements or labour savings, many farmers hesitate to invest (Banhazi et al. 2012; Eastwood et al. 2019; Gómez et al. 2021). Innovative financing mechanisms, subsidies or cooperative technology‐sharing schemes could help overcome affordability issues but remain scarcely implemented.

## Data Integration and Interoperability Issues
PLF systems commonly generate vast volumes of heterogeneous data from multiple sensor types, locomotion, physiological parameters, spatial positioning and environmental variables. Integrating these datasets into coherent, actionable insights remains a major technical hurdle (Wolfert et al. 2017). Lack of standardized platforms, incompatible sensor protocols and fragmented data architectures often lead to ‘data silos’ where information exists but is not effectively analysed or shared (Bahlo et al. 2019). This complicates multidimensional welfare assessment and decision‐making (Neethirajan 2020). Farmers and veterinarians frequently face user‐unfriendly interfaces and inconsistent reporting formats, undermining trust and the practical utility of PLF outputs (Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024).

## Ethical and Socio‐Cultural Considerations
Deploying PLF in sheep farming raises important ethical questions regarding the impact on the human–animal relationship, data privacy and the potential for over‐reliance on technology at the expense of traditional husbandry skills and experiential knowledge (Werkheiser 2018; Gómez et al. 2021). Automation may reduce direct human–animal contact, which can affect caretaker empathy and observational acuity, both critical for nuanced welfare assessment (Schilling et al. 2021a). Additionally, concerns about data ownership, privacy and security emerge as sensitive animal and farm data become digitized and possibly shared with third parties. Some farmers worry that PLF could commodify animals further and impose surveillance regimes that conflict with cultural or personal values. Addressing these concerns requires transparent governance frameworks, participatory technology development and ethical guidelines embedded within PLF systems (Werkheiser 2018).

## Future Directions in PLF for Sheep Welfare

## Cost‐Effective and Resilient Systems
Extensive sheep production systems pose challenges, such as large flock sizes, remote locations and variable environmental conditions (Silva et al. 2022). PLF tools designed for these contexts must be affordable, durable and energy‐efficient, capable of operating autonomously in areas with intermittent connectivity. Devices that withstand temperature extremes, moisture, dust and physical stress, while storing data locally, will facilitate broader adoption and reliable welfare monitoring (Odintsov Vaintrub et al. 2021; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025).

## Standardization of Welfare Indicators
The development of validated, standardized welfare indicators is critical for meaningful comparisons across farms and systems. Harmonized metrics ensure interoperability between sensors, analytics platforms and management software, supporting benchmarking, transparency and evidence‐based decision‐making. They also provide a foundation for regulatory frameworks and welfare labelling schemes that reflect scientifically robust assessments (Bolkhuis et al. 2003; Buller et al. 2020; Gomez et al. 2021).

## Positive Welfare Assessment
Although traditional PLF research has focused on negative welfare outcomes, there is growing interest in measuring positive welfare dimensions, such as play, social interactions and comfort (Muhammed et al. 2022). Integrating these indicators with multi‐modal sensors—accelerometers, GPS, video and acoustic devices—and advanced analytics allows a more complete understanding of well‐being. Such approaches support management strategies that not only prevent harm but also actively promote favourable behavioural and emotional states (Hlimi et al. 2024; Morgan‐Davies et al. 2024).

## Predictive Analytics and Digital Twins
AI and deep learning enable the detection of subtle changes in behaviour or physiology from complex data streams, offering predictive insights into welfare risks. Digital twins—virtual models of individual animals or entire flocks—can simulate scenarios, anticipate health or behavioural issues and guide interventions before problems arise (Neethirajan and Kemp 2021). This predictive capacity shifts PLF from reactive monitoring to proactive welfare management, improving efficiency, sustainability and animal outcomes (Tao et al. 2023).

## Multidisciplinary and Participatory Approaches
Closing the gap between technological innovation and real‐world application in PLF for sheep welfare requires an approach that actively engages all key stakeholders, particularly farmers. Involving farmers as active partners throughout the development process ensures that PLF tools are designed to address practical on‐farm realities, remain user‐friendly and provide tangible economic value to producers (Reeves et al. 2025). This co‐creation process fosters solutions that are not only technologically advanced but also operationally viable and adaptable to diverse production systems. Equally important is multidisciplinary collaboration, bringing together expertise in ethology, engineering, data science, veterinary medicine and the social sciences. Such integration ensures that technological designs reflect animal welfare science, adhere to robust engineering standards, leverage effective data analytics and account for the socio‐economic and cultural contexts of sheep farming (Groher et al. 2020; Losacco et al. 2025). These collaborative models enhance adoption rates by building trust, ensuring that welfare improvements are practical, sustainable and ethically grounded and aligning innovation with the daily realities and values of end‐users.

## Conclusion
PLF offers a robust framework for improving sheep welfare through continuous, individualized monitoring and earlier identification of health, behavioural and environmental risks. By combining wearable sensors, CV and environmental data streams, PLF enables objective welfare assessment and supports timely management decisions, particularly in extensive systems where direct observation is limited. However, widespread implementation remains constrained by economic costs, technical challenges related to data integration and variability in farmer acceptance. Addressing these limitations will require further validation under diverse production conditions, improved interoperability of digital tools and the development of cost‐effective, user‐oriented systems. Ethical considerations, including data governance and the preservation of stockmanship skills, must also be integrated into PLF design and deployment. Overall, PLF represents a promising but still evolving approach to sheep welfare management. Its successful adoption will depend on balancing technological innovation with practical feasibility, economic sustainability and responsible use within real‐world farming contexts. A key contribution of this review is the integration of recent evidence specifically related to sheep welfare in extensive production systems, bringing together technological, welfare and management perspectives that are often addressed separately in the literature. By highlighting current capabilities, adoption barriers and emerging developments, such as positive welfare assessment and predictive analytics, this review provides a focused framework to guide future research and practical implementation of PLF technologies in extensive sheep farming.
