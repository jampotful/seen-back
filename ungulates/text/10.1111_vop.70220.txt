Deep Feature-Based Normality Modeling for Automated Out-Of-Distribution Detection in Sheep Retinal Fundus Images.
Kibar B, Okur S, Baykal B, Arslan T, Özkalipçi Ç, Petek B. (2026). Veterinary ophthalmology. . doi:10.1111/vop.70220. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Deep Feature‐Based Normality Modeling for Automated Out‐Of‐Distribution Detection in Sheep Retinal Fundus Images

## Abstract
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo develop and evaluate a deep feature‐based normality modeling approach for automated out‐of‐distribution (OOD) detection in sheep retinal fundus images.Animals StudiedRetinal fundus images from 75 adult sheep (n = 271 images) and additional OOD images from non‐target species (cattle, dogs, and cats; n = 346 images).ProceduresDeep feature embeddings were extracted using a ResNet50 convolutional neural network pretrained on ImageNet. Normal retinal appearance was modeled in feature space using healthy images. Anomaly scores were calculated using a k‐nearest neighbor (k = 5) distance‐based approach. OOD detection was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The anomaly threshold was defined as the 95th percentile of validation scores.ResultsThe proposed framework demonstrated a clear separation between in‐distribution sheep retinal images and OOD samples. The model achieved an area under the ROC curve of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.99–1.00). At the predefined threshold, all OOD images were correctly identified (100% detection rate), with a false alarm rate of 11.9% in the sheep test set.ConclusionsDeep feature‐based normality modeling can characterize normal sheep retinal morphology and identify out‐of‐distribution samples. The proposed approach should be interpreted as a proof‐of‐concept screening and quality‐control tool, not a disease‐specific diagnostic system. Further validation using animal‐level partitioning and intra‐species pathological datasets is required to establish clinical utility. Normality modeling may hold promise for future screening of retinal disease within sheep; however, this has not yet been evaluated. Importantly, this study evaluates only cross‐species OOD detection and does not assess the detection of retinal abnormalities within sheep.

## Introduction
The retina, embryologically derived from the neural tube and diencephalon, is recognized as a peripheral extension of the Central Nervous System, sharing distinct vascular barriers and neuronal characteristics [1]. It represents a unique anatomical site where neuronal and microvascular architectures can be visualized directly, non‐invasively, and in vivo [2, 3]. Due to this biological disposition, retinal morphology serves as a critical ‘window’ into both brain and systemic health, offering diagnostic insights that extend beyond visual sensory function [3]. In veterinary ophthalmology, fundus examination constitutes a powerful tool for assessing vascular integrity and neurological status. However, while retinal manifestations of systemic conditions have been comprehensively reviewed in companion animals [2], the scarcity of publicly available retinal datasets for livestock and the necessity for defining species‐specific morphological baselines highlight a significant gap in the literature [4, 5]. Specifically, the development of automated screening systems for farm animals, particularly sheep, remains largely unexplored.
Despite the established diagnostic utility of retinal imaging, its routine application in large farm animals presents significant logistical and clinical challenges compared to human or small animal ophthalmology. In livestock settings, herd‐based ophthalmoscopic screening is constrained by difficult field conditions, often necessitating substantial physical restraint, additional handling staff, and pharmacological pupillary dilation to ensure adequate visualization [4, 6]. Furthermore, uncontrolled environmental variables typical of farm housing, such as variable lighting, dust, and unpredictable animal movements, frequently compromise image acquisition, introducing artifacts that render images ungradable [5, 7]. Beyond these acquisition difficulties, the manual interpretation of retinal findings is inherently time‐consuming and prone to inter‐observer variability, which limits the reproducibility of diagnoses [3, 8]. Consequently, the lack of standardized quantitative metrics for defining ‘normality’ in livestock hinders the scalable implementation of retinal health monitoring, underscoring the need for automated, objective screening tools [2, 9].
In the domain of medical imaging, Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Deep Learning (DL), has revolutionized the automated analysis of retinal pathologies. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have achieved expert‐level performance in screening for human ocular conditions such as diabetic retinopathy and glaucoma [10, 11]. However, the predominant methodology driving these successes relies on supervised learning paradigms, which are inherently data‐dependent and necessitate massive datasets of annotated pathological images to train classifiers effectively [11]. While DL applications are gaining traction in veterinary diagnostics, current research is disproportionately focused on companion animals, with studies on canines and felines accounting for the vast majority of the literature [12, 13]. In real‐world veterinary settings, acquiring comprehensive datasets of pathological conditions for sheep is often impeded by logistical constraints, ethical considerations, and the inherent rarity of specific ocular diseases. This scarcity of expert‐labeled pathological data renders standard supervised classification approaches unfeasible, creating a barrier to the development of robust automated screening systems [12].
To overcome this limitation, the current study adopts an unsupervised anomaly detection paradigm based on normality modeling. Unlike supervised methods that require balanced datasets of diseased samples to prevent bias, this strategy leverages deep learning to learn the manifold of ‘normal’ sheep retinal morphology exclusively, an approach successfully pioneered in human retinal imaging [14, 15]. We hypothesize that a deep feature‐based normality modeling approach, constructed using pretrained convolutional neural network embeddings, can effectively capture the normative morphological characteristics of healthy sheep retinal images. In this framework, feature representations extracted from a pretrained convolutional neural network are used to define a reference distribution of normal retinal images, and deviations from this distribution are quantified using a distance‐based metric. This approach enables the identification of anomalous samples, including non‐target species or atypical image characteristics, as out‐of‐distribution (OOD) instances.

## Materials and Methods

## Study Design and Ethical Considerations
This study was designed as a retrospective image‐based analysis. All retinal fundus images were obtained from routine clinical examinations; therefore, no additional animal handling, interventions, or experimental procedures were performed specifically for this study. All images were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure no identifiable information related to animal owners or individual cases remained. In accordance with institutional and national guidelines governing retrospective studies based solely on archived clinical images, formal ethical committee approval was not required.

## Image Dataset and Acquisition
Retinal fundus images were collected from sheep examined at the Food and Livestock Application and Research Center (Erzurum, Turkey). To ensure consistency in image quality, all images were acquired using the same handheld fundus camera (Optomed Smartscope M5, Optomed Plc, Finland). A total of 271 retinal images were obtained from 75 adult sheep of mixed breeds and both sexes. The studied animals consisted of clinically healthy adult sheep of mixed breeds, including both males and females. Exact age information was not consistently available due to the retrospective nature of the dataset. Health status was determined based on routine clinical examination, and only animals without observable ocular abnormalities were included in the normal reference dataset. Images represented routine clinical cases and were collected under standard examination conditions. Only images of adequate quality, allowing clear visualization of retinal structures, were included in the analysis.

## Dataset Partitioning
The sheep retinal dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets for normality modeling. Of the 271 total images, 189 were assigned to the training set, 40 to the validation set, and 42 to the independent test set. Because this was a retrospective archive‐based dataset and individual animal identifiers were not consistently available for all images, partitioning was performed at the image level rather than the animal level. Consequently, it was not possible to ensure that all images from a given sheep were confined to a single subset, and a grouped cross‐validation design could not be implemented. To evaluate OOD detection performance, an additional dataset comprising 346 retinal images from non‐target species (cattle, dogs, and cats) was assembled from the same clinical archive. ## Image Preprocessing and Feature Extraction
A standardized preprocessing pipeline was applied to all images prior to feature extraction. Images were resized to a fixed resolution of 512 × 512 pixels using bilinear interpolation and converted to RGB format. Pixel intensities were normalized using the ImageNet mean and standard deviation values to ensure compatibility with the pretrained network.
No explicit cropping, retinal masking, or border removal was applied. All images were used in their full‐frame format to preserve the original spatial context. To reduce the likelihood of artifact‐driven discrimination, all images were visually reviewed to confirm consistent fundus‐centered framing and adequate visualization of retinal structures. No artificial borders, text overlays, or device‐specific markers were present.
Deep feature representations were extracted using a ResNet50 convolutional neural network pretrained on ImageNet. The final fully connected classification layer was removed, and feature embeddings were obtained from the global average pooling layer, resulting in a 2048‐dimensional feature vector for each image.

## Normality Modeling and Anomaly Scoring
Normality modeling was conducted in the learned feature space using a k‐nearest neighbor (kNN) approach (Figure 1). Feature embeddings from the training set (healthy sheep) were used to construct a reference normal feature distribution. For each image in the validation, test, and OOD datasets, an anomaly score was calculated as the mean Euclidean distance to the five nearest neighbors within this reference training space. The number of nearest neighbors was set to k = 5 based on empirical observations during preliminary experiments, where this value provided a stable balance between sensitivity to local variations and robustness to noise in the feature space.
Schematic overview of the proposed deep feature‐based normality modeling framework for out‐of‐distribution (OOD) detection in sheep retinal fundus images. Retinal images are processed through frozen ResNet50 layers pretrained on ImageNet to extract deep feature embeddings. Normal sheep retinal images form the reference in‐distribution feature space, yielding low anomaly scores. Retinal images from non‐target species (e.g., dog) deviate from this learned normality and produce higher anomaly scores, enabling OOD identification through distance‐based scoring in feature space.

## Implementation Details
All image processing and model implementation steps were carried out using Python (version 3.10). Deep feature extraction was implemented using the PyTorch framework, while data handling and distance‐based computations were performed using standard scientific computing libraries (NumPy, scikit‐learn).

## Statistical Analysis
The proposed framework was evaluated at the image level using metrics standard to OOD detection studies. Model discrimination between in‐distribution sheep test images and OOD samples was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis. Threshold selection was conducted exclusively on the validation set. The anomaly score threshold was defined as the 95th percentile of the validation sheep scores, corresponding to an approximate false positive rate of 5% under normal conditions. This approach was chosen to simulate a screening‐oriented setting where sensitivity to outliers is prioritized while maintaining a controlled false alarm rate. Because animal‐level identifiers were not consistently available, all analyses were necessarily performed at the image level, and the reported performance metrics should be interpreted with this design constraint in mind. Confidence intervals for AUROC were estimated using non‐parametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. In each iteration, paired in‐distribution and OOD samples were resampled with replacement, and the AUROC was recalculated. The 95% confidence intervals were defined using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.

## Results

## Distribution of Anomaly Scores
The quantitative analysis of feature embeddings revealed a distinct distributional shift between in‐distribution sheep retinal images and out‐of‐distribution (OOD) samples. As illustrated in Figure 2, anomaly scores for healthy sheep images formed a compact cluster within the lower range of the distance metric, reflecting the model's ability to successfully learn the manifold of normal retinal morphology. In contrast, OOD samples exhibited significantly higher and more dispersed anomaly scores (Figure 3), indicating a substantial deviation from the learned normative baseline. The mean anomaly score for the sheep test set was 4.17 ± 0.61, whereas the OOD dataset showed a markedly elevated mean score of 9.33 ± 1.26. The visual overlap between the two distributions was not observed, indicating complete separation between in‐distribution and OOD samples in the evaluated dataset.

## Discriminative Performance (ROC Analysis)
The global discriminative capacity of the proposed framework was evaluated using ROC analysis. The model demonstrated near‐perfect separability between the target sheep class and non‐target outliers. As shown in the ROC curve (Figure 4), the system achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUROC) of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.99–1.00). This result indicates that, across all possible discrimination thresholds, the model consistently assigned higher anomaly scores to OOD samples compared to in‐distribution sheep images.
Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for out‐of‐distribution (OOD) detection based on anomaly scores. The ROC curve evaluates the ability of the proposed normality‐based framework to discriminate between in‐distribution sheep retinal fundus images and OOD samples across varying decision thresholds. The dashed diagonal line represents chance‐level performance. The model achieved an area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of 1.00, indicating strong separability between in‐distribution and out‐of‐distribution data.

## Screening Performance at Selected Threshold
For the operational evaluation, a decision threshold of 4.9996 was established based on the 95th percentile of the validation set to emulate a realistic screening scenario. At this fixed threshold, the system achieved an OOD detection rate (sensitivity to outliers) of 100%, correctly flagging all 346 images from non‐target species (cattle, dogs, and cats) as anomalous (Table 2). Regarding the in‐distribution performance, the False Alarm Rate on the independent sheep test set was observed to be 11.9%. Specifically, out of 42 sheep retinal images in the test set, only 5 were incorrectly flagged as anomalous, while 37 were correctly accepted as normal. Bootstrap‐based confidence intervals for the false alarm rate and OOD detection rate at the selected threshold showed minimal variability across resampled datasets, indicating stable performance under the current evaluation setting.

## Discussion
In the present study, a normality‐based deep learning framework was developed to model retinal fundus images from sheep and to identify retinal images that fall outside the expected normal range. Using this approach, sheep retinal images were consistently distinguished from retinal images of other animal species, with complete separation at the image level and a low false alarm rate in normal cases. The observed separation in anomaly scores indicates that the model was able to learn characteristic features of normal sheep retinas and to flag retinal images that differ substantially from this pattern. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that normality modeling may serve as a screening and data‐quality support tool to assist clinicians in identifying retinal images that deviate from the expected distribution, particularly in heterogeneous datasets, especially in settings where species diversity or variable image quality may complicate routine fundus assessment. It should be noted that the present approach does not involve generative modeling or density estimation, but instead relies on distance‐based analysis in a pretrained deep feature space.
Normality modeling and anomaly detection approaches have been increasingly explored in medical imaging as practical alternatives to fully supervised classification, particularly in scenarios where comprehensive pathological annotations are limited or unavailable. Prior studies have demonstrated that learning the distribution of normal images enables robust identification of atypical or unseen patterns without explicit disease labeling, thereby supporting screening‐oriented applications rather than disease‐specific diagnosis [14, 16]. Within this context, distance‐based representations in deep feature space have been shown to effectively separate in‐distribution samples from OOD data by quantifying deviations from learned normal patterns [17]. Consistent with these observations, the findings of the present study indicate that modeling normal sheep retinal fundus images allows reliable discrimination of retinal images originating from other animal species, as reflected by the clear separation of anomaly score distributions and perfect image‐level OOD discrimination. These results support the notion that normality‐based deep feature learning can capture species‐specific retinal characteristics and provide a robust framework for identifying retinal images that fall outside the expected normal domain, aligning with previously reported applications of anomaly and OOD detection in medical imaging. An important consideration is whether the observed separation between in‐distribution and OOD samples reflects true retinal morphological differences or is partly influenced by acquisition‐related cues such as borders, background, or device‐specific characteristics. In the present study, all images were acquired using the same imaging device under comparable clinical conditions, and no artificial borders, overlays, or device‐specific markers were present. Although no explicit masking or border‐cropping procedure was applied, the acquisition protocol was consistent across datasets, which reduces the likelihood that discrimination was driven primarily by trivial non‐retinal cues. Nevertheless, we cannot completely exclude the contribution of subtle background or framing effects, and future studies should include dedicated artifact‐ablation analyses and controlled preprocessing strategies to more rigorously evaluate this possibility.
Retinal fundus imaging has become a widely used modality for artificial intelligence–based analysis due to its non‐invasive nature and its ability to capture detailed vascular and neuroretina structures. Previous studies have demonstrated that deep learning models applied to fundus images can achieve high diagnostic performance for both ocular and systemic conditions, supporting their use in screening‐oriented clinical workflows [18, 19]. However, fundus images are inherently heterogeneous, with substantial variability arising from imaging devices, acquisition conditions, image quality, and subject‐specific factors, which can introduce domain shifts and adversely affect model reliability when encountering unseen data [13]. This challenge has been widely acknowledged in ophthalmic artificial intelligence literature, where performance degradation is often observed during external validation or deployment across different populations and imaging settings [13, 19]. In this context, the present findings demonstrate that normality‐based modeling of sheep retinal images enables robust discrimination of retinal images originating from other animal species, effectively identifying out‐of‐distribution samples without reliance on explicit pathological labels. Such an approach aligns with prior recommendations emphasizing the importance of handling domain variability and unexpected inputs to enhance the safety and generalizability of fundus‐based deep learning systems, particularly in heterogeneous clinical environments such as veterinary practice [18].
From a clinical and methodological perspective, the present evaluation should be interpreted primarily as a cross‐species OOD detection experiment. Accordingly, the findings demonstrate the model's ability to distinguish sheep retinal images from retinal images of other species, rather than its ability to detect pathological abnormalities within sheep. By identifying retinal images that deviate from the learned distribution of normal sheep retinas, the model may assist clinicians in flagging cases that require closer inspection or expert review, particularly as a screening or data‐quality support tool. Such decision‐support–oriented use of artificial intelligence has been widely recommended to enhance reliability and mitigate the risks associated with overconfident automated predictions in real‐world clinical workflows [20, 21, 22]. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, dataset partitioning was performed at the image level because individual animal identifiers were not consistently available in the retrospective archive. As a result, we could not exclude the possibility that multiple images from the same sheep were distributed across different subsets. This design may have introduced information leakage and may therefore have led to optimistic performance estimates compared with a stricter animal‐level partitioning strategy. In addition, grouped cross‐validation could not be performed for the same reason. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary image‐level results, and future studies should prioritize animal‐level dataset organization and grouped validation schemes. Second, all images were collected from a single center using a single imaging device, potentially limiting generalizability to other clinical environments. Third, the absence of pathological sheep retinal images restricts interpretation of anomaly scores to deviations from normal appearance rather than disease‐specific detection. Finally, the OOD dataset was primarily defined by interspecies differences, whereas more subtle distribution shifts may occur in routine clinical practice. Future studies incorporating multicenter data, animal‐level partitioning, pathological sheep retinas, and prospective validation are therefore warranted to further assess the clinical robustness of this approach. In addition, performance stability across different data splits could not be fully assessed due to the absence of animal‐level identifiers and the inability to perform grouped cross‐validation. Although bootstrap analysis suggested stable performance at the image level, further validation using multiple independent splits or grouped validation schemes is required.
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that deep feature–based normality modeling can effectively characterize normal sheep retinal fundus images and reliably identify out‐of‐distribution retinal samples from other animal species. By focusing on normal pattern learning rather than explicit disease classification, the proposed framework addresses key challenges related to data scarcity and distributional variability in veterinary ophthalmic imaging. The findings highlight the potential of normality‐based approaches as practical screening and safety tools to support clinical decision‐making in heterogeneous veterinary settings. While the current findings demonstrate strong performance in cross‐species OOD detection, the potential of this approach for detecting intra‐species retinal abnormalities remains to be established and should be addressed in future studies incorporating pathological datasets.
