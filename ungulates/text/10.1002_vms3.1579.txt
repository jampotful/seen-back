Determining orbital volumes of sheep, goats and gazelles via computed tomography

# Determining orbital volumes of sheep, goats and gazelles via computed tomography

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundOrbital volume refers to the sum of bulbus oculi volume and orbital cavity volume. Scientists use orbital volume as a guide to diagnose, treat and prognose various eye diseases such as thyroid eye disease and enophthalmos.ObjectiveThis study aims to examine the orbital volumes of sheep, goats and gazelles to demonstrate homotypic variations and interspecific differences between them.MethodsCrania of six male tuj sheep, five male hair goats and five male gazelle were used in this study. The crania were scanned using a 64‐detector multi‐detector computed tomography with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm at 80 kV, 200 MA and 639 mGy. The cross‐sectional images were stored in DICOM format and then transferred to MIMICS 20.1 software. Afterwards, models were made by establishing the orbital borders on the cross‐sections. Volume values were calculated based on the models.Results and ConclusionNo homotypic variation was found between the right and left volumes within the species (p > 0.05). A statistically significant difference was observed in terms of interspecific right and left orbital volumes between sheep and gazelles (p < 0.05). It was concluded that the orbital parameters of the sheep, goats and gazelles in the same family help reveal their differences in the visual anatomy of these species and provide reference data for clinical diagnosis, treatment and the prognosis follow‐up of ocular diseases in animals.Keywords: computed tomography, gazelle, goat, orbital volume, sheep

## INTRODUCTION
Organum visus consists of bulbus oculi, n. opticus and accessory organs (organa oculi accessoria) in the orbit (Demiraslan & Dayan et al., 2022). The location of the bulbus oculi in animals varies within and between species. The visual field varies depending on how the eyes are positioned inside the skull. Many game animals have 360‐degree binocular vision, with two lateral monocular fields. Understanding the anatomical features of the eyeball is essential in clinical practices related to vision (Demiraslan & Dayan, 2023; Long & Gutta, 2013).
The skeletal system is used to distinguish species close to each other. Cranial parameters show high intraspecific variations (Bärmann et al., 2013; Dayan et al., 2022; Kobryńczuk et al., 2008). Orbital volume may vary due to such pathological changes as orbital shifts, tumours cranial traumas, thyroid eye disease and low intracranial pressure (Bao et al., 2021; Smektala et al., 2015). Determining total orbital volume in animals helps identify orbital pathologies, age, sex, diseases and differences between breeds, alongside exophthalmos and enophthalmos (Adenis et al., 2002; Bentley et al., 2002; Bite et al., 1985; Erkoç et al., 2015; Grover et al., 2003; Ugradar et al., 2019).
Different methods exist to determine the volumetric values (Emirzeoğlu et al., 2011; Sarma, 2006)—the most common method in recent years is making measurements using imaging systems (Caruso et al., 2009; Sahin et al., 2001, 2003; Yılmaz & Tuğrul, 2019). Computed tomography (CT) is a prominent and proven imaging method in the realms of craniofacial anatomy and surgery (March & Vannier, 1983; Waitzman et al., 1992). CT imaging systems have been used in human medicine for many years. More recently, they have begun to be used in veterinary medicine, especially for diagnostics and anatomy (Bontzos et al., 2019; Dayan et al., 2022; Demircioğlu et al., 2021; Detorakis et al., 2010; Güzel et al., 2023; Yılmaz & Demircioğlu, 2021).
Several linear and volumetric studies on the bulbus oculi and auxiliary organs of the eye in different animal species have been published to date (Demircioğlu & Yılmaz, 2019; Doğan et al., 2020; Koçyiğit et al., 2024; Olopade et al., 2005; Verma et al., 2016; Yılmaz & Tuğrul, 2019). The bulbus oculi alone does not reflect orbital volume. There are anatomical formations in the orbit such as the optic nerve, extraocular muscles (including their nerves) and the apparatus lacrimalis.
Radiological imaging methods make it possible to determine the total volume of the orbit. Only a handful of studies on orbital volume parameters in animals exist. This study aimed to determine the orbital volumes of sheep, goats and gazelles to identify homotypic variations and interspecific differences.

## MATERIAL AND METHODS
Approval was obtained from Harran University's Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Decision no: 2023/007/07) for this study.
Crania of six adult male tuj sheep, five adult male hair goats and five adult male gazelle (Gazella subgutturosa) were used. These crania were scanned using 64‐detector multi‐detector CT with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm at 80 kV, 200 MA and 639 mGy). They were extracted from bone samples from healthy adult animals with no pathological findings. Prokop (2003) and Kalra et al. (2004) were taken as reference studies in the CT scanning and dosing protocol. First, the DICOM images were transferred to MIMICS 20.1 software. Then, the orbital borders were manually identified in each cross‐sectional image (Regensburg et al., 2008). The posterior border is defined as the intersection of the medial and lateral walls on the foramen opticum (Bontzos et al., 2019). Volumes were calculated using the ‘properties’ option on the software's interface (Figure 1). Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to determine whether or not the data were normally distributed. Paired t‐test was run on the normally distributed data for directional differences. One‐way ANalysis Of VAriance (ANOVA) and Tukey test were applied to the interspecific differences.

We could not find a homotypic variation within species (p > 0.05) or a statistically significant difference between sheep and goats in the right orbital volumes (p > 0.05, Table 2). However, we found a statistically significant difference between the sheep and gazelles in the right orbital volumes (p < 0.05). We did not detect any significant statistical difference between the sheep and goats in terms of left orbital volume (p > 0.05). By contrast, a statistically significant difference was observed between sheep and gazelles in terms of left orbital volume (p < 0.001; Table 2).

## DISCUSSION
Cranial parameters are commonly used to reveal intraspecific and interspecific differences in the skeletal system (Scheuer, 2002; Bärmann et al., 2013). Complex holes and fissures in the orbit make it difficult to measure orbital volume. Orbital volume parameters can be established using sand, hard seeds and water (Adachi, 1904; Schultz, 1940). As imaging systems advance, CT and magnetic resonance imaging are now commonly used alongside older methods to produce clearer and more accurate results. The current study aimed to determine the orbital volume parameters of sheep, goats and gazelles in horned ruminants (Bovidae) using CT, and then make interspecific comparisons of the results.
Orbital parameters can show inter‐specific variation. Furthermore, they are influenced by age, nutrition and environmental and hormonal factors within the same species (Gündemir et al., 2020; Keneisenuo et al., 2021; Sforza et al., 2009). Bontzos et al. (2020) conducted a study by using water flooding on sheep and reported that their orbital volume was 17.81 ± 0.59 cm3. Sarma (2006) reported that kagani goats had an orbital volume of 22.50 ± 0.54 cm3. Suri et al. (2003) discovered that Rambouillet sheep had an orbital volume of 23.74 ± 0.159 cm3. The study on sheep and goats reported that orbital capacities of each were 45.615 ± 8.827 and 31.692 ± 13.066 mL, respectively (Saber, 1989). In their study, Saber and Gummow (2015) studied the orbital capacity of different animal species using polymer clay and found that the orbital capacities were 80.5 ± 21.44 cm3 for lions, 10.9 ± 3.21 cm3 for dogs and 3.8 ± 1.6 cm3 for cats. In a study on blackbucks, the researchers calculated orbital capacity as 25.00 ± 0.56 cm3 in males and 27.50 ± 0.37 cm3 in females using mustard seeds (Choudhary & Singh, 2015). One study on red foxes reported that their orbital capacity was 9.34 ± 0.17 cc (Sayed & Hamoda, 2021). In the present study, the right and orbital volumes of sheep were 29.81 ± 2.07 and 29.76 ± 2.32 cm3, respectively. The right and left orbital volumes of goats were 32.90 ± 5.04 and 32.99 ± 4.57 cm3. The right and left orbital volumes of gazelles were 23.36 ± 2.4 and 23.50 ± 2.00 cm3. We also found significant statistical differences between all three groups (p < 0.005). Keneisenuo et al. (2021) reported that the orbital parameters of sambar deer were higher than those of other domestic small and wild ruminants. The present study has shown that the average orbital volume was the highest in goats and the lowest in gazelles, and there was a statistically significant difference between sheep and gazelles in the right orbital volume. We think that this might be due to the average weight differences between the animals (Aksoy et al., 2001; Gürler et al., 2015.)
The reproducibility, rapidity and reliability of the linear and volumetric parameters measured using CT stand out over other conventional measurement methods. Consequently, we aimed to determine the orbital parameters of sheep, goats and gazelles in the same family, to reveal their differences on the visual anatomy of these species, and provide reference data for clinical diagnosis, treatment and the prognosis follow‐up of ocular diseases in animals.
