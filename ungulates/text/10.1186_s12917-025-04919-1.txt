AI-enhanced infrared thermography for reliable detection and spatial mapping of temperature patterns in calf eyes and muzzles.

# AI-enhanced infrared thermography for reliable detection and spatial mapping of temperature patterns in calf eyes and muzzles

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundNon-invasive temperature measurement using infrared cameras has become increasingly important for monitoring physiological changes and stress responses in animals, offering advantages over traditional rectal thermometry. However, previous methods often suffered from limitations such as environmental interference, instantaneous measurement, and inaccurate region of interest (ROI) selection due to manual settings. To overcome these limitations, studies have combined infrared cameras with AI-based segmentation to enable accurate ROI detection and to capture temporal temperature change patterns in cattle. Furthermore, the interpretability of eye and muzzle temperature measurements can vary depending on which subregions are analyzed, as areas with richer vascularization tend to display more representative temperature characteristics. To address these issues, the present study applied AI-based segmentation to infrared thermography and focused on the analysis of high-temperature, vascularized subregions within the eyes and muzzles of calves. By doing so, we aimed to enhance the clarity and reliability of temperature change pattern analysis for non-invasive monitoring of physiological status in cattle.MethodsThermal images were captured using a mobile infrared camera, and video recordings were obtained simultaneously from 11 calves. AI-based segmentation, utilizing previously trained weights, was used to automatically extract eye and muzzle ROIs from video images. 33 imaging sessions where the majority of frames exhibited reliable segmentation were selected for analysis. In Experiment 1, temperature data corresponding to the mean, top 10%, and top 30% values within each ROI underwent preprocessing steps (outlier rejection, standardization, and low-pass filtering) to derive temperature change patterns. This process generated six patterns per session (three for eyes and three muzzle regions), yielding a total of 198 patterns across all 33 image sessions. Cosine similarity analysis was then applied to quantify similarity within the same session. In Experiment 2, each ROI was divided into a 3 × 3 grid to map the distribution of high temperature values for spatial analysis. Statistical analyses included Kruskal-Wallis tests with Bonferroni corrections to assess regional differences.ResultsIn Experiment 1, for the eyes, the patterns derived from the top 10% and 30% of temperatures had high cosine similarity (0.94). In contrast, the patterns based on the mean values had relatively lower similarities with the top 10% and 30% patterns (0.81 and 0.86, respectively). A similar trend was observed for the muzzle: the top 10% and 30% patterns had a high cosine similarity (0.93), while the patterns based on the mean values showed lower similarities (0.80, and 0.86). In Experiment 2, for the eyes, the top 10% of temperature values were mainly in the bottom region. In comparison, the top 30% of values were more evenly distributed in the mid and bottom regions. For the muzzles, the top 10% of temperature values were mainly distributed in both the top and bottom regions, and the top 30% of values were concentrated in the mid region.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that integrating AI-based segmentation with infrared thermography enables precise identification of thermally reliable subregions within the eyes and muzzles of calves, leading to the extraction of temperature change patterns with high temporal consistency. The top 10% and 30% temperature values within these regions show higher pattern similarity than mean values, with distinct spatial distributions reflecting underlying vascular anatomy. Focusing on these high-temperature, vascularized subregions enhances the interpretability and reliability of temperature change pattern analysis for non-invasive monitoring of stress and physiological status in cattle, contributing to enhanced animal welfare.Keywords: Infrared thermography, AI segmentation, Temperature change pattern, Cattle welfare monitoring

## Introduction
Recent studies have expanded methods for measuring animal body temperature, moving beyond traditional rectal measurements to include non-invasive techniques using infrared cameras. These cameras measure temperatures in areas such as the eyes, muzzle, and reproductive organs, and provide insights into physiological changes and stress responses in animals. This approach is non-invasive and has been demonstrated in studies assessing correlations between camera-generated temperatures and rectal temperatures; studied animals include cats [1], ponies [2], sheep, and cattle [3]. Eye and muzzle temperatures have been analyzed in research assessing physiological changes, including during horse training [4], jumping competitions [5], shearing and foot trimming in sheep [6], and transportation stress in horses [7]. These temperature measurement methods have several limitations: (1) they provide limited information because the measurement is instantaneous and not sustained; (2) infrared cameras are impacted by environmental factors like wind and external temperature [8, 9], affecting absolute temperature values; (3) manual settings of ROI as circles or squares can include the surrounding skin, reducing accuracy; and (4) stress causes short-term temperature changes [10, 11], but manual ROI settings make it difficult to extract and analyze temperature data over those short intervals.
Recent advances in object detection technology have helped overcome the limitations associated with manual ROI selection. Early methods relied on traditional image processing techniques—such as edge detection and geometric fitting—to identify key facial features in animals [12]. More recently, deep learning-based object detection approaches have significantly improved the accuracy and robustness of facial ROI extraction under diverse conditions, including agricultural and veterinary settings [13]. In addition, semantic segmentation techniques now enable pixel-level localization of facial features, further enhancing the precision of temperature measurement from specific anatomical regions [14]. Furthermore, there are two main approaches for ROI detection and subsequent temperature extraction: (1) direct detection from infrared images [12–14], and (2) detection from RGB images followed by mapping to infrared data [15, 16]. Direct detection from infrared images offers system simplicity, as both ROI detection and temperature extraction can be performed using a single modality. However, the lower resolution and sensitivity of infrared images can reduce detection accuracy, especially with animal movement or environmental interference. In contrast, extracting ROIs from higher-resolution RGB images and mapping them to infrared data requires careful calibration but allows for more precise and robust landmark detection. In our previous research, we prioritized accurate ROI extraction and therefore adopted the approach of detecting ROIs from RGB images using semantic segmentation to extract the eye and muzzle regions of calves, and then used the corresponding temperature data to generate temperature change patterns [15]. This method offers several advantages:1) The approach uses AI segmentation to extract ROIs (eyes and muzzles) from images, enabling accurate and continuous temperature measurements. 2) By focusing on relative temperature changes rather than absolute values, the approach reduces the impact of environmental factors on infrared camera readings. 3) The approach allows for diverse analyses of temperature change over time and facilitates the detection of physiological changes like stress in animals, extending beyond human applications [17].
Previous studies on the correlation between rectal temperature and eye/muzzle temperatures have yielded inconsistent results. These inconsistencies are due to variations in vascular distribution within the eye, which affect the thermal characteristics of each region [18, 19]. The medial part [20, 21] or lacrimal sac area [18] tends to have higher temperatures, while the central part of the eye tends to have lower temperatures. Previous studies using the highest temperatures within the eyes [2, 3] have often focused on the medial part of the eye, resulting in higher correlations. In contrast, using average temperatures [22, 23] or the center of the eye [24] has shown lower correlations due to lower vascularization. Furthermore, in studies on horse training [4] and jumping competitions [5], the temperature of the lacrimal caruncle was used because of its high vascularization and sensitivity to physiological changes. These differences highlight the need to focus on regions with rich vascularization when analyzing temperature change patterns obtained using AI-enhanced infrared thermography.
Based on the findings described above, this study set out to explore two main objectives. First, we aimed to determine whether regions within the eye and the muzzle that exhibit higher temperatures also demonstrate more consistent temperature change patterns over time (Experiment 1). Second, we sought to validate the anatomical locations of these regions within the eye and the muzzle (Experiment 2). Focusing on these high-temperature, vascularized regions is expected to improve the reliability and interpretability of temperature change patterns, thereby facilitating the detection of physiological changes, such as stress, in animals.

## Materials and methods

## Animals and devices
This study was conducted on 11 calves (12–14 weeks old) at the Kobe University farm, following the same experimental design and data collection procedures as previously described [15]. Each calf was imaged three times at four-week intervals, resulting in a total of 57 imaging sessions. For each session, approximately 1–2 min of RGB video was recorded, along with corresponding temperature data. Thermal and conventional video images were collected using a mobile infrared camera (FLIR One Pro for iOS; emissivity set to 0.95), under consistent environmental and handling conditions as detailed in our earlier work [15].

## Experiment 1. comparative consistency analysis of temperature change patterns
Experiment 1 compared the temporal consistency of temperature change patterns for the top (10% and 30%) and mean temperature values within the ROI. The procedures for region of interest (ROI) extraction (eyes and muzzles), temperature data derivation, and subsequent analysis were performed according to the protocols established in our previous publication [15]. In brief, we utilized a neural network model trained and validated in a previous study to automatically detect ROIs (eyes or muzzle) within RGB images. The model achieved a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 0.62 for the eye and 0.60 for the muzzle. The inference speed was 360 ms per image for the eye and 343 ms per image for the muzzle. Inference was performed on an AWS EC2 p2.xlarge instance equipped with a single NVIDIA Tesla K80 GPU (12 GB VRAM), an Intel Xeon E5-2686 v4 CPU (4 vCPUs), and 61 GiB of system memory. Of the 57 imaging sessions conducted in this study, 33 image sessions were selected for analysis based on the criterion that the majority of frames within each session exhibited reliable segmentation results. For each frame, temperature data were extracted from the corresponding regions identified by the detection algorithm, enabling the generation of time-series temperature change graphs (Fig. 1, top left). These data were subsequently processed through a standardized pipeline involving outlier removal (Tukey’s method [25]), normalization, and low-pass filtering for noise reduction (Fig. 1, top right). This workflow was applied to both eyes and muzzle regions (Fig. 2, bottom) using three distinct methods: mean, top 10%, and top 30% temperature values within each ROI. As a result, six patterns were obtained for each session (eye_mean, muzzle_mean, eye10, muzzle10, eye30, muzzle30), yielding a total of 198 unique temperature change patterns for analysis across all 33 image sessions.

In the statistical analysis, cosine similarity was used as the primary metric to analyze and compare the temperature change patterns. By representing these temperature patterns as vectors, the cosine similarity between them was calculated. This value, which ranges from − 1 to 1, indicates how similar the patterns are: values closer to 1 mean the patterns are highly similar, values near 0 indicate little to no similarity, and negative values suggest an inverse relationship. This approach allowed for an objective comparison of the structural patterns in temperature changes.
For all 33 image sessions, we compared cosine similarities between six temperature change patterns within each session (representing the same individual and time point). The analyzed pairs included: eye_mean vs. eye10, eye_mean vs. eye30, eye10 vs. eye30, muzzle_mean vs. muzzle10, muzzle_mean vs. muzzle30, muzzle10 vs. muzzle30, eye_mean vs. muzzle_mean, eye10 vs. muzzle10, and eye30 vs. muzzle30.

## Experiment 2. spatial distribution of high temperature values within the rois
Experiment 2 focused on mapping the spatial distribution of high temperature values within the eyes and muzzles. Building on the ROI extraction procedures (Figs. 2 and 3, left and top right) described in Experiment 1, the locations corresponding to the top 10% or 30% of temperature values were identified (Figs. 2 and 3, middle right). Each region was divided into a 3 × 3 grid, resulting in nine segments. For the eyes, the grid was defined horizontally as medial, center, and lateral, and vertically as top, middle, and bottom. For the muzzle, the horizontal divisions were right, center, and left, while the vertical divisions were top, middle, and bottom. For each image session, we determined which of the nine segments contained the top 10% or 30% temperature values (Figs. 2 and 3, bottom right) and counted the number of frames corresponding to each segment. The median number of frames for each segment was then calculated across all 33 sessions to assess whether high-temperature areas tended to cluster in specific regions. For statistical analysis, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to determine whether there were significant differences in the distribution of high-temperature values among the three regions (e.g., top vs. mid vs. bottom for eye and muzzle, medial vs. center vs. lateral for eye, right vs. center vs. left for muzzle). When significant differences were found, Bonferroni post-hoc tests were performed to identify which regions differed from each other. All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.8 with the NumPy and SciPy libraries, and the results were visualized using Matplotlib.

## Results

## Experiment 1
To evaluate the consistency of different temperature change patterns, we compared the patterns derived from the mean, top 10%, and top 30% values within the eyes and muzzles for each session across all 33 image sessions. Cosine similarity was used to quantify the degree of agreement between these patterns. For the eyes, the temperature change patterns based on the top 10% and 30% of values showed a high degree of similarity (cosine similarity = 0.94). The mean value pattern showed moderate similarity with the top 10% (0.81) and top 30% (0.86) patterns. Similar trends were observed for the muzzles, with the top 10% and 30% patterns exhibiting high similarity (0.93), and the mean pattern showing moderate similarity with the top 10% (0.80) and top 30% (0.86) patterns. When comparing the eyes and muzzles, the similarity was lower (mean: 0.72, top 10%: 0.75, top 30%: 0.71) (Table 1).

## Experiment 2
Experiment 2 aimed to identify the anatomical locations where high temperature values were concentrated within the eyes and muzzles. For each region, the top 10% and 30% of temperature values were mapped onto a 3 × 3 grid, and their spatial distributions were analyzed. The results showed that, for the eyes, the top 10% of the temperature values were mainly located in the bottom region (Fig. 4, top left). For the top 30% of the values, both the mid and bottom regions showed significantly higher distributions compared to the top region (Fig. 4, top right). However, no significant differences were observed when dividing the regions into medial, center, and lateral areas for the top 10% and 30% of the values (Fig. 4, bottom). For the muzzles, the top 10% of the temperature values were significantly distributed in the top and bottom regions (Fig. 5, top left). The top 30% of the values showed a significantly higher distribution in the mid region compared to the top and bottom regions (Fig. 5, top right). In terms of right, center, and left divisions, no significant differences were seen for the top 10% of the values (Fig. 5, bottom left). However, for the top 30% of the values, the center region showed a significantly higher distribution compared to the right or left regions (Fig. 5, bottom right).

## Discussion

## Summary and interpretation of results
This study applied AI-enhanced infrared thermography methods to assess the temporal consistency of temperature change patterns for the mean, top 10%, and top 30% of temperature values within the eyes and muzzles of calves. The results demonstrated that patterns derived from high temperature subregions (top 10% and 30%) exhibited stronger temporal coherence than mean-value patterns (Experiment 1). Regarding location within the ROIs, the top 10% of the eye temperature values were predominantly in the bottom region, and the top 30% values were more evenly distributed in the mid region. For the muzzles, the top 10% values were in both the top and bottom regions, and the top 30% values were concentrated in the mid region (Experiment 2).

## Comparison of consistency of temperature change patterns
Evaluating the similarity between different temperature change patterns using the average, top 10%, and top 30% of the temperature measures showed that both eyes and muzzles had the highest similarity with respect to the top 10% and 30% of the values (0.93 and 0.94). In contrast, patterns based on average temperatures showed lower temporal coherence (0.80 and 0.81). These results indicate that regions with richer vascularization demonstrated more clear and reliable temperature change patterns over time, likely due to reduced influence from low-vascularity areas that introduce noise. Conversely, when using the average temperature of the entire ROI, the inclusion of low-vascularity regions obscures temperature change patterns, resulting in reduced pattern clarity. The similarity between eye and muzzle temperature change patterns was lower (0.71–0.75) compared to within-region similarities. This was similar to previous studies [3] that reported simple correlations of 0.73 and 0.79. This study used high-frequency temperature measurements with segmentation (intervals of less than 1 s). This differed from previous studies [3], which used manual ROI settings and the highest detected temperatures at low frequency (approximately 12 measurements at hourly intervals). This difference indicates that the temperature changes between the eyes and muzzle vary regardless of the frequency of temperature measurements and the degree of blood flow distribution. For example, in addition to being influenced by overall body blood flow changes, distinct physiological functions impact the thermal distribution in each area. The muzzle, in particular, may be influenced by breathing or licking behaviors. Further, variations in the tissue thickness from the skin surface to the blood vessels may contribute to these differences.

## Distribution of high temperature values (comparison with previous studies)
The lacrimal caruncle and medial canthus have the highest temperatures within the eye. The high temperature in these areas is attributed to their rich vascularization, making them ideal for measuring sympathetic nervous system-related blood flow changes [26, 27]. As a result, these areas are highly correlated with physiological changes in the body. Illustrating this, one study [18] found that the lacrimal sac area had the highest temperature sensitivity when compared to respiratory rate and core body temperature. Other studies [4, 5] on stress-induced eye temperature changes in horses detected that the lacrimal caruncle area showed a significant correlation with stress levels. Similarly, a study on sheep stress [19] found that the medial canthus had the highest correlation with cortisol levels compared to other eye regions.
From a methodological perspective, detecting temperatures in specific areas (e.g., the eye) often involves using the highest temperatures within ROIs set as rectangles or circles, or manually specifying the specific areas. In contrast, this study used AI to automatically detect the eye regions. This approach revealed that the top 10% of the temperature values in the eyes were mainly in the bottom region, and the top 30% of the values were in the mid region. No significant differences were observed between medial, center, and lateral regions for the top 10% and 30% values. This differs from previous studies emphasizing high temperature distribution in the medial part. Three factors likely drive this difference: (1) the different ROI setting compared to previous studies, (2) the vascular distribution of calves, and (3) the grid setting. These are discussed as follows.
1) Concerning the different ROI settings, previous studies manually set the ROI using a rectangle or circle to detect eye temperature. In contrast, this study used AI to continuously detect the eyes and muzzle over short intervals, accurately identifying the eyeball as the ROI. This differs from previous manual ROIs, which included skin around the eyes. This study utilized pixel-level segmentation to detect the target ROI, excluding the surrounding skin. Since this study did not include the lacrimal caruncle in the ROI, the significance of the medial part, which is considered to have a higher temperature, was reduced. The medial and lateral parts, and the bottom part connecting them, were considered to have higher temperature values. A previous study [18] found that when excluding the skin around the eyes, such as the lacrimal caruncle, the temperature sensitivity changed; the breathing rate and core body temperature showed similar values between the medial and lateral canthi. The average temperatures of these areas were higher compared to other parts of the eye. Additionally, infrared images showed higher temperatures in the medial and lateral canthi and along the ventral edge of the orbit, which connects these two regions [18].
2) Regarding the vascular distribution in calves, in humans, the medial part around the eyes typically has higher temperature values than the center or lateral parts due to higher vascular distribution [20, 21]. This leads to divisions into medial, center, and lateral areas. The arteries contributing to the relatively higher temperature in the medial part include the ophthalmic artery, which branches from the internal carotid artery towards the medial side of the eye, and the angular artery, which branches from the facial artery. The angular artery runs upwards and towards the medial side of the eye from the lower part of the human face. However, calves have a different facial vascular structure compared to humans. There is no equivalent to the human angular artery. Instead, the malar artery and vein branch from the maxillary artery to surround the lower part of the orbit, extending from behind (caudal) and along the ventral edge of the orbit towards the cranial sides [28, 29]. This anatomical difference likely contributed to the classification by top, center, and bottom regions rather than medial, center, and lateral. This leads to the bottom part being the warmest.
3) Finally, concerning the grid setting, this study’s setting likely contributed to the relatively high temperature values in the bottom part of the eye. Calf eyes are positioned slightly lower (ventrally) on the medial side compared to the lateral side. However, the 3 × 3 grid used in this study was set horizontally. This meant that the eyes were positioned diagonally within the grid. Considering the anatomical structure, the medial part of the lower orbit may be warmer than the lateral part. The diagonal positioning of the eyes within the grid may have highlighted significant differences across the bottom part compared to other areas.One limitation of this study is the potential bias introduced by the grid segmentation method. Building upon previous studies [18, 19] in which the eye region was divided into three sections horizontally based on anatomical landmarks, this study employed a 3 × 3 grid. However, fixed grid ratios may introduce measurement bias due to inter-individual variations in facial dimensions in the image. Future investigations comparing alternative segmentation approaches (e.g., 2 × 2, 4 × 4 grids) could optimize the detection accuracy of thermosensitive zones in both eye and muzzle regions.
In summary, this study’s finding that the top 10% of the temperature values were detected in the bottom part of the eye does not contradict the established understanding that the medial part of the eye usually has higher temperatures. Rather, this difference is likely due to the technical aspects of using automated ROI settings and grid configurations, compared to the manual ROI settings used in previous studies. Additionally, it was deemed unavoidable when analyzing short-term temperature change patterns with AI.
When considering temperature distribution in the muzzle, many studies have examined temperature distributions within the eyes, but few have explored the muzzle. This study found that the top 10% of the temperature values in the muzzle were in the top and bottom regions, and the top 30% of the values were concentrated in the mid region. This is likely due to the blood supply from the labial artery, which branches from the facial artery and runs from the back (caudal) of the head towards the nose [28, 29]. The anatomical position of these vessels may explain why the top and bottom regions of the muzzle showed higher temperatures and more reliable temperature change patterns.

## Methodological considerations and limitations
In this study, the model used to detect the eyes and muzzle achieved a relatively low mAP of approximately 0.6. This suboptimal performance can be attributed to several factors: the small size and indistinct boundaries of the eyes and muzzle, the fact that Japanese Black calves have black hair that is similar in color to their eyes and muzzle—making these regions difficult to distinguish—and the susceptibility of detection to environmental factors such as shadows, lighting conditions, and camera angles. To address these limitations, temperature change pattern analysis was conducted only on imaging sessions in which the majority of frames exhibited reliably segmented ROIs, as confirmed by visual inspection. Sessions with a high proportion of frames containing incorrect segmentation were excluded from the analysis. As a result, we believe that segmentation errors did not directly affect the results of the similarity analysis (Experiment 1) or the localization of high-temperature regions (Experiment 2). Nevertheless, a low mAP indicates that the proportion of frames with accurately detected ROIs is limited, resulting in data loss and reduced reliability of the derived temperature change patterns. Therefore, for future research and practical applications, it will be important to improve the mAP of the ROI detection model. Potential strategies include increasing the size and diversity of the training dataset—especially by incorporating images captured under various lighting and environmental conditions—applying advanced data augmentation techniques to simulate shadows, different angles, and occlusions [30], utilizing more advanced or specialized deep learning architectures such as attention-based models or models pre-trained on similar animal datasets [31], enhancing image quality and contrast through the use of higher-resolution cameras or image pre-processing methods, and incorporating multi-modal data, such as combining RGB and thermal images, to improve discrimination between the ROI and background [32]. By implementing these approaches, it should be possible to achieve higher detection accuracy and reduce data loss in future studies.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that integrating AI-based segmentation with infrared thermography enables the identification of subregions within the eyes and muzzles of calves that exhibit clear and reliable temperature change patterns. The top 10% and 30% temperature values within these regions showed higher temporal consistency and pattern similarity than mean values, with distinct spatial distributions reflecting underlying vascular anatomy. Focusing on these high-temperature, vascularized subregions enhances the interpretability and reliability of non-invasive temperature monitoring for detecting physiological changes, such as stress, in cattle. Furthermore, mapping the precise locations of high-temperature areas within the eyes and muzzles provides new anatomical insight into the physiological mechanisms underlying thermal responses. This refined approach improves the effectiveness of non-invasive monitoring techniques and ultimately contributes to advancing animal health and welfare.
