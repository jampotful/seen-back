# Development of a novel duplex recombinase polymerase amplification (DRPA) assay for rapid detection of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii

## Abstract
Carbapenem-Resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) poses a major public health threat due to its multidrug resistance and high mortality rates, necessitating rapid and accurate diagnostic tools for timely intervention and infection control. This study developed and evaluated a novel detection method combining duplex recombinase polymerase amplification (DRPA) with a lateral flow strip (LFS) for the rapid visual identification of CRAB. The DRPA-LFS assay targets blaOXA−23 and blaOXA−51 genes, enabling isothermal amplification at 37 °C followed by a simple visual readout. The method demonstrated high sensitivity (102 CFU). Results can be obtained within 40 min without the need for complex instrumentation, making it suitable for resource-limited environments. the method offers a portable, user-friendly, and cost-effective solution for CRAB detection.

## Introduction
Acinetobacter baumannii (AB) has emerged as a significant threat to public health worldwide. This Gram-negative bacterium is known for its ability to cause a wide range of infections, including pneumonia, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and secondary meningitis [14]. The A. baumannii mortality rate of patients with hospital- and community-acquired infections is 23–68% and up to 64%, respectively [11]. According to the 2020 China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network, infections attributable to A. baumannii have demonstrated a continued upward trend, comprising 17.07% of all lower respiratory tract infections and associated with a mortality rate of 35% [15]. In light of its clinical significance and escalating resistance profiles, A. baumannii has been classified by the World Health Organization as one of the most critical priority bacterial pathogens [16, 17]. Carbapenems have traditionally constituted the primary therapeutic agents in the management of A. baumannii infections. Nevertheless, extensive utilization of these antimicrobial agents over recent decades, in conjunction with the organism’s inherent and acquired resistance mechanisms, has precipitated a marked increase in the incidence of carbapenem-Resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) infections [13]. CRAB infections are particularly concerning due to their high mortality rates and the limited treatment options available. Carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii is primarily mediated by the production of carbapenemases, which are enzymes that inactivate carbapenem antibiotics. The carbapenems are classified within class D β-lactamases, which encompass several variants including OXA-23-like, OXA-40-like, OXA-51-like, OXA-58-like, and OXA-23. These enzymes are predominantly responsible for conferring carbapenem resistance in AB. Empirical evidence indicates that the blaOXA−23 gene is the most frequently encountered among class D β-lactamases. This gene can be integrated into either plasmid or chromosomal DNA, thereby facilitating the rapid acquisition of drug resistance in AB [6, 4, 19]. The escalating prevalence of CRAB infections, coupled with the substantial therapeutic challenges they pose, necessitates the development of advanced diagnostic methodologies. These methodologies must demonstrate both rapidity and precision to facilitate early pathogen identification, thereby enabling healthcare practitioners to implement targeted therapeutic interventions in a timely manner.
Current diagnostic approaches for AB face significant challenges in speed, accuracy, and accessibility. Traditional culture-based techniques, such as blood or sputum cultures, remain widely used but are inherently slow, often requiring several days to yield results. This delay hampers timely clinical decision-making, particularly in critical care settings where rapid intervention is essential. Moreover, these methods may fail to detect CRAB during the early stages of infection, increasing the risk of treatment delays and contributing to higher mortality rates. Molecular techniques like polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and real-time PCR offer faster and more specific detection by targeting carbapenemase genes such as blaOXA−51 and blaOXA−23 [2, 3]. While these assays provide high sensitivity and specificity, their implementation is limited by the need for specialized equipment, trained personnel, and relatively high costs-factors that restrict their use in resource-limited environments. The method of loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) is also an isothermal detection technique; however, it requires a higher reaction temperature. In contrast, RPA technology can even carry out reactions in extreme environments with just hand-held heating, greatly increasing the portability of the detection. As a result, despite their diagnostic advantages, molecular methods are not always feasible for point-of-care applications. Lateral flow strip represents an alternative strategy that addresses some of the logistical barriers associated with traditional diagnostics. These assays are rapid, portable, and require minimal technical expertise, making them suitable for on-site testing. To overcome these limitations, isothermal amplification techniques combined with lateral flow detection have emerged as promising alternatives. One such method involves the use of Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) coupled with a lateral flow strip.
RPA is a novel molecular diagnostic technique initially reported by Piepenburg et al., [12] and represents a significant advancement in nucleic acid detection methodologies. This isothermal amplification technology distinguishes itself through its operational simplicity, requiring neither sophisticated instrumentation nor stringent reaction conditions; optimal amplification is achieved at 37℃ within a 30-minute timeframe [12]. The versatility of RPA is evidenced by its compatibility with multiple detection modalities, including gel electrophoresis, real-time fluorescence quantification, and lateral flow strip (LFS) analysis, as documented in recent studies [7, 20]. The reaction mechanism of duplex Recombinase Polymerase Amplification (DRPA) is illustrated in Fig. 1. Target gene fragments were selectively amplified using chemically modified primers and fluorescent probes. The probes were designed with a C3 spacer (SpC3) at the 3’ end to inhibit strand extension and a tetrahydrofuran (THF) moiety at the central region to enable cleavage by the Nfo enzyme. Cleavage by Nfo enzyme occurred exclusively when the nucleotide sequences flanking the THF site exhibited precise complementarity with the template, thereby releasing the 3’ end from SpC3-mediated inhibition. The 5’ termini of the probes were conjugated with either digoxigenin (DIG) or fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), while the 5’ ends of the reverse primers were labeled with biotin (Fig. 1A). Upon completion of the RPA reaction, two distinctively labeled products were generated: one bearing FITC and biotin, and the other containing DIG and biotin (Fig. 1B). These duplex RPA products were subsequently analyzed using a specifically designed lateral flow strip (LFS) for the simultaneous detection of two bacterial species (Fig. 1C). This dual-detection capability significantly enhances the diagnostic potential of RPA technology in microbial identification applications. Similar strategies have demonstrated success in detecting other pathogens [8, 21]. These advancements highlight the potential of integrating isothermal amplification with lateral flow readout systems to create robust, user-friendly diagnostic tools. Overall, current diagnostic methods for CRAB each present unique trade-offs between speed, accuracy, cost, and ease of use. The integration of isothermal amplification technologies with lateral flow platforms offers a compelling solution by combining rapid detection, high specificity, and operational simplicity. Such innovations hold promise for improving the management of CRAB infections, especially in settings where access to advanced laboratory infrastructure is limited.

## Materials and methods

## Collection of bacterial strains and clinical samples
This study used sixteen bacterial strains, comprising both reference and clinical isolates of Acinetobacter baumannii. Specifically, the reference strains included Acinetobacter baumannii (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC 19606), procured from Shanghai Covey Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Additional reference strains and sputum-derived isolates were maintained in our laboratory’s culture collection (Table 1). The sputum isolates of A. baumannii were collected from patients aged 20–50 years and hospitalized for at least one week in Lianyungang Second People’s Hospital. The 200 isolates were confirmed by 16 S rRNA PCR and real-time PCR (qPCR) [5, 10]. Prior to molecular analyses, all samples underwent heat treatment at 100℃ for 10 min to ensure optimal template preparation for subsequent duplex RPA and qPCR assays.

## Design of primers and probes
The duplex recombinase polymerase amplification assay was developed through the design of specific primers-probe sets targeting the blaOXA−51 gene (National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) reference sequence: CP043953.1) and blaOXA−23 gene (NCBI reference sequence: NG_049525.1). Primer design was performed using Primer Premier 5.0 software (Premier Biosoft, Palo Alto, CA, USA), with the following parameters: maximum amplicon size of 300 base pairs (bp), primer lengths ranging from 30 to 33 bp, and GC content varying between 20% and 80%. The designed DPCR primers and probes were checked for specificity using BLASTN and Primer-BLAST tools (NCBI) against Acinetobacter baumannii and other closely related species.The complete nucleotide sequences of the primers and probes are detailed in Table 2.

## Reaction procedure of duplex RPA method
The reaction system was meticulously configured in strict adherence to the manufacturer’s protocol. Given that the duplex RPA assay was designed to target two distinct genes, the reaction components of the RPA system were optimized to minimize redundancy. Specifically, each 50-µL reaction mixture was formulated to enable the simultaneous detection of dual targets, comprising 1.05 µL of each primer (10 µM), 0.3 µL of probe (10 µM), 1 µL of template, and other standard reaction components. Following a brief centrifugation step, a single tube containing the dual reaction systems was incubated at a temperature range of 33–41 °C for a duration of 10–30 min. Subsequently, 10 µL of the duplex RPA products were detected using a lateral flow strip (LFS) (Amp-Future (Changzhou) Biotech Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China). The Twist Amp® DNA Amplification Kits were procured from TwistDx Ltd. (Maidenhead, UK).

## Detection specificity and limit of the RPA assay
To evaluate the specificity of the DRPA assay, fourteen distinct genomic templates were employed as targets, each standardized at a concentration of 104 colony-forming units (CFU). For the assessment of analytical sensitivity, a bacterial suspension with a predetermined concentration was subjected to serial logarithmic dilutions ranging from 103 to 101 CFU/µL using RNase-free water. Prior to experimental procedures, all template preparations underwent thermal denaturation at 100 °C for 10 min to ensure DNA accessibility and consistency in subsequent analyses.

## qPCR assay
The qPCR was performed according to the following thermal cycling protocol: an initial denaturation phase at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 15 s [9]. The complete nucleotide sequences of the primers and probes are detailed in Table 2.

## Statistical analysis
Diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the DRPA assay were calculated against qPCR as the reference standard. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined using the exact binomial (Clopper–Pearson) method. Agreement between methods was assessed with Cohen’s kappa (κ). Analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

## Results

## Identify the availability of primers and probes for the detection of CRAB
Specific primers and probes targeting the blaOXA−51 and blaOXA−23 genes were designed for the detection of CRAB. Because the blaOXA−51 gene is universally present in all A. baumannii strains, carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii, which harbors both blaOXA−51 and blaOXA−23 genes, was utilized to validate the efficacy of the two primer-probe sets. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the application of either primer-probe set produced distinct bands on the corresponding detection lines, with no discernible red band observed in the no-template control (NTC). This confirms the utility of blaOXA−51 F/P/R and blaOXA−23 F/P/R for the detection of A. baumannii. To further assess the specificity of these primer-probe sets, two clinical isolates of carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii, characterized by the presence of both blaOXA−51 and blaOXA−23 genes, were successfully identified from sputum specimens using the DRPA assay. The results demonstrated that both primers-probe sets effectively detected their respective target genes (Fig. 2B). These findings substantiate that the DRPA assay is effective for the detection of CRAB.

## Detection specificity of the DRPA method
To validate the specificity of the primer-probe sets in exclusively amplifying either A. baumannii or carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii, a DRPA assay was performed. This assay incorporated four clinical sputum isolates of A. baumannii (two of them are CRAB), in addition to eleven other bacterial pathogens. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the detection of CRAB was confirmed by the presence of distinct red bands on both the blaOXA−51 and blaOXA−23 detection lines. Conversely, the identification of A. baumannii was indicated by a single red band exclusively on the blaOXA−51 detection line. The absence of discernible red bands signified the presence of other bacterial species. Based on these findings, the primers-probe sets targeting blaOXA−51 (blaOXA−51 F/P/R) and blaOXA−23 (blaOXA−23 F/P/R) were selected for subsequent experimental applications.

## Optimization of the DRPA conditions
To ascertain the optimal parameters for the DRPA system, a systematic evaluation of reaction temperature and duration was conducted. The temperature gradient was systematically varied from 33 to 41℃, while the incubation period was examined within the range of 10 to 30 min. As illustrated in Fig. 4A and B, the most intense red band, indicative of optimal amplification efficiency, was observed at 37℃ for both AB and CRAB strains. This temperature parameter aligns with both empirical data from previous studies and the manufacturer’s recommended specifications. Regarding temporal optimization, initial amplification products manifested as faint pink bands after 10 min of incubation, with progressive intensification observed from the 15-minute time point. Analysis of band intensity revealed stabilization of the red bands following 30 min of reaction time (Fig. 4C and D). Based on these empirical observations, The reaction temperature 37℃ and the reaction time 30 min were used as the reaction conditions for the subsequent DRPA.

## Sensitivity of the DRPA method
To ascertain the sensitivity of the DRPA, serial dilutions of heat-inactivated CRAB cultures were prepared, ranging from 103 to 101 colony-forming units (CFU) per reaction. The result revealed that at a template concentration of 101 CFU, the blaOXA−51 detection line exhibited a markedly attenuated signal intensity, while the blaOXA−23 detection line was scarcely discernible. Consequently, the sensitivity of the DRPA was determined to be 102 CFU, as illustrated in Fig. 5A. To evaluate the potential interference from co-existing bacterial species, 103 CFU of Streptococcus pneumoniae were introduced into the DRPA reaction system to assess detection specificity. Notably, the presence of S. pneumoniae did not significantly impact the assay’s performance, so the sensitivity of the DRPA remained consistent at 102 CFU, as demonstrated in Fig. 5B. The sensitivity estimates are based on qualitative strip interpretation. These findings demonstrate the robustness and specificity of the DRPA assay in detecting CRAB amidst potential bacterial interference.

## Clinical application of the DRPA for CRAB detection
To evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the DRPA assay, we analyzed a cohort of 85 clinical specimens collected from hospitalized patients. The DRPA assay detected positive amplification in 69 clinical samples for the blaOXA-51 gene, confirming the presence of AB. Furthermore, 45 samples showed concurrent positivity for both blaOXA−51 and blaOXA−23 genes, indicating CRAB presence. ## Diagnostic performance of DRPA assay
The diagnostic accuracy of the DRPA assay was evaluated against culture and sequencing as the reference standard. Among 85 clinical samples, the DRPA assay demonstrated perfect agreement with the reference method for CRAB detection, with sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV all at 100% (Table 4). The Kappa statistic (κ = 1.0) indicated perfect agreement between DRPA and the reference standard. These results confirm the high reliability and clinical applicability of the DRPA-LFS system for rapid blaOXA−23 and blaOXA−51 genes identification.

## Discussion
The infection of CRAB is a serious problem, Hence, a simple method for detection of CRAB is needed. A duplex RPA assay with one LFS was developed for detection of two target genes to distinguish AB and CRAB.
Molecular detection methodologies necessitate the strategic selection of diagnostic amplification targets to ensure precise identification of specific microbial species. Current evidence indicates that the blaOXA−51 gene serves as the predominant molecular marker for AB identification. However, it is noteworthy that while the blaOXA−51 gene exhibits species specificity for AB, it lacks diagnostic utility for CRAB detection. Consequently, the blaOXA−23 gene has been established as a reliable molecular target for CRAB identification [1, 18]. Based on these molecular distinctions, two distinct primer-probe sets were specifically designed and optimized for the differential detection of AB and CRAB strains. However, this DRPA assay is specifically optimized and validated for the detection of blaOXA−23-mediated carbapenem resistance and does not empirically detect other common mechanisms such as those mediated by blaOXA−24/40 or blaOXA−58 gene.
The timely identification of CRAB is crucial for improving patient outcomes and managing healthcare-associated infections. Traditional diagnostic methods, like culture-based assays and molecular detection, often have long processing times and limited sensitivity, delaying effective interventions. The DRPA is a highly sensitive nucleic acid amplification method, can detect 102 CFU of target DNA quickly. When combined with a lateral flow strip (LFS), it forms a portable, user-friendly platform that provides visual results without specialized equipment. The DRPA-LFS system significantly reduces diagnostic turnaround time, enabling prompt targeted antimicrobial therapy and infection control, which are vital in reducing the severe consequences of CRAB infections, including high mortality rates.
The DRPA-LFS method has rapid processing capabilities, primarily through its visual readout mechanism. This feature eliminates the dependency on sophisticated instrumentation, such as PCR machines or sequencing platforms, thereby enhancing its accessibility in resource-limited laboratory settings. Furthermore, the assay’s operational simplicity substantially reduces the training requirements for laboratory personnel, which consequently minimizes procedural errors and improves reproducibility across diverse operational environments. In contexts where access to advanced diagnostic infrastructure is constrained, particularly in rural healthcare facilities or low-income regions, the capacity for rapid CRAB detection emerges as a critical diagnostic necessity. The DRPA detection method has a good tolerance for the template, and the sample that has been treated at high temperature for ten minutes can be used as a template and has no obvious effect on the test results. But this approach is subject to several limitations. Notably, it entails higher economic costs compared to conventional PCR. Furthermore, improper handling may result in aerosol contamination, potentially producing false-positive outcomes. Additionally, the method does not allow for precise quantification of specific bacterial loads in patients.
Furthermore, this methodology enables precise differentiation of infection types through a single-tube testing procedure, with results being directly interpretable on one test strip. While numerous existing techniques are capable of detecting AB, they exhibit limitations in accurately distinguishing between AB and CRAB, a critical distinction in clinical microbiology and infection control. Its simplicity makes it accessible to healthcare personnel with varying training levels, enhancing its use across diverse clinical settings. Early detection also supports proactive infection control, such as identifying asymptomatic carriers and implementing isolation protocols. By enabling rapid recognition of CRAB colonization or infection, healthcare facilities can apply targeted disinfection and containment measures more effectively, reducing transmission risks and preventing outbreaks.

## Conclusion
The DRPA assay is a reliable, sensitive, and portable method for the detection of Carbapenem-Resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, Its simplicity and accuracy make it a valuable tool for clinical diagnosis, particularly in settings where laboratory infrastructure is limited.

## Figure Fig. 1: Principle of the DRPA assay. (A) Two distinct fluorescent probes were employed in the recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) reaction. (B) The RPA reaction was conducted under optimized conditions to ensure specificity and efficiency. (C) Following amplification, the reaction products were subjected to lateral flow strip (LFS) analysis. The differential labeling of the probes facilitated distinct antibody interactions at the detection lines, enabling precise identification of the target gene. The positions of the control and detection lines are demarcated on the right panel. NTC denotes the no template control, which served as a negative control to validate the absence of non-specific amplification

## Figure Fig. 2: Availability of primers and probes. (A) Different primers and probes were used to detect Acinetobacter baumannii (ATCC 19606) to ensure the feasibility of the DRPA reaction. (B) To confirm applicability, blaOXA−51 F/P/R and blaOXA−23 F/P/R sets were used to detect Acinetobacter baumannii strains isolated from sputum. The control and detection lines are indicated on the right. NTC, no tsemplate control. The image represents the results of three independent experiments
