# Clinical Course and Optical Coherence Tomography of Ocular Angiostrongyliasis: A Case Report

## Abstract
AbstractPurpose:To report the clinical course and optical coherence tomography (OCT) findings of ocular angiostrongyliasis.Methods:A 36-year-old female with a history of ingesting regular raw freshwater shrimp and other raw food presented with acute unilateral painless visual loss in the right eye. Her right eye’s best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 1 ft of the count finger. Fundus examination showed vitritis, generalized retinal pigment epithelial alteration, and a moving roundworm in the vitreous at the 6 o’clock position. Macular OCT of her right eye showed thinning of the retina, loss of the external limiting membrane and ellipsoid zone, subretinal hyper-reflective material clumping, and hyper-reflective foci at the superficial choroidal layer.Results:The patient was administered oral and topical prednisolone. The roundworm, identified as Angiostrongylus cantonensis, was wholly extracted from the vitreous using a 23G sclerotomy port and pars plana vitrectomy. The final BCVA was 1 ft of the count finger.Conclusion:This case report describes an infrequent presentation and illustrates the clinical course and OCT findings of ocular angiostrongyliasis.Keywords: Angiostrongylus cantonensis, Eye parasite, Ocular angiostrongyliasis, Optical coherence tomography

## INTRODUCTION
Human angiostrongyliasis is caused by the neurotropic nematode, Angiostrongylus cantonensis (A. cantonensis). As an incidental host, humans are infected by ingesting food containing its larvae, such as snails, slugs, and freshwater shrimp. After entering the body, these nematodes mainly migrate to the central nervous system (CNS) and cause eosinophilic meningitis. Although rare, some invade the ocular tissue and cause ocular angiostrongyliasis.
Ocular angiostrongyliasis is a very rare manifestation, with a prevalence of 1.2% in humans;1 however, patients often present with severe monocular visual loss, which usually persists despite treatment completion.
This report describes the early manifestations, clinical progression of ocular angiostrongyliasis, and macular optical coherence tomography (OCT) findings. Furthermore, although we offer a choice of management that lacks consensus, this will be helpful for ophthalmologists who encounter this condition in their practice.

## CASE REPORT
A 36-year-old female with a history of ingesting regular raw freshwater shrimp and other raw food had developed acute unilateral painless visual loss in the right eye and a mild headache without any other neurological deficit for 1 week. Her best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 1 ft. in the right eye and 20/32 in the left eye. The intraocular pressure was 27 mmHg in both eyes. A relative afferent pupillary defect was positive in the right eye. No abnormalities were observed in the anterior segment or retina. Enlarged cupping in both eyes were noted. No parasites were detected during the first visit. Macular OCT showed hyper-reflective dots in the vitreous; thinning of the retina in the right eye, macular thickness of 176 μm, loss of the external limiting membrane and ellipsoid zone, some subretinal hyper-reflective material clumping, and some hyper-reflective foci in the superficial choroidal layer of the right eye [Figure 1a]. During her first visit, she was diagnosed with macular degeneration in the right eye and glaucoma in both eyes. A topical antiglaucoma drug was prescribed for both eyes. Three weeks later, her right vision worsened to only detect hand motion and showed mild conjunctival redness and hypopyon. Fundus examination revealed vitritis, generalized retinal pigment epithelial alteration, and a moving roundworm in the vitreous. Macular OCT of the right eye showed loss of the external limiting membrane and ellipsoid zone, more subretinal hyper-reflective material clumping, and more hyper-reflective foci in the superficial choroidal layer [Figure 1b]. No signs or symptoms of meningitis or encephalitis were present; hence, no neurological consultation was conducted. There was no evidence of eosinophilia in total blood count. She was diagnosed with parasitic panuveitis of the right eye. She was treated with 1% prednisolone acetate eye drops every 2 h, combined with 0.2% brimonidine and 0.5% timolol eye drops twice a day, oral acetazolamide, oral prednisolone 25 mg/day, and oral albendazole 400 mg/day. One week later, she underwent pars plana vitrectomy and a 5-mm long nonmoving roundworm was removed wholly via a 23G sclerotomy port using a 23G soft-tip cannula [Video 1]. A parasitologist identified the roundworm as A. cantonensis [Figure 2] on microscopic examination and polymerase chain reaction. Two months after the parasite was removed from the eye, the BCVA was counted at 1 ft. Fundus examination showed generalized retinal pigment epithelial alteration and a subretinal white net at the macula [Figure 3].

## DISCUSSION
The diagnosis of ocular angiostrongyliasis is based on a history of ingesting raw or undercooked snails, slugs, paratenic hosts, or contaminated vegetables.2 Ocular findings include visual loss, lateral rectus palsy, glaucoma, uveitis, disc swelling, disc atrophy, retinal pigment alterations, subretinal tracks, necrotizing retinitis, and exudative retinal detachment.3 A definitive diagnosis of ocular angiostrongyliasis was established when larvae were found in the eye. Altogether, 35 cases of ocular angiostrongyliasis were reported between 1962 and 2011. Larvae were found in 32 of the 35 cases and mainly in the vitreous cavity.1 When the larvae are incidentally ingested, they penetrate the intestinal wall, gain access to the circulatory system, and migrate to the CNS.4 Occasionally, the larva accidentally travels to the eye between the optic nerve and sheath and then enters the eye via the cribriform plate. Subsequently, the larvae may migrate to the subretinal area, vitreous, or anterior chamber.5 A laboratory examination of complete blood count revealing eosinophilia may be helpful but may be absent and not diagnostic of ocular angiostrongyliasis. The immunochromatographic test for rapid serological diagnosis of human angiostrongyliasis was helpful in diagnosing ocular angiostrongyliasis when clinal was suspected and the larva was absent in the eye.6,7
Currently, no consensus exists regarding the treatment for ocular angiostrongyliasis; however, surgical removal of the larva from the eye is the typical treatment. Some ophthalmologists suggest laser to the pigmented gut of the living A. cantonensis to immobilize the larva before surgical removal.3 Other treatment modalities are oral or intravenous steroids that may help reduce intraocular inflammation.8 The use of anthelmintic drugs such as albendazole is controversial. Despite many treatment modalities, visual outcomes do not usually improve.1,3
Diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis (DUSN) is an ocular infection caused by nematodes that results in ocular inflammation. An early stage of DUSN usually presents with visual loss, vitritis, multifocal gray–white lesions in the retina, and optic disc edema. A late stage may show focal or diffuse retinal pigment epithelial and retinal changes, retinal arteriolar narrowing, or optic disc atrophy.9 In cases where the worm is not visible in the eye, OCT findings may help diagnose DUSN. Previous studies have reported that OCT in DUSN may reveal a decrease in the thickness of the retinal nerve fiber layer, focal or diffuse photoreceptor inner segment-outer segment junction disruption, and alterations in foveal contour with a loss of foveal depression.10,11,12
We report an early appearance of ocular angiostrongyliasis that did not show any fundus abnormalities and absence of worm during the initial complete fundoscopic examination performed by a vitreoretina specialist ophthalmologist. Three weeks later, we noticed eye inflammation, hypopyon, vitritis, generalized retinal pigment epithelial alteration, subretinal white net at the macula, and the worm in the vitreous cavity, which firmly diagnosed DUSN and ocular angiostrongyliasis when the worm was identified as A. cantonensis. The findings from macular OCT showed hyper-reflective dots in the vitreous, thinning of the retina, loss of the external limiting membrane and ellipsoid zone, some subretinal hyper-reflective material clumping, and some hyper-reflective foci in the superficial choroidal layer at the first and subsequent visits. These macular OCT findings may be related to pathogenesis, an inflammatory process when the worm is in the eye, and due to its movement in the subretinal space before entering the vitreous. Some OCT findings of DUSN in this case are similar to those reported in previous studies; however, subretinal hyper-reflective material clumping or hyper-reflective foci in the superficial choroidal layer has not been previously reported. This may have resulted from different nematode types and inflammatory reactions.
In conclusion, this report describes ocular angiostrongyliasis with early and late manifestations, including findings from macular OCT and management methods.
