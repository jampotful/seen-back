# Relating differential gene expression and genomic analyses to functional adaptation at various developmental stages of the blue crab, Callinectes sapidus

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundThe blue crab, Callinectes sapidus, exhibits a pelagic, planktonic larval, and benthic post-larval form before molting into a juvenile reminiscent of the adult body plan. This study examined the expression profile and genomic structure for key genes responsible for the adaptive functions involved in the blue crab at different developmental stages. The physiological behavior of each stage reflects unique life history strategies key for the successful recruitment and settlement of a species that supports one of the largest fisheries in the United States.ResultsThe mRNA was sequenced from 5 developmental stages (zoea stage Z3, zoea stage Z5, post-larval stage megalopa, juvenile stage C2/3, and juvenile stage C4/5) from lab-reared animals. The reads were mapped to the current reference genome to determine gene expression patterns. Genes coding for cuticle proteins vary in expression at different life stages and account for a significant portion of the top differentially expressed genes. Opsin genes are significantly upregulated in the megalopa stage, with particularly high expression of opsins sensitive to wavelengths corresponding to blue/green light. The blue-light-sensitive opsin gene family is a set of 7 genes tandemly repeated along the genome with moderate conservation at the amino acid level and large differences in relative expression between gene copies. The 3 highly conserved pseudo-hemocyanin proteins are the most significantly upregulated genes in C4/5-stage juveniles. Phylogeny of decapod pseudo-hemocyanin suggests that duplication of this gene family occurred after species divergence and conservation of introns suggests that duplication within species occurred at the DNA level.ConclusionsThe orthology analysis provides a deeper context for the gene duplication patterns within decapods. The differential gene expression of specific members of the cuticle, opsin, and pseudo-hemocyanin gene families are strongly tied with the changes in habitat, adaptive behavior, and physiology during blue crab metamorphosis and development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12344-9.Keywords: Transcriptome, Genomics, Blue crab larvae, Evolution, Life history strategies, Callinectes sapidus, Zoeae, Megalopa

## Background
The blue crab (Callinectes sapidus) fishery is a key industry in the Chesapeake Bay, with over $38 million in estimated dockside value from commercial landings in 2022 in Maryland alone [1]. To support and manage the fishery, it is important to study blue crab biology to better understand population structure and dynamics across the different life stages from larvae to harvestable adults. Blue crab development is key to understanding the organism as a whole and can be broken down into 4 general stages after hatching: pelagic, planktonic zoeae that metamorphose into benthic, post-larval megalopae, which then undergo a second metamorphosis into juveniles, which molt multiple times with the same body form into sexually mature adults [2].
Most animal species change their body form and behavior throughout their life cycle, and each form has unique properties advantageous to its corresponding life history strategy. Much research about the plasticity of larval development can be attributed to laboratory studies, where early growth and survivability were dependent on temperature, salinity, and diet [3–6]. In the environment, the distribution of blue crab larvae in the Chesapeake Bay by life stage can be attributed to multiple factors, with salinity a critical condition. Zoeae are stenohaline, requiring high salinity. Hence, they are hatched in the lower Bay closer to the open ocean, while juveniles and adults are euryhaline and tolerate a wide salinity range [7]. Behavior changes are another important adaptation throughout the different blue crab life stages. Blue crab zoeae are pelagic swimmers, while at the megalopa stage, the animal transitions and maintains a permanent benthic lifestyle [2]. Understanding these regulating factors regarding growth and larval development has important implications for tracking larval dispersal and settlement and juvenile recruitment [4].
Differential gene expression is an essential developmental process for animals to successfully adapt and respond to their internal and external environments. The metamorphic transitions to a benthic lifestyle and into a larger body size are expected to be reflected in changes to behavior and morphology. Hence, the following unique life stages of the blue crabs reared in the hatchery: two zoeal stages, the megalopa, and two juvenile stages were selected to determine the differential gene expression changes following procession through stages preceding and succeeding both metamorphoses.
Transcriptomic data reveals differential expression patterns for key genes involved in exoskeleton formation, eye development (opsin), and respiration (pseudo-hemocyanin) activity at various life stages. Here, a transcriptome dataset from five distinct blue crab developmental stages (zoeae stage Z3 (Z3), zoeae stage Z5 (Z5), postlarval stage megalopa (M), juvenile stage C2/3 (C2/3), juvenile stage C4/5 (C4/5)) was combined with 7 decapod genomes to better understand these gene families in a genomic, evolutionary, and developmental context. This work provides a baseline for investigations into molecular pathways utilized at various developmental life stages, and a comparative perspective among other representative decapods.

## Methods

## Animal culture
Primiparous C. sapidus adult females were collected in the mid-upper Chesapeake Bay in the fall of 2018–2019, with water temperature and salinity of 16 ± 2 °C and 10 ppt. Females were transported to the Aquaculture Research Center (ARC) [Institute of Marine and Environmental Technology (IMET), Baltimore, MD, US] and screened for CsRV1 infection [8, 9]. About 25 females total, all free of viral infection, were kept as broodstock and were first exposed to winter conditions from November to January at a water temperature from 17 to 19 °C in recirculating artificial seawater (ASW) at a salinity of 30 ppt for proper zoeal survival, with a 16:8 light/dark photoperiod [10]. Then, the animals were brought to summer conditions for spawning by increasing water temperature by 1 °C over two weeks to 21–23 °C with 16:8 light/dark photoperiod [10]. Females were fed a ~ 15 g piece of squid daily.
Females were examined daily for extrusion of eggs. Biopsies of the embryos on days 8–10 after spawning were taken to determine the exact embryo developmental stages [11]. Females carrying embryos at the hatching imminent stage, i.e., 1 to 2 days prior to hatching, were transferred from broodstock tanks to a hatching container holding ~ 50 L of ASW at 30 ppt [10]. The zoeae that were used in this study were produced from May 2019 to September 2020. Upon hatching, the zoeae displayed positive phototaxis by swimming toward a light source (a typical standard lamp with ~ 500 lx) and were collected for the experiments [12, 13]. These zoeae were first temporarily kept in a 1-liter beaker holding ASW at a salinity of 30 ppt with mild aeration for 1–2 h to let zoeae settle.
The experiment was carried out at a constant temperature (21–23 °C). At day 1 each larva was placed individually into a well containing 1 mL of ASW filtered using a 0.22 μm membrane at a salinity of 30 ppt in 24-well plates. The zoeae were reared to megalopae under a 12:12 light/dark photoperiod at room temperature over the course of 35–55 days. Water was exchanged every other day by gently transferring the zoeae to a new well-plate containing membrane-filtered, fresh ASW at 30 ppt. Megalopae were reared for 6–12 days to juvenile crab stage.
Every day between 9 and 11 am, the zoeae were examined for molting under a dissection microscope (magnification 20x) using the criteria as stated [3, 12]. The animals were fed daily around 11 am-12 pm. In brief, zoeal stage 1–5 were fed with ~ 100–500 small rotifers per zoea (Brachionus rotundiformis, https://reedmariculture.com, size 50–150 μm, Reed Mariculture) [12]. Artemia (n = 15/zoea) was introduced with rotifers at zoeal stages 5–6 and fed only to zoeal stage 8 (n = 30–50/zoea), megalopa (n = ~ 100) to crab stages 4–5 (n = 100–300), following the earlier reports [12, 14].

## RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing
Total RNA was isolated using Qiazol from laboratory-reared individuals (n = 6–7 for Z3,Z5,M; n = 4–5 for C2/3,C4/5) from the same family/brood at 5 different developmental stages during the intermolt stage (Table 1). Sequencing of the mRNA was performed using an Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Prep kit followed by sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform [Psomagen, Rockville, MD, US].
Read map statistics from hisat2 for each stage, where alignment equates to an RNA read mapping to the reference genome

## Assembly and annotation
The blue crab reference genome and annotation files from [15] were used as a reference for read mapping and annotation. The genome scaffolds correspond to GenBank accession: JAHFWG000000000.1 and the gene names are drawn from the GFF3 file previously deposited in figshare (10.25387/g3.14210594). Annotation of predicted genes was performed using InterProScan [16].

## Transcriptomics downstream analysis
Transcript reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic [17] and mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 [18]. De novo transcriptome assembly was foregone in place of mapping reads to the annotated genes within the reference genome, where the number of reads mapped to the gene regions was used to calculate transcript abundance and expression. Gene abundance was calculated using StringTie [19], and the transcript per million (TPM) values were converted into a gene expression count matrix from the 5 developmental stages for analysis using edgeR [20].

## Gene family analysis
For the gene families described in detail below, errors in the predicted intron-exon splice site boundaries for each gene were manually corrected from the original genome annotation. The complete coding sequence of each gene is available in GenBank with accessions listed in Supplemental Table 3. Gene arrangements on each scaffold were created using genomeGTFtools GitHub package [21]. Percent amino acid identity was calculated using NCBI blast. For duplication and gene family analysis we ran OrthoFinder [22] using the blue crab predicted proteins annotated by [15] and 7 decapod species with predicted protein sequence data downloaded from the NCBI genome database (Supp. Table 1). Phylogenetic analysis used extracted sequences from OrthoFinder to create a maximum-likelihood tree over 1000 iterations with the iqtree2 program [23]. Trees were visualized using FigTree (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) and bootstrap values greater than 70 were included in the final figures.

## Results

## Sequencing and read mapping
Across all five developmental stages, a cumulative 63,757,449 read pairs were sequenced, with an overall alignment rate ranging between 66 and 94% (Table 1). In the megalopa and both juvenile stages, most mapped reads aligned once to the current reference genome; however, in the third zoeal stage, more reads mapped to multiple regions of the genome and in the zoea 5 dataset, single alignments were almost equal to multiple alignments. Like many crustacean genomes, this may be because the blue crab genome is filled with highly repetitive regions (~ 45% of sequence) and has unresolved gaps [15]. Furthermore, while the alignment rate for juvenile stage C4/5 reads is lower than expected, the almost doubling of the total read pairs means that roughly the same amount of read pairs were mapped to the genome for each developmental stage. Potentially, there is an excess of contaminant reads in the juvenile C4/5 stage, artificially deflating the overall alignment rate, or potentially, those reads are transcribed from regions of the genome that currently reside in a gap of the assembly. Abundance values represented as TPM for each gene can be found in Supp. Table 2.
For analysis of the development time series from zoea to juveniles, the 5 different stages could be arranged into four pairs, from (1) Zoea 3 to Zoea 5, (2) Zoea 5 to Megalopa, (3) Megalopa to Juvenile C2/3, and (4) Juvenile C2/3 to C4/5, with each pair representing consecutive stages in development. Analysis in EdgeR was normalized for different numbers of reads mapping between experiments as well as expression values, accounting for false discovery as well as normalized abundance of expression as TPM. Thus, positive log-fold changes reflect increased gene expression from one stage to the next and negative changes reflect reduced expression from one stage to the next. Of the 25,250 predicted genes in the predicted protein-coding repertoire of the blue crab genome, 14,929 are differentially expressed throughout the time series (59.1%). After adjusting the p-value using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, the pairwise comparisons ranged from 644 to 4,054 genes that were significantly and differentially expressed, which represents 2.6 to 16.1% of the blue crab protein-coding gene repertoire.
For each time series comparison, the top 100 most significantly differentially expressed genes were examined. On average, these top-expressed genes had a 71.8% annotation rate, and between 61 and 90 genes had a known, conserved domain as identified through InterProScan. Thus, 10–39% of the top differentially expressed genes across development are currently unannotated and unable to be analyzed for further context. Excluding unannotated genes, three gene families with high differential expression and high overall abundance in expression were chosen for deeper analysis.

## Expression analysis of transcripts coding for cuticle proteins
Genes coding for cuticle proteins were identified based on a chitin-binding domain (IPR000618) from InterProScan and are overwhelmingly represented in the differential expression data for all blue crab stages. The blue crab genome holds 188 copies of genes coding for cuticle proteins, 165 of which are expressed, out of 25,249 total protein-coding genes. These genes are spread across 37 scaffolds and the majority (54.55%) range between 100 and 199 amino acids long with an average length of approximately 233 amino acids. Orthology analysis groups the cuticular genes into 61 OrthoGroups (OG).
Cuticular genes represent 0.7% of the blue crab protein-coding gene repertoire yet represent 18–40% of the top 50 differentially expressed genes when compared pairwise in consecutive developmental stages from early to late (Fig. 1). The expression patterning highlights strong positive log-fold changes when transitioning towards zoeal stage Z5 as well as juvenile stage C2/3 and strong negative log-fold changes when transitioning away from these two stages (Fig. 2).

## Diversity of MW opsins within the blue crab and across decapods
The opsin genes are an expansive gene family in decapods. In blue crab, this family was readily annotated by InterProScan and confirmed by alignments and phylogeny with other decapods. As determined by the genome, C. sapidus has 11 medium-wavelength (MW) and 2 ultra-violet (UV) opsin gene loci, which would produce at minimum 11 distinct MW opsin proteins. Based on GenBank predicted proteins of the following decapod species, which may include isoforms derived from one locus, Portunus trituberculatus has 11, Eriocheir sinensis has 5, and Scylla paramamosain has 16 reference proteins that fall into the MW opsin clades. (Fig. 3). In the related decapods, Homarus americanus has 5, while the three Penaeus shrimp have 6–14. This count is based on existing information in GenBank and thus dependent on the reliability of each species’ genome assembly and protein annotation.
Phylogeny of decapod opsins. The inset in the upper left shows the overall organismal relationships and provides a legend for each genomic dataset
The sequences were assigned to three OrthoGroups by OrthoFinder, formed three main clades in phylogeny, and recapitulated organismal relationships within the decapods four times. Two distinct OrthoGroups account for the predicted MW blue light-sensitive opsins and are labeled the MW_a and MW_b clades in the tree based on >80% amino acid identity BLAST comparisons to a recent decapod opsin classification [24]. The third OrthoGroup contains the UV opsins (Fig. 3). The UV opsins were not heavily duplicated within swimming crabs, and the blue crab had two genes in distinct clades.
There were three blue crab sequences in individual clades within the broader MW_a clade, one with no apparent closely related sequence from P. trituberculatus and the remaining two flanked by duplicated and relatively closely related P. trituberculatus sequences. In this clade, the three blue crab sequences were tandemly arranged on a single scaffold, and this arrangement was also found for the homologous P. trituberculatus and S. paramamosain proteins. The E. sinensis genome did not have any predicted genes in this clade.
The MW_b clade can be divided into two clades – one, the MW_b2 clade, contained single representatives from the four Brachyuran species P. trituberculatus, E. sinensis, S. paramamosain, and C. sapidus, but with duplication within Penaeus. In contrast, the second clade MW_b1 (highlighted in yellow) was not duplicated in Penaeus, but was duplicated in the Brachyurans, especially in the swimming crabs and included 7 gene product loci labeled OpnMW_b1c1 through OpnMW_b1c7 in C. sapidus, forming what will be called the OpnMW_b1 gene family in the remaining text. A single scaffold (CM035189.1, scaffold_15) contained the 7 consecutively arranged OpnMW_b1 copies from 15.78 to 15.85 Mb (Fig. 4a).

The blue crab OpnMW_b1 gene family members range in size from 375 to 377 amino acids, share 67.6 to 97.1% identity at the amino acid level, and have 6 or 8 introns (Suppl. Table 3). Three consecutive copies, OpnMW_b1c3, OpnMW_b1c4, and OpnMW_b1c5, were remarkably conserved at the protein level, being 91.7–97.1% identical.

## Expression analysis of transcripts coding for opsins
The overall transcript abundance for the opsin OrthoGroups was strongly biased for the OpnMW_b1 gene family. The opsin genes within other clades demonstrated relatively lower expression. In the megalopa stage, 4 of the 7 genes in the OpnMW_b1 gene family were dominantly expressed in terms of abundance, rank, and differential expression compared to the zoeal stage. Genes OpnMW_b1c1, OpnMW_b1c2, OpnMW_b1c5 and OpnMW_b1c7 had the highest estimated abundance in the megalopa (Fig. 4b), all with TPMs in the thousands. The OpnMW_b1c5 gene was the highest expressed gene at the megalopa stage based on the TPM of all genes in the blue crab repertoire. Across these 7 genes, the abundance values ranged from 83.0 to 4,716.0 TPM in the megalopa, reflecting highly variable and high expression for copies OpnMW_b1c1, OpnMW_b1c2, OpnMW_b1c5, and OpnMW_b1c7, and lower expression for copies OpnMW_b1c3, OpnMW_b1c4, and OpnMW_b1c6.
Proceeding the megalopal transitional stage, there was a pronounced shift during the juvenile C4/5 stage to favor a more relatively abundant expression of OpnMW_b1c1 through OpnMW_b1c4 and lower TPM values were seen for OpnMW_b1c5 through OpnMW_b1c7. Interestingly, although OpnMW_b1c5 was > 90% similar at the amino acid level to OpnMW_b1c3 and OpnMW_b1c4, implying structural conservation of function, they were markedly differentially expressed in megalopae.
Overall, this gene family on a single chromosome was strongly upregulated in the megalopa stage, with notable differential expression during the transition to and from the megalopa stage. In comparison to the abundance estimates, OpnMW_b1c3 and OpnMW_b1c4 were the most strongly differentially expressed between stages. The log-fold change values reached up to 5.7 for gene copy OpnMW_b1c4 when transitioning to the megalopa stage (Fig. 5, left panel). During the transition from the megalopa to juvenile C2/3, the overall log-fold change ranged from − 9.3 to 0.2 and genes OpnMW_b1c5, OpnMW_b1c6, and OpnMW_b1c7 had the top absolute log-fold change values (Fig. 5, right panel).

Gene expression abundance values (TPM) and log-fold change values for this gene family can be found in Supp. Table 3. As a control for possible read mapping artifacts, where short reads could map equally well to different members of the gene family, reads were mapped to a reference containing the spliced nucleotide coding sequences (CDS) for all 7 genes in the OpnMW_b1 gene family. In conclusion, out of the 11 Blue crab opsins divided into 3 clades, one clade was more abundantly expressed, and within that clade, three genes were the most dominant in the megalopa stage.

## Diversification of the hemocyanin family within decapods
The OrthoFinder analysis coupled with InterProScan annotation and BLAST annotated two decapod hemocyanin (Hc) clades (Fig. 6) with one contributing most of the diversity within swimming crabs and a second clade reflecting a greater diversity and duplication outside of the swimming crabs. The Portunidae, or swimming crabs, branch into three groups (Fig. 6). Branch 1 contains 2 predicted proteins for C. sapidus, 2 for P. trituberculatus, and 8 for S. paramamosain. Branch 2 contains 1 predicted protein for each C. sapidus, P. trituberculatus, and S. paramamosain. Branch 3 contains 4 predicted proteins for C. sapidus, 3 for S. paramamosain, and none for P. trituberculatus. The Varunidae clade contains 12 annotated predicted proteins in a single stretch from 5.38 to 5.46 MB of chromosome 70 (NC_066578) for E. sinensis which forms a single clade as an outgroup to the swimming crabs.
Phylogeny of hemocyanin and pseudo-hemocyanin in decapods. The inset in the top upper left shows the overall organismal relationships and provides a legend for each genomic dataset. The sequence logo inset in the lower upper left shows the frequency and information content of six key histidine residue sites for identifying hemocyanin
In summary, there are at least three copies of an ancestral hemocyanin-like gene within the Portunid family. Six key histidine residues are essential for copper binding for oxygen transport in genuine hemocyanin polypeptides [25]. The lack of all six key histidine residues within the C. sapidus sequences within branches 1,2, and 3 thus classify them as pseudo-hemocyanin. One C. sapidus pseudo-hemocyanin (PHc) tandem set belongs to branches 1 and 2 of one clade, while a second C. sapidus pseudo-hemocyanin tandem set belongs to branch 3 of the same clade. The shrimp and lobster pseudo-hemocyanin are contained within a separate lineage.

## Expression of pseudo-hemocyanin in blue crab stage C4/5 juveniles
The top three most significantly differentially expressed genes in the late-stage juveniles are pseudo-hemocyanin belonging to one clade (Fig. 6). Classification of blue crab genes labeled in the hemocyanin family is based on high blast identities and uses the nomenclature from [26]. While the two proteins are closely related, pseudo-hemocyanin differs from hemocyanin in the lack of histidine residues, which are linked with the ability to coordinate copper ions and bind oxygen [27]. Once again, this set of 3 pseudo-hemocyanin genes are arranged in series along a single scaffold (CM035205.1, scaffold_31) from 22.72 to 22.74 Mb. These genes will be referred to as the PHc_c1-3 gene family. Members of this gene family are 676 to 677 amino acids long and have 2 introns, and PHc_c1 has an approximately 1.4 kb long untranslated region at the 3’ end contributing to the 3’ exon length (Fig. 7). The different copies share 93.8 to 99.7% identity at the amino acid level and 95.3 to 99.1% identity at the CDS level (Suppl. Table 3). Gene copies PHc_c4-7 are found on a separate scaffold as a second pseudo-hemocyanin tandem gene set, and are approximately 80X less abundantly expressed (average gene abundance for PHc_c4-7 = 46.5 ± 29.4 TPM, compared to average gene abundance for PHc_c1-3 = 3,724.4 ± 1,626.9 TPM). Therefore, the Phc_c1-3 tandem gene set was chosen for further analyses based on high expression.

## Discussion
The present study examines and catalogs differential gene expression accompanying the physiological changes associated with distinctive zoea, megalopa, and juvenile life stages of C. sapidus. Drastic body changes are characteristic of arthropods throughout their life cycle. The two metamorphoses in blue crab body shape and lifestyle, as they develop from zoea to juveniles, are linked with transcript abundance and changes in gene expression. During these life-stage transitions, the animals change their behavior and ecological niche from pelagic to benthic by adapting to different internal and environmental conditions, including body form and light penetration through the water. This study reveals stage-specific differential expression on three gene families implicated in the physiological and behavioral traits linked to this transition, and the genomic data provides relevant duplication and evolutionary context across the decapod lineage.
The ability to conduct developmental studies with lab-reared crabs [12] has a clear advantage of timing and staging larval development and offers the first opportunity to study differential expression between sibling lines. Currently, transcriptomic analysis of genes that show the highest levels of differential expression between life stages remain enigmatic because the genes lack conserved domains or other features to drive functional annotation. However, with the advent of high-contiguity chromosome-scale genomes from multiple decapods, there is an opportunity to clearly describe differential gene expression in the context of gene families where these families can be accurately tabulated and gene duplication compared between species. Genome-guided transcriptomics also helps to confirm the accuracy of high-throughput gene prediction methods.

## Cuticle protein genes with chitin-binding domains make up a large fraction of differentially expressed genes during development at post-hatching
The rigid exoskeleton covers the entire arthropod body, providing protection from predation and disease and supporting internal structures [28, 29]. However, the cuticular structures differ across body parts, likely corresponding to a variety of different cuticle proteins with different structural properties [29]. Previous studies demonstrated highly selective expression of specific cuticle gene families in the C. sapidus female ovigerous hairs, where the eggs are attached [30].
With pooled data from many individuals, the data reflects relatively high amounts of RNA for analysis at the loss of individual-specific information. In this study, whole-body samples were used for sequencing. Hence, the specific function of each cuticle protein cannot be localized with specific body parts and remains to be characterized. Regardless, individual genes in this diverse group are strongly differentially expressed during blue crab development.
Cuticle proteins are readily identified based on the annotation of a conserved chitin-binding domain. A significant portion of arthropod cuticle protein sequences contain the R&R consensus domain, which has been demonstrated to be a chitin-binding domain and is readily found during domain-based annotation [31]. Despite sharing a conserved domain, this gene group demonstrates enough diversity for the 165 genes expressed in this study to fall into 61 different gene families based on OrthoFinder analysis. Of the differentially expressed genes during development, cuticle proteins play an oversized role. This agrees with other crustacean studies, where more than 30 cuticle proteins were differentially expressed between the zoeal stage Z5 and megalopae of the mitten crab, E. sinensis [32], 96 cuticle-coding transcripts were differentially expressed during the metamorphosis of the spiny lobster Sagmariasus verreauxi [33], and 188 diverse cuticle transcripts were significantly differentially expressed in Peneaus vannamei transitioning to the postlarval stage [34]. The expansive duplication of genes involved in development is not a novel mechanism, as duplicated genes often show increased gene expression during metamorphosis in the model fly genus Drosophila [35]. Patterning in blue crab larval development indicates strong upregulation in the zoeal Z5 and juvenile C2/3 stage, indicating that life stages near the metamorphosis transition period require a unique suite of cuticle proteins.

## Significance of megalopa expression of blue-green MW opsins
Larvae exhibit phototactic behavior [36, 37], and it has been suggested that larvae use visual cues for predation avoidance [38]. Light is only one of many stimuli, and the juvenile response to visual cues is also affected by various chemical cues [39]. Opsins can also have an array of non-visual functions [40]. There is conflicting evidence on how much the environment impacts opsin expression for different crustaceans [24]. However, with a switch from a pelagic to a benthic lifestyle, the visual system is expected to change to adapt to various environments and foraging strategies. Evidence of this in other crustaceans includes the upregulation of visual-sensing genes in E. sinensis megalopae, where light-sensing is hypothesized to develop alongside the formation of the compound eye [32], and the differential expression of three rhodopsins in S. verreauxi associated with the metamorphic transition from diurnal to nocturnal behavior [33].
Differential expression of opsins seems intimately associated with an ecological niche required by various Callinectes life stages. Medium-wavelength opsin genes were particularly highlighted as highly expressed in our data during C. sapidus development. Opsins tuned to a 400–500 nm wavelength are classified as middle-wavelength (MW) sensitive opsins [41]. Our current data on zoeal opsins is the first report on the larval opsin in decapod crustaceans. Megalopa and adult stages, nocturnal and benthic animals, utilize opsins tuned to a spectral maximum of 504 nm, which is blue-green light sensitive [42], possibly allowing them to be sensitive to light that penetrates the water. The genes within OpnMW_b1 are most likely blue-green light-sensitive opsins based on strong (80% or better) amino acid identity to previously classified genes [24]. However, it is important to note that BLAST and previous studies only suggest that this family is tuned to blue-green light, a result which functional studies would be needed to confirm [41].
Previous work suggests an ancestral pancrustacean likely had two MW opsins, one long-wavelength (LW) and one short-wavelength (SW) opsin [43]. One major theory is that opsin phylogeny in crustaceans corresponds to spectral sensitivity, matching specific opsin clades with specific wavelength ranges [44]. Based on the phylogeny presented here, two MW opsins were present prior to the divergence of the brachyuran crab lineage from other decapods, with many duplication events after brachyuran divergence and many instances of species-specific duplication events, agreeing with previous studies that determined two ancestral MW opsins, with a variety of duplication throughout evolutionary history [43]. Furthermore, the proteins extracted from GenBank for representative decapod species may contain isoforms, another mechanism for increasing gene product diversity.
Residues on opsins that bind to the chromophore are responsible for altering wavelength sensitivity for spectral tuning [24]. It is canonically difficult to tie opsin gene structure and expression to phenotypic response [45, 46]. There is moderate conservation at the amino acid level for the OpnMW_b1 gene family, and these amino acid differences may affect the retinal binding site, a possible cause for spectral tuning, which may explain the differential expression within this family. Alternatively, regulatory elements in the non-coding regions may exert control over the differential expression during different developmental stages not directly linked to light absorption [47]. Thus, the evolutionary advantage of this duplication and differential expression in blue crab larvae remains theoretical.

## Significance of late-stage juveniles expressing pseudo-hemocyanin
The hemocyanin protein family includes hemocyanin, prophenoloxidase, and pseudo-hemocyanin (which are alternatively named cryptocyanin) [26]. Hemocyanin is the oxygen transporter in crustacean hemolymph [26]. Exposure to hypoxia can cause hemocyanin levels to rise in crustaceans, as shown by experiments including species such as the brown shrimp, Crangon crangon [48], and the Dungeness crab Cancer magister [49]. Pseudo-hemocyanin lack key histidines present in hemocyanin, and are thought to function as possible storage proteins [50]. Pseudo-hemocyanin expression in C. magister closely follows the molting stage [27]. In the brachyuran crab P. trituberculatus, expression was upregulated in response to infection, reflecting a role in immune response [51]. In C. sapidus pseudo-hemocyanin expression dramatically increases between the C2/3 and C4/5 life stages, particularly for PHc_c1-3, suggesting a role during development in this species.
Pseudo-hemocyanin gene family evolution closely follows the broader taxonomy, and the blue crab PHc_c1-3 gene family is a set of three highly conserved genes derived from an ancestral Portunid PHc. Despite increased expression of this tandem gene set in later developmental stages, there is no significant selection for transcription between the three genes, which are nearly identical at the amino acid level. Cases of tandemly duplicated genes in Drosophila show disproportionally higher expression than copy number alone [52]. Thus, the current evidence does not suggest a novel function but perhaps a dosing effect to enhance expression.
In general, the adaptive physiology of decapod crustaceans, including C. sapidus, to stressful environmental conditions like hypoxia, anoxia, and temperatures has been well established [53–56]. If the expression of pseudo-hemocyanin is tied to genuine hemocyanin function, the upregulation of PHc in juvenile blue crabs may equip the animals to adapt to various oxygen levels in the benthos and could be advantageous for maintaining their metabolic capacity to transport oxygen [48, 49]. On the other hand, upregulation may be related to the open circulatory system of all invertebrates. Oxygen transport of hemocyanin to cells and tissues depends on diffusion distance. Hence, small zoeae present a larger surface area to body volume ratio than juveniles and adults and can get sufficient oxygen by diffusion [57]. Juveniles are larger and may rely on increased hemocyanin levels to assist with oxygen transport while also possibly enhancing immunity. Pseudo-hemocyanin seems to be involved in many different processes, but their exact role in each process is still unknown, so there remain many possible reasons for the strong upregulation. The expression and duplication data alone provide circumstantial evidence, which warrants future functional studies to examine the specific roles of such highly expressed hemocyanin and pseudo-hemocyanin gene sets during development.

## Conclusion
The Z5, Z3, M, C2/3 and C4/5 stages were chosen as early life stages post-hatching that are set around the two metamorphoses in C. sapidus. The three gene families described here were all markedly differentially and abundantly expressed. The opsin and pseudo-hemocyanin gene families were followed up with genomic and orthology analyses because they exist in tandem along one scaffold, allowing easy comparison of gene evolution between decapods. The orthology showed expanded species-level duplication within the swimming crabs, where duplication allows for subfunctionalization. Combined with selective transcription of specific genes, blue crab larvae have the genetic machinery to experience phenotypic flexibility during metamorphic transitions.
