Summary. The fine structure of the compound eye of adult specimens of
Squilla mantis was investigated. The eye consists of about 3600-3700 ommatidia, each containing a dioptric apparatus formed by a lamellated corneal
lens and a eucone-type crystalline cone. Each of the four cone cells give rise
to a cylindrical process (crystalline thread) inserted between the retinula cells
and extending down to the basement membrane. Two distal pigment cells
completely encompass the distal part of the crystalline cone, becoming progressively smaller and forming roundish processes. At the level of the tip of the
crystalline cone they split off into small pigment-containing processes, and a
central process leads down to the basement membrane. About 12-16 proximal
pigment cells surround the ommatidium and extend from the tip of the crystalline cone to the basement membrane. In addition to the two types of pigment
cell, three other types of pigment-containing cells were identified, one of which
possibly contains - on the basis of their ultrastructure - crystals of the respiratory pigment hemocyanin. The two other pigments are found respectively on
the surface of the retina (green pigment) and beneath the surface and in the
intraommatidial space (white pigment). The distal part of each ommatidium
consists of retinula cells of about equal size. Further proximal an 8th small
retinula cell is encountered. The fused, centrally located rhabdom, is built up
of the microvilli (rhabdomeres) of the 7 large retinula cells, the 8th has no
microvilli.
The structure of the ommatidia was also examined in relation to light-dark
adapted conditions (LA-DA). In DA the crystalline cone shortens and the
rhabdom becomes longer by an approximately corresponding amount and

I. Introduction

The Squilla mantis lives in the Mediterranean at a depth of 60-100 m (Schaller,
1953 and own observations) and may also be found at depths of less than 20 m in
sandy regions. The normal lighting condition of the biotope is dim light. Previous
behavioural physiological studies mentioned that captive Squilla feed exclusively
in darkness, or, after amputation of the eyes (Demoll, 1909; Giessbrecht, 1910;
Schaller, 1953). This statement has been refuted by Privat (1975), and Squilla
possesses a highly developed visual sense (Demoll, 1909). Electrophysiological
experiments (Schiff, 1963; Schiff and Sch6nenberger, 1971) lead to the same
conclusion.
The general ultrastructure of the ommatidium of the Squilla eye is not well
known, although there have been studies of various parts of it (Schiffand Gervasio,
1969; Perrelet et al., 1971). Light-microscopical descriptions were given by Radl
(1900), Hanstr6m (1931) and Schiff (1963).
Our studies on the visual capacity of the lateral eye and the ultrastructure of
the lamina ganglionaris of Squilla raised a number of questions which called for
details of the ultrastructure of individual ommatidia. The number and arrangement of the retinula cells, the orientation of rhabdomeric microvilli, and the
distribution of intracellular vacuoles and pigment granules, are of crucial importance in determining the efficiency of the compound eye (Eguchi and Waterman, 1966; Horridge, 1968; Kirschfeld and Franceschini, 1969; Menzel and Lange,
1971).
The present study describes the ultrastructure of the compound eye of Squilla
in light and dark adapted conditions. There are several differences between our
results and those of Schiff (1963, 1974), and Schiff and Gervasio (1969).

2. The Dioptric Apparatus
The dioptric apparatus of the Squilla eye is composed of the lens-shaped cuticular
corneal facet and the crystalline cone. Viewed from the surface (Fig. 1) each facet
appears as an hexagonally shaped convex lens. The diameter of the facets ranges
between 140 g (middle eye region) and 96 g (at the poles) and its radius of curvature
is 110 - 10 g. When examined in the scanning electron microscope, the surface of
the corneal lens appears smooth and shows a slight depression in the central area
which may be observed in both fresh and fixed eyes and the significance of which
is not yet known (Fig. 1 c). The inner surface of the cornea is also convex (with
about the same radius of curvature as the outer surface) and is separated from the
base of the crystalline cone by 2 corneagenous cells (Fig. 3 c). Only a small area of
the base of the crystalline cone is in intimate contact with the inner face of the
cornea. In toluidine blue stained semi-thin sections, the cornea appears to be composed of about 50 alternately dark and light bands (Fig. 3 b). When examined in
the electron microscope, the cornea has the characteristic appearance of cuticular
chitin (Locke, 1964), like that in insects, which is composed of alternately dark
and clear lamellae formed by curved filaments (Fig. 5 c). The cornea is formed by
two cube shaped corneagenous cells which surround the distal part of the crystalline cone, and which are comparable to normal epidermal cells (Waterman, 1961).
Fine protrusions reach from the cornea into the corneagenous cells (Fig. 4a). The
cytoplasm of the corneagenous cells is rich in organelles, including profiles of
rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum, free ribosomes, Golgi systems, masses
of coated, dense-cored vesicles, and mitochondria (Figs. 4 a, 4b, 5 b).
The second part of the dioptric apparatus, a crystalline cone of the eucone-type,
links the cornea with the rhabdomeric microvilli of the retinula cells. The tip of the
cone is in contact with the rhabdom as is characteristic of apposition eyes (Figs.
3c, 4c, 5 a). The crystalline cone has a diameter of 78-85 la at its base and tapers
progressively to the diameter of the rhabdom. Its length depends on the adaptation
state; in the light adapted state it varies from 360 la (middle eye region) to 220 la
(at the poles). Each of the four cone cells give rise to a long cylindrical process
(crystalline thread) inserted between pairs of retinula cells and extending down to
the basment membrane (Figs. 4 c, 5 a, 13). The crystalline cone is a product of the
four so-called Semper cells. In semi-thin sections under the light microscope, they
show differential affinity for toluidine and methylene blue. Correspondingly, in
the electron microscope, their cytoplasms have different electron densities. These

3. The Retinula Cells
The sensory portion of the ommatidium, the retinula, consists of 7 large retinula
cells in the distal part (Figs. 3 a, 15) and an additional, smaller, eighth cell in the
proximal part (Figs. 11, 13). This eighth, or basal retinula cell, measuring 2.5 kt in
diameter, begins at the proximal third of the rhabdom at the periphery of the
ommatidium and then gradually approaches the rhabdom. Its axon contains
microtubules and can be easily distinguished from the axons of the large retinula
cells at the level of the lamina ganglionaris because of its smaller diameter (Fig. 12).
The large retinula cells extend over the entire length of the retinula with their
distal end engulfing the tip of the crystalline cone. Their length, measured from
the basement membrane to their distal end, varies between 380~00 g in the middle
eye region and between 195-235 g at the poles. Their diameter in the distal cylindrical part is 14 kt. The seven retinula cells contribute their rhabdomeres about
equally to the fused rhabdom; the basal retinula cell does not contribute to the
rhabdom. The microvilli provided by the retinula cells are arranged in alternating
layers with their longitudinal axes regularly aligned in a given direction and perpendicular to those of the adjacent layer (Fig. 16). From the seven retinula cells
comprising each retinula, three contribute to one set of layers and four contribute
to the other in a manner characteristic of many Crustacea (Eguchi and Waterman,

The entire rhabdom is surrounded by an electron transparent space which is
about 0.5 la wide. This space (Schaltzone) is traversed at various locations by
protoplasmic bridges (Eguchi and Waterman, 1967) which connect a thin layer
of cytoplasm over the rhabdom (0.3 g in diameter) that we will call the perirhabdomal coat, with the rest of the retinula cell (Fig. 6). The spaces so-enclosed
are similar to the perirhabdomal vacuoles or palisades found in other arthropods
(Horridge and Barnard, 1965). However, a striking, feature in Squilla eyes is their
rather constant size and shape.
In the large retinula cells, all nuclei (which are 10 IX in diameter) lie in one
plane, approximately 100 g below the distal extremity of the cell. The usual array
of organelles is found within each retinula cell. Numerous cisternae of granular
endoplasmic reticulum, free ribosomes, glycogen particles, Golgi cisternae and
mitochondria are scatered throughout the cytoplasm. Golgi systems of 3 or 5
cisternae are common, often elaborating coated vesicles about 600-800 A in
diameter that accumulate into compact masses (Figs. 11, 16). In addition to these
structures, the retinula cells contain a large number of accessory (screening)
pigment granules dispersed throughout the cytoplasm, but localized for the most
part in the area around the rhabdom. The basal retinula cell does not contain
these organetles. The retinula cell axons above the basement membrane and for at
least another 50 ~a below it, also contain randomly distributed pigment granules
(Fig. 10). Most of the granules are small, averaging 0.2 g in diameter. Some granules, however, are larger, up to 0.8 tx in diameter. Similar pigment granules have
been described in the Limulus retinula cells by Fahrenbach (1969), who interpreted
the dark content, because of its solubility characteristics, as ommochrome. Also
present are a number of different inclusions, lipid droplets of various sizes found
particularely around and above the nucleus, and a variety of other cytoplasmic
bodies, which are dispersed over the whole cell. Perrelet et al. (1971) have shown
that some of the latter are involved in the process of granulolysis or crinophagy
(De Duve and Wattiaux, 1966). Coated vesicles at the base of the rhabdomeres,
some of them connected to the receptor membrane, are common (Fig. 4c). Their
possible function in the secretion of substances into the rhabdom is discussed by
Fahrenbach (1969).

The "green" pigment cells cover the distal surface of the retina and appear in
the living eye as a reflecting green layer (Fig. 3c). Under the electron microscope,
these pigment cells show a series of empty spaces, the content of which were
extracted during the preparation or shattered during sectioning (Fig. 7 b). These
spaces presumably contain reflecting platelets. They are similar in shape and size
to the iridiosomes of the iris of the Octopus (Fr6sch, 1973). The probable function
of this pigment layer is to reflect light rays, arriving at an angle from the ommatidia,
but it is also possible that they are only related to the superficial colour scheme
of the animal.
The "white" pigment cells occur immediately under the green layer, and in the
intercellular space above and below the basement membrane. The pigment of
these cells appears white in the living eye, and their cytoplasm contains numerous
roundish to oval vesicles of a diameter of 0.%0.9 la (Fig. 7 a). The content of these
organelles (white pigment) is also lost during preparation or does not take the
stain. The nuclei of these cells are at least partly situated in the retina near the
basement membrane. There are numerous microtubules in the cell and the vesicles
accumulate close to the microtubules. Similar structures were found in the chromatophores of Palaemonetes vulgaris by Robison and Charlton (1973). In

results indicate that the white pigments originate in the type of cell described here,
which has no connection with the black pigment cells.

5. Dark-Light-Adaptation
There are a number of structural differences between dark adapted (DA) and
light adapted (LA) ommatidia. After five minutes of darkness (shorter periods
were not examined), the following adaptive changes were noted in longitudinal
sections: the crystalline cone is significantly shortened and the diameter of its
distal portion is slightly increased; the retinula cells and the rhabdom are lengthened distally, and the outermost border of the distal pigment cells, which are filled
with pigment granules is displaced in the direction of the cornea. The distal twothirds of the crystalline cone remains free of a pigment covering as is found in the
LA eye. DA times of longer duration augment the photomechanic changes
described above, and they seem to stabilize at between 2 and 4 hours of DA time.
Still longer DA times (up to 72 hours) do not cause any further measurable changes.

IV. Discussion

The general organization of the double compound eye and its constituent ommatidia is similar to that of some decapod crustaceans (Parker, 1895; Eguchi,
1965; Rutherford and Horridge, 1965; Eguchi and Waterman, 1966). Double
eyes are found in various crustaceans, for example, in carnivorous Cladoceraca,
in some bathypelagic Amphipoda, in Mysidacea, Euphausiacea and in deep water
sergestidae Decapoda. On the basis of their ommatidial structure, the double
eyes of various Mysidacea and Euphausiacea are considered to be superposition
eyes. It has been supposed (Chun, 1896) that such double eyes are the result of a
pelagic adaptation for the capture of prey in dim light. Characteristically this
type of eye has as wide range of possible ommatidial angles, differences in the
corneal curvature over the eye, and ommatidia of different lengths, features, which
are all found in the Squilla eye. The physiological significance of these gradual
changes in the ommatidial patern from one eye region to the other has been discussed by various authors (for a review see Waterman, 1961).
The retinula of the Squilla eye consists of seven large retinula cells in the distal
part and an additional eigth in the proximal part. Because of its small diameter,
this basal retinula cell may have been overlooked in previous studies. Thus, Schiff
and Gervasio (1969) and Perrelet et al., (1971) only mention seven cells, whereas
the presence of an eighth cell is mentioned by Waterman (1961) in the Anthozoea,
and by Bullock and Horridge (1965) in Stomatopoda in general.
Small or basal retinula cells have been described in several insect species, for
example, Drosophilia (Waddington and Perry, 1961), Locusta (Horridge, 1966),
Lucilia (Melamed and Trujillo-Cen6z, 1968), and the drone of Apis mellifica
(Perrelet and Baumann, 1969 b). In the latter, the receptors possess a rhabdomere
which, according to the species, can assume different positions in the ommatidium.
The basal cell in the Squilla ommatidium, on the other hand, shows neither
rhabdomere nor screening pigment granules in its cytoplasm. Similar, non-typical
photoreceptors (Trujillo-Cenrz, 1972), which do not produce rhabdomeres are
also found in the dragonfly Aeschna (Eguchi, 1971). However, in this case we do
not know whether the screening pigment granules are also missing. It is not known
whether these basal retinula cells perform a function different to that of the large

cells. Keller (1975) found a thin fibre in Golgi preparations which starts at the
periphery of the retinula, crosses the zone of the optic cartridge without branching,
and terminates in the medulla externa. There is little doubt that these "direct nerve
fibres" are the processes of our basal cells. In the fly Lucilia the two small cells
(called central cells) have axons which do not synapse in the lamina, unlike those
of the large retinula cells. Because of their orthogonal rhabdomeres, they have
been thought to be involved in polarized light perception (Melamed and TrujilloCen6z, 1968).
The vacuole system underlying the rhabdom has been named Schaltzone
(Hesse, 1901); palisade (Horridge'and Barnard, 1965); perirhabdomal vacuoles
(Eguchi and Waterman, 1966; 1967); subrhabdomeric cisternae (Lasansky, 1967);
lacunae (Tuurala and Lehtinen, 1967) and extracellularspace (Schiffand Gervasio,
1969). It is found mainly in compound eyes adapted to darkness, and, almost
exclusively in those of the apposition type. In various insect species, for example,
Locusta (Horridge and Barnard, 1965), the drone ofApis mellifica (Perrelet, 1970),
Formica (Menzel and Lange, 1971), it could be shown that these structures represent greatly expanded elements of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum which
swell during dark adaptation (Horridge and Barnard, 1965; Fahrenbach, 1969;
Baumann et al., 1967) and thus probably modify the amount of light trapped in
the microvilli by changing the refractive index around the rhabdom. In Squilla
ommatidia, the size of the vacuoles system (Spaltbreite) is hardly changed by
LA-DA, and it is possible that the refractive index of this system remains unchanged.
Schiff and Gervasio (1969) use the expression "extracellular space" for the
vacuole system. This naming is based on the assumption that the perirhabdomal
vacuole system of Squilla is connected with the extracellular space through
openings in the retinula cells at the proximal end of the rhabdom. The extracellular
liquid is supposed to penetrate through these openings into the extracellular space
and function there as an ion pool. In spite of careful investigation we have not been
able to find such openings in the retinula cells. Perrelet and Baumann (1969a;
1969 b), on the other hand, could show that molecules of ferritin and lanthanum,
which are considered tracers for the extracellular space, diffuse along the intercellular clefts into the rhabdom of the ommatidium of the honey bee drone. Further
physiological research will be necessary to elucidate the possible pathway for ion
diffusion from extracellular space into the perirhabdomal vacuoles in Squilla.
The pigment cells with crystalline inclusions, which lie chiefly proximal and
distal to the basement membrane, were designated as red pigment cells by Schiff
and Gervasio (1969). These authors suppose that, because of their colour and
their distribution, these cells act as a reservoir for the visual pigments. During our
investigations, we found no indication which might support this suggestion. On
the other hand, we cannot exclude the possibility that the pigment cell types considered may be cyanoblasts or cyanocytes, and that the crystalloid inclusions of
these cells may be the respiratory pigment hemocyanin. The presence of free
hemocyanin in the hemocoelic fluid of Stomatopods has been reported by different
authors (for a review see Waterman, 1961), but to our knowledge the site of origin
of the cyanoblasts is not known. According to Fahrenbach (1970) several lines
of evidence point to the hepatopancreas as the principal organ involved in hemo-

cyanin metabolism. Our supposition is based on the close resemblance of the cell
as a whole, and of the ultrastructure of the crystal contents in particular, to the
cyanocytes described by Fahrenbach (1970) in Limulus polyphemus, and Ghieretti
et al. (1973) in Carcinus maenas.
Concerning the dimensions of the lattice of the cytoplasmic crystalline bodies,
there are some differences between our findings and those on Limulus by Fahrenbach (1970) who found a diameter of 190 A for the "hollow-appearing cylinders",
whereas in Squilla, a diameter of 125 A was found. Furthermore the center-tocenter spacing of the cylinders, which is 260 ,~ in Limulus, is only 150 A in Squilla.
A difference also exists regarding to the longitudinal stacking of the electron dense
granules (probabely hemocyanin molecules) comprising the cylinders. In Limulus,
Fahrenbach observed a transverse periodicity of 100 A, Ghiretti et al. (1973) in
Carcinus a period of 120/~, and in Squilla, we found a period of 60 ~..
The immunofluorescence technique should make it possible to establish
definitely whether these are crystals of the respiratory pigment, hemocyanin.
The differences in morphology between DA and LA Squilla ommatidia prove
the existence of an adaptation mechanism. Such mechanisms have been noted
in several Crustacea and have been described under the heading, photomechanical
phenomena (D6baisieux, 1944; Kleinholz, 1961; Eguchi and Waterman, 1966,
1967; Schiff and Gervasio, 1969).
The results of the experiments reported here suggest that the intensity of light
which reaches the rhabdom of a given ommatidium is controlled by several mechanisms: (1) the pigment sheath which almost entirely surrounds each ommatidium;
(2) the diameter and the length of the crystalline cone (Walcott, 1969); the accumulation and the dispersion of the pigment granules in the retinula cells (Kirschfeld
and Franceschini, 1969); and possibly, (4) the enlargement of the rhabdom.
Similar photomechanical changes have also been observed in other arthropods
(D6baisieux, 1944; Lfidtke, 1953; Horridge, 1968; Walcott, 1969, 1971; MeyerRochow, 1972).
It remains to be established whether the distal pigment cells contract as a whole
in DA, or whether the piments are only sliding within the cell. Microtubules
occur in great number in all the cells that show photomechanical movements
exept the retinula cells. In the cone cells extensions and in the distal pigment cells,
the microtubules are orientated in the direction of movement. Anatomically,
therefore, the microtubules could be the "motor" that causes the cell movement,
while the retinula cells are pulled along "passively". However there is no experimental evidence to support his hypothesis (see Walcott, 1975).
Different opinions exist regarding the function of the crystalline cone extensions.
Horridge (1969) attributes a light-conducting function to them, whereas Varela
and Porter (1969) consider them to be elements that bind the dioptric apparatus
to the retinula cells. Schiff and Gervasio (1969) described a "round, lengthwise
stretching structure" occuring between the retinula cells, which is attached to the
"black pigment cells" at its distal end, and proximally to the basement membrane.
They considered it to be connective tissue which holds the ommatidia in their
stretched position. As can be seen from their illustrations, these structures are
identical with the cone extensions described by us. However, their distal extremities
are not attached to the black pigment cells (distal pigment cells), but form a definite
extension of the crystalline cone cells.

In DA there is a sliding of the pigment of the distal pigment cells towards the
cornea, nevertheless, the distal part of the crystalline cone remains free of a pigment
covering. Thus it is possible that in DA or LA, the retinula is struck by light rays
from its ommatidium as well as by rays from neighbouring crystalline cones.
As Squilla lives mainly in dim light, it seems reasonable that its eyes should permit
the reception of as much light as possible from any object. Squilla may, therefore,
possess a pigment distribution similar to that realized in the superposition eye
(Exner, 1891) in dark adaptation (maximum light entrance into the rhabdom).
Our results confirm that in DA, a pigment shift also occurs in the retinula cells.
Evidently, therefore some of the pigment granules in the retinula cells move towards the rhabdom in DA, and others (a minority), found in the protoplasmic
bridges, move away from it. The fact that the granules are closely strung together
around the rhabdom in DA leads us to conclude that in this situation the refractive
index around the rhabdom is increased. This result is not in agreement with that
obtained in various species of insects (e.g., Kirschfeld and Franceschini, 1969;
Menzel and Lange, 1971; Kolb and Autrum, 1972; Walcott, 1975), in which pigment granules in the retinula cells migrate in general towards the rhabdom in LA
and in DA move away from it. The effect of these changes on the reflection of
light by the rhabdom wall of Musca has been evaluated by Kirschfeld and Franceschini (1969). However, until more detailed investigations concerning the process
of pigment movement in the retinula cells of Squilla are caried out, we cannot
extrapolate with confidence the functional data obtained from the eye of the fly.
To complement our LA-DA studies, we are now carrying out LA tests using light
of a given wavelength and intensity rather than diffuse daylight,
To evaluate the light-conducting properties, and thus the light flux in the rhabdom, one also has to take into account the condition of the perirhabdomal vacuoles
in LA and DA. According to Eguchi and Waterman (1967), Horridge and Barnard
(1965), Horridge (1966) and Fahrenbach (1969) their size increases in DA, while
in increasing LA they disappear to a large extent or change their location in the
retinula cell. Furthermore, it is known (Eguchi and Waterman, 1967; Kolb, 1974)
that various cytoplasmic inclusions occur in most retinula cells of arthropods.
In the retinula cells of Libina Eguchi and Waterman (1967) noticed in increase
in the number of multivesicular bodies in DA and a decrease of these inclusions
in LA. However, in Squilla, the number and type of inclusions, as well as the form
and size of the vacuole system around the rhabdom, is hardly changed by adaptation.
It is clear that the phenomenon of DA-LA, which involves retinula cells,
different kinds of pigment cells, and possibly also neurosecretory activity, requires
further study.
