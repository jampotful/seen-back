Multiplex CRISPR-Cas Assay for Rapid, Isothermal and Visual Detection of White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV) and Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei (EHP) in Penaeid Shrimp.
Kanitchinda S, Sritunyalucksana K, Chaijarasphong T. (2025). Journal of fish diseases. . doi:10.1111/jfd.14059. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Multiplex CRISPR‐Cas Assay for Rapid, Isothermal and Visual Detection of White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV) and Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei (EHP) in Penaeid Shrimp

## Abstract
ABSTRACTWhite spot syndrome virus (WSSV) and Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei (EHP) represent the most economically destructive pathogens in the current shrimp industry. WSSV causes white spot disease (WSD) responsible for rapid shrimp mortality, while EHP stunts growth and therefore reduces overall productivity. Despite the importance of timely disease detection, current diagnostic methods for WSSV and EHP are typically singleplex, and those offering multiplex detection face issues such as complexity, low field compatibility and/or low sensitivity. Here, we introduce an orthogonal, multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay for concomitant detection of WSSV and EHP. This method combines recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) for target DNA enrichment with Cas12a and Cas13a enzymes for fluorescent detection. This assay produces distinct fluorescent colours for different diagnostic outcomes, allowing naked eye visualisation without ambiguity. Further validation reveals that the assay detects as few as 20 and 200 copies of target DNA from EHP and WSSV, respectively, while producing no false positives with DNA from other shrimp pathogens. Moreover, the assay excellently agrees with established PCR methods in evaluation of clinical samples. Requiring only 37°C and less than an hour to complete, multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay presents a promising tool for onsite diagnostics, offering high accuracy while saving time and resources.Keywords: Cas12a, Cas13a, CRISPR diagnostics, Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei (EHP), penaeid shrimp, white spot syndrome virus (WSSV)

## Introduction
Penaeid shrimp such as Pacific whiteleg shrimp ( Penaeus vannamei ) and black tiger shrimp ( Penaeus monodon ) are integral to the global economy and food security, with their production reaching approximately 6.3 million metric tons in 2021 (FAO 2024). However, shrimp diseases causing mass mortalities or severe growth retardation pose significant challenges to sustainable shrimp aquaculture. One particularly devastating disease is white spot disease (WSD) caused by white spot syndrome virus (WSSV), an enveloped double‐stranded DNA virus. It belongs to the genus Whispovirus of the family Nimaviridae, and had been the sole member of this genus until the recent study classifying a new bacilliform virus in snow crab as a distinct variant, and potentially species, from WSSV (Eliseikina et al. 2024; Lo et al. 2011). Symptoms of WSD include white spots on the carapace, redness, reduced appetite and, if unchecked, can culminate in pond‐wide 90%–100% mortalities (Chou et al. 1995; Durand et al. 1996; Gallaga‐Maldonado et al. 2020; Thitamadee et al. 2016). In addition to the disease severity, WSSV's high prevalence (Talukder et al. 2021; Vijayan et al. 2024), large epidemic size (Omori et al. 2024), wide host range (Bandeira et al. 2019; Desrina et al. 2013, 2022; Pradeep et al. 2012; Xu et al. 2021), the ability to transmit both vertically and horizontally (Lo and Kou 1998; Soowannayan and Phanthura 2011; Tuyen et al. 2014; Vijayan et al. 2024) and the capacity to infect all life stages of shrimp (Kiran et al. 2002; Lightner 1996; Qayoom et al. 2023; Venegas et al. 1999; Zhang et al. 2024) further complicate the control and prevention of WSD. It is unsurprising, therefore, that the economic loss due to WSSV alone has been estimated at 10% of annual shrimp production output, twice the combined annual losses due to four other top viral pathogens (yellow head virus, infectious hypodermic and haematopoietic necrosis virus, Taura syndrome virus and infectious myonecrosis virus) (Patil et al. 2021; Stentiford et al. 2012).
Enterocytozoon hepatopenaei (EHP), a microsporidian parasite in the Enterocytozoonidae family, is another economically destructive pathogen that, unlike WSSV, does not cause immediate mortality (Chayaburakul et al. 2004). Instead, EHP infection severely impairs growth, decreases potentially beneficial microbes that may aid nutrient assimilation and increases susceptibility to opportunistic bacteria such as Vibrio sp. (Chaijarasphong et al. 2020; López‐Carvallo, Cruz‐Flores, and Dhar 2022; Piamsomboon and Han 2022). As EHP exists as robust spores during its life cycle, it can remain in the environment for prolonged periods, necessitating harsh chemical treatments of pond water for effective removal (Pattarayingsakul et al. 2022; Tourtip et al. 2009). Further intensifying the problem of EHP is its documented high prevalence. According to a cohort study conducted in Thailand, EHP was identified in 119 out of 196 shrimp farms surveyed, suggesting a 60.7% prevalence (Sanguanrut et al. 2018). A survey of 130 shrimp farms in India also revealed EHP infection in 32.4% of the collected specimens, the highest percentage of all diseases detected (Babu et al. 2021).
As cost‐effective prophylactics and therapeutics for WSSV and EHP are not yet available, routine disease surveillance remains the first‐line defence against the spread of these diseases. Most current detection methods, however, target single pathogens, raising costs per specimen for simultaneous detection of several diseases simultaneously (Jaroenlak et al. 2016; Lo et al. 1996; Soowannayan and Phanthura 2011). In addition, though less common than single infection, incidents of WSSV‐EHP co‐infection have been documented in several studies, with Babu and colleagues reporting that 7.6% of the shrimp specimens infected with both WSSV and EHP, a proportion higher than co‐infection by other pathogen pairs (Babu et al. 2021; Krishnan et al. 2021; Patil et al. 2021; Thamizhvanan et al. 2019). Therefore, the ability to detect both diseases at once, rather than relying on two separate reactions, is expected to conserve resources and time while providing valuable information for disease control and epidemiological investigations.
While conceptually attractive, the multiplex assays available for WSSV and EHP face several challenges. For instance, the multiplex PCR assay for WSSV and EHP requires expensive thermal cyclers and familiarity with molecular biology techniques (Kalaria et al. 2024; Koiwai et al. 2018; Thamizhvanan et al. 2019). The common use of agarose gel electrophoresis for PCR product visualisation further complicates the development of new assays, as primers must be carefully designed to generate non‐overlapping bands for different targets. Isothermal diagnostic methods, though optimised for field usage, present different sets of shortcomings. In the case of loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) assays, multiplex variations face a greater risk of non‐specific amplification due to the use of four to six primers per target (Hu et al. 2023; Notomi et al. 2000). Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), which has gained popularity owing to their requirement of only two primers per target and low operation temperature (37°C–42°C), does not readily offer naked‐eye visualisation of results. While a modified RPA kit called RPA‐exo provides fluorescent outputs, it is not optimised for naked eye visualisation and requires expensive probes that must be redesigned for each new target (Piepenburg et al. 2006). Non‐nucleic acid‐based strategies such as immunochromatographic assays are fast, readily multiplexable and easy to use, but suffer from lower sensitivity than nucleic acid‐based approaches (Sheng et al. 2018).
Aiming to address the collective constraints of these methods, we harnessed a multiplex alternative that simultaneously offers high sensitivity, specificity and user‐friendliness: CRISPR diagnostics. This paradigm uses CRISPR‐Cas endonucleases (henceforth ‘Cas’), guided by a short RNA molecule called guide RNA, to locate and cleave targets (Chen et al. 2018; Gootenberg et al. 2017). Two requirements for successful recognition include (1) a significant match between the guide RNA and 20–28 nucleotides on target nucleic acids and, depending on the type of Cas and (2) the presence of a 4–8 bp segment called protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) next to the bound site, with Cas13a as an exception that does not require PAM. For some types of Cas, notably Cas12a and Cas13a, this first cleavage event is followed by a secondary activity called trans‐cleavage, where Cas digests single‐stranded DNA (ssDNA) or single‐stranded RNA (ssRNA) in a sequence‐independent manner (Chen et al. 2018; Gootenberg et al. 2017). This unique property has been leveraged to visually report the presence of target nucleic acid by including an ssDNA‐ or ssRNA‐linked fluorophore‐quencher reporter: the presence of the intended target results in the trans‐cleavage of the reporter's linker and liberates the fluorophore from its quencher, thereby producing strong fluorescence that can be observed by the naked eye.
Besides allowing naked eye visualisation, the merits of CRISPR detection comprise high sensitivity, specificity and multiplexability. While assays using CRISPR‐Cas alone typically require at least 108–109 copies of target for successful detection, the sensitivity can be enhanced to 1–1000 copies through preamplification (Nouri et al. 2022). Cross‐reactivities with non‐target pathogens are unlikely owing to the low guide RNA‐target mismatch tolerance, where targets differing by only one nucleotide can be distinguished (Li et al. 2018). Importantly, there exist pairs of Cas proteins that exhibit mutually exclusive specificity, allowing different targets to generate visual signals without interfering with each other. An example of such pairs is Cas12a and Cas13a, which prefer ssDNA and ssRNA as trans‐cleavage substrates, respectively—the target of Cas12a generates signal through an ssDNA‐linked reporter whereas that of Cas13a does so through an ssRNA‐linked reporter (Gootenberg et al. 2018). By using different fluorophores on ssDNA‐ and ssRNA‐linked reporters, each of the two targets can be identified based on the colour of the fluorescence.
Here, we harnessed the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas detection method for simultaneous detection of WSSV and EHP (Figure 1). The DNA targets were first isothermally enriched by recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) before subjected to the multiplex Cas12a‐Cas13a reaction, where Cas12a's and Cas13a's guide RNAs targeted the polar tube protein 2 gene (ptp2) from EHP and the vp28 gene from WSSV, respectively. As Cas13a strictly recognises ssRNA targets, this reaction also contained T7 RNA polymerase and ribonucleoside triphosphates (rNTPs) to in vitro transcribe the amplicon from WSSV. The ssDNA‐linked ROX‐quencher pair (RQ reporter) and ssRNA‐linked FAM‐quencher pair (FQ reporter) were employed for fluorescent readouts, generating red, green and yellow fluorescence for EHP‐, WSSV‐ and co‐infected samples, respectively. In contrast to multiplex PCR, our assay, referred to as ‘multiplex CRISPR‐Cas’ for brevity, requires only simple heating devices that can maintain the temperature of 37°C, or even body temperature, approximately 1 h of sample‐to‐answer time and facial visual readouts, endowing it with strong potential for onsite applications.
Schematic illustrating the principle of multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay. Three different scenarios are displayed: Single infection with EHP (‘+EHP’), single infection with WSSV (‘+WSSV’) and co‐infection (‘+EHP + WSSV’). The DNA extract is first pre‐amplified using recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA). The forward primer for WSSV contains a T7 promoter (arrow) to allow subsequent in vitro transcription. This step takes 20 min at 37°C Then, the amplification product is transferred to another tube containing reagents for T7 in vitro transcription, Cas12a assay and Cas13a assay. If the specimen contains only EHP, the RPA product will directly serve as the substrate for Cas12a and trigger the trans‐cleavage of the ssDNA‐linked ROX‐quencher (RQ) reporter, resulting in red fluorescence. In the case of WSSV, the RPA product must first be transcribed into RNA, which is then cleaved by Cas13a and activates the trans‐cleavage of the ssRNA‐linked FAM‐quencher (FQ) reporter, producing green fluorescence. Co‐infected samples will generate both red and green fluorescence, therefore appearing yellow. The transcription and CRISPR detection step combined takes 30 min at 37°C.

## Materials and Methods

## Construction of Expression Plasmids for Cas12a and Cas13a
The expression plasmid for Cas12a from Acidaminococcus sp. (AsCas12a), referred to as ‘pC12’, was a gift from Jennifer Doudna (Addgene plasmid #113430). This plasmid was used for recombinant protein expression without modifications. The expression plasmid for Cas13a from Leptotrichia wadei (LwaCas13a), referred to as ‘pC13’, was constructed based on the LwaCas13a coding sequence from pC013 (a gift from Feng Zhang, Addgene plasmid #90097), with the fused N‐terminal SUMO tag removed. Briefly, PCR forward and reverse primers (named C13‐cloning‐F and ‐R, Table S1) were designed to amplify the Cas13a coding sequence (CDS) without the 5′‐SUMO tag sequence. The reverse primer also contained a hexahistidine (His) tag for subsequent protein purification by Ni‐NTA affinity chromatography. The PCR reaction was established with the following components: 1x Phusion GC buffer, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 1 U Phusion high‐fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs (NEB), USA), 0.5 μM of each primer, 10 ng pC013 and nuclease‐free water to adjust the total volume to 50 μL. Thermal cycling was conducted in a T100 thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad, USA) with the following temperature program: initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s; 35 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 s, annealing at 69°C for 10 s and extension at 72°C for 105 s and final extension at 72°C for 10 min. The PCR product was verified using agarose gel electrophoresis and the expected band (3212 bp) was subjected to gel purification using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean‐up kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Germany).
Once purified, the insert was cloned into a modified pET14‐b destination vector (pSAV038, a gift from David Savage, UC Berkeley) using Golden Gate Assembly (Engler, Kandzia, and Marillonnet 2008). The Golden Gate reaction consisted of 1x T4 ligase buffer, 5 U BsaI, 100 U T4 DNA ligase, 20 fmol of insert, 10 fmol pSAV038, with nuclease‐free water added to a total volume of 10 μL. The incubation was carried out with the following thermal cycling program: 25 cycles of 37°C for 2 min and 16°C for 5 min; 50°C for 10 min and 80°C for 10 min. The reaction was used to transform chemically competent Escherichia coli DH5ɑ cells, which were then plated on LB agar containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 10 μg/mL X‐gal. One of the resulting white colonies was cultured overnight at 37°C in LB broth supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and miniprepped using a Presto miniprep kit (Geneaid, Taiwan). The concentration and purity of the plasmid DNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop One UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The sequence of the insert, particularly the absence of a SUMO tag, was confirmed through Sanger sequencing (Macrogen, South Korea). For subsequent recombinant expression, E. coli Rosetta 2 (DE3) was transformed with pC12 and pC13 and stored as frozen glycerol stocks at −80°C until needed.

## Recombinant Expression and Purification of Cas12a
Escherichia coli Rosetta 2 (DE3) harbouring pC12 was cultured in LB broth containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37°C. The overnight culture was back‐diluted in 500 mL of LB‐ampicillin broth to an OD600 of 0.05 and allowed to grow at 37°C until OD600 of 0.5–0.7. To induce protein expression, Isopropyl ß‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, Merck, Germany) was added to the culture to a final concentration of 0.2 mM and the cultivation was resumed at 18°C for another 16 h. Induced cells were collected by centrifugation at 7000 g for 10 min and immediately subjected to purification or stored at −80°C until needed.
The cell pellet was resuspended in 20 mL lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8), supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Merck, Germany), 1 mg/mL lysozyme (VWR Life Science, USA) and 0.025 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). The mixture was sonicated with 40% amplitude in pulses (9 s on and 9 s off), for a total on‐time of 2 min. The lysed cells were centrifuged at 12,000 g and 4°C for 30 min to remove cell debris and the supernatant was incubated with 1 mL Ni‐NTA agarose resin (Qiagen, USA) on ice for 1 h for equilibration. After passing the mixture through an Econo‐Column Chromatography Column (Bio‐Rad, USA), the retained resin was washed with 30 column volumes (CV) of wash buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, pH 8) and eluted with elution buffer (20 mM Tris, 250 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, pH 8) in nine fractions. The first three fractions each had a volume of 0.5 CV, while the next six fractions each had a volume of 1 CV. The eluted fractions were evaluated with SDS‐PAGE and fractions 2–5, which exhibited intense Cas12a bands and minimal contaminant bands, were pooled. The pooled eluent was transferred to a D‐Tube Dialyzer Maxi, MWCO 3.5 kDa (Merck Millipore, Germany) and dialyzed against dialysis buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, 250 mM NaCl, pH 7.5). After quantifying the dialyzed protein with NanoDrop One UV–Vis Spectrophotometer, glycerol and dithiothreitol (DTT) were added to the protein preparation—with glycerol and DTT added, the final buffer composition became 20 mM Tris, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol and 1 mM DTT. The protein was flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further use.
The expression and purification of LwaCas13a followed a protocol similar to that for AsCas12a with some differences in the induction and Ni‐NTA purification steps, adapted from the previously reported protocols (Kellner et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2022). For induction, back‐diluted cells were grown to an OD600 of 0.4 before being induced with 0.25 mM IPTG and the cultivation was resumed at 16°C for 16 h. For Ni‐NTA purification, the following buffers were used: lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8), wash buffer 1 (50 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 8), wash buffer 2 (50 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 8), elution buffer (50 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, 150 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT, pH 8) and dialysis buffer (62.5 mM Tris, 750 mM NaCl, pH 7.5). It should be noted that, to improve purity, the loaded resin was washed with two wash buffers with different imidazole concentrations. After adding glycerol and DTT to the dialyzed protein, the final protein solution contained 50 mM Tris, 600 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol and 2 mM DTT at a pH of 7.5. The absence of a SUMO tag was not verified at this stage, as the expression plasmid had been confirmed through Sanger sequencing to lack a SUMO tag (Section 2.1).

## Preparation of Synthetic WSSV‐vp28 RNA for Cas13a Evaluation
The vp28 gene was amplified from a plasmid by PCR using primers T7‐promoter‐F and T7‐terminator‐R (Table S1). The PCR reaction consisted of 1x Phusion GC buffer, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 1 U Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase, 0.5 μM of each primer, 10 ng vp28 plasmid (‘pVP28’) and nuclease‐free water added to a final volume of 50 μL. Incubation was performed in a T100 thermal cycler with the following two‐step temperature program: initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s; 35 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 s and extension at 72°C for 20 s and final extension at 72°C for 10 min. After gel purification, the PCR product was subjected to in vitro transcription using the HiScribe T7 Quick High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, USA). Then, the product was treated with DNase I for 1 h to remove the DNA template and purified using the RNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, USA).

## Synthesis of Guide RNA for Cas12a and Cas13a
The Cas12a guide RNA targeting the polar tube protein 2 (ptp2) gene of EHP was prepared in the same manner previously described (Kanitchinda et al. 2020). The Cas13a guide RNA for WSSV detection was designed and prepared in this study. Using the coding sequence of VP28 from WSSV genomic DNA (Genbank accession number: AF369029.2) as the target, the candidates for Cas13a guide RNA (WSSV01, 02 and 03, Table S1) were designed following the strategy outlined in Kellner et al. (2019). Briefly, each candidate's spacer sequence (i.e., the segment of guide RNA that directly binds the target RNA) contained 28 nucleotides, showed no significant similarity to the genomic DNA of shrimp and other shrimp pathogens according to nucleotide BLAST and avoided regions in the target RNA predicted to have a high probability of forming secondary structure, which could decrease Cas13a activity (Bandaru et al. 2020). The prediction of RNA secondary structure was conducted in RNAfold with the simulated temperature set to 37°C (Gruber et al. 2008). To prepare the candidates, two complementary ssDNA oligonucleotides containing a T7 promoter, direct repeat and spacer were custom synthesised by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, USA) (WSSV01‐F/R, WSSV02‐F/R and WSSV03‐F/R in Table S1). Both ssDNA strands were added in equimolar amounts to STE buffer (10 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, pH 8), heated at 95°C for 5 min and cooled gradually for 10–15 min in the ambient temperature. The annealed duplex was then subjected to in vitro transcription and RPA purification following the procedure outlined in Section 2.3.

## Singleplex Cas13a Assay
In this study, the only experiments involving the singleplex Cas13a assay were the screening of guide RNA, evaluation of the Cas13a assay without RPA and the screening of RPA primers for WSSV detection (Sections 3.1 and 3.2). For guide RNA screening and sensitivity evaluation of the Cas13a assay without RPA, the Cas13a reaction (total volume 20 μL) consisted of 1x cleavage buffer (12.5 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM DTT, 1% glycerol, pH 7.5), 45 nM LwaCas13a, 23.5 nM guide RNA, 250 nM FQ reporter and synthetic WSSV‐vp28 RNA (1 nM for the guide RNA screening experiment, 101–10−6 nM for the Cas13a assay sensitivity evaluation). For RPA primers screening, the reaction was also supplemented with 5 U T7 RNA polymerase (NEB, USA) and 1 mM ribonucleotide solution mix (NEB, USA) to enable in vitro transcription and 2 μL of RPA product was added as the target DNA instead of WSSV‐vp28 RNA. For both experiments, the reaction was incubated at 37°C for 30 min before measuring the fluorescence signal using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 nm and 525 nm, respectively. For naked‐eye visualisation, the reaction vials were exposed to blue light on a BluPAD transilluminator (Bio‐Helix, Taiwan) inside a dark photo hood and the image was captured with an iPhone XR using the default camera settings.

## Recombinase Polymerase Amplification (RPA)
RPA was performed using the TwistAmp Basic Kit (TwistDx, UK) following the manufacturer's protocol with minor modifications. Each RPA reaction (10 μL) contained the following components: 1x RPA reagent mix, 1x rehydration buffer, 240 nM of each primer, 14 mM of magnesium acetate and DNA target(s). Per the kit's recommendation, these components were added in succession. First, a tube of lyophilized RPA reagent mix was dissolved in 29.5 μL of rehydration buffer. After adding primers, the volume of this solution was adjusted to 35 μL with nuclease‐free water. During this step, a single pair of primers was added for singleplex RPA, while two pairs of primers were added for multiplex RPA. After the master mix was divided into five vials, the DNA target(s) was mixed with the solution in each vial and 1 μL of 10× magnesium acetate (140 mM) was deposited under the tube lid.
To initiate the amplification, each vial was briefly centrifuged to mix in magnesium acetate, then gently vortexed and centrifuged again before incubating at 37°C for 20 min in a thermal cycler. For increased efficiency, the vials were removed for agitation 4 min into the incubation and then returned to the thermal cycler for the remaining 16 min. The RPA product was then visualised on a 3% agarose gel or analysed with the multiplex Cas assay for fluorescent readouts.

## Multiplex CRISPR‐Cas Assay
For brevity, the combination of RPA, Cas12a and Cas13a assays are referred to as ‘multiplex CRISPR‐Cas’. After the duplex RPA reaction using two optimal primer pairs (57F and 159R for WSSV; EHP‐F and ‐R for EHP; Table 1), 2 μL of the amplicon was transferred to a master mix containing following components: 1X cleavage buffer, 150 nM AsCas12a, 45 nM LwaCas13a, 62.5 nM Cas12a guide RNA (‘EHP‐gRNA’), 23.5 nM Cas13a guide RNA (WSSV01), 300 nM RQ reporter, 250 nM FQ reporter, 5 U T7 RNA polymerase and 1 mM rNTPs. After adjusting the volume to 20 μL with nuclease‐free water, the reaction was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 nm and 525 nm, respectively, for the FAM channel and 570 nm and 610 nm for the ROX channel. For naked‐eye colour detection, the vial was exposed to blue light on a BluPAD transilluminator inside a dark photo hood. The image was captured with an iPhone XR using default camera settings, or with a Xiaomi Redmi Note 8 using the following parameters: white balance (WB) = 2000, exposure time (S) = 1/8 s and ISO = 200.

## Analytical Sensitivity Assessment for Multiplex CRISPR‐Cas Assay
The analytical sensitivity of the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay was assessed on tenfold serial dilutions of a mixture containing pPTP2, a plasmid harbouring ptp2 from EHP and pVP28, a plasmid harbouring vp28 from WSSV. Each sample in the dilution series contained an equal amount of both plasmids (2–2 × 106 copies/μL). The limit of detection for each target was defined as the lowest copy number that tested positive in all three replicates and produced fluorescence intensity that was significantly different from the negative control according to the two‐tailed Student's t‐test (p < 0.05).

## Analytical Specificity Assessment for Multiplex CRISPR‐Cas Assay
The analytical specificity of multiplex CRISPR‐Cas was evaluated on 100 ng of archived DNA previously extracted from penaeid shrimp infected with various pathogens, including WSSV, EHP, infectious hypodermal and haematopoietic necrosis virus (IHHNV), Vibrio parahaemolyticus —acute hepatopancreatic necrosis disease strain (AHPND) and decapod hepanhamaparvovirus 1 (DHPV). DNA extracted from shrimp free from both EHP and WSSV was also tested as a negative control. To be deemed analytically specific, the fluorescence from reactions containing EHP and WSSV must be significantly different from the negative control according to the two‐tailed Student's t‐test (p < 0.05), while other pathogens must not produce significant fluorescence compared to the negative control (p > 0.05).

## Clinical Sample Evaluation
Archived DNA samples (n = 11, 50 ng/μL total DNA), extracted from disease‐free Penaeus vannamei and those previously challenged with WSSV and EHP in the laboratory, were used to assess the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas method. The stomach and hepatopancreas had been used for DNA extraction for WSSV and EHP, respectively. The following procedure a used to extract and purify DNA: The target tissues were homogenised in a tube containing lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 9.0, 100 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 2% SDS and 1 μg/mL proteinase K) using disposable plastic grinders. The stomach‐ and hepatopancreas‐derived homogenates were then subjected to total DNA extraction using the standard phenol‐chloroform method and a DNeasy blood and tissue kit (QIAGEN, USA), respectively. The purified DNA samples were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Co‐infected samples (n = 4) were artificially prepared by mixing WSSV‐ and EHP‐infected samples in a 1:1 ratio. Water and a mixture of pVP28 and pPTP2 (2 × 106 copies each) were analysed as negative and positive controls, respectively. Using 2 μL from each sample, the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay was performed as described in Sections 2.6 and 2.7. The results were compared with those obtained using SWP‐nested PCR for EHP (Jaroenlak et al. 2016) and VP28‐PCR for WSSV (Soowannayan and Phanthura 2011), performed with the procedures outlined in respective studies.

## In Silico Prediction of Primer Dimers
Potential self‐ and cross‐dimers of different primer pairs were predicted using Thermo Scientific's Multiple Primer Analyzer online tool (https://www.thermofisher.com/au/en/home/brands/thermo‐scientific/molecular‐biology/molecular‐biology‐learning‐center/molecular‐biology‐resource‐library/thermo‐scientific‐web‐tools/multiple‐primer‐analyzer.html). This tool had been employed to predict primer dimers in studies developing RPA assays (Kortli et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2021; Stringer et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2024; Zou, Mason, and Botella 2020). Briefly, two primer sequences, one forward and one reverse, were input at a time. The following primer pairs were analysed: 57F/159R and EHP‐F1/EHP‐R1. In addition, to evaluate how the presence of a T7 promoter sequence affects primer dimerization propensity of 57F, a variation of 57F with the T7 promoter omitted (‘57F*’), along with 159F, was also subjected to the same analysis. Primer and salt concentrations were set to 240 nM and 50 mM, respectively. The sensitivity level was set to 2. (Sensitivity levels of 1 and 10 indicate maximum and minimum sensitivity in primer dimer detection, respectively.) This sensitivity value, as opposed to the default value of 3, was chosen to reflect RPA's higher susceptibility to primer dimerization compared to PCR.

## Ethics
The DNA samples used to develop and evaluate the assays in this study were either synthetic DNA or archived samples available from previous studies. The protocols involving these DNA extracts were been approved by the Faculty of Science, Mahidol University (Thailand) Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol Number: MUSC64‐040‐589).

## Results

## Screening of Guide RNA Candidates for WSSV Detection With Cas13a
As we had previously identified efficient guide RNA and RPA primers for Cas12a detection of EHP (Kanitchinda et al. 2020), we set out to determine the best guide RNA and primers for WSSV detection using Cas13a. Three guide RNA candidates were prepared based on the criteria outlined in Section 2.3 and evaluated in the singleplex Cas13a assay without the RPA and in vitro transcription steps. As Cas13a exclusively targets RNA, 1 nM of synthetic WSSV‐vp28 RNA was used as target nucleic acids in this evaluation. All candidates produced significant fluorescence above that of the negative control, with WSSV01 imparting the highest signal (Figure 2A,B). When WSSV01 was used to analyse tenfold serially diluted RNA, the limit of detection was found to be 10−1 nM (Figure 2C,D), in line with a previous report employing LwaCas13a for direct RNA detection (Gootenberg et al. 2017).

## Screening of RPA Primers for WSSV Amplification
Three forward and three reverse RPA primers for WSSV amplification, forming nine pairwise combinations, were designed (Table S1). All forward primers contained a T7 promoter on the 5′‐end for the in vitro transcription of the RPA products prior to the Cas13a reaction. According to agarose gel electrophoresis, all combinations except 10F‐159R successfully amplified the 2 × 103 copies of pVP28 (Figure S1A). 57F‐159R, 57F‐163R, 57F‐167R, 61F‐159R and 61F‐163R generated comparably strong target bands. However, while the negative controls for 57F‐163R, 57F‐167R, 61F‐159R and 61F‐163R displayed multiple bands, the negative control for 57F‐159R generated a single band, suggesting a low degree of ‘primer noise’ (i.e., low‐molecular weight RPA byproducts) (TwistDx 2021). As primer noise is caused by primer dimerization, which competes with on‐target amplification for ATP, low primer noise is considered favourable for RPA sensitivity (Munawar 2022; Sharma et al. 2014; Zou, Mason, and Botella 2020).
To confirm that the results observed in agarose gel electrophoresis correspond to the actual performance in CRISPR detection, each RPA product was subjected to a singleplex Cas13a reaction containing in vitro transcription reagents. The fluorescence levels acquired aligned with the band intensity observed during agarose gel electrophoresis (Figure S1B,C). An exception was 10F‐163R, which displayed comparable fluorescence intensity to 57F‐159R, 57F‐163R, 57F‐167R, 61F‐159R and 61F‐163R despite its stronger agarose gel band. We surmise that the quantity of the RPA products from the abovementioned primer pairs may have saturated the capacity of Cas13a, causing the output fluorescence to no longer correlate with the amounts of RPA products. Interestingly, compared to the fluorescence intensity obtained when using synthetic WSSV‐vp28 RNA as a target, the fluorescence intensity observed in this experiment was approximately three to six folds higher. Based on its high on‐target amplification efficiency and low primer noise when the target DNA was absent, the 57F‐159R pair was chosen for the rest of the experiments in this study.

## Evaluating Potential Cross‐Talks Between Cas12a and Cas13a Reactions
Since the multiplex CRISPR assay contained Cas12a and Cas13a in the same reaction, we sought to ensure that both CRISPR systems would be truly orthogonal. Specifically, either of the CRISPR systems must not trans‐cleave the reporter for the other, a situation that had been reported to occur with Cas12a from Lachnospiraceae bacterium ND2006 under certain conditions, but not AsCas12a (Li et al. 2022).
To this end, the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay was performed in the presence of the RPA product for EHP (‘ptp2 amplicon’), the RPA product for WSSV (‘vp28 amplicon’) or both. These RPA products had been obtained by amplifying 2 × 104 copies of pPTP2 (representing EHP) and pVP28 (representing WSSV). The microplate fluorescence analysis using FAM and ROX channels revealed strong fluorescence only in the expected channel. For instance, when only ptp2 amplicon was detected, intense fluorescence was observed in the ROX channel, while the signal in the FAM channel was indistinguishable from the background (Figure 3A). Conversely, the presence of vp28 amplicon imparted strong fluorescence only in the FAM channel. Notably, when both types of RPA products were included, the signals from the FAM and ROX channels were comparable in intensity, suggesting the feasibility of distinguishing EHP‐, WSSV‐ and co‐infected pathogens by the naked eye. However, when the vials were exposed to blue light from a transilluminator, the colour contrast between the WSSV‐ and co‐infected samples was low (Figure 3B).

## Analytical Sensitivity of Multiplex CRISPR‐Cas
Samples containing 2–2 × 106 copies of pVP28 and pPTP2 at 1:1 ratio were evaluated using the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay. In this and subsequent experiments involving multiplex CRISPR‐Cas, the RPA step was performed with two pairs of primers: EHP‐F1 and EHP‐F2 for EHP detection and 57‐F and 159‐R for WSSV detection. Similarly, the CRISPR step contains both the Cas12a system for EHP detection and the Cas13a system for WSSV detection. According to Figure 4A,B, most of the copy numbers (200–2 × 106 for pVP28 and 20–2 × 106 for pPTP2) imparted strong fluorescence in both FAM and ROX channels. In addition, the fluorescence intensity was consistent across different copy numbers and showed minimal variations between replicates, as indicated by small standard deviations. This lack of correlation between signal and copy number was ascribed to the rapid saturation kinetics of RPA, which had been previously documented (Gootenberg et al. 2018).
Analytical sensitivity of multiplex CRISPR‐Cas. Samples contained 2–2 × 106 copies of pVP28 and pPTP2 DNA in a 1:1 ratio. (A) Fluorescence analysed by a microplate reader. n = 3. Bar and error bar represent average fluorescence and standard deviation, respectively. Circles represent individual data points. (B) Reactions from (A) visualised by exposure to blue light. (C) Multiplex CRISPR‐Cas performed on samples containing 200 copies of one target and 2 × 106 copies of the other. (D) Reactions from (C) visualised by exposure to blue light.
As 200 copies of pVP28 and 20 copies of pPTP2 were the lowest copy numbers that still produced robust fluorescence, these values were established as the limits of detection for the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay. We did not designate 20 copies of pVP28 and 2 copies of pPTP2 as the limits of detection because, while some of their replicates yielded positive results, the signals were highly variable. Such high signal variability at these low copy numbers was likely attributed to stochastic fluctuations commonly observed in nucleic acid amplification techniques at low template concentrations (D'Agnese, Chase, and Andruszkiewicz‐Allan 2023; Lucchi et al. 2016; Weusten and Herbergs 2012). To confirm 200 copies of pVP28 and 20 copies of pPTP2 as detection limits, the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay was performed on ten replicates containing 200 copies of pVP28 and 20 copies of pPTP2. The results show that fluorescence levels in these samples closely matched those of the samples containing only 200 copies of pVP28, only 20 copies of pPTP2 and 2 × 105 copies of both targets (Figure S2).
To further ensure that the excess quantity of one target would not mask the detection of the other, samples containing 200 copies of either pPTP2 or pVP28 and 2 × 106 copies of the other target were subjected to multiplex CRISPR‐Cas. The FAM and ROX fluorescence obtained from both samples were comparable with each other and on par with the fluorescence levels observed in the analytical sensitivity assessment (Figure 4C,D).

The multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay was used to evaluate other shrimp pathogens in addition to EHP and WSSV, including AHPND, IHHNV and DHPV. DNA from shrimp free from EHP and WSSV was analysed as a negative control. Consistent with the documented high specificity of CRISPR‐Cas detection, our assay produced strong fluorescence only for EHP and WSSV, while other pathogens were indistinguishable from the negative control (Figure 5).

## Discussion
In this study, we successfully established a multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay for WSSV and EHP detection that exhibited high sensitivity and specificity. With the detection limits of 20 copies for EHP and 200 copies for WSSV, our assay is as sensitive as one‐step PCR for EHP detection (LoD = 20 copies) (Hou et al. 2021) and ordinary RPA for WSSV detection (LoD = 200 copies) (Zhang et al. 2022). It is, however, slightly less sensitive than SWP‐nested PCR for EHP detection (LoD = 10 copies) (Jaroenlak et al. 2016) and IQ2000 and quantitative LAMP for WSSV detection (LoD = 5 and 100 copies, respectively) (Mekata et al. 2009). It is possible that the lower sensitivity of our assay for WSSV compared to EHP is due to the presence of an 18‐bpT7 promoter sequence on the forward RPA primer (57F). These extra nucleotides could increase non‐specific amplification, which RPA primers are susceptible to particularly at low quantities of target DNA (Munawar 2022; Sharma et al. 2014; TwistDx 2021; Zou, Mason, and Botella 2020). Consistent with this rationale, the in silico prediction of primer dimers using Multiple Primer Analyzer revealed three possible self‐dimers for 57F, but none for 159R, EHP‐F1, EHP‐R1 and a variation of 57F where the T7 promoter was omitted (Figure S3). Considering the reported success of RPA primers shorter than the manufacturer‐recommended 30 nucleotides, which is the minimum primer length recommended by the manufacturer, trimming the 3′‐end of the forward primer by a few nucleotides could potentially reduce non‐specific amplification and improve sensitivity (Lobato and O'Sullivan 2018). Nevertheless, these limits of detection proved to be sufficient in the evaluation of clinical samples, where multiplex CRISPR‐Cas perfectly agreed with SWP‐PCR and VP28‐PCR, although future confirmation with a larger sample size is recommended to further validate these findings.
Remarkably, our multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay generates three distinct colours corresponding to different diagnostic outcomes. While singly infected samples are straightforward to interpret due to the presence of only one unquenched fluorophore, obtaining a clear yellow fluorescence from co‐infected samples requires a precise balance between ROX and FAM. In this study, this balance was readily met due to the high efficiency of both the Cas12a and Cas13a guide RNAs, the latter's excellent performance probably due to the low likelihood of secondary structure in its spacer (Figure S4A). It is also worth noting that the short transcript generated from the RPA amplicon yielded several folds higher fluorescence than the long WSSV‐vp28 RNA (Figure S1B,C), and this improvement may have been attributed to their structural differences (Figure S4B,C), which is consistent with previous findings that RNA secondary structure influences Cas13a cleavage activity (Abudayyeh et al. 2016; Bandaru et al. 2020). In addition to the high signal outputs, the lack of correlation between fluorescence intensity and copy number, attributable to the rapid saturation kinetics of RPA, guarantees that co‐infected samples will appear yellow regardless of the relative abundance of WSSV and EHP, as substantiated by the results shown in Figure 4D. In other words, while the qualitative nature of our multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay can be perceived as a limitation, it is precisely this feature that makes naked‐eye classification of results possible. For applications requiring quantitative data, we recommend focusing the optimization on the RPA step to delay saturation, such as by reducing primer concentrations.
Beyond accuracy and simplicity, cost is an important factor when selecting a diagnostic assay. The total reagent cost for the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay (~35 THB per reaction) is higher than multiplex PCR (~10 THB) and multiplex LAMP (~10 THB) (Table S2). However, it is more affordable than the detection of WSSV and EHP via two separate singleplex CRISPR‐Cas assays to detect WSSV and EHP, which costs approximately 45.5 THB per specimen (~22.75 THB per pathogen). This comparatively high cost is primarily attributed to commercial RPA kit, reporters, T7 RNA polymerase and rNTPs—the costs of routine chemicals, buffers, RPA primers and guide RNA are negligible. We noted, however, that the combination of T7 RNA polymerase and rNTPs, which costs ~14.5 THB per reaction in this study, was reported to cost as low as 0.5 THB per reaction when purchased in bulk from a different manufacturer (Gootenberg et al. 2017). At this price, the total reagent cost would decrease to ~20.5 THB, and we surmise that bulk purchasing of other expensive reagents such as the RPA kit would further lower the cost. It is important to note that capital costs, such as the investment in instruments, are also important to consider. PCR, despite its lower cost per reaction, demands an expensive thermal cycler that is likely to be cost‐prohibitive for resource‐limited communities. In contrast, the isothermal nature and naked‐eye visualisation of the multiplex RPA‐CRISPR assay mean that users only need to invest in inexpensive heating devices and blue‐LED lamps.
Compared to the use of two parallel singpleplex RPA‐Cas12a and RPA‐Cas13a assays to detect EHP and WSSV, our multiplex CRISPR assay offers several advantages. First, the multiplex protocol requires only 2 μL of specimen, whereas the parallel singplex protocol requires 4 μL of specimen to detect both WSSV and EHP (2 μL per RPA reaction). Second, the multiplex CRISPR assay involves only two liquid transfers: one to add the specimen to the RPA reaction, and another to transfer the RPA product to the Cas12a‐Cas13a reaction. The parallel singleplex protocol, on the other hand, requires four liquid transfers (two to add the specimen to two RPA reactions, and two to transfer the RPA products to their respective Cas reactions). The reduced number of liquid transfers in the multiplex CRISPR assay lowers the risk of cross‐contamination and shortens the procedure. Furthermore, in terms of cost, the parallel singleplex protocol is estimated to cost more per specimen as it requires two RPA reactions (45.5 THB for parallel singleplex protocol vs. 35.1 THB for multiplex protocol).
In conclusion, our multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay enables the detection of two major shrimp diseases within 1 h using only simple equipment and facile readouts. Capable of detecting 2 copies of EHP and 200 copies of WSSV and showing no cross‐reactivities with other shrimp pathogens, the performance of this assay rivals that of the traditional counterparts while showing greater potential for onsite applications. With further validation and optimization into a ready‐to‐use‐kit, we believe that the multiplex CRISPR‐Cas assay will make routine shrimp disease monitoring more practical for resources‐limited settings, and prove to be a valuable asset in the ongoing battles against WSSV and EHP.

Three different scenarios are displayed: Single infection with EHP (‘+EHP’), single infection with WSSV (‘+WSSV’) and co‐infection (‘+EHP + WSSV’).
