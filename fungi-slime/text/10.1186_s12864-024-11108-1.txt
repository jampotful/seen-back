# Yield increment and transcriptome response caused by blue light treatment in Hericium coralloides

## Abstract
BackgroundHericium coralloides is a traditional edible and medicinal mushroom. Light is a key factor in forming fruiting bodies of fungi; however, the effects of different light on the yield and morphogenesis of H. coralloides are still unknown. Therefore, the morphology, yield, and transcriptome of H. coralloides under blue, red, and white light conditions were investigated.ResultFruiting bodies under blue light exhibited superior morphological traits, such as milky white color, larger size, elongated stalks, and higher spine count, leading to higher yields. Different light treatments led to dramatic transcriptome changes ranging from 10,827 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) induced by blue light in Blue-4d to 11,375 DEGs induced by red light in Red-4d and accounted for 64.56% to 67.81% of all expressed genes. This massive amount of light-responsive genes has never been reported in fungi. Gene Ontology analysis showed that light affected nearly all aspects of life in H. coralloides; suggesting that the influence of light on fungi may have been underestimated. Blue light-induced yield increment may be achieved by specifically upregulating the growth-related processes such as DNA replication, chromosomes, and cell division.ConclusionsThis study offers preliminary insights into the potential role of blue light in modulating gene expression and yield stimulation in H. coralloides, potentially improving cultivation practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11108-1.

## Background
Members of the genus Hericium produce fleshy, whitish basidiomata. Hericium coralloides (Scop.) Pers. (in Hericiaceae, Russulales, Agaricomycetes, Basidiomycota) [1] is an edible mushroom rich in protein, dietary fibers, polysaccharides, vitamins, minerals, and other nutrients [2–4]. The fruiting bodies or mycelia of H. coralloide contain many physiologically active compounds that have been widely applied in traditional Chinese medicine to treat various diseases, including potential hypolipidemic effects [5], degenerative brain diseases such as dementia, cancer [6, 7], and gastric ulcers [2]. Moreover, it was found to have anti-aging and antioxidant properties [4, 8], and it can enhance appetite, promote digestion, and improve human immunity [2].
Mushroom-forming fungi differentiate and form fruiting bodies by sensing several environmental factors, which is a characteristic of these fungi. Nutrients, temperature, and light conditions are crucial environmental factors for inducing fruiting bodies [9]. Light is vital for fungal growth [10–13] and development, and can affect the fungal physiological cycle and morphological characteristics [14–16]. The influence of light has been well characterized in ascomycetes [17]. Although light is essential for the induction and development of the fruiting body of mushrooms, not all mushroom fruiting bodies require light. Agaricus bisporus deviates from most cultivated basidiomycetes because it does not require light for fructification [18].
Furthermore, light signaling could be closely linked with other pathways, such as sporulation, primary metabolic pathways, or the production of secondary metabolites [11, 19, 20]. The numerous examples of light responses in fungi indicate the complexity of light sensing and signal transduction and a crucial role in regulating fungal life [21]. Low-intensity scattered light can induce spore germination, shorten the cultivation time, increase the biomass yield, and enhance bioactive components [22]. In addition to visible light, UV exposure could also stimulate the production of secondary metabolites and natural antioxidants, such as vitamin D and, especially, phenolic acids, in test fungi [23, 24]
Blue light is an essential environmental signal that promotes the growth and morphological development of Lentinula edodes [25], Coprinopsis cinerea [26], Cordyceps militaris, and Pleurotus ostreatus [25, 27–30]. In addition, blue light treatment can be used as a processing technology to improve the flavor quality of dried edible mushrooms [31]. On the other hand, red light has shown contrasting effects on the growth of fungi. Red light decreased the yield of Hypsizygus marmoreus and P. ostreatus, but increased the yield of C. militaris and P. eryngii [27–29, 32].
H. coralloides fruiting bodies are typically cultivated under white light conditions; the effects of blue or red light on their morphology and yield have not been reported. Furthermore, the growth of many fungal fruiting bodies is closely linked to light conditions, but the growth stimulation and the mechanism underlying the light response in fungi, including H. coralloides, have not been well studied. Therefore, the morphology, yield, and transcriptome of H. coralloides under different light conditions were investigated, aiming to improve its features and yields, and to uncover additional clues about the mechanisms underlying the light response in fungi.

## Results

## The influence of light on the morphogenesis and yield of H. coralloides fruiting bodies
Hericium coralloides was exposed to blue, red, and white light after the appearance of the primordia. The different light treatments affected the fruiting body morphologie (Fig. 1). The results indicated that exposure to blue light resulted in improved fruiting body characteristics, such as milky white color, larger fruiting bodies (150–230 mm in diameter), longer stalks, and more spines (90–110 spines per branch). ## Transcriptome changes in fruiting bodies of H. coralloides under different light treatments
The transcriptomes of the developing fruiting bodies, namely, those grown for four days after the appearance of the primordia under white, blue, and red light conditions, were analyzed to determine the expression of light response genes. Dramatic transcriptome divergence was found. Compared with white light treatment (White-4d), blue light treatment (Blue-4d) resulted in 10,827 DEGs (5836 upregulated genes and 4991 downregulated genes) (Fig. 3), accounting for 64.56% of all DEGs. Similarly, red-light treatment (Red-4d) led to 11,375 differentially expressed genes (6112 upregulated genes and 5263 downregulated genes) (Fig. 3), accounting for 67.81% of all expressed genes. These findings show that light significantly impacts the transcriptome of H. coralloides at the developing fruiting body stage.Fig. 3Number of DEGs at two developmental stages. The fruiting bodies that grew for eight days after the appearance of the primordia had reached their final size and did not continue to grow. At this stage, the transcriptome of fruiting bodies was also analyzed to assess the expression of light response genes. Compared with white light treatment (White-8d), blue light treatment (Blue-8d) led to 10,926 differentially expressed genes (5861 upregulated genes and 5065 downregulated genes) (Fig. 3), representing 65.14% of all expressed genes. Consistent with the earlier observations, the red light-treated fruiting bodies (Red-8d) showed similar trends. Compared with white light treatment (White-8d), red light treatment (Red-8d) led to 10,251 differentially expressed genes (5456 upregulated genes and 4795 downregulated genes) (Fig. 3), accounting for 61.11% of all expressed genes. Notably, despite the fruiting bodies having reached their final size and ceased growth, light continues to significantly impact a large portion of the H. coralloides transcriptome.
Most transcriptome changes induced by blue and red-light treatments were quite similar; specifically, Blue-4d and Red-4d treatments resulted in 5836 and 6112 upregulated genes, respectively. Remarkably, a substantial proportion of these upregulated genes (92.34% in Blue-4d and 88.17% in Red-4d) were shared in both treatments (Fig. 4a). Additionally, blue and red light treatment resulted in the downregulation of 4991 and 5263 genes in Blue-4d and Red-4d groups, respectively. Among these, 4716 genes (94.50% in Blue-4d and 89.60% in Red-4d) were shared (Fig. 4a). Blue and red light treatment upregulated 5861 and 5456 genes in Blue-8d and Red-8d, respectively, with 4438 (81.34% in Blue-8d and 75.72% in Red-8d) being shared (Fig. 4b). Moreover, blue and red light treatment resulted in the downregulation of 5065 and 4795 genes in Blue-8d and Red-8d groups, respectively. Among these downregulated genes, 4051 (84.49% in Blue-8d and 79.99% in Red-8d) were shared (Fig. 4b).Fig. 4Venn diagram illustrating the number of light response genes shared between the blue and red light treatments at different developmental stages. a Number of upregulated genes between Blue-4d and Red-4d. While most transcriptome changes induced by blue and red-light treatments were shared, each light treatment also led to unique transcriptome changes. Specifically, blue light treatment uniquely upregulated 447 and 1423 genes in Blue-4d and Blue-8d, respectively (Fig. 4a), and downregulated 275 and 1014 genes in Blue-4d and Blue-8d, respectively (Fig. 4b). Additionally, red light treatment uniquely upregulated 723 and 1018 genes in Red-4d and Red-8d (Fig. 4a), and downregulated 547 and 744 in Red-4d and Red-8d (Fig. 4b). Thus, light response genes can be categorized into two groups: genes that respond to both blue or red light, regardless of the wavelength of light, and genes that specifically respond to either blue or red light.
Although blue and red light treatments led to similar gene expression changes at both development stages, the expression changes overlapped less between stages than at the same stage (Fig. 4a, b). For instance, blue light treatment upregulated 5836 and 5861 genes in Blue-4d and Blue-8d, respectively, with 3039 (52.07% in Blue-4d and 51.86% in Blue-8d) were commonly upregulated (Fig. 4a). Similarly, blue light treatment downregulated 4991 and 5065 genes in Blue-4d and Blue-8d, respectively, with 3054 (61.90% in Blue-4d and 60.30% in Blue-8d) were shared (Fig. 4b). Similar results were found for the red-light treatment. Compared to the white light treatment, 6112 and 5456 genes were upregulated in the Red-4d and Red-8d treatment groups, respectively. Among these, 3445 (56.36% in the Red-4d and 63.14% in the Red-8d) were upregulated (Fig. 4a). Additionally, 5263 and 4795 genes were downregulated in the Red-4d and Red-8d treatment groups, respectively. Of these, 3422 genes (65.02% in the Red-4d and 71.37% in Red-8d) were shared (Fig. 4b).

## Gene Ontology analysis of light response genes
Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed to determine if the light response genes were associated with specific functions. The DEGs in the Blue-4d vs. White-4d, Blue-8d vs. White-8d, Red-4d vs. White-4d, and Red-8d vs. White-8d groups were involved in nearly all aspects of GO ontologies, including biological process, cellular component, and molecular function (Supplemental Figs. 1–4). This suggests that both blue and red light broadly impact the transcriptome of H. coralloides.
Under blue light, upregulated genes in the Blue-4d vs. White-4d were enriched in GO terms related to response to stimulus, DNA metabolic process, positive regulation of cell cycle, cell cycle phase transition, chromosome, and chromosome localization (Fig. 5a). Conversely, downregulated genes in the Blue-4d vs. White-4d were enriched in GO terms related to small molecule metabolism, RNA biosynthesis processes, gene expression regulation, molecular binding activities, and catalytic functions (Fig. 5b). The GO enrichment results revealed that exposure to blue light upregulated growth-related processes such as DNA replication, chromosome dynamics, and cell division. Simultaneously, it downregulated other biological processes like RNA biosynthetic processes, the regulation of gene expression, and binding and catalytic activity. The GO enrichment terms for light-responsive genes in the Blue-8d showed distinct patterns compared to those light-responsive genes in Blue-4d (Supplemental Fig. 5, 6). The genes that showed specifically upregulation expression in the Blue-8d vs. White-8d comparison were enriched in GO terms related to single-organism metabolic process, response to stimulus, catalytic activity, and membrane (Supplemental Fig. 5). ## Discussion
Light plays an essential regulatory role in the development of fruiting bodies in many fungi, such as Sparassis latifolia [33], P. eryngii [32, 34], H. marmoreus [27], Coprinus cinereus [35], P. ostreatus [28, 36], L. edodes [25, 37], and C. militaris [29]. Blue light regulates various processes, including carotenoid biosynthesis, spore formation, phototropism, photoadaptation, and the circadian clock in fungi [38, 39]. The effect of light on fruiting body differentiation in basidiomycetous fungi has been extensively studied. For example, blue light is essential for helping develop high-quality fruiting bodies in H. marmoreus [27] and is essential for fruiting body differentiation in Grifola frondosa [40]. Our results showed that different light treatments, such as blue and red light, can also affect the morphology and yield of H. coralloides fruiting bodies, which has not been previously reported for H. coralloides. The blue light treatment increased the yield of H. coralloides, consistent with the results for P. ostreatus [28] and L. edodes [25]. Furthermore, combined light treatments may also lead to better yields, as seen in C. militaris [30], where pink light (a combination of 1/3 blue light and 2/3 red light) resulted in a higher dry weight (40.06 g) compared to blue light (24.44% dry matter) and red light (22.69% dry matter). Improving features and increasing yields are top priorities for the industrial cultivation of H. coralloides, and our results will inform future cultivation practices. However, much effort should be made in the context of light treatment in future cultivation practices for H. coralloides.
Although it has been established that light plays a critical role in fungal growth and development, the specific genes responsible for light response in fungi have not been extensively studied. There is limited information on how various types of light affect the transcriptome of fungi and how many genes can be affected by light. Several genome-wide expression analyses have provided novel insights into light sensing in fungi [28, 34, 41–43]. It should be noted that fungi have up to 11 photoreceptors and can sense near-ultraviolet, blue, green, red, and far-red light [21]. Blue, white, and red light altered gene expression after treatment of the fruiting bodies of the H. coralloides, indicating that there is more than one type of photoreceptor in this species.
In H. coralloides, the characterization of photoreceptor genes, which play a crucial role in modulating light-mediated development, remains uncharted. The diversity of photoreceptors, characterized by chromophores, presents a significant challenge for identification. Furthermore, the intricate signaling pathways that photoreceptors initiate, such as protein–protein interactions and phosphorylation events, add another layer of complexity [21]. These pathways, which are initiated by photoreceptors, are vital for the growth of H. coralloides fruiting bodies, although their specific functions have not yet been conclusively established. Given the current gaps in our understanding, a systematic approach is warranted to elucidate the roles of photoreceptors and their downstream signaling mechanisms in H. coralloides. This endeavor, which is beyond the scope of the current manuscript, is essential for future research aimed at identifying light-responsive genes that influence yield and morphology. Our work lays the groundwork for future studies that will delve into the intricacies of light response pathways and signaling mechanisms in H. coralloides. Forthcoming research will benefit from a multifaceted approach, encompassing molecular biology, genetics, and systems biology, to comprehensively understand the complex network of light-responsive genes and their regulatory roles in morphogenesis and yield.
Blue and red light treatments were the most commonly used light conditions. Blue light treatment caused the differentiation of 539 differentially expressed genes from the primordium stage to the fruiting body stage in P. eryngii [34]. Another study at this stage shows that blue light, red light, and dark treatments led to 4561 to 6405 differentially expressed genes [42]. In the stipe, pileus, and gill of P. ostreatus, blue and red light treatments resulted in 1145 and 834 differentially expressed genes, respectively [28]. At the mycelia stage of C. militaris, blue light treatment led to 1722 differentially expressed genes [41]. In addition to blue and red light treatment, white light treatment was also used to treat fungi, as shown in the mycelium of Ophiocordyceps sinensis, which resulted in 3250 differentially expressed genes [16]. Different light intensities have also been found to affect the morphology of A. heimuer, resulting in 1388 differentially expressed genes in the fruiting body [43]. In H. coralloides, different light treatments induced up to 11,375 differentially expressed genes in developing fruiting bodies, accounting for 67.81% of all expressed genes. Even though the fruiting bodies have grown to their final size and will not continue to grow, different light treatments could also induce up to 10,926 differentially expressed genes in the grow-up fruiting bodies, accounting for 65.14% of all expressed genes. These significant numbers of light response genes have never been reported in fungi, implying that light may have a more extensive impact on fungi than previously thought.
Gene Ontology analysis of these light response genes revealed that nearly all GO terms associated with biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions were involved, indicating that light affects nearly all aspects of H. coralloides life (Supplemental Figs. 1–4). These results suggest that the influence of light on fungi may have been underestimated. Dramatic differences in light response genes were discovered between C. militaris [41], H. coralloides (as per this study), O. sinensis [16], P. eryngii [34, 42], P. ostreatus [28] and A. heimuer [43]. In H. coralloides (Fig. 4), the majority of changes in gene expression in response to blue and red light treatments are common, which is different from findings observed in P. eryngii [42] and P. ostreatus [28], where a smaller number of genes were affected. Fungi utilize light as a source of information [21]. These variations in the expression of light response genes among various fungi might be attributed to differences in light sensing and the various biological processes governed by light in these fungi, possibly reflecting their adaptation to different habitats or living strategies. Our results indicated that blue light could stimulate the growth of H. coralloides fruiting bodies, while red light did not have the same effect. It appears that the genes responsible for responding to blue light specifically regulated the growth stimulation in H. coralloides. Fungal response to light is mainly observed in carotenoid metabolism, polysaccharide, and carbohydrate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, nucleotide and nucleoside metabolism, and in the regulation of production of secondary metabolites [13], most of these light response processes are consistent with the results of our GO results. GO enrichment analysis of the genes whose expression was specifically upregulated by blue light suggests that the increase in yield induced by blue light may be achieved through the upregulation of growth-related processes such as DNA replication, chromosome, and cell division and the downregulation of other aspects of biological processes such as RNA biosynthetic processes, the regulation of gene expression, binding and catalytic activity. This reveals, possibly for the first time, a possible mechanism underlying the growth of H. coralloides stimulated by blue light.
In the current study, we did not include a dataset from the mycelium stage under different light conditions; which may result in some DEGs in fruiting body after the appearance of the primordia may be grouped into the basic life events in the fungal life cycle rather than specific functions in fruiting body development. In this study, we have proposed a possible mechanism underlying the growth of H. coralloides stimulated by blue light, which is achieved through the upregulation of growth-related processes. Incorporating data from the mycelium stage may help us distinguish whether the upregulation of growth-related processes are basic life events in the H. coralloides life cycle or has a specific function in the fruiting body development of H. coralloides, which could be carried out in future studies.
Blue light is used in the factory production of edible fungi, such as A. heimuer, P. ostreatus, H. marmoreus, Flammulina filiformis, which suggests that light, especially blue light, is indispensable in regulating many aspects of the growth and development of many edible fungi. In this study, blue light stimulated an increase of almost 38% compared with white light and led to superior features in the fruiting bodies of H. coralloides, significantly improving the production efficiency of H. coralloides. Usually, H. coralloides is cultivated industrially under white light conditions; however, according to our results, using blue light instead of white light is a better choice for getting superior morphologies and higher yields. However, according to the relevant literature, light intensity and duration are also important factors affecting the growth of the fruiting bodies of edible mushrooms. Light intensity and duration should be integrated in industrial mushroom cultivation to improve production efficiency and cost.

## Conclusions
The morphology of fruiting bodies was affected by different light treatments. The yield under blue-light treatment was 137.58% of that under white-light treatment, while red-light treatment did not yield significantly more than white light. Blue light stimulates the growth of H. coralloides fruiting bodies, while red light can also affect the fruiting body morphology but cannot improve the yield of H. coralloides. Different light treatments could lead to 10,827 to 11,375 differently expressed genes, accounting for 64.56% to 67.81% of all expressed genes. Light can affect a greater number of fungal genes than previously thought. Light affects nearly all aspects of H. coralloides life; the influence of light on fungi may be underestimated. The increase in yield caused by blue light may be due to the specific upregulation growth-related processes; while downregulating other biological processes. This study proves that light, particularly blue light, significantly influences the development and yield of H. coralloides fruiting bodies by modulating gene expression in cell proliferation, DNA synthesis, and chromosome. These findings underscore the complex regulatory networks controlled by light and offer valuable insights for optimizing cultivation practices to enhance the productivity and quality of this commercially important mushroom.

## Materials and methods

## Culture conditions and sample collection

## Liquid spawn preparation
The H. coralloides strain (JLAU_857) was obtained from the Mushroom Base of Jilin Agricultural University. For the pure culture of H. coralloides, the strain was maintained on potato dextrose agar (Combine the extract of 200 g potato, 20 g of glucose, and 20 g of agarose in 1000 mL of water.) and incubated at 25 °C for 5‒7 days. Then the agar with mycelium was cut into 1 × 1 cm pieces, and 8 pieces were placed into broth (500 mL) and shaken culture (160 rpm) at 25℃ for 6 to 7 days. The liquid medium consisted of 10% potato, 5% sawdust, 4.5% wheat bran, 2% maize flour, 0.3% yeast extract, 0.1% potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 0.05% magnesium sulfate, 0.01% vitamin B2, and 0.03% glycerin. Sterile water was added to make the total 1000 mL, and the pH was adjusted to 5.6 [44].

## Mycelium culture
A total of 20 mL of liquid spawn was inoculated into a sterilized polyethylene bottle containing 700 g of hardwood sawdust media. The sawdust media comprised 37% wood chips, 24% cottonseed hulls, 20% wheat bran, 19% maize flour, 1% gypsum powder, 0.01% vitamin B2, 0.01% potassium phosphate, with a moisture content of 65% and pH 5.6. The mixture was then placed in an incubator at 25 °C for 23–25 days. Each bottle contained 700 g of medium. Throughout the mycelium culture, the environmental conditions were maintained at 25°C, with air humidity ranging from 55 to 60%, and CO2 levels between 719.83 – 1799.58 mg/m3, without exposure to light.

## Fruiting body culture after light exposure and sample collection
After 25 days of incubation, the environmental parameters were adjusted to 10 °C, 90% air humidity, and 539.88–1439.67 mg/m3 CO2 for two days to stimulate primordia formation. Following this, the newly emerged primordia were exposed to blue, red, and white light for 6 h per day during the subsequent incubation process. Light exposure was conducted using an LED blue lighting unit (430–470 nm), an LED red lighting unit (610–640 nm), and an LED white lighting unit in the cultivation room. The light intensity was 3.6 µmol/m2.s. After growing for eight days following the appearance of the primordia, the fruiting body of H. coralloides reached its final size and ready to be harvested.
To assess whether there were significant differences in the yield of H. coralloides fruiting bodies under different light treatments, we set three treatments (blue light, red light, and white light), with 8–15 replicates for each treatment. The fresh weight of the fruiting bodies after the eighth day of light treatment was assessed, and white light was used as the control, and ANOVA was performed (Supplementary Table 1).
After the appearance of the primordia, the fruiting bodies were grown for four days under blue (Blue-4d), red (Red-4d), and white light (White-4d), the fruiting bodies also grown for eight days under blue (Blue-8d), red (Red-8d), and white light (White-8d). RNA was then extracted from three sets of biological replicates, which resulted in a total of 18 samples. For each biological replicate, 6 individuals were collected and mixed for RNA extraction. Samples for transcriptome were taken from two stages: young fruiting body (day 4 after primordia emergence [45]), and mature fruiting body (day 8 after primordia emergence). Ssampling on the eighth day is chosen because, in actual production, the H. coralloides fruiting body complete their growth process 7–8 days after the emergence of the primordium.

## RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and Illumina sequencing
Total RNA was extracted from the fruiting bodies using the RNA prep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE), and RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). One microgram of RNA was used for the library preparation. The sequencing libraries were generated using the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd.) in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Illumina sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and Illumina sequencing were performed by Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China). Approximately 6 GB of clean data were obtained for each sample, and the original data have been deposited into the NCBI SRA database under accession number PRJNA987750.

## Differentially expressed gene analysis
The raw reads underwent quality check using the NGS QC Toolkit v2.3.3 [46]. Subsequently, the clean data was aligned to the H. coralloides reference genome (PRJNA998484) via HISAT2 software [47] and then assembled with StringTie [48]. The FPKM value was used to define the transcript abundance of each gene [49], and DESeq2 was used to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a threshold of FDR ≤ 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2 [50]. For all differentially expressed gene analyses, white light-treated samples from the same growth period served as controls.

## Gene ontology analysis
The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the Singular Enrichment Analysis tool implemented in agriGO (http://bioinfo.cau.edu.cn/agriGO/index.php) [41, 51]. The Singular Enrichment Analysis (SEA) tool was used to perform the GO annotations and GO term enrichment analysis. Fisher’s exact test was used for statistical analysis; GO terms with a Hochberg FDR P value < 0.05 and enrichment greater than 50% were considered significantly enriched [52].
