ABSTRACT
Maternal effects are widely documented in animals and plants, but not in fungi or other

4	  

eukaryotes. A principal cause of maternal effects is asymmetrical parental investment in a

5	  

zygote, creating greater maternal versus paternal influence on offspring phenotypes.

6	  

Asymmetrical investments are not limited to animals and plants, but are also prevalent in fungi

7	  

and groups including apicocomplexans, dinoflagellates, and red algae. Evidence suggesting

8	  

maternal effects among fungi is sparse and anecdotal. In an experiment designed to test for

9	  

maternal effects across sexual reproduction in the model fungus Neurospora crassa, we

10	  

measured offspring phenotypes from crosses of all possible pairs of 22 individuals. Crosses

11	  

encompassed reciprocals of 11 mating-type “A” and 11 mating-type “a” wild strains. After

12	  

controlling for the genetic and geographic distances between strains in any individual cross, we

13	  

found strong evidence for maternal control of sporocarp production, as well as maternal effects

14	  

on spore numbers and spore germination. However, both parents exert equal influence on the

15	  

percentage of spores that are pigmented, and size of pigmented spores. We propose a model

16	  

linking the stage-specific presence or absence of maternal effects to cellular developmental

17	  

processes: effects appear to be mediated primarily through the maternal cytoplasm, and, after

18	  

spore cell walls form, maternal influence on spore development is limited. Maternal effects in

19	  

fungi, thus far largely ignored, are likely to shape species’ evolution and ecology. The

20	  

association of anisogamy and maternal effects in a fungus suggests maternal effects may also

21	  

influence the biology of other anisogamous eukaryotes.

INTRODUCTION

2	  

Mothers influence offspring phenotypes not only by transmitting genes but also by

3	  

directly altering the course of offspring development. Maternal influences extend beyond

4	  

inherited nuclear chromosomes. For example, they include cytoplasmic provisioning and the

5	  

environment provided by the mother during embryogenesis. These are named maternal effects

6	  

(Roach & Wulff, 1987). The consequences of maternal effects for evolution are diverse.

7	  

Maternal effects can alter the direction and strength of trait selection by introducing sources of

8	  

variation outside of an individual’s genes (Kirkpatrick & Lande, 1989). Maternal effects enable

9	  

the environmental influences on mothers to alter the development of her offspring, effectively

10	  

transferring environmental influences across generations (Galloway, 2005; Badyaev & Uller,

11	  

2009; Donohue, 2009). In clonal organisms, environmental maternal effects can increase

12	  

offspring heterogeneity (Sakwinska, 2004).

13	  

A basis for maternal effects can be traced to a common phenomenon apparent in most

14	  

animals and plants: the female gamete is much larger than the male gamete, enabling a greater

15	  

female influence on offspring (Westneat & Craig Sargent, 1996). When differently sized gametes

16	  

fuse, maternally derived cytoplasm predominates in the zygote (maternal cytoplasmic

17	  

inheritance) and disproportionately influences zygote development (Birky, 2001; Gosden, 2002).

18	  

Maternal effects are also found in species where offspring develop inside or on the mother, for

19	  

example, where an animal develops inside a womb (Lindström, 1999), or seeds develop in a fruit

20	  

attached to a plant (Roach & Wulff, 1987).

21	  

While breeders have long known that mothers exert more influence on offspring than

22	  

fathers (Brooks, 1905), maternal effects were first carefully described using quantitative

23	  

approaches in animals (Walton & Hammond, 1938) and more recently in plants (Roach & Wulff,

1987; Mousseau et al., 2009). Maternal effects are rarely discussed outside of these kingdoms.

2	  

The basic mechanisms causing maternal effects in species of these paradigmatic kingdoms—

3	  

anisogamy leading to disproportionate cytoplasmic inheritance and offspring development in

4	  

maternally derived environments—are also found among a variety of other eukaryotes, including

5	  

apicocomplexans, dinoflagellates, diatoms, red algae (Dacks & Kasinsky, 1999), and fungi

6	  

(Jinks, 1963; Billiard et al., 2010; Debuchy et al., 2010). Within the fungi, the prerequisites for

7	  

maternal effects are commonly found among filamentous species in the Ascomycota, and more

8	  

rarely, in the Basidiomycota (Billiard et al., 2010). Most species in the Ascomycota have distinct

9	  

male and female structures, whereas there is very little morphological specialization of sex

10	  

organs in the Basidiomycota. Anisogamy involving unequal parental investment in offspring has

11	  

been proposed as a basis for sexual selection in fungi (Nieuwenhuis & Aanen, 2012).

12	  

The genetic model Neurospora crassa (Fig. 1) is a well characterized and experimentally

13	  

tractable filamentous Ascomycete (Roche et al., 2014), and it provides an optimal system for

14	  

tests of maternal effects in fungi. The female gamete-forming organs (protoperithecia) are much

15	  

larger (up to 100 µm in diameter) than the male gametes (2.5-8.5 µm in diameter) and offspring

16	  

development takes place in a maternally produced “fruiting body,” or sporocarp (perithecium).

17	  

N. crassa is hermaphroditic, enabling reciprocal crosses to test for maternal effects.

18	  

To our knowledge, no breeding or quantitative genetics experiments have investigated

19	  

maternal effects in N. crassa or any other fungus, but post hoc evidence of reciprocal differences

20	  

in fertility among N. crassa strains suggests the presence of strong maternal control over

21	  

reproductive development. While every strain of N. crassa is hermaphroditic—and can function

22	  

as either a female or a male or both—there are mutant N. crassa strains with phenotypes that are

23	  

only apparent when the strain acts as the maternal parent; these kinds of mutations cause defects

in female gamete formation or fertilization and result in reciprocal differences in fertility (Raju,

2	  

1992). Experiments with wild strains of N. crassa also document reciprocal differences in

3	  

fitness, specifically, the ability to form perithecia suggests protoperithecial formation and

4	  

fertilization are influenced by the maternal parent even among wild fungi (Dettman et al., 2003b;

5	  

Turner et al., 2010). Reciprocal crosses between experimentally evolved Neurospora strains also

6	  

show maternal influence on mating success (Dettman et al., 2008).

7	  

Studies to date prove that female structures (protoperithecia and perithecia) in N. crassa

8	  

are truly female and play a dominant role in the sexual cycle of this fungus. However, by

9	  

definition, maternal effects act on offspring and can only be determined if offspring and their

10	  

traits are measured. Although many mutations affecting the production of sexual spores (termed

11	  

ascospores, and hereafter referred to as “spores”) have been identified in N. crassa, these

12	  

mutations either segregate after meiosis or are only expressed in crosses homozygous for the

13	  

allele, indicating they are direct genetic effects and not maternal effects (Leslie & Raju, 1985;

14	  

Raju et al., 1987; Raju & Leslie, 1992).

15	  

Experiments testing for maternal effects among fungi are long overdue. Fungi are critical

16	  

drivers of ecosystem function, with roles as pathogens, mutualists, and decomposers. The diverse

17	  

evolutionary consequences of maternal effects documented among animals and plants are likely

18	  

equally relevant to anisogamous fungi. The majority of fungal pathogens are Ascomycetes

19	  

(Guarro et al., 1999) and many of the most damaging plant diseases, including rice blast

20	  

(Magnaporthe oryzae) and grey mould (Botrytis cinerea) (Dean et al., 2012) have life cycles

21	  

very similar to the life cycle of N. crassa (Faretra et al., 1988; Saleh et al., 2012). If maternal

22	  

effects play a role in the evolutionary trajectories or ecological niches of these economically

23	  

relevant species, documenting the phenomena involved may provide novel tools to understand

pathogen biology. Moreover, testing for maternal effects among fungi provides an opportunity to

2	  

extend theory generated from two groups, animals and plants, to diverse eukaryotes.

3	  

To test whether maternal effects occur in Neurospora crassa, we mated genotyped strains

4	  

in a fully and reciprocally crossed experiment. Strains were originally isolated from a diversity

5	  

of sites across the globe. We used generalized linear mixed effects models to quantify the effects

6	  

of crossing distances (the genetic and geographic distances between parents in any particular

7	  

cross) and the variation in strains’ influence, as mothers or fathers, on traits (Table 1) associated

8	  

with the sexual ontogeny of the fungus. Geographic and genetic differences among strains may

9	  

influence reproductive success because, for example, crossing closely related parents results in

10	  

inbreeding depression or crossing distantly related parents results in outbreeding depression

11	  

(Lynch, 1991). Our analyses document clear maternal effects on offspring phenotypes, but the

12	  

presence and strength of these effects varies dynamically across development.

METHODS
Mating scheme and strain choice

15	  

We used a full diallel cross design to quantify parental effects in N. crassa. In a full

16	  

diallel, all possible combinations of parents are mated, along with reciprocals (i.e. each strain is

17	  

used as a mother, then a father, with every other compatible strain). The mating design

18	  

maximizes the statistical power for estimating maternal, paternal, and direct genetic effects on

19	  

offspring (Zhu & Weir, 1996). To control for the effects of crossing distances on reproductive

20	  

output, we first identified a set of 24 mat-A and 24 mat-a wild isolates of N. crassa (Fig. 2, Table

21	  

2), previously genotyped with RNAseq (Ellison et al., 2011).

22	  

We used the SNP data (Ellison et al., 2011) to calculate pairwise genetic distances

23	  

between all possible pairs of strains, measured as number of differences across all SNP sites. For

each of the 48 strains, we obtained collection site information from the Fungal Genetic Stocks

2	  

Center strain catalog (FGSC, www.fgsc.net) and calculated geographic distances between strain

3	  

collection sites.

4	  

The genotyped population included 24 strains of each mating type, but generating all

5	  

possible crosses with these 48 strains (a total of 576 crosses) was beyond our experimental

6	  

capacity. Using the SPREAD algorithm (Zimmerman et al., 2015), we selected a mating set of

7	  

11 mat-a and 11 mat-A strains for use in our study. SPREAD identifies the subset of crosses

8	  

most likely to mimic the original distribution of the entire set of all potential crosses. Briefly:

9	  

SPREAD selects a crossing-set from 1000 randomly generated crossing-sets (11 mat-a × 11 mat-

10	  

A) by maximizing the mean nearest neighbor distance of the subset of 121 crosses plotted

11	  

according to the geographic vs. genetic distances among crosses. We obtained the 22 strains

12	  

chosen by this algorithm (Table 2) from the FGSC (McCluskey et al., 2010).

13	  

Generating conidia for crosses

14	  

We inoculated each strain onto a slant of Vogel’s Medium N (Vogel, 1956) made with

15	  

2% sucrose and 1.5% agar. We harvested conidia from these cultures and preserved them on

16	  

silica gel for subsequent experiments. To grow conidia for crosses we used conidial stocks to

17	  

inoculate 50 mL narrow neck flasks containing 7 mL of Vogel’s Medium N with 2% sucrose,

18	  

1.5% agar, 0.1% yeast extract, and 0.1% casamino acids, incubated at 30°C with 12h/12h

19	  

light/dark illumination for 7 days. We harvested conidial inocula for crosses with 10 mL diH2O

20	  

and filtered suspensions through non-absorbent cotton to remove extraneous mycelia.

21	  

Crossing conditions and spore collection

22	  

We grew four replicate reciprocal crosses of each of the 121 strain pairs for a total of (4

23	  

replicates) × (121 mating pairs) × (2 reciprocals) = 968 crosses. We performed all crosses using

1	  

paper crossing media (Pandit & Russo, 1993) with slight modifications: we prepared crossing

2	  

plates by placing 4 × 3.5cm gel blot paper squares in 5 cm petri dishes and adding 1 mL of

3	  

synthetic crossing media (Westergaard & Mitchell, 1947) containing 0.1% sucrose, 0.5%

4	  

sorbose, 0.1% yeast extract and 0.1% casamino acids. We grew each of the 22 strains as a female

5	  

by evenly dropping 200 µL of a suspension containing 1×106 conidia in diH2O onto the paper.

6	  

We incubated plates in their plastic sleeves in the dark, at 25°C for 48 hours, and then fertilized

7	  

the plates by evenly dropping 200 µL of a suspension containing 1x106 conidia in diH2O onto the

8	  

filter paper. Because these conidia are dropped onto individuals already growing as females, they

9	  

will function as the male gametes. We incubated the fertilized crosses at 25°C in darkness for 20

10	  

days, except for occasional exposures to light during routine inspections. After incubation, we

11	  

harvested spores from lids with 1mL of diH2O and deposited spores in deep 96-well plates. To

12	  

allow for complete maturation of the spores, and to standardize spore age among crosses, we

13	  

stored the harvested spores at 4°C for 16 days before flow cytometry analyses of spore counts,

14	  

morphology, and germination.

15	  

Counting perithecia

16	  

We photographed all crosses at time of ascospore harvest for subsequent counting of

17	  

perithecia. Using the photographs, we determined total counts of perithecia per cross by manual

18	  

counting and marking in ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012).

19

20

21	  

cytometry to characterize spore samples collected from the crosses. In a single run of a spore

22	  

sample through the flow cytometer, we counted spores in the sample and measured the size and

Next, we added Spherotech Accucount 7.7 µm fluorescent beads (Spherotech Inc., Lake Forest,

10	  

IL) to the spore suspension in 50 µL of 0.4% Triton X-100 (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis,

16

17

19	  

width, and area for every spore in the sample volume (50 µL). The data from each sample were

20

Flow cytometry data processing

20	  

Our initial analysis of the flow cytometry data indicated the presence of both conidia and

21	  

ascospores, with ascospores exhibiting two clear sub-populations of pigmented and hyaline

22	  

(unpigmented) ascospores (Fig. S1). Hyaline ascospores are demarcated by greater forward

23	  

scatter height values, compared to pigmented ascospores; because they are clear they scatter light

1	  

differently and exhibit higher light transmittance. There was some overlap of conidia and

2	  

ascospores when plotted on forward-scatter-height vs. side-scatter-width axes, and the overlap

3	  

prevented manual two-dimensional gating. In order to differentiate the conidia and ascospores,

4	  

we performed multi-dimensional clustering analyses with all measured flow cytometery

5	  

parameters, using a custom clustering and sorting pipeline in R with the following packages:

6	  

flowCore (Ellis et al., n.d.), flowClust (Lo et al., 2008; 2009), and flowStats (Hahne et al., n.d.).

7	  

FCS files were imported into R using flowCore and sets of FCS files for each plate were

8	  

merged into a single dataset to facilitate clustering by increasing the density of measurements.

9	  

To distinguish the populations of conidia, pigmented ascospores, and hyaline ascospores, we

10	  

performed k-means clustering using the flowClust package. We manually adjusted the number of

11	  

clusters to be identified for each dataset to obtain clustered datasets whose clusters matched sub-

12	  

population characteristics obtained from analyzing pure conidia or pure ascospores. We then

13	  

computed summary statistics using flowStats for each measured sample including the total

14	  

number of ascospores, the number of pigmented spores, the number of hyaline ascospores, and

15	  

the size of pigmented ascospores. Fluorescent bead populations were identified based on their

16	  

high fluorescence parameter values (fluorescence-area). To standardize ascospore counts in

17	  

subsequent analyses, we used the flow cytometry sample measurement time of each sample. To

18	  

standardize spore size across samples we used the mean side-scatter width of the fluorescent

19	  

beads for each sample.

20	  

To calculate ascospore germination, we first drew rectangular gates around populations

21	  

of spores from pre-germination ascospore samples plotted on forward scatter height vs. side

22	  

scatter width. We applied these gates, representing the size of pre-germination ascospores, to the

23	  

post-germination ascospore samples and counted the ascospores outside of the previously drawn

germination sample).

Verification of flow cytometry methods

11	  

We verified the ability of our flow cytometry analysis to measure ascospore pigmentation

12	  

and germination by performing traditional counting and plating assays for a subsample of the

13	  

crosses used in the full crossing experiment. In a separate experiment, we replicated a subset of

14

Mixed effects models

4	  

We used the R package glmmADMB to compute mixed effects models for each trait

5	  

(Fournier et al., 2012; Skaug et al., 2013). Based on preliminary analyses of the trait data

6	  

distributions, we chose to model pigmented spore size with a Gaussian distribution and all other

7	  

response variables with a negative binomial distribution. Our initial models included the

8	  

following fixed effects: genetic distance (number of different SNPs between parents in a cross),

9	  

geographic distance (km between strain locales), the squares of both genetic and geographic

10	  

distance, and an interaction term for genetic and geographic distance. In each model, we also

11	  

included maternal and paternal parentage as random effects, specifically to test the hypothesis

12	  

that strains will vary in their ability, as mothers or fathers, to influence the target traits. For all

13	  

five phenotypic traits we computed full models and then selected simplified final models using

14	  

the quasi Akaike Information Criterion (qAIC) or Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) for

15	  

models with negative binomial or Gaussian response variable distributions, respectively.

16	  

Best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs), also referred to as random effect coefficients or

17	  

conditional modes, are model based estimates of the relative influence of a strain as a mother or a

18	  

father on a given phenotype, after controlling for fixed effects. For each trait, we calculated the

19	  

relative proportions of overall random effect variance attributed to the variance of maternal or

20	  

paternal BLUPs and assessed correlations between maternal and paternal BLUPs.

Highest posterior density of parameter estimates

10	  

We verified the significance of parameter values in each selected model by calculating

11	  

HPD intervals for parameter values. HPD intervals, also known as Bayesian confidence

12	  

intervals, are based on the observed data and indicate the range of values in which the true

13	  

parameter value is found with a 95% probability. HPD intervals of model parameter values that

14	  

do not span zero are deemed significant. We calculated HPD intervals of the parameters in each

15	  

model using the built-in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) functionality of glmmADMB that

16	  

uses parameter estimates calculated from the model as priors. We used a chain of 10000 MCMC

17	  

iterations and verified MCMC chain convergence by plotting the distributions of samples drawn

18	  

from the chain. Parameter estimates with HPD intervals that did not span zero were deemed

19	  

significant.

RESULTS

2	  

Offspring phenotypes from a full diallel cross show evidence of maternal effects in N.

3	  

crassa

4	  

All crosses resulted in perithecia and viable spores, except crosses involving strain FGSC

5	  

10968 (mat-a), which was infertile as both male and female and exhibited abnormal colony

6	  

growth with increased pigmentation of mycelia. We excluded crosses involving FGSC 10968

7	  

from all subsequent analyses.

8	  

Crosses involving different strains resulted in a diverse array of trait phenotypes, and

9	  

differences between reciprocal crosses were observed for most traits (Fig. 3). When crosses

10	  

involving a particular strain acting as a female are grouped, the maternal effects of different

11	  

strains are obvious. For example, when strain 10951 (mat-A) is used as a female, crosses appear

12	  

to have the same level of spore production no matter the identity of the male strain (Fig. 3C).

13	  

These kinds of patterns are also observed for data on pigmented spore germination, see for

14	  

example crosses involving 8850 (Fig. 3I) or 1165 (Fig. 3J). But when crosses involving a

15	  

particular strain acting as a male are grouped, patterns are more variable; for example, the

16	  

influence of strain 8783 (mat-A) when it is used as a male depends on the pairing: with 8845

17	  

(mat-a) many spores are produced, but with 1165 (mat-a), few spores are produced (Fig. 3D).

18	  

These patterns: a consistent maternal influence, and variable paternal influence, are not easily

19	  

observed within the data for proportion pigmented (Fig. 3E, F) or pigmented spore size (Fig. 3G,

20	  

H).

21

crosses, for example few spores are produced by crossing 10951 as female and 10961 as male

DISCUSSION

6	  

Maternal effects emerge as a major feature of the reproductive biology of Neurospora

7	  

crassa. We find strong evidence for maternal effects acting on spore production and spore

8	  

germination (Fig. 3). While phenotypes are consistent for any particular mother, they vary

9	  

widely among mothers, and fathers have no consistent influence (Roff, 1998). We find no

10	  

evidence for strong maternal effects on spore size or the proportion of pigmented spores (Fig. 3).

11	  

Our mixed effects models confirm the visual evidence by controlling for potentially

12	  

important confounding variables: genetic and geographic distances between parents. Crossing

13	  

distances account for a small proportion of the overall variation in reproductive outcomes,

14	  

suggesting that inbreeding and outbreeding depression do not substantially influence the success

15	  

of crosses among our collection of strains (Fig. 5 and Table 4).

16	  

In fact, after controlling for the fixed effects of crossing distances in our models, we find

17	  

that the random effects of parental strain identity account for most of the variance in

18	  

reproductive outcomes (Fig. 5). Furthermore, variation among strains acting as mothers

19	  

accounted for most of the explained variation in our models of perithecial production, spore

20	  

production, and spore germination (Fig. 6). Perithecial production appears to be under strong

21	  

maternal control (Raju, 1992; Turner et al., 2010), while maternal effects appear to play a

22	  

dominant role in spore production and spore germination. We distinguish perithecial production

	  

17	  

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/028092; this version posted October 2, 2015. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not
certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under
a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

1	  

as under maternal control—rather than influenced by maternal effects—because maternal effects

2	  

can only be determined by measuring offspring phenotypes.

3	  

Maternal effects do not play a role in spore size or pigmentation; these traits are equally

4	  

influenced by both parents in a cross, regardless of their roles as mother or father. In fact the

5	  

effect of each strain acting as a mother is highly correlated with that strain's effect when it acts as

6	  

a father (Fig. 7), and mothers exert no more influence than fathers (Fig. 6). Taken together, the

7	  

analyses suggest that spore size and pigmentation are controlled mainly by direct genetic effects;

8	  

the large proportion of variance not explained by crossing distance or parental effects for both

9	  

traits (92.92% and 87.16%) is consistent with direct genetic effects caused by specific allelic

10	  

variants at one or more loci.

11	  

Observed maternal effects are not an artifact of correlations in trait values, and the

12	  

number of perithicia generated by a cross does not predict subsequent spore phenotypes.

13	  

Perithecial production, spore production, and spore germination are not correlated with each

14	  

other (Fig. S4).

15	  

Mapping maternal effects onto reproductive development: A model

16	  

The dynamic maternal effects we document are a clear reflection of N. crassa ontogeny,

17	  

and correlate with what is known about the species’ reproduction (Fig. 8). Maternal effects

18	  

influence spore production, but are not a feature of spore maturation; as spores germinate,

19	  

maternal effects are observed once again. Spore production takes place within maternal tissues,

20	  

while spore maturation occurs after spore walls separate developing spores from maternal

21	  

cytoplasm [38]. Germination draws on resources provided by the mother.

22	  
23	  

The apparent lack of maternal effects associated with spore maturation, and the
suggestion that the spore cell wall limits contact between the maternal cytoplasm and the

	  

18	  

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/028092; this version posted October 2, 2015. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not
certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under
a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

1	  

developing spore (Fig. 8), is supported by analyses of spore maturation mutants. These mutants

2	  

suggest that spore maturation is controlled by the genetic material inherited by each spore (Raju

3	  

& Leslie, 1992). Genes appear to be expressed autonomously within each of the spores of an

4	  

ascus (Freitag et al., 2004). Both studies suggest mothers have limited control over spore

5	  

maturation, instead, the genetic material of the spores themselves appears to be in direct control

6	  

of pigmentation and size.

7	  

Mechanisms causing the maternal effects associated with the germination of pigmented

8	  

spores are less clear, but possible conduits include the carbohydrates and lipids packaged within

9	  

spores. The raw materials used to form a spore are derived from the maternal parent.

10	  

Germinating spores use stored materials for energy production during germination, and the

11	  

amount of lipids in a spore can influence germination (Lingappa & Sussman, 1959; Budd et al.,

12	  

1966). Maternal strains may differ in their ability to supply carbohydrates and lipids to

13	  

developing spores, resulting in maternal effects. Maternal effects may also be mediated through

14	  

mitochondria. Mitochondrial influences do not qualify as maternal effects under at least one

15	  

definition (Wolf & Wade, 2009), because mitochondrial DNA is a property of the offspring and

16	  

not the mother. However, in germinating spores of Neurospora tetrasperma, a species closely

17	  

related to N. crassa (Dettman et al., 2003a), the maternal mitochondria are responsible for all

18	  

energy synthesis until 90 mins. after germination initiates, at which point a germinating spore

19	  

begins to synthesize its own mitochondria (Hill et al., 1992). Germination success may be

20	  

influenced by both the maternal provision of nutrients, as well as maternal supply of

21	  

mitochondria.

Potential for maternal effects in other fungi

2	  

Maternal effects are likely to influence the life histories of other anisogamous

3	  

ascomycetes (Billiard et al., 2010), as well as anisogamous chytrids, including species of

4	  

Allomyces and Monoblepharis (James et al., 2006). Maternal effects may also play a role in

5	  

basidiomycete life histories. While the majority of basidiomycetes do not produce specialized

6	  

sexual structures, when small and large haploid mycelia fuse, disproportionate cytoplasmic

7	  

inheritance (from the larger mycelium) can result (Griffiths, 1996).

8	  

Anisogamy and maternal effects in diverse eukaryotes

9	  

Anisogamy is commonly referenced as the basis for maternal effects and is widespread

10	  

among eukaryotes, but maternal effects are thoroughly studied in only two kingdoms—animals

11	  

and plants. Our discovery of maternal effects in N. crassa, an anisogamous fungus, suggests

12	  

anisogamy as a strong predictor of maternal effects across the eukaryotic domain.

13	  

Other anisogamous species are found in groups including apicocomplexans,

14	  

dinoflagellates, diatoms, parabasalans, and red algae (Dacks & Kasinsky, 1999). The red algae in

15	  

particular have life cycles very similar to filamentous anisogamous ascomycetes (Demoulin,

16	  

1985). Cytoplasmic inheritance in the malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum, an anisogamous

17	  

apicocomplexan, is predominantly female (Vaidya et al., 1993). We hypothesize that maternal

18	  

effects occur in, and have important implications for the evolution of, all of these anisogamous

19	  

taxa.

20	  

Conclusions

21	  

A simple crossing experiment suggests maternal effects profoundly influence

22	  

fundamental aspects of individual fitness in the genetic model N. crassa. The maternal influence

23	  

on spore production and viability is stronger than the influence of genetic divergence or

	  

20	  

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/028092; this version posted October 2, 2015. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not
certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under
a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

1	  

geographic isolation—two essential aspects of any cross thought to influence reproductive

2	  

success and speciation in all sexually reproducing organisms. Our experiment increases the

3	  

number of eukaryotic clades known to house organisms influenced by maternal effects from two,

4	  

the plants and animals, to three—the plants, animals, and fungi. Given the distribution of

5	  

anisogamy across many other eukaryotic clades, and the correlation between anisogamy and

6	  

maternal effects, we hypothesize that maternal effects influence the evolution and ecology of

7	  

additional, as yet untested, eukaryotic organisms.

Figure 1. Life cycle of Neurospora crassa. N. crassa is a haploid filamentous fungus capable of
reproducing both sexually and asexually (Davis, 2000). Sexual reproduction is restricted to
parent strains different at a single two-allele mating-type locus and a single strain is either mat-a
or mat-A. Both mating-types can produce female and male gametes; all strains are
hermaphroditic. For example, reciprocal fertilization between mat-a and mat-A strains is shown
at the top of the figure. Nitrogen starvation induces the production of protoperithecia, which are
female reproductive structures that house female gametes termed ascogonia. Asexual spores
termed conidia (or potentially other cells from a compatible mate) act as male gametes.
Fertilization follows the growth of a trichogyne—a sexually receptive protoperithecial hypha—
and its fusion with a conidium of the opposite mating type. After fusion, the paternal nucleus
migrates to an ascogonium within the protoperithecium. The division and segregation of
maternal and paternal nuclei results in the proliferation of cells each containing one maternal and
one paternal nucleus (a-c). Within these cells, each of the two nuclei fuse to form a diploid
zygote (d, e) that rapidly undergoes meiosis I (f) and meiosis II (g), producing four haploid
nuclei. These nuclei then undergo mitosis, resulting in eight haploid nuclei per ascus (h).
Cytoplasmic cleavage occurs (i) and a cell wall develops around each of the eight haploid nuclei,
forming eight ascospores (j). In normal ascospores, the cell wall becomes pigmented as it
matures; in aborted or undeveloped ascospores, the cell wall remains unpigmented (k). Spores
are forcibly ejected from a mature perithecium one ascus at a time. Nuclei are colored black or
white to show that they are derived from the mat-a or mat-A parent, respectively. All nuclei are
haploid (1N) except for the diploid (2N) half-black/half-white nuclei shown in steps d, e, and f.

Figure 8. Maternal effects vary dynamically across N. crassa reproductive development.
Spores are generated within maternally derived asci, and maternal effects play a role in the total
number of spores produced. Maternal effects do not influence spore pigmentation or size; these
phenotypes develop after spore walls separate the spores from maternal cytoplasm. Maternal
effects influence germination; germinating spores use maternally supplied nutrient stores.
