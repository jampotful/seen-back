Biostimulant-mediated tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses in cotton: the roles of seaweed and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus.

# Biostimulant-mediated tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses in cotton: the roles of seaweed and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus

## Abstract
AbstractIn cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) production, soil-borne pathogens and salinity stress are among the major environmental constraints that severely limit plant growth and productivity, particularly when they occur simultaneously. This study investigated the individual and combined effects of Verticillium wilt and salinity stress, as well as the potential of seaweed extract and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) to enhance stress tolerance in cotton. Under controlled conditions, the growth substrate was amended with 10% (v/v) AMF (Funneliformis mosseae) and a seaweed derived from Ascophyllum nodosum. Plants were exposed to Verticillium dahliae inoculation (1 × 106 spores mL−1) and 100 mM salinity stress under single and combined stress scenarios. Throughout the experiment, plant growth traits, physiological and biochemical indicators, ionic balance, antioxidant defense responses, mycorrhizal colonization, and rhizosphere soil properties were evaluated using an integrated approach. Combined stress conditions markedly intensified growth reductions, impaired membrane integrity, plant water status, and photosynthetic capacity, and disrupted ionic homeostasis due to excessive Na+ accumulation. Notably, under disease stress, biostimulant applications substantially suppressed disease severity, reducing values from nearly 60% to approximately 30%. Similarly, the pronounced effects of salinity observed under salinity and combined stress conditions were significantly alleviated by seaweed and AMF treatments. Overall, the combined application of seaweed extract and AMF emerges as an effective, environmentally friendly, and sustainable agronomic strategy to enhance cotton resilience in production systems exposed to multiple stress factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51742-1.Keywords: Biostimulants, Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), Seaweed, Verticillium dahliae, Salinity stressSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

## Introduction
Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is a strategic agricultural crop that forms the basis of global fiber production; however, its productivity is severely constrained by both biotic and abiotic stress factors1. Among these stresses, Verticillium wilt—primarily caused by vascular infections of Verticillium dahliae—leads to substantial yield losses and deterioration in fiber quality, while soil salinity has emerged as an increasingly serious threat due to climate change, improper irrigation practices, and progressive soil degradation2,3. One of the earliest and most visible effects of salinity stress in plants is stunted growth, characterized by reduced plant height, biomass accumulation, and overall developmental retardation. More importantly, the simultaneous occurrence of these two stress factors in agricultural production systems gives rise to combined stress scenarios that challenge the physiological and molecular limits of plant resilience.
Although plants regulate their defense responses to biotic and abiotic stresses largely through distinct signaling pathways, recent studies have demonstrated that these stresses converge on common cellular targets, including membrane integrity, water balance, photosynthetic capacity, and redox homeostasis4–6. Under combined stress conditions, plant responses are often more complex and less predictable than those observed under single stress factors, thereby limiting the effectiveness of conventional breeding strategies and chemical input–based control approaches3. In this context, the development of ecologically sustainable strategies to enhance plant stress tolerance has gained increasing importance.
In recent years, plant biostimulants have emerged at the forefront of this effort, attracting considerable attention for their capacity to improve plant performance under stress by regulating plant–soil–microbiome interactions7–9. Seaweed-based biostimulants, in particular, promote plant growth and mitigate stress-induced physiological damage through their content of phytohormone-like compounds, polysaccharides, phenolics, and osmoprotectants, while simultaneously activating antioxidant defense systems10,11. In parallel, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), through their symbiotic association with plant roots, not only enhance water and nutrient uptake but also function as powerful biological regulators that strengthen plant defense capacity against both biotic and abiotic stresses12,13.
Although the beneficial effects of seaweed and AMF applied individually on plant stress tolerance have been well documented, knowledge regarding their combined application—especially under conditions where disease and salinity stresses occur simultaneously—remains limited in cotton. Most existing studies have focused on single stress factors, whereas integrated investigations addressing plant growth, physiology, antioxidant defense responses, and mycorrhizal dynamics under combined stress conditions are still scarce14–16. This knowledge gap represents a critical research area for sustainable cotton production.
In this study, the effects of seaweed and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus (AMF) applications on cotton growth performance, physiological traits, antioxidant defense systems, and disease severity were systematically evaluated under Verticillium wilt, salinity stress, and their combination. The central hypothesis of this research is that the combined application of seaweed and AMF exerts a synergistic effect by alleviating stress-induced physiological damage, preserving plant water status and photosynthetic capacity, enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms, and suppressing disease severity more effectively than single applications. Within this framework, the study aims to determine whether this biostimulant combination provides a functional and mechanistic basis for integrated, environmentally friendly plant protection strategies under multiple stress conditions.

## Materials and methods

## Materials and experimental conditions
In this study, cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L., cultivar Beyaz Altın BA-440), an economically important and widely cultivated crop, was used as the test plant. The seeds used in the experiments were obtained from a certified agricultural supplier (Bursa Tohum Company, Türkiye). Verticillium dahliae (VD1) was employed as the test pathogen, while the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus (AMF) Funneliformis mosseae (BEG12) was selected as the beneficial microorganism. Both microbial materials were provided by the Plant Pathology Laboratory of Van Yüzüncü Yıl University (Türkiye). A commercial seaweed-based biostimulant containing Ascophyllum nodosum, widely used in agricultural production, was applied as the biostimulant treatment (Gübretaş Co., Türkiye). To induce salinity stress, sodium chloride (NaCl), a readily plant-available salt commonly used in controlled agricultural studies, was applied. Salinity stress was imposed at a concentration of 100 mM NaCl, which is considered a moderate to severe stress level for cotton according to the literatüre17,18.
The plant growth substrate consisted of a soil, sterilized pumice, and sand mixture at a ratio of 2:2:1 (v/v/v). Both soil and sand were passed through a 2-mm sieve and subsequently sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C for 1 h to eliminate microbial contamination. All experiments were conducted in a controlled growth chamber at the Faculty of Agriculture, Van Yüzüncü Yıl University (38° 34′ 03″ N, 43° 16′ 50″ E). Throughout the experimental period, temperature was maintained at 24 ± 2 °C with a relative humidity of approximately 60 ± 3%. Light conditions were provided by a combination of natural daylight and fluorescent lamps, ensuring a 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod.
All plant materials used in this study (Gossypium hirsutum L.) were obtained from commercially cultivated sources and do not involve any endangered or protected species. Therefore, no specific permits were required for plant material use. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations.

## Experimental design and treatments
The experiment was conducted in sterile plastic pots (3 L capacity; 16 × 18 cm). Prior to use, all pots were disinfected by immersion in a 2% (w/v) sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution for 5 min and subsequently rinsed thoroughly with sterile water. The AMF inoculum was homogeneously mixed into the growth substrate at a rate of 10% (v/v), corresponding to an approximate density of 50–70 spores g−1 soil. For the non-mycorrhizal control treatments, an equal volume of sterilized sand was added instead of the AMF inoculum. Prior to sowing, cotton seeds were surface-sterilized in a 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 3 min, followed by thorough rinsing with sterile distilled water, and then sown into the pots. After seedling emergence, seaweed, disease, and salinity stress treatments were applied according to plant phenological stages. The seaweed treatment was applied as a soil/root-zone drench using a solution prepared at a concentration of 2 mL L−1. Applications were initiated when seedlings reached the 2–3 true-leaf stage and were repeated twice at 10-day intervals. All treatments are listed in Table S1.
To induce disease stress, Verticillium dahliae inoculation was performed when plants reached the 4–5 true-leaf stage. The pathogen was applied as a conidial suspension adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ conidia mL−1 and delivered uniformly to the rhizosphere of each pot. Salinity stress was imposed 7 days after pathogen inoculation and applied gradually to avoid osmotic shock. Sodium chloride (NaCl) was supplied in four successive applications at 25 mM on alternate days until a final salinity level of 100 mM NaCl was reached. The experiment was terminated approximately 10 weeks after sowing, at which point plant growth, physiological, biochemical, mycorrhizal, and soil-related parameters were analyzed.

## Assessment of plant morphological parameters
At the end of the experiment, the morphological growth parameters of cotton plants were comprehensively evaluated. These included shoot and root fresh and dry biomass (g), shoot and root lengths (cm), stem diameter (mm), and leaf number (leaves plant−1). Fresh biomass was determined by weighing the harvested plant material using a precision analytical balance. For dry biomass determination, plant samples were oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h until a constant weight was achieved. Stem diameter measurements were performed using a digital caliper (Insize 1112–150, Germany).

## Assessment of mycorrhizal parameters
At the end of the experiment, mycorrhizal colonization in cotton roots was assessed using a modified version of the method described by Phillips and Hayman19. Root samples were sequentially cleared in 10% (w/v) KOH at 70 °C for 20 min, acidified in 10% (v/v) HCl, and stained with 0.05% lactophenol blue. Stained roots were preserved in lactoglycerol, and microscope slides were prepared for microscopic examination. Mycorrhizal frequency (%F) was calculated as the percentage of colonized root segments relative to the total number of observed segments. ## Assessment of plant biochemical responses and antioxidant enzyme activities
At the end of the experiment, 5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in methanol, and the extraction was carried out under dark conditions at + 4 °C for 14–16 h. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were used for biochemical analyses. Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method as described by by Swain and Hillis22. Briefly, 150 µL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 300 µL of 20% (w/v) sodium carbonate (Na2CO₃) solution were added to the extract samples, and the mixtures were incubated in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 725 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and TPC was calculated accordingly. Total antioxidant activity (TAA) was assessed using the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay following the method of Benzie and Strain23. For this purpose, 150 µL of extract was mixed with 2850 µL of FRAP reagent, incubated at 25 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance was recorded at 593 nm.

## Assessment of of mineral nutrient contents and ionic ratios
At the end of the experiment, shoot tissues of cotton seedlings were harvested, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, and oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h. The dried samples were finely ground to a homogeneous powder prior to analysis. For elemental determination, 0.5 g of plant material was subjected to wet digestion using nitric acid (HNO₃) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) following the procedures described by Jones and Case30. Potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and sodium (Na) concentrations in the digested solutions were quantified using a Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (FAAS)31. Total phosphorus (P) content was determined spectrophotometrically using the vanadomolybdophosphoric yellow color method32. The K/Na and Ca/Na ionic ratios were calculated from the measured K, Ca, and Na concentrations and used as indicators of ionic balance and salinity tolerance in cotton plants33.

## Assessment of soil parameters
Soil moisture content was determined using the gravimetric method described by Schmugge et al.34. Briefly, soil samples of known fresh weight were oven-dried at 105 °C until constant weight was achieved, and moisture content was calculated based on the loss in weight after drying. Soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were determined according to the method of Jackson35. For these measurements, a soil–deionized water suspension was prepared at a 1:2.5 (w/v) ratio, and pH was measured using a digital pH meter, while EC was recorded using a conductivity meter.

## Assessment of salinity and disease stress
At the end of the experiment, salinity stress (SaS) and disease stress (DS) in cotton plants were assessed using visual rating scales. SaS was evaluated based on the extent of leaf chlorosis, necrosis, and growth inhibition using a 0–3 scale (0: no visible symptoms; 1: slight chlorosis; 2: pronounced chlorosis with partial necrosis; 3: extensive necrosis, severe growth retardation, or plant death), following the criteria described by Maas and Hoffman36, Lutts et al.37. Disease stress (DS) caused by Verticillium dahliae was assessed using a 0–5 scale based on the degree of vascular discoloration (0: no discoloration; 1: 1–25%; 2: 26–50%; 3: 51–75%; 4: 76–99%; 5: complete vascular discoloration and plant death), as described by Bhat et al.38. The scale values were converted into disease severity percentages using the formula of Townsend and Heuberger39.

## Data statistics
All data were analyzed using three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the main effects and interactions of seaweed and AMF biostimulants under disease stress, salinity stress, and their combined application. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 21.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) (Table S4). Differences among treatment means were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at a significance level of p < 0.05. Prior to analysis, all variables were subjected to normality tests, and when necessary, data were normalized using square-root (√x), logarithmic (log x), or Box–Cox transformations. To explore relationships among variables and reduce data dimensionality, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted using XLSTAT software (version 2022.3.1, Microsoft Excel) (Notes S1). In addition, a heatmap was generated using the ClustVis platform to visualize the effects of seaweed and AMF applications under stress conditions and to assess similarities and clustering patterns among treatments. Correlation analysis and network analysis were also performed to identify positive and negative relationships among variables. All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

## Results

## Growth respons of plant
Applications of Verticillium stress (VST), salinity stress (SST), and their combined stress (CST) in cotton plants resulted in pronounced reductions in plant growth parameters. Under non-stressed control conditions (CT), the highest growth values were observed in plants treated with seaweed (S) and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF; M). Under stress conditions, both S and M applications generally enhanced growth-related parameters compared with untreated plants. Group comparisons further revealed that M(+) and S(+) treatments achieved significantly higher values across all measured growth parameters than the corresponding untreated groups, M(–) and S(–) (Fig. 1). The results indicate that stress conditions significantly suppress plant growth, whereas AMF and seaweed applications effectively mitigate these adverse effects and promote overall plant performance. This improvement may be associated with enhanced nutrient uptake, better water status, and improved physiological functioning of plants under stress conditions.

## Physiological responses of plants
Stress treatments applied to cotton plants (CT, VST, SST, and CST) resulted in a marked increase in the Membrane Injury Index (MII), while causing significant reductions in relative water content (RWC) and chlorophyll content (Fig. 2). The highest MII values were observed under salinity stress (SS) and combined stress (CS), whereas the lowest MII values were recorded in the control (NC) and in plants receiving the combined application of seaweed and AMF (M + S). In terms of RWC and chlorophyll content, both seaweed (S) and AMF (M) applications—either individually or in combination—significantly enhanced these parameters. Moreover, M(+) and S(+) treatments exhibited higher RWC and chlorophyll contents compared with the untreated M(–) and S(–) groups, while simultaneously resulting in significantly lower MII values (Fig. 2). The results demonstrate that membrane damage increased markedly under salinity and combined stress conditions, whereas AMF and seaweed applications effectively preserved membrane stability, thereby improving plant water status and photosynthetic capacity.

## Biochemical and antioxidant responses of plants
Stress treatments applied to cotton plants (CT, VST, SST, and CST) generally led to increases in superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities, as well as in total antioxidant capacity and total phenolic compound levels, with the highest values most frequently recorded under combined stress (CST) conditions (Fig. 3). Under stress, particularly in the M + S + CS treatment group, CAT activity, total antioxidant capacity, and phenolic content reached their highest levels. The highest SOD activity was observed under CS application. In overall group comparisons, no statistically significant differences in SOD activity were detected between M(+) and M(–) or between S(+) and S(–) treatments. However, CAT activity was significantly lower in the S(+) group compared with the S(–) group. In contrast, total antioxidant capacity and total phenolic content were significantly lower in the M(+) group than in the M(–) group, whereas the S(+) group exhibited significantly higher values than the S(–) group (Fig. 3). The results indicate that oxidative load markedly increased under combined stress conditions, leading to maximal activation of antioxidant defense components. Seaweed application played a dominant role in enhancing total antioxidant capacity and phenolic compound accumulation, thereby strengthening redox homeostasis, whereas AMF application constrained excessive activation of oxidative defense responses, resulting in a more balanced defense profile. Combined applications demonstrated a more effective regulation of oxidative stress compared with individual treatments.

## Mineral nutrient status and ion homeostasis
Stress conditions in cotton plants (CT, VST, SST, and CST) generally resulted in decreases in phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and calcium (Ca) contents, accompanied by an increased accumulation of sodium (Na); these effects were particularly pronounced in the SST and CST groups (Fig. 4). Accordingly, the lowest K/Na and Ca/Na ratios were predominantly observed under combined stress (CS) treatments. Overall, the highest P and Ca contents were recorded in the M + S treatment, the highest K content in the S treatment, and the highest Na accumulation under SS and CS treatments. Evaluation of ionic ratios revealed that the K/Na ratio was highest in the S and M + S treatments, whereas the Ca/Na ratio reached its maximum in the M + S treatment. In contrast, VS, SS, and CS treatments generally exhibited the lowest values for all parameters except Na (Fig. 4). In overall group comparisons, P, K, and Ca contents as well as K/Na and Ca/Na ratios were higher in the M(+) and S(+) groups compared with the M(–) and S(–) groups, whereas Na content was higher in the M(–) and S(–) groups (Table S3). The results indicate that stress conditions disrupt ionic homeostasis by promoting Na accumulation and reducing essential nutrient uptake, whereas AMF and seaweed applications help maintain nutrient balance and improve ionic regulation.

## Mycorrhizal responses of plants
In cotton plants, salinity stress (SST) and combined stress (CST) treatments markedly restricted mycorrhizal intensity, whereas Verticillium stress (VST) exerted a stimulatory effect on AMF spore numbers (Fig. 5). The highest mycorrhizal intensity was recorded under the M + S treatment, while the lowest values were observed in the M + SS and M + CS treatments. In terms of AMF spore numbers, the highest values were detected in the M + S + VS and M + VS treatments, whereas the lowest spore density was observed in the M-only treatment. In contrast, none of the applied stress factors had a statistically significant effect on mycorrhizal frequency. In overall group comparisons, mycorrhizal intensity was significantly higher in seaweed-treated groups [S(+)] compared with S(–) groups, whereas no significant differences were detected for the other mycorrhizal parameters (Fig. 5). The results indicate that salinity and combined stress conditions suppress mycorrhizal development, while seaweed application enhances AMF functionality and partially mitigates stress-induced reductions in mycorrhizal intensity.

## Soil physicochemical properties
Stress treatments generally led to a reduction in soil moisture content, with the lowest moisture levels recorded particularly under salinity stress (SST) and combined stress (CST) conditions (Fig. 6). In contrast, the highest soil moisture content was observed in the AMF (M) treatment. Soil pH values remained within a relatively narrow range across all treatments. Electrical conductivity (EC), however, increased markedly under salinity stress (SST) and combined stress (CST) treatments. The highest EC value was recorded under SS conditions, whereas the lowest EC was observed in the M + S + VS treatment. In the overall group comparisons, soil moisture content was significantly higher in M(+) treatments compared with M(–) groups, while no significant difference was detected between S(+) and S(–) treatments. With respect to EC, both M(+) and S(+) applications resulted in lower EC values compared with their respective M(–) and S(–) groups (Fig. 6). The results indicate that stress conditions, particularly salinity, adversely affect rhizosphere physicochemical properties by reducing soil moisture and increasing EC, whereas AMF and seaweed applications help maintain soil moisture and mitigate stress-induced increases in EC.

## Plant responses to disease and salinity stress
Under Verticillium stress conditions, the highest disease severity (DS) was recorded in plants subjected to VS and CS treatments, reaching 59.36% and 58.32%, respectively. In contrast, the lowest disease severity (31.5%) was observed in the M + CS treatment. Moreover, DS under CS conditions was higher than that under VS alone (Fig. 7). In the overall group comparisons, M(+) treatments significantly reduced DS compared with M(–) groups, whereas S(+) treatments showed a decreasing trend in DS relative to S(–) groups, although this effect was not statistically significant. Under salinity stress (SS), the salinity stress scale (SaS) reached the highest levels in SS- and CS-treated plants. Both M and S treatments mitigated SaS under these stress conditions. While the effects of SS and CS treatments on SaS were comparable, the overall group comparisons revealed that M(+) and S(+) treatments exhibited significantly lower SaS than their corresponding M(–) and S(–) groups (Fig. 7). The results indicate that combined stress conditions intensify disease severity and salinity damage, whereas AMF—alone or in combination with seaweed—effectively mitigates these stress effects and enhances plant tolerance.

## Principal component analysis (PCA)
In the PCA analysis, the first two principal components (F1 and F2) explained 77.70% of the total variance balance (Fig. 9). The F1 axis was primarily defined by parameters related to plant growth, physiological status, and ionic. On the positive side of this axis, RWC, CHL, TPL, TDB, TFB, SD, LN, P, K, Ca, and the K/Na and Ca/Na ratios were located, showing strong positive associations particularly with the M, M + S, M + VS, and M + S+VS treatments. In contrast, the negative side of F1 was mainly associated with Na content, EC, and stress indicators. Stress treatments such as SaS (salinity stress) and SS and CS were positioned in this region, linking these treatments to ionic and physiological disturbances that adversely affected plant performance. The F2 axis primarily distinguished biochemical defense responses and oxidative stress. The positive side of this axis included SOD, CAT, TPC, and TAC, which showed strong positive associations with DS (disease stress). This finding indicates that disease stress markedly stimulated antioxidant defense systems and phenolic compound accumulation in plants. Moreover, the clustering of DS together with combined treatments such as M + CS and M + S+CS in the same region suggests that biostimulant applications enhanced defense-related and antioxidant responses under disease stress (Fig. 9). Overall, the PCA results clearly demonstrate that SaS is mainly associated with ionic imbalance and physiological impairment, whereas DS is more closely related to biochemical and antioxidant defense mechanisms. AMF and seaweed applications played a mitigating role under both stress types by improving plant performance and buffering stress-induced negative effects.

## General interpretation of the network analysis and correlation matrix
The circular correlation network (Fig. 10A) and the correlation matrix (Fig. 10B) and the circular correlation network (Fig. 10A) reveal a well-structured and statistically meaningful pattern of relationships among growth, physiological, biochemical, ionic, mycorrhizal, and stress-related parameters in cotton plants. In contrast, sodium (Na) content, electrical conductivity (EC), membrane injury index (MII), and stress indicators (DS and SaS) show predominantly negative correlations with growth and physiological traits. Antioxidant and biochemical defense parameters (SOD, CAT, TAC, and TPC) are positively associated with stress indicators, while exhibiting weak or negative correlations with growth-related traits. This pattern suggests that activation of the antioxidant system primarily reflects stress intensity rather than growth promotion, functioning as a defensive response under adverse conditions.

## Discussion
The findings of this study clearly demonstrate that biotic stress caused by Verticillium dahliae and abiotic stress induced by salinity in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) generate not merely additive effects but a reinforcing negative synergy on plant physiology and metabolism. In particular, the simultaneous imposition of both stresses resulted in profound and multilayered disruptions in growth, water relations, ionic homeostasis, and oxidative balance. This response is consistent with previous reports indicating that plants exposed to multiple stresses develop a distinct and non-additive “combined stress response” that differs fundamentally from the sum of individual stress effects3,40. In contrast, applications of seaweed and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) not only mitigated the severity of this complex stress scenario but also promoted the establishment of an integrated tolerance network by reorganizing key physiological and biochemical processes within the plant.
The pronounced biomass losses observed under stress conditions (Fig. 1) indicate that plants redirect their limited carbon and energy resources from growth toward defense and survival mechanisms4. The osmotic stress and ionic toxicity induced by salinity, when combined with the disruptive effects of the vascular pathogen Verticillium on xylem transport, severely constrained water movement and photosynthetic capacity41. This physiological impairment was reflected in the increased membrane injury index and the marked reductions in relative water content and chlorophyll levels (Fig. 2). The application of seaweed extract and AMF, particularly when applied in combination, alleviated these adverse effects, contributing to the maintenance of cellular integrity, plant water status, and photosynthetic function. Collectively, these findings suggest that biostimulants do not fully restore growth under stress conditions but instead play a regulatory role by mitigating the destructive impacts of stress42,43.
At the biochemical level, the stress-induced increases in antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD and CAT), total antioxidant capacity, and phenolic compounds (Fig. 3) indicate that cotton plants activated their defense systems in response to elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The pronounced effect of seaweed extract on phenolic metabolism and total antioxidant capacity suggests that this biostimulant induces a priming effect, thereby directly enhancing the synthesis of secondary metabolites44. In contrast, the relatively lower antioxidant responses observed under AMF application imply that mycorrhizal symbiosis alleviates stress perception at the root level, reducing the plant’s reliance on an intensive oxidative defense response45. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports describing AMF as a buffering mechanism that mitigates stress severity in host plants46.
Ionic analyses revealed that under salinity and combined stress conditions, the concurrent increase in Na⁺ accumulation and the reduction in K+ and Ca2+ contents fundamentally disrupted ionic homeostasis (Fig. 4). The marked declines in the K/Na and Ca/Na ratios indicate suppression of enzymatic activities and key metabolic processes47. Seaweed extract and AMF applications, particularly when applied in combination, promoted Na⁺ exclusion while maintaining K+ and Ca2+ selectivity, thereby exerting direct positive effects on membrane stability, plant water status, and photosynthetic capacity. The improved nutrient and ion regulation mediated by AMF may be associated with the regulation of plasma membrane H+-ATPase activity through the extraradical hyphal network48, whereas seaweed extract is thought to stimulate the synthesis of organic osmolytes that support osmotic balance49.
Mycorrhizal parameters exhibited dynamic responses to stress types that were sensitive not only to plant physiological status but also to changes in key rhizospheric physicochemical conditions, including soil moisture, electrical conductivity (EC), and pH (Figs. 5 and 6). The reduction in mycorrhizal colonization under salinity stress indicates that increased ionic strength and osmotic pressure associated with elevated EC constrained both root elongation and the extension of AMF hyphae within the soil matrix. The concurrent decline in soil moisture may have further limited root–fungus contact and carbon flux from roots to fungi, thereby negatively affecting the sustainability of the symbiotic relationship50. In addition, salinity-induced modifications in rhizosphere pH and excessive Na⁺ accumulation likely reduced the plant availability of essential nutrients such as Ca²⁺ and P, disrupting the carbon–nutrient exchange balance and further restricting mycorrhizal colonization51,52. In contrast, the observed increase in spore density under biotic stress may represent an adaptive response, whereby pathogen presence alters root physiology and the quantity and composition of root exudates, creating a relatively more favorable microniche for AMF establishment53. The highest mycorrhizal colonization levels recorded under the combined application of seaweed extract and AMF further indicate that these treatments restructured rhizosphere conditions in favor of AMF colonization by preserving soil moisture, relatively stabilizing EC, and maintaining pH within ranges more conducive to symbiosis. This improved soil environment supported root–fungus interactions and sustained carbon allocation, thereby contributing to the strengthening of the mycorrhizal association even under multiple stress conditions.
The findings related to disease severity and salinity injury scores (Fig. 7) clearly demonstrate that salinity-induced abiotic stress markedly increases the susceptibility of cotton plants to biotic stress. Under salt stress, disruption of the balance among salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ET) signaling pathways, together with osmotic pressure and Na⁺ accumulation, constrains the expression of defense-related genes and the allocation of cellular energy10,54,55. Concurrent ionic imbalance and membrane damage further weaken cell wall integrity, thereby facilitating more rapid colonization and spread of vascular pathogens such as Verticillium within the xylem tissues3. In contrast, the significant reduction in disease severity observed under AMF application indicates the activation of a Mycorrhiza-Induced Resistance (MIR) mechanism, which maintains the plant defense system in a primed state rather than at constitutively high levels56. This priming enables faster and stronger activation of defense-related genes upon pathogen challenge and restricts pathogen progression through enhanced deposition of cell wall components such as callose and lignin establishment53. The more moderate yet consistent suppression of disease pressure by seaweed application can be attributed not to the direct induction of defense signaling, but rather to the improvement of overall stress tolerance; bioactive compounds, including osmoregulators and hormone-like substances present in seaweed, enhance plant water status and membrane stability, thereby preventing stress-induced collapse of immune functions57. The more pronounced protective effect observed under the combined application of AMF and seaweed suggests complementary and synergistic modes of action, whereby strengthening of defense signaling networks and physical barriers by AMF is coupled with enhanced physiological resilience conferred by seaweed, collectively leading to the establishment of an integrated tolerance strategy that suppresses disease development in cotton under multiple stress conditions.
Heat map, correlation, and PCA analyses collectively supported these findings within an integrated framework (Figs. 8 and 9, and 10). The results revealed that stress-treated groups clustered predominantly along axes associated with ionic imbalance and physiological damage, whereas biostimulant-treated groups formed a more balanced physiological configuration centered on growth, defense, and homeostasis-related parameters. This holistic evaluation demonstrates that seaweed and AMF act as strong and complementary biological regulators, simultaneously modulating defense mechanisms, physiological resilience, and symbiotic interactions in cotton plants under combined biotic and abiotic stress conditions. These findings are in strong agreement with43, who reported that biostimulant applications effectively enhance plant tolerance by coordinating growth, ion homeostasis, and defense responses under both abiotic and biotic stress conditions.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that Verticillium wilt and salinity stress in cotton plants impose pronounced and interactive constraints on growth, physiological integrity, ion homeostasis, and mycorrhizal associations, particularly when these stresses occur simultaneously. The findings clearly indicate that salinity exacerbates plant susceptibility to biotic stress, thereby severely limiting overall plant performance.
Seaweed and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) applications elicited an integrated tolerance response in cotton under multiple stress conditions. These biostimulants mitigated stress-induced growth reductions by preserving membrane stability, improving plant water status and photosynthetic capacity, restricting Na accumulation, and maintaining K and Ca balance, thereby supporting ion homeostasis. While seaweed application enhanced redox balance and phenolic metabolism, AMF inoculation buffered stress perception and prevented excessive activation of defense responses. The sensitivity of mycorrhizal responses to stress intensity underscores the strong dependence of symbiotic functioning on environmental conditions. Notably, the combined application of seaweed and AMF substantially strengthened this interaction by improving rhizosphere conditions.
Overall, seaweed and AMF emerge as complementary and environmentally friendly biostimulant strategies that enhance cotton resilience against concurrent biotic and abiotic stresses, offering a robust solution for sustainable and climate-resilient cotton production.
