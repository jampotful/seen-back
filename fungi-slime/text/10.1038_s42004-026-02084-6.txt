# A bacteriophytochrome Pr/Pfr heterodimer studied through single-particle time-resolved cryo-electron microscopy

## Abstract
AbstractPhytochromes are dimeric photoreceptors found in bacteria, fungi, and plants that reversibly interconvert between a red-absorbing Pr state and a far-red-absorbing Pfr state. In bacteria, phytochromes (BphPs) regulate diverse responses through a two-component signaling pathway comprising a C-terminal histidine kinase (HK) and a response regulator. A previous cryo-EM study of the wild-type BphP from Stigmatella aurantiaca (SaBphP2) revealed a stable Pr/Pfr heterodimer in which the two protomers adopt distinct Pr and Pfr conformations. Here, using the Spotiton technique, we captured the same heterodimer by illuminating SaBphP2 particles on the cryo-EM grid and vitrifying them 10 ms later. Comparison with the Pr/Pr homodimer reveals a 180° rotation of the HK domain, driven by an unwinding of the coiled-coil helices that connect the photosensory core to the enzymatic domain. The large-scale reorientations provide mechanistic insight into light-triggered signal transduction mediated by bacterial phytochromes.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Kinases, Photobiology

## Introduction
Understanding the molecular mechanisms that regulate perception of essential environmental stimuli, such as light and activation of the subsequent signaling cascade, is imperative to comprehend and manipulate organismal responses1. Light affects the behavior of higher organisms with specialized organs (eyes) with various opsins2,3 that sense and react to this important stimulus. Prokaryotes sense light typically through molecular photoreceptors that are found in the membrane or in the cytoplasm4. These photoreceptors contain an enzymatic output module (OPM), which, in turn, activates a downstream effector protein. Bacterial signal transduction is achieved by a two-component signaling (TCS) system consisting of a histidine kinase (HK) and a response regulator (RR)5, whose activation results in various physiological responses6,7.
Bacteriophytochromes (BphPs) are part of a TCS system responsible for red and far-red light-sensing and signaling. Phytochromes were originally discovered in plants and can also be found in fungi, but not in animals8–10. BphPs consist of a light-absorbing photosensory core module (PCM) and a C-terminal OPM (Fig. 1a). The PCM consists of three domains: PAS (Per-Arnt-Sim), GAF (cGMP phosphodiesterase/adenylyl cyclase/FhIA), and PHY (phytochrome-specific GAF-related). In most BphPs, the light-absorbing chromophore is a bilin pigment called biliverdin (BV) (Fig. 1b). BV is a heme-derived open-chain tetrapyrrole (consisting of pyrrole rings A–D) covalently bound to a conserved N-terminal cysteine (Cys-13 in the BphP under investigation here, Fig. 1b). In typical BphPs, the OPM is a histidine kinase (HK)11,12 whose activity is controlled by light absorbed by the BV in the PCM. Light-dependent HK activity has been characterized for various BphPs such as those isolated from the photosynthetic Rhodopseudomonas palustris (RpBphP2 and RpBphP3)13,14, from the non-photosynthetic Stigmatella aurantiaca (SaBphP1 and SaBphP2)15,16 and from Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PaBphP)17. Certain BphPs exhibit OPMs with different enzymatic activities: for example, the Idiomarina sp. IsPadC features a diguanylyl cyclase18, while the Deinococcus radiodurans DrBphP19 exhibits phosphatase activity20. The HK, the OPM found in most BphPs, binds a RR, which is phosphorylated, typically at a conserved Asp21,22, and subsequently leaves the HK to initiate physiological changes. To date, the molecular mechanisms that explain how light regulates the HK activity and how the RR subsequently interacts with the OPM are yet to be determined.

In photosynthetic bacteria, BphPs are involved in the development of the photosynthetic machinery23–25, whereas their roles in non-photosynthetic bacteria are diverse9 and often related to specific situations prevalent in the natural habitat of the bacteria8,15,26. In the non-photosynthetic, soil-dwelling myxobacteria, the BphPs are intimately involved in the formation of fruiting bodies27, which are multicellular stages in their complex life-cycle15,28–30. Myxobacteria are also investigated as a biocontrol agent against plant disease31 and for the production of secondary metabolites with possible anticancer and antimicrobial properties32. The synthesis of bioactive compounds has been modulated by red light32,33, which suggests the involvement of BphPs. The BphPs from Stigmatella (SaBphP1 and SaBphP2) each exhibit an HK domain15,16. Both BphPs exist as homodimers where each of the two protomers binds the same BV chromophore and has the same amino acid sequence, respectively. The classical SaBphP2 has been critical to determining photocycle intermediates on ps, ns and ms timescales via time-resolved crystallography, and it has been established that its HK activity is light dependent34.
BphPs undergo a reversible photoconversion between a red-light absorbing Pr state and a far-red light absorbing Pfr state (Fig. 1b). When the BV chromophore is illuminated by red light, it isomerizes from an all-Z to a ZZE configuration by rotating ring-D about the C15-C16 double bond (Fig. 1b). After the isomerization, the protein undergoes conformational changes towards a stable state called Pfr that absorbs in the far-red. It is the balance between the Pr and Pfr states that is sensed by downstream effector proteins. The signature of the Pr to Pfr transition is the conformational transition of an extension of the PHY domain called the sensory tongue that exists in the dark state as a two-stranded β-sheet. During the Pr to Pfr transition, the two strands separate, and one of the strands forms an α-helix35,36 (Fig. 1b). Our recent characterization of the intact, wild-type SaBphP2 with single particle cryo-EM has shown34 that the Pr to Pfr transition proceeds through a Pr/Pfr heterodimer (Fig. 1c)37,38. One protomer is in the Pr state, with the BV in the all-Z configuration and the sensory tongue a β-sheet. The other is in the Pfr state, with the BV chromophore in the ZZE configuration and with the characteristic α-helix found in the sensory tongue. The transition from the Pr/Pr homodimer to the Pr/Pfr heterodimer is accompanied by large structural changes in the PCM (Fig. 1b) as well as in the linker region of the SaBphP2 (Fig. 1c), as outlined previously34,39. The time-resolved cryo-EM study presented in this manuscript confirms that the Pr/Pfr heterodimer (Fig. 1c) can be generated by actively exciting particles in the homodimeric Pr state by an intense light source. Thus, the photocycle needs to be modified to account for this heterodimer intermediate.

## Results

## Time-resolved cryo-EM
In our previous cryo-EM study on the SaBphP234, the structure of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer was discovered only after the protein was exposed to ambient white light. While this discovery itself was transformative, it left open the possibility that the heterodimer might represent a freezing or illumination artifact rather than a bona fide intermediate of the photocycle. Here, we demonstrate the formation of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer by exciting SaBphP2 Pr/Pr homodimers by a 75 mW continuous-wave (cw) laser in a single millisecond pump-freeze type40 experiment using the Spotiton technique41. In this time-resolved (TR) cryo-EM experiment, SaBphP2 particles on a cryo-EM grid were illuminated by red (660 nm) laser light for 4 milliseconds (ms), approximately 9.5 ms before vitrification in liquid ethane. The time resolution of the TR cryo-EM experiment is defined by the fastest time between laser excitation and vitrification in liquid ethane possible by the Spotiton setup. It is dependent on the plunger velocity and the shortest distance possible between the laser focus and the ethane reservoir. Here, it is about 5 ms. In addition to the illumination conditions, the relative concentrations of the protein conformers depend on the quantum yield of photoconversion qpc, which is estimated to be 12%. As a result of the photoexcitation, 38% of the molecules are in the Pr/Pfr state (see the Methods and the Supplementary Material, SM, for detailed evaluations).
From the TR cryo-EM experiment, 3D density volumes for both the Pr/Pr homodimer as well as the Pr/Pfr heterodimer were reconstructed at rather low resolution (~6.2 Å). The resolution of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer volume was improved to 5.01 Å (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S1) by using a tighter mask with a 3 Å resolution filter. To obtain an unbiased initial model, the partial (incomplete) model of the Pr/Pr homodimer determined earlier34 was modified in the PCM region to fit the cryo-EM density. The HK region of the density was modeled using the corresponding region of the complete Pr/Pfr model previously determined34. After refinement, a complete structure of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer is obtained and called SaBphP2-Pr/Pfr4ms (for 4 ms illumination). To determine a structural model of a Pr/Pr homodimer, cryo-EM data collected with Spotiton previously39 were reassessed because more particles are available for a reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. S2). This previous experiment did not reveal particles in the Pr/Pfr state, since illumination with a weak LED light source did not photoconvert a detectable amount of molecules. At an overall resolution of 5.85 Å (Table 1), the density in the HK region is meaningful and contingent. The reassessment allowed us to finish the partial structure of the Pr/Pr homodimer34 by adding the missing part of the linker helices, the 4-helix bundle and the HK lobes. We call the structure SaBphP2-Pr/PrLL (LL for low light). Density maps and their respective structures are shown in Fig. 2a, b.

## Rotation by 180 ° of the HK domain, including the 4-helix bundle and the corresponding lobes
The Pr/PrLL and Pr/Pfr4ms structures are quite distinct. Particular attention is drawn to the coiled linker helices that cross each other (black dots marked with 1 and 2 in Fig. 2; see also Fig. 4 and Supplementary movie 1). Another perspective (marked R in Fig. 2) is obtained by reorienting both structures so that the sensory tongues of both protomers are approximately in the same plane. Most peculiar, from the perspective of view R, the linker helices in the Pr/Pfr4ms structure are parallel (red arrow in Fig. 2b), whereas the ones in the Pr/PrLL structure cross two times (marked 1 and 2 in Fig. 2a and in Fig. 4a, b). Figure 4d–f presents mechanical models that consist of two strings in lieu of the linker helices of protomers A and B. Figure 4d demonstrates that the coiled helices in the SaBphP2 Pr/Pr homodimer (Fig. 4a, b) can only unwind to a parallel configuration when the entire OPM, including the 4-helix bundle and the HK lobes, rotates ~180° about the central axis. Supplementary Movie 2 provides an impression of this rotation. Supplementary Movie 3 demonstrates that the two linker helices would unphysically interpenetrate if the rotation is not allowed to take place. We conclude that the rotation is complete by 9.5 ms, corresponding to the time delay probed in this study. Shorter time delays will be required to resolve the rotation itself and the accompanying protein relaxations during the transition.

Although in view R helix intersection 1 unwinds, an additional intersection (marked 2 in Fig. 2b) remains after the rotation. When the Pr/Pfr state is excited, this intersection unwinds by an additional rotation of the HK lobes by ~60°, as shown earlier by the cryo-EM density of the Pfr/Pfr homodimer34. In summary, a complete Pr to Pfr transition occurs via a double-unwinding mechanism of the coiled-coil linker helices driven by the transition of both subunits to Pfr, that ultimately ends with all-parallel linker helices in the Pfr/Pfr homodimer when viewed from both the front and the side of the molecule34. Transitions from the Pr to the Pfr homodimeric states and back are accompanied by a “swivel-like” back-and-forth rotation of the entire HK domain by about 240°.

## Discussions
As described, the Pr/Pr homodimer to Pr/Pfr heterodimer transition is addressed by explaining structural changes necessary to unwind the linker helices starting from coiled-coil helices in the Pr/Pr homodimer. It does not require near-atomic resolution to resolve these large-scale topological changes. At ~5–6 Å resolution, α-helices and their connectivity are clearly discernible. This is adequate to establish domain rotations, resolve the coiled-coil helix rearrangement, trace secondary-structure elements and follow domain reorganization.
The string model (Fig. 4) and Supplementary Movie 2 demonstrate that a large-scale rotation of the entire histidine kinase (HK) domain about the central axis occurs. This observation suggests that the rotational dynamics of the HK domain may warrant additional consideration in phytochrome signaling. Rather than solely modulating HK enzymatic activity as demonstrated for other BphPs14,17, the light-induced OPM dynamics suggest an additional mechanism controlling RR binding and dissociation (see also below) through rotation.

## Implications for other proteins
Due to the lack of structures of the HK domains in BphPs, the prevailing opinion has been that the HK lobe of one protomer is positioned on top of the photosensory module (PCM) of the opposing protomer14,17,20,21,42,43. Our complete SaBphP2 structures in different signaling states demonstrate that this assignment is incorrect for the SaBphP2 Pr/Pr homodimer (Fig. 2a), where each HK domain is positioned above its corresponding PCM protomer (the HK lobe of protomer A is located on top of the PCM of protomer A, and that of protomer B is located on top of the PCM of protomer B). In our previous study34, we showed that this arrangement is reversed in the Pr/Pfr heterodimer (Fig. 2b), but the limited resolution at that time did not allow a definitive structural and mechanistic interpretation. The work presented here resolves this ambiguity by showing that a 180° rotation of the entire HK domain provides a coherent explanation for the observed topological differences between the homodimer and heterodimer. Caused by the rotation, the HK lobe of protomer A (B) is displaced so that it is found on top of the PCM of protomer B (A).
Other BphPs with HK activity, such as the bathy PaBphP42,44,45, also display 4-helix bundles in the HK domain (Figs. 4c and 5b), albeit sometimes smaller than that of the SaBphP2 (Fig. 5a). Recently, a Pr/Pfr heterodimer was detected in the thermal back reaction of the PaBphP from the (metastable) Pr/Pr homodimer to the Pfr/Pfr resting state, but the HK domain is not well resolved46–48. Note, a crossover of the linker helices near the PCM can be observed in the PaBphP and SaBphP2 Pr/Pr homodimers (Figs. 4c and 5b), yet the HK lobes in the PaBphP are already positioned at a large angular offset compared to those in SaBphP2 (Fig. 5c). Based on the rotations identified here, a sliding mechanism as proposed for the PaBphP42 (Fig. 4 e) could be re-evaluated, since a rotation would maintain the integrity of the 4-helix bundle (compare Fig. 4c, f). The unwinding of coiled-coil helices might not be restricted to the SaBphP. Other enzymes, in particular motor proteins such as the kinesin49 and others50,51, also display coiled coils. These molecular motors may use an analogous unwinding mechanism with a concomitant “motor-head” oscillation, which allows them to function efficiently.

## The bacteriophytochrome photocycle
The Pr/Pfr heterodimer is central to the photoreactions in the red and far-red branches of the photocycle shown in Fig. 6b, c, respectively. The absorption of a photon results in an electronically excited state of the BV chromophore in its particular protomer, which can then photoconvert, driven by BV isomerization. In the red-branch of the photocycle (Fig. 6b), the BV isomerizes to the E-configuration, resulting in the formation of the Pfr state. In the far-red branch of the photocycle (Fig. 6 c), isomerization continues towards the Z-configuration and the formation of the Pr state. It requires two absorption events to complete the photoreaction in each branch. The discovery of the cryo-EM structure of a Pr/Pfr heterodimer in an unmodified, wild-type BphP with HK activity34 is relevant for the understanding of the photocycle. Previously, a Pr/Pfr heterodimer has been predicted for plant phytochromes52 and observed in a crystal structure of a mutant BphP with diguanylyl cyclase enzymatic domain53,54. The integration of the heterodimer in the BphP photocycle (Fig. 6a) has now been confirmed by a controlled excitation of single wild-type BphP particles. In addition to the rather large structural changes in the PCM region of SaBphP2 (Table 2), the conversion to the Pr/Pfr heterodimer is accompanied by the rotation of the entire HK domain. Only after this rotation, the kink observed in the linker helix of the opposing protomer (the one that is not photo-activated) appears (Fig. 5a). Structural changes of the inactive protomer, therefore, are allosteric by nature. We propose that these allosteric changes may have an impact on the photoconversion quantum yield of the inactive protomer that might even be time-dependent during the relaxation of the activated protomer to Pfr. In any case, the completion of the half-photocycle from Pr to Pfr requires two sequential absorption events (Fig. 6b). The same should be true for the Pfr to Pr photoconversion with the presence of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer in the photocycle (Fig. 6c).

## Physiological implications
Our structures provide a hypothesis for the existence of a stabilized Pr/Pfr heterodimer in BphPs from soil-dwelling myxobacteria and maybe also in related BphPs. Depending on the predominant environmental stimulus, red or far-red light, this heterodimer could rapidly shift the signaling output by favoring either the enzymatically less active Pfr state (Fig. 6b) or the more active Pr state (Fig. 6c), respectively. Under low light intensity, prevalent in the myxobacteria’s soil habitat, it is likely advantageous to the bacteria to react quickly using the Pr/Pfr species, sensitive to both red and far-red light. Signal generation also competes with thermal conversion (Fig. 6b) from Pfr to Pr, which interferes with red/far-red light perception. Starting from the Pr/Pfr state rather than Pr/Pr, responses to various wavelengths of light could become quick and finely tuned. The accumulation of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer in the photocycle (Fig. 6a) then depends on the ratio of red and far-red light in the myxobacteria’s habitat. It is important to explore to which of the three states the RR binds and when it dissociates. The large rotation of the HK moiety, as modeled by the complete cryo-EM structures of SaBphP2 in two distinct signaling states, suggests that the heterodimer is intricately involved in the binding (and dissociation) mechanism of the RR. TR cryo-EM using chemical diffusion41,55,56 through mixing the RR with the wild-type SaBphP2 can shine light on the mechanism of this interaction.

## Methods

## Sample preparation
Full-length (intact) SaBphP2 is overexpressed and purified as previously reported34. The pure BphP is shipped on dry ice to the New York Structural Biology Center (NYSBC), where it is stored at −80 oC. An aliquot is thawed to prepare cryo-EM grids with the Spotiton technique41,57.

## Time-resolved pump-freeze experiment with Spotition (i), light activation
Spotiton was developed at NYSBC41,57 and was made available for our experiments. To avoid unintentional light activation of the SaBphPs, a lightproof enclosure was built around the Spotiton device, and a 540 nm safety light was used within the enclosure during grid preparation. Before the experiment, the SaBphP2 solution (0.5 mg/ml) was irradiated with 740 nm light for 1 min using a light-emitting diode to generate a pure Pr state. 5 μl of the solution (containing the SaBphP2 Pr homodimers) was loaded into the Spotiton sample cups, and 3 µl was aspirated into each dispense head. The dispensers delivered approximately 5 nl of solution to a self-wicking grid, which were applied in overlapping stripes. The grid was then plunged into liquid ethane for vitrification (Figs. 7 and 8). To photoexcite the sample, we installed a 75 mW Coeherent OBIS cw-laser (660 nm) into the Spotiton device immediately above the ethane reservoir. The laser beam was focused to a 3 mm spot centered on the grid plunge axis. The laser light illuminated the grid on its way to the liquid ethane for approximately 4 ms (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

## Time-resolved pump-freeze experiment with Spotiton (ii), timing
The pump-freeze time delay depends on the distance of the laser spot from the ethane surface, the velocity of the plunger, and its deceleration (Figs. 7 and 8). The pump-freeze time delay is given by the “illumination to ethane contact” distance, which is 7 mm. The time resolution can be defined as the shortest time between light excitation and freezing, which is, in our case, approximately 5 ms when the laser beam is positioned as close as possible to the ethane reservoir. The pump-freeze time-delay as well as the time-delays to characteristic points on the plunger trajectory (Supplementary Table S2) can be calculated (Eq. 1) from the Spotiton plunger velocity which is u = 1 m/s, the point of the onset of the deceleration (25 mm above the resting point (stop) in the ethane, Figs. 7 and 8, and Supplementary Table S2) and the deceleration constant a which is 20,000 mm/s2.
As an example, with the laser located at 7.2 mm from the start of the deceleration, the time when the grid reaches the laser midpoint can be calculated. Equation 1 yields7.2=1000⋅t−1220000⋅t2, with t in seconds. The two roots of the quadratic equation are 7.8 ms and 92 ms. Since the latter is unphysical, 7.8 ms is the correct time-delay. The time delays for the other points of interest are tabulated in Supplementary Table S2 and displayed in Figs. 7 and 8. Referring to Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table S2, a more generous estimate for the illumination time can be given by the time interval between the S4 and S1 positions, which is 7.1 ms. A more conservative estimate would yield 3.6 ms (4 ms for simplicity) between the S3 and S2 positions. The time between the light excitation and the ethane contact is 9.4 ms. We report this as the time delay in this paper. The time resolution is defined as the shortest time possible with this setup. It is dependent on the shortest distance possible between the laser focus and the ethane reservoir. This shortest distance is about 4 mm. Given the velocity of the plunger near the ethane surface, the time resolution is approximately 4–5 ms (Fig. 8). In summary, we measure a 9.4 ms time delay with 5 ms time resolution following 4 ms laser excitation.

## Phytochrome excitation
Illumination conditions are listed in Supplementary Table S1. During the 4 ms exposure, a total of 1 × 1015 660 nm photons reach the grid. The photon density at the grid is 1.42 × 1016 photons/cm2. Using the Pr spectrum of the full-length SaBphP2 (Supplementary Fig. S3), the molar extinction coefficient for SaBphP2 is roughly estimated to be ε700 = 80,000 cm2/mmol (see the SM). Based on the spectral form, at 660 nm, the extinction coefficient is about 52% of that at 700 nm, which amounts to ε600 = 41,600 cm2/mmol. Accordingly, the molecular absorption cross-section of a protomer at 660 nm is ln(10)×41,600/(6.024×1020)cm2=1.6×10−16cm2. With a photon density of 1.42 × 1016 photons/cm2, approximately Np = 2.3 photons are absorbed per protomer during the 4 ms illumination time. Absorption of a photon results in an electronically excited BV chromophore. The excited chromophore relaxes through a conical intersection seam58,59 either back to the dark state or towards the photocycle. The probability to enter the photocycle is given by the quantum yield of photoconversion qpc. ## Data collection
Cryo-EM Movies were collected from the Spotiton prepared grids as reported in detail62 using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Titan Krios electron microscope controlled by the Leginon software system63. The movies were sent from the NYSBC to the UWM High Performance Compute clusters, where they were processed further (Table 1).

## Processing of the TR-cryo-EM data
CryoSPARC v. 4.7.164 was used throughout to process the movies. Some details to analyze cryo-EM data of full-length BphP particles are also reported previously34,62. Data collection statistics for the Pr/Pfr heterodimer is shown in Tab. 1, a flow chart is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Initially, over 6.5 M particles were picked from over 7000 micrographs using the blob picker. 8506 of the best particles were used to produce a template that was used to pick about 5 M particles, of which 3.3 M particles were retained. After 2D classification and further curation, 379,306 particles remained. From these, a single-class reconstruction was generated. Non-uniform refinement produced a 6.4 Å map that was used again as a template to pick particles. After curation, 106,437 particles remained, a ab-initio volume was generated. Non-uniform refinement produces a 6.7 Å map. Out of these 106,437 particles, 69,189 particles were used to train a Topaz neural network65. Topaz picking yielded 383,989 particles. The ab-initio volume was further improved by using the Topaz picked particles, resulting in a 5.98 Å map. To address the mixture of Pr/Pr and Pr/Pfr particles in the ensemble, we classified the picked particles into 4 3D classes using a 7 Å resolution filter (Supplementary Fig. S1b). After homogenous and non-uniform refinement, the Pr/Pfr volume was present in class 1 with about 60,294 particles and the Pr/Pr volume was detected in class 0 with 55,977 particles (Table 1). We did not refine the Pr/Pr volume further, since more particles were contributing to the 3D volume in an earlier experiment39,66, and we expected higher resolution and better map quality from these particles. In order to obtain a volume for the Pr/Pfr species with better resolution, we started again from the Topaz-picked particles. 3D classification was achieved with a 3 Å resolution filter into 3 classes. After rejecting one class, down-sampling to a smaller, tighter (350 pixel) box and another round of 3D classification, a volume for the Pr/Pfr species was determined from 87 k particles at 5 Å resolution (Table 1). The resolution in the PCM region and that of the HK domain are listed in Table 1. The Fourier Shell Correlation to determine the resolution limit and the local resolution map is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1d, e.

## Revisiting cryo-EM data for the SaBphP Pr/Pr homodimer
The cryo-EM data of a Pr homodimer reported by Karki et al.39,66 were revisited (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. S2). The earlier experiment provided more particle images from which we reconstructed a higher resolution structure than from the limited number of Pr images collected in this study. Spotiton was also used to prepare the grids, but the sample was only illuminated with a light-emitting diode, resulting in a fraction of >85% of unexcited Pr/Pr homodimers. Because of the insufficient light illumination, we call the resulting structure SaBphP2-Pr/PrLL (LL for low light). 10207 raw movies were imported and motion corrected. Based on the contrast transfer function (CTF) fit resolution, relative ice thickness, and total full-frame motion distance, 8101 micrographs were selected (Supplementary Fig. S2). We used the previously determined volume39,66 to generate templates for repeating the particle picking. From template-based picking, 1,955,847 particles were extracted with a box size of 512 pixels. After iterative 2D curation, 363,029 particles were retained. Topaz was used for particle picking, yielding 251,896 particles. The particles from Topaz and template-based picking were down-sampled to a 350-pixel box. These particles underwent iterative 2D classification, resulting in 214,432 particles. A single ab initio volume was generated, followed by homogeneous and non-uniform refinement. The particles were then classified into three 3D-classes (Supplementary Fig. S2b). Class 0 with 70,908 particles was used for non-uniform refinement, resulting in a 5.85 Å map. The local resolution map and the Fourier Shell Correlation are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2d. By applying local refinement in CryoSPARC, the resolution in the HK domain region improves to >6 Å (Table 1).

## Structure determination and structural refinement
To determine the structure of the SaBphP2-Pr/Pfr4ms heterodimer, the structural model of the Pr/Pr homodimer determined by Malla et al.34,67,68 was used as the initial model to exclude bias. The PCM of the initial model and part of the linker helices were determined at near atomic resolution and, hence, include side chains. Although the resolution of the SaBphP2-Pr/Pfr4ms density was too low to assign side-chain positions, we kept the side chains in the initial model. The BV chromophore was removed from protomer B. Since there are large structural changes between the homodimer and the heterodimer, the initial model required extensive modifications of the protomer B structure to agree with the cryo-EM density. Judged by the separation of strands and the α-helix formation in the sensory tongue region, protomer B is clearly in the Pfr state with the BV in the E-configuration. Accordingly, we added the BV structure with ring-D in the E-configuration obtained from the Pfr protomer in the Pr/Pfr heterodimer determined earlier34–38 and adjusted the chromophore pocket residues accordingly. To complete the model, the HK region of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer that was also determined in the earlier study was added to the initial model. Note, this part, which includes a fraction of the linker helices, the four-helix bundle and the HK lobes, does not contain side chains but consists only of a poly-alanine protein backbone. Proline residues were also included since they can be considered part of the backbone. The complete model was refined against the Pr/Pfr density map using real-space refinement implemented in the program Phenix69 (Table 1). To obtain a model for the Pr/Pr state, the same incomplete Pr/Pr structure used above was oriented into the new cryo-EM density map of the Pr/Pr homodimer. Modifications were not necessary. To complete the structure, the linker region, the 4-helix bundle and the HK lobes were added, guided by the structures of the corresponding regions in the Pr/Pfr heterodimer. However, this time, the individual lobes of the HK are located on top of the PCM region of the same, corresponding protomer (Fig. 2a). This is in contrast to the model of the Pr/Pfr heterodimer, where the HK lobes are positioned on top of the opposite protomer (Figs. 2b and 5a). The resulting full-length Pr/Pr homodimer (SaBphP2-Pr/PrLL) was also refined with Phenix. Refinement and validation statistics are also provided in Table 1.
