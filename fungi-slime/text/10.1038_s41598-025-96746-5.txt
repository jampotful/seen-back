# Analysis of the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid microbial flora in COPD patients at different lung function during acute exacerbation

## Abstract
AbstractThere is a correlation between the dysbiosis of the respiratory microbiota and the occurrence, severity, frequency, and mortality of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). However, it is not unclear if there are differences in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) microbiota among patients at differente lung function. In this study, BALF samples were collected from 70 COPD patients experiencing acute exacerbations (AECOPD). The patients were divided into a mild group (FEV1/pre ≥ 50; PFT I, n = 50) and a severe group (FEV1/pre < 50; PFT II, n = 20) according to the lung function: or a frequent exacerbation (FE, n = 41) group and a non-frequent exacerbation (NFE, n = 29) group according to their exacerbation history. Microbiota analysis of BALF samples was conducted using mNGS and bioinfromatic analysis. Compared to PFT I group, PFT II group exhibited a significant decrease in species diversity (Shannon index), as well as a significant reduction in total species count and richness (Chao1, ACE indices). NFE group demonstrated diversity similar to that of FE group. Conversely, the microbial diversity of NFE group was comparable to that of FE group. The most abundant bacterial genera were Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Rod-shaped Bacillus, and Rothia. Aspergillus was the most dominant fungal genus in AECOPD. Lymphocryptovirus was the most prevalent virus in AECOPD.Compared to the PFT I group, Corynebacterium’s abundance significantly increased in PFT II group. Furthermore, FE group showed a notable increase in Streptococcus mitis abundance relative to NFE group. Bubble plot analysis revealed a significant increase in Moraxella, Fusobacterium, Haemophilus, Pseudomonas, Streptomyces, and Klebsiella in PFT II group, including a notable increase in typical Veillonella, Actinomyces, and Gordonia. The NFE group exhibited a significant increase in Bacteroides and Prevotella’s relative abundance. Spearman correlation analysis revealed strong positive correlations among certain microbial communities. There exists a significant variation in microbial composition across groups of AECOPD patients at different lung function. Specifically, patients with severe airflow limitations exhibit a significant reduction in microbial diversity. Additionally, distinct bacterial taxa are enriched in patients characterized by varying levels of airflow limitation and exacerbation frequency. These observations offer vital insights into the pathogenesis of AECOPD, suggesting a potentially crucial role for the microbiota in its development. Such findings pave the way for identifying potential therapeutic targets and intervention strategies, ultimately aiming to improve treatment outcomes for AECOPD patients.Keywords: Acute Exacerbation of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (AECOPD), Metagenomic next-Generation Sequencing (mNGS), Microbiome, Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF), Lung function, Exacerbation frequencySubject terms: Clinical microbiology, Respiratory tract diseases

## Introduction
Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a widely prevalent respiratory disorder marked by chronic airway inflammation, obstruction of the small airways, and destruction of alveoli. It has risen to be a major public health challenge, significantly contributing to the global burden of disease. Affecting over 300 million individuals globally, COPD is responsible for millions of deaths each year1. Growing evidence links variations in airway microbiota diversity with the severity of acute exacerbations, disease progression, and increased long-term mortality in COPD patients2–7. An elevated bacterial load in these patients correlates with deteriorating lung function8 and higher frequencies of acute exacerbations9, highlighting the critical role of bacteria in COPD’s pathophysiological mechanisms. Research demonstrates an association between airflow limitation severity and shifts in microbiota composition, notably an increased relative abundance of Pseudomonas in sputum and a decreased presence of Treponema10. Li et al. observed that frequent acute exacerbations in COPD patients are linked to diminished alpha diversity, distinct clustering in beta diversity, and alterations in taxonomic abundance11. Further, studies have shown that as disease severity escalates, there is a higher relative abundance of Haemophilus, whereas the relative abundances of Prevotella and Veillonelladecrease4.
Presently, a multitude of studies utilize sputum samples to examine variations in microbial communities relative to disease severity in COPD. While some research highlights similarities between oral and lung microbiota within sputum samples, discrepancies are observed within specific microbial groups12. Lung function tests and the frequency of exacerbations serve as critical metrics for evaluating the status of COPD patients during acute exacerbations. Consequently, this research employs mNGS to investigate the disparities in BALF microbial communities among AECOPD patients, categorized by different levels of airflow limitation and exacerbation frequency. The objective is to elucidate the association between microbial communities in BALF and the extent of airflow limitation severity.

## Methods

## Patient selection and grouping
In this study, 70 AECOPD patients hospitalized in the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January 2022 and October 2023 were retrospectively selected as study subjects.The specific inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in Attachment 1: Table S1.
Basic demographic information, gender, age, smoking history, Body mass index(BMI), lung function test (PFTs) and exacerbations frequency in the last 1 year were obtained. The study subjects are grouped based on two different criteria: Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs) and the frequency of acute exacerbations within the past year.The 70 AECOPD patients were classified into a mild group (FEV1/pre ≥ 50%; PFT I, n = 50) and a severe group (FEV1/pre < 50%; PFT II, n = 20) according to the lung function.Additionally, based on the frequency of exacerbations,patients with at least 2 treated exacerbation events in last 1 year divided into the frequent exacerbation group (FE,n = 29), and patients with 1 or less treated exacerbation events in last 1 year were divided into the non-frequent exacerbation group (NFE, n = 41).This study has been reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. The ethical approval document number is K202310 - 10. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University has waived the requirement for obtaining informed consent and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

## mNGS operational procedure
(1) Specimen Pre-treatment: Before nucleic acid extraction, use a biological sample homogenizer to disrupt the cell walls of microorganisms in the specimen to enhance nucleic acid extraction efficiency. Place 1.2 ml of the specimen in a shaking tube and place the tube in the biological sample homogenizer for homogenization. (2) Nucleic Acid Extraction: Utilize a nucleic acid extraction kit (magnetic bead method) in conjunction with an automated nucleic acid extractor to extract nucleic acids from the specimen. (3) Pathogen Enrichment: Employ a pathogen microorganism positive broad-spectrum enrichment kit to enrich pathogens via PCR, thereby increasing the load of the target pathogens and enhancing detection sensitivity. (4) Library Construction: Use the NGSmaster™ automated library construction platform to construct libraries from the extracted nucleic acids. Add specific adapters to the unknown nucleic acid sequences at the 5’ and 3’ ends to make them compatible with sequencing chips for high-throughput sequencing (Jieyi NGSmaster large volume human total nucleic acid extraction or purification kit (magnetic bead method)). (5) Library Quantification: Quantify the constructed libraries with a quantitative PCR instrument (Jieyi NGS library quantification kit (probe method)). Retest specimens with unsatisfactory quantification or re-sample and retest for those that do not meet the standards. (6) Sequencing: Sequence the quantified libraries on the Illumina platform using the Nextseq series sequencer for sequencing. (7) Bioinformatics Analysis: After sequencing, use pathogen microorganism gene data analysis software (Xiangyi approved 20,211,154) to automatically complete downstream data quality control, species sequence alignment, and antibiotic resistance gene analysis. Combine artificial intelligence algorithms for in-depth interpretation of the analysis results, ultimately generating the detection report.

## Sequencing bioinformatics analysis
Each library is expected to generate 20 million 50 bp-length single-end sequence reads. Utilize pathogen microorganism gene data analysis software (Xiangyi approved 20,211,154) to process these sequences, excluding human genome sequences (GRCh38.p13), and match the remaining sequences against the microbial reference database (NCBI GenBank) and internally collected microbial genome data. This database covers 35,257 pathogenic microorganisms. Through this process, the types and relative abundance of microorganisms can be identified. Furthermore, use artificial intelligence algorithms for in-depth analysis of these results to produce the final detection report. Each mNGS test includes a negative control (composed of plasma-free nucleic acids and fragmented human genome DNA) and a positive control (a mixture of inactivated bacteria, fungi, and viral particles).

## mNGS reporting rules
(1) Sequence data meets quality control requirements (library concentration > 50 pM, Q20 > 85%, Q30 > 80%); (2) The negative control does not detect the species in the same chip or (the number of effective reads per million reads in the sample/the number of effective reads per million reads) ≥ 5, which is determined based on previous experience as the threshold for distinguishing true positives from background contamination.

## mNGS positive criteria
① Common opportunistic pathogens and atypical respiratory opportunistic pathogens in the clinic, if the composite group or species is within 5 times of the first or the first genus with each million effective reads (reads per million, RPM), and the sequence number ≥ 10; ② Common respiratory colonizing bacteria, if the relative abundance of the genus is over 50%; ③ Patients with lung abscess or definitive anaerobic infection, colonizing bacteria among the top 5 genera with a relative abundance of ≥ 50%; ④ Common pathogens of community-acquired pneumonia (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Stenotrophilus maltophilia) and hospital-acquired pneumonia (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas putrefaciens, Stenotrophilus maltophilia, Burkholderia cepacia complex, Haemophilus influenzae), even if the relative abundance of the genus is ranked after the top 10 in all microbial genera in the library, as long as contamination and hopping are excluded, are included in the suspected list; ⑤ Fungi: If the sequence number of Candida species is ≥ 200; ⑥ Pneumocystis jirovecii sequence number > 50;Common respiratory pathogenic fungi + or fungi, and sequence number ≥ 10; ⑦ Viruses: If herpesvirus (except type 8) is detected, the sequence number ≥ 200; ⑧ Parasites: Sequence number ≥ 50.

## Bioinformatics analysis
In this study, we imported species count and relative abundance data into Python software (version 2023.3.2) and utilized an advanced bioinformatics toolkit for analyzing the complexity and composition of microbial communities. Initially, employing the scikit-bio library allowed us to precisely evaluate the variations in alpha diversity within each subject’s microbial community. This process involved calculating various levels of biodiversity indices, such as the Shannon index, to quantify the richness and evenness of microbial communities. Subsequently, to investigate the similarities and differences among samples, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) provided by the scikit-learn library. PCA, with its dimensionality reduction technique, effectively showcased the microbial composition characteristics at the genus or species level, enabling the visualization of clustering relationships between samples. To delve deeper into the differences in microbial communities between groups, we used permutation multi-variate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), implementable through the scipy or skbio libraries. PERMANOVA aided in determining whether the composition of microbial communities significantly varied due to grouping. Additionally, we further analyzed the microbial community’s taxonomic differences and richness using bubble plots, setting the enrichment criteria to log(fold change, 2)(logFC) > 1.4, P < 0.1. This approach provided a deeper understanding of the differences in relative abundance of microbes between groups.

## Statistical analysis
Results for categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequencies while results for continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD) if normally distributed. Chi squared test was conducted for all categorical variables. As for continuous variables, two independent-sample t-test was conducted for normally distributed continuous variables and Mann–Whitney U test was conducted for not normally distributed. Correlation analysis were performed through Spearman’s correlation analysis, where a Spearman coefficient > | 0.4| was defined as a strong correlation, and p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Result

## Patient characteristics
A total of 70 patients with AECOPD were enrolled in our study (Table 1). The range of the post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC was 21.89% to 69.88%. According to post-bronchodilator FEV1/pre values, 50 fell in PFT I group (FEV1/pre ≥ 50) and 20 in PFT II group (FEV1/pre < 50); and according to the history of exacerbations in the last 1 year, 29 fell in NFE group and 41 in FE group. No significant differences in gender, age, smoking history or BMI were observed among the groups. FEV1/FVC and FEV1/pre in NFE group were significantly higher than those in FE group (p < 0.001 for both).

## Diversity comparison between group
We compared alpha and beta diversity of the BALF microbiome between groups. Shannon index, Simpson index, and Chao1 index (representing alpha diversity) indicate microbial diversity, whereas Richness and ACE index (representing beta diversity) signify microbial abundance. Beta Diversity was analyzed to assess inter-group species differences, with P < 0.05 indicating significant differences and thus meaningful group segregation.
At the genus or species level, the Shannon index in PFT I group was significantly higher than in PFT II group (p = 0.0358, p = 0.0393) (Fig. 1a,b), but there was no significant difference in the Simpson index between the two groups (Fig. 1c,d). The Chao1 index at both the genus and species levels was significantly lower in PFT I group compared to PFT II group (p = 0.0239, p = 0.0007) (Fig. 1e,f); The ACE index was significantly higher in PFT I group at both levels (p = 0.0239, p = 0.0007) (Fig. 2 a,b), with the Richness index showing a significant difference only at the species level (p = 0.001) (Fig. 2c,d). Furthermore, PCA plots showed significant difference at the genus and species level between PFT I and PFT II groups which accounted for 57.8% of the total variance (PERMANOVA, F = 2.67; P = 0.007; F = 2.48; P = 0.005) (Fig. 2e,f). Overall, alpha and beta diversity analyses suggest a significant reduction in diversity, total species, and abundance in PFT II group compared to PFT I group. The Simpson,Shannon Chao1 index, Richness index, and ACE index indices were similar between NFE and FE groups at both the genus and species levels (Additional file 1: Fig S1,2).

## Differences in enriched microbial communities
Bubble charts were used to identify differences in microbial community composition and abundance between PFT I and PFT II groups, with enrichment defined as [(logFC) > 1.4, P < 0.1]. In PFT II group, significant increases in relative abundance were observed for genera such as Moraxella, Fusobacterium, Haemophilus, Porphyromonas, Actinomyces, and Klebsiella, alongside Veillonella atypica, Streptococcus mitis, and Actinomyces graevenitzii (Fig. 5). For fungi and viruses, no significant differences were noted between PFT I and PFT II groups (Table 2). Regarding differences in microbial community distribution between groups with different exacerbation frequencies, NFE group showed significant increases in the relative abundance of Corynebacterium and Prevotella nanceiensis (Supplementary Fig. 4), as well as increases in the relative abundance of Cytomegalovirus and Human betaherpesvirus 5 (Additional file 1: Fig S4 and Table S2).

## Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first application of mNGS technology to investigate the BALF microbiome in AECOPD patients with varying exacerbation frequencies and degrees of airflow limitation. The key findings of this study are as follows: First, the microbiome alpha diversity in the PFT II group was significantly reduced compared to the PFT I group, while the microbiomes of the FE and NFE groups showed similar diversity. Second, there were significant changes in microbiome composition. Within the BALF samples, the most abundant bacterial genera were Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Bacillus, and Rothia, which primarily belong to the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes. The PFT II group was enriched with more specific bacterial clades, primarily from the phyla Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Fusobacteria. Additionally, an enrichment of certain bacteria and viruses was observed in the NFE group, belonging to the phyla Actinobacteria and the family Herpesviridae.
Our study found that in AECOPD patients, there was a significant reduction in alpha diversity at the species level in PFT II group compared to PFT I group, further corroborating the inverse relationship between the degree of airflow limitation and alpha diversity. This is consistent with previous findings, such as those by Mayhew et al., who reported a significant decrease in bacterial diversity indices in sputum samples obtained during the acute exacerbation phase in patients with very severe COPD compared to those with moderate COPD4. However, in contrast to other studies, we did not observe a significant difference in diversity among groups with different exacerbation frequencies. For instance, Li et al.11 found, using deep mNGS technology for the analysis of COPD patient sputum samples, that diversity was significantly reduced in FE group compared to NFE group, indicating a negative correlation between exacerbation frequency and alpha diversity, yet no significant difference was found among groups with different degrees of airflow limitation. Additionally, Yang et al.13 employing 16S rRNA sequencing technology, discovered that the beta diversity (chao1 and Observed OTUs) was significantly lower in the high-risk exacerbation group (≥ 2 moderate or severe exacerbations or ≥ 1 hospitalization due to COPD exacerbation) compared to the low-risk group (< 2 moderate exacerbations per year with no severe exacerbations), while alpha diversity (Shannon) was similar between two groups. This suggests that there is no significant difference in the richness and evenness of the microbial communities between exacerbation risk groups, but there are differences in the number of species and their abundance. The discrepancies among these study outcomes could arise from different grouping standards and geographical environments. Moreover, different sequencing techniques (16S rRNA, mNGS) might also contribute to these variations.
The background microbiome in BALF samples may result from a combination of oral commensals (sputum specimens), local microbial communities (lung tissue and pleural fluid), and contaminants from bronchoscopy. Research indicates that the bronchial microbiome reflects 22.7% oral origin, 5.5% nasal source, and 71.7% of unknown origins13. BALF exhibits high efficiency in mNGS detection due to the high pathogen abundance with minimal influence from the common flora. Additionally, the microbial spectrum detected in BALF is the most extensive compared to other respiratory specimen types14. In healthy individuals, bacteria mainly enter the lungs through inhalation from the mouth or direct mucosal diffusion. Compared to other areas like the upper respiratory tract, oral cavity, and upper digestive tract, the oropharyngeal microbiome is similar in species to the lung microbiome15,16, but it displays higher diversity12. In a study on the lung microbiome of patients with mild to moderate COPD17, Actinomyces, Streptococcus, Propionibacterium, Corynebacterium, Delftia, and Rothia were identified as the most abundant genera in COPD lung microbiomes based on BALF studies. In our research, the most abundant microbes were Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Corynebacterium, and Rothia. Current research on the COPD microbiome primarily focuses on sputum specimens due to easier feasibility and lower costs from a research design and cost perspective. In other COPD-related studies based on sputum specimens13,18,19, the dominant genera were Streptococcus, Prevotellaceae, Veillonella, Neisseria, and Rothia, consistent with our findings. Additionally, the composition and abundance of the lung microbiome may exhibit dynamic changes in different states of acute exacerbation12. Additionally, studies have confirmed the correlation between the composition of blood microbiota and respiratory system diseases. The results of blood microbiome show distinct characteristics in patients with nodules. Yordan Hodzhev observed that in patients with nodules, an increase in bacterial abundance in blood samples is associated with five genera, which are Veillonella, Prevotella, Cutibacterium, Corynebacterium, and Streptococcus20. Overall, Streptococcusis considered the most common genus across all samples in AECOPD17,21. In contrast, Prevotellaceaeis considered dominant in stable COPD18. A multicenter study suggested that the abundance of Prevotellaincreases with the alleviation of airflow restriction, potentially offering a mitigating effect on the severity of airflow limitation in COPD22. These microbes are considered core members of the lung microbiome.
Among the top 10 most abundant bacterial taxa, we observed that in PFT I group, compared to PFT II group, showed higher enrichment for Streptococcus, Veillonella, and Rothia, with a significant reduction in the abundance of Corynebacterium, although only Corynebacterium showed significant differences (P < 0.05). Previous research has indicated that Streptococcuspneumoniae infection plays a critical role in COPD pathogenesis, associated with disease progression and worsening airflow limitation23. Recent findings suggest a positive correlation between the relative abundance of Streptococcus and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in peripheral blood, implying that Streptococcus may contribute to adverse outcomes, such as hospitalization or death in AECOPD, it has been found that Streptococcuscan activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs), leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines24. Possibly exacerbating COPD conditions through inflammatory responses; however, the mechanisms remain to be further explored25. However, prior studies were unable to further differentiate species within the genus. Streptococcus using the 16S rRNA sequencing platform. Veillonella, an anaerobic Gram-negative coccus commonly found in the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and vagina, was found18 to be significantly positively correlated with FEV1/pre (p < 0.05) and FEV1/FVC (p < 0.001). Some studies also observed a higher abundance of Veillonellain healthy populations compared to COPD groups, indicating its beneficial presence in both health and disease states26. Li W et al. observed11 that the Rothiagenus was more abundant in mild COPD compared to severe COPD, consistent with our findings and similar conclusions in previously published COPD studies3,4,11,13,22. Based on these studies, we speculate that Veillonella and Rothia may belong to beneficial microbial groups, potentially improving airflow limitation in patients, though the precise mechanisms of their effects remain unclear. In patients with cystic fibrosis, Rothiamucilaginosa might inhibit inflammation by reducing phosphorylation of IκB-α, thus inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation, suggesting a beneficial role in the respiratory tract27. Furthermore, our study found Rothia mucilaginosa to be more prevalent in PFTI group compared to PFTII group, yet its tolerance load in the body and its role in the degree of airflow limitation in COPD requires further investigation. It’s noteworthy that due to the small sample size of our study, correlation analysis failed to confirm a significant association between Streptococcus, Veillonella, Rothia, and Corynebacterium with the degree of airflow limitation or exacerbation frequency (Additional file 1:Table S3). Corynebacterium, a common skin microbe first observed in nasal samples17, was found in studies of the nasal microbiome in COPD to vary with symptom frequency, with a higher proportion of non-pathogenic Corynebacterium in the high-frequency occurrence group, consistent with our findings. fly, we found that Corynebacterium’s abundance percentage was significantly higher in PFTII group than in PFTI group (p = 0.0298). Research has confirmed that Corynebacterium(a genus of bacteria) can activate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines through the NLRP3 inflammatory pathway28,29. Suggesting that Corynebacterium might worsen airflow limitation, but further in-depth studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.
As the concept of the“microbiome”is explored in greater depth, our focus has expanded beyond bacterial communities to include fungal and viral populations. For instance, research by Nguyen and colleagues highlights the impact and importance of lung fungal communities on respiratory diseases30. The phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycotaare the most common31, encompassing genera such as Candida and Aspergillus, aligning with the findings of our study. Reports suggest that in COPD patients, both during stable phases and acute exacerbations, Aspergilluscan be detected in sputum in 13%− 29% of cases32,33. In related studies, 22% of COPD patients with Aspergillus-positive cultures were diagnosed with invasive pulmonary aspergillosis34. These findings underscore the significance of Aspergillus, warranting high suspicion for the development of Aspergillus-related diseases and potential clinical deterioration in AECOPD, thus necessitating cautious treatment approaches.
Research on the lung virome has shown infection rates ranging from 22–64%35,36. In our study, Herpesviridaewere most abundantly represented, yet no significant differences were observed between groups in terms of viral genera, consistent with other studies37,38. The dysbiosis of the virome may accompany bacterial imbalances, leading to bacterial overgrowth and infection39,40, highlighting the importance of virus-bacteria interactions. Historically, characterizing the mycobiome and virome in patients with airway diseases has been limited and constrained by technological challenges. However, the development of metagenomic technologies in recent years has provided technical support for a broader and more systematic study of the lung microbiome. Co-infections with bacteria, viruses, and fungi are quite common in respiratory diseases. Therefore, more comparative studies of the respiratory mycobiome and virome during stable and acute exacerbation phases are needed to identify potential correlations between the mycobiome or virome and disease progression or exacerbation frequency during stable COPD phases. Our understanding of the respiratory microbiome is primarily based on descriptive studies, with a lack of in-depth causal or translational research into disease stability and exacerbation phases31.
Bubble Chart analysis reveals a more diverse bacterial population in PFT II group, predominantly encompassing the phyla Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Fusobacteria. Additionally, a higher enrichment of certain bacteria and viruses is also observed in NFE group, mainly belonging to Actinobacteria and the Cytomegaloviridae. This aligns with recent findings on the sputum microbiome composition and diversity in COPD patients across different stages of the disease and levels of lung function11. Compared to prior studies, Pragman et al.17 also noted a more prevalent occurrence of Actinobacteria in the sputum of NFE group. Furthermore, research by Rigauts et al.27 highlighted that the respiratory mucosal surfaces of healthy individuals are primarily inhabited by Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes. The work of Cameron et al. discovered that community-acquired pneumonia in BALF samples is characterized by a depletion of certain genera within the Bacteroidetesphylum41. These findings are somewhat consistent with our research. We noted that the species enriched in PFT II and FE groups mostly belong to Proteobacteria, aligning with other studies that have found an overrepresentation of Proteobacteriain severe COPD25,41.
In our study, we encountered some regrets. Despite discovering strong positive correlations (r > 0.5, p < 0.05) between certain microbial communities, the current research on the coexistence relationships among these microbes is still insufficient. Consequently, we were unable to further describe the reasons and their biological significance.
It is noteworthy that our study is the first to utilize mNGS technology to compare the microbiomes of AECOPD BALF specimens across different degrees of airflow limitation. Compared to the traditional 16S rRNA method, mNGS technology offers several advantages: (1) it covers not only bacteria but also eukaryotes and DNA viruses; (2) it provides higher resolution at the species level compared to the genus level in terms of taxonomic resolution; (3) due to its non-targeted nature, the mNGS method is less likely to be biased; (4) it has the capability to discover new or unexpected organisms. Currently, mNGS technology may play a key role in identifying pathogens involved in the exacerbation of COPD. Our research employs mNGS technology to explore the association between the AECOPD BALF microbiome and the severity of airflow limitation, suggesting that mNGS technology could become an important tool for predicting the progression of AECOPD and guiding its treatment in the future.
However, it is important to note that this study has several limitations. First, the sample size is small, and there is considerable individual variation in microbial research, which can be easily influenced by environmental and climatic factors. Sampling of the lung microbiome requires strict negative control methods. Second, this study employs a cross-sectional design, which only allows for correlational analysis and does not facilitate in-depth exploration of causality. Lastly, although we excluded subjects who had received antibiotic treatment in the last month, patients who frequently exacerbate may have received more antibiotic treatments in the past year than others, which also represents a limitation of this study.

## Conclusion
In concluding our study on AECOPD, distinct microbial composition differences were observed between groups classified by varying degrees of airflow limitation and exacerbation frequency. Specifically, patients with severe airflow limitation exhibited significantly reduced microbial diversity. Conversely, microbial diversity was comparable across patients with varying frequencies of exacerbation. Furthermore, distinct bacterial taxa were enriched in groups differentiated by degrees of airflow restriction and exacerbation frequency. Interestingly, the most abundant microbial types in BALF samples mirrored those found in sputum samples. These findings provide crucial insights into the pathogenesis of AECOPD, potentially unveiling targets for therapeutic intervention and informing strategies to enhance treatment outcomes for AECOPD patients. Moreover, this research lays a foundation for developing personalized treatment plans tailored to the unique profiles of AECOPD patients, marking a significant step towards more effective and targeted management of the condition.
