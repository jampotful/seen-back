Abstract
The giant-fruiting body, sporangiophore, of the fungus Phycomyces blakesleeanus grows toward near-UV/blue-light
(phototropism). The blue-light photoreceptor, MadA, should contain FAD bound to the LOV domain, and forms a complex
with MadB. Both proteins are homologs of white collar proteins WC-1 and WC-2 from the fungus Neurospora crassa and
should be localized in nuclei, where they function as a light-sensitive transcription factor complex. The photoreceptor
properties of two further Wc proteins, WcoA and WcoB, remain unclear because of lack of mutants. We propose that WcoA
and/or WcoB play essential roles in photoreception by enlarging the dynamic range that help explain complex stimulus–
response relationships. Even though red light does not elicit photo-movement or -differentiation in Phycomyces, it affects the
effectiveness of blue light which indicates an underlying photochromic receptor. Protein sequence searches show that other
fungal red-light receptors are absent in Phycomyces. The solution to the red-light riddle is thus sought in the ability of Wc
complexes to generate after blue-light irradiation a neutral flavosemiquinone radical that absorbs red light and functions as
primary photochemical signal. Phototropism requires Ras-GAP (MadC) as part of the signal transduction cascade and, we
propose, to allocate photoreceptors in the plasmalemma of the growing zone, which allows for receptor dichroism, range
adjustment and contrast recognition for spatial orientation. Phototropic signal chains must entail transduction networks
between Wc receptors and small G-proteins and their associated Ras-GAP and Ras-GEF proteins. The interactions among
these proteins should occur in trans-Golgi vesicles and the plasmalemma of the growing zone.

It is also conceivable that the Mad complex is also located
outside of the nucleus to regulate the activity of Ras-GAP
(MadC).

1.2 Blue‑light receptors see red: historical note
For about a century prior the molecular identification of
the blue-light receptor of Phycomyces as a homolog of the
Neurospora white collar complex (WCC) with MadA as a
likely photoreceptor with a flavin bound chromophore [1],
research in Phycomyces photobiology was largely dominated by a blue-light physiology that entailed investigations
of stimulus–response relationships and action spectroscopy
combined with mutant analyses. Though the vast majority
of experiments were done with actinic blue light, while red
light was considered merely a “safelight”, it became apparent during the 1980s that also long-wavelength light ranging
from green to almost far-red could confer a great impact on
blue-light elicited responses. The motivation for directing
pure blue-light physiology in this direction originated in
1977 with a seminal insight of Karl Hartmann, the scientist who provided the explanation for the “high-irradiance
response” of phytochrome [16]. He recognized that not only
phytochromes but also blue-light receptors could in principle operate like photochromes, which implies photochromic
chromophores that manifest photoreversibility and complex
photoequilibria under mono- and dichromatic irradiation
[17]. Hartmann’s conjecture was based on the observation
that fluence(rate)-response curves generated at different
near-UV and blue wavelengths could display variable slopes
and plateau heights, a feature that was already well-known
from phytochrome physiology, specifically the high-irradiance response [16, 18]. This rather abstract, yet very fruitful
concept, which was, alas, hidden in a German textbook of
biophysics, was picked up in 1980 by Rainer Hertel [19],
who molded the original idea into a more tangible model
that drew from the photochemistry of a small group of flavoenzymes that display a two-step reduction instead of the
more common single-step reduction mechanism. The Hertel
proposal in favor of a hypothetical photochromic blue-light
receptor [19] implied a light-driven photocycle that entailed
a flavin chromophore in its four distinct redox states, i.e.,
FAD (or FMN), its neutral semiquinone radical F

Physiologic predictions of the Hertel model were tested
in a number of experiments that employed dichromatic
irradiation protocols: blue-red or red-blue pulses or even
long-term di- or even trichromatic irradiations. Of special
interest for Phycomyces became more elaborate protocols,
in which long-wavelength irradiations (> 520 nm) were
applied during the critical phase of dark adaptation. These
experiments allowed the prediction of red-light absorbing
flavosemiquinone intermediates that could operate as redlight absorbing chromophores [24–26]. A deeper molecular
understanding became possible only after isolation of plant
phototropins and cryptochromes and the photochemistry of
flavin-associated LOV domains [23, 27–29].

unaddressed since the identification of the Phycomyces
Wc receptors [1, 4] and to update the list of its identified
photoreceptors. The three major issues that require
clarification are:

WcoA and WcoB in enlarging the dynamic range. We argue

2.1 Sequence domain searches
The aim of the sequence domain searches was (i) to rule
out the existence of red-light receptors in Phycomyces such
as phytochrome and rhodopsins, and (ii) the existence of
the blue-light receptor VIVID, which could generate, beside
MadA, also red-light absorbing receptor intermediates.
We included in our sequence search also proteins that are
known to play major roles in sensory transduction chains
of Phycomyces and plants. Examples are Ras-GAP (MadC)
[11] and its associated proteins (Ras and Ras-GEF), because
of the altered red-light responses displayed by madC mutants
of Phycomyces. The small G-proteins play essential roles in
gravi- and phototropism of Arabidopsis (ARF-GAP, ARF,
ARF-GEF) [30]. The full triade should thus also exist in
Phycomyces (Table 1).

3 Arguments supporting the existence
of low‑ and high‑irradiance receptors
3.1 The “thresholds” of madA/B mutants define
the threshold of wcoA/B
The argument that speaks for an active role of wcoA and
wcoB in the light physiology of Phycomyces is rather
straightforward and relies on a few simple observations:
(i) None of the light-insensitive phototropism mutants
is completely “blind”. The residual phototropic threshold
of mutant madA, for example, is near ­10–5 ­Wm−2 [32]. The
residual light sensitivity must thus derive from another
blue-light receptor. The stimulus–response curves of the
mutants, display–provided their functions are knocked out

completely– the thresholds of the remaining blue-light
receptor, which can only derive from WcoA and/or WcoB
in combination with one of the Wc-2 proteins (Fig. 3).
Presently, it’s not known exactly at what fluence rate the
responsiveness of the MadA/MadB receptor complex drops
off to zero. Further below we present data that the drop off
must be somewhere near the middle of the dynamic range.
We propose that at intermediate irradiances photoreceptor
complexes containing WcoA/WcoB suppress the activity of
the MadA/MadB complex (see later in the text). At very high
irradiances near 1­ 02 ­Wm−2 the bending drops off to zero,
because the photoreceptors of the sporangiophore that are
proximal and distal to the unilateral light are saturated [34].
(ii) We propose that the Wc complexes MadA/MadB
and complexes containing the photoreceptors WcoA and/
or WcoB represent low- and high-irradiance receptors,
respectively. Theoretically, more irradiance domains are
possible, because the additional Wc-2 proteins shown in
Fig. 1 allow still further combinations of Wc proteins. A
closer examination of the requisite literature will show that
responses differ in the two dynamic ranges, a fact, which
helps explain several unresolved genetic and physiologic
riddles.

3.2 Possible interactions between Wc‑1 and Wc‑2
proteins
While MadA seems to be associated with the low-irradiance
region of phototropism (Fig. 3), WcoA and/or WcoB should
be operating in the high-irradiance region. In contrast,
the four different Wc-2 proteins could be able to operate
in the low- as well as in the high-irradiance region as can
be demonstrated by their potential ability to interact with
both the low- as well as the high-irradiance Wc-1 proteins
(Fig. 4). It is possible that the interaction between these
proteins occur through their conserved PAS domains [4].
We propose that the possibility of freely combining Wc-1
and Wc-2 proteins occurs not merely in silico, but that it
plays a crucial role in vivo. Complex formation has the
potential to affect the quantum effectiveness of the photoprimary reaction, as well as the kinetics of the photocycle
and adaptation, and further, also wavelength discrimination
(see below).

3.5 Action spectra of phototropic balance differ
in low‑ and high‑irradiance ranges
Phototropic balance of sporangiophores between a reference
blue light (450 nm) and a second, but opposed light source of
variable wavelength provides a convenient way of generating
an action spectrum for phototropism [40, 41]. The method
has the advantage that one can address the problem whether
or not the shape of an action spectrum depends on the irradiance of the reference wavelength. It became apparent that the
action spectrum in the low-irradiance region (3·10–8 ­Wm−2)
differed substantially from the one generated at intermediate irradiances ­(10–4 ­Wm−2) [39] (Fig. 6b). Of particular
interest is the observation that the relative effectiveness of
near-UV light at 386 nm dramatically decreases relative to

the 450 nm light when low fluence rates are employed. In
contrast, at elevated fluence rates near 1 ­Wm−2 near-UV is
1.3 times more effective than 450 nm light; its effectiveness approaches zero close to the absolute threshold slightly
above ­10–9 ­Wm−2 (Fig. 6a). We propose that the high-irradiance region is governed by photoreceptor complexes that can
be distinguished by their rather different spectral properties
from the complexes governing the low-irradiance region.
The data are in accordance with our hypothesis that
MadA/MadB governs the low-irradiance range, while the
two Wc-1 proteins WcoA and/or WcoB with the requisite
Wc-2 proteins govern the high-irradiance range. We
propose that both receptor complexes possess similar, but
not identical photocycles, which may involve light-induced
redox cycles that could include FAD, FADH,· and ­FADH2.
The two-photoreceptor hypothesis has a bearing on
the interpretation of action spectra of photo-gravitropic
equilibrium of madA, madB and madC mutants, because
the latter two possess altered action spectra [10, 12]. The
shape of the action spectrum of madA mutants is, however,
unaltered (only the effectiveness of each wavelength is
diminished by a constant factor of about 1­ 04). We conclude
that the remaining light-sensitivity is due to the activity
of the high-irradiance receptor WcoA and/or WcoB. In
contrast, madB mutants display defective action spectra
whose near-UV maximum is practically absent. The
observations indicate that the high-irradiance receptor
complex should be ineffective for near-UV, perhaps because
it is replaced by one of the three Wct proteins (Fig. 1) that
may form altered complexes with WcoA or WcoB proteins.
It is also possible that any of these Wct proteins replace
MadB in the Mad complex resulting in a partially functional
photoreceptor complex. The absence of a near-UV maximum
indicates in any case that MadB must play a crucial role
in the photocycle of the wild type. Future binding studies
and yeast-two-hybrid assays may indicate, whether or not
and to what extent the single components of the various Wc
complexes can be exchanged.
Because madC mutants display defective action spectra
of photo-gravitropic equilibrium, in which the prominent
near-UV maximum is conspicuously reduced [12], we
suggest an important role for MadC (Ras-GAP) in the
photocycles of the two photoreceptor systems. We will show
further below that loss of MadC activity causes very likely
an accumulation of the red-light absorbing flavosemiquinone
radical ­FADH·. The absence of MadC could possibly disrupt
molecular interactions within the Mad complex (Fig. 1b, c).
We can propose that MadC and the Mad complex interact as
part of the transduction chain and that the mutation in MadC
modifies these interactions in a way that the action spectrum
is modified. Similar conclusions had been reached earlier

The blue-light receptor VVD of Neurospora contains, like

3.13 How MadA/MadB and WcoA or WcoB
complexes could interact
The genomic presence of wc-1 genes madA, wcoA, wcoB
and of wc-2 genes madB, wctB, wctC and wctD (Fig. 1a)
allows theoretically for 12 different combinations of Wc
complexes in nuclei. Even though it remains presently
unknown how many active photoreceptor complexes do exist
beside MadA/MadB, one can already discuss, in which way
MadA complexes could possibly interact with WcoA and/or
WcoB complexes. We base our hypotheses on the seminal
findings of Idnurm et al. [1] that (i) madA is expressed in
darkness while being repressed in light, and (ii) that wcoA
is poorly expressed in darkness but strongly in light. These
observations are essential for our proposal of how the two
receptor complexes could possibly cooperate. We propose
that (i) WcoA or WcoB complexes suppress directly or
indirectly MadA/MadB or madA and madB gene expression,
and (ii) after light-absorption MadA/MadB stimulates
WcoA or WcoB or their respective gene expressions. The
two Wc complexes thus control each other in a feed-forward
and feed-back loop, respectively, which leads—depending

1598

on outer irradiances—to a dynamic maintenance of the two
receptor activities. Depending on the outer irradiances they
can thus shift the receptor mode toward the low- or highirradiances (Fig. 11). This autoregulation would be equally
relevant for photo-differentiation and phototropism.
We suggest that the biphasic nature of the
stimulus–response curves shown above could be the result of
the proposed inhibitory action that WcoA or WcoB exert on
the expression of the madA and madB genes. They could thus
operate in a manner analogous to VVD, which operates by
disrupting the interaction among two WCCs as they bind the
promoter [66]. One should also consider the possibility that
MadA of Phycomyces may operate in a manner analogous
to that of Neurospora, in which the Wc-1 protein can inhibit
the expression of the wc-2 gene [67]. The unusually large
number of different Wc-1 and Wc-2 proteins in Phycomyces
provides thus for a great variety of mutant phenotypes that
exceeds that of Neurospora, but that presently have still to
be discovered. It is expected that once more details of the
gene and protein expressions become available that also the
currently somewhat bewildering phenotypes of double mad
mutants (e.g., madA madB, madA madC) become easier
accessible to interpretation. It is interesting to note that the
double madA madB mutant is less sensitive to light than each
single mutant [59–61]. Perhaps the absence of both MadA
and MadB disrupts or modifies the activity of the additional
photoreceptor complexes in Phycomyces.

5 Discussion: hypotheses and prospects
5.1 Localization of the blue‑light receptors
inside the growing zone
The problem of photoreceptor anisotropy had been a
major topic in the Phycomyces photo-physiology. Early
investigations with polarized light were indicating that the
transition dipole moments of the blue-light receptor had to
be oriented relative to the orientation of the cell wall of the
sporangiophore [7–9, 78]. Later, mainly theoretical studies,
arrived at the conclusion that the photoreceptors could be
dichroic under the condition that only a subpopulation of
them is strictly oriented close to the plasmalemma, while
another population is randomly oriented [5]. A general
consensus was reached that the data indicate that the
photoreceptors for LGR and phototropism had to reside in
or at least close to the plasmalemma of the growing zone.
A seminal study that employed laser-generated grazing
microbeam irradiations for eliciting phototropism reached,
however, the conclusion that most photoreceptors had to be
localized inside the cytoplasmic cylinder that surrounds the
central vacuoleseven though some receptors could extend
to the plasmalemma and cell wall. Photoreceptor dichroism
was not postulated in this work [6].
In the context of the mode of action of the Wc
photoreceptors of Phycomyces the topic of receptor
dichroism is as relevant as it is puzzling, because Wc
receptors were identified as DNA-associated and lightinducible transcription factors that operate by definition
in nuclei (Fig. 1a) [79]. This view may be entirely correct
for long-term photoresponses such as photo-differentiation
of mycelia: (i) photo-carotenogenesis [62], (ii) photophorogenesis [58, 80], and also of sporangiophores, i.e.,
photo-sporangiogenesis [81]. Those light responses do not
require a madC gene (Ras-GAP). In contrast, the LGR and
phototropism of the sporangiophore occur rather quickly
with a lag phase as short as 5 min for phototropism [34,
47, 82], and depend on the presence of the madC gene. To
explain the phototropism of sporangiophores one requires
a very different receptor concept for its topology and
assembly as well as different transduction steps subsequent
to the primary photochemistry. To explain photoreceptor
dichroism, growth-rate modulations and phototropism
elicited with polarized light, it is required that photoreceptors
reside in the close proximity of or even in the plasmalemma
to stabilize their spatial orientation [5, 8]. Alternatively,
photoreceptors or a subpopulation of them should reside in
the cytoplasmic cylinder surrounding the central vacuoles
and a minority also in the plasmalemma [6]. A solution
to the problem could come from studies with trans-Golgi

Photochemical & Photobiological Sciences (2024) 23:1587–1607

vesicles of plants that demonstrate their ability to bind and
subsequently allocate proteins in the plasmalemma, among
them auxin related influx- and efflux-transporters (AUX
and PIN) and notably small G-proteins (ARF) and their
activating (ARF-GAP) and exchange factors (ARF-GEF)
(see below). The idea, that Wc blue-light receptors could
associate inside the growing zone with membrane vesicles
agrees with the finding of Meistrich et al. [6]. Of particular
importance is the notion that one part of the photoreceptors
should be dichroically oriented (Fig. 1c) and the rest
randomly [5]. It appears consistent to associate the dichroic
receptors with plasmalemma and the randomly oriented ones
with membrane vesicles that are localized between vacuoles
and plasmalemma. For this reason, it would be fruitful to
search in the Phycomyces genome for vesicle-associated
proteins that are orthologs to those in Arabidopsis. Good
contenders comprise ARF-type small G-proteins and the
related GAP and GEF proteins (see below).

5.2 Putative early photochemistry in mycelia
and sporangiophores
So far, the only identified blue-light receptor proper of
Phycomyces is the MadA/MadB complex, in which the
likely chromophore FAD is non-covalently bound to the
LOV domain of MadA [1, 4]. Like other Wc receptors with
LOV domain also those of Phycomyces should operate as
light-activated transcription factors that associate with DNA,
specifically LREs. It is pertinent that WcoB lacks a zincfinger domain [4] which suggests that, it may not operate as a
light-dependent transcription factor. It is possible, however,
that WcoB together with a Wc-2 protein may regulate
transcription using the Wc-2 zinc-finger domain, but the role
of WcoB as a blue-light photoreceptor that participates in a
signal transduction chain not linked to transcription should
be explored further.
Upon blue-light irradiation a neutral FAD-semiquinone
radical pair [­ FADH· cys-S·] is formed that absorbs blue and
red light (Fig. 13a). The neutral F
­ ADH· radical represents
the primary effector molecule that elicits subsequent steps of
the photocycle and further steps of the transduction chain. If
a red-light irradiation follows a prior blue-light irradiation,
the elicited response is (partially) suppressed, because the
semiquinone pool reverts back to FAD, before it can elicit
a response. From the radical pair a FAD cysteine adduct is
formed (absorbance maximum at 390 nm) that can decay in
darkness back to FAD. Alternatively, the reversal to FAD
can also be elicited by irradiation with near-UV at 390 nm
(Fig. 13a). It is known from work with the LOV domain
of VIVID that the replacement of the conserved cysteine,

Photochemical & Photobiological Sciences (2024) 23:1587–1607

which is required for adduct formation, by alanine does
not affect the formation of the flavosemiquinone radical
[53]. We propose that the photocycle can occur in nuclei,
and in a modified version, also in cytoplasmic vesicles of
the growing zone (Fig. 13b). The positive and negative
interactions between the two Wc complexes (Fig. 11) are
proposed to occur in nuclei, which entails regulation through
gene expression (Fig. 1a). The low- and high-irradiance
Wc receptors are proposed to occur also in vesicles
of sporangiophores, where they may possess different
mechanisms of negative and positive feedback interactions.

5.3 Phototropism and photo‑gravitropic
equilibrium of the sporangiophore
is not explained by nuclear localization of Wc
photoreceptors
To explain the complex light physiology of the
sporangiophore, specifically its photoreceptor dichroism,
one needs to postulate a modified setting for the primary
photochemistry, which we propose to occur in plasmalemma
and also in vesicles, which are localized in the cytoplasmic
cylinder of the growing zone that surrounds the central
vacuoles of the sporangiophore. How could the switch
from light-induced transcription in nuclei (Fig. 1a) to lightinduced phototropism and LGR (Fig. 1b) be brought about?
Because madC codes for a Ras-GAP protein, it is apparent
that the entire apparatus of the requisite small G-protein
triad should be involved in this process, i.e., Ras, Ras-GAP
and Ras-GEF, which represent proteins localized in transGolgi vesicles and plasmalemma (Fig. 1b). The binding
to vesicles, their transport and their actomyosin-driven
vesicle allocation in the plasmalemma [83] would allow for
photoreceptor dichroism and at the same time for transport
of auxin-related proteins [84].

5.4 The Wc complexes for phototropism and LGR
operate through the small G‑protein Ras and its
regulators GEF and GAP
We propose that the vesicle- and plasmalemma-associated
Wc receptors operate by affecting the physical states of the
small G-protein Ras. After near-UV/blue-light absorption
the neutral ­FADH· radical (Fig. 13a) could stimulate RasGEF and Ras-GAP (madC) indirectly via second messengers
(e.g., ­C a 2+, cAMP, CaM), which leads to a balanced
probability that Ras stays in the activated state (Ras-GTP),
from which subsequent steps of the transduction chain are
initiated (Fig. 13b). In Arabidopsis, for example, bluelight-induced ­Ca2+ can bind to Arf-GAP, not however,
to Arf-GEF, which lacks the requisite binding domain
[30]. In madC mutants (Ras-GAP) the GTPase enhancing
activity is absent, and as a consequence, the light thresholds

1601

for the subsequent reaction signals are raised, i.e., the
sporangiophore becomes phototropically “night blind”. This
is expected, because the pool of activated Ras-GTP stays
constitutively elevated.
The loss of phototropic sensitivity of madC mutants can
be partially healed, i.e., the blue-light threshold is lowered
100-fold, when sporangiophores are dichromatically
irradiated with unilateral blue and red light [13].
The in-vitro photocyle shown in Fig. 13a makes it likely
that the involved time constants would differ between MadA,
WcoA and WcoB, with the result that different photoequilibria and different pool sizes would be established for the
requisite chromophore intermediates. Phenotypically, these
effects would manifest for the low- and high-irradiance
receptors in changed wavelength-dependent light sensitivities and thus changed action spectra (Figs. 3, 4, 5).
The curious observation that a dichromatic blue-red
irradiation lowers the phototropic threshold of madC mutants
indicates that the lack of Ras-GAP causes a relative excess
of GEF and thus also of ­FADH·. Red-light-induced photoreversal lowers the pool of F
­ ADH· and at the same time also
the threshold. We envisage that the close physical contact
between the Wc receptors and the small G protein cycle
(G-cycle) proteins mutually influence each other. It is thus
expected that the Ras-related proteins, most prominently
GAP and GEF, affect the spectroscopic properties and time
constants of the different members of the photocycle.
The involvement of G-cycle proteins in the early
photochemistry has another crucial consequence. As
the vesicles represent not only a docking station for wc
receptors, but also for the allocation of auxin-related
transport facilitators such as AUX1 [75], which are required
also for gravitropism, we suggest that the photo-gravitropic
balance, i.e., the antagonistic interaction between photo- and
gravitropism, is likewise generated at the interface between
photocycle and small G-protein cycle. The model helps
explain why mutant madF of Phycomyces displays at the
same time a greatly altered action spectrum of phototropism
as well as a defective gravitropism and avoidance responses
[36]. And further, mutant C148 madC is not only phototropic
defective, but at the same time hyper-gravitropic [86], an
observation that could be attributed to the proposed excess of
­FADH·. Besides, it displays an abnormal phototropic action
spectrum, which has a prominent maximum at 390 nm [12]
that coincides with the 390 nm peak of the FAD-cys-adduct
of LOV domain receptors [12] (Fig. 13a).

5.6 Nuclear, vesicular, and plasmalemma localized
photoreceptors may mutually control each
other
The basic physiologic operations, such as sensitivity control
(threshold) and contrast recognition in space and time (e.g.,
phototropism), need to be under the control of the nuclear
and also the vesicular and plasmalemma photoreceptor
proteins. For example, the highly dynamic phototropic
threshold (Fig. 7) should depend linearly, or even nonlinearly, on the absolute amount of receptor protein. This
assumption should hold for the low- as well as in the highirradiance region. Since it appears likely that WcoA and
WcoB and the other Wc-2 proteins may have arisen from
an early gene duplication [31, 89], it becomes apparent,
how complex the fine adjustment between high-irradiance

Photochemical & Photobiological Sciences (2024) 23:1587–1607

proteins–the presumable evolutionary newcomers—and
low-irradiance proteins must have been in the distant past,
a process continuing to this day. The said fine-adjustment
comprises between the pool sizes of photoreceptor
populations also the requisite primary photochemical
products and the pool sizes of the subsequent members of
the transduction chain.

5.7 Specialization of Wc proteins after gene
duplication in Mucoral fungi
The expansion of wc genes in Phycomyces may allow the
formation of several types of photoreceptor complexes
for detection of light in the high or low intensity ranges.
However, other mucoral fungi with similar wc gene
repertoires may use the Wc proteins in different ways. In
Phycomyces, MadA is required for all the responses to light,
but in the related mucoral fungus Mucor circinelloides
the three wc-1 genes are specialized as photoreceptors for
specific responses. The Wc-1 protein encoded by mcwc-1a
is required for the phototropism of Mucor fruiting bodies,
while the Wc-1 protein encoded by mcwc-1c is required
for the activation of carotene biosynthesis by light. A third
wc-1 gene, mcwc-1b, encodes a Wc-1 photoreceptor that
participates in the regulation of carotene biosynthesis and
its activity is regulated by CrgA-dependent ubiquitylation
[90, 91]. We can only speculate about the role of the Wc-1
proteins in other Mucoral fungi, but it is very likely that
these proteins may have specialized to serve different
regulatory purposes in each group of Mucoral fungi.

5.8 New avenues of research
Max Delbrück and his colleagues actively promoted the
use of the sporangiophore of Phycomyces as a suitable
model to understand the molecular biology of sensory
perception in eukaryotic cells [34]. Among the advantages
where the large size of the sporangiophore (several cm in
length), the fact that the sporangiophore was a single cell
with no septa separating free-moving nuclei, and a genetic
system that allowed the mapping of blind mutants in a
genetic map. The sequencing of the Phycomyces genome
provided an additional tool to characterize the proteome
and identify candidate proteins that could participate in
signal transduction [31]. The combination of genome
sequencing and genetic mapping allowed the identification
of the repertoire of photoreceptors encoded in the genome
[1, 4, 11]. However, the transformation of Phycomyces
with exogenous DNA and the isolation of gene knockouts
has not been possible despite numerous attempts and the
use of a variety of technologies [92]. The absence of a

1603

suitable technique for deleting genes should not deter us
from attempting to explore the molecular photobiology of
Phycomyces further. With appropriate biochemical skills,
it should be possible to express the components of the Mad
complex and other Wc proteins in E. coli or yeast cells and to
assay their possible interactions in vitro, their photochemical
properties, and their DNA-binding capacities. In addition,
the protein sequences should allow the isolation of specific
antibodies for in vivo observation of the proteins, their
subcellular localization, and any changes in localization or
interactions caused by exposure to unilateral light. The large
size of the Phycomyces sporangiophore should be amenable
to modern cell biology techniques that, combined with
suitable biochemical characterization of the photoreceptors
and signal transduction proteins, will help us to understand
how this single giant cell can discriminate the direction and
intensity of light to modify its direction of growth.

6 Conclusions and directions for future
studies
(i) The Wc receptors of Phycomyces should be
localized in nuclei (gene expression) or should be
associated with trans-Golgi vesicles or plasmalemma
(phototropism and LGR of sporangiophores). The
photoreceptors can coexist in both places and are not
mutually exclusive.
(ii) Data from the blue- and red-light physiology of
Phycomyces agree with known spectroscopic data
from LOV domains of diverse fungal and plant
organisms.
(iii) PFAM based sequence analyses exclude the presence
of fungal red-light photoreceptors in Phycomyces.
(iv) It is likely that the wcoA and wcoB genes contribute
to the light physiology of Phycomyces by enlarging
the dynamic range and participating in sensor and
effector adaptation.
(v) It is proposed that the WcoA or WcoB complex
inhibits the expression of madA and madB, while the
MadA/MadB complex stimulates the expression of
wcoA and wcoB.
(vi) The evolutionary origin of sporangiophores
phototropism may be found in genome duplication,
which allowed the development of a second set of wc
photoreceptors that relied on interactions with small
G-proteins and requisite signal networks.
(vii) We propose that photoreceptor adaptation level
resides in the amount of a red-absorbing semiquinone
species.

1604

(viii) Future work should include the expression of the
Wc-1 and Wc-2 proteins to assay interactions
in vitro, and their DNA-binding capacities. Further
biochemical and molecular characterization of the
Wc proteins will help to understand their role in the
photobiology of Phycomyces and related fungi.
