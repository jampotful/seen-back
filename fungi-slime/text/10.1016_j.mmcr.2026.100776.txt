Severe fungal keratitis caused by <i>Lasiodiplodia theobromae</i> following grass trimmer-related ocular trauma: a case report.

# Severe fungal keratitis caused by Lasiodiplodia theobromae following grass trimmer–related ocular trauma: a case report

## Abstract
Lasiodiplodia theobromae is an uncommon but emerging filamentous fungus primarily recognized as a plant pathogen. We report a case of severe L. theobromae keratitis in a 57-year-old man who presented with left eye pain, redness, and progressive visual loss following grass trimmer–related ocular trauma. Ocular examination revealed diffuse corneal haziness, dense stromal infiltrate, total epithelial defect, and descemetoceles. The organism was isolated from corneal scrapings and identified by microscopy, culture, MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, and confirmatory ITS sequencing. Management required intensive topical, intravitreal, and systemic antifungal therapy in addition to surgical intervention. Visual acuity remained at light perception at three weeks postoperatively, with no further deterioration observed following combined surgical and medical management. Early diagnosis remains challenging due to overlapping morphological features with other filamentous fungi, necessitating molecular confirmation.

## Introduction
Microbial keratitis remains the fifth leading cause of blindness worldwide, with fungal species implicated in 6% to 60% of cases. Fungal keratitis, an infection of the cornea, represents a significant ocular condition [1]. Lasiodiplodia theobromae is a generally rare but emerging causative agent of fungal keratitis, accounting for approximately 6% of cases reported within selected published fungal keratitis case series. Its true prevalence remains largely unknown and is primarily documented through isolated case reports and small series [2,3]. Delays in initiating appropriate therapy, often due to their atypical presentation, contribute to the unfavorable clinical outcomes frequently observed [4] (see Table 1).
Lasiodiplodia theobromae, also known as Botryodiplodia theobromae, belongs to the Ascomycota phylum, subphylum Pezizomycotina. It is widely distributed in soil and decaying vegetation and is primarily a plant pathogen, causing fruit rot, stem cankers, and dieback across a broad range of tropical crops. Its widespread presence in soil, decaying plant material, and agricultural waste serves as a potential reservoir for human exposure, particularly in rural and farming communities [5,6].
Identification based solely on microscopy and culture is challenging, in addition to poor sporulation further complicating accurate morphological identification [7]. These factors often delay definitive diagnosis or lead to misidentification when relying solely on phenotypic characteristics. Thus, laboratory confirmation often requires advanced approaches, in which molecular methods such as sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of rDNA or Matrix Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) can facilitate accurate and rapid species identification [6,8].
We report a case of severe Lasiodiplodia theobromae corneal ulcer following grass trimmer related ocular trauma. This report emphasizes the microbiological and molecular methods necessary for accurate species-level identification and contributes to the growing recognition of this organism as an ophthalmic pathogen.

## Case presentation
A 57-year-old Malay gentleman with no known medical illness sustained left ocular trauma while cutting grass without eye protection (day 0), during which a foreign body allegedly entered the eye. The patient first sought treatment from a general practitioner on day 2 post-trauma and was prescribed artificial tears.
By day 4, symptoms progressed with worsening pain, redness, and blurring of vision. Despite this, no further intervention was initiated until day 14 post-trauma, when the patient presented to our centre with severe left eye pain, redness, and marked visual deterioration, prompting inpatient admission. There was no history of previous ocular disease, contact lens use, or ocular surgery.
On admission, physical examination reveal that the patient was afebrile, with a blood pressure of 158/77 mmHg, heart rate of 63 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation of 100% under room air. The pain score was 7/10, and blood glucose level was 7.1 mmol/L. Visual acuity was 6/12 in the right eye and perception of light in all four quadrants in the left eye. Pupillary examination revealed no relative afferent pupillary defect. Extraocular movements were full and painless.
Examination of the right eye showed normal eyelids, a white conjunctiva, and a clear cornea. The anterior chamber was deep and quiet, with a round, reactive pupil and lens nuclear sclerosis graded 2+. Fundus examination revealed a pink optic disc with a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.4, along with a flat macula and retina. Examination of the left eye showed no eyelid swelling but minimal discharge. The conjunctiva was injected with temporal chemosis. The cornea was diffusely hazy, with a dense stromal infiltrate involving the entire cornea, accompanied by a total epithelial defect, 360° peripheral thinning, and two descemetoceles: one central (4 × 4 mm) and another temporal at the 8 o'clock position (Fig. 1). Seidel's test was negative. Due to the dense opacity, anterior chamber details could not be visualized. Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (WCC 13.64 × 109/L) with normal hemoglobin level (13.5 g/dL), hematocrit (43.5%), and platelet count (401 × 109/L). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated (4.25 mg/L). Renal profile was unremarkable (Na 136 mmol/L, K 4.0 mmol/L, urea 4.3 mmol/L, creatinine 83 μmol/L). Radiological investigations revealed no intraocular foreign body (IOFB) on skull X-ray. A CT orbit performed showed no IOFB in the anterior chamber of the left eye.
Corneal scrapings were obtained under aseptic conditions by the ophthalmology team and inoculated onto Blood agar (BA), MacConkey agar (MAC), Chocolate agar (CBA), and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) for primary culture. The inoculated plates were then sent to the microbiology laboratory, where they were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 hours. On direct microscopy, Gram stain revealed numerous polymorphonuclear leukocytes (>25 per high-power field) with fewer than 10 epithelial cells observed. No bacteria or yeast cells were seen.
Following the appearance of fungal growth, the isolate was subcultured onto Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) for further morphological characterization. The colonies were greyish sepia to mouse grey to black, fluffy, with abundant aerial mycelium and reverse colonies are fuscous to black (Fig. 2), meanwhile the slide culture demonstrated both pigmented and hyaline hyphae (Fig. 3). Lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) preparation performed from colony growth demonstrated septate hyphae consistent with filamentous fungal infection (Fig. 4). The condition of the left eye worsened with the development of a descemetocele, impending corneal perforation and clinical features suggestive of endophthalmitis. B-scan ultrasonography demonstrated dense vitritis with loculations. The patient was treated with intensive medical therapy, including topical natamycin 5% hourly, topical amphotericin B 0.15% every 2 hours, topical ceftazidime 5% every 2 hours, topical vancomycin 5% every 2 hours, and topical atropine 1% once daily (OD). Oral voriconazole 200 mg twice daily (BD) was initiated. Intravitreal injections of vancomycin 2 mg/0.1 mL, ceftazidime 2 mg/0.1 mL, and amphotericin B 0.005 mg/0.1 mL were administered. Intravenous vancomycin 750 mg BD and oral voriconazole 200 mg BD were continued for six weeks.
Due to progressive deterioration, the patient underwent removal of the central tarsorrhaphy, sclerokeratoplasty, and intracapsular cataract extraction (ICCE). Intraoperatively, intracameral recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) and amphotericin B 0.005 mg/0.1 mL were administered, along with repeat intravitreal vancomycin 2 mg/0.1 mL and ceftazidime 2 mg/0.1 mL. The intracameral rtPA was given to dissolve fibrin membranes in the anterior chamber. All antimicrobial and antifungal therapies were continued postoperatively.
At three weeks postoperatively, the cornea remained diffusely edematous, however, iris details were visible and no hypopyon was present. Topical antibiotics were changed to levofloxacin 0.5% every 3 hours, and topical prednisolone acetate 1% commenced every 2 hours. Vision remained at perception of light, with a resolving hyphema. The corneal graft appeared hazy with a small epithelial defect. Unfortunately, the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up. ## Discussion
Lasiodiplodia theobromae is commonly found in tropical and subtropical environments, yet human infections remain uncommon. A recent review reported only 51 cases worldwide, including occurrences in Thailand and the Philippines, but none from Malaysia [5]. This rarity underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by L. theobromae and highlights the importance of clinician awareness in the Malaysian context. This report is, to our knowledge, the first reported case in Malaysia. Given the country's humid tropical climate and large agricultural workforce [9], maintaining a high index of suspicion is critical for timely recognition and management of such infections.
L. theobromae keratitis typically follows ocular trauma, particularly when the cornea is exposed to soil or plant material. Corneal abrasions resulting from such injuries provide an entry point for infection [1,2,4]. Additional risk factors include contact lens use and underlying ocular surface disease. While primarily a plant pathogen, exposure to humans is most common among those engaged in outdoor or agricultural work [7], consistent with the patient's history of unprotected eye contact during grass cutting.
The patient presented with rapidly progressive keratitis, which ultimately progressed to corneal ulceration. The severity of the infection was likely exacerbated by delayed diagnosis and the late initiation of targeted antifungal therapy, as the patient initially received only lubricants. Such delays are common in fungal keratitis, reflecting both low clinical suspicion and the typically slower progression of fungal infections compared to bacterial or viral keratitis, which can further postpone timely medical consultation [1,2].
Diagnosis of L. theobromae based on phenotypic characteristics is often challenging when relying solely on conventional phenotypic methods [7]. Colonies typically range from greyish sepia to mouse grey or black, are fluffy with abundant aerial mycelium, and display a fuscous to black reverse. Conidiophores are hyaline, simple, and occasionally septate. Conidia are initially unicellular, hyaline, granulose, sub-ovoid to ellipsoidal-oblong, and thick-walled; mature conidia are one-septate, cinnamon to fawn, cylindrical, and may occasionally be septate [10]. The fungal isolates from this patient exhibited characteristic morphological features that are phenotypically consistent with L. theobromae.
The culture characteristics were unable to provide definitive species-level identification. MALDI-TOF MS identified the isolate to the genus Lasiodiplodia with good score, but species-level classification was constrained due to limited reference spectra, reflecting the diagnostic challenges associated with uncommon environmental fungi. Accurate species-level identification can be achieved using PCR-based DNA sequencing [1,2]. In this patient, ITS sequencing provided precise taxonomic confirmation of L. theobromae, emphasizing its utility in clinical mycology for definitive fungal identification.
Management of L. theobromae infections is challenging, often necessitating surgical intervention alongside prolonged antifungal therapy to achieve favorable outcomes. The rarity of this human pathogen limits the availability of specific treatment guidelines, making management decisions difficult [6]. Delays in initiating adequate therapy, often due to the unusual nature of the infection, further contribute to poor clinical outcomes [4]. Moreover, the infrequent isolation of L. theobromae has resulted in very limited data on its in vitro antifungal susceptibility, complicating evidence-based therapeutic strategies [7].
This case contributes valuable microbiological data by documenting the complete diagnostic pathway from initial direct microscopy to culture morphology, rapid identification by MALDI-TOF, and definitive identification by ITS sequencing. Such laboratory-based case reports are essential to improve recognition of rare fungal pathogens, refining diagnostic algorithms, and strengthening regional surveillance in tropical countries.
Besides that, this case also contributes to the growing evidence that L. theobromae is an emerging pathogen capable of causing severe keratitis following even minor ocular trauma. It highlights the need for public health and clinical emphasis on early recognition, the use of protective eyewear during outdoor activities that pose a risk of exposure, and the integration of molecular tools in routine mycology diagnostics to allow targeted therapy and improved visual outcomes [1].
A limitation of this case was the unavailability of banana peel culture at our centre, which has been shown to facilitate pycnidia formation and aid identification of late-sporulating fungal pathogens [11]. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was established using standard culture media and microscopic morphology, this is further corroborated by molecular identification.
As a conclusion Lasiodiplodia theobromae is an emerging, plant-associated fungal pathogen in tropical regions that can cause severe ocular and, less commonly, extraocular infections. Accurate identification requires a combination of traditional microscopy, culture morphology, MALDI-TOF analysis, and ITS sequencing for accurate species level identification. Effective management of fungal keratitis requires heightened clinician awareness, rapid recognition, and the integration of advanced laboratory diagnostics. Early, targeted antifungal therapy and timely surgical intervention are critical for controlling infection, preventing permanent ocular damage, and preserving vision.
