# Cryptococcus neoformans invasion of the orbital compartment and periocular spaces following intracerebral infection in mice

## Abstract
SummaryCryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated and neurotropic fungus known for causing life-threatening meningoencephalitis in immunocompromised individuals. We used a murine intracerebral infection model to study C. neoformans brain infection and microglia-fungal interactions. Mice infected with the C. neoformans H99 strain exhibited hydrocephalus, cryptococcoma formation, and significantly higher brain fungal burden than the acapsular cap59 strain at 7-days post infection (dpi). Immunofluorescence imaging showed robust microgliosis, especially near cryptococcomas. Cytokine analysis revealed a host response consistent with brain tissue injury after C. neoformans H99 infection at 3 and 7 dpi. C. neoformans H99 presence and capsular polysaccharide secretion were observed in the orbital compartment and various periocular and extraocular structures, demonstrating a potential infection route between the basal ganglia and ocular tissue. Our results suggest that C. neoformans invasion of the orbital compartment from brain tissue is a complication of cerebral cryptococcosis that may affect treatment and promote relapse.Subject areas: neuroanatomy, mycology

## Introduction
Cryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated opportunistic fungus that primarily affects immunocompromised individuals including people with HIV/AIDS, organ transplant recipients, and patients with cancer or undergoing immunosuppressive therapy.1 C. neoformans is neurotropic, and infection frequently results in meningoencephalitis, a severe and life-threatening condition characterized by fungal dissemination to the central nervous system (CNS).2 Cryptococcal meningoencephalitis (CME) remains a major cause of mortality in immunocompromised populations, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa where access to antifungal treatment is limited.3 Of the approximately 152,000 annual cases of CME in AIDS victims worldwide, 112,000 resulted in death.3 Hence, C. neoformans was recently placed in the top list of priority fungal pathogens by the World Health Organization.
Microglia are CNS resident phagocytes that respond to C. neoformans infection in humans.4 They secrete cytokines and antimicrobial molecules to recruit peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils into the CNS.5,6 Postmortem examinations of brains with HIV infection and CME show microglia nodules with C. neoformans-associated multinucleate giant cells, a characteristic histological manifestation of AIDS-related encephalitis.7 Microglia exhibit close association with blood vessels during C. neoformans crossing of the blood-brain barrier (BBB),8 and it has been postulated that microglia may assist cryptococci in BBB transmigration and CNS invasion.9 In addition, cryptococcal elimination by microglia from the CNS is insufficient to resolve the infection, given that these brain resident cells have shown no protection in mice with CME.10 C. neoformans forms cryptococcomas, localized biofilm-like brain lesions consisting of yeast cell aggregates surrounded by vast amounts of capsular material and considerable neuronal loss.11 Microglia are often observed bordering cryptococcomas,12 which is a typical innate immune response seen in individuals with CME incapable of controlling fungal proliferation. Microglial responses to C. neoformans CNS infections are understudied and understanding their role in mitigating the progression of cerebral cryptococcosis or its complications is crucial for therapeutic development.
The mechanisms underlying fungal persistence in ocular tissue and the host immune response remain poorly understood. However, the capsule of C. neoformans is a primary virulence factor and the major component, glucuronoxylomannan (GXM), is extensively secreted during infection. GXM accumulates in serum, tissue, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF),13,14 contributing to immune cell evasion and fungal persistence in host tissues.15,16 GXM suppresses host immune responses by interfering with phagocytosis, inhibiting leukocyte migration, and modulating cytokine production.15 In this regard, we recently demonstrated that C. neoformans GXM significantly inhibits microglial migration and fungal phagocytosis.17 This immune-modulating effect may explain why C. neoformans can persist within immune-privileged sites such as the eye and CNS without immediate clearance by the host.18
While the CNS is the primary site of infection, increasing evidence suggests that ocular involvement is an underreported manifestation of cryptococcosis, potentially leading to visual impairment and blindness.19,20 Cryptococcal chorioretinitis, endophthalmitis, and optic neuropathy have been reported in cats and mice, but studies have also indicated that the eye serves as a potential reservoir for C. neoformans in humans.19,21,22,23 Cryptococcal ocular infections may arise from direct fungal dissemination via the CSF, optic nerve penetration, or hematogenous spread, contributing to persistent infection and immune evasion within ocular compartments.19 However, we observed during routine veterinary monitoring that mice also exhibited cryptococcal invasion of the orbital compartment in an experimental mouse model of intracerebral (i.c.) infection using stereotaxic surgery,24 indicating that the proximity of fungal infection in the striatum to CSF pathways,25 or the connection between the basal ganglia and the optic nerve, may serve as a conduit for C. neoformans.
In this study, we expanded our knowledge on a mouse model of C. neoformans brain infection that replicates the pathology of cerebral cryptococcosis in humans.24 We studied microglia-C. neoformans interactions using a CX3CR1-Enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) transgenic mouse, which drives EGFP expression at the endogenous Cx3cr1 locus, facilitating the visualization of microglia. We used H99 (wild-type) and cap59 (acapsular) C. neoformans strains to evaluate the importance of active capsular production in microglial cell responses, brain tissue cytokine production, and cerebral cryptococcosis progression. We further report that i.c.-infected mice with C. neoformans develop visual impairments and periocular cryptococcosis, described complications in patients with cryptococcal meningoencephalitis that could greatly affect the success of treatment and the possibility of relapse. These findings and observations support the validity of our in vivo mouse model of cerebral cryptococcosis and its use in understanding microglial responses to C. neoformans and potential complications of the infection, which are essential for the development of effective therapeutics and novel treatment strategies to combat this systemic mycosis.

## Results

## Hydrocephalus and increased fungal burden are present in the CNS of C. neoformans H99-infected CX3CR1-EGFP mice
We investigated the impact of C. neoformans capsular production and CNS infection in the progression of cerebral cryptococcosis in CX3CR1-EGFP mice (Figure 1). We compared the virulence of wild-type encapsulated C. neoformans H99 and acapsular cap59 strains. H99 is the parental strain of cap59, which is deficient in GXM export.26 H99 and cap59 strains were injected via i.c. into CX3CR1-EGFP mice (n = 5 per group), monitored daily for head anatomical changes, euthanized at 7 dpi, and their brains excised (Figure 1). H99-infected mice exhibited visible signs of hydrocephalus at 7 dpi, including dome-shaped cranial swelling (top and lateral views, yellow arrows) that were not observed in naive, saline-injected, or cap59-infected animals (Figure 1A). Upon dissection, gross anatomical analysis revealed marked brain swelling, superficial brain hemorrhage, edema or CSF accumulation, and liquefied brain tissue in H99-infected mice compared to the brains of mice in the other groups (Figure 1B). Naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected animals exhibited apparently normal gross anatomy. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E)-stained coronal brain sections from C. neoformans H99-infected mice demonstrated the formation of a large cryptococcoma or biofilm-like brain lesion and the development of lateral ventricle dilation, which is consistent with increased intracranial pressure (ICP) and hydrocephalus (Figure 1C). Naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected mice presented evidently normal tissue. Histological analysis using H&E staining of brains infected with C. neoformans H99 evinced cryptococcoma formation and extensive tissue disruption and damage (Figure 1D). In contrast, cap59-infected brains displayed a relatively preserved tissue architecture and minimal or no cryptococcal presence. Naive and saline-injected brain tissue also showed a well-maintained architecture. Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining showed cryptococci within the cryptococcoma (red arrows) of C. neoformans H99-infected brain tissue (Figure 1D). Fungi were not observed in the brain tissue of cap59-treated or control mice. We compared the fungal burden in brains removed from C. neoformans H99- and cap59-infected mice (n = 5 per group) by quantifying colony forming units (CFUs) at 7 dpi (Figure 1E). H99-infected brains had significantly higher fungal loads (7.62 × 107 CFU/g tissue) than those infected with cap59 (5 × 104 CFU/g tissue; p < 0.0001). These data validate that C. neoformans infection and active capsule production enhance the development of cerebral cryptococcosis due to cryptococcoma formation and indicate that brain swelling in this experimental mouse model is likely due to edema, raised ICP, and hydrocephalus.

## C. neoformans H99 infection induces robust changes in microglial distribution and clustering at 7 dpi
Microglia elicit a response to C. neoformans H99 invasion as early as 3 dpi,17 and behavior of microglia at later stages of infection may reveal mechanisms by which C. neoformans evades microglia-mediated clearance. Confocal microscopy was utilized near regions of cryptococcal infection in the basal ganglia at 7 dpi to assess the progression of microglial aggregation and density in the later stage of infection and colonization (Figure 2). To determine if microglia exhibit increased aggregation in the later stage of infection, 20× images (scale bars, 100 μm) of the basal ganglia from mice i.c-infected with C. neoformans H99 or the capsule-deficient cap59 mutant cells were analyzed with ImageJ FIJI software (Figure 2A). To quantify microglial density per micrometer square (μm2), the number of microglia in randomly selected regions surrounding the lesion area were quantified and divided by the area of the field (Figure 2B). Naive and saline-injected mice were used as controls in matched regions. Interestingly, only C. neoformans H99-infected tissue exhibited a robust increase in microglial density (p < 0.0001), with cap59-infected mice displaying densities indistinguishable from naive and saline-injected mice (Figure 2B). The similarly low levels of density between naive and saline-injected regions suggest minimal tissue damage resulting from surgery, reducing the likelihood of a confounding variable. Nearest neighbor index (NNI) is a parameter to assess aggregation by measuring the distance of each cell to its neighboring cells and comparing that distance to the expected mean distance in randomly distributed cells. High NNI values indicate a greater distance between a cell and its neighbors. We measured the NNI for labeled microglia in selected fields around cryptococcal lesions (Figure 2C) and observed substantially lower NNI values in H99-infected brains compared to naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected brains (p < 0.0001). No differences in NNI were observed between the other conditions, suggesting that microglia may exhibit enhanced migration or signaling functions during later stages of H99 infection, which is controlled when mice are infected with cap59.

We then assessed the clustering behavior of microglia in the same regions to evaluate if aggregation is induced by C. neoformans H99 (Figure 2D). Clusters were defined as microglia with NNI values less than 12-μm,17 and the number of clusters were divided by the measured region of interest. By 7 dpi, C. neoformans H99-infected brain tissue exhibited the greatest number of clusters compared with naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected tissues (p < 0.0001), suggesting that the active infection and cryptococcoma development may enhance aggregation and clustering of microglia, perhaps for increased direct cell signaling. Naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected mice did not display differences in microglia clustering, suggesting that cap59 infection may be resolved faster compared with H99. Finally, we calculated the spacing index of microglia, which is a densitometric parameter that calculates the square of the NNI multiplied by the cell density,27 from the same brain regions of naive, saline-injected, and C. neoformans H99 or cap59-infected mice (Figure 2E). Notably, there were no differences in this measurement parameter across the four conditions, indicating that microglia detect the presence of C. neoformans H99 infection and associated tissue damage and may participate in signaling to enhance or modify disease response. Taken together, these analyses indicate that active production of capsular polysaccharide during C. neoformans brain infection alters microglial cell tissue distribution and responses.

## C. neoformans H99 infection elicits distinct morphological changes in microglia at 7 dpi
The morphology of microglia can provide insight into its reactive state and associated functions during infection and injury.28 To evaluate changes in microglial functional states following i.c.-infection with C. neoformans H99 or cap59, confocal microscopy was used to image brain sections at 7 dpi (Figure 3). 40× images of microglia from mice infected with C. neoformans H99 or cap59 (scale bars, 50 μm) were acquired for morphological analysis (Figure 3A). Naive and saline-injected mice were used as controls. Microglia from naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected mice displayed long ramifications, indicating normal homeostatic function and possible resolution of cap59 infection (Figure 3A). Conversely, microglia from H99-infected tissue surrounding a cryptococcoma displayed retracted ramifications, suggesting an amoeboid or activated state (Figure 3A). To complement the qualitative observations, we quantified soma body size (Figure 3B) and arborization area (Figure 3C) in microglia from the same regions using ImageJ FIJI software, and we used these values to calculate the morphological index (Figure 3D). Each microglial soma was outlined along the perimeter (Figure 3A; pink outlines), and area was calculated. Interestingly, microglial soma area exhibited no differences between C. neoformans H99- and cap59-infected tissue; however, when compared to naive tissue, cap59 exhibited similar values, whereas H99 displayed a slight reduction (p < 0.01). Surprisingly, microglia from saline-injected tissue had the smallest soma area compared with naive (p < 0.0001) and cap59-injected (p < 0.01) mice; however, this was not significantly different from C. neoformans H99-infected animals, which parallel findings at 3 dpi,17 suggesting that microglial functions are likely complex and dynamic in varying environments. Arborization area (Figure 3C) was measured by outlining a polygon (Figure 3A; yellow outlines) around individual cells and designating each branch tip as a vertex to determine the branching area.17 Microglia from cap59-infected tissue exhibited similar arborization area to saline-injected mice, which was significantly reduced compared to naive tissue (p < 0.001). H99-infected tissue displayed the greatest reduction in arborization area compared to naive (p < 0.0001), as well as with saline-injected (p < 0.0001) and cap59-infected tissue (p < 0.001). Morphology index is used as a ratio to assess overall morphological change, calculated by dividing the soma area with the arborization area (Figure 3D). Larger morphology index values indicate greater morphological change. As expected due to the reduced arborization length relative to minor effects on soma area, H99-infected tissue displayed the highest morphological index compared with naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected tissue (p < 0.0001), suggesting microglia are significantly affected by capsular GXM during active infection and resulting tissue alterations from cryptococcoma formation. The microglial morphology index of cap59-infected mice was similar to control conditions, consistent with reduced infection severity. These findings suggest that C. neoformans H99 cells elicit alterations in microglia at 7 dpi that are reflected in their morphology, which may indicate diverse roles for these cells in the infection response.

## C. neoformans H99 and cap59 cells induce production of different cytokine profiles in brain tissue
We examined how the CNS immunity responds to C. neoformans H99 or cap59 infection by quantifying the cytokine levels in brain tissue homogenates at 3 and 7 dpi (Figure 4). TNF-α (Figures 4A and 4E) production was significantly higher in supernatants from brains infected with cap59 cells compared to all the other groups at 3 and 7 dpi. Saline-injected brains had more TNF-α levels than naive (p < 0.0001) and C. neoformans H99-infected (p < 0.0001) tissues at 7 dpi (Figure 4E), indicating that release of GXM suppresses natural TNF-α release in response to brain injury. Similarly, IL-4 (Figures 4C and 4G) levels were significantly higher in brain tissue infected with the acapsular mutant than all the other groups at 3 dpi. Interestingly, H99-infected mice had significantly higher IL-4 production in brain tissue than naive animals (p < 0.05) at 7 dpi (Figure 4G), suggesting that capsular inhibition of IL-4 release is transient. In contrast, IL-1β was significantly increased in C. neoformans H99-infected mice compared to all the other groups at 3 (Figure 4B) and 7 (Figure 4F) dpi, except for the naive group at 3 dpi. The levels of IL-1β in cap59-infected animals were significantly lower than naive (p < 0.01) and saline-injected (p < 0.05) mice at 3 dpi (Figure 4B). The opposite was observed at 7 dpi (Figure 4F), and this cytokine synthesis was significantly higher in mice infected with the acapsular mutant relative to the uninfected control animals (p < 0.001). Moreover, H99-infected brains exhibited the highest levels of IL-6 at 3 dpi (Figure 4D). In contrast, the brains of saline-injected (p < 0.01) and cap59-infected (p < 0.01) mice had significantly lower IL-6 levels than naive mice. Lastly, saline-injected brains showed significantly higher IL-6 production at 7 dpi relative to all the other groups (Figure 4H), although naive brain tissue homogenates had higher levels of this cytokine compared to H99-infected brains (p < 0.05). These findings demonstrate that C. neoformans H99 CNS infection stimulates early IL-1β and IL-6 production (3 dpi) and maintains IL-1β levels (7 dpi) while abrogating TNF-α levels (3 and 7 dpi) and IL-4 levels (3 dpi), thus resulting in the progression of cryptococcosis.

## C. neoformans H99 cells are present in the orbital compartment and periocular tissue
Ocular infections by C. neoformans have been previously reported in immuno-competent29,30 and -suppressed individuals,31,32 frequently leading to severe visual loss.33 To investigate the extent of the cryptococcal infection and capsular polysaccharide dissemination within the eye, immunofluorescence was performed on eye tissue sections collected at 7 dpi from naive (Figure 5A) and H99-infected (Figure 5B) CX3CR1-EGFP mice. EGFP-expressing microglia (green), 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)-labeled nuclei (blue), and GXM-positive capsule- or exo-polysaccharide (red) were visualized by confocal microscopy. Gross localization of cryptococci and GXM accumulation was observed across the infected periocular tissue, extraocular musculature, and outer layers of the optic nerve (Figure 5B). Higher magnification images (60×) from selected eye regions were obtained, and we observed C. neoformans H99 present in the middle vascular layers of the eye in infected mice (Figure 5B, a-d), with cryptococci detected outside the choroid (Figure 5B, a), within the periocular muscular tissue (Figure 5B, b), and between the conjunctiva and sclera (Figure 5B, c and d). To evaluate potential mechanisms of cryptococcal invasion, imaging of extraocular musculature (Figure 5B, e) and sheaths of the dural (SDM) and pia (SPM) mater surrounding the optic nerve and subarachnoid space (SAS) (Figure 5B, f) were examined, with cryptococci detected within transitional layers between CNS and ocular structures. These observations support the possibility of the optic nerve being a route of access for C. neoformans H99 to invade the ocular tissue.

Interestingly, formation of C. neoformans clusters or cryptococcoma were not detected at 7 dpi, likely due to the location of cryptococci localizing in posterior vascular and transitional tissues of the ocular region rather than within the optic nerve, scleral stroma, or retina. In a separate infected animal from the same time point, cryptococci were detected near the ciliary body (Figure 5C) and within the smooth muscle (SM) of the ciliary body (Figure 5D), suggesting the possibility for C. neoformans H99 colonization of the anterior segment of the eye at later (>7 dpi) time points. These observations demonstrate that C. neoformans H99 striatal infection involves GXM secretion and invasion within the orbital compartment and surrounding tissue of the optic nerve, potentially contributing to fungal immune evasion and persistence in this immune-privileged site.

## C. neoformans cells elicit minimal inflammation in the posterior region of the eye at 3 and 7 dpi
To investigate the impact of the fungal infection in the immune response of the posterior region of the eye, we removed the eyes of mice infected with C. neoformans H99 or cap59 at 3 and 7 dpi and performed histopathological analyses using H&E and PAS staining (Figure 6). Histological staining of the periocular tissue was examined to determine the presence of immune cell infiltration at 3 (Figure 6A) and 7 dpi (Figure 6B). Little to no inflammation was detected in H99- or cap59-infected mice, with C. neoformans detectable only in the H99-infected tissue (red arrowheads) at both time points. By 7 dpi, minimal inflammation (blue arrows) was visible in the H99-infected periocular tissue. At 7 dpi, inflammation was assessed near the optic nerve and within extraocular musculature (Figure 6C), with minimal inflammatory infiltrate detectable only in the cap59-infected tissue (blue arrows). Saline-injected and naive control mice only had minimal inflammation in the extraocular musculature, likely surveillance immune cells. C. neoformans H99 was observed in the extraocular musculature (red arrowheads) in the absence of inflammation.

Fungal burden was quantified at both time points (Figure 6D). CFU determinations demonstrated an early (3 dpi) and higher CFU count in eyes infected with cap59 (5.14 × 105 CFU/g tissue) compared to those infected with H99 cells (1.72 × 101 CFU/g tissue; p < 0.001). However, the fungal load of cap59-infected eyes cleared over time (7 dpi) whereas C. neoformans H99-infected eyes (4.7 × 105 CFU/g tissue; p < 0.0001) displayed high ocular burden and colonization. These findings suggest that capsular production plays an essential role in cryptococcal persistence and immune cell evasion within peri- and extraocular tissues.

## C. neoformans H99 cells are present in the sheath of the pia mater surrounding the optic nerve
To support our hypothesis that C. neoformans H99 utilizes the optic nerve as a route of access for invasion of the orbital compartment, we examined histological sections of the optic nerve and surrounding dura and pia mater sheaths (Figure 7). We previously described the ability of GXM to disrupt adherens junctions, specifically VE-cadherin, in the brain endothelium due to the presence of GXM,34 providing access for C. neoformans H99 to the brain parenchyma. As the pia mater consists of leptomeningeal cells connected by gap and adherens junctions including VE-cadherin35 in both humans36 and mice,37 as well as desmosomes in humans,38 we considered whether the optic nerve may be a route of access for C. neoformans invasion of the orbital compartment of the eye. Intriguingly, we were able to detect cryptococci within the sheath of the pia mater (SPM; Figure 7, red arrowheads) in mice infected with C. neoformans H99, however no cap59 cryptococci were detected near the optic nerve, suggesting the potential for GXM to disrupt the leptomeningeal layers encasing the optic nerve.

## Mice i.c. infected with C. neoformans H99 demonstrate progressive vision loss at 7 dpi
To examine the impact of basal ganglia infection on murine vision, we i.c.-infected mice with C. neoformans H99 or cap59 strains and performed a behavioral test measuring vision loss (Figure 8). Mice were lowered toward the ground by the tail at 3 and 7 dpi, and the height (cm) at which mice naturally reached for the ground with their frontal paws was recorded (Figure 8A). Failure to reach toward the ground indicated vision loss.39 Although we did not observe a statistical difference in the distance at which saline-treated (mean, 2.0 cm) and C. neoformans H99 (mean, 0.5 cm)- or cap59 (mean, 2.0 cm)-infected mice reached the ground (Figure 8B), there was a consistent trend for vision loss progression by the animals infected with the encapsulated strain compared to the other conditions. At 7 dpi, the distance at which H99-infected mice (mean, 0.2333 cm) reached for the ground was reduced compared to saline-injected (mean, 2.333 cm; p < 0.05) and cap59-infected (mean, 2.667 cm; p < 0.01) mice (Figure 8B).

A menace reflex test was performed to measure the integrity of the cortical components of the visual pathway.40,41 A non-tactile object was brought close to each eye three times to observe a blinking response, demonstrating visual detection of a potential threat (Figure 8C). No difference was observed in either the ipsilateral or the contralateral eyes (relative to right hemisphere injection) at 3 dpi, however at 7 dpi, H99- and cap59-infected mice demonstrated significantly reduced blinking in the ipsilateral eye (Figure 8D), suggesting the presence of fungal lesions in the cerebral cortex or basal ganglia, likely due to the location of the infection, or impairment of the visual pathway; however, these possibilities are beyond the scope of the current study and must be investigated in a separate and comprehensive study.

## Discussion
We demonstrated that the stereotaxic i.c. inoculation of C. neoformans H99 cells result in similar CME manifestations as described in human patients.4,42 These infected mice develop typical cerebral cryptococcosis stages including edema, ICP, and hydrocephalus,24 making it an excellent model to dissect the direct neurotoxic impact of C. neoformans infection, GXM release, and disease progression. In this regard, we validated17 that active synthesis of capsular polysaccharide by C. neoformans is required for cerebral cryptococcosis development, since mice infected with the acapsular mutant strain cap59 did not show anatomical nor histological changes in the basal ganglia even when fungi were reduced but not cleared, as demonstrated by CFU determinations. In contrast, mice infected with capsular H99 cryptococci in the brain exhibited cryptococcoma formation and higher fungal load by 7 dpi. Since a cryptococcoma is characterized by tissue damage,11 it is possible that the early (3 dpi) high brain tissue levels of IL-1β and IL-6 were responsible for mediating an acute-phase response that, as we previously described,17 promoted microglia agglomeration around the lesion. It is also conceivable that IL-1β and IL-6 production during H99-infection compensated for the low TNF-α synthesis in the basal ganglia, the region of infection. However, the diffusion of microglia away from the cryptococcoma as the H99-infection progressed (7 dpi)17 may be associated with signaling between microglial subsets at varying distances from the cryptococcoma or the reduction of IL-6 levels in brain tissue, suggesting that the acute-phase response initially observed cannot be only sustained by high levels of IL-1β, which is not sufficient to stimulate microglial chemotaxis and accumulation around the lesion.
As expected, acapsular mutant cap59 cells were avirulent in brain-infected mice due to the absence of the capsule and their inability to proliferate and form cryptococcomas. TNF-α and IL-4 were significantly more elevated in cap59-infected brains at 3 and 7 dpi compared to all the other groups. Interestingly, the disparate regulation of TNF-α and IL-1β in cap59-infected mice at 3 and 7 dpi raised questions about the inflammatory response elicited by the acapsular fungus. Based on in vitro results from Retini et al., after 6 and 18 h of stimulation by an acapsular strain of C. neoformans (CBS 7698), human polymorphonuclear cells produced lower levels of both TNF-α and IL-1β compared with encapsulated strains (CBS 6995 and NCPF 3168).43 A similar study by Vecchiarelli et al. found opposite results in cytokine levels produced by human alveolar macrophages and peripheral blood monocytes, with the acapsular strain CBS 7698 stimulating much higher levels of both TNF-α and IL-1β produced by both cell types after 4 h compared with the encapsulated strain CBS 6995.44 This contrast suggests that such inflammatory profiles are highly dependent on the immune cell profile stimulated by the presence of GXM, as well as the microenvironment and time frame of exposure. Moreover, different populations of microglia and infiltrating macrophages have been observed producing different levels of TNF-α and IL-1β,45 suggesting that cap59 may elicit microglia or infiltrating macrophages at time points that result in TNF-α reaching a peak earlier in the infection, followed by heighted levels of IL-1β as the infection is slowed and surrounding tissue is repaired. Microglia are a major source of TNF-α,46 and the increased TNF-α levels may explain the activation and massive migration of these CNS resident phagocytes during infection and the rapid control of cap59 cells.
As previously shown in vitro, cap67 yeasts,47 a mutant with defective capsule production derived from C. deneoformans B3501 (formerly C. neoformans var. neoformans or serotype D) strain, were more susceptible to phagocytosis and killing by BV-2 microglia than the parental encapsulated cells. However, C. deneoformans invades the brain in much lower rates than C. neoformans (formerly neoformans var. grubii or serotype A), suggesting that our studies not only complement these published results, but also were performed in a cryptococcal species that it is most likely to invade the brain. Interestingly, TNF-α was not secreted by BV-2 cells after their interactions with neither encapsulated nor acapsular cells, likely because the protocol utilized in these studies only activated these microglia-like cells with bacterial lipopolysaccharide but without interferon γ stimulus,47 which is currently a standard in the field when performing either phagocytosis or killing assays. Given the anti-inflammatory effects of IL-4, it is plausible that the elevated levels of this cytokine in cap59-infected brain tissue are related to the intense microglial response against the acapsular cells and their early reduction.17 Moreover, the high level of IL-1β produced in tissue infected with cap59 cells at 7 dpi were associated with the protective and natural anti-cryptococcal response in the brain previously shown in mice i.c.-infected with heat-killed cryptococci.48
Saline-injected brains evinced higher levels of TNF-α and IL-6 relative to those brains from naive mice at 7 dpi, which are consistent with the inflammatory response and tissue-healing observed in murine models of spinal cord49 and focal traumatic brain injury.50 Even though it is not a typical route for C. neoformans infections, our findings suggest that the i.c. infection model24 enables us to investigate the progression of cryptococcal meningitis with minimal interference from immune response changes related to the injection procedure. Furthermore, stereotaxic injections are widely utilized in neuroscience studies and represent the safest and most appropriate method for examining infectious disease mechanisms in the CNS, including cryptococcosis.24,51 We selected the i.c. infection model instead of intranasal, intratracheal (pulmonary), or intravenous (systemic) models because it allows for a localized development of cryptococcomas in the mouse brain that closely resembles lesions found in humans. Additionally, while pulmonary and systemic infections can produce comparable fungal brain damage, they differ significantly in the high prevalence of cryptococcomas and their extensive spread throughout the brain. These findings greatly hinder our capacity to investigate the effects of C. neoformans infection or its virulence factors on specific brain areas and their associated cognitive and motor functions.
We characterized microglia morphological changes and distribution around the cryptococcoma at 7 dpi using microscopy, image analysis, and spatial statistical techniques.17,52 The responses of microglia to direct infection of the basal ganglia by C. neoformans caused moderate phagocytic glial cell recruitment to the infection site, as evinced by the increased density and clustering of CX3CR1-EGFP+ cells and a more closely packed yet regularly spaced organization around the brain lesion, as shown by the NNI measurements. In contrast to our results at 3 dpi,17 we did not observe differences among all the groups in spacing index, which may correlate with the fact that microglia diffuse away from the cryptococcoma as the disease progresses, potentially making it difficult to eradicate the cryptococcal infection. Similar to results obtained 3 dpi,17 microglia activation near H99-formed cryptococcomas was associated with a decrease in arborization and an increase in morphology index, reflecting the morphological transition from a ramified or homeostatic to an activated amoeboid-like phenotype. Particularly, no major changes in microglial morphology and distribution were observed in naive, saline-injected, and cap59-infected brain tissue. These findings indicate that the i.c. mouse model injection does not affect microglial responses substantially; thus, corroborating the future use of this model for investigations involving these CNS surveillance glial resident cells in the setting of cerebral cryptococcosis. Moreover, these data confirm that active production of the capsule is necessary for C. neoformans survival and persistence in the CNS. Even though microglia were recently shown not to be protective in combating cerebral cryptococcal infection,10 the question of whether their responses are pathologic remain unclear. Chronic activation of microglia may contribute to neuroinflammation and exacerbate tissue damage, potentially playing a role in the neurological deficits observed in cryptococcal meningitis.53 The spatial association of microglia with cryptococcomas suggests localized immune surveillance, yet the presence of sustained fungal burden points to a potentially ineffective response.
Interestingly, we identified visual impairments and cryptococcal invasion of the mouse orbital compartment at 7 dpi after direct inoculation in the basal ganglia. As the optic nerve is encased by the pia mater sheath and consists of leptomeningeal and glial cells, we considered whether these cells could be invaded by C. neoformans H99 to transport through the SAS and arachnoid trabeculas to access the ocular tissue. We previously described the ability of GXM to disrupt the integrity of human brain endothelial cells in vivo and in vitro through the RhoA-Src pathway,54 reducing expression of VE-cadherin connecting adjacent endothelial cells.34 VE-cadherin has been described as a primary adherens junction connecting leptomeningeal cells,35 supporting the potential for C. neoformans H99 to transport through these layers trans- or paracellularly. Importantly, as cap59 was detected in CFU quantification but not histologically, it is possible it utilizes the Trojan horse mechanism of transport to access the ocular tissue, potentially within surveying phagocytes through lymphatic vessels. The high fungal burden of cap59 at 3 dpi compared to H99 offers contrasting results from the histology samples, as minimal colonization was detected; however, it is possible that cap59 becomes internalized by host macrophages and microglia to be filtered out within the lymphatic system, resulting in higher fungal count in the eyes, potentially due to its presence within the lymphatic vasculature surrounding the eye; however, this possibility must be dissected further in future studies.
Interestingly, while the H99- and cap59-infected mice exhibited increased menace reflex impairments, the saline-injected mice demonstrated similar menace reflex impairments to H99-infected mice in the contralateral eye, with cap59-infected mice having the least impairment. It is possible that healing of injection-related damage may have an indirect impact on the visual pathway, resulting in decreased detection of an approaching object in the saline-injected and H99-infected mice. The menace reflex test is a simple tool to measure the integrity of the visual pathway,39,40,41 however its use in this work highlights a limitation in our study regarding functional testing. Comprehensive functional testing is required to accurately determine the extent of visual impairment and distinguish which regions of the visual pathway are impacted by fungal infection.
While there is no well-described direct anatomical connection between the basal ganglia and the optic nerve, it has been proposed that meningoencephalitis and infection within the basal ganglia can lead to bilateral optic neuritis, potentially through irrigation pathways of the CSF.55 Moreover, large molecules up to 2,000 kDa have been observed to enter the optic nerve of mice from the CSF without penetrating the retina.56 Considering C. neoformans H99 enters the CSF during CME,57 it is possible that penetration of the optic nerve occurs through transfer within CSF25 and disruption of optic nerve adherens junctions to enter the extraocular tissue and periocular structures (graphical abstract). This possibility is supported by our lack of observation of C. neoformans H99 directly within the retina, lens, or vitreous humor, with most cryptococci present in transitional tissues and posterior ocular compartments.
Cryptococcal infection of the eye, although a rare complication, has been reported in humans29,58,59 and animals60,61,62 in the setting of fungal CNS involvement. In fact, cryptococcal neuroretinitis and choroiditis have been described in immunocompetent63 and AIDS64 patients, respectively. Notably, the cap59 load of the eye was higher than for H99 at 3 dpi, but it was likely controlled and cleared by the periocular tissue immunity by 7 dpi. Nevertheless, H99-infection of the eye was persistent at 7 dpi, highlighting the importance of capsule production and secretion. The detection of H99 cryptococci in multiple periocular compartments suggests that the capsule may inhibit immunity in this already immune-restricted environment.19 We have demonstrated that active capsular production by C. neoformans H99, and especially GXM secretion, impaired microglial motility and fungal phagocytosis.17 Unlike the CNS, which benefits from resident microglia as immune sentinels, the eye has limited immune surveillance, potentially making it a reservoir for fungal persistence.18,19 GXM’s known immunosuppressive properties may further dampen local responses, which may explain the lack of periocular tissue damage and limited immune activation we observed, enabling fungal survival despite systemic antifungal therapy.16 Based on our previous findings,17 the secretion of GXM within the ocular environment may suppress local immune responses, allowing C. neoformans to evade phagocytosis and antifungal immune mechanisms. These findings raise concerns about the potential for the eye to serve as a sanctuary or latency site for the fungus, contributing to relapse or reinfection after initial clinical resolution of CME. Hence, monitoring the eye environment in patients with or recovering from CME should be a routine part of the clinical management to avoid or reduce the possibility of relapse originating from these immune-privileged tissues. The immune-modulating effects of GXM in the ocular microenvironment also warrant further investigation, as they may represent a barrier to effective fungal clearance.16 These findings underscore the need for further investigation into ocular cryptococcosis and its clinical implications, particularly in patients with CME.
When describing eye infections, fundoscopy is typically employed to examine optic nerve swelling or retinal abnormalities,55 and optical coherence tomography is utilized in the detection of papilledema and optic nerve injury. A major limitation of our work is our lack of access to these technologies for our study, and it is important to recognize that the use of these tools offers a wealth of knowledge for comprehensive investigations of ocular infection in mice prior to or during the development of CME. Our work presents exploratory findings related to the presence of C. neoformans in the orbital compartment following stereotaxic inoculation into the basal ganglia. We specify that while fungal CFUs were detected in the eye and cryptococci were histologically observed in extraocular structures, future investigations are necessary to elucidate the mechanisms by which C. neoformans may invade the orbital compartment and potentially cause ocular infection, as seen in CME patients. Additionally, performing detailed measurements of cytokine release and immunoprofiling within the eye, periocular tissue, and optic nerve are necessary to understand the degree of infection and resulting inflammation at each stage of infection development; however, due to our limited detection of immune infiltration in these spaces at our chosen time points, such analyses should complement a comprehensive examination of the development of ocular cryptococcosis and potential optic neuropathy. Moreover, examining the ocular and periocular spaces at later time points would provide insight into infection persistence, particularly with C. neoformans H99; however, our previous studies show that survival using the i.c. infection model is limited past 10–15 dpi.17,24,65 To address this, future studies using either a lower inoculum or a systemic (intravenous) route of infection may be more useful in following infection development over an extended period of time and with multiple time points.
In conclusion, we showed that the stereotaxic mouse model of cryptococcal i.c. infection develops visible anatomical changes related to hydrocephalus, a major clinical manifestation in individuals with CME. This model is ideal to study the development of hydrocephalus and related conditions such as edema and ICP for better management and treatment of CME in patients. We also observed cryptococcoma formation in infected mice with H99 and alterations to microglial morphology and distribution. Since microglia alone do not provide protection in mice with C. neoformans infection,10 it is still critical to understand how these CNS resident cell responses may be contributing to the progression of cerebral cryptococcosis. Unexpectedly, cryptococcal cells were detected in the orbital compartment and, though rare, also within the sheath of the pia mater surrounding the optic nerve of mice i.c-infected with H99, raising many questions regarding C. neoformans invasion for future research. For instance, understanding the mechanisms of cryptococcal dissemination to the orbital compartment, including hematogenous and/or neuronal pathways, will be essential for developing preventive and therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, potential investigations should explore targeted antifungal therapies for ocular cryptococcosis, particularly in cases where systemic treatments fail to achieve fungal clearance. Incidentally, intravitreal antifungal injections may help improve outcomes in patients with ocular involvement. Additionally, investigating the efficacy of antifungal therapies that penetrate immune-privileged sites such as the eye environment more effectively could lead to improved treatment strategies. Identifying biomarkers for early detection of ocular involvement in cryptococcal infections could also aid in timely intervention, effective management, and improvement of patient outcomes. Understanding how C. neoformans exploit the eye’s immune privilege could provide crucial insights into its pathogenesis and long-term persistence especially during CME.

## Limitations of the study
This study presents the ability of C. neoformans H99 to colonize the orbital compartment and periocular spaces of infected mice following i.c. infection into the striatum, demonstrating a potential mechanism for the fungus to spread from the local point of infection, however certain limitations remain. A major limitation of this study was the use of young (6–8 weeks) female mice for the experiments presented in this investigation. While human cryptococcal infections are more common in males, females demonstrate higher mortality rates,66 prompting the decision to evaluate the role of microglia and dissemination of C. neoformans to the orbital compartment in females. Moreover, we used younger mice in this study due to the development of more severe infection compared to older (>33 weeks) mice.67 These limitations in our infection model prompt the need for further studies that comprehensively compare microglial responses and ocular complications between sexes and in different age groups.
Although confocal microscopy, CFU determinations, and immunohistochemical staining are useful tools to study pathology, this study did not use fundoscopy or optical coherence tomography, which can be performed on live animals to assess the architecture of the retina and optic nerve. In future investigations describing fungal ocular infection, visual acuity and retinal function can be measured using advanced technologies such as an electroretinogram and optokinetic tracking, respectively. Moreover, our study focused on 3 and 7 dpi time points due to the relevance to our infection model and microglial examinations. Investigating ocular involvement at multiple time points, potentially with a lower inoculum over a longer period, could provide additional details and explanations to the findings in our study. Finally, detailed experimentation of optic nerve involvement and CSF cycling following i.c. infection with C. neoformans could provide insight into the mechanism(s) by which C. neoformans causes eye infections, which can occur in patients suffering with CME.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Ethics statement
All animal studies were conducted according to the experimental practices and standards approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Florida (UF; Protocol #: 202011067). The IACUC at the UF approved this study.

## C. neoformans
C. neoformans H99 (serotype A) and cap59 mutant strains were grown in Sabouraud dextrose broth (pH 5.6; BD Difco) for 24 h at 30 °C in an orbital shaker (New Brunswick Scientific) set at 150 rpm (nominal to early stationary phase). H99 is a wild-type C. neoformans strain isolated and characterized by Dr. John Perfect at Duke University.69 The cap59 strain was generated by the insertion of a disruptive cassette into the cap59 gene of H99 using a biolistic DNA delivery method.26 The cap59 gene is required by C. neoformans to produce its capsule.70

## Animal model
C57BL/6 and CX3CR1-EGFP (The Jackson Laboratory) female mice (6–8 weeks old) were used in this study and present a limitation due to the sex bias resulting from excluding male mice. In humans, males demonstrate higher susceptibility to C. neoformans infection compared to females due to reduced CD4+ T cell abundance,71 however anemia and other unknown factors may potentially contribute to the higher mortality rates observed in females.66 In mice, similar mortality rates for both sexes have been reported,67 however young mice (6 weeks) develop more severe infection compared to older (>33 weeks) mice, influencing the evaluation of C. neoformans infection in young female mice for this study.

## Intracerebral (i.c.) infection with C. neoformans
C57BL/6 and CX3CR1-EGFP (The Jackson Laboratory) female mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized using isoflurane (3–5% for induction and 1–2% maintenance; model: VetFlo Vaporizer Single Channel Anesthesia System, Kent Scientific), placed in prone position over a heating pad (model: RightTemp Jr., Kent Scientific), and prepped using standard aseptic techniques. A local anesthetic, bupivacaine or ropivacaine (0.05%; Covetrus), was administered subcutaneously in the incision. The fur on the skull was carefully shaved off and the animal was securely placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (model: 940; Kopf Instruments). Using a small hand-held microdrill (model: Ideal microdrill; Braintree Scientific), the skull was thinned until the underlying dura matter was visible and a 26 G Hamilton syringe was brought to the correct stereotaxic position and lowered until it touched the exposed dura. The craniotomy was around 1-mm in diameter and the correct brain coordinates were identified using a stereotaxic brain atlas (e.g., The Allen Mouse Brain Atlas; https://mouse.brain-map.org/static/atlas). Using the C. neoformans H99 or cap59 strains, a 1-μL suspension containing 104 cryptococci in sterile saline was injected into the striatum [Stereotaxic coordinates: x (medial/lateral), −2; y (anterior/posterior), 0.2; z (dorsal/ventral), −3.5)], a region in the basal ganglia, using a 26 G Hamilton syringe connected to a pump (model: UltraMicroPump3; World Precision Instruments). We injected the fungal inoculum in a 1-μL volume to avoid tissue damage or diffusion of the cryptococci to other regions of the brain. The skin incision on the dorsal head was closed with sterile nylon suture and 2-4% topical chlorhexidine solution was applied over the closed incision. After the surgery, the mice were placed in a clean recovery cage and monitored the progression of neurocryptococcosis. The survival endpoints were inactivity, tachypnea, or loss of ≥25% of body weight from baseline weight. We monitored the mice twice daily for clinical signs, dehydration, and weight loss. Animals showing signs of dehydration or that lost more than 10% weight received supportive care such as 1 mL of parenteral fluid supplementation (saline) and moist chow on the cage floor was provided. Brain and eye tissues were removed at 3- or 7-dpi for CFU determinations, histopathological examinations using light microscopy, and immuno-histochemistry and -fluorescence microscopy, and cytokine assays (brain only).

## Behavioral tests to assess vision function
The vision loss test was performed in mice i.c.-infected with H99 and cap59 at 3- and 7-dpi, with saline i.c.-injected mice used as controls. Animals were carefully lowered by the tail toward a flat surface, and the height (cm) at which the mice reached with the frontal paws for the ground was recorded. For the menace reflex test, animals were held steadily as a non-tactile object was slowly brought close to the left and right eyes three times to observe the detection of blinking. A scale from 0–1.0 was used to grade reflex impairment, with 0 and 1.0 indicating no impairment and severe impairment, respectively. Tests were performed twice at both time points as part of the animals’ general health assessment. Each point represents one mouse (n = 3 mice per group).

## Photographs
The physical changes (e.g., hydrocephalus) and excised brains were photographed with a digital camera after approval by the University of Florida Animal Care Services (ACS) and the pictures were transferred to a secure computer following the University of Florida ACS policy.

## Method details

## CFU determinations
Brains and eyes were excised from euthanized mice and weighed at 3- and 7-dpi. The organs were homogenized in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS), serially diluted, a 100 μL suspension was plated on Sabouraud dextrose agar (BD Difco) and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Quantification of viable yeast cells from infected animals were determined by CFU counting of two dilutions per animal (Brain, n = 5 per day; Eyes, n = 6–7 per day).

## Histopathology
Mice were anesthetized at 7-dpi with a ketamine (100 mg/kg; Zoetis)/xylazine (20 mg/kg; Covetrus) cocktail. A 23 G needle was inserted into the left ventricle of the heart and the right atrium was nicked to perfuse the mice with sterile saline (Baxter) followed by 4% formaldehyde (Fisher Scientific). The brains and eyes were removed and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight (O/N).
H&E and PAS staining and light microscopy. Organs were then embedded in paraffin, and 4 μm coronal sections were serially cut using a cryostat (Tanner Scientific, model: TN50) and fixed onto glass slides and then subjected either to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain or to PAS stain, to examine host tissue or fungal morphology, respectively. For histopathological evaluations, whole slides scanning was performed using the Leica Aperio Scanscope CS (version 12.4.3). Moreover, each slide was carefully examined with a Leica DMi8 inverted microscope, and photographed with a Leica DFC7000 T digital camera using the Leica software platform LAS X.
Immunohistochemistry and confocal microscopy. Brains and eyes were washed thrice with sterile saline for 1 h and left O/N in 40% sucrose for cryopreservation. To embed the tissue, brains were frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and 8-μm coronal tissue sections were serially cut using a cryostat (Tanner Scientific, model: TN50) and fixed onto glass slides. The slides were air-dried at room temperature (RT) prior to staining, washed twice with Tris buffered saline (TBS; Thermo Fisher), incubated in TBS (0.3% Tween 20) for 15 min, and washed again with TBS. Slides were blocked with 10% goat serum in TBS for 20 min. GXM (mAb 18B7 is an anti-cryptococcal GXM IgG1 generated and generously provided by Arturo Casadevall68 at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health; 1:200 dilution; incubated O/N at 4 °C)-specific [conjugated to goat anti-mouse allophycocyanin (APC); Thermo Fisher; dilution: 1:100; incubated for 1 h at RT] immunostaining was performed to assess capsular release and distribution inside and around the cryptococcomas. Samples were sealed with ProLong Gold antifade mounting reagent with DAPI (Invitrogen). The slides were visualized using a Zeiss LSM 710 inverted confocal microscope, and images captured with a Zeiss AxioCam digital camera using ZEN microscopy software (Carl Zeiss). z stack and tile stitching were used to derive composite images of the brain coronal tissue sections. Individual images were taken at 10, 20×, and 60× magnification.

## Fluorescent imaging analysis
The distribution distance of microglia surrounding cryptococcomas was analyzed by measuring the radii from the microglia cell body to the edge of the cryptococcoma using NIH ImageJ software. The analysis of each microglia morphological parameters was performed using the ImageJ FIJI software. The microglia cell density and distribution were measured by placing individual dots in the cell soma or body. The NNI plugin was used to assess the nearest neighbor indices. The spacing index was determined by calculating NNI2 x microglial density, where the microglial density was the number of microglia divided by the region of interest (ROI) area.72 Microglia clusters were counted as any NNI that were ≤12-μm. To analyze the microglia cell morphology, the cell soma and branching area were outlined to generate the soma and arborization area, respectively. Finally, the morphology index was calculated by dividing the average soma area by the average arborization area in a ROI.

## Cytokine level determinations in brain tissue
To characterize the amount of TNF⍺, IL-1β, IL-4, and IL-6, the Peprotech, Inc enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit was followed according to manufacturer’s protocol for each cytokine. The day before tissue harvest, microtiter polystyrene plates were coated with capture anti-TNF⍺, IL-1β, IL-4, and IL-6 (1 μg/mL) overnight and incubated at 4 °C. The next day, each well was blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 2 h at room temperature (RT). Brain tissue was placed in RIPA buffer and homogenized, and the supernatant was processed. The brain samples were collected and serially diluted on the plate using a multi-pipette and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The ELISA was completed by adding 0.5 μg/mL of a specific detection Ab followed by a 2 h incubation at RT, then by avidin-horseradish peroxidase conjugate (HRP; 1:2,000 dilution) for 30 min at RT, and finally revealing by adding ABTS substrate. In each step, the wells were washed with 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS. The optical density was assessed at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy LX Multimode Reader and monitored every 10–15 min for 1 h until reaching detection threshold. Each sample was tested in triplicates in two independent ELISA measurements.

## Quantification and statistical analysis

## Statistical analysis
All data were subjected to statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3 GraphPad Software). p values for multiple comparisons were calculated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and were adjusted by use of Tukey’s post-hoc analysis. p values for individual comparisons were calculated using student’s or multiple t test analyses. p values of <0.05 were considered significant. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures using asterisks. Corresponding p value ranges represented by the asterisks (ns = not significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001), as well as the values represented by the error bars, are defined in the figure legends. For each figure, statistical details of experiments, including test type and statistical analysis, can be found in the table corresponding to that figure.
