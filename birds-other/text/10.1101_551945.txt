The keen visual systems of birds have been relatively well-studied. The foundations of avian

22

vision rest on their cone and rod photoreceptors. Most birds use four cone photoreceptor types

23

for color vision, a fifth cone for achromatic tasks, and a rod for low-light levels. The cones,

24

along with their oil droplets, and rods are conserved across birds – with the exception of a few

25

shifts in spectral sensitivity – despite taxonomic, behavioral and ecological differences. Here,

26

however, we describe a novel photoreceptor in a group of New World flycatchers (Empidonax

27

spp.) in which the traditional oil droplet is replaced with a complex of electron-dense

28

megamitochondria surrounded by hundreds of small, orange oil droplets. These photoreceptors

29

were unevenly distributed across the retina, being present in the central region (including in the

30

fovea), but absent from the retinal periphery and the area temporalis. Many bird species have

31

had their oil droplets and photoreceptors characterized, but only the two flycatchers described

32

here (E. virescens and E. minimus) possess this unusual structure. We discuss the potential

33

functional significance of the unique sub-cellular structure in these photoreceptors providing an

34

additional visual channel for these small predatory songbirds.

Introduction:

36

Birds are widely recognized for their specialized visual abilities1, which enable them to engage

37

in many diverse modes of life. For example, birds can be diurnal or nocturnal, predators and/or

38

prey, and flighted or flightless. The capabilities of avian vision have led to much interest in the

39

evolutionary design of avian visual systems2. The foundation of avian vision lies in their six

40

photoreceptor types3, compared for example to just four photoreceptors in humans. Birds, from

41

highly active predators such as hawks and falcons to passive foragers such as emus and

42

sparrows, have four single cone photoreceptors for color vision (ultraviolet or violet, short-

43

wavelength, medium-wavelength, and long-wavelength sensitive), a double cone for achromatic

44

discrimination/motion vision, and a rod for vision in dim light3,4. Within each cone

45

photoreceptor, birds have carotenoid-filled organelles called oil droplets, which are involved in

46

filtering light before it reaches the visual pigments, enhancing color discrimination3. Bird species

47

differ in the distribution, abundance, and absorbance spectra of these six photoreceptors3, but the

48

six fundamental building blocks are the same across species.

49

In this study, we report the discovery of a potentially seventh photoreceptor that contains

50

a cellular organelle not found in this form in any other vertebrate retina with important functional

51

consequences for visual perception. We found this photoreceptor in two species of New World

52

flycatchers of the genus Empidonax (E. virescens and E. minimus). We describe this new cellular

53

structure using light microscopy and transmission electron microscopy, analyze its spectral

54

composition using microspectrophotometry, and establish the distribution and densities of these

55

novel cones relative to the traditional cones across the retina. Additionally, we displayed the

56

presence/absence of this cellular structure in the avian phylogeny based on species whose cones

and oil droplets have been previously characterized. Overall, this cellular structure may allow

58

these sit-and-wait flycatchers to see their world from a different perspective than other animals.

Results:

61

We found that Empidonax flycatchers, like all birds, had four single cone photoreceptors that

62

each contained a spherical oil droplet in the inner segment of the photoreceptor. Like other birds,

63

the principal member of the Empidonax double cone also contained a spherical oil droplet. Each

64

type of cone had a different colored oil droplet that could be readily visualized under simple light

65

microscopy (Fig. 1A). In addition to these five traditional cones and their corresponding oil

66

droplets, we found that the Empidonax flycatcher retina contained what is probably an additional

67

photoreceptor with a novel, orange, conical structure in the apical end of the inner segment (Fig.

68

1B).

69
70

Electron Microscopy

71

Transmission electron microscopy revealed that this novel structure consisted of a cluster of

72

electron-dense giant mitochondria (hereafter megamitochondria) that were surrounded by

73

hundreds of small oil droplets (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1). These small oil droplets were the likely source

74

of the orange coloration seen under light microscopy. These megamitochondria were over two

75

times the diameter of the mitochondria in surrounding photoreceptors and the small oil droplets

76

associated with it had a diameter over seven times smaller than the traditional oil droplets found

77

in the other cone types (Fig. 1C). Given the unusual combination of megamitochondria and small

78

oil droplets, we coined the term megamitochondria-small oil droplet complex (hereafter,

79

MMOD-complex) to refer to this new cell structure.

80

The MMOD-complexes identified using electron microscopy were of the same shape and

81

size as those first observed on retinal wholemounts (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). Additionally, the MMOD-

82

complexes observed using electron microscopy were only present at the center of the flycatcher

83

retinae. No similar structures were present in the retinal periphery of flycatchers or in any part of

84

the retinae from two other species that we used for comparison purposes under electron

85

microscopy (white-throated sparrow and house sparrow, see Methods).

86

We also observed connecting cilia that linked the MMOD-complex to a photoreceptor

87

outer segment in flycatchers (Fig. S1). Therefore, we determined that the MMOD-complex was

88

likely housed at the apical end of the inner segment of a photoreceptor, just distal to the

89

traditional mitochondrial-rich ellipsoid. This is where a traditional oil droplet would normally be

90

located, which is absent in this new photoreceptor type. In addition to the connecting cilia

91

between the MMOD-complex and the photoreceptor outer segment, it would seem logical that

92

any structure occupying space in the photoreceptor array of the retina would serve a

93

photoreceptive function. Diurnal birds have retinas with dense photoreceptor arrays that have

94

little or no gap between adjacent photoreceptors. Non-photoreceptive cells inserted into the

95

photoreceptor array occupy valuable ‘real estate’ and would reduce visual acuity, seemingly

96

unnecessarily, although exceptions to this line of thinking are possible. Furthermore, it is not

97

likely that the MMOD-complex serves a specialized energy source for other photoreceptors or

98

other cells in the retina because cellular energy that is produced in a cell, generally, must remain

99

in the cell that produced it and cannot be shared with neighboring cells5. These lines of thought,

100

along with the connecting cilia between the MMOD-complex and a photoreceptive outer

101

segment, are why we think the most likely scenario is that the MMOD-complex is part of a

102

photoreceptor.

Microspectrophotometry

105

We found that the MMOD-complexes had λmid = 565 nm, which corroborates their orange

106

coloration under bright microscopy (Fig. 2b). Their λmid falls between the yellow Y-type oil

107

droplets (516 nm) and the red R-type oil droplets (580 nm). The small oil droplets surrounding

108

the megamitochondria work as long-pass filters, letting light longer the 565 nm pass through and

109

absorbing shorter wavelengths. Full microspectrophotometry results can be found in Table S1.

Photoreceptor Distribution

112

The Empidonax flycatcher retinae we examined contained a centrally placed fovea (i.e., an

113

invagination in the retinal tissue that corresponds to the highest photoreceptor densities in the

114

retina) and an area temporalis (i.e., an increase in cell density without a foveal pit in the

115

temporal region of the retina) (Fig. 4). The peak photoreceptor density for all counted cell types

116

(all single cones, double cones and MMOD-complex photoreceptors) was 118,300 ± 9,022

117

cells/mm2 and 58,533 ± 4,791 cells/mm2 in the fovea and area temporalis, respectively (Fig. 3a).

118

The peak foveal density was 33,700 ± 3,649 cells/mm2 and 85,600 ± 5,757 cells/mm2 for

119

MMOD-complex photoreceptors (Fig. 3i) and traditional oil droplets (Fig. 3b), respectively.

120

Interestingly, the MMOD-complex photoreceptors followed a different distribution pattern than

121

the other photoreceptors. Traditional cones were present throughout the retina and their density

122

decreased moving away from the fovea towards the periphery. However, MMOD-complex

123

photoreceptors were only present in the central region of the retina, including in the fovea. Their

124

density decreased slightly moving away from the fovea, but then these MMOD-complex

125

photoreceptors suddenly disappeared altogether (Fig. 3i-j). There were no MMOD-complex

We found no significant differences between typical passerine retinae and Empidonax

137

When comparing the center region of Empidonax retinae that contained MMOD-complex

138

140

142

143

144

S2).

Phylogenetic Representation

156

We reviewed the literature and also used unpublished data describing the retinal photoreceptor

157

layer (i.e., wholemounts of fresh retinas where the oil droplets were visible under bright and/or

158

florescent microscopy) of different avian species (Table S3). Considering the 48 bird species for

159

which information was available, the MMOD-complex was only found in the two flycatchers

160

described here (E. virescens and E. minimus) belonging to the Family Tyrannidae, Order

161

Passeriformes (Fig. 4). However, the MMOD-complex is absent in the other 46 species that

162

belong to other taxanomic Families or Orders (Fig. 4).

Discussion

165

The MMOD-complex is different in a number of ways from similar retinal structures that have

166

been described in other organisms. Birds from many taxa have been studied previously and all

167

share the same classes of photoreceptors, and none showed the MMOD-complex found in the

168

two New World flycatchers reported here (Fig. 4). Pigeons (Columba livia) do have red

169

microdroplets in a specialized area of the retina called the “red spot” or “red field” that is well

170

positioned for viewing green backgrounds in the pigeons’ binocular field6,7. Unlike flycatchers,

171

however, the pigeons’ microdroplets are present in addition to a principal oil droplet within the

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/551945; this version posted February 15, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

172

same inner segment and are not associated with a mitochondria6. Additionally, lamprey

173

photoreceptors contain either yellow/orange pigments (but not droplets) or large, electron-dense

174

mitochondria. However, the pigments and electron-dense mitochondria have not been found in

175

the same photoreceptor8, as is the case with flycatchers.

176

Megamitochondria have been found in the retina of tree shrews (Tupaia spp.)9 and

177

zebrafish (Danio rerio)10. However, the morphological novelty of the flycatcher

178

megamitochondria is that they are part of a larger complex that includes hundreds of small oil

179

droplets. These small oil droplets surround the megamitochondria, absorb visible light below 565

180

nm (λmid), and allow longer wavelengths – oranges and reds – to pass through (Fig. 2B, Table

181

S1). Therefore, the small oil droplets flood the megamitochondria (and the photoreceptor outer

182

segment) with long wavelength light. Put another way, the small oil droplets prevent short and

183

medium wavelength light from reaching the megamitochondria, thereby isolating any effects of

184

long wavelength light.

185

The cones of vertebrates have organelles in their inner segments called ellipsoids, which

186

are accumulations of mitochondria11,12. These ellipsoids are even present in vertebrates with oil

187

droplets, like birds13. From a developmental perspective, it is possible that the MMOD-complex

188

is a modification of these ellipsoids because in some species of fish without oil droplets, the

189

ellipsoids appear to resemble spatially and functionally the oil droplets14 and the ellipsoids have

190

been proposed to give rise to cone droplets through a process of metabolic turnover15.

191

Interestingly, the MMOD-complex photoreceptors do not follow the same distribution

192

pattern across the retina as other photoreceptors do. Traditionally, cones are at very high density

193

in the fovea centralis and decrease in density towards the retinal periphery16, which is also the

194

general case for traditional cones in the flycatcher retina (Fig. 3A-H). This heterogeneous

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/551945; this version posted February 15, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

195

distribution is because (1) cones are physiologically expensive17, and (2) the brain cannot

196

simultaneously devote visual attention to every photoreceptor17. However, the MMOD-complex

197

photoreceptors are only present at the center of the retina, with their highest density around the

198

fovea centralis, decreasing away from the fovea centralis but abruptly disappearing from the

199

retinal periphery entirely. In total, the MMOD-complex photoreceptors are only present in the

200

central ~25% of the retinal surface area (Fig. 3I-J, Fig. S2). The uncommon distribution pattern

201

of MMOD-complexes appears to be an exaggeration of the aforementioned traditional cone

202

distribution pattern. Therefore, we speculate that the MMOD-complex photoreceptors may be

203

even more physiologically-costly than traditional cones, which limits their presence exclusively

204

to the region where they are most beneficial for the animal. In addition to the fovea centralis,

205

flycatchers also have an area temporalis in the temporal region of the retina that has elevated

206

densities of traditional cones (Fig. 3A-H). But even the area temporalis is devoid of MMOD-

207

complexes (Fig. 3I).

208

The MMOD-complex appears to be present in addition to the traditional oil droplet types,

209

rather than at the expense of any one particular type. All five traditional oil droplet types are

210

present throughout the entire flycatcher retina in similar ratios to what one would expect to find

211

in any other passerine songbird (Fig. 2A, Table S2)3. Additionally, there are no significant

212

differences between the ratios of traditional oil droplets in regions of the retina with and without

213

MMOD-complex photoreceptors (Fig. 2A, Table S2). Furthermore, the extra photoreceptor

214

density in the central region of the retina (i.e., where the MMOD-complex photoreceptors are

215

present) is matched by an area of increased retinal ganglion cell density18 and a substantial

216

thickening of the retinal tissue (Fig. 3K, Fig. S2). Both lines of evidence suggest that there is a

217

need for more neurons in layers of the retina downstream from the MMOD-complex

photoreceptors and support the idea that the MMOD-complex photoreceptors may serve a

219

separate rather than incremental function relative to the other photoreceptors.

220

The function of similar structures in other organisms are not entirely understood, and it is

221

possible that the MMOD-complex could be serving any number of functions simultaneously.

222

Establishing the definitive function of the MMOD-complex was beyond the scope of this study.

223

To provide some context, however, we can make some non-exhaustive speculations about its

224

function. For example, zebrafish rods with experimentally expressed megamitochondria produce

225

over twice as much ATP as wild-type rods that lack megamitochondria10. Generally, ATP

226

produced within a cell must be used by that cell rather than sharing energy with the entire retina5.

227

Therefore, megamitochondria are likely energy powerhouses for only the photoreceptor that

228

contains them. The small orange oil droplets of the MMOD-complex could further increase

229

megamitochondrial energy production. Cytochrome c oxidase – the terminal enzyme in the

230

electron transport chain that is responsible for energy production in mitochondria – is a

231

photoacceptor that increases its activity and energy production under long-wavelength visible

232

light19–22. White light – which is the presence of all visible wavelengths – down-regulates other

233

enzymes in the electron transport chain that are photoacceptors of shorter wavelengths19.

234

Therefore, white light results in no net change of energy production, whereas long-wavelength

235

light increases overall energy production19. Thus, by absorbing short-wavelengths, the

236

flycatchers’ orange oil droplets only transmit the energizing long-wavelengths onto the

237

megamitochondria. Filtering out short-wavelengths would also protect the retina from photo-

238

oxidative damage8,23,24. Considering the high oxidative stress already present from extra energy

239

production, these protective aspects of MMOD-complex could reduce the probability of

240

apoptosis24,25.

With potentially large quantities of energy available to the flycatchers’ MMOD-complex

242

photoreceptor, it is likely capable of more rapid response rates26–28. Rapid photoreceptor

243

response rates may yield higher temporal visual resolution and spatio-temporal tracking of

244

motion29–31, which would ultimately give flycatchers a photoreceptor potentially specialized in

245

motion detection and motion tracking. This specialization can be particularly useful for

246

flycatchers because they use a sit-and-wait hunting strategy32 that requires considerable motion-

247

tracking as individuals sit almost motionless while their flying prey (flies, mosquitos, etc.) move

248

at high speeds33. Therefore, flycatchers must detect and track the absolute velocity of their prey

249

rather than the slower, relative velocity required by predators that actively chase prey (e.g.,

250

swifts and swallows). Empidonax flycatchers often hunt against blue or green backgrounds. The

251

filtering effect of the orange oil droplets may even shield the potentially motion-sensitive

252

MMOD-complex photoreceptors from the motion of green leaves blowing in the wind, isolating

253

the motion of the insect.

254

In summary, the retina of flycatchers from a single Family of songbirds have evolved a

255

novel cellular structure in what is probably a photoreceptor that may confer some benefits in

256

terms of detecting, tracking, and capturing fast-moving prey. Potential future approaches to

257

establish the function of this structure should include visual pigment absorbance spectra to

258

establish the sensitivity of the visual pigment of the photoreceptor with the MMOD-complex,

259

wavelength-specific electroretinograms to determine the critical flicker fusion frequency of

260

MMOD-complex photoreceptors compared to the other photoreceptor types, optokinetic

261

response to quantify motion tracking ability, retinal oximetry to determine the energetic demands

262

of these photoreceptors, and behavioral experiments to assess the ability of flycatchers to catch

263

prey under different wavelength specific light conditions.

Animals

267

268

269

270

271

272

273

(protocols 1201000567 and 1112000398).

Electron Microscopy

276

To determine the subcellular structure of the novel structures, the right eyes of one Acadian

277

flycatcher and one house sparrow were examined using transmission electron microscopy

278

(TEM). After enucleation, hemisection, and vitreous humor extraction, the eyes were fixed in

279

2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4) at 4ºC overnight. 2 mm2

280

sections of the house sparrow retina (three separate sections), the periphery of the Acadian

281

flycatcher retina (one section), and the center of the Acadian flycatcher retina (two separate

282

sections) were trimmed and embedded in epon resin. The house sparrow retina and the periphery

283

of the Acadian flycatcher retina were examined for negative confirmation because the novel

284

structure we sought is only present near the center of the Acadian flycatcher retina. Ultrathin

285

cross sections (50 nm) were inspected using a JEOL-100CX electron microscope (JOEL Ltd.,

286

Tokyo, Japan).

Retinal Wholemounting and Photoreceptor Distribution

293

One right eye from a least flycatcher, one right eye from an Acadian flycatcher, and two left eyes

294

from Acadian flycatchers (4 total individuals) were used for retinal wholemeounting and

295

photoreceptor distributions. Following euthanasia, eyes were immediately enucleated. We then

296

hemisected the eye at the ora serrata with a razor blade and removed the vitreous humor using

297

spring scissors, tweezers, and a dissecting microscope. We submerged the eyecup in cold 0.1 M

298

phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4) and placed it in a -20ºC freezer for three minutes to facilitate

299

the retraction of pigmented epithelium from the photoreceptor layer. The retina was removed

300

from the eyecup by separating the choroid from the sclera and severing the optic nerve. We then

301

gently peeled the choroid away from the retina and any pigmented epithelium that remained

302

attached to the retina to avoid damaging the photoreceptor layer. We transferred the retina

303

directly to a glass coverslip with the photoreceptor layer facing the coverslip. We then flattened

304

the retina onto the glass coverslip by making several radial cuts with spring scissors and

305

unrolling the retinal edges with a small paintbrush. We placed a drop of super glue onto each

306

corner of the coverslip, which was then flipped onto a microscope slide and sealed with clear nail

307

polish. We also wholemounted one white-throated sparrow retina for a qualitative comparison.

308

Retinae were then immediately imaged using SRS (Systematic Random Sampling)

309

StereoInvestigator software (MBF Bioscience, Williston, Vermont), an Olympus BX51

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/551945; this version posted February 15, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was

310

microscope, and an Olympus S97809 camera (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). A bright

311

field illuminated image and an epifluorescent-illuminated image were captured at 275 to 280

312

locations on each retina. A counting frame of 50 µm X 50 µm was used at each 579 ± 39 µm X

313

621 ± 16 µm location with a mean ± SE area sampling fraction (i.e., the proportion of each

314

location that was occupied by the counting frame) of 0.0071 ± 0.0008. Our mean ± SE

315

Schaeffer’s Coefficient of Error of 0.046 ± 0.002 is less than 0.10, indicating that our

316

stereological sampling strategy was appropriate34. We identified and counted all oil droplets

317

within each counting frame using ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). In birds, each cone type is

318

associated with a specific oil droplet type. Oil droplets are oil and carotenoid-filled organelles at

319

the apical end of the cone inner segment that spectrally filter light before the light reaches the

320

visual pigment in the outer segment where phototransduction occurs. Therefore, the densities of

321

each photoreceptor type can be quantified by counting oil droplets. Traditionally, there are five

322

types of cones each associated with its own type of oil droplet. Transparent (T-type) oil droplets

323

are found in ultraviolet or violet sensitive single cones, colorless (C-type) oil droplets are found

324

in short-wavelength sensitive single cones, yellow (Y-type) oil droplets are found in medium-

325

wavelength sensitive single cones, red (R-type) oil droplets are found in long-wavelength

326

sensitive single cones, and principal (P-type) oil droplets are found in the principal member of

327

double cones. We identified each of the five traditional cone types follow the criteria established

328

by Hart3. Briefly, R-type oil droplets appear red under bright field illumination, Y-type oil

329

droplets appear yellow under bright field illumination, T-type oil droplets appear clear under

330

bright field illumination but are absent under epifluorescent illumination, C-type oil droplets

331

appear clear under bright field illumination but fluoresce bright white under epifluorescent

332

illumination, and P-type oil droplets appear clear to yellowish-green under bright field

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/551945; this version posted February 15, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

333

illumination but fluoresce dull white to dull yellow under epifluorescent illumination. In addition

334

to the five traditional oil droplet types, we were easily able to identify the new MMOD-

335

complexes as the obvious, large, orange triangles juxtaposed with the smaller, circular traditional

336

oil droplets.

337

To visualize the distribution of photoreceptors across the retina, we constructed

338

topographic maps in R (version 3.3.0) following Garza-Gisholt et al.35. We also calculated cell

339

density ratios to determine if MMOD-complex photoreceptors are a subset of another type of

340

photoreceptor type or a present at the expense of any single photoreceptor type. First of all, all

341

five traditional oil droplet types are present in areas of Empidonax retinae containing MMOD-

342

complex photoreceptors. To calculate cell density ratios, we took the number of oil droplets of a

343

given type divided by the number of T-type oil droplets at each counting location. For example,

344

the C-type ratio is calculated as the number of C-type oil droplets present in a single counting

345

location divided by the number of T-type oil droplets at that location. And the Y-type ratio is

346

calculated as the number of Y-type oil droplets present in a single counting location divided by

347

the number of T-type oil droplets at that location, etc. We used T-type oil droplets as the

348

denominator because they are the least abundant oil droplet type in the retina. Ratios were

349

calculated for every counting location on each of the four Empidonax retinae. All the ratios from

350

an individual retina were averaged to yield a single data point per individual. Those data were

351

compared to typical retinae from Order Passeriformes – the same order that Empidonax

352

flycatchers belong to – using the t.test function in R to determine if the cell ratios of the

353

Empidonax retinae differed from expected values. Values for typical passerine retinae were

354

garnered from Hart3 as the species-level means for six passerine species (Entomyzon cyanotis,

355

Manorina melanocephala, Parus caeruleus, Ptilonorhynchus violaceus, Sturnus vulgaris, Turdus

not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

356

357

ratios were lower in MMOD-complex regions than regions lacking MMOD-complexes within

358

Empidonax retinae, which would indicate that MMOD-complex photoreceptors are replacing

359

other cones.

Microspectrophotometry

381

To determine the spectral absorbance characteristics of flycatcher oil droplets, the left eye of one

382

Acadian flycatcher was analyzed using microspectrophotometry. Immediately after capture, the

383

Acadian flycatcher was light deprived for three hours to facilitate visual pigment regeneration.

384

Euthanasia, enucleation, hemisection, and vitreous extraction were all carried out under dim red

385

light to avoid bleaching the photoreceptors. The retina was extracted from the eyecup in the same

386

fashion as described above for retinal wholemounts, and we used a small paintbrush to brush

387

away excessive pigmented epithelium that remained attached to the retina. We removed a ~6

388

mm2 piece of the retina, placed the retina section on a glass slip, and macerated the retina section

389

with a razor blade. The macerated retina was hydrated with a drop of phosphate buffered saline

390

(pH 7.4) and sucrose water, cover slipped, and sealed with black nail polish to prevent

391

desiccation.

392

We measured absorbance spectra of oil droplets and MMOD-complexes using a custom-

393

built microspectrophotometer (Dr. Ellis Loew, Cornell University, Ithaca, NY; described in

394

McFarland and Loew36). We used a dry Zeiss Ultrafluar Glyc objective (80x, NA 0.9) for

395

viewing and a second objective (32x, NA 0.4) as a condenser. We viewed cellular structures

396

using an EXVision Super Circuits CCD camera attached to a TFT Color LCD monitor that was

397

covered in red Plexiglas. After identifying an isolated oil droplet or MMOD-complex, we took a

398

baseline measurement in the surrounding interstitial space. Then, we measured the absorbance of

399

the target structure in 1 nm increments from 350-750 nm. The absorbance of MMOD-complexes

400

was measured through the thicker basal end and the thinner apical end of the structure separately.

401

We attempted to bleach a MMOD-complex by exposing it to white light for 60 s to determine

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/551945; this version posted February 15, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

402

whether it behaved as an oil droplet or a visual pigment. Visual pigments bleach (i.e., stop

403

absorbing light) under white light37, whereas oil droplets do not bleach.

404

Oil droplets are typically characterized by three parameters: λcut – the wavelength of

405

maximal absorbance; λmid – the wavelength with 50% of maximal absorbance; and λ0 – the

406

wavelength of 63% maximal absorbance. We determined these three parameters using the

407

MATLAB program, OilDropSpec. OilDropSpec normalized the long wavelength arm of each oil

408

droplet spectrum to one, found the wavelength where absorbance was 0.5 (λmid), fit a trend line to

409

the absorbance data 10 nm to either side of λmid, determined the intercept, slope, and R2 of the

410

trend line, used the trend line to determine the wavelength where the absorbance was 1 (λcut), and

411

calculated λ0. In total, absorbance spectra were measured for 1 T-type oil droplet, 1 C-type oil

412

droplets, 17 Y-type oil droplets, 9 R-type oil droplets, 13 P1-type oil droplets, 4 P2-type oil

413

droplets (there are two standard variations of P-type oil droplets), and 23 MMOD-complexes.
