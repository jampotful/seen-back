Spectral Transmittance of the Ocular Media of Eight Seabird Species from Two Orders: Procellariiformes (Petrels and Shearwaters) and Suliformes (Gannets and Shags).
Heswall AM, Hadden PW, Zhang J, Friesen M, Cain KE, Gaskett A. (2026). Brain, behavior and evolution. . doi:10.1159/000551766. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Spectral Transmittance of the Ocular Media of Eight Seabird Species from Two Orders: Procellariiformes (Petrels and Shearwaters) and Suliformes (Gannets and Shags)

## Abstract
AbstractIntroductionMany, but not all, bird taxa possess the ability to see ultraviolet (UV) light, but it is currently unclear the extent this ability exists among seabirds. Measuring retinal photoreceptor sensitivity presents many challenges, but the penetration of UV light through the ocular media (ocular transmittance) is a good proxy for UV vision.MethodsHere, we document the ocular transmission of light wavelengths using spectrometry through the eyes of eight Procellariiform and Suliform seabird species found in Aotearoa New Zealand.ResultsWe report that the eyes of most Procellariiformes, but not the Suliformes, can transmit UV wavelengths. Thus, there is the potential for those Procellariiformes to perceive UV.DiscussionBoth phylogeny and ecology could play a role in UV vision in seabirds and understanding the wavelengths that seabirds perceive can be crucial for conservation against visual threats.Keywords: Ocular transmission, Ultraviolet, Bird vision, Procellariiformes, Suliformes, Seabirds

## Introduction
Avian photoreceptors, located in the retina, contain various visual pigments, each with a peak absorption at a different wavelength (i.e., ultraviolet [UV], violet, green, or red) [1]. These photoreceptors enable colour discrimination and perception between different sections of the light spectrum. Additionally, coloured oil droplets may further refine this perception [2–4]. Bird taxa differ in their photoreceptor sensitivity to various wavelengths. For example, some birds’ shortest-wavelength photoreceptors have peak sensitivity (λmax) in the UV range (300–400 nm) with some dedicated UV pigments spectral ranges ∼360–370 nm, described in the blue tit (Parus caeruleus) [1, 5–7]. However, other species short-wavelength photoreceptors are not tuned to UV, being violet sensitive, having a peak sensitivity (λmax) between 402 and 426 nm [1, 5, 6].
Photoreceptors tuned to detect UV have not been detected in penguins, for example, the Humboldt penguin, Spheniscus humboldti, has three visual photoreceptor pigments and three different coloured oil droplets, giving λmax of 405 nm, 430 nm, and 555–560 nm [8]. However, this does not preclude the perception of UV light since there is a significant secondary absorption (β-band) in the UV range by all visual pigments [8]. An example is humans with aphakia who are UV sensitive regardless of the lack of UV-sensitive photoreceptors [9]. A recent study also suggests some penguin species including gentoo penguins, Pygoscelis papua, and little blue penguins, Eudyptula minor, can detect UV despite no current evidence of UV photoreceptor sensitivity [10, 11]. Further, the 405 nm pigment could confer considerable UV sensitivity having approximately 50% of its peak photon catch ability at 350 nm [8]. Therefore, all avian retinas are potentially sensitive to UV light even if their shortest-wavelength photoreceptor does not have a peak sensitivity (λmax) in the UV range [12].
The wavelengths available for photoreceptor detection is determined by the spectral transmission of wavelengths through the ocular media, i.e., the clear structures that light passes though, including the cornea, aqueous humour, lens, and vitreous fluid [12–14]. No biological tissue can transmit light below 310 nm because of absorption by nucleic acids and structural proteins [13]. However, for the rest of the UV range (310–400 nm and specifically UV-A 315–400 nm), the ocular media determines whether UV wavelengths are absorbed, or transmitted to reach the retina, and thus, ocular transmittance is a good proxy for avian species’ ability to detect UV [12–15]. Also, measuring the spectral sensitivity of retinal photoreceptors produces challenges including equipment and the immediate freshness of the eyeball, so ocular transmittance can be a more feasible method to assess UV sensitivity [11, 16, 17]. Previous research has assessed the ocular transmission on some seabirds including penguin species [8, 11] and the wedge-tailed shearwater (Puffinus pacificus; Hart, 2004). However, the ocular transmittance of most seabird species remains undocumented.
Importantly, seabirds are one of the most threatened groups of animals [18, 19]. Many of these threats likely have a strong link to a seabird’s visual ecology. For instance, light pollution results in many seabirds becoming disorientated by the lights leading to collision-related injuries [20–22] with some streetlights emitting UV wavelengths [23–25]. Analysing a seabird’s ocular transmittance would not only document valuable information on the spectral wavelengths, which could reach the retina but also could facilitate an improved understanding of visual threats to seabirds [26–28]. Here, our aim is to report on the ocular transmittance on various seabird species across two different orders, the Procellariiformes and Suliformes, in Aotearoa New Zealand.

## Methods

## Study Species
All eyeballs were obtained from seabirds that had recently died, or required euthanasia by veterinarians, at an avian rehabilitation centre. We only used eyes, which were assumed healthy, i.e., no sign of trauma or ocular diseases. Specimens were selected systematically determined by availability, with a focus on Procellariiformes and Suliformes given the lack of ocular transmittance data from these seabird orders, both globally and in New Zealand. We analysed eight seabird species: five Procellariiformes (Cook’s petrel, Pterodroma cookii, n = 1; white-faced storm petrel, Pelagodroma marina, n = 1; grey-faced petrel, Pterodroma gouldi, n = 1; Buller’s shearwater, Ardenna bulleri, n = 2; fairy prion, Pachyptila turtur, n = 2) and three Suliformes (Australasian gannet, Morrus serrator, n = 3; pied shag, Phalacrocorax varius, n = 2; little shag, Phalacrocorax melanoleucos, n = 1).

## Ocular Transmittance
Within 1–2 h post death, both left and right eyes were dissected from the seabird’s head and stored in a sealed container, refrigerated at 4°C. Following similar methods to the co-author’s previous penguin study, the ocular transmittance was assessed within 48 h post-mortality and immediately post-removal from refrigeration [11]. Axial length data were collected using both digital callipers and data from prior research [29, 30]. The whole eye, including the cornea, aqueous humour, and lens, was then enucleated and plated (Fig. 1) into the SpectraMax i3x spectrometer to analyse ocular transmittance via the SoftMax pro software [11].
Plate set-up of the seabird’s eyeballs prepared for analysis in the SpectraMax i3x spectrometer.
Cornea degradation can occur within hours of death if unrefrigerated [31], so to ensure accuracy of results, we assessed the ocular transmittance of multiple eyes from the same and extra species. Seabird eyes (any species, left or right), which had a prolonged refrigeration period and unknown time of death, resulting in strong graphical evidence of reduced/weak ocular transmission, e.g., flat and lacking sigmoid shape or constantly fluctuating peaks in the transmittance graph, were removed from this study as the sample was no longer viable and degraded. The transmittance through the vitreous humour was not documented as this study focused on the lens and cornea; however, future research should include it.
The ocular transmittance data were normalized at 100% to 700 nm for graphical analysis using Microsoft Excel 360. Seabirds were classified as being able or mostly unable to detect UV based on the λT0.5 and the shape of the curve. If the normalized peak ocular transmittance at λT0.5 was greater than 380 nm and the transmittance curve remained low and flat between 300 and 350 nm before steeply increasing until approximately 400 nm, we classified the species as one that had minimal or no ability to detect UV [11, 14, 32]. If the normalized peak ocular transmittance had a lower λT0.5 <360–400 nm with a transmittance curve gradually increasing between 300 and 350 nm, we classified those species as being able to detect UV. The normalized transmission of left and right eye spectra from individual birds were averaged together before averaging with other individuals of the same species (Table 1).

## Results
We found that UV wavelengths can transmit through the lens and cornea of four Procellariiform species: Buller’s shearwater, Cook’s petrel, grey-faced petrel, and fairy prion (all species λT0.5 <370 nm; all transmittance curves gradually increasing between 300 and 350 nm; Table 1; Fig. 2). We found minimal to no UV transmission through the lens and cornea of one Procellariiform species: the white-faced storm petrel (λT0.5 = 396 nm; Table 1; Fig. 2) and for the three Suliformes species: Australasian gannet (λT0.5 = 396 nm; Table 1; Fig. 2), the pied shag (λT0.5 = 395 nm; Table 1; Fig. 2), and little pied shag (λT0.5 = 396 nm Table 1; Fig. 2). Additionally, these species transmittance curve remained low and flat between ∼300 and 350 nm before sharply increasing (Fig. 2).

All species spectral transmission at λT0.25 were <400 nm (Fig. 2; Table 1) and most of the spectral transmission at λT0.75 was under 460 nm except the l white-faced storm petrel (λT0.75 = 465 nm; Table 1). We also identified variations in the spectral transmission among individuals of the same species for the Australasian gannet, pied shag, Buller’s shearwater, and the fairy prion (Fig. 2).

## Discussion
For the first time, we document the likely capacity for eight New Zealand seabird species to detect UV. There were variations among individuals within a species, which could have been caused by differences in storage times. However, distinct differences of ocular transmittance between species and orders were observed. Intriguingly, all the Suliformes had minimal to no UV transmittance, but four of the five Procellariiformes (excluding the white-faced storm petrel) could, indicating they are potentially UV sensitive. The wedge-tailed shearwater can transmit UV through the ocular media until 330 nm and has UV-sensitive photoreceptors suggesting it has considerable sensitivity to UV despite an SWS1 cone that has a pigment that peaks in violet (λmax = 406 nm) [33, 34]. Therefore, there is a high possibility that the Procellariiformes in our study transmitting UV through the ocular media can perceive it.

## Drivers of Ocular Transmittance: Phylogeny
Phylogeny is likely an important driver for UV transmittance among seabirds, consistent with several studies [14, 35]. Phylogeny contributes to morphological, genetic, and behavioural differences (including foraging and breeding ecologies), facilitating adaptations in the structure and shape of the eyeball, affecting ocular transmittance [14, 36, 37].
The white-faced storm petrel was the only Procellariiform species tested that could not transmit UV. White-faced storm petrels belong to the family Oceanitidae, differing from the other Procellariiform species, which belong to the Procellariidae family [38]. It may be that the Oceanitidae cannot transmit UV, but with a low sample number, further research should confirm UV transmission among storm petrels and other Oceanitidae species. However, many past papers documenting peak spectral sensitivity in the photoreceptors and ocular transmittance have only one-two samples per species [11, 16], so our result may well be accurate.

## Drivers of Ocular Transmittance: Ecology
The role of ecology, rather than phylogeny, has been suggested as a key driver for ocular transmittance of UV in mammals and amphibians [12, 39] and a recent study by Ausprey [40] investigated the foraging ecology and phylogeny of avian eyeball sizes. The study reported a strong correlation for eye size and foraging behaviour with plunge diving Suliformes having the largest eyeball size followed by surface seizing seabirds such as the Procellariiformes [40]. For some bird groups e.g., diurnal raptors, eyeball size is not correlated to UV transmittance [32]; however, for other bird groups, there is a positive correlation [11, 12, 14, 32]. Ecologically for certain species, it may not be beneficial to detect UV as UV transmittance may affect visual acuity as it increases chromatic aberration resulting in image deterioration and UV radiation can cause retinal damage [32]. In our study, the larger Suliformes, e.g., the Australasian gannet (∼2.3 kg [41]) and pied shag (1.3–2.1 kg [42]), could not transmit UV, and these seabirds are pursuit and/or plunge divers requiring precise vision for hunting in which an ability to detect UV could be a hindrance if their vision is deteriorated and damaged [11]. In comparison, the smaller Procellariiformes in this study, such as the fairy prion (120 g [43]) or Cook’s petrel (180 g [44]), perform shallow dives and surface foraging. These species are nocturnally active and rely heavily on olfaction [45–48] so may not need to block UV transmittance. Additionally, increased atmospheric scattering of smaller UV wavelengths may reduce visual clarity for detecting foraging aggregations as gannets often use foraging aggregations to locate prey, so being violet sensitive instead of UV sensitive may improve detection of distant foraging aggregations [49].

## Conclusion: Seabird Vision and Conservation Applications
Previous studies on seabirds have demonstrated their highly specialized visual features being crucial for their pelagic, ocean-going life history and predation behaviour [50, 51]. The eye structures and corneas of penguins (Spheniscidae) are highly adapted for their amphibious lifestyle in which they consistently transition between a marine (foraging) and subaerial (breeding) environment [52, 53]. Additionally, the albatross’ (Diomedeidae, Procellariiformes) retina has a high density of ganglion cells to enhance visual acuity, necessary for surface seizing prey [54].
This study has contributed to the growing knowledge of seabird vision and UV ocular transmittance. The ability of Suliformes to detect UV is much less than that of the Procellariiformes, suggesting some importance ecologically or behaviourally for the detection of UV among that order. Further study of the benefits of UV detection in Procellariiformes would be valuable.
Understanding how seabirds perceive threats using sensory ecology is extremely important and applicable for conservation [55–57] and it has even been employed to reduce seabird bycatch [58]. Understanding the spectral wavelengths perceived by seabirds provides critical information to develop mitigation techniques to conserve seabirds from threats such as light pollution, a major risk for petrels and shearwaters (Procellariiformes) [21, 59–61]. Guidelines on “seabird friendly” wavelengths from lighting sources can be developed using information on the Procellariiform ocular transmittance to reduce fatalities.
