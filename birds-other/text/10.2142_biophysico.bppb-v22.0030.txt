Schiff base deprotonation and structural changes in a mouse UV-sensitive cone visual pigment revealed by FTIR spectroscopy at 77 K.
Mizuno Y, Arai S, Katayama K, Kandori H. (2025). Biophysics and physicobiology. . doi:10.2142/biophysico.bppb-v22.0030. Licence: cc-by-nc-sa. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Schiff base deprotonation and structural changes in a mouse UV-sensitive cone visual pigment revealed by FTIR spectroscopy at 77 K

## Abstract
UV-sensitive cone visual pigments are widespread among vertebrates, including birds, fish, and rodents such as mice, and play essential roles in non-primate vision. Unlike visible-light pigments, which contain a protonated retinal Schiff base and absorb at 400–700 nm, UV pigments maintain a deprotonated Schiff base, but how this state is stabilized has remained unresolved.Here, we applied low-temperature FTIR spectroscopy to the mouse UV-sensitive cone visual pigment (MUV), capturing structural changes associated with Batho intermediate formation. Spectral features characteristic of a deprotonated Schiff base were observed in both the initial and Batho states. Importantly, analysis of the Glu113 mutant demonstrated that Glu113 is protonated under these condition. Combined with the analysis of protein-bound water signals, these results indicate that the hydroxyl group of Glu113 serves as a direct hydrogen-bond donor to the deprotonated Schiff base. Moreover, the Glu113 C=O stretching vibration appeared at an unusually low frequency, revealing the presence of an exceptionally strong hydrogen bond with its surrounding protein environment. Comparison with bovine rhodopsin and cone pigments further revealed that MUV binds fewer water molecules near the retinal. This reduction in hydration suggests that a more hydrophobic environment contributes to lowering the Schiff base pKa and stabilizing its deprotonated state. Sequence analyses across species support this view, highlighting conserved nonpolar residues in transmembrane helix 2 (TM2) that likely disrupt hydrogen-bonding networks and promote UV sensitivity. Together, these findings establish MUV as a model for understanding how specific amino acid environments and hydration patterns enable UV vision in vertebrates.

## Significance
This study elucidates, for the first time, the structural basis of ultraviolet (UV) light absorption in a vertebrate visual pigment through low-temperature FTIR spectroscopy. By directly detecting vibrational signals from the mouse UV pigment (MUV), we demonstrate that its deprotonated Schiff base is associated with an exceptionally strong hydrogen-bonding interaction involving Glu113 within a hydrophobic and weakly hydrated environment. Comparative and mutational analyses further reveal conserved nonpolar residues that shape this microenvironment and lower the Schiff base pKa. These findings provide a molecular-level understanding of how local amino acid composition and hydration govern UV sensitivity in vertebrate vision.

## Introduction
Vertebrate vision relies on a family of light-sensitive proteins called visual pigments, which have diversified through gene duplication and successive amino acid substitutions, generating molecules with distinct absorption maxima (λmax). Phylogenetically, vertebrate cone visual pigments are classified into four subgroups: LWS (long-wavelength sensitive), Rh2 (middle-wavelength sensitive), SWS2 (short-wavelength sensitive 2), and SWS1 (short-wavelength sensitive 1). Each subgroup contributes to color discrimination across different spectral ranges [1–3]. All four cone classes are conserved in most teleost fish, reptiles, and birds, whereas one or more classes have been lost during vertebrate evolution, particularly in amphibians and mammals [4].
Among these, the SWS1 subgroup is notable because its members can be either UV-sensitive or violet-sensitive, depending on whether the retinal Schiff base, which links 11-cis-retinal to a lysine residue in transmembrane helix 7, is deprotonated or protonated, respectively. This chemical state directly determines the λmax: UV-sensitive cone pigments absorb around 360–380 nm, whereas violet-sensitive cone pigments absorb around 400–420 nm [3]. Such protonation-dependent spectral tuning forms the molecular basis for the UV-violet differentiation observed among vertebrates.
In primates, including humans, trichromatic vision relies on cone pigments that are sensitive to red, green, and blue light. The green-sensitive pigment arose through gene duplication of the red-sensitive pigment, and their amino acid sequences are approximately 95% identical. This 5% difference accounts for a spectral shift of about 30 nm [5,6]. The blue-sensitive pigment, belonging to the SWS1 class, followed a distinct evolutionary trajectory: originally UV-sensitive, it acquired violet or blue sensitivity through amino acid replacements that minimized UV-induced photodamage and improved visual resolution [7–11]. In contrast, many vertebrates including birds, fish, and rodents retain SWS1-type UV-sensitive cone pigments, allowing them to perceive ultraviolet light and experience a color world distinct from that of primates [12]. The mouse UV-sensitive cone pigment (MUV) represents a typical UV-sensitive cone pigment, whereas the monkey blue-sensitive cone pigment (MB) is its violet-sensitive counterpart. Comparing their structures and photoreactions provides an ideal framework for understanding the molecular origin of UV versus violet sensitivity.
Visual pigments belong to the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily and share a conserved architecture of seven transmembrane α-helices. Each pigment binds a chromophore, 11-cis-retinal, covalently attached to a lysine residue, forming a Schiff base linkage that plays a central role in light absorption. In organic solvents, retinal absorbs UV light at 370–380 nm. When bound as a protonated Schiff base within the protein, its absorption shifts into the visible range, typically 400–700 nm [13,14]. Therefore, in UV-sensitive pigments, deprotonation of the Schiff base enables ultraviolet absorption. However, how this deprotonated state is stabilized within the protein environment remains unresolved.
Previous studies have primarily focused on the molecular basis of spectral tuning in visible-light pigments. For cone pigments, which mediate photopic and color vision, structural studies have been limited because their three-dimensional structures remain unsolved. As a result, critical residues for spectral tuning have been identified through amino acid sequence comparisons and spectral shifts induced by site-directed mutagenesis [8,15–18]. Furthermore, homology models based on bovine rhodopsin (bRh) structures, combined with quantum chemical calculations, have provided insights into the molecular determinants of spectral tuning [19–23].
Expanding on these approaches, we and others (e.g., Prof. Richard A. Mathies and colleagues; Prof. Yoshinori Shichida and colleagues) have applied FTIR and Raman spectroscopies to cone pigments, enabling direct observation of retinal structural changes, alterations in amino acid side chains, and internal water molecules [24–30]. These studies revealed that polar interactions within hydrophobic environments are key determinants of spectral tuning in primate red- (MR) and green-sensitive cone pigments (MG) [28], whereas water molecules proximal to the retinal play a central role in blue-sensitive pigment (MB) [29].
In contrast, UV-sensitive cone pigments have not been as extensively studied as other cone types, although several spectroscopic investigations have been reported. Previous studies combining mutagenesis with UV-visible spectroscopy and quantum chemical calculations have suggested that residues near the Schiff base on transmembrane helix 2 (TM2) play a critical role. In particular, nonpolar residues at positions 86 and 90 are thought to lower the Schiff base pKa, thereby stabilizing its deprotonated state [8,11,15,22,31–34] (amino acid numbering based on bovine rhodopsin). In addition, low-temperature UV-visible and ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopy of the MUV and the Siberian hamster UV pigment (SHUV) have provided key photochemical insights. These studies demonstrated that the Schiff base remains deprotonated in the initial and early photointermediate states, but becomes protonated in later intermediates [35,36]. Furthermore, spectroscopic analyses suggested that Glu113, which serves as the counterion in other cone pigments and in rhodopsin, is protonated in the dark state of MUV [35]. Nevertheless, the detailed molecular basis remains unresolved.
In this study, we applied FTIR spectroscopy to the mouse UV-sensitive cone pigment (MUV) and successfully captured structural changes accompanying the formation of the Batho intermediate from the initial state. Retinal vibrational modes revealed spectral features characteristic of a deprotonated Schiff base in both states. Furthermore, analysis of the Glu113 mutant directly demonstrated that Glu113 is protonated in both the initial and Batho states, thereby corroborating that the Schiff base remains deprotonated. By combining signals from the protein backbone, amino acid side chains, and internal water molecules, we provide new insights into the molecular basis of UV absorption in MUV and UV-sensitive pigments more broadly.

## Materials and methods

## Sample preparation
The cDNA of mouse UV-sensitive cone pigment (Uniprot ID: P51491, Short-wave-sensitive opsin 1) was tagged with the Rho 1D4 epitope sequence and inserted into the pFastBac HT expression vector (Bac-to-Bac, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the in-fusion cloning method. Site-directed mutagenesis was carried out using QuikChange Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Protein expression and purification are followed by ref37 [37]. Briefly, protein was expressed in insect cells (Sf9) and stored at –80°C. Sample was regenerated with 11-cis-retinal and purified using 1D4 antibody-conjugated resin after solubilization in n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM). The isolated protein was incorporated into egg-derived phosphatidylcholine (PC) liposomes and suspended in a buffer composed of 2 mM NaH2PO4 (pH 7.25) with 5 mM NaCl. The reconstituted sample was deposited onto a BaF2 window to form dry films. To obtain hydrated films, 1 μL of H2O, D2O, or D218O was added adjacent to the dried films, which were subsequently subjected to spectroscopic analysis.

## Low-temperature UV-visible and FTIR spectroscopic measurements
Hydrated films were mounted on a cryostat (Optistat, Oxford Instruments) attached to a UV-visible spectrophotometer (V750, JASCO) and an FTIR spectrometer (Cary670, Agilent). In low-temperature UV-visible spectroscopy at 77 K, intermediates were generated by irradiating the sample through a cut-on filter (<350 nm, ASAHI SPECTRA) and reverted to the initial state using cutoff filter (>400 nm, ASAHI Techno Glass) with a xenon light source (MAX-303, ASAHI SPECTRA). Difference spectra were calculated by subtracting the spectrum recorded before irradiation from that obtained spectrum after irradiation. For FTIR spectroscopy, the Batho intermediate was accumulated at 77 K by irradiating the sample for 2 min through a <350 nm cut-on filter (ASAHI SPECTRA) and reverted to the initial state by irradiation for 2 min through a >400 nm cutoff filter (ASAHI Techno Glass) using the same xenon light source (MAX-303, ASAHI SPECTRA). The same procedure was applied to the E113Q mutant, with irradiation through a <350 nm cut-on filter for 3 min and a >400 nm cutoff filter for 3 min. Difference spectra were obtained by subtracting the spectrum recorded before irradiation from that recorded after irradiation. FTIR spectra were obtained by averaging n=61 and 53 for WT in H2O and D2O, respectively, in the low-frequency region, and n=196 and 97 for wild-type (WT) in D2O and D218O, respectively, in the high-frequency region. For the E113Q mutant, spectra were averaged from n=104 and 57 in H2O and D2O, respectively.

## Results

## UV-visible spectroscopy of MUV
Previous studies on the mouse UV-sensitive cone visual pigment (MUV) employed samples expressed and purified using COS1 cells [38]. Here, we established expression and purification in insect Sf9 cells. Figure 1a shows the UV-visible absorption spectrum of the purified pigment in DDM solution, measured at room temperature around 293 K in the solubilized state; the absorption maximum at 360 nm agrees well with prior reports [38,39]. The purified sample was reconstituted into phospholipids (PC) extracted from egg yolk, then hydrated with H2O and prepared as a film sample. The UV-visible spectrum was measured at 77 K (Figure 1b, black). After irradiation with a <350 nm cut-on filter using a xenon light source, the spectra (Figure 1b, red traces) showed a slight red shift with an extended shoulder toward ~400 nm. The sample was intermittently irradiated for 1, 2, and 3 min. Figure 1c (solid lines) shows the difference spectra obtained by subtracting the spectrum before illumination from those recorded after each irradiation period. The negative band at 343 nm corresponds to bleaching of the initial state, whereas the positive band at 408 nm represents absorption of the photoproduct. The spectral changes reached saturation after approximately 2 min of illumination, as seen in Figures 1b and 1c. Based on previous work [39], this positive band is attributable to the earliest photointermediate, the Batho state.
Many visible-light pigments exhibit photoreversibility (photochromism) at 77 K [13,29,30]: illumination of Batho intermediate with a second photon regenerates the initial-state. Consistently, illumination of MUV Batho with >400 nm light for 2 min produced a near mirror-image difference spectrum (Figure 1c, dotted). This result demonstrates that MUV also exhibits photochromism at 77 K.

## Low-temperature FTIR spectroscopy of MUV at 77 K
To compare the structural changes in the Batho intermediate of MUV with those of visible-light pigments, we next measured FTIR spectra under the same low-temperature conditions as in the UV-visible experiments. As shown in Figure 2, the spectra for Batho formation (solid) and the reversion to the initial state (dotted) are nearly mirror images of each other, although some baseline distortion is evident below 1300 cm–1. These data clearly demonstrate that MUV exhibits photochromism in the FTIR measurements.
Figure 3 compares light-minus-dark FTIR difference spectra of Batho versus initial state for MUV (top) and MB (bottom). Since IR absorption intensities depend on changes in dipole moment, retinal vibrational bands, particularly the C-C and C=C stretching modes, are weak or absent when the retinal Schiff base is deprotonated. Spectra are therefore often normalized to the retinal C-C stretching band near 1200 cm–1; however, this band was not clearly detected in MUV, so we compared MUV and MB by overall spectral features. Indeed, the ~1200 cm–1 C-C stretching band is much weaker in MUV than in MB. This observation is consistent with a deprotonated Schiff base in both the initial and Batho states of MUV. It also agrees with the UV-visible spectral results shown in Figure 1. A small 1228 (–)/1201 (+) cm–1 pair band is present and is tentatively assigned to a C-C stretching band associated with 11-cis to all-trans isomerization.
In the 1600–1500 cm–1 region, where C=C stretching bands typically appear, no peaks equivalent to the 1575 (–)/1559 (+) cm–1 pair observed in MB were detected in MUV. However, a negative band at 1590 cm–1 is a plausible candidate. Retinal C=C stretching frequencies are known to correlate with the absorption maxima in the UV-visible region [29]. To examine this, we plotted the inverse of the absorption maximum (x-axis) against the C=C stretching frequency (y-axis) for MUV, MB, bRh, MG, MR, and their mutant variants (Supplementary Figure S1a). As shown in the Supplementary Figure S1a, a clear negative correlation was obtained, supporting assignment of the 1590 cm–1 band to the C=C stretch in the initial state of MUV.
For the Batho intermediate, possible positive bands include 1572 cm–1 and 1557 cm–1. When the same correlation analysis between C=C stretching frequencies and absorption maxima was extended to include UV-sensitive MUV along with visible-light pigments (Supplementary Figure S1b), the 1572 cm–1 band followed the same negative correlation trend, supporting its assignment to the retinal C=C stretch of the Batho intermediate. In contrast, the 1557 cm–1 band deviated from this correlation and is therefore more likely to arise from protein vibrations, such as amino acid side-chain modes or N–H bending vibrations of α-helices (amide II), rather than from retinal.
In the 1000–800 cm–1 region, hydrogen-out-of-plane (HOOP) modes serve as indicators of retinal distortion. In MB, a negative band at 961 cm–1 (initial state, C11H=C12H HOOP) is converted upon isomerization into positive bands at 931 and 878/862 cm–1 [29]. These changes indicate significant retinal distortion in the Batho state. MUV also exhibits a negative band at 961 cm–1. However, prominent positive HOOP bands characteristic of Batho are not observed. Because bending modes maintain a dipole moment regardless of Schiff-base protonation, such bands should appear if the retinal were twisted. The absence of strong positive HOOP features therefore suggests that retinal distortion in MUV Batho is minimal. This, in turn, implies that MUV may rely more strongly on protein-environment assistance during isomerization than visible-light pigments.
Figure 4a highlights the amide-I and carboxylic C=O stretching region. In MB, a complex band at 1666 (+)/1658 (–)/1651 (+) cm–1 corresponds to α-helical amide-I vibrations. We previously reported that MB shows larger amide-I intensity changes upon Batho formation than MR, MG, or bovine rhodopsin (bRh) [29]. These findings indicate more extensive structural rearrangements in MB compared with other pigments. MUV also exhibits pronounced bands at 1663 (+)/1657 (+)/1649 (–) cm–1. The overall pattern of these amide-I complex bands differs markedly from that observed in MB, suggesting that, although both MUV and MB belong to the same SWS1 opsin subfamily, the structural response to retinal isomerization is substantially different between the two. It should be noted that MUV additionally shows a distinctive 1636 (–)/1627 (+) cm–1 pair. This feature is likely attributable to β-sheet amide-I C=O stretches and is absent in MB, making it a characteristic signature of MUV.
A distinctive feature of MUV is the presence of a 1719 (+)/1704 (–) cm–1 pair band (Figure 4a). This pair shifts slightly to 1713 (+)/1703 (–) cm–1 in D2O, consistent with assignment to a protonated carboxylic C=O stretch. The small magnitude of this isotope shift is compatible with limited H/D exchange around the C=O group, although we note that in some microbial rhodopsins, partial cancellation between the downshift caused by O-D formation and an upshift arising from deuteration of the associated hydrogen-bond network can also yield similarly small net shifts. Thus, while the present data do not allow us to definitively quantify the extent of H/D exchange, the weak isotope effect remains consistent with a relatively rigid hydrogen-bonding environment that is shielded from bulk solvent, suggesting a hydrophobic microenvironment within the retinal binding pocket. The most plausible origin of this signal is Glu113, the residue that serves as the counterion to the retinal Schiff base in other cone visual pigments. Evidence from Figures 1 and 3 indicates that the Schiff base is deprotonated in both the initial and Batho states of MUV. Under these conditions, Glu113 would be expected to remain protonated, and the observed pair band likely reflects its C=O stretching vibration. In contrast, MB shows no corresponding features in the 1800–1700 cm–1 region. This absence is consistent with a deprotonated counterion in MB.
To test this assignment, we measured FTIR spectra of the E113Q mutant of MUV, in which Glu113 was replaced by Gln (Figure 4b). As expected, the 1719 (+)/1704 (–) cm–1 pair band disappeared. Instead, new bands appeared in the 1700–1670 cm–1 region, consistent with a C=O stretch from Gln113. These results confirm that the 1719 (+)/1704 (–) cm–1 pair band originates from protonated Glu113. The negative 1704 cm–1 band, observed in the initial state, indicates a strong hydrogen bond. Upon Batho formation, this band shifts to 1719 cm–1, suggesting a weakening of the hydrogen bond. Because the Schiff base is deprotonated under these conditions, the hydrogen-bond donor to the C=O group of Glu113 is likely provided by nearby protein residues or internal water molecules. Although the E113Q substitution induces relatively large spectral changes, which may reflect additional structural perturbations, the disappearance of the 1719 (+)/1704 (–) cm–1 pair band in this mutant strongly supports its assignment to protonated Glu113.
Figure 5 shows the X-H stretching region, which includes O-H and N-H stretches of amino acids and water molecules. Under D2O conditions (red trace), some peaks remain in the spectrum. These signals are attributed to groups that do not undergo H/D exchange, such as buried water molecules or amino acid side chains located in hydrophobic environments.
In MUV, the 3593 (+)/3578 (–) cm–1 pair band disappears clearly upon D2O substitution (see Figure 6 for details). In contrast, the 3440 (+)/3409 (–) cm–1 pair remains visible. In MB, a frequency-shifted but analogous pair at 3520 (+)/3475 (–) cm–1 has been assigned to the O-H stretch of Thr118 on TM3 [37]. Similar bands from Thr or Ser at position 118, along with interactions involving the Gly114 backbone, are also observed in bRh, MR, and MG [40,41]. These signals are known to be perturbed by retinal isomerization through changes in TM3 hydrogen bonding [42]. In MUV, residue 118 is Ser, as in MR and MG. If the 3440/3409 cm–1 pair originates from Ser118, the low frequencies suggest a particularly strong hydrogen bond. This may account for the distinct amide-I pattern observed in Figure 4a relative to MB.
Figure 6 presents the X-D stretching region, where O-D and N-D vibrations of water molecules and polar side chains are detected. Spectra recorded in D218O (blue trace) enable assignment of water O-D stretches by comparison with those in D2O (red trace). Peaks that shift between D2O and D218O (green tags) are therefore attributed to water molecules. Because of baseline distortion, the 2400–2300 cm–1 region did not yield reliable intensities in either D2O or D218O and is not considered further. In MUV, five negative and four positive water O-D bands were identified. This number is larger than in MB (four negative and three positive) but smaller than in MR (six negative and six positive), MG (seven negative and eight positive), or bRh (six negative and six positive) [28,43].
MB exhibits a broad complex at 2568 (–)/2537 (+)/2495 (–) cm–1 with larger half-widths than typical O-D bands, consistent with water clustering, that is, the presence of multiple hydrogen-bonded water molecules [29]. Such broad features were not observed in MUV. This finding suggests that water clusters are absent near the retinal in MUV. Instead, MUV shows a strikingly intense pair at 2673 (+)/2653 (–) cm–1. This pair likely originates from the same water molecules responsible for the 3593 (+)/3578 (–) cm–1 signals in the X-H stretching region of Figure 5 under H2O (black trace) conditions. In fact, applying a simple diatomic oscillator model gives a predicted frequency of 3674 (+)/3564 (–) cm–1 for the O-H stretch, derived from the observed O-D band at 2673 (+)/2653 (–) cm–1. This value is in close agreement with the experimental result. This finding strongly supports the conclusion that both signals arise from the same water molecules. A noteworthy feature is the ~20 cm–1 isotope shift observed upon substitution with 18O. Such an isotope shift corresponds to either weakly hydrogen-bonded or hydrogen-bond-free water O-D stretching vibrations [28]. Similarly, other O-D stretching bands observed at 2706 (+)/2684 (–), 2625 (–)/2604 (+), and 2583 (–)/2548 (+)/2502 (–) cm–1 appear in frequency regions characteristic of weak to moderate hydrogen-bond strengths and exhibit comparable isotope effects of approximately 20 cm–1. These spectral characteristics resemble those reported for MR and MG rather than for MB [29], suggesting that water molecules in MUV are sparsely distributed within the hydrophobic retinal-binding pocket.
Finally, as reported systematically for animal rhodopsins [44], no water bands were detected below 2300 cm–1 in MUV. This absence is consistent with the lack of very strongly hydrogen-bonded waters that are commonly observed in microbial rhodopsins [44].

## Discussion
In this study, we successfully captured the structural changes accompanying Batho formation in MUV using FTIR difference spectroscopy. Upon illumination at 77 K, the Batho intermediate showed an absorption maximum at 406 nm in the UV-visible difference spectrum. This wavelength suggests that, similar to the initial state, the retinal Schiff base remains deprotonated, consistent with previous reports [38]. FTIR difference spectra further supported this conclusion, as the intensities of retinal C-C and C=C stretching modes were weak. Most strikingly, C=O stretching bands from a protonated carboxylic acid were detected in both the initial and Batho states. Analysis of the E113Q mutant unambiguously assigned these bands to Glu113. These results together provide clear evidence that the Schiff base is deprotonated in both the initial and Batho states of MUV.
A distinctive feature is that the negative band in the initial state appears at 1704 cm–1, which is unusually low for protonated glutamate or aspartate C=O stretches. Such a low frequency indicates a very strong hydrogen bond. Possible hydrogen-bond donors to the Glu113 C=O group include internal water molecules and the O-H group of Ser90 on TM2. By analyzing the O-H and O-D stretching vibrations of internal water molecules in Figure 6, we found, as in other animal rhodopsins [44], that no water molecules form strongly hydrogen-bonded networks. This observation suggests that water is unlikely to serve as the hydrogen-bond donor to the C=O group of Glu113. Supplementary Figure S2 shows an AlphaFold2 [45] model of MUV. Notably, the distance between the O-H group of Ser90 on TM2 and an oxygen atom of Glu113 is 3.0 Å, which is compatible with a hydrogen bond. As discussed below, the residue at position 90 is known to contribute to lowering the Schiff base pKa in UV-sensitive cone pigments [8]. Thus, Ser90 is a plausible candidate for the donor forming the exceptionally strong hydrogen bond with the C=O group of Glu113.
In Figure 5, the MUV spectrum exhibits an H/D-insensitive pair at 3440 (+)/3409 (–) cm–1. In other cone pigments (MR, MG, and MB) and in rhodopsin, analogous bands appear 20–30 cm–1 higher in frequency and have been assigned to the O-H stretching vibration of Ser or Thr at position 118 on TM3. By analogy, we initially considered that the 3440/3409 cm–1 pair in MUV might arise from the corresponding Ser118 O-H stretch, although at somewhat lower frequencies. However, an alternative possibility is that this band instead originates from the O-H stretch of Ser90. In that case, the observed up-shift of the Glu113 C=O band upon Batho formation, that is, weakening of the hydrogen bond, would be naturally explained by a change in the Ser90-Glu113 hydrogen-bond interaction during the photoreaction.
In addition, MUV, like other cone pigments and animal rhodopsins, shows no evidence of water molecules forming strong hydrogen bonds. This finding implies that the nitrogen atom of the deprotonated Schiff base may directly form a hydrogen bond with the O-H group of protonated Glu113. Interestingly, in the AlphaFold2 model of MUV, one oxygen atom of Glu113 (the one hydrogen-bonded to Ser90) lies near Ser90, whereas the other oxygen atom is positioned farther away from the Schiff-base nitrogen, preventing direct hydrogen bonding in this static model. If Glu113 indeed forms a strong hydrogen bond with Ser90, the side chain of Glu113 could be pulled toward Ser90, allowing rotation around its C-C bond. Such a reorientation would enable the hydroxyl group of Glu113 to approach the Schiff-base nitrogen closely enough to form a direct hydrogen bond. Experimental verification of this interaction will be an important subject for future investigation.
In visible-light pigments, proton transfer from the Schiff base to Glu113 occurs during formation of the active Meta-II intermediate [13]. For example, in bovine rhodopsin, FTIR difference spectroscopy between Meta-II and the initial state revealed a band at 1713 cm–1, assigned to the protonated C=O stretch of Glu113 upon the formation of Meta-II [46]. Similarly, in our recent study of MB, Lumi formation was accompanied by proton transfer from the Schiff base to Glu113, producing a protonated C=O band at 1714 cm–1 [37,47]. Strikingly, the 1704 cm–1 band observed in MUV is significantly downshifted, clearly reflecting an exceptionally strong hydrogen bond. We therefore propose that this unusually strong hydrogen bond involving Glu113 plays a key role in stabilizing the deprotonated Schiff base, thereby maintaining a low pKa in MUV.
However, if the O-H group of Glu113 in MUV indeed forms a direct hydrogen bond with the nitrogen atom of the Schiff base, a critical question arises: why does proton transfer not occur upon Batho formation? Research on UV-sensitive pigments, whether animal or microbial, remains limited. Recently, we reported that the microbial TAT rhodopsin exists as an equilibrium mixture of UV- and visible-absorbing forms at neutral pH, in a roughly 1:1 ratio [48]. Interestingly, in the UV form, formation of the early K intermediate at 77 K is coupled to protonation of the Schiff base [49]. Although the identity of the proton donor remains unresolved, it does not appear to be the counterion, because no signals originated from a protonated carboxylic acid were observed [49]. Instead, our data suggests that nearby water molecules may serve as the proton source.
Moreover, recent quantum chemical calculations indicate that protonation of the Schiff base in microbial rhodopsins depends strongly on a hydrophilic environment [50]. Consistent with this, a newly identified microbial rhodopsin, ApuR, which functions as a light-driven anion channel, was recently reported to absorb in the 390–410 nm range [51]. It should be noted that substitution of nonpolar residues near the retinal Schiff base with polar amino acids shifted its absorption maximum to the visible region (450–480 nm), characterizing the importance of local hydrophilicity in determining protonation and spectral properties [51]. In light of these findings, inspection of the AlphaFold2 model of MUV (Supplementary Figure S2) reveals that Phe86 extends toward the Schiff base, while Val94 also occupies a nearby position, together creating a hydrophobic environment around the Schiff base. Supporting this interpretation, in the Siberian hamster UV-sensitive cone pigment (SHUV), Phe86 has been reported to disrupt the local hydrogen-bonding network near the Schiff base, thereby increasing hydrophobicity and lowering its pKa [22]. Similarly, in avian UV-sensitive cone pigments, Cys90 introduce nonpolar residues into the vicinity of the Schiff base, again exposing it to a hydrophobic environment [9,31].
By contrast, a recently reported high-resolution structure of bRh in the initial state revealed a different scenario [52]. One oxygen atom of the counterion Glu113 directly hydrogen-bonds with the Schiff base, and the same oxygen atom participates in a water-mediated hydrogen-bonding network with Thr94 and Met86 (Figure 7) [52]. In addition, our FTIR analyses of the Batho and subsequent Lumi intermediates of MG captured signals indicating that the glutamate at position 86 remains protonated throughout the photoreaction and undergoes changes in its hydrogen-bonding interactions [53]. In MUV, such hydrogen-bonding interactions cannot form, bringing Glu113 into closer proximity to the Schiff base and simultaneously excluding water molecules from its vicinity (Figure 7). This structural arrangement may account for the lower pKa of the Schiff base in MUV. Indeed, our FTIR analysis detected fewer water-derived vibrational signals in MUV compared with bRh and other cone pigments (Figure 6). While the number of observed peaks was lower in MB, MB also exhibited signals from water clusters [29], suggesting that the actual number of bound water molecules is smaller in MUV. Together, these results strongly support the conclusion that the Schiff base environment of MUV is more hydrophobic than in visible-light pigments.
Finally, an important question is to what extent the molecular basis of UV sensitivity revealed in our FTIR study of MUV, together with previous findings from SHUV [22] and avian UV-sensitive cone pigments [9,31], can be generalized. Figure 8 compares residues near the retinal among SWS1-type cone pigments across species. Positions 90 and 94 are largely conserved among SWS1 pigments, including both UV- and violet/blue-sensitive subtypes. Ser90 and Val94 are considered to maintain the overall structural framework of the TM2 region rather than directly determining UV sensitivity. An exception is found in avian UV-sensitive pigments, where Cys90 is conserved and is thought to contribute to Schiff base deprotonation. By contrast, Phe86 appears to be characteristic of UV-sensitive pigments, replacing more polar or smaller residues in violet- and blue-sensitive counterparts. This hydrophobic aromatic residue likely disrupts local hydrogen-bonding networks near the Schiff base, thereby stabilizing its deprotonated state. We therefore propose that Phe86 serves as a key molecular determinant of UV sensitivity, while Ser90 and Val94 provide a conserved structural scaffold common to the SWS1 family. This combination of residues likely lowers the Schiff base pKa and stabilizes its deprotonated state. Taken together, these observations suggest that such a molecular logic may represent a general principle underlying UV sensitivity in animal visual pigments.

## Conclusion
In this study, we applied FTIR spectroscopy to the mouse UV-sensitive cone pigment (MUV) and directly captured retinal structural changes and protein conformational rearrangements associated with cis-trans photoisomerization in an animal rhodopsin with a deprotonated Schiff base. Our analysis revealed that Glu113, the residue serving as the counterion to the retinal Schiff base in other cone visual pigments, forms an exceptionally strong hydrogen bond with its surrounding protein environment, most likely involving Ser90. In addition, comparison with other cone pigments and bovine rhodopsin (bRh) indicated that MUV contains fewer water molecules near the retinal. Taken together with amino acid sequence comparisons, these findings strongly suggest that a hydrophobic environment around the Schiff base lowers its pKa, stabilizes the deprotonated state, and thereby enables UV absorption.
This work represents the first structural spectroscopic analysis to uncover the molecular basis of UV absorption in UV-sensitive cone visual pigments. It extends our understanding of spectral tuning mechanisms beyond visible-light pigments, where most previous studies have been concentrated. Moreover, the molecular logic revealed here may provide a general explanation for UV sensitivity conserved across species, offering an important framework for future studies on the diversity of color vision and the evolution of photoreception.
