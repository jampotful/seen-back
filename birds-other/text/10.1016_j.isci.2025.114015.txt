Structure of European robin cryptochrome 1 reveals a role in circadian rhythms, not magnetoreception.
Wickramaratne AC, Rasmussen ES, Chelliah Y, Schuhmann F, Solov'yov IA, Mouritsen H, Green CB, Zoltowski BD, Takahashi JS. (2025). iScience. . doi:10.1016/j.isci.2025.114015. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Structure of European robin cryptochrome 1 reveals a role in circadian rhythms, not magnetoreception

## Abstract
SummaryCryptochromes (CRYs) play critical roles in regulating diverse physiological functions, including circadian rhythms and neuronal firing in light-dependent or -independent fashions. Structural studies of CRYs have highlighted common features, such as the photolyase homology region (PHR), but they also reveal key differences, particularly in the binding of the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor, leading to a long-standing debate, namely, whether Type I CRYs can function as FAD-dependent photosensors. This study solves the first crystal structure of a Type II CRY from a migratory songbird, namely, the European robin (Erithacus rubecula) CRY1. Structural, biochemical, and computational analyses of erCRY1 reveal that, unlike light-activated Type I and IV CRYs, Type II CRYs do not bind FAD and employ an open primary pocket for protein-protein interactions. These findings offer new insights into the structural basis of CRY function and suggest that migratory song-bird Type II CRYs function as circadian regulators, not magnetoreceptors.Subject areas: Zoology, Biochemistry, Evolutionary biology, Evolutionary ecology

## Introduction
Members of the cryptochrome/photolyase family (CPF) are ubiquitous in nature and regulate diverse aspects of physiology. Although structurally related, CPF members can broadly be divided into two main branches: photolyases, which repair DNA damage in a light-dependent manner, and cryptochromes (CRYs), which function as light-dependent or light-independent regulators of various physiological functions. These include circadian rhythms,1,2,3,4,5,6,7 neuronal firing rates,8,9 and plant growth and development.10,11,12,13,14 Recent research has also implicated CRYs as potential magnetosensors, allowing migratory organisms to sense and adapt to the Earth’s magnetic field.15,16,17,18,19,20,21
In animals, CRYs can be further categorized into three evolutionarily distinct classes: Type I, Type II, and Type IV CRYs. Type I CRYs (in insects) function as blue-light photoreceptors, affecting circadian rhythms,3,5,22 neuronal firing rates,8,9 and magnetosensing.23 Type II CRYs (in mammals) function in a light-independent manner, primarily regulating circadian rhythms,22 and have emerged as putative drug targets in various pathologies.24,25,26 Type IV CRYs (found in birds, fish, and turtles) have a largely unknown function, although they have been shown to be magnetically sensitive in vitro21 and are implicated in light-dependent magnetoreception in migratory songbirds.19,20,21,27
Due to their widespread role in critical physiological processes, CRYs have been intensely studied. While prior research suggested possible light-dependent functions for Type II CRYs in migratory birds,28,29 no biochemical or structural studies of Type II CRYs from migratory species existed. This gap has led to significant debates within the magnetosensor and circadian communities regarding FAD binding in Type II CRYs and their potential roles in magnetoreception. The present findings represent the first biochemical and structural studies of a Type II CRY from a migratory bird, resolving a highly debated question in the magnetosensor community: whether Type II (European robin CRY1 [erCRY1]) or Type IV (erCRY4) CRYs are involved in light-dependent magnetoreception.
Structural studies of the CPF family reveal that all members share a common fold composed of an N-terminal αβ domain and a C-terminal α-helical domain, known as the photolyase homology region (PHR). These structures differ by variable C-terminal tails (CTTs), which are unique to CRYs.18,30 Within the PHR, three structural elements mediate differences in photochemistry and functional activity.
A primary pocket within the α-helical domain, which binds flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) in an unusual U-shaped orientation.
A secondary pocket that binds antenna pigments in photolyases to extend the DNA repair action spectrum into the blue region.
A conserved set of three to four Trp/Tyr residues, termed the Trp-Triad/Tetrad, which mediate photoreduction of the bound FAD cofactor and allow interconversion between three flavin redox states. This interconversion alters signal transduction and induces conformational changes.18,21,31,32,33
Variations in these structural elements have been proposed to contribute to the distinct signal transduction mechanisms observed among different animal CRYs. To better understand how these structural differences relate to signaling in various CRY family members, we provide a brief overview of previous structural studies on Type I, II, and IV animal CRYs.
Type I animal CRYs are unique to invertebrates and function as blue-light photoreceptors. The best-understood role of Type I CRYs is in the circadian clock of Drosophila, where they regulate circadian timing through blue-light-induced degradation of CRY and Timeless (TIM) proteins.2,34 In Drosophila, TIM is part of the negative arm of the TTFL. Type I CRYs purify with FAD bound in the oxidized state, which interconverts between a ground-state oxidized FAD and an anionic semiquinone (FAD•-) signaling state.35 Structural and functional studies confirm that FAD binds to the primary pocket, with adjacent loops accommodating the CTT in a cleft similar to the DNA-binding cleft of photolyases (Figure 1A).31,39 Upon blue-light-induced photoreduction to FAD•-, alterations in H-bond interactions result in the release of the CTT from the DNA-binding-like cleft, exposing a protein-protein interaction surface that engages TIM.34,40,41 Although Type I CRYs contain a secondary pocket within the N-terminal αβ domain, they lack binding capability for antenna pigments and their function remains unclear.31

Type IV CRYs are primarily found in birds, fish, and turtles, and their function has largely remained unknown due to limited genetic studies in these species. Recently, however, they have garnered significant attention as potential magnetosensors in migratory organisms.19,20,21,27,42,43,44,45,46 Type IV CRYs share many structural features with Type I CRYs and function as blue-light photoreceptors.21,42,47 They purify with FAD bound to the primary pocket in the oxidized state. Photochemical excitation of FAD allows interconversion between all three flavin redox states (FAD, neutral semiquinone FADH⋅, and hydroquinone FADH−), coupled to conformational changes within the CTT.42,43 Although no full-length structures of Type IV CRYs exist, biochemical studies suggest that, in contrast to Type I CRYs, light induces folding of the CTT toward the PHR rather than dissociation.32,42 Like Type I CRYs, Type IV CRYs retain a secondary pocket that does not bind antenna pigments.
Type II CRYs retain a similar PHR fold but deviate significantly in the structure and function of the primary and secondary pockets. Unlike Type I and Type IV CRYs, Type II CRYs do not purify with bound FAD. Structural studies reveal that both pockets in Type II CRYs are empty.37,38,48 The primary and secondary pockets are involved in important protein-protein interactions related to clock function (Figure 1B). These include the following:
The CRY-interacting domain of Period (PER), which interacts extensively with a loop critical for FAD binding in Type I CRYs, as well as with the secondary pocket.38
The CRY:CLOCK complex, which requires insertion of a Trp residue from CLOCK into the secondary pocket of CRY.49,50
CRY degradation, mediated by FBXL3, which interacts with CRY by inserting a Trp residue into the empty FAD-binding pocket.37
Although full-length structures of Type II CRYs are not available, extensive genetic, biochemical, and biophysical studies suggest that CTTs likely interact with the periphery of both the primary and secondary pockets. Notably, variations within the CTT of Type II CRY isoforms have enabled the design of selective regulators targeting these pockets.51,52,53,54
Despite the absence of FAD binding in purified Type II CRYs, several experimental observations suggest that FAD may play a role in Type II CRY signaling.
Electron spin resonance (ESR) studies indicate an increase in FADH° generation in insect cells overexpressing a Type II CRY from humans.55
Exogenous FAD can influence Type II CRY signaling in mammalian cells.56
Light-dependent magnetic field effects suggest a role for Type II CRYs in magnetosensing.28,29,57,58
Magnetic fields affect reactive oxygen species generation in a Type II CRY-dependent manner.59
Immunoassays suggest light-dependent conformational changes in Type II CRYs in bird retinas.60,61
Given these observations and the lack of biochemical and structural characterization of Type II CRYs from migratory birds, we conducted extensive studies on a Type II CRY from Erithacus rubecula (European Robin) to directly examine the role of FAD in Type II CRY signaling and to resolve a long-standing debate in the magnetosensor community regarding the identity of the most probable light-dependent magnetosensor in migratory birds.

## Results

## Structural characterization of erCRY1b
The E. rubecula genome harbors orthologs analogous to two isoforms of mammalian Type II CRYs (termed CRY1 and CRY2). Prior examination of erCRY transcripts revealed that two primary isoforms of erCRY1 are produced and have been implicated as possible magnetosensors for seasonal migration (erCRY1a61,62,63,64 and erCRY1b65,66). At the sequence level, erCRY1a and 1b are identical within the PHR region and exhibit strong similarity to mammalian Type II CRYs, with 96% identity to human CRY1 and 81% identity to human CRY2 across the primary PHR region (Figure S1). Despite the high degree of identity with human CRYs, erCRY1a and b have been identified as putative FAD-binding photosensors capable of mediating seasonal migration.61,63,64 To elucidate whether there were any structural differences that may account for FAD binding and photoactivity, we obtained a high-resolution crystal structure of the PHR domain of erCRY1, which is identical at the amino acid level for erCRY1a/1b (Figures 2A and S1; Table S1 for refinement statistics). The missing C-terminal tail has a length of 98 amino acids.

Overall, the erCRY1 PHR structure is consistent with other CRY proteins, with a conserved N-terminal α/β domain and C-terminal α-helical domain (Figure 2A). Consistent with the high degree of identity to mammalian Type II CRYs, the structure of the erCRY1 PHR domain demonstrates structural elements consistent with Type II CRY function. First, erCRY1b does not purify with bound FAD and no density for an FAD cofactor is observed in the crystal structure. Second, two structural loops important for FAD binding in Type I and Type IV CRYs, termed the phosphate-binding motif and protrusion motif, adopt an open conformation inconsistent with FAD binding (Figure 2B). Third, the secondary pocket is empty and the entirety of the Ser-loop important for gating PER and CLOCK interactions is structurally analogous to Type II CRYs, suggesting a role in CLOCK binding and regulation of circadian rhythms (Figure 2C). Consistent with both aspects, ITC analysis of full-length erCRY1a and erCRY1b does not demonstrate affinity for FAD within physiologically relevant ranges. Furthermore, erCRY1a/1b can rescue circadian rhythms in CRY−/− cells consistent with a role in non-light-dependent regulation of circadian rhythms (Figures 2D and 2E). Despite the high degree of conservation between erCRY1a/b and mammalian Type II CRYs at both the sequence and structural levels, there are several notable differences within flexible loops on the periphery of the primary pocket, which may have implications for biological function.

## Primary pocket
Type II CRYs differ from photoactive Type I and Type IV animal CRYs in that they adopt an open conformation that is believed to result in the lack of FAD binding in Type II CRYs.37,67 Instead, the primary pocket functions as a protein-protein interaction motif that impacts interactions with PER and FBXL3, where CRY-PER interactions stabilize CRY1/2 in vivo and compete with FBXL3 for CRY1/2 binding in vitro.37,68 Competing interactions between CRY-PER and CRY-FBXL3 as well as development of small molecule regulators of CRY function involve three structural elements highly conserved in Type II CRYs: (1) salt bridges between positively charged Arg501/Lys503 in the mouse CRY2 (mmCRY2) C terminus and Asp1167 and Asp1206 in the mouse PER2 (mmPER2) CBD48,69 (Figures 1C and S2), (2) hydrophobic interactions involving Ile505/Tyr506 within the mmCRY2 C terminus and the mPER2 CBD38 (Figures 1C and S2), and (3) a lid loop that interacts with FAD in Type I/IV CRYs and gates isoform-selective small molecule regulators of CRY function and CRY-PER interactions.31,38,42,54 The lid loop is often partially disordered in crystal structures but houses two elements important for CRY function. These include a CCCH-type intermolecular zinc finger that serves as a “molecular clasp” to stabilize a flexible region of the PER-CRY interaction, and is involved in interchangeable disulfide bonds,70 and a Trp residue (Trp399 in mmCRY1; Trp417 in mmCRY2) that is argued to adopt isoform-specific rotamers that allow for the design of compounds selective to CRY1/252,54 (Figure 3). Although structural elements within the CRY C-terminal helix that are important for PER and FBXL3 binding are conserved in the structure of erCRY1a/b PHR (Figures 1C and S2), there are unexpected differences within the flexible lid loop region.

The primary differences center on the conformation of the lid loop and the conformation of the “gateway” Trp residue (CRY1:Trp399, CRY2:Trp 417) that is believed to gate selectivity of isoform-selective regulators of Type II CRYs. Specifically, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and crystallographic data indicate that CRY1 proteins adopt an “out” conformation, whereas CRY2 proteins adopt an “in” conformation that can be exploited to selectively target CRY isoforms.52,54 Despite erCRY1 being 96% identical to human CRY1 (91% identical to mouse CRY1), erCRY1 demonstrates an “in” conformation of the gateway residue (Figure 3A). Adoption of the “in” conformation is coupled to Trp292 moving further in to make stronger contacts with Phe256. The unexpected orientation of Trp399 highlights the subtle effect of residue variation on loop dynamics that can impact small molecule binding within the primary pocket.

## Computational analysis of FAD binding
To better understand factors that differentiate FAD binding in Type I, II, and IV CRYs, we investigated the binding affinities of FAD using steered MD and free energy perturbation (FEP). Steered MD allows us to slowly pull the FAD co-factor from the binding pocket and determine the maximal force required to remove FAD and to indirectly compare the differences in the binding pocket geometry between the CRY types. The FEP tells us the binding free energy of FAD, which together with the pulling should reveal the affinity of FAD in the CRY types.
We completed the constant velocity pulling simulations for four members of the CRY family, Type IV FAD-binding CRYs (erCRY4 and clCRY4) and Type II CRYs (mCRY1 and erCRY1a). Of these, crystal structures are available for all but erCRY4. For the erCRY4 studies, a homology model that was previously built and equilibrated was used.20 Although we would ideally like to also consider differences in a Type I CRY (dmCRY), dmCRY was excluded, since the structure includes the C-terminal end of the protein, which covers the binding pocket, and therefore the comparison with the other proteins would be inconsistent. The binding free energy simulations were completed for all five members, including dmCRY.
Figure 4 shows the results of the steered MD averaged over 10 independent simulations (Figure 4A) and the FEP (Figure 4B). The Type II CRYs (mmCRY1 and erCRY1a) have similar pulling force profiles requiring a maximal force of around 500–1,800 pN to remove FAD, which is consistent with the binding free energy that is −20 kcal/mol for mmCRY1 and −21 kcal/mol for erCRY1a. The low force required to remove FAD likely relates to the geometry of the binding pocket being in a more open conformation, allowing FAD to easily enter and exit the pocket. The force required to remove FAD from the Type II CRYs is considerably lower than the FAD-binding CRYs (clCRY4 and erCRY4a), which exhibit a force over 1.5× higher (∼3,000 pN). Furthermore, the Type IV CRYs exhibit a similar binding free energy of around −28 kcal/mol and −31 kcal/mol for erCRY4a and clCRY4, respectively. Type I CRY also shows a stronger binding free energy at around −35 kcal/mol (Figure 4B).

To further highlight the difference between Types I, II, and IV CRYs, we investigated the conservation of amino acids important for protein-protein interaction with the known circadian CRY interaction partners CLOCK, FBXL3, and PER (Figure 1). Type I and II CRYs share the same amino acids required for interaction with CLOCK, PER, and FBXL3, while Type IV CRYs have R to M substitution at the CLOCK-binding site, R to D and R to L substitutions at two important PER-binding sites, as well as L to R at the FBXL3-binding site (Figure 1C), indicating that Type IV CRYs are not involved in the circadian transcription-translation feedback loop.37,38 This is also supported by the lack of a circadian oscillation in the expression profile of erCRY4a over a 24-h period.20,71,72
In this manner, computational methods indicate that FAD binding is substantially weaker in Type II CRYs, both in terms of binding free energy and pocket availability. These results are consistent with the lack of FAD binding in purified proteins, the lack of any FAD binding in ITC analysis, and an FAD-independent signaling mechanism that relies on employing empty cofactor-binding pockets as a protein-protein interaction motif to engage CLOCK, PER, and FBXL3.
These results confirm that the primary pocket is functionally different within the different types of CRY and is designed to optimally bind FAD in the known photoreceptors Type I, Type IV, and plant CRYs but exhibits low affinity in the Type II CRYs, which employ an empty FAD-binding pocket to mediate protein-protein interactions in the mammalian circadian clock.

## Discussion
CRYs were initially discovered as blue-light photoreceptors in plants that employed FAD as a chromophore to regulate diverse aspects of plant physiology.10,13 Following the discovery of CRYs in plants, CRY homologs were quickly discovered in diverse species including animals.4,47 Due to the homology to photolyases and plant CRYs, animal CRY proteins were presumed to also be photoreceptors. Indeed, a definitive role in photoreception was relatively quickly identified in Drosophila CRY (dmCRY); however, distinct blue-light-regulated functions in mammals remained enigmatic. Initial studies of blue-light regulation of circadian rhythms and acute induction of PER in CRY knockout mice suggested that CRYs may function in light-dependent roles in the mammalian circadian clock,73,74 whereas subsequent studies revealed that mammalian CRYs functioned as light-independent repressors of CLOCK:BMAL-mediated transcription.6,7
Despite verified roles of mammalian CRYs as light-independent repressors of circadian transcription, ruling out FAD binding and light-dependent functions in mammals remained difficult, largely due to difficulties identifying a blue-light circadian photoreceptor in mammals and difficulties in expressing and purifying mammalian CRYs in high yields. Further, initial efforts to purify small amounts of CRY from mammalian tissue demonstrated fluorescent spectra that could indicate flavin or folate binding75 and ESR studies of insect cells overexpressing human CRYs indicated the presence of reduced flavins following blue-light irradiation.55 These studies and the strong homology to photoresponsive CRYs in diverse organisms led to general conclusions that mammalian CRYs likely bound FAD with an unknown function. These conclusions were bolstered by a subsequent study, where human CRY1 was purified in high yield in a yeast overexpression system with a bound non-photoactive FAD anionic semiquinone.76 Furthermore, studies of magnetoreception in birds and insects had identified CRY as the putative light-dependent magnetoreceptor, where mammalian-type CRYs demonstrated light-dependent magnetic field effects,28,29 which has now been disputed,57,58 and several studies reported purifying light-activated mammalian-like CRYs from birds.77,78
The culmination of these studies supported a role of a possible light-dependent mechanism in magnetoreception in migratory birds63,64; however, several studies refuted a role of FAD in mammalian CRY signaling. First, melanopsin was discovered as the blue-light photoreceptor regulating circadian rhythms in mammals,79,80,81 providing a CRY-independent explanation for blue-light sensing. Second, crystal structures of mammalian CRYs indicated that they purify without bound FAD, and bind FAD only at non-physiological levels.37 Further, structural studies indicated that the empty FAD-binding pocket functions as a protein-protein interaction site to target CRY for degradation via FBXL3.37,48 In addition, follow-up studies revealed that the presumed purification of FAD-bound mammalian CRYs from yeast and other sources likely resulted from contaminant proteins (e.g., alcohol oxygenase).67,82 Third, two additional experimental lines of evidence have emerged over the past 10 year that refute a role of FAD in mammalian CRY function. Namely, efforts to identify small molecule regulators of CRY have identified the FAD-binding pocket as a druggable site, where small molecules can bind to the empty FAD-binding pocket and block FBXL3-targeted degradation.24,25,83 Further, FAD can function as a competitor of FBXL3 binding to the primary pocket, with some effect at physiological levels of 10 μm and stronger effects only at concentrations exceeding physiological levels.56 Fourth, independent studies refuted a light-dependent expression of CRY1a in the retina of several night-migratory and non-migratory bird species,61 which had been the main argument for an involvement of CRY1 in avian magnetoreception. The combined data indicate that in its functional state, the FAD-binding pocket is empty and in normal cellular contexts, mammalian CRYs are not FAD-binding proteins.
Despite strong evidence that mammalian CRYs do not bind FAD, a role of FAD binding in migratory birds and in light-dependent magnetoreceptions in bird CRYs remained. To address this open question, we purified a mammalian-like CRY from European robin and solved the crystal structure of the isolated PHR domain. Both the full-length and isolated PHR domains purified without bound FAD, and ITC analysis indicated that FAD does not bind at physiological concentrations. Moreover, crystal structures of the isolated PHR domain indicate that the primary pocket adopts an open conformation inconsistent with FAD binding. Although these results alone strongly refute a role of FAD in erCRY1 signaling, we conducted rigorous computational studies to identify functional differences within the primary pocket that may differentiate FAD binding in photoresponsive (Type I and IV) and non-photoresponsive (Type II) CRYs. Both steered MD and free energy calculations of FAD binding indicated that Type II CRYs do not bind FAD with appreciable affinities, in part due to a difference in H bonding within the active site. It is worth noting that the missing C-terminal tail of the protein structures could influence the FAD binding mechanics, particularly when it comes to the lid loop, phosphate loop, and regions close to the binding pocket. The combined studies strongly indicate that mammalian-type CRYs do not function as FAD-binding photoreceptors in migratory birds but rather are involved in light-independent functions within the circadian clock akin to their mammalian counterparts, where an empty FAD-binding pocket is essential for function. As a result, CRY4 proteins that have been demonstrated to bind FAD and to be magnetically sensitive according to a radical pair mechanism remain the most viable candidates for a CRY-based magnetosensor.21,84

## Limitations of the study
The sex of the Cry1−/−/Cry2−/− mouse embryonic fibroblasts used in the bioluminescence rescue assays is unknown, which could potentially influence the outcome of the results. Having a positive control for the ITC binding experiment, using a CRY known to bind FAD (i.e., Type I CRYs) would have been ideal. However, type I CRYs have been shown to require FAD for proper folding.85 The inability of these proteins to adopt the native folded state in the absence of FAD makes conducting ITC experiments difficult and intractable.
Carrying out FEP with a large, flexible ligand with multiple charged groups such as FAD is very challenging. The exact binding affinities may not correspond to the absolute binding affinities, but taken in concert with the steered MD and experimental results, they paint a clear picture of relative binding affinity between the types of CRY’s studied here.

## Cell lines
Cry1−/−/Cry2−/− mouse embryonic fibroblasts were generated and generously gifted to us by Andrew Liu and Hiroki Ueda.92 Cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1× Pen/Strep antibiotics at 37°C under 5% CO2.
Sf9 insect cells were purchased from Gibco (cat# 11496015) were cultured in sf900 II SFM culture medium from Invitrogen (cat #10902096) and cells were cultured at 27°C.

## Method details

## Protein expression and purification
European robin Cry1b cDNA was obtained from Henrik Mouritsen. A fragment containing residues 1–510 was amplified using PCR and cloned into pFastBac HTb using BamHI/XbaI restriction sites (for crystallization). Full length erCry1a and erCry1b (used in ITC) were cloned and purified using the same protocol.
erCRY1b protein was expressed in Sf9 cells in the dark. Frozen pellets were lysed by sonication in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CHAPS, and protease inhibitors (Thermo, catalog no A32965). Clarified cell lysate was applied to a Ni-NTA agarose column (Qiagen, catalog no 1018244) and equilibrated with lysis buffer. Bound protein was eluted with 50 mM Tris 7.8, 300 mM NaCl, and 10% glycerol. Protein was desalted into 20 mM HEPES 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, and 2 mM DTT. The His tag was removed by treatment with TEV protease overnight at 4°C. Protein was further purified on an Ion Exchange column with a gradient buffer containing 750 mM NaCl. A final purification step using a Superdex 200 size exclusion column equilibrated in 20 mM HEPES 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 2 mM DTT was employed prior to crystallization or characterization.

## Crystallization and structure determination
erCRY1b was crystallized using the hanging drop method. Crystals were obtained at 21°C in the dark from a 1:1 mixture of protein (10 mg/mL) and reservoir solution consisting of 8–10% PEG 8000 and 0.1 M HEPES 7.5. Crystals reached maximum size after 2–3 days and were harvested and transferred into a cryoprotectant solution containing 10% PEG 8000, 0.1 M HEPES 7.5, 2 mM DTT and either 30% PEG400 or 30% Ethylene glycol.
Diffraction data were collected at the Advanced Photon Source 24-ID-E Northeastern Collaborative Access Team (NE-CAT) beamline. Data were collected at 100 K. Data were scaled and reduced with the XDS software suite89 and the initial model structures were obtained using molecular replacement in PHASER88 with mCRY1 (4k0r) as a search model. The final structure was generated through iterative rebuild cycles in Coot87 and refinement with Phenix.86

## Real-time bioluminescence rescue assays
A total of 4 × 105 Cry1−/−/Cry2−/− mouse embryonic fibroblasts were plated in 35-mm tissue culture dishes and transfected the same day with 4 μg of a luciferase reporter [pGL3-P(Per2)-Luc] and 150 ng of a cryptochrome rescue vector [pMU2-P(Cry1)-(intron336)-Cry-Myc] expressing mCry1, erCRY1a, or erCry1b using FuGENE 6 (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 72 h after transfection, the cells were synchronized by exchanging the growth medium for growth medium supplemented with 0.1 μM dexamethasone and then were returned to the incubator for 2 h. The medium was then replaced by LumiCycle recording medium (Prepared from powdered DMEM without phenol red containing 4.5 g/L glucose and supplemented with 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.20, 100 μM luciferin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.035% sodium bicarbonate, 2% FBS, 1× Pen/Strep antibiotics, and 2 mM L-glutamine).
The plates were sealed with vacuum grease and cover glass and transferred to the LumiCycle. Bioluminescence monitoring was performed using a LumiCycle to record from each dish continuously for 70 s every 10 min using a photomultiplier tube at 37°C. Rescue results were processed using the LumiCycle Analysis software package. The first 24 h of recording was discarded. Experiments were conducted in triplicate. Means are represented as solid lines and errors (SEM) are represented as shaded colors.

## Molecular dynamics
The structures for dmCRY (PDB ID: 4GU539), mmCRY1 (PDB ID: 7D0M54), clCRY4 (PDB ID: 6PU042) and erCRY1 (PDB ID: 9DC0) were prepared for simulations in the same way. Topology files for use with NAMD were created using psfgen in the VMD software package93 and missing hydrogen atoms were added. The proteins were solvated in a box of TIP3 water with a padding distance of 15 Å and any charges were neutralized with NaCl ions. Similarly, topologies for use with GROMACS94 were created using the pdb2gmx command and the resulting structures were solvated in a cubic box with ions added to neutralize the charges. The structures of mmCRY1 and erCRY1 do not have FAD bound, so the structures were overlaid with the structure of dmCRY and the FAD was copied into the pocket of mmCRY and erCRY1, before the equilibration simulations were carried out as described below. The structures and production simulations for erCRY4a were taken from earlier studies (20).
Molecular dynamics simulations in NAMD were carried out using the CHARMM36 force field for proteins,95 along with an earlier parameterization for FAD successfully employed in several MD studies of Cryptochrome.20,96 A time step of 2 fs was used to integrate the equations of motion, and the temperature was controlled at 310 K using the Langevin thermostat. Similarly, the pressure was held at 1 atm with the Langevin Barostat.97 The ShakeH algorithm98 was used to keep bonds involving hydrogen atoms at fixed lengths. Periodic boundary conditions were adopted in all MD simulations and the particle-mesh Ewald summation method was employed for evaluating Coulomb forces.99 The van der Waals energy was calculated using a smooth cutoff of 12 Å with a switching distance of 10 Å.
The erCRY1a model was simulated in 4 steps. (i) Energy minimization for 10000 NAMD steps. (ii) NPT equilibration with protein backbone constraints for 5 ns. (iii) NPT equilibration with no constraints for 500 ns. (iv) NPT production run for 500 ns.
The dmCRY simulations included 4 steps. (i) Energy minimization for 10000 NAMD steps, followed by 5 ns of NPT simulation with protein constraints. (ii) NPT equilibration for 10 ns with backbone constraints. (iii) 20 ns NPT simulation without constraints. (iv) 200 ns of NPT production simulation.
The mmCRY1 structure was simulated in 4 steps. (i) Energy minimization for 10000 NAMD steps. (ii) NPT equilibration with protein backbone constraints for 5 ns. (iii) NPT equilibration with no constraints for 250 ns. (iv) NPT production run for 250 ns.
The clCRY4 was simulated in 4 steps. (i) Energy minimization for 10000 NAMD steps. (ii) NPT equilibration with protein backbone constraints for 5 ns. (iii) NPT equilibration with no constraints for 250 ns. (iv) NPT production run for 250 ns.
Steered molecular dynamics simulations (SMD) of FAD were carried out using constant velocity pulling, with FAD pulling velocity of 0.025 Å/ps. For each individual studied CRY protein, the SMD simulations were averaged over 10 simulations. The pulling direction in all simulations was set toward the center of the entrance to the binding pocket. To evaluate the FAD binding energy, the free energy perturbation (FEP) method was employed, following the workflow suggested in.100,101 The calculations were carried out using the GROMACS package94,102 with the Amber99SB-ILDN forcefield for the protein, and the GAFF2 parameterization for FAD.103,104 The interaction between FAD and the environment (protein, water, ions) was turned off in 210 steps; each step was equilibrated in four rounds. (i) 5000 minimization steps (ii) 5000 equilibration steps in the NVT ensemble with restraints on the protein backbone and the FAD (iii) 50000 equilibration steps in the NPT ensemble with backbone restraints (iv) 500000 production simulation steps in the NPT ensemble without any restraints. A time step of 2 fs was used with the stochastic leap-frog integrator, and PME was used to calculate the long-range electrostatic interactions. H-bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm.105 The pressure was controlled using the Parinello-Rahman barostat and the temperature was held using the Nosè-Hoover thermostat.106,107 To ensure that the FAD cofactor doesn’t leave the binding pocket when the interactions with the environment are turned off, artificial restraints were applied to keep the FAD cofactor in place, using one distance restraints, two angle restraints and three dihedral restraints as defined by Boresch et al.108

## Isothermal titration calorimetry
ITC experiments were carried out in a Malvern iTC200 calorimeter at 20°C. ErCRY1a and erCRY1b were equilibrated in Working Buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol). The molar concentrations of the proteins (erCRY1a = 55 μM, and erCRY1b = 64 μM) were obtained by spectrophotometry using extinction coefficients calculated from the primary structure of the protein using ProtParam.90 For the putative ligand, FAD in powder form was dissolved in Working Buffer, resulting in a 95 mM stock solution. Approximately 200 μL of the respective proteins were placed in the sample cell. FAD was diluted (using Working Buffer) to concentrations of 570 μM for the titration into erCRY1a and 665 μM for the titration into erCRY1b; about 40 μL of these solutions were introduced into the syringe prior to the respective experiments. A first injection volume of 0.5 μL was employed, followed by 20 injections of 1.9 μL each; all injection rates were 0.5 μL/s. The stir rate was 750 rpm, and the reference power was 5.0 μcal/s. The time between injections was 120 s (except the first erCRY1a titration, which had in interinjection time of 180 s), with a data-filtering period of 5 s. The high-gain mode of the calorimeter was used. Visible precipitation was observed in the material that was retrieved from the calorimetric cell after all titrations. To examine how much (if any) of the protein was lost, a retrieved sample was centrifuged at 14,500 rpm at room temperature for 10 min, and then a spectrophotometric spectrum was taken of the supernatant. The absorbance of FAD at 280 nm was removed by multiplying the value at 376 nm by 2.22 and subtracting the result from the protein reading at 280 nm. In both cases, control titrations of FAD into Working Buffer using the same instrumental parameters were undertaken. The data were integrated and analyzed, and illustrated using NITPIC and SEDPHAT, respectively.109,110,111

A point mutation of mmCRY1 R109 has been shown to disrupt CLOCK binding. Type IV CRYs feature an R to M substitution in this location. mmCRY2 forms salt bridges with PER through R469, R501, and K503. Type IV CRYs have R to D substitution in the first location and R to L in the second. FBXL3 binding to mmCRY2 is disrupted by a charge mutation at location L517, which is naturally occurring in Type IV CRYs. Figure uses PDB IDs 4F3l,364I6J,374U8H.38
