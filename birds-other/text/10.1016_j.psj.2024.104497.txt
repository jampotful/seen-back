Prenatal light exposure affects diurnal rhythms and visual development of the layer embryonic retina.

# Prenatal light exposure affects diurnal rhythms and visual development of the layer embryonic retina

## Abstract
It is believed that some wavelengths of light penetrate through eggshell and are perceived by avian embryo, and may consequently affect rhythm establishment and development. This research aimed to explore the influence of prenatal light exposure on the morphological alterations of retinal tissue, the expression of visual developmental signaling systems (TGF-β/Smad pathway), the expression of clock related genes (cClock, cBmal1, cBmal2, cAanat), and melatonin concentration in the chicken embryonic retina. Layer eggs (Jingfen No.6) were subjected to white light (5000K, WL) and green light (520 nm/515-525 nm, GL) with a 12L:12D photoperiod throughout the entire incubation period, in contrast to no light incubation (NL). The results showed that the thickness of retina and each retinal lamina of chicken embryo in WL at E20 was much thicker than that of chicken embryo in GL (P < 0.05). In contrary, the expression level of TGF-β1 mRNA and Smad2/3 protein in retina was dramatically downregulated in WL when compared to that in NL and GL (P < 0.01). Furthermore, the incubation light simultaneously significantly affected the diurnal rhythms of the chicken embryonic retina. The expression of three clock genes (cBmal1/2, cClock) and cAanat exhibited significant diurnal rhythms in GL (P < 0.05). Additionally, green light stimulation significantly enhanced melatonin secretion but did not show diurnal rhythm. However, cBmal1, cAanat, and melatonin expression exhibited diurnal rhythms (P < 0.01), while the others did not in WL. In NL, only cBmal1 exhibited diurnal rhythmicity (P < 0.01). In conclusion, providing light of different wavelengths during the incubation process of poultry can have varying effects on embryonic visual development and the establishment of diurnal rhythms. WL had an advantage to GL and NL on retina development and diurnal rhythm through significantly influencing the expression of genes related to visual developmental signaling pathways and clock genes. A well-developed retina in WL exposure chicken embryo may be beneficial for establishing a melatonin rhythm. Conversely, the established circadian rhythm could improve embryonic development.

## Introduction
Birds are oriented towards vision and a significant portion of their nervous system is devoted to receiving and processing visual information (Daniloff et al., 1986). Chickens, like most birds, heavily rely on vision for various behavioral activities, including avoiding danger and obtaining food, possessing a highly developed visual system (Wisely et al., 2017). It has been demonstrated that light can penetrate the eggshell and be recognized by the chicken embryo during the embryonic stage, with a subsequent effect on the physiological development of the embryo (Ozkan et al., 2012). The development of the chicken embryonic eye begins with the differentiation of the optic vesicle neurons on embryonic day 2 (E2) (Yalcin et al., 2022). By E14, when embryonic eye growth is completed, the opsins in the photoreceptor cells, which start to express (Bruhn and Cepko, 1996), are capable of responding to light of different wavelengths in the spectrum (Perez et al., 2019). The visual system of chicken embryos starts to function at E18 (Yalcin et al., 2022). The retina, a highly specialized neural tissue, plays a crucial role in converting light signals into electrical and chemical signals, making it essential for the visual function of organisms (Bruhn and Cepko, 1996). The effects of light on the growth, development, and behavioral responses of chickens before and after hatching are primarily mediated through the reception of light by photoreceptors in the retina (Kuenzel et al., 2015). Nevertheless, the precise mechanism by which prenatal light affects the development of chicken embryonic retina remains unknown.
The development of the chicken embryonic retina involves a process where the retinal neuroepithelial cells proliferate and differentiate into seven distinct types of neurons and neuroglia, forming a layered structure (Vergara and Canto-Soler, 2012) including inner limiting membrane (ILM), nerve fiber layer (NFL), ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), outer limiting membrane (OLM), photoreceptor layer (PRL) and retina pigment epithelium (RPE). Previous reviews on the development of the chicken embryonic retina have indicated the involvement of various growth factors, integrins, cell cycle proteins, transcription factors, and enzymes in the control of retinal development (Mey and Thanos, 2000). Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), a multifunctional cytokine, exerts various biological effects on the eye, including cell growth, proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, migration, inflammation, and neovascularization (Saika, 2006). Li et al. (2018) have developed an optogenetic system (optoTGFBRs) to regulate the signaling of TGF-β by modulating the light stimulation pattern, demonstrating that the expression of TGF-β is influenced by light stimulation. Rao et al. (2013) have shown that providing light stimulation to pregnant mice in the late gestational period promoted the regression of the hyaloid vasculature and the formation of retinal vasculature in newborn mice. Therefore, we hypothesized that prenatal light stimulation might further impact the early development retina for chick embryos by influencing the expression of TGF-β.
In addition to the differentiation and formation of retinal layered structures, the maturation of the endogenous circadian clock and the biosynthesis of melatonin can serve as advanced indicators of photoreceptor cells and overall physiological differentiation in the retina (Willbold et al., 2002). Melatonin produced in the retina primarily exerts its effects locally, extracting photoperiodic information from the light environment, delegating image processing to neural circuits, and playing a crucial role in controlling rod outer segment shedding, aggregation of melanin granules in the retinal pigment epithelium, and regulating the release of dopamine as a neurotransmitter in dopaminergic amacrine cells (Krausruppert and Lembeck, 1965; Besharse and Dunis, 1983). A study by Storch et al. (2007) on retinal transcriptional and inner retinal electrical responses to light in clock gene (cBmal1)-deficient mice have indicated that the intrinsic circadian clock of the retina regulates visual information processing in vivo (Storch et al., 2007). Light also plays a crucial role in regulating the biological clock, Di et al.'s research has shown that varying wavelengths of light exposure exert a certain influence on the amplitude and phase of circadian oscillations in zebrafish larvae (Di Rosa et al., 2015). The studies on the pineal gland (Ma et al., 2018), hypothalamus (Jiang et al., 2017), and retina (Cao et al., 2017) of broiler chickens have demonstrated that light of different wavelengths could potentially affect melatonin secretion and its rhythm by influencing clock genes and the enzyme responsible for melatonin synthesis.
In previous research, our team utilized different light stimuli during a 21-day incubation period: white light (380-780 nm), blue light (447.5-462.5 nm), and green light (515-535 nm) in a 12L:12D photoperiod (Tang et al., 2023). The findings indicated that specific lighting conditions had positive effects on chick embryo development and plasma melatonin secretion, particularly with green light. White light also showed potential benefits for eye development. This study was a further investigation at the molecular biology level based on previous research, aiming to determine how prenatal light influenced retinal morphogenesis and diurnal rhythm establishment by affecting the expression of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway involved in visual development, positive clock genes (cClock, cBmal1, cBmal2), and the clock-controlled gene (cAanat) responsible for melatonin synthesis.

## Materials and methods

## Ethics approval and consent to participate
The study protocol was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethical Committee of China Agricultural University (AW712022-5-1), and all experiments were conducted in strict adherence to the approved guidelines.

## Incubation and light protocol
White light (5000K, WL) and monochromatic green light (520 nm/515-525 nm, GL) were utilized as the light groups, while no light (NL) was the control group (Fig. 1). The illumination scheme comprised 12 h of continuous illumination and 12 h of continuous darkness (12L:12D) for the entire 21-day incubation period. During the light phase, high-intensity light was applied for 1 h (10:00-11:00 / 2000 ± 500 lux), while low-intensity light was applied for 11 h (6:00-10:00 and 11:00-18:00 / 200 ± 50 lux) (Tong et al., 2018). A total of 392 fertilized pink shell eggs (60.20 ± 0.24 g) (Jingfen No. 6, Beijing Huadu Yukou Poultry Industry Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) from same age of breeder flock were selected and randomly distributed to 4 groups. As shown in Fig. 2, the experiment was conducted in two incubators (OVA-Easy 380, Brinsea Products Ltd., UK): one with darkness/white light and the other with darkness/green light, each treatment group consisting of two replicate trays (49 fertilized eggs/replicate tray). The dark and light groups within one incubator were separated using blackout curtains and ensure that the light intensity of the eggs in the dark group was 0. Twelve light sensors (Grove-Luminance Sensor, Grove-Digital Light Sensor) were used to monitor the light intensity in each treatment group and ensure the real light intensity was in the setting range (Fig. 3). ## Sampling methods
Three alive embryos were selected randomly from each group on the 8th, 11th, 14th, and 20th days of incubation (E8, E11, E14, and E20). After euthanizing the embryos by cervical dislocation, the right eyes of the embryo were dissected and stored in a fixation solution (10010094, China National Pharmaceutical Group Corporation, Beijing, China) for the analysis of retinal morphology.
Another three alive embryos were selected randomly from each group on the 8th, 11th, 14th and 20th days of incubation. After euthanizing the embryos by cervical dislocation, the right eye of the embryo was dissected and added 0.75% saline solution. The retina was extracted from each eye sample and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for further analysis of the expression level of TGF-β mRNA and Smad2/3 protein.
On the 20th day of incubation, six live embryos from each group were collected every 4 h between CT0 and CT20 (CT/circadian time, CT0/6:00 AM) and sampled 6 times in total. After euthanizing the embryos by cervical dislocation, the retina was extracted from the right eye of each sample and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for further analysis of the expression levels of cClock, cBmal1, cBmal2, and cAanat mRNA, and melatonin level.
All sampling was conducted under dim light conditions to prevent the potential confounding effects of additional light exposure on the retinal parameters.

## Retinal morphology
The eye samples were dehydrated with ethanol, embedded in paraffin, sectioned continuously at a thickness of 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E, Beijing Huaying Biotechnology Technology Research Institute, Beijing, China). Sections with well-preserved retina and optic disc were selected, and tissue observation and description were performed using a 3D digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). Five random areas were selected from the central region of the retina, and the thickness of different retinal laminae was measured using ImageJ software by comparing it to the standard scale length in the image.

## The expression level of TGF-β1, Clock, Bmal1, Bmal2 and Aanat mRNA
The extraction of total RNA 1 ml of TRIzol Reagent (15596-026, Life Technologies, Bleiswijk, the Netherlands) was added to a multi-sample homogenizer (Tissuelyser-32, Shanghai Jingxin Industrial Development Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), which had been pre-chilled on ice. 100 mg of retinal tissue was added to the homogenizer and thoroughly homogenized. The mixture was then centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Chloroform was used for phase separation, and isopropanol was added to the supernatant for RNA precipitation. The precipitate was washed with 75% ethanol, centrifuged again, and after removing the liquid, 20 μL of HyPure TMMolecular Biology Grade Water was added to dissolve the RNA. The mixture was then incubated at 55°C for 5 min.
Reverse transcription 2 μg total RNA from each sample was taken with the component of a 12 μL transcription system including oligo(dT)18 primer, nuclease-free water to be incubated for 5 min at 70°C. Then the component was mixed with 4 μL 5 × buffer, 1 μL RNA inhibitor, 2 μL 10 mM dNTPs, and 1 μL RT for 1 h at 42°C and then 5 min at 80°C.

## The expression level of Smad2/3 protein
The retina proteins were extracted using Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay buffer (RIPA, Beijing Huaying Biological Technology Research Institute, Beijing, China) supplemented with proteinase inhibitors (11697498001, F. Hoffmann-La Roche, Ltd., Basel, Switzerland) and Phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, 0754, Amresco, Inc, Solon, Ohio, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Beijing Huaying Biological Technology Research Institute, Beijing, China). Protein samples (105 μg) were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto Nitrocellulose membranes (NC, Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (BSA-TBST, 0332, Amresco, Inc, Solon, Ohio, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. During the incubation with the primary antibody and secondary antibody, the membrane was washed 11 times with TBST. The antibodies used included an anti-GAPDH antibody (BSM-33033M), anti-Smad2 antibody (BS-0718R), anti-Smad3 antibody (BS-3484R), anti-rabbit IgG (BS-0295G), and anti-mouse IgG (BS-0296G), all purchased from Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Equal volumes of Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) A and B reagents (WBKLS0500, Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, the USA) were mixed in a centrifuge tube. The protein side of the nitrocellulose membrane was placed in contact with the mixture for 1-2 min, followed by the removal of excess solution and careful packaging before exposure in a dark box. Image J software was used to analyze the band images and determine the grayscale values of the protein bands. The relative protein content of the target proteins Smad2 and Smad3 was calculated as the protein band grayscale value divided by the grayscale value of the internal reference protein GAPDH.

## Melatonin level
The retinal melatonin concentration of 3 samples in each sampling time of each group was detected using an ELISA kit (HY-D0042, Beijing Huaying Biological Technology Research Institute, Beijing, China). Following melatonin content detection, a standard curve was generated using the measured absorbance values. The regression equation was calculated based on the standard curve, and the absorbance values of the test samples were plugged into the equation to calculate the retinal melatonin concentration. The retinal melatonin concentration was expressed as the ratio of melatonin concentration to the corresponding total protein content, measured in pg/mg·protein.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 25.0, and all data were presented as mean±SEM. All variables were tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. No significant impact of incubator on dark groups, therefore samples of two dark groups were merged for following analysis.
A general linear model was used to analyze the main effects of different light treatments during the incubation period and sampling time on the thickness of the retina and distinct retinal laminae, the expression level of TGF-β mRNA and Smad2/3 protein:Yij=μ+Ti+Lj+TLij+ε
Where Yij is the thickness of the retina and distinct retinal laminae, TGF-β mRNA expression level, and Smad protein expression level, μ is the overall mean, Ti is the sampling time (i = E8, E11, E14, E20), Lj is the fixed effect of light treatment (j = the white light, green light or no light), TLij is the interaction effect, and ε is error effect. The interaction was removed from the original model when it was not significant. When the effect showed statistical significance (P < 0.05), further multiple comparisons were conducted using the LSD test.
Cosine function fitting was performed using MATLAB to determine the circadian rhythm of gene expression levels for cClock, cBmal1, cBmal2, and cAanat, and melatonin concentration in the retina within each treatment group. The single-modality cosine regression equation y = A + (B × cos (2π(x-C)/24)) was utilized for cosine regression analysis. Here, the rhythmic parameters A, B, and C represent the median, amplitude, and peak phase, respectively. The mean of mRNA expression levels for cClock, cBmal1, cBmal2, and cAanat, and melatonin concentration in the retina of different groups were further analyzed using ANOVA. Subsequently, in cases of statistical significance (P < 0.05), the means were compared through the LSD test.

## Results

## Retinal morphology and thickness
The chick embryonic retinal laminae stained with H&E from different light treatment groups are shown in Fig. 4. The NFL and GCL of the chick embryonic retina were observable clearly, while the IPL appeared indistinct, and the remaining layers could not be easily distinguished on E8. The NFL, GCL, IPL, INL, OPL, and ONL across all treatment groups could be clearly distinguished on E11, while the PRL was faintly visible. Moreover, the sub-layers appeared in the IPL, and the demarcation lines between sub-layers in the WL group were more distinct. Until E16 the retinal stratification morphology was stable, and PRL with neatly arranged cone and rod cells was observed clearly. The IPL exhibited four sublayers, and the OLM between the ONL and PRL was distinct. ## Expression levels of TGF-β mRNA and Smad2/3 protein
Variance analysis indicated that there was no effect of sampling time on retinal TGF-β1 mRNA (P = 0.40). However, light treatment had a significant impact on TGF-β mRNA (P < 0.05). As shown in Fig. 5B, the expression level of TGF-β1 mRNA in chicken embryo retinas under WL was significantly lower than the rest of the light treatments (P < 0.05). Both the expression level at a particular sampling time point and the average expression level throughout the embryonic period indicated that white light exposure significantly down-regulated TGF-β1 mRNA expression in chick embryonic retina. The results showed that the chick embryo retina had a significant circadian rhythm of cBmal1, cAanat, and melatonin in WL (P < 0.01), and their mRNA levels or concentration decreased in the light phase and then raised in the dark phase which reached the peak at CT18, CT4 and CT22, respectively. While cBmal1, cBmal2, cClock and cAanat exhibited circadian rhythmicity in GL (P < 0.05), peaking around CT16, CT12, CT17 and CT16, respectively. However, chick embryos incubated under NL only had a circadian rhythm of cBmal1 (P < 0.01) which peaked at CT16, with a mean value of 3.639±0.566 which was similar to that of cBmal1 in GL (4.694±0.505), and both were significantly lower than that of cBmal1 in WL (11.436±1.602).
In terms of mean mRNA expression levels, cBmal2 and cAanat in the GL (7.318±0.487, 2.748±0.287) were significantly higher than that in the NL (1.817±0.254, 1.126±0.095) and the WL (1.367±0.174, 1.328±0.164) (P < 0.05), indicating a promoting effect of green light on cBmal2 and cAanat expression. Similarly, the mean mRNA expression level of cClock in the GL (5.167±0.402) and WL (4.762±0.461) was much higher than that in the NL (1.809±0.187) (P < 0.05), showing that both white light and green light promoted the expression of the core clock gene cClock. The chick embryo retinal melatonin concentration only exhibited obvious diurnal rhythm in WL, although the mean melatonin concentration in the GL (7.632±0.470 pg/mg·protein) was significantly increased (P < 0.05) than that in the WL (6.334±0.522 pg/mg·protein) and NL (5.580±0.274 pg/mg·protein).

## Discussion

## The impact of prenatal light on diurnal rhythms
Diurnal rhythms exist in various aspects of animal physiology, biochemistry, and behavior (Wollnik, 1989). Currently, the main controllers of diurnal rhythms are internal "clocks" within organisms, also known as "circadian clocks", which operate within specific cells and manifest rhythmically in function (Kumar et al., 2004). The circadian clock consists of input pathways, a central oscillator, and output pathways, wherein the central oscillator serves as the core component responsible for stable oscillations. It is primarily composed of genes that regulate oscillatory rhythms and proteins encoded by clock genes (Bell-Pedersen et al., 2005). Unlike the mammalian circadian system, which has a single central oscillator located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, poultry circadian systems possess three oscillators situated in the pineal gland, hypothalamus, and retina. The roles of the pineal gland and retina in the organization of diurnal rhythms vary across species. For instance, the pineal gland plays a crucial role in the diurnal rhythms of sparrows, whereas in chickens and quails, the retina's contribution surpasses that of the pineal gland (Mishra and Kumar, 2017). In the feedback regulation pathway of rhythmic oscillations, cClock and cBmal1 are transcribed and translated in the cytoplasm. The resulting CLOCK and BMAL1 proteins form a heterodimer, which then translocates back into the nucleus to activate the E-Box promoter, thereby promoting the expression of negative clock genes such as cPer and cCry, as well as clock-controlled genes (e.g., cAanat (Tosini et al., 2008)) (Fig. 7). Arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AA-NAT), under the control of light, the retinal clock, and proteasome formation, represents the limiting enzyme for rhythmic melatonin secretion (Fukuhara et al., 2001). By investigating the expression of clock genes and the cAanat gene, as well as the levels of melatonin secretion and changes in the circadian rhythm after light stimulation during the incubation period, we aimed to further elucidate the impact of prenatal light exposure on the chicken embryonic retina.Fig. 7Feedback loops of core circadian genes and melatonin biosynthesis pathway in avian.Fig 7
Feedback loops of core circadian genes and melatonin biosynthesis pathway in avian.
From the perspective of gene expression and the establishment of melatonin secretion circadian rhythms, our study revealed that the expression of cBmal1, cBmal2, cClock, and cAanat genes in the chicken embryonic retina under green light incubation conditions exhibited circadian rhythmicity. However, melatonin secretion did not display a circadian rhythm. This finding was consistent with our previous research, indicating that 12L:12D light stimulation using green light during incubation for both laying hens and broiler chickens did not affect the melatonin rhythm (Tong et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2023). However, Jin et al. (2011) conducted short-term (24 h) and long-term (2 weeks) light exposure treatments (20L:4D, BL, GL, RL, WL) on newly hatched broiler chickens and found that regardless of the duration of exposure to the four monochromatic lights, melatonin secretion in the chick's plasma exhibited a circadian rhythm. Similarly, Bian et al. (2020) cultured retina tissues from 0-day-old chicks under four light treatments (WL, RL, GL, BL, 12L:12D) and measured melanopsin, clock genes, cAanat, positive clock proteins, and melatonin levels every 4 h. The results indicated significant circadian rhythms of cOpn4-1, cOpn4-2, cBmal1, cCry1, cPer2, cPer3, cAanat, and melatonin secretion in the cultured chicken retina tissues under different monochromatic lights. The observed discrepancies in the research findings may be attributed to variations in the species, life stages, and the specific light exposure protocols employed. It seemed that exposure to green light during incubation did not establish a diurnal rhythm for chicken embryos, but it worked for chickens after hatch.
In earlier studies, it was widely believed that the rhythmic synthesis and secretion of melatonin were primarily controlled by variations in the activity of the key enzyme AA-NAT, which is involved in its synthesis (Klein and Weller, 1970; Binkley et al., 1973; Fukuhara et al., 2001; Ostrin, 2019). However, in our study, although the expression of the cAanat gene exhibited a clear circadian rhythm under green light incubation, melatonin secretion did not display rhythmicity. Through the analysis of the melatonin synthesis process (Fig. 7), we discovered that AA-NAT is the penultimate enzyme required for melatonin synthesis, after catalyzing the formation of N-acetylserotonin (NAS), it undergoes further catalysis by hydroxyindole O-methyltransferase (HIOMT) to ultimately form the hormone melatonin (Cassone, 2014). Some studies have also indicated that AA-NAT is not the rate-limiting enzyme for melatonin rhythmic generation and synthesis. Instead, HIOMT is considered to be the true rate-limiting step in the conversion of NAS to melatonin (Steinlechner et al., 1985; Ribelayga et al., 2000; Liu and Borjigin, 2010; Acuna-Castroviejo et al., 2014). The presence of HIOMT protein has been detected in chicken retinal photoreceptors (Voisin et al., 2012). Moreover, the mRNA encoding the enzymes involved in melatonin synthesis, namely tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), AA-NAT, and HIOMT, exhibited circadian rhythms. In the normal chicken retina, all three mRNA species showed significant peaks during the nighttime, while the phase of HIOMT mRNA differed by 180 degrees compared to TPH and AA-NAT (Gauer and Craft, 1996; Bernard et al., 1999). Cardinal et al. (1972) conducted a study that found significant inhibition of HIOMT activity in the retinas of rats exposed to green, cool white, and Vita-Lite (full-spectrum white light) bulbs for up to 96 h. Blue light, on the other hand, exhibited a biphasic effect, with inhibition within the first 48 h and subsequent stimulation. Balemans et al. (1982) exposed 28-day-old male Wistar rats to white, red, and green light for 12 h each day (8:00-20:00) and found that compared to white light, red light caused an earlier shift in HIOMT activity in the pineal gland, reaching its peak between 12 and 16 h. Greenlight shifted HIOMT activity to a later period, peaking at 24 h. These studies indicate that the wavelength of light influences the secretion activity of HIOMT, and the phase of HIOMT secretion levels itself differs from AA-NAT. Therefore, it is speculated that green light during incubation may affect the secretion activity of HIOMT, resulting in the absence of rhythmic melatonin secretion despite the presence of clear circadian expression of the cAanat gene. More studies should be performed to investigate the expression of HIOMT in the retina during the incubation period.
The present study has shown that the peak phases of cBmal1, cClock, and cAanat under green light incubation were observed around CT16, CT17, and CT16, respectively. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that whether it is the retinas of hatched chicks (Cao et al., 2017) or chick retinal tissue (Bian et al., 2020) and pineal gland tissue (Ma et al., 2018) cultured in vitro when exposed to green light stimulation, the peak phase of cAanat was consistently delayed relative to the peak phases of cBmal1 and cClock. This observation aligned with the results expected from the clock gene regulatory pathway. Based on our analysis, it was believed that the phenomenon of the peak phase of cAanat in the retinas during green light stimulation being advanced in our study was due to the abundant expression and earlier peak phase of cBmal2 in the chicken embryonic retinas under green light. Previous research has reported that as a homolog of BMAL1, BMAL2 can also dimerize with CLOCK and form heterodimers, activating the transcription of negative clock genes and clock-controlled genes (such as cAanat) by binding to E-box (Okano et al., 2001a; Okano et al., 2001b). Some researchers have even proposed that cBmal2 can partially compensate for the loss of circadian locomotor behavior in mice caused by cBmal1 deficiency, suggesting that cBmal2 can to some extent substitute for cBmal1 (Shi et al., 2010).
In the white light group, the gene expression of cBmal1 and cAanat, as well as melatonin secretion, exhibited circadian rhythms in the chicken embryonic retinas, while the rhythms of cBmal2 and cClock were disrupted. Similar to our findings, Cao et al. (2017) exposed 240 newly hatched broiler chickens to blue light (480 nm), green light (560 nm), red light (660 nm), and white light (400-760 nm) emitted by an LED system under a 12L:12D light-dark cycle for 14 days. They found that under all four light treatments, cBmal1, cCry1, cPer2, cPer3, and cAanat exhibited rhythmic expression with a day-night pattern, while the rhythms of cBmal2, cClock, and cCry2 were disrupted. Likewise, Bian et al. (2020) observed the same pattern in chick retinal tissue cultured in vitro under white light. However, Ribeiro Graciani De Lima et al. (2011) investigated the expression patterns of the melanopsin genes Opn4x and Opn4m, clock genes cClock and cPer2, as well as the key enzymes AA-NAT and tyrosine hidroxylase (TH) of chicken embryonic retinal tissue in vitro under dark conditions and a 12L:12D light-dark cycle (cool white fluorescent light). They found that under constant darkness (DD), none of the genes in the preserved retinal cells exhibited circadian rhythmicity. However, under the light-dark cycle (LD), mRNA levels of cClock, cPer2, cOpn4m, AA-NAT, and TH showed rhythmic transcription patterns. Jiang et al. conducted a study on the circadian rhythms of clock genes in the hypothalamus (Jiang et al., 2017) and pineal gland (Jiang et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2020) of chicks. They found that after being raised under different light conditions (WL, RL, GL, BL) with a 12L:12D cycle for 2 weeks, clear circadian rhythmicity was observed in the expression of cBmal1, cBmal2, cAanat, and melatonin secretion in the hypothalamus and pineal gland. Ma et al. (2018) cultured primary pineal gland cells from chicks in vitro under different light conditions. They found that, except for the loss of rhythmicity in cCry1 in the blue light group, all other treatment groups showed significant circadian rhythmic oscillations in the 24-hour expression of core clock genes (cBmal1/2, cClock, cCry1/2, and cPer2/3), cAanat and melatonin. The discrepancies between their results and ours may be due to the different effects of light sources with different spectra and intensities on the endogenous circadian rhythms in different species, organs, and developmental stages of chickens.
Previous studies have repeatedly documented rhythm disruption of cClock in avian retinas (Bailey et al., 2004; Toller et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2013). Bian et al. (2020) and Ma et al. (2019) investigated the roles of cBmal1 and cClock in regulating the circadian clock and melatonin secretion in cultured avian embryonic retinal tissues and chick pineal glands using siRNA interference and overexpression techniques. The results demonstrated that the cBmal1 gene occupied the top position in the feedback loop of the circadian clock in avian retinas and pineal glands, regulating downstream molecular oscillations and output signals. On the other hand, cClock played a secondary role in maintaining the circadian oscillations of embryonic retinal cells and chick pineal gland cells under monochromatic green light, primarily contributing to the stability and amplification of the molecular oscillation loop of clock genes. The heterodimer formed by CLOCK protein and BMAL1 protein plays a critical and indispensable role in the generation of circadian rhythms. However, CLOCK can be functionally replaced by the paralogous transcription factor Neuronal PAS domain-containing protein 2 (NPAS2) in regulating circadian rhythms (Reick et al., 2001; Haque et al., 2010). Therefore, interfering with or knocking out CLOCK does not have a destructive impact on maintaining the circadian rhythms of cAanat and retinal melatonin. The absence of its rhythm also does not result in circadian rhythm abnormalities of cAanat and retinal melatonin. As a paralogous homolog of BMAL1, BMAL2 can partially rescue the circadian clock and metabolic phenotypes in cBmal1 knockout mice, including rhythmic locomotor activity and rhythmic metabolism (Shi et al., 2010). However, previous studies in mammals have demonstrated that cBmal2 is regulated by cBmal1 (Bunger et al., 2000). Sasaki et al. (2009) discovered that the transcriptional activation mediated by BMAL2-CLOCK and BMAL1-CLOCK in sequences containing E-box elements is not proportional. They speculated that each E/E'-box sequence has different regulatory mechanisms, with some clock genes primarily regulated by BMAL2-CLOCK and PER2, while others were regulated by BMAL1-CLOCK and CRYs. The target genes of BMAL1 may be more crucial for the core oscillatory mechanism than those of BMAL2. Our research findings further support the central role of cBmal1 in the oscillatory mechanism of the circadian clock.
In the no light group of chicken embryonic retinas in this study, only the expression of cBmal1 exhibited diurnal oscillation with a smaller amplitude, while the remaining clock genes and melatonin secretion showed no circadian rhythm. Similarly, a study demonstrated the absence of rhythmic expression of clock genes and cAanat in cultures prepared from the retinas of 6-day-old chicken embryos (E6) not exposed to LD cycles (Chaurasia et al., 2006). However, the studies conducted by Ma et al. (2018) and Bian et al. (2020) on the clock genes, cAanat, and melatonin in the pineal gland and retinal cells of broiler chickens in vitro, under continuous darkness conditions, revealed significant diurnal oscillations in all indicators except for cClock, cBmal2, and cCry2 in the retina, which exhibited arrhythmic patterns. While the core clock gene cBmal1 exhibited circadian rhythmicity in the NL, the downstream regulatory gene cAanat lost its circadian rhythm. This could be attributed to the regulation of cAanat in the photoreceptors of vertebrate retinas, which is influenced not only by the transcription/translation feedback loops of the circadian clock but also by the regulation of the Photic “Turnoff” mechanism (Klein, 2007). The Photic “Turnoff” mechanism can induce rapid inhibition of light by reducing cAMP levels, leading to the dissociation of the AANAT/14-3-3 complex (Gastel et al., 1998). In avian retinas, cAMP can also influence transcription through the cyclic AMP response element (CRE) in the Aanat promoter (Tosini et al., 2006). The levels of cAMP are influenced by both light exposure and the circadian clock (Fukuhara et al., 2004; Iuvone et al., 2005).
In terms of gene expression and melatonin secretion levels, compared to the NL, both light-treated groups showed significantly higher expression levels of the clock gene and melatonin secretion. In the WL, except for the significantly higher expression of cBmal1 compared to the GL, the expression levels of other indicators were lower than the GL. This was consistent with previous studies which have shown that green light could upregulate the expression of positive regulatory genes and the cAanat gene in the retina (Cao et al., 2017; Bian et al., 2020), pineal gland (Jiang et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2018), and hypothalamus (Jiang et al., 2017) of broiler chicks, thereby further enhancing melatonin secretion.

## The impact of prenatal light on visual development
In comparison to mammals, avian vision is more acute. Avian species possess two types of photoreceptors, one being the photoreceptors in the retina, including rod and cone cells (Lewis et al., 2007). The other type is the extraretinal photoreceptors located in the pineal gland and hypothalamus, with 80% of light signals entering and being conducted through the eyes, indicating that the eyes are the primary organs for the avian perception of light stimulation (Dawson et al., 2001). Research has demonstrated that exogenous light can penetrate the eggshell and be recognized by the eyes of the chick embryo, subsequently affecting the functionality of its photosensitive pineal gland in the later stages of incubation (Aigegil and Murilloferrol, 1992; Zeman et al., 1992). In studies on mice, researchers have also reported the prenatal effects of late-term light exposure on mouse embryo eyes, finding that light stimulation promotes the regression of the embryonic vitreous vascular system and the formation of the retinal vascular system (Rao et al., 2013).
Oishi and Murakami (1985) investigated the effects of light exposure duration and intensity on the eye weight, eyeball diameter, corneal diameter, and anterior chamber depth in chickens. The study found that continuous light, 22L:2D cycle, and continuous darkness led to an increase in the weight of the chick's eyeballs, as well as shallower anterior chamber depth and smaller corneal diameters as a result of continuous light exposure. Our study indicated that light stimulation during the incubation period had an impact on the thickness of the retina and its various cell layers. Compared to no light exposure, white light stimulation increased the thickness of the retina and its cell layers, while green light stimulation decreased the thickness of the retina and its cell layers. However, Lauber et al. (1961) found that during the first 6 weeks of chick rearing, continuous 24-hour light exposure after receiving 12 and 24 h of white light stimulation respectively, reduced the thickness of the chick's retina, nerve fiber layer, cone cells and rod cells. The study on zebrafish embryos conducted by Üstündağ et al. (2019) revealed that exposure to 7200 lux of white light compared to 3200 lux resulted in disrupted interphotoreceptor spacing in the embryos, leading to a decrease in the total thickness of the retina and the thickness of the photoreceptor cell layer. Therefore, the proper light exposure protocols during chicken incubation are vital for the positive effect on embryonic retina development.
TGF-β is a group of multifunctional cell signaling proteins that can bidirectionally regulate intercellular communication. It includes three types: TGF-β1, TGF-β2, and TGF-β3 (Ab, 1990). It can stimulate epithelial-mesenchymal transition during embryonic development, wound healing, fibrotic diseases, and cancer metastasis, participating in cell cycle control, early embryonic development regulation, angiogenesis, cell differentiation, and proliferation (Krieglstein et al., 2000; Verrecchia and Mauviel, 2007). When TGF-β binds to its receptor, it activates intracellular signaling Smad proteins downstream. Smad2 and Smad3 are phosphorylated by the TGF-β receptor, then form a complex with Smad4 and translocate to the cell nucleus, where they activate TGF-β-dependent target genes to assist in information transmission (Ten Dijke and Hill, 2004). Smad2 is present throughout the embryo, with prominent expression in the eye and brain, while Smad3 exhibits a higher expression level in the eye (Hsu et al., 2011).
In the retina, TGF-β is produced by RPE cells and pericytes (Simo et al., 2006; Hinck, 2012). Extensive research has reported that TGF-β1 induces angiogenesis in vivo, including by mediating the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (Ferrari et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2014), regulating endothelial cell proliferation and macrophage infiltration, as well as extracellular matrix deposition and protein hydrolysis remodeling (Nunes et al., 1996). The research by Wang et al. (2017) in mice has shown that the upregulation of TGF-β enhanced the protein expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), thereby promoting the formation of new blood vessels. The development of choroidal neovascularization (CNV) can be suppressed by inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. However, the study by Ohlmann et al. (2016) has shown that overexpression of TGF-β1 has led to the transformation of RPE cells, resulting in a reduction of VEGF originating from the retinal pigment epithelium. This further impedes the generation of choroidal vasculature, crucial for supplying blood to the photoreceptor cells. As the vasculature in the choroid primarily serves to nourish the photoreceptor cells, the overexpression of TGF-β1 may ultimately hinder the normal development of these cells. The aforementioned studies have illustrated the dual role of TGF-β1 in angiogenesis, with the complexity of this differential signaling regulation depending on the concentration of TGF-β, the presence of other regulatory factors, and the cell types involved (Wang et al., 2013). The TGF-β signaling pathway regulates the normal growth and maturation of the ocular vascular system in a dose-sensitive manner. Both an excess or deficiency in TGF-β protein can lead to ocular abnormalities, highlighting the necessity to tightly control the expression and activation of these proteins during normal developmental processes (Zhao and Overbeek, 2001).
In the current study, compared to the NL, incubation with white and green light during the incubation period suppressed the expression of TGF-β1 mRNA in the chicken embryonic retina, resulting in a downregulation of its expression. Both Smad2 and Smad3 were expressed in the embryonic retina, with the differences in expression levels consistent with TGF-β1 mRNA across the groups. A study housed guinea pigs under 250 lux of green light and found a significant decrease in the expression of TGF-β1 mRNA in Müller cells in the guinea pig retina (Chen et al., 2003). Fu et al. (2015) cultured Müller cells from rat retinas under 530 nm monochromatic light and investigated the effects of different durations of light exposure (6, 12, 24 h) on the expression of basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) and TGF-β1. The results indicated a positive correlation between bFGF expression and light exposure time across the three light exposure groups, while TGF-β1 showed a negative correlation with light exposure time. All studies have demonstrated that light stimulation hurts the expression of TGF-β mRNA in the retina. In addition to profoundly impacting angiogenesis in the eye, thereby affecting the development of retinal cells, Dünker et al. (2001) have demonstrated that TGF-β signaling also regulated the death of progenitor cells in the avian retina. Consequently, reduced TGF-β subtypes inhibit progenitor cell apoptosis and increase embryonic retinal thickness. The expression levels of TGF-β1 mRNA and Smad2/3 protein were all decreased in WL and GL compared to NL, but the impact on the chicken embryonic retina was contrary. Only white light had an advantage over green incubation in retina development, and dark incubation was in between.
Previous studies have already demonstrated that the normal ocular elongation and the development of refraction in vertebrates require a visual pattern of diurnal and nocturnal rhythms (Nickla et al., 1998a; Nickla et al., 1998b). Melatonin is rhythmically synthesized in response to the day-night cycle, serving as a messenger of temporal information to various tissues. Multiple studies have reported that the diurnal rhythm of melatonin synthesis and release by retinal cells plays a regulatory role in various biological rhythms within the eye (Besharse and Dunis, 1983; Pierce and Besharse, 1985; Pintor et al., 2001). Rada and Wiechmann (2006) have investigated the normal and deprived eyes of monocularly deprived chicks regarding melatonin receptors and melatonin secretion, and have explored the effects of exogenously administered melatonin on ocular growth and development. The results have indicated that melatonin acts through specific receptors in ocular tissues in the growth and development of the eye, resulting in a significant thinning of the retina in visually deprived eyes. When melatonin is administered systemically to albino rats, it has been found to lead to loss of photoreceptor cells and thinning of the retina (Wiechmann and Osteen, 1992). These findings suggest that melatonin appears to have a certain negative impact on retinal thickness, but the pathways and mechanisms through which it mediates this effect are currently not well understood. Studies on multiple animal species and various tissues have consistently demonstrated the effects of melatonin on TGF-β gene expression (Shin et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019) and signaling pathways (Hosseinzadeh et al., 2018; Piekarska et al., 2023), including the TGF-β/Smad pathway. Therefore, the intriguing question of whether melatonin influences retinal morphogenesis by affecting the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway warrants further investigation. Overall, the use of white light during the incubation period appeared to have a more positive impact on the development of the chicken embryonic retina. Compared to green light, chicken embryos incubated under white light conditions establish a circadian rhythm by the end of incubation with lower levels of melatonin expression, leading to a more robust morphological development characterized by thicker photoreceptor layers and retinal thickness.

## Conclusion
The results of our study indicated that prenatal light influenced the morphogenesis and establishment of circadian rhythms in the chick embryo retina by regulating the expression of relevant genes. The deprivation of light during incubation resulted in the overexpression of TGF-β1 mRNA, which in turn affected the expression of Smad2 and Smad3 in the retina. This might have inhibited the differentiation of cells in all layers of the retina, ultimately affecting retinal thickness and overall development. Furthermore, while both monochromatic green and white light were found to promote the transcriptional expression of positive clock genes and melatonin secretion during incubation, white light was demonstrated to be more advantageous than green light in the establishment of circadian rhythms in the chick embryo retina. The well-developed retina of chick embryos exposed to white light might have facilitated the establishment of melatonin rhythms. Concurrently, melatonin served as a pivotal mediator in the transmission of daily and seasonal temporal cues. The circadian rhythms of melatonin established during the prenatal period might have played a pivotal role in the rhythmically normal development of the chick eye. Future research is needed for identifying the mechanism of prenatal light.
