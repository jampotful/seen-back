Susceptibility of common dabbling and diving duck species to clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 high pathogenicity avian influenza virus: an experimental infection study.

# Susceptibility of common dabbling and diving duck species to clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 high pathogenicity avian influenza virus: an experimental infection study

## Abstract
AbstractIn the winter of 2010–2011, Japan experienced a large outbreak of infections caused by clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 high pathogenicity avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs) in wild birds. Interestingly, many tufted ducks (Aythya fuligula), which are migratory diving ducks, succumbed to the infection, whereas only one infection case was reported in migratory dabbling duck species, the major natural hosts of the influenza A virus, during the outbreak. To assess whether the susceptibility of each duck species to HPAIVs was correlated with the number of cases, tufted duck and dabbling duck species (Eurasian wigeon, Mareca penelope; mallard, Anas platyrhynchos; Northern pintail, Anas acuta) were intranasally inoculated with A/Mandarin duck/Miyazaki/22M807-1/2011 (H5N1), an index clade 2.3.2.1 virus previously used for experimental infection studies in various bird species. All ducks observed for 10 days post-inoculation (dpi) mostly shed the virus via the oral route and survived. The tufted ducks shed a higher titer of the virus than the other dabbling duck species, and one of them showed apparent neurological symptoms after 7 dpi, which were accompanied by eye lesions. No clinical symptoms were observed in the dabbling ducks, although systemic infection and viremia were observed in some of them sacrificed at 3 dpi. These results suggest that the susceptibility of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIVs might differ by duck species.Keywords: avian viral disease, experimental pathology, influenza virus, pathogenicity, wildlife disease
After an outbreak of H5N1 high pathogenicity avian influenza virus (HPAIV) in a goose farm in China in 1996, the virus progenies of HPAIV (Gs/GD-like viruses) evolved phylogenetically and antigenically, resulting in economic losses in the poultry industry [36]. Gs/GD-like viruses are phylogenetically organized into 10 clades, numbered 0–9, based on hemagglutinin (HA) genes. Initially, high pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) largely occurred in poultry populations, and lethal Gs/GD-like virus infections in wild birds were only sporadically reported [10]. However, after more than a thousand migratory birds in Lake Qinghai, China, died from HPAIV infection in May and June 2005 [8, 18], global dissemination of HPAIVs has occurred repeatedly [21], suggesting the involvement of the migration behavior of infected birds.
During the winter of 2010–2011, Japan experienced large outbreaks caused by clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 HPAIVs [14, 29]. The causal viruses were isolated from 63 wild and captive birds, including migrating and sedentary birds (Supplementary Table 1) [25]. Interestingly, a number of dead Aythya ducks (migratory diving ducks) and Mandarin ducks (Aix galericulata, known as sedentary birds in Japan) with HPAIVs were found, whereas infection cases were rarely observed in Anas and Mareca birds (migratory dabbling ducks), which are recognized as major natural hosts of influenza A viruses [2]. We have previously revealed that one of the HPAIV isolates in the season, A/Mandarin duck/Miyazaki/22M807-1/2011 (H5N1) (M807), sometimes caused lethal infection in Mandarin ducks [26], which explains why this bird species mainly succumbed to HPAIV infection at that time. The susceptibility of migratory dabbling and diving ducks to the M807 strain is still of interest to elucidate its correlation with the outcome of HPAI outbreaks. Previous studies have also assessed the pathogenicity of the M807 strain to raptors (Falco sparverius, American kestrel) [31], crows (Corvus frugilegus, rook) [27], and herons (Ardea cinerea, grey heron; Ardea intermedia, intermediate heron; Egretta garzetta, little egret; and Nycticorax nycticorax, black-crowned night heron) [30]. Some of these bird species died from experimental infection with M807. These results and further observations of M807 infections in migratory dabbling and diving ducks are expected to provide a comprehensive understanding of HPAIV ecology in field settings.
Although clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIVs have become minor in comparison with the globally circulating clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs, they have perpetuated in poultry populations in East Asian countries; Ge et al. reported clade 2.3.2.1 H5N2 and H5N6 HPAIVs in China from 2017 to 2018 [11, 12]. A clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV was also isolated from a domesticated Muscovy duck (Cairina moschata) in our surveillance study in Vietnam (A/Muscovy duck/Vietnam/LBM992c/2017 (H5N1); accession numbers: LC656314–21). The antigenicity of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIVs is known to be distinct from that of clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs [22], suggesting that clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIVs have the potential to be selected again as major circulating strains under antibody pressure in poultry populations. Furthermore, outstanding dissemination of H5N1 subtype HPAIVs has been recently reported in poultry and/or wild birds in Asian, European, African, and North American countries during the winter of 2021–2022 [9]. These data highlight the importance of assessing the pathogenicity of clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 HPAIVs in wild ducks not only to review previous outbreaks but also to deal with future HPAI outbreaks. In the present study, we assessed the pathogenicity of the clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 M807 strain in common migratory dabbling ducks (Mareca penelope, Eurasian wigeon, Anas platyrhynchos, Mallard, and Anas acuta, Northern pintail), and diving ducks (tufted duck, Aythya fuligula). HPAIV infection cases were confirmed mainly in tufted ducks during the outbreak in Japan in 2010–2011 (Supplementary Table 1) [25]. The present results demonstrate that clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 HPAIVs showed higher pathogenicity to tufted ducks in comparison with common dabbling duck species, correlating with the reported number of HPAIV infection cases at that time. On the basis of these results and the findings of our previous study using the M807 strain, the ecology of HPAIV during the outbreak was further discussed.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Virus
A clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV, A/Mandarin duck/Miyazaki/22M807-1/2011 (H5N1) (M807), was used for experimental duck inoculation. The strain was isolated from a mixture of tracheal and cloacal swabs of a Mandarin duck found dead in Miyazaki City, in the Kyusyu region of Japan [29], and has been used to evaluate its pathogenicity in other bird species, such as Mandarin duck [26], rook [27], American kestrel [31], and heron species [30]. The accession numbers of the M807 gene sequences are AB677872–79. The virus was propagated in 10-day-old chicken embryos (Aoki Breeder Farm; Nasu, Japan) for 48 hr at 35°C. After incubation, eggs were chilled at 4°C for 12 hr. The allantoic fluid was harvested and stored as a viral stock at −80°C.

## Birds
Fourteen birds each of three dabbling duck species (Eurasian wigeon, Mallard, and Northern pintail) and four tufted ducks were captured in Chiba, Fukuoka, and Miyazaki prefectures, Japan, in agreement with each governor (permission numbers: #1672 [Chiba], #11–10 [Fukuoka], and #298 [Miyazaki]). The ducks were confirmed to be in good health, and influenza virus-negative by testing pharyngolaryngeal/cloacal swab samples using an antigen detection kit (ESPLINE INFLUENZA A & B-N, Fujirebio Inc., Tokyo, Japan), and then transported to Tottori University. Blood was subsequently collected and checked for specific serum antibodies against the challenge virus, M807, by HA inhibition (HI) testing [35]. The ducks were fed with floatable pellet food for waterfowl (Feed One, Yokohama, Japan) in water, and with commercially available Japanese mustard spinach (Brassica rapa L. var. perviridis) processed in a kitchen blender. They were reared at Tottori University for a maximum of 10 weeks and grouped to include at least one duck with serum HI titer below the detection limit (<2 HI) in the 106 and 102 50% egg infectious dose (EID50) inoculation groups in Experiments 1 and 2, as described below. In addition, four ducks of each of the three dabbling duck species were subsequently shipped by air to Hokkaido University and used for the viral transmission study in Experiment 3, as described below.

## Measurement of the 50% egg infectious dose
Samples were serially diluted 10-fold in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Takara-bio, Kusatsu, Japan) supplemented with 10,000 units/mL of penicillin G potassium (Meiji Seika, Tokyo, Japan) and 10 mg/mL of streptomycin sulfate (Meiji Seika) (PS-PBS), and were inoculated at a volume of 100 µL into 10-day-old embryonated eggs, with 3–4 eggs per sample. Eggs were incubated for 48 hr at 35°C. A hemagglutination test [35] was performed using allantoic fluid, and the EID50 was calculated using the method described by Reed and Muench [24].

## Animal infection experiments
To assess intranasal pathogenicity of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV in both dabbling and diving duck species, we inoculated the captured ducks intranasally with 200 µL of allantoic fluid containing M807 at 106, 104, or 102 EID50 and observed for clinical signs at 24-hr intervals for 10 days (Experiment 1, see Fig. 1). Pharyngolaryngeal and cloacal swabs were collected at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 10 days post-inoculation (dpi) to assess viral shedding. The swabs were collected in 2 mL of nutrient broth medium (Nissui Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with penicillin G potassium and streptomycin sulfate. At the end of the 10-day period, the ducks were also examined for specific serum antibodies against the challenge virus by HI testing. Surviving birds were euthanized by inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) after blood collection at 10 dpi. Tissues (brain, trachea, lung, kidney, colon, pancreas, heart, and liver) from ducks showing clinical signs were sampled and subjected to histopathological analysis. Tissue samples were collected in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) for histopathological analysis, and another tissue sample was homogenized to create a 10% (w/v) organ emulsion in a nutrient broth medium with antibiotics for virus isolation. In Experiment 2, two or three duck species were intranasally inoculated with 200 μL of the virus at 106 EID50 and euthanized at 3 dpi to evaluate the extent tissue tropism of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV. Tissues (brain, trachea, lung, kidney, and colon) were collected for viral isolation and/or histopathological analyses. In Experiment 3, two pairs of three dabbling duck species (Eurasian wigeon, Mallard, and Northern pintail) were used, and one of each pair was intranasally inoculated with 200 µL of the virus at 106 EID50 to estimate the transmissibility of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV in each duck flock. The other duck was placed in contact with the challenged duck, and clinical signs were observed at 24-hr intervals for 10 days. Sampling of swabs and tissues and their analyses were conducted in the same manner as in Experiment 1.
Experimental design of H5N1 high pathogenicity avian influenza virus inoculation to ducks.

## Ethics statement
Experiments 1 and 2 were performed in self-contained isolator units (CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) at a biosafety level 3 (BSL3) facility at the Avian Zoonosis Research Center, Tottori University, Japan. All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tottori University (approval number: 11-T-98). Experiment 3 was performed in self-contained isolator units (Tokiwa Kagaku Kikai, Tokyo, Japan) at a BSL3 facility at the International Institute for Zoonosis Control, Hokkaido University, Japan. The protocol was approved by the Hokkaido University Animal Care and Use Committee (permit number: 12-1160). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments of the Science Council of Japan (http://www.scj.go.jp/ja/info/kohyo/pdf/kohyo-20-k16-2e.pdf). Humane endpoints were set as follows: birds unable to eat or drink were euthanized and recorded as dead on the following day’s observation period.

## Histopathological examination
The birds’ tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, processed using routine methods, and embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin sections (thickness, 4 μm) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological examination. Immunohistochemical staining was also performed using an antigen retrieval solution (0.05% citraconic anhydride, pH 7.4; Immunosaver; Nissin EM, Tokyo, Japan) and mouse anti-influenza A virus matrix protein monoclonal antibody (clone GA2B; Serotec Ltd., Oxford, UK), and the Simple Stain MAX-PO (M) kit (Nichirei Bioscience Inc., Tokyo, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

## RESULTS

## Intranasal pathogenicity of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV in dabbling and diving duck species
Captured ducks (seven Eurasian wigeons, seven mallards, seven Northern pintails, and two tufted ducks) were intranasally inoculated with the M807 strain at titers of 106–102 EID50 and observed for 10 days (Experiment 1; Table 1). All but one of the tufted ducks (T02) exhibited subclinical infection. T02 was temporarily depressed at 4–5 dpi, and then showed apparent neurological symptoms such as shivering, torticollis, and circling behavior after 7 dpi, which were accompanied by corneal opacity and mydriasis (Fig. 2). It was able to drink and eat under these conditions and eventually survived through the observation period (Table 1). Mild histopathological changes were found in the brain and heart of T02: mild encephalitis accompanied by perivascular mononuclear cell cuﬃng, neovascularization, and mild myocarditis with lymphocytic infiltration (Fig. 3A and 3B). Viral antigen-positive cells and cell debris were observed in the brain (Fig. 3C). All ducks inoculated with 106 EID50 of M807, except one Northern pintail (P03), orally shed the virus at titers of ≤105.5 EID50/mL at 1–7 dpi (Table 1). Among them, the tufted duck (T02), which showed clinical signs, also shed the virus via the cloacal route at 3 dpi. Most ducks inoculated with 106 EID50 of the virus showed seroconversion or HI antibody responses after the virus challenge. Similarly, both mallards (M04 and M05) inoculated with 104 EID50 of M807 mainly shed the virus via the oral route at 1 and 3–7 dpi, and showed clear seroconversion. In contrast, Eurasian wigeons and Northern pintails with 104 EID50 of M807 rarely shed the virus without showing any antibody response after challenge. Neither virus shedding nor seroconversion was observed in ducks with 102 EID50 of M807.

## Tissue tropism of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV in dabbling and diving duck species
Two to three ducks of each species were inoculated with 106 EID50 of M807, and their tissue and blood specimens were sampled for virus isolation and pathological analysis (Experiment 2; Table 2). One pintail (P10) apparently showed systemic infection with viremia; the virus was recovered at titers of 101.8–4.5 EID50/g or EID50/mL from all of the tested samples. The virus was also isolated from tracheas of pre-HI antibody-positive ducks (M08, M10, and P08). Among them, M10 also harbored the virus in the kidney and colon. Virus replication was rarely observed in Eurasian wigeons and tufted ducks. Viral antigen-positive cells were found in some tissues with virus titers of ≥104.0 EID50/g (trachea of M08, P08, P10; lung of P10; colon of M10) (Table 2). A small number of positive cells were observed in the pleura of the lungs at P08, from which no virus was isolated (Table 2 and Fig. 3D).

## Clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV transmission between dabbling ducks
To assess the transmissibility of clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV among ducks, the dabbling ducks were inoculated with 106 EID50 of M807, cohoused with another naïve contact duck, and observed for 10 days (Experiment 3, Table 3). All ducks survived during the observation period. Antibody responses were observed in all ducks except one contact Northern pintail (P12) with the pre-HI antibody. All of the inoculated ducks, except one mallard (M11), orally shed the virus at maximum titers of ≤105.7 EID50/mL 7 days after the challenge. Among them, one Eurasian wigeon and mallard (W13 and M13) also shed ≤101.8 EID50/mL of the virus via the cloacal route at 3–7 dpi. Among the contact birds, virus shedding was confirmed in only one Eurasian wigeon (W14).

## DISCUSSION
Tufted ducks, often known as diving ducks, mainly use large and deep freshwater lakes, ponds, and reservoirs for their habitats, unlike dabbling ducks, which feed primarily in shallow water [6]. In the present study, we could only capture four tufted ducks because the capturing spots and opportunities were limited. Nevertheless, both tufted ducks inoculated with 106 EID50 of the M807 strain in Experiment 1 orally shed higher titers of the virus than dabbling ducks, and one of them (T02) showed ocular and neurological symptoms. Although T02 was able to drink and eat after disease onset, birds with apparent infection were likely to die in field settings, when they are required to feed by themselves. Similar symptoms have also been reported in tufted ducks experimentally infected with clade 2.3.2 HPAIV [4]. Further, histopathological lesions or viral antigens have been found in the brains and hearts of tufted ducks that naturally succumbed during the HPAI outbreak in Japan in 2010–11 [1], as observed in the present study. T02 showed seroconversion and shed no virus in the late phase of infection, suggesting that virus clearance occurred successfully. Kajihara et al. also reported that surviving domestic ducks experimentally infected with HPAIV developed severe torticollis [13]. Although the mechanism underlying this presentation remains unclear, the results suggest that irreversible neurological defects could occur in both wild and domestic duck species. The tufted ducks inoculated with 106 EID50 of M807 and sacrificed at 3 dpi in Experiment 2 did not have viruses or pathological lesions in the tested tissues. Brojer et al. recovered the virus from some tissues of 5-month-old tufted ducks inoculated with clade 2.3.2 HPAIV, similar to the M807, at 3–4 dpi [4]. These differences might be explained by the age of the tufted ducks. Previous reports have suggested that younger domestic ducks are more vulnerable to H5N1 HPAIV infections [13, 23]. Tufted ducks begin to breed in May in the northern nesting areas [5]. Although the ages of the tufted ducks in this study could not be verified, they were captured at wintering sites in February, and were likely to be older than the 5-month-old birds in the study by Brojer et al. [4].
The M807 strain caused asymptomatic infection in dabbling duck species, consistent with the results of previous studies where clade 2.3.2.1 and/or clade 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs were inoculated into Eurasian wigeons [28, 32], mallards [15, 28, 32], and Northern pintails [17]. However, high mortality was observed in some Eurasian wigeons that were exposed to some clade 2.3.4.4 H5N6 HPAIV [3]. These studies collectively suggest that most clade 2.3.2.1 and 2.3.4.4 HPAIVs do not cause clinical symptoms in the dabbling duck species. Some dabbling ducks in the present study had pre-HI antibodies against M807, supporting the notion that inapparent clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV infection also occurred in these duck species in field settings. Throughout the study, dabbling ducks shed lower titers of the virus than those in the inoculum in Experiments 1 and 3. Although viral transmission was confirmed in Experiment 3, viral shedding from contact ducks was limited. These results suggest that clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIVs might not be amplified by viral dissemination among the flocks of these dabbling duck species. In other words, these species function as biological vectors and contribute to transboundary viral dissemination. Some dabbling ducks sacrificed at 3 dpi in Experiment 2 had higher titers of the virus in the trachea, which is likely to be linked to virus shedding via the oral route. Viruses were also recovered from the colons of ducks that showed systemic infections (M10 and P10). Delayed virus shedding via the cloacal route in some ducks in Experiments 1 and 3 might depend on establishment of systemic infection.
We have previously revealed that Mandarin ducks, which are known to be sedentary birds in Japan, are vulnerable to infection with the M807 strain [26], similar to the tufted ducks in this study. The differences in the pathogenicity of M807 to each duck species were correlated with the number of HPAI cases in each bird species in Japan in 2010–2011, as shown in Supplementary Table 1, indicating that assessments of the pathogenicity of HPAIV in wild bird species by experimental infection studies are important to draw conclusions from previous HPAI outbreaks and to improve monitoring systems for dealing with HPAI in wild birds. The results of the series of studies using M807 were shared in advance with the Ministry of Environment, Japan, and used to make a list of bird species that should be preferentially monitored and tested to prepare for HPAI occurrence [20]. The assumed scenario in HPAI outbreaks in Japan during the winter of 2010–2011 is as follows: (i) Clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV was brought transboundary by dabbling ducks, which are sensitive but not vulnerable to the virus, and silently perpetuated in their wintering spots. (ii) Environmental water was contaminated with the shed virus and disseminated to other waterbirds sharing the habitat, including migratory tufted ducks and sedentary Mandarin ducks, which are more vulnerable to clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIV infection. (iii) Mandarin ducks were close to wooded areas due to their specific habits, eating acorns and grains in autumn and winter [7], and sometimes approached poultry farms in mountainous areas: the M807 strain was isolated from a Mandarin duck succumbed to HPAIV infection near a chicken farm in which an HPAI outbreak occurred [26].
Previous studies have revealed that the M807 strain causes lethal infections in heron, raptor, and crow species [27, 30, 31]. Taken together with the findings of the present study, herons sharing habitats with HPAIV-infected dabbling ducks are likely to accidentally contract the virus by drinking virus-contaminated water or preying on fish in the water. An HPAIV strain similar to M807 was isolated from a gray heron during the 2010–2011 outbreak in Japan [29]. Moreover, mass HPAIV infections in herons were reported in two park ponds in Hong Kong with large duck populations [10], suggesting that HPAIV infection risk is correlated with susceptible bird density. Nine cases of infection in peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus) were reported during the HPAI outbreak in Japan between 2010 and 2011 [29]. The avian prey range of the peregrine falcon is 10–2,000 g [34], which includes the duck species assessed in the present study as well as Mandarin duck [7]. Carrion feeding behavior has also been reported [34]. These profiles suggest that infected live or dead ducks could be a source of infection. Ducks that succumbed to HPAIV infection could also be a source of infection for crow species via scavenging. Clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 HPAIV outbreaks in crows were confirmed in Bangladesh and India during 2010–2011 [16, 33]. Mass HPAIV infections in crows were also observed around a pond where migratory ducks wintered in Japan in 2017–2018 [19], although no cases of crow infection were reported in 2010–2011. Thus, accidental HPAIV transmission to a crow could cause viral dissemination to the whole flock via cannibalism, with the infected crows functioning as viral transporters between waterbird wintering sites and poultry farms.
In the present study, we assessed the pathogenicity of the M807 strain, which is the index strain of clade 2.3.2.1 H5N1 HPAIV, to common dabbling and diving ducks and discussed the results of previous studies using other bird species. Although the present study had some limitations, such as the low number and unknown profiles (neutralization antibody prevalence and age) of ducks examined, a series of reports investigating the susceptibility and vulnerability of various bird species to a single index virus is rare and valuable to understand the ecology of HPAIV during outbreak; the present study can be expected to complement this literature. As stated above, clade 2.3.2.1 HPAIVs have been perpetuated in poultry populations. We expect that a series of studies using the M807 strain will contribute to the control of HPAI outbreaks.
