# Impact of Bird-assisted Therapy in Anxious Pediatric Patients during Their First Dental Visit: A Randomized Controlled Trial

## Abstract
AbstractAim and backgroundManaging a child's behavior is often a major challenge for dentists. In the past, various behavior-modification techniques, such as modeling, tell–show–do, voice control, and desensitization, have shown limited results. Recently, bird-assisted therapy (B-AT) has gained importance in relieving patients' anxiety. This study aimed to examine the effect of the presence of birds on reducing patients' anxiety.Materials and methodsIn this randomized clinical trial, 40 healthy children aged 4–8 years who were attending their first dental visit were selected. Eligible participants had Frankl Behavior Rating Scale scores of 2 or 3 and were randomly assigned to group I, the control group without birds, or group II, the experimental group with birds. The children's anxiety before and after oral screening was recorded using three parameters: the Jeet Wheel Scale, pulse rate measured using a pulse oximeter, and the Modified Venham Anxiety Rating Scale.ResultsSignificant differences were observed between groups I and II. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in children's anxiety levels following the implementation of B-AT.ConclusionBird-assisted therapy was found to be an effective method for reducing anxiety in pediatric dental patients.Clinical significanceBird-assisted therapy is an effective and reliable method for reducing anxiety levels in children. Bird chirping has a profound effect on the human brain and provides more than an auditory experience. It also improves the visual appeal of the dental office and is interesting, affordable, and relatively easy to implement.How to cite this articleDandekar BT, Ahmed A, Agrawal A, et al. ## Introduction
The Latin words “ango, anci” (choking) and “angustus” (stiff) are roots of the word anxiety. According to Freud, anxiety is an ego function that alerts a person to the likelihood of impending danger so they can quickly plan a suitable adaptive response. Anxiety serves as a defense mechanism for the ego since it alerts us to danger; if we do nothing, the threat will only worsen until the ego is destroyed.1
A common ailment that affects people of all ages in many countries is dental anxiety. It is a common psychological response to dental stimulation that has been interpreted as detrimental to the individual. However, children who experience high levels of anxiety typically find dental appointments more difficult. It has been hypothesized that this type of anxiety generates a vicious cycle in which dread of dental pain and anxiety leads to avoidance behavior, which increases fear and anxiety over dental treatment.2
Although the terms “dental anxiety” and “dental phobia” (also known as “dentophobia”) are sometimes substitutable to describe a child's anxious behavior in dental chair, they should be distinguished as separate emotional states. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders,3 dental phobia is defined as “marked and persistent fear that is excessive or unreasonable.”
Excessive fear can lead to panic attacks or severe anxiety. Phobic patients inevitably neglect dental care, which has more serious effects than dental anxiety.
To manage children's dental anxiety, physicians must be ready with a number of strategies.
Several pharmacological and nonpharmacological behavior management techniques have been used. However, pharmacological approaches may not be readily accepted by parents and may include associated medical risk factors.2,4 In recent years, nonpharmacological treatments for anxiety disorders such as music therapy, aromatherapy, and hypnosis have grown in popularity.5–7
Animal-assisted therapy (AAT) is a goal-oriented, structured therapeutic intervention used in various medical specialties including health, education, and human services. Florence Nightingale, a pioneer in contemporary nursing, discovered the therapeutic potential of animals in the late 1800s.8 She observed that pets helped patients feel more relaxed, leading to the popularity of AAT as a relaxation technique and anxiety treatment.9 Recently, bird-assisted therapy (B-AT) has gained importance in relieving anxiety, with finches, budgerigars, and canaries being used with positive results. However, studies on lovebirds have not been conducted.
This study investigates the impact of lovebirds, Lutino Opaline Agapornis roseicollis var, on the human body.10 These colorful, playful, and social birds native to the African continent have a chirping sound that lowers cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine levels, reducing pain and anxiety and promoting relaxation and stress alleviation.11

## Materialsand Methods
This is an experimental study, and before the procedure, their cooperation and informed consent were obtained from the parent or guardian. The approval was obtained from the ethical committee of the institution; also, the CONSORT guidelines were followed in conducting the trial with CTRI [CTRI/2025/09/094114]. The study was conducted from January 2025 to April 2025.

## Study Design
After receiving ethical approval from the institutional review board, the randomized controlled trial was conducted in the pediatric and preventive dentistry department using a parallel arm design and a balanced allocation ratio of 1:1.
The study was a double-blind trial wherein the participants and the statistician were blinded.

## Sample Size Calculation
Sample size estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) by substituting mean and SD from the Modified Child Dental Anxiety Scale (MCDAS) scale obtained from a previous study.11 The power of the study was kept constant at 80%; the total sample size estimated for the present study is 40 at 95% CI; thus, 20 per group will be allocated for equal distribution.

## Randomization
Sixty children were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 13 did not fulfill the inclusion criteria and 7 declined to participate. The remaining 40 children were randomly assigned to the control group (n = 20) or experimental group (n = 20) using the lottery method.

## Inclusion Criteria
Patients experiencing their first dental visit.
Children with positive or negative Frankl behavior.
Children with no learning disability.
The guardian or parents who granted written informed consent.

## Exclusion Criteria
Children with definitely positive or definitely negative Frankl's behavior.
Noncooperative patients that require additional behavior management techniques during the treatment were excluded.
Children with visual and auditory impairment.
Parents who were allergic and fearful to birds.

## Procedure
Our study adhered strictly to the CONSORT guidelines (Fig. 1). 40 children between the age group of 4–8 years underwent dental check-up to record anxiety levels at baseline. Modified Venham's Anxiety Rating Scale (MVARS) (Fig. 2) was used to record anxiety levels, followed by fingertip pulse oximetry and Jeet Wheel Scale (JWS) (Fig. 3).

## Group II (Experimental Group)
Twenty children underwent a dental checkup in the presence of a caged bird. For B-AT, a pair of healthy lovebirds who had received certification from an animal practitioner were chosen. They were kept in a robust cage made of stainless-steel mesh that was 35 × 30 × 33.3 cm in size (Fig. 5).
A pair of lovebirds
The birds were fed organic mixed seeds and had daily access to clean water. The cage's cleanliness was meticulously preserved.
The natural color of the Lutino Opaline (lovebird) includes a bright yellow body and red head with a red-ringed eye. These creatures are small, bold, and chatty. Measuring from their beak to the tips of their tail feathers, they are 5 to 7 inches long. Their size makes them easy to house, which makes them a better choice for the study. They are child-friendly birds, and the female bird sings better than the male.
In order to keep a consistent distance from patient, the caged bird was tied to the handle of dental chair so that the bird was visible and audible from chair site (Fig. 6). After oral screening, children's anxiety levels were measured using same approach as before.
Oral screening with B-AT

## Assessment of Anxiety
Modified Venham's Anxiety Rating scale (MVARS): The scale has five points, each of which is based on measurable, objective, and easily observable behavior. The clinician uses it to evaluate child's situational anxiety. It is an interval rating scale with a valid and dependable rating process that is simple to use in clinical or research endeavors (Fig. 7).
Finger pulse oximeter (Dr. Morepen): For measuring pulse rate, which is a direct indicator of physiological arousal since its increase is attributed to stress during a dental visit (Fig. 8).
Jeet Wheel Scale (JWS): It is an entirely novel, kid-friendly instrument for evaluating dental anxiety. The eight emoticon images—excited, happy, angry, sad, sobbing, frustrated, scared, and shouting—are grouped in segments on a wheel and are each accompanied with a color, such as orange, yellow, red, pink, sky blue, green, purple, and white. The patients were asked to rotate the wheel and asked them to stop the wheel where they matched their feelings. All emotions were mixed up and not shown in a positive-to-negative or vice versa order in order to remove bias (Fig. 9). After being recorded, values were statistically analyzed.

## Statistical Analysis
All data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 19.0 statistical analysis software. The level of significance was set at 5% (i.e., p < 0.001). Intragroup comparisons for pulse rate were performed by paired t-test, for MVARS and JWS by Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For intergroup comparison, pulse rate was compared by unpaired t-test and for MVARS and JWS by Mann–Whitney U test.

## Results
Comparison of anxiety levels in control and experimental groups is depicted in Table 1, and Figure 10 shows a statistically significant reduction in anxiety in the experimental group following oral screening. Utilizing the JWS and the Mann–Whitney U test, we detected a decrease in anxiety scores after the intervention in the experimental group. The mean anxiety level in the control group after oral screening was found to be 3.3, and in the experimental group it was decreased to 1.5. The result advocates that B-AT might be effective in reducing anxiety levels in children.

## Discussion
The first dental appointment is crucial for reducing or extending dental fear in children, as an unpleasant experience can lead to a negative mindset toward the dentist, making future visits more stressful. Understanding emotions is essential for competent social behavior and an appropriate mental state level.
According to Harris, the majority of youngsters understand by the age of four that the events or results of a given circumstance might evoke emotions.12 A distraction technique is used to draw a patient's focus away from their present behavior and onto something else, and it has proven to be a great option with minimal risk.13 As there are two types of distraction (active and passive), children < 8 years old show more receptivity toward passive distraction.14 As children in the age group of 4–8 years show the most disruptive or negative behavior and are difficult to manage, they were selected in this study.15
The Lutino Opaline bird pair was utilized in this study due to their calming chattering and chirping, which allowed them to partially isolate themselves from the stressful dental environment, making them a useful tool in B-AT for reducing anxiety, utilizing both vision and hearing senses.
Anxiety measurement was done by objective, subjective, and physiological means, which were the modified Venham's anxiety rating scale, Jeet Wheel Scale, and Finger pulse oximeter, respectively.
Stress triggers the autonomic nervous system, causing adrenaline release and a rise in heart rate. The amygdala, the integrative center for emotions like fear and anxiety, is activated by stress.16 The central nucleus of the amygdala stimulates autonomic arousal responses to fear or threat signals, raising heart rate.17 This activation of the sympathetic nervous system is an index of a patient's response to dental stimuli, as it controls many aspects of the fear response.
The Jeet Wheel Scale (JWS) was used in this study because it satisfies every requirement for an ideal anxiety scale. It was found to be easy to understand by children of both sexes. The scale was scored from 1 to 8, with excited = 1, happy = 2, sad = 3, scared = 4, frustrated = 5, angry = 6, crying = 7, and shouting = 8. The children were instructed to indicate which figure best represented the feeling they were experiencing at the time. Additionally, it appears colorful and interesting. It provides an immediate dental anxiety score. Moreover, it has a number of benefits for younger kids with limited language and cognitive abilities. According to Chakraborty et al., the novel JWS is validated by the high degree of agreement between JWS and both the VPT and FIS.18
The Modified Venham's anxiety rating scale (MVARS) is a reliable, valid, and easily integrated five-point scale used by clinicians to measure a child's situational anxiety, focusing on specific, observable behavior and easily integrated into clinical or research activities.19
According to Joybell et al., claim that listening to birds chirping causes the release of oxytocin and endorphins. It helps reduce the patient's pain sensations by lowering cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine levels, thereby reducing anxiety and promoting tranquility. It promotes relaxation and stress alleviation by having a calming influence.11

## Conclusion
Bird-assisted therapy is a proven method to reduce anxiety levels in children, with its profound effects on the human brain, mood, stress, cognitive performance, and bond with nature. It also enhances the visual appeal of dental offices, is affordable, and is easy to use.

## Clinical Significance
Bird-assisted therapy is a proven method for reducing children's anxiety levels, enhancing mood, reducing stress, sharpening the mind, and strengthening connections to nature. It also enhances the aesthetic appeal of dental offices and is engaging, reasonably priced, and user-friendly.
