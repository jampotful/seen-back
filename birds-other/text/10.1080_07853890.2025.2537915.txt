A 20-year retrospective analysis of ocular and adnexal injuries resulting from animal attacks in Eastern China.

# A 20-year retrospective analysis of ocular and adnexal injuries resulting from animal attacks in Eastern China

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess the clinical characteristics, management strategies, and outcomes of ocular and adnexal injuries caused by animal attacks.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients hospitalized for animal-induced eye injuries at the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University (2003–2023). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University (Ref. No. 2024146, August 2024). Informed consent was waived. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical presentations, treatment approaches, and outcomes were systematically reviewed.ResultsSixteen patients with ocular injuries from animal attacks were included, with 75.0% being male (p < 0.05) and a median age of 13.5 years (range: 2–69 years). Injuries were most commonly caused by birds (5 cases), followed by dogs (4), cats (2), fish (2), wasps (2), and bees (1). Most injuries occurred during attempts to approach or interact with the animals. Corneal injury was most frequent (12 cases, p < 0.05), followed by eyelid and canalicular lacerations, cataracts, endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, globe rupture, penetrating ocular injuries, and orbital fractures. Treatments started on average 4.03 ± 4.98 days post-injury. Surgeries included eyelid/canalicular repair, globe reconstruction, lens extraction, and pars plana vitrectomy (PPV). Cataracts (p < 0.05) and retinal detachment (p ≤ 0.01) were linked to poorer initial visual acuity (LogMAR). Post-treatment VA improved significantly, from 2.4 to 0.19 (p ≤ 0.01), especially in lens surgery patients (p < 0.05).ConclusionsAnimal-induced ocular and adnexal injuries predominantly affect males and may cause irreversible visual impairment. Cataracts and retinal detachment predict poor initial vision. Early diagnosis and timely surgery, especially lens surgery, are essential for recovery. Public education and prevention are essential to reduce such injuries.Keywords: Animal attack, Ocular, Adnexal, Visual acuity

## Introduction
Animal attacks result in cause severe ocular injuries, posing significant social and medical challenges. The type of animal responsible for these injuries is influenced by demographic, lifestyle, and geographical factors, with dogs [1–3], cats [4,5], birds [6,7], bees [8,9], and arachnids [10,11] being the most common implicated. Despite the frequency of such incidents, there remains a notable lack of long-term, institution-based studies investigating the clinical characteristics and outcomes of animal-induced ocular trauma.
The demographics, clinical presentations, management strategies, and prognoses of patients with animal-induced ocular injuries vary widely depending on the type of animal involved. Children are particularly susceptible compared to adults [1,2,12–14]. A broad spectrum of clinical manifestations has been documented, with corneal disorders being the most commonly observed [4–6,9,15–17]. Periocular trauma—such as eyelid lacerations, canalicular injuries [1,3], and orbital fractures [18]—is also frequently encountered, although these injuries are generally less severe and tend to have a limited impact on visual acuity.
Nevertheless, a considerable number of patients sustain more severe injuries, including corneal lacerations [4–6,15], traumatic cataracts [4,6], endophthalmitis [6,8], globe ruptures [15], and optic neuropathy [19], all of which can lead to profound and often irreversible visual impairment.
Despite the existence of isolated case reports and short-term studies, long-term, institution-based evidence on animal-induced ocular trauma remains limited. To address this gap and gain deeper insights into the etiologies, clinical outcomes, and limitations of current prevention strategies, we conducted a comprehensive retrospective analysis of ocular injuries caused by animal attacks over a 20-year period (2003–2023) at our institution, a leading tertiary ophthalmic center in China. The extended study duration and high patient volume provide a strong foundation for identifying long-term trends and patterns in the epidemiology and management of these injuries.

## Methods
This retrospective study included 16 patients (4 females and 12 males) who were admitted to the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University, Shanghai, between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2023, for ocular and adnexal injuries resulting from animal attacks. As one of the largest referral centers for ophthalmic diseases in Eastern China, the hospital provides specialized care for complex and severe cases.
This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University (Reference No. 2024146; August 2024). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the Committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
Inclusion criteria consisted of a documented history of animal attacks leading to ocular and/or adnexal injuries, hospitalization for treatment, and a minimum 6 months of post-surgical follow-up. Due to the extended study period (2003–2023), some patients treated in earlier years could not be contacted for long-term follow-up, primarily due to the limited availability of mobile phones or other communication methods at the time. Consequently, a six-month minimum follow-up period was established to ensure consistency and reliability in data analysis.
Data collected included patient demographics, type of animal attack, slit–lamp examination findings, surgical interventions performed, and outcomes at final follow-up. Mechanical injury classification was based on the Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology (BETT) system [20], which defines an open globe injury as a full-thickness wound of the sclera and/or cornea. Within this system, a rupture is characterized as an inside-out injury caused by blunt trauma, while a penetrating injury refers to a sharp object causing a single full-thickness wound. These criteria were applied retrospectively in this study to categorize the types of mechanical ocular injuries.
Primary ophthalmic evaluations included documented visual acuity (VA) assessments and slit–lamp examinations of the anterior and posterior segments. VA measurements were reported using the logMAR scale [21–23]. Values for qualitative VA assessments were converted to logMAR equivalents as follows: ‘counting fingers’ (CF) corresponded to a logMAR value of 2.1, ‘hand motion’ (HM) to 2.4, ‘light perception’ (LP) to 2.7, and ‘no light perception’ (NLP) to 3.0, respectively.
The Seidel test was employed to evaluate corneal integrity, and B-scan ultrasonography was performed to evaluate posterior segment conditions in patients without open globe injuries. For those with open globe injuries, orbital computed tomography (CT) scans were reviewed to identify orbital abnormalities and assess posterior segment conditions. In cases diagnosed with endophthalmitis secondary to animal attacks, intraocular samples (aqueous and vitreous) were obtained and submitted for microbiological analysis, including Gram staining and culture. Although some patients had received initial first aid—such as tetanus or rabies vaccinations, wound debridement, or preliminary repairs at other facilities—definitive management of ocular trauma was primarily performed at Eye Trauma Center of our hospital. Surgical interventions were individualized based on each patient’s specific condition and included surgical repair of eyelid and canalicular lacerations, globe ruptures or penetrating injury repair, lens surgery, pars plana vitrectomies (PPV), intraocular foreign body removal, and orbital fracture reconstruction.
Data analysis was conducted using R statistical software (R Core Team, Version 3.6.1). Given the non-normal distribution of the data, continuous variables were presented as median with ranges (minimum-maximum), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Pre- and post-surgical VA values were compared using the Wilcoxon test. Given the inclusion of only 16 patients were included over a 20-year period, a formal power analysis was not conducted to assess sample size adequacy. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The median age of patients with animal-induced ocular injuries was 13.5 years (range: 2–69 years). Injuries were more prevalent in males, accounting for 12 cases (75%, p < 0.05). A history of hypertension was reported in two patients (12.5%) reported a history of hypertension. The types of animal encounters varied: five patients (31.2%) sustained injuries from birds, four (25.0%) from dog bites, two each (12.5%) from cat scratches, fish strikes, or wasp stings. One patient (6.3%) was injured by a bee sting.
Clinical manifestations varied significantly among patients. Eyelid lacerations were observed in two patients (Row 8 and 9; 12.5%) following dog bites, with one case (Row 8; 6.3%) involving canalicular (lacrimal system) injury. Corneal involvement was the most common presentation, occurring in 12 patients (75.0%, p < 0.05). Among these, corneal laceration was identified in nine patients (56.3%), and sterile keratitis was noted in four patients (25.0%). Penetrating globe injuries involving the cornea were observed in nine patients (56.3%), four of whom also had concurrent scleral involvement. Globe rupture with scleral laceration was diagnosed in one patient following a fish strike (Row 15; 6.3%%) at the initial clinic presentation.
Cataracts were present in 10 patients (62.5%) and endophthalmitis was diagnosed in five patients (31.2%). One patient injured by a bee sting presented with a corneal foreign body. Additionally, an orbital fracture was reported in one patient sustained following trauma from a fish head strike (Row 16).
These findings highlight that corneal disorders were the most prevalent clinical manifestation associated with ocular and adnexal injuries resulting from animal attacks (p < 0.05).

## Management
The mean interval from the animal attack to the initial intervention for ocular injuries was 4.03 ± 4.98 days (interquartile range: 0.67–17 days). Patients received a range of surgical treatments, with some requiring multiple surgeries based on the severity and complexity of their injuries. ## Follow‑up results
The median follow-up duration for patients who underwent ocular surgery was 4.41 ± 5.18 years, ranging from 0.5 to 20 years. VA values before treatment and at the final follow-up are summarized in Tables 1 and 3.
Pre-treatment VA was generally poor, with only five individuals (31.3%) achieving a VA of 0.7 or better (reported in logMAR). However, significant improvement was observed post-treatment, with mean VA improving from 2.40 to 0.19 logMAR (p ≤ 0.01). By the final follow-up, 11 patients had achieved a VA of 0.7 or better.

Cataracts were significantly associated with poorer baseline VA. Patients with cataracts exhibited a median pre-treatment VA of 2.70 logMAR, compared to 0.37 logMAR in those without cataracts (p1 < 0.05), indicating markedly worse initial VA in the cataract group. Following lens surgery, VA in cataract patients improved markedly from 2.70 to 0.52 logMAR, a significant gain compared to patients without cataracts (p2 < 0.05), underscoring the therapeutic benefit of lens extraction.
Patients with retinal detachment demonstrated significantly poorer baseline VA, with a median of 3.00 logMAR compared to 2.40 logMAR in those without retinal detachment (p1 ≤ 0.01). Postoperatively, VA in patients without retinal detachment improved markedly from 2.40 to 0.16 logMAR (p2 ≤ 0.01). In contrast, patients with retinal detachment showed no significant visual improvement following surgery, highlighting the limited prognosis and poorer visual outcomes associated with retinal detachment.
VA did not differ significantly between patients with corneal involvement or endophthalmitis. Furthermore, VA outcomes were not significantly influenced by gender, age, type of animal attack, or the laterality of the affected eye.

## Discussion
Ocular injuries resulting from animal attacks are relatively rare, with only 16 patients requiring hospitalization and surgical intervention at the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University over the past two decades. This study demonstrated that such injuries—caused by birds, dogs, cats, fish, wasps, and bees—predominantly affected males. Corneal disorders were the most commonly observed clinical feature. Patients presenting with cataracts or retinal detachment presented with significantly poorer VA at baseline. A range of surgical treatments were administered based on the specific type and severity of injury leading to substantial post-treatment improvement in VA, particularly among patients with cataracts and those without retinal detachment.
Previous research has documented ocular and adnexal injuries caused by birds, often attributed to their attraction to the cornea, the contrasting color of the iris, eye movement in humans [24]. The prevalence and type of bird-related injuries are also influenced by demographics and lifestyle factors. Chickens and roosters are frequently implicated in ocular injuries [12,13,25,26], whereas attacks by mynah bird are more commonly reported in Iran and the Middle East [6]. Children are particularly susceptible to these injuries due to their innate curiosity and the territorial nature of many bird species [12,13].
Consistent with prior findings, all five patients in this study who sustained bird-related ocular injuries were children between 4 and 14 years of age [12,13]. Among these cases, one injury was caused by a rooster, while the remaining four attributed to egret attacks. Egrets, long-necked waterfowl with sharp beaks appeared to attack the children during close-range interactions, likely driven by birds’ curiosity. Prior studies have reported cataracts in 36.7% of bird-related ocular injuries, corneal disorders in 80%, and endophthalmitis in 10% of cases [6]. In contrast, all five patients (100%) in this study developed both cataracts and ocular penetrating injuries, with two cases (40%) progressing to endophthalmitis following the bird attacks.
Surgical interventions included corneal repair, lens surgery, and PPV, as indicated by the severity of each case. Notably, one patient who had previously undergone primary corneal repair at a local hospital for a rooster-induced injury required subsequent enucleation. This was necessitated by a nonfunctional eye exhibiting extensive corneoscleral staphyloma, cataract formation, and retinal detachment three years post-injury.
Retrospective studies have reported that dogs are account for 5.8% to 27% of ocular and adnexal injuries [14,15,27,28], with children being disproportionately affected [1,2,14,15]. Prendes et al. found that children are 4.2 times more likely than adults to sustain ocular trauma from dog bites [15]. The average age of affected children was 14.1 ± 1.9 years, with reported cases ranging from 4 months to 5 years [29,30]. The dog breeds involved vary by region; however, some studies have identified pit bulls as common offenders, often being the victims’ own pets [15,18]. Dog bites can result in significant morbidity, with frequent injuries including canalicular damage, eyelid lacerations, and orbital fractures—typically managed in emergency or inpatient settings [1,3,18,31–34]. Dogs often target the central face region [3], and less common complications include corneal abrasions, conjunctival injuries, infections, and globe ruptures [15].
Consistent with previous studies [1,2,14,15], all four patients in this study who sustained dog bite–related ocular injuries were children aged 2 to 13 years. The injuries were inflicted by family pets or neighbors’ dogs during playtime in rural regions of China. Clinical features included eyelid lacerations (50%), scleral lacerations (50%), canalicular lacerations (25%), endophthalmitis (25%), ocular penetrating injuries (50%), cataracts (25%), retinal detachment (25%), and sterile keratitis (25%). Management strategies were individualized according to the specific injuries and involved corneoscleral and eyelid suturing, lacrimal duct surgery, lens surgery, and PPV.
Ocular injuries resulting from cat attacks or scratches are relatively rare. Cariello reported that such injuries accounted for only 0.4% of ocular trauma cases in Brazilian emergency departments [35]. Holzer described a case of a bobcat attack in a 33-year-old man that resulted in corneal laceration and traumatic cataract [4]. Chang documented three cases—two children and one adult—with full-thickness corneal lacerations by cat scratches [5]. Another study noted similar findings, with corneal lacerations and cataracts reported in two pediatric cases involving cat-related trauma [36]. In the present study, two adult patients sustained severe ocular injuries inflicted by domestic cats attempting to escape after being startled by unfamiliar individuals. Both patients presented with ocular penetrating injuries (100%), cataracts (100%) and endophthalmitis (50%). Immediate interventions included primary repair of ocular penetrating injuries, lens surgery, and PPV.
Bees and wasps, members of the order Hymenoptera, can induce both local and systemic reactions through their stings [37], including ocular injuries. The majority of reported cases occur in adults [16,17,38–43], with clinical manifestations ranging from relatively mild conditions such as conjunctivitis and retained foreign bodies to severe complications including cataracts, glaucoma, retinal detachment, endophthalmitis, and optic neuropathy [8,16,17,37–44]. Notably, visual outcomes tend to be worse following wasp stings compared to bee stings [37,45].
In this study, three adult male patients sustained ocular injuries resulting from bee or wasp stings. One patient, stung by a bee, presented with sterile keratitis and corneal foreign bodies. Following corneal debridement and removal of foreign body, his vision improved acuity to 20/20 on the Snellen chart (0 reported in logMAR). In contrast, the two patients who suffered wasps stings experienced severe ocular complications, including sterile keratitis (100%), retinal detachment (100%), cataracts (50%), and endophthalmitis (50%). Both patients had NLP immediately post-injury, and despite undergoing anterior chamber irrigation or PPV, their visual acuity remained NLP. The poor visual prognosis was attributed to the potent cytotoxic effects of wasp venom and associated immune-mediated inflammatory responses [46].
While ocular injuries caused by fish hooks are well-documented [47,48], direct trauma from the fish themselves is less frequently reported. In this study, two cases occurred during fish farming activities, where slippery fish caused accidental ocular trauma. One patient sustained an orbital fracture and underwent orbital reconstruction with a prefabricated titanium plate. The other patient suffered a complex injury, including scleral laceration, cataract, retinal detachment, and globe rupture. Multiple surgical interventions were performed, including primary repair, lens surgery, and vitrectomy. Despite these efforts, the patient developed phthisis bulbi and subsequently implantation of a FCVB. FCVB is a promising technique for preservation in cases of severe trauma cases requiring silicone oil tamponade [49–51]. In the current study, FCVB was successfully applied in a complex trauma scenario, reflecting adherence to contemporary best surgical practices.
Patients in our study presented with complex ocular and adnexal injuries resulting from animal attacks. Recognizing the specific injury patterns associated with different animal encounters and assessing the effectiveness of tailored treatment approaches are essential for guiding clinicians in formulating targeted management strategies to optimize visual outcomes. At presentation, many patients—particularly those with cataracts and retinal detachment—demonstrated significantly compromised visual acuity. Individualized treatments plans were implemented according to the nature and severity of the injuries, leading to substantial improvements in VA, particularly among patients who underwent lens surgeries. To further enhance visual prognoses in such patients, we advocate for the refinement of emergency response protocols and the standardization of advanced surgical care practices.
Our analysis of the injury circumstances revealed the median age of the patients was 13.5 years, underscoring that ocular trauma predominantly occurred during close interactions between children and animals. These findings highlight the urgent need for public education campaigns aimed at raising awareness of the risks associated with approaching animals—particularly in high-risk settings—as a preventive strategy to reduce the incidence of such injuries.
As a retrospective analysis of only 16 cases of animal attack-induced ocular and adnexal injuries hospitalized at the Eye and ENT Hospital of Fudan University from 2003 to 2023, this study is inherently limited by its small sample size. To enhance the robustness and applicability of future findings, longitudinal data collection is recommended to track patient outcomes over time. Moreover, multicenter studies would enhance the generalizability and clinical relevance of the results, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the risk factors, injury patterns, and optimal management strategies for ocular trauma resulting from animal attacks.

## Conclusion
Ocular and adnexal injuries resulting from animal attacks can lead to substantial visual impairment and present considerable clinical complexity. This study identified a predominance of male patients, with cataracts and retinal detachment significantly contributing to poor visual acuity at presentation. Timely intervention and individualized surgical treatment—particularly lens surgery—were crucial in achieving favorable visual outcomes. To strengthen the clinical evidence base and inform standardized treatment protocols, future research should focus on larger, multicenter cohorts to validate these findings and develop evidence-based guidelines for the management of animal-induced ocular trauma.
