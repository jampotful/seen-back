Photoperiodic schedules during rearing and laying stages modulate photoreceptors transcription and the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis in male White King pigeons.
Yan L, Feng M, Feng C, Chen Z, Sun X, Zhaorigetu, Guo B, Zhu H. (2026). Poultry science. . doi:10.1016/j.psj.2025.106367. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Photoperiodic schedules during rearing and laying stages modulate photoreceptors transcription and the hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular axis in male White King pigeons

## Abstract
AbstractThe photoperiod during rearing and laying period is a primary environmental regulator of reproductive performance in poultry, yet its effects on male breeding pigeons have been largely unexplored. Therefore, this study evaluated the impact of four photoperiod regimes on the reproductive performance, testicular morphology, hormone profiles, and gene expression of hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular axis in male White King pigeons. From 101 d of age, the pigeons in the control group were exposed to a natural photoperiod until 160 d, and then to a photoperiod of 16 h (16 light (L): 8 dark (D)) and lasted for 30 weeks. Pigeons in the three experimental groups were exposed to a short photoperiod of 8L: 16D until 160 d, and then to 14L: 10D (S-14L:10D), 16L: 8D (S-16L:8D), and 18L: 6D (S-18L:16D), respectively. The results showed that light restriction (8L:16D) during the rearing period and then 14L:10D or 16L:8D photostimulation produced the highest egg output and fertility rates. Pigeons under S‑14L:10D exhibited larger bilateral testes, increased seminiferous‑tubule diameter and epithelial thickness, elevated numbers of spermatogenic cells, significantly higher plasma FSH, LH and testosterone concentrations, while reduced prolactin. Under the S-14L:10D photoperiod, GnRH, FSH, LH-receptor and steroidogenic enzymes were up‑regulated, alongside down‑regulated GnIH, VIP, PRL. This optimal light program also increased expression of deep‑brain photoreceptors (neuropsin, melanopsin, vertebrate ancient-opsin, pinopsin) and suppressed retinal opsins, suggesting a shift toward non‑visual photoperception that drives reproductive activation. These findings demonstrate that a S-14L:10D light schedule from rearing period optimally stimulates hormonal, hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular (HPT) axis genes expression, and morphological pathways, thereby maximizing reproductive performance in commercial pigeon production.Keywords: Photoperiod, Male pigeon, Testicular development, Photoreceptor, Reproduction

## Introduction
The domestic pigeon (Columba livia) is type of poultry with considerable economic, ornamental, and racing value. Pigeon meat has gained popularity among consumers due to its low-cholesterol and high-protein content, as well as its favorable palatability (Pomianowski et al., 2009; Kokoszynski et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2024). Nevertheless, compared to other commercially used birds, pigeons exhibited relatively poor reproductive performance: the average clutch interval can be as long as 47.44 d (Khargharia et al., 2003), and a paired pigeon typically lays only two eggs per laying cycle. In addition to egg production, the fertilization rate of breeding eggs is a critical determinant of overall reproductive efficiency and economic return in the poultry industry. In fact, the fertilization rate of most pigeon breeds is below 90%, and in some cases it drops below 85% (Wang et al., 2015, 2017a; Yan et al., 2024). This suboptimal reproductive performance hampers the ability to meet the growing market demand for pigeon meat and eggs. Consequently, breeders and researches are actively seeking strategies to improve pigeon reproductive efficiency. Currently, although several interventions (e.g., nutrition adjustment, light management) have been explored to enhance egg production in breeding pigeons (Cooper, 1976; Wang et al., 2015, 2017a, 2019; Peng et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025), few studies have identified effective methods to increase egg fertilization rate (Wang et al., 2017a; Yan et al., 2024).
Fertilization success in poultry is predominantly determined by the combined contributions of both males and females. Accordingly, female egg quality and male semen quality are the two pivotal factors governing fertilization efficiency, rendering improvement a complex challenge in poultry production. Males can contribute up to 50 % of the maximum number of fertile hatching eggs in breeding stock flocks. Beyond genetic determinants, a range of environmental factors, including nutrition condition, photoperiod, ambient temperature, and health status-exert profound impacts on testicular development and semen quality in male poultry (Bartman et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2025). Notably, poultry exhibit high sensitivity to light, and light management systems have been shown to directly or indirectly regulate reproductive performance, including fertilization rates (Lewis and Morris, 2000). Hence, targeted differential photostimulation emerges as a promising strategy to mitigate low fertilization rates by acting as a reproduction-enhancing intervention.
In birds, light perception is a sophisticated process that occurs primarily at two functionally distinct classes of photoreceptors: retinal photoreceptors located in eyes and extra-retinal deep-brain photoreceptors (DBPs) situated in structures such as the pineal gland, pituitary, and hypothalamus (Menaker and Underwood, 1976; Chaurasia et al., 2005; Nakane et al., 2010). Both retinal and extra-retinal photoreceptive systems depend on a family of light-sensitive proteins known as opsins to convert light energy into biochemical signals. Identified opsin subtypes include neuropsin (OPN5), melanopsin (OPN4), encephalopsin (OPN3), pinopsin (OPNP) and vertebrate ancient-opsin (VA opsin) (Shichida and Matsuyama, 2009; Kang and Kuenzel, 2015; Kuenzel et al., 2015; Guido et al., 2022).
Upon illumination, photoreceptors convey light signals to the pars tuberalis (PT), which are subsequently transformed into hormonal cues within the hypothalamus, thereby regulating reproductive activities (Nakane and Yoshimura, 2010; Dardente et al., 2014; Zaguri et al., 2020; Pérez, 2022). This cascade constitutes the hypothalamic‑pituitary‑gonadal (HPG) axis. Specifically, the hypothalamus secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), whose activity is mediated by its receptor (GnRHR). The GnRH-GnRHR signaling pathway drives the synthesis and release of downstream hormones from the pituitary and gonads, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), estrodial (E2), progesterone (P4), and testosterone (T) (Padmanabhan et al., 2002; Stamatiades and Kaiser, 2018). Collectively, these hormones play pivotal roles in driving spermatogenesis in male and oogenesis in females-processes underpin normal fertilization potential. Beyond gonadotropin regulation, GnRH interacts with vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) to promote pituitary prolactin (PRL) secretion, a hormone that modulates reproductive timing and broody behavior in poultry. Conversely, hypothalamic gonadotropin inhibitory hormone (GnIH) exerts an opposing effect by suppressing GnRH synthesis and release, thereby fine-tuning HPG axis activity (Tsutsui et al., 2000; Ubuka et al., 2012).
To date, although the influence of light on poultry reproduction has been investigated for decades, the specific effects of differential photostimulation on male reproductive traits have received far less attention than those on females (Sharp, 1993; Zawilska et al., 2007; Geng et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2019a; Bartman et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2021). This research gap is especially pronounced in pigeons. As a photoperiod-dependent breeder species, pigeons require prolonged light exposure, and natural light may not fully satisfy reproductive needs. Moreover, gonadal development during critical phase-namely the rearing period or the pre-lay period (a window spanning puberty to sexual maturation) plays a decisive role in shaping long-term reproductive productivity and impacting on the overall laying performance of breeding poultry (Yuan et al., 1994; Bestman et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2017b; Dijkslag et al., 2023). Against this backdrop, the present study evaluated a 3-phase photoperiodic program applied during both the rearing and laying periods, assessing its impact on reproductive parameters, plasma hormone concentrations, and testicular development in breeding male pigeons. Particular emphasis was placed on elucidating the underlying regulatory mechanisms, including hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular (HPT) axis modulation and the expression of retinal and deep-brain photoreceptors.

## Materials and methods
All procedures employed in this study were approved by the Research Committee of the Jiangsu Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IACUC number: NKYVET 2014-63). Moreover, all methods and management procedures complied with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals issued by the State Council of the People’s Republic of China.

## Experimental animals and diets
The experiment was conducted in Nanjing (118° 61′ E, 31° 87′ N), Jiangsu Province, starting on July 28, 2023. A total of 400 young White King pigeons, 60 d old and with comparable body weights (518.3 ± 0.54 g), were provided by a commercial pigeon farm (Jiangsu Cuigu Pigeon Industry Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Throughout the one-year experimental period, ambient temperature (10-30 ℃) and relative humidity were maintained consistently across the four barns. From 61 to 160 d, pigeons were fed a corn- and pea-based grower diet (11.2 MJ/kg and 12.0% crude protein). Thereafter, the diet was switched to a breeding formulation (12.2 MJ/kg and 15.0% crude protein). Pigeons received feed twice daily (at 7:00 a.m. and 3:00 p.m.) and had ad libitum access to water for the entire study. Each breeding pair pigeons reared two squabs, which were nourished with crop milk secreted by the parents.

## Photoperiodic program
All pigeons were assigned to either a two-phase (natural-long photoperiod) or three-phase (natural-short-long) photoperiodic program, as described in our previous work (Yan et al., 2024). The detailed protocol was as follows. From 61 to 100 d of age, birds were exposed to natural daylight with photoperiod lengths ranging from 12.5 h to 11.3 h (derived from date supplied by the Nanjing Meteorological Bureau, Nanjing City, China). To prevent premature pairing, males and females were housed separately in two online flat cages within the same barn; sex was determined by gender identification and each bird’s feet were marked with gender-specific tags. Each flat cage accommodated 200 individuals of a single sex. Gender identification was conducted before 60 d of age and performed by the following molecular biology method. Briefly, due to the difference between the sex chromosomes ZW (female) and ZZ (male) of male and female pigeons, specific upstream and downstream primers (F:TCCTATGCCTACCACC TTCCT, R: 5′-TTGAGAAAGAATCTGACCTGAACC) were designed for a unique EE0.6 sequence fragment on a W chromosome of the female pigeons; then the PCR amplification was conducted using the genomic DNA from feathers with hair follicles as a template and using above primers; finally, PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis and stained with Ethidium Bromide, and the genders were judged according to the number of bands (one band can be identified as female, while no band can be identified as male).
Beginning on day 101, the pigeons were randomly allocated to four groups, each housed in a separate barn. Every group comprised 50 males and 50 females, distributed among 10 large cages (5 male cages and 5 female cages). Each cage contained 10 pigeons of the same sex. One male cage paired with one female cage constituted a single replicate; thus, each treatment group contained five replicates, yielding 20 squabs per replicate. The control group was maintained under a natural photoperiod until 160 d, during which daily light exposure varied between 13.5 h and 11.7 h. Beginning on 161d, the photoperiod for this group was gradually extended to a 16‑hour light (L)/8‑hour dark (D) schedule (N-16L:8D). The three experimental groups were initially subjected to a short photoperiod of 8 h (8L:16D) from 101 d to 160d. This regime was achieved by covering the windows of the pigeon barns with opaque sheets from 4:00 to 8:00 a.m. and from 16:00 to 20:00 p.m each day. From 161d onward, the photoperiods for these groups were incrementally increased to 14L:10D (S-14L:10D), 16L:8D (S-16L:8D) and 18L:6D (S-18L:6D), respectively. The detailed photoperiodic programs from the rearing period were summarized in Fig. 1. Thus, the total daily light exposure for all four groups comprised a combination of natural daylight and supplementary artificial lighting.
Schematic diagram of photoperiodic programs from the rearing period. A: Control group, a two-phase photoperiodic program combining natural light during the rearing period and 16 h of light during egg laying period (N-16L:8D); B − D: a three-phase photoperiodic program with a short photoperiod of 8L: 16D (from 101d) until 160 d, and then to 14,16 or 18 h of light during egg laying period (S-14L:10D, S-16L:8D and S-18L:6D).
From the age of 161 d, pigeons within each replicate were allowed to form breeding pairs (combination of free pairing and forced pairing modes), each consisting of one female and one male. Once a pair was successfully established, the birds remained in their original replicate and were housed in a three-layer, three-dimensional cage equipped with a perch and a nest. Artificial illumination was supplied by LED strips, providing an intensity of 10 lx at a head height during rearing (from 61 to 160 d) and 15–25 lx during the pairing and laying periods (from 161d-).
Reproductive performance for each treatment group was monitored for 30 weeks from the first day of laying the first egg first egg in each group, including 5-7 laying cycles. Egg production by each pair was recorded daily. After laying, eggs were marked and hatched by an incubator; the squabs were subsequently returned to their original breeding pair or to another pair within the same group for feeding. All laid eggs were collected and used to calculate the fertility rate. Fertility rate = (number of fertile eggs at 4 d of hatching/ number of incubated eggs) × 100. Hatchability was calculated by the eggs from first 4 laying cycles. Hatchability = (number of hatched squabs/number of fertile eggs) × 100. Each pair of parent pigeons reared two squabs (typical "2 + 2″ production mode in local pigeon breeding enterprise), which were nourished with crop milk secreted by the parent pigeons.

## Blood and tissue sampling
At the conclusion of the 30‑week observation period following the first egg laid in each group, and 12 h after feed withdrawal, two male breeding pigeons exhibiting comparable physiological stages as close as possible (7–10 d after the 2 eggs were laid by the paired pigeon. At this time, the follicles of the breeding pigeons begin to develop) were selected from the five replicates of each group and weighed. This means that 10 male pigeons were collected from each group for subsequent slaughter, hormone testing, gene expression research, and other studies. Blood samples were collected from the wing vein into heparinized syringes, then centrifuged at 3, 000 g at 4°C for 10 min to harvest plasma samples, which was subsequently stored at -20°C until analysis of reproductive hormone concentrations.
Then 40 pigeons (n = 10 per group) were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The entire hypothalamus, pineal gland, and pituitary gland were dissected, and the retina was collected from one eye. Both the left and right testes were weighed and measured to reveal their morphological characteristics. The left/right testicular organ index was calculated using the following formula: left/right testicular organ index (LTI/RTI) (%)= (left/right testis weight (g)/body weight (kg) ×100%. The long and short diameters of both left and right testis were also measured as long diameter of the left/right testis(LTL/RTL), and short diameter of the left/ right testis (LTS/RTS). Tissues samples for the subsequent detection were taken from left testis. All collected tissues from each group were removed concurrently, snap- frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until gene expression assays were performed.

## Histological evaluation of testes
The testes from 6 individuals per experimental group were used for histological examination. A piece of testicular tissue (∼0.5cm*0.5cm*1 cm) from the left testis of each male pigeon was immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h. Following dehydration and embedding procedures described in our previous work (Zhu et al., 2019a), 5-μm-thick sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using an automated slide stainer (Leica, ST5020, Germany). The stained sections were observed and photographed with an Olympus BX63 microscope (Olympus SZX10, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Images of the testicular sections were captured at 8.0 × and 30.0 × magnifications using Case Viewer, and quantitative analyses were performed with Image-Pro Plus 6.0. For each section, six randomly selected fields of view were measured to determine diameter of seminiferous tubule, thickness of seminiferous epithelium and the number of spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids and spermatozoa.

## Determination of hormone concentrations
The concentrations of steroid hormones were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Commercial ELISA kits for follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and testosterone (T) were purchased from Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), whereas prolactin (PRL) levels were measured using a sandwich ELISA developed in our laboratory (Chen et al., 2019). Optical density was read at 450 nm on an Infinite F50 microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland) within 15 min of substrate addition. Hormone concentrations were derived from standard curves with the following ranges: FSH, 0-8 mIU/mL; LH, 0-160 ng/mL; T, 0-320 pg/mL; PRL, 0-12.5 ng/mL.

## RNA isolation, and Real-Time Quantitative PCR
Total RNA (1 μg) was extracted from hypothalamus, pineal gland, pituitary gland, and testis samplesof three goose breeds and reverse-transcribed into cDNA as described in our previous study (Dai et al., 2024). Gene-specific mRNA expression was assessed by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Primers were designed based on the Columba livia reference sequences of in NCBI using Primer 6.0 (see Table 1 for details), except for primers for LH gene. Primers for LH gene are a gift from Prof. Wang in Yangzhou University and they used the primers in previous research (Miao et al., 2024). qRT-PCR reactions (20-μL) were performed with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Relative expression levels of these genes were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method and normalized to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

## Statistical Analysis
Data are presented as the arithmetic mean and standard error of the mean (Mean ± SEM) for each group. Plasma concentrations of steroid hormones across the four photoperiodic regimes were compared using ANOVA. All statistical analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 19.0; IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA). Differences were deemed statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05 and highly significant at P ≤ 0.01.

## Results

## Influence of photoperiods on reproductive activity and testicular development
Fig. 2 illustrates the reproductive performance of breeding pigeons subjected to four different photoperiodic programs during the rearing and laying periods. Egg production was significantly influenced by the lighting schedules (Fig. 2A). The S-16L:8D group produced an average of 13.6 eggs over the 30-week period, which was markedly higher than the control N-16L:8D group (P < 0.01), the S-14L:10D group (P < 0.05) and the S-18L:6D group (P < 0.05). Egg production in the S-14L:10D group also exceeded that of the control group and the S-18L:6D group (P < 0.05). Compared with the control group, the fertility rates of S-14L:10D and S-16L:8D groups were significantly increased (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B), whereas no significant difference was observed between the control (N-16L:8D) and the longer-day S-18L:6D regimen. Hatchability of fertilized eggs was not influenced by photoperiod (P > 0.05, Fig. 2C).
Egg production, fertility rate and hatchability of White King pigeons exposed to different photoperiods. A, B, and C: egg production, fertility rate and hatchability of fertilized eggs, respectively. Consecutive different letters (a-b, b-c) above the bars indicate P < 0.05, and discrete letters (a-c) indicate P < 0.01, the same as below.
Photoperiodic conditions from rearing through laying also modulated testicular development (Fig. 3). Bilateral testicular size and volume were larger in pigeons reared under short-day conditions than in those maintained on the control photoperiod during the rearing period. The left testicular organ index (LTI) of the S-16L:8D group was significantly greater than that of the N-16L:8D group (Fig. 3A, P < 0.05) and the S-18L:6D group (P < 0.01). Both the N-16L:8D and S-14L:10D groups exhibited higher LTI than the S-18L:6D group (P < 0.05), while differences between the S-14L:10D and S-16L:8D groups were minor. For the right testicular organ index (RTI), values were higher in the S‑14L:10D group than in the N‑16L:8D and S‑16L:8D groups (P < 0.05) and the S‑18L:6D group (P < 0.01).

LTI/RTI, the left/right testicular organ index. LTL/RTL, the long diameter of left/right testis. LTS/RTS, the short diameter of left/right testis.
The long diameter of the left testis (LTL) in the S-14L: 10D group were significantly higher than that of the N-16L: 8D (P < 0.05) and S-18L: 6D (P < 0.01) groups, and did not differ from the S-16L: 8D group (P > 0.05). No significant differences (P > 0.05) were detected among the four photoperiodic programs for the long diameter of the right testis (RTL). The short diameter of the left testis (LTS) did not differ among the three experimental groups relative to the control (Fig. 3C, P > 0.05). In contrast, the short diameter of the right testis (RTS) was significantly larger in the S-14L: 10D group than in the control and S-18L: 6D groups (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the S-14L: 10D and S-16L: 8D group (P > 0.05).
Hisological examination (Fig. 4) revealed that pigeons from all four photoperiodic programs possessed intact seminiferous tubules, abundant spermatogenic cells (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatid), and visible spermatozoa within the tubular lumen. Quantitative analysis showed that the diameter of seminiferous tubules (DST) was greatest in the S-14L:10D group, being significant higher than in the N-16L: 8D (P < 0.01), S-16L:8D (P < 0.05) and S-18L: 6D (P < 0.01) groups. The thickness of seminiferous epithelium (TSE) was also highest in the S-14L:10D group, exceeding that of the control N-16L: 8D group (P < 0.05). The number of spermatogonia, spermatid and spermatozoa were markedly lower in the S-18L: 6D group compared with the other three groups (P < 0.05). The number of spermatocytes was elevated in the S-14L:10D relative to the other (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed among the control, S-14L:10D and S-16L: 8D groups for the numbers of spermatogonia, spermatid, spermatocytes, or spermatozoa.

## Plasma reproductive hormone profiles
Plasma concentrations of FSH, LH, PRL and T in male pigeons subjected to the four photoperiodic programs are presented in Fig. 5. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 10% for FSH, LH, and T, and < 12% for PRL. Both FSH and LH levels were significantly higher in the S-14L:10D and S-16L:8D groups compared with the N-16L:8D and S-18L:6D groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A and 5B). Photoperiod also modulated PRL secretion: the S-16L:8D group pigeons exhibited the lowest plasma PRL concentration, which were significantly lower than those observed in the N-16L:8D and S-18L:6D groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C). Testosterone concentrations were significant higher in the S-14L:10D group than the other three groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5D).

## Expression of HPT axis-related genes
In the hypothalamus, GnRH mRNA expression was significantly higher in pigeons subjected to the S-14L:10D photoperiodic program than in those maintained under the control N-16L:8D (P < 0.01), S-16L:8D (P < 0.05), and S-18L:6D (P < 0.01) programs (Fig. 6A). Pituitary GnIH mRNA displayed a contrasting pattern: GnIH transcript levels in the 14L:10D and 16L:8D groups were significantly lower than in the control N-16L:8D birds (P < 0.05, Fig. 6B). Hypothalamic VIP mRNA was markedly elevated in the S-18L:6D group than the other three photoperiods (P < 0.05, Fig. 6C). Similarly, pituitary PRL mRNA levels in males under the S-18L:6D photoperiodic program were significantly higher than those in the control N-16L:8D (P < 0.05), the S-14L:10D and the S-16L:8D groups (P < 0.01, Fig. 6D). The abundance of pituitary FSH mRNA was lowest in the control N-16L:8D group and was significantly reduced compared the S-14L:10D (P < 0.05) and S-18L:6D (P < 0.01) groups (Fig. 6E). In contrast, pituitary LH mRNA expression peaked in the control N-16L:8D group and was significantly greater than that observed in the S-14L:10D group (P < 0.05, Fig. 6F).

(A − C) The relative abundances of mRNA for hypothalamic GnRH, GnIH, VIP, respectively. (D − F) The relative abundances of mRNA of the pituitary for pituitary PRL, FSH, LH, respectively.
In testicular tissue, photoperiodic treatments applied during the rearing period reduced PRLR gene expression relative to the control group (Fig. 7A). Specifically, PRLR mRNA levels in males from the control N-16L:8D group were significant higher than those in the S-16L:8D and S-18L:6D groups (P < 0.05). Likewise, FSHR mRNA expression was greatest in the N-16L:8D group among the four photoperiodic groups (Fig. 7B) and was significantly higher than in the S-18L:6D group (P < 0.05). However, the expression of LHR gene responded differently to the photoperiodic treatments (Fig. 7C). Compared with the control N-16L:8D photoperiod, both the S-14L:10D and S-18L:6D programs significantly up-regulated testicular LHR mRNA levels (P < 0.05).
Expression of HPG axis-related genes in testis of male pigeons exposed to different photoperiods during rearing and laying period (n = 10). (A − H) The relative abundances of mRNA for testicular PRLR, FSHR, LHR, StAR, HSD3β1, HSD17β1, CYP17A1, CYP19A1, respectively.
Different photoperiodic program applied from the rearing period also changed the expression of StAR and HSD3β1 genes, two key genes involved in testosterone biosynthesis (Fig. 7D and 7E). Both StAR and HSD3β1 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in the S-14L: 10D group than the control group and the other two treatment groups (P < 0.01), whereas no significant differences were detected between the control and the S‑16L:8D or S‑18L:6D groups (P > 0.05). Similarly, testicular HSD17β1 expression was highest in the pigeons under the 14L: 10D photoperiod (Fig. 7F); this increase was significant when compared with the control and S-18L: 6 D groups (P < 0.01) and also exceeded that of the S-16L: 8D group (P < 0.05). Photoperiodic treatment also up-regulated testicular CYP17A1 mRNA levels (Fig. 7G). The S-14L: 10D group displayed markedly higher CYP17A1 mRNA expression than the control N-16L:8D group (P < 0.01) and the two other treatments (P < 0.05). C Finally, all three experimental photoperiods (S‑14L:10D, S‑16L:8D, and S‑18L:6D) modestly increased CYP19A1 mRNA expression compared with the control (P < 0.05, Fig. 7H), although no significant differences were observed among the three treatment groups themselves.

## Expression of deep-brain and retinal photoreceptors genes
Providing different photostimulation during rearing and laying period altered the mRNA expression of deep-brain and retinal photoreceptors in male breeding pigeons. In the hypothalamus, OPN5 (opsin 5) and OPN4 (opsin 4) transcripts were significantly up-regulated in all three short-photostimulation treatment groups relative to the control (P < 0.05, Fig. 8 A and 8B). Hypothalamic VA opsin mRNA (Fig. 8C) reached its highest level in the S-14L: 10D group and was significantly greater than in the control (P < 0.01) and S-18L: 6D (P < 0.05) groups. In the pineal gland, the expression pattern of the pinopsin (OPNP) gene (Fig. 8D) was also changed: OPNP mRNA in the S-14L: 10D group was significantly higher than in the control (P < 0.01) and the other two treatments (P < 0.05).

For retinal photoreceptors, short-light exposure from rearing period suppressed their transcription. ## Discussion
Photoperiod is the primary environmental factor that regulates reproductive performance in poultry (Wang et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2019b; Rozenboim et al., 2022). Nevertheless, although the meat-pigeon industry exhibited relative low reproductive efficiency, no studies have examined the application of lighting systems to enhance the reproductive output of male pigeons. As consumer demand for pigeon eggs and meat continues to rise in China-driven by a growing emphasis on healthy nutrition- investigating the effects of different photoperiodic schedules during both the rearing and laying periods on reproductive parameters, hormone secretion, and testicular ultrastructure of breeder males is of considerable importance for enhancing the economic profitability of commercial pigeon breeding farm.
Previous studies proposed that a photo-program, consisting of a short photoperiod of 8-8.5 h followed by a long photoperiod can synchronize reproductive activities and improve reproductive performance (Sweeney et al., 1997; Chang et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2017). Moreover, the duration of light exposure in pigeon production is usually 14-16 h (Shao et al., 2023; Miao et al., 2024). Therefore, the photoperiod treatments in present study consisted a brief period of short photoperiod (8 h) during the rearing phase followed by a long photoperiod of 14, 16 and 18 h during the laying phase.
In terms of egg production, the S-14L:10D and S-16L:8D photoperiod treatments yielded the most favorable outcomes. Likewise, fertility rates were significantly higher under the S-14L:10D and S-16L:8D programs compared with the control photoperiod. Egg production and fertility did not vary monotonically with light duration; instead, they peaked when the birds received 14-16 h of light per day. Our previous work in geese demonstrated that extending the daily photoperiod from 8 h to 11 h produced a significantly higher fertility rate than extending it to 14 h (Zhu et al., 2019a). In Zi geese, 14-h light regime resulted in a higher fertilization rate than a 12-h sunlight exposure (Zhao et al., 2020). Conversely, short-day breeds such as Zhedong White geese laid more eggs under a photoperiod of 9L:15D than under a long-day (15L:9D) schedule (Chen et al., 2021). Moreover, moderate prolongation of light exposure can regulate reproductive activity in geese by down-regulating spexin mRNA expression in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (Liu et al., 2022). Similar to the long-day goose breeds, pigeons are also a kind of long day poultry, their egg laying performance is closely related to light duration, as pigeons naturally have the highest egg production rate from April to August and the lowest between November to February. Therefore, above findings suggest that, in breeding pigeons, a brief period of light restriction during the rearing phase followed by a moderate photoperiod of 14-16 h can promote follicular development, ovulation, and the quality of sperm or eggs, as well as improve mating behavior. In contrast, excessively illumination (≥18 h) may induce stress and fail to improve fertility, while both shorter (<14 h) and longer (≥18 h) light regimes can suppress reproductive efficiency. Additionally, an 8L:16D restriction during the rearing and pre-lay periods may prevent photosexual responsiveness, thereby maintaining maximally photosensitivity (Yan et al., 2024). A 6-week short‑light regime during the prelaying period increased fertility in White Roman geese relative to a long‑light treatment (Chang et al., 2016). Finally, variations in photoperiod did not significantly affect hatchability of fertilized eggs, indicating that the better hatch effect of fertilized eggs is mainly not affected by photoperiodic conditions during the rearing and laying periods, but is more likely to be influenced by incubation conditions and techniques.
Male fertility is intricately linked to testicular development, which constitutes a fundamental determinant of reproductive capacity by governing spermatogenesis (Amann, 1999). In the present study, bilateral testicular size and volume differed markedly among pigeons exposed to distinct photoperiods during the rearing and breeding periods. Specifically, pigeons reared under short-light conditions exhibited larger testis than those maintained on the control photoperiod. From the aspect of the left testicular organ index (LTI), moderate light durations of 14 h and 16 h conditions during the breeding period appeared more conducive to left testicular growth, whereas the right testicular organ index (RTI) reached its peak under the 14L:10D program, indicating a photoperiod‑specific advantage for right-testis development. Analogous findings have been reported in other avian species. Extending the photoperiod from 6.5 h to 11.5 h in geese increased relative testis weight and accompanied by elevated plasma T, FSH and LH concentrations (Feng et al., 2025). Conversely, excessively long photoperiods can trigger photo-inhibitory process, as evidenced by reduced bilateral testicular organ indexes. A similar testicular regression was observed in starlings following an acute shift from 8 L to 18 L (Dawson, 2015).
Detailed morphometric analysis of testicular long and short diameters further elucidated photoperiodic effects on testicular architecture. The patterns of left testicular long diameter (LTL) across the four photoperiodic programs mirrored the LTI results, confirming that moderate light exposures (14L:10D and 16L:8D) promote left-testis growth. In contrast, the right testicular long diameter (RTL) did not differ significantly across treatments; however, the right testicular short diameter (RTS) was significantly greater in the S-14L:10D group than in the other three groups, reinforcing the RTI finding that the 14L:10D schedule specifically favors transverse expansion and overall mass of the right testis. These observations suggest that short light exposure during the rearing stage can enhance gross testicular morphology, potentially improving subsequent reproductive function.
The diameter and epithelial thickness of seminiferous tubules are key indicators of spermatogenic capacity, which were maximal in the S-14L:10D group among. Increases in these parameters typically reflect heightened proliferation of spermatogenic cells and optimized differentiation environment. By contrast, excessively long photoperiods (e.g., 16L:8D and 18L:6D) appeared to impede seminiferous tubule development. The seminiferous tubules constitute the primary site of spermatozoa production; consequently, their structural integrity directly influences spermatogenesis and the resultant semen quality. The number of spermatocytes, which occupy a critical meiotic stage, was highest in the S-14L:10D group, directly indicating enhanced spermatogenic activity. Conversely, the S-18L:6D group exhibited significantly lower counts of spermatogonia, spermatids, and spermatozoa, underscoring the detrimental impact of ultra‑long light exposure on pigeon spermatogenesis. Comparable reductions in seminiferous‑tubule area, epithelial thickness, and all stages of spermatogenic cells have been reported in geese subjected to long‑day photoperiods (Pan et al., 2022), findings that align with the concept of photorefractoriness.
The results also demonstrate that photoperiodic regimens applied from the rearing phase exert distinct regulatory effects on plasma concentrations of key reproductive hormones-FSH, LH, PRL, and T in male breeding pigeons. FSH activates the cAMP/PKA signaling cascade, thereby enhancing the expression of genes involved in spermatogenesis and Sertoli cell secretory function (Ni et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). LH, in turn, serve as the principle stimulus for testosterone synthesis by the Leydig cells (Hansson et al., 1976). The synergistic action of these two gonadotropins promotes the proliferation and differentiation of spermatocytes (Thurston and Korn, 2000). Consistent with earlier work on female White King pigeons, which showed that a specific photoperiodic program shifts from 8L:16D during the rearing to 16L:8D during laying markedly increased the pituitary secretion of FSH and LH (Yan et al., 2024), our data indicate that similar photoperiodic programming modulates male hormone profiles.
Reduced PRL concentrations observed in the S-14L:10D and S-16L:8D groups support the model in which PRL functions as a gonadotropin antagonist (El Halawani and Rozenboim, 1993). Low PRL levels preserve LH activity, whereas the longer-day S-18L:6D schedule elevates PRL, suppressing reproductive activity and inducing brooding behavior. This inverse relationship between LH and PRL further confirms photoperiodic fine‑tuning of the trade‑off between reproductive activation and refractoriness (Dawson and Sharp, 2007).
Testosterone, essential for male sexual behavior, aggression, and secondary sexual characteristics, displayed a unique response, reaching peak plasma concentrations under the S-14L:10D photoperiod. Photoperiodic regulation of testosterone is well-established; for instance, appropriately extended photoperiods in roosters up-regulated LH secretion, raising testosterone levels and enhancing sperm concentration by ∼25% relative to short photoperiods (Kang and Kuenzel, 2015). In ganders, LH peaks preceded the highest testosterone values by one week following photostimulation (Gumułka and Rozenboim, 2015).
HPG axis-related gene expression further corroborated these hormonal patterns. The S-14L:10D group exhibited elevated hypothalamic GnRH, pituitary FSH, and testicular LHR transcripts, whereas GnIH, VIP, PRL, and LH mRNA expression abundance were down-regulated at a low level in the S-14L:10D group. GnRH, as the upstream regulator of the HPG axis, directly stimulates pituitary gonadotropin release thereby influencing testicular development and spermatogenic function (Adams, 2005; Tsutsui et al., 2013). Photoperiodic modulation of the hypothalamic GnRH/GnIH system has been shown to drive testicular growth or regression in Japanese quail (Banerjee and Chaturvedi, 2017) and to activate DBP‑mediated GnRH synthesis in geese (Zhu et al., 2019a; Pan et al., 2022). VIP, a key regulator of pituitary PRL secretion, was markedly up‑regulated under ultra-long light (18 h), likely disrupting circadian rhythms and leading to VIP hypersecretion. Elevated PRL then exerts negative feedback on the HPG axis by inhibiting GnRH and gonadotropin synthesis (Hodson et al., 2010). In Magang geese, long-day photoperiods similarly promoted VIP and PRL expression while suppressing GnRH, whereas short-day condition produces the opposite effect (Shi et al., 2007; Huang et al., 2008).
Light signals increase the secretion of GnRH and other gonadotropin-releasing factors, which in turn upregulate the transcription of the gonadotropin subunit genes, including FSHβ (Nakao et al., 2008). Transcriptional analyses of pituitary FSH and testicular FSH receptor (FSHR) revealed a nuanced photoperiodic regulation. Although plasma FSH peaked in the S‑16L:8D group, its pituitary FSH mRNA was not maximal, suggesting a temporal lag between transcription and secretion or an efficient mobilization of pre‑synthesized hormone stores. Conversely, the N‑16L:8D group displayed the highest testicular FSHR mRNA despite low FSH secretion, implying a compensatory up-regulation of receptor expression to maximize sensitivity to limited circulating FSH. The dissociation among hormone synthesis, secretion, and target-tissue responsiveness highlights the multifaceted nature of photoperiodic control.
LHR is crucial for transducing the LH signal from the pituitary to initiate testosterone production within Leydig cells (Thurston and Korn, 2000; Jin et al., 2022). LH and LHR expression patterns under different photoperiods patterns mirrored this complexity. While pituitary LH mRNA was highest in the control N-16L:8D birds, testicular LHR transcripts were up-regulated in the S-14L:10D and S-18L:6D groups, indicating independent photoperiodic regulation of hormone synthesis at the pituitary level and receptor responsiveness at the gonadal level. The up-regulation of LHR gene expression in the testes of pigeons under the S-14L:10D and S-18L:6D programs points to a direct photoperiodic influence on testicular sensitivity. Similarly, for short-day goose breeds, long-day conditions inhibited the expression and secretion of LH, while a short-day photoperiod has the opposite effect (Huang et al., 2008; Gumułka and Rozenboim, 2015).
Photoperiodic manipulation from the rearing period also impacted the steroidogenic cascade. Free cholesterol is converted into pregnenolone and ultimately synthesizes testosterone, which cannot be achieved without the help of StAR, HSD3β1, CYP17A1 (P450c17), and HSD17β1 (Tremblay, 2015). Key enzymes (StAR, HSD3β1, CYP17A1 and HSD17β) exhibited peak expression under the 14L:10D program, aligning with elevated testosterone levels. Long photoperiods have been shown to stimulate testicular melatonin pathways, thereby enhancing StAR transcription and testosterone synthesis in roosters (Xu et al., 2021). Similar up‑regulation of 3β‑HSD mRNA parallels seasonal patterns observed in ostriches, where Leydig cells display strong 3β‑HSD immunostaining during the active reproductive period and a decline during regressive periods (Madekurozwa et al., 2002). In contrast, excessively short day lengths in female quails up-regulated gonadal GnIH and down-regulated CYP19A1, HSD3β1, and StAR, suppressing follicle development (Zhou et al., 2022), underscoring the broader relevance of photoperiodic cues across avian species. CYP19A1 (encoding aromatase, which contributes to the fine‑tuning of estrogen production from testosterone) was upregulated by all experimental photoperiods relative to control. A study demonstrated that CYP19A1−/− male rabbits have decreased fertility with lower sperm count and lower spermatid number (Dewaele et al., 2022). Thus, further investigation is warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which photoperiod influences CYP19A1 expression and estrogenic balance. Collectively, these results reinforce the concept that photoperiod serves as a primary environmental regulator of the HPG axis and steroidogenic machinery in pigeons.
Deep-brain photoreceptors (DBPs) are essential for avian photoperiod perception, transducing light signals that modulate the HPG axis. The present results demonstrate that short‑duration photostimulation applied during the rearing period markedly up‑regulates the mRNA expression of several DBPs in male breeding pigeons. Transcripts of OPN4 and OPN5 were significantly elevated in all three short‑photostimulation groups, whereas VA opsin reached its highest level in the S‑14L:10D treatment. A comparable pattern was observed in the pineal gland, where pinopsin (OPNP) expression was strongly induced under S-14L:10D. These findings align with the growing body of evidence that DBPs act as the primary mediators of photoperiodic information in birds. Kuenzel et al. reported that long-day photoperiods increase OPN5, OPN4 and VA-opsin expression in the septal-hypothalamic regions of chicks, thereby enhancing the photoperiodic response (Kuenzel et al., 2015). In Magang goose ganders, prolonged light exposure up‑regulates hypothalamic OPN5 together with the TSH‑DIO2/DIO3 axis, linking DBP activation to neuroendocrine control of reproduction (Pan et al., 2022). Studies in Japanese quail have shown that knock‑down of VA opsin or OPN5 attenuates the acute long‑day induction of reproductive hormones, highlighting a dominant role of VA‑opsin and a supportive role for OPN5 (Pérez et al., 2023). A recent testable hypothesis proposes that VA-opsin can act directly in GnRH1 cells to regulate GnRH release and seasonal reproduction in birds (Stevenson et al., 2025). The present pigeon data extend these observations, suggesting that light-restriction during early development can prime the DBP system.
Conversely, retinal photoreceptor genes were suppressed: RGR, OPN4 and OPN3 mRNA levels were significantly lower under short photostimulation, particularly in the S‑14L:10D.​ RGR is expressed in the avian inner retina and modulates retinaldehyde levels in response to light, participating in retinoid recycling to maintain the balance of inner retinal retinoid pools (Díaz et al., 2017). Retinal OPN4 and OPN3 are involved in non-visual light processing (Guido et al., 2022). Similar suppressive effects of intense or prolonged light on retinal OPN4 and OPN3 have been reported in ducks, where early post‑hatch OPN4 expression declines as hypothalamic DBPs become dominant (Van Wyk and Fraley, 2021). Overall, the data support a model in which short‑duration photostimulation during the rearing period preferentially enhances deep‑brain opsin expression (OPN5, OPN4, VA opsin) and pineal pinopsin, while concurrently dampening retinal photoreceptor transcription. This pattern may suggest a “brain-activate/eye-deactivate” hypothesis, under suboptimal rearing light conditions, avian retinal photoreceptors prioritize non-visual (extra-retinal) photoperception over visual function, reallocating resources to hypothalamic and pineal photoreceptors that are critical for reproductive timing. Such re‑balancing of photoreceptive systems may have downstream effects on the HPG axis, as up-regulated hypothalamic DBPs enhance light signal transduction, trigger GnRH secretion, and promote gonadal activation-a process corroborated by the earlier observation that the S-14L:10D photoperiod maximized steroidogenic gene expression and testicular development.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, the study demonstrates that photoperiod constitutes a pivotal environmental cue for enhancing the reproductive performance of male breeding pigeons. Exposure to a short 8 h photoperiod during the rearing period, followed by a 14L:10D photostimulation during the laying period, markedly improved fertility rates and promoted testicular development, as evidenced by increased seminiferous‑tubule diameter, greater epithelial thickness, and a higher abundance of spermatogenic cells. Hormonal profiling revealed elevated plasma concentrations of FSH, LH, and testosterone, together with a concomitant reduction in PRL. Correspondingly, transcriptional analysis showed up‑regulation of key genes of the HPT axis (GnRH, FSH, LHR) and steroidogenic enzymes (StAR, HSD3β1, CYP17A1, HSD17β1), wherea inhibitory factors (GnIH, VIP, PRL) were down‑regulated under the S‑14L:10D treatment. Moreover, expression of DBPs (OPN4, OPN5, VA opsin, pinopsin) was enhanced, whereas retinal opsins transcripts were suppressed. Collectively, these findings indicate that an 8L:16D during rearing period and a 14L:10D during the laying period optimally stimulate hormonal, molecular, and morphological pathways, thereby maximizing reproductive efficiency in commercial pigeon production (summarized in Fig. 9). This provides valuable guidance for photoperiod management in pigeon farming. Future investigations should examine the interaction between light intensity and photoperiod, as well as the combined effects of photoperiodic treatments applied at different developmental stages, to furnish more comprehensive theoretical support for the precise regulation of breeding pigeons reproductive efficiency.
