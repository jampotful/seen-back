Carotenoid bioavailability in humans reflects the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck: a position paper.

# Carotenoid bioavailability in humans reflects the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck: a position paper

## Abstract
Recommended levels of dietary carotenoids remain uncertain, although their health benefits have gained renewed interest. One reason is that humans have a unique bioavailability of carotenoids compared to other mammals, which is not well understood. This position paper suggests that humans’ bioavailability of dietary carotenoids reflects the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck—a period when the ancestors of modern mammals became nocturnal during the Mesozoic era to avoid predatory dinosaurs. The roles of carotenoids are closely linked to evolutionary processes involving light and vision. Vertebrates developed advanced color vision by using specific xanthophylls in their photoreceptor cells. In contrast, mammals’ color vision declined due to their nocturnal lifestyle. Primates later returned to daytime activity and accumulated two xanthophylls, lutein and zeaxanthin, from their diet, in the macular lutea of the eyes, to protect color vision. Additionally, humans uniquely store β-carotene and lycopene in exposed skin. Throughout primate evolution, humans’ ability to accumulate dietary carotenoids shifted from xanthophylls toward less polar carotenoids, including β-carotene and lycopene, unlike the high selectivity of xanthophylls observed in birds. Overall, carotenoid deposition in human tissues is not highly specialized, likely due to remnants of the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck. We should consider this when evaluating recommended carotenoid intake.

## Introduction
Mammals vary in their ability to accumulate dietary carotenoids, including carotenes and xanthophylls, within their bodies, aside from their capacity to convert specific carotenoids into vitamin A. In 1984, Goodwin(1) classified mammals into three groups: those that indiscriminately accumulate both carotenes and xanthophylls, those that selectively accumulate only carotenes, and those that do not accumulate carotenoids at all. He suggested that humans are indiscriminate accumulators of both. Slifka et al.(2) found that primates possess the most diverse array of carotenoids and the highest carotenoid levels in their blood among 76 captive wild animal species. However, the reason why primates uniquely accumulate various carotenoids—while other mammals typically only accumulate carotenes or none at all—remains unclear. Further research showed that, of about 50 types of dietary carotenoids, only β-carotene, α-carotene, lycopene, β-cryptoxanthin, lutein, and zeaxanthin account for over 90% of the total carotenoids in human blood plasma.(3) An autopsy study indicates that β-carotene and lycopene are the predominant carotenoids found in liver, kidney, and lung tissues.(4) Therefore, humans may exhibit some selectivity regarding the bioavailability of dietary carotenoids.
This position paper proposes that the bioavailability of dietary carotenoids in humans is linked to the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck hypothesis, first introduced by Walls in 1942.(5) According to this hypothesis, the ancestors of modern mammals became nocturnal during the Mesozoic era to avoid predatory dinosaurs. As a result, they developed enhanced olfactory and auditory systems, along with scotopic vision. However, their photopic and color vision declined over time through evolution. Additionally, their skin coloration, used for communication and UV protection, decreased during prolonged nocturnal activity. Most mammals still are nocturnal, and their photopic and color vision have not fully recovered. This phenomenon is known as the nocturnal bottleneck in the early evolution of crown mammals.(6,7) The common ancestors of primates were also nocturnal but shifted to a diurnal lifestyle as they adapted to an arboreal environment. They transitioned from an insectivorous diet to mainly eating fruits and leaves.(8) Consequently, they regained their photopic and color vision, enabling them to spot colorful fruits and leaves during their extended life in the trees.
Primates accumulate lutein and zeaxanthin, which come from fruits and leaves, mainly in the macular lutea of the eye. These two xanthophylls act as antioxidants by filtering harmful blue light and neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), thus protecting the retina.(9,10) Interestingly, humans uniquely accumulate β-carotene and lycopene in the skin, where these carotenoids serve as antioxidants by quenching ROS generated by sunlight.(11) However, the bioavailability of carotenoids in humans is loosely regulated by an incomplete mechanism, partly due to the influence of the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck. This contrasts with the high selectivity of xanthophylls in birds.(2) Additionally, it has been reported that humans more effectively accumulate nonpolar carotenoids compared to polar ones.(12) Therefore, we suggest that humans have a lenient approach to carotenoid accumulation. The reasons for this idea are discussed in the following section.

## Carotenoids in Biological Evolution
Carotenoids are yellow, orange, and red pigments commonly found in living organisms. More than 1,100 carotenoids have been identified in various biological species, including bacteria and archaea.(13) In eukaryotes, carotenoids are C40-based tetraterpenoids consisting of eight isoprene units, although some bacteria and archaea can produce C30-, C45-, and C50-based carotenoids. They are often classified as acyclic carotenoids (lycopene), cyclic carotenoids (carotenes), and oxygenated cyclic carotenoids (xanthophylls).
Carotenoid synthetic pathways coevolved with the development of photosynthesis, starting with cyanobacteria about 2.5–3 billion years ago, playing a key role in protecting and regulating light energy input.(14) Carotenoids are produced by all photosynthetic eukaryotes through the non-mevalonate pathway (Fig. 1)(3) and serve as accessory light-harvesting pigments and photoprotective agents against excess light energy.(15) Animals cannot produce carotenoids de novo because they lack the enzyme needed to synthesize phytoene, a precursor of lycopene, from geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate.(16) Instead, animals obtain these compounds through their diet and accumulate various carotenoids in their bodies through a unique metabolic process involving oxidation, reduction, or transfer of double bonds.(17) For example, crustaceans absorb β-carotene from algae and convert it to astaxanthin via oxidative metabolism (Fig. 2).(3) Salmon give their muscles a red color by incorporating astaxanthin into their diet.
Vision evolved through the development of the photoreceptor protein opsin, which was produced by eukaryotic protists 1.5 billion years ago.(18) Retinal, an oxidative cleavage product of specific carotenoids, is used in the visual system by binding to opsin molecules.(19) Meanwhile, retinoic acid signaling, which plays a role in initiating differentiation and regulating development, began during the diversification of metazoans around 0.5 billion years ago.(20,21) Retinoic acid is enzymatically generated from retinal. Consequently, animals evolved not by synthesizing carotenoids but by utilizing them as intact molecules or through partial modifications.

## Mammals and Birds in Carotenoid Utilization
Despite their clear differences in appearance, body size, and life histories, mammals and birds share a common ancestry with amniote stem ancestors dating back about 300 million years (Fig. 3).(22) Mammalian and avian lineages have evolved separately, leading to notable differences in carotenoid bioaccumulation, especially on the body surface. Aves, descendants of theropod dinosaurs, mainly accumulate xanthophylls but not carotenes.(2,23,24) Their diet supplies lutein and zeaxanthin, which are partly converted into ketocarotenoids like canthaxanthin, rhodoxanthin, and doradexanthin, giving their plumage orange or red colors.(25) Carotenoid-based coloration acts as an honest indicator of quality(26) and signals sexual selection.(27,28) In mammals, a normally melanin-pigmented patch covers the body surface.(29) The evolution of color differences in mammals has been driven by functions such as protective camouflage and interspecific recognition signals. Carotenoids usually do not contribute to their body surface color, except in recent cases involving a tropical bat.(30) Most mammal species do not store large amounts of carotenoids; however, felids mainly accumulate β-carotene regardless of their diet, and bovids exclusively accumulate β-carotene despite eating plenty of lutein and zeaxanthin.(2)
The primate lineage is unique in its ability to accumulate carotenoids. Primates accumulate both carotenes and xanthophylls. Interestingly, humans accumulate lycopene alongside other carotenes and xanthophylls.(2) Research by Kachick et al.(31) found that phytoene and phytofluene are significantly present in human plasma, suggesting these carotenoid precursors are also obtained from fruits and vegetables. α-Carotene, β-carotene, and β-cryptoxanthin serve as provitamin A, as they can produce retinal through the central cleavage reaction with β-carotene 15,15'-oxygenase (BCO1). Conversely, intact carotenoids that do not undergo cleavage accumulate in various tissues and may function as antioxidants(32,33) or regulators of immune function.(34,35) Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of lutein and zeaxanthin in macular retinal health, since these xanthophylls specifically build up in the human macula and help protect the retina from excessive light energy.(36)

## Evolution of Mammals and the Nocturnal Bottleneck
Approximately 250 million years ago, prototypes of mammals evolved from synapsids. (37) Mammals identified as Adelobasileus cromptoni appeared during the Jurassic period (roughly 200 to 145 million years ago). During both the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods (145–66 million years ago), these mammals primarily inhabited nocturnal or underground niches, likely reducing their risk of predation from diurnal dinosaurs.(38) In this era, mammals developed protective coloring, became nocturnal or lived underground, and relied more on smell and hearing in dark environments. In contrast, their color vision diminished over time due to its limited usefulness in darkness. This phenomenon is known as the nocturnal bottleneck.(39) The sudden extinction of dinosaurs around 64 million years ago triggered significant adaptive radiation among mammals. Many diurnal mammals evolved to fill various ecological niches during the Cenozoic era. Reconstruction of ancestral mammal activity patterns shows that diurnal mammals still exhibit traces of nocturnal behavior.(40)
Plesiadapiformes, considered primitive primates, first appeared during the early Paleocene epoch, approximately 65 to 55 million years ago.(41) They had limbs similar to those of rodents and moved on all fours both in trees and on the ground, with no significant specialization for arboreal life. They are regarded as a paraphyletic group of mammals that transitioned between the Paleocene and Eocene epochs. Figure 4 shows the phylogenetic tree of primates.(42) During the Eocene epoch (55–38 million years ago), the adapids (lemurs) and omomyids (tarsiers), modern primates, emerged as prosimians. They became diurnal and were exclusively arboreal. Anthropoids appeared between 38 and 24 million years ago during the early Pleistocene epoch. Numerous hominoid primates arose during the Miocene epoch (24 to 5 million years ago). Humans, chimpanzees, and gorillas diverged from their common ancestors. The human lineage separated from chimpanzee ancestors in Africa approximately 6 to 7 million years ago. (43)
Primate diets are thought to have evolved from their small ancestors’ insect-based diets in two main ways. One group specialized in eating leaves, while the other mainly consumed fruit and forest resources, such as insects and sometimes leaves.(44,45) Primates lost the ability to synthesize ascorbic acid when prosimians first appeared 35–55 million years ago.(46) However, primates could obtain enough ascorbic acid from the leaves and fruits they ate. As a result, losing the ability to produce ascorbic acid did not pose an evolutionary challenge. Eating leaves and fruits actively may lead to the bioaccumulation of dietary carotenoids in primates.

## Carotenoids and Primate Color Vision Evolution: A Bird Comparison
The basic light sensors of unicellular eukaryotes gradually evolved into the high-resolution eyes of vertebrates.(47) Type II rhodopsin, which consists of an opsin protein covalently bound to retinal, functions as the molecular component responsible for photoreception in animals. This structure originated from a G protein-coupled melatonin receptor around 1–2 billion years ago. In vertebrates, ciliary opsin developed into rod-type rhodopsin for mesopic vision and four types of cone-type iodopsins for color and photopic vision.(48) Most living fish, amphibians, reptiles, and birds have tetrachromatic color vision with four types of cone cells sensitive to ultraviolet, blue, green, and red light. They detect wavelengths that extend into near-ultraviolet light.
Early mammals evolved from synapsids that had four types of cone cells but lost their need for color vision due to their nocturnal lifestyle.(49) During the Mesozoic Era, mammals lost two cone opsin types and became dichromats, capable of distinguishing only blue and red.(48) This is why mammals’ color vision is generally poorer than that of birds.(50) Primates evolved trichromatic vision—blue, red, and green—about 30 million years ago in the Cenozoic Era, when an opsin gene differentiated after Old World monkeys appeared.(51) This adaptation, along with stereoscopic and high-resolution vision, enables agile movement in trees.
Vertebrates developed color vision by improving the spectral sensitivity of cone photoreceptors, leading to various structural adaptations. The oil droplet is a unique intracellular light-sensing organelle found in vertebrate cone photoreceptors, from fish to early mammals.(52) This droplet contains neutral lipids and carotenoids and is positioned in the light path within the photoreceptors. The function of oil droplets has changed over time through variations in carotenoid content. In species active in low light, carotenoid levels have been decreased or removed to enhance light sensitivity. Conversely, species that are active in bright light have adapted the types and amounts of carotenoids in the oil droplet in different ways.
Avian cone photoreceptor oil droplets are located at the front of the outer segments in pyramidal cells.(53) These droplets vary in color from white to red, depending on their carotenoid pigment content. Astaxanthin and galloxanthin, derived from zeaxanthin, are found in the oil droplets of their respective cone photoreceptors along with zeaxanthin and lutein (Fig. 5).(52) Higher carotenoid levels in the colored oil droplets modify the incoming light spectrum, creating a spectral filtering effect. Additionally, oil droplets act as spherical microlenses; the presence of carotenoids increases the refractive index at longer wavelengths, helping to counteract the reduction in light collection efficiency caused by spectral filtering. Overall, oil droplets enhance the spectral discrimination ability of photoreceptors by boosting their spectral sensitivity.
As mammals adapted to nocturnal life, oil droplets in their cone cells disappeared, a change likely influenced by the long dominance of dinosaurs.(6) In primates, the macula of the retina, which gathers xanthophyll pigments, suddenly appeared in the cone cell-rich fovea and its surrounding area.(54) However, carotenoids accumulate in the cell membranes of photoreceptor axons. Lutein, zeaxanthin, and mesozeaxanthin reside within the macular region of the primate retina(55) and are known to protect this area from damage caused by exposure to short-wavelength visible light.(9,10) Mesozeaxanthin is a carotenoid unique to primate eye tissue, produced from lutein via the enzyme RPE65 (retinoid isomerohydrolase) (Fig. 6).(56) Xanthophyll pigments are most concentrated in the fovea and decrease towards the periphery. Lutein-specific binding protein (glutathione S-transferase 1)(57) and zeaxanthin-specific binding protein (StRAD3)(58) facilitate the selective uptake of xanthophyll pigments into the retina. Consequently, the primate macula likely employs a highly regulated mechanism for the targeted accumulation of specific xanthophyll types.

## Absorption and Metabolism of Carotenoids in Humans
In humans, dietary carotenoids combine with lipids to form micelles that are absorbed in the intestine.(59) Intestinal epithelial cells take up carotenoids from micelles either by simple diffusion, due to their hydrophobic nature, or through transporter-mediated diffusion. Transporter proteins such as the scavenger receptor class B type 1 (SR-B1), CD36 molecule (CD36), and Niemann-Pick C1-like 1 (NPC1L1) are believed to facilitate diffusion similar to how cholesterol does.(60) SR-B1 and CD36 are involved in the absorption of lycopene and provitamin A carotenoids—like β-carotene, α-carotene, and β-cryptoxanthin—in human cell models.(61,62) SR-B1 and NPC1L1 have also been identified as transporters that mediate lutein absorption within the same cellular models.(63) Carotenoids absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract may be effluxed by ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, similar to the process observed for plant sterols.(64) Because transporter proteins may differ in affinity among different animal species, carotenoid absorption could vary; however, specific data on these differences have not been publicly available.
In mammals, provitamin A carotenoids undergo partial conversion to vitamin A during intestinal absorption.(65) BCO1 catalyzes the central cleavage of these carotenoids in intestinal epithelial cells, producing two retinal molecules. Dietary carotenoids that bypass BCO1 in the intestinal epithelial cells are transported to target tissues via plasma lipoproteins through the liver. Intact carotenoids enter cells via transporters and are thought to reach organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, and nucleus.(66) Aster-B, an intracellular cholesterol transport protein, was recently shown to deliver xanthophylls to mitochondria.(67) Evidence indicates that Aster-B contributes to xanthophyll accumulation within the human macula lutea, as its gene is highly expressed in the human foveal region but is not detected in the mouse retina.(68)
Mammalian β-carotene 9',10'-oxygenase (BCO2) is an enzyme that cleaves β-carotene at the 9', 10' positions and also acts on lycopene and xanthophylls because of its broad substrate specificity.(69) BCO2 is found in various human tissues and operates independently of vitamin A production. BCO1 is located in the cytosol, while BCO2 resides in the inner membranes of mitochondria.(69,70) In mice, BCO2 deficiency is known to cause carotenoid toxicity in tissues by increasing mitochondrial oxidative stress.(71,72) BCO2 is consistently present in vertebrates, where it helps prevent toxicity from excessive carotenoid buildup in tissues.(73,74) In humans, BCO2 may also help reduce carotenoid toxicity. Human BCO2 might have low enzymatic activity because humans store high levels of various carotenoids in tissues, unlike rodents. It has been confirmed that BCO2 activity is relatively weak in the human macula.(75) Primate BCO2 is induced by oxidative stress(76), and its enzymatic oxidation products promote antioxidant enzyme production.(77,78) BCO2 expression may contribute to strengthening the human antioxidant defense system.(79)

## Accumulation of Lycopene in Human Skin and Its Function
HPLC analysis shows that lycopene is the most abundant carotenoid in human skin, followed by β-carotene, with smaller amounts of xanthophylls like lutein and zeaxanthin.(80) Plasma levels of β-carotene and lycopene are higher than those of lutein in humans, unlike in great apes.(81) Lycopene is a unique carotenoid that accumulates at significant levels in humans among primates.(2) Carotenemia and lycopenemia are conditions characterized by yellow discoloration of the skin.(82) These occur due to the abnormal buildup of β-carotene and lycopene, respectively, caused by excessive intake. However, these conditions do not result in severe biological damage and can be easily reversed through dietary changes. Therefore, humans, unlike most other primates, absorb β-carotene and lycopene more efficiently and direct them to various tissues, including the skin.
Interestingly, clinical studies with tomato juice show that lycopene cis-isomers (9, 13, and 15-cis) are absorbed more efficiently in the intestine than the all-trans isomer.(83,84) This preference is likely due to better uptake into micelles within the digestive tract system.(85) Additionally, cis-isomerization of all-trans isomer commonly occurs in the body, including in the digestive tract.(86) However, β-carotene shows no clear preference in the intestinal absorption between cis-isomers and all-trans isomer,(87,88) although cis-isomerization of all-trans carotene may also happen in the body.(89) The underlying mechanisms responsible for cis-isomerization of carotenoids within the human body have not yet been definitively identified.
Photosensitized oxidation of biological components contributes to skin damage caused by sunlight exposure. Human intervention studies on erythema formation from UV light exposure have shown that carotenoids, especially β-carotene and lycopene, act as skin protectors by accumulating in skin tissue and quenching ROS, which are produced by photodynamic action.(90,91) It can be said that actively consuming these carotenoids provides skin protection against daily sunlight exposure. Additionally, it has been reported that skin coloration linked to increased fruit and vegetable intake offers greater apparent health benefits than melanin pigmentation.(92) The selective buildup of β-carotene and lycopene in human skin tissue may be connected to the evolutionary loss of pelage in humans. As humans migrated to non-tropical regions in Africa and Eurasia, they might have balanced melanization(93) and carotenoid accumulation in the skin. Furthermore, it has been confirmed that humans specifically store lycopene in the testes.(94) This buildup is believed to help protect sperm from oxidative stress.(95) More research is needed to understand the exact role of lycopene in the testes.

## Poor Absorption of Epoxyxanthophylls in Humans
Fruits and vegetables commonly contain lutein 5,6-epoxide (taraxanthin), zeaxanthin 5,6,5',6'-diepoxide (violaxanthin), and neoxanthin as dietary carotenoids, which account for about 10% of total carotenoid intake.(96) Fucoxanthin is a unique allenic carotenoid found in edible brown algae such as wakame and kombu.(97) These carotenoids are classified as epoxyxanthophylls because their structures include one or two epoxy groups (Fig. 7). It was reported that neither taraxanthin nor violaxanthin was detected in human plasma after 9 ‍h of their 50 ‍mg oral intake.(98) Asai et al.(99) evaluated plasma epoxyxanthophyll concentrations before and after one week of spinach intake (3.0 ‍mg neoxanthin daily) or wakame intake (6.1 ‍mg fucoxanthin daily) and found that the concentrations of both epoxyxanthophylls and their metabolites are nearly 1 nM or less.
Sekiya et al.(100) measured the bioavailability of neoxanthin by giving human volunteers a raw young spinach leaf containing 3.5 ‍mg of neoxanthin daily for 4 weeks. They found that neither neoxanthin nor its metabolites appeared in the plasma, although β-carotene and lutein levels increased by 1.4 and 1.9 times, respectively. Hashimoto et al.(101) detected only a small amount of fucoxanthinol (Fig. 7), a metabolite of fucoxanthin, in human plasma after 4 ‍h of consuming wakame containing 31 ‍mg of fucoxanthin. Therefore, we can conclude that the bioavailability of these epoxyxanthophylls in humans is extremely low compared to β-carotene or lutein. In mice, however, there seems to be no such selectivity in carotenoid bioavailability.(102)
In conclusion, humans exhibit a selective process for absorbing dietary carotenoids, with poor absorption of epoxyxanthophylls. However, the reason for this remains unclear. 5,6-Epoxy-β-carotene (Fig. 7) was found to be absorbed efficiently by humans,(103) indicating that the epoxidation of the carotene structure itself is not responsible for the low bioavailability of epoxyxanthophylls. Neoxanthin is expected to serve as a chemopreventive agent and may function as an anti-cancer drug(104). Fucoxanthin is a candidate for anti-obesity effects.(105,106) Nevertheless, their limited bioavailability should be considered for clinical applications.(107) Strangely, epoxyxanthophylls, including violaxanthin and taraxanthin, are detected in human neoplastic breast tissue,(108) as well as in normal and neoplastic uterine tissue(109) and ovarian tissues.(110) The reason for this selective accumulation of epoxyxanthophylls in the body remains unclear. More comprehensive studies on the bioavailability of epoxyxanthophylls are necessary to fully understand their biological activity for future clinical use.(111)

## Conclusion
The health benefits of consuming carotenoids have gained renewed interest.(112,113) However, their recommended levels remain uncertain, except for the vitamin A equivalent of some provitamin A carotenoids. Currently, research on how carotenoid intake impacts human health is limited.(114) Böhm et al.(12) found that blood carotenoid levels below 1 ‍μM are associated with a higher risk of chronic disease. They also noted that levels up to 7.5 ‍μM are achievable after supplementation, but a plateau effect occurs at higher doses and with long-term use. However, meta-analyses of several extensive, long-term human intervention studies have shown that taking extra β-carotene increases the risk of lung and stomach cancers, especially in smokers and asbestos workers.(115,116) Therefore, some individuals might be at risk from taking additional β-carotene supplements. No animal model can fully replicate how carotenoids are absorbed and metabolized in humans, considering their dietary intake and role in health.(117) This suggests that the bioavailability of carotenoids in humans is unique among mammals, as discussed in this article.
Carotenoids’ roles are closely linked to biological evolution related to light and vision.(118) Carotenoids are functional compounds produced by photosynthetic bacteria and plants during photosynthesis. Animals use these pigments throughout the food chain, mainly to form light-sensing modules. In rod cells, vertebrates achieve high-resolution vision by using specific carotenoids as precursors of retinal, which makes up a photoreceptor called rhodopsin (provitamin A activity). Moreover, fish, amphibians, reptiles, and birds have advanced color vision due to cone cells containing photoreceptor iodopsin, resulting in tetrachromatic color perception. Besides retinol as a component of iodopsin, various dietary xanthophylls and their metabolites are incorporated into oil droplets within cone cells to boost color vision. Carotenoids have also been used as tools for body coloration, serving as communication signals within and between species. Diurnal birds have developed a high level of bioavailability for specific xanthophylls, supporting sophisticated color vision and body coloration.(53)
In contrast, mammalian color vision declined during the Jurassic period due to their nocturnal habits, which reduced the need for xanthophyll accumulation. Later, primates became active during the day again and developed trichromatic color vision to help find food while living in trees. They also evolved a system to store xanthophylls, such as lutein and zeaxanthin, in their retina to protect cone cells from light-induced oxidative damage, ultimately leading to the formation of the macula lutea.
Primates, including humans, can accumulate carotenoids such as carotenes, lutein, and zeaxanthin. Plasma lycopene levels are significantly higher in humans than in other primates.(2,81) Additionally, high levels of nonpolar carotenoid precursors with straight-chain hydrocarbons, like colorless phytoene and orange phytofluene, are found in human plasma after consumption.(119,120) Conversely, humans seem to absorb epoxyxanthophylls very little, indicating that the bioavailability of polar epoxyxanthophylls is much lower compared to less polar xanthophylls. Throughout primate evolution, humans’ ability to absorb dietary carotenoids shifted from lutein and zeaxanthin toward less or nonpolar carotenoids, including β-carotene and lycopene. This trend may differ significantly from how birds utilize dietary carotenoids, where each xanthophyll is deposited explicitly in different tissues (Fig. 8). Humans store various carotenoids in different tissues at varying concentrations. Unlike birds, humans do not show specific accumulation of each carotenoid in particular tissues, except in the retina. Overall, the pattern of carotenoid distribution in human tissues is not highly specialized, possibly because of remnants of the mammalian nocturnal bottleneck. We should consider the physiological roles of dietary carotenoids and their recommended intake by understanding mammalian evolution and the nocturnal bottleneck.
