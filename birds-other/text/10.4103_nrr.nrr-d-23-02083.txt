# Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals the heterogeneity and interactions of immune cells and Müller glia during zebrafish retina regeneration

## Abstract


## Introduction
Degenerative eye diseases, such as glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, and retinitis pigmentosa, lead to loss of retinal neurons, often resulting in blindness (Jin et al., 2019). Unlike mammals, zebrafish possess a remarkable capacity for retina regeneration and even vision restoration (Goldman, 2014; Lenkowski and Raymond, 2014). A crucial element in this regenerative process is a specific type of glial cells in the retina called Müller glia (MG). Upon retinal injury, zebrafish MG undergo reprogramming and proliferate into multipotent progenitor cells known as MG-derived progenitor cells (Fausett and Goldman, 2006; Nagashima et al., 2013; Gao et al., 2021). These MG-derived progenitor cells then migrate to different retinal layers and further differentiate into newborn retinal neurons, facilitating repair of the damaged retina (Gorsuch and Hyde, 2014; Song et al., 2023). A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms governing retina regeneration in zebrafish could provide valuable insights to help develop future therapies targeting retinal degenerative diseases.
MG reprogramming in the zebrafish retina is regulated by numerous intrinsic factors and extrinsic signals (Lahne et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2021). Recent studies have shed light on the important role of the regeneration niche, particularly inflammation and immune cells, in retinal regeneration (Nagashima and Hitchcock, 2021). Following retinal injury, immune cells such as microglia/macrophages, neutrophils, and regulatory T cells accumulate at the site of injury region in the zebrafish retina (Hui et al., 2017; White et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2022). We recently demonstrated that various levels of inflammatory cytokines were induced and reached their peak levels at 12 hours post-injury (hpi) in different retinal injury paradigms in adult zebrafish (Zhou et al., 2022). Suppressing inflammation using the glucocorticoid dexamethasone (Dex) inhibited MG proliferation and MG-derived progenitor cell formation, while augmentation of inflammation through zymosan A (Zym) injection exhibited context-dependent effects (Silva et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). Similarly, genetic or pharmacological ablation of microglia/macrophages results in reduced MG proliferation (Fischer et al., 2014; Conedera et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). At the molecular level, inflammation regulates MG proliferation through multiple signaling pathways, including the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) and nuclear factor kappa-B (NFκB) signaling pathways (Zhang et al., 2020; Palazzo et al., 2022). However, the key inflammatory molecules and the mechanisms by which they regulate MG reprogramming and proliferation remain poorly understood.
One limitation of traditional research methods is their inability to effectively analyze cell heterogeneity, which often leads to important but transient cellular and molecular events being overshadowed by “noise” from other cells. A previous study reported changes in MG status at the single-cell level following retinal injury in different vertebrate models (Hoang et al., 2020). However, the heterogeneity of different immune cell types in the retina and their interactions with MG during retinal regeneration process remain unclear. In this study, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) of zebrafish retinas subjected to stab injury to investigate the cell heterogeneity, cell state transition, and cell–cell interactions among MG and immune cells. Furthermore, we examined the connection between inflammation and Janus kinase–signal transducers and activators of transcription (Jak–Stat) signaling in MG, as well as how immune modulation influenced MG status.

## Methods

## Zebrafish and retinal injury
All zebrafish used in the study were treated in accordance with the Nantong University Guidelines for Animal Use and Care. All zebrafish experiments were approved by the Nantong University Experimental Animal Center (approval No. 20220313-001, approved on February 23, 2022). Zebrafish were maintained at 28°C with a 14/10-hour light/dark cycle in an automatic zebrafish breeding system (Haisheng Biotech, Shanghai, China). Adult wild-type (AB) zebrafish were obtained from the China Zebrafish Resource Center (Wuhan, China). Tg(gfap:GFP) and Tg(mpeg: nsfb-mCherry) transgenic zebrafish were described in our previous study (Zhang et al., 2020). All experiments were designed and reported according to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines (Percie du Sert et al., 2020). Adult zebrafish aged 4–6 months of both sexes with body weights ranging from 0.4 to 0.5 g were randomly assigned to the experimental groups. Each group contained at least three zebrafish.
The retinal stab-injury method has been described previously (Zhang et al., 2020). Briefly, fish were anesthetized in 0.02% Tricane (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in system water. Then, the back of the right eye was stabbed with a 30-G needle through the sclera, once in each quadrant. The needle was inserted to the length of the bevel to ensure that the same extent of damage occurred at each injury site. The uninjured left eye was used as a negative control. After retinal injury, the fish were returned to clean system water to recover.

## scRNAseq of the zebrafish retina
Zebrafish were anesthetized in 0.02% Tricane in system water. Next, the eyes were excised under a dissecting microscope. Retinas isolated at different time points (uninjured control, 12 hpi, 1 day post-injury (dpi), and 2 dpi) were washed in RNAase-free phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and digested in 4 mL 1000 U/mL papain solution (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 10 min. The cell suspensions were then centrifuged at 300 × g at 4°C for 5 minutes. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 3 mL cold 2% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and slowly aspirated through a 38-μm sieve to remove adherent cell clumps. The single-cell suspensions were stained with trypan blue to ensure that > 80% of the cells were alive and adjusted to a target concentration of 1000 cells/μL. Next, the single-cell suspension was added to a 10× Chromium Chip (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA), which was processed using a 10× Genomics Chromium Single-Cell 3′ kit (v3) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with the expectation of capturing 8000 cells. cDNA amplification and library construction were performed according to standard protocols. The libraries were sequenced by LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (double-end sequencing, 150 bp; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at a minimum depth of 20,000 reads per cell.

## Bioinformatics analysis of scRNAseq data
Bioinformatics analysis of the scRNAseq data was performed using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). The Illumina sequencing results were converted to FASTQ format using bcl2fastq software (version 5.0.1, Illumina). Reads were aligned to the zebrafish GRCz11_96 genome (http://www.ensembl.org/Danio_rerio/Info/Index) using CellRanger software (version 7.0.0, https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-gene-expression/software/pipelines/latest/what-is-cell-ranger), and cellular 3′ end transcripts in the sequenced samples were identified and counted.
The output CellRanger expression profile matrix was loaded into Seurat (version 4.1.0, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) (Hao et al., 2021) to filter out low quality cells using the following thresholds: > 500 genes expressed per cell, mitochondrial genes expressed in < 25% of cells. The cells were projected into two-dimensional (2D) space using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) or Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) according to the following steps: 1) gene expression values were calculated using the LogNormalize method of Seurat’s “NormalizeData” function; 2) principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the normalized expression values, with the top 20 PCs used for clustering and Findcluster analysis; 3) Findallmarker function was used to analyze the marker genes for each cluster based on the following criteria: expressed by > 10% of the cells in each cluster, P value ≤ 0.01, gene expression ploidy logFC ≥ 0.26. Differentially expressed genes were defined by the following criteria: P < 0.01 and log2FC ≥ 0.26.
To perform Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, we conducted hypergeometric testing of the differentially expressed genes in each cluster obtained from Findallmarker analysis compared with the other clusters. Pseudotime analysis was performed using Monocle2 software (http://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle-release/) (Qiu et al., 2017). Branch-dependent gene expression in single-cell trajectories was analyzed using the Branched Expression Analysis Modeling (BEAM) function of the Monocle2 software program. To perform RNA velocity analysis (La Manno et al., 2018), spliced and unspliced reads were recounted using the velocyto python package (https://velocyto.org/velocyto.py/) based on the previously aligned scRNAseq data bam ﬁles. RNA velocity values for each gene in each cell were calculated, and RNA velocity vectors were projected into lower-dimension embedding space, using the scVelo python pipeline. To infer the developmental trajectory of rod progenitors and MG, we embedded RNA velocity vectors to the UMAP space. Cell-cell interaction analysis was carried out using the CellPhoneDB database (https://www.cellphonedb.org) (Efremova et al., 2020), in which zebrafish gene names were converted to the names of their human homologs.

## Quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was extracted from the zebrafish retina using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Reverse transcription and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) were carried out as described previously (Zhou et al., 2022). At least three fish were used for each group, and the qPCR reactions were conducted in triplicate. Relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the ΔΔCt method (Rao et al., 2013), and values were normalized to rpl13 mRNA levels. The qPCR primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

## Tissue preparation and immunofluorescence staining
Tissues were prepared for and subjected to immunofluorescence staining as described previously (Zhang et al., 2020). The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-GFP (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and rabbit anti-Opn1lw1 (1:200, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China). The anti-Opn1lw1 polyclonal antibody detects endogenous zebrafish Opn1lw1 (Opsin1) protein, and its efficacy has been validated previously (Jia et al., 2022). 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining was performed using a BeyoClickTM EdU-488 Cell Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization
Fluorescence in situ hybridization was performed as described previously (Zhou et al., 2022). Sense and anti-sense digoxigenin-labeled RNA probes against zebrafish socs3b were transcribed using a digoxigenin-RNA labeling kit (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Upper Bavaria, Germany). No fluorescence signal above background was observed using the sense probe.

## Manipulation of the immune response and Stat3 signaling in the zebrafish retina
The methods used to manipulate the immune response in the zebrafish retina were described in our previous study (Zhang et al., 2020). Briefly, to suppress the immune response, fish were maintained in tank water with or without 15 mg/L Dex (Sigma-Aldrich) for 7 days before retinal injury. Dex treatment continued until the end of the experiment, with the tanks filled with fresh Dex-containing water each day. To induce inflammation in the uninjured retina, fish received an intravitreous injection of Zym (1 μL of 10 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) at the beginning of the experiment. To enhance the immune response in the injured retina, fish received an intravitreous injection of Zym (1 μL of 10 mg/mL) at the time of retinal injury; injection with PBS was used as a negative control. To inhibit the Stat3 signaling in the retina, fish received intravitreous injections of JSI-124 (1 μL of 5 μM, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) once daily for 4 days after retinal injury.

## Microglial ablation
The Tg(mpeg1: nsfb-mCherry) transgenic zebrafish and the method used to perform microglial ablation were described in our previous study (Zhang et al., 2020). Briefly, the transgenic and wild-type fish were maintained in tank water with or without metronidazole (final concentration 5 mM, M3764, Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 days before retinal injury. The retinas were subjected to stab injury on day 4, and metronidazole treatment continued until the end of the experiment, with the tanks filled with fresh metronidazole-containing water twice a day.

## Imaging and cell counts
Retinal cryosections were imaged using a Zeiss Imager M2 upright microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an Axiocam 506 mono camera. Images taken for cell counting were captured with a 10× or 20× objective, and the cells were counted using the ImageJ Cell Counter plugin (Schneider et al., 2012).

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com) was used to statistically analyze the experimental data. Student’s t-test (two tails, unpaired) was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed for comparisons among multiple groups. Error bars show the standard error. A P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

## Characterization of cell types in the intact and stab-injured zebrafish retinas
We previously demonstrated that retinal inflammation is an early event following stab injury to the zebrafish retina, with peak inflammatory cytokine expression occurring at 12 hpi and the peak microglia accumulation observed at 2 dpi (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). To gain a comprehensive understanding of the role that inflammation and immune cells play in retina regeneration, retinas from control zebrafish (uninjured, 0 day) or those subjected to stab injury (12 hpi, 1 dpi, and 2 dpi) were subjected to scRNAseq. A total of 28,969 retinal cells were counted, including 8048 cells from the 0-day timepoint, 6624 cells from the 12-hpi timepoint, 8748 cells from the 1-dpi timepoint, and 5549 cells from the 2-dpi timepoint (Figure 1A). The median number of unique molecular identifiers per cell was 2485, and the median number of genes per cell was 840.

Analysis of the scRNAseq data separated the retinal cells into 21 clusters, and the cell types represented by these clusters were identified based on known markers (Figure 1B–D) (Hoang et al., 2020). Clusters 2, 4, 8, and 12 were identified as MG given their expression of the MG markers rlbp1a, gfap, and glula (Figure 1D). Rod progenitor cells were identified by their markers hmgn2 and pcna. Because there are no definite markers for zebrafish microglia and macrophages, we designated clusters 9, 10, and 16, which expressed mpeg1 and csf1r, as microglia. Notably, these clusters could contain a mixed population of microglia and infiltrated macrophages. Neutrophils were identified by their expression of marker genes lyz and mpx. We also identified a small population of lymphocytes (cluster 18), which expressed the T cell marker lck and the pan–B cell marker cd79a. Analysis of cell composition revealed a higher percentage of microglia and neutrophils in injured retinas compared with uninjured control, while such a difference was not observed for lymphocytes (Figure 1E). This suggests a substantial increase in the population of microglia and neutrophils following retinal injury. Comparison of gene expression profiles before and after injury showed that the highest number of differentially expressed genes was observed in amacrine cells and retinal ganglion cells (Figure 1F). Among the three types of immune cells (microglia, neutrophils and lymphocytes), microglia exhibited the highest number of differentially expressed genes (Figure 1F), indicating a significant change in their cellular state after retinal injury.

## Novel Müller glia subtypes with distinct transcriptional profiles identified by scRNAseq analysis
UMAP analysis revealed a close association between MG and a group of rod progenitor cells (cluster 13, Figures 1B, C, and 2A), suggesting a strong relationship between these cell types. To better understand MG heterogeneity and state transition, this group of rod progenitor cells was selected for further analysis alongside MG. On the basis of the expression of known MG markers and enrichment analysis, the four MG clusters were designated as resting MG1, resting MG2, immunoregulatory MG, and activated MG, respectively (Figure 2A and B). Each subtype displayed distinct markers, as shown in Figure 2B. The majority of the resting MG1 (cluster 2) consisted of MG from the uninjured retina (0 days, Figure 2C) and expressed known markers of quiescent MG such as notch3 and tgfb3, as well as the bHLH transcription factor inhibitor id1 (Additional Figure 1) (Lee et al., 2020; Campbell et al., 2021). GO analysis indicated enrichment of various GO terms in genes that were differentially expressed in resting MG1 compared with other cell clusters, including the BMP, TGFβ and Notch signaling pathways, the sterol biosynthetic process, and negative regulation of cell population proliferation (Figure 2D), which aligns with the quiescent state of MG in the uninjured retina. The immunoregulatory MG (cluster 4) were a novel subtype observed in the injured retina, and their differentially expressed genes exhibited enrichment in GO terms related to response to calcium ion, regeneration, immune system process, immune response, and receptor signaling through JAK-STAT (Figure 2D). Gene expression analysis showed that the immunoregulatory MG expressed high levels of immune response–related genes, such as the complement component genes c4b and c7a, as well as jak1 and stat3, which encode Jak1–Stat3 signaling components (Additional Figure 1). Interestingly, the immunoregulatory MG retained expression of some quiescent MG markers, such as notch3, tgfb3, and id1, albeit at lower levels than resting MG1 (Additional Figure 1), indicating that they represent an intermediate subtype between quiescent and activated MG. Resting MG2 (cluster 12) were also a novel subtype not previously reported, and exhibited roughly equal representation in both the uninjured and injured retina (Figure 2C). GO analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of resting MG2 differentially expressed genes in various functional categories, including cytoplasmic translation, ATP-synthesis coupled proton transport, ribosome assembly, aerobic respiration, and visual perception (Figure 2D), suggesting a heightened state of protein synthesis and energy metabolism. Resting MG2 exhibited relatively elevated expression levels of vision-related genes compared with other MG subtypes, such as opn1lw1 and rom1b (Figure 2B). Indeed, immunofluorescence staining showed that some MG in the intact retina exhibited weak Opn1lw1 (Opsin1) expression (Additional Figure 1C), supporting the existence of this MG subtype. The differentially expressed genes associated with the activated MG cluster were enriched in GO terms associated with ribosome biogenesis, cytoplasmic translation, mRNA splicing, and actin filament organization and regeneration (Figure 2D). The cells in this cluster expressed known regeneration-associated genes, including hbegfa, lepb, crlf1a, the key reprogramming factors ascl1a and lin28a, and the cell proliferation markers ccnd1 and pcna (Figure 2B, and Additional Figure 1) (Goldman, 2014; Zhao et al., 2014; Lahne et al., 2020; Boyd et al., 2023), indicating that this cluster represents MG undergoing reprogramming and proliferation.

## Delineation of two Müller glia-mediated retina regeneration directions by cell fate analysis
To better understand MG reprogramming and state transition, we performed pseudotime analysis of MG and rod progenitor cells using Monocle2 software. We found that MG and rod progenitor cells were distributed across three major branches (Figure 2E and Additional Figure 2A). One of the three branches is the root branch (starting point) of regeneration direction. Pseudotime analysis indicated that, in one direction, resting MG1 transitioned to resting MG2, which then became activated and ultimately transformed into rod progenitor cells (Figure 2E, the upper left and upper right branches). In the other direction, resting MG1 transitioned to immunoregulatory MG and activated MG (Figure 2E, the upper left and bottom branches). RNA velocity analysis, which was used to examine the direction of cell state transition, yielded similar results (Figure 2F). The observation that resting MG1 transitioned to immunoregulatory MG and activated MG was consistent with our qPCR findings, which indicated that the expression levels of resting MG1 markers (cyp51 and msmo1) in the retina gradually decreased after injury, while those of immunoregulatory MG (c7a and steap4) and activated MG (crlf1a and lepb) increased following injury (Additional Figure 3).
To gain insight into the mechanisms regulating MG cell fate choice, BEAM analysis was performed to identify genes with branch-dependent expression patterns. The BEAM analysis showed that the two regenerative directions had distinct gene expression profiles (Figure 2G). The direction leading to rod progenitor cells exhibited significant up-regulation of genes encoding high-mobility group family proteins (hmgn2, hmgb2a/2b), phototransduction-related proteins (rho, pde6gb), and hemoglobin (hbaa, hbba1), as well as down-regulation of genes encoding inhibin proteins (inhbab, inhbb), glutamine synthetase (glula), Notch/TGFβ targets (her6, id1), PI3K inhibitor (pik3ip1), and the PDGF receptor (pdgfrb) (Figure 2G). Conversely, in the direction leading to activated MG, significant up-regulation of immune-response genes (tnfrsf11b, tnfrsf21, c7a, c7b, steap4), Jak–Stat signaling–related genes (socs3b, lepb, crlf1a), serine/threonine protein kinases (sgk1, sik2b), pluripotency factor (sox2), and IGF-binding protein (igfbp5b) was observed (Figure 2G). Next, we analyzed expression of differentially expressed transcription factors in the two regenerative directions (Additional Figure 2B). While there were many differentially expressed transcription factors that were shared in both directions, including hmga1a, ascl1a, foxp4, and her4.2, we also identified a large number of differentially expressed transcription factors that were unique to the rod progenitor cell direction, such as smarcc1a, insm1a, foxn4, and sox11b, or to the activated MG direction, such as zfhx4, bhlhe40, hif1ab, and hivep1. These findings indicate that the two regenerative directions undergo distinct reprogramming processes.

## Retinal microglia heterogeneity and status transition
Microglia constituted the largest population of immune cells in the retinal samples (Figure 1B, and C). Their abundance increased significantly at 1 and 2 dpi (Figure 3A), which is in line with findings from our previous study (Zhang et al., 2020). In contrast, neutrophil abundance peaked at 12 hpi, whereas the abundance of lymphocytes remained relatively stable across all four time points tested (Figure 3A). Analysis of the transcriptional profile of the microglia revealed a transient decrease in expression of the microglial markers mpeg1.1 and p2ry12 at 12 hpi, accompanied by up-regulation of the cell cycle markers pcna and mki67 at 1 and 2 dpi (Additional Figure 4A). These findings are consistent with the known response of microglia to central nervous system injuries (Hammond et al., 2019; O’Koren et al., 2019; Wieghofer et al., 2021; Benhar et al., 2023).

On the basis of their expression profiles and enrichment analysis, the three microglia clusters identified in the scRNAseq analysis were designated as microglia-1 (cluster 9), proliferative microglia (cluster 10), and microglia-2 (cluster 16) (Figure 3B). The cell type markers for each microglia subtype are presented in Figure 3E. Approximately 80% of the cells in the microglia-1 cluster were derived from the injured retina, while the remaining cells originated from the uninjured retina (Figure 3C). This subtype exhibited high expression levels of il1b and a homolog of HB-EGF (hbegfb) (Figure 3E) and was enriched in GO terms related to immune response, glycolytic process, response to cytokine, chemotaxis, inflammatory response, antigen processing and presentation, and fin regeneration (Figure 3D). The majority of the cells in the proliferative microglia cluster were obtained from retinal samples taken at 1 and 2 dpi and were enriched in GO terms including mitotic cell cycle, cell division, aerobic respiration, cell cycle, and DNA replication (Figure 3D). The cells in the microglia-2 cluster were predominantly derived from retinal samples taken at 2 dpi and were enriched in functional categories such as immune response, proteolysis, antigen processing and presentation, immune system process, leukocyte chemotaxis, and phagocytosis (Figure 3D and Additional Figure 4B). This subtype also displayed high expression levels of interferon-regulatory factors and interferon-response genes (Additional Figure 4C), indicating the involvement of these cells in interferon production and the interferon response. Further analysis showed that microglia-1 expressed many pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors (Additional Figure 4D). In contrast, the expression levels of these factors in proliferative microglia were considerably lower (Additional Figure 4D).
Pseudotime analysis revealed that retinal microglia were distributed across three major branches (Figure 3F). The upper branch consisted of microglia-2, as well as a fraction of the microglia-1 and proliferative microglia (Figure 3F). The lower left branch was mainly composed of microglia-1, with a small fraction of proliferative microglia (Figure 3F). The lower right branch consisted almost entirely of proliferative microglia (Figure 3F). Pseudotime analysis suggested that microglia underwent a transition from the upper branch to the lower two branches (Figure 3F, upper panels). Analysis of cell distribution along the branches showed that, in the uninjured retina, a small number of microglia-1 and proliferative microglia were located in the upper and lower right branches, respectively (Figure 3F, lower panels, 0 days). Following retinal injury, a decrease in microglia in the upper branch and an increase in microglia in the two lower branches were observed at 12 hpi and 1 dpi (Figure 3F, lower panels, 12 hours and 1 day). At 2 dpi, the number of cells in the lower two branches decreased, while the number in the upper branch considerably increased (Figure 3F, lower panels, 2 days), suggesting that some cells in the lower branches transitioned back to the upper branch. The temporal distribution of the different microglia subtypes largely aligned with the pseudotime analysis, indicating dynamic changes in microglia status following retinal injury. To gain further insight into the microglial state transition, we performed BEAM analysis to explore branch-dependent expression of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors. Our analysis revealed an increase in the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, such as il1b, il6, tnfa, mmp9, and mmp13a, along with a decrease in the expression of anti-inflammatory genes, including tgfb1a, pdgfba, and igf2a, during the transition from the upper to the lower left branch (Figure 3G). These findings suggest that the upper branch represents anti-inflammatory microglia (M2 phenotype), while the lower left branch represents pro-inflammatory microglia (M1 phenotype). Collectively, these results indicate that microglia transitioned to a pro-inflammatory and proliferative state following retinal injury, with some cells reverting to an anti-inflammatory state at 2 dpi. This observation is consistent with our previous study, which demonstrated that inflammatory cytokine expression in the retinas subjected to stab injury peaked at 12 hpi but rapidly declined by 2 dpi (Zhou et al., 2022).

## Cell–cell interaction analysis revealed extensive crosstalk between Müller glia and immune cells
To explore the possible cell-cell interactions in the regenerating zebrafish retina, we used the CellphoneDB database to identify ligand-receptor interactions among different retinal cell types. Our analysis demonstrated significant cellular crosstalk, with MG exhibiting the highest number of interactions with other cell types (Figure 4A). Among the cell types interacting with MG, the highest number of interactions occurred with retinal neurons (AC_RGC), rod progenitor cells, and MG themselves (Figure 4A and B). MG demonstrated extensive interactions with immune cells, particularly microglia (Figure 4B). Conversely, among the cell types interacting with microglia, the largest number of interactions was observed with MG (Figure 4C). We further analyzed potential interactions among MG and microglial subtypes. The largest number of interactions were observed among resting MG1, immunoregulatory MG, and activated MG (Figure 4D), indicating extensive crosstalk within the MG population in this regenerative direction. In contrast, resting MG2 exhibited the fewest interactions with other MG and microglial subtypes (Figure 4D). Within the microglial population, microglia-1 and microglia-2 exhibited more interactions with MG than proliferative microglia (Figure 4D–F), which is consistent with the aforementioned results showing lower expression of cytokines and growth factors in proliferative microglia (Additional Figure 4D).

We further examined the most significant interaction pairs between MG and immune cells, as identified using the CellphoneDB database. Importantly, immune cells expressed several ligands known to be involved in retinal regeneration, and MG expressed the corresponding receptors (Figure 4G and Additional Figure 5, red boxes). These ligands included Granulin (Tsuruma et al., 2018), PDGF (Saito et al., 2020), TNFα (Nelson et al., 2013), HB-EGF (Wan et al., 2012), VEGF (Mitra et al., 2022), and TGFβ (Lenkowski et al., 2013). Conversely, MG expressed chemokines such as CXCL12 and CXCL14, which facilitate immune cell chemotaxis, as well as cytokines such as MIF and TNFSF10, which regulate immune cell function (Figure 4H and Additional Figure 5, blue boxes). Many of these interaction pairs were shared among different immune cell types, suggesting a synergistic effect in the regulation of MG reprogramming and proliferation. Taken together, these findings indicate that immune cells and MG form a complex network of cellular crosstalk to regulate retinal inflammation and regeneration.

## Inflammation regulates Jak1–Stat3 signaling in Müller glia
It has been demonstrated that Jak-Stat signaling is required for MG reprogramming and proliferation (Zhao et al., 2014). However, the connection between this signaling pathway in MG and inflammation remains unclear. We first examined the expression of Jak1–Stat3 signaling components in different MG subtypes and rod progenitor cells using our scRNAseq data. Our analysis showed that genes encoding key components of the Jak1–Stat3 signaling pathway, including jak1, stat3, il6st (GP130), and the downstream reporter socs3b, were enriched in immunoregulatory MG and activated MG, but not in resting MG1/MG2 or in rod progenitor cells (Figure 5A). Pseudotime analysis further demonstrated that expression of the genes encoding Jak1–Stat3 signaling components increased significantly during the transition from resting MG1 to activated MG, but decreased in the rod progenitor cell direction (Figure 5A and B). MG expression of genes encoding Jak1–Stat3 signaling components peaked at 12 hpi (Figure 5C, 0.5 days), coinciding with peak expression of typical inflammatory cytokines in the injured retina, as determined by qPCR (Figure 5D). Further examination of genes encoding Stat3-activating cytokines in the retina revealed that many of them, such as IL-4, IL-6, IL-11β, IL-21, and LIF (m17), were expressed by immune cells (Additional Figure 6A). Importantly, MG expressed genes encoding the corresponding receptors for some of these Stat3-activating cytokines (Additional Figure 6B and C). These findings suggest that secretation of Stat3-activating cytokines by immune cells could activate Jak1–Stat3 signaling in MG, thereby regulating MG reprogramming and proliferation.

To investigate whether inflammation is required for activation of Jak1–Stat3 signaling in MG, zebrafish were treated with Dex or intravitreally injected with Zym to suppress or enhance retinal inflammation, respectively (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). qPCR analysis showed that Dex treatment significantly reduced the expression of genes encoding Jak1–Stat3 signaling component in the injured retina (Figure 5E). This was further confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization, which demonstrated that expression of the Jak1–Stat3 reporter socs3b was increased in MG at 12 hpi (Figure 5F), while Dex treatment nearly abolished the socs3b signal in the inner nuclear layer (Figure 5G). Conversely, intravitreal injection of Zym into the uninjured retina led to a significant increase in retinal expression of Jak1–Stat3 signaling components (Figure 5H), suggesting that inflammation alone was sufficient to activate this signaling pathway in the intact retina. Furthermore, Dex treatment markedly reduced the injury-dependent expression of IL-6–family cytokines in the retina (Figure 5I), providing further support for the role of inflammation in regulating Jak1–Stat3 signaling in MG. Consistent with this, microglial ablation in Tg(mpeg1:nfsB-mcherry) transgenic fish (Zhang et al., 2020) significantly decreased the expression of Jak1–Stat3 signaling components in the injured retina (Figure 5J and K), indicating that microglia are necessary for activation of this signaling. We previously demonstrated that enhancing retinal inflammation via Zym injection promotes MG proliferation in the injured retina (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). Importantly, this effect was abolished in the retinas of fish treated with a Stat3 inhibitor JSI-124 (Figure 5L), suggesting that inflammation regulates MG proliferation through Jak1–Stat3 signaling.

## Inflammation and Jak–Stat signaling regulate Müller glia state transition in the injured retina
Inflammation and Jak–Stat signaling are required for MG reprogramming and proliferation (Zhao et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2022). The findings described above showed extensive interactions between immune cells and MG, as well as activation of Jak1–Stat3 signaling in immunoregulatory MG and activated MG induced by inflammation in the injured zebrafish retina. To investigate the role of inflammation and Jak–Stat signaling in MG status transition, qPCR was performed to compare the expression of MG subtype–specific markers in retinas subjected to immune suppression or Stat3 inhibition with retinas from the control group. Our results revealed that Dex treatment significantly alleviated the injury-dependent reduction in the expression of resting MG1 markers, including cyp51, insig1, msmo1, tgfb3, and id1, in the retina (Figure 6A, left panel). Conversely, immune suppression significantly decreased the injury-dependent induction of immunoregulatory MG markers, such as c4b, c7a, steap4, and cebpd, as well as activated MG markers such as lin28a, clcf1, lepb, and crlf1a in the retina (Figure 6A, middle and right panels). These findings suggest that inflammation is necessary for the transition of resting MG1 to the immunoregulatory MG and activated MG status. Furthermore, inhibition of Jak1–Stat3 signaling using the Stat3 inhibitor JSI-124 had a similar effect on the expression of MG subtype markers in the retina (Figure 6B), consistent with the idea that Jak1–Stat3 signaling acts downstream of inflammation to regulate MG reprogramming.

We previously showed that inflammation alone can activate the mTOR signaling in MG (Zhang et al., 2020). However, it is insufficient to induce MG proliferation without causing retinal cell death (Zhou et al., 2022). To investigate the effect of inflammation on MG reprogramming in the uninjured retina, fish received intravitreal Zym injection to induce retinal inflammation, and qPCR was performed 24 hours later to analyze the expression of MG subtype markers. Our results showed that Zym injection significantly reduced the expression of resting MG1 markers and increased the expression of immunoregulatory MG markers in the retina (Figure 6C, left and middle panels). However, Zym injection had no significant effect on the expression of activated MG markers, except for a small increase in lepb expression in the retina (Figure 6C, right panel). These results indicate that Zym-induced inflammation is sufficient to promote MG status transition from resting MG1 to immunoregulatory MG, but these cells were unable to enter the activated MG state, likely due to the absence of other injury signals. In summary, our findings suggest that inflammation and Jak–Stat signaling are required for the status transition of MG in the injured retina, and shed light on the regulatory mechanisms underlying MG reprogramming during retinal regeneration.

## Discussion
Although inflammation and microglia are known to be required for MG proliferation and regeneration of retinal neurons, their cell heterogeneity and interactions with MG are not fully understood. In this study, we conducted scRNAseq analysis of intact zebrafish retinas and zebrafish retinas subjected to stab injury and identified multiple subtypes of MG and microglia. Following retinal injury, we observed a significant change in the state of MG and microglia, as well as extensive crosstalk between the two populations. We also found that Jak1–Stat3 signaling in MG was activated by inflammation, and that both of these factors regulated MG status transition and proliferation. Overall, our findings reveal the cellular heterogeneity of and crosstalk between MG and immune cells in the injured zebrafish retina, underscoring the pivotal role of inflammation and microglia in retinal regeneration.
Our scRNAseq analysis of zebrafish retinal cells separated MG into four clusters: two groups of non-reactive MG (resting MG1, resting MG2) and two groups of reactive MG (immunoregulatory MG, activated MG). In a previous scRNAseq study conducted by another group (Hoang et al., 2020), MG in zebrafish retina were categorized into three major groups: Rest, Reactivity, and Proliferation. We found that “Reactivity” markers such as il4r.1, lepb, and manf were highly expressed in the population that we defined as activated MG (Additional Figure 1). “Proliferation” markers such as asc11a, lin28a, and pcna were also expressed by the activated MG, albeit in a smaller proportion of these cells (Additional Figure 1). These findings suggest that the activated MG characterized in our study encompass both the “Reactivity” and “Proliferation” MG defined in the previous study. In this study, we also identified a group of MG expressing high levels of immune-response genes (immunoregulatory MG) in the injured retina. It is unclear why this subtype was not identified in the previous study. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that the previous study used pan-retinal injury models such as NMDA injury and light damage (Hoang et al., 2020), while a local stab-injury model was used in this study. These different injury paradigms may have elicited varying responses in MG, leading to differences in subtype identification. We also identified a second regeneration route in which resting MG2 became activated and transformed into rod progenitor cells. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that MG in teleost fish retina generate lineage-restricted rod progenitor cells located in the outer nuclear layer (Stenkamp, 2011; Lenkowski and Raymond, 2014). Why this regeneration route was not observed in the previous study is unclear. It is possible that rod progenitor cells were excluded from the pseudotime and RNA velocity analysis of MG in the previous study, or that the disparate injury paradigms employed in the two studies contributed to the discrepancy.
scRNAseq analysis of microglia demonstrated an increase in microglial abundance and proliferation in the zebrafish retina at 1 to 2 dpi (Figure 3A and Additional Figure 4A). This finding is consistent with a previous study showing that a significant proportion of L-plastin+ immune cells (presumably microglia and macrophages) were PCNA+ at 1 and 2 dpi in zebrafish retina injured by ouabain treatment (Mitchell et al., 2018). Twelve hours following retinal injury, the microglia exhibited down-regulation of the expression of marker genes (Additional Figure 4A). A similar finding was reported in mice retinal microglia 12 hours after optic nerve injury (Benhar et al., 2023), suggesting that this is a common response to central nervous system injury. Examination of the microglia transcriptome showed that these cells expressed genes encoding many cytokines and growth factors, among which grn1, grn2, m17, and tnfb were also reported to be up-regulated in microglia and macrophage from the injured zebrafish retina in a previous RNA sequencing study (Mitchell et al., 2019). We further separated the retinal microglia into three subtypes and performed pseudotime analysis, which indicated dynamic changes in their status following retinal injury. At 12 hpi and 1 dpi, microglia transitioned to the pro-inflammatory and proliferative branches, while at 2 dpi, they returned to the anti-inflammatory branch. Interestingly, the timing of microglial transition to a pro-inflammatory state coincided with the MG reprogramming phase, which mainly occurred within the first day, while the timing of microglial transition to an anti-inflammatory state aligned with the MG proliferation that occurred at 2 dpi (Goldman, 2014). This suggests that different microglia statuses may contribute to distinct stages of MG status transition. Indeed, an earlier study showed that immune suppression before retinal injury delayed MG proliferation, while immune suppression after retinal injury accelerated MG proliferation and rod regeneration (White et al., 2017). The divergent effects of immune modulation on MG proliferation during different time periods may be attributable to the different microglial statuses and functions associated with those stages.
Analysis of cell-cell interactions in the retina showed extensive crosstalk between MG and other retinal cell types. Unlike in the mouse retina after optic nerve injury, where microglia were identified as the strongest interactor (Benhar et al., 2023), we found that MG were the dominant cell type interacting with other cell types in the injured zebrafish retina. This underscores the pivotal role of MG in sensing the microenvironment during retina regeneration. Interestingly, the highest number of interactions involving MG was observed with retinal neurons. Given that glia-neuron crosstalk could promote neural stem cell proliferation and neurogenesis in the zebrafish central nervous system (Bhattarai et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2021), it would be valuable to investigate the potential contribution of these interactions to retinal regeneration. Another noteworthy interaction identified in the study is the crosstalk among different MG subtypes, particularly within the activated MG direction. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that MG regulate themselves or other MG through secreted factors in an autocrine or paracrine manner (Wan et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2020). We further demonstrated extensive crosstalk between MG and immune cells, particularly microglia. Among the ligand-receptor pairs identified between MG and immune cells, the VDR-HBEGF pair appears to be particularly important, as a previous study showed that HBEGF is necessary and sufficient for MG proliferation and retinal regeneration (Wan et al., 2012). The HBEGF gene identified in the previous study was hbegfa, and was expressed by MG, while we found that immune cells express both hbegfa and hbegfb, which encode ligands that could bind to the VDR receptor on MG. It will be intriguing to further explore the role of immune cell–secreted HBEGFs in MG reprogramming and proliferation. Additionally, we previously showed that inflammation and microglia promote MG proliferation through the mTOR signaling pathway, although the specific factors secreted by immune cells that were responsible for mTOR activation were not identified (Zhang et al., 2020). Given that we have now identified numerous growth factors expressed by immune cells, it would be worthwhile to further explore their role in mTOR activation in MG in future studies.
A previous study demonstrated that leptin and IL-6–family cytokines expressed by MG synergize to activate Jak–Stat signaling in MG themselves, promoting their proliferation (Zhao et al., 2014). Consistent with these findings, we observed that lepb and clcf1 were expressed by activated MG, and Jak1–Stat3 signaling was activated in MG in the injured retina. In addition, we found that several Stat3-activating cytokines, such as il4, il6, il11b, il13, and m17, were expressed by immune cells, while MG expressed many of the corresponding receptors. Although we did not detect expression of the il6r gene encoding the IL-6 receptor in MG, IL-6 could bind soluble IL-6R and activates Jak1–Stat3 signaling through trans-signaling (Rose-John et al., 2023). Importantly, our results showed that immune suppression or microglial ablation significantly reduced the expression of Jak1–Stat3 signaling components in the retina and MG, while Zym injection into the intact retina was sufficient to activate this signaling pathway. Furthermore, pseudotime analysis revealed that the expression of Jak1–Stat3 signaling components was already increased in immunoregulatory MG before lepb and clcf1 expression levels increased in activated MG. It is likely that immune cell–secreted factors initially activated Jak1-Stat3 signaling in MG, promoting their transition to the activated state. Subsequently, the activated MG further expressed lepb and clcf1 to sustain or enhance the activity of this signaling pathway in the regenerative niche. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that other injury signals induced lepb/clcf1 expression in MG, and that they functioned in parallel with inflammation to regulate Jak1–Stat3 signaling in MG.
Inflammation and microglia play crucial roles in MG reprogramming and proliferation in lower vertebrates such as fish and birds. However, these findings seem to contradict the results from a study by Todd et al. (2020), who found that microglia induced MG to adopt an inflammatory status and limited their neurogenic capacity in the context of Ascl1-mediated MG reprogramming. On the basis of their findings, they concluded that the innate immune response limits regeneration in the mammalian retina (Todd et al., 2020). Notably, Ascl1-mediated MG reprogramming in mice does not generate fully multipotent MG-derived progenitors such as those found in the zebrafish retina. The partial reprogramming of MG induced by Ascl1, which produces a limited number of bipolar and amacrine cells, may not necessitate the involvement of microglia or inflammation. However, complete reprogramming of MG into multipotent progenitor cells capable of producing all types of retinal neurons requires the presence of inflammation and microglia, as evidenced in numerous zebrafish studies. Furthermore, the role of inflammation and microglia in other MG reprograming strategies in mammals has not been studied extensively. Therefore, the findings from a study using one strategy may not be universally applicable to all reprograming approaches in mammals. We previously demonstrated that inflammation has complex and context-dependent effects on MG proliferation and retinal regeneration in zebrafish (Zhou et al., 2022). Consistent with this, a recent study showed that repopulated retinal microglia, following their ablation, promote MG reprogramming and preserve visual function in a mouse model of retinal degeneration (Cheng et al., 2023). Therefore, we believe that further research is needed to fully understand the role of inflammation and microglia in mammalian MG reprogramming and retina regeneration.
In summary, this study describes the cell heterogeneity, status transition, and interactions of immune cells and MG during zebrafish retina regeneration. Additionally, we present evidence demonstrating the crucial involvement of inflammation-activated Jak1–Stat3 signaling in the regulation of MG state transitions. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying retina regeneration in fish, but also hold promise for developing new strategies for retinal repair in humans. However, we acknowledge that more robust biological experiments are imperative to further validate the findings from our scRNAseq analysis.
