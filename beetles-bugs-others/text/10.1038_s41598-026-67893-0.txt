Earthquake magnitude estimation using an optimized CNN-DNN-GRU model with binary differential evolution and firefly algorithm.

# Earthquake magnitude estimation using an optimized CNN-DNN-GRU model with binary differential evolution and firefly algorithm

## Abstract
AbstractAccurate earthquake magnitude estimation is critical for mitigating seismic hazards; however, the inherently nonlinear and dynamic characteristics of geophysical data present persistent modeling challenges. To address this complexity, this study proposes a novel hybrid deep learning framework, CNN-DNN-GRU. This architecture synergizes Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for spatial feature extraction, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) for hierarchical data representation, and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) for capturing long-term temporal dependencies. The framework’s efficacy is further enhanced by integrating Binary Differential Evolution (BDE) for robust feature selection and the Firefly Algorithm (FA) for rigorous hyperparameter tuning. The model was trained and evaluated on a comprehensive tabular dataset comprising 3,500,000 seismic event instances—detailing parameters such as magnitude, depth, and geospatial coordinates—spanning from 1990 to 2023, utilizing a chronological 70%–15%–15% split for training, validation, and independent testing, respectively. Empirical evaluation demonstrates the proposed model’s high predictive accuracy, yielding a Mean Squared Error (MSE) of 0.0109, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of 0.1048, Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.0837, Median Absolute Error (MedAE) of 0.0709, Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 0.0230, and a Coefficient of Determination () of 0.9918. These findings validate the architecture’s superior generalization capabilities, establishing it as a highly reliable computational tool for a magnitude-estimation component that may support future early-warning-oriented systems when integrated with real-time waveform and station-level data.Keywords: Earthquake magnitude estimation, Deep learning, Artificial intelligence in geoscience, Firefly algorithm, Intelligent disaster management, Binary differential evolution, CNN, DNN, GRUSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

## Introduction
Earthquakes remain one of the most unpredictable and devastating natural disasters, posing significant threats to human life, infrastructure, and economies worldwide. Accurate forecasting of seismic events is a critical scientific challenge due to the complex, nonlinear, and dynamic nature of geological processes. In recent years, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the Internet of Things (IoT) has emerged as a transformative approach to enhancing earthquake magnitude estimation and monitoring capabilities. In1, AI and IoT-based systems are reshaping geoscience applications by enabling real-time data acquisition, intelligent sensing, and predictive analytics for seismic activity forecasting.
Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models have demonstrated significant potential for capturing hidden patterns in vast, heterogeneous seismic datasets. In2 highlighted the importance of intelligent technologies in enabling near real-time earthquake magnitude regression systems, particularly in regions with high seismic risk such as Chile. Their work underscores the value of combining historical seismic records with advanced learning algorithms to improve both the speed and accuracy of predictions.
Moreover, recent advancements in emerging technologies are driving innovations in disaster management frameworks. In3 emphasized the critical role of supporting tools, such as sensor networks, big data analytics, and cloud computing, in building responsive and adaptive earthquake disaster management systems. These technologies not only enhance early detection but also contribute to post-disaster response and resilience planning.
A growing body of literature also explores the development of AI-based Earthquake Early Warning (EEW) systems. In4 provided a systematic review of AI-enabled EEW systems, demonstrating that deep neural networks, support vector machines, and hybrid models have achieved promising results in predicting earthquake magnitude and arrival time with greater reliability than conventional methods.
Furthermore, AI-driven models have been employed to predict and map earthquake-related phenomena such as soil liquefaction, which can significantly amplify damage during seismic events. In5 demonstrated the effectiveness of deep learning models in assessing soil liquefaction risks in innovative urban environments, reinforcing the broader applicability of AI in seismic risk management and infrastructure resilience.
Despite significant advances in seismology and computational modeling, accurately predicting earthquake magnitudes remains a formidable challenge. Traditional statistical and physical models often fail to capture the complex, nonlinear interactions inherent in seismic activity, leading to limited accuracy and generalization6–8. While recent developments in AI and ML have shown promise in seismic magnitude estimation, many existing models suffer from shortcomings such as overfitting, poor temporal modeling, and inefficient feature selection6,7,9. Furthermore, most predictive frameworks either ignore the spatial dependencies of seismic parameters or inadequately tune their hyperparameters, resulting in suboptimal performance on real-world earthquake data7,10,11.
Recent advances in geophysical modeling further demonstrate the importance of metaheuristic optimization for solving nonlinear, ill-posed, and non-unique inverse problems. For example, Hunger Games Search has been successfully applied to lunar gravity anomaly inversion using a 2.75D source approximation, showing its ability to explore complex model spaces and recover reliable subsurface structures12. Similarly, Modified Barnacles Mating Optimizer has been used for full-parametric, transdimensional, and joint inversion of surface-wave dispersion, earthquake-based HVSR, seismic refraction, and resistivity data, indicating that advanced metaheuristics can improve the reliability of near-surface structural interpretation13. Other studies have applied Hunger Games Search, Particle Swarm Optimization, Barnacles Mating Optimizer, and improved Backtracking Search Optimization to gravity, magnetic, electromagnetic, self-potential, and Rayleigh wave inversion problems, confirming their effectiveness in reducing inversion uncertainty, improving convergence, and avoiding local optima14–21. These recent contributions highlight that metaheuristic algorithms are increasingly becoming powerful tools in geophysical data analysis, particularly when the search space is nonlinear, multimodal, and sensitive to parameter selection. In addition to sequence learning and time-series prediction, further support the use of hybrid deep learning and optimization-assisted models for complex estimation tasks. Qin22 investigated Transformer-XL for long-sequence learning behavior modeling, highlighting the importance of capturing long-range dependencies in sequential data. Chen23proposed an improved ARIMA–LSTM hybrid model for inflation-rate time-series forecasting, demonstrating the value of combining statistical and deep learning models for nonlinear prediction. Pan24 studied lightweight machine learning design for time-series forecasting, emphasizing computational efficiency and deployability. Wang et al.25 integrated LSTM with genetic algorithms for agricultural system modeling, showing that global optimization can improve the predictive capability of recurrent neural networks. Earthquake-engineering studies have also further demonstrated the value of machine learning for seismic response and damage assessment. Lazaridis et al.26 developed machine learning models for predicting structural damage in reinforced concrete frames subjected to single and multiple seismic events, showing that data-driven algorithms can support rapid evaluation of structural response under repeated earthquake loading. Similarly, Tao et al.27 proposed support vector regression models for predicting the maximum drift ratio of buildings using real monitoring data from the NDE1.0 flatfile. Their work showed that machine learning models can capture nonlinear relationships between structural characteristics, seismic intensity measures, and engineering demand parameters, and that reduced-feature models can improve practical applicability by relying on accessible input parameters. Motivated by these developments, the present study integrates Binary Differential Evolution for feature selection and the Firefly Algorithm for hyperparameter optimization to enhance the robustness, accuracy, and generalization capability of the proposed CNN–DNN–GRU earthquake magnitude estimation model.
To address these limitations, there is a pressing need for an intelligent, optimized deep learning architecture that can effectively extract spatial and temporal features, reduce redundancy through feature selection, and fine-tune learning parameters to improve accuracy and robustness. This research aims to fill this gap by developing a hybrid CNN-DNN-GRU model, empowered by BDE for feature selection and the FA for hyperparameter optimization, to provide accurate and reliable earthquake magnitude prediction.
The primary objective of this study is to develop an advanced, highly accurate model specifically for earthquake magnitude prediction by leveraging the strengths of hybrid deep learning architectures and intelligent optimization algorithms. It is important to clarify that the proposed framework is designed to forecast the magnitude of seismic events using historical and geospatial data; it does not predict the severity of surface strong motion or the precise time of an earthquake. Earthquake data is inherently complex, involving both spatial and temporal dependencies28,29. To effectively capture these patterns, this research proposes a hybrid framework that integrates a CNN for spatial feature extraction, a DNN for hierarchical representation learning, and a GRU for modeling temporal sequences in seismic events. In addition to model architecture, another key objective is to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of the prediction process through intelligent optimization techniques. The BDE algorithm is utilized to perform feature selection, reducing data dimensionality and eliminating irrelevant or redundant features that could degrade model performance9,29. This not only improves training speed but also enhances generalization by focusing the model on the most informative input variables. Furthermore, to ensure optimal model configuration, the FA is employed for hyperparameter tuning. Hyperparameter selection plays a critical role in the performance of deep learning models, and the use of FA helps automate and improve this process by exploring the search space more effectively than manual or grid-based approaches29.
This paper offers the following novel contributions to the field of seismic magnitude regression and intelligent geoscience systems:
A novel hybrid architecture (CNN-DNN-GRU) is proposed that integrates spatial, deep, and sequential learning components for enhanced earthquake magnitude forecasting.
Incorporation of metaheuristic optimization techniques, specifically BDE for feature selection and FA for hyperparameter tuning, to improve model accuracy and computational efficiency.
Comprehensive evaluation on a large-scale earthquake catalog comprising 3,500,000 seismic-event records from 1990 to 2023, demonstrating the robustness and scalability of the proposed framework under the adopted catalog-based evaluation protocol.
Superior performance results, achieving an of 0.9918 and low error metrics (MSE = 0.0109, MAE = 0.0837, RMSE = 0.1048), significantly outperforming CNN, LSTM, GRU, ResNet50, and VGG-19 models.
A reproducible and scalable framework that can be extended to other geospatial and temporal prediction tasks, supporting a magnitude-estimation component that may support future early-warning-oriented systems when integrated with real-time waveform and station-level data.

## Related works
The integration of AI, machine learning, and seismology has significantly improved earthquake magnitude estimation and post-disaster assessment. Traditional models struggle to capture complex nonlinear dynamics, leading researchers to use data-driven techniques like deep learning and ensemble learning. These techniques improve predictive accuracy and operational speed by extracting relevant features from seismic waveforms, ground motion data, and satellite imagery. However, challenges remain in generalization across regions, data imbalance, real-time deployability, and model interpretability. This section critically reviews key contributions in literature, highlighting their methodologies, results, and limitations to position the motivation and novelty of the present study.
Joshi et al.30 addressed the critical challenge of accurately estimating earthquake magnitudes in real-time, particularly when relying on data from a single seismic station. To improve early warning capabilities, they proposed an innovative deep learning architecture named AMagDN. This model integrates LSTM, bidirectional LSTM, an autocorrelation-based attention mechanism, and a machine learning module, enabling it to extract rich temporal and geospatial features from multivariate seismic waveform data. A key novelty of the model is its use of seven fused tabular features to enhance prediction accuracy. Trained and tested on a substantial dataset from Japan’s Kyoshin Network, covering earthquakes between magnitude 5.5 and 8.0, the AMagDN model achieved a 40% reduction in mean absolute error compared to the second-best baseline. Furthermore, its ability to accurately predict the magnitude of major events (7.7 and 7.3) using both single and multi-station data demonstrates the model’s robustness and potential in seismic hazard mitigation.
Ommi and Hashemi31 explored the application of machine learning techniques to predict the occurrence of large-scale earthquakes in the highly active North Zagros seismic zone, a region of significant concern due to its dense population and historical seismic activity. Recognizing the growing capability of machine learning in pattern recognition and parameter analysis, the authors evaluated three algorithms (ANN, RF, and SVM) using nine seismic parameters to predict potential earthquakes one month in advance. The performance of each model was assessed using recall, accuracy, precision, and F1-score metrics.
Karimai et al.32 addressed the critical problem of soil liquefaction-induced ground subsidence, a major hazard in earthquake-prone regions, which poses serious threats to infrastructure and public safety. Motivated by the extensive liquefaction damage observed in Urayasu City during the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake, the study goes beyond traditional binary liquefaction classification models by proposing a quantitative predictive model for estimating the magnitude of ground subsidence post-liquefaction. Leveraging data from Japan’s Nankai Trough Megathrust Earthquake scenario, the researchers constructed a GDBT model and used XGBoost to achieve superior prediction accuracy compared to LightGBM. To enhance model transparency and interpretability, they SHAP, identifying the most influential features contributing to subsidence predictions.
Liu et al.33 tackled the limitations of traditional on-site EEW systems, which rely on manually selected P-wave feature parameters for predicting PGA. To overcome the subjectivity and inefficiencies of feature selection, the authors proposed an end-to-end deep learning framework called DLPGA, based on CNNs. This model takes the initial 3–6 seconds of vertical seismic wave data from a single station as input and directly predicts PGA values. Trained on Japanese seismic records, the DLPGA model outperformed the widely used Pd method, showing a 12–23% improvement in correlation, a 22–25% reduction in error standard deviation, and a 6.9–19.7% decrease in mean prediction error. Notably, DLPGA with 3-second wave input outperformed Pd even with 6-second input, highlighting the model’s speed and precision. Furthermore, generalization tests using Chilean earthquake records demonstrated that DLPGA maintains superior accuracy across regions, improving the identification of destructive ground motion by 35–150%.
Demir et al.34 conducted an in-depth study on predicting the MDR of two types of reinforced concrete (RC) frames using advanced tree-based ML techniques. The models evaluated included RF, XGBoost, and SGB, trained on a dataset comprising 2,300 ground motion records and 21 input parameters derived from nonlinear time history analyses. A major focus of the research was assessing the role of data preprocessing in enhancing model performance. Techniques such as outlier detection, missing value imputation, log transformation, and feature selection using Hill Climbing were integrated into the ML pipeline. The evaluation was based on six performance metrics, with results showing that data preprocessing significantly improved predictive accuracy, particularly for the RF model. Post-processing, the RF model achieved 12% and 6.22% improvement for R8 and IR8 structures, respectively, outperforming both XGBoost and SGB.
Ju et al.35 investigated the quantitative prediction of slope topographic amplification of peak seismic acceleration. Recognizing the limitations of traditional regression models, which rely on subjective assumptions and offer limited accuracy, the authors introduced an artificial intelligence-based regression framework to model the amplification effect. The model uses slope inclination, slope height, and seismic wave frequency as input parameters and predicts the amplification ratio as the output. By capturing the nonlinear multivariate relationship between input variables and the amplification response, the proposed AI model significantly outperformed traditional methods (achieving an improvement of 17.84%–32.60% and a reduction in RMSE by 30.05%–77.36%).
Al-Rawashdeh et al.36 explored the effectiveness of Bayesian Optimization-enhanced machine learning models for the classification of earthquake-induced building damage. The study focused on addressing class imbalance, a common issue in seismic damage datasets where certain damage grades are overrepresented while others are underrepresented. To improve predictive reliability, the authors optimized three machine learning models (Random Forest, ElasticNet, and Decision Tree) using Bayesian Optimization, which targeted maximizing the Area Under the AUROC to enhance model discrimination. Results showed significant performance gains, with the best-optimized model achieving an AUROC of 0.9952. Among the models, ElasticNet achieved the highest test accuracy of 92.56%, while Random Forest showed superior generalization in terms of lower MSE and a train accuracy of 99.82%.
Reis et al.37 focused on the critical task of automatically detecting road cracks caused by the devastating February 6, 2023, Gaziantep–Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, which severely impacted infrastructure in several Turkish provinces. Recognizing the importance of maintaining safe and functional roadways for emergency response and recovery, the study applied deep learning and machine learning techniques to classify post-disaster road damage. Using a custom dataset collected from the Nurdağı and Adıyaman regions (categorized into cracked and uncracked road surfaces) the authors employed the ResNet152 deep learning model for feature extraction. These features were then classified using several traditional machine learning algorithms, including SVM, KNN, Adaptive Boosting, and Naive Bayes. To further enhance classification performance, an ensemble learning (EL) strategy was implemented. Experimental results demonstrated that SVM and the EL model achieved the highest accuracy at 98.68%, indicating their robustness in post-earthquake damage detection.
Sidik et al.38 demonstrated the application of machine learning classification techniques in the field of volcanology, specifically for classifying volcanic seismic events at Mt. Merapi. Utilizing a dataset comprising 266 earthquake samples, the authors aimed to automatically classify seismic time series data into five categories: VTA, VTB, MP, LF, and RF. Signal features were extracted based on dominant frequency, spectral pattern, and amplitude, which served as distinguishing attributes for each class. The study implemented the XGBoost algorithm for classification, experimenting with various train-test splits. The highest testing accuracy achieved was 76.67% using a 70:30 train-test split, outperforming previous approaches such as SVM, which had reached a maximum of 74.4%. Additionally, the spectral analysis plots demonstrated improved differentiation between event types, indicating that XGBoost not only improved classification accuracy but also enhanced interpretability.
Yamani et al.39 addressed the ongoing challenge in seismology of enhancing the accuracy and reliability of earthquake magnitude prediction, which traditional methods often fail to achieve effectively. In response, the study proposed a novel model combining ANNs with a tabu-search optimization strategy for hyperparameter tuning. This hybrid approach aims to refine the ANN’s configuration for improved forecasting performance. Using a comprehensive earthquake dataset, the model was trained in over 50 epochs with a batch size of 32 and evaluated using standard regression metrics. The results yielded a MAE of 0.0806 and an score of 33.9%, indicating moderate predictive power with promising accuracy. Although the is relatively limited, the low MAE reflects the model’s capacity to produce consistent and stable magnitude estimations.
Zhang et al.40 proposed an advanced hybrid deep learning model to analyze the seismic response of high-speed railway infrastructure, addressing the limitations of traditional monitoring techniques. Their framework integrates CNN and LSTM networks to enhance the accuracy of fiber grating-based seismic response prediction. Utilizing a quasi-distributed fiber optic sensing system, seismic data were collected from seven grating monitoring points installed along critical railway components, including the track plate, base plate, rail, and beam. The CNN component performs spatial feature extraction on time-windowed input sequences, while the LSTM network captures temporal dependencies to predict the central monitoring point’s response based on peripheral sensor inputs. Experimental results confirmed the model’s superior performance, achieving an RMSE of 0.3753, MAE of 0.2968, and an score of 0.9371.
Sadhukhan et al.41 investigated the potential of deep learning models to predict earthquake magnitudes based on seismic indicators, addressing the limitations of traditional prediction methods in capturing the dynamic nature of earthquakes. The study utilized eight mathematically derived seismic indicators from three seismically active regions (Japan, Indonesia, and the Hindu-Kush Karakoram Himalayan (HKKH) region). Three advanced deep learning architectures were explored: LSTM, Bi-LSTM, and a self-attention-based transformer model. Each model was trained and evaluated using robust regression loss metrics including MAE, MSE, log-cosh loss, and MSLE. The models achieved high prediction accuracy across regions, with LSTM performing best for Japan (MAE = 0.060), Bi-LSTM excelling for Indonesia (MAE = 0.073), and the transformer model leading in the HKKH region (MAE = 0.062).
Saida et al.42 proposed an automated structural damage detection framework for post-earthquake building inspections using UAV imagery and advanced computer vision techniques. Recognizing the limitations of manual assessments in post-disaster scenarios, the study developed a dual-model approach to identify both specific damage types and overall structural states. A FPN was employed to segment various damage features while UNet++ architecture was used to evaluate the damage severity state. The dataset consisted of 3,805 high-resolution UAV images, which were resized for model training and validation. FPN achieved high intersection-over-union (IoU) scores, including 0.93 for spall, 0.99 for components, and 0.59 for cracks, indicating strong segmentation accuracy. Meanwhile, UNet++ achieved an IoU of 0.72 for semantic damage classification.
Bilal et al.43 developed a graph-based deep learning model designed to enhance the performance of earthquake early warning systems by accurately predicting the magnitude and depth of seismic events across multiple stations and geographic regions. The proposed framework integrates a GCNN with batch normalization and an attention mechanism to improve training stability, speed, and feature focus. After preprocessing waveform data, CNN layers extract spatial features, which are refined by the attention mechanism to emphasize critical signals. These features, combined with event location information, are passed through the graph network to generate magnitude and depth predictions. The model was evaluated on two distinct seismic datasets from Japan and Alaska, capturing different tectonic environments. It achieved low RMSE values of 2.8 (Alaska) and 4.0 (Japan) for magnitude prediction, and 2.87 (Alaska) and 2.66 (Japan) for depth prediction.
While the reviewed studies demonstrate significant progress in applying ML and DL techniques to earthquake magnitude estimation, structural damage assessment, and post-disaster response, several limitations remain. Most existing models either focus on a single aspect of seismic magnitude estimation or lack integration of optimization strategies for enhancing model efficiency and accuracy. Additionally, few works leverage hybrid architectures that combine spatial, deep, and temporal learning components with advanced metaheuristic algorithms for both feature selection and hyperparameter tuning. Considering these gaps, this study introduces a novel hybrid deep learning framework (CNN-DNN-GRU) enhanced by BDE for feature selection and the FA for hyperparameter optimization. This approach aims to deliver a more accurate, robust, and generalizable solution for earthquake magnitude prediction, addressing key limitations observed in prior work.

## Methodology
This paper proposes a hybrid deep learning framework, CNN–DNN–GRU, for earthquake magnitude estimation, optimized using Binary Differential Evolution (BDE) for feature selection and the Firefly Algorithm (FA) for hyperparameter tuning. The raw dataset comprises 3,500,000 global seismic-event records spanning 1990–2023.The data were preprocessed by handling anomalous values, constructing chronological event-history sequences, and partitioning the resulting sequences chronologically into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing subsets. Normalization parameters were estimated exclusively from the training subset and subsequently applied to the validation and test subsets. BDE was employed to identify the most informative features, reducing redundancy and improving model efficiency. The hybrid model architecture combines a CNN for spatial feature extraction, a DNN for capturing complex nonlinear relationships, and a GRU for learning temporal dependencies. FA was used to optimize key hyperparameters such as learning rate, number of neurons, dropout rate, and GRU units by minimizing the validation error. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer and evaluated using standard regression metrics including MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and . Its performance was benchmarked against baseline models, including CNN, LSTM, GRU, ResNet50, and VGG-19, to demonstrate its predictive superiority and robustness. Dataset feature descriptions for ultimate earthquake dataset.
It is important to distinguish between independent input variables and target-derived or post-event descriptors. Although the raw dataset contains the significance variable, this feature was excluded from the final predictive input space. The significance score is strongly associated with earthquake magnitude and may also incorporate post-event impact-related information, such as felt reports, damage, casualties, or economic consequences. Therefore, it may not be available before or during the initial magnitude-estimation stage in real applications. Using this variable as an input for predicting magnitude would create a risk of information leakage and recursive prediction, where information partly derived from the target is used to estimate the target itself.
For this reason, significance was retained only for exploratory data analysis and descriptive visualization, but it was not used in model training, validation, testing, BDE-based feature selection, or FA-based hyperparameter optimization. The final leakage-free input space was restricted to independent earthquake-event metadata that are not directly derived from the target magnitude.

## Data preparation
Data preprocessing is a critical step to ensure data quality and compatibility with the deep learning model. The raw dataset, containing 3,500,000 event instances and seven features, underwent the following steps:
Handling Missing and Anomalous Values: The raw earthquake catalog contained 3,500,000 event records spanning 1990–2023. As shown in Table 3, all reported variables contained 3,500,000 observations; therefore, no conventional missing or NaN values were identified, and no records were removed because of missing data. The raw magnitude values ranged from -9.99 to 8.40. Negative magnitudes were not removed indiscriminately because small negative magnitudes may represent physically valid low-energy microseismic events. However, the specific value M=-9.99 was treated as an invalid catalog placeholder rather than a physically meaningful earthquake magnitude. A total of 57,974 records satisfying this criterion were removed, corresponding to 1.6564% of the original dataset. Other negative magnitude values were retained. The hypocentral-depth values ranged from -10 to 712.5 km. Negative depth observations were not considered invalid solely because they were below zero, because earthquake-catalog depths may be referenced to mean sea level and slightly negative values may occur for shallow events in elevated regions or because of hypocentral-location uncertainty. Consequently, no records were removed solely on the basis of a negative depth value. Following these record-level validity checks, 3,442,026 valid earthquake-event records remained. The subsequent removal of significance, status, and tsunami from the predictive input represents feature-level screening rather than record-level deletion. Significance was excluded because of its strong association with the target magnitude and the resulting risk of information leakage, whereas status and tsunami were excluded because of their negligible variability. The final leakage-free input variables were therefore longitude, latitude, and depth. As summarized in Table 4, the data-quality screening reduced the original 3,500,000 earthquake-event records to 3,442,026 valid observations. No records were removed because of conventional missing values or solely because of negative hypocentral depth. Record-level exclusion was primarily associated with the invalid magnitude placeholder M=-9.99, which accounted for 57,974 observations (1.6564% of the original dataset). In contrast, the exclusion of significance, status, and tsunami was performed at the feature level and did not further reduce the number of earthquake events. This distinction ensures that potentially meaningful low-energy and shallow seismic events are retained while invalid target values and leakage-prone or uninformative features are excluded.

Categorical Encoding: The ’status’ feature was label-encoded. However, given its lack of variability, it was considered non-essential and excluded from final modeling.

First, all earthquake records were ordered according to their event time. Then, a fixed-length sliding-window strategy was applied to construct sequential samples from previous events.

To prevent temporal information leakage, no random train–validation–test partitioning was performed. After chronological ordering and sequence construction, the resulting samples were divided according to event time into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing subsets. Specifically, the earliest 70% of the sequences were used for model training, the subsequent 15% were used for validation, BDE-based model selection, and FA-based hyperparameter optimization, and the most recent 15% were retained as an independent test set and were not involved in any training or optimization stage. Furthermore, the normalization parameters were estimated exclusively from the training subset and subsequently applied unchanged to the validation and test subsets. This chronological evaluation protocol preserves the temporal ordering of the earthquake catalog and ensures that information from future events is not used to predict earlier events.

The proposed CNN–DNN–GRU hybrid model is shown in Figure 7, which integrates convolutional, dense, and recurrent layers to effectively capture spatial, abstract, and temporal patterns from earthquake-related input data.

The use of kernel_size=3 is consistent with the Conv1D operation, where the convolutional kernel is applied along one input dimension. Algorithm 1 displays the CNN-DNN-GRU hybrid model.
CNN–DNN–GRU.
To clarify the design of the recurrent component, the first GRU layer was configured with return_sequences=True because its role is to preserve the complete learned temporal representation and pass the full sequence of hidden states to the following GRU layer. This allows the second GRU layer to further refine the sequential dependencies extracted from the CNN feature maps. In contrast, the second GRU layer was configured without returning sequences because the target task is a single-output regression problem, where the model estimates one earthquake magnitude value for each input sample. Therefore, the final hidden state of the second GRU layer provides a compact temporal summary that is suitable for the subsequent dense regression block.
A bidirectional GRU was not adopted in the proposed architecture for two main reasons. First, the intended application is earthquake magnitude estimation in a forecasting or early-warning-oriented setting, where future temporal context may not be available at inference time. Bidirectional recurrent layers process the input in both forward and backward directions and may therefore rely on information that would not be accessible in a real-time scenario. Second, the dataset used in this study consists of tabular earthquake-event metadata rather than continuous raw waveform sequences; therefore, using a bidirectional or full sequence-output recurrent structure may unnecessarily increase the number of trainable parameters, computational complexity, and overfitting risk. For this reason, the adopted two-layer GRU design provides a practical balance between temporal representation learning, model simplicity, and regression-oriented output generation.

## Coupling strategy between BDE and FA
The proposed optimization framework couples BDE and FA in a sequential manner rather than an alternating or co-evolutionary manner. In the first stage, BDE is applied for feature selection. Each BDE individual represents a binary feature mask, where a value of 1 indicates that the corresponding feature is selected and a value of 0 indicates that it is excluded. The objective of this stage is to identify the most informative feature subset that minimizes prediction error while reducing redundant or weakly informative variables.
After the BDE process is completed, the selected feature subset is fixed and transferred to the second optimization stage. In the second stage, FA is used to tune the hyperparameters of the CNN–DNN–GRU model, such as the learning rate, dropout rate, number of GRU units, and number of dense neurons. Thus, the information transfer between BDE and FA is one-directional: BDE provides the selected feature mask to FA, while FA optimizes the model hyperparameters using only these selected features. FA does not modify the BDE-selected feature subset, and BDE is not re-executed during FA iterations.
This sequential coupling strategy was adopted for three reasons. First, performing feature selection before hyperparameter tuning reduces the dimensionality of the input space and removes redundant variables before model optimization. Second, fixing the selected feature subset before FA optimization reduces the computational burden because FA evaluates candidate neural-network configurations on a reduced feature space. Third, separating feature selection and hyperparameter tuning improves the stability and interpretability of the optimization pipeline, since the contribution of each stage can be clearly identified. ## Firefly algorithm for hyperparameter optimization
In the proposed CNN–DNN–GRU model, the Firefly Algorithm (FA) is adopted as a metaheuristic strategy to optimize critical hyperparameters and enhance model performance. The FA simulates the natural flashing behavior of fireflies, in which less-bright fireflies move toward brighter ones, based on an attractiveness function that decreases with distance and is modulated by a light-absorption coefficient. Each firefly in the population encodes a candidate solution representing a combination of hyperparameters such as learning rate, dropout rate, and the number of units in GRU and dense layers. The brightness of each firefly is determined by the validation loss (e.g., mean squared error) obtained during training with the corresponding configuration. Through iterative updates influenced by attractiveness and random perturbations, the FA effectively explores the search space to locate optimal configurations. This approach significantly improves the convergence rate, generalization, and predictive accuracy of the proposed model for earthquake magnitude forecasting. The independent test subset was kept completely unseen during training, BDE-based feature selection, and FA-based hyperparameter optimization, and it was used only once for the final evaluation of the selected model.
Nevertheless, using the same validation subset repeatedly during the FA search may still introduce validation-set selection bias, particularly when the number of evaluated configurations is large. To reduce this risk, the search space was constrained to a limited number of meaningful hyperparameter ranges, and the final model was evaluated on an independent test set that did not participate in any optimization step. In future work, nested cross-validation and repeated independent train–validation–test splits will be adopted to further reduce optimizer-induced overfitting and provide a more robust estimate of generalization performance.
Although the FA is used to optimize the hyperparameters of the proposed CNN–DNN–GRU model, it is important to note that the FA itself contains several control parameters, including the population size, maximum number of generations, randomization factor α, base attractiveness , and light absorption coefficient γ. These parameters affect the balance between exploration and exploitation during the optimization process. In this study, the FA control parameters were selected based on commonly adopted ranges in previous metaheuristic optimization studies and refined through preliminary validation experiments. The final configuration was chosen according to the lowest validation MSE, while the independent test set was kept unseen until the final evaluation stage. Therefore, the selected optimizer parameters are not claimed to be universally optimal; rather, they represent empirically effective settings for the present earthquake magnitude estimation task. This validation-based selection strategy reduces the risk of test-set bias and provides a practical balance between optimization performance and computational cost. Similarly, the BDE parameters used for feature selection were selected to maintain sufficient population diversity while ensuring convergence within a reasonable computational budget. Since metaheuristic algorithms are stochastic by nature, their performance may vary depending on initialization and control-parameter settings. To reduce this effect, the optimization process was repeated, and the best feature subset was selected based on validation performance rather than final test performance. This procedure ensures that feature selection is guided by generalization behavior instead of direct fitting to the test set.

## Conclusions and recommendations
In this study, we proposed a hybrid deep learning framework (CNN-DNN-GRU) exclusively for earthquake magnitude prediction, enhanced by two powerful metaheuristic techniques: BDE for feature selection and FA for hyperparameter optimization. We emphasize that the scope of this model is limited to estimating the magnitude of an impending event and is not intended to forecast the precise time of occurrence or the resulting severity of surface strong motion. By integrating convolutional, dense, and recurrent layers, the model effectively captures spatial, hierarchical, and temporal dependencies in seismic data. The experimental results, based on a large-scale global earthquake dataset, demonstrate that the proposed model significantly outperforms conventional models and recent state-of-the-art approaches. It achieved () of 0.9918, (MAE) of 0.0837, and (RMSE) of 0.1048, reflecting its high precision, low error rate, and strong generalization capability. Comparative analysis further confirmed the superiority of our approach over existing models, including CRNNs, LSTMs, stacking ensembles, and hybrid machine learning frameworks, particularly in terms of prediction accuracy and optimization efficiency.
Despite the strong predictive performance achieved by the proposed CNN–DNN–GRU model, several limitations in this methodology. First, although the use of BDE and FA improved feature selection and hyperparameter tuning, the control parameters of these metaheuristic optimizers were selected empirically. As with most global optimizer-assisted deep learning frameworks, parameters such as population size, number of generations, mutation strategy, attractiveness coefficient, light absorption coefficient, and randomization factor may influence convergence behavior and final model performance. Therefore, the selected optimizer settings should be interpreted as validation-effective configurations for the current dataset rather than universally optimal values.
Second, the present study did not perform an exhaustive sensitivity analysis of the optimizer control parameters. Such an analysis would be valuable for determining how robust the proposed model is under different optimization settings and for identifying whether performance gains are mainly due to the hybrid architecture, the feature-selection stage, or the optimizer configuration. Third, the dataset used in this study is based on tabular earthquake-event metadata; therefore, the model estimates earthquake magnitude from historical and geospatial attributes but does not directly process raw seismic waveforms or real-time sensor streams. This limits its immediate applicability to operational earthquake magnitude-estimation components that may support future early-warning-oriented systems when integrated with real-time waveform and station-level data, where waveform arrival information, station-specific signals, and real-time latency constraints are critical.
Future work will focus on four main directions. First, systematic sensitivity analysis will be conducted for BDE and FA parameters to evaluate the stability of the proposed framework under different optimizer configurations. Second, nested cross-validation and repeated independent optimization runs will be adopted to reduce optimizer-selection bias and provide more statistically robust estimates of generalization performance. Third, adaptive or self-tuning metaheuristic algorithms will be explored so that optimizer parameters can be dynamically adjusted during the search process instead of being fixed manually. Finally, future versions of the model will integrate real-time seismic waveform data, station-level information, and explainable artificial intelligence techniques to improve operational applicability, interpretability, and reliability in practical seismic monitoring and earthquake early warning environments. It also extends the proposed framework beyond catalog-metadata-based magnitude regression by incorporating broader earthquake-related data-driven applications. First, waveform-level and seismological signal features will be integrated to support more physically meaningful earthquake detection and magnitude-estimation tasks, inspired by recent automated earthquake detection frameworks based on advanced seismological signal analysis50. Second, the proposed model can be extended toward earthquake-engineering applications by linking seismic-event characteristics with structural response and optimization tasks, such as AI-driven seismic optimization of high-rise building systems in earthquake-prone regions51. Third, future studies may consider post-earthquake impact assessment by integrating social, psychological, and disaster-response indicators, particularly for evaluating the wider consequences of major seismic events52. These directions would allow the framework to evolve from catalogue-based magnitude regression into a more comprehensive earthquake intelligence system that supports seismic-event analysis, structural resilience evaluation, and post-disaster impact assessment.
