Abstract
Phototaxis, a visual behaviour of insects toward light, is used to monitor and control insect pests
worldwide. However, certain pest control practices have raised concerns because of the unintended
elimination of non-target insects. Additionally, artificial lighting has expanded owing to urbanization. We
investigated the phototactic behaviour of the dominant predatory beetle, Propylaea japonica. We explored
its visual responses to seven monochromatic lights at 365–655 nm wavelength and various intensities at
the preferred wavelength. The highest response occurred at 365 nm (ultraviolet), followed by 465 and 525
nm. When only the light intensity at 365 nm was varied (10, 100, and 300 lux), the response to 365 nm at
10 lux decreased, and the strongest response shifted to 420 nm (violet), but the preference increased with
elevated light intensity at 365 nm. Examining behavioural responses to light intensities at 1–5000 lux
(365 nm), a preference for 1000–5000 lux was observed, although mixed populations (males and
females) exhibited increased responses at 10–100 lux. Our results indicate a preference for shorter
wavelengths (365 nm) across different light environments (wavelength) and underscore the role of light
intensity in influencing wavelength selection in P. japonica. This study provides insights for the
development of light sources for insect monitoring and pest control.

Introduction
Phototropism, an inherent behavioural response exhibited by insects in reaction to light (Menzel and
Greggars 1985; Uehara et al. 2019), can be categorized into two forms: positive phototropism, which
involves moving toward a light source, and negative phototropism, which involves moving away from a
light source (Park and Lee 2017; Kim et al. 2019; Fakhari et al. 2020). The phototactic behaviour of
insects is mainly affected by wavelength, light intensity, and the physiological state of the insect, such as
age, sex, feeding, and mating state (Yang et al. 2003; Carayon et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2021). This
behaviour plays a role in various aspects of insect life, including food search, mate selection, habitat
selection, and migration (Reeves 2011; Shimoda and Honda 2013; Liu et al. 2021). Insects exhibit
variations in compound eye structure and the characteristics of photosensitive pigments in the retina,
which result in distinct attractions to certain specific wavelengths and light intensities (Shimizu and Kato
1978; Briscoe and Chittka 2001; Wen et al. 2018).
Recently, there has been a notable advancement in the development and widespread use of light traps for
pest monitoring and control, leveraging the phototropism exhibited by insects. These traps are favoured
for their lower-carbon and plausible environment-friendly features (Kamminga et al. 2009; Böckmann and
Meyhöfer 2017). However, the presence of extensive artificial nighttime lighting has resulted in significant
ecological repercussions, affecting insect orientation, navigation, foraging, and reproduction (Owens and
Lewis 2018; McMahon et al. 2022). If emitted wavelengths align with those to which insects are visually
sensitive, certain species may gain or lose the ability to discriminate colours, hindering the reception of
visual signals (Johnsen et al. 2006). Moreover, the level of artificial light visibility at night may increase
predatory insect foraging activities or prolong daytime and nocturnal insect foraging activity (Kelber et al.
2006; Miller et al. 2017). Given that natural enemies play an important role as biological control agents
Page 2/16

against agricultural pests in China, they constitute a key component of integrated pest management.
Nonetheless, the presence of insect-trap lamps or artificial nighttime lighting has led to the accidental
elimination of some natural enemy insects and neutral insects, along with the disturbance of their
biological habits, which destroys the natural ecological balance and biodiversity (Owens and Lewis 2018;
Pan et al. 2020; Jaegerbrand and Spoelstra 2023). Light traps, to a certain extent, also respond to the
potential effects of artificial light on positively phototropic insects. Therefore, it is important to actively
explore their specificity for specific wavelengths and light intensities.
Coleoptera, also known as beetles, is the largest and most diverse insect group, with more than 300,000
species distributed worldwide, many of which are the natural enemies of pests (Vandenberg 2002; Zhang
et al. 2018). The highest numbers of natural enemy Coleoptera insects are caught by light traps during
their active season (Upadhyay et al. 1999; Ma and Ma 2013; Sharma et al. 2015). Propylaea japonica
(Thunberg, 1781) (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae) is distributed in various regions, including China, Japan,
India, and Russia. It is an important indigenous natural enemy in China used particularly for greenhouse
vegetables and field crops, where it targets pests like aphids, psyllids, mealybugs, and white flies (Zhang
et al. 2004; Zhang et al. 2014). Moreover, the predatory aspects of this species have been extensively
studied (Gao et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2022; Xiang et al. 2022), but not its phototropic
behaviour. Previous studies on the phototropism of P. japonica have demonstrated that this species has
the highest selectivity for ultraviolet light (Huang et al. 2019); however, the possible effect of light
intensity on wavelength selection and the range of light intensity preferred for wavelengths are yet to be
fully elucidated.
Herein, we investigated the sensitivity of P. japonica to different light environments and intensities and
assessed the phototropic selectivity of females, males, and mixed populations of females and males to
seven different LED light wavelengths: 655 nm (red), 605 nm (orange), 585 nm (yellow), 525 nm (green),
456 nm (blue), 420 nm (ultraviolet), and 365 nm (ultraviolet). In addition, we analysed the possible effect
of the variations in 365 nm (ultraviolet) light intensity on the wavelength selection of P. japonica and
compared the selection rate of P. japonica for the different light intensities of 365 nm (ultraviolet) to
determine their possible preference for higher light intensity. The aim of this study is to clarify the role of
wavelength and light intensity in the phototactic characteristics of P. japonica, and to provide a basis for
the integrated application of biological control and trap lights and the potential impacts of public lighting
systems.

Materials and Methods

Insects
Propylaea japonica adults were purchased from Beijing Kuoye Tianyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing,
China. They were placed in an artificial climate box (Ningbo Jiangnan Instrument Factory, Model: RXZ280B) set at 26 ± 1°C, with a relative humidity (RH) of 65% ± 5% under a 15 h:9 h light/dark cycle
(scotophase lasting from 20:00 to 5:00).
Page 3/16

Behavioural experiments against light wavelength
Experiment 1
Experiments were conducted using an eight-compartment response device (Fig. 1, S2) (releasing and
activity room: 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm; choice room: 30 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm) constructed from opaque
cardboard with rough white papers as a base and overlaid with plastic wrap for easy viewing. The choice
room was illuminated from the top (> 50 cm) using a set of LED lamps of different colours (Beijing Zhuoli
Hanguang Co., Ltd., MLED4-3). Experiments were performed under seven different light wavelengths: 655
nm (red), 605 nm (orange), 585 nm (yellow), 525 nm (green), 456 nm (blue), 420 nm (purple), and 365 nm
(ultraviolet).
This experiment was conducted in a dark room from 18:00 to 21:00 at 26 ± 1 ℃ and 55% ± 5% RH. Three
treatments were applied: females, males, and a 1:1 mixed population of females and males. Darkness
served as the control. Before the test, the light intensity at the bottom of each chamber was measured
and adjusted to 100 lux using an illuminometer (TES-139, TES Electrical Electronic Co., Taipei, Taiwan).
Each treatment involved 60 healthy test insects and was repeated 10 times, with no reuse of test insects.
Before the experiment, the test insects were allowed to dark-adapt for 30 min. Following continuous
irradiation for 60 min, the number of test insects in each room was recorded.

Experiment 2
The 365 nm (ultraviolet) light intensity was set to 10 lux, 100 lux, and 300 lux. Other wavelengths were
maintained at 100 lux. Darkness served as the control. The methodology employed was the same as that
in Experiment 1.

Behaviour experiment against light intensity
Experiments were conducted using a one-way behaviour response device (Fig. 2), including the light
source, observation room (3 m × 10 cm × 10 cm), and a releasing hole. The response chamber was
illuminated from the top. Experiments were performed under four different 365 nm (ultraviolet) (selection
of preferred wavelengths based on Experiment 1) light-intensity ranges: 1–10 lux, 10–100 lux, 100–1000
lux, and 1000–5000 lux. The box floor was covered with rough white papers and overlaid with plastic
wrap for easy viewing.
This experiment was conducted in a dark room from 18:00 to 21:00 at 26 ± 1 ℃ and 55% ± 5% RH. Three
treatments were applied: single females, single males, and a mixed population (female:male = 1:1). Each
treatment involved 60 healthy test insects and was repeated 10 times, with no reuse of test insects.
Before the experiment, the test insects were allowed to dark-adapt for 30 min. Following continuous
irradiation for 60 min, the number of test insects in each room was recorded.

Statistical analysis
Page 4/16

Phototactic responses were calculated as follows: (number of insects in the phototactic selective
chamber/total number of test insects – active chamber worms of test insects) × 100%; the theoretical
average phototactic response was calculated as follows: 1/phototactic selective chambers (8) × 100%.
One-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare the phototactic responses
to different light intensities or wavelengths. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v.26.0
(SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and determined to be statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Responses along different wavelengths
In the experiments testing the phototactic behaviour of P. japonica adults, findings revealed a preference
for 365 nm, followed by 456 nm and 525 nm, with weaker responses observed for 605 nm, 655 nm, and
no-light conditions under the mixed population (Fmix = 13.856, dfmix = 7, Pmix ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 3a). Propylaea

japonica exhibited somewhat similar light preferences under the all-female and all-male populations, with
a strong phototactic preference for 365 nm and 525 nm; fewer individuals preferred the other light
wavelengths (655 nm, 605 nm, 585 nm, 420 nm, and no-light condition; Ffemale = 12.261, dffemale = 7,
Pfemale ˂ 0.01; Fmale = 10.883, dfmale = 7, Pmale ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 3b, c). These results indicate that wavelength
exerts a certain effect on the phototactic behaviour of P. japonica. This species had a preference for
wavelengths of 365 nm, 456 nm, and 525 nm over others, suggesting the presence of short-wave
photoreceptors.
The phototactic behaviour of P. japonica adults in response to different 365 nm light intensities was
investigated using eight-compartment behaviour response experiments, encompassing a spectrum of
light wavelengths and a no-light condition (Fig. 2). In the presence of 10 lux 365 nm light, P. japonica
exhibited obvious light preferences, with a notable phototactic preference for 420 nm, and a lesser
preference for other light wavelengths (655 nm, 605 nm, 585 nm, 525 nm, 456 nm, 365 nm, and no-light
condition; F10lux, 365nm = 79.345, df10lux, 365nm = 7, P10lux, 365nm ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 4a). Under 100 lux 365 nm
light, 365 nm and 456 nm were preferred, whereas other light wavelengths were less preferred (F100lux,
365nm = 13.856, df 100lux, 365nm = 7, P100lux, 365nm ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 3a). Propylaea japonica preferred 365 nm,

whereas the attraction to 525 nm did not differ significantly from that of 456 nm and 585 nm. In contrast,
605 nm, 655 nm, and the no-light condition were significantly less attractive under 300 lux 365 nm
(F300lux, 365nm = 18.683, df300lux, 365nm = 7, P300lux, 365nm ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 4b). These results underscore the
role of wavelength in determining phototactic behaviour, with light intensity as one of the key factors
affecting this wavelength-related behaviour (Table 1).

Response to different levels of light intensity at 365 nm
The phototactic behaviour of P. japonica adults in response to 365 nm wavelength was investigated
using rectangle behaviour response chamber experiments, encompassing four light-intensity ranges (1–
10 lux, 10–100 lux, 100–1000 lux, and 1000–5000 lux) (Fig. 3). Propylaea japonica preferred light
intensity in the range of 1000–5000 lux over 10–100 lux and 1–10 lux; the range of 100–1000 lux proved
to be the least preferred under the mixed population (Fmix = 64.708, dfmix = 3, Pmix ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 5a). The
all-female and all-male populations preferred a high light-intensity range of 1000–5000 lux, with a lesser
preference for other light-intensity ranges (Ffemale = 148.446, dffemale = 3, Pfemale ˂ 0.01; Fmale = 285.141,

dfmale = 3, Pmale = ˂ 0.01) (Fig. 5b, c). These results indicate that sex influences light-intensity selection in
P. japonica.

Discussion
Propylaea japonica is a predatory natural enemy that preys on aphids and other pests in the field, but the
use of artificial light at night and light traps lead to luring and accidental killing. Thus, we aimed to
investigate the phototactic characteristics of P. japonica to different wavelengths and the effect of light
intensity on wavelength selection. The current results showed that P. japonica exhibits a preference for
shorter wavelengths of ultraviolet light (365 nm) in different light environments. Moreover, we observed
that light intensity played an important role in influencing light environment (wavelength) selection in P.
japonica, which mainly depended on the visibility of light intensity. This study provides a scientific basis
for developing new light sources for insect monitoring or pest control and for urban and rural public
lighting systems. These sources should be green, efficient, highly targeted, and bio-ecologically safe
under certain specific light scenarios.
Our results showed that P. japonica exhibited the strongest preference for 365 nm (ultraviolet), followed
by 456 nm (blue) and 525 nm (green) when they were exposed to LED lamps emitting wavelengths of
Page 6/16

655 nm (red), 605 nm (orange), 585 nm (yellow), 525 nm (green), 456 nm (blue), 420 nm (purple), and
365 nm (ultraviolet) simultaneously (Fig. 3). This result is nearly consistent with our previous results,
where the highest phototactic response was observed at 340 nm (ultraviolet), followed by 524 nm (green),
and then 400–440 nm and 483 nm (blue) when evaluating phototactic and photophobic behaviours in
response to 14 monochromatic lights at 340–605 nm (Chen et al. 2009). Considerable differences were
observed in the compound eye structure and adaptation to light sources between male and female adults,
a phenomenon widely observed in insects (Meyer 1978; Yathom et al. 1980; Lau et al. 2007; Altermatt et
al. 2009; Crook et al. 2009; Fischer et al. 2011; Sun et al. 2014). No significant differences were observed
in the wavelength-sensitive peaks between both sexes (Fig. 3). Both male and female individuals
preferred 365 nm, 456 nm, and 525 nm wavelengths, although differences were observed in their high
and low phototactic responses. This result is similar to that of the spectral-sensitive peaks of Coleoptera
species, such as Anomala corpulenta, Holotrichia titanis, and Holotrichia plunbea, where sex did not
affect the results (Johnsen et al. 2006). These results suggest that wavelength plays a key role in the
phototropic behaviour of P. japonica, and it is plausible that this species has three types of
photoreceptors—ultraviolet, blue, and green—with certain spectral sensitivities.
In addition to wavelength, light intensity is an important factor that affects insect phototactic behaviour.
However, previous studies primarily focused on the phototactic or photophobic behaviours of insects
under light sources with a single wavelength and different light intensities (Chen et al. 2012; Wang et al.
2021). In this study, only the light intensity of 365 nm (ultraviolet) was changed, which was the most
preferred light intensity for P. japonica, whereas the light intensity of other wavelengths remained
unchanged. This allowed us to observe the associated behavioural tendencies. At 10 lux of 365 nm, the
preference of P. japonica for 365 nm (ultraviolet) decreased, whereas that for 420 nm (purple) increased.
At 300 lux light intensity, 365 nm (ultraviolet) elicited a much higher phototactic response (Fig. 4),
indicating that P. japonica was more easily attracted to wavelengths within their visual sensitivity at
sufficient intensity. For example, the phototactic response of Exolontha castanea Chang and Lycoriella
ingenua increased with elevated light intensity (Kecskeméti et al. 2021; Shang et al. 2022). Additionally,
Dastarcus helophoroides was most attracted at an optical wavelength of 700 nm and an intensity of 7
lux, showing a downward trend with variations in light intensity (Wang et al. 2021). Insects are more
adept at foraging in suitable light environments (Miller et al. 2017), and insufficient wavelength-sensitive
brightness can lead to behavioural consequences if the insects are compelled to select certain light
sources (Meyer-Rochow et al. 2002; Shimoda and Honda 2013). This suggests that the visual perception
of P. japonica is limited by light intensity. The phototactic behaviour of P. japonica adults in response to
365 nm wavelength was assessed under four light-intensity ranges. P. japonica preferred high lightintensity ranges, specifically 1000–5000 lux. Different sexes had a certain influence on the behavioural
response of P. japonica to light intensity, with a preference for high light intensity. However, the selection
rate of mixed populations of males and females was significantly increased in the light-intensity range of
10–100 lux (Fig. 5). The male and female adults engaged in mating behaviour within this light-intensity
range. These results indicate that ultraviolet light at certain intensities facilitates mating in P. japonica.
Future studies could further corroborate this observation.
Page 7/16

In conclusion, we suggest that P. japonica is wavelength-directed in different light environments and that
changes in light intensity can trigger corresponding phototropic behavioural responses. This is the first
significant evidence that wavelength selection in P. japonica is regulated by light intensity. This will be
helpful in devising trap lamps with wavelengths that are not those to which P. japonica is sensitive under
specific habitat conditions, or scientifically rationalizing the use of trap lamps according to the peak
hours of P. japonica activities, so as to better monitor and control pests and achieve the purpose of
protecting natural enemies. Additionally, it provides a theoretical basis for the conservation of the
ecological security of insect species and overall biodiversity. This experiment was conducted indoors and
has some limitations. Future studies require further field validation or exploration of whether light
intensity at other wavelengths affects their phototropic properties.
