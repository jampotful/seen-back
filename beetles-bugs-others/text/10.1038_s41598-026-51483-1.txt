Comparative hyperparameter optimization of object detection models for precision monitoring of cucumber beetles and similar insects on yellow sticky cards.
Mafuwe K, Dulam RVS, Kambhamettu C, Crossley MS. (2026). Scientific reports. . doi:10.1038/s41598-026-51483-1. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Comparative hyperparameter optimization of object detection models for precision monitoring of cucumber beetles and similar insects on yellow sticky cards

## Abstract
AbstractComputer vision presents a great opportunity for improving pest monitoring in agriculture, particularly for yellow sticky traps, a critical component in IPM. However, despite the growing interest in applying object detection models for insect identification, insect datasets present unique challenges, and approaches for fine-tuning model parameters to achieve reliable performance remain limited. This study explores the influence of fine tuning three key hyperparameters (learning rate, optimizer type and batch size) on the performance of two popular object detection models (YOLO and RT-DETR), in detecting pests on yellow sticky traps, with a particular emphasis on identifying cucumber beetles. Results showed that higher learning rates reduced performance across precision, recall, and mAP50 for both models. In contrast, SGD improved outcomes, particularly for RT-DETR, while YOLO proved more robust to high learning rates. Our study also showed that both models achieved comparable accuracy levels, once optimal settings were determined for each model. These findings highlight the importance of hyperparameter tuning for reliable pest detection systems and support the development of scalable AI workflows for precision agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51483-1.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Computer vision, Precision agriculture, Pest detection, Object detection, Hyperparameter tuningSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Mathematics and computing, Zoology

## Introduction
Pest monitoring is a crucial component of sustainable agriculture, which contributes to maintaining crop health, productivity, and environmental sustainability1. Early detection of pests enables farmers to apply timely interventions and control measures that help reduce the use of chemical pesticides and maintain ecological balance, while effectively controlling pests2. For example, codling moths have one of the most well-established and successful Integrated Pest Management (IPM) programs. Das et al.3 noted that through sustained IPM practices in the Pacific Northwest, codling moth populations have been maintained at very low levels; thereby minimizing the number of insecticide applications that are needed to manage them.
Traditional methods of pest monitoring are labour-intensive and often require manual methods of data collection, processing of traps, assays, and other monitoring processes4,5. Additionally, misidentifications can also occur, especially if the insects are small or closely resemble other species in colour or morphology. Similarly, there is also the risk of miscounting, when the individual processing the traps and samples is fatigued or lacks experience6. When monitoring large agricultural areas, these challenges can limit efficient and timely pest monitoring, reducing the ability of farmers to respond quickly to emerging pest threats7. The need for efficient, accurate, and scalable monitoring solutions has become increasingly urgent in the face of global food security challenges and the push for sustainable farming practices.
Advancements in computer vision and artificial intelligence (AI) have opened new avenues for automating pest monitoring processes to help alleviate the aforementioned challenges associated with traditional pest monitoring methods8. Among several AI approaches, deep learning has emerged as one of the most promising techniques for image-based pest detection9,10, due to its ability to learn complex visual features and identify subtle patterns of objects directly from unprocessed images11,12. Two popular deep learning architectures are YOLO (You Only Look Once) and RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer). YOLO’s speed and accuracy make it suitable for rapid pest identification, while RT-DETR’s transformer-based architecture is known to excel in complex scenes, e.g., crowded and occluded insects on yellow sticky cards. Studies have demonstrated the potential of these models for pest detection. For instance, Wen et al.13 developed a custom YOLOv4 called Pest-YOLO, for the purpose of automatically identifying and counting small numbers of pests across 24 categories in multispectral trap images. Pest-YOLO achieved higher levels of accuracy and recall than many other models reported in literature. Similarly, Zhang et al.14 enhanced the RT-DETR capabilities to develop Pest-RTDetr for detecting and classifying multiple agricultural insect species, addressing the challenge of classifying and detecting densely arranged insects in field images. These developments show the applicability of deep learning to pest detection while underscoring the need to adapt and rigorously evaluate models under real-world agricultural conditions.
Studies have shown that hyperparameters play a crucial role in determining how a model learns and performs, affecting both efficiency and accuracy of model training, as well as how quickly the model converges (i.e., how well the model performs when given unseen data)15–17. Given the influence of hyperparameters on training accuracy and speed, they must be carefully configured before training begins18. Apart from the architectural hyperparameters of the convolutional and fully connected layers, training-related hyperparameters such as batch size, learning rate, and optimizer may be modified to enhance the performance of the deep learning models16. Batch size indicates the number of training samples processed before the model updates its weights, defined as numerical values that determine the strength of connections between neurons in the network. Batch size can influence learning stability and generalization19,20. The learning rate is a key hyperparameter that controls how much the model’s weights are adjusted at each learning iteration and how far the model moves toward an optimal solution during each learning iteration. If this number is set too high, the model may overshoot optimal solutions, while a low rate may slow convergence or cause the model to become stagnant19. The optimizer plays a crucial role in guiding the direction and magnitude of these weight updates, enabling the model to minimize error more efficiently during training20. Additionally, hyperparameters can interact in complex ways, with the optimal value of one parameter depending on the settings of others, as confirmed in a study by Liao et al.17. The study notes that interactions exist among multiple hyperparameters (particularly those within the same dimension) and that these influence each other to varying degrees across different deep neural network models and performance metrics. For example, a study by Li et al.19 found that the learning rate is highly dependent on the batch size used. Moreover, a study by Taylor et al.21 showed that the effectiveness of specific hyperparameters depends on model architecture and dataset complexity. These points highlight the importance of adopting a comprehensive tuning strategy that takes into consideration the individual and combined effects of hyperparameters, as well as the complexity of the dataset and the influence of model architecture.
While deep learning models have been widely adopted for many tasks, their application in agricultural pest monitoring remains relatively underexplored. This is particularly true for insect datasets, which are often complex and may present many challenges, such as limited sample sizes, imbalanced classes, and variations in object size and appearance, which can significantly impact model performance. Insect datasets also contain very small targets, cluttered backgrounds, and visually similar species, which can cause hyperparameters to behave differently than in standard object-detection benchmarks. This is because small objects contain fewer pixels, which results in diminished spatial information and low contrast compared to the surrounding background. Additionally, the noisy backgrounds make models more sensitive to choices like learning rate, batch size, and optimizer, compared to typical datasets with large, well-separated objects22,] 23,] 24. These challenges highlight the need for more targeted research to create datasets tailored for pest detection models and to understand how the performance of these models can be optimized through hyperparameter tuning. Despite their importance, hyperparameters such as learning rate, optimizer type, and batch size remain insufficiently studied in the context of agricultural pest monitoring. To address this gap, this study systematically evaluates and compares hyperparameter configurations for two state-of-the-art object detection models, namely YOLO and RT-DETR, selected for their proven efficacy in real-time object detection tasks. We focused on cucumber beetles (Acalymma vittatum Fabricius and Diabrotica undecimpunctata Howden) which are significant pests in various cucurbit crops as well as visually similar insects, particularly lady beetles (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae) and pigweed flea beetles (Disonycha glabrata Fabricius). By varying key hyperparameters, namely, learning rates (0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1), optimizer types (Adam, AdamW, SGD), and batch sizes (4, 8, 16, 32), we assessed their effects on accuracy, precision, recall, and mean average precision at 50% (mAP50). The dataset, collected from experimental plots at the University of Delaware, serves as a representative case study. This work aims to provide practical insights into model optimization for pest detection and to contribute to advancing AI-driven, site-specific monitoring strategies in precision agriculture. More specifically, this study contributes to the research gap by (i) evaluating how learning rate, batch size, and optimizer settings influence YOLO and RT-DETR performance on small-object insect data, and (ii) comparing optimized versions of a lightweight CNN model (YOLOv11n) and a larger transformer-based model (RT-DETR-L) to determine how these architectures behave under the same hyperparameter tuning framework.

## Materials and methods
This study presents a methodological approach to optimizing AI-based pest monitoring using yellow sticky cards as the detection medium. Drawing on data collected from experimental plots at the University of Delaware (Newark, DE, USA), the study evaluates the performance of object detection models. It explores their potential for broader, transferable applications in agricultural monitoring. The sections below describe the study site, data collection procedures, and model training process. An overview of the methodological workflow is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

## Object detection model selection
YOLO (You Only Look Once) and RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer) were selected for this study. YOLO version 11 was chosen for its known accuracy and speed25, as well as its potential for real-time object detection, particularly in situations where early detection of pests is crucial. The nano variant (i.e., YOLO11n) was selected due to its lightweight architecture, which ensures efficient inference with lower computational costs, making it suitable for real-time applications in the field. YOLOv11n is a lightweight object detection variant enhanced from earlier YOLO versions26 and has been used in several precision agriculture studies, such as the detection of guava defects across diverse conditions27. On the other hand, RT-DETR was chosen for its performance in handling small objects, occlusions, and cluttered backgrounds11, such as insects on yellow sticky cards. Specifically, RT-DETR-L (Real-time Detection Transformer Large) was chosen because it can be implemented using the Ultralytics PyTorch framework (Ultralytics LLC, Frederick, MD, USA), which allows for seamless switching between YOLO and RT-DETR models without modifying dataset formats or annotations. This ensured consistent data handling and minimized preprocessing efforts, enabling a direct comparison between the models under identical conditions.
Although YOLOv11n (2.6 M parameters) and RT-DETR-L (32 M parameters) differ in size, they were chosen to represent a lightweight YOLO model versus a much larger RT-DETR model, to determine whether the two approaches differ in their performance for real-time pest detection despite the difference in computational complexity.

## Target pests and other insect species
Watermelon [Citrullus lanatus (Thunb.) Matsum. et Nakai] was selected for this study as part of a broader integrated pest management research initiative to improve pest monitoring techniques and mitigate economic losses for watermelon growers in the USA. The primary target pests for this study were spotted cucumber beetles (Diabrotica undecimpunctata) and striped cucumber beetles (Acalymma vittatum Fabricius), which are known for causing significant damage to watermelon and other cucurbit crops throughout their life cycle. Additionally, lady beetles (Coleoptera: Coccinellidae) and pigweed flea beetles (Disonycha glabrata) were also selected for this study based on their potential morphological similarities to the spotted cucumber beetle and striped cucumber beetle, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S2). Their inclusion helps reduce false positives by improving the model’s ability to distinguish target pests from similar non-target species.

## Image acquisition
Two watermelon plots (3 × 10 m) were established at the University of Delaware (UD) “Fresh to You” farm, a student-operated farm located at the College of Agriculture and Natural Resources in Newark, Delaware. Ten yellow sticky cards were set up in and around each plot to trap insects. The sticky cards were collected once a week for 12 weeks during the summer months (May to August) of 2023 and 2024. An additional 20 yellow sticky cards were also randomly placed in an additional watermelon plot established by the farmer at the same farm, during the summer of 2024 to increase sample data and ensure variability in insect collection across different environmental conditions.
The sticky card images were captured using a Nikon COOLPIX B500 (4608 × 3456 px), an iPhone 12 (4032 × 3024 px), and a Samsung SM-A115F (4160 × 3120 px) to introduce image quality and resolution variability. Additionally, images were captured at a range of zoom levels (wide, medium, and close-up) and angles (frontal and oblique) and aggregated to enhance dataset diversity and improve model generalizability. Imaging was under consistent laboratory lighting conditions using regular daylight.

## Image annotation and dataset preparation
The LabelMe tool was used for manual annotation (labeling) of images of yellow sticky cards, by defining bounding boxes around the target classes (i.e., spotted cucumber beetle, striped cucumber beetle, pigweed flea beetle, and lady beetle). Each object was individually annotated and saved in JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) format following the YOLO structure (i.e., < class_id > < center_x > < center_y > < width > < height> ), where class_id corresponds to the assigned numerical label for each insect category, and center_x, center_y, width, and height are normalized coordinates relative to the image dimensions. A total of 619 images were annotated and converted from JSON to YOLO .txt format using a LabelMe-to-YOLO script28, producing label files paired with their corresponding images. The resulting dataset comprised 542 instances of striped cucumber beetles, 99 instances of spotted cucumber beetles, 262 instances of pigweed flea beetles, and 377 instances of lady beetles. This class distribution reflects natural field conditions, where some species are more abundant on sticky cards than others.

## Data preprocessing and augmentation
To improve class balance, oversampling was applied by duplicating and augmenting image-label pairs from underrepresented classes. Augmentation techniques used included hue and brightness adjustments, horizontal flipping and mosaic augmentation. These were applied to the data to enhance data variability and promote generalization. Bounding boxes were manually validated using a custom script that overlaid annotations on the corresponding image to ensure accurate alignment with target objects. A patch-based object detection approach was then used, in which images were divided into 512 × 512 patches using a sliding window with 50-pixel overlap to ensure complete object coverage and to minimize the risk of truncating insects at patch boundaries. Similar to other studies5,29–31, the use of 512 × 512 pixel patches allows for focused analysis of only the regions containing relevant information as well as the small insect targets. This resolution has also proven effective for CNN-based object detection in small to medium-sized datasets32,33. The above-mentioned steps were implemented using custom Python scripts.
After augmentation and patching, non-insect tiles were removed, resulting in 1,775 insect-containing patches (Table 1). The original images had been split into training and testing sets prior to data processing, with the test set fully held out before model training and cross-validation to ensure unbiased evaluation on unseen data. The resulting dataset after data processing consisted of training (n = 1,413) and testing (n = 362) insect-containing patches. To help the model learn to distinguish pests from non-target background areas, 144 background-only patches (∼10% of the insect-containing training set) were added exclusively to the training set, increasing it to 1,557 patches. The test set contained only insect instances and was used for final model evaluation.
Number of images and annotated beetle instances before and after augmentation and patch generation.
YOLO and RT-DETR models were trained using Python (v3.10, Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) along with NumPy and OpenCV Python bindings. For training, a five-fold nested cross-validation approach was used to generate training and validation subsets from the training dataset, for hyperparameter tuning and performance evaluation, with 80% of the training dataset used for model fitting and 20% for validation in each fold. The cross-validation folds were generated using random splits without explicit stratification. As a result, class distribution in the validation sets may have varied slightly. While oversampling was used to improve class balance in the training data, such imbalances are common in real-world pest datasets, where some species naturally occur more frequently than others. Therefore, the current setup still provides a realistic and practical evaluation of model performance. Cross-validation (k = 5) was implemented using the scikit-learn library (v1.5.1, scikit-learn developers, https://scikit-learn.org/).

## Grid search for hyperparameter optimization
A grid search was employed to identify the optimal combination of hyperparameters for model training. This method was selected for its systematic and exhaustive evaluation of all possible parameter combinations within a defined search space, providing a reliable baseline for tuning key training parameters34. Although more computationally demanding than random search or Bayesian optimization, grid search is particularly effective when the number of parameters is small and the parameter space is well-constrained, as was the case in this study.
The initial grid search tested combinations of learning rate values (0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1), batch sizes (4, 8, 16, 32), and optimizers (SGD, Adam, AdamW). These values were chosen based on established practices in training deep learning models for object detection35,36. The learning rate range included both conservative (0.001) and aggressive (0.1) values, capturing typical bounds used in training convolutional and transformer-based detectors37. Batch sizes from 4 to 32 were selected to balance computational efficiency with gradient stability, particularly considering GPU memory constraints38. Optimizer selection included both momentum-based (SGD) and adaptive gradient-based methods (Adam, AdamW), as performance often varies depending on the model architecture and dataset characteristics39.
In this study, early experiments showed that higher learning rates (≥ 0.05) led to poor performance for RT-DETR (see Data Availability). This observation prompted an extension of the grid to include lower learning rates (0.0001 and 0.00001), enabling more stable and controlled weight updates. The decision was supported by previous research showing that transformer-based models such as DETR and its variants typically require lower learning rates for stable convergence and optimal performance26,40,41. RT-DETR, a real-time variant of DETR, inherits this sensitivity due to its attention-based architecture. The refined grid ensured that RT-DETR was evaluated under conditions better suited to its training dynamics, allowing a fair comparison with YOLO under each model’s optimal hyperparameter configuration.

## Statistical analysis of hyperparameter effects
To investigate how different hyperparameter settings influenced model performance, a statistical analysis was conducted using evaluation metrics, precision, recall and mean Average Precision at 0.5 Intersection over Union (mAP50), averaged over the last five epochs from all training configurations to account for fluctuations and metric variance42, across both YOLO and RT-DETR models. This analysis aimed to address the following research questions: (i) How do learning rate, batch size, and optimizer influence model accuracy? (ii) Are hyperparameter effects consistent across YOLO and RT-DETR? (iii) Do hyperparameters interact differently across models?
Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) were used to evaluate the influence of hyperparameters on model performance. For pooled analysis across both models, the following structure was used:
performance_metric ~ model × batch_size × learning_rate × optimizer.
This allowed for testing both main effects and interaction terms to determine whether hyperparameter influence varied by model type. To complement this, additional GLMs were fit separately for YOLO and RT-DETR:
performance_metric ~ batch_size × learning_rate × optimizer.
This per-model analysis enabled more focused interpretation of hyperparameter effects within each model. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots to ensure the assumptions required for linear modeling were met. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3), and visualizations were generated using the sjPlot and ggplot2 packages.

## Model evaluation under optimized settings
Each model was tuned using the optimal hyperparameter configuration identified through grid search and selecting the combination of hyperparameters that gave the highest performance (mAP50) for each model34,43. Architectural parameters such as activation functions, number of filters, and layer configurations were retained as defined in the default model architecture as implemented in the Ultralytics framework and were not modified in this study. Standard augmentation settings of mosaic = 1.0 and horizontal flip = 0.5 were used to increase dataset diversity and improve generalization. All other hyperparameters were set to their default values. Ten random seeds were applied during training of the best-performing models to ensure reproducibility and reduce variability arising from stochastic processes in model initialization and training44,45. Both YOLOv11n and RT-DETR-L were trained on the same dataset with a maximum of 100 epochs. However, YOLOv11n used early stopping with a patience of 50 epochs, whereas RT-DETR-L was allowed to run the full training schedule to accommodate the slower convergence typical of large transformer-based models so as to give each model a fair chance to reach its peak performance prior to GLM comparison. Each model was subsequently evaluated on the same held-out test set comprising previously unseen images to assess performance under real-world conditions. Training was conducted on an Alienware workstation running Windows 11, using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 GPU (12 GB VRAM), an Intel Core processor, and 32 GB RAM.
To quantify model accuracy, predicted pest counts were compared with ground truth annotations using three regression-based evaluation metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Coefficient of Determination (R²). This evaluation approach assessed not only whether pests were detected but also how closely the predicted pest abundances matched ground truth counts across a range of test images. Predictions not matching any ground-truth insect annotation were counted as false positives, including those occurring in background regions within insect-containing test patches.

## Results

## Influence of hyperparameters on model performance
We found that higher learning rates significantly reduced performance across precision, recall, and mAP50 metrics for both models.
In the RT-DETR model, an increase in learning rate resulted in a statistically significant decrease in recall, mAP50, and precision (Fig. 1), with effect estimates of -5.144 for precision, -4.300 for mAP50, and − 5.415 for recall (Supplementary Table S1). Optimizer choice also had a statistically significant effect on recall, mAP50, and precision (Supplementary Table S1). Specifically, SGD resulted in higher performance across all metrics compared to Adam and AdamW (Fig. 2), with positive coefficients of 0.268 for precision, 0.306 for mAP50, and 0.254 for recall (Supplementary Table S1). Batch size had no significant effect on performance, and no interactions among hyperparameters were observed in the RT-DETR model (Supplementary Table S1).
Effect of learning rate on RT-DETR model performance. (a) Recall, (b) mAP50, and (c) precision all significantly declined as learning rate increased, indicating the model’s sensitivity to this hyperparameter (p < 0.01 for all metrics).
Effect of optimizer type on RT-DETR performance. (a) mAP50 (b) Precision and (c) Recall, were all significantly higher when SGD was used compared to Adam and AdamW, indicating its consistent superiority across evaluation metrics (p < 0.01 for all metrics).
In the YOLO model, significant interactions between learning rate and optimizer were observed for precision and recall. For precision, a significant negative interaction was found with SGD (estimate = -2.806, p = 0.02; Supplementary Table S1). Precision decreased with increasing learning rate across all optimizers; however, SGD yielded the highest precision at low learning rates (Fig. 3). In contrast, recall showed a significant positive interaction with SGD (estimate = 2.765, p = 0.01; Supplementary Table S1). Recall decreased with increasing learning rate across all optimizers. However, performance under SGD remained consistently higher than under Adam and AdamW across the learning rates, with a gradual decline in recall as the learning rate increased (Fig. 4). No significant interaction was observed among hyperparameters for mAP50. However, mAP50 decreased with an increase in learning rate (Fig. 5a) and was higher under SGD compared to the other optimizers (Fig. 5b).
Interaction between learning rate and optimizer on YOLO precision. Precision decreased across all optimizers with increasing learning rate. SGD achieved the highest precision at low learning rates but declined steeply at higher rates (p = 0.02).
Interaction between learning rate and optimizer on YOLO recall. Recall decreased with increasing learning rate across all optimizers, but SGD maintained the highest recall throughout (p = 0.01).
Effect of learning rate and optimizer on YOLO mAP50. (a) mAP50 decreased with increasing learning rate. (b) Across all optimizers, SGD consistently achieved higher mAP50 than Adam and AdamW.
The learning rate and optimizer effects were inconsistent across YOLO and RT-DETR. For mAP50, YOLO significantly outperformed RT-DETR, with a positive model effect estimate of 0.213 (p = 0.004; Supplementary Table S2). A significant interaction between learning rate and optimizer was also observed (estimate = − 2.844, p = 0.031; Supplementary Table S2), indicating that the positive effect of SGD on mAP50 was reduced as learning rates increased, an effect that was consistent across both models.
For precision, a significant positive interaction was observed between model and learning rate (estimate = 4.339, p = 0.001; Supplementary Table S3), indicating that although precision declined with increasing learning rate in both models, the decline was steeper in RT-DETR than in YOLO (Fig. 6a). A significant main effect of SGD as the optimizer was also observed, with SGD associated with higher precision than Adam and AdamW (estimate = 0.268, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S3), an effect that was consistent across both models. However, the effect of SGD was especially pronounced in RT-DETR, resulting in a substantially greater improvement in precision compared to Adam and AdamW (Fig. 6b).
Cross-model comparison of precision interactions. (a) Precision declined with increasing learning rate in both models, with a steeper decline observed in RT-DETR than in YOLO. (b) SGD led to higher precision than Adam and AdamW across both models, with the effect especially pronounced in RT-DETR, where it produced a substantially greater performance improvement.
For recall, a significant three-way interaction between model, optimizer, and learning rate was observed (estimate = 4.126, p = 0.007; Supplementary Table S4), indicating that the effect of learning rate and optimizer on recall also depended on model choice (YOLO vs. RT-DETR). Recall remained relatively high in YOLO under SGD even as the learning rate increased, whereas recall dropped sharply in RT-DETR across all optimizers. This indicates that, when it comes to recall as the performance metric, YOLO is more tolerant to the interaction between learning rate and optimizer, while RT-DETR shows a decline in recall with higher learning rates, particularly when SGD is not used as the optimizer (Supplementary Table S4).
Batch size had no significant effect on any of the performance metrics and did not interact with any other factors (Supplementary Table S2-S4).

## Optimized hyperparameter settings and comparative model performance
The best-performing hyperparameter combination for each model was selected based on the highest mAP50 value. For YOLO, the optimal configuration was a batch size of 16, a learning rate of 0.001, and the Adam optimizer (achieving a mean average precision at 50% IoU (mAP50) of 0.994 (Supplementary Fig. S3) at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 (i.e., at least 50% box overlap between predicted and actual bounding boxes) under these optimized settings. For RT-DETR, the best combination was a batch size of 32, a learning rate of 0.01, and the SGD optimizer, resulting in a mAP50 of 0.963 (Supplementary Fig. S4). Confusion matrices showed that YOLO achieved 100% precision for all beetles classes on the held-out test set, with no misclassifications observed for any of the classes. (Supplementary Fig. S5). In contrast, RT-DETR exhibited slightly more misclassifications, particularly for the striped cucumber beetle, which was misclassified as background in 4% of cases. RT-DETR also produced more background-region false positives than YOLO, labeling some non-insect regions as insect classes (Supplementary Fig. S6), possibly due to the heightened sensitivity of transformer-based architectures to small or subtle visual patterns that resemble insect features, allowing it to detect finer details that YOLO may have overlooked.
Comparing the average performance of YOLO and RT-DETR based on Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²) across multiple training seeds revealed that YOLO achieved slightly lower error rates (MAE = 0.0538, RMSE = 0.1232) and higher explanatory power (R² = 0.6847) than RT-DETR (MAE = 0.0540, RMSE = 0.1240, R2 = 0.6762). However, Welch’s t-tests indicated that these differences were not statistically significant across any of the three evaluation metrics (p > 0.05; Table 2). This suggests that both models perform comparably in terms of detection accuracy. Overall, the results indicate that YOLO and RT-DETR are both suitably reliable for detecting cucumber beetles and visually similar insects on sticky cards in agricultural settings.

Although YOLO showed slightly lower error rates and higher explanatory power, no statistically significant differences were found between the two models across any metric (p > 0.05). ## Discussion
Our evaluation of the effects of hyperparameters on the performance of computer vision models revealed that higher learning rates consistently reduced model performance across all key evaluation metrics, i.e., precision, recall, and mAP50, for both YOLO and RT-DETR. This aligns with previous findings that higher learning rates can destabilize training16,19, especially when dealing with small or cluttered objects such as insects on sticky cards13. In particular, Li et al.19 showed that the optimal learning rate initially increases with batch size but eventually declines, suggesting that beyond a certain point further increases in the learning rate may in fact negatively affect model stability and accuracy. In contrast, lower learning rates yielded more stable convergence and better detection performance. However, this may also extend training time due to slower progression toward optimal weights46. RT-DETR was particularly sensitive to this parameter, likely due to the inherent complexity of its transformer-based architecture, which relies on attention mechanisms that can become unstable when gradient updates fluctuate significantly, making the model more sensitive to higher learning rate settings36,47. Specifically, Zhai et al.47 noted that increasing the learning rate easily causes attention entropy collapse and training divergence in transformer-based models, while Liu et al.36 pointed out that starting with a small learning rate helps prevent unstable training by reducing large shifts in output during early training. Across both models, the choice of optimizer also played a significant role, with Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) resulting in improved precision, recall, and mAP50 values, especially in RT-DETR. This is consistent with prior studies showing that optimizer choice can influence generalization in deep learning models, with SGD sometimes outperforming adaptive methods such as Adam under certain training conditions35,51. Similarly, Hakim et al.48 reported a high mAP50 of 0.974 in detecting a diverse group of flying pests using a YOLOv8x model with an auto-optimized configuration that selected SGD with a learning rate of 0.01 and a batch size of 36.
Although both models experienced performance degradation at higher learning rates, YOLO exhibited greater resilience. Specifically, our findings showed that precision declined more sharply in RT-DETR than in YOLO as the learning rate increased, pointing to the transformer model’s heightened sensitivity to this hyperparameter. YOLO’s performance declined more gradually than that of RT-DETR, suggesting that the CNN-based architecture of YOLO is more robust to aggressive parameter updates during training. This observation is supported by past research showing the relative stability of YOLO models under a range of learning conditions, particularly in real-time object detection tasks13,48. This aligns with broader trends in agricultural computer vision studies, where YOLO-based models have consistently shown strong performance when appropriately tuned. For example, Lee et al.49 found that small adjustments in learning rate and batch size measurably improved mAP50, precision, and recall in a plant disease recognition task, highlighting the sensitivity of YOLO models to tuning across diverse agricultural tasks for pest and disease detection. Importantly, the interaction between learning rate and optimizer revealed model-specific dynamics in YOLO when paired with SGD, with higher learning rates leading to reduced precision but relatively sustained recall. This suggests that while SGD at high learning rates may introduce noise in classification decisions, it may also help the model retain broader detection sensitivity50,51. Supporting this, Liao et al.17 emphasized that hyperparameters should not be tuned in isolation, as their effects vary across models and performance properties, and are often influenced by interactions with model optimization techniques.
When trained using their respective optimized hyperparameter configurations, both models achieved comparable detection accuracy. While YOLO performed slightly better, t-tests indicated that these differences were not meaningful. This suggests that, for this type of sticky card pest detection task, a lightweight model may be sufficient for accurate monitoring and could be preferable in real-time or resource-limited settings. Lightweight models are also known to be faster, which is useful for field monitoring and early-warning systems where both speed and accuracy are important52,53. These results show that a smaller model can provide accuracy comparable to a much larger one, while offering practical advantages for deployment on devices with limited computing capacity.
The two models also differ in their architecture. Studies in other complex-scene detection contexts have shown that transformer-based models can be more sensitive to subtle or spatially distributed visual cues, which may help them detect faint or partially occluded insects that CNN-based models might miss54. However, our study shows that both models can be effectively tuned to detect cucumber beetles and similar insects on sticky cards with high and comparable accuracy. These findings are consistent with the broader object detection literature, which highlights that with appropriate tuning, both CNN- and transformer-based models are capable of achieving strong performance17, even under modest data constraints55. Several studies such as Hakim et al.48 and Tetila et al.56 have demonstrated that tailoring model architectures and training strategies to the dataset significantly improves detection accuracy for small objects and pests. However, RT-DETR exhibited more false positives in background regions, labeling them as insect classes in several cases due to the heightened sensitivity of transformer-based models. Moreover, studies comparing DETR variants and YOLO e.g.57,58, have noted that the global attention mechanisms in transformer models often result in higher recall, but may also produce more false positives, especially in visually complex or cluttered scenes. Despite these differences, the comparable outcomes across metrics in these studies reinforce the value of model-specific optimization, rather than reliance on default configurations or general performance assumptions.
On the other hand, false positives can create unnecessary alerts triggered by background noise. This may inflate perceived pest pressure and push growers to take action earlier than they need to59. This can also lead to financial losses, as farmers may end up spending money to control a non-existent pest outbreak. However, in some cases, missing the start of an infestation is far more costly than responding to a few unnecessary alerts. If pests arrive and the system fails to detect them (a false negative), the grower may lose yield or fruit quality. Therefore, models with higher recall can still be useful in early-warning systems where missing the start of an infestation is a much bigger risk than occasional false pest alerts, even if they produce more false positives. This is especially true for crops where aesthetics matter and even small scars or feeding marks can lead to major losses for the farmer60, for example watermelons. To mitigate this, the occasional false detections can be filtered through standard scouting routines. Additional mitigation strategies can include smoothing model outputs over time, using repeated detections before generating an alert, or combining image-based detection with trap-count thresholds to reduce unnecessary warnings. These approaches help maintain a good balance between high recall, which is important for early intervention, and a manageable false-positive rate during field deployment.
Collectively, the findings of this study emphasize the importance of tuning hyperparameters in combination rather than isolation. Although learning rates and optimizer types were individually influential, their effects often depended on one another and varied by model architecture. For example, the benefits of SGD were more evident at specific learning rates and differed across YOLO and RT-DETR. These interactions highlight the limitations of generalized tuning strategies and underscore the need for structured, model-aware approaches in agricultural contexts. While recent studies have shown performance gains from fine-tuning hyperparameters, few have systematically examined how these settings interact across model types or within insect pest datasets, such as those using sticky card imagery. This gap is critical, as insect datasets involve domain-specific challenges. Specifically, insects are typically small, visually similar, and unevenly distributed across sticky cards or field images, making them especially challenging for object detection models trained using default settings48,56,61. These challenges may influence how hyperparameters behave under different model architectures. Addressing this methodological gap, as done in the current study, is crucial for ensuring the reliable deployment of models in practical agricultural settings. Notably, batch size in this study had no significant main effect or interaction with other hyperparameters across either model, which is consistent with previous studies51,62 whose findings suggest that its influence is often secondary when other parameters, such as learning rate, drive optimization outcomes.
As AI tools continue to expand into applied domains such as entomology and agriculture, there is a growing need for model development workflows that are straightforward to understand, modify and implement63,64, . To support this goal, the present study employs grid search, a method known for its simplicity and systematic approach to hyperparameter tuning. It is easy to implement using standard machine learning libraries and does not require advanced knowledge of optimization algorithms34,65. The study also focused on a core set of widely used and well-documented hyperparameters, i.e., learning rate, batch size, and optimizer type, that are readily tunable and interpretable by non-specialist users66,67. While more advanced training strategies, such as CutMix, MixUp, or random erasing as well as extended hyperparameter schedules like cosine annealing and one-cycle learning rate policies, have been shown to improve generalization14,68, they also introduce additional complexity to the training pipelines. Consequently, this may deter the adoption of such technology by applied scientists. As such, our design choices reflect the practical realities of agricultural research, where computational infrastructure may be limited and where models may need to be deployed in field settings with minimal technical support. It complements similar studies such as Zhou et al.69, who evaluated YOLOv9 under constrained computational conditions and identified optimal combinations of learning rate, batch size, and optimizer to enable efficient training on small datasets in resource-limited contexts. The high accuracy and reliability achieved, even without advanced optimization strategies, demonstrates that meaningful performance gains can be realized through relatively simple, well-targeted tuning decisions.
This case study offers a replicable and scalable framework for optimizing object detection models in agricultural pest monitoring. By systematically evaluating the effects of core hyperparameters and analyzing their interactions within and across model architectures, the study bridges a practical gap between AI development and real-world entomological applications. The methodology and findings are particularly relevant for researchers and agricultural practitioners seeking to integrate AI into monitoring programs without requiring extensive computational expertise. Importantly, the use of real pest images collected under field-like conditions, along with a focus on commonly encountered insect pests, enhances the ecological and operational relevance of the results. It contributes by testing whether established optimization dynamics in deep learning generalize to the specific challenges of insect detection in precision agriculture. As precision agriculture increasingly adopts automated monitoring tools, approaches that are both scientifically rigorous and practically deployable will be essential to accelerating adoption, improving pest management outcomes, and promoting sustainable crop production. Future studies may build on this foundation by expanding the hyperparameter search space or testing the framework across different crops, environments, and real-time deployment scenarios.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that optimizing even a small set of core hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and optimizer can significantly improve the performance of object detection models for insect pest monitoring. By comparing YOLO and RT-DETR under controlled conditions and examining the responses of each model to changes in hyperparameter settings, this study demonstrates that both models can achieve high levels of accuracy when properly tuned, despite their substantial architectural differences. These results highlight the importance of model- and task-specific hyperparameter tuning strategies, particularly in agriculture, where insects vary in size, are visually similar, and are present at unequal rates. Furthermore, as interest in AI-assisted pest monitoring continues to grow, practical and reproducible optimization frameworks such as the one presented here will be essential for developing practical, field-ready tools in agriculture and beyond.
