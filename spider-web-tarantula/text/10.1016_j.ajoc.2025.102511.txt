Larvae of Gazalina chrysolopha can lead to atypical ophthalmia nodosa like features: A case report with imaging and management insights

# Larvae of Gazalina chrysolopha can lead to atypical ophthalmia nodosa like features: A case report with imaging and management insights☆

## Introduction
Ophthalmia nodosa is an inflammatory ocular condition caused by the penetration of barbed insect hairs (setae) into ocular tissues, commonly associated with exposure to caterpillars, moths, or tarantulas.1 The condition derives its name from the characteristic nodular granulomatous inflammation observed in the conjunctiva and iris, resulting from the mechanical and toxic effects of the setae.2 First described by Saemisch in 1904, ophthalmia nodosa encompasses a spectrum of ocular manifestations, ranging from mild conjunctivitis to severe endophthalmitis.3,4 The condition is particularly prevalent in regions where occupational exposure to caterpillars and moths is common, such as the Mediterranean and South Asia.5
Seasonal Hyperacute Panuveitis (SHAPU) is a severe ocular condition linked to adult white moth exposure, primarily in Nepal. While Ophthalmia nodosa (ON) is usually granulomatous and anterior-segment predominant, it can occasionally involve the posterior segment, including vitreous, choroid and retina. The distinction between ON and SHAPU is sometimes difficult but the ON is insidious in onset, recurrent in course and granulomatous in nature unlike SHAPU which has sudden onset without recurrence and has non-granulomatous inflammation. This report describes a case of atypical, predominantly anterior ON-like inflammation following exposure to second instar larvae of G. chrysolopha, with field verification of larval presence and careful assessment suggesting that adult moth contact was unlikely.

## Case report
A 56-year- male from Nuwakot district 51 km north of Kathmandu, weighing 62.5 kg and standing 5′6", presented to the outpatient department of B.P.Koirala Lions Centre for Ophthalmic Studies with complaints of acute redness, photophobia and foreign body sensation in his right eye. The patient reported that while cutting the branches of an Alnus nepalensis (Utis) tree near his house for the woods, he felt a sudden fall of a brunch of brown scaly larvas over his face, causing immediate ocular discomfort and difficult to open his right eye. Symptoms worsened over the next 24 hours, prompting medical consultation.
Ocular examinations revealed best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 6/12, N6 in the affected right eye and 6/6, N6 in the left eye. Slit-lamp biomicroscopy examination of the right eye revealed circumcorneal congestion and multiple translucent, sharp-ended setae (6–8 in number) embedded in the upper palpebral conjunctiva, along with multiple thorn like setae embedded within Bowman's layer and the anterior stroma of the cornea (Fig. 1A). Few corneal setae were surrounded by corneal infiltrations with diffuse superficial punctate keratitis. Corneal stain was positive in the superior half of the cornea, probably due to rubbing of the cornea by the palpebral conjunctival setae. The setae from the palpebral conjunctival setae were gently removed and placed over a slide (Fig. 1B). Under the light microscope, we could note a white wavy line pattern seta with a hollow body shown by a black arrow (Fig. 1C).
Clinical picture cornea showing multiple larval setae (white arrows) and areas of central superficial punctate keratopathy of RE (black arrow) in figure (A). The glass slide showing the larval setae in the center (B) and the demonstration of the setae (black arrow) under light microscope (C).
A mild anterior chamber reaction (1+ cells/flare) was noted. The vitreous and fundus findings were normal, and the left eye was normal. Specular microscopy demonstrated the central corneal thickness (CCT) measured 602 μm in the right eye and 531 μm in the left eye, increased endothelial polymegathism (coefficient of variation [CV] 49 % in the right eye vs. 35 % in the left eye) and reduced hexagonality (56 % vs. 66 %) indicates that the endothelial layer is likely not functioning optimally, and further clinical investigation may be warranted to assess corneal health (Fig. 2B). AS-OCT confirmed the presence of embedded setae in the anterior corneal stroma, with localized hyperreflectivity suggestive of inflammatory changes (Fig. 2A).
Anterior segment OCT showing larval setae in the bowman's layer and anterior stroma of cornea (black circle) and cells in anterior chamber (black arrows) in figure (A). The specular microscopy findings in figure (B) showed increased corneal thickness, reduced hexagonocity and increased coefficient of variation (CV) of the corneal endothelial cells in the right eye.
Based on the clinical history and findings, the patient was diagnosed with atypical ophthalmia nodosa with non-granulomatous reaction secondary to larvae setae. The patient underwent two separate episodes of mechanical removal of the setae from the conjunctiva under slit-lamp magnification, but the corneal setae could not be removed due to their presence at the Bowman's layer (Fig. 2A) and to avoid additional trauma. The retrieved setae were examined microscopically and compared with reference specimens of G. chrysolopha larvae and adult moths. The setae demonstrated the characteristic wavy-line, hollow morphology typical of larval setae, which are distinct from adult moth setae that are thicker, more rigid, and exhibit a different barb pattern. However, it is important to acknowledge that absolute certainty cannot be established since only setae, not the entire larva, were recovered. Nonetheless, both morphological and field findings consistently support larval origin as confirmed by the entomologist.
The patient was prescribed eyedrops (antibiotic, mydriatic, corticosteroid, and lubricant), and an oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication was prescribed for symptomatic relief. On a week follow-up, the ocular status was improving, oral medications were discontinued and corticosteroids were tapered over two weeks as the anterior chamber inflammation reduced.
At the two-week follow-up, BCVA remained stable at 6/12, with significant reduction in conjunctival congestion, anterior chamber reaction and no setae seen in the conjunctiva. However, retained setae in the peripheral cornea persisted without signs of progressive inflammation. At one month, BCVA improved to 6/6, and the corneal epithelium showed complete resolution of superficial punctate keratitis, and the corticosteroid regimen was tapered further, while carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) 1 % was maintained. Specular microscopy revealed partial normalization of endothelial morphology, though some polymegathism persisted. Right eye central corneal thickness (CCT) improved from 602 μm at baseline to 540 μm at 1 month. Coefficient of variation (CV) decreased from 49 % to 43 %, and hexagonality improved from 56 % to 55 %, indicating partial normalization of endothelial cell morphology. AS-OCT confirmed the continued presence of retained corneal setae in the Bowman's membrane and anterior stroma (Fig. 2A) without further tissue migration. The inflammatory response in this patient was confined to the anterior segment (conjunctiva and cornea), with no evidence of vitreous or posterior segment involvement on repeated slit-lamp and fundus examinations.
The patient followed up at 3 months and 5 months, where the chronic course of low grade anterior uveitis remained restricted to the anterior chamber, without posterior segment extension, thereby distinguishing it from classical SHAPU and favoring the diagnosis of ophthalmia nodosa.

## Discussion
Ophthalmia nodosa is a well-documented ocular inflammatory condition caused by the penetration of barbed insect hairs (setae) into ocular tissues, commonly associated with exposure to caterpillars, moths, or tarantulas.1 While Seasonal Hyperacute Panuveitis (SHAPU) is a severe ocular condition linked to exposure to adult white moths, particularly in Nepal and less commonly in Bhutan, the larval stage of the G chrysolopha has been less studied to ocular pathology. Neither is there a report in the literature that mention the development of ophthalmia nodosa like features from Gazalina larvae nor a report from a patient who had ocular insult from the exposure of these moths from the primary host plant "Alnus nepalensis". Thus, this case is unique as it depicts a typical scenario of the fall of the brunch of the Gazalina larvae from the Alnus nepalensis trees over the facial area, leading to the recurrent allergic inflammatory reaction in the eye which mimicks ophthalmia nodosa but differs due to absence of granulomatous reaction and limited recurrency. Hence, a triad relationship between the host plant (Alnus nepalensis), host insect (G. chrysolopha) and vulnerable human tissue (eye) has been depicted here.
The patient was a school teacher who was able to give an accurate history. Our team of entomologists and forest experts could visit the site of the incident and confirm the identification of the larvae moth as 2nd instar of G chrysolopha and identify the plant as the Alnus nepalensis. The confirmation of that insect was the 2nd instar larvae of G chrysolopha was done by the detection of the brown processionary caterpillars with a red head bearing a crescent mark and presence of thorny setae throughout the dorsal line. No adult moths were detected at the site of incidence.
The pathophysiology of ophthalmia nodosa involves both mechanical and immunological responses to the penetration of insect setae into ocular tissues. Due to their minute size and their friability, removing of the setae is not easy and may do more harm than good. Continuous monitoring is important to remove those hairs that may resurface.6 The barbed structure of setae facilitates their penetration into the conjunctiva, cornea, and even deeper ocular structures, triggering a localized inflammatory response.2 In this case, the moth larvae setae embedded in the corneal epithelium and anterior stroma elicited an inflammatory response, characterized by circumcorneal congestion, superficial punctate keratitis (SPKs), and mild anterior chamber inflammation. The presence of corneal filaments further underscores the mechanical irritation caused by the setae, while the mild anterior chamber reaction suggests an immune-mediated response to the foreign material.
The inflammatory response in ophthalmia nodosa is thought to be driven by both the mechanical irritation of the setae and the release of toxins or antigens from the insect hairs.7 Unlike SHAPU, which is characterized by severe panuveitis and is linked to adult moth exposure, ophthalmia nodosa primarily affects the anterior segment, with conjunctival and corneal involvement being the most common manifestations.8 This distinction is critical for accurate diagnosis and management, as the treatment strategies for ophthalmia nodosa and SHAPU differ significantly.
The diagnosis of ophthalmia nodosa can be challenging, particularly in cases where the setae are deeply embedded in the cornea or conjunctiva. In this case, the use of advanced imaging modalities, such as AS-OCT and specular microscopy, proved invaluable in confirming the diagnosis and guiding management. AS-OCT provided high-resolution, non-invasive visualization of the embedded setae in the corneal stroma, while specular microscopy revealed endothelial polymegathism and pleomorphism, indicative of mechanical and toxic stress on the corneal endothelium.9 These imaging techniques not only facilitated the diagnosis but also allowed for the monitoring of treatment response and the detection of residual setae, which could lead to chronic inflammation if left untreated.
The management of ophthalmia nodosa depends on the depth and location of the setae. Superficial conjunctival setae can often be safely removed under slit-lamp magnification, while deeper corneal setae pose a significant challenge due to their barbed structure, which increases the risk of intraocular penetration during attempted extraction.10 In this case, the patient underwent two separate episodes of mechanical removal of the setae from the conjunctiva, but the corneal setae at bowman's membrane and anterior stroma were left undisturbed to avoid additional trauma. Conservative management with topical antibiotics, corticosteroids, lubricants, and mydriatics resulted in resolution of inflammation and improvement in visual acuity, though retained corneal setae remained. This highlights the importance of a balanced approach to management, prioritizing the prevention of further ocular damage while controlling inflammation.
Topical corticosteroids play a crucial role in controlling anterior uveitis and reducing inflammation in ophthalmia nodosa. However, their long-term use must be carefully monitored due to the risk of complications such as elevated intraocular pressure and cataract formation.11 In this case, the corticosteroids were tapered as the inflammation subsided, and the patient was maintained on tear substitutes to support ocular surface health. The improvement in visual acuity from 6/12 to 6/6 within one month of treatment underscores the effectiveness of prompt and appropriate intervention. Mild anterior uveitis recurred at three and five months but remained limited to the anterior chamber without vitreous or posterior segment involvement. The rebound inflammation was monitored using slit-lamp biomicroscopy, anterior chamber cell grading, and patient symptom reporting. Further, emphasizing that inflammation was effectively managed with topical corticosteroids and mydriatics, and did not evolve into SHAPU or posteriorly penetrating ON.
While SHAPU and ophthalmia nodosa are both ocular conditions associated with exposure to moths, they differ significantly in their clinical presentation, pathophysiology, and management. SHAPU is characterized by sudden, painless vision loss, leukocoria, and severe panuveitis, often leading to blindness if not treated promptly.12 In contrast, ophthalmia nodosa primarily affects the anterior segment, with conjunctival and corneal involvement being the most common manifestations. However, it is to be clarified that the differentiation between larval-induced ON and SHAPU in this case is based on the anatomical distribution of inflammation, the absence of vitreous involvement, and the temporal course of recovery. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that overlapping presentations can occur.
This case underscores the importance of recognizing the ocular risks associated with the larval stage of the white moth, which can cause ophthalmia nodosa. Clinicians should be aware of the characteristic clinical features of ophthalmia nodosa, particularly in patients presenting with acute redness, foreign body sensation, and photophobia following insect exposure. Early diagnosis and appropriate management are crucial to prevent long-term complications, such as corneal scarring or chronic uveitis. Advanced imaging modalities, such as AS-OCT and specular microscopy, should be utilized to confirm the diagnosis and monitor treatment response.
Even though the management produced favourable results, there are still difficulties in forecasting the long-term prognosis and recurrence of the disease. To improve patient outcomes, future research should concentrate on innovative therapeutic approaches and long-term monitoring. To prove a clear causal connection between Gazalina exposure and SHAPU and support the case for preventative measures, more research is also necessary. Setae have the ability to penetrate biological barriers, and gaining deeper insight into the mechanisms that enable their movement through ocular tissues could contribute to the advancement of innovative drug delivery methods. The incidence of infection in ophthalmia nodosa (ON) is notably low, indicating that the setae might possess inherent antibiotic or antiseptic qualities.6 Thus, further research is needed to explore the pathophysiological mechanisms linking moth life stages to ocular inflammation. Specifically, studies should investigate the toxicological properties of white moth larvae setae and their role in triggering immune-mediated inflammation. Additionally, the development of standardized diagnostic and management protocols for ophthalmia nodosa would help improve patient outcomes. Longitudinal studies are also needed to assess the long-term effects of retained setae on corneal health and visual function.

## Conclusion
This case highlights the importance of recognizing the ocular risks associated with the larval stage of the white moth, which can cause recurrent ocular inflammation. It also draws attention to the possible involvement of adult Gazalina in SHAPU and its larvae in ophthalmia nodosa, which calls for more experimental and epidemiological studies.
Early diagnosis and appropriate management are crucial to prevent long-term complications. Advanced imaging modalities, such as AS-OCT and specular microscopy, play a pivotal role in diagnosing and monitoring the condition. Further research is needed to explore the pathophysiological mechanisms linking moth life stages to ocular inflammation and to optimize treatment strategies.
