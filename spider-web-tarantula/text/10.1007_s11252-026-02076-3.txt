Abstract
Anthropogenic noise can disrupt how predators use environmental cues to detect and localize prey. In vertebrates, noise
pollution has been shown to reduce foraging efficiency and alter predator–prey dynamics through masking and distraction;
much less is known about how acoustic noise influences invertebrate predation where significant sensory filtering occurs
outside the central nervous system. Here, we investigate how an added anthropogenic noise affects prey capture behavior
in the orb-weaving banded garden spider (Argiope trifasciata). In a controlled laboratory setting, spiders were exposed
to playback of recorded traffic noise while live crickets jumped into their webs. We quantified prey detection latency,
approach behavior, and attack execution using high-speed videography. Spiders exposed to acoustic noise responded
slower to prey compared to control spiders hunting in quiet conditions and noise-exposed spiders also showed increased
variability in duration of predation attempts. However, once spiders initiated an approach to prey, their attack execution
times were comparable across treatments. This suggests that anthropogenic noise primarily interferes with early-stage
prey detection and localization in web‑building spiders, likely by masking biologically relevant vibratory cues. As spiders
rely on peripheral sensory filtering before signals reach the central nervous system, such interference may alter how prey
information is processed at the onset rather than during later motor execution. Our results provide a foundational baseline
suggest that human-made noise can disrupt essential behaviors in invertebrate predators, such as the ability to detect and
capture prey. This interference could alter predator-prey interactions in natural habitats, a significant indirect impact of
urbanization.
Highlights
● Urban noise altered early prey detection and localization behavior in orb-weaving spiders.
● Noise-exposed spiders showed greater variability in duration of total predation attempts.
● Attack execution times were similar across treatments once spiders initiated an approach towards prey.
● Vibratory cue masking by anthropogenic noise disrupted initial sensory processing.

behaviors, survival strategies, and ecological interactions

Anthropogenic noise from traffic, industry, and infrastructure generates complex acoustic and vibratory disturbances
in terrestrial and aquatic environments (Anzibar Fialho et
al. 2025; Francis and Barber 2013; Raboin and Elias 2019).
While vertebrate-focused studies reveal that such noise
can disrupt acoustic communication in birds (Halfwerk et
al. 2011), impair echolocation in bats (Siemers and Schaub
2010), and interfere with marine mammal signaling (Clark
et al. 2009), these findings cannot be directly generalized
to invertebrates because invertebrate sensory systems and
ecological interactions differ fundamentally (Raboin and
Elias 2019; Wu and Elias 2014). Many arthropods, such
as orb-weaving spiders, depend heavily on substrate-borne
vibrations for prey detection, predator assessment, and mating coordination (Barth et al. 1988; Walcott 1969). In webbuilding spiders, the web itself functions as an extended
sensory organ that shapes information acquisition and
decision-making (Lott et al. 2022; Japyassú and Laland
2017; Mortimer 2019). Unlike vertebrates, which predominantly utilize central nervous system auditory pathways to
process sounds, spiders are equipped with specialized slit
sensilla and trichobothria that perform significant mechanical and neural filtering before signals are transmitted to the
central nervous system (Barth 2002, 2004; Liu et al. 2024;
Zhou et al. 2022). This suggests that vibratory noise may
disrupt invertebrate behaviors in unique ways compared to
vertebrates. Web-building spiders therefore serve as crucial
models for examining the propagation of human-induced
disturbances through trophic interactions and ecosystems.
Web-building spiders are especially interesting for studying the effects of anthropogenic noise because spider webs
act both as extended sensory structures and sensory filters
(Masters 1984). Spiders have evolved highly sensitive

mechanosensory systems that allow the spiders to interpret
vibrations on their webs and discriminate between prey,
potential mates, and predatory threats (Landolfa and Barth
1996; Barth and Geethabali 1982; Speck and Barth 1982).
Webs can also filter out environmental noise such as wind
or debris falling into the webs (Masters 1984). This precise
sensory discrimination enables spiders to make rapid decisions about whether to attack, approach cautiously, or retreat
(Frohlich and Buskirk 1982; Masters and Markl 1981;Mulder et al. 2020).
Most research on the response of spiders to urbanization focuses on changes in body size, web architecture,
and reproduction in response to urban environments which
encompass multiple stressors, including altered prey availability, chemical and light pollution, and habitat fragmentation (Gordon and Uetz 2012; Lowe et al. 2014, Lowe et
al. 2017; Gomes 2020). For instance, Trichonephila clavata
show reduced ‘prey-capture’ behavior near busy roads,
potentially due to sensory interference from traffic-induced
noise and vibration (Davis et al. 2024). Funnel-web spiders
exposed to loud urban noise build webs with reduced transmission properties compared to spiders acclimated to quiet
environments, demonstrating a substantial role for webs
pre-filtering signals before they reach the spiders’ sensory
systems (Pessman and Hebets 2025). These studies demonstrate significant impact of long-term exposure to anthropogenic noise, however, there is limited understanding of how
spider predatory behavior is affected by short-term exposure to noise. Our study addresses this gap by testing how
short-term exposure to urban vibratory noise file influences
prey detection and capture behavior in the orb-weaving spider Argiope trifasciata. We exposed spiders to playbacks
of recorded traffic vibrations while presenting live prey on
their webs. We then used high speed video to measure differences in speed of detection, orientation to and capture of
prey in noisy vs. quiet conditions.

Trial procedure
Spiders were allowed to acclimate for 5 min after the full
setup including camera, lights, and open cage was in place.
For the noise treatment, playback started immediately following this 5-minute acclimation period, continuing for an
additional ~ 5 min of noise exposure and control/quiet trials
received a silent interval of equivalent duration, ensuring
identical timing and handling protocols between experimental conditions. A live cricket was then poked until it jumped
into the web from ~ 30 cm. This simulated natural prey capture because Argiope often captures jumping crickets and
grasshoppers (Nyffeler et al. 1987). We excluded any trials where the cricket did not land directly into the capture
area of the web. There was no difference between the two

Video recording
Behavioral responses were recorded using a Photron SA4
high-speed camera (Photron, Tokyo, Japan) with Nikon
AF-S VR 105 mm f/2.8G IF-ED macro lens for high-resolution close-up documentation. Videos were recorded at 125
frames per second to provide optimal resolution and temporal precision. The camera was positioned perpendicularly
to the web to capture the full range of spider movements. A
shadow box was placed behind the web to enhance contrast
and improve visibility. Illumination was provided using a
Fovitec S-900D LED panel light (Fovitec, Hollywood,
California, USA) positioned laterally to the web. Recordings were managed and analyzed using Photron FASTCAM
Viewer (PFV) software. Behavioral events and response
latencies were annotated using PFV4 (Photron USA, Inc.,
San Diego, CA) post-processing tools to extract precise timing data.

Analysis
We measured multiple time-based and orientation-related
variables (Table 1) to describe distinct phases of the spider’s prey capture sequence and to produce ethograms of
potential responses (Fig. 2). In particular, we distinguished
between the initial detection and localization of prey impact
vs. subsequent approach and attack.

Results
A total of 15 webs built by 13 spiders were tested in the quiet
condition and 24 webs built by 15 spiders were exposed to
urban noise. In general, orb-weaving spiders exhibit a distinct behavioral sequence when capturing prey, they maintain a stationary position at the hub, detect prey through
vibrations transmitted via the web, then orient themselves
toward the vibration source by adjusting their position or
manipulating tension in radial threads and subsequently
initiate an approach and attack (Harwood 1974; Klärner
and Barth 1982). All continuous variables were compared
between treatments using two-sample t-tests.

to prey within 0.29 ± 0.19 s after impact, while three individuals failed to respond even after ~ 10 s (Fig. 2). In the
urban noise conditions, 63% (15/24) of spiders responded
to the prey in 4.89 ± 6.41 s. The probability of response
did not differ between treatments, but the response times

Discussion
As a primary experiment with urban noise profiles, our
results show that added urban noise significantly disrupts
prey capture behavior in the orb-weaving spider Argiope
trifasciata by slowing down the initial time to detect and
localize prey impacts. The disruption by urban soundscape
was very bimodal, with some spiders reacting as quickly as

One plausible explanation for the delayed reaction time
is that added anthropogenic stimuli interfere with biologically relevant vibrations within the spider’s web by producing masking. This interference occurs through two
primary mechanisms - mechanical coupling disruption
affecting vibration transmission or early sensory processing

(Barth 2002, 2004). Such disruptions create ambiguity about
vibration origin or identity, delaying the spider’s approach

reduces the signal-to-noise ratio detected by mechanoreceptors like slit sensilla on the legs (Wu and Elias 2014; Bee
and Swanson 2007). One explanation for the pronounced

If this explanation is correct, then there is significant potential for natural selection to shape web-building behaviors
in urban environments. Although our data cannot directly
test these mechanisms, they highlight potential pathways
through which interference in early prey detection can be
tested in multiple noise files. In addition to web-level variation, individual factors such as hunger state, developmental
history, or prior experience with noise could also contribute to the observed bimodality. We did not systematically
manipulate or quantify these covariates, so we cannot distinguish their effects from web mechanics, and future work
should explicitly balance or measure these factors across
treatments.
An alternative explanation for the increased response
times of some spiders in the noisy environment is a shift in
the motivational or arousal state in response to the noise. We
observed web-pumping immediately after turning on urban
noise playbacks in 5 of 24 noise trials while web-pumping
never occurred in quiet trials. Although this small number of
observations provides only preliminary evidence, it mainly
does indicate that spiders directly perceived the urban noise
stimuli, either through substrate‑borne vibrations or airborne sound waves detected by their trichobothria (Barth
2002). Depending on context, web pumping can be interpreted as signal exploration, vigilance, or defense (Jackson
et al. 1993; Robledo-Ospina et al. 2023) because it both

The attack time did not differ significantly between quiet
and noise conditions (Fig. 4), which suggests that once a
spider commits to an attack, the subsequent motor sequence
is largely insulated from acoustic interference. One possible explanation is that prey capture behavior in spiders
follows a threshold-based mechanism, wherein only spiders that have acquired sufficiently unambiguous sensory
input proceed to attack. Although our data do not directly
test this mechanism, this interpretation is consistent with
prior work showing that orb‑weavers rely on measurable
displacement thresholds before committing to an attack
(Klärner and Barth 1982). Once initiated, the strike may be
executed via a robust, possibly reflexive, motor program
that is not easily perturbed by external noise and spiders of
similar motivational states in both treatments, at this stage,
spiders may be fully committed to attacking potential prey,
allowing feeding motivation to override sensory ambiguity
caused by urban noise. Testing the role of changes in motivational state vs. sensory perception is an intriguing avenue
for future investigations and would contribute to a broader
understanding of how spiders integrate sensory inputs with
motor output, and how environmental noise or disturbance
might selectively affect perception versus spider’s action.

Exposure to urban noise soundscape significantly
impaired prey capture performance in spiders, though individual responses varied considerably (Fig. 5). The total
time from the onset of prey stimulus to prey contact was
significantly prolonged under noise conditions (t = − 2.009,
P = 0.0309) but the distribution of this metric under noise
exposure was broader, suggesting a disruption in the spiders’ ability to make and sustain spatial movement decisions. However, this difference was primarily driven by
variation in the initial response latency, rather than changes
in the execution of the approach itself (Fig. 5). Most spiders in the noise treatment responded rapidly, like those in
the quiet condition, but a smaller group hesitated for several
seconds before approaching. As a result, mean total time
under noise increased, but the broad variability suggests that
noise disrupts early sensory reception or decision-making in
only part of the sample size.
A. trifasciata showed broadly similar orientation behaviors in both treatments (Fig. 6). Collectively, orientation outcomes indicate that transient noise primarily compromises
spiders’ response initiation probability and latency, rather
than subsequent behavioral execution. Overall, spiders
consistently employed both continuous and discontinuous
approaches across quiet and noise conditions, suggesting
approach stability despite acoustic disturbance in provided
sample size. Although most observations were obtained
from different individuals, a small number of spiders contributed more than one trial. Consequently, some degree
of non-independence cannot be excluded. Future studies
with larger sample sizes would benefit from mixed-effects
approaches incorporating spider identity as a random effect
to account explicitly for repeated measurements.
We exposed spiders to added noise profile from urban
soundscapes to observe the immediate acute impacts of
short-term auditory interference on the spiders’ prey capture
behavior. While we did not examine long-term (chronic)
effects of noise exposure, acute noise plays a significant
ecological role, given that many human-generated sound
sources such as traffic, passing vehicles, and construction
activity can act as intermittent, unpredictable disturbances
that spiders must process rapidly. Such transient disruptions
may rapidly reduce vibrational signal clarity on the web,
impairing early sensory processing and demonstrating that
acute effects hold ecological relevance regardless of chronic
exposure duration.
Chronic noise, in contrast, can induce habituation or
long-term neural plasticity, as prior work on urban spiders
has shown changes in web architecture, body size, and
reproductive output in noisy environments, suggesting that
urban noise is a relevant selective pressure in spider ecology (Gomes et al. 2021; Lowe, Wilder, and Hochuli 2014).

remain not sufficient. Nevertheless, these long-term adaptive mechanisms do not negate the relevance of acute experimental studies. Immediate sensory interference directly
impacts short-term foraging efficiency and represents the
initial challenge an organism must overcome before habituation or compensatory behaviors emerge. Our experimental
approach specifically isolates transient disruption during
predation events, thereby complementing the research on
chronic noise effects. Also, our experiment focused on
acute, short‑term exposure to anthropogenic noise (~ 5 min)
in a quiet laboratory environment, which does not fully
replicate the chronic, fluctuating noise regimes that spiders
experience in urban habitats. As such, our results should be
interpreted as an acute test of how added urban noise affects
early‑stage prey localization rather than a comprehensive
assessment of long‑term adaptation to noise. Moreover, we
did not include an additional handling‑only control without
speaker presence, so we cannot fully exclude the possibility
that some behavioral changes reflect generalized arousal or
stress associated with the noise treatment rather than masking alone. Future work incorporating chronic exposure,
more naturalistic background noise, and explicit handling
controls will be essential to evaluate longer‑term plasticity
and adaptation.
Also, our speaker system attenuates frequencies below
~ 150 Hz and we did not directly quantify vibration amplitude at the web, we cannot establish a dose–response relationship between noise intensity and behavior, nor can we
fully assess the contribution of very low‑frequency components that often dominate traffic noise. Our experiment
used a single recording of traffic noise as a representative
exemplar of anthropogenic disturbance which represents
moderate urban noise stimuli and likely produced biologically relevant web vibrations. However, real-world soundscapes vary in spectrum and temporal patterns, which can
influence behavioral masking (Slabbekoorn and Ripmeester
2008; Purser and Radford 2011). This approach is consistent
with prior sensory-ecology studies that use exemplar stimuli
to test mechanistic responses. Future work incorporating
multiple noise exemplars and natural geophonic/biophonic
backgrounds will help determine the generality of our findings and explain the observed bimodal response latencies.
The behavioral disruptions we observed in orb-weaving spiders are consistent with patterns observed in other
arthropods where anthropogenic noise can reduce foraging efficiency in ants (Byrne et al. 2022) and alters insect
and arachnid behavior in such ways that can affect species
interactions (Classen-Rodríguez et al. 2021). As a preliminary study using one urban-noise profile, our findings
highlight that noise pollution can affect a wide range of ecologically important invertebrate behaviors. Given the ecological importance of spiders as invertebrate predators that

To conclude, our study reveals that short-term exposure
to anthropogenic noise significantly delays prey detection
and capture behavior in the orb-weaving spider A. trifasciata, primarily by interfering with early sensory processing
rather than motor performance. Although spiders ultimately
executed attacks with similar efficiency once they started
moving toward prey, the increased response latency under
noise suggests that urban vibratory disturbances can impair
critical prey localization cues. More broadly, few investigations have addressed urban vibratory noise as a disturbance,
particularly in non-hearing arthropods. As a preliminary
experimental assessment, our work demonstrates that
even a single exemplar of urban noise, delivered through
a limited playback system, can alter early‑stage predatory
decision‑making in a web‑building spider and our findings
emphasize that such “non-acoustic” species are not immune
to vibratory pollution, and that anthropogenic noise should
be considered a pervasive and underappreciated factor shaping the behavioral ecology of ground-dwelling and webbuilding invertebrates. Future research should extend these
findings by testing the effects of chronic noise exposure,
incorporating multiple noise spectra, and replicating across
additional taxa. If noise-induced sensory disruption proves
widespread among invertebrate predators, it could represent
an overlooked mechanism through which anthropogenic
noise alters ecosystem processes and predator–prey dynamics in terrestrial environments.
