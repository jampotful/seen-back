Abstract

16

Visual camouflage via background matching involves a variety of adaptative traits to

17

maintain crypsis, including intraspecific color variation, behavioral choice of substrates,

18

and color change. These non-mutually exclusive solutions frequently act together to

19

conceal and deceive prey and enemies. Here, we combine field observations, image

20

analysis, and laboratory experiments to investigate which processes drive camouflage in

21

the garden cross spider (Araneus diadematus), a species with body coloration that varies

22

in shades of brown. We demonstrate that A. diadematus does not change color

23

significantly, at least within the same instar, when retained on substrates of different

24

coloration. However, there is strong behavioral selection through active substrate choice

25

across spiders for color-matching substrates (dead brown leaves over green leaves)

1

26

when offered a choice under laboratory conditions. Similar background selection also

27

apparently occurs in nature, where spiders were often observed on brown leaves, even

28

though they are less common than green ones. In general, vision modeling shows that

29

there is a high overlap in the diversity of brown shades (from pale to dark) between

30

spider bodies and dead leaves available in the environment. Image analyses also

31

revealed that spiders fine-tune their camouflage on an individual level by matching the

32

tones (from pale to dark) of their host leaves. Therefore, we demonstrate how behavior

33

coupled with variation in color phenotypes facilitates camouflage at different scales.

INTRODUCTION

38

Animal camouflage has been a longstanding topic in behavioral and evolutionary

39

ecology as a powerful example of how natural selection works in nature, inspiring

40

numerous studies on animal coloration, perception, and sensory ecology (Stevens and

41

Merilaita 2011; Cuthill et al. 2017). The use of cryptic coloration for visual camouflage

42

(e.g. background matching) is widespread in the animal kingdom, occurring when the

43

coloration and patterning of an organism resembles the surrounding substrate (Cott

44

1940; Endler 1978; Cuthill et al. 2005; Merilaita and Lind 2005; Stevens and Merilaita

45

2009, 2011). Background matching can be improved by several traits beyond coloration

46

per se, related to behavioral (e.g. habitat choice and posture) and physiological (e.g.

47

color change) mechanisms (Duarte et al. 2017; Stevens and Ruxton 2019), or a

48

combination of these (Green et al. 2019). However, few studies have addressed

49

crypticity predictions under a multilevel approach and usually considered such

50

mechanisms in isolation when testing the adaptative significance of camouflage.

51

Species with cryptic coloration can improve concealment and reduce detection

52

by behaviorally choosing appropriate backgrounds, at several levels, from generic

53

(species-level) to specific (individual-level) substrate selection (Stevens and Ruxton

54

2019). Recent works have demonstrated that individuals of some species of birds

55

(Stevens et al. 2017), lizards (Marshall et al. 2016), frogs (Eterovick et al. 2010, 2018),

56

moths (Skelhorn and Ruxton 2012), and crustaceans (Green et al. 2019; Twort and

57

Stevens 2023; Dyer and Stevens 2024) may facilitate camouflage through a fine-scale

58

habitat selection. For example, the nesting substrate preference of Japanese quail

59

Coturnix japonica (Lovell et al. 2013; Alothyqi et al. 2024) is closely tied to the

60

individual appearance of eggs. Additionally, moths can change their resting posture

61

based on individual levels of camouflage (Skelhorn and Ruxton 2012), whereas some

3

62

prawns actively choose seaweeds that match their body coloration (Green et al. 2019).

63

Animals might use color change and behavioral choices to achieve camouflage, which

64

seems widespread in many taxa, from lizards to cephalopods and prawns. Surprisingly,

65

few studies to date have investigated color change, behavior, and individual variation in

66

color as mechanisms for facilitating crypsis (but see for example Green et al. 2019,

67

Duarte et al. 2024).

68

Spiders are considered good models in animal coloration research since they are

69

a highly diverse group that exhibits several different camouflage strategies. Such

70

strategies include color polymorphism and continuous individual variation (Messas et

71

al. 2014; Rao et al. 2015; Rao and Mendoza-Cuenca 2016; Ajuria-Ibarra et al. 2017),

72

background matching (Defrize et al. 2010; Robledo-Ospina et al. 2017), disruptive

73

coloration (Hoese et al. 2006; Robledo-Ospina et al. 2017), color change (Defrize et al.

74

2011; Yu et al. 2022 a), masquerading as bird droppings (Liu et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2022

75

b), leaves (Kuntner et al. 2016), and twigs (Zhang et al. 2015), in addition to several

76

types of mimicry (Vieira et al. 2017; Pekár 2014; Robledo-Ospina and Rao 2022).

77

However, much past work testing spider camouflage has relied on the human subjective

78

visual system, generally not linked to behavior, and background matching is often

79

inferred rather than objectively quantified.

80

Our model species, the European garden spider Araneus diadematus Clerck,

81

1758 (Araneidae), is one of the most abundant and common orb-web spiders in some

82

regions of Europe (Foelix 2011; Bee et al. 2020). This spider is target of visually-

83

oriented predators as it is active during the daylight (Nyffeler and Benz 1989). Web

84

construction occurs in the early morning, when spiders are highly visible and display

85

bold behavior. However, when preying on insects, they adopt an ambush-predation

86

strategy by hiding in vegetation and relying on vibrations from trapped insects.

4

87

Additionally, A. diadematus is considered a reliable species for behavioral studies as it

88

can be easily maintained in controlled conditions and readily produces orb-webs for

89

laboratory-based research (Zschokke and Herberstein 2005). Here we combine field

90

observations, digital image analysis, and laboratory experiments to investigate multiple

91

processes that could shape the camouflage of orb-web spiders. We observed in the field

92

that A. diadematus usually rests on brown leaves of shrub vegetation (Fig. 1A), with

93

individuals presenting color variation from pale to reddish and dark shades of brown

94

(Fig. 1B-C). From these field observations, we expect an overlapping distribution of the

95

color variation between spiders and the brown leaves available in the environment. If

96

so, we predict that spiders will be more effectively camouflaged on their chosen resting

97

substrates (brown host leaves) compared to other substrates available in the surrounding

98

environment. Additionally, we hypothesize that A. diadematus background matching

99

relies on phenotypic plasticity in spiders’ body coloration, with a fine-scale individual

100

resemblance to the color of the host leaf, or of behavioral selection of backgrounds

101

resembling an individual’s own appearance. Herein, we tested whether individual spider

102

camouflage in the field better matches the chosen host leaf than the leaves chosen by

103

sympatric conspecifics. To understand how camouflage is achieved, we tested in the

104

laboratory whether background matching of A. diadematus is mediated by one or a

105

combination of habitat choice by active behavioral selection of brown leaves on shrub

106

vegetation and color change according to substrate coloration.

METHODS

109

Sampling and photography

110

We collected spiders and leaves of their host plants during the spring-summer of 2022

111

on a coastal trail between Castle (50°08'54"N, 05°03'26"W) and Maenporth (50°7'28"N

5

112

05°05'36"W) beaches in Falmouth, Cornwall, Southwest UK. During this season, the

113

local vegetation holds a diverse set of native and alien herbaceous species that are used

114

by A. diadematus as a substrate for web anchoring with a predominance of fresh green

115

leaves (covering 80-90% of the foliage). The green vegetation is interspersed by a few

116

brownish-greyish structures (e.g. branches and dead leaves) that seem to be used by the

117

spiders for camouflage and resting substrates. The higher proportion of green vegetation

118

and the increased abundance of young-to-subadult spiders during the study period

119

contrast with autumn-winter seasons, marked by the presence of adults, when leaf-fall

120

results in more brownish vegetation and the cold temperatures negatively affect spider

121

survival.

122

Sampling was directed to juveniles and subadult spiders, avoiding the collection

123

of spiderlings, as they present low color variation, and adults, since they have stopped

124

their molting cycle and may present sexual dimorphism in behavior, size and coloration

125

after maturation. We transported spiders to the facilities of the University of Exeter

126

(Penryn campus) for standardized photography and laboratory experiments. We used

127

two sets of spiders and leaves for each procedure, which included investigating

128

background matching between spiders and host leaves (N = 68) and performing

129

laboratory trials of behavioral choice followed by color change (N = 30). During the

130

experimental time (~ 20 days), spiders were individually housed in circular plastic

131

containers (height = 76 mm, diameter = 100 mm), fed daily with Drosophila flies and

132

hydrated by cotton balls soaked in water. Spiders were maintained at ambient room

133

temperature and humidity under a 13:30-10:30 light-dark cycle with lights on at 06:30

134

to simulate the natural lighting conditions during the study period. All the research was

135

conducted with approval from the University of Exeter Bioscience Ethics Committee

136

(application number 2022/516616).

6

137

For the purposes of our study, we used a Canon EOS 5 DS-R camera fitted with

138

a Canon 100 mm macro lens and a tripod, a photography set-up that allowed us to

139

obtain high-definition photographs of small subjects. We obtained all images in high-

140

quality JPG format, with manual white balancing and fixed aperture (f/ 3.5) and ISO

141

(320) settings to avoid overexposed areas (Stevens et al. 2007). We decided to consider

142

only the visible light spectrum (400–700 nm) since pilot-UV photographs obtained for

143

both A. diadematus spiders and host leaves using a full-spectrum camera (Sony Alpha

144

A6000 fitted with a Nikkor 80 mm lens) indicated low reflectance in this part of the

145

spectrum, as is common in many natural backgrounds and camouflage markings. Image

146

acquisition occurred in natural lighting conditions during overcast-cloudy days to keep

147

light conditions uniform following current standard procedures (Troscianko and Stevens

148

2015). We used a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) diffuser around the subjects over a

149

grey background to ensure even lightning conditions and to reduce light reflection and

150

high contrasts. To automate image rescaling, calibration and standardization for the

151

image analysis (Troscianko and Stevens 2015), each photograph included a scale bar

152

with grey reflectance standards made from zenith diffuse sintered PTFE sheets

153

(Labsphere, Congleton, UK), which reflect light equally at 4.5% and 96.2% between

154

300 and 750 nm.

156

Image analysis and vision modeling

157

All image analyses were performed through standard routines using the Multispectral

158

Image Calibration and Analysis Toolbox (Troscianko and Stevens 2015) in ImageJ

159

software (Schneider et al. 2012). We first linearized all images (R2 ≥ 0.999 for all

160

camera channels) to correct for camera non-linear pixel responses to light intensity

161

using a color checker standard (X-Rite, Grand Rapids, MI) photographed under natural

7

162

light conditions (Troscianko and Stevens 2015; van den Berg et al. 2020). Further,

163

photographs were equalized for changes in light levels across photography sessions

164

using the 4.5% and 96.2% grey standards. This process resulted in multispectral images

165

with three color channels (red, green, and blue), where an image value of 255 pixels on

166

an eight-bit scale equals 100% reflectance (Stevens et al. 2007). Multispectral

167

normalized images are suitable for color analyses because they correspond to the

168

physical reflectance properties of spiders and backgrounds in three parts of the

169

spectrum, namely long (LW), medium (MW) and short wavelengths (SW).

170

Since diurnal birds have long been known to be important natural predators of

171

spiders (Gunnarsson 2007; Foelix 2011) and are relatively conserved in their visual

172

pigment characteristics (Hart 2001), we mapped the images of spiders and host leaves to

173

the vision of the Eurasian blue tit Cyanistes caeruleus (Hart et al. 2000), following

174

numerous previous studies. Blue tits are likely tetrachromatic and possess four single

175

cones responsible for color vision (with peak sensitivities at 371, 448, 503 and 563 nm)

176

and double cones for achromatic vision (with peak sensitivity at 503 nm). Although

177

accounting for UV information in our model may improve the understanding of spider

178

camouflage to the view of bird predators, as these and various other animals are

179

sensitive to UV, our photographic equipment did not allow capturing this part of the

180

light spectrum. However, excluding UV did not invalidate our results as other recent

181

studies using arthropods as prey and bird as predators have conducted similar model

182

adaptations (as in Kang et al. 2015; Kang et al. 2016; Baños-Villalba et al. 2018). In

183

addition, as stated above, there was very low UV information in the spider and

184

background samples based on our pilot imaging, and this is commonly the case with

185

many backgrounds, and any UV information tends to correlate very closely with other

186

parts of the spectrum (e.g. white markings). Furthermore, the low levels of UV in the

8

187

light conditions also necessitate very long exposures (often several seconds or more),

188

which can cause problems when any movement is involved. We considered a Weber

189

fraction (= cone receptor noise) of 0.05 for the most abundant cone type with a cone

190

ratio of 1.92:2.68:2.7 (SW/MW/LW sensitivity; Hart et al. 2000) and a D65 irradiance

191

spectrum as a measure of incident illuminance (Wyszecki and Stiles 2000). This

192

procedure resulted in cone catch values that closely follow spectrometry approaches

193

(Stevens et al. 2007). We marked regions of interest (ROIs) on both spiders (abdomen

194

region) and plant leaves (surface area of entire green and brown leaves), from which

195

cone catch data were extracted.

Spider background matching

198

As a first approach to spider background matching, we predicted that spiders are more

199

likely to be camouflaged on brown than green leaf substrates. To visualize and compare

200

the similarity of spider appearance between leaf types, we used the receptor noise-

201

limited (RNL) chromaticity color space (Vorobyev and Osorio 1998; van den Berg et al.

202

2020). By using this system, the cone catches based on bird vision are translated into

203

metrics of luminance (i.e. perceived lightness), saturation (i.e. the color richness), and

204

color, with the last being divided into different coordinates (X and Y for trichromatic

205

visual systems) that are broadly analogous to opponent color channels (i.e. X is

206

opponency between green and red variation, while Y between yellow and blue).

207

Therefore, we used the RNL color space to assess how close the spiders were to both

208

brown and green leaves of the vegetation where individuals were sampled. For this

209

procedure, we collected the spiders (N = 38) along with their corresponding substrates

210

(N = 38, the host brown leaf of each spider) and intact green leaves from the same host

211

plants (N = 38, one green leaf haphazardly selected from each spider host plant). In

9

212

addition, since spiders exhibit a considerable variation in brown tones, we also assessed

213

the spread of spiders and brown leaves in the color space by increasing our sample size

214

for both spiders (N = 68, additional spiders are those 30 used in the experiments - see

215

below) and brown leaves (N = 105, 38 from above together with 67 sampled leaves of

216

different brown tones). We performed the additional brown leaf sampling through

217

manual collection from shrubs and herbaceous plants along the same trail where the

218

spiders occur. To avoid sampling bias, when a plant bore multiple brown leaves, we

219

collected up to five leaves from different branches and haphazardly selected one.

220

We also modeled the ease with which an avian predator might discriminate

221

spiders from their host leaves under natural light conditions. For this, we used the

222

coordinates of the RNL space to calculate the chromatic (color vision mediated by

223

single cones) and achromatic (luminance mediated by the double cones) signal

224

discriminability as units of just-noticeable-differences (JNDs) (Vorobyev and Osorio

225

1998). The JND values are used to interpret the likelihood of an observer discriminating

226

two stimuli and indicate how good a background match is. Values lower than 1 predict

227

that one stimulus is virtually indistinguishable from the other by the receiver, indicating

228

better camouflage, whereas values higher than 1 are increasingly more likely to be

229

discriminable (Kelber et al. 2003; Siddiqi et al. 2004; Olsson et al. 2015). Using the

230

RNL model, we calculated color and luminance JNDs between (1) each spider (N = 38)

231

and its host leaf (i.e. the leaf where we found the spider, hereafter “local”) and (2) each

232

spider and color and luminance of all the leaves where we found the other spiders,

233

obtaining average color and luminance JNDs for each spider (hereafter “general”). We

234

predict that spiders should exhibit an individual-level camouflage, with a better match

235

(lower color contrasts) to local than to general backgrounds.

Behavioral choice trials

238

We designed decision arenas to test whether spiders actively select leaves that improve

239

their camouflage. Each arena consisted of a circular container (height = 76 mm,

240

diameter = 100 mm) with the internal surface covered half/half with green and brown

241

colored paper. We supplied each half with a fresh green or a dead brown leaf of the host

242

plant of each spider that we used for the experiment. We ensured that leaves were

243

similar in size and flattened to remove the effect of three-dimensionality on spider

244

choice. We released one spider in the center of each arena (N = 30) during the morning

245

and waited for 24 h to record the behavioral decisions of spiders after they had

246

established their orb-webs. We were interested in two major behavioral decisions: the

247

site where spiders set the hub of their webs and the host leaf on which spiders fix the

248

web guide thread (i.e. the line that departs from the web hub and connects the web to

249

the vegetation substrate). This approach allowed us to investigate where spiders choose

250

to settle their orb webs (foraging site) or to hide when under threat or not foraging (host

251

leaf) based on substrate coloration.

252
253

Color change trials

254

We used the same spiders from the behavioral choice trials to conduct a subsequent

255

experiment to test the ability of spiders to change color to improve their camouflage in

256

response to green and brown leaf substrates. For this, we constructed color-change

257

arenas very similar to the decision arenas but divided into two treatments (N = 15 green,

258

N = 15 brown). We supplied each green or brown arena with a leaf of the respective

259

color of the European blackberry (Rubus fruticosus), an abundant host plant of A.

260

diadematus in the study area. We photographed each individual and the leaf under

261

diffuse natural light conditions on day 0 and repeated this procedure on days 7 and 14 to

11

262

analyze any color change process. We maintained spiders under the same laboratory

263

conditions as the behavioral choice trials, and spider survival was 100% during all the

264

experiments. We inspected the arenas daily to ensure that the green leaves maintained

265

their coloration, replacing leaves showing any color change with freshly collected ones.

Statistics and reproducibility

268

To evaluate individual-level background matching (local versus general), we used linear

269

mixed-effects models to test for differences in color and luminance JNDs between

270

spiders and leaves. Each model included background type (local or general) as a fixed

271

factor and “spider identity” as a random factor to control for repeated measurements on

272

the same individual, as we compared each spider with the two backgrounds. Similarly,

273

mixed-effects models were used to test for changes in spider body color (i.e. the X

274

coordinate of the RNL color space, since it indicates green-red changes) and contrast

275

against backgrounds (i.e. chromatic JND) over time, with “day” (0, 7 and 14) and

276

“substrate” (brown or green) as fixed factors and “spider identity” as a random factor to

277

control for repeated measurements made on the same individual over time. All models

278

were fitted using the ‘lmer’ function in the ‘lme4’ package (Bates et al. 2015) and the

279

associated significance tests were performed through the ‘anova’ function in the

280

‘lmerTest’ package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017). We visually checked model residuals for

281

normal error distribution using histograms and q-q plots and tested the homogeneity of

282

variances using the Levene test in R. We applied Tukey’s post hoc test when the effects

283

were significant to compare mean differences between factor levels using the

284

‘emmeans’ function from the ‘emmeans’ package (Lenth 2019). Finally, for the

285

experiment of habitat selection, we analysed the two behavioral decisions separately

286

(selection of host leaves and web hub position) using exact binomial tests (‘binom.test’

12

287

function from ‘stats’ package), which considered the total sample size and the number

288

of successful choices (brown spider – brown rest positions). All analyses were

289

conducted in the software R v. 4.4.0 (R Core Team 2024). The supporting data

290

containing the original data and the scripts for all analyses are available in the

291

Supplementary Material.

RESULTS

294

Individual-level camouflage against dead-brown vegetation

295

We found that spider coloration (mean ± SD = 6.60 ± 1.34), expressed as the green-red

296

channel of the RNL color space, greatly overlapped with the color of the brown

297

vegetation (5.36 ± 1.49) but differed significantly from the color of green leaves (-0.79

298

± 1.24; Fig. 2A). In addition, spider coloration followed the same variation in the brown

299

hues observed for brown leaves (from pale to reddish and dark), with the data points for

300

both spiders and leaves exhibiting very similar spread across the color space (Fig. 2B).

301

Furthermore, spiders showed an individual-specific color match to their host leaves

302

(F1,37 = 17.69, p < 0.001), as color contrasts of spiders against local leaves (mean ± SE

303

= 2.59 ± 0.27) were lower than color contrasts against general leaves (3.55 ± 0.19) (Fig.

304

3). Luminance JNDs were also lower when comparing spiders to local (7.32 ± 0.80)

305

than to general (9.15 ± 0.30) backgrounds (Fig. 3).

306
307

308

The site where spiders established the hub of their webs was independent of substrate

309

coloration (Exact binomial test: proportion = 0.60, n = 30, p = 0.36), but spiders showed

310

a strong behavioral preference for brown host leaves over green ones (Fig. 4; Exact

311

binomial test: proportion = 0.93, n = 30, p < 0.001). Conversely, neither spider

312

coloration (substrate x days: F2,53.7 = 0.61, p = 0.547) nor color contrast

313

between spiders and leaves (substrate x days: F2,53.31 = 1.86, p = 0.1655)

314

changed against either substrate type, showing that there was no detectable color change

315

DISCUSSION

318

Our results demonstrate that spiders exhibit an active behavioral choice for brown

319

leaves of herbaceous vegetation, which confers improved camouflage and a higher

320

probability of concealing to visually-orientated avian predators. Even though spiders

321

may be unable to change their body coloration over two weeks, we show that a fine-

322

scale camouflage on an individual level is an important mechanism of chromatic

323

matching that reduces the expected probability of visual detection. When we gave

324

spiders a choice between a brown and a green leaf for each trial, spiders were more

325

likely to choose as camouflage sites (i.e. where the spider will hide when not foraging

326

or under threat) brown than green host leaves. However, the foraging site (i.e. web hub)

327

is independent of substrate coloration. Overall, our study demonstrates that garden

328

spiders are variable in coloration and well camouflaged to brown leaves, with matches

329

refined at an individual level, and that this is probably driven by behavioral choice of

330

substrates rather than color change.

331

The experiments here add to the growing body of research showing that many

332

animals are capable of selecting substrates that facilitate concealment, on both a general

333

species and individual level (Stevens and Ruxton 2019). For example, it is now known

334

that animals from taxa as diverse as moths (Skelhorn and Ruxton 2012), birds (Stevens

335

et al. 2017), lizards (Marshall et al. 2016), grasshoppers (Heinze et al. 2022), fish

336

(Kjernsmo and Merilaita 2012), crustaceans (Green et al. 2019; Twort and Stevens

14

337

2023; Dyer and Stevens 2024) and more besides are capable of such choices. In many

338

regards this should be expected; environments are constantly changing in space and

339

time, even before human interferences, imposing selection on mechanisms that enable

340

effective concealment to be maintained. Crypsis is ineffective the moment an animal

341

rests on a background against which its color pattern has not evolved to match, making

342

behavioral choice a valuable mechanism to maintain concealment. In many regards,

343

some of the most important missing information pertains to the sensory mechanisms

344

that enable choice. Here there are many ideas, but few have been well tested (Stevens

345

and Ruxton 2019). For example, an animal may have a genetic link between its

346

coloration and substrate preference, it may imprint at key life stages on priority

347

substrates or learn about appearance (Alothyqi et al. 2024), or it may be physically able

348

to see how closely its appearance matches the environment. Furthermore, while

349

camouflage here is in a visual sense, the actual choice of substrate may be guided by

350

other modalities, such as tactile cues, as in some moths (Kang et al. 2014, 2015). In

351

garden spiders, individuals may use visual cues as light intensity to orientate their webs

352

(Crawford 1984) and change coloration (Blanke and Marklinger 1983), but their vision

353

is relatively rudimentary, and it is unlikely that they can compare their own appearance

354

to the substrate in a fine scale. Rather, we think it is more likely that they have a genetic

355

preference for certain backgrounds that may be guided by visual discrimination of

356

substrate brightness (brown leaves being darker than green ones), tactile cues (green

357

leaves being smoother than brown ones), or chemical cues. However, this needs further

358

investigation, alongside the question of to what extent individual spiders of varied

359

appearance are able to make choices.

360

We cannot rule out the ability of spiders to change color, since it is possible that

361

they may be able to modify their appearance over timescales longer than we tested here.

15

362

For example, over an entire developmental sequence, spiders clearly change from the

363

pale yellow-brown appearance characteristic of spiderlings to the darker brown

364

patterning characteristic of adults. Plastic changes over longer time periods were indeed

365

observed for A. diadematus according to differences in the environment light intensity

366

(through the degradation of guanine crystals, see Oxford & Gillespie 1998), with the

367

effectiveness of color change varying drastically among individuals (Blanke &

368

Merklinger 1983). However, this may also reflect fixed ontogenetic changes in

369

appearance with size/age (Booth 1990) rather than only plastic changes in response to

370

the environment. Such change in coloration must especially occur between moults, and

371

this may enable some degree of background matching refinement. If so, this may be

372

broadly analogous to the observed for some crustaceans, such as crabs, which undergo

373

ontogenetic changes in appearance with a degree of reversable plasticity over a period

374

of several weeks (Carter et al. 2020; Nokelainen et al. 2019). Indeed, color change is

375

widely known to occur in some spiders. For example, crab spiders have the ability to

376

transit from white to yellow (morphological color change) to camouflage themselves on

377

yellow flowers, whereas some orb-web spiders undergo rapid (physiological) color

378

changes resulting in the appearance of white spots for interspecific communication

379

(Figon and Casas 2018).

380

Even if garden spiders can change color, the likelihood is that this operates in

381

combination with behavioral choice, rather than instead of it, as is also true in crabs

382

(Twort and Stevens 2023) and prawns (Green et al. 2024). Broadly, these two

383

approaches to facilitate camouflage can play different roles depending on the spatial and

384

temporal characteristics of the environment. For example, in some prawns, slow color

385

change likely enables matching to seasonal changes in seaweed colors, whereas

386

behavioral choice enables short term matching to local variations in the habitat (Green

16

387

et al. 2019). If spiders are unable to show rapid plasticity in coloration, then this also

388

opens questions regarding the genetic basis and driving factors behind the existing

389

intraspecific diversity, such as matching different areas of the environment, interference

390

of predator search behavior and cognition (e.g. hindering predator search images;

391

Troscianko et al. 2018, 2021). It is also essential to consider the dual function of

392

camouflage (see Pembury Smith and Ruxton 2020), as orb-web spiders are

393

simultaneously prey and predators of several visually-oriented animals. In this sense,

394

the ambush-predation strategy of hiding in vegetation and relying on vibrations caused

395

by trapped insects in their webs may maximize prey capture and represent another

396

driver for A. diadematus camouflage to be explored in further investigations.

397

Research into spider camouflage is a promising avenue for understanding the

398

adaptative values underlying animal coloration. Studies on spider coloration encompass

399

either descriptive information (e.g. Lim and Li 2007), investigation of mechanisms of

400

color production (e.g. Taylor and McGraw 2007; Insausti and Casas 2008) and the eco-

401

evolutionary functions of color (e.g. Taylor and McGraw 2013, Rao and Mendoza-

402

Cuenca 2016; Robledo-Ospina et al. 2017; Ximenes and Gawryszewski 2019, 2020;

403

Ximenes et al. 2020; Messas et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2022 b). These

404

arachnids inhabit virtually all terrestrial environments and display an impressive

405

diversity of colors and patterns that vary intra- and interspecifically. Potential model-

406

species include spiders that live in close association with specific vegetational structures

407

(e.g. bark, branches, flowers, and leaves) that vary in complexity, structure, and

408

coloration (see Rodríguez-Morales et al. 2018). Understanding how spiders deal with

409

natural and human stressors to maintain camouflage and concealment success is also a

410

promising research field. This includes active habitat selection, as shown here, and other

411

behavioral (e.g. posture and body alignment) and intrinsic (e.g. physiological and

17

412

genetic) components. Many spider species are sexually dimorphic or polymorphic,

413

providing valuable models as each sex or morphotype can potentially combine

414

particular strategies for camouflage. Moreover, coloration patterns of spiders are highly

415

diverse and dependent on many extrinsic biotic and abiotic factors that vary over time

416

and space, such as habitat composition, diversity and abundance of predators or prey,

417

and lighting. These variables may result in different processes and mechanisms

418

determining the adaptive success of camouflaged spiders. We expect that further

419

research on spider camouflage explore modern analytical methods, such as multispectral

420

photography, software and packages for image analysis and vision modeling, to test

421

strength hypotheses combining ecological, behavioral, and evolutionary approaches.
