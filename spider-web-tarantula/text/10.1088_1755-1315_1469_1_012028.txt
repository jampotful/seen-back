Abstract. Organic rice farming has been widely adopted. The study aims to
analyze the arthropod communities and their roles in organic and non-organic
rice. The study was conducted in Subang Regency, West Java, Indonesia, from 2016
to 2017. The methods used to observe arthropod populations were visual
counting on rice. Arthropod diversity was analyzed using the Shannon index, the
evenness using Pielou. The most abundant predator population found in organic
and non-organic rice was spiders. The population of Cyrtorhinus, Paederus and
Coccinela were relatively low in organic and non-organic rice. The arthropods that
dominated organic and non-organic rice plantations in the dry season were
Hemiptera, Coleoptera, Diptera, and Hymenoptera. Rice pests were brown
planthoppers, whiteback leafhoppers, and rice stem borers. The arthropods’
diversity in organic and non-organic rice plantations in the dry season was in the
low category based on the calculation of the Shannon index, which had the highest
value of 1.8. The Arthropods in organic and non-organic rice included Hemiptera,
Coleoptera, Collembola, and Araneidae, with 32 species. The dominant insect
pests were brown planthoppers, whiteback leafhoppers, and rice stem borers.

Introduction

Rice production plays an important part in the world’s food supply for world population growth.
However, the dominant focus on chemical inputs, such as synthetic fertilizers and pesticides, and
intensive farming practices has raised concerns about the long-term sustainability of rice
ecosystems [1]. While these conventional farming methods has successfully increased
productivity, they have also lead to negative consequences for the environment, including the
decline of biodiversity in agroecosystems The use of synthetic pesticides and fertilizers have
demonstrated its negative impacts on ecosystem biodiversity. such as the loss of arthropod
population. The approach taken is to maintain biodiversity in the agroecosystem of paddy fields
to improve natural pest control. Arthropods, including predators, parasitoids, and pest, play a
vital role in rice fields by regulating pest populations through predator-prey interactions.
Understanding the composition and dynamics of arthropods communities under different
farming practices is crucial for assessing the ecological impacts of these methods and for
developing sustainable pest management strategies.

Organic farming has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional practices, offering
the potential to enhance rice production while addressing environmental and health issues [2].
One of the main advantages of organic farming in rice agro-ecosystem is its ability to enhance the
diversity and abundance of beneficial insect. Organic inputs, such as compost and natural fertilizers, had
shown to support the population of arthropod predators and parasitoids., which are effective in
controlling major rice pests such as brown plant hoppers (Nilaparvata lugens), rice stem borers
(Scirpophaga incertulas), and leaf folders (Cnaphalocrosis medinalis)[3]. In contrary, heavy
reliance on synthetic pesticides in conventional rice production can disrupt the natural balance
of arthropods communities [1]. The organic and non-organic farming methods have different
approaches to pest management and soil fertility, which have an effect on the diversity and
composition of arthropod communities.
The differences between organic and non-organic farming in terms of pest management
strategies and overall ecosystem functioning can have significant effects on the diversity,
abundance, and composition of arthropod communities The objective of the study was to analyze
the arthropod communities focusing on their composition, diversity and their roles in organic
and non-organic rice. The findings will contribute to a better understanding of how farming
practices influence arthropod dynamics and will provide insights to promote sustainable rice
production systems.
2. Material and Methods
The study was out conducted on rice plantations during two rice planting seasons, the 2016
Planting Season and the 2016/2017 Planting Season, in Subang Regency. The research was
carried out in farmers' certified organic rice fields and farmers' certified organic and non-organic
rice fields. Samplings were conducted from April 2016 to January 2017. Five plots of 100 m x 100
m were randomly selected for each sampling. Observations were made on 20 rice hills per
treatment plot. Rice insect pests and natural enemies, predators, and parasitoids were collected
from each plot of rice paddy field at 14-day intervals from planting until harvesting. The insects
and spiders were placed in 70% alcohol for subsequent analysis.

3. Results and Discussion
Rice field ecosystems include various groups of interacting communities. The soil arthropod
community plays an important role in the ecosystem. Various types of arthropods, from various
orders and families, have their respective functions in the food web in the agroecosystem of rice
fields. Not all insects can be classified as pests or harmful to humans; some are beneficial insects
to our environment, such as those that act as natural enemies (parasitoids and predators),
pollinating insects, and decomposers. Meanwhile, the role of neutral insects as prey for predators
is a significant factor in maintaining the balanced the rice ecosystem. [4]. A study conducted in
2016 at the locations of both organic and non-organic rice fields in Subang District during both
the dry and wet seasons, revealed a diverse range of arthropods. A total of 32 species belonging
to nine orders of arthropods were identified.

previous planting season, with a peak population at the previous planting season, a peak
population at the previous planting season, and a peak population at the flowering stage. The
brown planthopper population in organic rice increased dramatically at the flowering stage,
reaching the highest peak. The population of Cyrtorhinus as a specific predator of brown
planthoppers also increased at the flowering stage. There is a clear predator-prey relationship,
where an increase in the brown planthopper population is followed by an increase in the
Cyrtorhinus population. After the peak of the brown planthopper population, the Cyrtorhinus
population remained high as the planthopper population began to decline, suggesting a control
effect by the predator. Organic rice showed greater potential for natural control or ecological
services. In non-organic rice, brown planthopper populations remained low at all rice stages.
Cyrtorhinus populations also remained low and stable throughout the rice stages. Non-organic
rice showed more stable but low populations, probably due to pesticide use that suppressed
populations of both species. Non-organic rice showed a controlled population but may be less
beneficial to the natural balance of the ecosystem's natural balance.

on non-organic systems that reduce the population of natural enemies so that pests can thrive
better.
The evenness index also tends to be higher in rice organic in all rice stages, ranged 0.16 to
0.36. It indicated relatively low evenness in the distribution of individuals among species within
the arthropod’s population. A study showed that organic farming systems tend to have higher
species diversity and evenness than non-organic systems [7]. This can be due to more
environmentally friendly cultivation practices in organic systems, such as not using synthetic
pesticides, as well as more diverse habitats supporting various species. However, individual
abundance is higher in non-organic systems, which can be attributed to the better growth of
certain pests due to reduced natural enemies from synthetic pesticides.

This means that one or few species dominate the community, which is common, especially in food

brown planthoppers then occurred in February 2017 or the following season in the Subang

Organic farming methods, which rely on natural pest control and soil enrichment through
compost and other organic materials, have been shown to promote biodiversity and ecosystem
services in rice ecosystems. A study [9] showed that organic rice fields had higher species richness
and evenness of arthropods compared to non-organic fields, with a greater proportion of
predators and parasitoids in the organic fields. This suggests that organic farming practices can
create a more balanced ecosystem with a greater variety of beneficial insects. In contrast, nonorganic farming methods often rely on chemical pesticides and fertilizers, which can on negatively
impact arthropod populations. In a non-organic rice field had lower species richness and
evenness of arthropods, with a dominance of herbivorous insects. This imbalance can lead to a
loss of ecosystem services and increased pest pressure. The use of organic inputs in rice fields has
been shown to have a positive impact on soil health and quality [10] [11], microbiome diversity,
and overall ecosystem functioning [12]. Providing organic matter into the soil would improve soil
properties (physical, chemical and soil biology) and will stimulate the activities of soil flora and
fauna. In soil biology, compost can increase the population of microorganisms in the soil because
the composted straw is used as an energy source so that the decomposition process of organic
materials in the soil increases [13]. The need for a more holistic management approach in organic
farming, especially with pest pressure during favourable environmental conditions for brown
planthoppers breeding. Strategies such as increasing habitat diversity, strengthening natural
enemy populations, and using pest-resistant varieties may be necessary to improve ecosystem
evenness and stability, and reduce the risk of severe pest infestations.
4. Conclusion
This study demonstrates differences in arthropod communities between organic and non-organic
rice cultivation systems in Subang Regency, West Java Provinces, Indonesia. The findings reveal
that 32 species identified across nine orders (Hemiptera, Lepidoptera, Coleoptera, Diptera,
Hymenoptera, Collembola, Araneidae, Thysanoptera, Orthoptera). The Shannon diversity index
calculation indicated relatively low diversity in organic and non-organic rice, with the highest
value reached 1.8. The study revealed distinct predator-prey dynamics, particularly between
brown planthoppers and their natural predator Cyrtorhinus. Organic rice showed more natural
and dynamic predator-prey relationship, reflecting more natural and balanced ecosystem. While
organic fields occasionally had higher pest populations, especially during the wet season, they
also supported larger population of natural enemies. In contrast, non-organic rice fields showed
more stable but generally lower populations of both pests and predators, probably due to
pesticide use. The dominant insect pests that attack rice plantations are brown leafhoppers,
white-backed leafhoppers, and rice stem borers. Spider populations, become a crucial natural
predator, their population closely following the pest abundance. Organic rice farming promotes
greater biodiversity and natural pest control, meanwhile non-organic rice shows a controlled
