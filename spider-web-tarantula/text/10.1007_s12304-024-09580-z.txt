Abstract
Starting from Jakob von Uexküll’s theory of meaning, the article explores the semiotic functions of the spider’s web, examining in depth its material characteristics
and relationship to communication. This study reinterprets the biologist’s concepts,
highlighting the vibration of webs as a mode of interspecific communication. By
inquiring into the physical composition of spider webs, the research proposes artistic performances that seek to extend material vibration by exploring subjective
experience. Thus, a performance-based biosemiotic and materialist approach is
proposed to recreate the umwelt of the spider, bridging the human and arachnid
realms through minimalist constructions by focusing on vibrational communication
processes. In conclusion, the intricate interplay between material manifestations and
subjective interpretations in the spider’s ecological niche will be outlined.
Keywords Material semiotics · Spider web · Umwelt theory · Vibrational
communication

Introduction
In the article “The Theory of Meaning” republished by the journal Semiotica in 1982,
Jakob von Uexküll devotes a chapter to the spider entitled “The interpretation of the
spider-web”. The analyses offered in this chapter start from observations regarding
certain structural features of the web, such as the diameter of the threads and their
strength, which is determined by the relationship to the prey. The idea is that the resistance of the threads is related to the strength and speed of the fly, just as the thickness
is defined based on the visual characteristics of the insect-prey to be invisible.

The focus of the chapter innovatively addresses the relationship the spider has
with its environment and prey. This involves an analysis of the semiotic processes
that stand out along the species-specific characteristics of the spider in a given environment. According to Uexküll’s umwelt theory analysis, it is possible to identify the
emergence of meaning in the animal world where environmental signs and animal
interpretations meet in a circularity of meaning (Zengiaro, 2023b). The umwelt theory is based on the analysis of the functional circles of a given animal, in which from
an environment constellated with relevant signs– the perceptual marks–, responses
are activated by the animal’s capacity and qualities– the operational marks.
This article presents an investigation into the material manifestations of meaningmaking, focusing on modelling the analysis of the umwelt in the context of arachnid
species and the physical composition of their webs. Unlike the elaboration of umwelt
theory or Uexküll’s (1982) arguments, the study offers a reinterpretation of his concepts, emphasising the materiality of webs as a form of interspecific communication.
The research aims to identify the structural conditions that facilitate spiders’ ability to
perceive and interpret their environment. In particular, it is proposed to demonstrate
that the web operates as an extended cognitive apparatus, expanding the boundaries
of the spider’s sensory perception and subjective experience. The spider’s web functions as an extended cognitive apparatus, enhancing its sensory perception and subjective experience without resorting to anthropomorphic interpretation. This means
that, without activating a sort of intentionality attribution of cognitive processes, the
web acts as a sensory extension, transmitting vibrations that inform the spider about
its environment, thus amplifying its ability to detect and respond to stimuli. This
increases the spatial awareness of the spider and its range of action. Spiders also
modify their web-structure strategies based on experience and environmental conditions, reflecting a species-specific form of learning and memory. This is in line
with the theory of extended cognition, according to which cognitive processes extend
beyond the brain to include external tools that support the spider’s cognitive functions and enrich its environmental interactions in a manner consistent with its natural
behaviour and ecological niche. In essence, the spider’s web acts as an externalised
organ, providing precise environmental information through its material structure,
which the spider can then decode. This implies that communication between the spider and its environment occurs through an informational filter, while maintaining
material-structural autonomy for both entities involved.
In this paper we will attempt to provide an overview of the potential for empirical exploration of the intricate subjective realm of spiders. Instead of approaching
the topic only from a theoretical point of view, we propose along the article a performance directed at clarifying the discourse from a material perspective. The idea
is as follows: how can we construct a basic reproduction of the spider’s umwelt?
This performance aims to reveal both the interaction between different umwelten–
the subjective worlds of humans and spiders– and the material processes underlying communication through webs and vibration as a material quality. The proposed
approach to understanding a universe so different from our existence involves performance art centered on a minimalist material construction. In this way, we will attempt
to replicate the signification processes of living beings through their material webs.

The Symphony of Spiders: Exploring Uexküll’s Metaphor
Jakob von Uexküll, in elucidating the intricate relationship between organisms and
their environments, employs the metaphor of a symphony to illustrate the dynamic
interplay of interspecific adaptations within ecosystems. This metaphor provides a
compelling lens through which to examine the semiotic processes underlying the
subjective world of arachnids, particularly in the case of spiders. In contemporary
ethology, one can see how for the spider world the concept of vibration is central
(Tarsitano et al., 2000). Despite their lack of sight, spiders adeptly navigate their
environment by interpreting vibrations, using their webs as specialized organic
instruments for sensing seismic signals. In this context, Uexküll’s metaphor of the
symphony takes on profound significance: in the same way that the symphony is perceived through the recognition of patterns and sensations in sound vibrations, so too
does the spider perceive its world through vibrational interpretations of environmental signals. Indeed, there exists a striking parallel between the perceptual mechanisms
involved in appreciating a symphony and those governing the spider’s interaction
with its surroundings through vibrations. This analogy between sound and vibration
in the natural world is a focal point of inquiry within biotremology (Hill et al., 2019),
a subdiscipline of bioacoustics dedicated to studying animal communication through
the analysis of vibrational phenomena.
Uexküll’s attempt to indicate the balancing relationships between the living and
its surroundings has often been taken to its extreme consequences, where a kind
of perfect symphony is theorized between the animal and the umwelt, as well as
between different species. This categorization of nature inherent in the musical metaphor draws on the idea of a symphony that can be understood as a basic semiotic
process in nature. Uexküll’s non-Darwinism evolution (Kull, 2004) ends up being a
kind of “bizarre creationism” (Stjernfelt, 2007: 275) in which the biological world
is created by a “composer”. Such a metaphor comes from Ernst von Baer’s theories
of biological lifespan and biological correspondence. The use of musical metaphors
was quite frequent in the biological context (Kull, 1999). This view inspired the general theory of Bedeutungslehre and is already found partially present in Theoretische
Biologie. In Uexküll this idea of perfect adherence is found in the functional circle,
through which the animal world is constituted in a perfect correspondence between
Merkmals and Wirkmals.
The melody, in reference to the metaphor of life, succeeds in constructing the
idea of an organized structure disconnected from the here-and-now of the physical
world, coordinating a correspondence in which the last note is already in the tune of
the first, delineating a teleological circle. Merleau-Ponty had already observed that
such a metaphor allows one to observe the life of the individual organism as a kind
of realization, in a continuous variation of the theme. This allows one not necessarily
to use an external vitalistic view, but rather structurally can offer the conditions of
possibility for the modification of the animal’s system of functional circles acting as

Improvisation
Here, the lucky possibilities of Uexküll’s melody metaphor goes beyond his own
static perfections intentions: music may be perfect, but it is far from always the case.
Sometimes only dissonant tuning of the instruments may be heard, in other cases
false breaks during a melody or more or less successful new variations on a theme; in
any case, evolution as a whole might be a symphony, but only if it is one of continuous improvisation (Stjernfelt, 2007: 283).
Thus, it seems that even in Stjernfelt’s analysis, nature may be a symphony, but not
as perfect as a score can be. The idea is that the different relationships among living
things are constituted by continuous improvisation without composers or conductors.
Moreover, the continuous performance translation and invention of new melodies,
harmonies and scales bring a new perspective on the analysis of semiotics toward the
relationship of the living. Musical metaphor, its interpretive and translational referencing, gives biology relevance concerning the notion of meaning in interspecific and
individual relationships. The living becomes thus a kind of “open symphony” with
modular composition (Brentari, 2015: 163), in which individual performances come
from predefined meanings at the outset, what Uexküll (2010) calls “formative melodies”, in which they «take their motifs from the formative melodies of other subjects
which they will encounter on their life’s stage» (von Uexküll, 2010: 198).
The fundamental concept of a living organism’s interaction with its environment,
known as the umwelt, was elucidated through the Ich-Ton principle by Jakob von
Uexküll. This principle encapsulates an internal code or set of rules governing an
organism’s acceptance or rejection of external stimuli. Uexküll proposed that organisms operate according to an intrinsic “score”, guiding their perception and actions, a
concept he termed Merken und Wirken. Drawing parallels to music, one could envision each composer and musical piece possessing a unique Me-Tone, determining
which musical signals are assimilated into the environment and musical tradition, and
which are disregarded. The application of biosemiotics further extends this discourse,
particularly in exploring the metaphor of the musical organism and the concept of
music’s organic nature. As Tarasti (1996) suggests, the exploration of the organic
qualities of music provokes intriguing research in the field of biosemiotics. This perspective invites examination of how musical structures, compositions, and traditions
evolve and adapt, like living organisms interacting with their ecological niches. By
looking at music through the lens of organicism, researchers can explore the intricate dynamics between composers, musical works, performers and cultural contexts,
shedding light on the symbiotic relationship between music and its environment.

Limits of Interpretation
The discourse surrounding musical metaphor leads us to examine the structural
coupling between the vibrations of the spider’s web and the animal’s interpretation
thereof. Uexküll, in his exploration of the relationship between organism and environment, reinterprets the environment as a product of the animal subject. However,
this interpretation appears limited, as we aim to demonstrate that the spider and its
web are fundamentally interconnected. In fact, unlike Uexküll, who had a static idea
of the environment, today we know that the environment is constantly changing, as
one can also see from the ecological and climate crisis (Tønnessen, 2009, 2024). «To
inhabit is to refuse any supremacy of the subject or Idea, to posit nature as the site of
a vast and diverse set of processes of experimentation, to encounter and to express,
to be implicated in the transitory, partial organisation of intensive signs: to take part
in a semiorhythmology» (Campbell, 2019: 367). Moreover, thanks to ecosemiotics one can account for a distributed agency, which also concerns a genuine agency
of the environment acting with its own force on individuals in a mutual exchange
(Zengiaro, 2023a). Deleuze and Guattari (2004) further illuminate this interconnectedness, suggesting that the spider web contains sequences of the fly’s code within the
spider’s own code, implying a complex interplay between predator and prey. This
notion of transcodings, as elucidated by Uexküll, portrays nature as a symphony of
melodies in counterpoint, each motif giving rise to another, thus constituting new
planes of existence.
It has often been noted that the spider web implies that there are sequences of
the fly’s own code in the spider’s code; it is as though the spider had a fly in
its head, a fly “motif”, a fly “refrain”. [:::] Jakob von Uexküll has elaborated
an admirable theory of transcodings. He sees the components as melodies in
counterpoint, each of which serves as a motif for another: Nature as music.
Whenever there is transcoding, we can be sure that there is not a simple addition, but the constitution of a new plane, as a surplus value (Deleuze & Guattari,
2004: 346).
The spider is not simply the complex composition of its parts, including the glands
capable of secreting silk, but is the web itself and also its prey. It is the whole of the
web and its prey, its species-specific endowments and the environment that surrounds
and encompasses it. We could say that every animal is an “opera aperta” (sensu Eco,
1962), where internal and external relational intensities characterize organisms without clear boundaries. All organisms are an “opera aperta” in the Echian sense of the
term, where they appear as a kind of author whose bodily work, made up of organic
and inorganic, internal and external materials, must be continually reorganized by the
users, who are continually challenged to make interpretive choices. The emergence
of meaning between organism and environment therefore is structured through the
specialization of internal and spatial functions which intersect and produce a relationship that is not simply the totalizing sum of their encodings, but rather a new assemblage that combines elements of these codes in novel forms.

From an ecosystemic perspective, the differential layers that separate entities and
interpretive planes are perceived as porous thresholds between the animal and its
surroundings. Each singularity within the ecosystem represents a fusion of organs,
hybridizing through varying degrees of intimacy. This perspective extends to encompass elements traditionally viewed as external to the organism, such as the wind,
which becomes an integral part of the spider through its interaction with the vibrant
web. Therefore, the resilience of the spider’s web against the wind must be understood
as a quality attributed to the spider in relation to its environment. This is particularly
pertinent given the diverse composition of spider silk, which can vary significantly
across species, with each type produced by distinct glands in the abdomen. Such
considerations drive an investigation of the boundaries of techniques within an ecosystem framework. Where does the chain of nonlinear causality underlying spider
techniques conclude? To what extent does the relational capacity of web properties
extend within a complex environment? How expansive is the umwelt of the spider? These questions underscore the need for a holistic understanding of organisms
within their ecological contexts, emphasizing living systems’ interconnectedness and
dynamic nature.

The Intelligence of the Web
Spiders, varying from species to species, possess three to seven types of glands in
their abdomen, which are used to produce different types of silk. The spider’s use
and application of silk has served along its evolution and colonization of landforms,
some 400 million years ago, to reinforce its shelters as well as to protect its offspring,
in addition to mere hunting and movement. The common spiral web, characteristic
of the Araneidae family, dates back 200 million years, while those predating it are
hypothesized to have had multiple forms other than the verticalized ones. Evolutionary change in spiders was largely driven by a series of small changes at the molecular
level of silk that allowed them to build functional and complex structures, ensuring
their survival in increasingly diverse environments (Pennisi, 2017).

The toughness, strength and viscosity of the web, are attracting the attention of scientific research about the properties of the material (Gosline et al., 1999). The most
relevant ability, in fact, seems to be that of responding to stimuli by adapting its characteristics according to environmental conditions. Silk transforms its structure from
liquid to solid in a very short time, depending on the speed of the stimulus to which
it is subjected. Thus, the free-falling spider as well as an insect that at high-speed
runs into the threads, the material rapidly restructures due to the phenomenon of
“hysteresis”. The notion of hysteresis, in physics but also in biology and economics,
refers to a phenomenon of inheritance in which the instantaneous value is determined
by a value preceding it instantaneously. When applied to materials, this refers to the
behavior of matter to change its response to a stimulus based on its previous history.
This can be understood as a memory effect of the material in which, the moment it
is subjected to tension, the silk responds by reorganizing its molecular structure to
dissipate as much energy as possible (Tripaldi, 2022).
The intelligence of this material is determined not only by the composition of
the structure, but also by the ability of the spider to interpret information using it
as an extended sense organ. Material capabilities are based on a form of material
intelligence and structural directionality. Intelligence, after all, is a decentralized and
diffuse phenomenon that emerges from the way different bodies relate to each other.
Because of its structure, thickness, and tension, silk transmits and amplifies vibrations through its filaments. There is no intentional patterning in these process of natural assembly (even if there were, it would be a design entirely internal to the physical
system that produces it), but it is a process that reveals meaning through spontaneity.
In scientific terms, spontaneity is related to the way a physical phenomenon manages
its energy balance in relation to its environment. Spontaneous transformation of the
system occurs without necessarily referring to an external force acting on it.
The spider, depending on the situation it is in (from the relationship with food,
enemy, partner or medium, i.e., through interpretation of the environment), focuses its
attention on the most sensitive areas (Viera & Gonzaga, 2017). Thus, it is possible to
demonstrate that the web is an extension of the spider’s mind, through which it communicates and interprets information. The spider alters its perception of the world by
altering the web (shape and tension), both from the point of view of chemistry structure and design, but at the same time the silk, as a complex and sensitive material,
influences how the spider perceives the world and acts in its environment (Yarger et
al., 2018). In general, the most significant emergent property of a complex system is
its very existence as a system, that is, its ability to keep its relational structure intact,
without which it would fall back into a disorganized state. This capacity of materials, in relation to living beings, derives from the phenomenon of self-organization of
complex structures in a certain environment. A system capable of self-organization is
able to increase its level of internal order spontaneously, restoring its structure within
certain limits even when it has been changed by an external force. For example,
spider silk proteins to construct spider silk proteins to construct, transform and reconstruct their hierarchical structure in a self-organizing manner. It is in such a complex
relationship that the continuous influence between the animal mind and the material
mind emerges. Similarly, semiotics investigates our cultural relationship with materi-

Spider silk undergoes significant structural changes when exposed to water or
ambient humidity, shortening and substantially increasing in diameter, a phenomenon known as supercontraction. This results in the stiffening of the fibers, likely
necessary to support the weight of raindrops or dew, and may also function as a selfrepair mechanism. When water is absorbed, the protein chains in the silk become
misaligned but return to their orderly fibrous structure upon drying. This process
facilitates the formation of new physicochemical interactions, regenerating damaged
parts of the silk. The self-regenerative capability of spider silk is a remarkable trait,
inspiring research into new self-healing synthetic materials (Tripaldi, 2022: 32), and
demonstrating its responsiveness to environmental stimuli.
Vibration as Interspecific Performance
An example of the communicative modalities of spider web vibration was offered
by the research group “Arachnophilia”, coordinated by artist and architect Tomás
Saraceno. In fact, in the project, not only the structures of the design and planning
of the web are investigated, but also the sound it makes according to the situations
in which the spider is in its environment. The sound produced changes according
to the types of situations it faces, such as defense, regulation of territoriality about
other conspecifics, social exchanges, and signs of mimicry. The key characteristics
of vibration propagation are linked to the material properties of substrates (Mortimer,
2017; Mortimer et al., 2018; Hill et al., 2019; Cividini & Montesanto, 2020).
Saraceno’s project concerning the spider’s web is to try to make the vibrations
imperceptible by the human converse with the technological tools that amplify them
to make them audible, and therefore interpretable. Starting from the issue that each
umwelt is a world unto itself, that is, it is a subjective world, the attempt is to create
a web that connects the different umwelten through interspecific translation. In fact,
all umwelten are connected, «as if in a gigantic musical score» (Agamben, 2004:
40). In this sense he interprets the spider’s web properly as if it were an instrument
capable of communicating and reproducing specific meaning depending on its vibration. The interaction between the vibration of the web and the movements of the
spider becomes an opening toward possibilities of communication that crosses different species.
In 2014, Tomás Saraceno ventured into creating a musical dialogue between spiders and humans with his installation Cosmic Jive: The Spider Sessions at the Museo
di Arte Contemporanea di Villa Croce in Italy. This project built upon Saraceno’s
collaborative research with Roland Mühlethaler and Hannelore Hoch. The installation allowed audiences to encounter Saraceno’s unique multispecies “Hybrid Spider Web” sculptures and to understand these webs as musical instruments through
a sensorial exploration of their bioacoustic properties. Cosmic Jive: The Spider Sessions uses the vibratory signals of spiders as its core acoustic motif. These include
the vibrations produced in the web when the spider plucks, tunes, and repairs it;
tremors caused by events such as trapped prey, wind, or other mechanical interventions; and tremulations used by spiders to send conspecific signals to potential mates.

From a biosemiotic perspective– whose methodology can intertwine with physiosemiotic working hypotheses (Deely, 1990, 2001; Zengiaro, 2022)– life can be
thought of as both a process, which therefore favours continuous change and constant vibration, and a network, which integrates and interacts with non-life along
intersemiotic and ecosystemic channels. In these properties (an ongoing process and
composing a heterogeneous network) we can read life as a hybrid device forming a
reticular pattern of semiotic resonance. With the notion of “resonance” we refer to
Rene Thom’s (1974) theory, in which the notion of resonance is presented as a linear
oscillator subjected to an impulse of a frequency equal to its own frequency. Only
when an object finds its own correspondence does the resonance branch out into a
heterogeneous relationship of correspondences. In this sense, the model of the living
being is interpreted as the result of interacting regulatory mechanisms.
In these material embeddings that lead the theoretical enquiry to an epistemological search, we discern some proposals of neo-materialist philosophy that lead us
to understand natural phenomena as communicative phenomena. The new materialisms (Coole & Frost, 2010), or neo-materialisms (De Landa, 2012), properly derive
from the linguistic turn within deconstruction, reacting to the exclusive focus on
the human discursive plane, leading to a return to the materiality of the body which
produces its own discursive plane. The new materialisms operate collectively against
inert, extra-discursive and non-generative conceptions of matter, giving rise to a plurality of innovative methodological approaches.

Contemporary materialism in philosophy, but also in semiotics (Law, 2009), gives
us the opportunity to understand the physical dimension of humans and living things
in general by placing them in a signifying material continuum in which they do not
stand out as singularities. If we follow Timo Maran’s (2021: 524) definition of the
ecosemiosphere, one notices immediately the materialist direction that contemporary
ecosemiotic methodology is taking: «we can describe the ecosemiosphere as a semiotic system that comprises all species with their umwelts and the diverse semiotic
relations (including humans with their culture) that they have in the given ecosystem,
as well as the material support structures that enable the ecosemiosphere to thrive».
This allows us to understand how cognitive regimes (sensu Maturana and Varela), the
ontological positions that make such regimes possible, and the ways of dealing with
complex phenomena in nature all change in the same measure.
It is an issue that concerns the new applications of umwelt theory in semiotics
as well as in anthropology and biology, showing that there is a need not so much
to demonstrate that each organism lives in its own world-environment, but rather
to investigate how this relationship is inherently dynamic and open. The theory of
umwelt must be rethought from the critique regarding monadological closure and the
claim to a static nature. Saraceno’s performance, the umwelt theory and our proposal
for a materialist biosemiotics appear, as we articulate it, to be an effective tool for
understanding how ecological meanings are composed without automatically resorting to the particular human way of composing signifiers. An important feature is
the ecological materialisation as a complex, pluralistic and relatively open process,
on the one hand, and, on the other, the fact that human beings and biological life in
general are recognised as fully immersed in the productive contingencies of materiality. In contrast to some already articulated examples of contemporary biosemiotics,
Saraceno’s proposed investigation emphasises the productivity of matter in artistic
expression in order to understand the signifying relations of living and non-living
beings in an ecosystem, not privileging exclusively biological agency or what we call
(according to a certain paradigm) “living”.
If we follow Barad (2003), in her materialist discourse, one can assert that matter
is never a tabula rasa, nor does it passively await signification. Furthermore, matter
is not a support, a place, or a referent; it does not need the imprint of an external
force like culture to be complete. Matter is a process of materializing phenomena.
In this sense, materiality is discursive, reconfiguring boundaries in a mutual articulation through intra-action. Barad (2007) articulates an alternative to the closed ontology with which we are accustomed to thinking about things in the world. Without
intending to reinterpret this idea (thus betraying the author to whom we feel akin),
but without clinging too tightly to it (as it is difficult to conceptualize except through
metaphor and imagery), the notion of “intra-action” seeks to deactivate those defined
borders with which we typically categorize objects in the world and differentiate
them from a certain type of undifferentiated background. The deactivation of ontoepistemological borders reawakens a relational ontology based on indeterminacy,
used by the author as a performative force of nature. This concept is antithetical to
that of interaction, which presumes the existence of independent entities extracted
from the context in which they manifest. In plain terms, intra-action is the process by
which entities exist in their relational and inseparable being from a mutable, hybrid,

mestizo, queer context that makes them what they are. As we understand it, “intra”
refers to the impossibility of separating subject and object, as well as object and
object (unless by separating them through a Cartesian metaphysical fallacy) in a continuous mingling, interference, and simultaneous modification; “action” concerns the
processual activity in which everything is caught, thus becoming the cognitive process and the ontologically dynamic entity within this vortex constituted of material
and discursive mixtures.
It is important at this point those ecological elements (such as the spider’s web,
vibration, but also wind and the technological tools used by Saraceno), by the very
nature of their connections to the actions of living things, quickly move from the
status of intermediaries to that of mediators. In Bruno Latour’s (2005) conceptualization, intermediaries are characterized as socio-technical agents that merely convey
information, while mediators possess the capacity to not only convey but also to alter,
translate, distort, and thereby shape the meaning of the information they transmit.
Intermediaries refers to that which carries meaning or force without transformations,
so that once the input is defined, the output is also known. Conversely, a mediator
transforms what it carries (meaning, force, or anything else), its output cannot be
entirely predicted based on the input. This makes it possible to use biosemiotics as a
tool for revealing actions and material performance.
Performing the Umwelt: Spiders Whispers
The idea of trying to empirically interpret the umwelt of the spider seeks to represent
an artistically performed model with basic material elements. The encounter with
the heterospecific moves along two precise coordinates: the recognition of similarity
(biological and phylogenetic unity)– a feeling amplified by the extraordinary capacities of biosemiosis– and then the recognition of diversity. In order for the encounter
to be meaningful and at the same time translative (and transformative), the human
must be able to project himself into the dimension of the spider, a space that is foreign to him but precisely for this reason full of creativity and possibility, and the only
place where the event of reading, interpretation and understanding is possible. Art
together with semiotics become an important tool to immerse the human within a
world-environment that is other, remote, and often impossible to describe in linguistic terms. Precisely because of this difficulty of expressing another world, art allows
us to access a plane of sense-making that brings interpretative immersiveness.
This last part proposes to translate the structure of the spider’s web concerning a
game we used to play as children, namely that of the so-called “Chinese whispers”3.

Conclusion
Certainly, we know that it is not possible to have a direct experience of other
umwelten. However, such artistic performance is situated as a possible involvement
that helps to understand the immersion in the spider environment. The idea is not to
reproduce an identical experience, but to approach and overlap according to certain
dynamics two umwelten that are radically different. The purpose is to intuit how the
vibration resulting from information, filtered through to materiality that is not merely
vocal but also by all the environmental elements, is interpreted by an animal like
the spider. It is a matter of establishing how the spider’s environment can be reconstructed, based on the functional circles of arachnids, in order to understand, through
artistic immersion, how the interpretative process, hence semiosis, arises. Moreover,
we wanted to clarify that the spider’s perception, as well as its world, is not constituted simply through a physiological activity of the animal, but is constituted by
material semiotics that extend beyond mere organismic capacity. The idea is to show
that the environment has an agency that participates in the emergence of meaning in
the animal world, actively creating communicative pathways that express material
signification. The purpose of presenting vibration as an emergent process of nature is
meant to indicate that the meaning and organization of signs are natural phenomena
that extend horizontally in every environment in a layered manner. This makes us
reflect on the importance of biosemiotics and material semiotics in understanding the
phenomenon of umwelt in its complexity.
There are various performances that connect umwelt theory and art. For example,
“Treehugger” and “In the Eyes of the Animal” are immersive multisensory installations that illuminate the invisible connections between humans and animal and plant
life forms, bridging the human and natural worlds. Another example of experiential
connection in children and environmental sustainability is the “Umwelt Arena”, an
interactive experience. With hands-on exhibits, children can explore the fascinating
realm of sustainability, delving into concepts such as renewable energy, waste reduction, and conservation. Art thus offers a valuable approach to practical explorations
of the umwelt from a biosemiotic perspective. In this article, we have attempted to

In conclusion, starting from the material vibration of the spider’s surroundings,

one offered a selection of stimuli and a range of information that make up its umwelt.
Experiential immersion is part of the phenomenological understanding of the animal
umwelt, seeking to understand more intuitively how the spider reads, interprets, and
acts in its surroundings. The structural complexity of the spider’s subjective world is
far more layered and complex; however, this project presents itself as a small attempt
to understand worlds unknown to us through performance art.
