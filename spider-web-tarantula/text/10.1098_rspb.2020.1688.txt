ABSTRACT

15

Tarantulas paradoxically exhibit a diverse palette of vivid colouration despite their crepuscular

16

to nocturnal habits. The evolutionary origin and maintenance of these colours remains a

17

mystery. In this study, we reconstructed the ancestral states of both blue and green colouration

18

in tarantula setae, and tested how these colours correlate with the presence of stridulation,

19

urtication, and arboreality. Green colouration has likely evolved at least eight times, and blue

20

colouration is likely an ancestral condition that appears to be lost more frequently than gained.

21

While our results indicate that neither colour correlates with the presence of stridulation or

22

urtication, the evolution of green colouration appears to depend upon the presence of

23

arboreality, suggesting that it likely originated for, and functions in, crypsis through substrate

24

matching among leaves. We also constructed a network of opsin homologs across tarantula

25

transcriptomes. Despite their crepuscular tendencies, tarantulas express a full suite of opsin

26

genes – a finding that contradicts current consensus that tarantulas have poor colour vision on

27

the basis of low opsin diversity. Overall, our results support the intriguing hypotheses that blue

28

colouration may have ultimately evolved via sexual selection and perhaps proximately be used

29

in mate choice or predation avoidance due to possible sex differences in mate-searching.

INTRODUCTION

32
33

The large, hirsute spider family Theraphosidae includes some of the most visually striking

34

spiders. Commonly known as tarantulas, these animals are notable for the fact that they are

35

often brightly coloured, with violet, blue, green, red, and purple hues in particular, present

36

throughout the family1,2. However, a long-standing enigma has been the evolutionary origin

37

and maintenance of such vivid colouration in a group that is almost entirely crepuscular, if not

38

nocturnal3,4. The evolution of colouration in tarantulas has previously received only scant

39

attention in the literature, cf. Hsiung et al2. A well-resolved phylogeny for this group did not

40

exist until recently5 and previous attempts2 to reconstruct the ancestral states of blue

41

colouration were based upon a poorly resolved and outdated supertree. Hsiung, et al.2 also used

42

vaguely defined criteria to identify blue and green colouration. For instance, no data was

43

provided to validate their colour scorings, some of which appear to be erroneous (E.g. blue

44

colouration was listed as present in Haplocosmia / Nhandu, and absent in Cyrtopholis; but see

45

Table S3).

46

Here, we reconstruct the evolutionary histories of both blue (“blueness”) and green

47

(“greenness”) integumentary colouration in the setae of tarantulas via quantitative ancestral

48

state reconstructions. We use a robust phylogeny5,6 and set strict criteria for assigning colour

49

states by calculating just-noticeable difference (JND) values7 for at least one representative

50

species for each genus represented in our phylogeny. In order to address the function of colours

51

in tarantulas, we also constructed data matrices based on prior publications detailing the

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.04.25.061366; this version posted April 25, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

52

presence or absence of (i) stridulatory setae, (ii) urticating setae, and (iii) arboreal tendencies

53

for each genus in this study, and conducted a series of correlative tests to determine whether

54

any of these traits might inform the function of blue or green colouration in tarantulas. We

55

hypothesize that blue colour should be associated with stridulating / urticating setae, or both,

56

if it either originally evolved or has been exapted (sensu8) for an aposematic function.

57

However, we posit that green colouration could aid in crypsis and should show an association

58

with arboreality.

59

It is generally thought that tarantulas possess little or no colour vision, with only a weak

60

colour spectrum discrimination2,9, with Hsiung, et al.2 noting that sexual selection as a driving

61

force behind the evolution of tarantula colouration is unlikely. Morehouse et al.10 argue that

62

tarantulas are colour-blind on the basis of finding a low opsin diversity in Aphonopelma10. We

63

investigate whether this claim holds true for other tarantulas by scoring the presence and

64

relative expression of opsins from transcriptomic data in 25 tarantula genera. Opsins are

65

specialized, transmembrane proteins belonging to the superfamily of G-protein coupled

66

receptors that convert light photons to electrochemical signals through a signalling cascade11.

67

While the expression of multiple opsin species does not necessarily imply greater colour

68

discrimination, the absence of opsin orthologs could provide support to the hypothesis that

69

tarantulas, broadly speaking, cannot perceive colours.

DISCUSSION

131
132

Blueness is Likely Ancestral to Theraphosidae

133

As seen in Figure 1, the outgroup taxa are blue. This polarity, along with the posterior

134

probabilities on the ancestral-most nodes, suggest that the earliest tarantulas were likely blue.

135

This early origin of blueness appears to be retained in the present-day members of the New

136

World subfamilies Theraphosinae, Aviculariinae, and Psalmopoeinae (and at least some of the

137

taxonomically unresolved Ischnocolinae), with 4 losses distributed mostly at the tips or

138

shallower nodes. Blueness is even retained throughout some Old World subfamilies, namely

139

Ornithoctoninae, Poecilotheriinae, and Harpactirinae, while it has been lost twice in older

140

clades and re-evolved four times at the tips or shallower nodes.

141

While Hsiung, et al.2 also recovered blueness as ancestral, our estimates of the

142

frequency and pattern of evolution differ. Hsiung, et al.2 suggested that blueness evolved at

143

least eight independent times in tarantulas, and that the true number of gains could be much

144

greater still. Hsiung, et al.2 supported their claim that blueness evolved many times

145

independently in part by arguing that distinctly different nanostructures have converged to

146

produce the same colour. By comparison, our results (Figure 1) indicate that blueness has

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.04.25.061366; this version posted April 25, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

147

evolved at most five times, and was lost more often than it was gained (seven times).

148

Interestingly, of the five gains reported here, all occur in Old World groups (three tips), and

149

five of our seven losses are in New World groups at the tips or relatively shallow nodes.

150

Hsiung, et al.2 also characterised the photonic nanostructures within the setae of

151

selected blue theraphosid tarantulas as being either amorphous spongy or multi-layer, and

152

concluded that these distinct nanostructures are a product of convergent evolution to produce

153

similar blue colours. They argued against sexual selection as the driver behind blue colouration

154

because typically sexual selection causes colour divergence, not convergence, at least in

155

sympatry13,14. However, their character scorings are based on apparently low-resolution and

156

low-magnification 2D transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of what are otherwise

157

complex nanostructures. The EM data for species with multilayer nanostructures in Hsiung, et

158

al.2 suggests that the nanostructure can be more disordered, especially toward the setal interior.

159

This is analogous to the interplay between order and disorder often seen in the same individual,

160

for instance in many beetles15,16. In addition, some amount of disorder could be an artefact of

161

the sectioning and invasive TEM imaging process. The TEM data in Hsiung, et al.2 together

162

with precise, bulk 3D SAXS structural information from taxa in both Old World

163

(Ornithoctoninae and Poecilotheriinae) and New World (Psalmopoeinae) theraphosid

164

subfamilies16 suggests there is only one fundamental type of setae photonic nanostructure in

165

tarantulas — a perforated lamellar or a multilayer nanostructure with random perforations as

166

seen in certain species of Polyommatine and Papilionid butterflies17,18, with differing degrees

167

of structural disorder.

168

These observations are consistent with a single ancestral origin of blue colouration in

169

tarantulas and thus does not preclude a role for sexual selection in their evolution. It is well

170

appreciated that certain tarantula species exhibit sexual dimorphism, including age-related

171

dichromatism across several genera e.g. Haplopelma, Xenesthis2,19, Psalmopoeus20,

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.04.25.061366; this version posted April 25, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

172

Aphonopelma21, and many Aviculariinae22. Penultimate males frequently display vivid metallic

173

colourations, and having examined a mature Pamphobeteus antinous male with vivid iridescent

174

blue bristle ornamentation on the legs and pedipalps, Simonis, et al.23 proposed that such

175

colouration might play a role in sexual signalling.

176

Alternatively, given that upon maturity many male tarantulas will leave their retreats

177

and wander in search of females24-26, it is plausible that different selective pressures other than

178

sexual selection could be driving sexual dichromatism. For instance, certain colours are either

179

aposematic or cryptic under moonlight27,28, and therefore useful to the wandering male but not

180

to the fossorial female.

Does Arboreality Precede the Evolution of Greenness?

183

Blomberg’s K values for greenness (Table 1) support the hypothesis that green taxa tend to

184

cluster together in our phylogeny. Despite the small K values, the significant P-values imply

185

that closely related taxa are probably more similar in their greenness than random pairs chosen

186

from across the phylogeny. However, Pagel’s  analyses indicate that the phylogeny alone

187

cannot explain the observed patterns. When both measures are considered together, it’s

188

reasonable to infer that it is slightly more probable to find greenness clustered among closely

189

related taxa, but the overall structure of the topology fails to explain why this is the case. This

190

observation is generally consistent with Figure 2. One can observe that if a given genus exhibits

191

either arboreality or greenness, then its close relatives also tend to exhibit one or both traits.

192

In agreement with Hsiung, et al.2, green colouration was found to be rare in our

193

phylogeny. However, our ancestral state reconstruction (Figure 2) has revealed a previously

194

undocumented relationship between greenness and arboreality. Of the 11 green genera in this

195

study, six taxa are arboreal. Both traits appear to have evolved within the genus of

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.04.25.061366; this version posted April 25, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

196

Poecilotheria (i.e. emerged as tip states in this analysis). For Avicularia, Caribena, and

197

Ybyrapora, both greenness and arboreality are shown to emerge in the common ancestor to the

198

three. However, in the cases of Stromatopelma and Tapinauchenius, arboreality is reported in

199

the immediate ancestors of these genera, but greenness is only reported as a tip-state in each –

200

arboreality preceded the evolution of greenness. Our dependency tests (Table 3) support an

201

evolutionary scenario where evolving greenness depends on the a priori presence of

202

arboreality. We hypothesize that this is the case for Stromatopelma and Tapinauchenius, with

203

some caveats (see below). We cannot conclusively say whether this is true for either

204

Poecilotheria or the subfamily Aviculariinae (Avicularia, Caribena, and Ybyrapora), but the

205

addition of further taxa at the species level could assist in determining whether greenness or

206

arboreality emerged first in these clades.

207

There are five genera for which greenness is reported without arboreality. Of those five,

208

neither Aphonopelma nor Phormictopus have any close arboreal relatives. However,

209

Ephebopus, Pterinochilus, and Harpactira are all sister to arboreal clades that do contain green

210

members. The case of Ephebopus is particularly interesting. It is the only fossorial member of

211

the subfamily Psalmopoeinae, which includes the above-mentioned Tapinauchenius, and

212

greenness is only restricted to a small abdominal patch in our representative species (E.

213

cyanognathus). West, et al.29 reported that certain Ephebopus species may colonize arboreal

214

domains, e.g. early instars of E. murinus have been observed creating tube webs in bromeliads,

215

and E. rufescens can be found in hollow rocks and tree-holes. The study also noted, however,

216

that Ephebopus is primarily a fossorial genus, and even those species that display arboreal

217

tendencies are more often found in burrows, which informed our treatment of the genus as non-

218

arboreal. Within the context of our results, it is plausible to speculate that, as a genus whose

219

members contain localized patches of green along with fossorial species that occasionally

220

display arboreal tendencies, Ephebopus may represent a taxon that is in the process of evolving

221

an increasingly arboreal lifestyle, concomitant with the emergence of greenness and the crypsis

222

that it confers.

223

Four genera are listed as arboreal and non-green. Heteroscodra’s closest relative,

224

Stromatopelma, is both arboreal and green, and the sister clade to the pair also contains green

225

members (Pterinochilus and Harpactira). Cyriopagopus forms a clade with Poecilotheria (a

226

genus that’s both arboreal and green) and Haplopelma (fossorial and non-green). For

227

Psalmopoeus, the ΔE b value for this genus was calculated to be 53 (Table S3), which was very

228

close to the cut-off (<50) required to be considered green. Its closest relatives in this study all

229

contain green members.

Opsin Diversity and Evolution in Tarantulas

232

Our results document that the same suite of opsins as in jumping spiders are likewise expressed

233

in a wide array of tarantula species. In particular, both homologs and orthologs to short-

234

wavelength and medium-wavelength sensitive opsins that can detect blues and greens30 were

235

found in all whole-body transcriptomes, except for Hysterocrates sp., Lasiodora parahybana,

236

Pterinochilus chordatus, and the diplurid Linothele sp. Morehouse, et al.10 reported that

237

Aphonopelma only possesses RH2A and UV-sensitive short-wavelength opsins. While we

238

acknowledge that some of our tarantula opsin sequences show homology to many types of

239

reference opsins, our results nonetheless suggest that Morehouse et al.’s observation

240

underestimates true opsin diversity among tarantulas when more broadly sampled. All

241

subfamilies possess the full complement of typical arthropod opsins. Given that jumping

242

spiders are known to discriminate colours, including reds, greens, and blues30, we cannot

243

discount the possibility that tarantulas are able to perceive colours based on our results (Figure

244

3). While Dahl and Granda 9 noted that Aphonopelma chalcodes lack the neurological response

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.04.25.061366; this version posted April 25, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

245

complexities seen in colour discriminating eyes, we encourage future behavioural studies from

246

a variety of genera to see if this reported inability is broadly representative of tarantulas. In

247

short, we cannot rule out that blue colouration could not have evolved in tarantulas for the

248

purposes of sexual signalling due to their poor eyesight e.g.2, or on the basis of low opsin

249

diversity. However, given that blueness is lost more frequently than it is gained, we suggest

250

that it is perhaps under weak selection or has been exapted for cryspis or aposematic signalling

251

in twilight or perhaps moonlight27,28.

253

CONCLUSIONS

254

Our research offers novel insights into the evolutionary origin and potential functions of blue

255

and green colouration in tarantulas. We report that blueness is an ancestral condition for

256

tarantulas and was likely present among the groups’ earliest representatives. This finding leaves

257

open the possibility that blueness may have originally evolved for the purposes of sexual

258

signalling, and may continue to serve in this role given that most tarantulas still possess the

259

typical, full suite of arthropod opsins. On the other hand, greenness has likely evolved at least

260

eight times, primarily at the tips, and does not appear to have been lost at any point in the

261

evolution of tarantulas. We provide support for a conditional relationship where evolving

262

greenness depends likely on the prior presence of arboreality, and compare the ancestral state

263

reconstructions of both greenness and arboreality to further support this idea. We hope that

264

future work will include comparative studies on the visual perception and putative mate-choice

265

experiments in tarantulas to better understand the selective pressures involved in the origin and

266

maintenance of vivid colouration in this enigmatic group.

Phylogeny Construction

457

The phylogeny published by Foley, et al.5, based on 2460 orthologous genes was used as a

458

constraint tree for this study, but taxa were reduced to the genus level for ease of assigning

459

character states in subsequent steps. The R package “PASTIS”34 facilitated the inclusion of a

460

further 11 genera that lack sequence data by constraining their clade memberships as

461

determined by previous studies (Table S1). Branch lengths for these additional taxa were

462

estimated by MrBayes v3.235, which ran under a birth-death model for 100,000 generations,

463

with sampling every 160 generations and the burnin set to 25%.

Ancestral State Reconstructions and Correlative Tests

513

Phylogenetic signal of discrete blue, discrete green, Δb values against the blue reference, and

514

Δa values against the green reference were assessed using both Blomberg’s K50 and Pagel’s

515

51. A set of functions in the R package “phytools”52 facilitated ancestral state reconstructions

516

and correlative tests. The ancestral states for discrete blue, discrete green, and arboreality were

517

separately estimated using 10 million generations of Bayesian MCMC with the ancThresh()

518

function53, and the posterior probabilities were plotted at ancestral nodes as pie charts. We

519

coded the traits as present at any internal nodes whose corresponding posterior probabilities

520

were ≥66% (e.g.54,55), and made note of any nodes whose corresponding posterior probabilities

521

were ≥95%. The JND (continuous) colour data was then traced through the phylogeny using

522

the contMap() function. The ancThresh pie charts corresponding to ancestral nodes whose

reconstructions of the continuous ΔE Lab values (Right), with the posterior probabilities of
ancestral nodes plotted as pie charts. Left panel shows our conservative ΔE b mapping, with
the discretization of the ΔE Lab values (Right) suggesting 5 gains (single blue bars) and 7
losses (double brown bars). Old World subfamilies are shown in red, and New World
subfamilies in orange. Pie charts adjacent to taxon names depict the proportions of currently

Figure 2: Mirror topologies showing the distribution of greenness (left) and arboreality (right).
Posterior probabilities for select (for clarity) ancestral nodes are shown as pie charts. Trait gains
are represented by single green bars. In the left panel, trait values and branch shading refer to
the ancestral state reconstructions of continuous ΔE a values. The ancestral posterior
probabilities of continuous ΔE Lab values of relevant nodes that were used to discretize the
greenness trait are also shown. Greenness is gained eight times, primarily at the tips with no
loss. Arboreality is gained five times, and is never lost. Pie charts adjacent to taxon names
depict the proportions of currently described species in each genus with digital photographs
available (black = images found, white = no images), and the proportions of species with
images that possess green colouration (grey = no available images) – see Table S7. Green *
denote undescribed species that can be unambiguously attributed to the corresponding genera
with green colour.

three tools predict the homology, and the connected nodes can be considered orthologous. Thin,
