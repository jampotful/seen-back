SUMMARY

Vision is one of the most important senses used by animals and contributes to fundamental behaviors,
including foraging, navigation, and mate detection and selection.1 Although much is known about how eye po­
sition and orientation correlate to ecology in the context of binocularity,2 animals with multipartite visual sys­
tems (more than two eyes) remain comparatively neglected. Spiders are highly successful predators that
occupy a range of ecological niches and usually possess eight eyes. Here, we use three-dimensional geometric
morphometrics and evolutionary modeling to test whether eye positions, orientations, and interocular angles
correlate with hunting strategies in 52 species across the spider phylogeny. We demonstrate that eye config­
urations diversified from an ancestral medial cluster, as seen in modern trapdoor spiders, to a halo-like config­
uration in orb-weavers, and to the frontal clustering of eyes in several members of derived spider lineages. We
show that visual hunters have the highest disparity and evolutionary rates in the configuration of their eyes but
display a distinct morphological signature with multiple eye pairs concentrated at the front of the carapace.
Moreover, we quantify the extent to which eye configuration is modular and show that the position and orien­
tation of the eye pairs evolve semi-independently of each other. Our findings demonstrate that modularity in the
spider visual system facilitates not only the specialization of individual eyes but also the whole architecture of
the visual system, in line with different hunting strategies, body plans, and ecological niches.
RESULTS
The arrangement of eyes provides a classic example of the
interaction between behavioral ecology and morphological evo­
lution. Field of view (FOV) overlap between eyes can confer
increased sensitivity, stereopsis, and redundancy,2 but at the
cost of overall coverage. In fishes, birds, mammals, and in­
sects, the linear and angular separation of the two eyes is often
linked to environmental and ecological factors, including tro­
phic position and habitat complexity.1–5 Visual systems with
more eyes have additional degrees of freedom in their arrange­
ment, yet neither the evolutionary and ecological drivers of
complex eye configurations nor their functional impacts have
been explored. Spiders offer an excellent model to address
this: they possess up to eight eyes and exhibit plentiful varia­
tion in eye position and orientation (Figure 1A) across diverse
visual ecologies.6–8 Using three-dimensional geometric mor­
phometrics, we analyzed the diversity, evolutionary history,
and ecological correlates of eye configurations in 52 species,

representing most major families of true spiders. We quantified
configurations using 13 markers on each corneal lens: 11
sliding semilandmarks around the lens perimeter and fixed
landmarks at the centers of the outer and inner surfaces to cap­
ture the primary lens axis. We also measured the smallest angle
between the primary axes of adjacent eyes (interocular angle;
Figure 1B). Across one pair of principal (also called anterior me­
dian eyes [AMEs]) and three pairs of secondary eyes (anterior
lateral eyes [ALEs], posterior median eyes [PMEs], and poste­
rior lateral eyes [PLEs]), we generated a dataset comprising
52 markers per species.

The specialization and division of labor between eye pairs11,12
and the distinct developmental origins of the principal and sec­
ondary eyes13 suggest a capacity for semi-independent evolu­
tion14 (modularity15), which is cited as a key advantage of the spi­
der visual system.16–18 We tested four modularity hypotheses
using Procrustes coordinates: all four eye pairs evolving as sepa­
rate modules; anterior and posterior eyes as separate modules;
median and lateral eyes as separate modules; and principal and
secondary eyes as separate modules. We found a significant
modular signal, with greatest support for separate median and
lateral modules (effect size = − 11.70, p = 0.001; Figure 3C;
Table S2). Pairwise comparisons returned greater support for
‘‘one module per eye’’ than for ‘‘anterior versus posterior eyes’’
(p = 0.009), but no other comparisons reached significance.
We also found significant differences in evolutionary rates be­
tween eyes (ratio = 1.5, p = 0.001), with the AMEs evolving the
slowest and the PMEs fastest (Table S3; Figure 3D).

across many animal groups, including mammals, birds, fish,
and insects.4,19–21 A classic hypothesis predicts that carnivorous

mammals have forward-facing eyes (maximizing binocular over­
lap), whereas herbivorous mammals have laterally placed eyes
(affording a panoramic FOV)22,23 (however, see Kopania and
Clark24). Similarly, forward-facing eyes in primates may enhance
depth perception and support rapid movement through complex
environments.4,25
Among spiders, visual hunters typically exhibited anterior
convergence of the AMEs, ALEs, and often of the PMEs. This im­
plies that the frontal region is sampled by multiple ipsilateral eye
pairs, as well as bilaterally within eye pairs, as demonstrated
empirically in salticids, thomisids, and lycosids.7,26,27 Binocular
overlap within eye pairs may confer advantages2 including stere­
opsis and improved sensitivity that could enhance prey detec­
tion or capture. Many visual hunters also move through complex
habitats. Overlap between different eye pairs would suggest
they extract different information from this region of visual space;
indeed, studied visual hunters demonstrate greater functional
and morphological divergence between (forward-facing) eye
pairs.18,28 Lycosid AMEs detect polarized light, whereas the
ALEs and PMEs likely monitor substrate texture.11 Likewise, salt­
icid ALEs detect motion and direct high-resolution form vision by
the AMEs.12 Our data suggest that this strategy is common and
convergent across other visually hunting families. The PLEs were
oriented more posteriorly in visual hunters, with wider ALE-PLE
interocular angles, suggesting they compensate for covering
the remaining visual field. That visual hunters have convergently
evolved to cluster in this region of component space is extremely
compelling and suggests an advantageous strategy to leverage

their modular visual system. The ‘‘visual-hunting’’ group is inde­
pendently recovered via unbiased analysis, with the interesting
exception of Salticus and addition of Uloborus. The assignment
of Salticus, which is located at the periphery of component
space, to a different morphotype may result from the extreme
lateral placement of the PMEs and/or relative size of the
AMEs.28 Intriguingly, although visual behavior in Uloboridae
has not been studied, it has been suggested that vision contrib­
utes to predator avoidance or prey capture, based on their
diverse eye configurations and retinal organization.29,30
While web hunters and other hunters lacked distinct morpho­
logical signatures, lens axes were consistently evenly distributed
among the four eye pairs in both groups. This presumably max­
imizes total FOV coverage and could enhance predator detec­
tion, directional phototaxis, and other broad-field tasks. This
may particularly benefit aerial web hunters, which are vulnerable
to predation on exposed webs.31,32 Simultaneously, these
species do not exhibit substantial morphological divergence
between eye pairs: in many web-builders, the secondary eyes
are similar in size and structure.33–35 Our findings support the hy­
pothesis that they collect similar information from a wider total
FOV; however, our data do not account for lens optics or retinal
position and structure, which are crucial contributors to visual
function.
In addition to correlation with specific eye arrangements, we
found that visual hunters had the highest morphological disparity
and evolutionary rates, corroborating patterns in eye size28 and
indicating that their eye configurations are diversifying. Visual

hunting includes a range of behaviors across varied contexts,
which may exert different selective pressures. For example, spi­
ders that pursue prey across open ground36 likely have different
needs than ambush hunters37 or net casters.38 Species that rely
primarily on other sensory modalities, such as mechanorecep­
tion, likely do not experience similar selection pressures on their
visual systems, despite occupying diverse niches.
Modularity in spider visual systems
Our results support evolutionary modularity in eye configura­
tions. While developmental homology would predict principal
and secondary eye modules, we found the greatest support
for median (AMEs and PMEs) and lateral (ALEs and PLEs) mod­
ules. This contrasts with the recovery of anterior (AMEs and
ALEs) and posterior (PMEs and PLEs) modules in the evolution
of eye diameters,28 potentially highlighting the distinct selective
pressures acting on eye size versus shape, position, and orien­
tation, and suggesting that these pressures transcend shared
developmental origins. Modularity in spider visual systems is
typically credited with the potential for the division of labor
among eyes.12 In the context of this study, modularity in eye po­
sition and orientation could reflect constraints on visual field
sampling; for example, altering the position of one eye pair
might leave a ‘‘gap’’ in the overall FOV for which another eye
pair may need to compensate. The median versus lateral eye
modules could reflect central-field versus peripheral-field
sampling. Modularity is also apparent in the evolutionary rates
of individual eyes. The AMEs evolve the slowest, possibly due
to stabilizing selection that preserves frontal sampling, or
indeed, the developmental canalization of the principal eyes,
whereas the PMEs are the most labile. However, until more
data within and across species are available, we present these
results cautiously.
This is the first attempt to quantitatively compare the spatial
arrangement and distribution of eyes in 3D in a multipartite visual
system. Our work shows how eye configurations vary across
true spiders and how they correlate with hunting strategies.
Visual hunters convergently exhibit greater anterior concentra­
tion in the positions and orientations of their eyes, suggesting
that the frontal visual field is sampled by multiple eye pairs, as
demonstrated in selected species.7 The convergence in their
eye arrangements, orientations, and interocular angles, and the
effect on evolutionary rates and morphological disparity, under­
scores the powerful role of ecological pressures in shaping visual
system architectures.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
This study used ethanol-preserved museum specimens from Museum für Naturkunde Berlin and Oxford University Museum of Nat­
ural History. Females were preferentially chosen to avoid effects of sexual dimorphism. Specimens were stored in microtubes filled
with 70% ethanol. Details of the specimens analyzed are provided in Table S1, key resources table, and method details.
METHOD DETAILS
Taxon Sampling and X-ray Tomography
A total of 52 spider species, plus an outgroup, Amblypygi (Table S1), were selected, representing 48 families. Most families were
represented by single species except for Araneidae, Malkaridae, Miturgidae, and Oecobiidae, which were each represented by
two species. To avoid conflating differences in visual morphology between the sexes with interspecific differences, we chose to
only measure female specimens where possible. Specimens were borrowed from Oxford University Museum of Natural History
and the Museum für Naturkunde Berlin. Synchrotron radiation micro-computed tomography (SR-μCT) and laboratory microcomputed tomography (μCT) were used to image the cephalothorax. See Table S1 for scan settings (exposure times, propagation
distances, magnification, filtering, scintillators, number of projections).

Synchrotron scans were performed at the TOMCAT beamline of the Swiss Light Source (SLS) at the Paul Scherrer Institut, Villigen,
Switzerland,50 using a monochromatic beam, and at the I13-2 Manchester beamline at Diamond Light Source, Harwell, UK,51 using a

distances between the sample and the objective. In-house software pipelines were used to reconstruct slices from projections (tomo­
grams),52–56 apply noise reduction, ring artefact removal,57 Paganin filtering,58 and reduce image depth to 8 bits.
Laboratory μCT was performed at the Museum für Naturkunde Berlin using a Phoenix nanotom X-ray|s tube (Waygate Technolo­
gies, Wunstorf, Germany; RRID:SCR_022582) at 70kV and 150 or 200 μA, generating 1000 projections with 750ms per scan. The
cone beam reconstruction was performed using the datos|x 2.2 reconstruction software (Waygate Technologies, Wunstorf, Ger­
many) and the data were visualized in VG Studio Max 3.5 (Volume Graphics GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany). Samples were stained
in 10% iodine solution in 70% ethanol for 3–4 days prior to scanning. Nano-CT scans of ten additional species were used from
the dataset of Kallal and Wood39 and a micro-CT scan of an amblypygid, Phrynus sp., from the dataset of Long et al.,59 was used
as an outgroup.
Cropping, Segmentation, and Landmarking
Image stacks were cropped in ImageJ41 and imported into Amira version 2025.1.42 All four corneal lenses on one side of the ceph­
alothorax were manually segmented. Thirteen landmarks were placed on each lens: 11 semilandmarks around the perimeter, pro­
ceeding anticlockwise from the anteriormost edge, to capture the shape of the eye, and one fixed landmark each at the centre of
the outer and the inner lens surfaces, to capture the primary axis of the lens (Figure 1). Landmarks were exported as .landmarkAscii
files for analysis in R. Landmark sets contained data for all four eye pairs (i.e., AMEs: landmarks 1&2, semi-landmarks 3–13; ALEs:
landmarks 14&15, semi-landmarks 16–29, etc.). Where scan and specimen quality allowed, we segmented and landmarked the
lenses on the right side of the specimen. When the left-sided lenses were analysed, we corrected for this by reflecting the landmarks
to the right side and correcting the order of the perimeter semi-landmarks in R.
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Landmark files were read as an array using the read.amira.set and read.amira.dir functions in the ‘Arothron’ package version 2.0.543 in
R version 4.3.1.60 Landmarks were registered in a common coordinate space using generalized Procrustes analysis (GPA). Using
sliding semilandmarks can be problematic during GPA because they can slide past each other and lead to twisted, unnaturally trans­
formed shapes. This can be particularly problematic when each landmark set contains clustered data (different phenotypic struc­
tures, such as the four eye pairs) because the convergence during GPA is based on all the landmarks, and not on separate clusters.
To avoid these problems, we first performed separate Procrustes analysis for each of the eyes, treating the rim landmarks for each of
the lenses as sliders. The resulting Procrustes coordinates for the landmarks in each eye were then transformed back to their original
sizes and positions in the overall configurations. A final Procrustes analysis was then performed, treating all the landmarks as fixed, to
produce a set of Procrustes coordinates (i.e. registered landmarks) (Collyer pers. comm.). All Procrustes analyses were performed
using gpagen in ‘geomorph’ version 4.0.10.44,61 Landmarking was performed by the same author for all specimens.
We used the time-calibrated phylogeny of Wolff et al. (2022)40 for the comparative analysis. We estimated the phylogenetic signal in
the Procrustes coordinates using phylosig in ‘geomorph’. Phylogenetically aligned component analysis (PACA)9 was performed on
the Procrustes coordinates using gm.prcomp in ‘geomorph’. PACA does not provide the proportion of variance explained by each
component, but rather the relative amount of covariance between the phylogeny and the data (as partial RV scores), with phyloge­
netic signal most strongly aligned with the first component (RVPAC1=80.31%). We visualised the configurations of Procrustes coor­
dinates at internal nodes using anc.ML from ‘phytools’ version 2.4-4.45 All the ancestral state estimates had relatively large confi­
dence intervals (provided as tables in Data S1). Hierarchical clustering was performed on the first five PAC scores obtained from
PACA, using the HCPC function in ‘FactoMineR’ version 2.1147 without forcing the number of clusters, and the resulting dendrogram
was plotted using the Fviz_dend function in ‘factoextra’ version 10.0.7.46 We also performed cluster analysis on the first five PC
scores from a non-phylogenetic PCA, to test how much our results are affected by the incorporation of phylogeny (Figure S1C).
We quantified morphological disparity using the morphol.disparity function in ‘geomorph’ to compare Procrustes variance
between groups, including a phylogeny using a ProcD.pgls model fit; significance was assessed via a randomized permutation of
residuals, whereby the Procrustes residual vectors were shuffled among the group labels across many iterations to build a null
distribution of variance differences, and the observed Procrustes-variance difference was then compared to that distribution to
assess significance.
Rates of evolution of Procrustes coordinates were calculated using compare.evol.rates in ‘geomorph’ using 1000 permutations.
This function first transformed the phenotypic data (Procrustes coordinates) by the phylogeny, and then calculated and compared
the transformed euclidean distances of the species from the origin. Significance was assessed by permuting group labels across the
phylogenetically transformed trait data, recomputing the rate ratio for each permutation, and deriving a p-value as the proportion of
permuted ratios at least as extreme as the observed ratio. We also computed evolutionary rates for each of the eye shapes using
compare.multi.evol.rates in ‘geomorph’ using 1000 permutations.62 P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the
Bonferroni correction.

Modular signal in the data was estimated using the covariance ratio (CR), which is the ratio of covariances between modules (hy­
pothetical independently evolving subunits within the data) to the covariances within modules.15 Thus, a low value of CR indicates
modularity in the data. For these modularity analyses, landmarks were aligned by Generalized Procrustes Analysis with semiland­
marks slid using the Procrustes-distance criterion (ProcD), following Zelditch and Swiderski,63 who found that ProcD sliding reduces
spurious modularity signals relative to bending-energy sliding. Four different modularity hypotheses were tested using the phylo.mo­
dularity function: all four eyes as separate modules, anterior and posterior eyes as two separate modules, median and lateral eyes as
two separate modules, and principal and secondary eyes as two separate modules. All hypotheses were statistically evaluated using
the covariance ratio effect size (ZCR) as implemented by the function compare.CR in ‘geomorph’.
Finally, to evaluate the likelihood of overlap between the visual fields of the different eye pairs, we quantified the angles between
the primary lens axes of adjacent eyes (the interocular angle). This was taken as the angle between vectors connecting the fixed
landmarks (inner and outer centre of the lens). Phylogenetic signal for interocular angles was evaluated using phylosig from ‘Phy­
tools’. We assessed differences in interocular angles among ecological groups using a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA).
To identify which angles contributed to any overall group differences, we performed follow-up univariate analyses using generalized
least squares (GLS) models with group as a predictor, in ‘nlme’ version 3.1-168.48 For variables showing significant group effects,
pairwise comparisons among groups were conducted using estimated marginal means with Tukey-adjusted p-values in ‘emmeans’
version 1.11.1.49
Based on existing categorisations,28,40 we divided our species into three broad hunting ecologies: visual hunters, capture webhunters, and other hunters (e.g., Hersiliola sp., which encircles prey using its long spinnerets to entangle it in silk, and Agroeca, a
cursorial hunter that captures prey without building a web and which has not been shown to use vision for prey detection/capture).
We used phylogenetic ANOVA to test for correlations between eye arrangement (Procrustes coordinates) and hunting mode, using
procD.pgls in ‘geomorph’. To test whether the eye configurations have evolved convergently in visual hunters, we computed four
distance-based convergence metrics (C1-C4) on all PC scores from a non-phylogenetic PCA and assessed their significance with
1,000 Brownian-motion simulations using convSig in the ‘convevol’ package version 2.2.1.10 The function compares the phenotypic
distances between tips to the largest phenotypic distances present in the dataset (including phenotypic distances between internal
nodes and tips) to assess the proportion of phenotypic distance ‘closed’ by evolution. We also performed the convergence analysis
similarly on the interocular angles (Table S4).
