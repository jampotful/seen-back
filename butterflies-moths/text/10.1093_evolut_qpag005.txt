Abstract

38
39

Acquisition of novel behaviour is reflected in changes in sensory investment or integration,

40

but the exact nature of these changes is often unclear. Within the Neotropical butterfly tribe,

41

Heliconiini, the genus Heliconius possess 4-fold larger mushroom bodies, an insect learning

42

and memory centre, than closely related Heliconiini. Mushroom body expansion in

43

Heliconius co-occurred with a dietary innovation, and is associated with systematic spatial

44

foraging and extended lifespans. Heliconiini therefore offer an attractive system for studying

45

how behavioural evolution is facilitated by changes in neural systems. Heliconius’ foraging

46

relies on visual scene memories and, indeed, Heliconius have more stable visual long-term

47

memory, and evidence of visual specialisation in the mushroom bodies. Here, we explore

48

how vision-specific neuroanatomical and behavioural enhancement in Heliconius impacts

49

sensory pathways upstream of the mushroom bodies by assessing investment across the

50

eyes, sensory structures and projection pathways. Despite evidence of refinement in

51

visually-based behaviour, we found no increased investment in visual structures, brain areas

52

or pathways. This suggests that the rapid expansion of the Heliconius mushroom body

53

occurred in a context of conserved detection and processing of visual cues, and that a

54

localised shift within integrative brain centres facilitated the evolution of Heliconius’ novel

55

behaviours.

Introduction

73

The sensory and cognitive abilities of a species are dependent on the neural systems that

74

produce them. These systems can evolve through changes in cell number, including by

75

replication of whole neural circuits and associated cell populations, or through altered

76

patterns of connectivity, dendritic branching or neuromodulation (1,2). Evolutionary

77

specialisations in neural systems have allowed species to exploit a diverse range of

78

ecological niches, and thus, underpin the diversity of animal behaviour (2). The close

79

correspondence between neural investment and ecological need is illustrated by divergent

80

investment in the peripheral and central sensory processing structures of sensory stimuli

81

across species (3–5). Consequently, shifts in behaviour can often be accompanied by

82

corresponding shifts in sensory perception and processing. For example, changes in the

83

peripheral structures, such as differences in the size or structure of the eyes, or in

84

downstream visual neuropils, are often found between nocturnal and diurnal species (6).

85

However, behavioural adaptations can also occur in the context of conserved sensory

86

perception but altered integration of sensory responses in integrative brain centres (7–10).

87

Understanding the extent to which peripheral and integrative systems can evolve

88

independently, and which may most frequently underpin behavioural novelty, is a central

89

challenge in evolutionary neurobiology (2).

90

The Neotropical butterfly genus Heliconius provides a suitable case study in adaptive

91

evolution of brain centres associated with behavioural novelty. Heliconius have a unique

92

dietary adaptation, which involves active pollen feeding, a foraging behaviour not observed

93

in other butterflies (11–13). The innovation of pollen feeding has had profound implications

94

for Heliconius’ adult life history. Protein acquired through pollen feeding is linked to an

95

extended lifespan and increased fecundity in Heliconius in comparison to their Heliconiini

96

relatives (14–18), but with seemingly conserved juvenile development (19). Pollen feeding,

97

therefore, has major fitness benefits, and Heliconius display derived foraging behaviours to

98

support this behaviour (11,17,20). Heliconius collect pollen from a restricted range of floral

99

plant resources in a systematic manner, repeatedly visiting specific plants in a spatially and

100

temporally faithful manner, reminiscent of trap-line foraging in some Hymenoptera (11,21).

101

This systematic foraging behaviour relies heavily on spatial memory, likely through learning

102

visual scenes to support recall of spatial routes (22–27). Therefore, Heliconius are thought to

103

make greater use of spatial and temporal cues, and retain visual memories for longer

104

periods of time than their non-pollen feeding close relatives in Heliconiini. Indeed, in

105

Heliconiini there are modality-dependent shifts in memory stability between species, with

106

Heliconius exhibiting greater retention of visual, but not olfactory, memories compared to

107

outgroup genera (27,28).

108

These behavioural differences in foraging, learning, and memory have been strongly

109

linked to the expansion of the mushroom bodies, the site of learning and memory in insects

110

(29). In comparison to their close relatives in the Heliconiini tribe, Heliconius have ~4-fold

111

larger mushroom bodies relative to the rest of the brain, with around eight times the number

112

of Kenyon cells, the intrinsic mushroom body neurons (30). Mushroom body expansion is

113

also associated with internal specialisation, with an increase in the size and proportion of the

114

mushroom body calyx receiving afferent visual input in Heliconius (30), as well as

115

disproportionate expansion of specific mushroom body lobes, which make efferent

116

connections to other brain regions (31). This major shift in mushroom body size and

117

structure has occurred among relatively closely related species (Heliconius originated ~8-14

118

mya; 32). Heliconiini, therefore, provide a tractable opportunity to study the links between the

119

evolution of cognitive enhancement and neuroanatomical adaptations between species

120

which, aside from the evolution of pollen feeding, are largely similar in terms of ecology,

121

behaviour, and life history (13,33).

122

Until now, however, whether the evolution of Heliconius’ systematic foraging

123

behaviour and mushroom body expansion was also accompanied by major changes in the

124

upstream sensory organs and associated sensory neuropils was unclear. Given the reliance

125

of Heliconius on visually guided foraging, it is a plausible hypothesis that adaptations in the

126

visual pathway could enable Heliconius to utilise visual cues in ways their non-pollen feeding

127

outgroup Heliconiini relatives cannot. Mushroom body expansion in Heliconius could,

128

therefore, have occurred in response to increased visual capacities, rather than a direct

129

response to selection linked to the information storage requirements of spatial foraging. To

130

determine the extent to which changes in the integrative circuits of the mushroom bodies

131

and downstream targets facilitate Heliconius’ cognitively demanding foraging behaviours,

132

variation in the sensory structures peripheral to the mushroom bodies must be considered.

133

In insects, variation in ommatidia of the compound eye are responsible for many of the key

134

components of visual perception, including the ability to distinguish between different

135

spectral wavelengths, different light intensities, different degrees of detail and temporal

136

resolutions (34–37). Like other insects, visual information in Heliconiini is relayed from the

137

eye through a series of visual neuropils making up the optic lobe (lamina, medulla,

138

accessory medulla, lobula, ventral lobula and lobula plate in Heliconiini) before their output is

139

relayed to integration centres of the brain through distinct populations of sensory projection

140

neurons (38–40). Similarly, olfactory input is received from the antennae and processed

141

initially in the glomeruli of the antennal lobe, the primary olfactory neuropil, before being

142

relayed to other brain centres, primarily the mushroom bodies and lateral horn (41,42), by

143

olfactory projection neurons. In previous work, volumetric investment in the medulla, as the

144

largest visual neuropil, and the ventral lobula, as a likely relay point to the mushroom body,

(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is

145

was assessed across Heliconiini, with no evidence of divergent evolution in Heliconius (30),

146

but major aspects of the sensory pathways remain unassessed.

147

Here, we explore how sensory systems upstream of the mushroom bodies vary

148

between Heliconius and outgroup genera within Heliconiini. We focus on five species of

149

Heliconiini, with additional species included where available, which serve as representatives

150

of these groups, to analyse the size and structure of peripheral visual structures, from the

151

eyes, through to the major sensory neuropils, and the major tracts connecting them to the

152

mushroom bodies. These species have overlapping ecological and host-plant preferences

153

(33,43), negating confounding effects of habitat on sensory investment (44,45). We

154

hypothesised that previous evidence for visual specialisation in mushroom body structure

155

and memory stability (30,46) could be reflected in modifications in the visual circuitry

156

peripheral to the mushroom bodies. We show that, despite evidence of visual specialisation

157

in Heliconius’ mushroom bodies (30), investment in the sensory processing structures and

158

their pattern of downstream projections is largely conserved across Heliconiini.

Materials and Methods

162
163

i. Eye morphology and imaging

164

Samples for the analysis of eye structure included a total of 84 adult butterflies (Table S1a),

165

including two representatives of Heliconius (21 Heliconius erato demophoon and 22

166

Heliconius melpomene rosina) and three from Heliconiini outgroups (19 Dryas iulia, 10

167

Dryadula phaetusa and 12 Agraulis vanillae). Dryas iulia and Dryadula phaetusa specimens

168

were collected from stock populations at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute

169

insectaries, Gamboa, Panama (May-June 2023). Stocks were established from local

170

populations and maintained in 3×3×2 m mesh cages in ambient conditions and with natural

171

lighting containing pollen resources (Lantana camara, Palicourea elata and Psiguria sp.) and

172

supplementary artificial feeders containing 20% sugar-water. Agraulis vanillae specimens

173

were ordered as pupae from commercial suppliers (The Entomologist;

174

https://butterflypupae.com/), as stocks of this population were not available at the time.

175

Following eclosion, they were kept in greenhouses in the UK in 2 x 2 x 2 m mesh cages at

176

26°C, 80% humidity and a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. Cages contained supplemental 20%

177

sugar-water as well as Lantana camara. All butterflies were cold anesthetised and the head

178

then removed and fixed for 16 hours with agitation in zinc formaldehyde solution (18.4 mM

179

ZnCl2, 135 mM NaCl, 35 mM sucrose, and 1% formaldehyde) (47). The heads were then

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.08.663516; this version posted July 11, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

180

transferred to Dent’s solution (80% methanol/ 20% DMSO) for 2 hr before being transferred

181

to 100% methanol for -20°C storage.

182

To quantify variation in eye anatomy, we followed established protocols (48,49). We

183

removed and imaged the eye cuticle, allowing visualisation and quantification of the number

184

and size of the ommatidia present across the eye. Whole heads were imaged using LAS X

185

software and a Leica EZ4 W stereo microscope with a 5 MP camera at 16× magnification.

186

For each individual, the antennae, proboscis and maxillary palps were removed to allow

187

measurement of the interocular distance (IOD), which served as our allometric control (50).

188

Following whole-head imaging, the left eye cuticle was removed for analysis unless it was

189

damaged, in which case the dissection was repeated with the right eye. Once removed, the

190

eye cuticle was placed in 20% NaOH for 16-20 hours to loosen remaining eye tissue behind

191

the cuticular cornea, which was then removed under a 95% ethanol solution (51). Incisions

192

were made along the dorsal-ventral axis and anterior-posterior axis of the cuticle, to allow it

193

to be mounted flat on a microscope slide in Euparal, and left to dry for 24 hours before

194

imaging. Mounted cuticles were imaged using GXCapture software and a Leica M205 C

195

stereo microscope with an integrated camera at 1.6× and 2.0× magnification, depending on

196

cuticle area.

197

Whole-head and cuticle images were imported into FIJI (52) and scale-corrected.

198

From these images, four morphological variables were recorded: IOD, eye surface area,

199

ommatidia number, and ommatidia size. IOD was taken as the distance between the eyes

200

directly above the proboscis using FIJI’s ‘line tool’. Eye surface area was determined from

201

the cuticle images using the ‘threshold’ and ‘measure’ tool in FIJI (51). Number and size of

202

ommatidia was calculated using the ommatidia detecting algorithm (ODA) python-based

203

pipeline (53).

204

Using R v4.3.0 (54), linear models were used to analyse inter- and intra-specific

205

differences in eye surface area and ommatidia size across Heliconiini. In all of these models,

206

IOD was included as an allometric control to account for body size differences between

207

species and sexes. Ommatidia diameter, eye surface area and IOD were log10 transformed

208

to meet residual-distribution assumptions of linear models. Linear models were tested

209

sequentially against a null model including only IOD as a control for body size (Table S1B,

210

S1F) to determine best fit. Where differences were found, post-hoc tests with Tukey

211

correction for multiple comparisons were carried out.

iii. Neuropil staining, imaging and segmentation

232

To examine investment in sensory neuropils, we segmented brain images from 10 H.

233

melpomene, 10 H. erato, 14 D. iulia, 7 D. phaetusa and 11 A. vanillae. These images were

234

previously obtained from specimens collected from across French Guiana, Panama and

235

Ecuador by Couto et al. (30). Imaging was performed using immunohistochemical staining

236

against a synaptic marker and confocal microscopy, using a standardised protocol. Full

237

details of staining and imaging methods are available in Couto et al. (30) and Montgomery et

238

al. (58). The antennal lobes (AL), medulla (ME), ventral lobula (vLO), the mushroom body

239

calyx, peduncle and lobes, and the rest of the central brain (rCBR) had been previously

240

segmented by Couto et al. (30). For Heliconius erato and Heliconius melpomene, the

241

additional optic neuropils, the lamina (LA), lobula (LO), lobula plate (LOP), accessory

242

medulla (AME) and the anterior optic tubercule (AOTU), had also been segmented (19).

243

Here, using Amira 2021.1 (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA), the optic lobe neuropils in D.

244

iulia, D. phaetusa and A. vanillae were segmented using the ‘labelfield’ module to construct

245

outlines of each structure. For larger structures (LAM, LO and LOP), every fifth image in the

246

stack was manually segmented before interpolating between the images using the

247

‘interpolation tool’. The interpolated layers were then edited to check for deviations from the

248

staining boundaries and for consistency across the xy, xz and yz views. The volumes of the

249

respective neuropils were extracted using the ‘measure statistics’ module and the 3D models

250

generated using the ‘SurfaceGen’ module. Neuropils were only segmented for one of the

251

brain hemispheres, owing to the consistency in volumes found between each side, and then

252

doubled for statistical analysis (58).

253

Statistical analyses of investment in the sensory neuropils was carried out using

254

linear models in R v4.3.0 (54) to test for divergence in investment between Heliconius and

255

outgroups Heliconiini in scaling and volume of the neuropils compared to the rest of the

256

brain. The volumes of all structures were log10 transformed, and rCBR was used as an

257

allometric control. One A. vanillae was removed from the dataset following data visualisation,

258

as it was a clear and consistent outlier in terms of overall size (~ 3× smaller overall than all

259

other A. vanillae in the dataset). Each of the sensory neuropils (LAM, ME, LO, LOP, aME,

260

vLO, AOTU and AL) were analysed separately, testing for an effect of group membership

261

(Heliconius vs. all non-Heliconius Heliconiini outgroups), as well as the influence of sex

262

differences. Following p-adjustment for multiple testing, sex was found to be insignificant

263

across all structures. Model fit was determined through sequential comparisons to a null-

264

model including only the focal neuropil and rCBR (Table S2a, S2c). The package emmeans

265

v1.8.6 (59) was used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons between species, using the

266

estimated marginal means with Tukey correction for multiple comparisons. Allometric scaling

267

relationships between neuropils were analysed using sma from the smatr v 3.4-8 package in

268

R (60).

iv. Sensory projection pathways

271

Finally, to understand how sensory information is relayed to downstream neuropils,

272

particularly the mushroom body, we used injections to mass label projection pathways from

273

the injection site to the terminal sites of the intersected projection neurons, and

274

immunohistochemistry, which were then reconstructed. We made use of samples initially

275

produced for a previous study (30), where tracts from the antennal lobe and optic lobe were

276

stained in two Heliconius species (H. melpomene and Heliconius hortense) and three

277

Heliconiini outgroup species (D. iulia, A. vanillae and Eueides isabella) using Dextran-

278

conjugated dyes (Fluoro-ruby: 10,000 MW, D1817, Thermo Fisher Scientific; and Alexa fluor

279

647; 10,000 MW, D22914, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to visualise terminal projection sites

280

within the mushroom body calyx. In brief, these butterflies were immobilised and dye was

281

injected into the antennal lobe, and the dorso-medial optic lobe (targeting the vLO and LO),

282

using a stretched capillary tip and the head was submerged in Ringer solution (30). The dye

283

was left to migrate overnight before the brain was dissected out and fixed. Tracts to the calyx

284

were also stained using retrograde tracing through injection into the mushroom body calyx

285

itself in H. melpomene and D. iulia. We then mounted the brains in methyl salicylate and

286

imaged them using a laser-scanning microscope (Leica SP8) with a 10× dry objective (NA =

(Figure 3, S3, S4) to illustrate the identified tracts and commissures.

3. Results

312
313

i. Eye anatomy and visual acuity is largely conserved across Heliconiini.

314

To explore evidence of visual specialisation in Heliconius, we first examined whether

315

Heliconiini species differed in ommatidia size. A model including species, IOD (as an

316

allometric control) and eye surface area showed that ommatidia size varied at both the

317

species level (F4,76 = 24.169, p < 0.001), and in association with surface area (F4,76 = 10.579,

318

p = 0.002). The outgroup Heliconiini species, A. vanillae, had larger ommatidia than all other

319

species, with D. phaetusa also having larger ommatidia than H. erato (Table S1Ciii). In a

320

model including a group level factor rather than species, differences were found between

321

Heliconius and outgroup Heliconiini (F1,79 = 4.638, p = 0.034), but with smaller diameters on

322

average in Heliconius. Across both the species level (F4,76 = 14.980, p < 0.001) and group

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.08.663516; this version posted July 11, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

level (F1,79 = 18.051, p < 0.001) analyses, males had significantly higher average ommatidia

324

diameter than females, apart from in H. melpomene, where intraspecific sexual dimorphism

325

in ommatidia size was not detected (Figure 1B, Table S1Cii).

326

Next, we analysed variation in eye surface area overall, and found there to be

327

considerable interspecific differences (F4,77 = 16.264, p < 0.001; Table S1Div). However,

328

these were consistent within groups, and as such group level differences between

329

Heliconius and outgroup Heliconiini species were not significant (F1,80 = 0.094, p = 0.761).

Despite this, we still identified pronounced variation found between most species (Table

331

S1Fv) including within the Heliconius genus, and among outgroup Heliconiini. Surface area

332

333

334

ii. Heliconius do not invest more in visual processing centres of the optic lobes

387

Measurements of eye anatomy suggest Heliconius capture visual information similarly to

388

Heliconiini outgroups. We therefore assessed whether Heliconius have increased investment

389

in the processing of visual cues through increases in the size or one or more of the six visual

390

neuropils (Figure 2). Sex did not have an explanatory relationship with sensory neuropil

391

volumes (Table S2Ai) and was, therefore, excluded as a variable in further analyses. The

392

volume of the antennal lobe, the primary olfactory neuropil, was conserved across species

393

(F4,45= 1.396, p = 0.251; Figure 2G). In contrast, interspecific variation was found in seven of

394

the eight neuropils (Table S2Aii). In the majority of these cases, interspecific differences

395

were a result of increased investment in the non-Heliconius Heliconiini outgroups rather than

396

Heliconius species. The lamina (F1,41= 44.717, p < 0.001), lobula (F1,48= 21.460, p < 0.001)

397

and AOTU (F1,48= 30.338, p < 0.001) were all significantly larger in the outgroups compared

398

to Heliconius (Table S2c, Figure S2). In contrast, at the group level the ventral lobula was

399

larger in Heliconius than the outgroups (F1,48= 19.724, p < 0.001; Figure S2), but was

400

consistent between Heliconius and the outgroup species A. vanillae when examining

401

species-specific differences, suggesting a degree of overlap between the groups (Table

402

S2b). Comparisons of allometric scaling relationships further confirmed these results,

403

highlighting non-allometric grade-shifts between species, with the exception of a major axis

404

shift in the antennal lobe (Figure 2G, Table S3Ai). Where grade-shifts were present, most

405

reflected either an increased or conserved investment in the outgroup species relative to

406

Heliconius species (Table S3Aii; Figure 2D-F, Figure S2). In fact, across most of the

407

neuropils, D. phaetusa and D. iulia investment was significantly higher than other species,

408

with neuropil volume largely conserved between these two species (Table S3Aii; see

409

supplementary text for further details), which is consistent with their phylogenetic

410

relatedness. Ultimately, these results show there is no consistent, significant pattern of

411

enlargement in the sensory neuropils in Heliconius that is coincident with the expansion and

412

visual specialisation of the mushroom body (30).

iii. Sensory projection pathways are qualitatively conserved across Heliconiini

429

In light of the lack of increased investment in visual acuity or visual processing in the primary

430

sensory neuropils in Heliconius, we aimed to further understand whether increased visual

431

reliance in the mushroom bodies and visual performance is the result of differences in supply

432

of visual and olfactory information to the mushroom bodies. Generally, our labelling of the

433

projection neurons from the optic lobe were consistent with projection neurons identified in

434

other species (6,40,62). We saw major inputs to neuropils of the central brain, the mushroom

435

bodies, and to the contralateral optic lobe (Figure 3, Figure S3). Here, we focused on

436

projections to the mushroom bodies, which are necessary for sensory integration, and major

437

associated neuropils such as the AOTU, which has important functions in processing skylight

438

cues and encoding of visual components for navigation through the anterior visual pathway

439

(63,64). Additional anatomical descriptions can be found in the supplementary material

440

(Supplementary Results S2).

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.08.663516; this version posted July 11, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is

441

In both Heliconius and outgroup Heliconiini species, the major input to the mushroom

442

body calyx from the optic lobes was via a thick tract connecting the optic lobe to the

443

ipsilateral calyx (Figure 3; Figure S3). A similar tract has been identified in other Lepidoptera

444

and insect species, but with variation in where the tract originates and pattern of projection.

445

In Heliconiini, calyx injections in D. iulia and H. melpomene revealed this tract to be

446

composed of projections from the lobula and the ventral lobula that converge in the central

447

brain before reaching and innervating the base of the calyx. Fibres from across the lobula

448

converge medially to form the more dorsal lobula tract from the lobula to the calyx (Figure 3,

449

S3). The ventral lobula tract projects dorsally from the ventral lobula into the central brain

450

where it converges with the lobula tract. In addition to the visual projection to the calyx, a

451

prominent thick projection, the anterior optic tract (AOT), connects the lobula with the AOTU,

452

and from the lower AOTU subunit some neurons project further to the lateral complex

453

(Figure S3). This tract was observed in both Heliconius and the outgroup Heliconiini species

454

and is located anteriorly to the tracts that connect the optic lobe and calyx (Figure S3). The

455

tubercle to tubercle tract (TUTUT) connected the ipsilateral and contralateral AOTU across

456

the hemispheres (Figure S3).

457

We also considered whether the disproportionate expansion of the visual calyx may

458

reflect a reduction of projection neurons from the antennal lobe. In both Heliconius and their

459

outgroup genera, we identified a pattern of four major olfactory projections from the antennal

460

lobes, as observed in other insects (6,41,65). Injections into the calyx reveal that two of the

461

four antennal lobe tracts project to the calyx (Figure 3). The medial antennal lobe tract

462

(mALT), projects from the antennal lobe posteriorly and dorsally where it sends branches

463

directly to the calyx, before projecting laterally to the lateral horn (Figure 3). The lateral

464

antennal lobe tract (lALT), projects first laterally from the antennal lobe up to the lateral horn

465

before a small number of fibres innervate the calyx. This tract was present in both antennal

466

lobe and calyx injections, with the calyx injections revealing that only a small number of

467

neurons in the lALT project further from the lateral horn to the calyx (Figure 3), with the

468

majority terminating in the lateral horn, revealed through antennal lobe injections. The

469

mediolateral antennal lobe tract (mlALT; 58) and transverse antennal lobe tract (tALT; 41),

470

both project from the antennal lobe to the lateral horn (Figure S4). The orientation of these

471

two tracts varied slightly between Heliconius and the outgroup Heliconiini, owing to the

472

expansion of the mushroom bodies in Heliconius. Generally, however, qualitative

473

comparisons of both sensory and olfactory projection pathways downstream of the

474

mushroom body reveal a high degree of conservation across Heliconiini in the supply

475

channels leading to the mushroom bodies.

4. Discussion

494
495

Increased mushroom body size and Kenyon cell number in the genus Heliconius is strongly

496

linked to the evolution of a cognitively demanding foraging behaviour associated with pollen

497

feeding (30). Furthermore, expansion of the mushroom bodies largely reflects an increase in

498

the volume of the visual mushroom body calyx (30) and lobes (31), which, along with

499

evidence of improved long-term visual memory (46), suggests a strong degree of visual

500

specialisation in learning and memory circuits. However, our data illustrating conservation of

501

the sensory structures and pathways across multiple Heliconiini support an isolated change

502

in the Heliconius mushroom bodies in facilitating this behavioural shift, while also providing a

503

previously uncharacterised map of key projection pathways within the Heliconiini brain. Our

504

analyses of eye anatomy, sensory neuropil volume and pattern of sensory tracts reveal that

505

the evolutionary modification of the neural circuitry in Heliconius is seemingly highly localised

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.08.663516; this version posted July 11, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

506

to the mushroom bodies, with the sensory structures peripheral to the mushroom bodies

507

found to be highly conserved between Heliconius and the outgroup Heliconiini. Below, we

508

discuss each of the components analysed starting from the periphery and ending with the

509

projection pathways innervating the mushroom bodies.

510

Despite Heliconius’ capacity for spatial learning (24,25), improved visual memory

511

(27), and expanded visual calyx (30), we found no evidence for Heliconius-specific increases

512

in visual acuity. Indeed, the Heliconiini outgroups had higher acuity than the Heliconius

513

genus, which indicates that spatial foraging does not require increased resolution of visual

514

scenes, but rather differences in how these senses are processed in integration centres

515

such as the mushroom bodies (30), and potentially the central complex (66). It is possible

516

that the evolution of higher acuity in the outgroups may be explained by factors such as flight

517

dynamics or differences in motion detection, which also correspond with ommatidia number

518

and size across the eye (55,67,68). Despite this, visualisation of Heliconius’ ability to

519

perceive details of visual scenes suggests that landscapes could be resolved in sufficient

520

degree for their use in guiding spatial learning (24). Our data build on previous research on

521

acuity and eye morphology within Heliconius (37,48,51,69,70), but importantly, provide new

522

data for outgroup Heliconiini.

523

We note that our acuity estimates for the eye are based on structural differences in

524

the eye cuticle, which does not fully account for the downstream processing in the eye the

525

way behavioural assessments of acuity would (55,71). Indeed, behavioural assays produce

526

increased estimates of acuity over morphological measures, although they also mirror

527

intergroup patterns seen in morphological tests (51), suggesting they remain a useful

528

indicator of relative performance. Additionally, if processing differences were present

529

between the Heliconius and outgroup species, this would likely be represented in the

530

volumes of sensory neuropils, in particular, the lamina and medulla, which receive the main

531

direct photoreceptor input from the eye (72). Although neuropil investment differences were

532

found between species, there was no consistent pattern of variation between species that

533

would explain the behavioural differences seen in Heliconius. In fact, contrary to prior

534

expectations, greater investment was consistently seen in the outgroup Heliconiini across

535

the sensory neuropils. The exception to this was the ventral lobula where, overall, Heliconius

536

had greater investment. The ventral lobula is a visual neuropil closely linked to the

537

mushroom bodies (39,40), and, as confirmed by the pathway analysis, one of the major

538

mushroom body inward projection tracts originates from this area (30). The significant grade

539

shift found in Heliconius specifically would, therefore, align with the expansion of the visual

540

calyx. However, when differences were assessed at a species level, Heliconius had

541

consistent investment with the outgroup Heliconiini species A. vanillae. Furthermore, a

542

previous study of a wider range of Heliconius and outgroup Heliconiini species also found

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.08.663516; this version posted July 11, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

543

that expansion of the ventral lobula was not unique to Heliconius (30). As a result, we

544

suggest that there is limited evidence that greater investment in the visual neuropils

545

facilitates the systematic foraging seen in Heliconius.

546

We also found no obvious differences in the sensory projections to the mushroom

547

bodies and other integrative neuropils. Both the visual and olfactory projections found were

548

both highly conserved within the Heliconius and non-Heliconius Heliconiini, as well as

549

consistent with sensory pathways observed in other Lepidoptera (40,41,73) and even other

550

insect orders (74). Although the diameter of the projection pathways were not quantified, we

551

did not observe any obvious qualitative variation in the parameters of the tracts between

552

species. This suggests a potential difference in the degree of Kenyon cell sampling of the

553

visual projection neurons between the Heliconius and outgroup Heliconiini, given the

554

increase in Kenyon cell number but no obvious increase in visual projection pathway density

555

(30,75). Segregation of the visual and olfactory inputs to the calyces has been demonstrated

556

across Heliconiini genera (30), as well as in other species within and outside of Lepidoptera

557

(40,76,77). Indeed, visual input to the mushroom bodies in Heliconiini is similar to that

558

observed in the swallowtail, Papilio xuthus, with regards to the projection neurons originating

559

in both the ventral lobula and lobula (40). Projection neurons from the lobula to the calyx are

560

commonly seen in visually orientated insect species, with species in Hymenoptera exhibiting

561

both lobula and medulla input (62,78–80). In Heliconiini, however, there were no clear

562

indications of direct input from the medulla to the calyx or AOTU via the AOT from the

563

injections into either the calyx or the optic lobes. This does not confirm their absence, but the

564

lack of staining observed in the medulla compared to the strong presence of the lobula

565

complex suggests there are, perhaps, different integration needs in Heliconiini in comparison

566

to other insect and Lepidoptera species. Olfactory input to the calyces in Heliconiini was also

567

consistent with that seen in other Lepidoptera, with two tracts from the antennal lobes (mALT

568

and lALT) projecting to the mushroom bodies (40,41,65) and the patterns and apparent size

569

of olfactory projections conserved between the Heliconius and outgroup Heliconiini species.

570

Although analysis of the tracts was largely observational through 3D reconstruction and

571

image analysis, and the mass staining methodology may be obscuring finer, detailed

572

differences, the lack of obvious differences suggests any changes between Heliconius and

573

the outgroup Heliconiini are minor in comparison to the large changes seen in the mushroom

574

bodies.

575

Although not the primary focus of the current study, our results on eye structure

576

suggest divergent patterns of sexual dimorphism in specific traits. Sexual dimorphism was

577

only detected in the number and size of ommatidia, and not in investment in downstream

578

neuropils, suggesting a primary importance in acuity and light capture. We also found

579

consistent sexual dimorphism in ommatidia number, and therefore visual acuity, between

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.08.663516; this version posted July 11, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

580

Heliconius and their outgroups. Sexual dimorphism was only significant in Heliconius

581

species, as previously observed (37,51), but was not evident in the three outgroup species.

582

In contrast, ommatidia size showed interspecific differences in levels of sexual dimorphism,

583

but not consistently between Heliconius and outgroups. As such, sexual dimorphism in visual

584

acuity in Heliconius may be the result of Heliconius males actively seeking out mates (51).

585

Mating behaviour in at least one outgroup species, Dryas iulia, has been suggested to

586

involve less complex recognition of conspecifics than in Heliconius (81), supporting a

587

potential link between mating behaviour and increased male visual acuity in Heliconius.

588

Notably, sexual dimorphism has previously been described in the retinal mosaic of

589

Heliconius eyes, reflecting sex differences in the combinations of opsins expressed within an

590

ommatidia among species in the Erato and Sara/Sapho groups (36,82,83). Although the

591

outgroup genera have not been studied in depth, Euiedes isabella has a sexually

592

monomorphic retinal mosaic (83). As such, our data on ommatidia number adds to evidence

593

that the Heliconius eye may be adapted to meet the demands of sex-specific behaviours.

594

Changes in ommatidia size may instead be indicative of selection towards greater photon

595

capture in male Heliconiini (69), but not in a manner that distinguishes Heliconius from

596

outgroups.

597

In summary, contrary to prior expectations, we found no increased investment in the

598

sensory structures peripheral to the mushroom bodies in Heliconius that would facilitate their

599

systematic foraging and increased visual-memory stability. Surprisingly, whilst there are still

600

open questions with regards to the morphology and associated cells of the sensory

601

projection neurons, Heliconius showed conserved, or even lower, investment in the visual

602

domain compared to the outgroup Heliconiini. We therefore infer that the expansion of the

603

mushroom bodies in Heliconius, and the associated cognitive enhancements, are the result

604

of circuit restructuring, particularly in regard to the synaptic fields within the mushroom

605

bodies (1,2). This is supported by previous evidence of increased synaptic plasticity in the

606

mushroom bodies of Heliconius over the outgroup Heliconiini species (27). The restructuring

607

of these learning and memory circuits may be facilitating a shift in the weighting of Kenyon

608

cell input sites, allowing for evolutionary changes in specific functional domains. Additionally,

609

the conservation of the sensory structures suggests that sufficient sensory processing had

610

already evolved prior to the split of Heliconius from Heliconiini, and instead highlights the

611

importance of the evolution of visual learning and long-term memory as primary adaptations

612

underpinning behavioural innovation in Heliconius foraging behaviour. Heliconiini, therefore,

613

present an interesting case of genus-specific mosaic brain expansion allowing exploitation of

614

a new niche, furthering our understanding of cognitive evolution.
