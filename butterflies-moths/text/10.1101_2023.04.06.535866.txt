Abstract

16

The selective pressures leading to the elaboration of downstream, integrative

17

processing centres, such as the mammalian neocortex or insect mushroom bodies,

18

are often unclear. In Heliconius butterflies, the mushroom bodies are three to four

19

times larger than their Heliconiini, and the largest known in Lepidoptera. Heliconiini

20

lay almost exclusively on Passiflora, which exhibit a remarkable diversity of leaf

21

shape, and it has been suggested that the mushroom body expansion of Heliconius

22

may have been driven by the cognitive demands of recognising and learning the leaf

23

shapes of local host plants. We test this hypothesis using two complementary

24

methods: i) phylogenetic comparative analyses to test whether variation in

25

mushroom body size is associated with the morphological diversity of host plants

26

exploited across the Heliconiini; and ii) shape learning experiments using six

27

Heliconiini species. We found that variation in the range of leaf morphologies used

28

by Heliconiini was not associated with mushroom body volume. Similarly, we find

29

interspecific differences in shape learning ability, but Heliconius are not overall better

30

shape learners than other Heliconiini. Together these results suggest that the visual

31

recognition and learning of host plants was not a main factor driving the diversity of

32

mushroom body size in this tribe.

Introduction

34

Patterns of investment in neural structures and pathways are shaped by the

35

cognitive demands imposed by ecology, including foraging behaviours [1–3], mate

36

detection and selection [4], and predator avoidance [5]. While increased investment

37

in the sensory centres of the brain is largely driven by the sensory conditions

38

experienced by an individual [6–10], the factors leading to the elaboration of

39

downstream, integrative processing centres, such as the mammalian neocortex or

40

insect mushroom bodies, are often less clear [11].

41

The Neotropical butterfly genus Heliconius, consisting of approximately 50

42

species, and nine closely related genera in the tribe Heliconiini (Lepidoptera:

43

Nymphalidae), provides a novel and potentially powerful comparative framework for

44

exploring the evolution of central brain structures [12]. In Heliconius, the mushroom

45

bodies are markedly expanded, being three to four times larger than is typical for

46

Lepidoptera, relative to brain size, including other Heliconiini genera ([13–15]). The

47

mushroom bodies are historically studied as olfactory learning and memory centres

48

in the predominant insect model species, Drosophila melanogaster [16–20].

49

However, across different taxonomic groups, mushroom bodies are innervated by a

50

variety of sensory projection neurons, including visual and olfactory pathways, and

51

the extent of innervation by different sensory modalities varies substantially across

52

species [21]. Indeed, across insects, the mushroom bodies play a role in many

53

visuals tasks including colour learning [22], visually-guided spatial navigation [23–

54

25], reversal learning [26,27], and non-elemental associative learning [28].

55

Variation in the size and internal structure of the mushroom body likely

56

reflects the relative importance of different sensory modalities in learnt behaviours

57

and behavioural coordination, and selection for increased precision or fidelity of

58

learnt associations. The expansion of the mushroom bodies in Heliconius therefore

59

implies a major shift in information processing capacity in this genus, and the

60

number of learnt patterns, or engrams, these butterflies can support [29]. The major

61

expansion event in Heliconius co-occurs with the emergence of a dietary innovation

62

unique amongst Lepidoptera – adult pollen feeding – and associated foraging

63

specialisations (Figure 1) [30]. In collecting pollen, Heliconius establish ‘traplines’,

64

foraging routes along which specific plants are regularly visited, suggesting a

65

sophisticated capacity for spatial memory, probably using learnt visual landmarks

66

[31–34]. The apparent cognitive demands of traplining have been hypothesised as

67

driving the elaboration of the mushroom body in Heliconius [12–15]. Consistent with

68

the explanation, the expansion of the mushroom bodies in Heliconius is primarily

69

driven by increased visual, rather than olfactory input [15]. In addition, relative to the

70

non-pollen-feeding Dryas iulia, Heliconius erato exhibit improved visual long-term

71

memory and non-elemental learning, cognitive abilities which are important for the

72

establishment and maintenance of traplines [15].

73

However, although the largest shift in mushroom body size is observed in

74

Heliconius, earlier bursts of mushroom body expansion are detected at the base of

75

the Heliconius-Eueides clade, and independently in Dryadula. Additionally, the

76

secondary loss of pollen feeding in H. aoede is not accompanied by a decrease in

77

mushroom body size [15]. Thus, pollen feeding alone does not explain all variation in

78

mushroom body size across the tribe. Increases in Heliconius mushroom body size

79

have also been speculatively linked to the cognitive demands of identifying and

80

learning host plants [35]. Herbivorous insects, which generally specialise on a limited

81

range of plants, must solve the ecological challenge of identifying suitable host

82

plants in complex, heterogenous environments [36]. Visually-oriented insects may

83

identify host plants based on either a learned or innate “search image” for particular

84

leaf shapes [37,38], with evidence for shape recognition in insects primarily deriving

85

from studies on foraging bees [39–42]. However, within butterflies, Eurema brigitta

86

exhibit a preference for leaves shaped like those of their larval host plant [43], while

87

shape learning has been demonstrated in Danaus plexippus, Battus philenor and

88

Heliconius erato in the context of oviposition and/or feeding [38,44–46].

89

Heliconius lay eggs almost exclusively on Passifloraceae, most commonly

90

Passiflora, with varying degrees of specialisation. Passiflora display a remarkable

91

diversity of leaf shape [47], and host plant use in Heliconius appears to be partly

92

based on leaf shape recognition and learning through associative conditioning [46].

93

Leaf shape, therefore, seems to act as a long-distance cue for identifying suitable

94

host plants, which are then confirmed by olfactory and gustatory signals at closer

95

distances [46]. Given that many Heliconius species can lay on a range of Passiflora,

96

there may have been selection for the ability to efficiently learn and remember leaf-

97

shape search images for the suitable host plants species that are locally common. It

98

has therefore been suggested that the diversity of Passiflora leaf shapes was driven

99

by selection to escape visual identification of host plants by Heliconius [31,35,46,48].

100

Mushroom body expansion may, therefore, support a greater array of search images

101

and enhanced shape-learning ability in Heliconius, facilitating improved visual

102

identification of local host plants [35].

103

To date, shape learning has only been assessed in a single Heliconius

104

species, H. erato, which was shown to learn shape information from both floral and

105

host plant stimuli [46]. The degree to which this ability varies across species is

106

therefore unclear. However, within butterflies, there is some evidence linking the

107

mushroom bodies to host plant use and learning. When exposed to host plant

108

environments of varying complexities, the relative generalist butterfly Polygonia c-

109

album exhibited greater mushroom body plasticity than two relative specialists,

110

Aglais io and Aglais urticae [49]. Similarly, in the cabbage white butterfly, Pieris

111

rapae, larger mushroom body calyces were associated with an improved ability to

112

locate difficult-to-learn red host plants, and experience with red hosts was positively

113

related to increased mushroom body lobe size [50]. While previous analyses suggest

114

mushroom body size in the Heliconiini is not explained by the number of host plants

115

used by a species [15], it remains possible that generalist Heliconiini are targeting

116

Passiflora species with morphologically similar leaves, meaning the number of host

117

plant species used does not necessarily represent the diversity of search images

118

used in host plant foraging.

119

Here, we therefore test whether mushroom body expansion in Heliconius is

120

related to shape identification and learning using two complementary methods: i)

121

phylogenetic comparative analyses to test whether variation in mushroom body size

122

is positively associated with the morphological diversity of host plants exploited

123

across the Heliconiini; and ii) comparative shape learning experiments across six

124

Heliconiini species, using artificial feeders. We hypothesise that, if mushroom body

125

expansion is linked to shape learning, Heliconius species would consistently

126

outperform the non-Heliconius species in this task. Although our results suggest

127

interspecific variation in the range of leaf morphologies used by Heliconiini, as well

128

as interspecific variation in shape learning ability, we find no evidence that this

129

explains the striking diversity of mushroom body size in this tribe.

Methods

132

i) Quantification of leaf morphospace and comparative analyses

133

We investigated whether the volumes of Heliconiini mushroom bodies vary with host

134

plant use, controlling for the size of the rest of the central brain, using a recently

135

published neuroanatomical dataset [15], combined with an established dataset on

136

Heliconiini host plant use [51]. In total, 36 Heliconiini species had both host plant and

137

neuroanatomical data. For these species, we used morphometric analysis to quantify

138

the morphospace of host plant leaf shapes used by Heliconiini species, following

139

[47].

140

JSTOR, the Encyclopedia of Life and the digital collections of the Muséum national

141

d'histoire naturelle, Paris, and the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew. These images were

142

cropped to images of single leaves and edited to remove the petiole and non-leaf

143

material (Figure S1). In total, we collected images for 686 leaves from 150 Passiflora

144

species and 19 leaves from 11 Dilkea species.

Leaf images were sourced from the Global Plants database managed by

145

We used SHAPE v 1.3 [52] to characterise leaf outlines with Elliptical Fourier

146

Descriptors (EFDs), following established methods [47,53–57]. Using SHAPE, we

147

binarized these images and performed a chain-code analysis (Figure S1) which we

148

used to calculate normalised EFDs based on 40 harmonics [52]. We manually

149

ensured that each leaf outline was consistently aligned. The R package Momocs v

150

1.3.2 was used to convert the resulting normalised EFD .nef file into a COE object,

151

which was then subject to principal component analysis using Momocs [58]. With the

152

R package dispRity v 1.6.0 [59] we used the first four principal components to

153

calculate the four-dimensional volume of host plant morphospace exploited for each

154

Heliconiini species. The four-dimensional morphospace volumes were then square-

155

root transformed for all further analyses to better fit a normal distribution. We then

156

conducted a series of phylogenetic GLMMs with MCMCglmm, using a recent

157

phylogenetic tree [60] to test for relationships between host plant morphospace and

158

the relative volumes of brain regions [61]. Controlling for allometric scaling with an

159

independent measure of brain volume (rest of central brain, rCBR), we tested

160

whether

161

morphospace, while also testing whether these relationships varied between

ii) Shape-learning experimental protocol

172

To complement our comparative analyses, we performed shape learning assays in

173

six Heliconiini species. All individuals used in the shape-learning experiments were

174

reared from stocks established with locally caught, wild butterflies using the

175

insectaries at the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute (STRI) in Gamboa,

176

Panama, in 2018. Stock butterflies were kept in 2x2x3 m mesh cages in ambient

177

conditions with natural light. Larvae were reared in mesh pop-ups and provided with

178

fresh leaves daily. H. erato, Dryas iulia, Dryadula phaetusa and Agraulis vanillae

179

were reared on P. biflora, H. melpomene on P. triloba, and H. hecale on P. vitifolia.

180

Training and testing of butterflies were conducted in 2x2x3 m mesh cages in ambient

181

conditions under natural light. A single Psychotria elata, with all flowers removed,

182

was placed in the rear right corner of these cages as a roosting site.

183

All individuals were freshly eclosed, to control for prior feeding experience.

184

The day after eclosion, butterflies were transferred to a pre-training cage to

185

familiarise them with the use of artificial feeders. Here, individuals were fed solely

186

with red, circular feeders (Figure S2(a)), filled with a sugar-protein solution (20%

187

sugar, 5% Vertark Critical Care Formula, 75% water, w/v) for one day. Artificial

188

feeders were made from coloured foam with a centrally placed 0.5 ml Eppendorf

189

tube. Throughout the experiment, all feeders were presented with a large, circular,

190

green background so that the silhouette of the red shapes was clearly delineated in a

191

consistent manner. After pre-training, butterflies were introduced to a testing cage to

192

determine initial preference between two shapes – a “diamond” and a “star” shape

193

(Figure S2(b)). The choice of these shapes was based on a previous study showing

194

that H. erato can distinguish between them and tend to prefer star-shaped feeders

over the diamond shape [46]. The testing cage contained 3 feeders of each shape,

196

arranged randomly for each trial, and separated from each other by 15 cm (Figure

197

S2(b)). To ensure that butterflies responded to visual cues only, feeders in the

198

testing cages were empty. Preference testing lasted for four hours from 08:00 to

199

12:00 and was filmed using a GoPro Hero 5 camera mounted to a tripod. The film

200

was then reviewed to count the number of feeding attempts per individual on each

201

shape, with up to 40 attempts recorded. A feeding attempt was only counted if the

202

butterfly landed on the feeder and probed it with its proboscis. Butterflies were then

203

placed in a training cage for ten days which contained diamond-shaped feeders filled

204

with sugar-protein solution and star-shaped feeders filled with an aversive saturated

205

quinine solution. Through this combination of positive and negative stimuli we aimed

206

to condition the butterflies to favour the diamond-shaped feeders over the star

207

shapes. This training period lasted for 10 full days to provide ample opportunity for

208

butterflies to learn [46], after which the trained feeding preferences were tested

209

following the same protocol as the initial preference test.

210

iii) Shape learning statistical analyses

211

Shape preferences and learning performance were analysed with generalised linear

212

mixed models (GLMMs) using a binomial distribution with the glmer function from the

213

package lme4 v 1.1-27.1 in R v 4.1.2 [63]. Diagnostics for these GLMMs were

214

assessed using the R package DHARMa v 0.4.4 [64]. All post hoc comparisons were

215

made by obtaining the estimated marginal means using the R package emmeans v

216

1.7.0 and were corrected for selected multiple comparisons using the Tukey test

217

[65]. Interspecific differences in initial shape preference were tested using a GLMM

218

with species as a fixed effect and an individual-level random effect. For each

219

species, we then tested whether initial preference towards a certain shape differed

220

significantly from random using a GLMM with only individual-level random effects.

221

Interspecific differences in shape learning performance were tested for using a

222

GLMM with species and training as fixed effects, with an individual level-random

223

effect. We also tested for an overall difference between Heliconius and non-

224

Heliconius individuals using a GLMM with membership in Heliconius and training as

225

fixed effects, with individual and species-level random effects. Sex was also initially

226

included as a fixed effect in these models but was non-significant and so removed.

Results

228

i) Host plant generalism and the diversity of host plant morphologies

229

Principal component analysis of normalised EFDs based on 40 harmonics revealed

230

that 93.3% of variation in Passiflora and Dilkea spp. host plant leaf shape is

231

explained by the first four principal components, accounting for 60.8%, 17.8, 11.1%

232

and 3.6% of variation, respectively (Figure 2). There was a significant positive

233

relationship between the number of host plants recorded as being used by a species

234

and the leaf-shape morphospace covered by those plants (F-value=199.8, d.f.=58,

235

P<0.0001, R2 = 0.775, Figure 3(a)). However, over 20% of the variance in

236

morphospace of host plants used by Heliconius was not explained by the number of

237

plants used by the given Heliconius species. Several marked outliers further

238

demonstrate the value of accounting for phenotypic diversity of host plants in

239

characterising the search image landscape of a butterfly species (Figure S2).

240

Overall, the morphospace volumes of host plants used by Heliconius did not

241

significantly differ from other Heliconiini (Figure 1; pMCMC = 0.120). Comparing

242

between Heliconiini genera, Agraulis host plants have a significantly larger

243

morphospace than those of Eueides, Heliconius and Philaethria (Table S1), but

244

these differences are vulnerble to correction for multiple comparisons (Table S2).

245

ii) Diversity of host plant use not associated with mushroom body size in

246

Heliconiini

247

Across all Heliconiini, we found no relationship between mushroom body size and

248

host plant morphospace (pMCMC = 0.214; Figure 3(b)). This was true both within

249

Heliconius (pMCMC = 0.876), and among non-Heliconius Heliconiini (pMCMC =

250

0.391) separately. We further explored whether investment in the medulla, the

251

largest neuropil of the optic lobe, could be associated with host plant use. Again, we

252

did not detect an association between medulla volume and host plant morphospace

253

in all Heliconiini (pMCMC = 0.818), Heliconius (pMCMC = 0.964), or the outgroup

254

Heliconiini (pMCMC = 0.940) individually.

255

Finally, we repeated our analyses within a specific Heliconiini community of

256

14 species (Figure 1) around Gamboa, Panama, and the Soberanía National Park

257

[62]. Nine Passiflora species are known to be used by this community, with

258

butterflies ranging from specialists laying on a single species to relative generalists

259

laying on five species. Neither host plant number (pMCMC=0.313) nor leaf shape

260

diversity (pMCMC=0.309) was significantly associated with investment in mushroom

261

body size.

ii) No evidence Heliconius are better shape learners than other Heliconiini

264
265

In our shape learning experiment, we assayed 162 individuals from 6 species with

266

well characterised host plant use. All six species showed a significant initial

267

preference for the star-shape feeders over the diamond feeders, with stars

268

accounting for approximately two-thirds of all feeding attempts (Figure 4, Table S3).

269

Initial shape preference did not differ between species (χ2=2.709, d.f.= 5, P=0.746).

270

Learning ability, however, varied significantly between species (χ2=18.565, d.f.= 5,

271

P=0.002; Figure 4), but Heliconius as a whole were not superior shape learners than

272

the other Heliconiini (χ2=0.128, d.f.= 1, P=0.720; Figure 4). Rather, shape learning

273

ability was scattered across the phylogeny. Agraulis vanillae, Dryadula phaetusa, H.

274

melpomene and H. erato showed a significant shift in preference towards the

275

diamond shape after training, while Dryas iulia, and H. hecale showed no shift (Table

276

S4, Figure 4). Notably, even the species that did show a significant shape-learning

277

effect only showed a slight shift in preference and still maintained a bias towards

278

stars over diamonds (Figure 4). This low effect size, which is consistent with

279

previous experiments in H. erato [46], suggests that increasing the difficulty of the

280

shape-learning task would not reveal a difference in shape-learning ability between

281

Heliconius and the outgroup Heliconiini. We also note that visual acuity is unlikely to

282

explain these results as naïve butterflies from all species show a similar preference

283

for the star-shape, suggesting that all species are capable of distinguishing between

284

these

shapes.

Discussion

287

Heliconius butterflies exhibit a dramatic expansion of the mushroom bodies, which

288

has been hypothesised to have been driven by the cognitive demands of trapline

289

foraging for pollen [15]. However, separate instances of mushroom body expansion

290

in non-pollen-feeding Heliconiini suggest that pollen feeding does not fully explain

this variation. Another hypothesis posits that mushroom body expansion in

292

Heliconius is related to the cognitive demands of identifying suitable host plants [35],

293

which relies, in part, on leaf shape learning and recognition [46]. We tested this

294

hypothesis using a combination of phylogenetic comparative methods to analyse

295

neuroanatomical and host plant morphospace data. Further, we tested shape

296

learning ability in six Heliconiini species.

297

It was previously found that, within the Heliconiini, mushroom body size is not

298

associated with host plant number [15]. However, our results show that the diversity

299

of host plant leaf shapes is largely, but not fully, explained by host plant number

300

(Figure 3(a)). Our first test of the host plant hypothesis, therefore, used phylogenetic

301

comparative analyses to test whether host plant morphospace explained any

302

variation in mushroom body size across the Heliconiini. Our results showed no

303

evidence of an association between mushroom body size and the leaf shape

304

diversity of a species’ host plants (Figure 3(b)). Additionally, interspecific variation in

305

medulla volume, the largest visual neuropil, was also not associated with host plant

306

morphospace. This suggests that the visual learning and recognition of host plants

307

was likely not an important driver of the several incidences of mushroom body

308

expansion seen in the Heliconiini [15].

330

Panamanian community. The six species assessed in this study exhibited a clear

331

ability to distinguish between shapes, all showing a marked preference for star-

332

shaped feeders over diamonds, consistent with previous experiments showing a

333

similar bias in H. erato (Figure 4) [46]. This preference is likely explained by the

334

resemblance of the star-shape to the radial symmetry of the flowers typically

335

exploited by these Heliconiini [31,67,68]. Visual cues, including “search images”,

336

therefore appear to play an important role for Heliconiini in identifying key resources

337

in their environment. Notably, naïve Heliconiini possess an inherent attraction

338

towards certain shapes, similar to previous findings in honeybees [42] and the

339

butterflies Battus philenor and Eurema brigitta [38,43].

340

Our results also corroborate previous findings of shape learning in H. erato

341

[46], while also demonstrating comparable abilities in H. melpomene and the non-

342

Heliconius Heliconiini Agraulis vanillae and Dryadula phaetusa (Figure 4). However,

343

we also found significant variation in shape-learning performance between species,

344

with neither H. hecale and or Dryas iulia showing evidence of having learned the

345

shape cue. Contrary to our prediction however, Heliconius, as a group, were not

346

superior shape learners than the non-Heliconius species (Figure 4). The lack of

347

difference in shape-learning performance between Heliconius and non-Heliconius

348

Heliconiini, suggests that mushroom body expansion in Heliconius is not associated

349

with enhanced shape learning ability. This result casts doubt over the hypothesis that

350

the expanded mushroom bodies of Heliconius are adapted for shape learning related

351

to the visual recognition of Passiflora host plants.

352

In addition, even amongst the Heliconiini species which did exhibit a

353

significant shape learning effect, the frequency of feeding attempts on diamond

354

shapes remained below 50% (Figure 4). The innate preference for the star shape

355

appears difficult to alter through associative conditioning, even when the star shape

356

is presented with an aversive stimulus. This suggests that visual identification of

357

resources in Heliconiini butterflies may be driven more by innate search images than

358

learned shapes. One important caveat, however, is that to test shape learning in an

interspecific, comparative framework, we conducted the experiment in a feeding,

360

rather than oviposition context. The Heliconiini involved in this study use of a range

361

of different host plants with varying leaf shapes and using the same shape cues for

362

each butterfly species would not have been possible in an oviposition context.

363

Additionally, testing the learning abilities of butterflies in a feeding context also has

364

the advantage of far greater tractability, permitting the sample sizes necessary for a

365

comparative behavioural experiment across six species. In some cases the

366

importance of different types of cues can be context dependent [69–71]. However,

367

the shape learning abilities of H. erato translate across both feeding and oviposition

368

contexts [46], and a similarly fixed preference to the shape preference we observe

369

was seen in the selection of host plants by H. erato phyllis, which was not able to be

370

altered by experimental conditioning [66]. This contrasts with the apparent ease with

371

which Heliconiini can learn, and modify, colour associations [72], a contrast also

372

observed in monarch butterflies [73] and honeybees [74,75] (but not, interestingly, in

373

the Hymenopteran parasitoid Microplitis croceipes [76]). We therefore suggest that

374

shape-learning performance in a feeding context can be interpreted as a measure of

375

general shape-learning ability in Heliconiini butterflies.

376

Combining the shape-learning experiment with comparative analyses of host

377

plant diversity provides a complementary, alternative test of our overriding

378

hypothesis. Together, our results do not support the hypothesis that visual

379

identification of host plants based on leaf shape has been an important factor in

380

driving MB expansion in Heliconius. The cognitive demands of traplining, therefore,

381

remains as the most likely selective pressure, driving mushroom body expansion in

382

Heliconius butterflies. Nevertheless, it is possible that Passiflora plants are

383

incorporated into Heliconius pollen traplines [31], although this has not been formally

384

observed. Further data, both from behavioural experiments, including assessments

385

of spatial learning ability, and more comprehensive field observations of traplining

386

behaviour across a wide sampling of Heliconiini species are necessary to directly

387

test these hypotheses.
