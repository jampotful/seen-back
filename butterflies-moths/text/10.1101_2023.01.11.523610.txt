Abstract

12

Animal vision is important for mediating multiple complex behaviours. In Heliconius

13

butterflies, vision guides fundamental behaviours such as oviposition, foraging and

14

mate choice. Colour vision in Heliconius involves ultraviolet (UV), blue and long-

15

wavelength sensitive photoreceptors (opsins). Additionally, Heliconius possess a

16

duplicated UV opsin, and its expression varies widely within the genus. In Heliconius

17

erato, opsin expression is sexually dimorphic; only females express both UV-sensitive

18

opsins, enabling UV wavelength discrimination. However, the ecological pressures

19

that have driven these sex-specific differences in visual perception remain unresolved.

20

Heliconius females invest heavily in finding hostplants to lay their eggs, a behaviour

21

heavily reliant on visual cues. We tested whether UV vision is used for oviposition in

22

H. erato and Heliconius himera females by manipulating the availability of UV in

23

behavioural experiments under naturalistic conditions. We found that UV did not

24

influence the number of oviposition attempts or the number of eggs laid. In addition,

25

their hostplant, Passiflora punctata, does not reflect UV wavelengths, and models of

26

H. erato female vision suggest only minimal stimulation of the UV opsins. Overall,

27

these findings suggest that UV wavelengths do not directly affect the ability of

28

Heliconius females to find suitable oviposition sites. Alternatively, UV discrimination

29

could be used in the context of foraging or mate choice, but this remains to be tested.

Introduction

31

Animal colour vision mediates a multitude of complex behaviours. Colour vision is

32

achieved via wavelength discrimination (independent of intensity), where the inputs of

33

two or more different photoreceptors (i.e., opsins, which differ in spectral sensitivity)

34

are compared (Kelber, 1999; Kelber & Pfaff, 1999). In most insects, colour vision is

35

based on three photoreceptor classes, encoded by different opsin genes, sensitive to

36

ultraviolet (UV), blue (B) and long-wavelengths (LW) (Briscoe & Chittka, 2001; Kelber,

37

2006). In contrast, the visual systems of butterflies are highly diverse with differing

38

numbers of photoreceptor classes and sensitivities among families, genera, species

39

and even between sexes within a species (Briscoe, 2008; McCulloch et al., 2017; Van

40

Der Kooi et al., 2021). Visual system diversification in butterflies and moths has

41

occurred through independent opsin duplication and has been attributed to changes

42

in light availability and habitat use (Sondhi et al. 2021).

43
44

Neotropical butterflies of the genus Heliconius are a well-studied example of

45

adaptation and speciation (Merrill et al., 2015). These butterflies possess striking wing

46

patterns that often converge between two or more distantly related species due to

47

Müllerian mimicry (Müller, 1879). Wing colours are also involved in interspecific and

48

intraspecific communication, and contribute to assortative mating (Jiggins et al., 2001;

49

Estrada & Jiggins, 2008; Melo et al., 2009; Merrill et al., 2014). However, only a few

50

studies have investigated the role of Heliconius visual systems from an ecological

51

context (Finkbeiner et al., 2017; Dell’Aglio et al., 2018), despite vision being largely

52

responsible for mediating these complex behaviours.

53
54

Vision plays a crucial role in Heliconius behaviour and guides fundamental processes

55

such as intraspecific communication (Estrada & Jiggins, 2008; Merrill et al., 2014),

56

foraging (Toure et al., 2020) and hostplant selection (Gilbert, 1982). Relative to other

57

butterflies, Heliconius have some of the largest brains and invest heavily in the visual

58

neuropile, suggesting selection for well-developed vision (Montgomery et al., 2016).

59

In addition to the ultraviolet (UVRh1), blue (BRh) and long-wavelength (LWRh)

60

sensitive opsins found in most insects, Heliconius butterflies possess a duplicated

61

ultraviolet opsin (UVRh2) (Briscoe et al., 2010). Additionally, some LWRh-expressing

62

cells possess lateral filtering pigments that shift the spectral sensitivity towards red

63

enabling Heliconius to discriminate wavelengths in the long-wavelength range

64

(Zaccardi et al., 2006; McCulloch et al., 2022).

65
66

Within the Heliconius genus, opsin expression is variable. Some species, such as

67

Heliconius melpomene, have independently lost expression of one of the two UV

68

opsins, with documented pseudogenization events (McCulloch et al., 2017).

69

Heliconius erato, on the other hand, is sexually dimorphic - males only express UVRh2

70

whereas females express UVRh1 and UVRh2 (Figure S1)(McCulloch et al., 2016,

71

2017). It is possible that in H. erato, UVRh1 is located in the W-chromosome such as

72

in H. charithonia (Chakraborty et al., 2022). A recent study investigated whether H.

73

erato was capable of discriminating UV wavelengths: Finkbeiner and Briscoe (2021)

74

tested H. erato females (which expresses UVRh1 and UVRh2), H. erato males

75

(expresses only UVRh2) and H. melpomene (both sexes express only UVRh1) in a

76

laboratory experiment and found that only H. erato females are capable of

77

discriminating between UV wavelengths. However, the ecological pressures that have

78

driven these species- and sex-specific differences in visual perception remain

79

unresolved.

80
81

While changes in opsin expression patterns within the Heliconius clade are well-

82

documented (McCulloch et al., 2016, 2017, 2022; Catalán et al., 2019), the adaptive

83

function of these changes in gene expression remains unclear. Understanding the

84

ecological pressures for UV discrimination in H. erato females may give us insight as

85

to why expression of this gene varies between and within Heliconius species. In

86

contrast to males, female Heliconius spend most of their time searching for host plants

87

for oviposition (Benson, 1978), which involves careful visual inspection (Benson, 1978;

88

Brown Jr, 1981). Females also avoid laying on hostplants where other eggs or larvae

89

are present. Indeed, some Passiflora species have evolved extra-floral nectaries that

90

reassemble yellow eggs to discourage ovipositing females, but when these egg-

91

mimics are experimentally painted green, females tend to lay more eggs (Williams &

92

Gilbert, 1981). H. erato females use leaf shape as an oviposition cue and can learn

93

new shapes, which may explain the plasticity of leaf shape in Passiflora (Dell’Aglio et

94

al., 2016). The importance of choosing suitable Passiflora vines suggests that this

95

behaviour is under strong natural selection (Jiggins, 2017), and evidence suggests

96

that Heliconius female vision plays a key role for finding suitable oviposition sites.

97

Therefore, UV wavelength discrimination in H. erato females may be an adaptation to

98

facilitate hostplant recognition, but the role of UV vision in this regard has not been

99

tested.

100
101

Here, we test whether UV vision is used for oviposition in H. erato females and its

102

closely related species Heliconius himera by manipulating the availability of UV

103

wavelengths in behavioural experiments. In particular, we address the following

104

questions: (1) Does UV light affect oviposition behaviour in H. erato cyrbia and H.

105

himera females? Given that UV is found under natural sunlight conditions, we expect

106

that reducing UV availability will lead to a reduction in the number of eggs laid. (2)

107

Does this behaviour differ between species? Compared to H. himera, H. erato has a

108

larger investment in the visual system (Montgomery & Merrill, 2017), so we predict this

109

effect to be stronger in this species. (3) Are there UV cues in the hostplant and how

110

are these perceived by females? Given that young shoots are the most nutritious parts

111

of the hostplants and where H. erato females preferentially lay eggs (Benson et al.,

112

1975; Jiggins, 2017), we predict that UV reflectance will be highest for this part of the

113

hostplant. Finally, we use visual modelling to quantify the stimulation of the H. erato

114

visual system by its hostplant P. punctata

Materials and Methods

116

Butterfly rearing and maintenance

117

Wild Heliconius erato cyrbia were caught in forests near Balsas (3°43′60′′S,

118

79°50′45′′W) and H. himera near Vilcabamba (4°15′38′′S, 79°13′21′′W), in Southern

119

Ecuador. Wild individuals were used to establish stocks at the Universidad Regional

120

Amazónica Ikiam in Tena, Ecuador. Butterfly stocks were kept in outdoor insectaries,

121

in 2 x 2 x 2.3 m cages, fed a 20% sugar solution and had access to pollen from Lantana

122

sp. and Psiguria sp. flowers. Eggs were collected regularly from hostplants (Passiflora

123

punctata - a hostplant of both species (Jiggins et al., 1997)), and the larvae were

124

individually reared in pots on fresh leaves from P. punctata. For behavioural trials we

125

tested a total of 26 H. erato cyrbia and 10 H. himera females, due to low butterfly

126

stocks.

Experimental Design

129

Experiments were conducted between November 2021 and January 2022, under

130

natural sunlight. To manipulate UV in the light environment, two experimental cages

131

(100 x 200 x 235 cm; Supplementary Figure 2) were fitted with either clear UV-blocking

132

(transmission 400-750 nm; LEE #226) or UV-transmitting filter sheets (transmission

133

300-750 nm; LEE #130). The filters were attached to the top, left and outwards-facing

134

sides of the cage in order to filter the morning sunlight coming from the southeast

135

(Supplementary Figure 2).

136
137

The experimental assay lasted six days, during which, a group of females (1-6

138

individuals) was introduced into each experimental cage. Some individuals (10 H.

139

erato cyrbia and 1 H. himera) were tested twice (thus, these individuals were tested in

140

12 trials instead of 6). All females were introduced into the experimental cages at least

141

24 hours prior to the experiment to acclimate and were confirmed to have laid eggs on

142

a P. punctata hostplant that was left in the experimental cage overnight. On the first

143

day of the behavioural experiment, the two cages were randomly assigned a light

144

treatment (UV+ or UV-), thereby controlling for changes in natural sunlight during the

145

assay by testing both treatments in parallel. The following days, each group was tested

146

with the opposite treatment, alternating until both cages had each light treatment (UV+

147

/ UV-) three times under (Figure 1C).

148
149

Before each trial, the filters were fitted to the experimental cages and a P. puctata

150

hostplant was placed at the centre of the cage. For each trial, the butterflies were

151

observed for 2h in the morning (between 8:30-12:00). Females were free to fly around

152

the cage, feed on the artificial feeders and a pollen plant, and lay eggs on the plants.

153

For each female, we recorded the number of oviposition attempts, number of eggs

154

laid, and the hostplant “part” (shoot, leaf, tendril, or stem) where the egg was laid

155

(Figure 1B). Oviposition attempts were scored as each time a butterfly landed on a

156

hostplant and moved its abdomen with the ovipositor towards the plant, and each

157

movement of the abdomen towards the plant was counted as an individual oviposition

158

attempt. Egg oviposition after an attempt was scored as an attempt and an egg. The

159

sum of the three trials with the same treatment was combined for analysis.

162

163

Inc., Dunedin, FL, USA) connected to a UV-VIS optical fibre (P400-2-UV-Vis) with a

164

165

167

168

(>50% cc.) and overcast (100% cc.). All irradiance measurements were processed

169

and visualized using the pavo 2.2.0 package (Maia et al., 2019) in R (R Core Team,

170

Reflectance spectrometry and visual modelling

174

Reflectance measurements of the hostplant P. punctata used were taken using a

175

Flame Miniature Spectrometer connected to a PX-2 xenon light source (spectral

176

range 220nm - 750nm) and a UV/Vis reflection probe (Ocean Optics Inc., Dunedin,

177

FL, USA). All reflectance measurements were standardized with a white reflectance

178

standard (Ocean Optics WS-1). For reflectance measurements, the illuminating and

179

reflection probe was placed at a 45° angle at a distance of 1 mm from the plant tissue,

180

and we recorded three measurements per plant (integration time: 2500 milliseconds

181

per scan). All reflectance measurements were then processed and visualized using

182

the pavo 2.2.0 package (Maia et al., 2019) in R (R Core Team, 2021). For each plant

183

tissue, 3 biological replicates were measured across 5 individual plants (3

184

measurements x 3 biological replicates (shoot, stem, leaf) x 5 individuals) = 45

185

measurements per plant part).

186
187

The visual perception of the hostplant was modelled with previously published H. erato

188

visual system data (McCulloch et al., 2016, 2022) using the pavo 2.2.0 package (Maia

189

et al., 2019). For the visual model, we used the following photoreceptor sensitivities of

190

H. erato females: UVRh1 λmax 355nm, UVRh2 λmax 390nm, BRh λmax 470nm, LWRh-

191

green λmax 555nm and a fifth photoreceptor class LWRh-red λmax 590nm that occurs

192

through expression of a red filtering pigment in combination with the green rhodopsin

193

(McCulloch et al., 2016, 2022). We then calculated the photoreceptor quantum catch,

194

which estimates the light captured by the visual system (Kelber et al., 2003) under

195

each experimental light environment condition (UV+ / UV-) against a green foliage

196

background (Maia et al., 2013). The quantum catches were calculated as:

Statistical analysis

204

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2021) and plots were

205

created with the ggplot2 package (Wickham, 2011) (see supplementary material for

206

script in R Markdown). We fitted generalized linear-mixed models (GLMM) with the

207

glmer function in the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) to test if oviposition behaviour

208

was affected by the presence or absence of UV, and tested how the number of

209

oviposition attempts and/or eggs laid was influenced by the fixed effects (and their

210

interactions): (i) treatment (UV+ / UV-), (ii) weather (< 50% cloud coverage / >50% cc.

211

/ 100% cc.) and (iii) species (erato / himera). Where GLMMs with Poisson distribution

212

were overdispersed, we fitted negative binomial models with the glmer.nb function in

213

the lme4 package. To avoid pseudoreplication (individuals were tested multiple times),

214

individual id was included as random factors. The random effect structure of the full

215

models was selected based on Akaike comparisons, choosing the model with the

216

lowest AIC value (ΔAIC > 4; (Sakamoto et al., 1986; Burnham & Anderson, 2004).

217

Stepwise model reduction of the fixed effects based on statistical significance

218

(Crawley, 2002) was then conducted using likelihood ratio tests (LRT) via the drop1

219

function to identify the minimum adequate statistical models. To estimate the

220

parameters of significant fixed effects, we used parametric bootstrapping (nsim= 1000,

221

pbkrtest package (Halekoh & Højsgaard, 2014)). For fixed effects with more than two

222

categories (e.g., weather), we conducted pairwise comparisons using post hoc Tukey

223

corrections with the emmeans package (Lenth et al., 2019).

Results

226

UV does not affect oviposition behaviour

227

The availability of UV wavelengths did not significantly affect the number of oviposition

228

attempts (LRT=0.0855, df=1, p= 0.39) (Figure 2A). Similarly, there were no species

229

differences in the number of oviposition attempts (LRT=0.1459, df=1, p= 0.72). Neither

230

the UV treatment (LRT=1.6258, df=1, p= 0.20) (Figure 2B) nor species identity

231

(LRT=1.0624, df=1, p= 0.31) had a significant effect on the number of eggs laid. The

232

number of oviposition attempts significantly differed by weather (LRT= 21.764, df=2,

233

p= 0.001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that females had fewer attempts

234

on days with full cloud coverage than on sunny days (Z=-2.837 p=0.0127). Weather

235

also had a significant effect on the number of laid eggs (LRT=11.641, df=2, p= 0.004);

236

more eggs were laid on sunny days (<50% cc.) than on overcast (100% cc.) days (Z=

237

-2.446, p=0.038) (Supplementary Figure 4).

Hostplant does not reflect UV and visual models show little-to-no stimulation of

275

the UV photoreceptors

276

The spectral reflectance curves of different parts (shoot, stem, leaf, and white patches

277

on the leaf) of the hostplant P. punctata are presented in Figure 4A. The observed

278

reflectance curves are likely due to the presence of light-absorbing chlorophyll in all

279

plant parts (Chappelle et al., 1992); reflectance peaks are present at ~550 nm and

280

>680nm, and there is low reflectance below 500nm, with very little reflectance in the

281

UV range (300-400 nm).

282
283

To estimate visual perception of the host plant by females in the UV-manipulated

284

treatments, we calculated the photoreceptor quantum catches for the shoots of the

285

hostplant - the part where most eggs were laid - against a green foliage background

286

under each experimental condition (UV+ / UV-) (Figure 4B). Under natural sunlight

287

(UV+), UVRh2 was minimally stimulated, while our models predicted that UVRh1 was

288

not stimulated. In UV-absent conditions (UV-), neither UV opsin (UVRh1 or UVRh2)

289

was stimulated. In contrast, the blue opsin (BRh) and the long-wavelength opsin with

290

and without filtering pigments (LWRh-green and LWRh-red) were similarly stimulated

291

under both lighting conditions. The long-wavelength receptor with red filtering

292

pigments (LWRh-red) had the highest quantum catch, followed closely by the long-

293

wavelength opsin (LWRh-green) (Figure 4B).

Discussion

299

Vision plays a crucial role in Heliconius behaviours, including mate choice (Estrada &

300

Jiggins, 2008; Merrill et al., 2014), foraging (Toure et al., 2020) and hostplant selection

301

(Gilbert, 1982). Through a gene duplication event at the base of the Heliconius genus,

302

these butterflies have gained a secondary UV-sensitive opsin (Briscoe et al., 2010;

303

Bybee et al., 2012). However, expression of this opsin varies both between and within

304

species. For example, numerous species have independently lost expression of one

305

of the two UV opsins, with documented pseudogenization events (McCulloch et al.,

306

2017). In Heliconius erato, UV opsin expression is sexually dimorphic: females

307

express both UV opsins, whereas males only express one (McCulloch et al., 2016).

308
309

In a recent laboratory study, male and female H. erato differed in UV wavelength

310

discrimination (Finkbeiner & Briscoe, 2021). However, the ecological pressures that

311

have driven these sex-specific differences in visual perception remain unresolved.

312

Given the differences in life history between male and female Heliconius butterflies,

313

we predicted that UV wavelength discrimination might benefit female-specific

314

behaviours such as oviposition. A strong body of evidence suggests the importance

315

of visual cues for finding suitable hostplants for oviposition in Heliconius females.

316

However, our experiments suggest that UV wavelength discrimination in H. erato

317

females is not an adaptation associated with oviposition behaviours.

318
319

In our experiments, the availability of UV light did not influence the number of

320

oviposition attempts or the number of eggs laid by Heliconius females. Our spectral

321

reflectance measures of the hostplant P. punctata provides a possible explanation for

322

these results. Overall, we found only minimal UV reflection in any of the hostplant parts

323

(shoot, leaf, or stem) of P. puctata where female butterflies laid eggs (Figure 4A). This

324

result is in line with the fact that UV reflectance is usually – but not always – low on

325

leaves (Archetti et al., 2009), whereas, in contrast, many flowers reflect UV (Arnold et

326

al., 2008). We also used female H. erato-specific opsin sensitivities (McCulloch et al.,

327

2016, 2022) to estimate the photoreceptor quantum catches when viewing the shoots

328

of P. punctata - where most eggs were laid - in both light conditions (UV+ & UV-).

329

Neither UV photoreceptor (UVRh1 or UVRh2) was stimulated under the UV-absent

330

conditions, and under natural sunlight (UV-present), only UVRh2 was minimally
13

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.01.11.523610; this version posted January 15, 2023. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

331

stimulated (Figure 4B). The absence of UV reflectance in the hostplant and little-to-no

332

stimulation of the UV photoreceptors suggests that UV discrimination does not directly

333

affect Heliconius female oviposition. However, it is important to note that these

334

conclusions are based on estimates of visual system stimulation which are inherently

335

limited (Dell’Aglio et al., 2018; Drewniak et al., 2020; Finkbeiner & Briscoe, 2021),

336

further highlighting the importance of our behavioural studies.

337
338

The circuitry required for UV discrimination is metabolically costly and may have trade-

339

offs with other components of colour vision (McCulloch et al., 2016). Our experiments

340

make an important contribution by ruling out the possibility that UV perception in H.

341

erato females is used during oviposition. One alternative is that H. erato females may

342

use UV discrimination to detect previously laid eggs. Because of cannibalism in

343

Heliconius larvae, females avoid ovipositing in the presence of conspecific eggs on

344

the hostplants. However, neither H. erato eggs nor Passiflora egg-mimics reflect

345

wavelengths in the UV range (300-400 nm) (Finkbeiner & Briscoe, 2021) so this

346

explanation seems unlikely.

347
348

Another possibility is that UV wavelength discrimination is used in female mate choice.

349

Studies in other butterfly groups, such as Colias and Eurema, have shown that UV

350

reflectance is used by females for conspecific recognition and mate choice (Silberglied

351

& Taylor, 1973; Kemp, 2008). In Heliconius, UV opsin duplication co-occurred with the

352

evolution of a yellow pigment (3-hydroxyDL-kynurenine) that reflects UV (Briscoe et

353

al., 2010; Bybee et al., 2012). However, there are populations of H. erato which do not

354

show these yellow patterns, and it is currently unknown whether variation in UV vision

355

exists between populations. Experiments have shown that both male and female H.

356

erato individuals prefer to approach UV+ over UV- models (Finkbeiner et al., 2017);

357

however, Heliconius females do not generally approach males to solicit mating, and

358

these experiments cannot distinguish between UV-guided mating preference

359

behaviours, or more general attraction to UV reflecting cues, which are common in

360

flowers used by these butterflies (see below). Other experiments have manipulated

361

UV reflectance on the wings of H. erato and its co-mimic H. melpomene, by applying

362

UV-blocking sunscreen, and have found that H. erato males more often approached

363

H. melpomene females when the UV signal was blocked (Dell’Aglio et al., 2018).
14

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.01.11.523610; this version posted January 15, 2023. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

364

However, this does not explain the sexual dimorphism in UV opsin expression in these

365

species. Nevertheless, visual modelling does suggest that female H. erato may be

366

able to distinguish between the yellow colours of H. erato and H. melpomene

367

(Dell’Aglio et al., 2018), so although there is little evidence that wing colours play a

368

role in female mate choice in Heliconius, it remains an intriguing hypothesis.

369
370

A more likely alternative function of UV discrimination in H. erato females could relate

371

to foraging. Many insects that forage on flowers, such as bees and butterflies, can

372

perceive UV (Briscoe & Chittka, 2001). Analysis of the reflectance of Psychotria and

373

Psiguria – two pollen plants used by H. erato – found a UV component on the

374

reflectance spectrum of their flowers (Finkbeiner & Briscoe, 2021). Female Heliconius

375

have higher nutrient requirements than males, due to egg production, and may need

376

to invest more in foraging for pollen resources. In particular, in H. charathonia, which

377

also has sexually dimorphic vision (McCulloch et al., 2017), females have been shown

378

to collect significantly more pollen than males (Mendoza-Cuenca & Macías-Ordóñez,

379

2005). Using a similar experimental design as the one used in the present study, future

380

research could investigate the function of UV discrimination in the context of foraging.

381
382

An important caveat of our study is that we used individuals from populations collected

383

from the wild on the western slopes of the Andes in southern Ecuador. Previous

384

studies, which reveal evidence of sexually dimorphic expression of the UV-opsins,

385

used H. erato petiverana individuals supplied from a breeder based in Costa Rica

386

(McCulloch et al., 2016, 2017). The same subspecies, H. e. petiverana, and supplier

387

was also used for laboratory based UV wavelength discrimination experiments

388

(Finkbeiner & Briscoe, 2021). The most recent common ancestor of the H. erato clade

389

dates to 200,000-500,000 years ago and since then, over 15 H. erato races with

390

different wing patterns have evolved (Van Belleghem et al., 2017). Gene expression

391

evolution can be rapid, and especially in visual systems (Seehausen et al., 1997;

392

Nandamuri et al., 2017). Therefore, it is possible that different H. erato populations

393

might differ in their opsin expression patterns, though this has not yet been explored.

394
395

To our knowledge, this is one of the very few studies (but see also Veen et al., 2017),

396

that have used filters to modify natural sunlight conditions in a behavioural experiment
15

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.01.11.523610; this version posted January 15, 2023. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

397

Using natural sunlight conditions as opposed to standardized artificial lighting has

398

advantages and disadvantages. First, sunlight is likely to better represent the lighting

399

conditions found in the habitats of these butterfly species and may elicit more natural

400

behaviour. However, when conducting experiments under natural sunlight conditions,

401

there is considerable variation in the light intensity (see Supplementary Figure 2).

402

Thus, an unintentional difference in light intensity may affect the results. Indeed,

403

weather significantly affected oviposition attempts and the number of eggs laid in our

404

study (Supplementary Figure 4). Specifically, compared to overcast weather,

405

butterflies made more attempts and laid more eggs on sunny days. For this reason,

406

the majority of behavioural studies that have manipulated the presence of UV in the

407

environment using UV-blocking filters have used standardized artificial lighting (Lewis

408

et al., 2000; Greenwood et al., 2002; Honkavaara et al., 2008; Hiermes et al., 2021).

409

Nevertheless, under natural conditions - particularly in rainforests - light intensity

410

varies rapidly (Endler, 1993), which will be better reflected by experiments

411

manipulating wavelength under more natural conditions such as ours.

412
413

In conclusion, Heliconius colour vision plays a crucial role during fundamental

414

behaviours, including mate choice, oviposition and foraging. In contrast to H. erato

415

males, H. erato females express two UV-sensitive opsins and can discriminate

416

between UV wavelengths, but the ecological significance of this sexual dimorphism

417

remains unresolved. Further research is required to better understand the evolutionary

418

processes that have sex-specific differences in visual perception in Heliconius.

419

Nevertheless, by manipulating the light environment under naturalistic conditions, we

420

have made an important contribution by ruling out UV perception in H. erato females

421

as an adaption relating to oviposition behaviours.

opsins with peak sensitivities: UV 390 nm (UVRh2), blue 470 nm (BRh) and long-wavelength (green)
555 nm (LWRh) (A) Females possess an additional UV opsin (UVRh1) that has a peak sensitivity at

of the green rhodopsin by a red filtering pigment (red dashed line, from McCulloch et al. 2021).
