Significance statement

35

By integrating broad surveys of wild populations, common-garden rearing, and

36

behavioral assays in Heliconius butterflies, we show that visual adaptation along

37

elevational gradients is a multi-layered process. Eye morphology, neuroanatomy,

38

and spectral sensitivity display independent, habitat-associated shifts, with peripheral

39

and neural components specifically evolving under divergent selection. Crucially,

40

behavioral tests in hybrid crosses reveal that eye and brain traits interactively

41

influence visual acuity, showing that neural expansion must accompany peripheral

42

changes to improve visual performance. This modular yet co-evolving response

43

across the visual pathway provides a flexible mechanism for sensory adaptation

44

across terrestrial environments.

Abstract

47

Sensory systems mediate the interaction between organisms and their environment,

48

but how complex sensory pathways evolve and relate to variation in perception and

49

behavior across ecological contexts, remains poorly understood, especially for

50

terrestrial taxa. Here, we investigate whole-visual-system adaptation in Heliconius

51

erato butterflies. Using continent-wide sampling, we demonstrate that within H. erato,

52

facet count significantly decreased with increasing elevation. Common-garden

53

rearing of low-elevation H. erato populations from Ecuador and their high-elevation

54

sister species, H. himera, showed that eye and brain morphology are heritable, and

55

comparisons to genomic measures of divergence indicates that this variation is due

56

to divergent selection. Parallel comparisons from Colombia involving H. chestertonii

57

(high elevation) and H. erato venus (low elevation) further revealed that eye and

58

brain morphology can evolve as independent, decoupled traits. For both locations,

59

differences in visual acuity correlated with variation in facet count. We also observed

60

parallel evolution of spectral sensitivity, with independent high-elevation populations

61

having fewer red-reflecting lateral filtering pigments. To experimentally link visual

62

system morphology to behavior, we assessed visual acuity in second-generation H.

63

erato cyrbia-H. himera hybrids. Overall, acuity was influenced by facet count, and

64

when analyzed together with brain morphology, by a positive interaction between

65

facet count and optic lobe volume, demonstrating that structural investment in the

66

eye and neural expansion combine to maximize visual perception. This work shows

67

that visual adaptation is a multi-layered process whereby sensory traits can evolve

68

independently under localized ecological pressures, but evolution across the visual

69

pathway contributes to refinements in behavioral performance.

Introduction

72

Understanding how populations adapt and diverge is a central theme of evolutionary

73

biology. While focus often falls on morphological traits, the interface between any

74

individual and its environment is mediated by its sensory systems. In many animals,

75

the visual system plays an integral role in dictating how individuals perceive and

76

interact with their surroundings, directly shaping both survival and reproductive

77

success. Consequently, natural selection is expected to favor divergent visual traits

78

in populations exposed to different sensory conditions, or which exploit different

79

resources. Habitat-associated variation in visual traits is well-documented in aquatic

80

taxa, influencing both local adaptation and species divergence (1, 2). However,

81

although similar processes are expected on land (3), sensory conditions are more

82

variable than in water, and fewer examples of visual and behavioral divergence

83

between terrestrial species are known (3). Furthermore, how selection shapes

84

adaptation across different levels of the visual pathway is rarely investigated (but see

85

(4, 5)), and formal links to changes in behavior limit clarity over the behavioural

86

importance of case studies of sensory evolution. Addressing both these limitations is

87

crucial to fully appreciate how visual adaptations track changes in local ecology, and

88

how this may contribute to species divergence.

89

Neotropical Heliconius butterflies offer an excellent opportunity to determine

90

how terrestrial environments may shape different components of the visual pathway.

91

These butterflies are found throughout tropical and subtropical regions of the

92

Americas, inhabiting variable forest habitats (6), and are well known for their mimetic

93

warning patterns. Because these patterns experience strong divergent selection due

94

to predation (7, 8), and are also used during mate choice (9–13), their divergence

95

has been considered a key step during Heliconius speciation (14). However, the

96

maintenance of within-species geographic morphs suggests that color pattern

97

divergence must be coupled with additional adaptations and/or ecological transitions

98

to permit species divergence.

99

Heliconius rely on vision for a number of key ecological tasks, including

100

foraging and host plant selection (15, 16), in addition to mate choice. As in all

101

insects, visual signal integration begins with the compound eye that is comprised of

102

numerous independent photosensitive units (ommatidia) that receive light

103

information and transfer it to the brain. While opsin expression shows primarily

104

clade-level shifts, diverging between lineages separated by ~5-10my and remaining

105

more conserved at shallower phylogenetic scales (17–19), eye morphology in

106

Heliconius appears to differ between closely related species inhabiting different

107

forest types. For example, compared to H. melpomene, which is found at the forest

108

edge, the closed canopy specialist, H. cydno, has larger eyes with more facets (the

109

hexagonal lens of each ommatidia) (20–22). These differences mirror adaptations

110

within the brain, as H. cydno also has increased investment in several visual

111

neuropils compared to H. melpomene (20, 23). This suggests that selection is

112

distributed across the visual pathway, but whether the visual system evolves as a

113

unified system or as a mosaic of distinct adaptations, and how these vary more

114

broadly across habitat types both within and between species, is unresolved.

115

Here, we use an integrative approach to test how whole visual system

116

adaptation may influence behavioral evolution and species divergence within the H.

117

erato species complex. H. erato populations occur widely from sea level to ~1600m

118

(24), an elevational gradient characterized by shifts in canopy height, gap density,

119

and understory light availability (25), and previously linked to genomic and

120

phenotypic divergence (26–31). In particular, two high-elevation species within the

erato group, H. himera and H. chestertonii, have reduced investment in several

122

visual neuropils compared to parapatric lower-elevation H. erato populations (32,

123

33). Building on this, we generate eye morphology data from wild H. erato

124

populations spanning Central and South America to test for general effects of

125

elevation. We then explore whether divergence in multiple visual system traits is

126

produced by adaptive processes, and test how these traits together predict variation

127

in visual acuity. Collectively, our data show that visual adaptation in Heliconius is a

128

multi-layered process whereby different traits can evolve independently, but

129

coordination across components is required to increase visual acuity. Such modular

130

sensory architectures provide a highly responsive pathway for generating ecological

131

divergence.

Results

134

Elevation predicts variation in facet count. To begin to investigate the evolution of

135

the visual system in H. erato and its related species, we quantified eye morphology

136

of 314 wild butterflies from 32 sampling locations (Fig. 1A, B; Table S1). Facet count

137

was strongly predictive of overall corneal area across all populations (R2 = 0.81, p

138

<0.001, Fig. 1C), suggesting that eye size variation within the erato species complex

139

is primarily driven by changes in facet count, rather than facet size, with one

140

exception, H. erato venus (see below; otherwise analyses of corneal area were

141

quantitatively similar and are presented in supplementary information). For all

142

analyses presented below, we always account for sex and an allometric control (i.e.

143

tibia length or central brain volume) unless otherwise specified (see methods for full

144

model descriptions).

148

149

150

151

152

153

154

155

156

Compared to the other sampled populations, H. himera also stood out as having the

157

fewest facets (Fig. 1E).

158

Because elevation correlates with changes in climate and habitat type (25,

159

35), we further explored how facet count was influenced by the local environment.

160

We modeled the relationships among forest cover and mean annual temperature,

161

precipitation, and solar radiation and evaluated their effects on facet count

162

independently of elevation (all four variables were negatively correlated with

163

elevation; Fig. S4). Our structural equation model was well-specified (Fisher’s C =

164

165

166

solar radiation and forest cover, a measure of habitat continuity, had no significant

167

effects (p <0.2; Figs. S5C, D). Together, these results show that variation in H. erato

168

eye size is largely due to differences in facet count, and that higher elevations are

169

associated with fewer facets. Elevation is likely a composite variable representing

Heritable changes in the visual system of H. himera are likely driven by

187

selection. Having established population differences in eye morphology across the

188

erato group, most notably in the high-elevation specialist H. himera, we next

189

examined whether this variation is heritable, as previously reported for neural

190

investment (33). Because population-level differences in sensory phenotypes are

191

likely influenced by both genetic and environmental effects, we reared H. himera

192

alongside H. erato from lower elevations under common-garden conditions in

193

Ecuador. To further rule out that the differences we observe are not due to shared

194

evolutionary history, we included both H. erato cyrbia (western clade) and H. erato

195

lativitta (eastern clade) in our analyses (Table S2). Importantly, H. himera (eastern

196

clade) is more closely related to H. erato lativitta than to H. erato cyrbia (Fig. 1B;

197

(34)), though it meets both along narrow contact zones (36), and H. erato lativitta

198

and H. erato cyrbia live in similar habitats (Figs. 2A, B).

199

200

217

To test for selection acting across the visual system, we calculated estimates

218

of PST for eye and brain morphology in our insectary populations. PST is a phenotypic

219

analogue of FST that measures variation among populations relative to total variance

220

across populations (37). Comparisons between PST and FST can be used as a test of

221

divergent selection, where PST values that exceed genome-wide FST suggest greater

222

phenotypic divergence than expected by neutral genetic divergence (37). Inferences

223

made from PST can be vulnerable to underlying assumptions regarding trait

224

heritability (h2) and the proportion of the total variance that is presumed to be due to

225

additive genetic effects across populations (c) (37). Heritability estimates for eye and

226

brain morphology are unknown for Heliconius, so we used a wide range of c/h2 ratios

227

to test different quantitative genetic scenarios (see methods for complete

228

description). After accounting for allometric effects, facet count PST exceeded the

229

95th percentile of genome-wide FST in all but one scenario for H. erato cyrbia vs H.

230

himera (Fig. 2E; Table S10), and comparisons with the individual neuropils indicate

231

that the signal of selection is strongest for the medulla and, to a lesser extent, the

232

lobula (Table S13). Optic lobe PST also exceeded the 95th percentile of genome-wide

Components of the visual system can evolve independently. The second high-

261

elevation specialist included in our wild survey, H. chestertonii, also tended to have

262

fewer facets than the lower-elevation eratos (Fig. S1), though this was far less

263

pronounced than for H. himera (perhaps due to a less extreme shift in habitat (29)).

264

Previous results showed an adaptive shift in visual neuropil investment between H.

265

chestertonii and its lower elevation relative, H. erato venus (Fig. 3C), with which it

266

shares a contact zone in western Colombia (29, 32). These shifts mirror those in H.

267

himera suggesting a degree of parallel evolution in neuropil investment (33). In

268

contrast, we observed no significant differences in facet count for wild or insectary-

269

reared H. chestertonii and H. erato venus (p >0.2, Figs. 3C, S7; see supplementary

270

271

272

venus had significantly larger corneal area than H. chestertonii (p <0.001; Tables

273

S18-S19). This seems to reflect a broader shift in H. erato venus, in which the

274

275

276

277

278

279

venus has evolved an increase in mean facet size.

Elevation-associated visual system divergence extends to spectral sensitivity.

While natural selection is expected to shape visual systems, how distinct

403

components of the visual pathway co-vary to drive divergence across terrestrial

404

populations has remained largely untested. By leveraging continental-scale sampling

405

and our ability to raise and cross ecologically divergent Heliconius populations, our

406

results reveal heritable variation both within and between species across multiple

407

components within the visual system. Our population comparisons show that these

408

different components can evolve independently, but together are broadly associated

409

with elevation, representing a major axis of ecological transition. Finally, both eye

410

and brain morphology, independent of body size, shape visual acuity, likely

411

influencing visual perception across diverse forest types.

412

Elevation represents a major ecological transition, especially in the Andean

413

tropics (25, 35), influencing the visual environment in which animals must perform

414

key behaviors. Across Heliconius erato populations, we saw a striking reduction in

415

facet count with increasing elevation. This negative correlation aligns with a growing

416

body of evidence documenting altitudinal adaptations across this genus (26–29, 31),

417

including reduced visual neuropil investment in the high-elevation specialists H.

418

himera and H. chestertonii (32, 33). Crucially, the reduction in peripheral visual

419

investment we observe cannot be explained by allometric scaling (Fig. S3), but

420

instead reflects a targeted, adaptive down-scaling of the visual system at higher

421

elevations. Although elevation was a significant predictor of facet count, we expect

422

that elevation per se is not the causal factor influencing peripheral morphology.

423

Instead, elevation likely acts as a proxy for habitat-type, with concurrent changes in

424

several environmental factors that together influence the local forest habitat (Fig.

425

S4). The significant effects of temperature and precipitation suggest that other micro-

426

climatic or biotic variables not captured in our model—such as plant density,

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.27.747543; this version posted August 28, 2026. The copyright holder for this
preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

427

vegetation type, or the ambient light spectra—may be the proximate drivers of facet

428

count divergence (Fig. S5).

429

We also provide evidence for elevation-associated variation in lateral filtering

430

pigments: both high-elevation residents, H. himera and H. chestertonii, possessed

431

fewer red ommatidia than the lower-elevation populations. Within the erato species

432

complex studied here, this variation may reflect adaptation to the local light

433

conditions. Compared to open habitats, forested environments possess

434

proportionally more long-wavelength light (6), and the greater abundance of lateral

435

filtering pigments among lower-elevation H. erato may tune spectral sensitivity to the

436

wavelengths most prevalent in forests. Increased long-wavelength sensitivity may

437

also amplify color contrast against a complex forest background (45). However, this

438

is somewhat difficult to reconcile with results from more distantly related Heliconius

439

(17), or other Nymphalids (5), where species in more open habitats have more red-

440

reflecting lateral filtering pigments. Possibly, increased filtering pigments allows

441

better color discrimination in Heliconius, which may be beneficial for males that rely

442

on colour to find suitable mates. This could be particularly valuable within more

443

diverse lower-elevation, forest-edge communities (10), inhabited by both H. erato

444

and H. melpomene, where males must discriminate among females of multiple co-

445

occurring Heliconius species. Regardless, these contrasting patterns suggest that

446

the adaptive value of filtering pigments may vary among lineages, even within

447

Heliconius, depending on their interaction with species-specific ecology and

448

behavior.

449

450

and reduced investment in several optic neuropils in comparisons involving both wild

451

and insectary-reared butterflies. These heritable differences seem to represent

453

PST exceeded genome-wide FST across a broad range of quantitative genetic

454

scenarios, and previous results implicating selection during Heliconius eye (20) and

455

brain evolution (23, 32). However, our results also suggest that the peripheral eye

456

morphology and visual neuropils can evolve as independent, decoupled traits.

457

Although shifts in neuroanatomy have been identified between high-elevation H.

458

chestertonii and low-elevation H. erato venus from Colombia (32), we did not

459

observe any corresponding shift in facet count. Notably, H. erato venus has

460

significantly larger corneal areas, as well as a pronounced grade shift in the

461

relationship between corneal area and facet count, relative to all other populations

462

tested (Fig. S8). While we did not measure individual facet diameters, this strongly

463

implies a larger mean facet size for H. erato venus, perhaps reflecting an alternative

464

sensory strategy. Unlike the other populations included in our study, H. erato venus

465

was sampled from coastal habitats. This environment potentially introduces several

466

visual challenges—such as high-intensity surface glare from water and sand,

467

alongside abrupt spatial transitions between deep forest shade and open

468

shorelines—that are largely absent in interior tropical rainforests. These conditions

469

may favor larger facets to optimize light capture and, together with higher lateral

470

filtering abundance, may help maximize the visual signal-to-noise ratio (46) in this

471

coastal environment. Thus, while clear evolutionary patterns exist within the visual

472

system, our results highlight how localized ecological pressures can drive complex

473

and divergent sensory strategies. Future sampling of other coastal Heliconius

474

populations and/or H. erato venus from additional areas (24) will help to explore

475

these patterns further.

479

481

482

483

484

Possibly, this is because the peripheral optics represent the first limiting step in the

485

486

quality of information captured by the external receptors.

487

To directly test the relationship between visual acuity and visual system

488

morphology, we evaluated second-generation hybrids between H. erato cyrbia and

489

490

491

physiological models of compound eyes (48), and representing the first large-scale

492

493

494

495

496

497

498

499

500

involved in motion vision (50, 51). Our analyses also suggest that the accessory

505

506

507

508

Tests of selection

609

As in prior work (20), we calculated PST values using pst() (Pstat package (59)) and

610

used hind tibia length for allometric corrections [res()]. PST calculations include the

611

ratio c/h2, where c represents the proportion of the total variance due to additive

612

genetic effects across populations and h2 is heritability (37). Heritability estimates for

613

Heliconius eye and brain morphology are unknown, so in addition to the default

614

value of 1 (common for morphological traits (37)), we used c/h2 ratios ranging from

615

0.33 to 4 (as in (20, 23)).

616

The distribution of windowed FST values at 4-fold degenerate sites derives

617

from genome-wide polymorphism data including wild individuals of H. erato cyrbia (n

618

= 15), H. erato lativitta (n = 27), and H. himera (n = 31) (Table S9). Illumina short

619

reads were processed with bbduk from BBMap/BBTools (60) for quality and adaptor

620

trimming and mapped to the H. erato demophoon reference genome (v1) (61;

621

LepBase, downloaded 29 August 2023) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) (62). Only

622

properly paired reads with a mapping quality (MAPQ) ≥20 were retained. Duplicated

623

reads were subsequently removed using the MarkDuplicates function from Picard

624

Tools (v2.0.1) (63). Variant calling was conducted using Freebayes (v1.0.2) (64).

Visual acuity

640

We used an optomotor assay to measure behavioural visual acuity (42). The device

641

consisted of interchangeable visual stimuli of alternating vertical black and white

642

stripes that rotated around a fixed base. The width of one cycle (a set of alternating

643

black and white stripes) was calculated as cycle width (mm) = [(C/360) / a], where ‘C’

644

is the circumference of the experimental arena and ‘a’ is the intended visual acuity in

645

cycles-per-degree (c.p.d.) (69). For most butterflies (n = 563), we used stimuli with

646

spatial frequencies of 0.3 c.p.d. (cycle width = 9.95 mm) to 1.0 c.p.d. (cycle width =

647

2.98 mm), though 16 hybrids were opportunistically tested to 1.6 c.p.d. (cycle width =

648

1.87 mm). For each trial, responsiveness was first confirmed at 0.3 c.p.d. As visual

649

acuity depends on the distance between the stimulus and the perceiver, all

butterflies were restrained in a clear PLEXIGLAS cylinder (4 cm radius; 15 cm

653

scored if the butterfly changed orientation of its head/antenna in the direction of the

654

655

were confirmed from videos, and butterflies with unclear responses were excluded

Eyeshine

659

Eyeshine measurements followed previously established protocols (4, 5). In short,

660

butterflies, held in a slotted plastic tube and immobilized with plasticine, were

661

suspended from the arm of a micromanipulator on a goniometric cradle, and the

662

663

on the cornea until the optical axes of several ommatidia were directly facing the

664

objective lens (10×0.25 NA; Plan N, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), thus viewing the

665

pseudopupil. After dark adaptation, a broadband (470–850 nm) LED (MBB1F1

666

Thorlabs, Munich, Germany) was switched on to reveal eyeshine. The light was

667

projected along an optical fiber, and a beam-splitter (Thorlabs, Newton, MA, USA)

668

provided co-axial illumination to the eye and attached UI-3590CP-C-HQ-R2 camera

669

with a CMOS colour sensor (Imaging Development Systems, Germany). Eyeshine

670

was recorded with the uEye Cockpit software (IDS Software Suite 4.95) and

671

quantified as the proportion of red ommatidia.

Eye and brain morphology. We used linear mixed models [lmer()] in R to explore

675

how wild H. erato eye morphology (facet count or corneal area) varied across

676

677

random effect). For comparisons within Ecuador and Colombia (wild and insectary-

678

reared), we used linear models [lm()] to test how eye morphology or neuropil volume

679

differed between populations, while accounting for sex and an allometric control

680

(tibia length or rCBR). For analyses including cyrbia-himera hybrids, we also

681

included family identify as a random effect in the lmer() models. In all comparisons,

682

log10-transformations were used to normalize the residuals around the allometric

683

relationships to meet the assumptions of normality (70). Model assumptions were

684

confirmed via visual inspection (residual vs. fitted and normal Q-Q plots). The

685

significance of fixed effect parameters was determined by likelihood ratio tests via

686

drop1(), and minimum adequate models (MAM) were selected using statistical

687

significance (71, 72). We used Anova() (car package (73)) to estimate significant

688

fixed effect parameters, and in the case of more than two categories per fixed effect

689

parameter, we conducted posthoc comparisons with pairs() (emmeans package

690

(74)) and report p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons (adjust = “bonferroni”).

691

To accurately visualize multiple significant fixed effects, we extracted and plotted the

692

estimated marginal means from each MAM using the emmeans package (74).

693

We also explored whether the scaling relationships between facet count and

694

corneal area differed for H. erato venus vs the other eratos (H. erato venus had

695

709

on facet count, we implemented a piecewise structural equation modeling framework

710

[psem() (76, 77)]. For each sampled butterfly (GPS coordinates per individual), we

711

incorporated mean annual temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation from

712

713

714

718

720

721

722

726

727

728

729

730

731

732

6). For these butterflies, we applied right censoring when visual acuity = 1.6 c.p.d.

734

735

736

737

738

739

740
