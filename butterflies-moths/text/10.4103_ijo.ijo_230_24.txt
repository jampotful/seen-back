Performance of a handheld ultrasound device in detecting retinal detachments.

# Performance of a handheld ultrasound device in detecting retinal detachments

## Abstract
AbstractPurpose:To assess the accuracy and performance of the Butterfly IQ+ handheld ultrasound (HHUS) in detecting retinal detachments (RDs).Methods:A cross-sectional observational study of eyes with (n = 20) and without (n = 20) RD imaged using a conventional B-scan ophthalmic ultrasound device Ellex Eye Cubed by Clarion Medical Technologies (Cambridge, ON, CA) and a portable HHUS (Butterfly IQ+). Images were compared between the modalities for qualitative differences. Agreement between the HHUS and standard ultrasonography equipment and with clinical examination was quantified using Cohen's kappa coefficient.Results:Forty eyes of 33 patients were included in the study. Twenty eyes of 18 patients had RDs, and 20 eyes of 18 patients did not. The observed agreement in RD diagnosis between the Butterfly and the Ellex Eye Cubed was 97.5%., and the Cohen's Kappa was 0.950 (95% CI: 0.85–1.00). The observed agreement in RD diagnosis between the Butterfly IQ+ and diagnosis confirmed by the gold standard of clinical exam was 92.5%, and Cohen's Kappa was 0.850 (95% CI: 0.69–1.00). The sensitivity and specificity of the Butterfly ultrasound in RD diagnosis were 90% (95% CI: 68.3–98.8%) and 95% (95% CI: 83.2–100), respectively.Conclusion:There was a high degree of agreement between the systems for the identification of RD. The Butterfly IQ's extreme portability and ease of use make it a viable option to screen for RDs in hospital-based consults, emergency departments, or low-resource settings.Keywords: B-scan, Butterfly IQ+, handheld ultrasound, retinal detachment
Ophthalmic B-scan ultrasound is frequently used in ophthalmology departments to help better identify and confirm ocular pathology, such as retinal detachments (RDs). Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) devices in the emergency department setting similarly aid in the detection of RDs,[1,2,3] with average sensitivity and specificity of 94.2% and 96.3%, respectively.[1] However, both ophthalmic B-scan and POCUS devices are cart-based systems and have limitations in portability and cost. The newer handheld ultrasound device, the Butterfly IQ+ (Butterfly Network Ink), allows for a much smaller footprint in the form of a single handheld transducer that connects to a smartphone or tablet display and is available at a significantly lower cost [Fig. 1].
Butterfly probe with Smartphone application (Image from Butterfly Network)
The Butterfly IQ+ probe is primarily targeted at emergency medicine applications with only two studies thus far in ophthalmology. One study compared the Butterfly IQ+ to the Ellex Eye Cubed ophthalmic B-scan ultrasound device and showed similar accuracy distance measurements greater than 200 microns in phantom eye models.[4] The second study compared the image detail, image resolution, image quality, and diagnostic confidence of the Butterfly IQ+ versus the Accutome B-Scan Pro in healthy eyes and found no significant difference in ratings between devices except for in image detail.[5] These initial studies suggest early promise for the use of Butterfly IQ+ in ophthalmic settings, and our objective was to further assess its feasibility for ocular use by determining its accuracy in detecting RDs.

## Methods
This research was approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board under STUDY00014817. This cross-sectional observational study of eyes of patients at Harborview Ophthalmology clinics compared the use of a conventional B-scan ophthalmic ultrasound device, Ellex Eye Cubed, by Clarion Medical Technologies (Cambridge, ON, CA), with a portable POCUS device (Butterfly IQ+) in the detection of RDs. This was not a sponsored study, and there are no financial implications for the authors from this study. No author is associated with the manufacturing company in any manner.
Study participants included consenting patients at least 18 years old presenting to Harborview Medical Center/University of Washington ophthalmology clinics over a 6-week period from February 14 through April 24, 2022 with concern for RD. Patients were excluded if there was any suspicion of open globe injury, corneal thinning, or perforation.
The participants received a standardized ultrasound exam of one or both eyes by an ophthalmologist with both the Butterfly IQ+ and Ellex Eye Cubed during the single clinic visit. The ophthalmologist was a resident ophthalmologist trained by a retina specialist in ophthalmic ultrasound imaging. The ophthalmic setting for the Butterfly IQ+ probe was used for image acquisition, with an ISPTA of 44 mW/cm2 and a thermal index (TI) of 0.289. Axial scan still images in primary gaze and transverse scans of each of the cardinal directions (T12, T3, T6, and T9) were first recorded, with the transducer positioned in the horizontal positions when imaging the superior and inferior portions of the globe, and the transducer positioned in the vertical position when imaging the temporal and nasal aspects of the globe. The gain of each device was adjusted by the operator to obtain optimal visualization of the globe. The gain was not adjusted according to the other device. Dynamic videos of the eye moving superior to inferior and back, as well as right to left and back, were recorded, with the transducer positioned transverse to the imaged portion of the globe. Images and videos were stored in a HIPAA-compliant file. Each patient's ophthalmic diagnosis – determined by clinical exam and confirmed by operating room exam if available – was also recorded.
A masked ophthalmologist reader was trained using sample still images and dynamic scans of the following: eyes with no vitreoretinal pathology, eyes with rhegmatogenous, tractional and funnel RDs, eyes with posterior vitreous detachment, and eyes with vitreous hemorrhage. Images and videos from the two ultrasound devices were deidentified and assessed by the trained, masked reader for the presence or absence of a RD. The trained, masked reader determined the presence of the RD by using the characteristics of the scans, including the presence of a thick hyperechoic membrane (similar in echogenicity to choroid/sclera) that was attached at the optic nerve, with less mobility than posterior vitreous detachment in dynamic scans.
Agreement in RD diagnosis between conventional ophthalmic B-scan ultrasound versus the Butterfly IQ+ was calculated using Cohen's Kappa. Agreement in RD diagnosis between the Butterfly IQ+ and clinical or operating room diagnosis was also calculated. The sensitivity and specificity of the Butterfly IQ+ for detecting RD as found on clinical exam were also reported.
Statistical analysis was performed using Prism Graphpad (San Diego, CA, USA) to calculate agreement in the detection of RD between the two ultrasound devices and between the Butterfly IQ+ and clinical exam using Cohen's Kappa test.

## Results
The demographics of the study participants are shown in Table 1. There were 40 total eyes of 33 patients. Twenty eyes of 18 patients had RDs, and 20 eyes of 18 patients did not. The mean overall age was 53.3 years; the mean age for the patients with RDs was 52.6 years, and the mean age for patients without RD was 54.3 years. There were a similar number of males and females in each group. Of those who did not have RDs, clinical diagnoses included anterior segment pathology obscuring view to the posterior pole (9), vitreous hemorrhage (2), vitritis (2), proliferative diabetic retinopathy (2), posterior vitreous detachment (2), macular hole (1), and no abnormal ocular findings on clinical exam (3). Of those with RDs, seven had vitreous opacities and two had anterior segment pathology obscuring adequate view to the periphery.

The agreement in RD diagnosis between the ophthalmic B-scan ultrasound (Ellex Eye Cubed) and the Butterfly IQ+ probe is shown in Table 2. There were 18 eyes with RDs identified by both the Butterfly IQ+ and the Ellex Eye Cubed, one eye with detachment identified by the Ellex Eye Cubed that was not identified by the Butterfly IQ+, and 21 eyes without identified RDs by either device. The observed agreement was 97.5%, compared to the expected agreement by chance of 50%. The calculated Cohen's Kappa was 0.950.

The agreement in RD diagnosis between the Butterfly IQ+ and diagnosis confirmed by the gold standard of clinical exam is shown in Table 3. Operating room visualization provided further verification of diagnosis in all these eyes except for the three eyes with inoperable funnel RDs. The Butterfly IQ+ identified 18 RDs that were confirmed on exam, missed two shallow RDs identified on exam, and determined absence of RD in 20 eyes that examination confirmed were without RD. The observed agreement was 92.5%, and Cohen's Kappa was 0.850. The sensitivity of the Butterfly ultrasound was 90%, and the specificity at 95%; this was identical to the sensitivity of the Ellex Eye Cubed in RD diagnosis for the same sample set. The Butterfly IQ+ also produced greater specificity as the Ellex Eye Cubed produced one false positive RD diagnosis that the Butterfly IQ+ did not.

## Discussion
While prior studies have examined the use of the Butterfly IQ+ in phantom eye models[4] and healthy eyes,[5] we evaluated the accuracy of the Butterfly IQ+ in detecting retinal pathology. The Butterfly IQ+ was as effective as the conventional ophthalmic B-scan in identifying the presence of RD in our study group. There was high observed agreement and Cohen's Kappa in the identification of RD for the Butterfly IQ+ compared to both the Ellex Eye Cubed and exam-based diagnosis. Moreover, the sensitivity of both devices was identical (90%), and the Butterfly IQ+ produced an even greater level of specificity (100%) than the Ellex Eye Cubed (95%). These findings were determined despite a notable difference in observed image quality between the two devices. This may be because exquisite image quality is not actually necessary in the identification of a finding like RDs, which tend to be well-demarcated, discreet, and hyperechoic.
While the resolution and quality of Butterfly IQ+ images do not match that of conventional B-scan, they are comparable to traditional POCUS devices used in the emergency department settings. Despite the lack of ophthalmic design specialization of these devices, a meta-analysis by Gottlieb et al.[1] revealed that they can detect RDs with a high degree of sensitivity (94.2%, 95% CI: 78.4%–98.6%) and specificity (96.3%, 95% CI: 89.2%–98.8%). The Butterfly IQ+ is similar to these traditional POCUS devices and has the added benefit of portability and affordability for use in the emergency department setting. It has also been evaluated in the emergency department and other settings for use in cardiac, lung, gastrointestinal, obstetric, musculoskeletal, vascular, and central nervous system applications.[6,7,8,9,10]
Study limitations overlap with the limitations of the Butterfly IQ+ probe itself. For one, the performance of any ultrasound device is operator- and reader-dependent. This is particularly true for the Butterfly IQ+ as the head of the probe is not designed specifically for ocular use and is large relative to the surface of most participant eyes. In this study, both the primary ultrasound operator and the trained reader had significant experience with using and interpreting scans from ophthalmic B-scan devices. The use of a handheld POCUS device such as the Butterfly IQ+ may have more limited use among physicians without similar ultrasound experience, but under appropriate training, technicians or physicians would be able to acquire images allowing for diagnosis of RDs without specialized equipment or other ophthalmic training. The intuitive accompanying smartphone application with informative training videos, simple controls, and encrypted cloud storage allow for accessibility and ease of use in many clinical settings. The smartphone application allows for real-time sharing of videos, leading to potential applications in telemedicine in which non-ophthalmologists could share findings with specialists.

## Conclusion
The use of the Butterfly IQ+ single probe for multiple settings allows it to be a versatile tool in low-resource emergency settings,[6] and its utility in the detection of RDs adds to its versatility. While the clinical exam remains the mainstay of detection of RDs, the ability of POCUS devices allows for non-ophthalmologists to triage patients better appropriately with concern for RDs and to communicate these findings with outside ophthalmologists, particularly in settings where fundus photography is not available or is inadequate to visualize the periphery. In addition, in cases where the presence or absence of an RD cannot be ascertained by clinical exam or fundus photography alone, such as in dense cataracts or vitreous hemorrhages, our data support the ability of the Butterfly IQ+ to be used to detect RD, similar to the use of ophthalmic B-scan. The Butterfly IQ+'s portability, ease of use, and lower price make it a viable option to screen for RDs in emergency or low-resource settings in which ophthalmologists are not readily available, as well as in hospital consult settings where ophthalmic B-scan is not readily available.
