Many animal species remain separate not because they fail to produce viable hybrids,

15

but because their individuals “choose” not to mate. However, we still know very little of

16

the genetic mechanisms underlying changes in these mate preference behaviours.

17

Heliconius butterflies display bright warning patterns, which they also use to recognize

18

conspecifics. Here, we couple QTL for divergence in visual preference behaviours with

19

population genomic and gene expression analyses of neural tissue (central brain, optic

20

lobes and ommatidia) across development in two sympatric Heliconius species. Within a

21

region containing 200 genes, we identify five genes that are strongly associated with

22

divergent visual preferences. Three of these have previously been implicated in key

23

components of neural signalling (specifically an ionotropic glutamate receptor and two

24

regucalcins), and overall our candidates suggest shifts in behaviour involve changes in

25

visual integration or processing. This would allow preference evolution without altering

26

perception of the wider environment.

27
28

The evolution and maintenance of new animal species often relies on the emergence of

29

divergent mating preferences1,2. Changes in sensory perception or other neural systems must

30

underlie differences in innate behaviours between species, and will ultimately have a genetic

32

33

34

these genes will provide an important route towards understanding how behavioural

35

36

Previous studies of isolating preference behaviours have largely been limited to the

37

identification of causal genomic regions, which almost invariably contain many genes6,7,8.

38

Only a handful of studies have identified likely candidate genes that contribute to species

39

behavioural preferences. These are largely limited to chemosensory-guided mating

40

preferences9,10,11 but see12, and have identified changes at chemoreceptor genes. To our

41

knowledge, only two studies – in incipient fish species – have identified candidates for visual

42

preference evolution, albeit indirectly, both suggesting a role for sensory perception mediated

43

by changes in the peripheral visual system (e.g. opsins)13,14. Whether or not visual preference

44

evolution generally involves shifts at the sensory periphery, or in downstream processing,

45

remains unknown.

46

The closely related species Heliconius melpomene and H. cydno differ in warning

47

patterns, which are both under disruptive selection due to mimicry15 and are important mating

48

cues16. As a result, these divergent patterns couple ecological and behavioural components of

49

reproductive isolation, which (as predicted by so-called “magic trait” models17) is expected to

50

facilitate speciation in the face of gene flow. In central Panama, H. melpomene shares the

51

black, red and yellow pattern of its local Heliconius erato co-mimic. In contrast, H. cydno

52

mimics the black and white patterns of H. sapho. H. melpomene and H. cydno remain separate

53

largely due to strong assortative mating18. Visual preferences for divergent patterns are

54

particularly apparent in males, which strongly prefer to court conspecific females16,19,20.

55

Differences in warning pattern between H. melpomene and H. cydno are largely due to

56

expression differences in just three genes, specifically optix21, WntA22 and cortex23.

Quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping of H. melpomene and H. cydno has revealed

58

three genomic regions of major effect that influence the relative time males spend courting

59

red H. melpomene or white H. cydno females20. Notably, the best supported QTL was in the

60

same genomic region as optix, the gene responsible for presence and absence of red colour

61

pattern elements in Heliconius21. Genetic linkage will facilitate speciation by impeding the

62

breakdown of genetic associations between ecological and mating traits23. Nevertheless, this

63

QTL, and its associated candidate region, contain hundreds of genes, and the exact genes

64

responsible for differences in preference behaviour are not known.

65

Here, we first confirm that behavioural QTLs identified previously are associated with

66

variation in male courtship initiation. We then identify genes within the major QTL, which

67

were differentially expressed in the neural tissue (central brain, optic lobes and ommatidia) of

68

H. melpomene and H. cydno, or have protein coding changes predicted to alter protein

69

function. Out of 200 genes within the QTL region, we identify just five candidates likely to

70

underlie assortative mating behaviours.

73

Chromosome 18 is associated with differences in courtship initiation.

74

Our previous results reveal that QTLs on chromosomes 1, 17 and 18 influence the relative

75

time hybrid males spend courting red H. melpomene or white H. cydno females19. However,

76

the time males spend courting a particular female might depend not only on male attraction,

77

but on the female’s response (and in turn his response to her behaviour). To confirm that these

78

previously reported QTLs influence male approach behaviours (as opposed to other traits that

79

may influence courtship, for example male morphology25), we reanalysed our previous data,

80

by explicitly considering whether males initiated courtship towards H. melpomene, H. cydno

81

or both types of female during choice trials.

161

Causal changes in gene regulation underlying phenotypic variation associated with the QTL

162

must result from cis- rather than trans-regulation. In other words, if changes in expression of

163

Grik2 and regucalcin2 account for the observed shifts in behaviour associated with the QTL

164

these must be due to changes within the cis-regulatory regions of the genes themselves (as

165

opposed to of other trans-acting genes elsewhere in the genome); and these causal mutations

166

should be within the QTL region on chromosome 18. To determine whether differences in

167

gene expression levels between parental species were due to cis- or trans-regulatory changes,

168

169

sequencing batches). In F1 hybrids, both parental alleles are exposed to the same trans-

170

environment, and consequently trans-acting factors will act on alleles derived from each

171

172

alleles). Therefore, differences in allele specific expression indicate changes in cis-regulatory

173

174

Candidate genes occur in regions with reduced gene flow.

242

Of our six candidate genes for preference behaviours that contribute to reproductive isolation

243

between H. cydno and H. melpomene (regucalcin2, Grik2 and the four genes with protein

244

coding modifications), five are found within the QTL peak (Figure 4). Genetic changes

245

246

247

Discussion

253

Behavioural isolation is frequently implicated in the formation of new species1,39, and often

254

involves the correlated evolution of both mating cues and mating preference. Here we have

255

analysed a genomic region in a pair of closely related sympatric butterflies, H. cydno and H.

256

melpomene, that contains genes for divergence in both an ecologically relevant mating cue

257

and the corresponding preference. Physical linkage between ecological and mating traits will

258

facilitate speciation by allowing different barriers to act in concert to restrict gene flow40,41.

259

Although the genes underlying changes in the warning pattern cue in Heliconius are well

260

characterized21,22,23,42 (e.g. optix), those underlying the corresponding shift in behaviour have

261

not previously been identified20,43,44. We have pinpointed a small number of genes that fall

262

within the QTL peak, which show either expression (regucalcin2 and Grik2) or protein
10

(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

263

coding differences (HMEL013551g3, HMEL009684g2, and regucalcin1) and fall within a

264

region of reduced admixture, that are strong candidates for modulating mating behaviour.

265

Two broad neural mechanisms could underlie the evolution of divergent visual

266

preferences, involving changes in either i) detection at the sensory periphery or ii) the

267

processing and/or integration of visual information. Although H. melpomene and H. cydno

268

have the same retinal mosaics/class of photoreceptors45, spectral sensitivity in the Heliconius

269

eyes could be altered by filtering pigments46, or other physiological processes taking place at

270

the photoreceptors/sensory periphery, eventually shifting sensitivity towards different

271

wavelengths (and possibly colour patterns). It has previously been hypothesized that the gene

272

regulatory networks for ommochrome deposition in the Heliconius eyes might have been co-

273

opted in the wings47, where optix plays a central role, and therefore that optix might play a

274

role in eye pigmentation in Heliconius. However, the protein product of optix has not been

275

detected in pupal or adult retinas of various Heliconius species tested48, and therefore has no

276

obvious link to ommochrome deposition in the eyes. More generally, the underlying

277

evolutionary mechanism is unlikely to involve detection at photoreceptors, as this would

278

probably have a broad effect on downstream processing2 and alter the visual perception of the

279

animal’s wider environment.

280

The second mechanism, involving changes in the processing, and/or integration, of

281

visual information, could act through an alteration of neuronal activity or connectivity. For

282

instance, different levels of gene expression in conserved neural circuits between H.

283

melpomene and H. cydno may affect overall synaptic weighting and determine whether a

284

signal (e.g. colour and motion) elicits a motor pattern (response towards a female) or not.

285

Consistent with this scenario, the composition of ionotropic receptors at post-synapses is a

286

key modulator of synaptic transmission49, implicating Grik2. Differential expression of

287

ionotropic receptors is also associated with variation in female preference behaviours in

288

fish50,51.52, raising the possibility that ion channels might provide a likely route to modulate

mate preferences across taxa more broadly. Regucalcins are involved in calcium signalling53,

290

which regulates synaptic excitability and plasticity54, and has an important role in axon

291

guidance55 (albeit alongside additional roles across a broad range of biological processes),

292

making the two regucalcins we identify strong candidates for behaviour.

293

Changes in the regulation of genes with pleiotropic effects are likely to be less

294

detrimental compared to changes in their protein-coding sequences56 (although emerging

295

evidence has begun to suggest that enhancer/repressor elements may be more pleiotropic than

296

previously thought57,58). Furthermore, there is considerable evolutionary potential in the co-

297

option of transcription factors/networks56 that regulate neural patterning or neuron-type

298

activity, possibly resulting in novel adaptive expression patterns. In line with this, regucalcin2

299

and Grik2, which are differentially expressed in the eyes and brain in both our species and

300

hybrid comparisons, are likely to be involved in multi-functional processes, such as calcium

301

signalling and ion transport, and likely have pleiotropic alleles. We also found evidence of

302

cis-regulatory effects for both genes, which would be required of the causal genetic change

303

within the QTL, if it were to be in gene regulation.

304

Neither regucalcin2 or Grik2 show male-biased gene expression, which might be

305

expected of candidate genes for a behaviour that is evident only in males. It is possible that

306

the lack of sex-biased expression indicates that visual cues are similarly important in female

307

mating preference in this species pair. Indeed, Chouteau et al59 report that female preferences

308

contribute to disassortative mating between colour pattern morphs of H. numata, and it is

309

possible that in H. cydno and H. melpomene females share the same genetic basis for colour

310

pattern-based discrimination as males. Alternatively, if visual preference behaviour is

311

restricted to males, changes in gene expression may be integrated differently in the female

312

and male nervous systems. The role of female preference in Heliconius mate choice remains

313

poorly understood. Although emerging data suggests that female choice does contribute to

12

314

reproductive isolation between melpomene-cydno clade taxa60,61, it remains to be tested if

315

there is a strong visual component to this preference similar to that observed in males.

316

Despite expectations that non-coding, regulatory loci may provide a flexible route to

317

divergent mating preferences, we also found substitutions in coding regions at the QTL,

318

which are predicted to have an effect on protein functioning and therefore remain strong

319

candidates. These genes include regucalcin1, which is distinct from, but located next to,

320

regucalcin2 (which is differentially expressed). Notably, the eye transcript of regucalcin1 was

321

recently characterized as fast-evolving across Heliconius species62. Other candidates include a

322

cysteine protease, which functions in protein degradation, and might be linked to behaviour

323

for example through degradation of neurotransmitters, a MORN motif containing protein

324

(function unknown), and a WD40 containing protein. WD-repeat containing proteins have

325

been implicated in a wide array of functions ranging from signal transduction to apoptosis

326

(https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro).

327

Although preference for red colouration and the optix gene are tightly linked, we find

328

no evidence that optix is differentially expressed in the eyes or brains of our two species. It is

329

also not located within the QTL peak (and it contains no non-synonymous changes in protein

330

coding regions21). It seems unlikely therefore that changes in cue and preference are

331

pleiotropic effects of the same allele. More generally, although we have pinpointed the

332

strongest candidates yet identified for assortative mating behaviours in Heliconius, it is

333

possible that actual causal changes in gene regulation are restricted to developmental stages

334

other those sampled, or restricted to a few neuronal populations not detected with

335

transcriptomic data from eyes and whole brain tissue. Nonetheless, by sampling at two pupal

336

stages (around the time of optix expression/ommochrome pigment deposit in the wing/eye and

337

halfway through pupal development) and at the adult stage, we should have captured

338

important transitions for the behavioural programming of the two species.

Work in the past decade has shown that complex innate behavioural differences

340

between species can be encoded in relatively few genetic modules63,64, but very few

341

studies65,66,67 have identified specific genes underlying behavioural evolution. In particular,

342

traditional laboratory organisms continue to provide important insights into the evolution and

343

genetics of behaviour,28,66,68, however, comparative approaches are required to determine if

344

developmental principles can be broadly applied, and also to incorporate a wider range of

345

phenotypic variation and sensory modalities. The challenge now is to increase the resolution

346

of studies in non-traditional systems, in order to link individual genetic elements to

347

behaviours, and the sensory and/or neurological structures through which they are mediated.

348

In this light, we have identified a small handful of strong candidate genes associated with the

349

evolution of visual mate preference behaviours in Heliconius. These genes are in tight

350

physical linkage with the locus for the corresponding shifts in an ecologically relevant mating

351

cue, providing an important opportunity to investigate the build-up of genetic barriers crucial

352

to speciation. The candidate genes identified seem more likely to alter visual processing or

353

integration, rather than detection at photoreceptors, consistent with permitting changes in

354

mate preference without altering perception of the animal’s wider environment.

357

Courtship initiation analyses. Butterfly rearing, crossing design and genotyping are

358

described in detail elsewhere20. In brief, we assayed male preference behaviours for H.

359

melpomene, H. cydno, their first generation (F1) hybrids and backcross to hybrids to both

360

parental species in standardized choice trials. Males were introduced into outdoor

361

experimental cages (1x1x2m) with a virgin female of each species and courtship behaviours

362

recorded. Whenever possible, trials were repeated for each male (median = 5 trials). To

363

determine whether previously identified QTLs for courtship time contribute to variation in

364

courtship initiation behaviours, we performed a post-hoc analysis using categorical models in
14

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.03.22.002121; this version posted July 7, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

365

a Bayesian framework with a multinomial error structure, using the R package brms. All

366

models were run under default priors (non- or very weakly informative). In contrast to our

367

previous analysis20, in which we considered the number of minutes (i.e. time) for which

368

courtship was directed towards H. cydno or H. melpomene females, here the response variable

369

was number of trials in which male courtship was initiated towards H. cydno females only, H.

370

melpomene females only, or both female types (hereafter referred to as “initiation”). Across

371

males the median number of trials with a response was 3. Using backcross-to-cydno males

372

only, we fitted initiation as a response variable to genotype (cyd/cyd or cyd/melp) at each

373

QTL, which were included as separate fixed effects. Individual ID was fitted as random

374

factor. To test the effect of each QTL on male initiation, we compared the saturated model

375

incorporating all three QTL with reduced models excluding each QTL in turn, using

376

approximate leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation69 as implemented in brms, and based on

377

expected log pointwise predictive density (ELPD). Normal distribution of ELPD can be a

378

straightforward approximation given our large samples sizes (n=139)69. Therefore, we

379

considered an absolute value of ELPD greater than 1.96 units of its standard error as

380

indicative of the reduced model being less-informative than the saturated model (95%

381

confidence). Males that did not initiate courtship to any female across trials were excluded

382

from analyses, resulting in a dataset of 139 males, from a total of 146 backcross males for

383

which we had genotype data. Finally, we extracted predictors and credibility intervals for

384

backcross males with differing genotypes from the minimum adequate model. Credibility

385

intervals for H. melpomene, H. cydno, F1 hybrid and backcross to melpomene males

386

displayed in Figure 1 were generated following the same procedures. Raw data and analysis

387

Butterfly collection, rearing and crossing design for expression analyses. Wild H.

390

melpomene rosina and H. cydno chioneus individuals were caught along Pipeline Road near

391

Gamboa, Panama, in the Soberania National Park, and used to establish stocks at the

392

Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute insectaries in Gamboa. Butterflies were reared in

393

common garden conditions, in 2x2x2m cages, and provided with fresh Psiguria flowers and

394

10% sugar solution. Larvae were reared on fresh Passiflora shoots/leaves until pupation. H.

395

cydno, H. melpomene and hybrid individuals used for RNA-seq (see below) were reared

396

concurrently and under the same conditions. F1 hybrids were obtained by crossing a wild-

397

caught H. m. rosina male to an insectary-bred virgin H. c. chioneus female.

398

Third-generation backcross hybrids (BC3) were generated by outcrossing a hybrid

399

male with a red forewing band (crossing design shown in Supplementary figure 3) to virgin

400

H. cydno females, over three generations. The peak of the behavioural QTL reported

401

previously20 on chromosome 18 (at 0cM) is in very tight linkage with the optix colour pattern

402

locus (at 1.2cM), which controls for the presence and absence of the red forewing band seen

403

in H. melpomene rosina. Presence of the red forewing band is dominant over its absence so

404

that segregation of the red band can be used to infer genotype at the optix locus. Specifically,

405

hybrid individuals with a red forewing band are heterozygotes for H. melpomene/H. cydno

406

alleles at the optix locus, whereas individuals lacking the red band are homozygous for the H.

407

cydno allele. Due to the tight linkage we expected little recombination between optix and

408

QTL peak even after three generations of introgression, allowing us to infer genotype at the

409

preference-optix locus (which we confirmed with genetic data, see below).
