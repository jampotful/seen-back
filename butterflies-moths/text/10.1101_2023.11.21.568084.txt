Abstract

26

Nearly all animals have a preferred period of daily activity (diel-niche), which is strongly

27

influenced by the light environment. Sensory systems, particularly vision, are adapted to light, and

28

evolutionary transitions to novel light environments, especially light limited ones, can impose

29

strong constraints on eye evolution, color, and motion vision. The adaptive changes in sensory

30

abilities of animals during these transitions, both at the genetic and neural levels, are largely

31

unexplored. Butterflies and moths, with their diverse diel-niche shifts, are an ideal group for

32

investigating the gene evolution linked to these transitions. While most butterflies are day-flying,

33

hedylid butterflies are unique in being primarily nocturnal, and they represent an important

34

evolutionary shift from diurnality to nocturnality in this clade. Here, we sequence the first high-

35

quality Hedylidae genome and functionally annotate genes to understand genomic changes

36

associated with shifts in diel niche. Comparing Hedylidae visual genes against day- and night-

37

flying Lepidoptera species revealed that visual genes are highly conserved, with no major losses.

38

However, hedylid butterfly opsins were more similar to nocturnal moths than their diurnal

39

congeners. Tests on the evolutionary rates (dN/dS) confirmed that color vision opsins were under

40

strong selection, similar to nocturnal moths. We propose that a convergent event of sequence

41

evolution took place when these butterflies became nocturnal, approximately 98 million years ago.

Introduction

43

The natural world is subject to a continuous day-night cycle, with drastic changes in both intensity

44

and spectral composition of the light environment1,2. For animals, this cycle represents a critical

45

temporal framework within which they conduct their activities. A clear dichotomy often exists

46

between diurnal (day-active) and nocturnal (night-active) animals, with many insects3,4, birds5 and

47

mammals6 restricted to either of the activity periods, often called diel-niche. While binning into

48

nocturnal and diurnal categories is a simplification, given that animals occupy varied activity

49

periods, this allows the examination of diel patterns and how lifestyles shift over an evolutionary

50

time scale7,8.

51

Shifting from bright to dim environments presents unique challenges for an animal's sensory

52

systems, especially vision. Mammals, for instance, have evolved a wide range of eye shapes, such

53

as large corneas and pupils that maximize light intake during nocturnal forays9,10. In birds, eye size

54

is linked to light habitat and foraging behaviour11. Smaller animals such as insects are often limited

55

by absolute eye size. Insects have developed intricate visual systems, featuring compound eyes

56

with distinct arrangements of ommatidia. While mammals can use muscles to contract their pupils,

57

insect eyes have a system of migrating pigment and graded refractive indices that can bend and

58

manipulate light, pooling it as required to increase sensitivity or resolution. These eyes are often

59

categorized into two broad classes: superposition and apposition eyes, each tailored to suit the

60

animal’s preferred light environment12,13.

61

Adapting to dimly lit environments also entails subtle adjustments of the visual and nervous

62

system. For example, many animals, including humans, trade off color vision for more sensitive

63

monochromatic vision at night14,15. Similarly, nervous systems slow down and often sacrifice

64

spatial and temporal resolution to increase sensitivity16. Studying visual systems usually

65

necessitates time-intensive techniques such as behavioral observations or electrophysiology.

66

However, the advent of more accessible genome and transcriptome sequencing methods have

67

allowed for increased exploration of these patterns. For example, through the examination of genes

68

responsible for vision, it was found that nocturnal owls have a reduced set of color vision genes

69

compared to diurnal birds17. The same variation has been reported in fishes, where species adapted

70

to bright environments have a much greater set of color vision genes than species that live in the

71

deep sea18. In insects, multiple cases of gene duplications and losses have been observed, owing

72

to the strong selective pressure imposed by light availability19,20. Butterflies and moths

73

(Lepidoptera) are a prime example, where duplications and color vision gene diversification is

74

much more prevalent in diurnal species as compared to nocturnal species21–23. This diversification

75

of color vision genes aligns with the over 100 diel transitions recorded in Lepidoptera, featuring

76

multiple evolutionary switches between nocturnality and diurnality24.

77

Butterflies have emerged as a valuable model system to explore the evolution of color vision in

78

insects. Hedylidae, commonly known as American moth-butterflies, comprise a single genus,

79

Macrosoma, with 36 described species25 and stand out among their butterfly counterparts in that

80

nearly all species are nocturnal. Marked by moth-like attributes such as filiform antennae and

81

nocturnal flight, hedylids were long classified within the moth superfamily Geometroidea26.

82

Hedylids also possess tympanal ears on their wings, which they use to detect echolocation and

83

defend themselves from nocturnal bat predation27,28. Recent phylogenetic analyses have revealed

84

that Hedylidae likely diverged from their sister group, the skippers (Hesperiidae), less than 30

85

Mya29,30. Studies exploring the sensory ecology of hedylids suggest they possess refracting

86

superposition eyes, which evolved exclusively in Hedylidae and Hesperiidae among

87

butterflies31,32. Because they are nested in a diurnal clade, hedylids are an ideal model to study

88

how shifts to a new light environment influence gene evolution. We present the first annotated

89

genome assembly of the hedylid Macrosoma leucophasiata, generated with PacBio Hi-Fi

90

sequencing. Leveraging this new resource, we examined the visual gene repertoire and conducted

91

a comparative analysis of curated phototransduction genes using genome assemblies of candidate

92

Lepidoptera species. Since the repertoire size of phototransduction genes is thought to be generally

93

consistent across species with different diel niches, we expected to see the gene repertoire in M.

94

leucophasiata to follow the same tendency. We focused our hypotheses on opsin genes, given their

95

strong genotype-phenotype links and key role in optimizing vision. Firstly, we tested if opsin genes

96

in species sharing the same diel niche show sequence similarity and whether hedylid opsins are

97

more closely related to butterflies or moths. To infer the selection pressure on these genes, we also

98

tested for positive selection. These analyses, with a focus on Hedylidae, provide new insights into

99

the molecular adaptations associated with the switch to a nocturnal niche in butterflies.

Results

101

Contig-scale functionally annotated reference genome assembly for Hedylidae

102

We sequenced the genome of Macrosoma leucophasiata (Supplementary Figure 1) using PacBio

103

high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing. A total of 2.85 million HiFi reads were obtained, resulting in 32x

104

read coverage. A preliminary survey using Genomescope 2.0 estimated the genome size to be 452

105

Mbp by k-mer analysis, with a heterozygosity rate of 1.61% (Supplementary Figure 2). The

106

curated Hifiasm assembly resulted in a genome size of 616 Mbp comprising 66 contigs and N50

107

value of 22.3 Mbp (Supplementary Data 1). The draft assembly was generated after filtering

108

haplotigs via purging and non-target sequence removal using BlobTools33. We identified and

109

removed 152 contigs containing 2.82 Mbp (0.11%) of contamination linked to Euglenozoa from

110

the draft assembly. Similarly, haplotig purging led to further removal of duplicated and

111

mismatched contigs (Supplementary Data 2). We assessed BUSCO completeness at each step to

112

ensure consistency and prevent the loss of meaningful information. Assembly statistics and

113

BUSCO scores are shown in Table 1.

114

The circular mitochondrial genome of M. leucophasiata was also assembled into a single contig

115

with a length of 15,209 bp. The mitogenome was annotated with 36 protein-coding genes, 22 t-

116

RNA genes and two ribosomal RNA genes. The mitogenome is similar in size to the mitogenome

117

of M. conifera (15,344 bp)34.

118

To assess the extent of repetitive sequences in the final assembly of M. leucophasiata, we modeled

119

and soft-masked repeat regions using RepeatModeler235 and RepeatMasker36. A library of 1082

120

transposable elements (TE) repeat families was generated corresponding to the Dfam database37.

121

Repeat sequences accounted for 56.79% (349 Mbp) of the assembly. For gene annotation, we used

122

the resulting soft-masked genome to run the BRAKER3 pipeline with protein evidence from the

123

OrthoDB v1138 catalog for Arthropoda to obtain the gene model. A total of 19,292 protein-coding

124

genes were predicted in the resulting gene model, and were used for functional annotation

125

(Supplementary Data 3). We annotated gene function primarily using eggNOG-mapper39 and by

126

performing sequence-similarity searches against the Swiss-Prot arthropodan database using

127

DIAMOND v2.0.940. In eggNOG, a total of 13,169 (68.26% of the predicted genes) were

functionally annotated (Table 1). Gene ontology (GO) terms for 6,598 (~50%) of the annotated

Gene family evolution analysis suggests conservation in vision-related genes with variable

131

gene tree topologies in Opsins

132

Our ML tree based on 3,376 BUSCO single-copy orthologs was well-supported (100% support for

133

SH-aLRT and ultrafast bootstrap, Figure 1). Relationships of butterfly families are consistent with

134

most previous studies where Hedylidae and Hesperiidae are sister groups, and Papilionidae

135

(swallowtails) are the first branching family within the Papilionoidea superfamily29,30. We

136

identified 26,990 hierarchical orthogroups (HOGs) with OrthoFinder (Supplementary Data 5)

137

from the primary transcripts of Augustus gene models of the 20 species generated from the

138

BRAKER3 pipeline (Supplementary Table 1). We evaluated gene repertoire size evolution on

139

the branch leading to our focal species, M. leucophasiata, using CAFE41 and found 64 HOGs at

140

this branch are under rapid gene expansion and eight are under rapid gene contraction. These

141

HOGs include many retrotransposon-related genes, odorant receptors, and cytochrome P450

142

genes. However, none of the candidate vision-related HOGs had a significant repertoire size

143

change (Supplementary Data 6).

144

We searched the vision gene sequences against the database of 32 phototransduction-related gene

145

families (from Macias-Muñoz et al.42) using BLASTp, and identified 142, 149, and 180 putative

146

phototransduction-related genes, clustered in 179 HOGs, for Danaus plexippus, Heliconius

147

melpomene, and Manduca sexta, respectively. A total of 3,503 genes from the 20 species in these

148

179 HOGs were considered putative phototransduction-related gene orthologs. In addition,

149

keyword searches in the EggNOG annotations of the remaining 17 species resulted in the

150

extraction of 23 additional HOGs. The unfiltered vision model from the 20 species thus consisted

151

of 3,721 genes in 202 HOGs (Supplementary Data 7). A total of 345 genes were removed and

152

three HOGs lost all their genes after filtering out the putative vision genes without corresponding

153

function (defined from the EggNOG annotation; Supplementary Data 8). Among these genes in

154

the filtered gene set, 90% (2995 genes) are single copy and 10% are duplicated. Among duplicated

155

orthologs, 45 (1.35%) genes from 26 HOGs met the first criterion where the sum of sequence

156

lengths are in the range of mean ± standard deviation of other single copy sequences, 40 (1.2%)

157

from 22 HOGs passed the second criterion, and 34 (1%) gene from 21 HOGs passed all criteria

The 32 phototransduction-related gene family trees differed, but genes from butterfly species

165

generally tended to cluster together (Supplementary Data 10). Notably, three out of the seven

166

opsin genes (UVRh, BRh, and LWRh) in M. leucophasiata were grouped with nocturnal moths

167

rather than butterflies with fairly strong branch supports (>80 supports from SH-aLRT and ultrafast

168

bootstrap) (Figure 2 & Supplementary Data 11). Surprisingly, this pattern is only found in a few

169

other genes (Supplementary Data 10).

Selection of opsin genes

171

Our selection analyses on opsins uncovered evidence of positive selection acting on multiple

172

branches and nodes within the gene trees. Specifically, we tested the hypothesis that opsin genes

173

in nocturnal and diurnal taxa vary in their rates of selection (dN/dS ratio) owing to adaptive

174

(positive) selection. We used branch-site (aBSREL43) and site-substitution (MEME44) models to

175

detect branches and identify the individual sites under selection. BRh showed significant evidence

176

of positive selection on the branch leading to A. ipsilon (LRT 11.01, p= 0.018). In UVRh1, the

177

clade representing all nocturnal taxa, including M. leucophasiata, was detected to have undergone

178

diversifying selection (LRT 9.89, p= 0.037). The inferred ω rate classes among the branches were

179

evenly split (ω1 = 49%, ω2= 51%) in the UVRh1 phylogeny with no ω3 classes identified. Similarly,

180

LWRh opsins indicated signal for positive selection with inferred fluctuating selection pressure on

181

the nodes, representing four nocturnal taxa with LW duplications (LRT 13.65, p= 0.007) (Figure

182

2). In Pteropsin, we identified sites on two branches of the nocturnal clade classified as being

183

under positive selection (LRT 9.41, 40.96; p= 0.047, <0.001) including a disproportionately higher

184

distribution of ω2 rate class (62% of branches, 92% of tree length). We recovered the M. sexta

185

duplication of pteropsin as shown in Macias-Muñoz et al.42, and detected a high proportion of sites

186

with dN/dS > 1 on that branch. No significant signal of selection was observed for the RGR-like

and Unclassified (UnRh) opsins. Branches under positive selection are annotated on the opsin gene

188

family tree in Figure 2 and detailed selection statistics are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

189

Furthermore, employing the site-substitution model, MEME, within the HyPhy suite, we

190

conducted a comprehensive examination of branches identified as being under positive selection

191

by aBSREL. We examined specific sites within these genes and compared them, following the

192

methods of Murrell et al.44. Based on the likelihood ratio test, we found that episodic diversifying

193

selection has acted on multiple sites in the opsin sequences (Supplementary Data 12). The sites

194

detected to be under diversifying selection were examined using the interactive plots on

195

ObservableHQ. We used the Empirical Bayes’ Factor evidence ratio as an exploratory tool to

196

assess the support for positive selection at the reported sites. Notably, multiple sites in the BRh,

197

UVRh1, Pteropsin and LWRh sequences were observed with strong support for positive selection

198

in M. leucophasiata (Supplementary Figure 3).

Discussion

200

In this study, we generated the first, high-quality genome of Hedylidae to understand the genetic

201

components underlying transitions to nocturnality. While visual opsin genes were a major focus,

202

we also examined a range of phototransduction genes that displayed a conserved pattern of gene

203

copy numbers across all species. To achieve this, we employed a comparative genomics approach,

204

analyzing multiple high-quality genomes of butterflies and moths to gain evolutionary insights

205

into their visual systems. We further examined the discordant gene tree topologies and signatures

206

of diversifying selection; potentially tied to the emergence of nocturnality in this unusual butterfly

207

lineage.

208

Novel genome assembly for Hedylidae

209

New genome assemblies are now being generated at a rapid pace, and they have created the

210

opportunity to study molecular signatures of adaptation across diverse lineages. Genomic studies

211

of non-model species have uncovered a wealth of new knowledge on diverse topics, such as

212

polyphagy-linked gene family expansions45, long-distance moth migration46, genome size

213

evolution47 and opsin evolution across Lepidoptera22. However, this influx of genomic data varies

214

in taxonomic coverage and quality; often obscuring the genetic underpinnings of such non-model

215

species48,49. Within Lepidoptera, Hedylidae and their sister group Hesperiidae (Skippers)

216

demonstrate this disparity in genomic resources. In Hesperiidae, ~12% (n= 420) of the total species

217

have publicly available reference genomes48,50 that have been widely utilized in evolutionary

218

studies51,52, while Hedylidae have none. Previous studies have highlighted the need to address the

219

paucity of hedylid genomes29,53,54.

220

Phototransduction gene evolution

221

Gains and losses of phototransduction genes are generally thought to be infrequent in insects

222

despite a wide range of photic niches covered by different species55,56. This trend was also

223

supported by our orthology analysis (Figure 1) with some exceptions in specific taxa and gene

224

families including duplications of opsin genes in many butterfly species. Notable duplications are

225

also found in individual species, such as the DAGLβ gene in M. leucophasiata and H. pyritoides,

226

Ddc gene in M. sexta and B. betularia, Pis gene in M. sexta and Pid gene in D. plexippus.

227

Many opsin genes have undergone numerous gene duplication events in dragonflies and

228

damselflies (Odonata)57. Parallel gene losses of phototransduction genes also have been reported

229

in subterranean water beetles (Coleoptera: Dytiscidae)58. In general, opsin is the most well

230

explored among the phototransduction gene families for its duplications and losses, where LWRh

231

shows highest variability among the opsin genes59. Indeed, our opsin gene family tree shows more

232

gene duplications in the LWRh clade than any others (Figure 2). The accumulation of LWRh in

233

butterflies has been found to modify or expand spectral sensitivity59,60. Nevertheless, our analysis

234

shows that no LWRh gene duplication is detected on the branch leading to M. leucophasiata,

235

suggesting that the adaptation of night vision of this nocturnal butterfly was unlikely achieved

236

through LWRh gene duplication.

237

Despite various approaches that we applied to detect phototransduction genes across genome

238

assemblies, the absence of orthologous genes occurred in some species (Supplementary Data 7).

239

Considering that the quality of the genomes are generally high, the absence of orthologous genes

240

is likely to be the result of gene loss or substantial divergence of the sequence. Interestingly, more

241

than half (4/6) of innexin genes were absent in M. leucophasiata (only inx3 and inx9 were

242

detected). Innexin proteins were found forming gap junction channels and playing important roles

243

in nervous system development during embryogenesis in Drosophila61, as developmental defects

244

are observed when depleting or down-regulating innexin genes in Drosophila62,63. In addition, inx2

(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

245

and inx3 play significant roles in vision, including eye disk development and possibly

246

phototransduction processes in different insect species64–67. Macrosoma possesses adaptive

247

characteristics for nocturnal vision, including larger relative eye size and more corneal nipples on

248

the facet surface of compound eyes28. Experimental approaches testing the relationship between

249

adaptive eye morphology and the gain and loss of innexin genes may improve our understanding

250

about the underlying genetic basis of nocturnal butterfly vision evolution. Finally, an unexpected

251

DAGLβ gene duplication in M. leucophasiata was detected. DAGLβ was single-copy in most of

252

the species studied and is thought to be involved in the phototransduction cascade and neural

253

development55. The rare duplication of the DAGLβ gene in M. leucophasiata implies a potential

254

role in the species’ adaptation to night vision.

255

Adaptive molecular evolution and signatures of selection in opsin genes

256

Visual opsins play a key role in initiating the phototransduction cascade and are known to be

257

considerably variable in their repertoire size across the animal kingdom. They represent major

258

targets of adaptive evolution with direct, well-characterized connections established between opsin

259

gene sequences and resulting wavelength sensitivities. While non-adaptive mechanisms can also

260

affect the evolution of opsin sequences, adaptive forces such as changing light environments are

261

more likely to cause consistent diversification21. Intense selective pressure imposed by changing

262

light environments on insect visual systems has led to changes in the diversity of opsin gene

263

repertoire to adapt to altered sensory demands. Insects, due to their varying lifestyles, often exhibit

264

distinct patterns of opsin gene expression. Notable differences in opsin expression between diurnal

265

and nocturnal Lepidoptera were reported by Macias-Muñoz et al.42, contrasting with Akiyama et

266

al.68 who found no significant differences. This suggests that while light environments can

267

influence opsin expression and loss, other factors also play a role in shaping these patterns.

268

Molecular adaptations of visual opsins are hypothesized to accompany diel niche transitions in

269

Lepidoptera, conferring enhanced sensitivity under dim conditions69. Our finding that diversifying

270

selection is targeting opsins in moth and moth-butterfly lineages, support this premise.

271

Intriguingly, we observed a unique clustering of hedylid opsins with moths in our dataset, which

272

runs counter to species relationships. While other factors could be involved, grouping with

273

nocturnal taxa hints at possible convergent sequence evolution stemming from shared selective

274

pressures. Previous studies have used selection analyses to further examine such discordance and

275

characterize genes that evolve more rapidly than expected under neutral evolution70–72. We applied

276

rigorous branch-site (aBSREL) and site-specific (MEME) models in HyPhy to identify specific

277

lineages experiencing diversifying selection and reported signatures of episodic diversifying

278

selection across multiple opsin sequences (Supplementary Data 12). Positively selected sites and

279

evidence of diversifying selection on the branches of blue (BRh) and UV (UVRh1) opsin

280

sequences leading to M. leucophasiata hints at an adaptation to a crepuscular niche. Sensitivity to

281

UV signals is important for night-active insects as it assists integral functions such as detecting

282

foraging cues in night-blooming flowers73. Alternatively, the long-wavelength (LWRh) opsin

283

expressing photoreceptors play an even more critical role in maintaining sensitivity in dim-light

284

environments and are most numerous in both diurnal and nocturnal species. A clear clustering of

285

LWRh opsin sequences was observed but no evidence for positive selection was observed on the

286

hedylid branch (Figure 2). It is possible that M. leucophasiata has retained the extended spectral

287

sensitivity to longer wavelengths; generally prevalent and recurrently evolved in butterflies74,75.

288

While multiple sites in the LWRh sequences were identified to be under diversifying selection,

289

our analyses are limited by the lack of a proper characterization of the spectral tuning sites, which

290

would enable us to understand whether these positively selected sites affect the visual range of LW

291

opsins in Hedylidae. While selection analyses on opsins yielded insights, our study was limited by

292

the lack of RNA sequencing data, which could have refined our gene models and helped validate

293

exon-intron boundaries.

294

Conclusions

295

Our genome assembly of Macrosoma leucophasiata provides the first reference genome for

296

Hedylidae, which was of high quality (Table 1). Addressing this knowledge gap collectively

297

reinforces the significance of genomics in broader ecological and evolutionary contexts. Beyond

298

vision genes, these assemblies could elucidate genetic underpinnings of other aspects of hedylid

299

biology such as the evolution of tympanal ears, circadian rhythm and hostplant associations. The

300

findings from selection analyses reported here can likely be examined further with transcriptomic

301

profiling across diel categories in Hedylidae. Our findings showcase the power of leveraging

302

genomic resources across lineages occupying diverse ecological niches. Future efforts to improve

303

the assembly could also focus on generating chromosomal-scale data with synteny mapping.

Materials and methods

305

Sample preparation and sequencing

306

Two adult individuals of Macrosoma leucophasiata were collected by AYK in August, 2016 at

307

the Wildsumaco Biological Station, Napo, Ecuador (0°40'17.2"S, 77°35'55.1"W, 1400m a.s.l). The

308

butterfly wing vouchering and tissue storage methods followed Cho et al. (2016). Wing vouchers

309

(LEP36876, LEP36882) and a tissue sample (MGCL_1059464) are stored at the McGuire Center

310

for Lepidoptera and Biodiversity (MGCL), Florida Museum of Natural History (Gainesville,

311

USA).

312

We extracted high molecular weight DNA from muscle tissue obtained from the abdomen and

313

thorax using a Qiagen Genomic-tip DNA extraction kit. Following DNA extraction, DNA was

314

visualized using pulse-field gel electrophoresis. DNA was sheared to ~20 kbp using a Diagenode

315

Megaruptor 3. We size-selected the DNA for fragments greater than 10 kbp using a Sage Science

316

BluePippin for library preparation. We prepared a PacBio HiFi library with the SMRTbell Express

317

Template Prep Kit 2.0 (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA). The library was sequenced on a PacBio

318

Sequel II instrument on an 8M SMRT cell in CCS mode with a 30-hour movie time. The library

319

preparation and the sequencing of the DNA were conducted at the DNA Sequencing Center at

320

Brigham Young University (Provo, Utah, USA).

321

Genome profiling and coverage estimation

322

We performed quality control checks on the read quality of the raw high-fidelity (HiFi) sequence

323

reads using FastQC v 0.11.776. The k-mer density distribution was assessed from the HiFi reads

324

using K-Mer Counter (KMC) v.3.2.177 with the k-mer length being 21 nucleotides. We surveyed

325

the genome characteristics based on the k-mer distribution analyses, using GenomeScope v2.078

326

to predict the genome size, heterozygosity and assess genome quality. The GenomeScope 2.0

327

profile (Supplementary Figure 2) was generated using default settings for a diploid species. We

328

used the resulting profile and other metrics to inform the choice of assembly parameters and allow

329

stringency in error correction.

Genome assembly and decontamination

331

To assemble the HiFi reads into contigs, we used Hifiasm v 0.16.179 using default parameters with

332

aggressive purging level 3 (-l 3) to filter erroneous and low-quality reads. We used QUAST v5.2.0

333

to assess the assembly statistics and generate a summary table for assessing contiguity and GC

334

content80 (Supplementary Data 1). We followed the Purge Haplotigs pipeline, purge_haplotigs

335

v1.1.281 to sequentially identify and remove haplotigs and re-assign allelic contig pairings to

336

generate a deduplicated assembly. The pipeline uses a haplotype-resolved assembly to generate

337

mapped read coverages and aligns the contigs using Minimap2 v. 2.2182 which are flagged as

338

suspect and junk contigs, for haplotig removal.

339

We screened the purged assembly for contamination, in the form of non-target sequences, by

340

examining GC-coverage plots using BlobTools v1.033. To assess the read coverage, we aligned the

341

HiFi reads to the assembly using Minimap282 and sorted the aligned BAM file using samtools

342

sort83,84. We then used BLASTn85 with an E-value of 1e-25 against the NCBI nucleotide database

343

to facilitate the taxonomic assignment of these contigs. The resulting mapped files, coverage

344

results, and BLAST output were used to generate BlobPlots33 (Supplementary Figure 4). We

345

identified and removed the non-target sequences from the assembly after inspecting the sequence

346

coverage, proportion, and variation in the GC content. Final assembly completeness was

347

determined by repeating the BUSCO v5.3 calculation86,87, and the QUAST reports were compared

348

to evaluate changes in assembly statistics after contamination removal (Supplementary Data 2).

349

Mitogenome assembly

350

We assembled the mitochondrial genome for Macrosoma leucophasiata using MitoHiFi v3.288.

351

The raw PacBio HiFi reads and contigs from the unpurged Hifiasm assembly were used in two

352

separate runs. We used the mitogenome of Macrosoma conifera with accession number

353

NC_05085634 as a reference mitogenome. MitoFinder v1.4.089 was used to annotate the final

354

mitogenome (Supplementary Figure 5).

Repeat region annotation

356

For the genome annotation pipeline, we first generated a de novo library of repeat sequences in the

357

assembly using RepeatModeler235. We soft-masked the repeat regions in the assembly with

358

repetitive elements from various lines of evidence that were incorporated. This included the

359

integration of simple and short repeats and the initially identified elements from RepeatModeler2.

360

Additionally, repeats sourced from the lepidopteran entries in Repbase90–92 were utilized. We used

361

the RMBLAST search within the RepeatMasker v4.1.1 software to complete the soft-masking

362

steps36.

363

Gene prediction and functional annotation

364

We used the fully automatic BRAKER3 pipeline for gene structure prediction of protein-coding

365

genes in the soft-masked assembly40,93–105. We ran BRAKER using the arthropod protein

366

sequences from OrthoDB v11 protein database38, following the GeneMark-EP+ pipeline for a final

367

gene model predicted by AUGUSTUS and GeneMark-EP combined97,98. By relying solely on

368

orthologous protein evidence, we aimed to overcome artifacts encountered during the transcript-

369

mapping step. We assessed the completeness of the predicted gene models by comparing the

370

BUSCO scores from the lepidoptera_odb10 database for each BRAKER3 run. gFACs v1.1.2 was

371

used to analyze the summary statistics and report the gene model profiles106 (Supplementary

372

Table 3).

373

Functional annotation of the predicted genes from the transcript sequences was performed using

374

the eggNOG-Mapper v2.1.9 web server39. We used stringent parameters for the annotation by

375

restricting the taxonomic scope to ‘Arthropoda’ and including annotations with ‘experimental

376

evidence only’. Additionally, a separate annotation file was also generated using the recommended

377

default settings. Gene ontology (GO) terms were filtered and extracted from the eggNOG database

378

annotations (Supplementary Data 4).

379

Species tree and orthology inference

380

We constructed a butterfly-focused lepidopteran phylogeny with genomes from 20 species,

381

including M. leucophasiata, 11 species from the other six butterfly families, and eight moth species

382

including one each of Sesiidae and Zygaenidae, using BUSCO single-copy orthologs. Specifically,

383

we downloaded genome assemblies of these species from GenBank, RefSeq, and Darwin Tree of

384

Life to run BUSCO using the same settings as those that were applied for the M. leucophasiata

385

genome (Supplementary Figure 6). The 3,376 BUSCO single-copy orthologs (amino acid

386

sequences) were aligned using MAFFT v7.490 (the more accurate alignment option “mafft-linsi”

387

was used) and concatenated into supermatrix alignment107. The maximum likelihood (ML) tree

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.11.21.568084; this version posted November 21, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

388

was constructed using IQ-tree v.2.0.3 with ''Q.insect+FO+G4'' substitution model108,109. Branch

389

supports were assessed using 1,000 replicates SH-aLRT support and ultrafast bootstrap with the

390

''bnni'' option to avoid overestimating ultrafast bootstrap support110,111. The ML tree was converted

391

to an ultrametric tree using treePL, and this tree served as the basis for subsequent gene family

392

evolution analyses112. We re-annotated the 19 soft-masked publicly available genome assemblies

393

using the BRAKER3 pipeline and the Augustus models generated were selected for downstream

394

analyses. For these gene models, together with the model of M. leucophasiata, we used the longest

395

transcript of each gene to perform orthology analysis using OrthoFinder v2.5.2, and the output

396

phylogenetic hierarchical orthogroups (HOGs) were used to represent the orthologs113. The gene

397

count matrix and the ultrametric tree were jointly used to detect the rapid repertoire size changes

398

for each HOGs using CAFE v5.0.041,114. We report our results on the rapidly evolving genes for

399

the M. leucophasiata branch with a more stringent significance level of p-value= 0.01.

400

Vision gene evolution

401

Since M. leucophasiata and many other hedylid species are well known for their distinctive

402

nocturnal circadian rhythm compared to other butterfly species, we investigated the gene evolution

403

of the putative vision-related genes. We used three lepidopteran species (Danaus plexippus,

404

Heliconius melpomene, and Manduca sexta) that have been closely examined with their

405

phototransduction-related genes in recent published work as benchmark species to identify the

406

vision genes55. We downloaded phototransduction amino acid sequences for each of the three

407

species and used BLASTp to identify these genes from the BRAKER gene models with a strict E-

408

value threshold of 1E−50, as high sequence similarly was expected for intraspecific orthologous

409

gene blast115. For the other 17 species, genes from the same HOGs with the blast-identified

410

phototransduction genes were considered orthologs. In addition, we manually extracted the

411

putative phototransduction-related genes by searching keywords from the eggNOG annotations of

412

all species (Supplementary Data 7). Keywords were selected based on the eggNOG annotations

413

of the BLASTp-determined phototransduction-related genes of the three benchmark species. To

414

ensure the accuracy of the orthology of these annotations, we further blast the sequences against

415

the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database using DIAMOND v2.1.8 with an E-value threshold

416

of 1E−5040,116. Genes without phototransduction-related results in the top hits (max 50 hits) were

417

removed from the vision gene model (Supplementary Data 8).

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.11.21.568084; this version posted November 21, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

418

Considering that the recruited genome assemblies are not all chromosomal or near-chromosomal

419

level, and not all the gene predictions have training transcriptomic data involved (Supplementary

420

Table 4), the false gene duplication (an ortholog of a species that was divided into multiple small

421

fragments due to the fragmented genome assemblies or incorrect gene prediction, and each was

422

predicted as an independent gene and annotated with same function thus being identified as gene

423

duplication) were further corrected. Specifically, we applied three criteria to determine the false

424

gene duplication. First, the sum of duplicated gene sequence lengths is within the range of mean ±

425

standard deviation of other single-copy orthologs. Second, the sequence similarities of the

426

duplicated genes are less than mean pairwise sequence similarity of the ortholog. Third, genes

427

were annotated in close tandem, in this case, we defined the distance as a span of five predicted

428

genes. Sequence similarities (percent identities) were calculated using Clustal Omega v1.2.0 and

429

the falsely duplicated genes were assembled using emboss v6.6.0 with other orthologous

430

sequences as reference117,118. The falsely duplicated genes were removed from the gene count

431

matrix which was jointly visualized using R package “phyloheatmap”119 (Figure 1 and

432

Supplementary Data 9). Finally, we clustered the genes of vision related HOGs and aligned

433

sequences of each family using MAFFT v7.505 (mafft-linsi) to construct gene family trees using

434

IQ-tree v.2.0.3107,108. We applied the best substitution model selected by ModelFinder and the

435

remaining settings were the same as those applied to reconstruct the species phylogeny120.

436

Selection analyses

437

Since some phototransduction-related genes, especially in the opsin gene family, showed

438

discordant topology with the species tree (see results), we further tested for positive selection on

439

the three opsin genes (i.e., BRh, UVRh, and LWRh). Testing for positive selection could provide

440

additional clues in determining whether the discordant gene tree topologies are driven by selection

441

or methodological limitations. We first built the individual gene trees using the same approach we

442

used for gene family trees and viewed them on FigTree v1.43 (Supplementary Data 11)

443

(http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). The gene trees were then reconciled with the rooted

444

species tree (Figure 1) using GeneRax121. We employed the undated DTL reconciliation model

445

and SPR correction strategy in GeneRax to identify duplications, losses, and transfers. The

446

reconciled gene trees were assessed using the ThirdKind reconciliation viewer122 (Supplementary

447

Data 13). Codon alignments (DNA sequences) were generated using aligned protein sequences as

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.11.21.568084; this version posted November 21, 2023. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

448

references by PAL2NAL v14123. These codon alignments, along with the reconciled gene trees,

449

were utilized for detecting positive selection. We employed the adaptive branch-site random

450

effects model (aBSREL43) available in the HyPhy124 suite via the Datamonkey 2.0 webserver125.

451

The foreground branches were defined by the branches of nocturnal species (Figure 1). The

452

mixed-effects model of evolution (MEME44) was employed to detect episodic positive selection

453

on individual sites, allowing for variations in selection pressures across the different branches and

454

sites in the gene trees. Additionally, aBSREL was utilized to identify positive selection on specific

455

branches, employing a random effects framework to model variable selection pressures. The

456

branches detected to be under positive selection were identified using the likelihood ratio (LRT)

457

test statistic and Bonferroni-Holm corrected p-values. We marked the branches and nodes with

458

signatures of positive selection on to the gene family tree (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table

459

2). We visualized and annotated the opsin gene family tree using the Interactive tree of life (iTOL)

460

v. 6.5.7 tool126.
