# Effects of light pollution on development rate of the painted lady butterfly (Lepidoptera: Nymphalidae)

## Abstract
AbstractArtificial light at night (ALAN) can disrupt circadian rhythms and phenology for lepidoptera since photoperiod is a dominant cue for their life cycles. The goal of this study was to discover how ALAN exposure affects the timing of caterpillar development into chrysalis and metamorphosis into a butterfly. The painted lady butterfly (Vanessa cardui) frequents Texas from spring through fall. For this project, commercially sourced V. cardui caterpillars were used to study effects of ALAN in controlled indoor conditions, at a constant temperature of 23 °C. Throughout October 2020, April 2021, October 2021, and March 2022, caterpillars were exposed to 0, 9.5, 17.5, or 24 h of 300 lux white light emitting diode (LED) lights, and the days until chrysalis formation and butterfly emergence were observed. The 17.5 and 24 h ALAN light cycles caused butterflies to emerge 1 to 3 days faster than 9.5 h exposure. V. cardui development was expected to be faster under blue versus orange LED lights as their photoreceptors are more sensitive to blue wavelengths. This was tested in July through September of 2022 with exposure to 24 h white, blue, or orange LEDs versus no ALAN. Orange LED exposed butterflies emerged a day or 2 slower than blue or white LED exposed ones. Studies in 2023 with 12 h ALAN from white, blue, or orange LEDs yielded similar results, and hemolymph melatonin was reduced by blue LED exposures. Therefore, orange ALAN seems to be less disruptive to V. cardui metamorphosis than blue or white ALAN.

## Introduction
Light pollution from artificial light at night (ALAN) can disrupt insect circadian rhythms and seasonal phenology, disorienting them with respect to time of day or year. Direct insect mortality occurred due to attraction to high-intensity light sources such as hot sodium and metal halide lamps (National Geographic Society 2019). Arthropod communities redistributed to ALAN-rich zones, increasing local predation and herbivory (McMunn et al. 2019). Streetlight surveys (Bolliger et al. 2020, Schroer et al. 2021) and different colored light traps (Kühne et al. 2021) have demonstrated that shorter-wavelength lights (ultraviolet to blue) attract more insects than longer-wavelength (orange to red) lights (Donners et al. 2018). These findings have led to recommendations for the use of “warm” or amber streetlamps, instead of “cool” ultraviolet to blue ones, to protect insects including Lepidoptera from ALAN (Donners et al. 2018, Bolliger et al. 2020, Boyes et al. 2021).
In addition to attracting insects, ALAN has less obvious but far-reaching physiological effects. Daylight drives insect circadian rhythms and biological clocks; while insect photoreceptors can detect twilight, moonlight generally falls below their photoreceptor sensing threshold (Gullan and Cranston 1994). However, ALAN, combined with forest-clearing, increases nocturnal light to intensities insects can sense (Seymoure 2018). Recent evidence also shows that ALAN disrupts developmental physiology via biological clocks in insects (Owens and Lewis 2018). Blue light penetrates lepidopteran brains to activate cryptochromes (CRY), the photoreceptors regulating insects’ biological clocks (Brady et al. 2021). Most insects, except Schizophora, have 2 CRY types: Type 1 initiates signaling pathways to entrain the core clock if activated by blue light. Type 2 inhibits the CLOCK (CLK):BMAL1 transcription factor complex generating circadian rhythms (Brady et al. 2021, Iiams et al. 2024). Deletion of CRY1 resulted in delayed eclosure and flight phases, along with desynchronized expression of period (PER) and timeless (TIM) genes (Iiams et al. 2024). However, temperature cycle entrainment restored CRY1 mutant circadian rhythms (Iiams et al. 2024). Thus, to study photoperiod and the disrupting effect of ALAN in isolation from other cues, temperatures must be held constant.
Regarding photoreception, Lepidoptera also detect light via opsins in compound eyes, ocelli and potentially also via extraocular opsins in genitalia and antennae (Seymoure 2018). Most caterpillars have 6 pairs of stemmata or opsin-expressing larval eyes (Gilbert 1994, McCulloch et al. 2022). During metamorphosis, painted lady (Vanessa cardui) stemmata are internalized in the head capsule and remain sensitive to ultraviolet to green wavelengths (Briscoe and White 2005). In hawk moths, opsins were found co-occurring with melatonin in stemmata and optic lobe neurons that functioned as circadian rhythm modulating centers (Lampel et al. 2005). Lepidoptera make melatonin in darkness via cycle and clock-controlled expression of the key enzyme serotonin N-acetyltransferase (SNAT), yielding peak melatonin levels 4 hours after lights out (Mohamed et al. 2014). Light inhibits SNAT activity via CRY1 and opsins to reduce melatonin production (Mohamed et al. 2014, Zhao et al. 2019). Thus, ALAN can inhibit lepidopteran melatonin production.
Phenological or developmental disruptions by ALAN were previously found in several nocturnal insects such as moths and fireflies, but studies in diurnal Lepidoptera are rarer (Grenis and Murphy 2019, Boyes et al. 2021, Merckx et al. 2021). Moreover, ALAN from new-to-market light emitting diode (LED) bulbs, which yield a variety of colors but generate little heat, have not generally been studied for their effect on insects (Dietenberger et al. 2024). While one study found that cool white LED streetlamps increased nocturnal moth caterpillar weights and reduced population size (Boyes et al. 2021), the effect of LED lights on diurnal insects or eclosure timing remains generally unexplored, even though prior research suggests a link between ALAN exposure and endocrine changes in Lepidoptera.
To address these gaps, this study examines the effects of duration and color of LED exposures on timing of chrysalis formation and butterfly eclosure at a constant temperature. V. cardui were used because they are common in North America, their opsins are characterized, and wild and commercially sourced caterpillars thrive and undergo successful metamorphosis in California through Texas (Williams 1970, Briscoe et al. 2003, Saldivar et al. 2022). The hypothesis was that at a constant temperature, extended white LED exposure will accelerate V. cardui development and eclosure because of cool blue wavelengths, but orange LEDs will mitigate the impact of ALAN on melatonin and timing of chrysalis and butterfly appearance.

## Materials and Methods

## Caterpillars
For each experiment, 40 to 60 V. cardui caterpillars were obtained from Insect Lore (Shafter, CA). Since V. cardui are not endangered or considered pests, no permit was required to express ship them from California to Texas (Wehling 2019, Insect Lore.com 2021). Caterpillars arrived in 8 cups, 5 per cup. Cups were 8 cm tall × 5 cm in diameter and their bases contained 20 to 35 g of proprietary caterpillar food. Upon arrival, they were unboxed and caterpillars rested for a day at 23 °C in the study room with maximal indirect sunlight intensity of 10 lux at 0900 h (with 650 nm peak wavelength measured 1 m above the floor with Light Spectrum Pro Evo on Apple iPhone). The next evening each caterpillar was weighed on a scale (ZEO-50, American Weigh Scale, Norcross, GA) and put back into a cup with forceps. Lids were replaced and 2 cups each were put inside 4 cardboard boxes to shield on top, bottom, and 3 sides from stray lighting. Styrofoam boards with a metallic foil coating were placed at the back of each box opposite the light source for each treatment. Lamps with LED bulbs were placed so that light exposures came from both the source bulb and light reflected from the foil in the back of the box. The room temperature was a constant 23 °C.

## Effects of White LED ALAN Cycle Duration
The goal of these experiments was to determine how different durations of ALAN impacted caterpillar development. Five sets of experiments were conducted, 3 in fall starting 26 October 2020, 9 October 2021, and 29 October 2021 and 2 in spring starting 26 April 2021 and 23 March 2022. These dates were chosen since they are at the start and end of high frequency V. cardui observations peaking in September (https://www.inaturalist.org/taxa/48548-Vanessa-cardui). Light cycle treatments were a constant dark negative control, 9.5 h of light on from 07:00 h to 16:30 h to simulate the photoperiod of December in Texas, 17.5 h of light on from 06:00 h to 23:30 h to simulate the photoperiod of June in Texas, and a constant 24 h light on positive control. Three 60 W equivalent 9.5 W LED soft white bulbs (A19 Earth Bulb, L7ECOA19927, Earthtronics, Muskegon, MI) were put into lamps plugged directly into a wall power outlet, or into a programmable outlet timer (Micronta 63-862, Radio Shack, USA). The white LED bulb intensity was 300 lux according to the Light Meter Pro app on an Apple iPhone held 30.5 cm from the lamp base, where cups of caterpillars were placed. Caterpillars were checked daily, morning and evening, for chrysalises hanging from the lids. When chrysalises formed dates and times were recorded. Cup tops with chrysalises were turned sideways and put in plastic holders. Four mesh butterfly habitats (Insect Lore, Shafter, CA) were placed inside the boxes and lids with chrysalises were gently placed inside. The lamps were put back inside the tape guides on the floor at the same angle, so the light treatments were maintained at 300 lux. Chrysalises were checked twice daily for butterfly emergence. When butterflies emerged, dates and times were recorded. A sugar solution in mini sponges and cut fruit (watermelon and oranges) were put on habitat floors to feed butterflies. Figure S1 shows cups, caterpillars, chrysalises, butterflies, and habitats.

## Effects of Color LED in 24 h and 12 h ALAN Exposures
Next the effects of ALAN colors on the rate of chrysalis formation and eclosure were compared. Cool white, blue, and orange 40 W equivalent 4.5 W LED bulbs (A19, Feit Electric, Pico Rivera, CA) were used. Their spectral profiles measured with Light Spectrum Pro Evo on Apple iPhone are shown in Fig. 1. LED bulbs were put in lamps placed 20 cm in front of caterpillars in cups to obtain 300 lux intensity. Other conditions are described under Caterpillars section.
Spectra of reflected light cast by 40 W equivalent LED bulbs that were colored orange (A), blue (B), or white (C). Wavelength and relative intensity were measured by Light Spectrum Pro Evo on an iPhone.
Two different experimental light cycles were used for the color LED experiments. The first, in 2022, was 24 h a day exposure to white, blue, or orange LED versus an 11:13 h light/dark (08:00 h on, 19:00 h off) white LED negative ALAN control. Caterpillars arrived on 14 July 2022, 20 July 2022, and 17 September 2022 for these constant on, colored LED experiments.
The second experimental light cycle that was used in 2023, was a 12 h overnight (20:00 h on, 08:00 h off) ALAN light cycle for cool white, blue, and orange LEDs, as compared to a no ALAN negative control. These experiments started on 6 June, 19 June, 15 July, 28 August, and 26 September 2023, and with exception of the experiment starting 19 June, 5 caterpillars were used instead of 10 caterpillars per group.
A time lapse camera (TLC 200, Brinno, Taipei City, Taiwan) was set up for each group. Each camera took photos of cups in each light group every 20 s. Caterpillars were observed daily, and cameras were checked to ensure continuous recording. Time for caterpillars to form chrysalises was recorded on the day found, and time-lapse camera feed was used to discover specific date and time in both 24 h (2022) and 12 h (2023) color LED experiments.
When chrysalises formed, cameras were stopped and cup tops with hanging chrysalises were transferred to plastic holders. Mesh habitats were placed inside the boxes and the chrysalises were put inside. The lights were placed back within tape guides and time lapse cameras were placed and re-started to record butterfly emergence. Chrysalises were checked daily for eclosure. When butterflies emerged, date and time was recorded. A sugar solution was added to small sponges and cut fruit was put on the floors of the habitats to feed butterflies.

## Melatonin Measurements
To measure melatonin in chrysalises, hemolymph was collected from a parallel cup of 5 caterpillars from each 24 h color LED ALAN exposure group in 2022, and then from the 12 h color LED exposure groups on 22 July and 4 September 2023, each starting at midnight. This time was used since peak melatonin levels were reported to occur 4 hours after lights out in lepidoptera (Mohamed et al. 2014). To collect chrysalis hemolymph 100 ml of anticoagulant buffer: 98 mM NaOH, 180 mM NaCl, 17 mM EDTA, and 41 mM citric acid was made, and 10 μl each was pipetted into 40 labeled microcentrifuge tubes. Chrysalises were weighed and hemolymph was collected by cutting the thorax just below the bulge covering the legs with small dissecting scissors (Fine Science Tools, Foster City, CA). Hemolymph was dripped into microcentrifuge tubes up to 100 μl. Tubes were capped and immediately put in a -20°C freezer in a paper sack to protect from light until extraction. A kit for measuring melatonin by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used (Catalog # ENZ-KIT150-0001, Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY). Frozen hemolymph was defrosted in a covered ice bath and 50 μl of stabilizer solution from the kit plus 300 μl ethyl acetate was added to each tube. Tubes were capped, vortexed, and placed in an ice bath for 3 min, then vortexed and placed in the ice bath for 2 min more. The organic layer was removed (250 μl) and transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and dried without heat in a SpeedVac concentrator for 1 h. Dried pellets in tubes were capped and stored at -20°C until melatonin measurement. For the assay, the pellets were reconstituted in 200 μl stabilizer from the kit. A serial dilution of the melatonin standard was performed, and 100 μl of the controls, standards, and samples were pipetted into the antibody-coated 96 well plate from the kit. Next 50 μl of tracer and 50 μl of antibody were added to most wells except assay controls. The plate was put on an orbital shaker for 1 h at 25 °C. Then the well plate was emptied and washed 3 times with wash buffer and turned upside down on paper towels to dry. Next 200 μl of conjugate was added to each well, it was put on an orbital shaker for 30 min more, then emptied and washed 3 times and dried again. Then 200 μl of substrate was pipetted into each well, and the plate incubated at room temperature for 30 min on an orbital shaker. Finally a stop solution (50 μl) was added to each well. The optical density was read at 450 nm on a SpectraMax plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Sample values were extrapolated using a 4-parameter logistic curve fit to the standards on GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, CA, USA).

## Data Analysis
Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare average values between ALAN treatments for initial caterpillar weights, melatonin levels, time of chrysalis formation, and time of butterfly emergence for each experiment. One-way (ALAN treatment) ANOVA was performed for each experiment, if the tests showed at least one group differed from others with odds greater than chance, then Tukey’s test was performed to tell which groups were different from others. Also, 2-way (experiment × ALAN) ANOVA was used to compare averages across experiments for white light exposure and LED light colors. Data were analyzed and graphs, which show either box with minimum to maximum whisker plots or mean ± standard error of the mean, were created using Prism (Graph Pad Software, La Jolla, CA).

## Results

## White LED ALAN Accelerated Butterfly Metamorphosis
At the start of each experiment, caterpillar weights did not differ between light cycle treatments. No weight differences were evident between light cycles on 26 October 2020 (F(3, 36) = 0.35, P = 0.78), on 9 October 2021 (F(3, 36) = 2.3, P = 0.10), on 29 October 2021 (F(3, 36) = 2.9, P = 0.05, with no significant differences in Tukey’s test), on 26 April 2021 (F(3, 36) = 0.91, P = 0.45) and on 23 March 2022 (F(3, 36) = 1.9, P = 0.14), so initial weights were controlled within experiments as shown in Fig. 2A-E. However, 2-way (experiment × treatment) ANOVA revealed that between experiments, caterpillar initial weights differed (F(4, 180) = 183, P < 0.001), with no effect of light treatment (F(3, 180) = 0.13, P = 0.94) or light treatment × experiment interaction (F(12, 180) = 1.2, P = 0.31). Tukey’s post hoc test showed initial weights of every experiment differed from every other experiment, 26 April 2021 versus 23 March 2022 P = 0.04, all other comparisons P < 0.0001.

There were no initial weight differences between light treatment groups within any experiment, and no interactions. Yet, between all experiments, caterpillar weights differed significantly (P < 0.05 to 0.001) indicating age or instar variability.
For the experiment beginning 26 October 2020, the first caterpillars to form chrysalises on 1 November 2020 were exposed to 24 h light per day (Fig. 3A). The next chrysalises formed half a day later, got 17.5 h light per day, and surprisingly also ones kept in dark 24 h per day, which formed chrysalises on 1 and 2 November. The 9.5 h per day light group were the last to form chrysalises on 3 November, 1.5 days later (F(3, 36) = 54, P < 0.0001). Tukey’s multiple comparisons revealed 0 h and 17.5 h light groups, which did not differ from each other, formed chrysalises 1.5 days sooner than the 9.5 h light ones (P < 0.05). The 24 h light group formed chrysalises faster than other groups (P < 0.05). Caterpillars starting 9 October 21 formed chrysalises on the same day with 0 h, 9.5 h, and 17.5 h light cycles, but 24 h light cycles emerged days earlier (F(3, 36) = 24, P < 0.0001) as shown in Fig. 3B. Caterpillars starting 29 October 2021 in 24 h dark and light formed chrysalises around the same time, followed by 17.5 h light and finally 9.5 h light days later (F(3, 36) = 47, P < 0.0001) as shown in Fig. 3C. So, overall in fall, caterpillars not exposed to light formed chrysalises soon after ones exposed to 24 h light and often faster than 9.5 h light exposed ones. In the spring experiment starting 26 April 2021, 24 h light exposed ones formed chrysalises first, followed by 24 h dark, with 9.5 and 17.5 h groups forming at the same time (F(3, 36) = 90, P < 0.0001 shown in Fig. 3D). Finally, in caterpillars starting 23 March 2022, the 9.5 h light group was the last to form chrysalises, while all other groups formed them around the same time just before 4 April (F(3, 34) = 9.2, P < 0.0001 shown in Fig. 3E). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant experiment date and light cycle interaction (F(12, 178) = 9.159, P < 0.0001) and significant light cycle (F(3, 178) = 71.61, P < 0.0001) and experiment start date effects (F(4, 178) = 165.8, P < 0.0001). Tukey’s post hoc test revealed in all experiments except 23 March 2022 caterpillars in 24 h light formed chrysalises more rapidly than 9.5 h or 17.5 h light treatments, and with exception of experiments starting 9 October 2021 and 23 March 2022 chrysalises formed faster in 24 h darkness than in 9.5 h light.

About 1 to 2 weeks later butterflies started emerging from chrysalises. The first butterflies out on 8 November 2020 were from the 24 h light group. They were followed surprisingly by 0 h light butterflies on 10 November. Two of the 0 h light butterflies looked deformed, one had an underdeveloped wing and the other had 2 curly wings. One never came out of the chrysalis. The next butterflies eclosing were 17.5 h light exposed on 11 November. The last butterflies to emerge were the 9.5 h light group on 13 November (F(3,36) = 151, P < 0.0001). Tukey’s test showed each group was different all other groups, so butterflies emerged at different times depending on h of light they were exposed to as caterpillars and chrysalises, as in Fig. 4A. Butterflies also emerged with this pattern on 9 October 2021 (Fig. 4B) and, with exception of the 17.5 h light group emerging later, the same pattern occurred on 29 October 2021 (Fig. 4C). A similar pattern was observed in May 2021 to 29 October 2021(Fig. 4D.), but in March 2022 there were no differences between light groups in butterfly emergence timing (Fig. 4E). Two-way ANOVA of experiment date × light cycle revealed a significant interaction (F(12, 173) = 35.06, P < 0.0001), and both light treatment (F(3, 173) = 354.8, P < 0.0001) and experiment start date (F(4, 173) = 1343, P < 0.0001) had significant effects on butterfly emergence timing. The start date effect was due to the 23 March 2022 experiment wherein butterflies from most groups eclosed together in April. Excepting the 23 March 2022 group, butterflies exposed to 24 h light and 24 h dark emerged significantly sooner than ones exposed to 9.5 h light in most cases. The 17.5 h light group butterflies emerged earlier than the 9.5 h light group on 26 October 2020 and 29 October 2021 but in no other experiments, so they appeared more ­sensitive to light pollution.

## Blue Wavelengths in White LED ALAN May Accelerate Butterfly Development
The purpose of the 2022 study was to determine if short wavelengths of visible light, such as blue, in the spectrum of cool white LED lights are more responsible for speeding up caterpillar development with ALAN exposure than orange. First 24 h colored LED exposures were studied in 2022: The first experiment started 16 July 2022, the second experiment started 30 July 2022, and the third experiment started 17 September 2022. There was no significant difference in initial body weight among LED color groups in the first (F(3, 52) = 0.65, P = 0.59), second (F(3, 36) = 2.0, P = 0.13), or third experiment (F(3, 36) = 1.1, P = 0.36). However, the starting weight of caterpillars in the second experiment was greater than in the first or third experiment (F(2, 31) = 254, P < 0.0001, Tukey’s P < 0.005). Essentially caterpillars in the third experiment were heavier than in the first experiment (Tukey’s P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 5.

There was no difference between color LED ALAN groups in chrysalis formation time between the first (F(3, 51) = 1.9, P = 0.14), second (F(3, 36) = 0.90, P = 0.45), and third experiment (F(3, 36) = 0.27, P = 0.85), as shown in Fig. 6. Mean time to chrysalis formation was 9 to 29 h longer on average in the first than in the third or second experiment (F(1.3, 48) = 51, P < 0.0001, Tukey’s P < 0.0001). Chrysalis formation in the third experiment took on average 20 h longer than in the second (Tukey’s P < 0.0001). Thus, 24 h colored ALAN treatments did not speed up chrysalis formation as much as initial body weight differences may have, since the second experiment caterpillars that were larger formed chrysalises earlier.

In 2023, a similar study was conducted with color LED exposures that were restricted to 12 h overnight ALAN versus no ALAN control. Caterpillar body weights did not differ between ALAN colors at the start of any of the 5 experiments (F(3, 140) = 1.5, P = 0.22). The initial caterpillar weights did not have any significant interactions between experiment start date and ALAN color (F(12, 140) = 1.0, P = 0.42). Also, caterpillar batches weighed more on 28 August and 26 September than 6 June or 15 July 2023 (P < 0.0001). Chrysalis formation times did not differ between color LED groups.

Accelerated development for all 12 h ALAN LED colors (P < 0.0001) were evident in the 19 June 2023 experiment. In the July experiment, acceleration of development by white ALAN only was evident (P < 0.005). In the August experiment, only blue ALAN accelerated metamorphosis (P < 0.005). Finally, only white ALAN accelerated butterfly emergence in the September experiment (P < 0.05). ## Blue ALAN Reduced Melatonin in Hemolymph
In the 2022 experiments with 24 h artificial light versus 11:13 h light/dark no ALAN control, 1-way ANOVA revealed reduced melatonin in the hemolymph in blue and orange LED exposure groups (F(3, 52) = 4.1, P = 0.01, Tukey’s P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 9A. For each of the first two 24 h artificial light experiments 5 chrysalises were used and in the third 4 chrysalises were used, so each group has a sample size of 14. In 2023, experiments reduced melatonin levels in hemolymph were evident in 12 h blue and white LED ALAN groups in melatonin from hemolymph (F(3, 36) = 7.2, P < 0.001). Tukey’s multiple comparisons showed melatonin in chrysalises was higher in the orange ALAN group than in the white or blue ALAN group (**P < 0.001), and that levels in the no ALAN group were higher than in the blue ALAN group (*P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 9B. July and September hemolymph samples were pooled to produce sample sizes of 10 per ALAN group since they were not different. Hence, this study revealed that short wavelength ALAN can reduce melatonin levels in V. cardui.

## Discussion
Based on the butterfly emergence patterns under varying cool white LED light cycles, it seems ALAN can accelerate caterpillar development by several days relative to a winter cycle. In Texas, the light cycle in June is 17.5 h and in October it is 9.5 h on the 15th day of the month (Time and Date.com 2021). V. cardui are found in the wild in Texas between these times of year (iNaturalist 2022). Extended light exposure hours do not seem to hurt the development of V. cardui. However, caterpillars exposed to constant dark also came out earlier, and 2 of 9 were deformed and one died, so light must be important for proper development of V. cardui. For the most part, the data supported the hypothesis that longer daily light exposure correlated with V. cardui caterpillars more rapidly developing into butterflies. This was true when comparing the 9.5 h light to the 17.5 and 24 h light group. However, data from the 0 h light did not support the longer light exposure hypothesis, since caterpillars robbed of a daily light cycle still formed chrysalises and emerged as butterflies.
The butterfly emergence patterns under different colored LED lights on for 24 h per day showed that short wavelengths of visible white light (such as blue) are sufficient to accelerate metamorphosis. Specifically, the chrysalises developed into butterflies more slowly under constant orange light than under cycling white light with no ALAN in the first experiment, and under constant white light in the second experiment. As summer turned to fall, the slowing effect of orange light was no longer evident in butterfly emergence time in the third experiment. Mature V. cardui are unlikely to detect longer wavelengths of visible light such as orange or red since their retinas lack red-sensitive rhodopsins (Briscoe 2001, Briscoe et al. 2003). Also, V. cardui caterpillar stemmata express opsin mRNA for ultraviolet, blue and green light, but not red (Briscoe and White 2005). This, together with the discovery that the butterflies develop more slowly with orange ALAN, indicates V. cardui caterpillars and chrysalises may be less sensitive to orange light than shorter wavelength blue light.
While white-light exposed butterflies developed faster than orange exposed ones, the color of ALAN had no effect on the speed of chrysalis formation in any of the three 24 h color LED ALAN experiments. With the 4.5 W LED bulbs, even under white and blue light, caterpillar development into chrysalises did not accelerate. A puzzling outcome was that even caterpillars exposed to cycling white light with no ALAN developed more rapidly into butterflies than ones under constant orange light. This may have been due to the choice of an 11:13 h light/dark cycle, which, corresponded with the light cycle of 15 November 2022 (Time and Date.com 2022). At that time, observations of V. cardui butterflies in Texas sharply declined as winter approached (iNaturalist 2022).
In the 12 h ALAN exposures of 2023, blue and white ALAN also appeared to speed up metamorphosis leading to butterfly eclosure a day or 2 earlier in late summer relative to orange or no ALAN exposures. This finding is consistent with prior reports on other lepidoptera species. For example, in Noctuidae moths, blue and green ALAN accelerated both pupation and emergence of the adults, and body and pupa weight was lower in males but not females (van Geffen 2014). Urban ALAN induced extended migration and delay of seasonal diapause that together promoted the production of an extra generation of caterpillars in both moths and butterflies (Merckx et al. 2021). While butterfly emergence was only a few days faster in this study, speeding up development in V. cardui could certainly yield extra generations over time, especially under favorable conditions. In their migratory range through Scandinavia, Europe, and Africa, V. cardui is a polyvoltine species that can produce 8 to 10 generations (Menchetti et al. 2019, Stefanescu et al. 2021, Talavera et al. 2023).
Blue and white ALAN corresponded with lower levels of melatonin in the hemolymph of V. cardui chrysalises relative to orange or no ALAN. This is consistent with prior studies in silkworms wherein levels of melatonin extracted from heads and hemolymph measured by high-performance liquid chromatography and radioimmunoassay were higher if the caterpillars were always kept in dark or if tissues were collected during the dark phase of the light cycle (Itoh et al. 1995). In a different species of silkmoth, melatonin consistently peaked 4 hours after lights off 12:12 h or 18:8 h light:dark cycle (Mohamed et al. 2014). In this study, V. cardui no ALAN controls had a 11:13 or 12:12 light dark cycle with lights off at 7 or 8 PM and hemolymph was collected at midnight, and both no ALAN control groups had more melatonin than the blue ALAN groups. It was surprising that the no ALAN and white ALAN groups were not significantly different, but this might be due to the small sample size and variability within groups. More replicate studies would be able to clarify this anomaly. Pathways of melatonin production from tryptophan or serotonin in insects is similar to that in most other animals, and blue light inhibits one key enzyme, serotonin N-acetyltransferase (Zhao et al. 2019).
The importance of these studies is that light pollution may confuse caterpillars and cause them to become butterflies when their host plant is not in season (Gullan and Cranston 1994). They may be more active, eat more food and go through metamorphosis faster than caterpillars do in natural light each day (Seymoure 2018). With this knowledge, policies on lighting in communities and urban areas can be designed or changed to preserve insect populations (Merckx et al. 2021, Schroer et al. 2021, Dietenberger et al. 2024).
Hours of daylight drive insect diurnal circadian rhythms and biological clocks for seasonal events. Artificial light at night (ALAN) has the effect on insect photoreceptors of extending daylight into normal dark night hours. V. cardui caterpillars have stemmata that detect light; their extended activation by ALAN may trigger changes in the timing of pupation (Gilbert 1994, Briscoe and White 2005). Butterflies detect light with compound eyes, stemmata, antennal and genital photoreceptors, and it is probable that ALAN penetrates the chrysalis to impact it also (Seymoure 2018). This study is important because there are few controlled studies of effects of ALAN on the timing of butterfly emergence (Seymoure 2018). Light pollution may mislead butterflies to emerge at times when their host plant is not suitable for their survival or reproduction (Gullan and Cranston 1994). Extra generations, if they survive, can also pose environmental and economic problems. The long-term effect of ALAN might be to increase generation numbers or population size of V. cardui. Under these conditions V. cardui caterpillars might turn into a pest on crops like soy. Typically, V. cardui caterpillars are not major crop pests, but they can feed, grow and develop on soybean (Glycine max), with the potential to cause severe damage during outbreaks (Williams 1970, Poston et al. 1977, O'Neill et al. 2008, Saldivar and Wilson Rankin 2024). Extra generations of V. cardui might also deplete foliage or nectar of the plants that other butterflies, including endangered or environmentally sensitive ones, depend upon (Saldivar et al. 2022).
This temperature controlled (23 °C) indoor study of the effects of artificial LED light cycles and colors on V. cardui development has several limitations. Chief among them was use of commercially sourced caterpillars, instead of ones reared from eggs laid on host plants at the research site. V. cardui from Insect Lore, while generally healthy, are bred year-round in controlled settings and shipped on demand. As a result, across experiments caterpillars were exposed to different temperatures before each light exposure experiment under constant 23 °C began. Fortunately, typical outdoor temperatures in late summer/early fall in San Antonio, TX (24 to 35°C) and Shafter, Ca (20 to 36°C) are similar, and in this controlled study, the same light treatment groups were present in every experiment. Another limitation was lack of control of caterpillar age at the onset of each new experiment. While within experiments there were no initial weight differences, between experiments some caterpillar batches were heavier and appeared to be an instar or 2 ahead of those used in other experiments. A third limitation of this study is that caterpillars may have adapted physiologically (and potentially through genetic drift) to the unspecified temperature and lighting conditions of the large-scale commercial breeding conditions where they hatched. The caterpillars from Insect Lore were fed and shipped with a proprietary artificial diet described as sufficient for their development and metamorphosis. Saldivar and Wilson Rankin (2024) found high larval mortality when testing host plant suitability using similarly commercially sourced larvae, and high survivorship when using wild-caught larvae. Comparison of wild caught and commercially sourced V. cardui revealed slower growth, lower chrysalis weight, reduced hemocyte counts, and poorer performance on various diets with first-generation commercial caterpillars (Hernandez and Bowers 2024). The unknown environmental conditions of the caterpillar supplier (Insect Lore, Shafter, CA, USA) for this study complicates and potentially confounds translation of these research findings to wild butterflies.
In the United States, V. cardui migrates North toward Canada in Spring and South to overwinter in Mexico in the fall. It does not travel to South America, since it prefers such drier climates, and the species is not able to withstand extreme cold temperatures (Williams 1970). These extra generations can be sensitive to heavy losses due to cold weather (Kerr et al. 2020), and population losses of butterflies due to cold can be devastating to the ecosystems they are a part of (Seymore 2018). Climate change can shift butterfly phenology, and even in areas where the trend of warming is not constant, in some species earlier adult flight onset occurred and increased voltinism with warmer temperatures (Hamon et al. 2025). With the combination of light pollution and climate change growing, changes favoring warming and dryer climates in South America, any expansion of its habitat may render V. cardui a pest in a new area (Williams 1970).
