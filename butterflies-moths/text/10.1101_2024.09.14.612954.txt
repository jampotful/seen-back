Abstract:

39

How animals perceive, process and respond to environmental cues is tightly tuned to the

40

species-specific demands, and reflected by the structure of neural systems. In the Neotropical

41

butterflies, Heliconius, the mushroom bodies, insect learning and memory centres, are

42

significantly expanded compared to their closest relatives. This expansion coincided with the

43

evolution of a novel diet, pollen feeding, and a spatial foraging behaviour consistent with trap-

44

lining. Previous research has shown that Heliconius have more accurate visual long-term

45

memory than other Heliconiini. Here, we tested whether this enhanced memory stability is

46

specific to visual cues by conducting a long-term olfactory memory assay in two Heliconius

47

species and two outgroup species. We found no differences in the long-term olfactory memory

48

between Heliconius species and the outgroup species. Combining data from olfactory and

49

visual memory trials confirms a significant shift in performance among sensory modalities

50

between Heliconius and outgroup genera. In contrast, tests of how Heliconiini prioritise olfactory

51

and visual cues when in presented in conflict also show no consistent shift in attentiveness to

52

sensory cues between species. Our data provide a rare case where memory performance has

53

been compared across species and sensory modalities, to identify evidence of a modality

54

specific shift.

Introduction:

76
77

How animals perceive, process and respond to the sensory world they inhabit is likely tightly

78

tuned to the species-specific demands imposed by their ecology and life history. Debates

79

concerning the evolution of cognition (broadly defined as the acquisition, processing, storage

80

and retrieval of information) have often focused on the importance of specific or general

81

adaptations – for example through the relative importance of domain general or domain specific

82

cognitive enhancements (Burkart et al., 2017; Poirier et al., 2020), or region-specific

83

adaptations in the brain versus the importance of whole brain size (Barton and Harvey, 2000;

84

Montgomery et al., 2016b). The expansion of specific brain regions can provide specific

85

cognitive advantages (Schumacher and Carlson, 2022; Sukhum et al., 2018; Ward et al., 2012),

86

and some species show adaptive shifts in specific learning and memory tasks (Sherry and

87

Hoshooley, 2010). However, there are few cases where test paradigms have been combined to

88

compare memory performance across species and sensory modalities, to test whether selection

89

shapes the retention and recall of associative memories in a modality specific manner.

90

In the insect brain, the mushroom bodies are responsible for sensory integration,

91

learning and memory (Menzel and Müller, 1996; Modi et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2006; Zars, 2000).

92

Intrinsic mushroom body neurons, the Kenyon cells, branch out to form the calyx, where they

93

synapse with projection neurons carrying sensory information before extending axonal

94

projections to form the mushroom body lobes. Across insects, differences in species’ sensory

95

ecologies are often reflected in the calyx structure and sensory input (Gronenberg, 2001;

96

Kinoshita et al., 2015; Lin and Strausfeld, 2012; Masuda-Nakagawa et al., 2009), with

97

multisensory input occurring in many species, often within topographically separated calyx

98

regions (Couto et al., 2023; Kinoshita et al., 2015). Changes in connectivity within the

99

mushroom body are thought to be the physical basis of memory formation, and show specific

100

effects on topographical regions of the calyx dependent on the sensory cue (Hourcade et al.,

101

2010). Mushroom body expansion is also observed in several orders, including Hymenoptera,

102

Coleoptera, Dictyoptera and Lepidoptera (Farris, 2013). Within Lepidoptera, the most

103

substantial known expansion of the mushroom bodies occurs in the genus Heliconius

104

(Montgomery et al., 2016a). These Neotropical butterflies are part of a larger tribe of butterflies,

105

Heliconiini, found throughout central and south America. Mushroom body size in Heliconius is

106

around four times larger than other, closely related outgroup Heliconiini with whom they share

107

habitats and most aspects of their ecology (Couto et al., 2023; Montgomery et al., 2016a;

108

Sivinski, 1989). This volumetric expansion is driven by increased Kenyon cell number, and an

109

increased proportion of calyx that receives visual input (Couto et al., 2023).

110
111

Mushroom body expansion in Heliconius butterflies has been linked to the evolution of
pollen feeding, a novel foraging behaviour not seen in other Lepidopterans (Gilbert, 1972;

112

Young and Montgomery, 2020). Heliconius actively collect pollen from a restricted range of

113

plants, providing an adult source of amino acids (Harpel et al., 2015; Krenn et al., 2009). Pollen

114

feeding in Heliconius is associated with cognitively demanding foraging behaviours and

115

changes to life-history traits not seen in other genera in Heliconiini (Young and Montgomery,

116

2020). This includes the evolution of trapline foraging, whereby long-term foraging routes are

117

established between reliable pollen resources (Ehrlich and Gilbert, 1973; Gilbert, 1991; Mallet,

118

1986). In other insects, trap-line foraging, and allocentric foraging more generally, is supported

119

largely by memorisation of visual landscape cues (Menzel and Greggers, 2015). Heliconius are

120

capable spatial learners in both field and insectary conditions (Moura et al., 2023, 2022). Allied

121

to the evolution of pollen feeding, Heliconius are also significantly longer lived than other

122

Heliconiini (Gilbert, 1972; Hebberecht et al., 2022), with longer reproductive lifespans (Dunlap-

123

Pianka, 1979; O’Brien et al., 2003), creating the context for increased benefits of long-term

124

memories of foraging routes (Gilbert, 1972). Indeed, they are proficient visual learners (Young

125

and Montgomery, 2023), and possess impressive long-term visual memory (Couto et al., 2023;

126

Young et al., 2024). In previous comparative visual learning assays, all six Heliconiini species

127

tested were able to learn colour associations, but only the Heliconius species remembered

128

these associations after eight days, and, in some cases, their memory persisted beyond 13

129

days (Young et al., 2024). This suggested that Heliconius’ enhanced visual-memory co-evolved

130

with mushroom body expansion, trap-lining and the evolution of pollen feeding.

131

Heliconius, therefore, present an interesting opportunity for comparative experiments

132

across closely related species with specific anatomical and behavioural differences, but

133

otherwise similar ecologies, to explore the evolution of neural and behavioural change. Here,

134

we compare long-term olfactory memory across four species: two representatives from

135

Heliconius (Heliconius erato demophoon and Heliconius melpomene rosina) and two

136

representatives from outgroup Heliconiini (Agraulis vanillae and Dryas iulia). Although existing

137

behavioural data strongly suggests that Heliconius have superior visual long-term memory in

138

comparison to outgroup Heliconiini (Young et al., 2024), a generalised improvement across

139

sensory modalities has not been ruled out. By mirroring Young et al.’s long-term visual memory

140

experiment (2024), we provide a direct comparison between long-term olfactory and long-term

141

visual memory data to test the hypothesised modality-specific enhancement in Heliconius’ long-

142

term memory. Using the same two Heliconius species (H. erato and H. melpomene) and D. iulia

143

as an outgroup Heliconiini representative, we further test the capacity of Heliconiini to learn

144

visual and olfactory cue combinations. Finally, we then test whether Heliconius prioritise visual

145

over olfactory information when making foraging decisions in comparison to the outgroup

146

species, to explore whether variation in memory performance between sensory modalities could

147

reflect general downregulation of attentiveness to one sensory domain.

Methods:

150
151

i. Animal husbandry

152

Rearing for long term memory assays was performed in January-April 2023. Butterflies of four

153

species within Heliconiini, two Heliconius species (H. erato and H. melpomene) and two

154

outgroup species (D. iulia and A. vanillae), were raised from stock populations at the

155

Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute insectaries in Gamboa, Panama. Stocks of each

156

species established from local populations were kept in 3×3×2 m mesh cages in ambient

157

conditions and with natural lighting containing pollen resources (Lantana camara, Palicourea

158

elata and Psiguria sp.) and supplementary artificial feeders containing 20% sugar-water. Larval

159

host plants were introduced several times a week to allow for egg collection. Once hatched,

160

larvae were raised in mesh pop-up cages using the preferred host-plant(s) of each species.

161

Passiflora biflora was used for all four species, and H. erato and D. iulia were raised exclusively

162

on P. biflora. H. melpomene were raised on either P. biflora, Passiflora menispermifolia or

163

Passiflora riparia. A. vanillae were raised on P. biflora or Passiflora tenuifila. After eclosion,

164

naïve butterflies were moved into the experimental cages. Experimental cages contained

165

artificial feeders and P. elata that had had the flowers removed. There were four cages: a pre-

166

training cage (3×3×2 m), two training cages (2×3×2 m), and a test cage (2×3×2 m). Rearing for

167

sensory conflict assays was performed in January-May 2024, in the same manner. These

168

experiments include D. iulia, as an outgroup representative, and H. erato and H. melpomene as

169

representatives from Heliconius. A. vanillae stocks were unavailable for this experiment.

ii. Long-term olfactory memory assay

172

Long-term olfactory memory was assessed by invoking odour–reward associations using

173

lemongrass (Cymbopogon schoenanthus steam-distilled oil; referred to here as lemongrass

174

odour) and orange (Citrus aurantium dulcis, cold-pressed oil; referred to here as citrus odour)

175

essential oil solutions (0.66% oil and 99.34% water). We used lemongrass and orange essential

176

oils as Dell’Aglio et al. (2022) found no evidence of a bias towards either the lemongrass or

177

citrus odour in two species of Heliconius. Citrus (orange) and lemongrass scents have no

178

known ecological relevance to any of our four species, and are, therefore, presumed to be

179

largely neutral stimuli.

180

Artificial feeders were made from orange-coloured foam star-shapes that were ~3 cm in

181

diameter and had a 0.5 ml Eppendorf in the centre that could be filled with sugar–protein

182

solution (2% Critical Care Formula, 20% sugar and 78% water; positive stimulus) or saturated

183

quinine solution (negative stimulus). Feeders were placed in a 4×6 feeder stand, with 12

184

feeders evenly distributed across each stand. Each artificial feeder was adjacent to at least two

185

0.5 ml Eppendorf tube odour-wells that were filled with the essential oil solutions, providing a

constant odour stimulus across the feeder stand (Figure S1A). Separate feeder stands were

187

used for each odour and were kept at least 1 m apart to minimise odour mixing.

188

Naïve butterflies were transferred to the pre-training cage on the day of eclosion

189

(between 11:00am and 1:00pm). Each individual was sexed and assigned a unique ID, which

190

was written on both sides of the wing in permanent marker to allow identification. The pre-

191

training cage contained unscented artificial feeders filled with a sugar–amino acid solution. The

192

butterflies were held in this cage for 1–2 full days after eclosion to ensure they were viable and

193

associated the artificial feeders with a food source. After the initial pre-training period (Figure

194

S1A), the butterflies were moved into the test cage for a naïve preference test. The test cage

195

contained two feeder stands, one scented with the citrus odour and the other with the

196

lemongrass odour and empty artificial feeders. Two cameras (GoPro Hero 5) were mounted

197

above each of the feeder stands for two hours between 9am and 11am to record feeding

198

attempts at each stand. The behavioural footage was analysed using BORIS, the Behavioral

199

Observation Research Interactive Software (Friard and Gamba, 2016), to log the number of

200

feeding attempts (defined as a butterfly probing the feeder with the proboscis) and the time at

201

which each attempt was made, for each individual. The naïve preference test was conducted

202

across two days to determine which, if either, of the odours they innately preferred.

203

After the initial preference test period, butterflies were moved into one of two training

204

cages depending on their behaviour in the initial preference test. In one cage, the citrus odour

205

was positively reinforced (sugar–amino acid solution) and the lemongrass odour negatively

206

reinforced (quinine solution) and, in the other, the lemongrass odour was positively reinforced

207

and citrus odour negatively reinforced. Butterflies were trained on their non-preferred odour, or

208

were randomly assigned to one of the two training cages if they showed no preference in the

209

naïve test. Butterflies remained in the same training cage for four full days. Each day, the

210

reward and non-reward solutions and odour solutions were replaced.

211

Following the training period, butterflies were moved back to the test cage to establish

212

whether they had learnt the odour–reward association (Figure S1B). The recall test used the

213

same protocol and behavioural analysis as the naïve preference test. After the recall test, the

214

butterflies were moved back to the pre-training cage, where they stayed for eight days with no

215

exposure to the odour stimuli. After an eight-day period, the butterflies were moved a final time

216

to the test cage for the long-term memory (LTM) test, following the same protocol as the other

217

preference tests. An eight-day period was chosen following Young et al.’s (2024) long-term

218

visual memory experiment protocol.

iii. Conflict assay

224

Previous protocols have explored how different Heliconius species weigh visual and olfactory

225

cues following positive and negative reinforcement (Borrero et al., 2024; Dell’Aglio et al., 2022),

226

based on experimental designs in prior work in hawkmoths (Stöckl et al., 2016). In this

227

experiment, we adapted these protocols to investigate the colour cues used in Young et al.

228

(2024) in combination with the long-term olfactory memory test odour cues above. The

229

experiment used naïve butterflies. After eclosion, butterflies were sexed and ID-ed before being

230

placed in a waiting cage. In the waiting cage, each butterfly was trained to feed from white

231

artificial feeders so that they recognised these as a food source. Butterflies remained in the

232

waiting cage for 1 to 3 days post-eclosion before being transferred to the naïve preference test

233

(Figure S2C). As sensory cues, we used artificial flower feeders made of five-pointed foam stars

234

in two different colours, yellow and purple, with a 0.5 ml Eppendorf tube in the centre,

235

containing either the positive (1% pollen, 20% sugar and 79% water solution) or negative

236

(saturated quinine solution) reinforcement (Figure S2A). Similarly to the long-term olfactory

237

memory assay, the feeders were evenly distributed across a 6×4 feeder stand, which could be

238

introduced and removed from the experimental cages as needed. As an olfactory cue, the

239

feeder stands had evenly distributed odour-wells, which contained one of two essential oil

240

solutions also used in the previous experiment, lemongrass (Cymbopogon schoenanthus,

241

steam-distilled oil) and orange (Citrus aurantium dulcis, cold-pressed oil), following Dell’Aglio et

242

al. (2022). Each essential oil solution was prepared with 0.66% oil and 99.34% water. This

243

coupled the visual cue of the feeder with a particular odour, such that one combination was

244

positively reinforced, and one was negatively reinforced. As in the long-term memory

245

experiment, the test were recorded using two cameras (GoPro Hero 5) and the videos were

246

annotated using BORIS software (Friard and Gamba, 2016).

247

After 4-5 days in a waiting cage, butterflies were exposed to two different colour–odour

248

combinations: yellow-lemongrass and purple-citrus. The feeders were left empty, and butterfly

249

behaviour was recorded for two hours to check for individual, naïve preferences. After this test,

250

the butterflies were trained for 3 full days against their initial preference (Figure S2C). They

251

were transferred to a training cage where their initial preference was negatively reinforced,

252

while the non-preferred combination was positively reinforced. If a butterfly did not exhibit a

253

clear preference, one of the training combinations was randomly assigned. After the training

254

period, the butterflies were returned to the test cage, and a recall test was conducted following

255

the same protocol as the naïve preference test. This recall test aimed to determine whether the

256

butterflies had learned the associations during the training period. After the recall test, butterflies

257

were moved back to their training cages for an additional 1 to 2 days to further reinforce the

258

colour–odour associations. On the 14th /15th day, a conflict test was performed, in which the

259

positively reinforced colour was paired with the negatively reinforced odour, and vice versa, so

iv. Statistical analysis

267

In the long-term memory assay, as we were interested in retention of the trained association,

268

individuals that did not successfully learn during the training period (accuracy < 50%) were

269

removed from the dataset. This resulted in the removal of 37 individuals: 10 of 65 A. vanillae, 13

270

of 50 D. iulia, 8 of 45 H. erato, and 6 of 51 H. melpomene. Analyses of feeding preference were

271

then carried out using generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a binomial distribution in

272

R v4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2020) using the glmer function in the lme4 package v1.1-33 (Bates et

273

al., 2015). When looking at interspecific differences, species and trial (naïve test, recall test and

274

LTM test) were treated as fixed effects. When looking at intraspecific differences, only trial was

275

used. To determine whether feeding attempts for the two odours were random in the naïve

276

preference test and the LTM test, deviation from 50% was assessed using a null GLMM. The

277

long-term olfactory memory protocol followed Young et al.’s 2024 visual long-term memory

278

experiment as closely as possible. This included using four of the same populations/species to

279

allow comparisons between visual and olfactory long-term memory performance. Although the

280

experiments were performed in different years, the cage structure, environmental conditions,

281

and origin of the butterflies (i.e. from wild derived stocks) were consistent. The data for both

282

experiments were combined and differences in the species’ ability to learn and retain olfactory

283

versus visual associations were analysed using GLMMs. Interspecific differences were

284

analysed using species, trial and experiment (visual or olfactory) as fixed effects and ID and

285

observation-level random effects to account for overdispersion. Additionally, differences in

286

performance between the Heliconius genus and outgroup Heliconiini group were analysed, also

287

with trial and experiment as fixed effects.

288

In the conflict experiment, we aimed to assess the preference for odour or visual cues

289

after the butterflies had learned a colour–odour association. Therefore, we excluded individuals

290

that did not successfully learn this association. During the video analysis, we observed that a

291

few butterflies landed on the odour Eppendorf odour-wells rather than the artificial flowers,

292

seemingly attracted to the source of the odours. We therefore created two datasets: one

293

accounting for these odour-well landings and one that focuses solely on feeding attempts at the

294

artificial flower. The results and analysis from the second dataset are included in the

295

supplementary materials (Figure S4, Table S12, S13). To analyse the conflict data, we used

296

generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a binomial distribution to analyse cue

Results and discussion

314
315

i. Proficient long-term memory of olfactory associations across Heliconiini

316

Heliconius possess expanded mushroom bodies ~4× larger than their outgroup genera Dryas

317

and Agraulis. We compared olfactory memory across two Heliconius species (H. erato and H.

318

melpomene) and two outgroup species (D. iulia and A. vanillae). There was no significant

319

variation between the species in their naive odour preferences (Figure S3, χ2 =4.0164, d.f.=3, p

320

= 0.258). H. melpomene, D. iulia and A. vanillae all showed modestly biased preferences

321

towards the citrus odour (Table S1, S2, Figure S3), but the spread of data covered the full range

322

of feeding preferences in all species. All four species were able to learn the odour associations

323

with high fidelity (Figure S4), showing significant shifts in preference between the naïve test and

324

the recall test (χ2 = 400.817, d.f.=1, p <0.001) (Figure 1A, Table S3). There were no differences

325

between the species’ abilities to learn the odour associations (χ2 = 3.024, d.f.=3, p= 0.388), with

326

all species performing with similar accuracy. Compared with their performance in the recall test,

327

all species showed a decline in accuracy in the long-term memory test after the eight-day period

328

without exposure to the odour cues (χ2 = 57.398, d.f.=1, p < 0.001; Figure 1A, Table S3). This

329

suggests some memory loss, however, all four species showed accuracy greater than chance,

330

suggesting functional long-term olfactory memory lasting over a week (Table S4, Figure 1A).

331

There were no interspecific differences in long-term memory performance (χ2 = 2.445, d.f.=3, p

332

= 0.485), and belonging to the Heliconius genus did not equate to greater ability to recall the

333

olfactory association (χ2 = 0.280, d.f.=1, p = 0.597).

Visual cues are thought to be the primary sensory modality exploited during the derived

335

foraging behaviour of Heliconius. Nevertheless, olfactory cues are important for all Heliconiini

336

during intraspecific communication, mate choice and detection of close range floral and

337

hostplant cues (Andersson and Dobson, 2003a, 2003b; Estrada et al., 2009; González-Rojas et

338

al., 2020; Kroutov et al., 1999). Our results build on existing evidence of olfactory learning in

339

Heliconiini species: learning of artificial odour cues shown in H. erato and Heliconius himera

340

(Dell’Aglio et al., 2022), and recognition of vegetative and floral odours shown in H. melpomene

341

(Andersson and Dobson, 2003b, 2003a). The mushroom bodies’ role in the formation of

342

olfactory memories and its importance for olfactory processing in insect species is widely

343

recognised (Hourcade et al., 2010, 2009; Menzel and Müller, 1996; Pascual and Préat, 2001).

344

The expansion of Heliconius mushroom bodies is primarily driven by an increase in the volume

345

of calyx receiving input from visual projection neurons, with a much lesser expansion of the

346

olfactory calyx. This indicates a degree of visual specialisation in the Heliconius mushroom

347

bodies, supported by previous evidence showing increased stability of visual memories in

348

Heliconius compared to outgroup Heliconiini (Young et al., 2024). The absence of enhanced

349

olfactory memory may, therefore, suggest a modality specific memory enhancement that mirrors

350

neuroanatomical variation across the tribe.

ii, Long-term memory recall varies across sensory modalities between pollen feeding

353

and non-pollen feeding Heliconiini

354

To formally test the hypothesised modality specific shift in memory, we combined data (Table

355

S5) on performance in the olfactory and visual experiments. We found no differences in

356

behaviour between the Heliconius and outgroup butterflies in the naïve tests for either

357

experiment (Table S6). In contrast, we found sensory-modality-specific effects on learning and

358

memory. All four species demonstrated comparable or more accurate visual learning than

359

olfactory learning (χ2 = 28.331, d.f.=1, p < 0.001; Table S6, S7). Heliconius’ long-term visual

360

memory was also superior to their olfactory long-term memory (Table S6; Figure 1C), and to the

361

long-term visual memory of the outgroup genera (as reported in Young et al., 2024; Table S6).

362

In contrast, the outgroup genera had superior olfactory memory to visual memory (Table S6),

363

and no group differences were found in olfactory memory (χ2 = 2.579, d.f.=1, p = 0.612; Figure

364

1C). These group level effects are borne out in patterns of intra-specific performance (Table S7,

365

Figure 1A,B). Both Heliconius species were more accurate in the visual recall test than in the

366

olfactory recall test, and had more accurate long-term visual memory than long-term olfactory

367

memory (Table S6, S7, Figure S5). In contrast, there were no differences in accuracy in learning

368

olfactory versus visual cues for D. iulia (Table S7), which showed higher accuracy in the

369

olfactory long-term memory test (Figure S5). In the recall test, A .vanillae were better at learning

370

visual associations than olfactory associations, but performed equally well in both long-term

iii. No consistent shifts in prioritisation of visual and olfactory cues during foraging

378

decisions by Heliconiini

379

We next tested how Heliconiini butterflies prioritise visual or olfactory cues when presented in

380

conflict to test whether the apparent shift in visual memory stability was specific to learnt

381

preferences or reflected changes in sensory attention between Heliconius and other Heliconiini.

382

All three species successfully learnt the colour–odour association when trained against their

383

initial preference (see recall test in Figure 2; Table S8, S9). The cues were subsequently

384

presented in conflict, with the positively reinforced colour now paired with the negatively

385

reinforced odour. With these conflicting cues, there were significant differences between the

386

proportion of feeding attempts made towards the positively reinforced colour between the recall

387

test and the conflict test (Table S8). This suggests both visual and olfactory cues are important

388

when making foraging decisions in Heliconiini. We also observed an increase in the variability of

389

responses, which likely reflects a greater degree of ‘uncertainty’ during decision making. In the

390

conflict test, the proportion of feeding attempts on the positively reinforced colour was

391

significantly above 50% in H. erato, suggesting a preference for visual cues over olfactory ones

393

394

396

404

iv. Visual specialisation of memory performance and circuitry in Heliconius

411

Our results support the hypothesis that increased investment in visual processing in the

412

mushroom body calyx is linked to specific, derived behaviours of Heliconius. It is likely that,

413

when trap-lining, Heliconius use a combination of sensory stimuli but rely on visual cues for

414

navigation and continued daily use of foraging routes (Moura et al., 2023; Moura et al., in

415

review). As these routes can be utilised for many weeks or months (Gilbert, 2014; Mallet, 1986;

416

Young and Montgomery, 2020), Heliconius may have experienced increased selection for

417

greater retention of visual long-term memories. Indeed, our data suggest a modality specific

418

memory enhancement, specifically increased long-term stability of visual memories over

419

olfactory memories. Our conflict experiments further suggest that this occurs in the absence of

420

a consistent shift in how visual and olfactory associations are prioritised during memory

421

formation, implying that the greater retention of visual memories in Heliconius is unlikely due to

422

any shift in sensory reception.

423

Our conclusions align with both the inferred conservation of sensory pathways across

424

pollen feeding and non-pollen feeding Heliconiini (Hodge et al., in prep.), and with the specific

425

expansion of visual processing regions of the mushroom body calyx, which dominate the

426

volumetric increase observed in Heliconius (Couto et al., 2023). Heliconius have a 6–8-fold

427

increase in Kenyon cell number and, whilst the proportion of cells receiving visual versus

428

olfactory input has not been clearly delineated, it is likely that these additional cells

429

predominantly receive visual information. Evidence also suggests that a change in the degree

430

of post-eclosion synaptic plasticity accompanies this Kenyon cell increase. Heliconius have a

431

greater degree of, and environmental sensitivity to, synaptic pruning compared to D. iulia

432

(Young et al., 2024). In Hymenoptera, synaptic plasticity in the calyx is linked to long-term

433

memory formation (Hourcade et al., 2010) and occurs in a sensory modality specific manner,

434

with olfactory experience predominantly effecting the Hymenopteran calyx lip, while visual

435

experience affects the collar (Gronenberg, 2001; Hourcade et al., 2010). To our knowledge, no

436

study in insects has reported evidence of an evolutionary changes in memory performance that

437

is specific to a sensory modality. Whether the improved visual long-term memory in Heliconius

438

is directly linked to increased Kenyon cell number, or plasticity, remains to be determined.

439

However, the absence of detectable changes in upstream sensory pathways (Couto et al.,

440

2023) strongly implies these behavioural changes are based in altered mushroom body

441

function.

iv. Summary

445

To our knowledge, we have demonstrated the first case of an evolved, memory enhancement

446

that is specific to certain sensory modalities in any insect. This specificity likely reflects the lack

447

of modality general mechanisms for increasing memory enhancement, perhaps due to the high

448

cost of long-term memory (Mery and Kawecki, 2005). Given that long-range navigation of

449

learned spatial routes is supported by visual cues in insects (Collett and Collett, 2002; Graham

450

and Philippides, 2017), and the likely greater stability of landscape cues compared to olfactory

451

plumes in tropical forests, we suggest the enhanced long-term memory of visual cues is

452

adaptive for Heliconius as it improves foraging efficiency, while similar changes in olfactory

453

memory would be less beneficial. Combined with neuroanatomical evidence for visual

454

specialisation of the mushroom body, our results provide evidence that selection shapes

455

specific aspects of cognitive performance to meet a species’ ecological learning and memory

456

needs.
