Butterfly wing patterns create powerful illusory

2

motion cues

3
4

The dazzling stripes found on zebras and snakes have long been thought to interfere with

5

predator motion perception, but this hypothesis has received little empirical support to date.

6

Butterflies demonstrate enormously diverse and high-contrast wing patterns, the function of

7

which has inspired intense theoretical debate and research. Here we suggest that butterfly wing

8

patterns work in tandem with flight dynamics to create visual illusions that confuse predators.

9

We use multiple independent lines of enquiry with biologically inspired modelling to support

10

this hypothesis: free-flight butterfly take-offs filmed at high speed demonstrate the creation of

11

misleading motion cues; phylogenetically controlled analyses demonstrate that this is an

12

evolutionarily widespread strategy; and in silico evolution experiments independently converge

13

on butterfly-like wing patterns. Our results show that butterfly wing patterns and flight

14

dynamics create powerful illusory motion effects, potentially representing one of the most

15

successful and hitherto unknown visual defence strategies in moving animals.

16
17

Movement can break even the most effective camouflage 1–3, rendering almost any animal highly

18

conspicuous. Animal markings have evolved to exploit limitations in the vision of their predators, and

19

this principle should hold true for animals in motion, where patterns have long been thought to

20

interfere with a predator’s perception of the speed and direction of their prey 4,5. High-contrast stripes

21

and patterns are known to create illusory motion 3,6,7, but studies testing their effects in animals such

22

as zebras, snakes and lizards have been somewhat equivocal in their support 8–12. Importantly, these

23

studies have exclusively used rigid prey, despite animal locomotion requiring limb movements that

24

oppose the body’s main direction of travel. Biological motion therefore introduces unexplored

25

potential for visual interference that is potentially common in nature 5.

26
27

The striking colouration of butterflies has captivated the attention of evolutionary biologists for well

28

over a century, making them one of the most studied groups in terms of how their appearance is

29

linked to their evolution and behaviour 13. Research has focused on the adaptive significance of

30

warning signals, mimicry, sexual signals, thermoregulation and camouflage, and the underlying

31

genetics and evolution 14. It is striking then that these fundamental adaptive visual strategies often fail

32

to readily explain the high-contrast dorsal wing patterns seen in many butterflies. We propose another

33

key function of butterfly wing patterns is anti-predator defence through creating illusory motion.

34

There are multiple factors that suggest flight behaviour and wing patterning may interact to create

Pg. 1

35

misleading motion cues in butterflies; many species have high-contrast patterns similar to those

36

suggested to affect motion detection, as well as low wing beat frequencies that are resolvable by avian

37

vision 15. They also often display evasive flight behaviour, and features such as eyespots and

38

hindwing tails can act to deflect predatory attacks 16,17. Furthermore, strikingly few predators have

39

specialised on catching butterflies on the wing despite their extreme conspicuousness 18. This is in

40

contrast to nocturnal moths, which are targeted by a wide range of avian predators when in flight, and

41

generally have lower-contrast patterns and high wingbeat frequencies that would likely produce too

42

much motion blur to create illusory motion cues 19,19–22.

Results and Discussion

45
46

Motion confusion

47

We quantified motion detection in each video frame as the mean elementary motion detector (EMD)

48

motion ‘energy’ in four cardinal directions (forwards, backwards, left and right) 23, and defined

49

motion confusion in two directions: forwards-confusion (the Michelson contrast between forwards-

50

energy and backwards-energy), and sideways confusion (the Michelson contrast between the mean

51

left-right energy and forwards-energy). Forwards-confusion is likely to interfere with a predator’s

52

estimation of speed (e.g. deflecting attacks behind a butterfly), while sideways confusion is likely to

53

deflect attacks laterally.

54
55

Butterfly wing patterns create motion confusion during takeoff

56

First, we experimentally tested whether the natural wing patterns maximised illusory motion cues in

57

free flight compared to unpatterned treatments. This was tested by filming butterfly take-offs at 1,000

58

frames-per-second, and segmenting the footage in order to manipulate and test how patterning affects

59

early-stage motion detection (see Supplementary Video 1). Butterflies with real wing patterning

60

created significantly more motion confusion than experimentally unpatterned treatments (Tukey post

61

hoc comparisons for unpatterned treatments compared to patterned, forwards-confusion: average grey

62

β = 0.079, p <0.001; black β = 0.078, p <0.001; white β = 0.078, p <0.001; sideways-confusion:

63

average grey β = 0.044, p <0.001, black β = 0.044, p <0.001, white β = 0.044, p <0.001; see Figure 1

64

and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). In line with predictions of EMDs, higher contrast (being lighter

65

or darker against the background) resulted in higher estimates of speed. However, patterning

66

influenced the ratio of energy in different directions, creating both forwards and sideways confusion

67

effects.

Multiple character traits that are widespread in European butterflies maximise motion

79

confusion

80

Next, we determined the properties of natural wing patterns that maximise motion confusion effects

81

and explored whether they are evolutionarily widespread. We rendered the simulated flight of 757

82

unique butterfly morphotypes across 397 European species at 2,000 frames per second to achieve

83

fluid motion (see Supplementary Video 2). European butterflies provide an ideal system because there

84

are no major mimicry rings (comparatively few are chemically defended) 24, yet many have otherwise

85

unexplained high-contrast markings, and there is a detailed phylogeny available 25,26.

86
87

Using random forest models we identified which pattern features best explained our motion confusion

88

metrics (forwards-confusion: %var exp = 65, R2 = 0.002; sideways-confusion: %var exp = 69, R2 =

89

0.002; forwards-energy: %var exp = 90, R2 = 0.001). During flapping flight, forwards-confusion was

90

most effectively maximised through (in order of importance, with all highly influential): increased

91

forewing contrast; resolvable vertical stripes on the hindwing, uneven stripe distribution (generally

92

meaning a single stripe, either relatively central across both fore and hindwing, or a contrasting edge),

93

higher hindwing luminance, higher hindwing shape roughness (e.g. hindwing tails), and vertical

94

stripes on the forewing. Sideways-confusion similarly increased with vertical patterning and hindwing

95

tails but was less affected by contrast and also increased with butterfly size. As with our take-off

96

analyses, being whiter increased forwards-energy (see supplementary material, random forests for

97

motion confusion metrics, for a full table of results).

98
99

Natural butterflies were found to vary in their level of motion confusion with forwards and sideways

100

confusion correlating with one another (linear model for forwards-confusion against sideways-

101

confusion: β = 0.518, p <0.001; fig 2a). Controlling for phylogenetic relatedness using principal

102

coordinates showed that motion-confusing phenotypes are widespread among butterflies during

103

flapping flight (Fig.2b), with Papilionidae demonstrating the highest overall motion confusion as a

104

taxon, and Nymphalidae with a single vertical stripe extending across both wings achieving the

105

106

107

108
109

Comparisons between flapping and gliding flight showed that forwards-confusion can only be created

110

by wing motion, whereas gliding flight is still able to generate powerful sideways confusion. This

111

may explain why Papilionidae (a group that glides during flight) have wing patterns that maximise

112

Artificially evolved butterflies selected for motion confusion alone mimic real butterflies

129

Finally, we used genetic algorithms to evolve 57,600 unique butterfly wing patterns in silico, selecting

130

for maximal motion confusion, free from evolutionary and developmental constraints of true butterfly

131

wing patterns 13 (see Supplementary Video 3), and tested for pattern convergence with real butterfly

132

wings. The genetic algorithm (GA) consistently evolved butterfly patterns that maximised either

133

forwards-confusion, sideways confusion, or forwards energy (i.e. the absolute motion energy in the

134

forwards direction), replicated across three wing shapes (Fig.3a). As with real butterflies, forwards

135

and sideways-confusion significantly correlated (linear model for sideways-confusion against

136

forwards-confusion for GA generated butterflies; sideways-confusion: β = 0.733, p <0.001).

Summary and implications

163

We provide the first empirical evidence that many butterfly wing patterns are consistent with selection

164

for motion confusion likely to deflect predator attacks in flight. Our study uses converging evidence

165

from three biologically inspired frameworks; motion vision modelling, three-dimensional butterfly

166

flight dynamics, and genetic algorithms. Taken together, these demonstrate how motion confusion

167

could operate from mechanistic to evolutionary levels in a system long used as a test-bed for
Pg. 6

168

evolutionary theory. We show that butterfly motion confusion can be achieved through multiple

169

routes, with contrasting vertical forewing wing stripes, a singular vertical band, or internal contrasting

170

wing margin patterns increasing motion confusion, while immaculate white maximises forwards-

171

energy. Critically, the patterns that cause motion confusion are not mutually exclusive - and may be

172

complementary to - many of the other proposed adaptive functions of butterfly wing patterns such as

173

signalling and thermoregulation 14,18,27.

174
175

Our research goes beyond previous motion illusion literature in using ‘fluid motion vision’ 23,28,

176

protecting our findings from temporal aliasing artefacts that may have limited prior findings. We

177

show for the first time that these confusion effects are likely widespread in a taxon that is both

178

phenotypically and evolutionarily diverse. We predict that motion confusion could offer an adaptive

179

function for an enormous range of high-contrast patterns found in nature, such as those on the

180

flapping wings of birds or the flicking tails of lizards and fish 11,29,30. These findings also highlight the

181

importance of studying the adaptive function of animal patterning in tandem with realistic movement,

182

as only together do the striking effects we observe here emerge.

183
184

EMD-based motion vision has been well validated 9,31–33, and is increasingly being used to investigate

185

animal patterns during movement 9,23,34. Behavioural experiments have shown that motion confusion

186

from patterning can influence perceived direction 35,36 and speed, e.g. a 1.5 deg/sec decrease in

187

perceived speed for fast moving targets 37. Whilst small changes in animal patterning can

188

meaningfully deflect predator attacks, future behavioural work should quantify how EMD-based

189

motion confusion predictions translate to attack deflection. Further work should also incorporate more

190

realistic simulations that take into account butterfly wing ventral patterning, as well as a wider range

191

of flight dynamics, viewer angles and distances, and background types. However, we argue that these

192

will likely add nuance to our findings, rather than changing any overall conclusions: for example,

193

these factors may help to explain when a higher frequency striped pattern evolves over a singular

194

distal stripe. Our work also suggests that motion confusion may be ancestral in butterflies 26; indeed,

195

the presence of butterfly-like striped wings in fossilised lacewings 38 may suggest that early butterflies

196

only later converged on this same defence strategy, with implications for the fundamental nature of

197

the predator visual mechanisms that these patterns exploit.

198
199

Our findings call for a dramatic reassessment of the mechanistic function of animal patterns while in

200

motion. We trigger exciting new avenues for research into the effects of motion confusion in other

201

systems, together with applied uses in civil and defence sectors, such as improving biologically

202

inspired motion detection for robotics.

Methods

218

Temporally fluid elementary motion detection

219

To quantify the influence of butterfly wings and their patterns on motion detection, we used a custom-

220

written biologically informed elementary motion detector (EMD) 23,39, using ImageJ. The model

221

operates using spatiotemporal correlation of pixel intensity values across consecutive frames,

222

quantified with arrays of vertically and horizontally orientated EMDs to measure motion in four

223

cardinal directions (forwards, backwards, left, and right) 9. The model was first validated by Santon et

224

al 2025 where it was used to quantify high-speed recordings of broadclub cuttlefish illusory hunting

225

displays, Sepia latimanus, using the temporal and spatial resolution of green shore crabs, Carcinus

226

maenas.

227
228

For our model, videos were first processed by spatially and temporally filtering contrasts not visible to

229

the viewer. Spatial contrast limits were based on those of small passerines (~ 6 cycles/degree), while

230

temporal limits were chosen based on a 100Hz CFF for a passerine within a forest habitat 40,41.

231

Filtering was achieved by interpolating frames and pixels using temporal (σ = 5) and spatial (σ = 8)

232

Gaussian blur. Temporal blurring at σ = 5 reduced a 100hz flashing stimulus recorded at 1000Hz to

233

1% of its original amplitude 23. In the absence of both spatial and temporal filtering, EMD outputs

234

would have been strongly influenced by temporal aliasing resulting in “wagon-wheel” type motion

235

artefacts that would not occur in nature 28. The model then outputs motion energy values in each

236

direction (forwards, backwards, left, and right) for every pixel and for each consecutive pair of

237

frames.

238

Pg. 8

239

To quantify motion confusion the strength of motion in each direction was measured as the mean

240

energy across each frame giving a measure of forwards-, backwards-, left-, and right-energy. To

241

measure how wing patterns ‘confuse’ motion detection relative to the dominant direction of

242

movement (forwards), we calculated Michelson's contrast of motion for backwards compared to

243

forwards (forwards-confusion), and for sideways compared to forwards (sideways-confusion).

244

Sideways motion was measured using the mean of left-energy and right-energy.

245
246

ForwardsConfusion = (BackwardsEnergy - ForwardsEnergy) / (BackwardsEnergy + ForwardsEnergy)

247
248

SidewaysEnergy = (RightEnergy + LeftEnergy) / 2

249
250

SidewaysConfusion = (SidewaysEnergy - ForwardsEnergy) / (SidewaysEnergy + ForwardsEnergy)

251
252

Forwards-confusion gives an estimation of how patterning alters the perceived speed of a moving

253

object, with greater values indicating a greater weighting of motion backwards compared to forwards

254

and a slower speed. For most forward moving objects the Michelson’s contrast of backwards to

255

forwards energy should be negative, with biological motion such as flapping wings and running limbs

256

generating cyclical periods of greater backwards compared to forwards-energy, e.g. when a limb

257

swings backwards. Sideways-confusion on the other hand gives an estimation of how patterning alters

258

the perceived turning rate of an object, with greater values exaggerating or dampening turns.

259

Forwards-energy on its own can also mislead observers, with more contrasting objects, namely white,

260

resulting in overestimation as opposed to underestimation of object speed by humans 42,43. Both failure

261

to accurately evaluate the speed and turning rate of an object can result in failure to intercept a target

262

44

.

Slow motion analysis of free flying butterfly takeoff

265

To pilot whether and how butterfly wing patterns influence early-stage motion detection of their

266

flight, we recorded the take offs for five Euro-African butterfly species, two of which were recorded

267

for dimorphic sexes, giving a total of 7 morphotypes. These were: Anthocaris cardamines (m),

268

Hypolimnas misippus (m & f), Papilio dardanus ochracea (m & f), Papilio machaon (n/a), and

269

Vanessa atalanta (n/a). For H.misippus and P. d. ochracea the females are mimics of aposematic

270

species of the subfamily Danainae 45,46.

271
272

Butterfly takeoffs were recorded against a greenscreen background with a scale bar and Xrite color

273

checker using a CHRONOS 1.4 (Kron Technologies, Burnaby, Canada). Footage was taken in RAW

274

format at a framerate of 1057 fps and a resolution of 1280 x 1024 pixels. Each species was recorded

275

between 2-3 times. Videos were converted from RAW to a DNG stack using the python function
Pg. 9

276

pyraw2dng, these could then be imported into ImageJ and saved in a .tif format. Weka trainable

277

segmentation followed by manual screening and adjustments was used to cluster the video, replacing

278

the background with a dark colour and the butterfly patterning with up to a maximum of four colours

279

47

.

280
281

Once clustered, videos were converted from sRGB to avian luminance using blue-tit double cone

282

quantum catch 48–50. The background area for each video was replaced with the average luminance for

283

a grassy background (87,87,87 RGB). Videos were reorientated and cropped in length such that the

284

first and last position of the butterfly would follow a linear vertical line upwards (up = forwards) and

285

the butterfly was entirely in frame for its final position. Each video was then passed through our

286

temporally-fluid EMD using the methods described above. For each frame the forwards-confusion

287

and sideways-confusion metrics were calculated using the measured forwards, backwards, left, and

288

right motion energy (supplementary video 1). This process was repeated for each video with the

289

butterfly wing patterns replaced with the averaged luminance of the butterfly, 0% reflectance (black)

290

and 100% reflectance (white), giving a total of 4 pattern treatments: natural, averaged, black, and

291

white.

292
293

294

78750), housed temporarily in individual plastic pots with airholes, and filmed outdoors in a netted

295

enclosure. To film the take-off, the pot was placed upside down in front of a green screen, and lifted

296

298

307

To capture the breadth of European butterfly species, we scanned pages from Collins Butterfly Guide

308

as JPEGs using a Konica Minolta bizhub C368 office scanner [Konica House, Miles Gray Road,

309

Basildon, Essex, SS14 3AR] 51. As we were interested in patterning more than exact colour values, the

310

311

dorsal images of 397 butterfly species and a total of 757 unique morphotypes (subspecies & sexes).

For each scanned page, individual butterfly dorsal images were cropped and saved in .tif format using

314

315

316

317

318

3D Rendering in Blender

321

To simulate flight for the European butterfly morphotypes, we constructed a 3D animated model of a

322

butterfly in Blender 4.0, supplementary video 2. The butterfly consisted of five 2D planes: the body,

323

2x forewings (left & right), and 2x hindwings (left & right). The image of each plane could be

324

replaced with one of our avian double cone catch converted scans for the butterflies body, hindwing,

325

and forewing. These planes were rigged with an armature for the forewings and hindwings used to

326

generate flapping flight. The wings were animated symmetrically to match the clap and fling flight of

327

one of our recorded Hypolimnas misippus, with the wings deforming and rolling and yawing distally

328

during the upstroke and downstroke, as opposed to simply pitching upwards and downwards. When

329

animated, the butterfly would fly forward linearly from its start frame to the end, carrying out two

330

wing beat cycles.

331
332

A custom-written ImageJ + Python script was then used to automatically replace the images used for

333

the butterfly’s body, hindwings, and forewings with those of a selected butterfly, as well as to adjust

334

the wing beat frequency and render the animation 52. Across Lepidoptera a smaller wing area

335

corresponds with a higher wing beat frequency 22,53. Higher wing beat frequencies are likely to interact

336

with how patterning influences motion detectors, resulting in increased motion blur. Wing area for

337

each butterfly was converted to the estimated wing beat frequency in Hz using an equation calculated

338

from the near-linear relationship between butterfly mass, wing area, and wing beat frequency (see

339

supplementary material for formula calculation) 54:

340
341

freq = pow( 10, (-0.134346*((log(Wing_Area_mm)/log(10))*1.23952-7.843389 )+0.53418) )

342
343

The calculated wing beat frequency was then used to rescale the length of the animation and adjust the

344

start and end frame so that the animation consisted of one wing beat cycle with 10 additional frames

345

lead-in and lead-out for fluid frame interpolation. When rendered, butterflies were filmed from a static

346

bird's eye view (camera looking down on the butterfly), flying forward at a speed proportional to its

347

body length and wing beat frequency (speed = length*1.93*wbf). Animations were rendered as a

348

stack of .pngs at a frame rate of 2000 frames per second.

349
Pg. 11

350

Each stack was imported into ImageJ and quantified using our temporally-fluid EMD, taking the

351

average forwards, backwards, left, and right energy across each frame. These were used to calculate

352

the forwards-confusion, sideways-confusion, and forwards-energy for a butterfly in flapping flight.

353

Each butterfly was rendered with three colour treatments: its natural pattern (natural), the averaged

354

grey value of the wing (grey), and in white to account for the effect of the wing shape alone.

355

Additionally, each colour treatment was rendered with and without wing beat animations to determine

356

how gliding influences motion, giving a total of six treatments. This process was carried out for all

357

757 morphotypes.

Butterfly wing features

360

To assess which features of butterfly wings influenced our EMD model, we quantified the colour,

361

patterning, and wing shape of our butterflies using image analysis. All measures used were repeated

362

separately for the forewing and the hindwing. Simple measures of colour were obtained by converting

363

the butterfly scans to the CIE 1976 Lab colour space 55. This colour space is frequently used for the

364

measurement of biological colouration and allows for the quantification of colour across three discrete

365

image channels, L* (achromatic luminance), a* (opponent red-green), and b* (opponent yellow-

366

blue). For each image, we measured the mean and standard deviation (contrast) of a*, b*, and of the

367

Euclidean distance of the a* and b* channels from zero (saturation).

368
369

For patterning, we used Gabor filters set to six different orientations (0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150

370

degrees) and six octaves, the largest being 1/4x the wavelength of the wing (square root of wing area)

371

to measure the pattern contrast, size, and orientations of our blue-tit double cone catch images 56,57.

372

The resulting stack of 24 images were then used to create spatial maps for periodicity (how big the

373

pattern is), absolute energy (how contrasting the pattern is), direction encoded as two separate

374

channels VH (0-90 | vertical - horizontal) and OA (135-45 | obtuse - acute), as well as the

375

directionality (anisotropy = √(VH2 + OA2) - average energy) (See Supplementary Material). These

376

five maps (periodicity, energy, VH, OA, and directionality) allowed us to quantify pattern variation

377

across the wing, not just the global average. For each map, we measured the pixel mean, standard

378

deviation, horizontal gradient (x), and vertical gradient (y). For instance, a high positive mean VH

379

would indicate a vertical rather than a horizontal (negative) pattern, a positive horizontal gradient for

380

periodicity would indicate that periodicity increases distally from the body, and a high standard

381

deviation of directionality would indicate that directionality varies substantially across the wing.

382
383

Lastly, butterfly size and wing shape were measured by calculating the total wing area, the estimated

384

wing beat frequency, and the body length. Forewing and hindwing shape measurements were made by

385

recording the length of the wing from the centre of the wing base to the most distal point on the wing

386

58

, the breadth of the wing at the centroid of the line for the length of the wing, the rectangular length

Pg. 12

387

and width of the wing, the area of the wing relative to the body length (area/bodyLength), the aspect

388

ratio of the wing (length/breadth), the circularity of the wing (4pi(area/perimeter^2))39, and the

389

roughness of the wing (the convex area / the actual area). Altogether, this gave us 73 measures for

390

wing features.

392

In Silico butterfly wing pattern evolution

393

The diversity of butterfly wing patterns are the product of their own unique genetic and

394

developmental pathways, some of which have been accurately mapped 13,59,60. These could either limit

395

the adaptive potential of their patterning for motion confusion or could create patterns that fit our

396

motion confusion hypothesis by coincidence. To validate whether or not our motion confusion metrics

397

corresponded with actual butterfly wing evolution, we opted to simulate evolution and selection for

398

motion confusion with genetic algorithms (GAs) 61,62. GAs provide a powerful tool for exploring the

399

fitness landscape of animal phenotypes, and have seen increasing use within visual ecology research,

400

notably for animal camouflage research 63–65. These algorithms mimic evolution by natural selection

401

to efficiently explore vast multi-dimensional solution spaces, such as those of animal patterns.

402
403

For our algorithm, we modified the existing animal pattern evolution framework of the CamoEvo

404

Toolbox, which utilises decimal encoding of reaction-diffusion patterns and subsequent image

405

modifications to produce artificial animal patterns, with mutation operators tailored for the evolution

406

of patterning 65. This framework was altered to produce images of artificial butterflies where the

407

parameters for pattern shape were separate for the forewing, hindwing, and body of the butterfly,

408

allowing for them to evolve independently from one another. As with the real European butterflies the

409

butterfly images were then transferred to blender and animated with our 3D butterfly rig.

410
411

To account for any influences wing shape might have had on evolution we selected three distinct

412

butterfly wing shapes from three of the butterfly families: the proportionally small wings of a

413

‘Skipper’ Hesperiidae (Pyrgus andromedae), the distinct tailed wings of a ‘Swallowtail’ Papilionidae

414

(Papilio machaon), and the more classically shaped wings of a Nymphalid butterfly (Pseudochazara

415

anthelea) 66. For each wing shape we assigned populations of N=24 butterflies to one of three

416

different measures of ‘fitness’ representing different selection pressures: forwards-confusion,

417

sideways-confusion, and forwards-energy. For each generation, the top 8 individuals (i.e. those with

418

the highest motion confusion/energy metrics) would ‘survive’, while the remaining 16 ‘died’ and

419

were replaced by mutant recombinant offspring of the survivors in two rounds of random pairings.

420

Additionally, we evolved butterflies under random selection, where the rank order was randomly

421

generated each generation: this was done to ensure butterflies didn’t evolve patterns simply because

422

of drift. Each population evolved for 20 generations, starting with a population of randomly

Pg. 13

423

generated individuals, and with 10 repeats for each fitness metric per wing shape, giving a total of 120

424

populations (3 wing shapes, 4 selection pressures, and 10 repeats).

425
426

Butterflies were expected to become ‘fitter’ with each generation, and to select for similar features to

427

those identified to predict motion confusion in real butterflies. As high spatial frequency patterns and

428

speckles weren’t under selection due to spatial filtering from our fluid EMD model, both the patterns

429

of real butterflies and the GA generated in silico butterflies underwent Gaussian spatial filtering (σ =

430

8) prior to measurement. For both sets of butterflies, we used the same wing pattern metrics as our

431

European butterfly analysis, excluding measures of wing shape or CIE Lab colour.

433

Statistical analyses

434

All statistical analyses for our study were carried out using R version 4.3.3.67.

435
436

To determine how wing pattern influenced our motion confusion metrics for real butterfly take-offs in

437

free flight we used linear mixed models (LMMs), with the “lme4” package 68. For our analysis, the

438

motion confusion metrics (forwards-confusion, sideways-confusion, and forwards-energy) were

439

considered as dependent variables in separate models, with wing pattern type (natural, averaged,

440

black, and white) as a factorial predictor variable. The ID number for each unique flight and the

441

species were used as random effects. To compare differences between our factor levels, we used

442

emmeans Tukey post hoc comparison tests 69.

443
444

Given the vast array of wing features measured, we opted for a Random Forest (RF) model to

445

evaluate the predictive value for each of our measures for each of our motion confusion measures 70.

446

RF provides a non-parametric ensemble learning method, utilising multiple constructed decision trees

447

trained with random subsets of the data and the predictors. Predictions are then aggregated across the

448

trees helping to prevent overfitting. For each of our metrics RF models were trained with 1500 trees

449

with four predictors per split. To evaluate model performance we used the R² to estimate the

450

proportion of explained variance.

451
452

Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was used to improve model interpretability by reducing

453

redundant predictors. Predictors were systematically ranked based on their weight and model

454

performance was evaluated for subsets from 1 to the total number of variables. R² values of the

455

complete model and the reduced models were compared to ensure that the predictive accuracy was

456

retained. To evaluate which features of our reduced model were the most important we used SHAP

457

(SHapley Additive exPlanations) as a measure of feature importance 71. Greater absolute SHAP

458

indicates a greater importance, with positive and negative values corresponding with a positive or

459

negative correlation, respectively.
Pg. 14

460
461

RF models were performed using the “randomForest” and “caret” packages 70,72. As many of our wing

462

features are potentially linked to phylogeny, we incorporated Wiemers et al., 2020’s complete

463

European butterfly phylogeny into our model. Cophenetic distances were extracted from the

464

phylogeny and converted into three phylogenetic principal components with principal coordinate

465

analysis (PCoA) using the “phytools” package 73. Similar to how principal component analysis (PCA)

466

can reduce multiple numeric variables into singular components, PCoA allows an individual's position

467

within the phylogeny to be redefined as a set of multidimensional coordinates. Our three phylogenetic

468

components were included alongside our wing parameters as predictor variables within our model.

469

Circular coordinates for our butterfly phylogeny were extracted with the “ape” package 74.

470
471

For the in silico butterflies LMMs were used to validate whether butterflies significantly improved in

472

fitness for our evolution treatments. RF models were again generated for the motion confusion metrics

473

using our reduced set of spatially filtered wing pattern variables. These models were created using the

474

measured phenotypes from the first and last generation of the entirety of our in silico generated

475

dataset, rather than for butterflies under one selection pressure, using the initial randomly generated

476

butterflies to provide a wider range of variation. Lastly, to determine whether or not butterflies under

477

a particular selection pressure evolved to be more or less like natural butterflies, we generated

478

principal components for the wing pattern metrics (PC1 and PC2) and used pairwise comparison to

479

calculate the Euclidean difference of principal components between every butterfly, both real and in

480

silico, from every natural butterfly.

481
482

Instances where real butterflies of the same morphotype were compared were removed as their

483

distance would equal 0. Butterflies were divided into six groups: natural, forwards-confusion selected

484

(gen = 20, top 1 per population), sideways-confusion selected (gen = 20, top 1 per population),

485

forwards-energy selected (gen = 20, top 1 per population), random-selection (gen = 20, top 1 per

486

population), and unevolved (gen = 0, all individuals). We then used a linear model (LM) to test which

487

group generated the lowest difference from real butterflies and whether the pairwise distance levels

488

were significantly different from one another with Tukey post hoc comparisons. We hypothesised that

489

the difference should be lower for those selected for motion confusion than for those that were

490

randomly generated at the start.
