Abstract

24

For navigation, animals use a robust internal compass. Compass navigation is especially crucial for long-

25

distance migrating animals like monarch butterflies, which use a sun compass to navigate every fall over

26

4,000 km to their overwintering sites. The central complex, a brain region equipped with sun-compass

27

neurons, is proposed to control the butterfly’s heading. Although the activity of central-complex neurons

28

exhibits a locomotor-dependent modulation in many insects, the consequences of such modulations for

29

the coding of heading remain unexplored. Here, we developed tetrode recordings from tethered flying

30

monarch butterflies to reveal how flight modulates heading representation. We found that during flight,

31

heading-direction neurons change their tuning, transforming the central-complex network to function as

32

a global compass. This compass coding is characterized by the dominance of steering feedback and

33

allows for robust heading representation even under unreliable visual scenarios, an ideal strategy for

34

maintaining a migratory heading over enormous distances.

Introduction

36

Goal-directed navigation requires animals to constantly register their heading in space. This is

37

of particular importance during long-distance migration, as exhibited by many vertebrates, such as birds

38

and bats 1. Similarly, monarch butterflies (Danaus plexippus) annually migrate from southern Canada

39

and the northern United States to their overwintering habitat in central Mexico 2,3. To maintain proper

40

migratory direction, monarch butterflies rely on an internal compass that integrates multimodal

41

orientation signals, including the sun 4,5. Sun compass information is processed in a brain region termed

42

the central complex 6,7. In this brain region, compass neurons are thought to encode the butterflies’

43

heading, like head-direction cells in vertebrates 8-12. Data from the central complex of Drosophila show

44

that heading is topographically represented in a neural network composed of E-PG cells 13-15. Within the

45

E-PG network, heading is represented by a single activity bump that changes its position when the insect

46

changes its heading direction 13,16. The activity bump in E-PG cells results from the integration of visual

47

signals and mechanosensory information 14,17-19, as well as idiothetic, self-motion signals 13,20,21.

48

Although the multimodal input may explain locomotor-dependent modulations in the activity of central-

49

complex neurons 22,23, the function of these state-dependent modulations is still poorly understood. To

50

shed light on this, we investigated how the locomotory state affects sun compass coding in the monarch

51

butterfly.

52

In the past, due to methodological limitations, butterfly’ sun compass neurons have only been

53

recorded in restrained animals 6. Here, using monarch butterflies, we investigated how the heading-

54

direction network operates when the animal is free to steer with respect to a sun stimulus. We observed

55

that when the animals were free to steer during flight, the heading-direction network modified its angular

56

tuning, i.e. remapped from a vision-based sun-bearing coding found in quiescent animals to a compass

57

coding during flight. The transformation to a compass was reflected by a state-dependent change in

58

angular sensitivity and tuning. At flight onset, a substantial number of neurons started to encode the

59

solar azimuth, while others ceased to encode the sun position, a process that depends on changes in

60

octopamine levels. Changes in angular tuning were accompanied by a state-dependent switch in cue

61

hierarchy between visual and idiothetic inputs. Importantly, the heading-direction network transformed

62

into a compass that was dominated by idiothetic cues when the tethered flying butterflies were allowed

to perform actual turns. The dominance of idiothetic cues allowed a robust heading representation even

64

under ambiguous or conflicting visual settings. Thus, in the monarch butterfly central complex, flight

65

activity shapes the heading-direction network into a multimodal, global compass that allows these

66

insects to reliably maintain their migratory southward direction.

Results

69

State-dependent changes in angular sensitivity of central-complex neurons

70

To perform tetrode recordings from tethered flying butterflies, we designed an indoor flight

71

arena (Fig. 1A). The arena’s upper inner circumference was equipped with a strip of individually

72

switchable green light spots serving as sun stimuli that butterflies could use for orientation. During

73

neural recordings from the central complex of tethered butterflies centered in the arena (Fig. 1B,

74

Supplementary Figure 1), an optical encoder measured the animal’s heading direction. First, we recorded

75

from quiescent butterflies that rested on a platform at a fixed heading (0°; quiescent pre-flight;

76

Supplementary Movie 1) and that were stimulated by moving the sun stimulus along a 360° circular

77

path around the animal. To investigate the influence of proprioceptive effects from flight activity on the

78

neural response, we then presented the same sun stimulus, but now with the butterfly flying at a fixed

79

heading (0°; fixed flight; Supplementary Movie 2). A subset of central-complex neurons (66 of 150

80

recorded neurons; 44%) exhibited state-dependent angular tuning to the sun stimulus. During fixed

81

flight, 50 neurons (33%; 8 + 29 + 13 neurons outside the gray area in Fig. 1C) encoded the solar azimuth

82

as indicated by an increase in firing rate at neuron-specific azimuthal positions of the sun stimulus

83

(Rayleigh test: p < 0.05). In contrast, during quiescence, the same neurons showed stable firing rates

84

across different sun positions (Rayleigh test: p > 0.05) and therefore did not encode the solar azimuth

85

(Fig. 1D, green dataset). Conversely, 16 neurons (11%, 2+14 neurons of gray area in Fig. 1C) showed

86

angular sensitivity during quiescence but ceased to encode the solar azimuth during fixed flight (Fig.

87

1D, blue dataset). The remaining 84 of the 150 recorded neurons (56%) did not change their angular

88

sensitivity to the sun stimulus during fixed flight: 76 neurons were sensitive (51%; 56 + 20 neurons in

89

merged gray and blue areas of Fig. 1C) and 8 neurons insensitive (5%; green area in Fig. 1C) during

90

both locomotory states. To test whether changes in angular sensitivity reflect recording instability, e.g.

subtle electrode movements induced by flight, we allowed the butterfly to settle on the platform after

92

flight and retested the neural sensitivity in 63 neurons (quiescent post-flight). All 63 neurons retained

93

their quiescent pre-flight angular sensitivity (example shown in Fig. 1D), suggesting that changes in

94

angular sensitivity observed during flight depended solely on the animal’s locomotory state. We also

95

excluded that we recorded from different neurons across the behavioral states by assessing the spike-

96

shape stability. This was done by computing the averaged spike shapes from each neuron and

97

locomotory state (i.e., quiescent pre-flight, fixed flight, and quiescent post-flight). These averaged spike

98

shapes were correlated with each other, and the correlation coefficients were statistically compared with

99

the ones obtained by correlating neuron-specific spike shapes with averaged spike shapes of randomly

100

chosen neurons (Fig. 1E-1F, cf. Online Methods). For all neurons of the present study, the averaged

101

spike shapes within spike-sorted neurons statistically correlated better than with averaged spike shapes

102

across different neurons. This strongly suggests that we recorded from the same neurons throughout the

103

entire experiment.

104

Next, we examined how state-dependent changes in angular sensitivity are reflected in the

105

broadness of neural tuning. For each neuron and locomotory state, we calculated the neuron’s vector

106

strength. The higher the vector strength, the narrower the neural tuning, and vice versa. As expected,

107

neurons that started to encode the solar azimuth during fixed flight (Rayleigh-test: p < 0.05) exhibited

108

higher vector strengths during fixed flight compared to quiescence (Wilcoxon signed rank test: p <

109

0.0001; green data points in Fig. 1G-1H). In contrast, neurons that ceased to encode the solar azimuth

110

during fixed flight (Rayleigh test: p > 0.05) had lower vector strengths during fixed flight compared to

111

quiescence (Wilcoxon signed rank test: p < 0.0001; blue data points in Fig. 1G-1H). Neurons encoding

112

the solar azimuth irrespective of the animal’s locomotory state showed broader tuning during fixed flight

113

compared to quiescence (Wilcoxon signed rank test: p < 0.0001; orange data points in Fig. 1G-H).

Remapping of the heading-direction network during steering flight

132

To test if changes in heading coding occurred when tethered flying butterflies were free to rotate

133

around their yaw axis while the sun stimulus was set at 0° (steering flight; Supplementary Movie 3). We

134

classified 70 of the 150 recorded neurons as encoding the solar azimuth during quiescence and steering

135

flight (Fig. 2A-C; 47% of the neurons; 14 + 56 neurons of gray portion in Fig. 1C). While some neurons

136

showed a similar angular tuning across quiescence, fixed, and steering flight (magenta neuron in Fig.

137

2A-C), many others shifted their preferred firing direction, during steering flight compared to quiescence

138

(Fig. 2C, black lines in circular plots of blue neuron; Supplementary Figure 2), suggesting a remapping

139

of the neural network. When comparing the neural response across a population of 27 neurons that were

140

sensitive at each behavioral state, we found that the remapping mainly occurred during steering flight

141

(Fig. 2D) but rarely between quiescence and fixed flight, and quiescence pre- and post-flight (Fig. 2E

142

and 2G; respectively, n = 76 and 46 for quiescent versus fixed flight and quiescent pre- versus quiescent

143

post-flight; V-test: p < 0.0001). The shifts in angular tuning during steering flight were arbitrary across

144

neurons, indicated by a uniform distribution (Fig. 2F; Rayleigh test: p = 0.09, n = 70). While, during

145

flight, the relative angular position between butterfly and sun stimulus dynamically changed with the

146

butterfly’ heading direction, stimulus history and velocity were constant in quiescence. To exclude the

147

possibility that the observed remapping resulted from differences in the spatiotemporal dynamics of the

148

stimulus, we measured the neural tuning when the sun stimulus unpredictably moved at different

149

directions and velocities in quiescence (Supplementary Movies 4 and 5; Supplementary Figure 3A-C; n

150

= 42 neurons). We found that the neural tuning was neither affected by stimulus history nor stimulus

151

velocity (V-tests: p < 0.001). Remapping could also be evoked by the coding of turns 20,21,24, which may

152

obscure angular tuning to our sun stimulus. Only 7 (5%) of our 150 recorded neurons conjunctively

153

encoded flight turns and heading (example neuron in Supplementary Figure 3D-E), confirming that

154

coding of angular turns was not responsible for remapping. Taken together, our data suggest that the

155

remapping in the heading-direction network mainly occurs during steering flight and is dependent on

156

the animal’s locomotory state.

A 360° compass of heading angles is computed during flight

171

While in Drosophila, remapping of the heading-direction network could be induced by

172

presenting different visual scenes 14,15, a state-dependent remapping was not found 14. In contrast to this,

173

we here demonstrate for the first time a state-dependent remapping in the heading-direction network of

174

a long-distance migrating insect. To understand the biological role of state-dependent remapping in

175

monarch butterflies, we compared the distribution of preferred firing directions during quiescence with

176

the distribution during steering flight. During quiescence, the neurons predominantly encoded the

177

butterfly’s frontal hemisphere (nquiescent = 92 neurons; Fig. 3A; V-test: p < 0.001), while during steering

178

flight the neurons fully tiled 360° representation of heading (n = 107; Fig. 3B; Rayleigh test: p = 0.83).

179

These results strongly suggested that the heading-direction network changes its coding strategy and only

180

functions as a compass when the butterfly is flying.

Octopamine changes angular sensitivity

226

What mechanisms underly the state-dependent transformation into a sun compass during flight?

227

As some neurons increased their firing rates during flight (e.g., Fig. 1D, green neuron), we wondered

228

whether changes in neural firing rate contribute to the state-dependent changes in angular sensitivity.

229

We found that this was not the case as changes in neural firing rates during quiescence (pre- & post-

230

flight) and flight (Fig. 5A) were not sufficient to induce any state-dependence changes (orange data

231

points in Fig. 5A). In flies, the neuromodulator octopamine, the invertebrate analog of noradrenaline 25,

232

is expressed during flight and has been linked to state-dependent changes in the neural sensitivity of

233

optic lobe and descending neurons 26,27. To test if octopamine could mediate the state-dependent changes

234

we observed in the heading-neural network, we topically applied chlordimeform (CDM), an octopamine

235

agonist, onto the brain of quiescent butterflies (N = 13 animals; n = 50 neurons) to mimic potential

236

flight-induced physiological effects 26,28. Following CDM application, eight neurons (15 %, Fig. 5B-C,

237

cyan neuron; neuron ID 17-24 in Fig. 5D) started to encode the solar azimuth, whereas 16 neurons (31

238

%, Fig. 5B-C, magenta neuron: neuron ID 1-16 in Fig. 5D) lost angular sensitivity. For the remaining

239

26 neurons (51 %), we could not detect an effect of CDM on angular sensitivity. The vector strength of

240

neurons from CDM-treated quiescent butterflies was similar to that for neurons of non-treated butterflies

241

during fixed flight (compare magenta /cyan data points in Fig. 5E-F and blue/green data points in Fig.

242

1G-1H). While flight-induced changes in angular sensitivity could be mimicked by CDM treatment,

243

CDM did not however induce any remapping (Fig. 5G, V-test: p < 0.001). These results suggest that

244

idiothetic cues exclusively processed during steering flight are responsible for remapping the butterflies’

245

heading-direction network into a sun compass.

Integration of idiothetic and visual inputs induces remapping during flight

264

During steering flight, insects receive a multitude of different idiothetic signals, including

265

proprioceptive signals from flight muscles 29, vestibular-like signals 30, and motor efference copies.

266

Vestibular-like signals inform animals about rotations and provide sufficient information to encode the

267

insect’s heading even when the animals orient in darkness 13,30. To test the relevance of vestibular-like

268

signals for heading coding in monarch butterflies, we mounted the butterflies on a rotation stage in the

269

arena’s center. This allowed us to rotate the butterflies by 360° in the presence of a static sun stimulus

270

(quiescent rotation; Fig. 6A and Supplementary Movie 6) followed by a rotation in darkness (darkness

271

rotation). Similar to results in cockroaches 30 and rodents 31,32, in these conditions, the preferred firing

272

283

Idiothetic cues processed during steering flight dominate over visual cues

311

The explicit heading representation observed during flight in our “2-sun experiment” (Fig. 4)

312

might be established by a hierarchy of cues during steering flight. To examine which sensory input, i.e.

313

visual or idiothetic, is the most relevant for the heading-direction network in different behavioral states,

314

we performed experiments in which both inputs were set in conflict in quiescent and steering flying

315

butterflies. This was accomplished by displacing the sun stimulus by 180° during the experiment. When

316

displacement occurred in quiescent rotating butterflies, the neural tuning shifted with the sun by about

317

180° (Fig. 7A-B; n = 37 neurons; V-test: p < 0.001). These results show that the coding of heading is

318

dominated by the sun stimulus when quiescent animals are rotated when these receive vestibular-like

319

inputs (see Fig. 6C-E). Interestingly, when we repeated the same experiments with steering flying

320

butterflies, the neural tuning did not shift after stimulus displacement (Fig. 7C-D; n = 22 neurons; V-

321

test: p = 0.02 with 0° as expected value). Thus, although the butterflies had the same stimulus setting

322

during quiescence and steering flight, we demonstrate the existence of a state-dependent switch in the

323

cue hierarchy in monarch butterflies. While the sun position dominated the heading coding in

324

quiescence, idiothetic cues such as the “efference-reafferent loop” were ranked higher than the sun

325

during flight.

Discussion

337

In the present study, we show substantial state-dependent effects in the heading-direction

338

network of the monarch butterflies, including flight-induced octopaminergic changes in angular

339

sensitivity and steering-induced changes in angular tuning. We provide the first evidence in insects that

340

actual steering has a strong impact on the processing of heading information in the brain, as previously

341

shown in mammals 31,37, and demonstrates the biological relevance of state-dependent changes in the

342

central complex. Depending on the locomotory state, the heading-direction network switches from a

343

sun-bearing coding in quiescence to a proper sun compass coding during flight. A compass tiling 360°

344

of azimuth allows a robust representation of the butterfly’ heading even under ambiguous (2-sun

345

experiment) or variable (conflict experiment) visual settings. Robust heading representation is essential

346

for migratory butterflies as they must maintain a stable flight course over an enormous distance while

347

travelling through diverse visual scenes.

348
349

Changes in angular sensitivity of heading-direction neurons
While recent studies reported state-dependent sensitivity changes of central-complex neurons

350

, the neural mechanisms underlying these changes have remained elusive. State-dependent changes

351

22,23,38

352

in more peripheral brain regions like the optic lobes of blowflies 26 and Drosophila 39-41 have been

353

attributed to a flight-induced increase in the octopamine levels. Our results suggest that octopamine

354

contribute to flight-induced changes in angular sensitivity of central-complex neurons. Unlike what has

355

been shown in visual neurons of fruit flies 41, changes in angular sensitivity did not correlate with a state-

356

dependent change in the firing rate. In Drosophila, it has been reported that ring neurons sending visual

357

information into the E-PG network alter their sensitivity to visual cues in a state-dependent manner 14,17,

358

making this cell type a possible neuronal substrate for the state-dependent changes in angular sensitivity

359

found in monarch butterflies. By modulating the visual input from the population of ring neurons in a

360

state-dependent manner, the heading-direction network of monarch butterflies could gain coding

361

flexibility, similar to what has been reported in Drosophila 14,15.

362
363

Heading coding in quiescence
19

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.04.07.438824; this version posted July 23, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

364

Our methodological approach allowed us for the first time in any insect to discriminate between

365

the influence of different idiothetic inputs on the heading coding, i.e. vestibular-like, proprioceptive and

366

motor efferences coupled with reafferent signals. In addition to visual input from ring neurons, E-PG

367

cells receive idiothetic input in the form of angular movement from P-EN cells 20,21. Since only 5% of

368

our recorded neurons conjunctively encoded flight turns and heading, it is highly unlikely that our results

369

originate from recordings of P-EN cells or neurons downstream of E-PG cells that themselves encode

370

angular movements 42. Thus, E-PG cells represent the most plausible candidate recorded neurons in

371

which remapping occurs in our study.

372

When quiescent butterflies were confronted with an ambiguous 2-sun stimulus, the heading-

373

direction network was not able to unambiguously encode a particular heading. This is because visual

374

cues dominated the heading coding in this situation, as also observed in our cue conflict experiment.

375

Visual inputs are transmitted via the R2/4d ring neurons into the E-PG network 14,17. As ring neurons’

376

receptive fields are biased towards the fronto-lateral visual field in monarch butterflies 43, and also in

377

fruit flies 17, the dominance of visual input is expected to bias the heading-direction network to the

378

frontal visual field, which is what we observed in quiescent monarch butterflies. However, comparable

379

to what has been found in cockroaches 30, the butterfly’ heading is not purely based on visual

380

information. It also relies on vestibular-like idiothetic cues, such as mechanosensory inputs. As

381

previously shown in fruit flies 18, mechanosensory signals from the antennae may be integrated via

382

another set of ring neurons into the butterfly’ heading network to enable the actual heading to be

383

sustained even in darkness. However, our conflict experiments demonstrate that in quiescent butterflies

384

vestibular-like inputs would be less relevant than visual information from the sun stimulus, as allowing

385

butterflies to fly while mechanically blocking their steering was not sufficient to induce a change in the

386

preferred firing directions. This indicates that proprioceptive information from the flight apparatus is

387

not responsible for changes in the neurons’ preferred firing direction. Thus, even in a flying butterfly

388

deprived from turns, the sun stimulus, and thus the ring neurons activity, dominate the coding of heading

389

in the central complex. The coding of heading based on visual inputs – and in the absence of steering

390

commands – will therefore manifest in a body-related sun-bearing coding in quiescence and during fixed

391

flight.

Flight steering-dependent transformation into a sun compass

394

When the tethered flying butterflies were free to steer, we observed substantial shifts in the

395

neurons’ preferred firing directions extending the heading-direction network to a 360° representation of

396

angular space. Notably, the steering-induced switch to a 360° sun compass leads to an unambiguous

397

heading coding during flight even when animals oriented with an ambiguous 2-sun stimulus. This

398

requires the heading-direction network to relate the angular position of multiple cues with respect to the

399

animal’s orientation, which results in an allocentric compass coding. We showed that this switch to a

400

compass coding depends on the integration of motor efference copies and reafferent signals into the

401

central complex. While the ring neurons dominate the coding in quiescence, our cue conflict experiment

402

suggests that the heading coding in E-PG cells is mainly controlled by idiothetic signals from P-EN

403

neurons during flight. The dominance of the P-EN input during flight was unexpected since results in

404

Drosophila demonstrated that the coding of heading is dominated by visual information 13,44, similar to

405

what has been found in vertebrates, including fish 9, birds 45, and rats 10. The differences observed in

406

modality weighting in monarch butterflies could represent adaptations to a migratory lifestyle.

407

Anatomical differences between the heading-direction systems of Drosophila and migratory desert

408

locusts have been proposed to increase the robustness of the compass during long-distance navigation

409

46

410

changes in the solar azimuth. Under these conditions, the heading-direction network primarily relies on

411

idiothetic cues. The difference in modality weighting we observed in butterflies compared to previously

412

reported in Drosophila may also be of a methodological nature. In Drosophila, the heading-direction

413

network has been studied in head-fixed preparations with the visual stimulus moved according to

414

intended angular movements 13-15,18. In our experiments, the monarch butterflies were actually steering

415

which results in more naturalistic idiothetic inputs, including motor efference copies, reafferent signals,

416

and vestibular-like signals than in head-fixed preparations.

417

Taken together, the transition of the heading-direction network into a sun-compass during flight requires

418

the integration of multimodal information into the central complex. In nature, additional inputs, such as

419

the time-compensation of the sun position 4,47,48 and/or the earth’s magnetic field 49,50, may provide

. Consistent with this idea, our results show that heading representation remains robust to sudden

additional cues to form a robust compass system that monarch butterflies use to efficiently maintain

The influence of stimulus history and velocity on neural tuning

573

To test for the influence of stimulus history on neural tuning, the simulated sun was moved with a

574

575

stimulus history). To test for changes in preferred firing directions associated with stimulus velocity, we

576

also varied the velocity of the sun stimulus between 20-80°/s while it was moved in an unpredictable

577

manner around the animal (unpredictable stimulus velocity; stimulus details are plotted in Fig. S3A).

578

The preferred firing directions obtained in these experiments were compared to the neural response of a

579

predictable moving sun stimulus (quiescent). At the end of each experiment, the preferred firing

580

directions were also measured during flight (steering flight). The preferred firing directions in

581

quiescence and during steering flight were compared with the preferred firing directions obtained in

582

response to the unpredictably moving sun stimulus conditions (n = 42 neurons). A V-test (expected

583

mean: 0°) was used to test if the change in preferred firing direction was similar between two conditions.

584

If the V-test was significant (p < 0.05) this means that data were clustered around 0° implying that the

585

preferred firing direction did not change between the tested conditions.

Testing for coding of turning behavior

588

To test whether the recorded central-complex neurons encoded flight turns, we selected the flight

589

sections in which the animal’s heading changed more than 9° from its actual heading direction. We set

590

9° as threshold for turns because the angular sensitivity of the optical encoder was 3° and deviations of

591

± 3° could represent variations in flight direction rather representing flight turns. We then examined the

592

firing rate prior to and after the moment of a turn during a one-second window (500 ms prior and 500

593

ms after a flight turn). The neural activity during the turn was normalized to the firing rate 500 ms prior

594

to the turn and was compared to the firing rate of sections in which the animal flew straight. Neurons

595

were categorized as coding for flight turns if (i) changes in the normalized neural activity during flight

596

turns were higher/lower than the changes occurring during periods of no turns (Wilcoxon p-test < 0.05)

597

or (ii) if changes in the normalized neural activity during time windows of flight turns fitted a Gaussian

598

distribution (> 0.6). 12 of the 150 (8%) neurons passed both criteria and were defined as neurons coding

599

for flight turns. Only 7 of these 12 neurons additionally encoded the angular position of the light spot

600

during flight.

611

612

638
