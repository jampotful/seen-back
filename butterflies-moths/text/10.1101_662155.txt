Abstract

23

Forests are a mosaic of light spectra, and colour signal efficiency might change in

24

different light environments. Local adaptation in Heliconius butterflies is linked to

25

microhabitat use and the colourful wing colour patterns may be adapted for signalling in

26

different light environments. These toxic butterflies exhibit conspicuous colours as a

27

warning to predators that they should be avoided, but also find and choose potential mates

28

based on colour signals. The two selection pressures of predation and mate preference are

29

therefore acting together. Colour conspicuousness should show habitat-specific contrast

30

for the butterflies, which would facilitate detection and species identification. On the

31

other hand, selection for signal stability would be stronger in the avian visual system. In

32

this study we analysed the contrast of two Heliconius mimicry rings in their natural

33

habitats under varying degrees of forest fragmentation and light conditions. We used

34

digital image analyses and mapped the bird and butterfly vision colour space in order to

35

examine whether warning colours have greater contrast and if they transmit a consistent

36

signal across time of the day and habitat in a tropical forest. We tested conspicuousness

37

using opponent colour channels against a natural green background. For avian vision,

38

colours are generally very stable through time and habitat. For butterfly vision, there is

39

some evidence that species are more contrasting in their own habitats, where

40

conspicuousness is higher for red and yellow bands in the border and for white in the

41

forest. Light environment affects Heliconius butterflies’ warning signal transmission to a

42

higher degree through their own vision, but to a lesser degree through avian predator

43

vision. This work provides insight into the use of colour signals in sexual and natural

44

selection in the light of ecological adaptation.

Introduction

55

The success of a signal is related to its effectiveness in a specific environment and

56

how strongly it influences the behaviour of the receiver (Endler 1978). Forests are a

57

mosaic of light colours, and the same colour pattern can have an altered appearance in

58

different light environments (Endler 1993). If an individual shows high reflectance of a

59

specific wavelength, but the environment lacks light in that part of the spectrum, the

60

region of high reflection will be unimportant as a signal (Stevens et al. 2007). Ambient

61

light spectra also vary from dawn to dusk, hence species that signal only at certain times

62

and places are expected to evolve characteristics and predictable combinations of colours

63

for particular environments (Endler 1993). Therefore, ambient light characteristics should

64

be included together with the receiver visual system to understand the microhabitat choice

65

and behaviour of animals.

66

In the case of colour signals, perception can be altered by the habitat they live in

67

by filtering wavelengths and altering visual backgrounds (Endler 1993; Lovell et al.

68

2005). Sensory drive explains the process of adaptation of signalling and sensory systems

69

to the local environment (Endler 1992; Endler and Basolo 1998). Environment tuned

70

spectral sensitivity is better known in aquatic habitats, such as in guppies (Endler 1980)

71

and cichlid fish (Seehausen et al. 2008), as compared to terrestrial light environments. On

72

land, colour depends on the reflection of the surroundings and has greater variability over

73

time (Boughman 2002). Habitat signal transmission can favour diversification of mating

74

signals through local adaptation, leading to reproductive isolation. Distinct Anolis lizards

75

male dewlaps are found in different microhabitats (Fleishman et al. 1997). Male dewlap

76

colours are more conspicuous in their own habitat than in other habitats, mainly because

77

of the contrast against the background in the ultraviolet (UV) range (Leal and Fleishman

78

2002). Perception of colours in different light conditions can also influence attacks by

79

predators, for example among butterflies in an environment with high UV light, birds

80

aimed at the butterfly wings, more specifically the marginal white eyespots that have UV

81

reflectance, instead of the head (Olofsson et al. 2010).

82

Local adaptation in Heliconius butterflies commonly involves adaptation to

83

specific microhabitat use (Estrada and Jiggins 2002; Elias et al. 2008; Jiggins 2008).

84

Mimicry rings are groups of unpalatable species that share the same warning colour, and

85

these tend to be found in specific microhabitats such as forest or open areas. The

86

Heliconius habitats are associated with the use of larval host-plants, adult food plants,

87

sexual behaviour and gregarious roosting (Mallet and Gilbert 1995). Species that lay eggs

88

on Passiflora species that occur in second growth tend to be seen in open areas, while

89

species that lay eggs on canopy Passiflora vines are seen flying high in the forest. The

90

choice of microhabitat also might be connected with light differences between those

91

environments, such as the choice of using shady areas in communal roosting (Mallet and

92

Gilbert 1995; Finkbeiner 2014).

93

Therefore, different light environments should create microhabitats where

94

butterfly signals would be more efficient. Although mimicry rings differ in their

95

microhabitat, the light environment has not been measured to verify whether colour

96

patterns could be specifically adapted to particular light environments. The colourful

97

wing colours of Heliconius butterflies may also be subject to evolution caused by sensory

98

drive due to their potentially conflicting roles in predation and mate preference. Many

99

species exhibit Müllerian mimicry (Müller 1879), in which two or more species share the

100

same conspicuous colour as a warning to predators that they are toxic and should be

101

avoided (Benson 1972). Also, these butterflies find and choose potential mates based on

102

colour signals, which can lead to reproductive isolation (Jiggins et al. 2001; Sweeney et

103

al. 2003; Kronforst et al. 2006). Furthermore, communication between conspecifics might

104

be based on UV signals, since H. erato females express the duplicate UV opsin gene,

105

which allows a greater degree of discrimination of the UV-yellow wing patches (Briscoe

106

et al. 2010; Bybee et al. 2012; McCulloch et al. 2016; Dell’Aglio et al. 2018).

107

This microhabitat structuring allows mimicry rings to remain distinct. This may

108

be because there are sets of predators in different habitats, each of which perceive a

109

different mimicry ring as the most abundant pattern (Joron and Mallet 1998). Although

110

little is known of the specific predators that attack Heliconius, it seems likely that their

111

aposematic signals are directed at several predators with different visual abilities and

112

spectral sensitivities (Dell’Aglio et al. 2018). Ambient light together with predator

113

sensitivity can interfere with the interpretation of the information perceived from colour

114

signals.

115

Warning coloration should, therefore, be easy to detect and memorize even in

116

heterogeneous environments and light conditions (Guilford and Dawkins 1991; Endler

117

1992). Warning signals are often dominated by red, yellow and orange, frequently

118

contrasting with black, which are the main colours in Heliconius. The reason why these

119

long-wavelength colours are widely represented in aposematic coloration is that they are

120

highly conspicuous against natural backgrounds, are more stable across light conditions,

121

allowing long distance discrimination and detectability, and influence memorability

not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available

122

(Guilford and Dawkins 1991; Stevens and Ruxton 2012; Arenas et al. 2014; Dell’Aglio

123

et al. 2016).

124

Perception of colour depends on several neurophysiological mechanisms, such as

125

the presence of opponent colour channels. This chromatic mechanism involves

126

comparisons of receptors outputs, in which opposite neural pathways are either activated

127

or inhibited depending on the stimuli reaching the eye (Kelber et al. 2003; Renoult et al.

128

2015). This mechanism is useful especially regarding colour stability against spatial and

129

temporal variation in illumination (Lovell et al. 2005; Renoult et al. 2015). For example

130

birds, the major predator of aposematic butterflies, have tetrachromatic vision and

131

seemingly have at least three opponent channels, as found in domestic chicks (Vorobyev

132

et al. 1998; Osorio et al. 1999). Opponent channels have also been described for insects

133

(Chittka et al. 1992; Chittka 1996) and butterflies (Kelber 1999), and have been

134

hypothesized for Heliconius butterflies although more behavioural analyses are needed

135

to confirm which opponent channels are actually used (Swihart 1971, 1972; Bybee et al.

136

2012). Butterflies in the genus Papilio have duplicate LW opsin genes to see in the red

137

and green range (Kelber 1999; Briscoe 2008), while Heliconius has differences in the red

138

and green photoreceptor sensitivity associated with the presence of red filtering pigments

139

in the ommatidia (Zaccardi et al. 2006; McCulloch et al. 2016). Thus, we expect avian

140

predators and butterflies to rely on these high-contrast systems to process information

141

under a changing light environment.

142

The aim of this study was to analyse Heliconius warning colouration under

143

different light conditions in their natural habitats. In particular, to test conspicuousness of

144

the wing colouration against a natural green background, encoded by opponent colour

145

channels. Using digital image analyses, butterfly wings were photographed and mapped

146

to UVS and VS avian predator vision and to Heliconius erato vision (Figure 1). Our

147

predictions are that (1) signal contrast and conspicuousness for avian predators should

148

have stability, that should be reliable throughout the day and in different light

149

environments (Stevens and Ruxton 2012; Arenas et al. 2014). Warning signals might be

150

honest indicators of prey unprofitability to predators, and if signals fluctuate through the

151

day and between light environments, we would predict that this could delay learning by

152

predators and be costly to the prey. Similarly for internal contrasts (i.e. contrast between

153

black and the coloured bands), therefore conspicuousness would not rely totally on

154

background contrast but also on internal patterns which account for close-distance

155

conspicuousness (Endler 1978; Aronsson and Gamberale-Stille 2009). (2) From a

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/662155; this version posted September 26, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was

156

Heliconius butterfly perspective; we predict that signal contrast and conspicuousness

157

should show habitat-specific maximum background contrast and higher colour

158

differences in their own habitats (Table 1), which would facilitate detection and species

159

identification. We therefore predict that selection for signal stability will be much stronger

160

in the avian visual system as compared to the butterfly visual system.

Material and Methods

166

Study site and species

167

Fieldwork was performed during the dry season, along Pipeline Road, a tropical

168

lowland rainforest in the Panama Canal Zone (Parque Nacional Soberanía, 9º7’33”N,

169

79º42’90”W). Pipeline Road makes a transect through the forest, creating a

170

heterogeneous habitat with open sunny areas and close canopy exceeding 30 m in height.

171

All specimens were collected in the area. Two pairs of co-mimics that live in sympatry

172

were selected, H. erato demophoon (n = 8) and H. melpomene rosina (n = 8), H. sapho

173

(n = 5) and H. cydno (n = 5), each pair belonging to a different mimicry ring, red and

174

yellow, and white, respectively (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Digital photography

177

The general approach and methodology for this work was based on previous work

178

with colour stability using opponent signals (Lovell et al. 2005; Arenas et al. 2014). The

179

spectral reflectance of mimetic pairs was investigated using digital photography. This

180

provides a way to control for natural variation in luminance intensity (shadowing) that is

181

not captured by spectrometry, and also allows non-invasive colour measurements easily

182

applied in the field (Stevens et al. 2007; Troscianko and Stevens 2015). Therefore,

183

through this method we could obtain colour measures under the sensitivity of all receiver

184

photoreceptors (300-700 nm) in the actual viewing conditions of conspecifics and avian

185

predators.

186

Fresh wings of each specimen were photographed following the same methods of

187

image collection in Dell’Aglio et al. (2018). The camera was fitted to a tripod and pointed

188

towards the ground (90º) at a height of approximately 80 cm. Each photo setup included

189

two individuals, one of each species of the co-mimic pair, a 40% grey standard

190

(Spectralon® Labsphere) used for calibration and a leaf freshly collected to make

191

background measures. The species used was Guazuma ulmifolia (Sterculiaceae), a small

192

abundant shrub across all Pipeline Road, which facilitated the collection of fresh leaves.

193

Photos were taken under three different arboreal canopy conditions, forest border,

194

closed forest, and open area, where those butterflies are usually seen (Table 1). All photos

195

were taken under sunny to part-cloudy days, with three replicates in each habitat making

196

sure that the amount of light was similar. In order to standardize the replicates, light

197

measures were taken with a digital light meter (Digital Lux meter, Tondaj LX-1010B),

198

which measures the total amount of LW (555 nm) per square meter (Lux) (Figure 2). Also

199

photographs of the canopy were taken in order to measure vegetative cover, which was

200

81.5% (SE ± 0.2) for closed forest, 59.8 % (SE ± 3.4) for forest border, and 0% for open

201

area. The aim was to analyze how colour signals are perceived throughout the morning

202

when butterflies are most active. Therefore, photos were taken at dawn (7 am), morning

203

(9 am) and noon (12 pm) during a short period of 15min as light conditions change

204

rapidly. Open area photographs were taken only at 12 pm to represent a highly used

205

environment by butterflies at this period of day.

Image analysis and visual modelling

209

All images were processed and analysed into the imaging software ImageJ

210

(Rasband, 1997-2012). RAW human-visible and UV images were linearized and aligned

211

following the methodology of Troscianko and Stevens (2015) and Arenas et al. (2014).

212

Normally, photos would be normalized to the grey standard, which removes effects of

213

light conditions (Arenas et al. 2014). Since our main interest was to measure how

214

coloration changes in different light environments, the images were not normalized.

215

Instead, an average grey standard value was obtained from all photographs. Photon catch

216

values were obtained for each colour using the entire patch from linearized photos, and

217

subsequently these values were multiplied by each photo exposure time and normalized

218

with the average grey standard. With this methodology we were able to calculate how

219

particular environment and time varies from the natural average light (Stevens et al. 2007;

220

Arenas et al. 2014). We used the average photon catch results from the three habitats

221

replicates. Predicted photon catch values were obtained using spectral sensitivity for each

222

cone type of the blue tit (Cyanistes caeruleus) for the UV-sensitive vision (UVS) (Hart et

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/662155; this version posted September 26, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

223

al. 2000), peafowl (Pavo cristatus) for the violet-sensitive vision (VS) (Hart 2002) and

224

the female Heliconius erato (Briscoe et al. 2010; McCulloch et al. 2016).

225

Background of many terrestrial habitats is dominated by greenish vegetation;

226

therefore, a green leaf was chosen to make contrast calculations. Differences between

227

light environment and time of the day were calculated using the contrast of warning

228

colours against an average green leaf. Channel activation in avian vision was calculated

229

using the Red-Green (RG), Blue-Yellow (BY) and Blue-UV opponent channel (Osorio et

230

al. 1999; Lovell et al. 2005; Stevens et al. 2009). For the achromatic signal, we used avian

231

double cones (DBL). Using a ratio-based approach suggested by Lovell et al. (2005), we

232

calculated the opponent channel responses as follows:

233

RG = LW – MW / LW + MW

234

BY = SW – (LW + MW) / SW + (LW + MW)

235

Blue-UV = SW – UV / SW + UV

236

Achromatic = DBL

237

The Heliconius opponent channels were calculated based on what has been

238

proposed in earlier studies regarding Heliconius vision (Swihart 1971, 1972; Bybee et al.

239

2012). The H. erato compound eye has red filtering pigments that shift LW photoreceptor

240

sensitivity from green to red and as the physiological mechanisms underlying these two

241

LW photoreceptors are not known, sensitivities in Green (560 nm) and Red (600 nm)

242

were used (McCulloch et al. 2016, 2017). To investigate differences between co-mimic

243

species, we calculated opponent channel activation based on the prediction that

244

Heliconius mating system might use UV and Red/Green light for mate choice (Bybee et

245

al. 2012; McCulloch et al. 2016, 2017). Opponent channels in Heliconius vision was

246

calculated using as follows:

247

RG = Red – Green / Red + Green

248

BY = Blue – (Red + Green) / Blue + (Red + Green)

249

Blue-UV2 = Blue – UV2 / Blue + UV2

250

UV2-UV1 = UV2 – UV1 / UV2 + UV1

251

To examine whether warning colours have greater contrast against green

252

background we calculated the Weber Contrast (Whittle 1994), which takes into account

253

the image value of the objects of interest as a fraction of background appearance using

254

the formula:

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/662155; this version posted September 26, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

C = (object − background) / background

255
256

Where background corresponds to the green leaf opponent channel values, and

257

object corresponds to warning colour opponent channel values. This measure is suited to

258

comparisons between small objects against larger backgrounds, such as butterflies against

259

the green forest. For internal contrast, achromatic values of the warning colours were used

260

against the black of each individual wing as background (Arenas et al. 2014). We plotted

261

the mean absolute contrast of each colour signal as a function of time and light

262

environment for the three vision models.

263

To test contrast stability through time and habitat, we used the coefficient of

264

variation (CV) of each colour in each opponent channel for each visual system. The CV

265

is an effective measurement to determine how relatively stable a measurement is around

266

a mean value. The CV can be interpreted as greater its value, more variance and instability

267

the signal would be, following methodology from Arenas et al. (2014).

Statistical analyses

270

All statistical calculations were processed in the software R 3.5.3 (R Core Team

271

2020). Our approach was to model colour contrasts over the course of a day and under

272

different habitats in term of both predator and butterfly vision. General linear mixed-

273

effects models were performed using random effects (package lme4) followed by Tukey’s

274

post-hoc (package multicomp). Normality tests showed that contrast data were not

275

normally distributed, therefore glmer tests were performed using Gama distribution. Raw

276

data was plotted to illustrate the results (package ggplot2). The models were fitted

277

accordingly to the predictions outlined above. Analyses were carried out using contrast

278

values as the dependent variable, and fixed and random factors varied depending on the

279

question. Factors were individuals, colour (red, yellow, white), habitat (border, forest,

280

area), time (7am, 9am, 12pm), and bird vision (UVS, VS). For Heliconius vision, we also

281

added side of the wing (dorsal, ventral).

Signal contrast and conspicuousness for Heliconius conspecifics

302

In some cases, contrasts followed our prediction that species would be more

303

contrasting in their own habitats (Figure 4). The yellow colour was more contrasting in

304

the border in the UV2-UV1 channel, especially early hours such as 7 am (t = 6.9, P <

305

0.001, Table S5) and 9 am (t = 354.3, P < 0.001, Table S5), but not at 12 pm (t = 2.2, P =

306

0.218, Table S5). In the Blue-UV2 channel, white was more contrasting in the forest than

307

in the border at 7 am (t = -3.9, P < 0.001, Table S5). Although, while white colour contrast

308

decreased during the day in the forest, it increased at 12 pm in the border (t = 4.77, P <

309

0.001, Table S5) (Figure 4).

310

The red colour showed large differences in the RG channel between dorsal and

311

ventral side, with dorsal side with the higher contrast (t = 10.1, P < 0.001, Table S6).

312

Same results were found for red in the Blue-Yellow and Blue-UV2 opponent channel

313

(Table S6) (Figure 4).

314

The contrast of signals against the background revealed greater differences across

315

habitat and time when seen through the Heliconius vision model (Table 2). Heliconius

316

317

318

Discussion

328

The bright and contrasting Heliconius wing patterns appear well adapted for

329

signalling distastefulness to predators. However, their colour constancy and appearance

330

in different light environments remains poorly studied. Here we have shown that colours

331

are indeed very stable for avian predator vision, but somewhat less so for Heliconius

332

vision. This is consistent with the idea that wing patterns are primarily selected for their

333

role in signalling distastefulness to predators.

334
335

Signal stability and conspicuousness to avian predators

336

Generally, warning signals involve combinations of long-wavelength colours such

337

as red, orange, and yellow which are highly conspicuous against natural backgrounds and

338

stable under different natural conditions (Lovell et al. 2005; Stevens and Ruxton 2012).

339

In our results, Heliconius red colouration has higher detectability against average green

340

background in the RG output and these results are consistent regardless of habitat and

341

time of the day. Previous work has investigated colour stability through opponent colour

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/662155; this version posted September 26, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

342

channels and also showed that red coloration is more contrasting and stable against green

343

backgrounds over the course of a day and across light conditions to the bird visual system

344

(Lovell et al. 2005; Arenas et al. 2014). Moreover, the Heliconius yellow colouration also

345

is highly conspicuous against its internal black pattern in the achromatic output.

346

Achromatic information is one of the main cues used for motion detection (Hämäläinen

347

et al. 2015). Therefore, our results suggest that red and yellow signals work together and

348

are likely effective in stimulating avian opponent channels in order to be conspicuous in

349

all light environments.

350

There is evidence that red and yellow colouration serve as reliable warning signal

351

to avian predators (Ham et al. 2006; Svádová et al. 2009; Arenas et al. 2015), but that this

352

is less true of white colouration. One explanation is that white is more variable across

353

time and habitat, so provides a less reliable signal under varied light conditions (Stevens

354

and Ruxton 2012; Arenas et al. 2014). As an example, field and aviary experiments with

355

polymorphic yellow and white wood tiger moths, Parasemia plantaginis, showed that

356

yellow males are avoided more than white males by predators, but white males have

357

higher mating success (Nokelainen et al. 2012). Our results showed that white contrasts

358

against green background were lower and rather variable for avian vision. The co-mimics

359

H. sapho and H. cydno also contain iridescence blue that was not measured with this

360

methodology. However, the lack of high contrast in white colouration might be balanced

361

with the fact that polarized light might act as a signal, especially in forest habitats

362

(Sweeney et al. 2003; Douglas et al. 2007; Pegram et al. 2015).

363

Highly conspicuous warning signals are expected to evolve to be stable in their

364

appearance throughout the day and between light environments, in order to remain honest

365

indicators of prey unpalatability (Blount et al. 2009; Cortesi and Cheney 2010; Stevens

366

and Ruxton 2012; Arenas et al. 2015). If warning signals fluctuate through time and space

367

this could alter bird foraging experiences and reduce the effectiveness of the aposematic

368

signal. The final decision on whether or not to attack a prey results from a combination

369

of information reaching the predator brain, and for greater efficiency, aposematic

370

coloration needs to be easy to remember (Endler 1988). Our results support this

371

prediction, as colours were generally stable through time and light environments in all

372

opponent systems with only a few exceptions. Notably these occurred where contrasts

373

were higher in open areas and in the early morning. This might also be favourable as the

374

prey would be more conspicuous when they are most vulnerable to predation, since birds

375

are more active and forage early in the morning (Buskirk et al. 1972; Poulin et al. 2001;

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/662155; this version posted September 26, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

376

Steiger et al. 2009). In agreement with this, Heliconius predation and roost disturbance

377

has been observed in the early morning (Mallet 1986; Finkbeiner 2014).

378
379

Habitat and time influence conspicuousness in Heliconius conspecifics

380

Butterflies belonging to the two mimetic rings studied here tend to be segregated

381

between habitats, corresponding to areas where the photographs were taken, although

382

there is considerable overlap (Estrada and Jiggins 2002). We showed that the colours

383

were more unstable when seen through Heliconius vision as compared to avian vision and

384

some colours tend to be more contrasting in their respective habitats.

385

Our results provide some evidence that co-mimic rings are more conspicuous in

386

their own habitat as seen through Heliconius vision, reinforcing the idea that ecological

387

adaptation leads to spatial segregation to where detection would be facilitated. Some

388

colours had higher contrast against green backgrounds in their respective habitat, such as

389

yellow in the border and white in the forest. Nonetheless, red showed the opposite trend

390

and was generally more contrasting in the forest. Differences across light environments

391

could affect mating preferences by altering search costs for a specific colour pattern, and

392

perhaps changing the fitness of different colour patterns. Adaptation in different

393

microhabitats within the forest might have an influence on how closely related species

394

commonly differ in pattern, while convergence in pattern occurs between more distantly

395

related species (Joron and Mallet 1998). Ecological adaptation is attributed to habitat

396

preference and leads to assortative mating (Jiggins 2008). The two sister species studied

397

here, H. melpomene rosina and H. cydno, are known to rarely hybridise in the wild, hence

398

microhabitat segregation reduces potential mating encounters between these two species

399

and reduces gene flow (Mallet et al. 1998; Merrill et al. 2013). Subtle environmental

400

conditions could affect recognition in mating behaviour as seen in the jumping spider,

401

Habronattus pyrrithrix, which red males were more successful in approaching females in

402

the sunlight (Taylor and McGraw 2013).

403

The activation of opponent channels was often higher in the early hours of the

404

morning, at the time when the butterflies are more active and leave their roost or perches

405

to forage (Mallet 1986; Finkbeiner et al. 2012). This was especially the case for

406

Heliconius white and yellow wing colours in the UV channel, which might act in

407

intraspecific communication (Briscoe et al. 2010; Bybee et al. 2012). There is evidence

408

that distinct UV colour signals are being transmitted between co-mimics, which may

409

reduce costs of mating confusion (Dell’Aglio et al. 2018). Similarly, in two species of

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/662155; this version posted September 26, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was
not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available
under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

410

newt, belly colour is distinct in the UV range and females often made mistakes choosing

411

the wrong males in the absence of UV light (Secondi and Théry 2014).

412

In this context, the duplicate genes encoding two distinct visual pigments with

413

sensitivity peaks in the UV range in H. erato females offer the potential for enhanced

414

spectral discrimination in light environments and time of the day where UV is more

415

prominent. A UV2-UV1 opponent channel was proposed by Bybee et al. (2012), who

416

showed that this receptor combination would have lower error rates for discrimination

417

between Heliconius and Dryas yellows. There is no direct evidence for such a mechanism

418

yet, but the fact that males and females show differences in the expression of the two UV

419

proteins suggests that UV2-UV1 contrasts could be an important opponent channel for a

420

female specific behaviour, perhaps mate recognition or host plant finding (McCulloch et

421

al. 2016, 2017). On the other hand, there is some evidence for a Red-Green opponent

422

channel seen in behavioural trials, which showed that Heliconius erato use both green

423

and red photoreceptor to detect colours in the 590-640 nm range (Zaccardi et al. 2006)

424

and the presence of red filters in their eyes means that this is a possibility (McCulloch et

425

al. 2016). Differences between species in Red-Green channel activity for red colouration

426

might have a role in mate recognition since Heliconius tend to be attracted to red (Merrill

427

et al. 2011).

428

The sensory drive hypothesis describes evolutionary relationships among visual

429

systems, conditions of the light environment and mating preferences (Endler and Basolo

430

1998). Heliconius mating preference is highly linked to colour and in H. melpomene, the

431

gene responsible for red colour pattern is genetically linked to the preference for the same

432

pattern (Jiggins et al. 2001; Naisbit et al. 2001; Merrill et al. 2011). Visual sensitivity data

433

used here is only from H. erato females whereas it might differ for other species in their

434

visual systems and perhaps match with colour preference or habitat (Frentiu et al. 2007;

435

Briscoe et al. 2010; McCulloch et al. 2017). In addition, mating behaviour might benefit

436

from some habitats in maximizing conspicuousness, such as in tropical dwelling birds

437

and wire-tailed manakins which visual contrast is increased during display by habitat

438

choice (Endler and Théry 1996; Heindl and Winkler 2003). Nevertheless, our results

439

suggest that selection for conspicuousness in the preferred habitat could explain in part

440

the divergence in colour pattern in these species.

442

Conclusion

443

In conclusion, the transmission of Heliconius warning signals varies due to light

444

environment to a much greater degree through their own visual system, but to a smaller

445

degree through avian predator vision. Selection for signal detectability under different

446

habitat conditions is a mechanism that is proposed to lead to evolution of signal diversity,

447

as seen in species of Anolis lizards that occupy habitats that match their visual system and

448

signal design (Leal and Fleishman 2002), in species of warblers which different cone

449

opsin gene expression correlate with sexual selection and habitat use (Bloch 2015) and

450

also colour patterns of guppies are more conspicuous to guppies at the times and places

451

of courtship and relatively less conspicuous at times and places of predator risk (Endler

452

1991). Heliconius butterfly warning colours are highly contrasting against the forest

453

background and stable through time and habitat in terms of predator avoidance but also

454

conspicuous to attract the attention of conspecifics. However, more extensive studies

455

considering spectral sensitivities of different Heliconius species and their responses to

456

environmental changes in their signal visibility are needed to confirm the

457

conspicuousness to mates. Opponent channel colour contrasts can predict behaviour of

458

perceivers, however, additional behavioural experiments on how light environment

459

influences prey detectability, such with poison frogs (Rojas et al. 2014), are necessary to

460

verify our results.
