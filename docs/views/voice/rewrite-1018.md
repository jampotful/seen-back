| alligator | Field of view, sharpness, dim light and depth | i | Nobody has measured how wide you see, how sharply you see, how little light you need or how you judge distance. Crocodilians have a reflecting layer in the eye that helps them see at night, and you very likely have one as well. | bee | Red | g | Honeybees and bumblebees have no color-sensing cell for red, so red flowers and other red things look dark to them. You very likely have none either. |
| bee | Speed | m | If you are a honeybee, you can see a light flicker even when it flashes 200 times a second. Humans stop seeing flicker at about 60 flashes a second, so a lamp that looks steady to a human can look like it is flashing to you. |
| bee | Ultraviolet | m | If you are a honeybee or a bumblebee, you have three kinds of color-sensing cell, as a human does. Yours pick up ultraviolet, blue and green, where a human's pick up blue, green and red. Ultraviolet is light that humans cannot see at all. |
| cat | Depth | i | If you are a domestic cat, you judge how far away things are partly by comparing what your two eyes see. Your eyes can change their focus only about half as much as the eyes of a thirty-year-old human. Nobody has measured how lions, lynxes, bobcats and most other cats judge distance. |
| deer | Color | g | If you are a white-tailed deer, you have two kinds of color-sensing cell, one most sensitive to blue light and one most sensitive to green, where a human has three. | dog | Sharpest spot | m | If you have a long nose, like a wolf, a red fox or a whippet, you see most sharply along a band level with the horizon. If you have a short nose, like a pug, you see most sharply in one spot in the middle. |

Nobody has measured how fast you see flicker if you are a lady beetle, a stink bug, a dragonfly or most other butterflies. |
| jumper | Eight eyes (live: Eight eyes, one picture) | m | You have eight eyes. Six of them watch for movement all around you. When they see something move, you turn to face it so your two big front eyes can take a close look. |
| jumper | The side eyes | m | Your front and back side eyes see little detail, and they see mostly in green. If you are a Phidippus, your front side eyes see about a fiftieth of the detail a human sees, and your back side eyes see less than a hundredth. |
| jumper | Living things stand out | m | If you are Menemerus semilimbatus, your front side eyes can tell the movement of a living animal from random movement. Your back side eyes notice anything that moves. |

| jumper | Jumps and scans | m | The retinas inside your front eyes jump to a new target and follow it if it moves. While you look at something new, they sweep slowly from side to side across it and twist back and forth. If you are Metaphidippus harfordi, each jump takes about a tenth of a second. |
| jumper | Looking away on purpose | m | If you are Menemerus semilimbatus, your front side eyes are slow to notice anything new at a spot your front eyes have just looked at. After about five seconds, they notice things there as well as anywhere else. |

| plant | No picture | g | You have nothing like a lens to focus light into an image. In thale cress and the other plants studied closely, cells sense how much light there is, what color it is and which direction it comes from, and you very likely sense light in the same ways. |
| plant | Neighbors | i | If you are a white mustard seedling, a fence of green grass beside you lowers the amount of red light reaching you compared with far-red light, even before it shades you, and your stem grows longer. Far-red is a color just past the edge of what humans can see. Nobody has measured this in most other plants. |
| plant | Green and time | i | If you are a thale cress, extra green light makes your leaf stalks grow longer and your leaves tilt up, as they do in the shade of other plants. If you are a young sunflower, your stem follows the sun across the sky by day and swings back toward the east at night, kept in time by an inner clock. Nobody has measured how most other plants sense green light or keep time. |
| spider | The web | i | If you are a golden silk spider, you feel your prey through the silk, and a trapped insect shakes your web 5 to 30 times a second. | spider | Eyes | g | Most spiders have eight eyes, and you very likely have eight as well. Only a few families, among them the recluse spiders, the woodlouse hunters and the goblin spiders, have six, and they lack the middle pair at the front. |
