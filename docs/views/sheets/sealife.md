## 1. Scope

The largest orders (assignment figures) are Actiniaria 177,139 (sea anemones), Forcipulatida 111,985 (Asterias, Pisaster), Venerida 106,803 (venus clams and kin), Valvatida 88,528 (cushion stars and others), Unionida 88,507 (freshwater mussels), Cardiida 78,447 (cockles, giant clams), Scleractinia 73,099 (stony corals), Camarodonta 65,111 (sea urchins), Mytilida 52,383, Ostreida 47,008, Pectinida 46,126 (scallops), Semaeostomeae 45,846 (Aurelia, Chrysaora), Arcida 33,494 (ark clams) and Anthoathecata 30,168.

In Alachua County the group is rare. The repository's file `docs/views/no-view-us-fl-alachua.csv` lists 9 bivalve species with 38 records, led by the Asian Clam (*Corbicula fluminea*, 14) and the Florida Spike (*Elliptio jayensis*, 11), and one moss animal, the Magnificent Bryozoan (*Pectinatella magnifica*, 4). I opened no source on how either senses light.

**One view cannot be true for this group.** The sources describe three ways of sensing light among commonly photographed members:

1. **No image at all.** Corals and sea anemones sense light with pigments in their tissue and use it to time spawning by the moon (Levy and colleagues, 2007; Lin and colleagues, 2021; Grawunder and colleagues, 2015). A surf clam senses a shadow through a nerve and pulls in its siphon (Kennedy, 1960). The moon jelly has cups of light-sensing cells, and it swims faster in brighter light (Wikipedia, citing Nakanishi and colleagues, 2009).
2. **A coarse image from a few directions.** A starfish has an eye at each arm tip, with about 8 degrees between its facets in the crown-of-thorns starfish, slow and colour blind, and uses it to walk home to a reef (Garm and Nilsson, 2014; Petie, Hall, Hyldahl and Garm, 2016). A purple sea urchin has no eyes, yet turns toward or away from a dark disc 10 degrees wide, its spines shading the light-sensitive cells between them (Yerramilli and Johnsen, 2010; Ullrich-Lüter and colleagues, 2011).
3. **Many small eyes.** A scallop has up to 200 mirror eyes along its mantle, resolving about 2 to 5 degrees (Speiser and Johnsen, 2008; Palmer and colleagues, 2017). A giant clam has several hundred pinhole eyes (Land, 2003). Box jellyfish have 24 eyes, some with lenses (Garm and colleagues, 2011).

**Where the line falls.** The line falls between the echinoderms (starfish and sea urchins, 401,633 records, 27.4%) and everything else (1,066,071 records, 72.6%).

- **Sea anemones**, the largest order, are on the side with no image.
- **Starfish** (Forcipulatida and Valvatida, 200,513 records together) are on the side with a coarse image. The measurements are from two Valvatida, the blue sea star and the crown-of-thorns starfish. No measurement in Forcipulatida was opened.
- **Clams** are on the side with no image. The commonest orders (Venerida, Unionida, Mytilida, Ostreida) have no eyes in any source I opened, and the one clam measured, the surf clam, senses shadow without an eye.
- The eyed bivalves and box jellyfish are a small part of the no-image side. Scallops hold 46,126 records (3.1% of the group). Cardiida mixes eyed cockles and giant clams with others, and its split was not fetched. No cubozoan order is among the fourteen largest orders.

**Recommendation.** Build two views.

- **`sealife`**, the plant's kind of view with no picture, for Cnidaria, Bivalvia, Porifera, Tunicata, Bryozoa, Brachiopoda and Ctenophora. It keeps the key and wording already in `GROUPS` ('a sea creature'). It departs from the plant by keeping quick change, because the shadow responses of clams are sudden. Scallops, giant clams, cockles and box jellyfish stay in it for now, and an `x` row names their eyes.
- **`starfish`**, a coarse grey picture that runs slow, for Echinodermata. Sea cucumbers and brittle stars would share it without measurements of their own, and one `i` row says so.

A later `scallop` view could be drawn from the numbers in section 3, if the owner wants the eyed bivalves out of `sealife`.

Two implementation notes, for the owner:

- `tools/species/eyes.py` would route phylum Echinodermata to `starfish`, and the other phyla and the class Bivalvia to `sealife`.
- `GROUPS` in `src/cam.a.js` already holds `sealife: 'a sea creature'`. A `starfish` key would be new.

## 2. The rows

### View `sealife` (corals, anemones, jellyfish, clams, sponges, sea squirts, moss animals, comb jellies)

```
['i', 'No picture', 'Sea anemones and corals have no eyes. The picture keeps only how much light there is, what colour it is and where it comes from.'],
```
Source: none opened for the absence of eyes, so the row is graded `i`. Levy and colleagues (2007) place the light sense of a coral in cryptochromes, a family of blue-light pigments.

```
['m', 'Full moon', 'A reef coral makes more of one blue-light pigment on full-moon nights than on new-moon nights. Hundreds of kinds of coral spawn together on a few nights each year, and moonlight helps set those nights.'],
```
Source: Levy and colleagues, 2007, in *Acropora millepora* (a stony coral, Scleractinia). *cry2* expression was higher on full-moon nights than on new-moon nights, and the authors suggest a role in timing mass spawning.

```
['m', 'The dark hour', 'One reef coral spawns after the moon starts rising late, leaving a stretch of darkness after sunset. A dim light of about 0.3 lux at night held its spawning back.'],
```
Source: Lin and colleagues, 2021, in *Dipsastraea speciosa*. The dark period between sunset and moonrise after the full moon triggers spawning; moonlight suppresses it; dim light at night, matched to the full moon at about 0.3 lx, suppressed spawning; one hour of darkness at the start of the night was enough, but less efficient than full darkness.

```
['m', 'Blue moonlight', 'A small sea anemone released its eggs and sperm in step only when blue light, standing in for a full moon, shone through five nights of each month. White light did little.'],
```
Source: Grawunder and colleagues, 2015, in *Aiptasia* sp. (Actiniaria). Blue LEDs (400 to 460 nm) through the 12-hour dark period on nights 1 to 5 of a 29-day cycle, at 10 to 16 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹; white light with about 20% blue stimulated some spawning, mostly of eggs, spread over days 16 to 21; under blue light spawning fell on days 13 to 20. Gonad development depends on food, so the row speaks of release (audit correction).

```
['g', 'Shadows', 'A surf clam has a nerve that senses light by itself. When a shadow falls, the nerve fires a burst and the clam pulls in its siphon.'],
```
Source: Kennedy, 1960, in *Spisula solidissima*. One fibre in each pallial nerve fires steadily in the dark, is silenced by light and bursts when light ends, and apparently drives the siphon's shadow response; its inhibition peaks at 540 nm. Graded `g` because it is one clam, extended here to clams, mussels and oysters.

```
['m', 'Moon jellies', 'A moon jelly has small cups of light-sensing cells around its rim, and it swims faster in brighter light. Without those cups it no longer does.'],
```
Source: Wikipedia, "Rhopalium", citing Nakanishi, Hartenstein and Jacobs (2009) for the pigment-cup ocellus, the faster swimming and its loss after removal. **secondary**. The audit could not open the abstract of Nakanishi and colleagues, so the finding is confirmed only as Wikipedia's report, and the owner may prefer `i` until that paper is read. The first draft also said the cups make no picture; Wikipedia supports that only with a general sentence citing a paper on box jellyfish (Stamatis, Worsaae and Garm, 2018), so the audit removed it.

```
['x', 'Scallops and giant clams', 'A scallop has up to 200 eyes along the edge of its shell, each focusing light with a mirror, and a giant clam has several hundred pinhole eyes. This view does not draw them.'],
```
Source: Palmer and colleagues, 2017 (*Pecten maximus*: "up to 200 minute eyes lining the mantle tissue"; a mirror of 20 to 30 layers of guanine crystals). Land, 2003 (*Tridacna maxima*: several hundred pinhole eyes on the exposed mantle).

```
['x', 'Box jellyfish', 'Box jellyfish have 24 eyes, some with lenses, and one kind looks up through the water to steer by the trees above it. This view does not draw them.'],
```
Source: Garm, Oskarsson and Nilsson, 2011, in *Tripedalia cystophora*: 24 eyes of four types; an eye type that stays pointed upward detects the mangrove canopy and guides the medusae to the lagoon edge. Nilsson and colleagues, 2005, for the lenses.

It departs from the plant in one way. The plant averages quick change away over 2.5 seconds, while a clam's shadow response and a giant clam's withdrawal happen at once (Kennedy, 1960; Land, 2003), so a hand passed over the lens should darken the screen as it happens. The monthly timing by the moon cannot be drawn and is named in the rows.

## 6. Open questions for a sensory scientist

1. Have the arm-tip eyes of Forcipulatida (*Asterias rubens*, *Pisaster ochraceus*) been measured for resolution, field and speed? This sheet borrows from two Valvatida.
2. What is the flicker fusion rate of a starfish eye, measured directly, and what is the blue sea star's resolution? The 1 to 2 hertz used here comes from a news report of Garm and Nilsson (2014), and a 16 degree figure could not be confirmed in the audit.
3. Do freshwater mussels, marine mussels, oysters and the Asian Clam show a shadow response, and how large a dimming and how fast a change set it off? Only the surf clam was found.
4. For scallops, which resolution should a picture use: about 2 degrees (Speiser and Johnsen, 2008) or about 5 degrees (Palmer and colleagues, 2017)? Do the two figures measure different things?
5. Do sea anemone tentacles respond to a passing shadow within seconds? The answer would decide whether `sealife` keeps quick change or averages it away as the plant does.
