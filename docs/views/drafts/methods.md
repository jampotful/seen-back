## 1. How each trait is simulated from images

### 1.0 The order of operations

Every published pipeline that turns an image into an animal's view applies the same steps in the same order, and the order changes the result. AcuityView linearises sRGB before filtering and re-encodes afterwards (AcuityView source, `AcuityView.R`: "Convert the original 3 color channels into linear RGB space"). micaToolbox linearises RAW images, normalises them to a grey standard, then maps camera channels to receptor catches (Troscianko and Stevens 2015, sections "Extracting linear images", "Normalisation", "Mapping to cone-catch values"). QCPA models acuity first, then applies its receptor-noise-limited ranked filter to "recreate sharp edges and reduce pixel noise in a cone catch image", then clusters (van den Berg and colleagues, bioRxiv 592261). Vasas and colleagues 2024 align, linearise, then transform camera catches to receptor catches with linear models, and only then choose a false-colour display.

For the phone the order I recommend is: (1) decode the camera's sRGB to linear light; (2) put the picture on a map of directions in the world, using the camera's real field of view (1.1); (3) estimate receptor catches with a matrix (1.2, section 2); (4) apply the eye's optics as a point-spread function in world degrees, separately per region of the eye (1.1, 1.8); (5) sample on the eye's lattice where the lattice is coarse enough to see (compound eyes, 1.8); (6) filter in time like the receptors (1.5); (7) add photon noise for the light level (1.7); (8) compute motion channels from the filtered signal (1.6); (9) map receptor signals to display colours (1.2); (10) only then compress or warp the field for the screen (1.8). Steps 1 to 9 are evidence-led; step 10 and the colour mapping are display conventions and are the app's choices. This ordering is my own construction from the four pipelines above.

Caves and colleagues 2018 (p. 359) state the limit of any such picture: AcuityView images "do not show us what the animal actually 'sees'. Rather, they show us the spatial information content of a scene given acuity and viewing distance", and later processing "cannot add information to an image". The rows should say the same thing in plain words.

### 1.1 Spatial acuity

**The measured quantities.** Acuity is the finest grating an eye resolves, in cycles per degree (cpd). The sampling limit is ν_s = 1/(2Δφ), where Δφ is the angle between neighbouring receptors or ommatidia (Land 1997, eq. 1; Land and Nilsson 2012, eq. 3.2). In a camera eye Δφ = s/f (receptor spacing over focal length); in an apposition compound eye Δφ = D/R (facet diameter over local radius of curvature) (Land 1997, p. 150; Caves et al. 2018, Box 4). The optical limit is the diffraction cut-off ν_co = D/λ cycles per radian for aperture D (Land 1997, eq. 4; Land and Nilsson 2012, eq. 3.4). For a compound eye the combined blur of lens and rhabdom is the acceptance angle Δρ ≈ √((d/f)² + (λ/D)²) (Land 1997, eq. 7, Snyder's approximation; it overestimates by up to 30% for narrow rhabdoms).

**The published simulation: AcuityView.** The R package (Caves and Johnsen 2018; source read from the CRAN mirror, version 1.1.1) does four things. It computes the image's angular width as 2·atan(realWidth/(2·distance)) in degrees. It builds a modulation transfer function on the image's two-dimensional frequency grid, MTF(f) = exp(−3.56·(a·f)²), where f is spatial frequency in cycles per degree and a is the viewer's minimum resolvable angle in degrees (1/cpd). For eyes whose resolution differs horizontally and vertically it uses a(θ) = a_x·a_y/√((a_y·cos θ)² + (a_x·sin θ)²). It multiplies the Fourier transform of each linearised colour channel by the MTF, transforms back and re-encodes to sRGB. The same Gaussian form is Land's (1997, eq. 9) MTF for a Gaussian acceptance function of half-width Δρ, M(ν) = exp(−3.56(νΔρ)²); AcuityView puts the minimum resolvable angle where Land puts Δρ. At the acuity limit (f = 1/a) the MTF is exp(−3.56) = 0.028, so 2.8% of contrast survives.

**What the shader should use.** The constant 3.56 is π²/(4 ln 2) = 3.5597, which makes AcuityView's MTF exactly the transform of a Gaussian point-spread function whose full width at half maximum equals a. So the blur is a Gaussian with σ = a/2.3548 = 0.4247/cpd degrees. CompoundRay uses the same convention for each ommatidium: its shader draws ray directions with standard deviation equal to the acceptance angle divided by 2.35482 (`shaders.cu`, `FWHM_SD_RATIO`). The equivalence is my derivation; both sources use the convention. A Gaussian of σ in degrees becomes σ·(pixels per degree) in the texture. On a phone a Gaussian is drawn as a separable pair of passes (about 6σ + 1 taps each), or approximated by sampling the mipmap pyramid at level log2(σ_px) with a few taps (my judgement of cost: cheap at either size; the per-frame FFT AcuityView uses is heavy on a phone and is not needed).

**Checking build 22.** The engine draws a 16-tap disc of radius 0.6·(longPx/VIEW_DEG)/cpd pixels (`src/cam.b.js`, line 218). A uniform disc of radius R has a standard deviation of R/2 along each axis, so the engine's σ is 0.30/cpd degrees of the viewer's field, 71% of the AcuityView width. Matching AcuityView in variance needs R = 0.85/cpd, or a Gaussian-weighted tap set with σ = 0.4247/cpd. A disc also has a ringing transfer function that reverses contrast at some frequencies, which a Gaussian does not; with 16 golden-angle taps read from a blurred mip level the effect is small. (Arithmetic mine.)

**The scale question, which decides whether the blur is true.** AcuityView blurs in degrees of the scene. Build 22 blurs in degrees of the viewer's view of the screen, taking the screen's long side to fill 24 degrees on a phone (`VIEW_DEG`, `src/cam.a.js`; the convention is stated in `docs/SOURCES.md`). The two differ by the camera's own field. A phone's main camera sees roughly 65 to 75 degrees across (a lens equivalent to 26 mm on a 36 mm frame gives 2·atan(36/52) = 69 degrees; the 26 mm figure is an assumption, and iOS reports the real value in `AVCaptureDevice.Format.videoFieldOfView`, documented as "the format's horizontal field of view in degrees"). At the engine's 1280-pixel working width that is 18.4 pixels per world degree, a Nyquist limit of 9.2 cpd: the camera itself sees about as finely as a cat. Shown in 24 degrees of the viewer's field, the scene is minified 2.9 times, so build 22's cat blur (4 pixels for 8 cpd) corresponds to about 2.8 cpd in the world. (Arithmetic mine.)

Both conventions can be defended, and they answer different questions. The world-degree blur answers "what detail could a cat get from this scene", and for any animal sharper than about 9 cpd the honest answer on a 1280-pixel frame is "more than this camera picture holds". The viewer-degree blur answers "how would a cat see this screen". I recommend the world-degree blur on a true-scale window: crop the camera to the angle the screen fills in the viewer's eye (24 degrees on a phone), which shows the scene at its real angular size, and blur in world degrees there. At 24 degrees the 1280-pixel frame holds 53 pixels per degree (Nyquist 27 cpd), so cats, dogs, most birds (median 11 cpd, Caves et al. 2018), fish, sharks and every insect fall inside what the camera can show, and the person sees exactly the loss of detail the animal has. Views with a wide field then compress the field as a separate, labelled step (1.8), with the blur already applied in world degrees. Hawks and eagles (138 to 140 cpd) stay as they are, magnified, because no phone picture holds that detail.

**Parameters.** Acuity for the app's groups is in section 3 and the log: people 60 cpd at the fovea falling to a tenth of that 7 degrees out (Caves et al. 2018, p. 365); birds 4 to 138 cpd, median 11; mammals 0.1 (*Myotis*) to 64 (chimpanzee); ray-finned fish 0.56 to 40; sharks 2 to 11; insects median 0.25, at most about 2 (*Anax junius*); decapods at most 0.4; *Octopus vulgaris* 46; *Portia* 13 (Caves et al. 2018, Box 3; Land and Nilsson 2012, Table 3.1).

**Three refinements the evidence supports.**
- *Colour detail is coarser than brightness detail.* "Acuity for color patterns is typically lower than for brightness-based patterns", because colour needs a comparison between receptors, which makes "a functionally larger 'pixel'" (Caves et al. 2018, Box 1). The engine could blur the chromatic part of the signal more than the luminance part, as image compression does with chroma. No compilation gives the ratio per species, so the factor (twice the luminance σ, say) would be invented. Cost: one extra mip read per pixel.
- *Acuity falls in dim light.* With 10 photons per integration time the contrast floor is 0.32, which limits resolution to about 60% of the cut-off (Land 1997, p. 156; Land and Nilsson 2012, Fig. 3.11 gives 58%). See 1.7.
- *Motion blurs.* Image motion widens the acceptance angle: Δρ_v² = Δρ² + (vΔt)², with Δt the receptor's integration time, 5 to 50 ms in most insects; blur begins when motion exceeds one acceptance angle per integration time (Land 1997, eq. 12 on p. 158, and p. 157). The phone's gyroscope gives v for the phone's own turning (my proposal). Cost: trivial once σ is computed per pixel.

**Edges after the blur.** QCPA's ranked filter restores sharp edges in a blurred catch image before clustering. That suits measurement; on screen a mild edge enhancement after the blur would be an invented stand-in for neural sharpening, and Caves and colleagues note that such processing "cannot add information". I would leave it out of every view, or mark it invented.

### 1.2 Colour (dichromats, trichromats, tetrachromats and more)

**Step one: receptor catches from the camera.** The method every source uses is a fitted map from camera channels to receptor quantum catches, built from a library of natural reflectance spectra under a chosen illuminant. micaToolbox fits polynomial regressions per receptor (with optional square terms and interactions, simplified by AIC or BIC) on 3,139 natural spectra; fits on natural spectra averaged R² = 0.999 (minimum 0.996), and camera against spectroradiometer on 48 pastels averaged 0.981 (minimum 0.951) (Troscianko and Stevens 2015, "Mapping results"). Vasas and colleagues 2024 use linear models without an intercept, fitted on 2,244 spectra from the Floral Reflectance Database (FReD), with R² that "invariably exceeds 0.90, and in most cases performs above 0.99" (as returned by the fetch tool). Catches are made relative to a white or grey standard under the same light (von Kries adaptation: pavo's `vismodel(vonkries = TRUE)`; micaToolbox's grey-standard normalisation), which also stands in for colour constancy.

Two limits apply to a phone. First, "a camera with three channels (RGB) can reliably map to trichromatic humans, dichromatic mammals or achromatic dogfish cone catch, but it would not be possible to map to species that see into ultraviolet (UV), or have particularly narrow spectral sensitivities" (Troscianko and Stevens 2015). Second, Tedore 2023 (abstract, collection index) found statistical maps trained on natural spectra "lag behind in accuracy, particularly for dark colours, with R2 values dipping as low as 0.23", improving to R² > 0.96 only when the full UV to visible range is used and the training library includes simulated low-reflectance spectra; maps trained on colour charts "should not be trusted". Section 2 gives my own estimate of how well each receptor of the app's animals can be predicted from a colour-correct phone picture, and the matrices that result.

**Receptor sensitivity curves.** Where a sheet has a λmax but no measured curve, the standard is the Govardovskii and colleagues 2000 template, as implemented in pavo (`R/sensmodel-templates.R`): with x = λmax/λ, S(λ) = 1/(exp(A(a − x)) + exp(B(b − x)) + exp(C(c − x)) + D), A = 69.7, a = 0.8795 + 0.0459·exp(−(λmax − 300)²/11940), B = 28, b = 0.922, C = −14.9, c = 1.104, D = 0.674, plus a β-band 0.26·exp(−((λ − (189 + 0.315λmax))/(−40.5 + 0.195λmax))²) for the A1 chromophore. Birds need the oil-droplet filter as well (pavo uses Hart and Vorobyev 2005; T-type droplets transmit from 330 to 800 nm, R-type cut off below about 570 nm, Hart 2001, pp. 684 and 691) and the ocular media (Hart 2001, Fig. 2 caption: catch = pigment absorptance × droplet transmittance × ocular media transmittance). The lens alone sets the ultraviolet limit: the wavelength of 50% transmission is 316 to 343 nm in UV-sensitive passerines and 358 to 380 nm in turkey, peafowl and mallard (Hart 2001, p. 686), and 313 to 478 nm across 38 mammals (Douglas and Jeffery 2014, Table 1; all logged).

**Step two: showing the catches to a person.** Every published display of an animal's colours is a convention, and the sources disagree on which convention to use.

- *Dichromats (cats, dogs, deer, most mammals).* (a) Human dichromat simulation, which projects a colour onto the plane a human protanope or deuteranope can see: Brettel, Viénot and Mollon 1997 (two half-planes) and Viénot, Brettel and Mollon 1999 (one plane, a single 3 by 3 matrix in linear RGB). Build 22's `PROTAN` and `DEUTAN` matrices, entered "from memory", match libDaltonLens's Viénot 1999 matrices digit for digit (`libDaltonLens.c`, computed with the sRGB primaries and the Smith and Pokorny 1975 cone fundamentals); the comment in `src/cam.a.js` can cite that file. libDaltonLens warns that Viénot 1999 "is not accurate for tritanopia". These matrices assume human cones (L near 564 nm, M near 534 nm, Land and Nilsson 2012, p. 34), so they fit an animal only as far as its two cones resemble a human dichromat's. Using `DEUTAN` (L kept) for animals with a long-wave cone near 555 nm and `PROTAN` (M kept) for the ground squirrel's 518 nm, as build 22 does, follows that logic. (b) Receptor false colour: Vasas and colleagues 2024 show a dog's short and long cones in the blue and green channels, with red as the average of the two, and a mouse's UV and green cones in blue and green, with red as their average. (c) My proposal, which keeps the animal's own cones and still looks like dichromat vision: estimate the animal's catches (S, L) with the section 2 matrix, set human L and M excitations both to L and human S to S, and convert to display RGB. The three steps fold into one 3 by 3 matrix per animal, so the cost is the same as today's.
- *Trichromats with ultraviolet (bees, most insects).* Vasas and colleagues colour "the UV, blue, and green quantum catch images as blue, green, and red, respectively". Build 22's `BEE` matrix follows this convention but feeds camera channels straight through (bee green from camera green, bee blue from camera blue). The section 2 matrix gives the bee's green and blue receptors from all three camera channels, and the UV channel has to be invented (1.3).
- *Tetrachromats (birds, turtles, many fish).* Vasas and colleagues depict birds in receptor-noise-limited opponent coordinates in one figure, and in another show the blue, green and red catches as blue, green and red with UV overlaid as magenta. Build 22's `sheen` overlay is of that second kind.
- *More than four classes.* *Graphium sarpedon* has 15 spectral sensitivities, and *Papilio xuthus* has eight classes but "behaves as a tetrachromat in its choice of flower colors" (van der Kooi et al. 2021, section 5). A view could treat such animals as tetrachromats. A display of 12 mantis shrimp channels would be wholly invented.

**Making colour differences mean the same thing across animals.** The receptor-noise-limited (RNL) model of Vorobyev and Osorio 1998 gives the distance between two colours in units of just-noticeable differences. For any number of receptor classes, (ΔS)² equals the sum over all pairs of (Δf_i − Δf_j)²/(e_i²e_j²), divided by the sum of 1/e_i² over all classes, where Δf_i is the difference in log catch and e_i the noise of class i (Clark, Santer and Brebner 2017, eq. 4.5, which reproduces Vorobyev and Osorio's di-, tri- and tetrachromat formulas). Noise is usually set from a Weber fraction for one class and the relative numbers of each cone type: pavo defaults to a Weber fraction of 0.1 and relative densities 1:2:2:4; the blue tit's single cones are 1:1.9:2.7:2.7 (UVS:SWS:MWS:LWS; Hart 2001, Fig. 2 caption, logged). The model can drive the display: scale each pixel's displayed colour contrast so that one just-noticeable difference for the animal is the same step on screen for every animal. An animal with fewer or noisier cones then gets a paler picture for a reason the sources give. The mapping is the app's own choice (invented); the noise values are measured where the log has them. Cost: a few logarithms and multiply-adds per pixel, trivial.

**Colour in dim light.** Birds have one rod class, so their rod vision "cannot be used for colour vision" (Hart 2001, p. 689), and the same holds for most vertebrates (my generalisation). Some nocturnal insects keep colour vision in dim light (van der Kooi et al. 2021, section 7: "Color vision is found in diurnal as well as nocturnal insects"). A dim-light switch should therefore desaturate vertebrate views as the light falls, and leave hawkmoths and nocturnal bees coloured. Needs the light level (1.7).

### 1.3 Ultraviolet

**What can be measured.** A phone cannot record it in any usable way (section 2): the tested phones and other consumer cameras showed "no significant response" outside 390 to 700 nm (Burggraaff et al. 2019). Research cameras record it by removing the internal filter (full-spectrum conversion) and adding a UV-pass filter. Their UV sensitivity "is often around 100 times lower than human-visible light" (Troscianko and Stevens 2015, who use Baader Venus-U filters passing about 320 to 380 nm), or they split the light at 425 nm with a dichroic mirror into a converted and a stock camera, with a 390 nm short-pass filter on the UV side (Vasas et al. 2024).

**What cannot be estimated.** In my computation (section 2) no linear or quadratic function of a colour-correct RGB picture predicts the catch of a true UV receptor better than its mean: honeybee UV (peak 345 nm), blue tit UVS (372), housefly UV (338), *Drosophila* R7 (325) and the jumping spider *Habronattus* short receptor (382) all give leave-one-out R² at or below 0.1. Violet receptors are partly predictable (average violet-sensitive bird 416 nm, R² 0.90 with a median error of 26%). So every UV picture the app draws is invented, and build 22's rule (blue excess plus bright whites, `src/cam.b.js` line 79) is acceptable as an invention and should not be described as an estimate.

**Better inventions, grounded in evidence.**
- *The sky.* Rayleigh scattering is "proportional to the reciprocal of the fourth power of the wavelength" (Hart 2001, p. 694), so open sky is bright in the ultraviolet against land and leaves. A view could brighten the UV channel in sky pixels (bright, blue, upper frame, or above the horizon from the tilt sensor). Cost: trivial.
- *Named flowers.* The app names plants. FReD holds flower reflectance spectra "at wavelengths from 300 to 700 nm", searchable by genus, species and family, downloadable as CSV (Arnold et al. 2010, PLoS ONE). For a named species with a record, the petal's measured UV reflectance could colour the flower's pixels in bee and bird views. The spatial pattern (a dark UV centre, say) would still be invented unless a source shows it. Cost: a lookup table in the species data and a mask; the mask needs a segmentation of the flower, which the recognizer does not give today (moderate).

### 1.4 Polarization

**The evidence.** Microvillar receptors of insects and crustaceans respond selectively to the plane of polarization, and vertebrate rods and cones do not when light arrives along their axis (Land and Nilsson 2012, pp. 39 to 40). Skylight is polarized in a pattern set by the sun, which bees and ants use as a compass and which "largely persists" under cloud; water reflects light polarized parallel to its surface, completely at Brewster's angle, 53 degrees for water, and water bugs use this to find water (Land and Nilsson 2012, pp. 40 to 41). Cuttlefish and mantis shrimps carry polarization patterns on the body, and mantis shrimps resolve circular polarization (Land and Nilsson 2012, pp. 41 to 44).

**What the camera gives.** A phone's sensor has no polarizing filter over its pixels, so the picture carries no polarization (my inference; no source opened says it of phones). Research rigs use polarization cameras or rotating polarizers, and How and Marshall 2014 (Proc R Soc B, abstract only in the collection) frame the detection of objects by polarization contrast as a "polarization distance".

**The stand-in.** The sky's pattern can be computed. In the single-scattering Rayleigh model the degree of polarization is δ = δmax·sin²γ/(1 + cos²γ), where γ is the angle between the viewed point and the sun, cos γ = sin θs·sin θ·cos ψ + cos θs·cos θ (zenith angles of sun and point, ψ their difference in azimuth), and the direction of polarization is "perpendicular to the plane of scattering" through observer, point and sun; it peaks about 90 degrees from the sun (Pomozi, Horváth and Wehner 2001, eqs 1 and 2; the extracted text dropped subscripts, so the reading of θs, θ and ψ as zenith angles and azimuth difference is mine). The phone's orientation sensors give the camera's direction; the date and time give the sun's position once latitude and longitude are known. The owner decided that the full database learns its place from the time zone and from the creatures already met, and does not ask for location (docs/COVERAGE.md). The time zone fixes longitude roughly and latitude not at all, so for a polarization view the sun's elevation would be a guess unless the owner allows asking for location here, or the sun is found in the camera picture (the brightest region of a clear sky). A view for bees, ants or crabs could draw the computed pattern over the sky as faint bands of E-vector direction. For water bugs, water surfaces seen at angles near 53 degrees from the vertical could glow (the tilt sensor gives the angle for a level surface). Cost: per-pixel geometry, trivial; the hard part is the sun's position.

### 1.5 Flicker fusion and temporal resolution

**The measured quantity and its dependence on light.** Critical flicker fusion (CFF) is the lowest flicker rate seen as steady. It rises with light: "with CFF increasing linearly with the logarithm of light intensity (within certain limits) for humans" (Inger et al. 2014, quoted through the fetch tool), and "CFF decreases with light intensity" (same). A honeybee's 200 Hz was measured at 3000 lux and 144 Hz at 300 lux, and a tsetse fly's 286 Hz at 300 lux and 101 Hz at 3 lux (Inger et al. 2014, text as returned by the fetch tool; not logged because no verbatim sentence came back). Across 34 vertebrates, maximum CFF = 118.6 − 0.0002·M + 13.2·log10(q) − 41.1·[low-light species] (Healy et al. 2013, Table 2, model 1; M body mass in grams, q mass-specific resting metabolic rate in W/g corrected to 25 °C, R² = 0.79). That model underpredicts small birds by 30 to 50%: blue tits and flycatchers reached 131 to 146 Hz where it predicts 95 to 97 Hz (Boström et al. 2016).

**Methods.**
- *Animals slower than the screen (toads 6.7 Hz, eels 14, many fish and reptiles 15 to 40; log).* A temporal low-pass filter is a truthful simulation: each frame is mixed into a running average, y ← y + α(x − y), α = 1 − exp(−Δt/τ). A single first-order stage has its −3 dB point at 1/(2πτ) (standard filter theory). Insect receptor integration times of 5 to 50 ms (Land 1997, p. 157) can set τ directly. For vertebrates no source in this unit gives integration times, so τ would be fitted to the CFF (invented): one stage falls off too gently, and two or three stages in cascade give a sharper fall. Build 22's `persist` is this filter with one stage. Cost: trivial.
- *Animals faster than the screen (most birds 70 to 146 Hz, bees and flies 100 to 300).* No 60 Hz display can show detail above 60 Hz, and no 30 Hz camera captures it. The truthful options are to say so in the rows, or to capture a short burst at the phone's highest frame rate and replay it slowed, framed as a slowed clip. Build 22's `rate` (the picture runs slow, then jumps ahead) is an invented metaphor, as `docs/SOURCES.md` already says ("A higher flicker rate does not establish that time feels slower").
- *Space-variant timing.* Böhme and colleagues 2006 filtered video in time differently at each pixel by building "the pyramid ... along the temporal dimension" and interpolating between levels per pixel, at 30 frames per second on 960 by 540 video on a 2006 desktop processor. The same idea lets one region of a view (a dragonfly's dorsal band, a jumping spider's principal eyes) run on different timing from the rest. Cost: the engine's frame ring already holds the levels; moderate.

### 1.6 Motion detection

**Insects.** The standard model is the Hassenstein-Reichardt elementary motion detector: two neighbouring receptors separated by the interommatidial angle ΔΦ; the low-pass-filtered signal of one is multiplied by the unfiltered signal of the other, and the two mirror products are subtracted. For a moving sinusoid of wavelength λ (degrees) and temporal frequency ω, R = ΔI²·sin(2πΔΦ/λ)·τω/(1 + τ²ω²), with τ = 10 ms in the worked example (Lecoeur, Baird and Floreano 2018, Methods and eq. 2). The response depends on pattern as well as speed: EMDs are "tuned to a specific ratio between image speed and angular period" (same). On a phone the detector runs on the lattice of 1.8 (a 64 by 64 map, say): per site, four or six neighbours, one running average per site for the low-pass. Cost: cheap. What the person would see: moving edges glow in a colour that shows direction, with fine fast patterns weak and coarse slow ones strong, which is what a fly's motion system reports.

**Frogs and toads.** Build 22's `gate` (only change is drawn) rests on Lettvin and colleagues 1959 through lecture notes (`docs/SOURCES.md`). An EMD or a temporal band-pass at the toad's slow CFF would make small moving objects stand out and large slow drift fade, which is closer to the published account of frog detectors than drawing every change. The frog sheet should check this against the primary paper.

**The phone's own movement** has to be removed before any motion map means anything. The gyroscope gives the phone's rotation (`DeviceMotionEvent.rotationRate`); subtracting the mean image motion is the fallback. This is the engine brief's plan and my recommendation too.

### 1.7 Dim light and photon noise

**The physics.** The photons one receptor absorbs per second from an extended scene of radiance I (photons m⁻² sr⁻¹ s⁻¹) are N ≈ 0.62·I·D²·d²/f², with D the aperture, d the receptor diameter and f the focal length; d/f can be replaced by the acceptance angle (Land 1997, eq. 11). A white card emits about 10²⁰ photons m⁻² sr⁻¹ s⁻¹ in bright sunlight, 10¹⁷ in room light, 10¹⁴ in moonlight and 10¹⁰ in starlight (Land 1997, p. 157; Land and Nilsson 2012, p. 65). For a diurnal insect (D = 25 µm, d = 2 µm, f = 60 µm) that is 4×10⁷ photons per receptor per second in sunlight, 4×10⁴ in room light, 40 in moonlight and 0.004 in starlight, reduced by perhaps two log units more for integration time and losses (Land 1997, p. 157). Photon arrivals are Poisson, so the noise is √N, and detecting a contrast C needs N > 1/C² photons per integration time: 4 for C = 0.5, 100 for 0.1, 10,000 for 0.01 (Land 1997, eq. 10; Land and Nilsson 2012, Box 3.3, eq. 3.5).

**The simulation (my construction from these equations).** Per pixel and frame: expected catch n = k·(linear pixel value)·(scene light level)·(eye's sensitivity, from D, d, f or Δρ)·(integration time); draw a noisy catch with mean n and variance n (a Gaussian draw is close enough above about 20 photons; below that a Poisson draw); divide by n and multiply back onto the picture. Then apply the animal's strategies as the evidence describes them: spatial summation (pool neighbouring receptors, which is a wider blur) and temporal summation (a longer integration time, a slower running average), which trade acuity and speed for fewer photons' worth of noise (Caves et al. 2018, Box 2; Land 1997, p. 157 on fivefold longer integration in the dark; Land 1997, p. 160 on the √7 gain of neural superposition in flies). Warrant 1999 derives the optimal mix for each light level; it is on the wanted list. Cost: a hash-based random number per pixel per frame and one multiply-add, trivial.

**The missing input is the light level.** A web page's camera frames are auto-exposed, so the brightness of a pixel says nothing about the absolute light. The native shells can read the exposure time and sensor gain of each frame, which with the frame's brightness give the scene's luminance (my knowledge of the Android and iOS camera interfaces; no documentation page opened). Without that, the app could ask "day, dusk or night" or use the clock, and the rows should say the light level is assumed. Build 22's `gain` brightens shadows as a sign of sensitivity; under this model a sensitive eye would instead keep a clean, contrasty picture at a light level where a person's equivalent picture is noisy and grey, and the honest display of "a cat sees in light six to eight times dimmer" is a split or a toggle against the human version at the same measured light.

### 1.8 Field of view and resolution maps

**The evidence.** Eyes sample unevenly: foveas and areas, horizontal streaks (rabbits, fiddler crabs, some sharks, Caves et al. 2018, Box 1; water striders' acute band about 10 degrees high, Land 1997, p. 173), dorsal acute zones for mates or prey against the sky (male blowflies 20 to 30 degrees above the equator; *Anax junius* a band of 5 ommatidial axes per square degree, Land 1997, pp. 171 to 172), and a forward-flight pattern in bees and butterflies with the finest horizontal sampling ahead and the finest vertical sampling at the equator (Land 1997, pp. 167 to 168). Land maps these as "axis density", the number of ommatidial axes per square degree, 1/(2Δφ_h·Δφ_v) (Land 1997, p. 167).

**Methods.**
- *A map of acuity.* Build 22 shapes sharpness with `topoA`, `topoS` and `fall`. A small texture holding σ (and the chromatic σ, and τ) for each direction of the animal's field generalizes these and lets a sheet hand the engine an actual map. The engine already chooses a mip level per pixel (`textureLod` in `FS_VIEW`), which is the core of Perry and Geisler's 2002 real-time method of blending pyramid levels per pixel (as described by Böhme et al. 2006). Cost: one extra texture read per pixel, trivial.
- *Compound eyes sampled truly.* CompoundRay renders a compound eye by tracing, for each ommatidium, rays spread as a Gaussian around its axis with SD = acceptance angle/2.355, from an eye file of ommatidial positions, axis directions and acceptance angles (`data/eyes/eye-specification.txt`, `shaders.cu`). It needs a 3D scene and an NVidia ray-tracing card. The phone analogue samples the camera picture at each ommatidial axis direction with that Gaussian, then reconstructs smoothly between axes. Where Δρ/Δφ is below 2 the eye is undersampled (light-adapted diurnal insects average 1.07, Land 1997, p. 167) and fine gratings alias; showing that aliasing would be true, and showing hexagons would not (`docs/SOURCES.md`: "An insect does not see hexagons"). Cost: cheap at the lattice resolution (a few thousand to tens of thousands of sites).
- *Fields wider than the camera.* The camera sees about 69 degrees; animals see 200 to 360. Build 22 shrinks the picture and invents the rest. Two truthful options exist. (a) Show only the part of the animal's field the camera sees, at true scale, and draw the edge of the animal's field as a mark (honest, less dramatic). (b) Build a panorama as the person turns: write each frame into an equirectangular environment map at the direction the orientation sensors report, and draw the animal's whole field from that map, older parts fading (my proposal; the fading should be shown, since those parts are memory). Cost of (b): one write into a large texture per frame and one lookup per pixel, moderate; drift of the orientation sensors limits it.
- *Projection.* Any field over about 120 degrees has to be projected onto a flat screen; an equidistant fisheye (radius proportional to angle from the centre) keeps angular sizes readable at the centre. This is a display choice (invented) and the rows should say so.

### 1.9 Depth of field

**The formula.** The angular diameter of a blur circle is b ≈ A·|ΔD| radians, where A is the pupil's diameter in metres and ΔD the difference in dioptres between the focused distance and the object's (b = A·s₀·(1/z₀ − 1/z₁) on the retina) (Banks et al. 2015, eqs 1 and 2, read from the eScholarship copy; the extraction garbled eq. 1, and this form is the fetch tool's reconstruction, which agrees with the small-angle eq. 2). A vertical slit pupil gives "astigmatic depth of field": "the retinal images of horizontal contours are more blurred than the images of verticals", and a horizontal pupil in grazers "increases depth of field for horizontal contours" and "reduces the amount of overhead sunlight" (same; 214 terrestrial species classified). Build 22's `pupil` and `dof` follow this qualitatively; with a depth map the blur could be computed from the animal's pupil size, which no compilation in this unit gives (gap).

**The missing input is distance.** Build 22 guesses depth from height in the frame (`docs/SOURCES.md`). Monocular depth networks estimate it from one picture: Depth Anything V2 releases models from 25 million to 1.3 billion parameters (Yang et al. 2024, arXiv 2406.09414, abstract page). On a phone even the smallest would run a few times a second at best (my judgement: heavy), so depth would be computed at a low rate and carried between frames, or computed once for a photo. The native shells could use the phone's own depth sources where a device has them (no source opened).

**Cost and beauty.** The engine's anisotropic disc already draws pupil-shaped blur; driving its radius from b·(pixels per radian) with a real depth map would make the cat's vertical smear and the deer's horizontal smear appear only where the scene is out of focus, which is both truer and more striking than height in the frame.

## 2. What an RGB phone camera can and cannot capture

### 2.1 Spectral range

- **Visible only, in the phones measured.** Burggraaff and colleagues 2019 calibrated five smartphones (iPhone SE, 6S and 7 Plus; Samsung Galaxy S6 and S8) and several other consumer cameras with a monochromator scanned over 390 to 700 nm: "This wavelength range was chosen because no significant response was found outside it on any of the test cameras." Consumer cameras' red, green and blue peaks lie at "approximately 600, 520, and 470 nm, respectively", and their responses "differed wildly between camera models", notably in "the locations of secondary peaks and near-infrared (NIR) and ultraviolet (UV) cut-off wavelengths" (read from the arXiv text through the fetch tool; published in Optics Express, doi 10.1364/OE.27.019075, as the project's repository README gives it).
- **Ultraviolet A reaches the sensor weakly.** In one phone (a Samsung Galaxy 5), "The CMOS image sensor in the camera possesses inherent sensitivity to UVA", the lens's transmission "drops from 18.9% at 390 nm to 1.1% at 340 nm", and in darkness "the green channel still yielded measurable signals" under UV light (Igoe, Parisi and Carter 2012, accepted manuscript). In daylight that UV signal is mixed into the colour channels with visible light thousands of times stronger, so it cannot be separated (my inference, as the crab sheet also says).
- **Far-red and near-infrared.** Silicon responds far past 700 nm, and converted research cameras are sensitive "often up to around 900 nm", with infrared sensitivity "generally far higher than UV" (Troscianko and Stevens 2015). Stock phones block it: the five phones above showed no significant response past 700 nm. So the plant view's red against far-red comparison (red near 660 nm, far-red near 730 nm) cannot be measured; the camera's red channel sees the red, and the far-red has to be invented. The plant row "Phone cameras capture very little far-red light" is consistent with Burggraaff and colleagues and can cite them.
- **No polarization** (1.4) and **no distance** (1.9) in an ordinary frame.

### 2.2 What reaches the page

- **Processed, nonlinear pictures.** The page receives video frames that the phone has demosaiced, white-balanced, tone-mapped and denoised. Burggraaff and colleagues found "high linearity in RAW but not JPEG data"; RAW (DNG) exists on iOS and Android "though not on all devices", and EXIF metadata can be wrong enough to cause "photometric errors up to 30%". Decoding sRGB to linear light, as the engine does by uploading frames as `SRGB8_ALPHA8` textures, undoes the standard sRGB curve but not the phone's own tone mapping (my inference).
- **Auto exposure hides the light level** (1.7). The native shells could read exposure time and gain per frame; a web page cannot (no source opened for either statement).
- **The field of view varies by phone and lens** (1.1). iOS reports it per camera format; on the web it has to be assumed or calibrated.
- **Frame rate.** Most pages get 30 frames per second, sometimes 60 (no source opened); temporal effects above that rate cannot be measured from the camera.

### 2.3 Which receptor catches can be estimated from RGB, and with what error

No published study I opened tests a phone picture against animal receptors directly. micaToolbox's authors state the rule ("a camera with three channels (RGB) can reliably map to trichromatic humans, dichromatic mammals or achromatic dogfish cone catch, but it would not be possible to map to species that see into ultraviolet (UV)"), and Tedore 2023 found that maps from cameras lose accuracy for dark colours. To put numbers on the rule for the app's animals, I ran a computation of my own on open data.

**Method (my own computation, not a published result).** Spectra: the 69 natural reflectance spectra shipped with pavo 2.11 (36 flowers, 21 plumage spectra of *Sicalis citrina*, 12 of teal), 300 to 700 nm at 1 nm. Light: CIE D65 (pavo's `bgandilum`). Camera: an ideal colour-correct phone, represented by the CIE 2-degree colour matching functions in pavo (`cie2_X`, `cie2_Y`, `cie2_Z`), which is what a phone's colour pipeline aims to deliver. Receptors: pavo's built-in sensitivity curves (`vissyst`): honeybee, blue tit, average violet-sensitive bird, peafowl, chicken double cone, dog, housefly and its R1-6 receptors, *Drosophila*, the jumping spider *Habronattus pyrrithrix*, the triggerfish *Rhinecanthus aculeatus* and the ornate dragon lizard *Ctenophorus ornatus*. Catches were made relative to a perfect white under D65 (von Kries). For each receptor I fitted the catch from the three camera values, with no intercept (as Vasas et al. 2024), and with a quadratic polynomial (as micaToolbox can), and scored each by leave-one-out cross-validation: R² and the median absolute percentage error. "Share below 400 nm" is the fraction of the receptor's catch from a white surface in D65 that comes from wavelengths the phone does not see.

**Reading the table.**
- Green, yellow and red receptors and the long-wave double cones that drive brightness and motion (R² 0.99 to 1.00, median errors 0 to 5%) can be computed from the phone picture with confidence.
- Blue receptors (420 to 480 nm) come out with R² 0.96 to 1.00 and median errors of 3 to 17%: good enough for a picture, with the largest errors in dark and saturated colours.
- Violet receptors (VS birds near 416 nm, peafowl 432) come out partly (R² 0.90 to 0.97, errors 16 to 26%). A pigeon, chicken or heron view can use the estimate and say that its violet is approximate.
- True UV receptors (325 to 382 nm) cannot be estimated at all (negative R² means the fit predicts worse than the average). Every UV channel in the app is invented.
- The quadratic map improves blue and violet errors by a few points and nothing else, so the linear map (a 3 by 3 matrix, free on the graphics chip) is the right choice.

**Limits of this estimate.** The library is small (69 spectra) and lacks leaves, bark, soil, skin, fur, water and sky; flowers and plumage dominate it. Tedore 2023 shows that libraries without dark spectra flatter the fit. The camera is ideal; a real phone's colour errors add to these, and Burggraaff and colleagues found models "differed wildly". The illuminant is D65 only. With FReD's 2,000-odd flower spectra and a library of foliage, soil and fur spectra, the same computation would give firmer numbers; the method is the one Vasas and colleagues used for their camera.

**For the rows.** Three findings bear on build 22's wording directly. Dogs and cats have lenses that pass most UVA (61% and 59%), and Douglas and Jeffery argue that "an animal with a lens transmitting UV radiation will inevitably be sensitive" there because every pigment absorbs a little; the dog and cat rows can say that their eyes let in ultraviolet that ours block, graded `g` until a behavioural test is found. Diurnal squirrels block it entirely, so the squirrel view should never show ultraviolet. And the bird group splits by ultraviolet: passerines and parrots have UVS pigments near 355 to 380 nm with ocular media passing half the light at 316 to 343 nm, while pigeons, gamebirds and ducks have VS pigments near 409 to 426 nm and media that pass half only at 358 to 380 nm in the three measured (turkey, peafowl, mallard) (Hart 2001). One "violet tint" for every bird is false for one side or the other.
