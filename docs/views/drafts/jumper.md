### Where the views should split

As far as anyone has looked, the whole family shares one design: two principal eyes, each with a narrow, movable retina in four tiers (L24, L45, L143, L205), and six secondary eyes that between them watch nearly the whole circle and answer to movement (L41, L62, L87). Four places were examined for a split.

1. **Colour in the principal eyes. Split taken, at genus *Habronattus*.** *Habronattus* lays a red filter over some green-sensitive receptors at the centre of the retina, which makes a third colour class there (L104 to L107); the same filter occurs in four more species across the genus (L108), and the spiders learn and use red independently of brightness (L126, L155). *Phidippus* and *Salticus* have no such filter (L109). The other genera measured have two classes, ultraviolet and green (L21 to L23, L143, L163 to L165, L198), apart from a disputed report of four in *Menemerus confusus* (L80 to L82, L32). A picture with red in it is false for 97.5% of world records, and a picture without red is false for *Habronattus*, which holds 2.5% of world records and 0.3% of Alachua's.
2. **The spider-eating spartaeines (*Portia*, *Cyrba* and relatives). Not split.** Their posterior median eyes still work (L136), their secondary retinae differ (L206), and *Cyrba* sees in dim light (L152, L235). None of these genera is among the 18 above or in the Alachua table, so a second view would serve a fraction of a per cent of records. *Portia* is listed for a profile of its own in section 4.
3. ***Lyssomanes*, 21.9% of Alachua records. Not split.** The genus has vestigial posterior median eyes and a fovea of high-acuity receptors (L203, L204). The Magnolia Green Jumping Spider recognizes rivals by face and legs (L207), judges their size (L208), chooses perches by brightness (L239), and males of its subfamily follow the scent of females (L253). No measured difference in acuity, field or colour separates it from *Phidippus*, so the evidence does not show one picture to be false for it. The gap is in section 5.
4. ***Maratus*, the peacock spiders, 2.8% of world records. Not split.** A fourth colour class (blue, 480 to 500 nm) is claimed from a conference abstract cited in a review (L191). That is too weak to split on; the gap is in section 5.

**The set of views.** One view, `jumper`, for all Salticidae, with one genus setting: in *Habronattus* the centre of the gaze strip takes a three-class colour matrix and the colour row changes (section 2). In `tools/species/eyes.py` this is the family rule `Salticidae` to `jumper` already proposed in `docs/views/sheets/spider.md`, with the genus passed into the view; if the engine prefers separate entries, a genus rule `Habronattus` to `jumper_red`, placed before the family rule, with every field shared except the centre's colour and the colour row.

### The account put to this research

The research was asked to test, against the literature, a summary of jumping spider vision: the front two eyes see nearly all, or all, of the spectrum people see, plus ultraviolet; the side eyes lack colour and act like motion detectors that complicate and inform the picture; nobody knows exactly how the eyes' information comes together; the summary never described a split view; and an ordinary scene for a jumping spider is something like Times Square, full of signals calling for attention.

**A fork in the first claim.** "Seeing the spectrum" can mean sensing light across it or telling its colours apart. The two lead to different pictures, so the table keeps them apart: sensing decides whether red light shows at all, and discrimination decides whether it shows as a colour of its own.

**The most boring answer that could be true.** The busy street may describe a person's day better than a spider's. A jumping spider on a wall sees mostly still things, and still things draw nothing from the side eyes (L56, L62); with nothing moving, its retinas make slow spontaneous sweeps or drift back to rest (L53, L55). On that reading its world is quiet with interruptions. The measurements fit both readings, because what decides between them is how often something small moves near a spider in the wild, and no source opened here counts that. The view lets the scene decide: a still room glows little and a street glows a lot.

### The picture

**The geometry first.** A phone camera covers about 60 to 70 degrees, which lies inside the front side eyes' field of about 100 to 120 degrees (L5, L100, L146) and within the reach of the principal eyes' sweeps, which run up to 45 to 50 degrees (L53). Everything the camera sees is therefore front-of-spider territory: the principal eyes' strip and the front side eyes. The scene itself stays still while the sharp strip moves over it, as the spider's brain neurons with body-fixed receptive fields suggest (L269). The rear side eyes' field, from about 60 degrees round to the back (L6, L40), has no camera image at all. The view draws it as the engine's invented surround, coarse and dim, and the first row says so.

### New effects the evidence calls for

1. **The sweeping blade (gaze strip).** What the person sees: a thin upright blade of sharp, coloured picture inside a soft green world. It leaps to whatever moves, sweeps across it once every second or two, and slowly turns like the hand of a clock, lying along edges as it turns. The evidence is L49 to L55, L228 and L229. Input: the camera. Difficulty: moderate (one strip zone and the gaze controller, both in the engine track). For beauty: let what the blade has crossed stay vivid for a second before it sinks back into green, a wake that shows where the spider has looked. The wake is invented; it rests on the reading that scanning gathers detail and colour over time (L86; the authors' suggestion in L107).
2. **Glow for movement, with the phone's own movement taken away.** What the person sees: anything that moves gives off a soft light; turning the phone makes nothing glow. A light that blinks in place stays dark. Tiny movements glow even though the surround is drawn coarse, because the side eyes detect displacements finer than their receptor spacing (L157). The figures are in the salience table. Inputs: the camera, with the gyroscope (`DeviceMotionEvent`, permission on a tap) or the mean image motion standing in for the spider's knowledge of its own turns. Difficulty: moderate.
3. **Living motion.** What the person sees: a walking ant, a cat's paw or a person's stride shimmers more brightly than a branch in the wind. The cue is local motion that is coherent over a small patch and rhythmic (the engine track's definition), standing in for the front side eyes' preference (L1, L145 to L147). Difficulty: moderate to hard.
4. **Flinch from looming.** What the person sees: a hand or a face approaching the lens makes the picture recoil, a quick step backward in zoom, as *Phidippus audax* backs away from an expanding shape (L113). Shrinking things do nothing (L196). Input: the camera (expansion of the motion field). Difficulty: moderate. The recoil distance is invented.
5. **One thing at a time.** What the person sees: in a busy street many things glow, and the blade visits them one by one, never returning to a place it has just left for about five seconds, and leaving a thing that keeps repeating the same movement. Evidence: L7, L67, L190, L195. Difficulty: easy once the gaze exists.
6. **The second image (depth).** What the person sees: a faint, blurred copy of the blade's image, whose blur spreads wider as things come nearer. Under strongly red light the copy blurs more, as the spider's distance sense was fooled by red (L144), which the row reports alongside an attempted replication that did not get the same result (L230). Inputs: a depth estimate from the camera, which the engine track names as possible at a cost; height in the frame or a tap as the fallback. Difficulty: hard for true depth, easy for the fallback.
7. **Night twitching.** What the person sees: when the phone has lain still in the dark for a minute, the blade dims and twitches in small rapid jumps for about 77 seconds, then rests for about 17 minutes. Evidence: L168. Inputs: device motion for stillness, the camera's brightness for darkness. Difficulty: easy. The minute before the first bout is the app's choice, so that someone can see it.
8. **Dim light.** What the person sees: as the light falls, the blade loses its colour and detail first, and in near darkness it stops jumping. *Cyrba algerina*, a dim-light specialist, still identified prey at 1.35 cd/m², fewer did at 0.54 and none at 0.24 (L152); juvenile *Phidippus audax* eyes are 12 to 23 times less sensitive than adults' (L119); the ogre-faced spider *Dinopis* absorbs about 2,000 times more light per receptor than *Phidippus* (L267). Input: the camera's mean brightness and exposure, which give only a rough light level. Difficulty: easy. The mapping from phone brightness to candelas per square metre is invented.
9. **Freeze to a hum (optional).** What the person sees: a low hum stops the blade's sweeps for a second or two, as a Bold Jumping Spider freezes at 80 Hz (L247; tones of 50 to 400 Hz were tested, L248). Input: the microphone, with consent asked in plain words first. Difficulty: moderate. If built, the `x` row becomes an `m` row about hearing.
10. **Taps through the table (optional).** What the person sees: with the phone lying on a table, a tap on the wood makes the picture start and the blade jump. Jumping spiders signal and learn through vibration of the ground (L249 to L252). Input: the accelerometer. Difficulty: easy. The startle itself is invented.

## 5. Gaps and how to fill them

- **Flicker fusion.** No jumping spider measurement was opened. The only figure, about 40 Hz, is cited second-hand (L11). The nearest measured spiders are a diurnal crab spider, 10 Hz in the principal eyes and 20 to 30 Hz in the secondary eyes (L172, L173), and a nocturnal wandering spider, 4.3 to 8.6 Hz (L210). Inference: the jumping spider's side eyes start turns 80 to 120 ms after movement begins (L64) and its saccades finish within 0.1 s (L49), which fits a system faster than the wandering spider; a figure between 20 and 40 Hz for the secondary eyes is this sheet's guess from these. The view keeps the phone's pace. The one temporal figure for jumping spider receptors is an estimate of 24 ms for their response time, calculated from scanning speed on the assumption that scanning stays just inside the blur limit (L263). A usable figure cannot be drawn from Tan et al. 2026: its thin stripes passed a point at up to about 150 cycles per second, by this sheet's arithmetic (9.8 cm/s over a 0.66 mm stripe pair), on a 60 Hz projector, so the moving pattern was sampled below its own frequency. A flicker electroretinogram on any common species would settle it (Clark and Uetz 1990 is wanted).
- **Colour classes in the common Alachua genera.** *Anasaitis*, *Lyssomanes*, *Platycryptus*, *Colonus*, *Hentzia* and *Eris* are unmeasured, as are *Menemerus bivittatus*'s principal eyes. Nearest: *Phidippus regius*, *Plexippus validus*, *Hasarius adansoni* and *Saitis barbipes*, all two classes, with *Habronattus* the one measured exception (L109 found no filter in *Phidippus* or *Salticus*). Inference: two classes unless a red filter is found, graded `g` for these genera.
- **Colour in the secondary eyes.** Opsins suggest a possible blue class in some species (L150); no physiology. The full text of Steck et al. 2024 is wanted.
- **Acuity of the common genera.** Measured in *Phidippus johnsoni*, *Portia fimbriata*, *Metaphidippus*, *Evarcha* and a few others (L35, L71, L129, L130). Inference for the rest: use *Phidippus*. Juvenile *Phidippus audax* keep the adult number of receptors in the front side eyes and pay in sensitivity (L119, L120), which suggests that small species keep acuity and lose sensitivity; no rule across species was opened. Blest 1985 (wanted) compares genera.
- **Depth.** Brain neurons with receptive fields of constant physical size across distances, driven by one eye, show that depth reaches the brain without binocular disparity (L271). The defocus result as the source rests on one species and one laboratory (L144), and one attempted replication of one of its experiments did not get the same result (L230). The thesis chapter with the details could not be read past page 32. Until it is, the depth row keeps its `m` grade and states the attempted replication.
- **How the eyes' information comes together.** Largely unknown (L95). The brain anatomy (L8, L9), behaviour (L19, L229, L234) and neurons that combine eye position with the image (L269) show interaction; the brain recordings of Menda et al. 2014 are wanted.
- **The posterior median eyes.** Vestigial in most species (L69, L166, L204, L214), working in spartaeines (L136); a review adds coarse detection overhead (L121). The view leaves them out.
- **Polarization.** Every rear side eye cell recorded in *Plexippus validus* was sensitive to polarized light (L161), and an arboreal species steers home by sky cues (L225); no behavioural use of polarization was opened. Nothing is drawn.
- ***Lyssomanes* optics.** Its retina has a high-acuity fovea and pooled periphery (L203); its acuity, fields and colour are unmeasured. Inference: the family picture, graded `g`.
- **Night and dim light.** One dim-light specialist (L152, L235, L236) and juvenile *P. audax* sensitivity (L119). The dim-light effect borrows these.
- **The Twin-flagged Jumping Spider, *Anasaitis canosa*,** the county's most photographed jumping spider, has no vision study among the sources opened; only its courtship is described (L209).

**Unfinished.** Two books could not be read in full: Barth 1985 stops inside chapter IV, and nothing in Foelix 2025 could be read (section 7). The rows have not been read by anyone who works on these spiders. The new effects are proposals, untested on a phone.

## 8. Questions for a specialist

1. In the genera most photographed in Florida (*Anasaitis*, *Lyssomanes*, *Platycryptus*, *Colonus*, *Hentzia* and *Menemerus bivittatus*), do the principal eyes have only ultraviolet and green classes, or has any shown a filter or a third class?
2. Has anyone measured flicker fusion, or the response time of the photoreceptors, in the principal or secondary eyes of any jumping spider?
3. At any instant, how wide is the region the principal eyes resolve sharply in *Phidippus*, and does the rotation during scanning lay the strip along edges?
4. Has the red-light depth result of Nagata et al. 2012 been repeated in other species or laboratories, and what did the University of Canterbury attempt find?
5. Do the opsins found in some secondary eyes (a possible blue class in *Menemerus bivittatus*) give those eyes colour, or only brightness?
6. Is anything known about whether colour seen by the principal eyes is carried over to the wider scene the secondary eyes see, or does it stay with the narrow field?
