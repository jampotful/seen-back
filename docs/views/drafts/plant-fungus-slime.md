## 1. Scope

**Plants.** The `plant` view covers 64,187,111 world records, 37.8% of the 169,720,759 iNaturalist research-grade records in GBIF (`docs/VIEWS.md`, read 11 October 2026). Summed from `docs/views/world-orders.csv` by class: flowering plants 59,697,190 (Magnoliopsida 50,870,345 and Liliopsida 8,826,845; 93.0%), ferns and horsetails (Polypodiopsida) 2,182,380, conifers 1,275,105, mosses 515,888, liverworts 176,367, lycophytes 196,462, red algae 48,300 (Florideophyceae 47,402 and two small classes), green algae of the Chlorophyta 45,080 (Ulvophyceae, the sea lettuces and their kin, 34,398) and charophyte algae 14,558. The commonest families worldwide (GBIF facet read today, `https://api.gbif.org/v1/occurrence/search?datasetKey=50c9509d-22c7-4a22-a47d-8c48425ef4a7&kingdomKey=6&facet=familyKey&limit=0`) are the daisies, Asteraceae (7,547,411), the legumes, Fabaceae (4,046,858), the roses, Rosaceae (2,448,442), the mints, Lamiaceae (2,101,003), and the grasses, Poaceae (1,691,380); family keys were resolved one by one at `https://api.gbif.org/v1/species/<key>`.

**Alachua County.** The same facet with `gadmGid=USA.10.1_1` (checked: the records it returns are in Alachua County, Florida) gives 78,728 plant records, led by Asteraceae (9,488), Fabaceae (5,628), Lamiaceae (3,967) and Bromeliaceae (3,437). The county has essentially no seaweeds; it is inland.

**Seaweeds and other algae** moving into `plant` (`docs/COVERAGE.md`): 168,308 records, of which brown algae (Phaeophyceae) hold 158,556 (Fucales, the rockweeds, 77,182; Laminariales, the kelps, 64,433), diatoms 7,046 and the yellow-green alga *Vaucheria* and its kin (Xanthophyceae) 1,577.

**Fungi.** 4,768,141 world records (2.81%; `docs/views/sheets/fungus.md` and its audit). From `docs/views/world-orders.csv`: Agaricomycetes, the mushrooms and brackets, 3,275,167 (68.7%); lichens about 791,052 (16.6%, the earlier sheet's sum); rusts (Pucciniales) 99,519 (2.1%); the stinkhorns (Phallales) 83,754. The moulds in which most light measurements were made are almost never photographed: Mucorales, which holds the pin mould *Phycomyces*, 3,227 records, and Sordariales, which holds the bread mould *Neurospora*, 1,264. In Alachua, GBIF gives 5,657 fungus records today; the county's species list (`docs/views/no-view-us-fl-alachua.csv`) holds 599 fungus species, led by the Christmas lichen *Herpothallon rubrocinctum* (414 records), the Ringless Honey Mushroom *Desarmillaria caespitosa* (281) and the Tropical White Polypore *Favolus tenuiculus* (167). Nothing I found was measured in those three.

**Slime moulds.** 113,223 world records: Physarales 49,284 (43.5%), Ceratiomyxales 27,114 (23.9%), Cribrariales 17,276, Trichiales 14,170, Stemonitidales 5,255. GBIF files them under Protozoa, apart from the fungi. In Alachua: 15 species and 184 records, led by Common Coral Slime *Ceratiomyxa fruticulosa* (72) and Dog Vomit Slime Mold *Fuligo septica* (66). Slime moulds are met at two stages, the crawling plasmodium and the fruiting bodies it turns into; the light measurements are of the plasmodium of *Physarum polycephalum*.

**What the four groups share.** None forms an image. Land and Nilsson (2012, ch. 1) sort light-guided behaviour into four classes: non-directional monitoring of light (class 1), directional light sensing used for steering, which "is generally not considered as true vision" (class 2), low-resolution spatial vision at 5 to 30 degrees (class 3), and high-resolution vision (class 4). Every response logged here is of class 1 or 2: amount, colour and the difference between two sides of a stem or a cell. The engine's field mode should therefore show no spatial pattern finer than one direction (section 2.1, effect P1).

### Where the views should split

1. **Seaweeds apart from land plants.** Split by lineage: red algae (Rhodophyta), the Chlorophyta (sea lettuce and the other green seaweeds) and the Ochrophyta (brown seaweeds, diatoms, *Vaucheria*) go to a new view `seaweed`; land plants and the charophyte algae stay in `plant`. Evidence: no phytochrome genes were found in red algae or in the chlorophytes (Li and colleagues 2015, from 28 red-algal transcriptomes and 5 genomes, and every chlorophyte transcriptome examined; the microscopic prasinophytes, also in Chlorophyta, do have them), so the plant view's red and far-red rows would be false for the seaweeds of those groups. Brown algae have a phytochrome of their own kind, closest to fungal phytochromes (Li and colleagues 2015), and a blue-light receptor found in their branch and not in green plants (aureochrome; Takahashi and colleagues 2007). Under water, far-red from the sun survives only at the surface (Fortunato and colleagues 2016) and the light becomes blue and green (Duchêne and colleagues 2026). The charophytes have the land-plant phytochrome (Li and colleagues 2015) and stay with `plant`. Shares, after the move: seaweed and alga about 261,000 records (brown 158,556, red 48,300, chlorophyte 45,080, the rest of the Ochrophyta about 9,750), 0.4% of the merged plant group; land plants and charophytes 64.09 million, 99.6% (the merged group is 64,355,419).
2. **No split within land plants.** Ferns (3.4% of plant records) and some mosses steer by red light as well as blue, through a hybrid receptor, neochrome (Suetsugu and colleagues 2005; Kanegae and Kimura 2015); the Direction row says so, and the picture's gradient uses all colours, so it is true for them. Touch-fast plants (the sensitive plant *Mimosa pudica*, the Venus flytrap) and the sun-tracking sunflower differ in what they do with touch and light, and they are given species profiles (section 4) to be applied when the full database names the species.
3. **One fungus view.** The commonest members, mushrooms and brackets (68.7%), read blue and green light and gravity. Lichens (16.6%) are carried by rows: the light they use feeds their algae, and the orange pigment of *Xanthoria* screens blue. Rusts (2.1%) read red and far-red as plants do (Schneider and Murray 1979) and feel the ridges of a leaf (Allen and colleagues 1991); at 2.1% they get a species profile, and a rust-specific setting could follow if the database names rusts often.
4. **One slime mould view, `slime`**, drawn from *Physarum polycephalum*. Slime moulds differ in their use of light (*Didymium bahiense* ignored light in Latty and Beekman 2015; *Physarum roseum* barely responded in Masui and colleagues 2025), and the second-largest order, Ceratiomyxales (23.9%), has no measurements I could find. The rows say so.

**Commitment: four views.** `plant` (revised), `seaweed` (new), `fungus` (new, building on `docs/views/sheets/fungus.md`) and `slime` (new).

**New effects**

- **P1. One direction in place of a grid (for all four views).** Today `field: 1` samples mip level `maxLod - 2` (`src/cam.b.js`, line 75), which for a 1280 by 720 frame is 5 by 2 cells (1280 and 720 divided by 256, rounded down), each about 14 degrees wide in the 70-degree camera view that `docs/SOURCES.md` assumes. That is low-resolution spatial vision, Land and Nilsson's class 3 (5 to 30 degrees per sample), and no plant, fungus or slime mould reads a pattern. What the evidence supports is a mean colour and one gradient, the difference between two sides (Nawkar and colleagues 2023; Matton, Oskam and Pierik 2026). The person would see a soft wash of colour, lighter toward the brighter side, swinging as they turn. Input: the camera. Difficulty: easy; sample the top mip for the mean and a 2 by 2 level for the left-right and top-bottom differences, then draw a linear ramp in the shader. To make it beautiful, ease the ramp's angle and contrast slowly, like light moving across a wall.
- **P2. Neighbour glow (far-red estimate).** From the camera image, take a greenness index (for example the share of pixels where green exceeds red and blue) as the fraction of the view filled with leaves, and map it to an estimated red to far-red ratio between 1.2 (no leaves) and 0.1 (a wall of leaves), after Huber and colleagues 2020. Show low ratios as a shift of the whole field toward deep red-violet. The person would see a hedge flood the screen with red-violet and an open sky return it to gold. Input: the camera. Difficulty: easy. The mapping is **invented**; it rests on leaf optics (Land and Nilsson 2012; Huber and colleagues 2020; Ballaré and colleagues 1987) and on the camera's blindness above 700 nm (Burggraaff and colleagues 2019).
- **P3. Lean.** The field's ramp drifts toward the brightest direction over about 10 seconds and holds there (phototropism; Nawkar and colleagues 2023). Easy.
- **P4. Clock.** The phone's clock and time zone give the hour. Within about an hour of dusk the neighbour glow of P2 is strengthened (shade is most effective at dusk; Martínez-Vasallo and colleagues 2024), and after dark the view folds shut from the edges, as many legumes fold their leaves at nightfall (Mano and Hasebe 2021); it opens again in the morning. Input: the clock, which needs no permission. Difficulty: easy with fixed hours, moderate with a sunset estimate. The strength of the dusk boost (1.5 times) and the fold are **invented**.
- **P5. Statoliths.** The tilt sensor gives the direction of gravity. A thin layer of fine grains settles toward the true low edge of the screen and flows like a liquid at the slightest tilt, as statoliths do ("as a liquid clinometer would do", Bérut and colleagues 2018); the field's ramp turns back toward true upright at a speed proportional to the sine of the tilt (Chauvet and colleagues 2016), reaching upright in tens of seconds where a shoot takes hours (Chen, Rosen and Masson 1999). Input: the motion sensors (on iOS, a permission prompt). Difficulty: moderate (a small particle overlay and a rotation). The speeds are **invented**. Amyloplasts do settle toward gravity (Nakamura, Nishimura and Morita 2019) and respond to the weakest angle (Bérut and colleagues 2018); I found no opened figure for how fast they settle, so the grains' speed is a guess. Beauty: the phone becomes a clinometer, which is what a statocyte is.
- **P6. Touch.** A tap sends a slow ring out from the finger and dims the field a little; the ring fades over about 25 seconds (touch genes peak at 22 to 25 minutes, Darwish and colleagues 2022, scaled about 60 times; **invented**). Repeated taps make the dimming longer, after the shortening of plants touched day after day. Input: the touch screen. Easy. Species settings in section 4 make *Mimosa* fold at once and the Venus flytrap count to two.

**Deeper evidence, by topic**

- **Phytochromes.** Two forms: Pr absorbs red, peak about 667 nm, and Pfr absorbs far-red, peak about 730 nm (Zhang and colleagues 2023; Kusuma and Bugbee 2021 give 668). Recombinant phyA: 665 nm, shifting to 725 nm after red light (Zhang and colleagues 2023). A minute of red light starts lettuce seeds germinating and a following minute of far-red cancels it (Borthwick and colleagues 1952, in Huq, Lin and Quail 2024). Phytochrome B leads shade avoidance and phytochrome A tempers it in deep shade (Fraser and colleagues 2016; Martínez-García and colleagues 2014, read in the collection). Warmth returns phytochrome to its inactive form faster, so it also serves as a thermometer (Huber and colleagues 2020). Chlorophyll inside a leaf distorts what phytochrome receives (Kusuma and Bugbee 2021).
- **Cryptochromes.** Flavin (FAD) receptors for UV-A and blue (Huq, Lin and Quail 2024). Action spectrum: maximal and fairly flat from 390 to 480 nm, responsive to 530 nm (Ahmad and colleagues 2002). Green light reverses them, so they read the ratio of blue to green, which halves under grass (Sellaro and colleagues 2010). Dim green light alone can set the clock through them (Battle and Jones 2019).
- **Phototropins and the direction of light.** Flavin (FMN) receptors whose absorption matches the action spectrum of phototropism (Christie and colleagues 1999). Phototropic bending extends to 540-550 nm and stops beyond 560 (Steinitz, Ren and Poff 1985); red light given hours before raises it through phytochrome, with action maxima at 669 and 378 nm (Janoudi and Poff 1992). Seedlings bend toward unilateral blue light of 0.025 µmol m-2 s-1; one-minute pulses from 0.0017 to 1.7 µmol m-2 s-1 give a bell-shaped curve; between two lights they choose the stronger; the time to full bending lengthens with brightness (Nawkar and colleagues 2023). Air channels between cells steepen the light gradient (same paper). Chloroplasts move out of the path of strong light (Matton, Oskam and Pierik 2026). Sunflowers track the sun by day and face east again by night, keep doing so for days in constant conditions, and keep tracking when red and far-red or blue are filtered out (Brooks, Atamian and Harmer 2023).
- **Red to far-red in shade.** Sunlight about 1.2, deep canopy down to 0.1; reflected far-red lowers the ratio before shading (Huber and colleagues 2020; Ballaré and colleagues 1987, 1990). Sunflecks, seconds to minutes long, are read by phytochrome B, both cryptochromes and UVR8 (Gautrat and colleagues 2025); one working definition puts a sunfleck under 8 minutes (Sellaro and colleagues 2025). Under a forest canopy far-red makes up to 32% of the light a lichen can use, against 11% in the open (Solhaug and colleagues 2026).
- **Ultraviolet-B.** UVR8 has no added pigment; two tryptophans absorb UV-B (280-315 nm), and the receptor splits from a pair into single units at once (Chen, Dong and Huang 2022; Rizzini and colleagues 2011). Its action spectrum peaks near 280 nm in vitro and reaches into UV-A as far as 350 nm (Cunningham and colleagues 2024). It signals open sunlight, holds back shade avoidance (Hayes and colleagues 2014), and leads to sunscreen flavonols (Tilbrook and colleagues 2013). Seedlings can even bend toward UV-B (Matton, Oskam and Pierik 2026).
- **Circadian timing.** Rhythms of 24 hours in growth and leaf movement continue in constant conditions (Brooks and colleagues 2023), in constant light and darkness (Millar 2003). Phytochromes A and B and cryptochrome 1 carry light to the clock (Somers, Devlin and Kay 1998). The clock gates shade, most effective at dusk (Martínez-Vasallo and colleagues 2024), and drives the nightly folding of legume leaves through clock-controlled channel genes (Mano and Hasebe 2021).
- **Touch.** Touch genes peak 22 to 25 minutes after brushing; repeated touch stunts plants (Darwish and colleagues 2022). The sensitive plant folds its leaflets within seconds, typically in under one second at the hinge, once the hinge bends past 23 degrees (Tran and colleagues 2021; Mano and Hasebe 2021). The Venus flytrap closes after two touches within 15 to 20 seconds, counts further touches, and adds up calcium with each one (Böhm and colleagues 2016; Mano and Hasebe 2021).
- **Gravity.** Starch grains settle in gravity-sensing cells (Nakamura, Nishimura and Morita 2019). Shoots respond to the angle of tilt as the sine of that angle, the same from 0.1 g to 3 g (Chauvet and colleagues 2016). Flattened corn stands up within hours (Chen, Rosen and Masson 1999). Seed-plant roots bend faster than fern and lycophyte roots (Zhang and colleagues 2019).
- **Airborne signals between plants.** Leaves answer volatiles from a neighbour being eaten with a calcium rise within 30 seconds, starting in the guard cells (Aratani and colleagues 2023). Across 48 studies, plants with damaged neighbours became more resistant (Karban, Yang and Edwards 2014). Dodder seedlings grow toward the smell of tomato (Runyon, Mescher and De Moraes 2006). Ethylene builds up in canopy air under low red to far-red (Huber and colleagues 2020).
- **Vibration and sound.** Chewing vibrations raise defences, and plants tell them from wind and insect song (Appel and Cocroft 2014). Beach evening primrose flowers make sweeter nectar within 3 minutes of bee sound or 50-1000 Hz tones (Veits and colleagues 2019).

## 4. Species with their own measurements

- **Sunflower, *Helianthus annuus*.** Tracks the sun by day and turns east by night before flowering; continues for days in constant light; tracking survives filters removing red and far-red or blue (Brooks, Atamian and Harmer 2023, three lines). A profile could pin the ramp of P1 to the compass and swing it east at night.
- **Sensitive plant, *Mimosa pudica*.** Folds within seconds of a touch, under one second at the hinge, once bent past 23 degrees (Tran and colleagues 2021; Mano and Hasebe 2021). A profile: a tap folds the picture shut at once; the time to reopen is **invented** (no opened source).
- **Venus flytrap, *Dionaea muscipula*.** Two touches within 15 to 20 seconds close the trap in a fraction of a second; digestion genes need more than three; calcium adds up with each touch (Böhm and colleagues 2016, two lines; Mano and Hasebe 2021). A profile: the first tap glows, a second within 20 seconds snaps the view shut.
- **Beach evening primrose, *Oenothera drummondii*.** Sweeter nectar within 3 minutes of bee sound (Veits and colleagues 2019). A profile could listen through the microphone for sound at 50-1000 Hz and warm the view when a bee-like hum is heard.
- **Dodder, *Cuscuta pentagona*.** Grows toward tomato volatiles (Runyon and colleagues 2006). No phone input stands in.
- **Boquila trifoliolata.** Copies its host's leaves, and in one study a plastic plant's; vision is proposed and untested (White and Yamashita 2022).
- **Maidenhair fern, *Adiantum capillus-veneris*.** Neochrome (phy3) lets it bend toward red light; far-red given after blue partly undoes bending (Suetsugu and colleagues 2005; Kanegae and Kimura 2015). A fern setting could weight red as heavily as blue in effect P1's gradient.
- **Rockweed, *Fucus*.** Aureochrome (Takahashi and colleagues 2007, in *Fucus distichus*); the embryo's axis set by light (Bogaert and colleagues 2015).
- **Split gill, *Schizophyllum commune*** (76 Alachua records). Stalk bends to 420 and 540 nm, not 600 or 660; gills face down (Schwalb and Shanler 1974, two lines). The fungus view is built on it.
- **Field mushroom, *Agaricus campestris*.** Gills readjust to vertical after a tilt of up to 30 (Moore 1994).
- **Oyster mushroom, *Pleurotus ostreatus*.** Electrical spikes of 62 s and 0.48 mV (Adamatzky and Gandia 2022); the king oyster *P. eryngii* cannot finish its cap in darkness (Ye and colleagues 2021).
- **Orange lichen, *Xanthoria parietina*.** Parietin screens blue and UV-B and increases with exposure (Gauslaa and Ustvedt 2003).
- **Wheat stem rust, *Puccinia graminis*, and bean rust, *Uromyces appendiculatus*.** Red and far-red control of spore germination (Schneider and Murray 1979); ridges of a narrow range of heights from about 0.4 µm trigger infection structures (Allen and colleagues 1991). A rust profile could restore red to the matrix.
- **Many-headed slime mould, *Physarum polycephalum*.** Every slime row.
- **Dog vomit slime mould, *Fuligo septica*** (66 Alachua records). Cannot fruit without light (Gray 1938, through Sobels and van der Brugge 1950). The only commonly photographed slime mould with a light measurement I found.

## 5. Gaps and how to fill them

- **Far-red, which the camera cannot record.** No phone measures it (Burggraaff and colleagues 2019). The nearest evidence is leaf optics: leaves absorb red and reflect and transmit far-red (Huber and colleagues 2020; Land and Nilsson 2012). Effect P2 infers the ratio from how much of the view is green leaf; a specialist could give the relation between visible leaf cover and red to far-red (question 1).
- **Colour weights.** Every `M` matrix is invented. Plants: the cryptochrome and phototropism action spectra (Ahmad and colleagues 2002; Steinitz and colleagues 1985) could give measured weights for the camera's three channels if the full curves were read (wanted). Fungi: four wavelengths in the split gill; the full spectra are from moulds (*Neurospora*, De Fabo and colleagues 1976, maxima 450, 481, 370 and 280 nm; *Phycomyces*, maxima near 450, 370 and 280 nm). Slime: four peaks (Hato and colleagues 1976; Nakagaki and colleagues 1996); the full 1996 spectrum is wanted.
- **Pace.** All four views compress minutes and hours into seconds. The compression factor (about 100) is invented; the measured latencies are logged (30 s to hours in plants, 5-15 min in *Phycomyces*, a 1-2 min pulse in *Physarum*).
- **Mushrooms photographed most.** Nothing measured in the honey mushrooms, the polypores, the boletes or the Christmas lichen. The nearest measured relatives are the split gill and *Coprinopsis* (Agaricales) for light and gravity; ground mushrooms are said to ignore light and follow gravity, wood and dung mushrooms to follow both (Moore 1994).
- **Lichens.** Whether the fungus in a lichen reads light itself is unknown to me; the measured responses belong to the algal partner (Avalos and Vicente 1985) or to its photosynthesis (Solhaug and colleagues 2026). The Water row rests on Lange, Green and Heber 2001; the earlier sheet's primary source (Lange, Kilian and Ziegler 1986) could not be re-opened (Springer refused) and is on the wanted list.
- **Seaweeds.** What brown-algal phytochrome reads, and whether kelps and red seaweeds bend toward light, is not in what I opened (wanted: Rockwell and colleagues 2014; Dring 1984; Lüning 1981; Lüning and Dring 1975).
- **Slime moulds other than Physarum polycephalum.** Nothing for *Ceratiomyxa*, 23.9% of records. The rows use *P. polycephalum* with grade `m` for it and say plainly that others differ.
- **Gravity in slime moulds.** Wanted (Block and Briegleb 1989); no tilt effect proposed until then.
- **How fast statoliths settle.** I found no opened figure; they flow at any angle (Bérut and colleagues 2018), and P5's grain speed is a guess.
- **Unfinished.** No row draws on the touch-sensitive tendrils of climbing plants; their papers are in the collection (for example Engelberth 2003, abstract not available) and could add a species profile. The GBIF counts for Alachua fungi by class were read as unnamed keys and are not given.

## 8. Questions for a specialist

1. How well does the share of a camera view filled with green leaves predict the red to far-red ratio a plant there receives? Is there a published relation, for example from canopy photographs?
2. For the camera's three channels, what relative weights would a plant physiologist give blue, green and red to stand for phototropin, cryptochrome and phytochrome activity together?
3. Do kelps, rockweeds or red seaweeds bend toward light as adults, and what do the brown seaweeds' phytochromes do under water?
4. Does the fungus partner of a lichen sense light through its own receptors, and does parietin change what it receives?
5. Has the light response of *Ceratiomyxa* or *Fuligo septica* plasmodia been measured, and do they flee blue light as *Physarum polycephalum* does?
6. How long do amyloplasts take to settle after a shoot is tipped, and is it fast enough that the statolith layer of effect P5 could run at a real speed?
