- **Licence on the exact copy (PDF).** "Copyright © 2022 Huang, Liao, Chou, Lin and Huang. This is an open-access article" + "distributed under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY)." Publisher's own copy. Met.
- **Quotes.** The PDF's Table 1 rows were returned as "Naja atra T D Ac W/o 98 24.43 ± 4.609 4.30 ± 0.530" and likewise for the other eight, identical to each record's quote (Oreocryptophis porphyraceus wraps across two lines in the PDF; the joined row matches). Family headers in the PDF match each record's family except line 8 (below). Met for all nine.
- **Methods and value scope.** "We examined the specimens of 33 snake species deposited in the National Museum of Natural Sciences (NMNS) in Taiwan."; "The sample size of each species ranged from 3 to 207 individuals, for a total of 1,176 individuals."; "We measured the diameter of the left eye and head width of the specimens using a vernier scale" + "(accurate to 0.1 mm; electronic vernier calipers; code number 500–138;"; "We defined the eye diameter as the maximal diameter of the visible spectacle as the horizontal width" + "of the inner edge of the eye contact from the nearest scale of the eye". Preservation, wild or captive origin, age class and sex are not stated anywhere read (no limitations passage in the Discussion). Each value is the printed species mean, the SD and N are in `conditions`, and the record says fixation is unstated. The value says no more than the row. Met.
- **Paper's purpose and omitted results.** Abstract: "terrestrial and/or diurnal snakes tended to exhibit the larger relative eye size"; "no evidence of how foraging strategies and the presence of pits affected snake eye size was found". Results: "there was a significant negative allometric growth of eye diameter vs. head width in snakes" (median slope 0.469), steepest in Colubridae and weakest in Elapidae. These are cross-species findings on relative (head-width-corrected) eye size; none qualifies a raw per-species mean diameter, and no record claims an activity or habitat effect. The head width that sets the allometric context is in every quote. No SVL or sex data exist in the paper. Uncounted.
- **Tu (2004) attribution.** Legend: "The information of habitat types, diel activity patterns, and foraging strategies were based on Tu (2004)." Every record says "Habitat, activity and pit codes were taken from Tu (2004)". The paper attributes foraging, not facial pit, to Tu. The codes are context for a morphometric value and the misattribution changes no interpretation of ED. Uncounted; optional fix below.
- **Trait key.** `eye_diameter_mm` holds 305 rows on main, including external eye diameters on preserved squamate specimens (e.g. Gekko fengshanensis, "maximum eye diameter (ED) with digital calipers ... on the ri[ght]"). `eye_axial_length_mm` is the anterior-posterior eyeball length, which a spectacle width is not. Key right; the record's "Not claimed: ED is of the visible spectacle, not the eyeball" is accurate.
- **Ptyas korros 7.13 mm.** The full text confirms it is the largest mean ED in Table 1, and congeners in the same table are close: P. dhumnades 6.53 mm, P. mucosa 6.18 mm ("Ptyas mucosa T D Ac W/o 5 18.86 ± 3.591 6.18 ± 0.783"). Large eyes are consistent across the genus; nothing indicates a typo. The paper prints no comment on Ptyas anywhere in the text, so nothing was omitted. Genuine.
- **Names (Reptile Database, opened).** Naja atra CANTOR, 1842 (Elapidae); Deinagkistrodon acutus (GÜNTHER, 1888) (Viperidae, Crotalinae); Daboia siamensis (SMITH, 1917) (Viperidae, Viperinae); Oligodon ornatus VAN DENBURGH, 1909 (Colubridae, Colubrinae); Myrrophis chinensis (GRAY, 1842) (Homalopsidae); Ptyas korros (SCHLEGEL, 1837) (Colubridae, Colubrinae); Oreocryptophis porphyraceus (CANTOR, 1839) (Colubridae, Colubrinae; Taiwan population O. p. kawakamii, still a subspecies); Trimerodytes annularis (HALLOWELL, 1856), Higher Taxa "Colubridae (Natricinae), Colubroidea, ..."; Bungarus multicinctus BLYTH, 1861 (Elapidae; no subspecies recognised).
