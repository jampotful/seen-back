# Ocular Manifestations of Infectious Salmon Anaemia Virus Infection in Atlantic Salmon ( Salmo salar )

## Abstract
ABSTRACTEyes are essential sensory organs needed by teleost Atlantic salmon for high visual acuity and survival in both the wild and in aquaculture settings. In this work, we assessed the ocular manifestations of Infectious Salmon Anaemia Virus (ISAV) infection in Atlantic salmon by a cohabitation‐mediated infection assay and histological and immunohistochemical approaches. The findings reveal that Atlantic salmon with a systemic ISAV infection displayed ISAV antigen accumulation in specific ocular tissues and significant ocular morphological changes that could compromise visual acuity. Immunohistochemical analyses showed that ISAV‐related ocular pathological changes correlate with changes in expression levels and/or spatial localizations of IgM, Sox6, Sox9, collagen type 1, Gata1, and CD10 in specific compartments of the eye. The cornea is likely one of the first ocular tissues to be exposed to the cohabitation‐mediated ISAV infection. The ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon showed corneal dysplasia, which correlated with increased corneal stromal ISAV antigen expression, dysregulated IgM, Sox6, and collagen type 1 expression, and an induction of Sox9 expression at the corneal surface. The choroid rete mirabile of ISAV‐infected eyes showed a decreased complement of erythrocytes, consistent with anaemia, while Gata1 expression, a key mediator of erythropoiesis, was upregulated compared to non‐infected eyes, suggesting a potential erythropoietic compensatory response. These findings provide a basis for further study of ISAV‐mediated ocular pathological changes and new insight into the pathogenesis of orthomyxoviruses in the eye.

## Introduction
Infectious salmon anaemia virus (ISAV) is a frequent pathogenic virus that causes a fatal disease called Infectious Salmon Anaemia (ISA), characterized by acute anaemia and internal organ haemorrhage in Atlantic salmon [ Salmo salar ] (Dannevig et al. 1995). In salmon farms, ISA can cause high mortality rates which can lead to high social‐economic impacts (Dannevig et al. 2008). This virus belongs to the family Orthomyxoviridae and the genus Isavirus, sharing many structural, morphological, and physiochemical properties with other viruses in this family (Falk et al. 1995). The ISAV particles are 100–120 nm, containing an external envelope and granules (Dannevig et al. 1995). The genome consists of eight segments of single‐stranded RNA with 8 segments and a tentative negative polarity (Mjaaland et al. 1997).
Two phenotypes of ISA infections exist and are detectable using molecular approaches (Rounsville Jr et al. 2024). Virulent variants are generated by inducing deletions in the highly polymorphic region (HPR) of coding segment 6, and specific modifications in other coding segments such as coding segment 5 can also contribute to the pathogenicity (Cárdenas et al. 2019). HPR0 is a non‐virulent strain causing infection without disease where the main target is epithelial cells in gills and skin while the virulent HPR‐deleted ISAV strain causes a systemic endothelial infection (Aamelfot et al. 2016). Additionally, the levels of virulence in the HPR‐deleted ISAV can vary. Low‐virulence ISAV replicates and is transcribed more rapidly in the gills while the high‐virulence ISAV induces a higher viral load and mortality (McBeath et al. 2014). Evidence suggests that the HPR0 variant can evolve into low‐virulence strains, underscoring its significance as a risk factor in the emergence of ISA (Christiansen et al. 2017, 2011; Madhun et al. 2019).
ISAV has various clinical signs including severe anaemia, leukopenia, congestion of the spleen, foregut, and liver and it also affects both erythrocytes and leukocytes (Falk et al. 1998, 1995). It has been suggested that gills may be an important portal of entry and exit, with the virus entering cells via clathrin‐mediated endocytosis and macropinocytosis (Rimstad et al. 1999; Cárdenas et al. 2024). Further studies have shown that mucosal surfaces such as the pectoral fins and skin also function as entry points for the virus (Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015). The ability of ISAV to invade the salmon's immune system and progress is heavily dependent on temperature (Groves et al. 2023). Salmon were found to have a higher viral load and mortality at lower temperatures (10°C) compared to higher temperatures (20°C) (Groves et al. 2023). This is potentially due to a greater response by the T helper 1 cells and antigen presenting cells at 20°C, which may play a role in viral clearance, as well as the virus's difficulty to replicate at 20°C (Groves et al. 2023). Moreover, ISAV‐HPR0 was found to be at the highest prevalence during the winter (Christiansen et al. 2011). Parasite infestation, such as sea lice, was also found to significantly increase mortality and death rates associated with ISAV in Atlantic salmon (Zhong et al. 2023). Although ISAV has been detected in other salmon species, these species act as carriers of the virus without developing clinical disease (Snow et al. 2001).
While other orthomyxoviruses have been known to infect the eyes, to our knowledge there is limited literature on whether ISAV infects the eyes (Belser et al. 2018; Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015). For instance, the influenza virus H1N1, a member of the Orthomyxoviridae family, can infect and replicate within human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (Yang et al. 2024). H1N1 causes cell death, cell rounding and detachment, leading to ocular manifestations including retinal lesions, vasculitis, retinitis, and conjunctivitis (Yang et al. 2024). While ISAV has been detected in the eye, it was only found following the infection of other organs, suggesting the eyes were infected through the cardiovascular system as part of a systemic infection rather than acting as a point of entry (Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015).
Eyes are essential for visual acuity and survival in both wild and farmed finfish, emphasizing the importance of our understanding of Atlantic salmon ocular health. In the present study, we assessed Atlantic salmon moribund from a systemic ISAV infection as a first step toward determining its influence on the eye. We believe that our findings will allow a better understanding of ISAV pathobiology in the eyes, while also increasing the knowledge of ocular pathogenesis of orthomyxoviruses in general. We intended this study to serve as a basis for further exploration of the ocular manifestations of ISAV disease.

## Materials and Methods

## Fish Cohabitation‐Mediated ISAV Infection
To assess the ocular pathological changes associated with ISAV infection of Atlantic salmon, a cohabitation‐mediated infection paradigm was used. Viral preparations of infectious salmon anaemia virus were obtained from the Department of Fisheries and Oceans (Moncton, NB), originally provided by the Research and Productivity Council (RPC, Fredericton, NB). The high‐virulence ISAV isolate (ISAV‐HPR4; RPC/NB 04‐085‐1) was suspended in L‐15 culture medium following isolation from Atlantic salmon head kidney tissue, as described by Ritchie et al. (2009). Viral titre (TCID50 = 1 × 105/mL) and virulence were confirmed in vitro using an Atlantic salmon kidney (ASK) cell line monolayer and the Spearman–Kärber method.
For challenge, viral stocks were thawed on ice and aliquoted into 1 mL syringes. Atlantic salmon maintained in a quarantine module were lightly anaesthetised with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS‐222; Syndel, Canada; 100 mg/L) and intraperitoneally injected with 0.1 mL of the ISAV‐HPR4 suspension. Following recovery in aerated auxiliary tanks, Atlantic salmon were identified via PIT tags and transferred to their designated tanks. Donor Atlantic salmon remained in quarantine for 6 days post‐injection before being introduced to experimental cohabitation tanks at a 1:5 donor‐to‐cohabitant ratio. Fish were monitored several times daily for morbidity and mortality.
Figure 1 shows total mortality per tank observed in our study. Overall mortality reached 83.5%, which was slightly higher than expected. The cohabitation challenge began on May 19, 2024, and the first mortality in cohabitant fish was recorded on June 7, corresponding to 20 days post‐infection. Consistent with this progression, clinical and pathological signs were observed within the first 30 days, including pale gills, darkened skin, exophthalmia, haemorrhages at the base of fins and skin, ascites, as well as congested and enlarged liver and splenomegaly. Fish selected for sampling were in a moribund state. Our analyses often include the detection of ISAV in additional tissues, such as head kidney and spleen. In this study, viral load in these tissues was quantified following the protocol described by Snow et al. (2006). However, these data fall outside the scope of the present study and will be reported separately in a dedicated manuscript.

At ~30 days post‐infection, fish showing clinical signs of ISAV infection were euthanized with a lethal dose of MS‐222 (250 mg/L) followed by cervical separation. Fish were necropsied and examined for internal and external signs of disease. Both eyes were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, and processed for histological analysis. Both eyes from three non‐infected Atlantic salmon from the same cohort served as controls. All husbandry and experimental procedures followed the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care and were approved by the University of Prince Edward Island and Atlantic Veterinary College Animal Care Committee (Protocol #21‐029).

## Sequence Analysis
Sequence comparison of human (AAK26115.1) and Atlantic salmon (XP 045545203.1) Sox6 protein revealed a high degree of conservation between the two species. The BLAST alignment for human and Atlantic salmon Sox6 resulted in 74% identity and 84% similarity at the amino acid level. The mouse monoclonal anti‐Sox6 Ab we used for IHC in our studies was raised against amino acids 506–600 of the human Sox6, a region in which several stretches of sequence of 4–9 consecutive amino acids are 100% identical between the two species (Figure 2). Comparison of human (CAA86598) and Atlantic salmon (ACN10975.1) Sox9 protein also revealed high conservation; BLAST alignment search resulted in 73% identical and 80% similar amino acids (Figure 3A). The mouse monoclonal anti‐Sox9 Ab we used for IHC in our studies was raised against amino acids near the amino terminus of the human Sox9, in which several stretches of sequence of 4–17 consecutive amino acids are identical between the two species (Figure 3A). Sequence comparison of human (AAH09797.1) and Atlantic salmon (NP_001133239.1) Gata1 protein revealed a significant percentage of conservation of this protein between the two species (47% identical and 56% similar amino acids). Of the full length human Gata1 protein, Finger 2 domain sequences contributing to nuclear localization, DNA binding and protein stability (human amino acids 181–289 in Figure 3B) share the highest level of sequence homology with the Atlantic salmon Gata1 ortholog and this is consistent with the high evolutionary conservation known for this region (Yang and Evans 1992). The sequences against which the anti‐Gata1 mouse monoclonal Ab we used here are proprietary; but, outside of the Finger 2 domain, the level of sequence homology between several other regions of human and Atlantic salmon Gata1 are also sufficiently high for potential cross‐reactivity (Figure 3B). Sequence comparison of rat collagen type 1 protein (AAI33729.1) with that of Atlantic salmon (XP 014059932.1) revealed many regions of 4–26 consecutive amino acids which are identical between the two species. The BLAST alignment for rat ( Rattus norvegicus ) and Atlantic salmon collagen type 1 resulted in 77% identical and 83% similar amino acids (Figure 4). The two mouse monoclonal anti‐collagen type 1 Ab we used for IHC in these studies were both raised against full length rat collagen type 1.

## Histology, Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence
Both histological and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses of ocular tissues were used to characterize the impact of the infection.
Paraffin blocks of Atlantic salmon eye tissues were sectioned onto positively charged slides (Superfrost Plus [VWR]) and processed for IHC as previously described (Gendron et al. 2020, 2022) or for standard Giemsa, Prussian blue, Herovici, RGB‐trichrome and Verhoeff/elastin histological stains. Tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in decreasing ethanol steps. Following deparaffinization, sections were processed for antigen retrieval for all IHC using a 10 mM citrate buffer pH 6 with 0.05% Tween 20, at 97°C for 40 min. Antigen retrieval was performed consistently and accurately using 125 μL volume Gene Frame manifolds (Thermo Fisher Scientific Canada) on a PCR instrument (Eppendorf Master Cycler gradient) fitted with a 4‐slide adapter (Eppendorf in situ Adapter). Slides were blocked for 1 h in 2% ECL Advance Blocking Reagent (Fisher Scientific) in Tris buffered saline pH 7.6 (TBS) at room temperature. Antibodies were applied in a vehicle of 2% ECL Advance Blocking reagent in TBS (overnight for primary Ab and 2 h for secondary Ab) at room temperature. Alkaline phosphatase (AP) colour reactions were developed using a Vector Red AP Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories).
Expression of markers including ISAV, Sox6, Sox9, CD10, Gata1, and collagen type 1 was explored using specific Ab directed against the mammalian counterpart of these proteins. The selection of a panel of marker Ab reagents to analyse these markers was based on both the literature and previous work in our group. Previous work has shown an induction of an anti‐ISAV IgM response upon vaccination against ISAV (Caruffo et al. 2016; Tobar et al. 2015). Our previous work also suggests that IgM positive cells mitigate damage to marine teleost eye structures (Gendron et al. 2020). We previously showed that Sox9 positive cells are involved in an intraocular response to temperature stress in spotted wolffish [ Anarhichas minor ] (Kwabiah et al. 2024). Since ISAV infection causes anaemia, we targeted two key proteins, Sox6 and Gata1, known to orchestrate vertebrate erythropoiesis (Gutiérrez et al. 2008; Chennupati et al. 2018). Sox6 also harbours other multifaceted functions in tissue development and thus could be impacted by ISAV pathogenesis (Hagiwara 2011). We previously showed that CD10 positive cells are involved in an intraocular response to infection in lumpfish [ Cyclopterus lumpus ] (Gendron et al. 2020). Finally, regulation of expression of corneal collagen type 1 has been implicated in congenital eye disease (Li et al. 2025).
Anti‐ISAV antigen IHC was performed using anti‐ISAV P10 IgG2b monoclonal Ab (Aquatic Diagnostics Ltd.) at a dilution of 1:10 of the original P10 stock which was prepared by adding 1 mL of phosphate buffered saline containing 0.01% thimerosal to the dried P10 Ab. The ISAV antigen IHCs were performed using goat anti‐mouse‐IgG2b‐AP‐conjugated secondary Ab (Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories) and was applied at a dilution of 1:250. Following incubation and washing, AP substrate solution (Vector Red) was applied to the slides and red reaction colour was monitored on an inverted microscope. Anti‐salmon IgM IHC was performed using a chicken anti‐salmon IgM (Ghasemieshkaftaki et al. 2024), IgY isotype (Somru BioScience), at a dilution of 1:500. A goat anti‐IgY(H + L)‐AP conjugated secondary Ab (Southern Biotech) was used at a dilution of 1:250. Anti‐Sox6 IHC was performed using a mouse monoclonal anti‐Sox6 primary Ab (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.) at a dilution of 1:50. A goat anti‐mouse‐IgG2b‐AP conjugated secondary Ab was used at a dilution of 1:250. Anti‐Sox9 IHC was performed using a rabbit monoclonal primary Ab D8G8H (Cell Signaling Technology), at a dilution of 1:50. A goat anti‐rabbit IgG‐AP conjugated secondary Ab (Promega, Thermo Fisher Scientific Canada) was used at a dilution of 1:250. Anti‐Gata1 IHC was performed using a mouse monoclonal anti‐Gata1 primary Ab (Proteintech Inc.) at a dilution of 1:50. A goat anti‐mouse‐IgG1‐AP‐conjugated secondary Ab (Jackson Immunoresearch Laboratories) was used at a dilution of 1:250. Anti‐collagen type 1 IHC was performed using a mouse anti‐rat‐collagen 1 monoclonal primary Ab cocktail of Ab 8‐3A5 and 7‐2C12, both raised against rat collagen 1, from Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, both at a dilution of 1:10. A goat anti‐mouse IgG1‐AP‐conjugated secondary Ab was used at a dilution of 1:250. Anti‐CD10 IHC was performed using rabbit monoclonal anti‐mammalian CD10 (Abcam SP67) at 1:25 dilution as previously described for lumpfish [ Cyclopterus lumpus ] (Gendron et al. 2020). An anti‐rabbit IgG‐AP‐conjugated secondary Ab was used at a dilution of 1:250. Information for all Ab used herein is summarized in Table 1.

## Western Blotting
To generate control Atlantic salmon protein lysates, head kidney and/or ocular tissue were harvested post‐mortem from 3 normal healthy adult Atlantic salmon being euthanized for other research purposes (Memorial University Institutional Animal Care Committee protocols 17‐03‐RG; 18‐01‐JS; 18‐02‐JS). Protein lysates of human lung epithelial cell line A549 (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA) were prepared and were used as a positive control tissue. Tissue samples and cells were homogenized in Triton X‐100 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% [w/v] sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% [w/v] SDS, 1% [v/v] Triton X‐100 and 10% [v/v] glycerol) supplemented with 1 mM dithiothreitol, 10 μg/mL leupeptin, 0.3 U/mL aprotinin, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 50 mM sodium fluoride and 25 mM β‐glycerophosphate. Whole cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation and protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as a standard. Protein extracts were analysed for Sox6, Gata1 and actin by western blot as described previously (Ho et al. 2015; Gendron et al. 2022; Kwabiah et al. 2024) using the same primary Ab used for IHC followed by an anti‐mouse IgG‐horse radish peroxidase conjugated secondary Ab (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin). Western blot membranes were reprobed using an anti‐actin monoclonal Ab (C4, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA) which has previously been used for detecting lumpfish actin (Gendron et al. 2022), to ensure protein integrity and loading control. Western blot signal was revealed by chemiluminescence using LumiGLO substrate kit (KPL, Gaithersburg, MD) and images were captured using a Fuji LAS 4000 digital imaging system.

## Results
Western blotting for Sox6 and Gata1 demonstrated specific discrete bands in Atlantic salmon tissues, the observed molecular weights of which were similar to molecular weights for these proteins in the human A549 positive control (Zhou et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2017) cell line (Figure 5). Sox6 western blots revealed a main band of 112 kDa in salmon choroid rete mirabile tissues. However, this main 112 kDa band was not detected in salmon head kidney tissues. Gata1 western blots revealed a main band of 44 kDa in salmon tissues co‐migrating with Gata1 expressed in A549 cells. Additional anti‐Gata1 reactive bands of 36 and 38 kDa were observed in salmon choroid rete mirabile and head kidney, respectively. The sequence analysis results for Sox6, Sox9, collagen type 1, and Gata1, taken together with our western blotting results for Sox6 and Gata1, support cross reactivity between mammal and Atlantic salmon for the Ab we used for the IHC studies herein.

Immunohistochemistry for Sox6 revealed the presence of Sox6 in the central corneal epithelium of the ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon (Figure 6D) while no staining was detected in the non‐infected fish (Figure 6C). Sox6 staining was observed primarily in the basal region of the central corneal epithelium in the ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon, particularly near Bowman's membrane (Bm). In contrast to Sox6, Sox9 staining was observed in the outer (more anterior) region of the central corneal epithelium in ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon (Figure 6F, arrows), while no staining was detected in the non‐infected fish (Figure 6E).
The central corneal stroma of both ISAV‐infected (Figure 6H) and non‐infected Atlantic salmon (Figure 6G) revealed CD10 staining in a pattern that was similar to the IgM central corneal stromal staining. This staining pattern was consistent with the distribution of keratocytes in the cornea (Jester et al. 1994). Low levels of basal epithelial central corneal CD10 staining were observed in some samples (Figure 6H).
Giemsa (Figure 6I,J) and Herovici (Figure 6K,L) histological stains were used to reveal the changes in structure, organization, and thickness of the central corneal epithelium and stroma associated with ISAV infection. Both Giemsa and Herovici stainings confirmed a change in the stratification of the cells within the central corneal epithelium of the ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon, indicating disorganization and the loss of the defined layers (Figure 6J,L) relative to the non‐infected fish (Figure 6I,K).
The cornea limbic region of ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon showed upregulation of both ISAV antigen immunoreactivity and collagen type 1 in cellular elements of the limbic corneal stroma relative to non‐infected fish (Figure 7B versus Figure 7A and Figure 7D versus Figure 7C). A panel of various histological stains (Giemsa, Prussian blue, Herovici, RGB‐trichrome and Verhoeff elastin staining) indicated cellular infiltration of the limbic corneal stroma, possible extracellular haemoglobin deposition, changes in collagen distribution patterns and limbic corneal dysplasia of ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon relative to the non‐infected animals (Figure 7A,C,E,G,I,K,M vs. Figure 7B,D,F,H,J,L,N). Most of the results described herein were consistent in all animals investigated. While some animals served as better examples, either due to having more evident pathological changes or the tissues were more intact for imaging, all pathological changes that we discussed in the manuscript were present in all the ISAV infected specimens and absent or observed at a much lesser extent in the non‐infected specimens. While both eyes from the same individual were examined, the extent of variation of these findings within animals was more difficult to determine since, for example, fellow eyes were sometimes damaged during the retrieval process.

## Discussion
In this work, we assessed the ocular manifestations of ISAV infection in Atlantic salmon using both histological characterization and marker expression analyses. The findings revealed that moribund salmon with a systemic ISAV infection show specific morphological changes and changes in protein markers as determined by IHC that provide insight into the molecular pathways that might be involved in the ocular pathogenesis. These findings provide a basis for expanding our understanding of ISAV infection ocular pathobiology and contribute to the general knowledge of the ocular pathogenesis of orthomyxoviruses.
Some of the Ab reagents used in these studies to characterize the expression pattern of specific markers were raised against either ISAV‐specific epitopes (Falk et al. 1998) or Atlantic salmon IgM specific, where epitope reactivity is established by complete sequence identity. Other Ab reagents we used herein, raised against mammalian epitopes, such as the anti‐Sox9 and anti‐CD10 Ab, have been previously validated by us as cross‐reactive in North Atlantic marine teleost tissues (Kwabiah et al. 2024; Gendron et al. 2020). The transcription factor Gata1 plays an essential role in steady‐state and stress‐induced erythropoiesis, and several studies have characterized Gata1 orthologs in teleosts (Tian et al. 2021; Belele et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2015; Gallo and Civinini 2003). Although there is no extensive literature on collagen type 1 in teleost tissues, the level of sequence homology we found between mammalian and Atlantic salmon proteins is sufficiently high for expecting cross reactivity. Furthermore, the deduced polypeptide of collagen type 1 of the teleost Chu's croaker ( Nibea coibor ) contains the same signal peptides, C‐propeptide and N‐propeptide domains, and triple helix domains, all of which are characteristics of type I collagen in vertebrates, and further supports the cross reactivity of the anti‐mammalian collagen type 1 Ab reagent we used herein with teleost (Rong et al. 2022). The anti‐Sox6 and anti‐Gata1 Ab we used recognized proteins within Atlantic salmon tissues with molecular weights that are similar to those appearing in a control human cell line. Additional lower molecular weight anti‐Gata1 reactive bands were also detected in the fish tissues. Shorter isoforms of Gata1 protein have been reported and studied in human tissue (Aluri et al. 2025). Species variations in splicing, as we have presented and discussed previously in other marine teleosts (Gendron et al. 2020, 2022; Kwabiah et al. 2024), could produce isoforms of different sizes. However, further work will be required to explore what these shorter anti‐Gata1 reactive bands are in Atlantic salmon tissue. Overall, our sequence and western blotting analyses, taken together with our previously published work and additional information in the literature, strongly supports the existence of Atlantic salmon orthologs of the proteins we assessed herein.
Our results indicating an increase in expression level and/or a redistribution of ISAV, IgM, Sox6, Sox9, and collagen type 1 in central corneal tissues of ISAV‐infected salmon suggest an IgM‐related immune response to ISAV in central corneal tissues accompanied by Sox family protein and extracellular matrix (ECM) regulation. Sox9 and Sox6 play critical roles in ECM‐rich tissues such as cartilage (Lefebvre 2010), but their roles in response to pathogens in marine teleost cornea, which is also an ECM‐rich tissue, have not been reported. In ISAV‐infected salmon, we observed that the outermost layer of central corneal epithelial cells, which are the cells that would be the first in line to receive a waterborne cohabitation‐delivered ISAV insult, upregulates Sox9, while more basal central corneal epithelial cells upregulate Sox6. Our present studies were not performed at the resolution levels necessary to determine if Ig‐related responses in these animals are restricted to B cells, extracellular fluids, or Ig‐binding macrophages. Results shown here in Figure 6B might suggest that the localization of IgM in corneal tissues from ISAV‐infected fish changes noticeably, but further work will be needed to determine where IgM expression is located exactly. We recently described the use of an anti‐CD14 antibody in studies of ocular pathology in lumpfish infected with the Vibrio anguillarum marine bacterium. As infection proceeds in this model, CD14‐like expression was found to be increased in head kidney and the ocular choroid rete mirabile vasculature (Kwabiah et al. 2025). Since CD14 is a well‐established macrophage marker, future multiplex studies applying anti‐CD14 reagents to the Atlantic salmon ISAV ocular infection model should shed light on these questions.
Previous studies have indicated significant differences in the biology of the central versus limbic corneal regions. In mammals, central versus peripheral corneal regions show differences in ECM, biomechanical and immunochemistry properties (Jan et al. 2018; Hamrah et al. 2003). Mammalian corneal epithelium is renewed by limbal corneal epithelial stem/progenitor cells (LEPC) which are located at the corner‐scleral junction (Mikhailova et al. 2016) at which collagen fibre orientation differs significantly from central cornea (Girard et al. 2011). Human peripheral corneal cell division has been observed to be up‐regulated when the tissue is exposed to differentiation media with collagen substrates (Yoon et al. 2013). Therefore, the significant differences in the biology of central versus limbal corneal regions might impact host susceptibility and response to ISAV. Several of our histological stains did indeed highlight cellular changes (either infiltration or proliferation) and changes in collagen distribution patterns specific to the stroma of the corneal limbic region of ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon compared to non‐infected fish. On the other hand, corneal epithelial dysplasia found associated with ISAV infection was observed in both the limbic and central regions. A previously published study associated increased nuclear localization of Sox9 with the activation of human LEPC in response to corneal epithelial wound healing (Menzel‐Severing et al. 2018). Interestingly, the Menzel‐Severing et al. (2018) study also showed predominantly cytoplasmic Sox9 expression in basally located LEPCs but showed nuclear expression in the supra‐basal cells (Menzel‐Severing et al. 2018). Our histochemical analyses using special stains indicating cellular changes and dysplasia of the corneal epithelium and stroma in ISAV‐infected salmon further supports a corneal pathological response to ISAV infection. Similar to low pathogenic influenza, epithelial cells are the main target of HPR0, the non‐virulent ISAV strain (Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015; Aamelfot et al. 2016), which does not cause systemic endothelial infection or appreciable immune responses. Whether HPR‐del, the virulent strain of ISAV, definitively targets corneal epithelial cells in vivo has not been previously reported. Our results herein suggest that Atlantic salmon corneal epithelium is indeed a target for pathogenic ISAV.
We also observed an upregulation in expression of nuclear Sox9 within cells near the outer edge of the ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon eyelid skin. The eyelid skin tissue in ISAV‐infected specimens also showed morphological disorganization of the tissue. Therefore, eyelid skin could also potentially serve as a point of entry for ISAV or high‐virulence ISAV.
Using various histological stains and marker analyses, we observed vascular reorganizational changes that correlated closely with ISAV antigen immunoreactivity and increase in Gata1 expression in choroid rete mirabile of ISAV‐infected salmon eyes. The recently introduced RGB‐trichrome staining method developed by Gaytan et al. (2020) incorporated fast green and was designed for the histological assessment of skeletal tissues but is also useful for labeling erythrocytes bright green (Gaytan et al. 2020; Xuereb and Beesley 1954). Our observations in choroid rete mirabile tissue of a robust presence of bright green stained erythrocytes of non‐infected fish versus a paucity of bright green‐stained erythrocytes in ISAV‐infected fish suggests an ocular manifestation of the systemic anaemia in the ISAV‐infected animals. The increased density of small red/orange stained leukocytic‐like cells across the whole section of choroid rete mirabile in RGB‐stained sections from the ISAV‐infected specimens suggests that additional cellular changes might be occurring in this tissue. Mammalian Gata1 is important for homeostatic and stress‐induced erythropoiesis (Gutiérrez et al. 2008; Chennupati et al. 2018) and Gata1 orthologs have been characterized in teleosts (Tian et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2015; Gallo and Civinini 2003). In light of ISAV‐caused anaemia, which is a stress to erythropoiesis, we hypothesized that Gata1 might be deregulated in choroid rete mirabile tissues of ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon. The nuclear and cytoplasmic subcellular localizations we observed for Gata1 in the choroid rete mirabile tissues of infected fish is consistent with that previously described in mouse bone marrow derived cells which undergo erythropoiesis (Gutiérrez et al. 2007). The increase we observed in Gata1 immunoreactivity in both vascular wall structures and tightly packed pockets of leukocytic‐like round cells in choroid rete mirabile tissues of ISAV‐infected fish could be the result of a compensatory response for ISAV‐driven anaemia by inducing erythropoiesis. However, as a transcription factor, Gata1 is known as a master regulator in programming haematopoietic progenitors toward both megakaryocyte and erythrocyte development (Ferreira et al. 2005). Ferreira et al. (2005) and Iwasaki et al. (2003) also highlight Gata1's role in development and terminal differentiation of eosinophils and mast cells. Both eosinophils and mast cells are known to participate in the host response to pathogens in mammals (Fulkerson and Rothenberg 2008; Migliaccio et al. 2007). Regardless of phenotype, we do not yet know if these Gata1 positive cells accumulate in the eye after entry from the systemic circulation or if they originate from precursors within the eye. Extramedullary haematopoiesis is rare but can occur in some cases in ocular tissues (Arredondo et al. 2008). In teleosts, it is generally accepted that the barrier between haematopoietic tissue and blood within the circulation is relatively weak, allowing immature erythrocytes into tissues (Witeska 2013). Future studies applying additional phenotyping reagents could address these questions.
Previous studies described ISAV in the eye but following infection of other organs, which was taken to suggest that the eyes were infected through the cardiovascular system rather than acting as an actual point of entry (Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015). Relevant to the vascular route, we observed increased expression of ISAV in the choroid rete mirabile of ISAV‐infected specimens. The choroid rete mirabile receives blood from the pseudobranch which contacts water (Wittenberg and Haedrich 1974). Gills are thought to be a point of entry for ISAV (Rimstad et al. 1999; Cárdenas et al. 2024). If the pseudobranch is an entry point, any incoming retinal ISAV could originate from the choroid rete mirabile either as water or blood‐borne material. Thus, it is possible that the choroid rete mirabile vasculature may be a mode of entry of ISAV into the eye. The other potential route of entry for ISAV is the central cornea or the limbic regions (Gendron et al. 2022). Since both non‐infected and ISAV‐infected fish showed a low level of corneal epithelial ISAV immunoreactivity, it is also possible that high‐virulence ISAV replicates in the corneal limbic stroma, after which it could penetrate the inner ocular tissues such as retina. Our results showing significant molecular changes (ISAV, IgM, Sox6, Sox9, collagen type 1 immunoreactivity) and morphological dysplasia in both central and limbic corneal tissues in the ISAV‐infected samples indicates that the corneal tissues in ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon might lose the microbiological barrier properties that healthy corneal tissues normally serve in most animals (Kinoshita et al. 2001).
The neural retinal dysplasia and degeneration we observed in the ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon is consistent with the loss in integrity of Bruch's membrane barrier in these animals. This level of retinal pathological changes, if observed in human subjects, would likely cause significant vision loss. While the most abundant protein component of mammalian Bruch's membrane is collagen (Clark et al. 2025), elastin also plays an important role in neural retinal barrier and Bruch's membrane integrity. Abnormal elastin turnover/degradation is associated with several ocular diseases (Navneet and Rohrer 2022). The Verhoeff elastin histology results herein indicating a degeneration of Bruch's membrane in ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon suggest that the ISAV infection in these fish can progress to retinal barrier breakdown. This observation is important since retinal barrier breakdown at the level of Bruch's membrane is a hallmark of some human retinal diseases including age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Clark et al. 2025; Biasella et al. 2023).
Surprisingly, we observed CD10 expression in the extraocular muscle myofibrils of both non‐infected and ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon, with general upregulation of CD10 in the infected specimens. CD10 is a marker for haematopoietic progenitor cells in mammalian bone marrow (Wang et al. 2024), plays a role in B cell development (Torlakovic et al. 2005) and chondrogenesis (Ding et al. 2020). CD10 is an endopeptidase expressed both on cell surfaces of fibroblasts and neutrophils, and as a soluble form observed in serum and cerebrospinal fluid (Bayés‐Genís et al. 2016; Komada et al. 1986). The soluble form of CD10 retains its catalytic activity similar to that of the membrane‐bound form (Bayés‐Genís et al. 2015). Extraocular muscles are specialized skeletal muscles responsible for eye movement. While they share characteristics with other skeletal muscle types, they are unique in that they are typically unaffected by muscular dystrophies as well as resistant to fatigue (Titova et al. 2024). Extraocular muscles are divided into two layers: The outer orbital layer that contains thin muscle fibres, and the global layer that contains thicker muscle fibres (Horn and Straka 2021). After injury, satellite cells and fibro‐adipogenic progenitor cells (FAPs) both play key roles in the repair process. Satellite cells are muscle resident stem cells that differentiate into myocytes, and FAPs are mesenchymal stem cells embedded in muscle connective tissue that maintain structural integrity for regenerating muscles (Collins and Kardon 2021). Human FAPs, as well as satellite cells, express CD10 and CD10 have been shown to be upregulated in cases of rhabdomyolysis (Schutz et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2018). Herovici histological stain revealed possible ocular rhabdomyolysis or degeneration of the ocular muscle myofibrils in ISAV‐infected Atlantic salmon. As CD10 is a marker of FAPs and satellite cells, the increase in expression might be related to an attempted muscle regeneration in response to tissue degradation mediated by ISAV infection but further studies will be needed to explore this.
Important concerns about results interpretation in histopathology and comparative pathobiology in aquatic animal health sciences have been raised and recently reiterated (Wolf et al. 2015; Speare et al. 2025). Several aspects of the work herein address these concerns. Tissue fixation, embedding and processing involved a fish pathology diagnostics laboratory (Aquatic Diagnostic Services, AVC‐UPEI) and was standardized and consistent between the control and experimental groups. Care was taken to embed the eyes at the same orientation to avoid any plane‐of‐section artefacts. Histological staining was done by qualified expert technologists at a Histology Core Facility (Histology Core Facility Faculty of Medicine, Memorial University). Microscopic imaging was performed using state‐of‐the‐art high‐resolution instrumentation at optimized light and colour balance levels. While the observations were qualitative, sufficient numbers of tissue blocks, fellow eyes, tissue sections, histological stainings and IHC were conducted to increase the confidence level of the observations and findings. The IHC approach itself served as further validation of the histological changes observed since in many cases the histopathology correlated with distinctive marker protein expression changes. Western blotting was also used to further validate cross reactivity of Ab reagents used in IHC in cases where such literature in Atlantic salmon is not yet available.
This study has several limitations and caveats. First, ISAV‐moribund animals were assessed at only one time point, 30 days post infection, of the co‐habitation infection process. The study of earlier time points might provide better insight into points of entry and the tissue changes underlying subsequent ocular pathology. Second, IHC and histology are only correlative and cannot prove the function of specific proteins in pathogenesis. However, since there is limited literature in marine teleosts for the role of the protein markers we assessed in ISAV disease, we view these studies as a springboard for future exploration. Third, as we have reported previously, marine teleost orthologs of marker proteins can display interesting divergence from their mammalian counterparts (Gendron et al. 2022; Kwabiah et al. 2024, 2025).
Previous studies on ISAV pathological changes have indicated that different tissues such as head kidney, gills, and spleen respond differently to the infection (Gervais et al. 2022). Our results herein reflect this pattern of diversity in response to ISAV pathology in the Atlantic salmon eye. Previous work on ISAV has shown that early replication of ISAV occurs in various mucosal surfaces such as pectoral fin and skin, with multiple potential entry points for the virus other than the gills (Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015). Aamelfot, Dale, et al. (2015) and Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. (2015) reported that initial infection begins in epithelial cells and progresses to endothelial cells. It was suggested that eye tissue is infected through a systemic route rather than primary viral entry (Aamelfot, Dale, et al. 2015; Aamelfot, McBeath, et al. 2015). Our results raise the new possibilities of a choroid rete mirabile and/or corneal entry route for ISAV. Recent studies have implicated aerosol transmission of ISAV (Krishna et al. 2025). Farmed Atlantic salmon can display above‐ocean‐surface swimming behaviour (Gamperl et al. 2021). Therefore, corneal points of entry of ISAV might be relevant to aerosol‐based ISAV infection. Regardless of the entry route, our results also suggest that ISAV ocular pathological changes might include an ocular erythropoietic or leukocytic response to the infection. Whether such a response originates inside or outside of the eye remains to be explored. Previous work has shown that the nonvirulent ISAV‐HPR0 strain acts as a reservoir and is a risk factor for emergence of ISA disease. There is also evidence of genetic and functional evolution from an ISAV‐HPR0 variant to a low‐virulence ISAV variant (Christiansen et al. 2017). These studies, taken together with our work herein, raise the possibility that the Atlantic salmon eye, which is a barrier organ, might serve as an isolated reservoir for the pathogenesis of virulent ISAV. Orthomyxoviruses such as H1N1 have been recognized to infect eye tissues, including human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (Belser et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2024). H1N1 can lead to ocular manifestations including retinal lesions, vasculitis, retinitis, and conjunctivitis (Yang et al. 2024). Avian and human influenza A and H7 viruses affect eyes, use ocular tissue as a portal of entry, and show ocular tropism (Belser et al. 2018). Human/avian influenza virus is capable of replicating in distinct ocular cell types, and conjunctivitis is the primary complication that occurs in the eye with an influenza infection (Belser et al. 2018). Our current findings on the ISAV orthomyxovirus broaden our basic understanding of how these viruses use and damage ocular tissue and suggest that ISAV might serve as a new model for studying orthomyxovirus‐related ocular disease.
