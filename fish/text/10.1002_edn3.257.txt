Abstract: Indonesia is the heart of the Coral Triangle, the world’s most diverse marine

43

ecosystem. Preserving the biological and economic value of this marine biodiversity requires

44

efficient and economical ecosystem monitoring, yet our understanding of marine biodiversity in

45

this region remains limited. This study uses environmental DNA (eDNA) to survey fish

46

communities across a pronounced biodiversity gradient in Indonesia. A total of 12,939,690

47

sequence reads of MiFish 12S rRNA from 39 sites spanning 7 regions of Indonesia revealed

48

4,146 Amplified Sequence Variants (ASVs). Regional patterns of fish diversity based on eDNA

49

broadly conformed to expectations based on traditional biodiversity survey methods, with the

50

highest fish biodiversity in Raja Ampat and generally lower diversity in Western Indonesia.

51

However, eDNA performed relatively poorly compared to visual survey methods in site-by-site

52

comparisons, both in terms of total number of taxa recovered and ability to assign species names

53

to ASVs. This result stands in a stark contrast to eDNA studies of temperate and tropical

54

ecosystems with lower diversity. Analyses show that while sequencing depth was sufficient to

55

capture all fish diversity within individual seawater samples, variation among samples from

56

individual localities was high, and sampling effort was insufficient to capture all fish diversity at

57

a given sampling site. Interestingly, mean ASVs recovered per one-liter seawater was

58

surprisingly similar across sites, despite substantial differences in total diversity, suggesting a

59

limit to total ASVs (~200) per one-liter eDNA sample. Combined, results highlight two major

60

challenges of eDNA in highly diverse ecosystems such as the Coral Triangle. First, reference

61

databases are incomplete and insufficient for effective ASV taxonomic assignment. Second,

62

eDNA sampling design developed from lower diversity temperate marine ecosystems are

63

inadequate to fully capture diversity of biodiversity hotspots like the Coral Triangle.

Introduction: Indonesia is the heart of the Coral Triangle, a region in Southeast Asia that

69

includes the Philippines, Malaysia, Timor L’Este, Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands.

70

Defined by the presence of more than 500 species of scleractinian coral (Veron et al., 2009), the

71

Coral Triangle is the world’s most biologically diverse marine ecosystem (Allen, 2008; Roberts

72

et al., 2002; Veron et al., 2009). This remarkable diversity has made the Coral Triangle the focus

73

of numerous biogeographic studies addressing the origins of this biodiversity hotspot (Ekman,

74

1953; Ladd, 1960; Woodland, 1983), as well as phylogenetic/phylogeographic studies examining

75

speciation processes in this region (e.g., Barber & Bellwood, 2005; Barber et al., 2011; Carpenter

76

et al., 2010; DeBoer et al., 2014; Timm, Figiel, & Kochziuz, 2008).

77

The biological importance of the Coral Triangle is matched only by its cultural and socio-

78

economic importance. More than 370 million people depend on the ecosystem goods and

79

services of the Coral Triangle, 120 million of which directly benefit from coastal and offshore

80

fisheries production, and marine tourism (Coral Triangle Initiative, 2009; Foale et al., 2013). For

81

example, the fishing industry in Indonesia accounts for 21% of Indonesia’s agricultural economy

82

and 3% of the national GDP in 2012 (FAO, 2018a). Across the Coral Triangle marine-based

83

tourism is valued at nearly US$ 10 billion per year (NOAA, 2012).

84

Despite their biological and economic importance, 85% of coral reefs in the Coral

85

Triangle are threatened or extremely threatened (Burke et al., 2012). Major threats include

86

habitat degradation, marine pollution, increasing global demand for marine products, coastal

87

deforestation, and unsustainable fishing (Hoegh-Guldberg et al., 2009). Not only are reefs

88

degrading, but even healthier reef systems like Raja Ampat, Indonesia, larger members of the

fish community, such as grouper and Napoleon wrasse, have greatly reduced population sizes

90

(Allen, 2003) as do reef sharks (Sembiring et al., 2015). These losses don’t just impact

91

biodiversity, they also impact ecosystem function and the human communities that depend on

92

them (Burke et al., 2012). The latter is particularly concerning because nations like Indonesia

93

lack sufficient terrestrial habitat to compensate for lost marine productivity and have insufficient

94

livestock production is to meet local protein demands (Permani et al., 2016). As such, it is

95

critical to preserve the Coral Triangle marine ecosystems and the socio-economic benefits that

96

these ecosystems provide.

97

One important aspect of sustainability in the Coral Triangle is effectively monitoring

98

change over time. However, there are two major challenges to effective monitoring. First,

99

research effort in the Coral Triangle is not proportional to its exceptional biodiversity (Barber et

100

al., 2014; Fisher et al., 2011; Keyse et al., 2014) leading to an incomplete understanding of

101

marine biodiversity in this ecosystem. A second challenge is that the most common biodiversity

102

survey method, particularly for corals and fishes, is visual census. Visual census is expensive,

103

time intensive, potentially biased by observer skill, and can overlook rare or cryptic taxa (Edgar

104

et al., 2004). The latter two are particularly problematic in Indonesia because areas like Raja

105

Ampat have at least 1,704 species of marine fish (Allen, 2002), and few scientists have the

106

taxonomic expertise to conduct such surveys. Furthermore, logistical issues and methodological

107

biases hinder researchers’ ability to do time-series visual surveys, limiting our ability to

108

understand how these ecosystems are changing over time, precluding the collection of

109

monitoring data that is critical to inform marine conservation efforts. As such, it is essential to

110

develop novel methods that are 1) efficient, 2) inexpensive, 3) require no specific taxonomic

111

expertise, and 4) are amenable to large scale spatial and temporal sampling.

Environmental DNA (eDNA) is a rapidly evolving approach to the study of biodiversity

113

(Deiner et al., 2017). eDNA is widely applied to document the presence of endangered species

114

(e.g., crested newt; Biggs et al., 2015; Rees et al., 2014, 2017), invasive species (e.g., the

115

American bullfrog; Dejean et al., 2012; Rees et al., 2014; Takahara et al., 2013) and diversity of

116

freshwater fish communities (Rees et al., 2014; Thomsen et al., 2012b). eDNA also shows great

117

promise for studies of marine biodiversity (Closek et al., 2019; Cordier et al., 2017), recovering

118

commercially valuable species as well as species rarely or never recorded by conventional

119

monitoring methods (Thomsen et al., 2012a). Port et al. (2016) showed that eDNA can

120

differentiate fish communities between adjacent habitats within a coastal kelp forest ecosystem

121

even in the presence of strong water movement(Jeunen et al., 2019), and more recently, Gold et

122

al (2021) showed that eDNA can differentiate fish communities inside and outside of marine

123

protected areas. Importantly, a common theme of marine eDNA studies is that eDNA recovers a

124

greater diversity of taxa than traditional visual surveys (e.g., Gold et al., 2020; Kelly et al., 2014,

125

2017), making it a particularly attractive approach for biodiversity monitoring in regions like the

126

Coral Triangle (Juhel et al., 2020).

127

While eDNA may revolutionize monitoring of marine biodiversity, to date most marine

128

eDNA studies have focused on relatively low diversity temperate marine environments

129

(Bohmann et al., 2014; McElroy et al., 2020; Rees et al., 2017; Thomsen et al., 2012a).

130

Similarly, most coral reef eDNA studies to date have occurred in Japan, Central Pacific, Western

131

Australia, and the Red Sea (Alexander et al. 2020;Bessey et al. 2020; DiBattista et al. 2017;

132

Lafferty et al. 2020; Oka et al. 2020;Stat et al 2017; Stat et al. 2019; West et al. 2020), where fish

133

biodiversity is far lower than the Coral Triangle (Bellwood & Meyer, 2009; Roberts et al., 2002).

134

Most recently, Juhel et al. (2020) used eDNA to examine fish diversity in Raja Ampat,

Indonesia, home to the world’s most diverse reef fish fauna (Allen, 2002), reporting a strong

136

correlation between eDNA results and checklists from visual data. However, species

137

accumulation curves indicated that eDNA greatly under sampled species diversity, suggesting

138

that the methods, sampling design, and bioinformatics approaches employed in temperate eDNA

139

studies may not be equally effective in megadiverse tropical regions like the Coral Triangle.

140

This study employs eDNA to examine the distribution and diversity of marine fishes

141

across the Indonesian archipelago and compare these results to patterns of region diversity

142

resulting from conventional visual census that show a strong biodiversity gradient. Specifically,

143

we test the potential of eDNA to provide a simple, cost-effective method for assessing and

144

monitoring fish communities in a threatened global biodiversity hotspot by asking (1) whether

145

eDNA effectively captures regional patterns of fish biodiversity across the Indonesian

146

Archipelago, and (2) whether eDNA captures more fish diversity than traditional survey

147

methods, including rare and cryptic taxa, as is common in lower biodiversity marine ecosystems.

eDNA Sampling

161

To assess marine fish diversity with eDNA, we employed a hierarchical sampling design across

162

7 regions (Table S1). Each sample consisted of one liter of seawater that was collected on

163

SCUBA at depths between 11-15 m, 1 m off the reef, to minimize variation in community

164

composition associated with depth. Following standard sampling protocols used in temperate

165

ecosystems (Miya et al., 2015), we collected three one-liter water samples at each sampling site

166

to maximize species diversity and to account for fine-scale heterogeneity in local eDNA

167

signatures. To further maximize species diversity, we sampled multiple sites within each region,

168

with sampling sites separated by at least five kilometers to capture spatial variability in habitat

169

and eDNA signatures (Andruszkiewicz et al., 2017; Miya et al., 2015), except for Wakatobi,

170

where sites were 2.5 km distant. For example, on the island of Derawan, we took three one-liter

171

water samples from each of four sites, totaling 12 individual eDNA samples. To compare eDNA

172

to visual census surveys, we selected sites in Raja Ampat where Allen and Erdmann conducted

173

intensive visual fish surveys (pers. comm.).

174

To isolate eDNA from seawater samples, we filtered one liter of seawater through a 0.22-

175

micron Sterivex™ filter (Millipore®, SIGMA MILLIPORE) following the methods of Miya et

176

al. (2015) and Curd et al. (2018) with one key modification: we collected individual water

177

samples in sterile one liter Kangaroo™ Gravity Feeding Bags (similar to intravenous drip bags)

178

that allow for gravity filtration through the Sterivex columns, a method ideally suited to remote

179

field locations. In addition, we filtered one liter of bottled drinking water at each location to

eDNA Extraction, Amplification, and Sequencing

184

We extracted eDNA samples and blanks using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN,

185

Hilden, Germany) following the modified extraction protocol of Spens et al. (2017), adding 720

186

µL of ATL buffer and 80 µL of proteinase K directly into the filter cartridge. Extracted DNA

187

was then stored at -20˚C and transported to UCLA for amplification and library preparation.

188

We amplified extracted eDNA using the Multiplex PCR Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden,

189

Germany), targeting two DNA markers, the MiFish Teleost and MiFish Elasmobranch, 12S

190

rRNA mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) primers (Miya et al., 2015). We conducted each individual

191

eDNA sample via Polymerase Chain Reactions (PCR) in triplicate to account for potential PCR

192

bias (Andruszkiewicz et al., 2017; Miya et al., 2015; Taberlet et al., 2012). Each PCR reaction

193

consisted of 12.5 µL Qiagen 2x Master Mix, 2.5 µL (2 mM) of the primer, 6.5µL nuclease free

194

water, and one µL the DNA extract. Thermocycling parameters utilized a touchdown protocol,

195

beginning with a 15-minute pre-denaturation step at a 95 °C, followed by a touchdown

196

thermocycling profile consisting of 30 seconds denaturing at 94 °C, 30 seconds annealing at 69.5

197

°C, and 30 seconds extension at 72 °C, with the annealing temperature dropping by 1.5 °C per

198

cycle until 50 °C. Following this initial touchdown phase, the main cycle consisted of 25 cycles

199

of 94 °C for 30 seconds for denaturation, 50 °C for 30 seconds for annealing and 72 °C for 45

200

seconds for extension, concluding with a 10-minute final extension at 72 °C. We confirmed

201

successful PCR reactions through electrophoresis of 5µL products for 30 minutes at 150 volts on

2% agarose gels stained with 6x SYBR™ Green (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA)

204

To create the sequencing libraries, we pooled the triplicate PCR products from each

205

individual eDNA sample into a single tube and purified these pooled PCR products using the

206

serapure bead protocol (Faircloth et al. 2014). Next, we quantified the DNA concentration

207

(ng/µL) of each pooled PCR sample using the Qubit Broad Range dsDNA assay (ThermoFisher,

208

Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer protocol, and then then subsequently

209

normalized pooled PCRs to equimolar concentration. We then used the Nextera DNA Library

210

Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to index each pooled sample using a unique

211

combination of Nextera i5 and i7 primers in a second PCR reaction, following the manufacturer

212

protocol (Curd et al., 2018). The indexing PCR reaction consisted of 12.5 µL Kapa High Fidelity

213

Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) 0.625 µL of 1 µM i5 Illumina Nextera indices, 0.625 µL

214

of 1 µM i7 Illumina Nextera indices, and 11.25 µL of PCR product for a total of 10ng of DNA.

215

The indexing PCR thermocycling parameters began with an initial denaturation of 95 ˚C for 5

216

minutes, followed by 8 cycles of: 98 ˚C denaturation for 30 seconds, 56 ˚C annealing for 30

217

seconds, and 72 ˚C extension for 3 minutes, ending with a 72 ˚C extension for five minutes. To

218

ensure the indexing PCR was successful, we electrophoresed indexed PCR products at 120 V for

219

45 minutes on a 2% agarose gel. We then created the final sequencing library by combining the

220

cleaned and quantified indexed PCR products in equal concentration of 10 ng/µl per sample.

221

Libraries were pooled by barcode resulting in two pooled libraries. We also note that the final

222

MiFish Elasmobranch library contained samples collected from surf zones in Southern California

223

which were removed at the end of the decontamination process described below. The final

Bioinformatics and Data Analysis

228

To determine fish community composition, we used the Anacapa Toolkit (version: 1) to conduct

229

quality control, amplicon sequence variant (ASV) parsing, and taxonomic assignment using user-

230

generated custom reference databases (Curd et al., 2018). The Anacapa Toolkit sequence QC and

231

ASV parsing module relies on cutadapt (version: 1.16) (Dobin et al., 2013), FastX-toolkit

232

(version: 0.0.13) (Gordon & Hannon, 2010), and DADA2 (version 1.6) (Callahan et al., 2016) as

233

dependencies, and the Anacapa classifier modules relies on Bowtie2 (version 2.3.5) (Langmead

234

& Salzberg, 2012) and a modified version of BLCA (Gao, et al., 2017) as dependencies. We

235

processed sequences using the default parameters and assigned taxonomy using a CRUX-

236

generated reference database supplemented with additional 12S reference sequences generated

237

by the Smithsonian (GenBank Accessions Table S2). We note that CRUX relies on ecoPCR

238

(version: 1.0.1) (Boyer et al., 2016), blastn (version: 2.6.0) (Altschul et al., 1990; Camacho et al.,

239

2009), and Entrez-qiime (version: 2.0) (Baker, 2016) as dependencies.

240

Raw ASV community table was decontaminated following Kelly et al. (2018) and

241

McKnight et al. (2019) (See Supplemental Methods). However, we did not conduct site

242

occupancy modeling as we were specifically interested in exploring under sampling of total ASV

243

diversity. We then transformed all read counts into an eDNA index for beta-diversity statistics

244

(Kelly et al., 2019). Because of noted issues with incomplete reference databases (Juhel et al.,

245

2020), analyses focused on total ASVs instead of ASVs successfully assigned to species.

Patterns of Biodiversity Across Indonesia

248

We conducted analyses of fish biodiversity in a hierarchical fashion. First, we examined

249

diversity at the level of individual sample replicate, where fish diversity was represented by

250

eDNA sequences amplified from each of the three one-liter water samples at an individual

251

sampling site. Second, we examined diversity at the site level, where fish diversity was

252

represented by combining replicate eDNA sequences amplified from each site (Table S1). Lastly,

253

we examined diversity in each of the seven sampled regions by combining diversity across all

254

sites sampled. To compare ASV recovery in lower diversity temperate marine ecosystems to

255

high diversity reefs of the Coral Triangle, we compared the above to eDNA fish diversity data

256

from Scorpion Bay, Santa Cruz Island, California (Gold et al., in press) as samples were

257

collected, processed, an analyzed using the same methods outlined above. All analyses were

258

conducted in in R (R Core Team 2020).

259

We examined patterns of alpha diversity by exploring species richness at each

260

hierarchical level. We then compared sample coverage estimates by conducting ASV

261

accumulation curves using the iNext package (version: 2.0.20) in R (Hsieh et al., 2016).

262

Specifically, we explored the accumulation of ASVs 1) across all replicate samples within each

263

site, 2) within all replicate samples within each region, and 3) within all replicate’s sites within

264

each region. We then explored betadiversity patterns across sample replicates, sites, and regions

265

by calculating jaccard-binary dissimilarity indices and running PERMANOVA and companion

266

homogeneity of dispersion test (adonis and betadisper functions from vegan (version: 2.5-6) in

267

R). Results of the PERMANOVA were visualized using a nonmetric multidimensional scaling

268

(NMDS) through the phyloseq (version: 1.32.0) and vegan packages in R (R Core Team, 2020).

To explore the scale of species turnover across the biogeographic regions, we conducted

270

zeta-diversity analyses using the zetadiv (version 1.2.0) package (Latombe et al., 2017). Zeta

271

diversity measures the degree of overlap between any set of observed communities (Hui &

272

McGeoch, 2014), in contrast to betadiversity which only compares pairwise overlap (Hui et al.,

273

2014; Latombe et al., 2017; McGeoch et al., 2019). Importantly, the zeta diversity framework

274

allows for the calculation of diversity metrics across multiple samples and sampling strata to

275

better understand patterns of biodiversity (McGeoch et al., 2019), particularly the scale and

276

shape of community turnover (Hui et al., 2014; McGeoch et al., 2019; Simon et al., 2019). From

277

this framework there are two important zeta diversity metrics: zeta diversity decay and species

278

retention rates (McGeoch et al., 2019). Zeta diversity decay measures the decreasing number of

279

shared taxa across greater observed communities (McGeoch et al., 2019). For small numbers of

280

samples (n<5) this can be visually represented as the center of the Venn Diagram between

281

multiple observed communities (i.e., individual eDNA samples).

282

We compared species retention rates and zeta diversity decay (i.e., the decreasing number

283

of shared taxa across more observed communities) across 1) all replicate samples within each

284

site, 2) within all replicate samples within each region, and 3) within all replicate sites within

285

each region. In particular, we compare the rate of zeta diversity decay and species retention rates

286

across the region to identify sites with lower community turnover across sampling strata. We

287

further explore zeta diversity decay over distance to explore the scale of community turnover

288

across sample replicates, sites, and regions through eDNA data.

RESULTS

301

Raw eDNA Sequences

302

eDNA vs Visual Census

458

A common feature of marine eDNA studies is their ability to return more diversity than

459

traditional survey methods (Kelly et al., 2017; Stat et al., 2017, 2019). However, in all but one

460

case in this study, eDNA recovered substantially less fish biodiversity than visual census data

461

from the same location. In side-by-side comparisons in Raja Ampat, not only did eDNA recover

462

much less fish diversity than visual census methods, but it also recovered completely different

463

fish diversity. At most, only ~17.4% of taxa overlapped in eDNA and visual census surveys

464

(Table 2).

465

While this result, in some ways, highlights the challenge of eDNA approaches in

466

megadiverse ecosystems, it also highlights its utility. In total, eDNA recovered an additional 168

467

species of marine fishes spanning 59 families and 125 genera that were missed by visual census

468

surveys. While some of the diversity captured by eDNA included cryptic taxa like Blennidae that

469

are easily missed in visual census surveys, it also captured a large number of taxa like Lutjanids,

470

Pomacentrids, Siganids, and Epinephalids, among others, that are large, conspicuous fish that

471

should be easily observed.

472

473

nearby reef, or represent eDNA from larval stages or eggs, the comparison of taxa identified

Importance of Sampling Intensity.

487

The results of this study are likely a function of sampling intensity. Visual census of fish

488

communities on coral reefs are done in multiple habitats over multiple days to maximize the

489

amount of diversity recovered (Allen, 2002; Erdmann & Pet, 2002). While we sampled multiple

490

liters of sea water from across individual reefs and from multiple reefs within a given region,

491

visual census data required multiple divers spending 6hrs/day for 10-21 days for each of the

492

regions we sampled in Raja Ampat. Given that visual sampling dwarfed our sampling design, it

493

is perhaps unsurprising that eDNA recovered much less diversity.

494

However, results also suggest limits to the diversity eDNA can recover. One striking

495

pattern revealed in this study is that despite greatly under sampling total fish diversity, we

496

achieved saturation in sequencing depth for every individual sample. Thus, despite the Coral

Biodiversity patterns across Indonesia

546

One of the best-documented patterns of marine biodiversity is the extreme concentration of

547

biodiversity in the Coral Triangle (Bellwood & Meyer, 2009; Roberts, 2002). Given that our

548

study stretched from regions like Raja Ampat, known for the highest marine fish diversity in the

549

world (Allen, 2002, 2008) to regions in Western Indonesia that are outside of the Coral Triangle

550

(Veron et al., 2009), and given that these patterns are based partially on fish biodiversity

551

(Bellwood & Meyer, 2009; Roberts et al., 2002), we expected to see clear gradients in diversity

552

based on eDNA. However, while the Eastern Indonesian reefs of Raja Ampat had the highest

553

fish biodiversity, and the Western Indonesian reefs of Batam-Bintam had the lowest, there was

554

no clear gradient in between.

555

There are four potential explanations for the failure of eDNA to capture this east-west

556

biodiversity gradient in Indonesia. First, the rarefaction plots and models of sampling depth

557

required to achieve ASV saturation show that eDNA recovered a higher percentage of fish

558

diversity present in Western Indonesia than in Eastern Indonesia. Thus, the inability to recover a

559

well-known biodiversity pattern could be an artifact of severely under-sampling the most

560

biodiverse reefs in Indonesia, and that higher intensity eDNA sampling design could yield results

561

that conform to predictions based on previous studies.

562

A second, but not mutually exclusive explanation, is that visual surveys may not fully

563

capture all of the diversity present. There are many taxa like blennies and gobies that live within

564

the reef matrix (Böhm & Hoeksema, 2017; Kotrschal, 1988; Nursall, 1977; Wilson, Fisher, &

24

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.02.19.432056; this version posted February 20, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

565

Pratchett, 2013).

Moreover, visual surveys in Indonesia have been done on open-circuit

566

SCUBA, which can negatively impact fish counts (Lindfield et al., 2014). As such, the well-

567

known biodiversity gradients in Indonesia may reflect visually conspicuous biodiversity, but not

568

cryptic diversity or fish diversity sensitive to SCUBA.

569

A third potential explanation to the mismatch between eDNA and visual census data is

570

that these studies were not conducted contemporaneously. Given that marine ecosystems are

571

dynamic, conducting eDNA and visual surveys at different times could result in capturing

572

different communities (Beenties et al., 2019). This possibility would be easily tested by including

573

eDNA sampling as part of any fish visual census protocol (Alexander et al., 2020) and doing so

574

could add value to visual surveys as eDNA may provide insight into parts of the fish community

575

(e.g., blennies) that are difficult to fully capture in visual surveys (Alexander et al., 2020; Stat et

576

al., 2019).

577

Finally, because eDNA and visual census survey data showed minimal taxonomic

578

overlap it is possible that the more cryptic diversity captured by eDNA does not follow expected

579

regional patterns in biodiversity, a result that could be confirmed through studies of cryptic

580

invertebrate taxa or fish surveys on rebreathers, rather than open-circuit SCUBA. Given the

581

above, combined with the fact that 1) Aceh is located at the nexus between the Indian Ocean and

582

Java Sea (Fadli et al., 2012, 2014), 2) this region is a suture zone between Pacific and Indian

583

Ocean basins (Crandall et al., 2012), and 3) this region is receiving less scientific study thatn

584

regions like Raja Ampat, it is possible that Western Indonesia isn’t as biologically depauperate

585

as presently believed.

While eDNA did not perfectly capture patterns of Indonesian fish biodiversity, results broadly

589

conform to expectations. While eDNA did not perform as expected in comparison to visual

surveys, it is important to put these numbers into the context of sampling effort and cost—eDNA

591

592

593

596

highlighting the immense value and promise of eDNA data.

597

eDNA is clearly a valuable new tool for examining biodiversity across a variety of

598

habitats (Closek et al., 2019; Jeunen et al., 2019; Port et al., 2016; Stat et al., 2017; Stoeckle, et

599

al., 2020). As the field of eDNA continues to mature, it is critical to better understand the

600

performance of eDNA in highly diverse ecosystems, such as Indonesia (Juhel et al., 2020).

601

Important next steps include working to develope reference databases in diverse tropical marine

602

ecosystems like the Coral Triangle to increase the number of ASVs that can be assigned to

603

species (Ward et al., 2009). In addition, performing eDNA and visual surveys at the same time in

604

the same location, and increasing either the volume or number of eDNA water samples, could

605

provide important insights into the best sampling design for eDNA research, creating standard

606

field protocols for eDNA and visual census surveys in the future (Deiner et al., 2017).
