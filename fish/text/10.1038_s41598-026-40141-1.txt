Müller glial and microglial responses coupled to recovery of cone photoreceptors following limited cone ablation in zebrafish retina.

# Müller glial and microglial responses coupled to recovery of cone photoreceptors following limited cone ablation in zebrafish retina

## Abstract
Zebrafish can spontaneously regenerate retinal neurons after acute damage. Ablation and regeneration of retinal neurons can be achieved in transgenic zebrafish with cell-selective expression of bacterial nitroreductase (NTR/nfsb) upon exposure to pro-drugs such as metronidazole (Mtz). We found that Mtz exposure of gnat2:nfsb-mCherry adult zebrafish at certain dose/duration could limit death to only a small number of cones, while causing transient stress to the surviving cone photoreceptors, providing insight into glial and immune cell responses to transient neuronal perturbations. Using this approach, we identified molecular changes in multiple retinal cell types following Mtz washout. Cones exhibited evidence of stress including downregulation of phototransduction genes and upregulation of oxidative stress response genes. Even with only limited cone death detected, the Müller glia (MG) response involved features known to occur during retinal regeneration, including proliferating progenitor production. Microglia/myeloid cells showed evidence of two response waves. The first response occurred within a day of drug washout while the second response occurred around 4 days after drug washout and was associated with cone photoreceptor stress recovery. Single cell RNA-seq suggested two subpopulations of microglia/myeloid cells participated in this response. Our results provide insight into triggers of proliferative responses from the MG, microglial/myeloid responses coupled to neuronal stress recovery, and potential molecular changes involved with cone recovery to stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40141-1.

## Introduction
Regeneration of damaged tissue is largely restricted in mammals, while it is spontaneously induced in other vertebrates such as zebrafish, salamanders, and newts. Such disparate outcomes between species have become the foundation of much work to understand mechanisms of successful regeneration of retinal neurons. The zebrafish has emerged as a crucial animal model to study retinal regeneration because of conserved retinal cell types, its inherent regenerative capacity, and accessibility for genetic and pharmacological manipulations. Work in the zebrafish has led to significant advances in our understanding of the endogenous cell types that can act as a source of regenerated retinal neurons, which in zebrafish retina are the Müller glia1–4. The study of cellular, transcriptional, and epigenetic changes of zebrafish Müller glia has resulted in discoveries that have since been translated to mouse5–10 and human organoid11 systems. In addition to the Müller glia, retinal microglia (which are of hematopoietic myeloid lineage) have gained attention as potentially providing functional impact on retinal regeneration in zebrafish12–15, chick16, and mouse17. In contrast to work probing regenerative responses of the zebrafish retina, more limited attention has been given to glial responses in models of chronic retinal degeneration18–20 and there is a general lack of work probing glial responses to targeted perturbations of retinal neurons that do not ultimately result in neuronal death.
To study regenerative responses in the zebrafish retina, experimental systems must result in cellular death/damage to trigger regeneration. The nitroreductase/metronidazole (NTR/Mtz) system is popular with zebrafish researchers as it can inducibly target cell ablation/death to specific cell types through the experimental use of zebrafish carrying transgenes consisting of cell-specific promoters to drive expression of bacterial nitroreductase (Nfsb/NTR) enzymes21–23. Mtz is a pro-drug, but in contexts such as anaerobic conditions or via NTR enzymatic activity, it is converted to a cytotoxin24. Therefore, in zebrafish, only the cell type expressing NTR is subjected to the cytotoxic effects of modified Mtz25. This provides an excellent non-invasive experimental system to investigate the impact of targeted cell death within specific tissues, the cellular response to such death26, the impacts on biological processes in the absence of certain cell types13,27, and to trigger the intrinsic regenerative response of the zebrafish in numerous tissues including the retina13,21,28–30. However, based on accumulating results with transgenic NTR-expressing zebrafish lines, it has been noted that in some cases certain cell types, and fish of different ages/life stages, require higher doses or longer exposure to Mtz to achieve cell death/ablation21,26,31–33. As a potential complication, prolonged and/or high concentrations of Mtz exposure potentially leads to undesirable off-target toxicity or effects34,35. To address such challenges, alternate versions of the NTR enzyme (deemed “NTR1.1, NTR2.0”) have been developed to improve cell ablation with reduced Mtz duration/concentration35,36. Though the goal of most experiments using the NTR/Mtz system in zebrafish is to ablate specific cell types (i.e. induce cell death), this system has the potential to be modified to obtain transient impacts of Mtz-induced effects at a sublethal level or to reduce the numbers of ablated cells within a larger population21,37. Such an outcome could be achieved using existing “NTR 1.0” transgenic zebrafish lines and modifying duration or concentration of Mtz exposure.
In this paper, we used a previously published transgenic zebrafish line gnat2:nfsb-mCherry37, which encodes the E. Coli Nfsb (“NTR1.0”) enzyme under control of the cone photoreceptor specific gnat2 promoter. This zebrafish line has been used previously to ablate a centrally localized patch of cone photoreceptors in the larval zebrafish retina37. We found that as adult fish, gnat2:nfsb-mCherry transgenics did not achieve reliable pan-cone ablation with 24-hour immersion exposure to 10mM of Mtz. Instead, such an exposure regime resulted in detectable death of a limited number of cone photoreceptors. Despite the lack of widespread cone photoreceptor ablation, the remaining cones showed evidence of transient stress and recovery in the days that followed Mtz exposure and washout that resulted in responses from both the Müller glia and microglia. Taking advantage of this system and combining fluorescent imaging of retinal tissue with single cell RNA-sequencing, we describe novel changes at the molecular, cellular, and tissue level in cones, Müller glia, and microglia/myeloid populations within this context.

## Results

## Cone photoreceptor death, stress, and recovery upon exposure of gnat2:nfsb-mCherry adult fish to Mtz
As discussed above, expression of bacterial Nfsb/NTR enzyme renders cells sensitive to treatment with the pro-drug metronidazole (Mtz). We subjected adult gnat2:nfsb-mCherry transgenic zebrafish, which restricts Mtz cytotoxicity to cone photoreceptors, to 24-hour immersion exposure to 10mM Mtz in 0.1% DMSO (Mtz treated) or DMSO (vehicle alone), followed by washout into clean water (Fig. 1A). Upon netting the fish after 24 h of treatment, we observed general features and behaviors that indicated visual impairment in the gnat2:nfsb-mCherry Mtz treated fish (though we did not complete systematic analysis of visually mediated behaviors). The gnat2:nfsb-mCherry fish treated with Mtz were easier to catch in the net, often ran into tank walls, and generally swam lower in the water column. These behaviors became less pronounced over time and generally resolved by 72 h post Mtz treatment. In contrast, the DSMO treated fish, as well as non-transgenic (no Nfsb transgene) fish treated with Mtz, avoided the net and tank walls and placed themselves higher in the water column.

We examined retinal cryosections for TUNEL and mCherry reporter (cone photoreceptor) expression at 24-hour timepoints following DMSO or Mtz exposure. The only timepoint in which TUNEL+ cones were significantly detected was at 48 hours post treatment (hpt) following Mtz exposure and the numbers ranged from 0 to 17 per entire retinal cryosection; mean = 5.8 +/- 6.6 s.d. (Fig. 1B, p < 0.01 compared to DMSO group at 48 hpt). We also quantified TUNEL signal by retinal layer (outer nuclear layer (ONL), inner nuclear layer (INL), and ganglion cell layer (GCL)) and found that detection of TUNEL was restricted to the ONL (Fig. 1C-C’’’ and Supplemental Fig. 1). TUNEL signal was not significantly detected outside of this 48 hpt timepoint, in any retinal layer (Fig. 1B and C-C’’’, and Supplemental Fig. 1). We also observed that the mCherry reporter signal in cones in retinal cryosections appeared reduced in intensity after Mtz exposure/washout, and this was notable through 120 hpt (Fig. 1C’-C’’’). Further, at 48 hpt, many cones appeared disorganized though they were still highly abundant (Fig. 1C’), suggesting that they were experiencing effects of Mtz. We also observed some instances of collapsed, rounded mCherry+ cells that could represent dying cones that were not detected by TUNEL staining (Fig. 1C’). By 96–120 hpt Mtz treatment, cone morphology was more similar to controls, though putative aggregates of mCherry fluorescent protein signal were noted (Fig. 1C’’-C’’’). Given that the gnat2 promoter is the driver of the transgenic nfsb-mCherry reporter, we used RT-qPCR to examine expression of gnat2 mRNA in whole retinas following Mtz exposure (Fig. 1D). Consistent with reduction of the gnat2-promoter driven transgene, we also found that endogenous expression of gnat2 mRNA was reduced in cones at 96 and 120 hpt following Mtz treatment and washout (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these results indicate that 24-hour immersion exposure to 10mM Mtz induces a limited level of cone photoreceptor death, detectable primarily at 48 hpt. Further, following washout of Mtz, other cellular changes in the surviving cone photoreceptors of adult gnat2:nfsb-mCherrry zebrafish occur through at least 120 hpt.
Given that microglia respond to neuronal stress and death in the retina38–40, we also stained retinal sections with the 4C4 antibody used to label microglia in zebrafish41(Fig. 1C-C’’’). To further visualize changes across the retina for selected timepoints, we also provide full retinal cryosection images showing signal from gnat2:nfsb-mCherry transgene, TUNEL, 4C4 stain, and DAPI for selected samples (DMSO, 48 hpt Mtz, and 120 hpt Mtz, Supplemental Fig. 2). Microglia localized to several of the TUNEL+ cones at 48 hpt following Mtz exposure (Fig. 1C’, Supplemental Fig. 2) where they were likely engulfing such cones. The full retinal section images also reveal that microglial response was detected in patches or regions and not the entire ONL; these regions in the ONL included TUNEL signal and mCherry+ cones that had altered morphology (Supplemental Fig. 2), again consistent with limited death of cones as a result of this Mtz exposure regime. Interestingly, at 96 and 120 hpt, when cones again had longer visible outer segments (OS) but putatively aggregated mCherry, microglia were observed to localize apically near the cone OS and regions consistent with the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the subretinal space (Fig. 1C’’ and C’’’, Supplemental Fig. 2). The images visualizing all of these markers together indicate that microglia respond to the limited cone death detected at 48 hpt, but in addition, microglial response continued through 120 hpt following Mtz treatment. This later response involved different localization patterns and was not necessarily associated with detection of cell death by TUNEL staining yet corresponded temporally to later changes in cones that survived following Mtz washout.

## Single cell RNA-sequencing identifies molecular changes in surviving cones in response to Mtz exposure
To better understand changes to photoreceptors and other cell types after Mtz washout, we performed single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) of dissociated, single retinal cells isolated from gnat2:nfsb-mCherry zebrafish retinas treated with DMSO or Mtz for 24 h via immersion, followed by washout. We selected 96 and 120 hpt timepoints in order to better understand changes in cones and glia during this apparent “recovery” window (Fig. 2A). Using the 10X Genomics platform and standard bioinformatic pipelines (described in Methods), we identified 22 distinct clusters in our single cell dataset, and all major retinal cell types were identified using known markers (Fig. 2, Supplemental File 1). Figure 2 shows UMAP plots of the integrated dataset (Fig. 2B, C) as well as UMAP plots from cells in each distinct timepoint/sample (Fig. 2D). Cones were identified as two adjacent clusters (clusters 2 and 12) that remained detectable in all samples, consistent with minimal ablation by Mtz treatment as described in Fig. 1. Microglia/myeloid populations showed changes over the two timepoints following Mtz treatment (Fig. 2D, cluster 5) and interestingly, clusters pertaining to Müller glia (MG) and putative progenitor cells, some of which were likely MG-derived, also showed changes in response to Mtz treatment (Fig. 2D, clusters 0,1,4,6), indicating a MG and neuronal progenitor cell (NPC) response to sub-lethal cone stress.

Given the downregulation of gnat2 detected in retinas following Mtz exposure (Fig. 1), we examined expression patterns of other cone phototransduction genes in cone clusters of the scRNA-seq dataset (Fig. 3A, B). Consistent with our previous results described in Fig. 1, gnat2 mRNA levels were reduced in cone clusters in the single cell dataset following Mtz treatment (Fig. 3A). We also found reduced expression of several other cone phototransduction genes, including arr3a (cone arrestin, Fig. 3A) and notable reduction of grk7a, pde6ha, and guca1c (Fig. 3B), in cone clusters following Mtz exposure. Evidence of oxidative stress in the cone photoreceptor clusters was noted, with increased expression of mRNA for several oxidative stress response genes and notably for gpx4b, prdx6, and txn (Fig. 3C). Examining these oxidative stress genes in all cell types indicated that cones were not the only cell type to upregulate oxidative stress genes such as txn following Mtz exposure, with increased mRNA levels for txn detected in nearly all cell types (Fig. 3D), suggesting that, even though only cones express Nfsb, other retinal cells were experiencing transcriptional changes at 96–120 h after Mtz exposure/washout. RT-qPCR confirmed that txn mRNA was increased in whole retinas at 96–120 hpt following Mtz exposure (Fig. 3E). In contrast, gpx4b appeared more selectively upregulated in the cone clusters following Mtz treatment (Fig. 3F), though it was found to be expressed in most cell type clusters in the DMSO control retinas. Other gene expression patterns further indicated that cones had activated and were potentially resolving or using selective stress response pathways likely to restore homeostasis. This included changes (both increase and decrease) in mRNA for genes encoding ubiquitin proteins and several cellular chaperones (Supplemental Fig. 3).

## Müller glia respond to Mtz exposure by entering the cell cycle and producing proliferating progenitor cells
Given that Müller glia (MG) are highly responsive to retinal perturbations42,43and contact photoreceptors via their apical processes44,45, we further examined the Müller glial response in this experimental system. Within the single cell transcriptome dataset, we noted changes to the MG and progenitor cell clusters in samples collected after Mtz treatment compared to DMSO controls (Fig. 2). Examining known glial reactivity genes gfap and vim, UMAPs generated selectively from the Müller glia cluster within the single cell RNA-seq dataset indicated increased expression of both gfap and vim within the MG population in response to Mtz treatment (Fig. 4A, B). RT-qPCR using RNA isolated from whole retinas confirmed that overall, mRNA transcripts for gfap and vim were increased at 96 and 120 hpt Mtz treatment compared to DMSO controls (Fig. 4D, E). Interestingly, violin plots indicated that the overall increase in gfap and vim mRNA abundance is likely due to increased numbers of MG expressing these genes at the single cell level at these timepoints, as opposed to increases in per-cell expression of these transcripts, post Mtz treatment compared to DMSO controls (Fig. 4F). Given that our sampling/analysis was performed at 96 and 120 hpt, it is possible that there were increases in the per-cell expression levels of gfap and vim at preceding timepoints that we did not capture in our dataset, and our results for these genes at 96–120 hpt could potentially represent reduction of these reactivity genes following a prior increase upon cone stress. In response to retinal damage and during initiation of regenerative responses, zebrafish MG have also been shown to increase expression of mmp946, and we found robust increase in mmp9 mRNA within MG cluster in retinas following Mtz exposure, but not until the 120 hpt timepoint (Fig. 4C, F), suggesting the upregulation of mmp9 by MG is delayed relative to general MG reactivity.

Although reactivity of MG in response to cone stress is not surprising, we also found evidence of perhaps unexpected production of MG-derived progenitor cells (MGPCs) in retinas exposed to 24 h Mtz treatment. This was surprising at least to us given the limited number of TUNEL+ and collapsed cones we detected at 48 hpt (Fig. 1B). The generation of MGPCs in response to acute damage is a well-appreciated, well-described feature of the retinal regenerative response in zebrafish. However, it has been postulated that a certain level of cell death is required for such a response29,47, potentially intertwined with inflammatory cues18,20,47. Populations of progenitor cells (clusters 1,4,6) expanded in retinas experiencing Mtz treatment (Fig. 2B-D). In particular, cluster 6, which appears adjacent to MG (cluster 0) and clusters 1 and 4 (progenitors) in UMAP space, expands robustly in response to Mtz treatment, suggesting that MGPCs are generated even though detected cone death was minimal. The MG and progenitor clusters all upregulated expression of the S-phase cell cycle gene pcna (Fig. 4G), and mRNA for pcna was also found to be increased in whole retinas using RT-qPCR through 120 hpt Mtz treatment (Fig. 4H). Other cell cycle genes were also increased in MG/MGPC clusters in the single cell dataset, though at more modest levels (ccnd1, ccna2, mych, Supplemental Fig. 4).
Immunostaining of retinal cryosections over timepoints ranging from 24 to 120 hpt revealed that PCNA+ cells in the inner nuclear layer (INL), many of which were surrounded by GS (glutamine synthetase) labeled signal, became detectable in a limited number of samples around 48–72 hpt Mtz treatment but were increased in most samples by 96 hpt (Fig. 4I, J-J’’’,K). Some samples showed further increased numbers of PCNA+ cells at 120 hpt Mtz, while others instead showed reduced PCNA+ numbers (Fig. 4J’’’’,J’’’’’,K). At 96 hpt Mtz, most PCNA+ cells were localized to the INL, while retinas with higher levels of proliferation at 120 hpt Mtz showed many of the PCNA+ cells had shifted to the ONL (Fig. 4J’’’’), consistent with interkinetic nuclear migration of MGPCs described previously1. Examining PCNA+ cells by region of retinal cryosections did not find bias for proliferating progenitors in central versus more peripheral retinal regions (Supplemental Fig. 5). In addition, intact cones were observed in regions with PCNA+ cells (Fig. 4J-J’’’’’) again indicating that the production of proliferating MGPCs was not necessarily localized to possible patches of cone loss in the ONL. The range of PCNA+ cell numbers exhibited by different samples at 120 hpt Mtz may suggest that some of the retinas have only transient production of MGPCs, while others have MGPCs that continue proliferation/sustain PCNA protein levels and begin interkinetic nuclear migration. The RT-qPCR results indicate even with changes in protein expression, elevated pcna mRNA remains through 120 hpt (Fig. 4H). This could indicate that PCNA protein may be degraded and/or that other cell types (such as microglia, seen in the single cell UMAPs to also increase pcna in Mtz treated retinas Fig. 4G), retain elevated pcna expression.
We also examined re-expression of the stem-like genes ascl1a and its transcriptional target lin28 in MGPC populations48following sub-lethal Mtz exposure to cones. Both ascl1a and lin28a mRNA were not strongly detected in MG or even putative progenitors in the DMSO control sample in the scRNA-seq dataset, but numerous cells within these clusters showed increased mRNA for both ascl1a and lin28a by 120 hpt Mtz treatment (Fig. 5A, C). These findings were overall confirmed by RT-qPCR using whole retinas (Fig. 5B, D), however the range of induction of these genes was variable in Mtz treated samples. Fold increases in ascl1a ranged from ~ 3 to 48 fold at 96 hpt Mtz and ~ 7 to 43.5 fold at 120 hpt. Fold increases in lin28a ranged from ~ 3 to 1,550 fold at 96 hpt, and from ~ 45 to 288 fold at 120 hpt. In sum, despite limited cone death detected by TUNEL staining at 48 hpt following Mtz treatment, MG became reactive and produced proliferating MGPCs around 2–4 days after drug washout but this production of MGPCs and their continued proliferation and/or ability to re-access stem-like genes was variable and potentially limited.

## Microglia/myeloid cell response following Mtz treatment
We visualized microglial responses in whole, flat mounted gnat2:nfsb-mCherry retinas, following DMSO or Mtz exposure and washout, using the antibody “4C4” which is frequently used to label zebrafish microglia41 (Fig. 6). Within the microglia localized to the ONL (Fig. 6A-F’’’), morphological changes to microglia were observed by 48 hpt Mtz treatment in regions of some samples (Fig. 6C) (24 h after drug washout), in which many microglia showed rounding and retraction of branched processes compared to DMSO controls (Fig. 6C). We also observed regions in samples at 48 hpt Mtz treatment that looked more like controls (Fig. 6C’). At 72–96 hpt Mtz, microglia appeared to re-extend their processes and become more branched and elongated (Fig. 6D, E). In samples collected at 120 hpt following Mtz treatment, we observed a variety of morphologies across the retinas sampled and in different regions within these samples, with some regions where microglia again appeared rounded with compact morphologies with possible cellular compartments while others showed microglia that were less rounded/compact but still appeared different from those in controls (Fig. 6F-F’’’). Timepoints where microglia showed the most diverse morphologies correspond temporally to acute stress/death of some cones (at 48 hpt Mtz) and transcriptional changes within cones indicating stress recovery (at 120 hpt Mtz, described above). Consistent with these changes to cones, we also saw differences at these timepoints in the appearance of the cone mosaic visualized by the mCherry reporter (Fig. 6C, C’, and 6F-F’’’). In addition, pcna was modestly increased in the microglia/myeloid cluster at 120 hpt Mtz (Fig. 4G) suggesting that some microglia may proliferate at this timepoint. Quantification of microglia numbers in the ONL/OPL over time revealed that there were only modest changes to actual microglial numbers in this region, with two possible “waves” of increased microglial abundance at 48 hpt Mtz and 120 hpt Mtz (Fig. 6G). Interestingly, these moderate “waves” in numbers of 4C4+ microglia in the ONL coincided with reduction of microglia counts in the INL and moderately in the GCL that suggest a redistribution of microglial populations away from the inner retina particularly in the early response around 48–72 hpt following Mtz treatment (Fig. 6H, I).These results indicate that microglia respond to acute Mtz effects as well as respond to or participate in changes involved with recovery of cones after Mtz exposure.

We further investigated microglia/myeloid cells in the single cell RNA-seq dataset. Microglia were assigned to cluster 5 (Fig. 2), and cells within this cluster strongly expressed the myeloid lineage transcription factor spi1b (Fig. 7A). We49 and others50 previously noted strong expression of the gene apoc1 in zebrafish retinal microglia. Within the myeloid cell cluster, we found that apoc1 expression was not uniform and instead revealed the potential of two sub-clusters of microglia/myeloid populations with strong/high or weak/low expression of apoc1 (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, the “apoc1-low” population showed high expression of genes such as c1qa, c1qb, c1qc, cxcr4b, and lygl1 all of which were notably lower in the “apoc1-high” population (Fig. 7C and Supplemental Fig. 6). Further, expression of genes such as p2ry12 and apoeb generally corresponded to cells expressing apoc1 (Supplemental Fig. 6). The single cell dataset suggested that two such populations, based on variability of apoc1 expression, may exist in undamaged adult retinas as well, though the “apoc1-high” population is most abundant, and the “apoc1-low” population is minimally detected (Fig. 7D, DMSO group). The “apoc1-low” population appears to expand in retinas at 96 and 120 hpt Mtz treatment with increased expression of genes including c1qb (Fig. 7E, F). These results suggest heterogeneity in myeloid/microglial cell populations in adult zebrafish retina, particularly in response to Mtz-induced death/stress to cone photoreceptors.

Using fluorescent mRNA HCR in situ hybridization (HCR-FISH) and confocal microscopy, we labeled and visualized transcripts for apoc1 and pfn1 in whole, flat mounted retinas from DMSO control fish or collected at 120 hpt Mtz treatment (Fig. 8). We selected pfn1 as a pan-myeloid cell transcript because it was strongly, and uniquely, expressed in cells of cluster 5 similar to spi1b (Fig. 8A, also noted in50). We observed cells consistent with microglia (based on morphology) expressing apoc1 in both DMSO and Mtz treated retinas (Fig. 8B, C). Across the samples and even within each retina, signal intensity from apoc1 probe was detectable but variable in intensity in individual microglial cells (Fig. 8B, C). A region from a selected HCR-FISH sample containing examples of pfn1+ microglial cells with variable detection of apoc1 signal (apoc1+ and apoc1-neg/low) are shown in Fig. 8D-F. We scored individual cells for expression of pfn1 and apoc1 transcripts in our images from DMSO and Mtz treated samples, in cells consistent with microglia (based on morphology) in the different retinal layers (Fig. 8G). In the inner retinal layers (INL/OPL, GCL) of both DMSO and Mtz treated samples we detected a small population of cells that we scored as pfn1+, “apoc1-low/neg” based on undetectable expression of apoc1 probe signal by HCR-FISH (Fig. 8G), suggesting that an “apoc1-low” subpopulation of microglia may already exist in adult zebrafish retina. In three of the five retinas collected at 120 hpt following Mtz exposure, we saw expansion of ONL-localized microglia/myeloid cells that expressed both pfn1 and apoc1 (Fig. 8G), consistent with trends for 4C4+ microglia counts in the ONL at 120 hpt (Fig. 6). The HCR-FISH analysis of microglia within the retina itself did not find an expansion of apoc1-negative/low populations as was suggested by the scRNA-seq, but it is important to emphasize that the whole, flat mounted retinas prepared for HCR-FISH would not include myeloid cells/microglia that may be localized to the RPE and subretinal space due to the removal of the intact retina from the whole eye, and removal of the RPE for imaging purposes, in this procedure. Further, it is difficult to equate signal from HCR-FISH to expression levels obtained in the single cell RNA-seq analysis. The “apoc1-low” myeloid cell population detected in the scRNA-seq dataset are either not localized to the neural retina in a way that is captured by our whole retina dissection procedures and/or we could not clearly validate them with this approach (for example, sensitivity for detection of apoc1 mRNA may differ between HCR-FISH and scRNA-seq). Notably, RPE/subretinal space myeloid cell populations, which we detect by 4C4 staining in retinal cryosections (Fig. 1C’’’ and Supplemental Fig. 2), would have been included in our single cell preparations for scRNA-seq and could underlie at least some of the transcriptional heterogeneity within the myeloid cluster found in the scRNA-seq analysis. Unfortunately, we could not successfully execute HCR-FISH with our probe sets/reagents on retinal cryosections. Therefore, we acknowledge that our attempts to verify such populations were limited in the genes and methods used to probe them, and additional work is required to verify additional myeloid cell populations that may be involved in response to cone death or stress.

## Discussion
We found that the Mtz exposure regimen of gnat2:nfsb-mCherry adult transgenic fish used in this work (24-hour immersion in 10mM Mtz) resulted in limited detection of cone death (by TUNEL staining). The surviving cone photoreceptors likely experienced transient stress and recovery, providing an unanticipated opportunity to probe molecular and cellular changes involved with cone stress recovery in the adult zebrafish retina. Molecular changes at the mRNA level included alterations in expression of phototransduction genes in cones, most prominently the downregulation of genes such as gnat2, arr3a, grk7a, pde6ha, and guca1d. In addition, we found evidence of oxidative stress response in cones, through increased mRNA for genes such as gpx4b and txn which were also upregulated in zebrafish rods in a transgenic model of rod degeneration50. Given the timing of these changes, which were detected between 3 and 4 days after Mtz washout, and in the absence of evidence of substantial cell death in this timeframe, we consider that these changes could represent a recovery trajectory for cones after stress induced by the Mtz/NTR reaction.
We found that even in the absence of widespread cone death upon Mtz treatment, the Müller glia showed changes associated with reactivity and regenerative responses. This included, at least on the population level, increased expression of reactivity genes gfap and vim. Also evident was robust upregulation of mmp9 by the Müller glia, which has been shown to be involved in inflammatory and regenerative responses in the adult zebrafish retina after light damage to photoreceptors46. It is perhaps not surprising that Müller glia reactivity would be detected upon Mtz-induced perturbation to cones. However, although we detected limited numbers of TUNEL+ cones in retinal cryosections in our experiments, we found that MG re-entered the cell cycle and produced proliferating MGPCs in many sampled retinas. We note, however, that a few TUNEL+ cones per section that we detected at selected individual time points could, on the scale of the whole retina and continuously over time, potentially amount to more substantial numbers of dying cones. Further, TUNEL labeling may not fully represent the extent of cone death and/or regions that we did not sample could have patches of cone death that we did not detect. This net sum of dying cones could potentially reach some threshold of cell death required for Müller glia to re-enter the cell cycle and produce proliferating MGPCs. We could not longitudinally study retinas following specific cell death levels and PCNA+ MGPC levels in the same animals, but as a collective dataset nearly all samples had elevated numbers of PCNA+ cells by 96 h following Mtz treatment (3 days after Mtz washout). The variability seen in our TUNEL+ cone counts as well as PCNA+ MGPC could indicate that the Mtz exposure dose and duration used in our experiments results in cone death outcomes that are just at or very near a threshold of death that leads to a MGPC response. Of note, our results may suggest an alternative hypothesis that signals other than level of cell death may be able to induce MG proliferation/regenerative responses. Consistent with the idea that a cell death threshold is either minimal or not required for MG/MGPC proliferation, previous work has shown that stab injury to the zebrafish retina which is limited in the region and number of cells effectively damaged, also induces MG/MGPC proliferation4,51, though we speculate that the number of cells in stab injury probably exceeds that of what we detected in our system. Additionally, in chronic degeneration models in zebrafish, hundreds or more neurons undergo degeneration/death, but MG do not readily enter the cell cycle or produce MGPCs18,19. We consider that certain cell death pathways, ensuing molecular pathways including inflammatory signals13,15,20,46,52, contact mediated changes sensed by MG19, and/or permissiveness at certain ages52 could contribute to MG cell cycle entry and sustained proliferation of MGPCs. Further, the microglia could provide signals to MG in our experimental context, since they initially responded to the few dying cones at 48 hpt, which preceded cell cycle entry of MG and MGPC detection at 72–96 hpt (1–2 days later). To better test such alternative triggers of MGPC production and avoid complications of uncertainty about death versus only stress of cones would require further empirical optimization of Mtz exposure, such as titration of Mtz dosage or immersion time, and careful analysis for evidence of induced cell death or lack thereof, along with detection of MGPC production.
At 96–120 h post Mtz treatment, expression of the progenitor genes ascl1a and lin28a were variable. The variability in expression of these progenitor-associated genes could represent correlation to the variability in PCNA detection within retinal tissue seen between 96 and 120 hpt and indicate that production of MGPCs is not sustained in all responses. Alternatively, such results could be related to timing; these genes may be more strongly induced prior to 96 hpt, which represents the first timepoint that we examined by scRNAseq and RT-qPCR. It is possible that by 96–120 hpt, these genes are following a trajectory of downregulation. However, given the time range for PCNA detection (72–120 hpt), we instead favor the interpretation that expression of progenitor genes likely correlates to detection of PCNA+ MG/MGPCs at the tissue level. In other words, samples that have minimal numbers of PCNA+ cells also would likely have minimal expression of ascl1a and lin28a, representing variability in sustained MGPC production. We did not perform experiments to track or localize potential neurons produced from the MGPCs detected in response to cone stress, therefore such a question would need to be addressed in future studies.
We found that microglia/myeloid cells responded after Mtz exposure. If not necessarily by substantially increasing their numbers in the outer nuclear layer itself, they changed morphologically and transcriptionally, and this response appeared to occur in two waves. The microglial response at 48 hpt following Mtz treatment, when they were observed to localize to and likely engulf TUNEL+ cones, was not surprising since microglia are known to engulf dying neurons. However, the second microglial response that occurred around 120 hpt following Mtz treatment, which is temporally coupled to molecular changes in cone photoreceptors, and in which microglia instead localize to the apical ONL near photoreceptor outer segments or in the putative subretinal space, is intriguing. The single cell RNA-seq suggested there could be two populations of microglia/myeloid cells responding at this timepoint, and these two populations could be differentiated bioinformatically by expression of apoc1. Heterogeneous populations of zebrafish microglia have been described in other papers using single cell RNA-sequencing50,53,54. We do not think that in the context of this system, we are missing significant numbers of microglia/myeloid cells using the 4C4 antibody for staining because lgals3bpb (also called lgals3bp.1), which is thought to encode the antigen detected by the 4C4 antibody41, was expressed in nearly all cells in the microglia/myeloid cluster, regardless of apoc1 expression (Supplemental Fig. 7). Our whole retina HCR-FISH results supported that there are populations of resident microglia/myeloid cells with differential expression of apoc1 in adult zebrafish retina, however, bona fide existence of such subpopulations, as well as the expanded apoc1-negative/low population detected in the single cell RNA-seq dataset, requires further confirmation through labeling of additional mRNAs and examining populations that may instead localize to the subretinal space/RPE and are not represented in dissected retinal flat-mounts used for confocal imaging. In addition, given the limited sample replicates in our RNA-seq dataset, we refrained from performing differential expression analysis of gene expression changes over time since we could not perform rigorous pseudobulk bioinformatic pipelines. Therefore, gene expression changes and other possible myeloid cell populations associated with microglial responses in this context remain to be determined.
It is tempting to speculate that the expanded apoc1-negative/low population detected in the single cell RNA-seq dataset could represent myeloid cells infiltrating from other locations. This “apoc1-low/neg” population also shows cells with reduced expression of several microglia “markers” (e.g. p2ry12 and apoeb, Supplemental Fig. 6) but high expression of putative monocyte/macrophage markers (for example, cxcr4b55, Supplemental Fig. 6). There have been many efforts to distinguish microglia from infiltrating myeloid cells for the purpose of determining shared versus distinct functions in retinal responses. A previous paper56 described an expression panel for several markers in mouse retina that distinguished resident microglia from monocyte-derived cells. Most of the markers named for this mouse panel56 do not have clear orthologs in zebrafish, therefore this panel cannot simply be applied to zebrafish myeloid populations. In the absence of rigorous experimental approaches, for example through lineage tracing, we cannot determine the origins of our myeloid cell populations. Alternatively, or at the same time, these gene expression patterns on a per cell basis could represent molecular changes for responding microglia that could underlie the heterogeneous gene expression detected in the microglia/myeloid population in our dataset. Further, as discussed above, in our whole, intact retina samples we are likely missing myeloid cells that may not localize to the neural retina, and are lost during whole retina tissue dissection, and additional approaches and experiments are necessary to interrogate such populations.
Subpopulations of microglia and myeloid cells in homeostasis and in response to photoreceptor degeneration have been described using single cell transcriptomics for mouse retina57. The authors identified a population of microglia that localized to the subretinal space in response to photoreceptor degeneration, both following light damage and in RhoP23H/WT mouse retinas with chronic rod degeneration57. The subretinal space-localized mouse microglia population upregulated genes such as Cd68, Lgals3, and Lpl while downregulating P2ry12 and Tmem119. In the absence of rigorous bioinformatic comparison that can reliably determine genetic orthologs between mouse and zebrafish, and because the numbers of myeloid cells in each of our samples are not sufficient for rigorous subcluster analyses, we qualitatively looked at some selected major genes identified in the mouse retina subretinal space microglia by O’Koren et al.57. In the single cell RNA-seq dataset presented here, we identified two potential subpopulations of myeloid lineage cells in zebrafish retina, using apoc1 which was highly expressed in one population and very low/absent in the other. The “apoc1-high” population contained cells with high p2ry12 expression though within this population, cells with low p2ry12 mRNA levels were also present (Supplemental Fig. 6). Both myeloid populations in our dataset (apoc1-high and apoc1-low) increased expression of lgals3a (orthologous to mouse Lgals3), but several other orthologous genes (tmem119a/b, cd68, and lpla) did not show significant mRNA detection in our dataset. Interestingly, the subretinal space microglia population described for mouse retina57 showed upregulation of lipid regulatory genes Apoe, Fabp5, and Plin2 compared to the homeostatic microglia. Our apoc1-high population also showed positive correlation for expression of the zebrafish orthologs of these lipid regulatory genes (apoeb, fabp4a, and plin2, respectively), suggesting that microglial functions involving lipid regulation could be important in their responses to photoreceptor degeneration or stress.
Although the gnat2:nfsb-mCherry line has been used to ablate cones in larvae37, to our knowledge this particular line has not been published previously for use in adult retinal regeneration experiments, and as described in our work here, using standard Mtz immersion approaches did not provide reliable ablation of cones. It is not clear why ablation of cones was not achieved with the 24-hour Mtz exposure of gnat2:nfsb-mCherry adult fish, but it could be related to immersion of adult vs. larval zebrafish and the effective concentration of the drug within the retina. Work from other labs using different Nfsb-expressing photoreceptor ablation lines obtained more reliable ablation in adult fish though some with different Mtz dosage or duration29,58,59. The range of TUNEL+ cones detected between different samples may be related to variability of Mtz concentrations within retina of adult fish, or perhaps although gnat2 is a pan-cone gene, different subpopulations of cones may be more or less susceptible to Mtz mediated effects when they express Nfsb.
If such an outcome of reliable and more abundant cone death could be achieved, in addition to a tool for probing cone stress responses, the gnat2:nfsb-mCherry line would be a welcome addition to a toolkit for probing regeneration of cone photoreceptors in adult zebrafish as it does not require specialized procedures or equipment such as are required for light damage60, which also damages rod photoreceptors. There is potential to overcome the challenge of cone ablation with this line by testing alternative approaches, such as using the drug ronidazole in place of metronidazole61,62 if it can be sourced, injecting Mtz intravitreally as is done for chemicals such as ouabain38,63–70 and NMDA58,71, or potentially a tapered regime of Mtz immersion exposure58. Nonetheless, we found that the unanticipated outcome suggesting cone stress and recovery could provide a novel experimental context to study glial responses that are not yet well understood, and that could be relevant to retinal degeneration, injury, and/or disease. Future work could use modulation of the Mtz/NTR system appropriately to probe triggers of MG cell cycle entry and MGPC production, differentiation of neurons, glial functions related to photoreceptor resilience, and mechanisms involved with photoreceptor recovery to applied stress.

## Methods

## Zebrafish
Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were housed in an aquatic system with filtered, recirculating water following standard practices72. Water was maintained at 28.5 °C with a 14-hour:10-hour light: dark cycle. All experiments included zebrafish of both sexes, and zebrafish were generated from in-house breeding and rearing. Anesthesia was performed using tricaine/MS-222 (buffered to pH 7.0). At time of tissue collection, zebrafish were euthanized via immersion in tricaine/MS-222 solution (0.25 mg/mL) until fully unresponsive to touch, after which spinal transection was performed to allow rapid dissection of viable retinal tissue for sample collection. All procedures described were approved by the University of Idaho Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the IACUC and followed ARRIVE guidelines.
The gnat2:nfsb-mCherry transgenic line37, in which cone photoreceptors express a mCherry fluorophore-tagged nitroreductase enzyme (nfsb) driven by the cone-specific promoter gnat2, was originally created in Dr. Rachel Wong’s lab (University of Washington) and was gifted to our lab by Dr. Mikiko Nagashima (University of Michigan). Zebrafish carrying the gnat2:nfsb-mCherry transgene were sorted by visible expression of the mCherry reporter in eyes as early as 4 days post fertilization.

## Treatments
Zebrafish used in experiments were age 6–18 months old (adults). Adult zebrafish were exposed to 10mM Metronidazole (Mtz, Thermo Scientific 210341000) dissolved in system water with 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in fish tanks housed in an incubator (Thermo Scientific Heratherm) set to 28.5 °C. Control groups were immersed in 0.1% DMSO in system water (vehicle). Due to the photosensitive nature of Mtz, tanks for both treatments were loosely covered in aluminum foil to protect from light. Immersion in Mtz or DMSO lasted 24 h. At the end of treatment, fish were washed by two transfers into fresh system water, then returned to housing tanks in the zebrafish facility.

## Single cell RNA sequencing

## Single cell dissociation
Whole retinas from Mtz- or DMSO-treated gnat2:nfsb-mCherry zebrafish—hereafter referred to as DMSO (collected at 96 h post DMSO treatment), MTZ96 HPT (collected at 96 h post Mtz treatment), and MTZ120 HPT (collected at 120 h post Mtz treatment)—were dissected from whole eyes then dissociated to single cell suspension following a protocol modified from50. Euthanized zebrafish were kept on ice prior to eye enucleation. Eyes were transferred to Leibovitz’s L-15 Medium (L-15) for further dissection. First, the lens was removed via a hole punctured in the cornea. Then, the sclera and iris were carefully peeled away from the retina along with choroid. The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) was left attached to the retina. Retinas were transferred to 300µL 3:1 L-15:Earl’s Basic Salt Solution (EBSS) in 15-mL conical tubes that were pre-blocked with 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). Retinas from the same fish were pooled together for dissociation to generate each sample and placed on ice. Once all samples were collected, L-15:EBSS solution was replaced with 200µL pre-warmed papain solution consisting of ~ 2 mg/mL papain (Worthington Biochemical) in 3:1 mixture of L-15:EBSS spiked with DNAse I and RNAse Inhibitor and incubated for 20 min in an Enviro-Genie hybridization oven (Scientific Industries Inc) set to 28 °C. Retinas were gently triturated 20 times with a fire-polished glass pipette. Samples then underwent four cycles of a 10 min incubation at 28 °C for 10 min followed by gentle trituration 10 times. To end papain digestion, 400µL 0.1% BSA (Fisher Scientific, prepared in 3:1 L-15:EBSS) was added and then mixed by gentle trituration. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C at 250xg for 5 min to pellet cells. The overlying supernatant was removed and then cells were resuspended in 400µL 0.1% BSA. Cells were pelleted again with another round of centrifugation at 4 °C at 250 xg for 5 min and then resuspended in 100µL 0.1% BSA. Cells were counted and assessed for viability using trypan blue and counted on a hemacytometer. Viability (93.75–96.5%) and total cell counts for all samples are shown in Table 1. Once the total number of cells per sample was determined, cell suspensions were pelleted via centrifugation at 4 °C at 250 xg for 5 min. Finally, cells were resuspended with an appropriate volume of 0.1% BSA to bring the sample to 1000 cell/µL and mixed well by gentle pipetting. Samples were transferred to 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes and placed on ice for transportation to Washington State University.

## cDNA library preparation, 10X genomics, and sequencing
The single cell samples were loaded onto the 10X Genomics fluidics platform at Washington State University Gene Editing Reagent Core (WSU; Pullman, WA, USA). Single cell cDNA libraries were prepared using Chromium iX system and a GEM-X Single Cell 3’ v4 kit (10X Genomics) following manufacturer instructions. Briefly, GEM-X kit reagents, cell suspensions, and gel beads were loaded onto the Chromium iX microfluidic chip for partitioning into Gel Beads-in Emulsion (GEMs) so that a single cell would be contained in each microdroplet alongside the barcode containing gel bead. The cDNA was generated and barcoded within the GEM so that the transcripts from each cell had a unique identifier. Subsequently the cDNAs were processed in bulk for library preparation including fragmentation, amplification, and addition of adaptors for sequencing. cDNA and libraries were subjected to quality control using Fragment Bioanalyzer (Supplemental File 1). Libraries were transferred to WSU Molecular Biology and Genomics Core for sequencing using Illumina Next Seq 2000.

Output files were downloaded for processing and analysis in the R coding environment using the library package Seurat V5.0.074. The dataset used for bioinformatic analysis was generated by loading each sample and merging them together with treatment coded into the metadata. Doublets, low quality cells, and empty microdroplets were filtered out by removing samples with more than 2500 and less than 200 gene reads. Cells with high mitochondrial gene content (more than 5% of genes per cell) were also removed from the main dataset using subsetting. The subsetted dataset (after removing doublets, low quality reads, empty droplets, and filtering mitochondrial genes) consisted of 9,321 cells for DMSO, 6,596 cells for MTZ 96 HPT, and 10,003 cells for MTZ 120 HPT samples. The dataset was then normalized, and highly variable genes were determined using standard Seurat workflow. Then, the dataset was scaled and run through principal component analysis (PCA). To uncover common clusters across treatments, the dataset was integrated using Seurat’s CCAIntegration method. After rejoining the layers, clusters were determined and plotted using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projections (UMAPs). Dimensionality of the dataset was determined qualitatively and quantitatively, which revealed 22 unique clusters. Clusters were identified using established biomarkers known to be expressed in major retinal cell types (Supplemental File 2). Once cluster identity was added to the metadata, the unintegrated data was used for all subsequent analyses.
Following pre-processing, bioinformatic analysis and graphing were completed in Seurat. Clusters of interest were subset and differentially expressed genes between treatments and timepoints were assessed using FindMarkers, with statistical significance determined using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Plots were generated using common Seurat graphing commands, including VlnPlot (violin plots), DimPlot (UMAP plots), DoPlot (heatmaps), and FeaturePlot (gene expression on UMAP). We acknowledge that the lack of replicates for our single cell samples is a caveat of our dataset, hence we largely focused on describing changes that we could validate or investigate using additional methods and approaches (imaging, RT-qPCR).

## RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
RNA was isolated from whole retinas from Mtz and DMSO treated zebrafish. Retinas were transferred to RNA lysis buffer (Macherey-Nagel) and briefly homogenized with a handheld homogenizer (Bio-Gen Series PRO200, Pro Scientific). Retinal homogenate was stored at -80 °C overnight until RNA could be extracted using NucleoSpin® RNA kit (Macherey-Nagel), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Following extraction, quality and quantity of RNA was assessed using a NanoDrop™ One Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). RNA samples were stored at -80 °C until cDNA synthesis using SuperScript IV First-Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Invitrogen). Once synthesized, cDNA was stored at -20 °C. Primers used in qPCR for selected genes of interest are shown in Table 3. The specificity of primers was confirmed using NCBI Primer Blast against the zebrafish genome (GRCz11). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) reactions were prepared using PowerTrack SYBR-Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with 5 ng cDNA template per reaction and each sample run in triplicate. Nuclease-free water was used in place of cDNA for no-template controls. Reactions were amplified and quantified on QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher) over 40 amplification cycles. Genes of interest were normalized against expression of calibrator genes, 18s and elf1a, and fold change was calculated using the ΔΔCT method and the geometric mean of 18s and elf1a.

## Preparation of retinal tissue
Eyes were enucleated from euthanized zebrafish and lenses removed following corneal puncture and incision. One eye was prepared for retinal cryosections, and the other was further dissected to remove whole retinal tissue. For retinal sections, eyes were fixed in phosphate buffered, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) containing 5% sucrose at 4 °C overnight with constant rocking. Eyes were then washed in a graded series of 5% to 20% sucrose in phosphate buffered (pH 7.4) solution. The following day, eyes were transferred to a 1:2 solution of OCT embedding medium (Sakura Finetek) and 20% sucrose phosphate solution for 30 min at room temperature then frozen by embedding in OCT:20% sucrose phosphate upon immersion in 2-methylbutane supercooled on liquid nitrogen. Tissue blocks were stored at -20 °C before sectioning. Eyes were sectioned into 10 micron slices using a Leica CM3050 cryostat and mounted onto poly-L lysine coated glass slides (FisherBrand Superfrost Plus). Tissue sections were desiccated overnight then stored at -20 °C.
For whole retinal preparations, the other eye was further dissected in PBS to remove intact retinal cups and RPE was removed with a soft paint brush and/or fine forceps. Whole retinas were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS at 4 °C overnight with constant rocking, then washed several times in PBS containing 0.01% Triton-X-100, then dehydrated in a graded series of methanol solutions prior to storing in 100% methanol at -20 °C.

## Staining of retinal tissue
Prior to staining, retinal cryosections were thawed in a humidified chamber at room temperature for at least 10 min then blocking solution (1% normal donkey serum (NDS), 0.1% azide, 0.1% Triton-X-100 in PBS). Primary antibody was prepared in antibody dilution buffer (1% NDS, 0.1% sodium azide, 0.1% TritonX-100 in PBS) and added to slides for incubation overnight at 4 °C. The next day, slides were washed in PBS+ 0.1% Triton-X-100 (PBSTr) at room temperature for 30 min. Secondary antibodies (fluorophore conjugated) were prepared in antibody dilution buffer including DAPI and slides were incubated in secondary antibody solution at room temperature for at least 1 h. Slides were washed in PBSTr and rinsed in PBS, then mounted with Vectashield Vibrance with coverslips. For PCNA staining, antigen retrieval was performed prior to blocking step as described in75. For TUNEL staining, slides were subjected to TUNEL procedure following manufacturer’s instructions (Roche 1168795910) then rinsed in PBS several times prior to blocking/primary antibody steps.
Whole retinas were rehydrated in a series of PBSTr washes at room temperature, then blocked with blocking solution for one hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies were prepared in antibody dilution buffer and incubated with retinas overnight at 4 °C with rocking. Retinas were washed several times in PBSTr then stained with secondary antibodies and DAPI in antibody dilution buffer at room temperature for at least 2 h with rocking. Retinas were washed several times in PBSTr then rinsed in PBS. Radial incisions were made to allow for flat mounting of the retinas on glass slides, which were prepared in Vectashield Vibrance and cover slipped.
Primary antibodies and dilutions were as follows: mouse 4C4 antibody (1:100, used as hybridoma supernatant from clone 7.4.C4 sourced from Sigma-Aldrich 92092321), rabbit anti-proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA; 1:200, Proteintech 10205), mouse anti-Glutamine Synthetase (GS; 1:200, BD Biosciences 610517), rabbit anti-mCherry (1:500, Abcam Ab167453). Secondary antibodies were used at 1:200 dilution and included donkey anti-mouse Alexa-Fluor488 (Jackson ImmunoResearch), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa-Fluor647 (Jackson ImmunoResearch), and goat anti-mouse Cy7 (AAT Bioquest).

## In situ hybridization chain reaction (HCR)
Probe sets and hairpins to detect mRNAs specific to pfn1 (NM_001305588, Lot #RTD400) and apoc1 (NM_001291886.1, Lot #PRE867) were purchased from Molecular Instruments76. Probe sets were designed by Molecular Instruments; the apoc1 probe set matched that published in49. Whole retinas from Mtz and DMSO treated zebrafish were fixed overnight at 4 °C in freshly prepared phosphate-buffered 4% PFA. Retinas were washed in phosphate buffer and dehydrated with 100% methanol. Samples were stored at -20 °C in 100% methanol until the HCR procedure, which followed protocols developed by Molecular Instruments. To rehydrate retinas, samples underwent a series of graded methanol/phosphate buffered-0.1% Tween (PBSTw) washes. Rehydrated retinas were subjected to Proteinase K digestion (10 µg/mL) for 10 min at 37 °C, post-fixed again in fresh 4% PFA in phosphate-buffered saline at room temperature for 20 min and washed again with PBSTw. Pre-hybridization (30 min) and hybridization with probe sets (overnight) was performed in a hybridization oven at 37 °C. Probe sets were prepared for hybridization in a single master mix by adding 2 pmol of each probe set to 500 ul of probe hybridization buffer. The following day, retinas were washed with probe wash buffer (Molecular Instruments) at 37 °C, then 5X SSC containing 0.1% Tween20 (SSCT) at room temperature, then transferred to amplification buffer. Hairpin mixtures were prepared using 30 pmol of each hairpin. Hairpins were heated to 95 °C for 90 s in a thermocycler and snap cooled in the dark at room temperature. The snap cooled hairpins were added to room temperature amplification buffer and retinas were incubated overnight at room temperature protected from light. Samples were washed at room temperature with 5X SSCT, with the final wash containing DAPI (1:1000). Retinas were flat mounted onto glass slides in glycerol, cover-slipped, and imaged within 24 h using confocal microscopy.

## Confocal microscopy, image viewing, and image analysis
All imaging was performed on a Nikon Crest Optics X-Light spinning disc confocal microscope running Nikon Elements software, using 20X dry objective (Plan Apo λ 20X Air 0.75 NA DIC), in some cases using 1.5X magnification boost, and in some cases using image stitching to obtain images of entire retinal cryosections or images of whole flattened retinas. Z stacks (optical sections) were collected at 2 micron step sizes. Additional images of selected regions of retinas for HCR in situs were collected with a 40X water immersion objective (Apo LWD λS DIC N2 1.15 NA) and a 0.5 micron step size. Images were viewed and analyzed using ImageJ (FIJI) or Nikon Elements software. For retinal cryosections, 3–6 sections per eye were imaged (representing technical replicates) from which the average of technical replicates was determined for each sample that was quantified/counted. Counting of cells/features from retinal cryosections was performed manually in both central and peripheral retinal regions and normalized to curvilinear distance. Counting of microglia in flat-mounted whole retinas was performed in a 200 × 200µm2 square region of interest in both central (500–1000 μm radial distance from the optic nerve head) and peripheral regions (more than 1000 μm from optic nerve head). Counting was performed in eight regions per retina (4 central, 4 peripheral). Analysis of microglial gene expression by HCR-FISH in whole, flat mounted retina was performed manually in three randomly selected 333 × 333µm2 regions of interest per retina. A cell was considered “positive” for apoc1 expression if probe signal was visible in the image for a cell that also had visible signal from pfn1 probe. Retinal layers were determined by DAPI signal. Given the optical section spacing (z-step) increment for the HCR-FISH images, and instances of microglia spanning between layers, the IPL and INL regions were combined for this analysis.

## Statistics
Graphing and statistical analysis for cell counts and qPCR data was completed using the R coding environment. Statistical analysis of qPCR data used a Kruskal-Wallis test with Conover post-hoc, with statistical significance cut-off set as p < 0.01. For count data with multiple timepoints, main effects of treatment and timepoint, as well as interactions between these factors were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by (if warranted) Tukey’s post-hoc, with significance cut-off set as p < 0.05. For pairwise comparisons at single timepoints, Mann-Whitney U test was applied with significance cut-off set as p < 0.05.
