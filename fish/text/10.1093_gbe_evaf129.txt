Evolution of the Nonvisual and Visual Opsin Gene Repertoire in Ray-Finned Fishes

# Evolution of the Nonvisual and Visual Opsin Gene Repertoire in Ray-Finned Fishes

## Abstract
AbstractPhotoreception—the detection of light for image formation (vision) as well as for nonimage-forming purposes (circadian regulation and DNA repair)—is critical to the survival of most animals. In vertebrates, photoreception is mediated by opsin proteins, which are classified, according to their function, into visual and nonvisual opsins. Here, we provide the most comprehensive study to date on the evolution of the opsin gene family in the largest class of vertebrates, actinopterygians, with a particular focus on the understudied nonvisual opsins. Based on an in-depth analysis of 535 high-quality genomes, we document great variation in gene numbers in the different opsin gene subfamilies across ray-finned fishes and show that visual opsins are more prone to duplications and losses than nonvisual opsins. We provide evidence that visual and nonvisual opsins coevolve in ray-finned fishes, both in terms of copy numbers and selective pressures acting on their coding sequences, probably in response to the different photic environments they inhabit. Species that live in dim light or in the dark (such as in caves or the deep sea) had reduced visual and nonvisual opsin gene repertoires, while polar species feature accelerated evolution in both. Fishes that rely on electroreception show a slight reduction in the number of visual and nonvisual opsin genes and accelerated evolution of the remaining genes. We further found that genes of the phototransduction cascade coevolve with opsins. Finally, the finding that nonvisual opsins are mainly expressed in the testes and ovaries (after the eyes) supports a function in gamete biology.Keywords: photoreception, Actinopterygii, gene family evolution, vision
Opsins are light-sensitive proteins essential for photoreception, enabling animals to sense and perceive their environment. While visual opsins mediate vision, nonvisual opsins regulate the circadian clock and DNA repair systems. This study examines the diversity and evolution of visual and nonvisual opsins in 535 species of ray-finned fishes, which constitute the largest class of vertebrates and are adapted to a wide range of photic conditions. We show that opsin evolution was highly dynamic across fishes and that they coevolve with genes from downstream pathways. We further document repeated losses of opsins in species living in dark or reduced light conditions. Nonvisual opsins are highly expressed in gonadal tissues, supporting the view that light sensing is important for reproduction in fishes.

## Introduction
The sensory systems of animals play fundamental roles in vital tasks such as orienting themselves in space and time, tracking down food, avoiding predators, finding shelter, and selecting mating partners (Dall et al. 2005; Osorio and Vorobyev 2008; Huijbers et al. 2012; Musilova, Salzburger, and Cortesi 2021). Animal sensory systems, and the genes underlying them, are thus under strong Darwinian selection (Osorio and Vorobyev 2008; Baldwin et al. 2014; Niimura, Matsui, and Touhara 2014). One essential sensory system, especially in vertebrates, is photoreception, ie the detection of photons for image-forming (visual) and nonimage-forming (nonvisual) purposes (Andrabi et al. 2023). Among the core elements of photoreception are opsin genes, which encode for G protein-coupled receptors (Shichida and Matsuyama 2009) and are classified, according to whether or not they are involved in image formation, into visual and nonvisual opsins (Shichida and Matsuyama 2009).
The function and evolution of visual opsin genes have been well characterized in many vertebrate clades (Musser and Arendt 2017; Musilova, Salzburger, and Cortesi 2021; Baden 2024; Schott et al. 2024). Most vertebrates possess two types of visual opsins, rhodopsin(s) (rh1) that mediate scotopic (dim light) vision and cone opsins that are responsible for photopic (bright-light) and color vision (Lamb, Collin, and Pugh 2007). The vertebrate ancestor most probably possessed a single rh1 gene and members of four cone opsin gene subfamilies, each permitting the detection of a specific waveband of the visible light spectrum: the short wavelength sensitive opsins sws1 and sws2, the mid-wavelength sensitive rh2, and the long wavelength sensitive lws (Lamb, Collin, and Pugh 2007). Vision is initiated by a photon-induced conformational change of a visual opsin protein covalently bound to a vitamin-A derived chromophore, forming the photopigment. This activates transducins, which in turn activate phosphodiesterases that degrade cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), ultimately leading to the closure of cGMP-gated ion channels and a hyperpolarization of the photoreceptor cell (Larhammar, Nordström, and Larsson 2009). Other genes required for vision include regulators of this cascade (such as arrestins and guanylyl cyclases), guanylyl cyclase-activating proteins, and retinoid isomerohydrolases (Redmond et al. 1998; Larhammar, Nordström, and Larsson 2009), but also crystallins, which are necessary for the proper transparency and refractive index of the lens (Delaye and Tardieu 1983; Kappé et al. 2010).
Much less is known about the evolution of nonvisual opsin genes and the associated phototransduction cascade in vertebrates. This is surprising, given that nonimage-forming photoreception initiates several critical biological processes, including circadian clock photoentrainment (Cavallari et al. 2011), DNA repair activation (Hirayama et al. 2009 ; Zhao et al. 2018), neuronal development and activity (Friedmann et al. 2015), and seasonality, eg in reproduction (Nakane et al. 2010). It is most likely because of these diverse functions that the nonvisual opsin gene repertoire is much larger than that of visual opsin genes (Davies et al. 2015; Andrabi et al. 2023), with more than twenty subfamilies of nonvisual opsins known to date (Davies et al. 2015; Beaudry et al. 2017), including pinopsin (Sato et al. 2018), melanopsins (opn4) (Davies et al. 2011), teleost multiple tissue opsins (tmt) (Moutsaki et al. 2003), retinal G protein receptors (rgr) (Jiang, Pandey, and Fong 1993), and neuropsin (opn5) (Tarttelin et al. 2003).
The most species-rich class of vertebrates, ray-finned fishes (actinopterygians), are particularly interesting in the context of opsin evolution, as members of this group experience a diverse range of photic environments (Musilova, Salzburger, and Cortesi 2021), depending on water depth and turbidity, latitude, and diel activity patterns, among others (Warrant and Locket 2004; Warrant and Johnsen 2013; Tran et al. 2016; Cortesi et al. 2020; Ortega et al. 2020; Perrigault et al. 2020; Fogg et al. 2022; Castiglione et al. 2023; Malard, Griebler, and Rétaux 2023). Accordingly, actinopterygians feature substantial interspecific variation in the extent and composition of their visual opsin gene repertoire (Cortesi et al. 2015; Hagen, Roberts, and Johnston 2023). This is partially due to differences in gene retention after the teleost-specific whole-genome duplication (WGD) (Beaudry et al. 2017; Lin et al. 2017) and more recent genome duplications in certain groups (Glasauer and Neuhauss 2014). However, the dynamics of nonvisual opsin evolution in fishes remains elusive.
Here, we provide the most comprehensive examination to date of opsin gene evolution in ray-finned fishes, focusing on the understudied nonvisual opsin genes. By analyzing 535 high-quality fish genomes, we characterize their opsin gene repertoire and show that visual and nonvisual opsins coevolve in this group, driven mainly by the prevailing light environment. The evolution of other genes involved in photoreception or circadian clock regulation is tightly linked to the opsin gene repertoire. We also analyzed gene expression patterns in 75 species, and found that, next to the eye, nonvisual opsins are highly expressed in gonadal tissues, suggesting that—just like in jellyfishes, mice and humans (Suga, Schmid, and Gehring 2008; Pérez-Cerezales et al. 2015; Quiroga Artigas et al. 2018)—they also function in gamete release, maturation and guidance in fish.

## Results and Discussion

## Characterization of the Opsin Gene Repertoire in Ray-Finned Fishes
We first characterized the entire opsin gene repertoire in 535 high-quality genome assemblies representing the taxonomic and ecological diversity of ray-finned fishes (Figs. 1 and S1 to S3). To this end, we applied a standardized gene mining procedure to investigate the five subfamilies of visual opsins and 22 subfamilies of nonvisual opsins. Our procedure yielded reliable estimates of the number of opsin genes and reliable sequence information (Materials and Methods; Fig. S4). In addition, we found that the sequencing method and the genome assembly quality did not have a significant impact on the number of opsins retrieved per genome (Materials and Methods; Figs. S5 and S6).

A mean number of 25.1 nonvisual opsin genes (between 7 and 60) was retrieved per actinopterygian genome (Figs. S2 and S3). Tetraploid fishes, which experienced an additional round of WGD after the teleost specific WGD, featured about twice as many nonvisual opsins as diploid species (mean of 47 and 24 genes, respectively, Fig. S7), with up to 60 genes found in the goldfish (Carassius auratus). Hence, tetraploid fishes represent the vertebrates with the by far most extensive repertoires of nonvisual opsin genes known to date (Beaudry et al. 2017). Among diploid species, the migratory hilsa herring (Tenualosa ilisha) had the highest number of nonvisual opsins (38 genes) (Figs. S2 and S3; Data S1). The smallest nonvisual opsin gene repertoires were found in the abyssal Mariana snailfish (Pseudoliparis swirei) with seven genes, followed by two mormyrids (Mormyrops zanclirostris and Stomatorhinus walkeri) with eight genes each (Figs. S2 and S3; Data S1).
In agreement with previous results (Musilova et al. 2019), we found a mean number of 7.3 visual opsin genes (0 to 19) per species. The largest visual opsin gene repertoire in our set of species was found in T. ilisha (19 genes), followed by the Indian oil sardine (Sardinella longiceps; 18 genes) (Figs. S2 and S3; Data S1). However, the teleost species with the greatest number of visual opsins observed to date (Diretmus argenteus) (Musilova et al. 2019) was not included in this study due to poor genome assembly quality. The species with the fewest visual opsins were the Mexican blind catfish (Prietella phreatophila), which lives in constant darkness and entirely lost its visual opsins, two abyssal snailfish species (Pseudoliparis sp. Yap Trench: one gene; P. swirei: two genes), and two deep-sea eelpouts (Melanostigma gelatinosum and Ophthalmolycus amberensis) with two genes each. Interestingly, P. phreatophila is the only cave species found so far with such a complete loss of visual opsins, suggesting that this species colonized caves longer ago than other blind fishes that have their genomes available (Yang et al. 2016; Aardema, Stiassny, and Alter 2020; Policarpo et al. 2021).
At the order level, we found that tarpons (Elopiformes), and herrings and anchovies (Clupeiformes), many of which are migratory and experience diverse photic conditions over their lifespans (Bloom, Burns, and Schriever 2018), had the largest nonvisual opsin repertoires (mean = 34 and 33.5, respectively, Figs. 1 and S2). Clupeiformes also had the largest visual opsin repertoire (mean = 12.8) (Figs. 1 and S2; Data S1). On the other hand, knifefishes (Gymnotiformes), which rely on electric communication, are primarily nocturnal and live in turbid waters (Bray, Alshami, and Kudoh 2022), had the lowest number of nonvisual (mean = 9.8) and visual opsin genes (mean = 3.3), lacking entirely representatives of nine opsin subfamilies (Figs. 1 and S2). We also detected a complete loss of 13 opsin subfamilies in Batrachoidiformes and 7 in Ophidiiformes (Figs. 1 and S8). However, these were only represented by a single species each in our data set.
The number of putative pseudogenes also varied substantially between species and correlated negatively with the number of intact genes (Fig. S9). Species that experienced recent WGDs had more putative pseudogenes than diploid ones (Fig. S9), likely due to the nonfunctionalization of redundant ohnologs (Lynch and Conery 2000; Lynch and Force 2000). The highest number of putative opsin pseudogenes was detected in 2 tetraploid and blind cave cyprinids (Sinocyclocheilus anophthalmus and S. anshuiensis with 29 and 23 putative pseudogenes, respectively). Many putative ospin pseudogenes were also found in 2 diploid deep-sea species (P. sp. Yap Trench: 25; Dissostichus mawsoni: 21).
Overall, our results show that the nonvisual and visual opsin gene repertoires of ray-finned fishes are shaped by recurrent gene duplications and losses and are much more diverse than previously assumed (Beaudry et al. 2017; Musilova, Salzburger, and Cortesi 2021).

## Pinopsin Was Present in the Teleosts' Most Recent Common Ancestor
It was assumed that pinopsin, mainly expressed in the pineal gland of nonteleost fishes, was lost before the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of teleosts (Sato et al. 2018; Fujiyabu et al. 2019). However, our opsin gene mining efforts revealed a complete pinopsin gene in Megalops cyprinoides, evolving under negative selection, with a low ω (=dN/dS) (Fig. S10), similar to the pinopsin of nonteleost species (ω = 0.057) (Fig. S10). It is highly unlikely that this pinopsin originated from lateral transfer or from contamination, as the phylogeny of pinopsins is congruent with the species phylogeny, and the gene in M. cyprinoides was located next to the same genes as its ortholog in Lepisosteus oculatus, ie, the gene synteny was maintained (Fig. S10). Using our opsin genes mining pipeline, we also found putative pinopsin pseudogenes in M. atlanticus and in the Anguilliformes species of the genus Ilyophis. While the pinopsin in Ilyophis spp. was highly degenerated, with only two remaining exons (out of five) and two premature stop codons, the pinopsin gene of M. atlanticus seems to have been lost recently, with only one frameshift mutation detected (Fig. S10). Thus, the evolutionary history of pinopsin seems much more intricate than previously thought, with at least three independent losses: (i) before the MRCA of Clupeocephalans, (ii) before the MRCA of Osteoglossiformes, and (iii) before the MRCA of Albuliformes, Notacanthiformes, and Anguilliformes (Figs. 1 and S8). Further investigation of pinopsin expression in M. cyprinoides will provide insights into the timing of exorh's replacement of pinopsin as the primary pineal opsin in teleosts, which was previously assumed to have occurred before the teleost radiation due to its presumed absence in nonteleost species (Sato et al. 2018).

## Dynamics of Opsin Gene Evolution in Actinopterygians
Next, to reconstruct the evolutionary history of each opsin gene subfamily in ray-finned fishes, we performed gene tree–species tree reconciliations, using TreeRecs (Comte et al. 2020) and NOTUNG (Chen, Durand, and Farach-Colton 2000). The most dynamic subfamilies among nonvisual opsins were va (birth rate = 0.0018 and death rate = 0.0064 per gene per million years), opn4m1/3 (0.0017/0.008), and opn3 (0.0017/0.006). In contrast, the least dynamic subfamilies were pinopsin (0.0004/0.002), opn8c (0.001/0.005), and opn8a (0.001/0.006) (Fig. 2a). The evolutionary rates (taking dN/dS as a proxy) of visual opsins were significantly higher than those of nonvisual opsins (Wilcoxon test P-values = 1.7e−4 and 5e−5 for birth and death rates respectively, Fig. S11), with, among visual opsins, rh2 having the highest rates (0.0031/0.01), followed by lws (0.003/0.0095) and sws1 (0.0023/0.008) (Fig. 2a). This suggests stronger selective pressures to modulate the gene repertoire involved in image-forming compared with nonimage-forming photoreception.

The names of the three subfamilies with the highest and lowest birth rates are indicated. b) Distribution of dN/dS (ω) per opsin gene subfamily. c) Correlation between the mean ω of nonvisual opsins and the mean ω of visual opsins. pGLS R2, P-value, and λ, as well as regression lines considering all ray-finned fishes (gray) or only diploid teleosts (black) are shown. Only species with dN/dS computed for at least three nonvisual and three visual opsins were retained (Materials and Methods; Data S1). N reports the number of genomes used.
We then computed dN/dS (ω) for every opsin gene in each subfamily and found significant differences between subfamilies (Figs. 2b and S12). The lowest mean ω was found for pinopsin, exorh, and va (0.09, 0.1, and 0.11, respectively), and the highest mean ω was detected for opn8a, opn8b, and opn8c (0.297, 0.303, and 0.33, respectively) (Fig. 2b). No differences were found in the distribution of ω between nonvisual and visual opsins (Wilcoxon test, P-value = 0.3; Fig. S13). We found no evidence of an association between birth and death rates and differences in the mean or median ω across opsin subfamilies (Pearson's P-values >0.05; Fig. S14). However, we observed that episodes of positive selection typically follow opsin gene duplications, as branches resulting from duplication events had significantly higher (BUSTED P-value < 0.05) ω-values than branches resulting from speciation events (except for pinopsin and opn9, both with BUSTED P-values of 0.5; Fig. S15). This is a strong indication of functional diversification in the aftermath of duplications in opsin genes, for example, by changing spectral sensitivity or thermal stability (Castiglione et al. 2023; Hagen, Roberts, and Johnston 2023).
Most species had a mean ω-value for their opsin genes of around 0.2 (mean ± standard deviaion [SD]: 0.19 ± 0.1; Fig. S16). Again, tetraploid species had higher ω-values than diploids (mean ± SD: 0.25 ± 0.14), likely because many of their opsins either evolve neutrally (pseudogenes with no apparent loss-of-function mutations yet) or are subject to neo- or subfunctionalization (Lynch and Conery 2000). The highest mean ω (0.8) was observed in the tetraploid and eyeless cave cyprinid S. anophthalmus (Fig. S16). Accelerated opsin sequence evolution was also detected in the weakly electric fish Mormyrus iriodes (mean ω = 0.78) and in two deep-sea fishes, the scaly rockcod (Trematomus loennbergii; mean ω = 0.56) and the eelpout (O. amberensis; mean ω = 0.57) (Fig. S16). Importantly, using phylogenetic generalized least squares (pGLS), allowing to take into account the shared evolutionary history of species, we observed a modest positive correlation between the mean ω of nonvisual opsins and the mean ω of visual opsins (pGLS: R2 = 0.24; P-value < 2.2e−16, Fig. 2c). These findings suggest that the coding sequences of nonvisual and visual opsins evolve, at least in part, in concert, likely under shared selective pressures from the photic environment. However, the selective pressure acting on opsins is also likely influenced by additional ecological factors, such as prey or mate detection.
Our analyses also revealed a modest positive correlation between the total number of nonvisual and visual opsin genes in actinopterygian genomes (pGLS: R2 = 0.23; P-value < 2.2e−16), as well as between the number of genes in many of the opsin gene subfamilies (Fig. S17), irrespective of whether all species or only diploid species were considered (Fig. S17). Thus, this supports that nonvisual opsins and visual opsins coevolve at the macroevolutionary scale of ray-finned fishes concerning both sequence evolution (see above) and copy number. This pattern of coevolution among gene families involved in light perception is similar to our previous findings on chemoreception-related gene families, where three olfactory receptor families (OR, V2R, and TAAR) coevolve, not only in fishes but also in tetrapods (Policarpo et al. 2022, 2024). Contrary to the vast opsin repertoire diversity we found for actinopterygians, opsin gene numbers seem much lower and stable across tetrapods (Beaudry et al. 2017; Baldwin and Ko 2020; Upton et al. 2021), and it is likely, although to be investigated, that this coevolution between nonvisual and visual photoreception does not hold true in this vertebrate clade.

## Coevolution of Opsins and Other Genes
Similar to strategies that have recently been successfully applied in other contexts, eg, to build functional gene networks in the baker's yeast (Steenwyk et al. 2022; Little, Chikina, and Clark 2024) or to identify new homologous recombination repair genes across eukaryotes (Sherill-Rofe et al. 2019), we tested for coevolutionary signatures between opsins and other genes. We took advantage of 155 annotated fish genomes (RefSeq and GenBank annotation) to classify genes in orthogroups (OGs), representing group of genes derived from a single origin in the MRCA of ray-finned fishes (see Materials and Methods). We then we inspected, through pGLS analysis, if the opsin gene numbers and mean ω-values correlated with those of OGs (Figs. 3 and S18). We found that the number of nonvisual opsins was positively correlated with gene numbers in 48 OGs, and negatively with 115 OGs (Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05; Fig. 3a). By performing 10,000 simulations, we observed that, among positively correlated OGs, there were much were much more light-perception and circadian clock OGs than expected at random (probability of 0 and 0.03 respectively; Fig. S19; see Materials and Methods), including phosphodiesterases (pde6g), guanylate cyclases (gcap7), two γ-crystallin gene families (one of which had the highest R2 value of all OGs), and the cryptochrome circadian regulators (cry) involved in the circadian clock feedback loop (Vatine et al. 2011). Functional enrichment analysis of these 48 OGs also revealed an overrepresentation of genes in 3 reactome (Milacic et al. 2024) pathways: neuronal system, transmission across chemical synapses, and postsynaptic signal transmission (Table S1). Seventy-one OGs were under stronger purifying selection in species with a larger nonvisual opsin gene repertoire (Fig. 3b). Among these were three genes known to be involved in vertebrate eye development: bicdl1, fzd5, and rp2 (Schlager et al. 2010; Shu et al. 2011; Jiang et al. 2021). These 71 OGs were enriched for 3 reactome pathways (transport and synthesis of PAPS [3'-phosphoadenosine-5'-phosphosulfate], Ca2+ pathway, and beta-catenin independent Wnt signaling) and 1 KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway (starch and sucrose metabolism) (Table S1).

The number of visual opsin genes correlated positively (Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05) with the number of copies in 11 OGs (Fig. S18A). The most significant OGs were as follows: (i) capn15, known to be involved in eye defects in humans and mice (Zha et al. 2020); (ii) crebl2, known to be involved in retinal neovascularization (Recchia et al. 2010); and (iii) zdhhc7, known to be expressed in the primate retina (Mungale et al. 2022) and implicated in human neurological disorders (Liao et al. 2024). Six OGs were found to have higher ω-values in species with more visual opsins (Fig. S18B), and four OGs had lower ω-values (Fig. S18B). Among those four, the OG oflm2 is known to be involved in vertebrate eye development (Sultana et al. 2011). We believe that this coevolutionary approach provides a powerful framework for identifying genes potentially involved in light perception.

## Ecomorphological Factors Associated with the Evolution of Opsin Genes
We then tested, using pGLS, the relation between ecomorphological factors and the number of opsin genes or their ω-values. First, we found that a species' standard length, diet, and absolute or relative eye diameter could not predict the number of opsin genes nor their ω-values (Fig. 4). In contrast, we observed that species capable of emitting electric discharges had fewer genes in seven different nonvisual opsin subfamilies (tmt3, opn8b, opn7b, opn6, rrh, opn4m1/3, and opn4x; pGLS R2 between 0.02 and 0.2, Data S1), leading to an overall slight but significant reduction in the number of nonvisual opsins (pGLS R2 = 0.024) and in the total number of opsins (R2 = 0.017) (Figs. 4a and S20). This is concordant with the reduced visual sense in such species (Crampton 2019) and likely the result of a sensory tradeoff, as these species primarily rely on electroreception and not on photon detection to perceive their environment. This is further supported by the anatomy of their brains, which exhibit enlarged cerebella at the expense of the optic tecta (Sukhum, Shen, and Carlson 2018). In addition, we observed small accelerations of evolution of three nonvisual opsins subfamilies (tmt3, opn7b, and opn5) in electric fish species (Figs. 4b and S21). Using RELAX, a method based on a branch-site model, we found that this shift in ω was due to relaxation of selection for opn5, which is involved in short wavelength-mediated photoentrainment of the vertebrate retina (Buhr et al. 2015; Sato et al. 2016), but increased positive selection for tmt3, likely to be also involved in photoentrainment (Frøland Steindal and Whitmore 2019) (Fig. S22). No significant shifts in ω were detected for opn7b with this method (Fig. S22).

Next, we found that catadromous fishes tended to have more rh2 genes than others (pGLS R2 = 0.08; Figs. 4a and S23A). At the same time, anadromous species showed the highest ω-value for this visual opsin subfamily, followed by catadromous species (pGLS R2 = 0.085; Figs. 4b and S23B). Diel activity patterns explained 6% of the variance in the number of opn4x genes (more copies in diurnal species; Figs. 4a and S24A; pGLS R2 = 0.064), in line with the role of opn4x in locomotor activity of Danio rerio during its wake state (Dekens et al. 2022). There was also 6% of rgr genes ω-ratio variance explained by diel activity patterns (pGLS R2 = 0.063; higher ω-values in crepuscular species; Figs. 4b and S24B).
Furthermore, we found a weak correlation between the climate zone (temperate, subtropical, tropical, boreal, polar, or deep water) and the number of opn8b genes (pGLS R2 = 0.07; Figs. 4a and S25). The water salinity (fresh, brackish, salt, or combinations thereof) was slightly correlated to the number of genes in one nonvisual opsin subfamily, opn9 (pGLS R2 = 0.03), and two visual opsin subfamilies, rh2 and sws1 (pGLS R2 = 0.027 and 0.033 respectively, with freshwater fishes having fewer genes than others, Figs. 4a and S26). Climate zone could explain between 4% and 23% of the variance in ω-values for three nonvisual opsin subfamilies (va, opn8a, and opn9), as well as between 4% and 11.4% of the variance in ω-values for three visual opsin subfamilies (rh1, rh2, and sw2), whereby, in most of these cases, polar species followed by deep-water fishes had the highest ω-ratios (Figs. 4b and S27). When considering the entire opsin gene repertoire, polar species again stand out, with higher mean ω-values than other species (pGLS R2 = 0.05; Figs. 4b and S27). This signature of accelerated sequence evolution in the opsins of polar species probably reflects elevated pressures to adapt to the challenging conditions in polar waters, including variable light environments and extreme temperatures (Williams, Barnes, and Buck 2015; Castiglione et al. 2023).
The strongest predictor of the number of nonvisual opsins, associated with 11 subfamilies (Figs. 4a and S28), was the water depth at which a species lives (pGLS R2 between 0.03 and 0.07). For all these subfamilies, except for va, bathypelagic (living below 1,000 meters) species had the lowest number of genes, followed by mesopelagic species (300 to 1,000 meters) (Fig. S28). We also found that depth had a weak association with the mean ω-values of the exorh and opn8b subfamilies (pGLS R2 = 0.05 and 0.09, respectively), with rariphotic (150 to 300 m) and mesopelagic species having slightly higher ω-ratios than others (Figs. 4b and S29). Bathypelagic and mesopelagic species also had slightly higher ω-ratios for rh2 (pGLS R2 = 0.07). Similarly, when considering the mean ω of nonvisual opsin genes or the entire opsin repertoire, bathypelagic and mesopelagic species had higher values than others (pGLS R2 = 0.065 and 0.072 respectively; Fig. S29).

## Opsin Gene Expression Across Ray-Finned Fishes
Finally, we investigated the gene expression patterns of nonvisual and visual opsin genes across tissues and species. We analyzed 582 publicly available transcriptomes representing up to 14 tissues from 75 ray-finned fish species (Fig. S30). As expected, visual opsins were primarily expressed in the eye (mean relative expression [RE] in the eye: 3.95% [95% confidence interval {CI}: 2.6 to 5.3]; RE in other tissues usually <0.01%; Fig. 5). The RE of nonvisual opsin genes was also highest in the eye (mean = 0.032; 95% CI: 0.023 to 0.041), followed by the ovary (mean of 0.012%, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.014), testis (mean of 0.011%, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.012), and the brain (mean of 0.01%, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.011) (Fig. 5).The high RE of nonvisual opsins in testis and ovaries supports recent evidence for their roles in gamete biology, including sperm thermotaxis (Pérez-Cerezales et al. 2015) and the light-controlled maturation and release of gametes (Suga, Schmid, and Gehring 2008; Quiroga Artigas et al. 2018). The lowest RE of nonvisual opsins was found in the liver (Fig. 5).
Distribution of the RE of visual and nonvisual opsins across different tissues in ray-finned fishes.
To further investigate the RE of opsin gene subfamilies per tissue, we retained 65 samples, which had at least 1,000 reads mapped to opsins (if most opsins are expressed, this would represent ∼30 reads per opsin on average), and we restricted our analysis to 6 tissues with at least four samples per tissue: brain, eye, heart, liver, ovary, and testis (Figs. 6 and S31). In all species investigated, and similar to previous findings (Musilova, Salzburger, and Cortesi 2021; Ricci et al. 2023), rh1 represented the vast majority of visual opsin expression in the eye, ranging from 74.1% in Chaenogobius annularis to 99.8% in Electrophorus electricus (mean = 93.4%) (Fig. 6). Importantly, the RE of rh1 was not correlated with the number of rh1 copies in the genome (Pearson's R = 0.128; P-value = 0.55; Fig. S32). Among the cone opsins, expression was dominated by rh2 and lws, with a mean cone opsin RE across species of 41.6% and 43.9%, respectively. The expression of sws2 and sws1 was usually much lower, with a mean cone opsin RE of 14.7% and 3.7%, respectively (Fig. 6) (Musilova, Salzburger, and Cortesi 2021; Musilova and Cortesi 2023; Ricci et al. 2023). These proportions align well with the 1:2:2 ratio of single to double cones found in many teleost fishes (Carleton et al. 2020; Lupše et al. 2022). Contrary to rh1, the RE of cone opsins was correlated with their copy numbers (note that D. rerio had an exceptionally high RE of sws1; Fig. S32).
Relative opsins expression levels in the eye. RE of (i) visual and nonvisual opsins, relative to the entire opsin repertoire, (ii) rhodopsin and cone opsins relative to the whole visual opsin gene repertoire, (iii) each cone opsin relative to the entire cone opsin repertoire, and (iv) each nonvisual opsin subfamily relative to the whole nonvisual opsin gene repertoire. The RE of nonvisual opsins for the other tissues investigated (brain, heart, ovary, testis, and liver) can be retrieved in Fig. S31.
The expression of nonvisual opsins in the eye was almost always dominated by the same 3 subfamilies, opn6, rgr, and opn4m2 (mean nonvisual opsin RE of 30.3%, 27.5%, and 13.9%, respectively). The high expression of rgr is not surprising, given that this gene is a retinal photoisomerase, which facilitates the regeneration of a chromophore used by visual opsins: the 11-cis-retinol (Morshedian et al. 2019; Morimoto, Nagata, and Inoue 2023). Similarly, opn6 has already been shown to be expressed in the photoreceptors of D. rerio, where it likely plays a role in their differentiation (Man et al. 2023), and the melanopsin opn4m2 is known to be expressed in the retina to regulate the circadian clock of fishes (Cavallari et al. 2011; Matos-Cruz et al. 2011). The other nonvisual opsin gene subfamilies had a mean RE of 5% or less (Figs. 6 and S31). There was no correlation between the RE and the gene copy number of nonvisual opsins (Table S2). Overall, there was greater interspecific variability in the nonvisual opsin RE in brain, ovary and testis than in the eye (Figs. S31 and S33). We also found that the diversity of expressed nonvisual opsins was highest in the brain and testis (mean Shannon's H of 2.18 and 1.96, respectively, Fig. S34), followed by the eye and the ovary (1.69 and 1.24, respectively, Fig. S34). In contrast, nonvisual opsin expression diversity was much lower in the liver and the heart (0.577 and 0.308, respectively, Fig. S34), where typically one subfamily dominated the RE (>90%; Fig. S31): opn3 in the heart of Alosa alosa and Coilia nasus, tmt1 in the heart of Caranx ignobilis and in the liver of Albula goreensis, opn9 in both organs of Amia calva, and parapinopsin in the liver of Coregonus clupeaformis. The two exceptions were the liver of Gasterosteus aculeatus, which had strong expression of both rgr and opn9 (58.5% and 38.8%, respectively), and the liver of Polyodon spathula, for which rrh represented around half of the opsin expression, while the other half was a mix of several other subfamilies (Fig. S31).

## Conclusion
The diversity of aquatic habitats and light conditions where ray-finned fishes live render this group an ideal system to scrutinize the evolution of photoreception. While previous studies mainly focused on visual opsins and used relatively small numbers of species, we thoroughly analyzed the evolution of both visual and nonvisual opsins based on 535 high-quality genome assemblies. We found modest evidence for coevolution between visual and nonvisual opsins, which is likely impacted by the photic environment in which fishes live, but also by other ecological factors. While we did not find strong associations between ecomorphological parameters and the number of opsins, or their evolutionary rate (using dN/dS as a proxy), we found that species with the smallest opsin repertoires inhabit dim light or dark environments, such as the deep sea or caves. Similarly, our results support a sensory tradeoff in electric fish species, commonly inhabiting turbid environments, with slightly fewer opsins than in other fishes. We also found that many opsin genes were evolving significantly more rapidly in polar species, although the differences in dN/dS values with other species were not very high. Furthermore, we took advantage of our dataset to investigate genes coevolving with the copy number and selective pressure of opsins. Many of these genes are related to the eye and to photoreception (eg pde6g, cryg, cry, capn15, oflm2, crebl2, zdhhc7, bicdl1, fzd5, and rp2). We hypothesize that other coevolving genes with yet unknown roles are also involved in light perception or related functions, both at the image- or nonimage-forming level, producing a suite of new candidates for future in-depth studies. Furthermore, we confirm that, in addition to being the primary organ for visual photoreception, the eye was also the organ with the highest nonvisual opsin expression (Davies et al. 2015). Finally, the high expression levels of nonvisual opsins in gametes compared to other tissues strongly support the hypothesis that nonvisual opsin genes play essential roles in the reproduction of ray-finned fishes.

## Materials and Methods

## Genome Data
We used genome_updater (https://github.com/pirovc/genome_updater) with the option -T “7898” to download 1,203 ray-finned fish genome assemblies available at the NCBI database as of 2023 May 30 and with the option -A 1 to retain the best assembly per species, according to four filters: (i) refseq category, (ii) assembly level, (iii) relation to type material, and (iv) release date. The completeness of each genome was then assessed using BUSCO v5.1.274 (Manni et al. 2021) with the Actinopterygii odb10 database, which includes 3,640 genes (Data S1). We retained 527 teleost assemblies with a BUSCO score ≥90% and 8 nonteleost assemblies with a BUSCO score ≥80%, for a total of 535 ray-finned fishes genome assemblies.

## Species Tree
We extracted the protein sequences of the 2,000 most represented BUSCO genes, and for each gene, protein sequences were aligned using MUSCLE v5.1 (Edgar 2022). Alignments were trimmed using trimAl v1.4 (Capella-Gutiérrez, Silla-Martínez, and Gabaldón 2009) with the option “-automated1.” IQ-TREE2 was then used to build a maximum likelihood (ML) tree for each gene, using the optimal model found by ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017), and the robustness of the nodes was assessed with 1,000 ultrafast bootstraps (Hoang et al. 2018). Low-supported nodes (<10% bootstrap support) were collapsed using the R package ape v5.0 (Paradis and Schliep 2019). We then used ASTRAL-III (Zhang et al. 2018) to build an unrooted species tree using this set of 2,000 unrooted gene trees. The unrooted species tree was rooted and dated with IQ-TREE2 and the least square dating method (To et al. 2016) using three calibration dates extracted on TimeTree.org (Kumar et al. 2017): (i) 250 My between D. rerio and M. atlanticus, (ii) 396 My between D. rerio and Polypterus senegalus, and (iii) 224 My between Denticeps clupeoides and G. aculeatus aculeatus. The order of each species was extracted from the NCBI taxonomy database (Schoch et al. 2020) using the R package “taxonomizr” (Sherrill-Mix 2023).

## Opsin Gene Mining Procedure
Known and curated D. rerio opsin coding sequences were retrieved using GenBank accessions and supplementary data from previous studies (Davies et al. 2015; Lin et al. 2017). Corresponding protein sequences were then used as queries in a blastp (Camacho et al. 2009), with an e-value of 1e−5, against all annotated proteins, retrieved on RefSeq, of ten other ray-finned fish genomes: P. senegalus, Poecilia reticulata, Sebastes umbrosus, L. oculatus, Anguilla anguilla, Takifugu rubripes, Hippocampus zosterae, Oreochromis niloticus, Gadus morhua, and G. aculeatus aculeatus. All protein sequences that matched to an opsin were then used as queries in a second blastp against the UniProt database (The UniProt Consortium 2021). All sequences that best matched against a known opsin gene present in the UniProt database were extracted and aligned using MUSCLE v5.1 (protein alignment), as well as with zebrafish opsins and with a GPCR gene closely related to vertebrates opsins, gpr52 (Gene ID: 101884270) (Fredriksson et al. 2003). The alignment was trimmed using trimAl, and a ML tree was built with IQ-TREE2 using the optimal model found by ModelFinder. The tree was manually rooted using the outgroup gene gpr52 in iTOL v5 (Letunic and Bork 2021). All protein sequences that clustered with zebrafish opsins were retained to build a “known-opsins database.” This database was then used as query in a tblastn against the 535 genome assemblies, with an e-value of 1e−05. tblastn matches were extended 30,000 bp upstream and downstream, and resulting nonoverlapping regions were extracted. EXONERATE (Slater and Birney 2005) was then used to predict opsin genes in these regions, and resulting predictions were classified into four categories: (i) “complete” if an intact coding sequence (CDS) was found with a length ≥80% of the closest opsin length in the known-opsins database, (ii) “putative pseudogene” if at least one LoF mutation was retrieved on the coding sequence, (iii) “edge” if no intact CDS and no LoF mutation was retrieved and if the sequence was near a contig or scaffold border, and (iv) “truncated” if no intact CDS and no LoF mutation was retrieved and if the sequence was not near a contig nor scaffold border, or if the sequence contained ambiguous nucleotides (“N,” representing assembly gaps). Genes falling in the edge and truncated categories are referred as “incomplete” thereafter. All complete and incomplete predicted sequences were then translated into protein sequences and used as queries in a blastp against the UniProt database. Sequences that best matched to an opsin gene were retained and considered as opsin sequences. The same procedure was used for putative pseudogenes but by using DNA sequences in a blastx against the UniProt database. A total of 17,318 complete opsins, 1,090 incomplete opsins, and 2,207 putative pseudogenes were retrieved.

## Assessment of Opsin Gene Mining Results
The number of opsins retrieved using our pipeline was compared with the number of opsins found in genome annotations available on NCBI (RefSeq and GenBank annotations). Among the 535 genome assemblies, a total of 178 had annotations. The corresponding GFF3 files were downloaded, and the longest transcript per gene was retained and extracted using AGAT (Dainat 2022) commands “agat_sp_keep_longest_isoform.pl” and “agat_sp_extract_sequences.pl.” respectively. All sequences were used as queries in a blastx against the UniProt database, and only sequences that best matched against an opsin gene were retained. Proteins were classified as complete if they had an intact coding sequence and a length ≥80% of the closest opsin length in our known-opsins database. Otherwise, the sequence was classified as incomplete, or as putative pseudogene if they were annotated as so in the GFF3 file (annotated by NCBI as “LOW QUALITY PROTEIN”). For each opsin gene retrieved in both the NCBI annotation file and by our pipeline, both DNA sequences were aligned using MUSCLE v5.1, and their percentage identity was computed using the R package “bio3d” (Grant et al. 2006). The number of opsins retrieved by our pipeline and present in NCBI annotations were highly correlated (Pearson's R = 0.93, P-value < 2e−16, Fig. S4A), and opsin DNA sequences were highly similar. In a total of 6,372 opsin genes retrieved by both our pipeline and by NCBI annotations, 5,213 had a prefect DNA identity (82% of opsins) and 6,100 had more than 95% identity (96% of opsins) (Fig. S4B).
We then compared the number of cone opsins (sws1, sws2, lws, and rh2) we retrieved with the number of cone opsin genes reported in a previous study (Musilova and Cortesi 2023). A total of 85 species were compared. Among them, cone opsin repertoires were extracted from the exact same genome assembly in 47 cases, while in 38 cases, different genome assemblies were used. The number of cone opsin genes was equal in the 2 studies for 39 species, while we retrieved more cone opsins in 44 species (Fig. S4C). Only two species had a repertoire of genes higher in the previous study: Poecilia formosa with two more lws genes (for a total of four lws against only two retrieved by our pipeline) and Chaenocephalus aceratus with one more sws1 gene (no sws1 gene retrieved for this species by our pipeline). However, another study by the same authors (Cortesi et al. 2021) reported the presence of only two lws genes in P. formosa genome, and only two lws genes are present in its annotation file (XM_007545451.2 and XM_007565629.2). For C. aceratus, no sws1 gene is present on its annotation file (carefully searched by the mean of blastp, blastx, and gene name), but it could be the case that this gene is present in the previous genome assembly version (GCA_900302675.1) but not in the one used in the current study (GCA_023974075.1). There was a high correlation between the number of cone opsins retrieved in both studies (Pearson's R = 0.9, P-value < 2e−16, Fig. S4C).
We also manually verified the visual opsin gene sequences for 24 species that had publicly available eye transcriptomes. First, raw transcriptomic reads, retrieved in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database, were aligned to the opsin coding sequences retrieved by our pipeline using STAR v.2.7.9a (Dobin et al. 2013) and an initial screen based on raw read coverage was performed. Read coverage across the CDS was visually inspected in Geneious Prime v.2022.2.2 (Biomatters Ltd) and 18 sequences with irregular coverage were identified as candidates for manual validation following the method described in (de Busserolles et al. 2017; Fogg et al. 2022). For manual gene extraction, the most similar published opsin CDS was obtained for each candidate using a blastn search against the NCBI nr database and selecting only results that were derived from manual methods (eg sequences predicted by genome annotation software were discarded). Filtered raw reads were then mapped to candidate-specific references with default medium sensitivity settings (30% identity threshold) in Geneious Prime. Reads derived from the same sequence were identified by following single nucleotide polymorphisms across each gene, with regular visual inspection for ambiguity, and were extracted as paired mates to reduce sequence gaps. The extracted reads were assembled into contigs by mapping them back to the reference and extracting the consensus. Any partial CDS extractions were completed through cyclic mapping using low sensitivity (high accuracy, 100% identity threshold) settings to repeatedly extend the CDS. The manual extraction method yielded CDS which were identical to those mined using the automated pipeline.
Finally, we compared the number of nonvisual opsin genes we retrieved with a previous study (Beaudry et al. 2017). In a total of eight species that were compared, the numbers of nonvisual opsins were equal in three cases, while we retrieved more nonvisual opsins in five cases. Nevertheless, there was a positive correlation between the numbers of nonvisual opsin genes in the two studies (Pearson correlation: R = 0.75, P-value = 0.03, Fig. S4D).

## Impact of the Genome Assembly Quality and Version on the Number of Opsins
We evaluated if the number of opsin genes retrieved was dependent on the assembly quality (chromosome or scaffold level), on the scaffold N50, on the sequencing technology, or on the year of release. The sequencing technology was set to “long reads” if the genome was sequenced with MinION, Nanopore, or PacBio and to “short reads” if it was only sequenced with Illumina HiSeq, Illumina NovaSeq, or BGISEQ-500. We observed that there was a similar number of visual opsins in chromosome and in scaffold-level assemblies (pGLS P-value = 0.48, mean of 7.18 and 7.02 genes, respectively, Fig. S5) and a slightly higher number of nonvisual opsins in chromosome-level assemblies than in scaffold-level assemblies (pGLS P-value = 0.02, mean of 25.8 and 22.3 genes, respectively). The number of visual and nonvisual opsins were not correlated to the scaffold N50 (pGLS P-value = 0.13 and 0.36, respectively, Fig. S5) and were similar in long-read and short-read assemblies (pGLS P-value = 0.054 and 0.39, respectively, Fig. S5). The release date of the genome assembly also had no impact on the number of visual or nonvisual opsins (pGLS P-value = 0.5192 and 0.2113, respectively, Fig. S5)
We then assessed the variation in the number of visual and nonvisual opsins across different genome assemblies of the same species. We used genome_updater to find species in our genome dataset that had another genome assembly in NCBI (called alternative assemblies thereafter). Among the 535 species of our dataset, 90 species had 1 other assembly, and 47 had 2 or more other assemblies, for a total of 222 alternative assemblies. We then used BUSCO v5.1.274 with the Actinopterygii odb10 database to retain 142 alternative assemblies with a BUSCO score ≥90% (Fig. S6), representing 84 species (Data S1). Opsin genes in these alternative genome assemblies were extracted following the procedure described above, and extracted genes were classified based on their best blastp match against our database composed of 17,318 complete opsin genes. For each alternative assembly, we then computed the absolute difference in the number of visual opsins and in the number of nonvisual opsins retrieved in comparison to the main assembly used in this study. We found a very low mean absolute difference of the number of visual opsins 0.4 (min = 0; max = 2, Fig. S6), while it was slightly higher, 1.6, for the number of nonvisual opsins (min = 0; max = 7, Fig. S6). Furthermore, there was a very strong correlation between the number of visual opsins retrieved in the alternative assemblies and the assemblies used in this study (Pearson's R = 0.96, P-value < 2e−16, Fig. S6), and the slope of the linear regression was almost equal to 1 (number of visual opsins in the alternative assembly = 0.2 + 0.97 × number of visual opsins in the main assembly, Fig. S6). The same was true for the number of nonvisual opsins (Pearson's R = 0.97, P-value < 2e−16, number of nonvisual opsins in the alternative assembly = −0.43 + 0.98 × number of nonvisual opsins in the main assembly, Fig. S6). Thus, we expect the results of this study to be robust and only slightly impacted by the genome assembly versions used (provided that these assemblies have a BUSCO score ≥90%).

## Opsins Classification and Gene Trees
A backbone opsin alignment was built by aligning complete opsin protein sequences retrieved in the nonteleost species L. oculatus, using MUSCLE v5.1. MAFFT v7.467 (Katoh and Standley 2013) was then used to align all the other complete opsins to this backbone alignment, using the options “–add” and “–keeplength.” The alignment was trimmed to remove positions where more than 50% of sequences had a gap using trimAl. We used IQ-TREE2 to build a ML phylogeny from this alignment, using the optimal model found by ModelFinder and the robustness of the nodes was assessed with 1,000 ultrafast bootstraps. Genes were assigned to a subfamily (defined as a monophyletic clade in the phylogeny), based on their position on the phylogenetic tree and with the help of already classified D. rerio sequences from a previous study (Davies et al. 2015). Our opsins ML phylogeny (Fig. S1) was similar to previously published phylogenies (Davies et al. 2015; Beaudry et al. 2017). Incomplete genes and putative pseudogenes were then assigned to a subfamily based on their best blastx match against complete genes. For each subfamily, we aligned all retrieved protein sequences, with the addition of complete proteins of Chondrichthyes or Agnatha opsin sequence(s) of the same subfamily, or of a close subfamily, (retrieved in the NCBI nonredundant protein database) using MUSCLE v5. These alignments were trimmed using trimAl and ML trees were computed for each subfamily using IQ-TREE2, with the same strategy mentioned before. We also used trimAl to reverse translate these amino acid alignments into codon alignments, using the option “-backtrans.” All the subfamilies ML trees and alignments (DNA and proteins) can be found on FigShare.

## Opsin Gene Subfamilies Dynamic and Evolutionary Rate
For each opsin subfamily ML gene tree, we used “ape” to remove the Chondrichthyes or Agnatha sequence(s) and to collapse nodes with a bootstrap support lower than 95%. The best root of each subfamily, ie the root that minimizes the number of duplication and loss events in the species tree, was found using TreeRecs (Comte et al. 2020). Duplication and loss events were mapped to the species tree using NOTUNG and using those rooted trees (Chen, Durand, and Farach-Colton 2000). Mean birth (number of gene duplications per My per gene) and death rates (number of gene losses per My per gene) were computed for each gene subfamily using equations retrieved from a previous study (Niimura, Matsui, and Touhara 2014). Since some opsin subfamilies were entirely lost at certain nodes, all descendant nodes had duplication and loss values of zero, artificially lowering the average birth and death rates. To address this, we excluded nodes where the ancestral gene count was zero when calculating the mean birth and death rates for each opsin subfamily.
For each opsin subfamily, we then fitted the Muse Gaut (+ GTR) model of codon substitution (Muse and Gaut 1994), using HyPhy (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2019) and, as inputs, the corresponding codon alignment with the reconciled gene tree or with the ML gene tree. This allowed us to obtain ML values of ω (= dN/dS) per branch. dN is defined as the number of nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site, while dS represents the number of synonymous substitutions per synonymous site. Thus, assuming that most synonymous subtituons are neutral, the dN/dS is a good indicator of the evolutionary rate of a protein coding gene.
Branches with a saturation of synonymous or nonsynonymous substitutions (dS or dN ≥1) or with too few synonymous mutations (dS < 0.01) to allow a reliable computation of ω were discarded. There was an extremely high correlation between ω-values computed on terminal branches of both the reconciled and ML trees (Pearson's R = 0.962, P-value < 2e−16), but also between the mean ω-values (mean of every branch ω) obtained for each subfamily (Pearson's R = 0.996, P-value < 2e−16). Thus, ω-values reported in this study are those obtained using the reconciled trees, but results are consistent when considering ω-values obtained with the ML gene trees. For each species, a mean opsin ω-value per species was obtained by averaging the ML ω-values of every opsins present in its genome. The same procedure was used to obtain mean ω-values for visual opsins only and mean ω-values for nonvisual opsins only.
To assess if opsin duplications were followed by higher dN/dS values, indicative of positive selection, we used the program BUSTED (Murrell et al. 2015) implemented in HyPhy. We used as input the codon alignments and the reconciled gene trees, on which we assigned daughter branches from duplications nodes to “test” branches, while all other branches, resulting from speciation events, were assigned to “background” branches. BUSTED allowed to compute a ML value of ω per branch type (here “test” vs. “background”) and to perform a likelihood ratio test to infer if “test” branches experienced significant positive selection relative to “background” branches.

## Ecological and Morphological Data
The mean depth of occurrence of 399 species was extracted from previous studies (Mu et al. 2021; Duhamet et al. 2023; Musilova and Cortesi 2023). For species not present in these three studies, data were extracted from OBIS (http://www.iobis.org/) catch records using the method described in (Musilova et al. 2019) (N = 225), and the R package “robis” (Provoost et al. 2022). Finally, for species not present in previous studies and for which no catch records were found in OBIS, depth data were retrieved from fishbase (Froese and Pauly 2022) (N = 30). Following the methods described in a recent study (Musilova and Cortesi 2023), riverine/pond/spring living species for which depth data were not retrieved in the various sources described above, we assigned a depth of 2 m (N = 63). We then classified species in five depth categories (Duhamet et al. 2023): (i) shallow above 30 m, (ii) mesophotic between 30 and 150 m, (iii) rariphotic between 150 and 300 m, (iv) mesopelagic between 300 and 1,000 m, and (v) bathypelagic below 1,000 meters. The habitat salinity (fresh, salt, brackish water, or a combinations thereof), trophic level, electrogenic ability, the climate zone (EnvTemp), the migratory behavior (AnaCat) as well as the standard length and eye diameter for each species were extracted from fishbase. Diel activity patterns were extracted from a previous study (Shafer et al. 2023). Diet categories (herbivore, omnivore, and carnivore) were inferred from the trophic level according to fishbase recommendations (https://www.fishbase.se/manual/English/fishbasethe_ecology_table.htm).
Associations between opsin number or ω and ecological factors were assessed using pGLS, allowing us to take into account the nonindependence of variables due to the shared evolutionary history of species. These pGLS were conducted using the R package “caper” (Orme et al. 2013). For pGLS analysis involving a categorical variable as the predictor, we first removed categories represented by less than three species. For each pGLS response, the P-value obtained for each predictor was corrected using the Bonferroni method. pGLS results were relatively similar when considering teleosts without recent WGD or when considering all ray-finned fishes (Fig. S35).
Finally, to assess if the increase in dN/dS observed for tmt3, opn5, and opn7 in electric fishes was caused by positive selection or by a relaxation of selection, we used the program RELAX (Wertheim et al. 2015), implemented in HyPhy. Branches of these opsins subfamily trees corresponding to electric fish species were assigned to “test” branches, while all other terminal branches were assigned to “foreground”. RELAX computed the values and distribution of three ω using a branch-site model for both test and foreground branches and tested for the convergence of ω toward one (toward relaxed purifying selection). The extent of this convergence depends on a parameter (K), which tends to approach zero as selection nears complete relaxation (Wertheim et al. 2015).

## Genome-Wide Orthologous Genes Identification and Gene Ontology Analyses
A total of 4,617,897 annotated protein sequences were retrieved in the 178 annotated species (RefSeq and GenBank annotations) of our dataset. These proteins were used as input in OrthoFinder (Emms and Kelly 2019), which retrieved 35,448 OGs, containing a total of 4,538,760 genes (98.3% of input proteins). In this study, OGs are defined as groups of genes derived from a single origin in the MRCA of ray-finned fishes. Twenty-two OGs corresponding to opsin genes (identified by BLAST against our opsin sequences dataset) were removed. For each remaining OG, protein sequences were aligned using MUSCLE v5.1, and the alignment was trimmed and reverse translated using trimAl. ML trees were computed for all the OGs, with IQ-TREE2, using the general amino acid replacement matrix (Le and Gascuel 2008) and a discrete gamma model with four rate categories (LG + G4). As described above for opsin genes, we used HyPhy and the Muse Gaut (+ GTR) model of codon substitution to compute ML values of ω per branch, using the ML phylogenies and codon alignments as input. Again, to only keep reliable estimates of dN/dS, we discarded branches with dS < 0.01, dS ≥ 1, or dN ≥ 1. We then conducted two sets of pGLS analyses: (i) between the number of opsins and the number of genes in 33,018 of these OGs which had a nonnull variance, and only considering teleost species with no recent (ie post teleost-specific WGD) WGD (155 species), and (ii) between the number of opsins and the mean dN/dS per OG, for 14,740 OGs for which the dN/dS was available for at least 20 species, again only considering teleost with no recent WGD. To take into account the large number of tests performed, pGLS P-values were corrected using the Bonferroni method.
OGs containing at least one zebrafish gene (14,451 OGs) were assigned to a unique name (Maglott et al. 2007) corresponding to one zebrafish Entrez gene name. If an OG contained more than one zebrafish gene, then the gene name was chosen at random among the possible names. We then used the R package “gprofiler2” (Kolberg et al. 2023) to conduct an overrepresentation enrichment analysis. The Entrez gene names of significantly correlated OGs were used as queries and, as background, we used the gene names of the 14,451 OGs for which at least one zebrafish gene was retrieved. Organism was set to “drerio” and we used three sources: GO biological process (Thomas et al. 2022), Reactome (Milacic et al. 2024), and KEGG (Kanehisa et al. 2023).
Then, to assess if OGs coevolving with opsins were enriched in light-related functions, we retrieved a list of 53 genes involved in light perception and 42 genes involved in the circadian clock regulation of ray-finned fishes from a previous study (Policarpo et al. 2021). These genes corresponded to 31 and 23 OGs in our dataset respectively. Given that the number of nonvisual opsins was associated with the number of genes in 48 OGs, including 4 light-perception OGs and 1 circadian clock OG, we performed 10,000 simulations where 48 OGs were drawn at random, followed by a count of the number of light-perception and circadian clock OGs. The probability of having four light-perception OGs among the significantly associated OGs was computed as the number of simulations where four or more light-perception OGs were drawn divided by the total number of simulations (10,000). The same was performed to have the probability of picking one circadian clock OG. The same procedure was also performed to assess the probability of having one light-perception OG among 71 OGs for which the dN/dS was negatively associated with the number of nonvisual opsins.

## Pinopsin Gene Evolution
All complete pinopsin genes and putative pseudogenes retrieved in ray-finned fishes were aligned using MACSE (Ranwez et al. 2011), which can handle premature stop codons and frameshifts. Because the pinopsin putative pseudogene retrieved in the three species of the genus Ilyophis were identical, only one was retained. The alignment was visualized using AliView (Larsson 2014). We extracted the regions around the pinopsin gene (100,000 bp upstream and downstream) in species that had a NCBI (RefSeq or GenBank) annotation (Erpetoichthys calabaricus, P. senegalus, P. spathula, Atractosteus spatula, L. oculatus, M. cyprinoides, and M. atlanticus), and all genes present in these regions were extracted. Genes were defined as orthologous if they were in the same OG found by OrthoFinder (as described above). Regions, genes and links between orthologous genes were visualized using the R package “gggenomes” (Hackl, Ankenbrand, and van Adrichem 2024).
To assess if the complete pinopsin gene found in M. cyprinoides was under negative selection, positive selection or under relaxed selection, we used the pinopsin gene tree (computed from a protein alignment, using IQ-TREE2, as mentioned above) and codon alignment as input in PAML (Yang 2007). Three branch models were tested: (i) a free-ratio model assuming a different ω per branch, (ii) a one-ratio model where all branches are assumed to evolve under the same ω; and (iii) a two-ratio model where the M. cyprinoides pinopsin evolves under ω1, while all other branches evolve under ω2. Models were compared with the mean of likelihood ratio tests and P-values were computed based on a χ2 distribution. To support PAML results, we further used RELAX, implemented in HyPhy, with M. cyprinoides as the test branch, and all other branches as foreground.

## Transcriptome Data
RNA-sequencing data for a set of 75 phylogenetically representative ray-finned fish species from 39 different orders were manually retrieved on the SRA website. For each species, we systematically searched for RNA data extracted from 14 different tissues, representing the most frequently sequenced organs in fishes: eye, liver, gut, fin, heart, skin, gill, muscle, brain, kidney, bones, spleen, testis, and ovary.
We only retained RNA-seq data from adult specimens and removed those from specimens treated with chemical products. A total of 799 SRA runs (accessions can be retrieved in Data S1) were downloaded using “fastqer-dump” implemented in the SRA toolkit. For each species and each tissue, reads were concatenated if different sequencing runs were downloaded. Thus, a total of 601 different species/tissue combinations were retrieved (one species/tissue combination will be named sample thereafter). Reads were then trimmed and filtered using fastp (Chen et al. 2018) and aligned to their corresponding species genome assembly using STAR (Dobin et al. 2013). We further removed 17 samples for which less than 70% of reads mapped to the genome and removed two samples that had <5 million mapped reads, for a final dataset of 582 samples (Fig. S36). For each species, a custom GFF3 containing curated opsin genes was generated by remapping opsins to their respective genome assembly, using a modified version of EXONERATE (https://github.com/hotdogee/exonerate-gff3), and using the options “–gff3 yes” and “–showtargetgff yes.” The number of reads mapped to each opsin was counted with HTSeq (Anders, Pyl, and Huber 2015). In addition, for 45 species that had an annotation file available on NCBI (RefSeq or GenBank), the number of reads on each annotated gene was also counted with HTSeq (retaining the longest transcript per gene), by first replacing automatically annotated opsin genes with our curated opsin annotations. For each gene, we computed the reads per kilobase (RPK) by dividing the number of reads by the gene length (in kbp). A per million scaling factor was computed for each sample as the sum of all RPK values divided by 1,000,0000. Finally, RPK values were divided by this scaling factor to obtain transcripts per million (TPM) per gene in each sample. By using the 45 annotated species, we ensured that these public RNA samples clustered by tissue type on a principal component analysis (PCA) of TPM values for 5,782 shared OGs (Fig. S30). The RE of opsins per sample was computed as the sum of opsins TPM divided by the sum of all genes TPM. Finally, to investigate the RE of each opsin subfamily (relative to all opsins), we directly used TPM values. For opsin subfamilies with two or more genes, the TPM values of these genes were summed to obtain the TPM at the subfamily level.
