Rapid Divergence of Visual Systems and Signaling Traits to Contrasting Light Regimes During Early Speciation of African Crater Lake Cichlid Fish.

# Rapid Divergence of Visual Systems and Signaling Traits to Contrasting Light Regimes During Early Speciation of African Crater Lake Cichlid Fish

## Abstract
AbstractSensory adaptation is widely hypothesized to drive ecological speciation, yet empirical evidence from natural populations undergoing early stage divergence remains limited. In Lake Masoko, a young crater lake in East Africa, the haplochromine cichlid Astatotilapia calliptera is undergoing early stage sympatric speciation into shallow-water littoral and deep-water benthic ecotypes that experience contrasting light environments. Here, we integrate retinal transcriptomics, phenotypic analyses, and visual modeling to uncover rapid sensory divergence associated with this ecological transition. We find striking shifts in cone opsin expression, with the benthic ecotype exhibiting a switch from short-wavelength sensitive SWS2B to SWS2A and an overall narrowing of cone sensitivity toward the center of the light spectrum, consistent with changes in deep-water light environment. In contrast, coding sequence variation in opsin genes was limited and no significant differences in allele frequencies were detected across nine polymorphic sites, pointing to expression regulation as the primary axis of early divergence in visual systems. In parallel, we observed divergence in male signaling traits, with benthic males displaying deeper red egg-spots, aligning with predictions from visual modeling of signal efficiency in different light environments. These results demonstrate rapid transcriptomic and phenotypic divergence in associated signaling traits—within ∼1,000 years—supporting a potential role for regulatory evolution in sensory adaptation during early ecological speciation.

## Introduction
Sensory systems mediate key ecological and social behaviors—including foraging, predator avoidance, and mate choice—that can directly impact individual fitness within a given environment. As such, adaptation of sensory traits to local environments is thought to play a central role in ecological diversification and speciation. Visual systems in particular, are shaped by the spectral properties of the environment, and adaptive divergence in vision can facilitate reproductive isolation by altering how organisms perceive their habitats and each other (Carleton et al. 2005; Maan et al. 2006, 2017; Seehausen et al. 2008). This process, known as sensory drive, has been widely implicated in the early stages of ecological speciation across diverse taxa, from insects to vertebrates (Boughman 2002; Rundle and Nosil 2005; Tobias et al. 2010; Matsumoto et al. 2014; Dalbosco Dell’Aglio et al. 2024).
Freshwater ecosystems provide a valuable natural laboratory for testing hypotheses about sensory adaptation and speciation, as they often exhibit steep environmental gradients in light intensity and spectral composition across depths and habitat zones. Organisms inhabiting these ecologically divergent habitats can experience rapid evolutionary transitions as they adapt to contrasting light environments. Despite substantial progress in understanding the role of sensory adaptation during ecological speciation, current insights are predominantly based on experimental studies (Rennison et al. 2016; Stieb et al. 2016; Maan et al. 2017; Nandamuri et al. 2017; Wright et al. 2020) or interspecific comparisons (Carleton and Kocher 2001; Seehausen et al. 2008; Hofmann et al. 2009, 2010; Musilova et al. 2019a,b; Tettamanti et al. 2019; Ricci et al. 2022, 2023; Stieb et al. 2023). Comparatively, little is known about the role of sensory adaptations during the very early stages of incipient speciation.
African cichlid fishes provide a useful model to test the role of visual system divergence during incipient speciation. These radiations encompass extreme diversity in ecology, visual systems, and color/signaling traits. Cichlids possess an unusually rich complement of opsin genes. These include seven cone opsins involved in photopic (color) vision, ranging from UV light wavelength sensitivity (SWS1) to violet- and blue-light sensitive (SWS2B and SWS2A), to green-light sensitive (RH2B, RH2Aβ, and RH2Aα), through to red-light sensitive (LWS). In addition, there is one rod opsin gene (RH1) involved in scotopic (dim-light) vision. Due to their recent evolutionary history, opsin genes in African cichlid are largely conserved at the sequence level across species (Spady et al. 2005), suggesting that regulatory changes in opsin gene expression may be a primary driver of functional divergence. Although there have been studies demonstrating the importance of small spectral shifts caused by changes in opsin sequence (Terai et al. 2002; Seehausen et al. 2008; Miyagi et al. 2012), larger sensitivity shifts can be achieved by altering the complement of opsin genes that are expressed. Typically, African cichlids express a subset of three of the seven cone opsin genes, enabling fine scale shifts in visual sensitivity, including spectral tuning to local light environments.
Several recent studies have demonstrated that visual system divergence in African cichlids occurs through changes in opsin gene expression profiles, rather than through mutations at spectral tuning sites within opsin gene coding sequences (Dalton et al. 2014; Meyer et al. 2019; Musilova et al. 2019a). For example, in deep-water species from Lake Barombi Mbo, rapid changes in visual systems are linked to shifts in expression favoring mid-spectrum sensitivity—consistent with depth-related attenuation—and not via mutational changes in coding sequences (Musilova et al. 2019a). These findings underscore the potential importance of transcriptional regulation in shaping sensory phenotypes during speciation.
Here, we investigate the molecular basis of early stage visual divergence in a natural population of Astatotilapia calliptera, a widespread haplochromine cichlid currently undergoing the early stages of ecological speciation in Lake Masoko (also known as Lake Kisiba), a small East African crater lake (∼700 m diameter, 36 m deep; Fig. 1a–c). In Lake Masoko, Astatotilapia calliptera are diverging along the habitat-depth axis into genetically distinguishable shallow-water littoral and deep-water benthic ecotypes that differ in male breeding colors, craniofacial morphology and diet (Malinsky et al. 2015). Importantly, the ecotypes are largely separated by strong thermocline between ∼11 and 16 m and oxycline at ∼11 to 20 m that separates two discrete habitats differing in temperature, oxygen concentration, and light conditions (Fig. 1d). The littoral ecotype primarily inhabits the warm, well-oxygenated, and well-lit surface waters above 10 m, while the benthic ecotype primarily inhabits the cooler, poorly oxygenated, dimly lit deeper waters below 20 m (Fig. 1). Given that visual sensory adaptations to different local environments are thought to accumulate during ecological speciation, the recent divergence of the Lake Masoko Astatotilapia ecotypes, estimated to have taken place within the last 1,000 years (Malinsky et al. 2015), offers a valuable opportunity to investigate divergence in sensory systems during the initial stages of speciation.

In addition to visual system divergence, we also investigate differences in male signaling traits—specifically, anal fin egg-spots, which are considered to act as a key fitness trait (Santos et al. 2014). Egg-spots are made up of pigment cells—silver iridophores and yellow–orange xanthophores. Importantly, egg-spots can vary in size, number, color, and pattern, both within and among species and play an important role in the breeding behavior of these fishes, being sexually selected via female choice and/or male–male competition. Prior work shows that these signals influence mating success and social dominance, with color properties, size, and number of egg-spots reflecting individual fitness (Lehtonen and Meyer 2011; Henning and Meyer 2012; Theis et al. 2012, 2015). As such, egg-spots are thought to act as an honest signal of male quality. Importantly, their efficacy as visual signals depend on their visibility in the local light environment, suggesting that they may coevolves with the visual system under ecological divergence.
To test the relationship between visual system divergence and male signaling traits, here we integrate retinal transcriptomics, quantitative color analysis of male anal fin egg-spot coloration, and visual modeling. Visual models allow us to assess whether shifts in opsin expression enhance signal detectability under specific light environments. For instance, Stieb et al. (2023) showed that higher expression of the LWS opsin in algivorous damselfish species improves detection of the red and far-red components of algae, as well as the detection of yellow, orange, and red colored conspecific signals. Here, we model cichlid visual perception to evaluate whether opsin expression differences between shallow-water littoral and deep-water benthic ecotypes enhance detection of their egg-spot colors under their respective local light environments. Given the importance of this trait for reproduction and behavior, we predicted that egg-spot colors will covary with divergence in ecotype visual systems, and that these associations are mediated by differences in the light environment, enabling maintenance of their signaling function.
This study addresses four key questions: (i) How does the underwater light environment differ between the shallow-water (<10 m) and deep-water (>20 m) habitats of Lake Masoko? (ii) Do shallow-water littoral and deep-water benthic ecotypes differ in retinal opsin expression profiles and coding sequences in ways that reflect their light environments? (iii) Do male egg-spot traits differ between ecotypes, and do they covary with divergence in visual systems and differences in local light environments? (iv) Do observed trait differences enhance signal visibility under local light conditions, consistent with predictions from sensory drive?
By linking gene expression, phenotype, and environmental variation with predictive modeling approaches, our study reveals how regulatory evolution may drive sensory optimization and facilitate rapid, early stage ecological divergence in natural populations.

## Results

## Variation in the Visual Environment Between Shallow and Deep-Water Habitats
To assess differences in the visual environments experienced by benthic ecotypes in the deep-water zone (below 20 m; Fig. 1b) and littoral ecotypes in the shallow-water zone (above 10 m; Fig. 1b) we collected underwater light measurements across the depth gradient in Lake Masoko, ranging from 2 to 30 m. We found that light intensity declined substantially between shallow habitat (maximum intensity ∼11,600 units, mean intensity across the habitat depth range ∼1,260 units) and deep habitat (maximum intensity ∼175 units, mean intensity across the habitat depth range of ∼11 units). Thus, deep-benthic ecotypes experience less than 3% of the light intensity experienced by shallow-littoral ecotypes. We also found that light spectra bandwidth narrowed with increasing depth, with strong attenuation of both the short-wavelength violet light and long-wavelength red light, resulting in a green-shifted light spectrum in the deep-water environment (Fig. 1f). These results illustrate a dramatic shift in the visual environment experienced between individuals occupying the deep-water and shallow-water habitats, with regards to both light intensity and light spectra bandwidth.

## Deep-Benthic vs. Shallow-Littoral Cone Opsin Expression
To examine overall variation in cone opsin gene expression we generated RNA-seq data from 18 individuals (n = 9 per ecotype, ∼30 million reads per individual) and performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the complete set of seven cone opsins present in cichlids (supplementary tables S1 and S2, Supplementary Material online). We found that benthic and littoral individuals separated along the first two major axes of variation (Fig. 2a), demonstrating clear biological differences in the visual system between the ecotypes (PERMANOVATOTAL CONE OPSIN EXPRESSION: F1,16 = 9.9674, P = 0.003, permutations = 1,000). Comparisons of proportional expression levels revealed that neither ecotype uses the blue–green sensitive RH2B opsin gene, while UV sensitive short-wavelength opsin gene SWS1 was only used in the littoral ecotype, based on a threshold of 3% of total single or double cone opsin expression (Musilova et al. 2019a; Fig. 2b and supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online). Within the expressed genes we found a significant downregulation of violet sensitive SWS2B in benthic ecotypes relative to the littoral ecotype (Generalized linear models: F1,16 = 11.8720, Bonferroni corrected P = 0.0116; Fig. 2b and supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online), coupled with an upregulation of blue sensitive SWS2A (F1,16 = 12.748, Bonferroni corrected P = 0.0116; Fig. 2b). Overall, these results demonstrate a narrowing of cone opsin sensitivity toward the center of the light spectrum, enhancing blue–green sensitivity in deep-water dwelling benthic individuals.

## Deep-Benthic vs. Shallow-littoral Rod Opsin Expression
To explore signals of dark adaptation associated with divergence of the benthic ecotype into the deep-water habitat zone, we looked at differences in expression in the rod opsin gene RH1 between the deep-benthic and shallow-littoral ecotypes. In line with previous findings in African and Amazonian cichlids (Escobar-Camacho et al. 2017), we found that RH1 was the most highly expressed visual pigment in both ecotypes (based on the proportional expression of RH1 across all opsin types; supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online). However, we found no evidence that RH1 was differentially expressed between the benthic (92.12 ± 4.09%, mean ± SD), and littoral (86.61 ± 10.54%, mean ± SD) ecotypes (Fig. 3). These results suggest that differences in rod-mediated vision between the benthic and littoral ecotypes in Lake Masoko are not necessarily associated with differences in rod opsin expression.

## Coding Sequence Variation in Opsin and Visual Cycle Genes
We found only minor differences in sequence variation within opsin gene coding regions (supplementary table S5, Supplementary Material online). Only two of the seven cone opsins showed sequence variation, with a single nonsynonymous allelic variant identified within the blue-light sensitive SWS2B opsin (resulting in an amino acid substitution at position 91) and three synonymous allelic variants identified within green-light sensitive RH2Aβ opsin (no change to the amino acid sequence). We also identified two nonsynonymous allelic variants within the dim-light sensitive rod opsin gene RH1 (resulting in amino acid substitutions at positions 162 and 166; Fig. 4b). There was, however, no clear association with ecotype in any of seven variants (supplementary table S6, Supplementary Material online).

Additionally, we identified coding sequence variation in two isoforms of retina-specific retinol dehydrogenase RDH8 and RDH12. The genes RDH8 and RDH12 take central roles in the visual cycle, contributing to the reduction of all-trans retinal and subsequent regeneration of rhodopsin and both genes have been previously implicated in dim-light adaptation (Chen et al. 2012; Xue et al. 2015). Expression of RH1 was highly correlated with expression of RDH8 (r2 = 0.904, d.f. = 16, P = 2.642e−07) and RDH12 (r2 = −0.922, d.f. = 16, P = 5.013e−08) but this did not differ between ecotypes (Fig. 4c). However, coding sequence variation in both RDH8 (single site) and RDH12 (two sites) was associated with allele frequency differences between the shallow-littoral and deep-benthic ecotypes (Fisher's Exact Tests: PRDH8_SNP1 = 0.0294, PRDH8_SNP2 = 0.0146, PRDH12 = 0.0471; Fig. 4d and supplementary table S6, Supplementary Material online). Notably, all alternative alleles identified in opsin and vision gene coding sequences of our focal population pair are present in A. calliptera populations across the Lake Malawi catchment. Thus, the variation in opsin haplotypes in Lake Masoko is likely to have been sourced from standing genetic variation.

## A1/A2 Chromophore Usage
To explore potential differences in chromophore composition between ecotypes, we compared expression of the gene CYP27C1, which is known to regulate the A1-to-A2 chromophore conversion pathway (Enright et al. 2015). Typically, higher expression of CYP27C1 is associated with vitamin A2 derived chromophores, and consequently with long-wavelength shifted visual sensitivities. While we observed a slightly higher expression of CYP27C1 in benthic ecotypes, this was not significantly different to expression levels in littoral ecotypes (Generalized linear model: F1,16 = 1.8364, P = 0.1944; supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). This suggests that both ecotypes likely use similar proportions of A1- and A2-derived chromophores.

## Global Expression Divergence in the Retina of Deep-water and Shallow-Water Ecotypes
In addition to opsin gene expression patterns, we examined global patterns of gene expression divergence across the whole retinal transcriptomes of deep-water benthic and shallow-water littoral ecotypes. Differential gene expression analyses identified 33 genes that were significantly differentially expressed between ecotypes, all of which were upregulated in the benthic ecotype (Fig. 5 and supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online). We found that seven (21%) of these 33 genes were associated with vision, more than expected by chance (Hypergeometric test: P = 1.895e−14; supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online). Two of these genes have previously been implicated in dark vision adaptation; RPGRB (Retinitis pigmentosa GTPase regulator protein 2), which is involved in retinal rod development (Shu et al. 2009), and SLC1A (Solute carrier 1a), which mediates glutamate pathways and the transfer of visual information from the retina to the brain (Guoping et al. 2017). Four of these genes are involved in retina development, CLDNC (Claudin c), CRB3A (Crumbs homolog 3a), GATA6 (GATA-binding protein 6), and WT1A (Wilms tumor 1a) (Crawford et al. 2002; Kojima et al. 2002; Wagner et al. 2002, 2005; Omori and Malicki 2006). We also identified significant up regulation of GGT1A (Gamma-glutamyltransferase 1a), which is involved in defending the eye (retina, cornea, and lens) against oxidative stress (Winkler and Giblin 1983; Puertas et al. 1993; Ganea and Harding 2006). Interestingly, other differentially expressed genes were associated with oxidative stress response, as well as blood vessel development and regulation of blood pressure (supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online). Two genes were involved in cranial skeletal system development (CYLDB, Cylindromatosis; Tse 2017) and bone remodeling (SNX10A, Sorting nexin 10A; Coudert et al. 2015), which is striking given craniofacial differences between the ecotypes. We also found significant upregulation of muscle contraction (ATP2A1, Calcium-transporting ATPase) and central nervous system (NTRK3A, Tyrosine-protein kinase receptor) processes. The topmost up regulated gene in benthic individuals was SPACA4 (sperm acrosome associated 4), which is involved in sperm development and sperm–egg interactions (Bianchi 2021; Fujihara et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023).

## Divergence in Male Color Traits Between Fish in Shallow and Deep Light Environments
To determine if egg-spot color traits were different between the littoral and benthic ecotypes we measured color differences using calibrated digital photographs. Egg-spot visibility depends not only on their inherent color but also on the contrast with the colors of the surrounding anal fin background. Therefore, for each individual photograph, we quantified the color from three anal fin regions: (i) egg-spot color, (ii) proximal black region located above the egg-spot pattern, and (iii) the red distal rim located below the egg-spot pattern (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). We further quantified a control orange color present in each photograph. Control color measurements were taken to account for potential batch effects in color calibration performance since benthic and littoral individuals were collected and photographed in consecutive days.
Color was quantified in the CIE 1976 L*, a*, b* (CIELAB) color space, which measures the perception of color along a white–black lightness axis (L*), a red–green axis (a*), and a yellow–blue axis (b*) (supplementary table S7, Supplementary Material online). Egg-spot color was significantly different between ecotypes along the a* (Two-tailed unpaired t-test; t = 6.654, d.f. = 101, P < 0.0001) and b* axis (Two-tailed unpaired t-test; t = 5.426, d.f. = 101, P < 0.0001) (supplementary fig. S3 and table S7, Supplementary Material online). Using the a* and b* coordinates for each individual, we calculated egg-spot color hue (angle in the a*b*-plane) and chroma (distance from the L*-axis which is saturation). While no differences were detected in egg-spot chroma (supplementary fig. S4, Supplementary Material online), egg-spot hue was significantly different between the benthic and littoral ecotypes (Two-tailed unpaired t-test; t = 8.258, d.f. = 101, P < 0.0001) with benthic individuals having redder egg-spots than the relatively more yellow egg-spots of littoral individuals (Fig. 6a). We also observed this same trend in hue for the control color, indicating a batch effect derived from collecting and photographing benthic and littoral individuals during different days with potential differences in ambient light conditions (supplementary fig. S4, Supplementary Material online). Despite this batch effect, the difference in average egg-spot hue between benthic versus littoral ecotypes was four times higher (0.207) than the difference between average benthic versus control measurements (0.055). We subtracted the magnitude of the artefactual difference between benthic and littoral groups quantified using the control measurements from the magnitude of the observed difference in average egg-spot hue between groups in order to estimate the true difference, Δhue,true. The observed Δhue,true between the benthic and littoral groups (0.153) was consistent with a highly significant difference between the groups (P < 0.001) based on a nonparametric bootstrap procedure for which we assumed that all individuals belong to the same group (see methods). These results consistently indicate that on average the benthic ecotype has redder egg-spots than the littoral ecotype.

With respect to anal fin background colors, both the black region located above the egg-spot pattern and the red distal rim located below it were significantly different between ecotypes in the white–black lightness (L*) axis (Two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test; U = 655, P = 0.0004). Both regions were significantly blacker (darker) in the benthic ecotype than the littoral ecotype (Fig. 6b and supplementary figs. S3 and S4, Supplementary Material online). No batch effects were observed along the lightness axis. Taken together, these results show that the benthic ecotype has redder egg-spots on a darker background, in comparison to the littoral ecotype which has yellow egg-spots on a lighter colored anal fin. We hypothesize that the observed coloration of the anal fins in the benthic ecotype maximize egg-spot contrast and visibility in a low intensity, green-shifted light environment.

## Visual Models Clarify Relationship Between Visual Sensitivity, Signaling Traits, and Ambient Light
We performed visual modeling to assess the conspicuousness of different colors under different environmental light conditions, as seen through the perspectives of Astatotilapia visual systems. Our goal was to identify the optimal color pairs and determine if they differed by visual system or habitat. We modeled a series of color reflectance spectra with colors taking two forms. They were either shaped like a Gaussian peak, with low reflectance at short and long wavelengths and high reflectance in the middle, or shaped like a step, with low reflectance at shorter wavelengths, which then increased (following a half Gaussian pattern) and remained high at longer wavelengths. The set of peaked colors had peak reflectance from 375 to 549 nm (i.e. blues and greens), and the set of stepped colors reached maximum reflectance at 550 to 625 nm (i.e. yellows and reds). Gaussian peak positions and widths were chosen based on previous cichlid color reflectance measurements (Dalton et al. 2010). We calculated color discrimination between all possible color pairs to identify optimally discriminated pairs.
In the simplest comparison, we assumed that visual systems did not differ between habitats and were based on three visual pigments (SWS2A, RH2Aα, and LWS) utilizing an A1 chromophore. Because of the similarity in opsin sequences, the visual sensitivities were set similar to those of the related species, Astatotilapia burtoni (Fernald and Liebman 1980). In this case, the color pair, which produced the maximum discrimination, was a shorter wavelength peak and a longer wavelength step. This makes sense as the shorter wavelength peak color would maximally stimulate the SWS2A visual pigment while the longer wavelength step color stimulates the LWS visual pigment. Having each color predominantly stimulate one visual pigment, maximizes the visual contrast between the color pair. In noting the optimal colors, we refer to them using either P (peak) or S (step) to indicate their shape, followed by a number that is the peak wavelength of the Gaussian distribution used to construct the modeled color (Table 1).

When we model the visual systems to include the differential opsin coexpression of the A. calliptera ecotypes, the optimal color pairs for the two habitats they occupy are even more different—for visual models we assumed coexpression of opsins within photoreceptor types based on established patterns in other cichlid systems (Carleton et al. 2008; Dalton et al. 2017; see Materials and Methods and supplementary table S9, Supplementary Material online). The optimal color pair for the littoral fish in the shallow habitat is even shorter wavelengths (P389, S560) than for the benthic fish in the deep habitat (P422, S582). These shifts are now 20 to 30 nm, suggesting that visual sensitivity differences due to opsin coexpression increase the differences in best color pairs. We explored whether A1/A2 chromophore shifts might also alter the optimal color pairs. Changing from 100% to 75% to 50% A1 chromophore caused only a 1 to 2 nm shift in the optimal color pairs and so has minimal effect.
In addition to comparing all pairwise saturated colors, we also compared saturated colors to broad white or black spectra, since the anal fins are whitish (littoral ecotypes) or black (benthic ecotype) in color. In each case, we found that the optimal color to contrast with black or white was the same long wavelength step color from the optimal color pairs (supplementary fig. S6, Supplementary Material online). This step color optimally stimulated one cone type and increases the contrast with the other visual pigments. The optimal color again changed with habitat. The optimal deep color (S582) was longer wavelength than the optimal shallow color (S560), with a 22 nm longer wavelength shift with depth.
In comparing the absolute just noticeable difference (JND) values for different scenarios, we found that the discrimination of egg-spots was always highest in the shallow habitat. This is true whether it is compared to a short wavelength color like blue (P400-425) or to white or black. It is also true that in all scenarios, optimal colors shifted to longer wavelength. These shifts are consistent with observations that egg-spots shift from yellows to orange–reds, for wild caught fish from the littoral and benthic habitats, respectively. As both model and actual colors appear to shift to longer wavelengths, this suggests that egg-spot color is adapting to better stimulate the viewer.
Although we do not have actual spectral reflectance data for the egg-spots from wild caught fish, egg-spots appear yellow in the shallows and orange–red at depth, through human eyes. Based on spectra for egg-spots and other colors in wild caught Lake Malawi cichlids, these step colors could be shifting from yellow that peak at 550 to 570 to oranges that peak at 590 to 610 nm (Dalton et al. 2010). If so, then the wild caught orange colors would be slightly longer wavelength that the optimal step colors identified here by the model. Although the actual spectra of egg-spots will require in situ measurements, the relative shifts predicted by the models make sense based on our knowledge of cichlid colors.

## Discussion
Our study integrates transcriptomic, phenotypic, and visual modeling data to provide new insights into the early stages of sensory divergence within a rapidly diverging African cichlid system. Our focus on A. calliptera from Lake Masoko—where ecological divergence is occurring within a single species—offers a rare window into sensory adaptation before reproductive isolation is complete (Malinsky et al. 2015), which we place into context with patterns observed in more derived ancestral cichlid radiations. By combining environmental light measurements, opsin expression profiling, egg-spot color analysis, and perceptual modeling, we link ecological variation to trait divergence in a predictive framework. This integrative approach reveals marked differences in visual sensitivity and signaling traits between shallow-littoral and deep-benthic ecotypes that align with depth-related light conditions—consistent with environmentally induced plasticity, and potentially underpinned by emerging genetic differentiation. To our knowledge, this is the first study to couple these axes of variation at such an early stage of speciation, providing a valuable foundation for future experimental work on the role of sensory adaptation in ecological diversification.

## Light Environment Changes Rapidly With Depth
We quantified the ambient light conditions across the habitats occupied by littoral (above 10 m) and benthic (below 20 m) ecotypes and found that both light intensity and spectral bandwidth decline steeply with depth (Fig. 1f). This pronounced shift coincides with the presence of the thermo-oxycline at around 11 to 16 m (Fig. 1d), which likely contributes to reduced water quality and altered light scattering. Irradiance dropped by ∼300 a.u. between 10 m (the lower boundary of the shallow-water habitat) and the start of the thermocline at 11 m, and by ∼500 a.u. between 10 and 16 m at the lower boundary of the thermocline (supplementary figs. S9 and S10, Supplementary Material online). These changes are not gradual but reflect a sharp transition in light availability, consistent with the known effects of stratification on phytoplankton composition and water optics (Hocking and Straškraba 1999; Rinke et al. 2010; Heiskanen et al. 2015). In Lake Masoko, short and long wavelengths attenuate rapidly with depth, resulting in a broad spectrum light environment in the shallow-water habitat, and a narrower, blue–green shifted spectrum in the deep-water habitat. This depth-related spectral transition mirrors patterns seen in Lake Malawi, where such shifts have been linked to visual system and color pattern divergence in cichlids (Sabbah et al. 2011). These parallels position Lake Masoko as a useful model to test how light regimes can shape sensory divergence and may have influenced depth-related speciation events in the older and larger Lake Malawi.

## Divergent Light Environments Shape Visual System Tuning
Across diverse animal lineages, divergence in sensory systems can promote ecological specialization and contribute to reproductive isolation, playing a key role in the speciation process (Boughman 2002; Rundle and Nosil 2005; Seehausen et al. 2008; Tobias et al. 2010; Matsumoto et al. 2014; Dalbosco Dell’Aglio et al. 2024). In fishes, insects, and even vertebrate terrestrial systems, tuning of sensory perception to local environmental conditions can shape ecological interactions, drive behavioral differentiation, and ultimately generate barriers to gene flow (Boughman 2002; Rundle and Nosil 2005; Tobias et al. 2010; Matsumoto et al. 2014; Dalbosco Dell’Aglio et al. 2024). Our study contributes to this growing body of work by showing that divergence in visual system gene expression in A. calliptera closely parallels ecological differences across different habitat light conditions in Lake Masoko—providing a clear case of early stage sensory divergence associated with ecological specialization.
We found marked shifts in cone opsin gene expression between littoral and benthic ecotypes that match the blue–green-shifted light environment of deeper water (Figs. 1f and 2). Benthic individuals exhibit reduced expression of short-wavelength opsins (SWS1 and SWS2B) and elevated expression of mid-wavelength opsins (RH2Aβ and RH2B), consistent with spectral tuning to local light conditions. This mirrors patterns observed in deeper-water cichlid species from older African lake radiations (e.g. Lakes Malawi and Victoria) where narrower ambient light spectra select for reduced opsin diversity (Schneider et al. 2020). Intriguingly, both ecotypes retain broader opsin repertoires than typically seen in older radiations, potentially reflecting their recent derivation from riverine ancestors (∼10,000 years ago; Malinsky et al. 2015) and an intermediate stage of sensory specialization.
Critically, our findings demonstrate that visual system divergence can arise rapidly and at early stages of ecological divergence, well before complete reproductive isolation evolves. This challenges the traditional view that sensory specialization is a late-stage reinforcement mechanism, and aligns with evidence from a range of taxa—including birds (e.g. Tobias et al. 2010), butterflies (e.g. Merrill et al. 2015), and freshwater invertebrates (e.g. Tierney et al. 2018)—that sensory adaptation can initiate or accelerate speciation.
Plasticity likely plays a key role in facilitating such rapid shifts. Opsin expression is known to be highly plastic in cichlids (Hofmann et al. 2010; Härer et al. 2019; Bertinetti et al. 2024; Irazábal-González et al. 2024) and other fishes (Shand et al. 2008; Ehlman et al. 2015) and can respond dynamically to changes in environmental light. The ability to plastically adjust visual sensitivity may have enabled colonization of the deeper-water habitat, setting the stage for ecological divergence. This highlights a more general mechanism by which phenotypic plasticity can facilitate niche shifts and promote diversification—a concept increasingly recognized across plant and animal systems.
We also show that the ecological divergence in A. calliptera aligns with sensory specialization in behaviorally relevant contexts. Visual sensitivities affect critical tasks such as foraging, mate choice, and social signaling, which are tightly linked to reproductive success. In both aquatic and terrestrial systems, including frogs, birds, and arthropods, divergence in sensory tuning has been shown to influence mate preferences and signal detectability, reinforcing ecological divergence (Boughman 2002). In A. calliptera, the tuning of the benthic ecotype to the blue–green ambient spectrum echoes similar habitat-specific opsin divergence seen in crater lake cichlids from Lake Barombi Mbo (Musilova et al. 2019a), further supporting the role of sensory divergence in facilitating ecological specialization even in young, incipient species.
Additionally, we explored expression of the rod opsin gene RH1, which governs scotopic (dim-light) vision. While expression did not differ significantly between ecotypes (Fig. 3), possibly due to shared nocturnal activity (Lloyd et al. 2023), previous studies have revealed RH1 coding sequence divergence between ecotypes (Malinsky et al. 2015). Although we did not detect strong expression differences or allelic divergence in RH1 or cone opsins in our dataset, coding sequence evolution may still play a role, particularly under relaxed or divergent selective pressures—a pattern observed in sensory genes across many animal systems, e.g. beetles (Sharkey et al. 2021), bats (Gutierrez et al. 2018; Simões et al. 2019) and primates (Tan et al. 2005).
We did however, we did identify strong ecotype-associated coding divergence in RDH8 and RDH12 (Fig. 4d and supplementary table S6, Supplementary Material online), genes involved in rhodopsin regeneration and critical for dim-light vision. While expression levels of these genes were similar (Fig. 4c), coding sequence differences may fine-tune visual performance to local demands, such as enhanced dark adaptation in the benthic ecotype and crepuscular foraging activities as observed in the littoral ecotype (Turner et al. 2021). These genes are known to influence visual sensitivity across vertebrates (Chen et al. 2012; Xue et al. 2015), and our findings highlight their potential as conserved targets of adaptation across taxa facing different light environments.
Taken together, our results illustrate how rapid, fine-scale visual system divergence—driven primarily by changes in gene regulation, with contributions from coding sequence variation—can facilitate ecological specialization. This mechanism is likely generalizable across diverse animal systems, where sensory traits mediate interactions with the environment and conspecifics. By integrating ecological, behavioral, and genomic perspectives, our study reinforces the broader principle that sensory system evolution is a powerful and repeatable driver of diversification.

## Expression Analyses Identify Additional Genes Potentially Involved in Deep-water Adaptation
Given that we know the Lake Masoko ecotypes show divergence in craniofacial morphology, diet (Carruthers et al. 2022) and male breeding colors in the natural environment (Fig. 1b), we tested for broader differences expression beyond visual opsins. Our global gene expression pattern analysis highlighted significant differential expression (DE) of several nonopsin genes involved in vision, as well as other ecologically relevant functions between the ecotypes (supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online). For example, we found significant upregulation of genes involved in central nervous system functions, and more specifically divergence in genes linked to mechanosensory traits, such as the lateral line system which is used for navigation, prey detection, and communication. Cranial lateral line canal morphology is strongly correlated with diet and trophic niche in cichlids (Edgley and Genner 2019), and behavioral studies in Lake Malawi species have shown that species that differ in cranial canal morphology also differ in their ability to locate live prey in dark environments (Schwalbe et al. 2012; Schwalbe and Webb 2014). Notably, cranial lateral line systems are under divergence in the Lake Masoko ecotypes (Edgley et al. 2024). Overall, these results highlight an important role for regulation of genes associated with sensory traits during the evolutionary transition to the deep-water habitat in Lake Masoko.
The topmost differentially expressed gene between ecotypes was SPACA4, which is crucial for species-specific sperm–egg interaction and fertilization in mammals and other vertebrates, including fish (Bianchi 2021; Fujihara et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023). Interestingly, we found that SPACA4 was significantly upregulated in the deep-living benthic ecotypes. This is intriguing given that reproductive behaviors may vary subtly between habitats. While no formal quantification of breeding phenology exists, field observations suggest that shallow-littoral ecotypes breed seasonally, with reproductive activity peaking between March and May (Turner et al. 2021). Gene expression samples were collected in December, so outside of this window and it is possible that the benthic ecotype is reproductively active earlier. Also, the marked DE of SPACA4 may reflect ecological or developmental differentiation in reproductive strategies between ecotypes, independent of breeding season. However, future investigations combining fine-scale behavioral observations with temporal sampling of gene expression across breeding and nonbreeding seasons would help to uncover the ecological and reproductive drivers underlying SPACA4 expression divergence between ecotypes.

## Divergence in Male Signaling Traits Reflects Habitat-specific Light Conditions
To investigate how divergent light environments may shape secondary sexual selected traits during early stage speciation, we examined natural variation in male anal fin egg-spot phenotypes—an important signaling trait involved in both mate choice and male–male competition in haplochromine cichlids (Lehtonen and Meyer 2011; Henning and Meyer 2012; Theis et al. 2012, 2015). We found that deep-living benthic males displayed significantly redder egg-spots set against a darker anal fin background, in contrast to the more yellow egg-spots and lighter anal fins observed in shallow-living littoral males (Fig. 6). These findings are consistent with previous work in Astatotilapia burtoni, where egg-spot color varied with habitat light conditions: riverine populations showed more saturated and intense egg-spots than their lake-dwelling counterparts (Theis et al. 2017). Together, these results suggest a broader capacity for haplochromine cichlids to fine-tune color signals to their ambient light environment.
It is well established that sexual signals are shaped by the interaction between natural and sexual selection and must remain both perceptible and conspicuous to intended receivers in a given visual environment (Endler 1992; Maan and Seehausen 2011; Schaefer and Ruxton 2015). In blue–green-shifted light habitats—such as those in deeper water—red pigmented traits often enhance signal contrast and visibility, as seen in the red body coloration of bluefin killifish (Lucania goodei, Fuller 2002) and the red throats of male three-spined sticklebacks (Gasterosteus aculeatus, Reimchen 1989). The shift toward redder egg-spots in benthic males likely serves a similar function, enhancing signal efficiency under the altered spectral conditions of the deep-water habitat in Lake Masoko.
More broadly, these findings underscore the role of the sensory environment in shaping sexual signals across animal systems, from fish to birds and insects. Our study adds to growing evidence that signal-environment matching can evolve rapidly, even within populations experiencing recent ecological divergence (Boughman 2002; Seehausen et al. 2008; Cummings and Endler 2018; Fuller and Endler 2018; Wright et al. 2020). This supports a central tenet of sensory drive theory—that traits under sexual selection can coevolve with sensory systems and environmental conditions to reinforce reproductive isolation. The Lake Masoko system therefore offers a valuable model for understanding the early steps of this process, with implications for speciation research in both aquatic and terrestrial organisms.

## Linking Habitat Light, Visual Systems, and Male Signaling Traits
To test whether divergence in visual sensitivity corresponds with shifts in signaling traits, we examined natural variation in egg-spot coloration between littoral and benthic males of A. calliptera. Visual modeling revealed that optimal egg-spot colors—maximizing signal detectability under local light conditions—shift from yellow (∼560 nm) in the shallow littoral habitat to more orange–red hues (∼582 nm) in the deep-water benthic habitat (Table 1). These predictions closely match our observed phenotypic data: benthic males displayed significantly redder egg-spots against darker anal fins than littoral males (Fig. 5), suggesting that signal conspicuousness has coevolved with visual sensitivity under divergent light conditions.
These findings provide empirical support for sensory drive theory, which predicts that visual signals and sensory systems coevolve in response to environmental light regimes, reinforcing behavioral isolation (Endler 1992; Boughman 2002). Our results align with studies in other freshwater fish systems, such as Gasterosteus sticklebacks, where female preference for red males covaries with local red-light environments (Boughman 2002), and Pundamilia cichlids in Lake Victoria, where male nuptial color, female preferences, and LWS opsin genotypes are tightly linked to habitat light (Seehausen et al. 2008). More broadly, similar dynamics are observed in other vertebrates and invertebrates, including damselfishes (Stieb et al. 2023), butterflies (Dalbosco Dell’Aglio et al. 2024), and birds (Tobias et al. 2010), suggesting that environment-mediated sensory adaptation and signal evolution is a widespread driver of diversification.
However, our visual models also revealed that signal discrimination was consistently lower in the benthic light environment, despite the shift in signal hue. This suggests that shifts in sensory tuning to ambient light may initially outweigh the strength of sexual selection on signal elaboration during early stages of divergence (Schneider et al. 2020; Bertinetti et al. 2024). In this context, signaling traits may evolve secondarily to maintain effective communication within the constraints of a novel visual environment, supporting the idea that signal divergence may often follow, rather than lead, sensory adaptation (Fig. 7; Stieb et al. 2023).
Hypothesized stages of divergence in visual systems and signaling traits in Lake Masoko. Step 1. Colonization of the shallow-littoral habitat in Lake Masoko by riverine A. calliptera ∼10,000 years ago (Malinsky et al. 2015) with visual system adaptation matched to the broad spectrum, daylight environment. Step 2. Colonization of the deep-benthic habitat ∼1,000 years ago (Malinsky et al. 2015). Occupancy of the darker, blue–green shifted light environment is facilitated by shifts in visual systems, with fish in the deep-water habitat exhibiting narrower, mid-spectrum, blue–green visual sensitivities. Step 3. Establishment in the deep-benthic habitat. Adaptation of the visual system to the darker, deep-water habitat promote shifts in the color of male signaling traits, maximizing their conspicuousness in the deep-water light environment and perceptibility to conspecifics. We suggest that the rapid adaptation of visual systems to contrasting light regimes is largely facilitated by shifts in opsin expression profiles, followed by divergent adaptation of male signaling traits (male anal fin egg-spots), with trait color evolving to maximize their visibility and function within a given light environment. Overall, our results suggest a primary role of natural selection in driving the early stages of ecological speciation in Lake Masoko, reinforced by subsequent selection on male secondary sexual traits.
Overall, our findings highlight the power of integrating behavioral ecology, visual modeling, and transcriptomics to uncover the early drivers of phenotypic divergence. The Lake Masoko system provides a compelling model for studying the initial steps of ecological speciation, with broader relevance to systems where communication and perception are shaped by environmental heterogeneity.

## Conclusions and Future Directions
Our results demonstrate that habitat-specific light conditions exert powerful selective pressure, driving rapid coordinated shifts in both visual systems and signaling traits during the early stages of cichlid fish diversification and speciation. We show that ecologically distinct populations of A. calliptera exhibit substantial shifts in visual sensitivities and opsin expression profiles, finely tuned to the spectral properties of their respective shallow- or deep-water habitats. Male anal fin egg-spot color also differs significantly between ecotypes, with color shifts closely matching those predicted to enhance signal detectability under each visual system—strongly suggesting that natural and sexual selection act in concert on both sensory perception and visual signaling. These shifts possibly reflect a combination of phenotypic plasticity and heritable changes in gene regulation, underscoring the complex interplay of ecological, genetic, and plastic responses in sensory evolution. Taken together, our study provides novel insights into how environmental light landscapes shape visual systems and signaling traits during the initial stages of ecologically mediated speciation.
This work sets the stage for future research into the genomic and regulatory basis of visual plasticity, the developmental timing of opsin expression shifts, and the role of sensory systems in mediating behaviors leading to reproductive isolation. More broadly, our findings contribute to understanding how organisms adapt to local environments, and how fine-scale sensory divergence may initiate ecological speciation.

## Materials and Methods

## Sample Collection for Transcriptomic Analyses
A. calliptera were sampled from Lake Masoko, Tanzania (coordinates: 9°20′N, 33°45′E) between the 10th and 13th of December 2014. Fish were collected using nets and SCUBA from two zones within the lake, the shallow-littoral zone (above 10 m, littoral ecotype) and the deep-benthic zone (below 20 m, benthic ecotype) (Fig. 1b). Immediately after collection fish were euthanized with MS-222 (Sigma-Aldrich). All individuals were then photographed on their left side, standard length was measured to the nearest millimeter, and body color was recorded. The right eye was then dissected stored in RNAlater for subsequent RNA extraction. RNA samples were collected within 5 min of euthanasia and stored at room temperature until shipment to the UK, where they transferred to a −80 °C upon arrival. All further processing was carried out at the University of Bristol, UK. A total of 18 fish were sampled for retinal gene expression analyses. Sample details are in supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online.

## Environment Light Measurements
Underwater light measurements were collected at multiple time points including 2 days during the dry season (August 2015) and 2 days during the wet season (May 2016). Light intensity was measured with an Ocean Optics spectrophotometer (USB2000+VIS+NIR+ESUSB200+ with enhanced sensitivity, preconfigured 250 to 1000 nm; SN: USB2+U07030, CC3 cosine corrector). The probe was orientated upwards for down-welling light measurements and horizontally to measure side-welling light. Light measurements were collected every 2 m across the complete depth gradient in the lake, from 2 to 30 m. Relative irradiance was calculated from raw light intensity “count” values by correcting for the spectral response function of the spectrophotometer CCD. All calculations were performed in R v.4.2.1 (R Core Team 2022; https://www.R-project.org/) using the calibration data supplied from the manufacturer. Briefly, we fitted a third order polynomial model to the calibration data to recalculate the calibration multiplier for our measured spectra wavelength values (supplementary fig. S11, Supplementary Material online). We then divided the light intensity measurements by the calibration values to convert the light measurements from counts to relative irradiance. The calibrated light measurements for all four side-welling datasets were averaged for each depth. Side-welling irradiance presented in the main text is averaged across both dry and wet seasons.

## RNA Extraction, Sequencing and Alignment
We sequenced the whole transcriptomes from retinal tissue all of 18 samples (n = 9 deep-benthic and n = 9 shallow-littoral individuals). RNA-sequencing raw data have been deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) database under the project accession number PRJEB91374. Retinal tissue was dissected from whole eye samples under a dissection microscope and then homogenized prior to RNA extraction to avoid bias in opsin expression between retinal regions (Dalton et al. 2014). Total RNA was extracted from retina tissue using RNeasy Mini Kits (Qiagen), following the manufacturer's instructions, including an on-column DNase purification. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with an RNA600 Nano Chip kit (Agilent). RNA-seq libraries were prepared and sequenced at the Bristol Genomics Facility (University of Bristol). Separate cDNA libraries were prepared for each individual. Libraries were prepared using TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation Kits (Illumina), in combination with a Poly-A selection step. Rico-zero Gold rRNA depletion was carried out prior to cDNA synthesis. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, to generate 100 bp paired-end reads at a sequencing depth of 30 million reads per individual.
Raw reads were processed prior to alignment. TruSeq adapters and low-quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v.0.36 (Bolger et al. 2014) with a minimum phred quality score of 20 and minimum read length of 50 bp. FastQC v. 0.11.8 (Andrews 2010) was used to assess the quality of reads before and after preprocessing. Cleaned reads were aligned to the Maylandia zebra reference genome (UMD2a, NCBI assembly: GCF_000238955.4; Conte et al. 2019) with STAR v.2.7.1a (Dobin et al. 2013), and gene expression was quantified using HTSeq v.0.11.1 (Anders et al. 2015). See supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online for sequencing and mapping statistics.

## Global vs Opsin-specific Expression
Read counts were normalized and filtered using the R-package DESeq2 v.1.28.1 (Love et al. 2014), removing genes with less than 10 read across 50% of samples. Filtered counts were then log transformed using the rlog function in DESeq2. To determine the extent of expression variation between benthic and littoral ecotypes, we performed two separate PCAs to visualize differences in the overall transcriptional profiles (based on the complete expressed gene set), and the subset of seven cone opsins (SWS1, SWS2A, SWS2B, RH2Aα, RHAβ, RH2B, and LWS). PCAs were performed using the R-package PCAmethods v.1.81.0 (Stacklies et al. 2007). To further examine overall differences in the retinal transcriptomes between benthic and littoral ecotypes, we conducted a DE analysis using negative binomial distribution models in DESeq2. All P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) correction (FDR < 0.05).

## Quantification of Opsin Coding Sequence Variation
To identify whether differences in visual systems were associated with changes in coding sequences of opsin genes, we conducted a genome-wide search of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and indels. First, variants were called using the physical mapping information from the STAR mapping alignments, for all 18 samples. Prior to calling SNPs and indels, duplicates were marked using Picard-tools v. 2.20.0 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Genetic variants were then called using Freebayes v. 0.9.21 (Garrison and Marth 2012), specifying a minimum coverage of three reads per sample to process a site and a minimum of 10 reads per sample to consider an alternative allele. Using VCFtools v. 0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011), we filtered the resulting SNP dataset to retain only biallelic SNPs, with a phred quality above 20, genotype quality above 30, a minor allele frequency of 5%, and were present in at least 80% of all individuals. Further filtering was performed using the vcffilter program within vcflib (Garrison 2022), specifying an allele depth balance 0.30 and 0.70. We then extracted all genetic variants present within opsin gene coding regions and quantified allelic variation at these sites across both ecotypes.
To determine whether identified allelic variants within opsin genes were associated with substitutions in amino acid sequences, we extracted the coding sequence alignments for each opsin from our STAR alignments using the view command in Samtools v. 1.8 (Li et al. 2009). We then imported our extracted opsin-specific alignments into Geneious Prime v. 2024.0 (https://www.geneious.com) and generated a consensus CDS for each individual (with a 50% strict threshold). Consensus files were the translated to protein coding sequences and aligned against the M. zebra reference proteome to identify amino acid substitutions produced by allelic variants.

## Sample Collection for Anal Fin Pigmentation Analysis
Shallow-littoral (n = 43) and deep-benthic (n = 68) specimens were collected at the depths of 3 and 20 m, respectively, with deep and shallow fish being collected across consecutive days from the 11th to 13th of April 2018. Once at the surface, fish were anaesthetized by immersion in 0.1% MS222. Specimens were then pinned against a styrofoam board and photographed using a Panasonic Lumix DMC GX7 Lumix camera with a Panasonic Lumix G 20 mm pancake lens in automatic mode. A CameraTrax 24Colorcard color checker card was included in the photo of each fish for color calibration purposes. We highlight that depth-related differences may have introduced variation in stress exposure between the shallow and deep ecotypes. While some degree of sampling-induced color variation is possible, we believe it is unlikely to systematically bias our comparisons of egg-spot traits between ecotypes.

## Anal Fin Pigmentation Analysis
Color was quantified from these digital photographs in the CIE 1976 L*, a*, b* (CIELAB) color space, which measures the perception of color along a white–black lightness axis (L*), a red–green axis (a*), and a yellow–blue axis (b*). The average CIELAB values for each color square in the color checker card are given on the file CTrax_lab.txt as specified by the manufacturer (downloadable on https://github.com/Santos-cichlids/carruthersetal2024-masoko-vision-eggspots).
We corrected photo colors in R v.4.1.2 using the relationship between observed and true color values in order to avoid confounding by differences in ambient lighting and camera settings. Specifically, we used the calibrateLab function of the quantifyColor.R script (https://github.com/Santos-cichlids/carruthersetal2024-masoko-vision-eggspots). As input files we used the specimen and color card photograph in jpeg format (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online), a text file with the true CIELAB values for each color square in the CameraTrax 24Colorcard provided by the manufacturer (CTrax_lab.txt), and text files with the XY coordinates of each color square in the jpeg file. To generate the XY coordinate files, we opened each jpeg in ImageJ v.2.0.0-rc-68/1.52i, selected a region of interest (ROI) for each of the 24 color squares and saved it as an XY coordinate file. Note that during the course of the experiment some isolated regions of the CameraTrax card experienced water damage so care was taken to exclude these regions from the XY coordinate selection.
The function calibrateLab converts the image files from sRGB to CIE 1976 L*a*b* (CIELAB) color space with Illuminant D50 as the reference white light color. Then the average L*, a*, and b* values are quantified among the pixels contained in each XY coordinate selection. The measured color card L*a*b* values with respect to Illuminant D50 were linearly related to the values specified by the manufacturer, and so for each photo we fit a linear model that mapped the observed CameraTrax colors to their true values. These mapping functions were then used to recalibrate colors for anal fin features, thereby reducing color variation among photos attributable to technical artifacts such as lighting differences (see supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online: c–e for examples of fit between observed and true (expected values).
Color quantification of different anal fin regions was then performed using the function roilab also coded in the quantifyColor.R script (https://github.com/Santos-cichlids/carruthersetal2024-masoko-vision-eggspots). As inputs, we used the mapping functions described above and image files (png format) containing only the region of the photograph for which color was measured. To generate such files, GIMP software version 2.10 was used to manually select and extract the regions of interest. The color from three anal fin regions was measured: (i) egg-spots, (ii) proximal black region, and (iii) the red distal rim (see example in supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). We further quantified the color from one of orange color squares in the CameraTrax 24Colorcard color (square number seven in supplementary fig. ## Inputs to Visual Modeling Including Visual Sensitivities, Light Environments and Color Reflectances
We used visual modeling to test whether shifts in visual systems between the shallow-littoral and deep-benthic ecotypes might enhance their ability to detect male signaling traits. Modeling was performed from the visual perspectives of both ecotypes. Visual systems were constructed by determining the visual pigment peak (λmax) for each opsin. To do this, A. calliptera opsin sequences were aligned with the corresponding opsin sequences of cichlid species with known peak spectral sensitivities. Most of the sequences were nearly identical. Peak spectral values were taken as those of A. burtoni [455 nm (SWS2A), 523 nm (RH2Aa), and 562 nm (LWS)], Pseudotropheus sp. “acei” [378 nm (SWS1)] or the average of several other cichlids [420 nm (SWS2B), 481 nm (RH2B), and 512 nm (RH2Aβ)] (supplementary table S8, Supplementary Material online; gained from Fernald and Liebman 1980; Parry et al. 2005; Spady et al. 2006). Visual pigments were simulated using the equations of Govardovskii et al. (2000) by assuming either pure A1 chromophore or a mixture of up to 50% A1 and A2 chromophore, calculating the A2 visual pigment λmax,A2 from λmax, A1 following Parry and Bowmaker (2002). Finally, we calculated sensitivity curves for three different pigments sets each with three visual pigments. The first assumed pure expression of the primary opsins (SWS2A, RH2Aα, and LWS). This assumes that the cichlid retinal area centralis used to focus on objects is simpler in its gene expression than the retinal periphery (Dalton et al. 2017). The other two were weighted by the proportional expression of each opsin in all individuals from the corresponding ecotypes (supplementary table S9, Supplementary Material online). In our models, we assumed coexpression of opsins within photoreceptor types based on established patterns in other cichlid systems (Carleton et al. 2008; Dalton et al. 2017). This assumed one cone coexpressing SWS opsins (SWS1, SWS2B, and SWS2A), another cone coexpressing RH2 opsins (RH2Aβ, RH2Aα, and RH2Aβ) and the last cone expressing LWS opsin.
We utilized the different light environments as the illuminants of the different fish colorations. For this, we used normalized side-welling irradiance measurements obtained from Lake Masoko, using measurements between 2 to 10 m and 20 to 24 m to represent the shallow- and deep-water habitats, respectively. The spectra were normalized to the peak and then averaged across different depths to obtain an average for the two habitats.
Because we did not have actual color reflectance spectra for wild caught A. calliptera, we took an agnostic approach that utilized a set of colors spread across the color spectrum but motivated by previously measured Lake Malawi cichlid color reflectances (Dalton et al. 2010). Cichlid colors are typically either peaked shape for violet, blue or green colors or a rising step for yellow, orange and red colors. Peaked colors were calculated as Gaussians with a peak wavelength, a peak width (55 to 75 nm) and a 10% baseline offset. Stepped colors were calculated from a half of a Gaussian peak with a flat baseline (10%) for shorter wavelengths and flat peak at longer wavelengths. We also included black and white, which were very broad Gaussians with a 500 nm peak and a 350 nm width. Examples of these Gaussian colors in comparison to color reflectances measured from wild caught Lake Malawi Maylandia zebra are shown in supplementary fig. S12, Supplementary Material online. A set of Gaussian colors with variable peaks was generated with peaked shapes below 550 nm and stepped shapes at 550 nm and higher. The colors were spaced at 1 nm and then complimented with broad black and white Gaussians (peak 550 nm; peak width 350 nm, white magnitude 1.0, black 0.1).

## Figure Fig. 7.: Hypothesized stages of divergence in visual systems and signaling traits in Lake Masoko. Step 1. Colonization of the shallow-littoral habitat in Lake Masoko by riverine A. calliptera ∼10,000 years ago (Malinsky et al. 2015) with visual system adaptation matched to the broad spectrum, daylight environment. Step 2. Colonization of the deep-benthic habitat ∼1,000 years ago (Malinsky et al. 2015). Occupancy of the darker, blue–green shifted light environment is facilitated by shifts in visual systems, with fish in the deep-water habitat exhibiting narrower, mid-spectrum, blue–green visual sensitivities. Step 3. Establishment in the deep-benthic habitat. Adaptation of the visual system to the darker, deep-water habitat promote shifts in the color of male signaling traits, maximizing their conspicuousness in the deep-water light environment and perceptibility to conspecifics. We suggest that the rapid adaptation of visual systems to contrasting light regimes is largely facilitated by shifts in opsin expression profiles, followed by divergent adaptation of male signaling traits (male anal fin egg-spots), with trait color evolving to maximize their visibility and function within a given light environment. Overall, our results suggest a primary role of natural selection in driving the early stages of ecological speciation in Lake Masoko, reinforced by subsequent selection on male secondary sexual traits.
