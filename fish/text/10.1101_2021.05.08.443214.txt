Abstract

13

Photopigments, consisting of an opsin protein bound to a light-sensitive chromophore, are at

14

the centre of vertebrate vision. The vertebrate ancestor already possessed four cone opsin

15

classes involved in colour perception during bright-light conditions, which are sensitive from

16

the ultraviolet to the red-wavelengths of light. Teleosts experienced an extra round of whole

17

genome duplication (3R) at their origin, and while most teleosts maintained only one long-

18

wavelength-sensitive opsin gene (LWS1), the second ancestral copy (LWS2) persisted in

19

characins and osteoglossomorphs. Following 3R, teleost opsins have continued to expand and

20

diversify, which is thought to be a consequence of the different light environment fishes

21

inhabit, from clear streams to the relative darkness of the deep-sea. Although many recent and

22

a few ancestral opsin duplicates can be found, none predating the 3R were thought to exist. In

23

this study we report on a second, previously unnoticed ancestral duplication of the red-sensitive

24

opsin (LWS3), which predates the teleost-specific genome duplication and only persists in

25

gobiid fishes. This is surprising, since it implies that LWS3 has been lost at least 19-20 times

26

independently along the teleost phylogeny. Mining 109 teleost genomes we also uncover a

27

third lineage, the elopomorphs, that maintained the LWS2 copy. We identify convergent amino

28

acid changes that green-shift ancestral and recent LWS copies, leading to adaptive

29

differentiation and the functional replacement of the original green-sensitive RH2 opsin.

30

Retinal transcriptomes and in-situ hybridisation show that LWS3 is expressed to various extents

31

in gobies and in the case of the whitebarred goby, Amblygobius phalaena, it occurs in a separate

32

photoreceptor to LWS1. Our study highlights the importance of comparative studies to

33

comprehend evolution of gene function.

Introduction

37

Animals rely on vision for a variety of fundamental tasks such as foraging, mating, to avoid

38

predators, and to navigate through their environment. At the molecular level, the visual process

39

is initiated when light is absorbed by photopigments, located in the outer segments of the retinal

40

photoreceptors (Wald 1968; Hunt et al. 2014). Vertebrate photopigments consist of light-

41

sensitive vitamin-A derived chromophores which are covalently bound to one of five different

42

visual opsin protein types. These five types were already present in the vertebrate ancestor and

43

are thought to be the product of two rounds of whole-genome duplication (2R) ~ 500 million

44

years ago (Mya) (Lamb, 2013). They are the dim-light active rod opsin (rhodopsin; RH1) which

45

typically operates in the blue-green light spectrum [peak spectral sensitivity (λmax) ~ 445 to

46

525nm], and four differently tuned cone opsins: short-wavelength-sensitive 1 and 2 (SWS1:

47

λmax ~ 345 to 440 nm; SWS2: λmax ~ 395 to 490 nm), rhodopsin-like 2 (RH2: λmax ~ 450 to 540

48

nm), and mid/long-wavelength-sensitive (MWS/LWS: λmax ~ 490 to 575 nm) (Yokoyama

49

2008; Carleton et al. 2020). The different cone-based photopigments are active during bright-

50

light conditions where they are often involved in colour vision (Hunt et al. 2014).

51

The evolution of vertebrate visual opsins is characterised by changes in opsin gene

52

numbers, gene structure and gene expression (Hunt et al. 2014; Lamb 2019). Unlike in

53

terrestrial vertebrates, where the basic opsin setup was either maintained [e.g., birds (Borges et

54

al., 2015), reptiles (Katti et al., 2019)] or reduced [e.g., mammals (Jacobs, 2013)], opsin genes

55

have continued to proliferate in teleost fishes (reviewed in Carleton et al. 2020 and Musilova

56

et al., 2021) – with nearly 35,000 species, the most species-rich clade of vertebrates (Fricke et

57

al., 2021). Teleosts are extremely diverse in ecology, physiology and morphology as they have

58

conquered almost every aquatic habitat (few species have even transitioned to land) from clear

59

mountain streams to the relative darkness of the deep-sea. It is those differences in photic

60

environment and life history traits that are the main drivers for opsin gene diversity and

61

evolution in fishes (Carleton et al. 2020).

62

Opsin gene evolution affects teleost vision at all taxonomic levels from species over

63

families to entire classes (Cortesi et al. 2015; Lin et al. 2017; Musilova et al. 2019; reviewed

64

in Carleton et al. 2020 and Musilova et al., 2021). Novel opsins commonly arise through

65

tandem duplication but, as for the ancestral five types, may also be the product of whole-

66

genome duplications (reviewed in Musilova et al., 2021) and in some rare cases may arise from

67

retrotransposition (Mano et al. 1999; Ward et al. 2008). While most genes are lost shortly after

68

duplication and disappear or become non-functional pseudogenes (Walsh, 2003), some genes

69

persist by acquiring novel functions. This neofunctionalization of visual opsins usually

70

involves changes in ‘key’ amino acid sites of the retinal binding pocket, that shift the spectral

71

sensitivity of the photopigment (Yokoyama 2008; Carleton et al. 2020). Neofunctionalization

72

can also occur due to differences in gene expression with ontogeny (Carleton et al. 2008;

73

Cortesi et al. 2016), or location in the retina or other tissues (Davies et al. 2015; Morrow et al.

74

2016). Teleost opsins are also prone to gene conversion, a form of reticulate evolution whereby

75

sequence information is unidirectionally exchanged between sister genes (Cortesi et al. 2015;

76

Sandkam et al. 2017). Gene conversion has the effect of homogenising paralogs, which may

77

repair and sometimes even resurrect gene function (Cortesi et al. 2015).

78

The genomes of extant teleosts contain a median of seven visual opsin genes: one rod

79

opsin and six cone opsins (Musilova et al. 2019). Most of the cone opsin expansions can be

80

attributed to more recent lineage or species-specific tandem duplications of the RH2 gene,

81

which is sensitive to the most common, blue to green light spectrum underwater (Carleton et

82

al. 2020). Several older tandem duplications have also persisted, such as found in the neoteleost

83

and percomorph violet to blue sensitive SWS2 duplicates (Cortesi et al. 2015). Interestingly, at

84

their origin ~ 320 Mya, teleosts underwent a third round of whole-genome duplication (3R)

85

(Ravi and Vankatesh 2018) and two extant opsin gene duplicates, one RH1 (Chen et al., 2018)

86

and one LWS (Liu et al. 2019), have been attributed to this event. The ancestral LWS2 duplicate,

87

has long been overlooked but has recently been described from bony tongues

88

(Osteglossiformes) and characins (Characiformes) (Liu et al. 2019; Escobar-Camacho et al.

89

2020).

90

Most teleosts possess a single LWS copy (LWS1) which is sensitive from the yellow to

91

red spectrum (Carleton et al. 2020), or they might have lost the gene altogether (Musilova et

92

al. 2019). LWS2 in extant species, but likely already in the ancestor shortly after its duplication,

93

has shifted its spectral sensitivity to shorter, green-wavelengths as compared to LWS1

94

(Escobar-Camacho et al. 2020). This is similar to the green shifted MWS/LWS duplicate in

95

primates, which re-enabled trichromatic vision after the loss of RH2 in the mammalian ancestor

96

(Carvalho et al., 2017). Concurrently, both bony tongues and characins have either lost or have

97

severely reduced the expression of the green-sensitive RH2, suggesting that there is a trade-off

98

between the overlap of opsin spectral sensitivities and the potential for evolutionary retention

99

(Liu et al. 2019; Escobar-Camacho et al. 2020). Interestingly, a shift towards shorter spectral

100

sensitivities can also be found in some more recent LWS1 duplicates but also between different

101

alleles of the same gene (Seehausen et al. 2008; Sandkam et al. 2018). Given the repeated

102

pattern of LWS duplication followed by occupation of the green-light niche, it is surprising that

103

teleosts only possess such a limited number of these genes. It is also intriguing, considering

104

that the predicted duplication rate of genes is 100 My (Lynch and Conery, 2000), that no opsin

105

gene duplicates from the time between 2R and 3R (~ 200 My) have been found so far.

106

In this study, we explored the evolutionary history of the LWS opsins in teleost fishes

107

from a phylogenetic representative sample of more than 100 species. Combining next

108

generation genome and transcriptome sequencing with open-source data mining, we uncover a

109

rich history of gene duplication, loss and gene conversion. This includes several recent LWS1

110

duplications and an extra order, which has retained the LWS2 copy. Surprisingly, we also

111

discovered another major LWS duplication that predates the teleost-specific genome

112

duplication and has only been retained in gobiid fishes. In a remarkable example of convergent

113

evolution, this ancestral LWS3 copy has also shifted its spectral sensitivity to shorter

114

wavelengths. Like for the species that have retained LWS2, retinal transcriptomes in various

115

goby species revealed a reduction or a loss in expression of the green sensitive RH2 opsin.

116

Using in-situ hybridisation in a species that expresses LWS1 and LWS3, the whitebarred goby,

117

Amblygobius phalaena, we show that each gene is expressed in a separate photoreceptor. A.

118

phalaena also expresses two SWS2 opsins located in a third photoreceptor, thus this goby is

119

likely to have a trichromatic visual system sensitive to blue, green and red wavelengths.

Results and Discussion

122

LWS duplication and gene synteny

123

Reconstructing a comprehensive LWS phylogeny based on 109 teleost genomes and including

124

non-teleost ray-finned (Actinopterygian), lobe-finned (Sarcopterygii), and several ancestrally

125

derived fishes as outgroups, we first uncover an ancestral LWS3 duplication in gobies

126

(Gobiiformes), which seems to predate the teleost-specific genome duplication (Fig. 1, and Fig.

127

S1). We also find that Elopiformes (tarpons, ladyfish and relatives) in addition to the bony

128

tongues and characins have retained the LWS2 copy, which emerged during 3R (Liu et al. 2019;

129

Escobar-Camacho et al. 2020). Since LWS in non-teleost ray-finned fishes (bichir, sturgeon,

130

gar and bowfin) is ancestral to the LWS1-3 paralogs found in modern teleosts, it is likely that

131

all three duplicates originated in the teleost ancestor (Fig. 2 and Fig. S1).

132

Gobies are part of the crown euteleosts and diverged from their closest sister lineage,

133

cardinalfishes and nurseryfishes (both Kurtiformes), around 100 Mya (Rabosky et al. 2018).

134

Considering the most direct phylogenetic route from the teleost ancestor to the goby ancestor

135

and ignoring any possibly extinct lineages, LWS3 must have been lost at least 19-20 times

136

independently in the teleost radiation; a truly amazing feat considering how numerous and

137

diverse teleost fishes are (Fig. 1). An alternative explanation for how gobies might have

138

acquired LWS3 is through horizontal gene transfer. While arguably unlikely, horizontal gene

139

transfer has been shown to contribute to genetic diversity in sea-snakes which have several

140

transposable elements that are most like the ones found in teleosts and corals (Galbraith et al.

141

2020). However, based on the vertebrate genomes we surveyed; it is currently not possible to

142

discern a potential hypothetical donor for LWS3.

143

Another astounding finding from our phylogenetic reconstruction is that the LWS genes

144

of some tetrapods (amphibians, snakes, lizards, mammals) and the non-tetrapod lungfish form

145

a cluster within the teleosts, while the LWS genes in other tetrapods (birds, turtles) follow the

146

species tree with a topology outside the ray-finned fishes (Fig. S1). This pattern is puzzling,

147

but it does indicate that there has been even more ancestral LWS duplications, possibly in the

148

common ancestor of ray-finned and lobe-finned fishes. More exhaustive sampling of the

149

tetrapod lineage is needed to fully resolve the LWS evolutionary history in this case.

150

A closer inspection of the syntenic region (10 genes upstream and downstream)

151

surrounding the different teleost LWS opsins, revealed a highly conserved structure for LWS1,

152

which is located between HCFC1 or SWS2 upstream and GNL3L downstream. The synteny for

153

LWS2 is less conserved as it is located on different chromosomal stretches in bony tongues,

154

and characins. Lastly, LWS3 in gobies is located between RAB32 upstream and STXBP5 or

155

androglobin downstream, a different chromosomal region compared to LWS1. In other teleosts

156

including the cardinalfishes, this region contains no traces of an opsin gene (Supp Fig S2). It

157

is possible that both LWS2 and LWS3 have relocated over evolutionary time or that LWS3

158

emerged from transposition in the first place. Indeed, a recent study in African cichlids has

159

revealed that transposable elements are a key factor in shaping the evolutionary dynamics of

160

opsin genes in fishes (Carleton et al. 2020). This is also highlighted by the fact that both, the

161

teleost RH1 and one of the LWS1 copies in guppies and other livebearers (Atherinomorpha),

162

originated from retrotransposition (Mano et al. 1999, Sandkam et al. 2017, Ward et al. 2008).

163

Alternatively, the synteny might no longer be discernible as the duplicated genes in the original

164

syntenic region were lost at different rates between fish lineages or the chromosomes were

165

rearranged.

LWS evolutionary history

168

Overall, we found that most shallow marine and freshwater fishes have kept at least one copy

169

of the longer wavelength sensitive LWS1 gene. On the contrary, many deeper-living species,

170

including members of the anglerfishes (Lophiiformes), cusk-eels (Ophidiiformes) and toad

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.08.443214; this version posted May 9, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

171

fishes (Batrachoidiformes), have lost LWS altogether. Similarly, the deep-water Sanzo’s goby,

172

Lesueurigobius sanzi, has lost both LWS1 and LWS3 copies (Fig. 1 and Fig. 3). This might not

173

be surprising as longer wavelengths are quickly absorbed with depth (Jerlov 1976), which

174

would make the use of LWS obsolete (also see Musilova et al. 2019). Various groups also have

175

more recent LWS1 duplicates (Fig. 1). These are the result of lineage specific whole-genome

176

duplications (e.g., Cypriniformes, Salmoniformes, Esociformes) and in many cases tandem

177

duplications (e.g., in Atherinomorpha and Anabantiformes). LWS1 duplications are common

178

in species that live in murky, red-shifted waters such as some characins (Escobar-Camacho et

179

al. 2020) and pike (Esociformes). However, LWS1 also seems to proliferate in species that

180

show elaborate yellow, orange and red body patterns such as wrasses (Labriformes) and

181

livebearers (Phillips et al., 2016). In the most extreme cases, brown trout, Salmo trutta

182

(Salmoniformes), fighting fish, Betta splendens (Anabantiformes), the humphead or Napoleon

183

wrasse, Cheilinus undulatus, and the slingjaw wrasse, Epibulus insidiator (both Labriformes),

184

have acquired five LWS1 paralogs (Figs. 1 and 2). Tandem duplications can also be found for

185

both, LWS2 and LWS3. While bony tongues have a single copy of LWS2, the Indo-Pacific

186

tarpon, Megalops cyprinoides (Elopiformes), has two copies and characins may have up to

187

three copies of the gene (Fig. 1) (see Escobar Camacho et al. 2020 for three LWS2 copies in

188

Piabucina panamensis). In the cases where LWS2 has duplicated, it always co-occurs with

189

LWS1 (Fig. 1; Escobar Camacho et al. 2020). However, this is not the case for the only LWS3

190

duplication we found, in which case LWS1 is missing from the genome of the bluebanded goby,

191

Lythrypnus dalli (Fig. 4).

192

Gene conversion between LWS1 paralogs but also between LWS1 and LWS2 (Escobar-

193

Camacho et al. 2020) is quite common (e.g., Sandkam et al. 2017; Escobar-Camacho et al.

194

2020). In characins, gene conversion seems to affect mostly the first couple of exons, while the

195

regions that carry the key tuning sites on exons 3 – 5, remain unaffected (Escobar-Camacho et

196

al. 2020). This is not the case in poeciliids, where gene conversion between LWS1-1 and LWS1-

197

3 seems ubiquitous, albeit in the guppies (Poecilia) which rely more heavily on red-colouration

198

for mate choice, gene conversion seems less prevalent (Sandkam et al. 2017). Using GARD

199

(Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2006) to detect recombination points between LWS1 and LWS3, we

200

found two weakly supported break points in exons 2 (position 287 bp; 42% support) and 5 (903

201

bp; 33% support). A subsequent sliding window analysis measuring the neutral divergence

202

along duplicates [rate of synonymous substitutions per synonymous sites (dS)] supported

203

partially by the single-exon phylogenies, confirmed that gene conversion has most likely

204

affected the region surrounding at least the first break point. However, the strength of the signal

209

210

211

212

213

Convergent shifts to green vision in LWS duplicates

216

Changes in five key tuning sites, the so called ‘five-sites rule’ (human MWS/LWS, amino acid

217

position: 180, 197, 277, 285 and 308; bovine rhodopsin equivalent: 164, 181, 261, 269 and

218

292), can explain most, albeit not all (e.g., Chi et al. 2020), major spectral sensitivity shifts of

219

the LWS photopigments in vertebrates (Yokoyama and Radlwimmer 1998; Yokoyama et al.

220

2008). As for the primate MWS/LWS case (Carvalho et al., 2017), LWS2 in characins and

221

bony tongues but also several recent teleost LWS1 copies have functionally shifted to green

222

sensitivity via convergent changes in these five sites (Liu et al. 2019; Escobar-Camacho et al.

223

2020; Sandkam et al. 2018). Comparing these key tuning sites in LWS3, we also find a

224

convergent shift to shorter wavelength sensitivity in gobies (Table 1). Like the substitutions in

225

LWS2, changes in LWS3 mostly affected sites 180 and 277, where shorter shifts are caused by

226

substitutions from serine to alanine and tyrosine to phenylalanine, respectively. However, as

227

opposed to LWS2 where changes in 285 were also common, we only found one such change

228

from the longer shifted threonine to the shorter shifted alanine in the LWS3 of the great blue-

229

spotted mudskipper, Boleophthalmus pectinirostris (Table 1) (also see You et al. 2014).

230

Spectral sensitivity predictions for the goby LWS paralogs largely overlapped with measured

231

mid-wavelength-sensitive (MWS) and long-wavelength-sensitive photoreceptor sensitivities

232

from a variety of other goby species. However, several of the measured MWS values (λmax =

233

527 – 531 nm) were slightly shorter than many of the predictions for LWS3 (λmax = 533 – 550

234

nm) (Pierotti et al. 2020 and references therein). This could be explained if amino acids at sites

235

other than the five commonly acknowledged ones are also tuning LWS3. Alternatively, it may

236

be that the goby MWS photoreceptors express the green-sensitive RH2 opsin or a mixture of

237

RH2 and LWS3 rather than pure LWS3. Coexpression of RH2 with LWS2 has been reported

238

for some shorter shifted characin photoreceptors (Escobar-Camacho et al. 2020).

Neofunctionalization of LWS3 in gobies

241

To test which visual opsins gobies use and what photoreceptors they might be expressed in, we

242

sequenced the retinal transcriptomes of ten species from nine different genera and representing

243

most goby subfamilies (Fig. 3). Five out of the ten species were found to express LWS3 to

244

various degrees. In eight species LWS1 was the dominant paralog. However, LWS3 was the

245

highest expressed copy in the sub-family Sycidiinae, with the red lipstick goby, Syciopus

246

rubicundus, only expressing this copy and the rainbow goby, Stiphodon ornatus, showing very

247

low levels of LWS1 expression (Fig. 4C). All gobies also expressed at least one copy of SWS2,

248

albeit at very low levels in the Sycidiinae and the spinycheek sleeper, Eleotris pisonis. RH2

249

on the other hand, was either not found to be expressed or only very marginally expressed in

250

most species, except for the Sycidiinae where RH2 was expressed at levels similar to the SWS2

251

expression in other species.

252

Using fluorescent in-situ hybridisation in A. phalaena, revealed that the SWS2 opsins,

253

SWS2B and SWS2Aβ, were expressed exclusively in the single cones (Fig. 4 A, B). Among

254

single cones, SWS2B and SWS2Aβ showed a varying degree of coexpression in the same single

255

cone photoreceptors, with SWS2Aβ expressed in virtually every single cone, and SWS2B only

256

expressed in some but not (or perhaps only lowly expressed) in others (Fig. 4 A-C). LWS1 and

257

LWS3, were exclusively expressed in double cones, two single cones that are fused together

258

and may also be optically coupled (Fig 4 D-F). Among these, LWS1 was expressed in one

259

double cone member and LWS2 was expressed in the other double cone member (Fig. x F).

260

This shows that for A. phalaena at least, LWS3 has acquired an independent function which

261

together with the SWS2 and LWS1 expressing photoreceptors, is likely to provide the species

262

with trichromatic vision.

Transcriptome sequencing

335

Retinal RNA was extracted using a Qiagen mini kit column extraction following the

336

manufacturer’s protocol and including a DNAse step. Library preparation (unstranded, 300 bp

337

insert) and transcriptome sequencing (RNAseq HiSeq Paired-End PE150) was outsourced to

Assembly and gene extraction

341

Transcriptome raw reads have been filtered and mapped with Medium sensitivity settings

342

against the general goby reference (based on the genome of Neogobius melanostomus; Adrian-

343

Kalchauser et al., 2019) in the Geneious 9.0 software (https://www.geneious.com). Species

344

specific gene sequences have been retrieved from the mapped alignments and used as a species-

345

specific reference for another round of mapping with the Lowest sensitivity settings. Number

346

of reads have been calculated to the FPKM (fragments per kilobase and million reads) and the

347

proportion of each opsin from the total cone opsin gene expression has been calculated. More

348

details on the protocols described in de Busserolles et al. 2017 and Tettamanti et al. 2019.

Whole genome analysis

351

High quality teleost whole genomes have been downloaded from GenBank (accession numbers

352

as per sample Table). Majority of the high-quality genomes has been sequenced by the Sanger

353

Institute (Rhie et al., 2021). We further complemented this data set by the genomic data of

354

lower quality (draft genomes as per Cortesi et al., 2015 and Musilova et al., 2019). Assembled

355

genomic scaffolds / chromosomes have been mapped against the general single exon reference

356

of the example LWS1 (Oreochromis niloticus), LWS2 (Astyanax mexicanus) and LWS3

357

(Neogobius melanostomus) copy. Mapping has been performed in Geneious 9.1.8

361

363

Phylogenetic analysis

364

Coding sequences of the single LWS genes have been aligned in Geneious 9.1.8 using the

365

MAFFT Alignment plugin (Katoh et al., 2019). Five independent runs (each with two parallel

366

runs) have been performed in MrBayes 3.1.2 (Ronquist and Huelsenbeck, 2003) software for

367

50 million generations. The output files have been manually inspected in the Tracer 1.5

368

(https://github.com/beast-dev/tracer/releases) for reaching the lnL plateau, the best -lnL score

369

and to identify the burnin break point. Three out of the ten independent output runs have been

370

used for the consensus tree construction with the burnin set to 50%.

371
372

Gene conversion analysis

373

GARD (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2006) was run using general discrete site-to-site variation with

374

three rate classes on the Datamonkey platform (www.datamonkey.org) (Weaver et al. 2018)

375

on an alignment of LWS1 and LWS3 from goby species for which both copies were recovered

376

(Fig. 3). The sliding window analysis followed the methods described in Cortesi et al. 2015. In

377

short, the intraspecific dS (neutral process) along the coding sequences of LWS1 and LWS3

378

was calculated using a window size of 30 and a step size of 1 in DNAsp v.6 (Rozas et al. 2017).

In-situ hybridisation

381

Dual-labelling RNA-FISH was performed on two wholemount retinas (left and right)

382

of one adult A. phalaena collected at Lizard Island on 16 March 2021 following standard

383

protocols (e.g., Barthel and Raymond 2000, Raymond and Barthel 2004, Allison et al. 2010,

384

Dalton et al. 2014, Dalton et al. 2015). Retinas were prepared as follows. After one-hour dark

385

adaptation, the eyes were enucleated, vitreous was enzymatically digested, and retinas were

386

extracted from the eyecups. The retinal pigment epithelium layer (RPE) was removed by PBS-

387

jetting using a single use syringe and needle (Terumo). Retinas were flattened and pinned,

388

photoreceptor side down, in Sylgard-filled petri dishes, and fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight.

389

Fixed retinas were triple washed in PBS for 10 min, briefly rinsed in 70% methanol in PBS,

390

and then transferred to 100% methanol and stored at -20°C.

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.05.08.443214; this version posted May 9, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is
made available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

391

Previously extracted retinal mRNA was reverse transcribed using a High Capacity

392

RNA-to-cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Riboprobe templates were

393

synthesized from cDNA via standard PCR using opsin specific primers (Table S1) and Q5

394

High Fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs). Primers were designed to bind to the

395

coding sequence of target opsins (SWS2B, SWS2Aβ, LWS1, LWS3) and to contain a T3 or T7

396

RNA polymerase promoter sequence at their 5’-ends (T3, reverse primer; T7, forward primer).

397

Amplicons were isolated via gel-electrophoresis and gel-extraction (Qiagen Gel Extraction

398

Kit), and subsequently used as template in enrichment PCR using the same primers. Anti-sense

399

riboprobes labelled with either digoxigenin-UTP (DIG) or fluorescein-UTP (FL) were

400

synthesized from cDNA templates via T3 RNA polymerase mediated strand-specific RNA

401

transcription using DIG/FL RNA labelling mix (Roche). Hybridised, labelled riboprobes were

402

detected using anti-digoxigenin or anti-fluorescein antibodies conjugated to horseradish

403

peroxidase (Roche). Fluorescent tagging was performed using Alexa Fluor 594 and 488 dyes

404

with streptavidin Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA, Invitrogen). Finally, retinas were

405

mounted in 70% glycerol in PBS, photoreceptor side up, on microscopy slides and covered

406

with a coverslip.

407

Fluorescent tagged photoreceptor cells were imaged using a CFI Apo Lambda 60x/1.4

408

NA oil immersion objective on a spinning disk confocal microscope (Diskovery, Andor

409

Technologies, UK) built around a Nikon Ti-E body (Nikon Corporation, Japan) equipped with

410

two Zyla 4.2 sCMOS cameras (Andor Technology), and controlled by Nikon NIS Elements

411

software (Nikon Corporation, Japan). Exported images were further processed (max. intensity

412

z-stack projection, channel merging) with ImageJ v. 1.52p (National Institute of Health, USA).
