Seeing in the Deep: Evolution of the Opsin Gene Expression in Bermin Crater Lake Cichlids
Monika Kłodawska, Adrian Indermaur, Arnold Roger Bitja Nyom, Dmytro Omelchenko, Oldřich Bartoš, Zuzana Musilová (2025). Molecular Biology and Evolution. https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msaf286. doi:10.1093/molbev/msaf286. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Seeing in the Deep: Evolution of the Opsin Gene Expression in Bermin Crater Lake Cichlids

## Abstract
AbstractCichlid visual systems can evolve rapidly during adaptive radiations. The Bermin crater lake species flock in Cameroon, comprising 13 (nine valid and four undescribed) Coptodon species, offers an ideal model to investigate visual adaptation to the deep-water light environments. Here, we examine visual opsin genes sequences and expression using 109 retina transcriptomes, focusing on interspecific variation with habitat depth, as well as on seasonal changes in the migratory species between depths. All species possess a multichromatic system with at least five cone opsins. While opsin coding sequences show limited divergence—consistent with the flock's recent origin—opsin expression profiles vary substantially. Deep-water species showed reduced sws1 and sws2b expression, in line with lower UV and violet light availability in deeper waters. Unexpectedly, proportional expression of the red-sensitive lws opsin gene increases with depth, contrasting with patterns in other lacustrine cichlids. In the seasonally migrating species Coptodon imbrifernus, opsin expression is plastic, with decreased sws2b levels in deeper-dwelling dry-season individuals. To contextualize our findings, we compared Bermin cichlids to the older Barombi Mbo crater lake radiation. While single cone adaptations to the depth were convergent (loss of UV/violet sensitivity, enhanced blue sensitivity), double cone response diverged: lws expression was lost in Barombi Mbo while increased in Bermin deep-water species. Our findings suggest that plasticity in opsin expression plays a crucial role at the onset of sensory evolution, potentially paving the way for future genetic change. This study underscores the power of young systems like Bermin for uncovering the mechanisms driving early visual system diversification.

## Introduction
Vision is a crucial sense for an organism's survival. Numerous aquatic species possess specialized adaptations in their visual systems to better suit the light conditions of their habitats (Hauser et al. 2021; Musilová et al. 2021). In fishes, visual spectral sensitivities are often shaped by heterogeneous light environments depending on water clarity and ambient light (e.g. Seehausen et al. 2008).
The evolution of visual opsin genes has been more dynamic in teleost fishes than in other vertebrate clades, as evidenced in the repeated expansion and contraction of the visual opsin gene repertoire. On average, teleosts possess seven visual opsins genes in their genome, in comparison to two to four in most tetrapods (Cortesi et al. 2015; Lin et al. 2017; Musilová et al. 2019a, 2021), but in an extreme case of deep-sea spinyfins, there are as many as 40 visual opsins (Musilová et al. 2019a). Generally, teleost fishes have good color vision enabled by different photoreceptor types (one rod and four cone cell types; Baden et al. 2025), and these types differ in the opsin usage. The evolution of the visual system in teleosts is also shaped by environmental conditions, with different fish groups evolving adaptations to thrive in a wide range of habitats—from coral reefs (e.g. Tettamanti et al. 2019; Stieb et al. 2023) and deep lakes (e.g. Hunt et al. 1996; Ricci et al. 2022) to the most extreme modifications found in deep-sea species (Musilova and Cortesi 2025).
The visual system of cichlids has the evolutionary capacity to rapidly integrate multiple adaptations to the changing light environments (Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017) or to different habitats, such as in deep water of lakes (Musilová et al. 2019b). Extensive research of the evolution of East African cichlids' visual systems has revealed multiple mechanisms influencing visual adaptations: mutations in coding sequences, the expression of opsins, as well as chromophore usage (Carleton et al. 2016).
Cichlids possess eight visual opsin genes: one rhodopsin rh1 expressed in rods (also referred to as the standardized photoreceptor type PR0 by Baden et al. 2025) and seven cone opsin genes: sws1 (max. sensitive at 360 nm in Nile tilapia; Spady et al. 2006), sws2b (425 nm), and sws2a (456 nm), which are expressed in the single cones (PR4 and PR3 cell types, respectively), and rh2b (472 nm), rh2aβ (518 nm), rh2aα (528 nm), and lws (561 nm), which are expressed in the double cones in the retina (PR2 and PR1 cell types; note that fish double cones are not homolog to tetrapod double cones; Baden et al. 2025). In these photoreceptors, visual pigments are composed of the opsin protein and the retinal chromophore—a light-absorbing component. The absorption maximum of the light spectrum of the chromophore-opsin complex might be altered by opsin gene mutation leading to the amino acid substitution in the opsin protein (Yokoyama 2008). Some mutations have larger effect on the function, and these so-called key tuning sites tend to be placed in the seven transmembrane regions close to the retinal-binding pocket and usually involve changes in amino acid polarity or charge (Yokoyama and Yokoyama 1990; Chang et al. 1995; Yokoyama and Radlwimmer 1998; Kochendoerfer et al. 1999). Single amino acid changes might lead to shifts in chromophore-opsin complex sensitivity of between 2 and 35 nm (Asenjo et al. 1994; Bowmaker and Hunt 1999; Yokoyama 2000; Carleton and Kocher 2001).
Among the available visual opsin genes, usually only a subset is expressed in cichlids (Bowmaker 2008; Carleton et al. 2010; Carleton and Yourick 2020; Torres-Dowdall et al. 2021). The overall spectral sensitivities are tuned by different opsins included in these subsets, or by coexpression of multiple opsin genes in individual photoreceptors, and additionally by using different chromophores: A1 (11-cis retinal) or A2 (11-cis-3,4-dehydroretinal), or a mixture of both, which can shift the photopigment peak sensitivity toward longer wavelengths (Carleton and Kocher 2001; Dalton et al. 2014; Carleton et al. 2016). Hence, their visual systems can vary widely, even among closely related cichlid species within adaptive radiations (Levine et al. 1979; Fernald and Liebman 1980; van der Meer and Bowmaker 1995; Parry et al. 2005; Jordan et al. 2006; Carleton et al. 2016; Musilová et al. 2019b). In Malawi cichlids, for example, the short (expressing sws1, rh2b, rh2a), medium (expressing sws2b, rh2b, rh2a), and long (expressing sws2a, rh2a, lws) visual palettes are found in different species albeit having nearly identical seven cone opsin genes in their genomes (O’Quin et al. 2010; Carleton et al. 2016). In fact, the long palette with the dominant red-sensitive lws gene is the most common opsin set found in the ray-finned fishes in general (Lupše et al. 2022). The short and medium palettes dominated by green-sensitive opsins have been associated with clear water, nocturnality (Fogg et al. 2022), or deep-water habitat (Musilová et al. 2019b), or has evolved as a result of the lws gene loss (Lupše et al. 2021; Musilová and Cortesi 2023), whereas the longer palette is commonly found in species from the turbid, eutrophicated environment, such as Lake Victoria (Seehausen et al. 1997) or rivers (Escobar-Camacho et al. 2017).

## Seasonal and Phenotypic Plasticity in Fish Visual Systems
Seasonal plasticity of the visual system can be caused by differential opsin gene expression or by changes in the chromophore usage. For example, a coral reef species, Nagasaki damsel (Pomacentrus nagasakiensis), lives in habitats with variable water clarity altering the light spectrum. It has higher sws1 opsin expression in winter corresponding to clear conditions enhancing UV availability, whereas the turbid red-shifted waters reduce UV exposure and result in reduced sws1 expression in summer (Stieb et al. 2016; Shaughnessy and Cortesi 2024). A chromophore usage may also exhibit seasonal plasticity; in the red shiner (Cyprinella lutrensis), significantly higher proportions of the long-wavelength-shifting A2 chromophore were observed during the winter season and at turbid sites, indicating a potential seasonal and environmental modulation of chromophore-based spectral tuning (Foster et al. 2025).
In general, the phenotypic plasticity of the visual system (not necessarily related to seasons), as seen by the alternation of opsin gene expression, has been also demonstrated in cichlids (e.g. Nandamuri et al. 2017; Härer et al. 2019), or other coral-reef damselfishes and cardinalfishes, which alter their opsin expression within weeks to months (Luehrmann et al. 2018). Surgeonfishes also show remarkable plastic response to short-term deviations in light conditions (Fogg et al. 2023). Notably, opsin plasticity among damselfish is highly species-specific, potentially reflecting ecological divergence in visual tasks or phylogenetic constraints (Stieb et al. 2016). Interestingly, the differences observed in Masoko crater lake Astatotilapia cichlids are most likely result of plastic response of the visual system to the deep habitats (Carruthers et al. 2025). The meta-analysis of experimental studies on visual system plasticity revealed that opsin phenotypic plasticity is common among fish, but future research is still needed (Bertinetti and Torres-Dowdall 2025).

## Speciation and Environment
Cichlids are one of the most diverse teleost families famous for their rapid diversification and adaptation to various ecological niches utilizing different depths, foraging modes, food sources, and breeding systems (Barlow 2008). Adaptive radiation of cichlids, whether in the African Great lakes such as Malawi (Genner and Turner 2005; Turner 2007), Tanganyika, and Victoria (Bezault et al. 2011); in the smaller satellite (Masoko; Talbi et al. 2025); or in crater lakes, such as those in Nicaragua (Torres-Dowdall et al. 2021), or Barombi Mbo (Burress and Tan 2017), and Bermin (Stiassny et al. 1992), is often characterized by evolution of highly diversified phenotypes. These adaptations, such as body shape, morphology, or nuptial coloration (Salzburger 2018), may evolve over very short evolutionary timescales. The less diverse and evolutionary younger systems, such as crater lakes in Cameroon with 11 (Barombi Mbo), or 9 (plus four undescribed; Bermin) species, or a crater lake Masoko in Tanzania with two ecomorphs of one species provide an excellent opportunity to study visual evolution in greater detail focusing on the early phases of these adaptive evolutionary processes (Stiassny et al. 1992; Musilová et al. 2019a, 2019b; Talbi et al. 2025).
Bermin lake hosts an evolutionary young species flock (Stiassny et al. 1992; Schliewen et al. 1994), which is one of the youngest cases of cichlid adaptive radiation with no available age estimate, but similarly diversed Coptodon species from Lake Ejagham are dated to be only 9,000 year old (Stager et al. 2018). Nine valid species have been described from the lake (Stiassny et al. 1992), and we recognize further four undescribed species based on our field observation and unpublished data. These species are referred to as Coptodon sp. In relatively older radiation (around 1 million years ago; Schedel et al. 2019) from the Barombi Mbo lake in Cameroon, two deep-water species convergently shifted their visual sensitivity toward the center of the light spectrum prevalent in the deep environment (Musilová et al. 2019b). Similarly, Midas cichlids in clear Nicaraguan lakes also shifted their visual sensitivity toward shorter wavelengths as compared to the ancestral populations from the turbid environment (Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017). Speciation through the visual sensory system is well documented in Lake Victoria cichlids, where sexually selected blue and red color morphs are associated with shallow and deep waters, respectively (Seehausen et al. 2008). In Lake Malawi cichlids, a relationship between foraging mode and retinal sensitivity showed that SWS1, UV-sensitive opsin, is significantly associated with foraging preferences. In cichlids foraging on algae and phytoplankton, the overall retinal sensitivity is shifted toward the shorter wavelengths, and they display higher sws1 expression, than species foraging on fish or invertebrates (Hofmann et al. 2009).
Here, we examine the visual system in 13 (nine described and four undescribed) species of cichlids from the Coptodon species flock, from Lake Bermin, Cameroon, West Africa. Bermin is a small crater lake with 700 m in diameter and up to 14.5 m deep located in the volcanic chain in the South-West Region of Cameroon (Kling 1988; Stiassny et al. 1992). Due to its small size and nine described endemic cichlid species, it is considered to be a place of high endemism (Thieme et al. 2005). Similar to other crater lakes, Bermin is a deep, relatively oligotrophic lake with species differing partially in the habitats they occupy (Stiassny et al. 1992). While most of the species can be found in the shallow water, the deeper zones of the lake are inhabited by a strict deep-water specialist (Coptodon bythobates) and by a seasonal species residing in the deep during the dry season but migrating into a shallow waters for spawning in the rainy season (Coptodon imbrifernus) (Stiassny et al. 1992). This seasonal pattern resembles that of Myaka myaka, a species from an unrelated endemic radiation in Barombi Mbo (Trewavas et al. 1972; Musilová et al. 2019b). To complete the list, the deep zone of the Bermin lake is also inhabited by Coptodon spongotroktis, which is found commonly in the deep as well as in shallow habitats (Stiassny et al. 1992; our observation).
In this study, we focus on the molecular mechanisms of the visual adaptation, such as opsin gene diversity and gene expression, of what might be one of the evolutionary youngest examples of adaptive radiation in cichlids (Stiassny et al. 1992). We specifically tested whether opsin nucleotide sequences and/or opsin gene expression differ among the Bermin species. We hypothesized that species occupying deeper zones within the lake would exhibit differences in their visual systems, namely, in the proportions of different opsin genes in the expression profile. Additionally, we examined whether a species with seasonal depth preferences shows variation in opsin expression between the dry season—when it resides in deeper waters—and the rainy season, when it inhabits shallower zones. Lastly, we aim to compare Bermin cichlid species flock with another adaptive radiation from the Barombi Mbo lake, and we hypothesize that the visual systems of the deep-water species in both lakes could show similar ways of adaptations despite its evolution in parallel and from different ancestors (Musilová et al. 2019b).

## Results and Discussion
In this study, we investigated molecular mechanisms of the visual system adaptation in cichlids from the Bermin crater lake in Cameroon. Thanks to the relatively small size of this species flock, we were able to cover all 13 species (nine described plus four undescribed) from the Bermin radiation and examine their visual system in great detail. We analyzed both DNA and RNA to provide a comprehensive overview of visual evolution in the Bermin species flock. We believe that comparable adaptive radiations such as those in Barombi Mbo (Musilová et al. 2019b), Masoko (Carruthers et al. 2025), or the Nicaraguan crater lakes (Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017) will eventually allow for cross-system comparisons, identifying general mechanisms of adaptation of vision at the initial stages of diversification. To generalize beyond cichlids, certain patterns of opsin evolution regarding opsin expression are shared across teleosts, including developmental trajectories (Lupše et al. 2022) and phenotypic plasticity (Bertinetti and Torres-Dowdall 2025).
Our results, based on 109 sequenced retina transcriptomes and over 500 individual opsin gene sequences (Tables S1 and S2), show that visual system adaptation occurs primarily at the level of gene expression (Table 1), whereas there is little variation in opsin nucleotide sequences (Fig. 1; Table S3). More specifically, we found differences in proportional opsin gene expression that correlate with the depth at which different species occur (Figs. 2 and 3 and Table 1; Tables S4 to S7). Additionally, we report seasonal changes in expression between the dry and rainy seasons in a seasonal spawner, C. imbrifernus (Fig. 4 and Table 2). We have further performed the overall differential gene expression analysis for deep vs. shallow-water species pairs and for the dry- vs. rainy-season samples of C. imbrifernus (Figs. 3 and 4 and Table 3; Table S8). Lastly, we compare directions of visual adaptations in cichlids from the Bermin and Barombi Mbo crater lakes (Fig. 5).

In evolution, opsins have adapted to serve best given the particular habitat, life history, and developmental stages of the species (Seehausen et al. 2008; Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017; Lupše et al. 2022). Our results suggest very fine-tuning of the photoreceptors in evolutionary young species flock, but the photoreceptors carry long evolutionary history since the vertebrate ancestor who already had all types of cone opsins (Musilová et al. 2021; Baden 2024). The lws is the highest abundant cone opsin in the expression profiles of many species (Cortesi et al. 2021), and it is expressed in the most ancestral cone cell type, PR1 (as per Baden 2024; Baden et al. 2025). Some authors even claim the PR1 cones not to be lost in any vertebrate species except for the cone-less species (Baden et al. 2025), although the opsin itself, however, must be replaced by rh2 in cases where lws is not found (Musilová and Cortesi 2023). The fine-tuning of the lws to rh2 ratio involves the double cones in teleosts, whereas the sws2a to sws2b impacts the single cones (Musilová et al. 2021). Lws and rh2 are ancestral vertebrate genes, and sws2a and sws2b are younger, emerging at the Neoteleostei ancestor (Cortesi et al. 2015). In cichlids, all ancestral opsin types are present and actively involved in the visual system tuning, in many cases complemented also by the differential roles of younger evolutionary genes, namely, the percomorph rh2aα and rh2aβ (Musilová et al. 2019b; Ricci et al. 2022), but this is not the case in Bermin cichlids (Fig. 2). Applying single-cell transcriptomics to larger comparative datasets beyond traditional model species will enhance our understanding of the evolutionary origins of cell types and provide new insights into the regulation of opsin gene expression in the future (Baden et al. 2025; Musilova and Cortesi 2025).

## Nucleotide Diversity in the Opsin Gene Sequences
Coptodon from Lake Bermin, like other African cichlids, possess seven cone opsin genes in their genome (Carleton et al. 2016), and we sequenced all of them to explore nucleotide diversity within the entire Bermin flock. We found very low or no variation in opsin gene haplotypes, with SWS1, SWS2B, SWS2A, RH2B, and LWS also showing no variability in amino acid sequences (Fig. 1b). Within all species, only the two green-sensitive rh2aα and rh2aβ genes exhibited multiple alleles differing in their protein sequences (Fig. 1b). None of the variable amino acids (i.e. position 10 and 159; numbering respective to the bovine rhodopsin) are located in known key tuning sites or transmembrane regions, and thus, we do not expect major functional changes to result from these substitutions (Table S3).
In the evolutionary older fish assemblages, such as Tanganyika cichlids (Ricci et al. 2022) or Baikal sculpins (Hunt et al. 1996), amino acid mutations also alter the sensitivity of the rhodopsin (RH1) in the deep-water species. This is not the case in the much younger Bermin species flock with which has the same amino acid sequence of RH1 across all species (Fig. 1b).
The low sequence variation aligns with findings from other crater lake systems, such as the Barombi Mbo radiation (15 variable amino acid sites for all cone opsins, 0 to 4 per opsin; Musilová et al. 2019b), Midas cichlids from Nicaragua (eight sites; 0 to 4 per opsin; Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017), or the Pundamilia genus from Lake Victoria (at least eight sites—two genes not tested; 0 to 4 per opsin; Carleton et al. 2005). The Bermin species flock exhibits the lowest diversity among these systems, with three variable sites in total (two in rh2aβ and one in rh2aα and no sites in other opsins; Fig. 1b; Table S3). This is most likely due to its young evolutionary age, for which no direct estimate currently exists (Stiassny et al. 1992). However, indirect evidence points to an age of several thousand years, based on comparisons with Lake Ejagham—another crater lake in Cameroon that harbors a comparably aged and genetically diverse Coptodon radiation (Schliewen et al. 1994, 2001; Martin et al. 2015). Lake Ejagham has been estimated to be as young as 9,000 years (Stager et al. 2018).
With the Bermin species flock being one of the youngest known cichlid adaptive radiations (Stiassny et al. 1992; Schliewen et al. 1994), we faced challenges when reconstructing phylogenetic relationships among the different Bermin species. All nine valid (plus four undescribed) species are clearly distinguishable in vivo in the lake, mainly by their distinct nuptial coloration, size, and body/mouth shape. To provide an evolutionary context for our study, we relied on relationships reconstructed using RAD-seq (with multiple individuals per species; Fig. 2a; Table S1), which was the only method capable of distinguishing different species and providing their phylogeny. Contrarily, we were unable to reconstruct their relationships using either the set of expressed transcripts, the set of BUSCO genes, or whole mitogenomes retrieved from sequenced transcriptomic data. This strongly resembles the situation observed in Victoria cichlids before the widespread application of whole-genomic methods (Wagner et al. 2013).
On a larger phylogenetic context, we further compared the opsins from Lake Bermin Coptodon with other African cichlid lineages, focusing on key tuning amino acid sites known to shift the spectral (λmax) sensitivity of opsin pigments (Yokoyama 2008). Our results show that Bermin cichlids (and Coptodon species more broadly) possess the longer-wavelength sensitive amino acid variant for all opsin genes, except for sws1 (the UV opsin), where both tuning sites (i.e. sites 49 and 118) retain the shorter-wavelength sensitive variant (Fig. 1a). In other words, all cone opsins—except for one—exhibit a shift in sensitivity toward longer (red) wavelengths of light based on the nucleotide sequence. This could be possibly related to the higher turbidity conditions compared to other lakes; however, limited visibility measurements are reported for Lake Bermin (Secchi disk 4.5 m of visibility during dry season (Kling 1988), complemented by our field measurements of 3.15 m in the peak rainy season in July 2017). This is in accord with notes that turbidity is expected to increase in rainy season, likely due to debris runoff from the steep crater edges (Stiassny et al. 1992; our field observation). Such conditions could potentially lead to a red-shifted ambient light environment. Whether the observed patterns reflect these environmental factors or instead represent phylogenetic inertia remains to be determined.

## Opsin Gene Expression in Bermin Cichlids and Visual System in the Deep-Water Species
Opsin gene expression patters in Bermin cichlids suggest potential for multichromatic color vision with five cone opsins abundantly expressed: the short-wavelength-sensitive sws2b and sws2a found in single cones and the middle-to-long-wavelength-sensitive rh2aβ, rh2aα, and lws in double cones (Fig. 2). The ratios of these opsins vary slightly between species, with a notable exception in the single cones of Coptodon flavus predominantly expressing the sws2b gene, whereas all other species primarily express sws2a (Fig. 2). Apart of these five cone opsins, the sws1 and rh2b are also detected at low levels, suggesting that Bermin cichlids could retain a full cichlid opsin repertoire (Fig. 2 and Table 1). This pattern suggests some opsin coexpression in different photoreceptor types, which have been observed in other cichlid systems (Dalton et al. 2014, 2015). For comparison, the opsin gene repertoire of East African and Neotropical cichlid species typically includes three or four expressed cone opsin genes (Parry et al. 2005; Carleton 2009; Escobar-Camacho et al. 2017). Overall, the opsin expression profile of Bermin cichlids is shifted toward longer wavelengths (i.e. with dominant lws expression), a pattern commonly observed in cichlids inhabiting riverine or turbid lake environments (Halstenberg et al. 2005; Carleton et al. 2008; Hofmann and Carleton 2009). Beyond cichlids, the dominance of lws in double cones and sws2a in single cones appears to be a widespread pattern among various teleosts and even non-teleost ray-finned fishes (Lupše et al. 2022; Musilová and Cortesi 2023).
We then compared opsin expression in shallow- and deep-water Bermin species and found depth-associated significant differences in proportional opsin gene expression of three single cone opsins (sws1, sws2a, and sws2b) and two double-cone opsins (rh2aβ and lws) (Fig. 3 and Table 2; n = 88 for dry-season samples). While these differences are not as drastic as in other systems—where deep-dwelling species exhibit a complete opsin loss (e.g. Barombi Mbo; Musilová et al. 2019b)—we identified significant reductions in the expression of two short-wavelength-sensitive opsins in the single cones of deep-water species (Table 2 and Figs. 2 and 3). Specifically, deep-water species express lower levels of UV-sensitive sws1 (P = 0.00192**) and violet-sensitive sws2b (P = 0.00846*), leading to an increased proportional expression of blue-sensitive sws2a (P = 0.00408*), likely reflecting the narrowed light spectrum at depth, where UV and violet wavelengths are largely absent (De Lange 2000). This trend aligns with patterns observed in other cichlid systems (e.g. Masoko—Carruthers et al. 2025; or Barombi Mbo—Musilová et al. 2019b), or mirrors the evolutionary loss of sws1 in deep-sea fishes (Musilová et al. 2019a).
Surprisingly, on the other hand, we observed the opposite trend at the long-wavelength end of the spectrum. The red-sensitive opsin lws exhibited increased proportional expression in deep-water species of the Lake Bermin (P ≤ 0.001***) (Figs. 2 and 3 and Tables 2 and 3; Tables S4 to S7) and accordingly mildly decreased expression of one of the green-sensitive opsins rh2aβ (P = 0.00203*). In most other deep-water systems, the proportional expression of red-sensitive lws is lowered (e.g. in Masoko—Carruthers et al. 2025) or even completely lost (e.g. Barombi Mbo—Musilová et al. 2019b; Tanganyika—Ricci et al. 2023). This reduction is typically attributed to the absence of red wavelengths at greater depths, as both ends of the light spectrum diminish with increasing water depth (Michiels et al. 2008).
The reason for the higher proportion of red-sensitive lws opsin in Bermin deep-water species remains unclear. One possibility is the aforementioned increased water turbidity in Bermin compared to Barombi Mbo or Tanganyika, although the water is still somewhat clear. Additionally, the Bermin lake has a maximum depth of 14.5 m (where all three deep-water species, C. bythobates, C. imbrifernus, and C. spongotroktis, have been observed), which is shallower than Barombi Mbo (max. depth = 110 m, with fish observed around 30 m). In such shallower depths, the filtering of the red and UV spectra by water column may not be as pronounced as in deeper systems with red color absent (Carleton 2009; Hofmann and Carleton 2009), and therefore, red light may still play important role in the deep zone of Bermin. Another potential explanation for importance of seeing red may be in the role of breeding coloration—many Coptodon species exhibit yellow and red hues, which may constrain selection against red sensitivity loss. Maintaining or upregulating lws expression in deep-water species might also serve to enhance the detection of conspecific signals under limited light conditions, improving contrast sensitivity when red wavelengths are scarce. This could be especially important during courtship or territorial displays in deeper and turbid habitat. Finally, this pattern may also stem from the evolutionary history of the Bermin species flock: all Bermin cichlids belong to the genus Coptodon typically associated with riverine environments. Bermin species were likely derived from riverine ancestors that colonized the crater lakes in the region (Schliewen et al. 1994; Dunz and Schliewen 2013). As such, the observed opsin expression profiles may partially represent evolutionary inertia from their ancestral habitats. Genus Coptodon is only distantly related to the Oreochromini lineage (i.e. the lineage with the Barombi Mbo species) (Schedel et al. 2019; Astudillo-Clavijo et al. 2023). For these reasons, Bermin cichlids represent an important addition to comparative cichlid studies alongside oreochromine (Barombi Mbo; Musilová et al. 2019b), pseudocrenilabrine (Masoko; Carruthers et al. 2025; Talbi et al. 2025), and heroine cichlids (Nicaragua; Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017).
Interestingly, the observed gene expression changes associated with the deep-water environment are more pronounced in males for single cone opsins (sws1—P = 0.00427; sws2b—P = 0.00367; sws2a—P = 0.00179; n = 58), whereas in the double cone opsins (rh2aβ—P = 0.00173; lws—P = <0.001; n = 25), the effect is primarily driven by females (Tables S6 and S7). The nature of this observation is not yet fully understood; however, it may suggest that the depth effect is modulated by additional factors in one or the other sex. In other species, for example, sexual maturity influences opsin expression in both male and female sticklebacks (Shao et al. 2014), or testosterone levels have been shown to affect the rh2-to-lws ratio in lizard cones (Tseng et al. 2018) in a manner similar to what we observe in Coptodon—that is, females have higher lws-to-rh2 ratio, while males exhibit lower values. In these lizards, however, dichromatic coloration plays a significant role in sexual selection (Tseng et al. 2018), whereas in Coptodon, nuptial coloration is present in both sexes and does not differ between males and females (pers. observation). Although our sampling was not specifically controlled for reproductive state, all individuals included in the study were adults. It is also possible that the observed pattern is influenced by the smaller sample size when analyzing each sex separately particularly for females (Tables S6 and S7).
The spectral sensitivity of visual pigments can also be altered by switching between retinal chromophores: a shift from A1 (11–cis retinal) to A2 (11-cis-3,4-dehydroretinal) moves the opsin λmax toward longer wavelengths (Carleton et al. 2016). The use of A2 or a combination of both A1 and A2 has been documented in several freshwater fish, including cichlids (Carleton et al. 2008; Terai et al. 2017), and is also found in deep-sea fishes adapted to red-light sensitivity (Douglas et al. 2000). The cyp27c1 gene, a molecular marker for A2 synthesis, was identified in zebrafish (Enright et al. 2015) and can serve as a proxy for detecting A2 retinal in transcriptomic data (Carleton et al. 2016). To determine whether A2 plays a role in Coptodon vision, we analyzed the expression of the cyp27c1 synthesis marker and found no expression in the retina transcriptomes, regardless of sex or season. Notably, cyp27c1 expression has circadian nature and peaks at night (Temple et al. 2006; Corbo 2021), hence we should not rule out minor effect, since we sampled during a day. Yet, other cichlid studies reporting the presence of A2 chromophore (e.g. Torres-Dowdall et al. 2017) found sufficient expression of cyp27c1 even during a day. Our results therefore suggest predominant A1 usage in Bermin Coptodon cichlids.

## Plasticity of the Opsin Gene Expression in the Seasonally Spawning and Migratory Species
The seasonally deep-water species, C. imbrifernus, resides in the deep during the dry season, while it migrates to the shallow waters for spawning in the rainy season (Stiassny et al. 1992). The expression profiles show that the visual system differs between seasons in the ratio of sws2b to sws2a expression in the single cones (P = 0.00283*; n = 19; Table 2 and Fig. 4; Tables S6 and S7), while no differences between seasons have been found for the other opsin genes. The increased sws2a: sws2b ratio in the single cones follows the same pattern observed in the comparison of the deep- and shallow-water species within the Bermin species flock, i.e. shallow-water species exhibit a similarly elevated sws2a: sws2b ratio as C. imbrifernus during its spawning period, when it occupies the shallow zone. This pattern is also consistent with findings from other cichlid studies focused on depth-related visual adaptation (Musilová et al. 2019b; Carruthers et al. 2025). The seasonal migration could be therefore seen as a functional parallel to the deep vs. shallow-water species comparison. Hence, the observed plasticity of the visual system in the seasonal migratory species may represent a mechanism by which the visual system adapts: a plastic response to the environment, which may subsequently lead to fixed differences among species. We further tested whether seasonal differences could be found in three other non-seasonal species in the lake (of which we had sufficient samples from both seasons), and no differences were observed for three other species sampled in both seasons (Table S7). Therefore, the seasonal differences are likely found only in C. imbrifernus, the species known to reproduce seasonally and to shift habitat depth accordingly. Interestingly, when C. imbrifernus samples are analyzed separately by sex, significant differences in the expression of sws2b (P = 0.000464**) and rh2aα (P ≤ 0.001***) in males (n = 10) or rh2aβ (P = 0.00352*) in females (n = 7) are also observed between seasons (Table S7). However, due to the limited sample size for this test, we chose not to draw any strong conclusions from these results. Similarly, to investigate for differences between sexes, we complemented our seasonality analysis in C. imbrifernus by testing the effect of sex in the dry season samples of this species. We found differences in the proportion of the green to red opsins, i.e. rh2aβ:lws (Table S6), with males having more rh2aβ (P = 0.000467**), whereas females having higher proportion of lws (P = 0.00242*) in the double cones. These differences in rh2aβ:lws ratio between sexes of C. imbrifernus again correspond to the differences found in the all-species test of the depth (with males and females analyzed separately; Table S6). No differences were found when testing samples from the rainy season, as well as in four other species with male and female samples (Table S6), yet again we reached to the limited sample sizes in these tests.

## Comparison of Visual Systems in Bermin and Barombi Mbo Cichlid Radiations
The two Cameroonian crater lakes, each hosting an endemic adaptive radiation—Barombi Mbo (Trewavas et al. 1972) likely hundreds of thousands to one million years old, and a much younger Bermin species flock (Stiassny et al. 1992) probably only few tens of thousands of years (see age justification above)—warrant a more detailed comparison (Fig. 5). Both lakes are inhabited by a similar number of species: 11 in Barombi Mbo and 9 (valid)/13 (incl. undescribed) in Bermin, as well as a remarkably comparable number of deep-water species (Trewavas et al. 1972; Stiassny et al. 1992; Musilová et al. 2019b). Both radiations have evolved one strict deep-water specialist (Konia dikume in Barombi Mbo; Trewavas et al. 1972, C. bythobates in Bermin; Stiassny et al. 1992) and one seasonal migratory species (Myaka myaka in Barombi, C. imbrifernus in Bermin) residing in the deep zone throughout the year and migrating to the shallow zone only for spawning in the rainy season (Trewavas et al. 1972; Stiassny et al. 1992). On the other hand, the phylogenetic origins of these two cichlid radiations differ between the lakes with Barombi Mbo hosting species from the tribe Oreochromini and Bermin from the tribe Coptodonini (Dunz and Schliewen 2013). The modes of reproduction also differ between lakes with mouthbrooders in Barombi Mbo and substrate spawners in Bermin (Schliewen et al. 1994). Further differences include the striking yellow and sometimes red-belly coloration of Bermin cichlids during breeding, whereas Barombi Mbo cichlids do not show much color change during reproduction (Stiassny et al. 1992; Musilová et al. 2019b). We performed the comparison of their visual system and found that the directions of adaptations in the visual system also differ: Barombi Mbo cichlids exhibit a larger divergence in the double cones between shallow and deep-water species (mainly due to the loss of lws expression in double cones in deep-water species), whereas Bermin cichlids show greater variability along the single cone axis (Fig. 5). A particularly striking difference is caused by the aforementioned adaptation of deep-water species: Barombi cichlids shift their double cones toward shorter wavelengths (due to the replacement of red-sensitive LWS with green-sensitive rh2aα) (Musilová et al. 2019b), whereas Bermin cichlids shift toward longer wavelengths in their double cones (due to increased lws expression). This differential direction of adaptation is obvious in the “visual niche” plot with weighted single-cone to double-cone overall sensitivity as per Hofmann et al. (2009) (Fig. 5); while the shallow-water species visual niche overlaps, the deep-water species of Bermin and Barombi Mbo are found at opposite edges of the plot mainly along the double-cone (y) axis (Fig. 5). Note, however, that this approach using overall sensitivity weighted by opsin expression is a simplified way to visualize color vision and does not account for potential opsin coexpression or neural processing. More direct approaches, such as microspectrophotometry, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), or behavioral assays of color discrimination, would be required to fully characterize spectral sensitivities and test color vision in these fishes.

## Differential Gene Expression of Retinal Transcriptomes: Genes Associated With Deep-Water Species and With Seasonality
We took advantage of the sequenced retina transcriptomes search for candidate genes associated with adaptation to the deep-water environment, as well as with seasonality in C. imbrifernus.
To search for the deep-water associated candidates, we used the retina transcriptomes of two deep-water species (C. bythobates and C. imbrifernus) and two representatives of shallow-water species (Coptodon bakossiorum and Coptodon gutturosus). We were specifically interested in genes that were differentially expressed in all pairwise (i.e. shallow-deep) comparisons, as this design should reveal the putative candidate genes associated with depth. We identified 14 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), all of which were upregulated in the deep-water species (Fig. 3c and Table 3; Table S9). Interestingly, in a similar comparison in the Masoko cichlids, all DEGs were also upregulated (i.e. no downregulated genes were found) in the deep-water ecomorph (Carruthers et al. 2025). Of the 14 identified DEGs in Bermin cichlids, five are associated with vision and are involved in retinal repair and differentiation, cone development, or blood flow control (Table 3; Table S9). Specifically, HEY1 (hes-related bHLH transcription factor) is involved in regulating the regenerative response of Müller glia in the retina, as tested in zebrafish after injury (Maier and Gessler 2000; Sahu et al. 2021). Endothelin-1 (ET-1) is involved in blood flow control in the retina (Salvatore and Vingolo, 2010; Freeman et al. 2014). Fibrillin 1 (FBN1) is a crucial ocular glycoprotein and plays a role in the structural and functional stability of retinal arterioles (Hubmacher et al. 2014; Alonso et al. 2023). Collagen COL25a1 is associated with the structure and function of direction-selective retinal ganglion cells, which are essential for motion detection and orientation (Barlow and Hill 1963; Vaney et al. 2012; Baden et al. 2016; Rheaume et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2022) (Table 3; Table S8). Interestingly, none of the DEGs found between shallow and deep-water species overlaps either with the genes found in the Barombi Mbo analysis using the same design (Musilová et al. 2019b), nor with the DE analysis in the deep- and shallow-water Masoko ecomorphs (Carruthers et al. 2025), although the functional categories do overlap (claudins, circadian genes, etc.). Similarly, there is no overlap between Masoko and Barombi Mbo set of DEGs (Musilová et al. 2019b; Carruthers et al. 2025) albeit some categories of the genes are similar (slc transporters, circadian genes). This suggests that overall changes in gene expression may not be conserved across different cichlid systems, in contrast to specific mechanisms, such as opsin expression tuning. The DGE analysis across systems is limited due to the varying methods and teams involved in sample collection and likely due to other factors that may obscure the signal.
Focusing on seasonality, we analyzed the dry and rainy season transcriptomes of C. imbrifernus, with sexes analyzed separately (Fig. 4c and Table 3; Table S8). We aimed to similarly compare all pairwise comparisons (i.e. dry vs. rainy) and identified 11 genes that were differentially expressed across comparisons (four upregulated and seven downregulated during the dry [=deep-water] season). Among these, two downregulated genes are associated with visual processes: MAG (myelin-associated glycoprotein), which is involved in neural regeneration and structural stabilization via Müller glial cell maintenance (Bringmann et al. 2006, 2009), and claudin-19 (CLDN19), which is associated with tight junctions in the blood-retinal barrier and is critical for retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) integrity and retinal homeostasis (Naylor et al. 2019). Two upregulated genes, per3 and csnk1db, are known to be related to circadian rhythms (Constance et al. 2005).
Further, we tested whether any of the DEGs overlapped with those found in the deep vs. shallow analysis. Comparing the two seasons of C. imbrifernus essentially means comparing when the species inhabits the deep and shallow environments. Surprisingly, we did not detect any overlap between the two analyses suggesting that a similar molecular trajectory is not used in the plastic response to the depth (seasonal species) and the lifelong adaptation (deep-water species). However, we did find similarities between the seasonality analysis and the deep vs. shallow analysis in Barombi Mbo, namely, the upregulation of several circadian rhythm genes (although not the same ones) in the Barombi deep-water species and in C. imbrifernus during the dry season (=deep). Circadian genes are known to change expression throughout the day (e.g. Bolton et al. 2021), and the two genes, reported as upregulated in the dry (=deep) season, per3 and csnk1db, are both known to have a low peak of expression during the daytime (Constance et al. 2005; Wu et al. 2023). This suggests less prominent periodicity in expression, or compensation for the lack of light in the deep habitat. Alternatively, the circadian clock in deep-water species could be phase-shifted due to the shorter and more limited daylight exposure in their environment, adjusting their gene expression rhythms accordingly. Although we have not specifically controlled for the time of day during sample collection, both shallow- and deep-water samples were collected around the same time of day (7 AM to 13 PM), irrespective of the season. The expression of circadian genes in the deep-water habitat therefore deserves specific focus in future studies. Circadian genes commonly appear among the differentially regulated genes in deep vs. shallow water comparisons, alongside other categories such as immunity genes, hypoxia tolerance, and maintenance and endurance of visual system tissues (Musilová et al. 2019b; Ricci et al. 2023).

## Conclusion
In this study, we provide a comprehensive molecular-level analysis of visual system evolution across the entire adaptive radiation of Bermin crater lake cichlids. Opsins as the visual photoreceptor proteins are typically key targets in many evolutionary studies because the relationship between genotype and phenotype is well understood in this context, including a detailed knowledge of (rhod)opsin protein behavior and properties (Carleton et al. 2016). By including all 13 species (nine described and four undescribed), we identify a clear association between opsin expression profiles and habitat depth, as well as we report on differences in rainy and dry seasons, within the evolutionary young species flock. We aimed to identify both shared and unique adaptations to deep-water environments in other cichlid (and teleost) fishes, offering insights into the broader mechanisms of cichlid visual evolution. In Bermin, a typical deep-water adaptation known for other fishes is observed in the single cones of the retina, characterized by an increased expression ratio of short-wavelength-sensitive opsins (sws2a: sws2b). Contrarily, we also detect an unexpected increase in the expression of the red-sensitive lws opsin, suggesting a unique visual adaptation specific for the Bermin species flock. Our study enables broader comparisons with other fish systems primarily focusing on the adaptive processes (gene expression, nucleotide mutations), as well as while generating further questions about other underlying molecular mechanisms (regulation, cell type identity). Future applications of comparative transcriptomics, complemented by single-cell transcriptomics of the retina, will allow us to address many questions related to the general patterns in the evolution of cone cells.

## Materials and Methods

## Samples Collection
Cichlid specimens were collected in the crater lake Bermin (5°09′30″N 9°38′0″E), South-West Cameroon, between 2013 and 2017 (research permit numbers: 0000047,49/MINRESI/B00/C00/C10/nye, 0000116,117/MINRESI/B00/C00/C10/C14, 000002-3/MINRESI/B00/C00/C10/C11, 0000032,48 50/MINRESI/B00/C00/C10/C12) during both dry (November to March) and rainy (April to October) seasons. Most of the samples were collected within a consistent time window between 7:00 and 13:00, regardless of the season. Between 5 and 15 of adult individuals per species were collected, covering all nine valid and four undescribed species inhabiting Bermin Lake (Stiassny et al. 1992). Shallow-water species were captured using gill nets and minnow traps, as well as selective catching with the use of gill nets while snorkeling. Deep-water species, C. bythobates, C. imbrifernus, and C. spongotroktis, were captured into gill nets and minnow traps. We also sampled riverine cichlid species, Heterochromis multidens (SRX6435738), Hemichromis elongatus (Batchenga, Sanaga River; SRX6435739), Etia nguti (Nguti village; SRX6435784), Pelmatolapia mariae (SRX6435737), Gobiocichla ethelwynnae (Mamfé city; SRX6435741), Coptodon kottae (Barombi Kotto; Table S1), and Benitochromis conjunctus (SRX6435725) in Cameroon, for the purpose of whole-genome sequencing also previously used in Ronco et al. 2021. See Tables S1 and S2 for full set of samples. Fin clips from all the captured specimens were sampled and stored in 96% EtOH for DNA extraction and further molecular analysis. From freshly euthanized specimens (by anesthetic overdose or percussive stunning), entire eyeballs were dissected and fixed in RNAlater (RNAlater™ Stabilization Solution, Invitrogen™) for the retinal RNA analysis. The samples were stored at room temperature during the sampling, after which they were transferred into a field refrigerator. Upon arrival at the university facility, samples were stored at −80 °C for long-term preservation.

## Sanger Sequencing, Haplotype Network Construction, and Amino Acid Diversity
We sequenced 567 single opsins sequences from 109 individuals of Bermin cichlids (partially overlapping with individuals used for the transcriptome sequencing) using a conventional Sanger sequencing method to cover multiple individuals per species for the haplotype network and allelic variability check (Tables S2 and S9). We complemented our sequence dataset by exploring genomes of 11 other Africa cichlids to provide a broader context of opsin evolution (Tables S1 and S3; Fig. 1). DNA was extracted from fin clips stored in 96% EtOH with the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (www.qiagen.com) following the manufacturer's protocol. Seven cone opsin genes, sws1, sws2b, sws2a, rh2b, rh2aβ, rh2aα, and lws, were Sanger-sequenced for 4 to 10 individuals per gene per species. The opsin genes were amplified as two (rh2aβ, rh2aα), three (sws1), four (sws2a), or five (sws2b, rh2b, lws) fragments, depending on their length (see Table S9 for a list of primers used). PCR reactions contained 1 μL of DNA, 5 μL of Master Mix Polymerase (PPP Master Mix, Top-Bio s.r.o.), 0.25 μL of each primer (forward and reverse), 0.25 μL of MgCl, and 3.25 μL water up to 10 μL volume. The PCR conditions were initial denaturation: 94 °C for 300 s, 35 cycles of denaturation: 94 °C for 40 s, annealing between 56 and 64 °C depending on the pair of primers, for 30 s, extension: 72 °C for 60 s and after 35 cycles final extension in 72 °C for 300 s. PCR products were then purified with ExoSAP-IT PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Applied Biosystems) and service-sequenced in Macrogen Europe B.V. (https://dna.macrogen.com/eng/). Species-specific rh1sequences from Bermin cichlids were obtained by mapping their transcriptomes to a C. bakossiorum RH1 reference, which was generated by mapping the C. bakossiorum transcriptome (sample 58G4) to the Oreochromis niloticus rh1 gene (LOC100691236) using Geneious 9.1.4 software (https://www.geneious.com, Kearse et al. 2012).
The obtained sequences (BankIt, accession numbers: rh1: PX223317-PX223331; sws1: PX436730 to PX436751; sws2b: PX436765 to PX436786; sws2a: PX436752 to PX436764; rh2b: PX436787 to PX436808; rh2aβ: PX436823 to PX436842; rh2aα: PX436809 to PX436822; and lws: PX436706 to PX436729) were checked for heterozygous sites, trimmed, and aligned to the annotated opsin genes of O. niloticus (GenBank, accession number version MKQE00000000.2) using Geneious 9.1.4 software (https://www.geneious.com, Kearse et al. 2012, using Geneious mapper with medium sensitivity). Intron sequences were removed for the subsequent analyses. Individual alleles were reconstructed using the PHASE algorithm in DNAsp version 6 (Rozas et al. 2017). To visualize the relationships among the different alleles, haplotype networks have been created for each gene with PopART (Bandelt et al. 1999; Leigh and Bryant 2015) using the minimum spanning network method (ε = 0) and phased sequences from 4 to 10 individuals per species per gene. Additionally, all obtained opsin gene sequences were translated into protein sequences and aligned against the bovine rhodopsin reference (AH001149) using Geneious v9.1.4 (https://www.geneious.com; Kearse et al. 2012). For each gene, unique haplotypes containing amino acid substitutions were identified and retained. These were then analyzed with a focus on substitutions occurring at previously identified key spectral tuning sites (Yokoyama 2008).

## Transcriptome Sequencing
We sequenced 109 individual retina transcriptomes of all Bermin species (Table S1). The retinas were dissected from the eyeballs preserved in the RNAlater solution. RNA was extracted from the dissected retina using Qiagen RNeasy Micro Kit (www.qiagen.com). The extracted RNA concentration and integrity were verified using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent) and Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were prepared in-house with NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England BioLabs Inc.) and service-sequenced with Novogene Co., Ltd. Prepared libraries covered 5 to 11 individuals per species. Additional information regarding the specimens' sex and seasons during which they have been collected are listed in Table S4. Sequenced libraries were quality-checked and trimmed using FastQC (Andrews 2010) and Trim Galore ver.0.4.4 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/).

## Opsin Gene Expression Analysis and Statistical Tests
Relative opsin gene expression was calculated using Geneious software 9.1.4. For each sample, the pair-end library has been mapped against the reference dataset made of seven cone opsin genes (sws1, sws2b, sws2a, rh2b, rh2aβ, rh2aα, lws) from one selected species, C. bakossiorum. Coptodon bakossiorum reference sequences were generated by mapping transcriptome reads to annotated O. niloticus opsin gene sequences (sws1, opn1sw1, 100710249; sws2b, opn1sw2, 100695018; sws2a, LOC100695287; rh2b, LOC100711209; rh2aβ, LOC100710942; rh2aα, LOC100710676; and lws, LOC100694761) using Geneious 9.1.4, and consensus sequences were called for each opsin gene. Additionally, the reference sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing data. These annotated sequences were then used as references for mapping transcriptomes of the remaining cichlid species.
Additionally, to distinguish between similar rh2aβ and rh2aα genes, we subsequently mapped each library against the fourth exon of rh2aβ and rh2aα genes (with ten SNPs that are different between rh2aβ and rh2aα copies in Bermin cichlids) to get the ratio between these two genes (BankIt, accession numbers: rh2aβ: PX436823 to PX436842; rh2aα: PX436809 to PX436822). Based on the number of mapped reads to each reference gene, we calculated the FPKM (fragments per kilobase and million reads) taking into account the library size and the length of each gene. We then calculated the proportional expression of opsin genes as a percentage of a relative opsin gene expression, separately for single-cone (sws1, sws2b, sws2a) and double-cone opsins (rh2b, rh2aβ, rh2aα, and lws) (Table S4; Fig. 2).
Additionally, to determine whether Bermin cichlids utilize only A1 retinal or a mixture of A1 and A2, we mapped all libraries against the cyp27c1 gene of C. bakossiorum reference (obtained after mapping the reads against the cyp27c1 gene from Danio rerio; acc. no: NM_001113337). This gene encodes an enzyme, cytochrome P450 27C1 that converts vitamin A1 to A2 by adding a double bond to the chromophore (Enright et al. 2015). The presence of A2 shifts opsin sensitivity toward longer wavelengths and affects the accurate estimation of λmax (Carleton et al. 2016). To calculate the potential expression level of cyp27c1, we also mapped the libraries to several housekeeping genes: ef2z and actb (from C. bakossiorum) as a normalization control to estimate the relative expression levels of cyp27c1 as those are typically constitutively and stably expressed (Yourick et al. 2019).
We used beta-regression analysis to formally test for significant differences among the proportional expression between deep-dwelling species (C. bythobates, C. imbrifernus, C. spongotroktis) and shallow-water species (remaining six valid + four undescribed species). The same method was also used for the comparison of the rainy season and dry season samples in C. imbrifernus. We used the R package betareg v3.2-1 (Cribari-Neto and Zeileis 2010), which allows handling of non-transformed data. This analysis is suitable to fit the dependent variable (in our case the proportional expression of each opsin gene) in the interval (0,1). We tested the effect of depth for selected cone opsin gene class separately (i.e. sws1, sws2b, sws2a, rh2aβ, and lws following the preselection based on the order of species by expression as seen in Fig. 3b and Table S5). We tested the effect of season and sex in the seasonal migratory species, C. imbrifernus, on the expression of sws1, sws2b, rh2aβ, rh2aα, and lws (see Fig. 4 for visualized results). In this case, sws2a was showing a reciprocal pattern to sws2b (since it is a complementary gene in single cones and there is virtually no expression of SWS1 in this species), and it was hence not tested to avoid redundancy.
To present the overall retina sensitivity, we show single and double cone sensitivity averages, as per Hofmann et al. (2009). We calculated the weighted average of all seven opsin genes expression within single and double cones separately, following the equation in Hofmann et al. (2009), and we used previously, experimentally measured values of cone opsin sensitivities in tilapia, λmax, i.e. SWS1 (360 nm), SWS2B (425 nm), SWS2A (456 nm), RH2B (472 nm), RH2Aβ (518 nm), RH2Aα (528 nm), and LWS (561 nm) (Spady et al. 2006). This method assumes equal outer segment volumes across cones within each cone class (single or double cones) and similar metabolic turnover and physiological properties among the expressed opsins. In a similar fashion, we compared average single and double cone sensitivity between Bermin and Barombi Mbo cichlids as both flocks are sympatric and share similarities such as comparable species richness and inhabiting crater lakes in close proximity, albeit with differing ages; Barombi Mbo cichlids are relatively older (hundred thousand to 1 million years), compared to ten(s) thousand(s) years of age of Bermin cichlids (Stager et al. 2018; Schedel et al. 2019).

## Phylogenetic Relationships Between Species
To reconstruct tentative phylogenetic relationships among the cichlid species from the Bermin species flock, we selected 34 individuals (two to three individuals per species; Table S1) and performed the RAD sequencing. We followed the pipeline as described in Roesti et al. (2012). Briefly, we used sbfI restriction enzyme for the DNA digestion and followed with the RAD-seq library preparation (barcode + index ligation). The libraries were then pooled and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq 2500 for 100 bp (accession numbers found in Table S1) and subsequently mapped against the O. niloticus (GCF_000188235_2) reference genome using NovoAlign (v2.07.06 (http://novocraft.com). We exported the consensus and obtained the SNP matrix (4,183 sites) using a series of custom scripts and we set thresholds as per Roesti et al. (2012). We used the matrix as an input for Bayesian analyses in MrBayes3.2 (Ronquist et al. 2012) and performed the phylogenetic inference for 5 million generations in two independent runs.

## Differential Gene Expression
To investigate differential gene expression, we applied a comparative transcriptomics pipeline across two distinct analyses: Lake Bermin deep- vs. shallow-water species comparison and seasonality (and sex) comparison in C. imbrifernus. In the former case, we aimed to identify genes differentially expressed between deep-water and shallow-water Bermin species, following a comparative transcriptomics approach similar to Musilová et al. (2019b). Here we compared retinal transcriptomes from adult individuals from two shallow-water species (C. bakossiorum and C. gutturosus) and two deep-water species (C. bythobates and C. imbrifernus). In the latter analysis aiming to assess seasonal effects and detach it from the sex-specific expression, we analyzed gene expression across four groups of C. imbrifernus: males and females collected during the dry and rainy seasons. We then performed the DGE on all rainy vs. dry season pairwise comparisons (i.e. males rainy vs. males dry, males rainy vs. females dry, females rainy vs. females dry, and females rainy vs. males dry). The overlap among the resulting genes among these four comparisons would identify genes associated with seasonality, irrespective of sex (i.e. not specific for one or the other sex). The transcriptomes selected and used in the DGE analysis are marked in Table S1.
Quality assessment and filtering were performed as previously described. Reads were additionally filtered for PhiX contamination and residual adapter sequences using bbduk.sh v38.86 (Bushnell 2014).
Filtered reads were aligned to the Nile tilapia reference genome using BWA v0.7.17 (Li and Durbin 2009), with no read deduplication performed. Gene-level coverage was calculated using BEDTools v2.30.0 (Quinlan and Hall 2010). DESeq2 v1.38.3 (Love et al. 2014) was used to identify DEGs across species comparisons applying a false discovery rate threshold of 0.05 and a log2 fold change cutoff of ±1. Venn diagrams were created for deep-water and shallow-water comparisons to visualize DEGs. After DESeq2 analysis, genes were filtered for statistical significance (adjusted P < 0.1) and directional log2 fold change (negative for downregulated, positive for upregulated). DEG lists from each species comparison were processed, and the intersections were visualized with ggvenn v0.1.9 (Yan 2021) using distinct colors for each comparison.
