Carbon Dots Infused 3D Printed Cephalopod Mimetic Bactericidal and Antioxidant Hydrogel for Uniaxial Mechano‐Fluorescent Tactile Sensor
Poushali Das, Sayan Ganguly, Parham Khoshbakht Marvi, Masoomeh Sherazee, Xiaowu Shirley Tang, Seshasai Srinivasan, Amin Reza Rajabzadeh (2024). Advanced Materials. https://doi.org/10.1002/adma.202409819. doi:10.1002/adma.202409819. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Carbon Dots Infused 3D Printed Cephalopod Mimetic Bactericidal and Antioxidant Hydrogel for Uniaxial Mechano‐Fluorescent Tactile Sensor

## Abstract
AbstractCephalopods use stretchy skin and dynamic color‐tuning organs for visual communication and camouflage. Inspired by these natural mechanisms, a fluorescent biomaterial for deformation‐induced illumination and optical communication is proposed. This is the first report of 3D printed soft biomaterials infused with carbon dots hydrothermally derived from chitosan and benzalkonium chloride. These biomaterials exhibit a comprehensive array of properties, including significant uniaxial stretching, near‐instantaneous response to tactile stimuli and pH, UV resistance, antibacterial, antioxidant, noncytotoxicity, and highlighting their potential as mechano‐optical materials for biomedical applications. The hydrogel's durability is evaluated by cyclic stretching, folding, rolling, and twisting tests to ensure its integrity and good signal‐to‐noise ratio. The diffusion mechanism is determined by water imbibition kinetics, network parameters, and time‐dependent breathing. Overcoming the common limitations of short lifespans and complex manufacturing processes in existing soft hybrids, this work demonstrates a straightforward method to produce durable, energy‐independent, mechano‐optical hydrogel. Combined with investigations, molecular dynamic modeling is used to understand the interactions of hydrogel components.

## Introduction
The integration of nanoparticles into macroscopic materials is an important goal in the fields of materials science and nanotechnology. It aims to create new materials that harness synergistic interactions and emergent phenomena of the optical, electrical, and mechanical properties of individual nanoparticles.[ 1 ] Using biocompatible nanohybrids is an innovative and promising method for creating soft robotics and devices with unique functionality, high energy economy, strong adaptability, and intelligence. Various soft robotic devices have recently incorporated the capacity to visually detect mechanical deformations or forces for proprioceptive and exteroceptive sensing.[ 2 ] For example, soft elastomers that have the ability to change colors when deformed, known as mechanochromism, have been utilized in the fabrication of a soft gripper to visually demonstrate the degree of deformation.[ 2a ] Furthermore, liquid crystal elastomer‐based soft actuators have also demonstrated a similar mechanochromic response.[ 2b ] Additionally, photonic crystal arrays have been investigated to visualize mechanical deformations and stresses.[ 3 ] Nevertheless, all of these designs lack the ability to produce light, making it highly challenging to visually perceive deformations or forces in a dark environment.
Mechano‐responsive materials possess light emission resulting from mechanical action applied to a material, which can be readily observed in dark environments.[ 4 ] They usually consist of luminescent compounds that emit light when stimulated by an energy source, like electricity or ultraviolet light. The majority of mechano‐responsive materials are inorganic solids, such as SrAl2O4:Eu2+ and ZnS:Cu/Mn.2+[ 5 ] These materials are stiff and brittle, making them unsuitable for use in flexible and stretchable devices. Polymer‐embedded optical sensing has become more popular as a straightforward visual method for evaluating mechanical deformation.[ 6 ] Typically, optical tensile sensing of mechano‐responsive polymers involves incorporating dyes,[ 7 ] functional molecules,[ 8 ] metallic and inorganic nanoparticles,[ 9 ] or excimers[ 10 ] into the polymeric matrix that undergoes shape changes. These composite materials have notable challenges in implementing these sensing techniques on a larger scale. These challenges mostly originate from the intricate synthetic processes involved and the absence of a quantitative relationship with real mechanical parameters. Recently, mechanoluminescent particles have been integrated into polymer networks to generate soft and flexible elastomers, the majority of which can emit light due to bond breakdown. Nevertheless, this destructive characteristic prohibits the material from being reused, and the intensity of the resulting light is typically very weak. In contrast, luminescent phenomena of carbon dots are unique in nature and generate light with high energy efficiency. Therefore, the development of a biocompatible nanohybrid mechano‐optical system with a simple fabrication procedure, fast response, high sensitivity, and a long lifetime could potentially revolutionize untethered, small‐scale, and intelligent systems.
The fluorescence of quantum dots (QDs) arises from quantum confinement.[ 11 ] Carbon QDs (CDs)[ 12 ] and graphene QDs (GQDs)[ 13 ] have garnered significant interest due to their chemical inertness, low toxicity, hydrophilicity, and photostability, making them highly desirable among the various QDs. Recent studies have explored the potential of nanoparticles' fluorescence properties in various fields, such as solar cells,[ 14 ] analytical science,[ 15 ] biosensing,[ 16 ] and biomedical applications.[ 17 ] Nevertheless, the inclination for the aggregation of these luminescent nanoparticles is regarded as a constraining element that can impact the photoluminescence (PL) characteristics. To prevent this aggregation, nanoparticles can be incorporated into suitable matrices.[ 18 ] The present study aims to explore the potential benefits of developing a fluorescent CDs‐integrated, soft and robust nanohybrid mechano‐optical system, as well as pH sensitive wound monitoring tool.
Additive manufacturing (AM), often known as 3D printing, has attracted significant attention in the past decade due to its groundbreaking benefits.[ 19 ] The faster prototyping and the ability to print on demand provide 3D printing with several advantages compared to conventional manufacturing techniques.[ 20 ] There are many opportunities to use 3D‐printing and computer aided design (CAD) to solve different clinical issues and offer upgraded healthcare services.[ 21 ] With advancements in 3D printing technology, it is now possible to create new organs, including livers, kidneys, and hearts. 3D printing enables the production of drugs in various forms, including tablets, suppositories, and capsules like FDA‐approved Spritam (levetiracetam), particularly benefiting the fabrication of computer‐aided personalized medicines.[ 22 ]
This study proposes a simple approach for combining fluorescent CDs with soft materials to develop a very flexible, energy‐independent, and sensitive mechano‐optical system. These fluorescent hydrogel strips exhibit immediate signal change when subjected to small tensile deformation. This study includes: (1) the synthesis of CDs by simple hydrothermal method using benzalkonium chloride and chitosan as precursors. The morphology, structural, and optical properties of the CDs are studied; (2) ink optimization for the 3D printing of hydrogels followed by the infusion of different concentrations of CDs to the hydrogel matrices. An evaluation of the water imbibition kinetics, network parameters, time‐dependent breathing, and swelling behavior has indicated the presence of an anomalous diffusion mechanism; (3) demonstration of the soft nanohybrid mechano‐optical system's potential use cases, including visualizing mechanical deformation‐induced illumination, and optical communication in the dark. The fabricated fluorescent strips are stretched, folded, rolled, and bent repeatedly to ensure their high signal‐to‐noise ratio. The CDs introduced here offer the combined advantages of fluorescence, transparency, flexibility, and antioxidant properties; (4) pH sensing using the soft nanohybrid hydrogel, which can be applied to monitoring wound pH fluctuations. Surface functional groups undergo deprotonation and protonation in response to changes in pH, affecting the fluorescence emission; (5) testing the fluorescent hydrogels for noncytotoxicity and their capacity to prevent infection and eradicate both gram negative and gram positive bacteria. Coupled with experiments, molecular dynamic simulations were implemented to elucidate the nature of interactions between different hydrogel constituents in tandem with the structural behavior of polymers present in the hydrogel network. We believe that combining fluorescent CDs with engineered soft materials will lead to the development of more intelligent and functional applications for in vivo imaging and optical sensing. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first report of CDs‐infused 3D printed soft biomaterials with different functional properties that serve as an outstanding mechano‐optical system.

## Experimental Section

## Materials
Chitosan (medium molecular weight, 190–310 kDa; deacetylated 75%+), acrylamide (AM), N,N′‐methylenebisacrylamide (MBA), lithium phenyl‐2,4,6,‐trimethylbenzoyphosphinate (LAP) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, USA. Benzalkonium chloride (C21H38ClN, 339.99 g mol−1) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific, USA. Cellulose nanocrystal (CNC) was purchased from Celluforce, Canada. All the aqueous solutions were prepared by using Milli‐Q water (18.2 MΩ cm−1). All other chemicals used here are of analytical grades and used without further purification.

## Synthesis of CDs
The CDs were synthesized by simple hydrothermal method using benzalkonium chloride and chitosan as precursors. The method used for experimentation is demonstrated as follows: 6.25 × 10−3 m benzalkonium chloride solution (aqueous) was freshly prepared before the synthesis. The solution was stirred for 20 min in order to get a well‐distributed solution. To this, 0.2 g chitosan (dissolved in 1% acetic acid) solution was added and stirred for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous solution. The mixed solution was then transferred to a Teflon‐lined stainless steel autoclave and heated to 200 °C for 48 h. After the completion of the reaction, a yellow solution was acquired. The solution was centrifuged at 10 000 rpm for 30 min, followed by filtering through a Polyvinylidene flouride (PVDF) filtration membrane (pore size = 0.22 µm) to eliminate larger and unreacted particles. The obtained solution was then further purified by dialysis using a cellulose ester membrane tubing (MWCO: 2 kDa) for 72 h with stirring. The water was changed every 12 h. Finally, the yellow solution was kept at 4 °C for further analysis.

## Ink Formulation
To prepare the ink, an 8.5% w/v CNC aqueous dispersion was first prepared by mechanical stirring. Next, AM monomer (total monomer concentration 16%) is dissolved into it by vortexing for 5 min. 3 mg mL−1 chitosan was dispersed to form a homogenous solution by adding 0.5 m acetic acid. The chitosan dispersion was then added into the CNCs dispersion followed by the addition of MBA (0.5 wt%) and LAP (0.5 wt%) and rigorously agitated. After complete dissolution, the whole mixture was wrapped by aluminum foil and degassed for 45 min with 15‐min interval pulses. After the removal of entrapped air bubbles, the whole pregel ink was kept in static condition for another 30 min and used after for printing.

## Fabrication and 3D Printing of Hydrogel
The hydrogels were fabricated through 3D printing using an in‐house modified FlashForge Creator Pro, United States. The printing process involved the use of a 365 nm UV goose neck LED accessory. A 10 mL single‐use UV protective syringe (EFD Nordson, USA) equipped with a 27‐gauge needle tip was utilized for the entire printing procedure. Mesh‐type models were created using FreeCAD software and saved as STL files. Subsequently, ReplicatorG was transformed the files into G‐code files. The ink flow rate was regulated by altering the extruding pressure within the range of 10–22 psi with the nozzle's velocity of 30 and 15 mm−1 s feed rate. The printing process was maintained under pulsatile UV irradiation at a wavelength of 365 nm. For purification, the gel was immersed into a 70% v/v ethanol–water mixture to eliminate the unreacted reagents and oligomeric fractions. Subsequently, the gel was immersed in water, washed thoroughly and dried at room temperature.
The dried hydrogel samples were immersed in solutions containing different concentrations of CDs until the hydrogel reached equilibrium. The samples were then transferred into 50 mL beaker containing water and thoroughly washed to remove unbound CDs followed by drying at room temperature. The synthesized hybrid hydrogels were labeled as BCGx, where “x” denotes specific amounts (1, 3, and 5 mg mL−1) of CDs used. BCG0 stands for pure hydrogel without loading of CDs.

## Results and Discussions

## Characterization of CDs
In this study, a simple hydrothermal approach using chitosan and BKC was used to synthesize the BCCDs (Figure 1a). The plausible mechanistic pathway of the formation of proposed CDs could be categorized into three consecutive stages, viz. thermal decomposition, amidation‐esterification, and surface passivation‐based functionalization. When chitosan is heated, it undergoes pyrolysis, which causes it to break down into smaller organic molecules. These molecules subsequently carbonize, forming the core of the CDs. The amine groups present in chitosan can undergo interactions with carboxyl or ester groups that may be produced during the degradation of BKC, resulting in amidation or esterification reactions. These reactions can lead to the incorporation of nitrogen and other functional groups into the structure of CDs. BKC molecules can bind to the CDs’ surface, resulting in surface passivation and the addition of hydrophobic and aromatic properties from the benzyl group. This modifies the chemical composition on the surface and potentially affects the optical properties of BCCDs. The TEM image of BCCDs (Figure 1b) and the corresponding size distribution indicate that the synthesized BCCDs are well dispersed and quasi‐spherical, with an average diameter of 5 nm. Figure 1c shows the aqueous solution of the different concentrations of synthesized BCCDs have yellow to orange color under visible light and are stable at room temperature. The aqueous solutions of the BCCDs emit bright blue fluorescence under UV light (365 nm) (Figure 1d,e) shows the ink optimizations for the 3D printed hydrogel followed by different concentrations of BCCDs infusion to the hydrogel matrix.

FTIR analysis was conducted to understand the surface functional groups on the BCCDs. Peaks in the range of 3100–3643 cm−1 correspond to amine and hydroxyl groups. The broadening of the peak is attributed to hydrogen bonding (Figure 2a). The peaks that appeared at 2925, 2854, 1706, and 1407 cm−1 are associated with C─H, and C═O stretching vibrations, respectively.[ 18a ] The peak at 1584 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of CO‐NH groups. Furthermore, peaks at 1590 and 2350 cm−1 attributed to the vibration and deformation bands of N─H, and the presence of C≡N, respectively which suggest the existence of amino‐containing functional groups.[ 23 ] The characteristic peaks at 1457, 1127, and 1070 cm−1 are attributed to C─N, C─O─C, and C─O, respectively.[ 24 ] The results indicate the presence of −OH, −COOH, and −NH2 groups on the surface of BCCDs. The functional groups enhance the hydrophilicity and stability of BCCDs in aqueous solutions and enable them to conjugate with the polymers, and specific targets, expanding their utility in various applications. XPS was applied to investigate the chemical composition and functional groups of the CDs. Figure 2b demonstrates that the XPS survey curve reveals the presence of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) elements in the CDs. The high‐resolution C1s curve has three distinct peaks at 284.7, 286.0, and 288.1 eV. These peaks correspond to different chemical bonds C−C/C═C, C−N/C−O, and C═O, respectively as shown in Figure 2c. The N1s curve (Figure 2d) exhibits four distinct peaks at 398.4, 399.4, 400.5, and 402.1 eV, corresponding to C═N─C, C─N─C, N─H, and quaternary N, respectively.[ 25 ] The O 1s XPS spectrum (Figure S1a, Supporting Information) can be deconvoluted into peaks at 530.8, 532.2, and 534.2 eV, which correspond to the presence of C═O, C─O, and phenolic OH, respectively.[ 26 ] The combined findings from FTIR and XPS analysis demonstrate that the CDs consist of C, H, N, and O elements and have various bonds or functional groups including OH, CH2, C−C/C═C, C−N/C−O, C═O, and quaternary N. The high water‐dispersibility of the CDs may be attributed to the presence of OH and quaternary N groups on their surface. The UV–vis absorption spectrum of BCCDs (Figure 2e) exhibits a broad absorption peak at 300 nm. For pure benzalkonium chloride and chitosan UV–vis spectra showed no characteristic peak at 300 nm as shown in Figure S1b,c (Supporting Information), respectively. The peak can be ascribed to n→π* transitions of the C═O and C═N bonds.[ 27 ] The fluorescence investigations of the BCCDs indicated excitation‐dependent emission behavior (Figure 2f). Figure 2f demonstrates that the emission peak changed from 405 to 494 nm when the excitation wavelength increased from 300 to 420 nm. This phenomenon is attributed to the different sizes of the BCCDs and the distribution of many surface states that arise from the different functionalities on the surface of the BCCDs. The emission intensity attained the highest point at 415 nm when excited at 340 nm. Figure S2 (Supporting Information) also examined the fluorescence stability of the CBCDs. The fluorescence intensity of the BCCDs exhibits barely any change over time, indicating the excellent stability of the BCCDs. The fluorescence spectra shown in Figure 2g indicate that the fluorescence of the BCCDs varied with the concentration (C1 to C4: 0.3 to 5 mg mL−1). At low concentrations, the intensity of the BCCDs shows an increase. However, at a high concentration, the fluorescence intensity is decreased. This observation can be attributed to the growing interaction between a large number of polar functional groups, which leads to the formation of agglomerations at high concentrations as shown in Figure 2f.

The antioxidant activity of BCCDs was assessed using a DPPH free radical scavenging test. Using a DPPH radical is a common method for assessing antioxidative activity. This involves measuring the changes in spectrophotometry occur when the DPPH• reacts with an antioxidant. This method is quick, simple, and cost‐effective. DPPH is a stable free radical due to its nitrogen atoms with lone‐pair electrons and its three benzene rings. When DPPH interacts with an antioxidant, it transforms into a DPPH‐H complex, resulting in a change from a deep violet to a light yellow color.[ 28 ] Figure S3 (Supporting Information) illustrates the study of DPPH radical scavenging activity by BCCDs at various concentrations, ranging from 0 to 1000 µg mL−1. The absorbance of DPPH solution decreased as the concentration of BCCDs increased gradually up to 500 µg mL−1. With the addition of more BCCDs, there is just little effect on the antioxidant activity. Eventually, the activity level reaches saturation. These results show that BCCDs have a high scavenging activity (≈80%) that varies depending on the dose. It is believed that the surface oxygen and nitrogen functionalities play an important part in scavenging reactive oxygen species.[ 18 , 29 ] This could be attributed to the valence states and covalent radius. Studies have found that the presence of electron‐donating doped sites and sp2 carbon domains are closely related to the primary radical scavenging sites. These sites play a crucial role in destroying free radicals by forming adducts and facilitating spin delocalization across the conjugated carbon backbone.[ 29 , 30 ] The presence of dopants can promote the radical scavenging reaction by transferring electrons to radicals, followed by proton transfer or the formation of an adduct.[ 31 ] The ROS scavenging activity of CDs could be attributed to the H‐donor behavior of the edge‐site functional groups, such as OH, COOH, and NH2, combined with the free electron delocalization and stabilization at the sp2‐hybridized carbon network.
Fluorescence intensities of the BCCDs were monitored in a series of pH solutions ranging from 3.5 to 12.5. The fluorescence intensity shows variations in response to pH changes (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The fluorescence signal of the resulting CDs reveals a nearly 4.5‐fold increase when the pH was altered from 12.5 to 7.3. The pH sensitivity of CDs to the external environment is influenced by surface passivation, which plays a significant role in affecting the surface chemistry and physical properties of the CDs. This phenomenon could be attributed to the varying degrees of protonation of different functional groups at different pH levels. As the pH values increased, the surface of the CDs evolved into functionalities with negative charges. This was because of the dissociation of amide and carboxylic groups, which could potentially lead to a decrease in PL. As a result, CDs exposed to high pH values dissociated into isolated particles, causing weak fluorescence.[ 32 ] The excitation‐emission contour plot at various pHs and their corresponding 3D excitation–emission survey spectra are shown in Figure 2i–m.

## Fabrication of Neat‐Hydrogel and F‐Strips
An overview of the chitosan‐graft‐polyacrylamide hydrogel's fabrication is depicted in Figure 3 . CNCs behave as thixotropic agents, forming a physical gel when in the presence of water, which leads to the hybrid ink exhibiting shear thinning behavior. CNCs are generated through the process of sulfuric acid hydrolysis, resulting in a surface that is rich in negative charges. The CNCs control the rheological properties of the ink, allowing for the creation of a suitable yield stress to temporarily secure an extrusion‐printed layer. Rheology measurements were conducted to enhance comprehension of ink behavior throughout different printing phases. In order to avoid ink leakage from the syringe when not in use, it is necessary for the ink to have a sufficiently high viscosity at a low rate of shear. However, during the movement of the syringe piston, it is crucial for the viscosity to rapidly decrease in order to facilitate extrusion. The gelation process of chitosan‐g‐polyacrylamide (CS‐g‐PAM) comprises multiple sequential steps. To begin with, chitosan is activated by undergoing radical formation, which results in the formation of reactive sites on its backbone resulting in chitosan macroradicals. Subsequently, sites for initiation are generated on the chitosan structure through the utilization of radical initiators as a dissociation product of LAP. Graft chains are formed when acrylamide monomers react with the initiating sites on chitosan during polymerization. The addition of crosslinking agents (here MBA) has been used as a chemical crosslinker to form spatial network morphology.
a) Plausible reaction scheme of synthesizing polysaccharide‐g‐polyacrylamide hydrogel. b) CDs infusion with time.
To make a CDs‐infiltrated elastic fluorescent hydrogel strip (F‐strip), two primary stapes are needed: homogenization of CDs in liquid and infusion of CDs into the elastic hydrogel. At the first step, we prepared aqueous CDs dispersion without any agglomeration and confirmed its optical translucency. We immersed the printed hydrogel strip into CDs dispersion. As the dispersed medium of CDs is water, thus the hydrogel strips are easily prone to swell due to polarity matching. After equilibrium swelling, the gel strips were taken out and air dried for further use and named as F‐strips. The water facilitates the movement of CDs from the dispersion into the pores in the hydrogel that have been opened by exposure to water. Thorough washing and drying remove any CDs attached to the surface, resulting in gel strips infused with CDs. All steps in this process are essential for obtaining high‐quality F‐strips. For the hydrogel samples, the amounts of CDs infused were calculated, and they varied from ≈0.98% to 4.9% CDs infused to gel matrices compared to the total gel mass. The variation of CDs infused into hydrogel matrices is based on the concentration gradient of CDs. For a diluted system, the uptake was initially slow, and it reached a plateau prematurely due to a low concentration gradient. But for 5 mg mL−1 system, the CDs infusion was initially fast and the loading was high compared to the 1 mg mL−1. The hydrogels loaded with carbon dots (CDs) show a noticeable change in appearance, as illustrated in Figure 1e.

## Hydrogel Rheology/Hydrogel Nanocomposites
Swollen hydrogels were tested for gel strength by subjecting them to shear stress. Figure 5a displays the results of the frequency sweep experiment, illustrating the elastic moduli of the fabricated hydrogels. The high elastic component (G′) for the gel is supported by the fact that CNCs are the main strength‐bearing component. For the neat‐hydrogel and the infused hydrogel, there were no significant changes in elastic moduli which could imply their identical gel strength. The F‐strip specimen shows very little enhancement in elasticity. The damping behavior of the gels is also depicted in Figure 5b. Similar to the elastic moduli plot, it is shown that the damping nature of the neat hydrogel was a little higher than the infused hydrogel specimen due to the reinforcing nature of CDs. Figure 5c represents the complex viscosity of the hydrogels in the frequency sweep experiment. The complex viscosity of a hydrogel is the viscosity it exhibits under oscillatory shear conditions, usually determined by rheological methods. A hydrogel often exhibits a frequency‐dependent behavior in a complex viscosity versus frequency plot due to its viscoelastic nature. At low frequencies, the complex viscosity is higher, suggesting that the hydrogel displays more solid‐like characteristics and resists flow. With increasing frequency, the complex viscosity may drop, suggesting a more fluid‐like behavior that enables the material to flow more easily under shear stress. The rise in viscosity at low frequencies is typically linked to the elastic component outweighing the viscous component. With higher oscillation frequency, there is reduced time for polymer chains to relax during stress cycles. As a result, the substance shows characteristics similar to a fluid, leading to a decrease in complicated viscosity. High‐frequency decreases in viscosity are usually linked to the predominance of the viscous component over the elastic component. As the infusion of CDs is not severely reacted to the hydrogel matrix, hence there would not be any drastic change in internal morphology. So, both the hydrogels show a similar trend in frequency‐dependent complex viscosity.

Mechanical properties of the F‐strips were evaluated by uniaxial tension and compression modes. Figure 5d is the tensile stress versus strain plot of all the F‐strips. The base material is BCG0 which was infused by colloidal carbon dots dispersion. So, the ultimate tensile strength and the nature of the plot are almost similar for all infused hydrogels. There are two slopes observed in the plot; the initial domain shows a higher slope than the later portion. This could signify the elastic nature of the hydrogel followed by plastic deformation. This elasticity of the hydrogel was affected by the infused CDs as well. The tensile strength increased gradually with the CDs concentration. When CDs are imbibed into the hydrogel matrices by infusion mechanism, they get anchored to the macrochains. The mode of anchoring is hypothesized as a polar–polar interaction between the CDs surface and macrochain functionalities.[ 33 ] This attachment is purely physical and there is no chemical bond formation during this infusion. During the application of stress, the hydrogels are prone to elongate because of their rubber‐like elasticity. CDs are incompressible pseudospherical entities which are susceptible to anchor polymer chains by physisorption.[ 34 ] Such physisorption behaviors are stringent due to compatibility effect of functional groups. Moreover, CDs are the “fix points” inside polymer gel matrices which affect the stress dissipation during the stretching. When externally applied forces are applied, the viscoelastic gel phase absorbs the stress and transfers to the elastic phases (here nanoparticles CNCs and CDs) present in the matrices. The elongation at break was gradually decreasing with increasing the ultimate tensile strength of hydrogel (Figure S5, Supporting Information). As the main gel template (BCG0) was fabricated with CNCs, the ultimate tensile strength and plot behavior are quite similar to the other CDs infused gels. For CDs‐loaded hydrogels, the stress dissipation was alleviated synergistically as shown in the compression stress–strain plot as well (Figure 5e). For better clarification, a graphical illustration has been given in Figure 5f which demonstrates CDs encapsulated macrochains inside hydrogel matrices. The perspective view of the model signifies the physiosorbed assembly of CDs and polymer chains. This “fix points” are the stress transfer centers for the hydrogel. The claimed rubber‐like elasticity has been supported also by the uniaxial cyclic loading‐unloading test as shown in Figure 5g. The cyclic loading‐unloading of the hydrogel both in tension and compression modes are shown in Videos S2 and S3 (Supporting Information), respectively. For the first cycle the loading–unloading lines show hysteresis loops of the hydrogel but for multiple times cyclic loading–unloading there were no significant changes in their degradation or irreversible configurationality. Moreover, in some soft materials fatigue is also observed during the cyclic stress–strain behavior which is reflected into their graphs with significant deformation in their hysteresis loops are higher cycle number. This phenomenon is sometimes related to “cyclic softening” which results eventual failures. But as of our test, there were no such failures happen even after 100 cycles which supports our rubber‐like elasticity as well. The data fluctuation was also measured for the cyclic stability of the hydrogels in both tensile and compression testing (Figure 5h), but the error was in between 2% and 3% throughout the whole procedure. The Young's moduli of the hydrogels are also depicted in Figure S6 (Supporting Information) which shows CDs infused specimens have better elasticity compared to their BCG0 template specimen.
Toughness is a critical mechanical characteristic that governs a material's capacity to resist breaking and endure mechanical forces. Hydrogels can be made more robust by adding reinforcing fillers like nanoparticles, fibers, or micro/nanoparticles to make them more resistant to fracture and stop cracks from spreading.[ 35 ] The filler's stress‐distribution and energy‐absorbing properties under deformation are commonly thought to be responsible for this increased toughness.[ 36 ] Incorporating CDs into a hydrogel can improve its toughness by increasing energy dissipation via processes like plastic deformation and debonding. The CDs and polymer matrix form strong interfacial bonds, which enhance load transfer and delay failure. Additionally, the two components can work together to provide additional toughening processes through synergistic effects. Similarly we observed enhancement of toughness after infusion of CDs and this toughness values show monotonic growth (Figure S7, Supporting Information) with the gradient concentration of colloidal CDs phases. The instantaneous strain recovery could also be understood by their resilience measurement. The resilience data obtained from the tension mode testing shows gradual increment in rebound resilience (Figure S8, Supporting Information) with increasing the CDs concentration in hydrogel. Resilience is the property of a material that allows it to undergo elastic deformation, store that energy, and then return to its original shape when released from the deformation. The increasing resilience could infer their increasing elasticity with infusion doses as discussed earlier.

## Uniaxial Tactile Sensing in Tension Mode

## Water Uptake, Diffusion Mechanism, and Network Parameters
Water uptake behavior of the hydrogels is plotted in Figure 7a where time dependent gravimetric method has been applied to estimate the swell ratio. Diffusion into a hydrogel is proportionate to the gradient of water concentration across the matrix, as stated by Fick's first law. This suggests that, within the hydrogel, water molecules have a tendency to migrate from areas of higher to areas of lower concentration.[ 19b ] Assuming a homogeneous hydrogel network and relatively free‐moving water molecules due to the polymer chains, Fickian diffusion can characterize water uptake. In general, this is the case for hydrogel systems with a high water content or polymer networks that are only crosslinked.[ 37 ] Diffusion in the polymer gels occurs passively, but it can be stimulated by swelling in the release medium, specifically water in this case. Additionally, it can be activated by external physical forces such as polar, osmotic, or convective forces. In order to comprehend the mechanism of diffusion, the swelling data was subjected to Equations (2) and (3) to assess the diffusion characteristics, namely the diffusion constant (k D), diffusion exponent (n), and diffusion coefficient (D) of the hydrogels. Incorporating nanoparticles into the hydrogel matrix causes nanocomposite hydrogels to behave differently in terms of diffusion than conventional hydrogels. Several factors contribute to this shift, including improved mechanical properties brought about by nanoparticle reinforcement, which in turn can limit the mobility of polymer chains and, consequently, the diffusion of water molecules.[ 38 ] Another way nanoparticles can change diffusion behavior is by adding tortuosity effects, which change the length of the diffusion path. Additional factors that affect the rate of water uptake and diffusion include the size distribution of the nanoparticles within the hydrogel matrix and any surface modifications they may have. The diffusion exponent “n” for Fickian diffusion is usually close to 0.5, whereas the Fick's second law obeys the square root dependency on time. The diffusion exponent can be different from 0.5 in non‐Fickian diffusion mechanisms like anomalous or case II diffusion, suggesting a different release mechanism in which the release rate is not solely dependent on the square root of time. When the water uptake rate is not dependent on time, this phenomenon is called Case II diffusion, which is also called non‐Fickian or zero‐order kinetics. It is observed in specific hydrogel systems. It is commonly believed that this behavior is caused by the simultaneous relaxation of polymers and chains along with drug release, resulting in a continuous and steady release profile. In this case as well the “n” values of the BCG hydrogels are deviated from ideality (i.e., 0.5) signifying a combinational phenomenon of chain relaxation and elasticity of the gel networks. The diffusion parameters are given in Figure 7c where it is evident that the D values of the hydrogels significantly changed with the infusion concentration. It implies the CDs are interacting to the polymer network by some physical attachments. This affects their water uptake rate during infusion mechanism.

According to 1st order kinetics, the time dependent water uptake is the consequence of the rate of swelling being directly proportional to the concentration gradient of water within the hydrogel. The rate of swelling, on the other hand, is dependent on the concentration of water and the availability of unoccupied sites for water absorption, according to pseudo 2nd order kinetics. According to this model, the process is not limited by diffusion but rather by chemisorption or surface adsorption of water molecules onto the hydrogel network. Hydrogel systems are evaluated using experimental data and model fitting to establish the most suitable kinetic model, with 1st order kinetics describing diffusion‐controlled swelling and pseudo‐second‐order kinetics describing adsorption‐controlled swelling. The network parameters are also depicted in Figure 7k and Table 1 in detail. During crosslinking, carbon dots within the polymer matrix can affect the arrangement of polymer chains, which in turn changes the network structure (especially the crosslink density; CLD) and mesh size.[ 40 ] This phenomenon is known as nanoparticles induced steric hindrance. Furthermore, interactions between carbon dots and polymer chains can change the crosslinking density. These interactions can change the crosslinking efficiency or distribution within the hydrogel. The alteration in the density of cross‐linking, induced by CDs, can be ascribed to their interaction with the polymer matrix. CDs with functional groups like carboxyl, amine, or hydroxyl can form covalent connections or hydrogen bonds with polymer chains.[ 41 ] This interaction can potentially increase cross‐linking density if CDs serve as additional agents, or it can modify the existing network if they disrupt or enhance the polymerization process. As previously discussed, water imbibition and the rate of diffusion are key indicators of their networking capability. As shown in Figure 6k, the CLD was drastically changed even after low dose of infusion (BCG1) which was gradually enhanced with CDs content in infusion media. This definitely implies the proneness of CDs to hydrogel matrix forming physiosorbed nanohybrid system which triggers the transition between viscoelastic to more elastic hydrogel.

## pH Sensitivity and Wound Monitoring Tool
pH levels have a significant impact on the healing process of both acute and chronic wounds.[ 48 ] Figure 8j illustrates the standard pH fluctuations observed in both acute and chronic wounds.[ 49 ] The skin adhesion test of hydrogels is essential for wound healing applications because strong adhesion ensures the stable placement of the hydrogel, allowing uninterrupted protection, moisture retention, and therapeutic delivery at the wound site. CDs promote hydrogel adherence to the skin by increasing cross‐link density and surface contact. Their functional groups have the ability to establish hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with skin proteins, which increases adhesive strength. The digital images of skin adhesion test is depicted in Figure S12a in the Supporting Information. In addition to the qualitative assessment, quantitative measurements of adhesion strength were performed on porcine skin. The results (Figure S12b, Supporting Information) demonstrate that the BCG1 specimen exhibited a slightly higher adhesion strength compared to the control. For the BCG3 sample, a more pronounced increase in adhesion strength was observed. However, the BCG5 sample displayed adhesion characteristics similar to those of the BCG3 sample. This was probably caused by extensive crosslinking, which may have blocked active sites on the hydrogel surface.
As depicted in Figure 8k, the pH levels in acute wounds exhibit a straightforward profile. However, there is a noticeable shift between acidic pH levels (around 5–6) and basic pH levels (7.5–8) during the healing process. This suggests a more significant pH alteration in acute wounds compared to chronic wounds. Chronic wounds have a less pronounced oscillation of pH (7–8) compared to acute wounds, indicating a more complex and prolonged healing process. It has been indicated that the primary cause of the pH fluctuations is related to the presence of bacterial colonization. In the literature, various factors have been suggested as potential sources of pH oscillation in the wound‐healing process.[ 48 ] These include the type of bacteria present, their toxicity, the type of tissue involved, angiogenesis, protease activity, and oxygen release.[ 49 ] Herein, the pH sensitivity of BCG hydrogel was determined through fluorescence analysis. The emission intensity was measured while varying the pH from 4.9 to 10.2, as depicted in Figure 8l. The BCG hydrogel surface was characterized by the presence of carboxyl and amine groups. These groups play a role in the pH sensitivity of the surface, as they can undergo deprotonation and protonation with changes in pH. The fluorescence emission intensity noticeably dropped as the pH decreased from 6 to 4 and in the alkaline pH medium. Given the pH fluctuations observed in the wound healing process (Figure 8j), it is evident that the pH levels primarily vary between 4 and 9. The excitation–emission contour plot at pH 7.7 and 9.1 shows the differences in intensities (Figure 8m,n). The different pH levels in healthy skin and wounds are shown in Figure 8o. Thus, the suggested pH‐sensitive BCG hydrogel can be considered a promising option for tracking pH levels during the process of wound healing. Regarding the concept of the desirable pH range for wound healing, the Figure 8l shows the detailed responses of the pH‐sensitive nanocomposites to pH changes ranging from 4.9 to 9.1. The results demonstrates a clear and suitable linear correlation between the pH values and fluorescence intensities. The regression coefficient (R 2 = 0.98) indicates a decent linear relationship between the pH values and fluorescence intensity. The findings demonstrate that the developed BCG hybrid hydrogel can serve as an efficient pH‐sensitive probe for biomedical purposes, particularly in monitoring pH levels throughout the wound healing process.

Cell viability assays are essential to analyze the impact of biomaterials on cellular health and have been widely used in biomedical research. ## Molecular Dynamics Simulation Studies
The MD simulation was primarily conducted to explore the potential interactions between the components of the hybrid hydrogel. Each component—CD, CNC, chitosan, and polyacrylamide (PAM)—was designed and modified to closely replicate the experimental features, details of which is observable in the Supporting Information.
Figure 8p illustrates the ultimate molecular snapshot of the hydrogel components after a 60 ns absorption process. It is evident that the nanofillers can form a stable complex with both polyacrylamide chains and the chitosan chain. This self‐assembly is primarily driven by non‐bonded van der Waals (vdW) and Coulombic interactions,[ 50 ] with the contributions of each for the different components during the last 5 ns displayed in Figure 8q. Accordingly, the non‐bonded interaction energies predominantly consist of interactions between the CD and PAM chains, as well as between the CNC and PAM chains. In the CD‐PAM interaction, both the non‐polar graphene‐like sheets and polar functional groups in the CD model interact with the amide groups present in PAM. In contrast, electrostatic interactions are more pronounced in the CNC‐PAM interaction due to the abundance of surface polar groups (i.e., ─OH and ─COOH functional groups) in the CNC component. A similar pattern can also be identified in CD‐chitosan and CNC‐chitosan interactions. At any rate, interactions between the nanofillers are minimal as the gaps are primarily filled with polymeric chains.
Hydrogen bond (HB) formation is another principal factor in defining the mechanical properties of the hydrogel matrix.[ 51 ] The geometrical criteria for HBs were specified as a donor–acceptor distance of less than 0.35 nm and an acceptor–donor–hydrogen angle of less than 30°. Similar to other nonbonded interactions, the average number of HBs formed between nanofillers and PAM during the last 5 ns exceeds that of other components, as shown in Figure 8r. This underscores the role of CD and CNC in reinforcing the binding strength within the hydrogel network.
Lastly, the three incorporated PAM models, along with the chitosan chain, were analyzed for conformational changes during the absorption process. To this end, the radius of gyration, a crucial parameter for understanding the structural behavior of polymeric chains, is defined as follows (6) Rg=∑i∥ri∥2mi∑imi1/2 where mi is the mass of atomic site i and ri is the position of atomic site i relative to the center of mass of the polymeric model. The time‐dependent R g for all four models is plotted in Figure 8s. While the Rg values of the chitosan chain fluctuate around the initial value of 2.2 nm, a perceptible decline occurs in the case of the PAM models, decreasing from the initial 1.9 nm to final values of 1.1, 1.15, and 1.25 nm. Consistent with previous results, polyacrylamide tends to entangle more with present nanofillers compared to chitosan, highlighting the stronger absorption of PAM within the hydrogel matrix.

## Antioxidant and Antibacterial Properties
A high level of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the wound environment might cause damage to proteins in tissues, leading to inflammation. Hydrogel dressings containing antioxidant characteristics can efficiently eliminate highly produced free radicals to expedite the process of wound healing.[ 52 ] To assess the antioxidant properties of hybrid BCG hydrogels, we measured the capacity to scavenge the stable 1‐diphenyl‐2‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical. Upon exposure to the hydrogel samples, the purple color of the DPPH solution begins to fade over time. Further, the hydrogels show a better scavenging effect on free radicals as the BCCDs concentrations increased as shown in Figure 9a. The BCG3 hydrogel showed a significant DPPH scavenging percentage of ≈57%. The BCG5 hydrogel containing a higher amount of CDs unveiled a notable increase in antioxidant activity, reaching 84%. The free radical scavenging activity of the BCG0 film was less than 7% in ABTS assays. When CDs were introduced, the ABTS scavenging activity of the BCG1 hydrogel increased significantly to about 65% after 30 min (Figure 9b). The ABTS scavenging activity can reach a maximum value of over 90% for the higher‐loading CDs (BCG3). Prior studies have noted that the electron‐donating functionalities present at the surface of the CDs including hydroxyl, carboxyl, and other chemical groups have a strong ability to capture free radicals.[ 18b ] Further, there have been reports of interactions between the primary amino groups and free radicals.[ 53 ] As mentioned earlier, the BCCDs' surface contained these groups, offering the hydrogel a remarkable antioxidant capacity.

The disc diffusion experiment was conducted to evaluate the antimicrobial properties of the hydrogel samples. Skin wounds are extremely vulnerable to bacterial infections, which can cause inflammation and impede the process of tissue healing. Wounds can create an environment conducive to the colonization of microorganisms, which can hinder the healing process and potentially lead to infection. For a hydrogel to be effective as a wound dressing, it needs to act as a protective barrier against harmful microorganisms and can combat a wide range of bacteria, thus preventing the growth of these microorganisms in the wound. The antibacterial hydrogel dressings can rapidly eliminate pathogenic microbes and significantly reduce the risk of bacterial infections, which are important for promoting wound healing procedures. Herein, we have studied the antibacterial activity of the samples on both gram‐negative E. coli and gram‐positive B. subtilis bacteria. The nanoparticles CDs were tested for their antibacterial properties using the MIC assay on the two bacterial strains. The antibacterial activity of the BCCDs samples was tested in 96‐well microtiter plates. Samples were prepared and treated with 106 CFU mL−1 bacteria. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were determined using the reported method.[ 54 ] The MIC refers to the lowest concentration of a sample that can prevent visible bacterial growth after being incubated overnight. Using a 96‐well microtiter plate, different concentrations of CDs samples were exposed to diluted bacteria. Wells containing only diluted bacteria were used as a control. Following a 24 h incubation period at 37 °C, the MIC was determined by monitoring the wells where no visible bacterial growth was observed. The growth of E. coli and B. subtilis strains was effectively inhibited by the CDs, with MICs determined to be 0.030 mg mL−1.
The in vitro antibacterial activities of nanocomposite hydrogels against gram‐negative and gram‐positive bacteria were assessed using the disc diffusion test. We measured the diameters of the hydrogels' zones of inhibitions against Gram‐negative bacteria E. coli and Gram‐positive bacteria B. subtilis, respectively. No inhibitory region was detected around the pure hydrogel sample for both B. subtilis and E. coli tests, as depicted in Figure 9. The antimicrobial assessment technique is displayed graphically in Figure S15 in the Supporting Information. On the other hand, hydrogel containing CDs exhibited distinct zones of inhibition against both B. subtilis and E. coli. Figures S16 and S17 (Supporting Information) depict the visual identification of an inhibitory zone for the growth of bacteria in the tested materials. The findings suggest that the nanocomposite hydrogel containing CDs exhibited good bactericidal activity compared to the pure hydrogel when tested under similar experimental conditions, as evidenced by the observed inhibition zone. Furthermore, the antibacterial efficacy of the hydrogels were further assessed by exposing bacterial suspension to the hydrogel samples at 37 °C for 24 h with continuous shaking. Interestingly, after 24 h of incubation, the CDs infused BCG hydrogels showed a significant decrease in bacterial growth in comparison to the control hydrogel (BCG0). Figure suppinfo1 (Supporting Information) depicts the antibacterial activity of BCG hydrogels against E. coli and B. subtilis. A significant bacterial growth were observed after contact with the control BCG0 hydrogel. However, no detectable bacterial growth was observed following exposure to the BCG5. The BCG5 hydrogel exhibited excellent antibacterial activity, achieving almost complete eradication of bacteria. The results revealed that BCG hydrogels are efficient against all tested bacteria, but the unloaded hydrogel (BCG0) exhibited no antibacterial activity.
The observed antibacterial activity of the hydrogels can be attributed to the incorporation of CDs. Regarding the antibacterial mechanism against E. coli and B. subtilis, it is widely accepted that the following events took place: the presence of a positive charge at the surface of the CDs enables the electrostatic interaction and facilitates the absorption onto the negatively charged cell surface of bacteria. The positive charge on the surface enhances the attraction to the cell membrane, resulting in the process of agglomeration and disruption of membrane equilibrium.[ 55 ] As a result, the outer membranes of the bacteria undergo a significant alteration in their structure. This alteration causes the release of proteinaceous material from within the cell.[ 56 ] Furthermore, it also results in the release of cytoplasmic contents, including K+ ions, DNA, and RNA, ultimately causing bacterial death.[ 57 ] This study presents the incorporation of metal‐free surface quaternized CDs, which possess antibacterial properties, into a hydrogel matrix. The objective is to develop a novel metal‐free biocompatible dressing with both antibacterial and antioxidant properties. Therefore, we envision the hybrid hydrogel is promising to be used as a safe wound dressing.

## Conclusion
In this study, fluorescent CDs is developed through a straightforward one‐step hydrothermal process. Through in situ UV‐induced free radical gelation during 3D microextrusion printing, semi‐interpenetrating polymer networks are produced followed by infusion with colloidal CDs dispersion for easy and facile fabrication of stretchable fluorescent strips. The hybrid gel was developed by incorporating chitosan as the non‐crosslinked component, polyacrylamide homopolymer as the crosslinked component, and CNCs as the shear thinning agent. The modeling approach was employed to unveil the essence and intensity of interactions between the present components, leading to the formation of the final complex. Our findings describe a new class of semi‐interpenetrating polymeric hydrogels based on infused CDs with desirable properties such as shear‐thinning, zero compression set, rapid curing, consistent compression moduli in dynamic conditions, noncytotoxicity. The hydrogels loaded with CDs demonstrated remarkable antibacterial properties, successfully eradicating bacterial strains from both the Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative categories.
