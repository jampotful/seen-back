Abstract

Purpose
to evaluate the agreement and reliability of sensitivity measurements obtained with the AVA virtual
reality perimeter compared to conventional Octopus 101 perimeter in patients with glaucoma.

Methods
A cross-sectional pilot study was conducted in 24 patients with stable glaucoma. Perimetric
examinations were performed using AVA (Elisar Fast strategy) and Octopus 101 (TOP strategy). Levels
of agreement were analyzed for global, sectorial, and pointwise sensitivity, as well as for the detection of
topographic defects and classification according to the Glaucoma Staging System 2.

Results
Global sensitivity showed strong correlation and good reliability (rho = 0.78; ICC = 0.85), as did sectorial
sensitivity by eccentricity (rho = 0.85; ICC = 0.80), while pointwise sensitivity was moderate (rho = 0.50;
ICC = 0.64). Bland–Altman analysis of global sensitivity revealed a mean bias of 0.057 dB and limits of
agreement between − 0.129 and 0.243 dB, with no significant proportional bias. Detection of topographic
defects coincided in 50% of cases, with moderate agreement (kappa = 0.483; 95% CI: 0.23–0.74). Ninetytwo percent of cases corresponded to stage S1 or lower, with weak agreement in glaucoma staging
(kappa = 0.327; 95% CI: 0.03–0.62; p = 0.038).

Conclusions
The AVA perimeter demonstrates solid agreement at global and sectorial levels, with moderate
pointwise consistency compared to Octopus 101, supporting its clinical applicability in glaucoma. Its
ability to detect topographic defects and classify disease severity is acceptable, though algorithmic
adjustments and normative cut-offs may enhance performance. Longitudinal studies with larger sample
sizes and healthy control groups are recommended to validate and optimize its utility.

Key points
1- AVA demonstrated consistently higher pointwise sensitivity values within the central 30° visual field,
while maintaining strong concordance with Octopus 101, enhancing its potential applicability in routine
clinical practice.
2- Both devices showed strong correlations and good reliability across global and sectoral analyses, with
minimal bias; however, they should not be considered interchangeable for longitudinal patient follow-up.
Page 2/18

3- Glaucoma Staging System 2 severity classification revealed only fair agreement between AVA and
Octopus, highlighting the need for specific cut-off values tailored to virtual reality perimeters for
consistent clinical staging.

Introduction
Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting more than 79.6 million
people in 2020, with projections indicating an increase to 111.8 million cases by 2040. Standard
automated perimetry remains an essential tool for detecting functional alterations in glaucoma patients,
as well as the reference method for determining disease severity, estimating prognosis, and monitoring
progression over time. [1–3]
Several studies have suggested that virtual reality devices, such as the Advanced Vision Analyzer (AVA),
may provide performance comparable to, or even superior in certain parameters, to conventional
perimetry. However, there remains a need to validate the agreement between both technologies,
particularly regarding visual field sensitivity and glaucoma damage staging. Such validation would
support the consolidation of more precise and effective diagnostic strategies, ultimately contributing to
improved prevention of progression toward irreversible blindness. [4, 5]
The objective of this study was to evaluate the agreement and reliability of sensitivity measurements
obtained with the AVA virtual reality perimeter compared to those acquired with the conventional
Octopus 101 perimeter in patients with glaucoma. Furthermore, the study assessed concordance in the
detection of topographic defects and in the classification of glaucoma severity. The Octopus 101 is often
the device available in clinical practice in many developing countries.

Methods
A cross‑sectional, observational, and analytical pilot study was conducted in 24 right eyes of 24 patients
diagnosed with primary open angle glaucoma who attended the Glaucoma Department of the Cuban
Institute of Ophthalmology “Ramón Pando Ferrer” between January 2023 and January 2024. Inclusion
criteria comprised patients over 40 years of age with aided monocular distance visual acuity ≤ 0.20
logMAR (6/9.5 Snellen) and otherwise normal ophthalmological examination, except for findings
consistent with primary open‑angle glaucoma. Exclusion criteria included history of ocular trauma,
previous ocular surgery, systemic diseases with ocular involvement, refractive error greater than 5
spherical diopters or 2 cylindrical diopters, coexistence of neuro‑ophthalmological disorders or other
conditions that could independently explain visual field alterations, and unreliable tests with more than
25% false positives, false negatives, or fixation losses.
Glaucoma diagnosis was defined by intraocular pressure > 21 mmHg and the presence of glaucomatous
optic neuropathy or visual field findings. Glaucomatous optic neuropathy was defined by the presence of
generalized, wedge-shaped, or slit-like defects of the retinal nerve fiber layer in the superotemporal or
inferotemporal sectors; generalized or sectoral thinning of the neuroretinal rim; a cup to disc ratio greater
Page 3/18

than 0.5; or an interocular asymmetry exceeding 0.2. A visual field was classified as glaucomatous if at
least three contiguous points within the central 30° were affected, with at least one showing a probability
of less than 1% in the normal population, and if the defect exhibited a typical pattern consistent with the
distribution of the retinal nerve fiber bundles.
All patients underwent interview and complete ophthalmological examination according to the hospital
protocol for glaucoma care. For visual field testing, two devices were used: the AVA virtual reality
perimeter and the conventional Octopus 101 perimeter. All examinations were performed by an
experienced optometrist in a dark room, with perimeter calibration according to manufacturer standards
and continuous monitoring of central fixation. The first test was performed with the Octopus 101
perimeter, corresponding to routine clinical follow‑up (TOP strategy, program 32). After one hour, patients
were evaluated with the AVA perimeter (Elisar Fast strategy, 24‑2 pattern). In patients under glaucoma
follow‑up, a single visual field test was performed, while newly diagnosed cases underwent two
evaluations. Reliability indices and scotoma topography were corroborated in each case.
The AVA perimeter consists of four main components: a virtual reality headset, a patient response
button, an operator control device, and a cloud server for storing examinations. The headset
incorporates an optical system and a liquid crystal display. The screen is coupled to convex lenses that
present a magnified virtual image at 60 cm, observed during the test. The device is dichoptic, presenting
images to each eye without requiring occlusion, and can also be performed binocularly. Eyetracking is
achieved through two infrared cameras, one for each eye, and an infrared LED system to illuminate the
pupil. Pupil images are wirelessly transmitted to the operator’s device. This technology employs the
Elisar Standard Algorithm (ESA), with a normative database of 100 patients, to determine threshold
values within normal limits. [6]
The TOP strategy of the Octopus 101 perimeter (Haag‑Streit International) uses mathematical
extrapolation after testing each visual field point once, with Goldmann III stimulus size, 100 ms duration,
and background luminance of four apostilbs. The normative database was established from a
multicenter study conducted in 1985 including approximately 350 normal subjects. [7]
Agreement between technologies was evaluated for mean sensitivity at the global level, across visual
field zones according to eccentricity, pointwise and within the sectors corresponding to the Glaucoma
Hemifield Test (GHT) (Figure 1). Agreement was not determined for mean defect and loss variance due
to inherent differences between technologies. Specifically, the Octopus 101 calculated mean defect from
74 locations and did not generate measurements when point defect reached 5 dB, representing a
methodological limitation for this study.
The Spearman’s rho was categorized according to Schober et al. [8]: < 0.10, negligible; 0.10–0.39, weak;
0.40–0.69, moderate; 0.70–0.89, strong; > 0.90, very strong. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass
correlation coefficient (ICC) following Koo and Li [9]: < 0.50, poor; 0.50–0.74, moderate; 0.75–0.90, good;
> 0.90, excellent. Concordance was analyzed using the Bland–Altman method, with natural logarithmic
transformation of mean values to reduce variability and improve distribution symmetry. Kappa index was
Page 4/18

interpreted according to Landis and Koch [10]: 0, poor; 0.01–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60,
moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; > 0.80, almost perfect.
This study was approved by the Scientific Council and Research Ethics Committee of the Cuban Institute
of Ophthalmology Ramón Pando Ferrer. Informed consent was obtained from all participants for the use
of their data in scientific research, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. [11]

Results
Data were obtained from 24 patients with a mean age of 59 years, of whom 70% were women and 62%
Caucasian. The mean sensitivity measured with the AVA perimeter was significantly higher than that
obtained with the Octopus 101, globally (1.5 ± 2.2 dB; p < 0.05) as well as in the 10–20° (1.8 ± 2.6 dB)
and 20–30° (1.6 ± 2.2 dB) zones. No significant differences were found in the central 0–10° zone (0.8 ±
2.3 dB) or in global mean defect (0.3 ± 2.4 dB). The mean examination duration exceeded five minutes
with the AVA Elisar Fast strategy, whereas it was less than four minutes with the Octopus 101 TOP
strategy (p < 0.001). (Table 1)
The mean global sensitivity measured with AVA showed a strong positive correlation with Octopus 101
(rho = 0.78; p < 0.001; 95% CI: 0.55–0.90). The reliability between both technologies was good (ICC =
0.85; p < 0.001; 95% CI: 0.65–0.94). The Bland–Altman analysis of log‑transformed mean sensitivity
revealed a mean bias of 0.06 and limits of agreement between –0.13 and 0.24, with 96% of points within
limits. The regression analysis of differences versus means showed no evidence of proportional bias (B
= 0.06; p = 0.711), indicating stable agreement across values. These findings support adequate
concordance between technologies, independent of measurement magnitude, validating AVA’s clinical
applicability for visual field sensitivity assessment.
Across the three eccentricity zones, strong and statistically significant correlations were observed
between the two technologies (rho = 0.77, 0.70, and 0.72; p < 0.001, from center to periphery). The
reliability was good, with ICC values of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.66–0.94), 0.80 (0.54–0.91), and 0.84 (0.62–0.93),
all p < 0.001. The Bland–Altman analysis of log‑transformed sensitivity values across visual field zones
showed small mean biases (0.03–0.07) with 95% limits of agreement (–0.15 to 0.29). The regression
slopes were non‑significant (p > 0.05), confirming the absence of proportional bias and the stability of
differences between AVA and Octopus across sensitivity levels.
At individual locations, Figures 2a and 2b show that AVA sensitivity averages exceeded Octopus 101 by
0–4 dB (mean 1.5 0.9 dB). Figures 2c and 2d show absolute differences ranging from 2–5 dB (mean
3.3 ± 0.6 dB). These findings indicate that although variability existed among individuals at some points,
discrepancies remained within clinically acceptable ranges.

The similar pointwise mean sensitivity between AVA and Octopus, with low differences and variability,
suggests good agreement in most locations, particularly in central zones. This figure supports the utility
of AVA as a functional assessment tool in clinical settings, with no evidence of systematic bias
The Figure 3a shows that in 83% of locations, threshold sensitivity measured with AVA correlated
moderately to strongly with Octopus 101 (rho = 0.42–0.79; p < 0.05), with mean rho of 0.50. In 77.8% of
locations (Figure 3b), pointwise sensitivity measurements were consistent between technologies (ICC >
0.50; p < 0.05). Seven locations (13%) achieved good reliability (ICC = 0.76–0.85; p < 0.05). The overall
mean ICC was 0.64, indicating moderate consistency across all locations.
With AVA, the mean sensitivity showed a moderate inverse correlation with examination duration,
indicating that patients with better sensitivity completed the test faster (rho = –0.60; p = 0.002).
Conversely, greater mean defect was associated with longer test times (moderate direct correlation, rho
Page 6/18

= 0.54; p = 0.007), and higher pattern deviation also correlated with longer durations (rho = 0.44; p =
0.034). This suggests that more impaired visual function results in longer test times. In contrast, the
Octopus perimeter showed no significant association between test duration and measures of visual
function, indicating that examination time was not influenced by visual field status.
The analysis of defect detection showed moderate agreement between AVA and Octopus 101. In 45.8%
of cases (11/24), both technologies agreed on absence of topographic defects, while one case showed a
central defect detected by both. A nasal step was detected by AVA in three cases and by Octopus in four.
Superior, inferior, or combined arcuate defects showed partial agreement, with AVA detecting more
cases. Cohen’s kappa was 0.48 (95% CI: 0.23–0.74), indicating moderate agreement in identifying
glaucomatous patterns.
Evaluation of GHT sectors showed that AVA yielded higher mean sensitivity in all sectors, with significant
differences (p < 0.05) in sectors 3 (superior and inferior), 4 (superior and inferior), and 5 superior (Table
2). Spearman’s rho coefficients evidenced significant correlations (p < 0.05) across all sectors,
predominantly moderate to strong, suggesting high functional concordance between devices in regions
typically affected by glaucoma (Figure 4). Inferior sectors 1 and 2 showed the highest correlations (rho =
0.76–0.80). In contrast, superior sector 5 presented the lowest correlation (rho = 0.53), which may reflect
anatomical variability or reduced agreement in less compromised zones. The reliability was good across
all sectors (ICC = 0.76–0.87), except for sector 5 inferior, which was moderate.
The Bland–Altman analysis showed that 92–100% of points fell within the limits of agreement across
GHT sectors. Regression analysis confirmed stable concordance without proportional bias in most
zones. In superior sector 1 and inferior sector 3, proportional bias was statistically significant,
suggesting that AVA may overestimate sensitivity at higher values compared to Octopus. However, the
low coefficients of determination (R² = 0.21 and 0.17) indicate that this bias accounted for only a small
fraction of the differences between devices. The remaining variability is likely influenced by uncontrolled
factors such as stimulus design, fatigue, virtual environment conditions, or learning effects. Overall, both
technologies demonstrated moderate agreement; however, they should not be considered
interchangeable for longitudinal follow‑up of the same patient.
Regarding glaucoma severity according to the Glaucoma Staging System 2, 87.5% of patients (21 cases)
were classified as stage S1 or lower with the conventional Octopus 101 perimeter, while 91.7% (22
cases) were classified similarly with the AVA perimeter. Both devices coincided in the same stage in 13
patients (54.2%). Cohen’s kappa index was 0.33 (95% CI: 0.03–0.62; p = 0.038), indicating fair agreement
in the assessment of glaucoma severity.

Discussion
This study represents the first comparative analysis between the AVA virtual reality perimeter and the
conventional Octopus 101 perimeter, both equipped with rapid strategies that balance diagnostic
accuracy and operational efficiency in specialized glaucoma management. Results demonstrate that
Page 8/18

AVA provides solid agreement at both global and sectorial levels compared with the conventional
standard, supporting its clinical utility. The moderate consistency in pointwise analysis, consistent with
the inherent topographic variability of the visual field, together with the absence of proportional bias and
stability of differences across measurement magnitudes, reinforces the technical validity of the AVA
device.
Previous studies have reported both overestimations [12, 13] and underestimations [6, 14] when
comparing virtual reality platforms with conventional perimeters, highlighting heterogeneity among
devices and algorithms. Stronger correlations than those observed in the present study have been
described in patients with moderate or advanced glaucoma (rho ≈ 0.80), where sensitivity curves are
more linear within the 5–25 dB range. In contrast, in healthy eyes, ceiling effects limit response variability
and reduce correlation strength (rho = 0.50–0.67). [14, 15]
At the sectorial level, this study shows good correlation in most sectors, except in peripheral regions
such as the superior nasal retina. This discrepancy may be attributed to functional preservation of these
regions in early stages or technical limitations of the headset in stimulating peripheral zones. [12]
Pointwise variability between devices, as reported by Phu et al. [16], may depend on factors such as
attention, fatigue, background luminance, and differences in thresholding strategies. While global
metrics average fluctuations, pointwise measures are more sensitive to these effects. In this context,
AVA employs low luminance levels, potentially activating mesopic as well as photopic pathways, which
may influence stimulus perception and recorded sensitivity. Differences in optical design and algorithms
also explain part of this variability: AVA uses an aspheric lens projecting to infinity and the ESA strategy
combining adaptive staircase and eye tracking; HFA employs SITA with real-time filtering; and Octopus
101 implements TOP based on normative population thresholds with progressive adjustments. Although
effective, these strategies may generate inconsistencies when compared directly or alternated between
successive examinations. Unreliable visual fields may increase up to 16% with rapid strategies,
particularly in cases of low sensitivity. [6, 7, 13, 17]
Examination time is also affected by the degree of visual damage, with adaptive algorithms such as ESA
prolonging duration when sensitivity is low, while TOP maintains more stable timing. This difference
suggests that AVA accurately reflects functional impairment, although its efficiency could be optimized
through algorithmic improvements. [18] Regarding defect detection, some studies have reported inferior
performance of virtual reality devices compared with conventional perimetry. [12, 16] However, the
present results show that AVA demonstrates comparable capacity to identify functional alterations.
Longer test duration compared with TOP may have facilitated detection of subtle defects.
Topographic analysis revealed a higher prevalence of alterations in the superior hemifield, consistent
with previous reports. [19–21] This pattern reflects structural vulnerability of the inferior temporal retina,
with greater exposure to glaucomatous damage due to higher fiber density from the inferior macula.
According to Hood et al. [22], this zone represents a critical area for early detection, consistent with AVA
findings and reinforcing the functional validity of observed defects.
Page 9/18

Regarding staging classification, regular agreement was observed, consistent with reports by Greenfield
and Mees. [23, 24] These differences may be attributed to distinct normative databases, as AVA employs
data generated under specific virtual environment conditions, including background luminance. In this
context, proposals such as those by Bradley et al. [25], who adjusted mean defect cut-offs for virtual
reality (–5 dB and − 8 dB) and improved concordance with HFA, open the possibility of harmonizing
criteria and enhancing classification across platforms.
Overall, the results of this study align with the literature in showing that conventional and virtual reality
systems present good correlation, though not full interchangeability. Differences may be explained by
factors such as adaptation thresholds, spatial summation (Ricco’s law), signal detection theory, and
variability among normative databases, underscoring the need to interpret clinical results within the
technological context of each tool. [26–28]
Previous studies have shown variability in virtual reality examination times depending on device,
algorithm, and patient type. While Narang and Razeghinejad [6, 15] reported longer times with virtual
reality, other authors such as Berneshawi and Bradley [12, 25] observed reductions or similar durations
compared with HFA. This behavior may depend on the degree of visual damage, as algorithms such as
RATA are shorter in patients with extensive scotomas, requiring fewer presentations to define low
thresholds. This suggests that optimized headsets may offer specific advantages in monitoring
advanced defects. [25]
Virtual reality perimetry presents several strengths that may favor its clinical adoption. AVA allows free
head movements during testing, improving patient comfort. Its portable design facilitates extra-hospital
monitoring, as demonstrated by Hu et al. [29] in home-based testing with high acceptance. Users also
reported the experience as clearer and more understandable, potentially enhancing adherence,
particularly in limited-access settings. [30]
Study limitations include the small sample size and absence of a healthy control group, restricting
generalizability of findings. Additionally, although AVA compensates for head movements via gyroscope,
it lacks active eye tracking, which may require frequent postural adjustments and cause fatigue in
patients with limited mobility. Focal discrepancies in defect detection were also observed, suggesting
the need for more detailed regional functional analysis.
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the AVA virtual reality perimeter achieves solid agreement at
global and sectorial levels compared with the conventional standard, as well as moderate consistency in
pointwise comparison, without biases attributable to measurement magnitude. It has also shown
capacity to identify characteristic glaucomatous patterns, supporting its applicability in clinical practice.
Nevertheless, longitudinal studies including healthy controls and larger samples, together with costeffectiveness, durability, and maintenance analyses compared with traditional equipment, will be
necessary to consolidate these findings and facilitate systematic implementation.
