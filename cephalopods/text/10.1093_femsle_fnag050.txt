Isolation of luminescent symbiont bacteria from marine cephalopods: a practical activity for the study of bacterial quorum sensing.
Pérez-Etayo L, Salvador-Bescós M, Aragón-Aranda B, Alonso-Urmeneta B, Moriyón I, Conde-Álvarez R. (2026). FEMS microbiology letters. . doi:10.1093/femsle/fnag050. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Isolation of luminescent symbiont bacteria from marine cephalopods: a practical activity for the study of bacterial quorum sensing

## Abstract
AbstractThis work describes a laboratory activity designed to illustrate the phenomenon of bacterial quorum sensing (QS), a communication mechanism in bacterial communities. The activity focuses on the bioluminescence production regulated by QS of bacteria that live in symbiosis with cephalopods. This activity targets undergraduate students in biology, biochemistry, or other sciences and aims to promote their interest in microbiology and to help students to understand the role and mechanism of QS in microorganisms by means of a visual example of symbiotic interactions between bacteria and animals. At the same time, students are expected to develop lab skills in bacterial isolation, pure culture obtention, and interpretation of microbiological results. The work also provides references and resources to help students understand the subject and teachers assess student learning.Keywords: quorum sensing, bacteria, luminescence, cephalopod

## Introduction
One of the challenges of 21st century society is to generate educational proposals that allow students to learn science, learn about science and learn to do science (Godoy 2018). For this reason, scientific education must be interdisciplinary, involving the development of skills, problem solving, decision making and the promotion of critical thinking (Salvadó et al. 2013). This is how the new trends in science, such as inquiry (IBSE: “Inquiry Based Science Education”) and modeling, are postulated. Since there are different ways to incorporate these elements into a curriculum, we have therefore developed an activity designed to maximize student interest and participation. In this activity, we aim to illustrate bacterial quorum sensing (QS) with a concrete example: bioluminescence production QS regulated by of bacteria that live in symbiosis with cephalopods. The QS is a secretion system controlled by population size and it represents a bacterial communication mechanism subject to genetic regulation to express genes that are only useful when bacteria are in community (Bassler and Losick 2006). In bacteria, the QS regulates the production of different substances such as antibiotics, toxins, enzymes, pigments, etc. (Henke and Bassler 2004).
Bioluminescent bacteria (such as Photobacterium phosphoreum, Aliivibrio fischeri, and Vibrio harveyi) possess the lux operon, which permits them to emit light in an active process involving the enzyme luciferase and the electron transport chain (O’Kane and Prasher 1992, Widder 2010). The QS controls the lux operon that regulates genes for luciferase and a complex reducer of fatty acids necessary for the activity of luciferase. Luciferase converts an aliphatic acid from long chain carboxylic acid, coupled to the oxidation of FMNH2, releasing H2O and generating light (Dunlap 1999, Bellissimo et al. 2024). The carboxylic acid can be reduced back to its aliphatic state by the complex fatty acid reducer, with new expenditure of NADPH2+ and ATP. LuxR is an active regulator that detects concentrations of the inducer (cell population dependence), in this case AHL (N-AcylHomoserine Lactone). Binding of LuxR-AHL complex to the promoter activates the bioluminescence genes. AHL is diffusible through the plasmatic membrane, allowing quorum communication (Fuqua and Greenberg 2002).
The symbiotic relationship between A. fischeri and the squid Euprymna scolopes (Pacific Ocean), in which A. fischeri lodges in an organelle inside the squid’s body providing shelter and nutrients to the bacteria, has long been well studied (Belcaid et al. 2019, Bongrand et al. 2020, Septer and Visick 2024). Under these conditions, A. fischeri is able to emit light, which has been postulated to serve as a deterrent to the cephalopod’s predators. This phenomenon is not exclusive to E. scolopes and there are other examples of symbiosis between luminescent bacteria (mainly Vibrionaceae) and different cephalopods such as the European squid or common squid (Loligo vulgaris), European flying squid (Todarodes sagittatus), and Carol bobtail squid (Neorossia caroli, Sepiolidae, Cephalopoda) (Fidopiastis et al. 1998, Urbanczyk et al. 2011, Calogero et al. 2022), extensively found in the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. In this sense the global distribution of the bioluminescent cephalopods across different ocean basins have been recently described (Otjacques et al. 2023).
In this laboratory activity we aim to maximize student interest in QS and symbiosis, while working on basic practical concepts of microbiology such as bacterial isolation and pure culture obtention, as well as molecular biology skills such as PCR identification and Sanger sequencing. Therefore, this activity aims to achieve different skills, (i) promote students' interest in microbiology, (ii) train students in the handling and interpretation of microbiological samples and QS, (iii) promote teamwork and (iv) encourage students to ask and formulate questions specifically about quorum sensing and culture media.

## Materials and methods

## Intended audience and prerequisite knowledge
This activity targets undergraduate students in Sciences (Biology, Biochemistry, etc.). It may be suitable for students in basic or advanced microbiology courses. Although not required, students may benefit from an overview of ecological relationships of microbes with animals and zoology. Before students get started, it is recommended to introduce the following topics: quorum sensing, lux operon and Vibrio genus (See Supplementary Appendix 1). There are also available videos that might help to understand the subject and encourage learners. (https://www.biointeractive.org/classroom-resources/natures-cutest-symbiosis-bobtail-squid, https://www.ted.com/talks/bonnie_bassler_how_bacteria_talk?utm_campaign=tedspread&utm_medium=referral&utm_source=tedcomshare, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KCobcWsYOS8&t=2s)

## Learning time
This laboratory exercise requires 5 days, with an average of 30 min per day, which can be extendable in order to complete the questions and the report (see below in the “suggestions for determining student learning” section). Furthermore, with another laboratory session and additional time, students could extend the study as indicated in the “Possible modifications and extensions” section. The content of practical work of each day is detailed below.

## Learning objectives (LO)
The curriculum covers a range of fundamental concepts and scientific practices, in particular: review of general microbiology techniques such as streak plate, gram staining and aseptic techniques and understanding of specific bacterial physiology concepts: the role of temperature in bacterial growth (cardinal temperatures), the effect of oxygen on bioluminescence, and the importance of bacterial density and genome regulation. Thus, through execution of this practice of isolating luminescent symbiont bacteria from marine cephalopods, the following learning objectives are to be addressed.

## LO1: Isolating and obtaining pure bacterial cultures
Isolate and obtain pure bacterial cultures from complex samples by applying aseptic techniques, the streak plate method, and Gram staining.

## LO2: Understanding quorum sensing and symbiotic interactions
Describe and explain the role and molecular mechanism of quorum sensing in micro-organisms and relate it to bacterial symbiosis with animal hosts.

## LO3: Application of microbial growth kinetics and metabolism knowledge
Explain how microbial growth kinetics and metabolism influence bacterial cultivation conditions and apply this knowledge to select appropriate growth media and environmental parameters (e.g. salinity and temperature).

## LO4: Bacterial identification through various techniques
Identify bacterial isolates using bacteriological techniques, Gram staining, 16S rRNA gene amplification by PCR, and sequence analysis.
LO4 and part of LO3 are dealt with in the “Possible modifications and extensions” section.

## Student instructions and sample data
Students will previously receive support materials and the instructions in form of a laboratory manual, as described below.
Day 1 (15 min): Incubation of fresh cephalopod in saline solution and at low temperature to enrich the growth of halophile/halotolerant and psychotropic bacteria (LO3)
• Place the fresh cephalopod in a tray and add the NaCl solution (1.5 l) so that approximately 10%–20% of the sample is above the level of the liquid (Fig. 1).
Preparation of the cephalopod with the NaCl solution.
• Cover the trays with aluminum foil.
• Incubate the cephalopod in the tray in a refrigerator at 4°C–8°C for 24–48 h in the dark.
Day 2 (30 min): Isolating of luminescent bacteria from the cephalopod surface (LO1 and LO3)
• Observe the sample in a completely darkened room to determine if there are any luminescent areas (wait a little while to get your eyes used to the dark) (Fig. 2).
Observation of luminescent symbiont bacteria in a marine cephalopod.
• Use a sterile swab to collect material from the areas where luminescence is seen.
• Seed the contents of the swab on one third of BBCA plates (under aseptic conditions) and isolate the bacteria by streak plate method with the aid of a sterile seeding loop.
• Incubate plates at 4°C–8°C for 24–48 h.
Day 3 (30 min): Obtaining pure culture of luminescent bacteria from the cephalopod surface by streak plate method (LO1 and LO3)
• Observe the plates in a completely darkened room to determine if luminescent colonies are present (accustom the eyes to the dark).
• Mark the location of the brightest luminescent colonies on the plate and isolate the bacteria by streak plate method in aseptic conditions in order to obtain a pure culture.
• Incubate plates at 4°C–8°C for 24–48 h.
Day 4 (30 min): Observation of pure culture of luminescent bacteria in plates and inoculation of BBCB broth to activate QS (LO2 and LO3)
• Confirm the presence of pure culture (macroscopically in the dark and with light and perform a gram staining) (Fig. 3).
Isolation of luminescent bacteria by streak plate method.
• Take abundant inoculum from the pure culture plate and seed it in 10 ml of BBCB. If pure culture is not obtained, pick only luminescent colonies (if there is time you can try again to obtain pure culture by streak plate method of the brightest colonies).
• Incubate at the same temperature (previously chosen from 4°C–8°C).
Day 5 (30 min) Observation of BBCB cultures an evidencing the effect of oxygen in bioluminescent production (LO2 and LO3)
• Observe liquid culture (Fig. 4).
Liquid culture of luminescent bacteria.
• Compare static and shaking the flask (Video 1).
Liquid culture of luminescent bacteria in shaking and static.

## Possible modifications and extensions
Depending on the time and resources available, this laboratory practice can be modified and extended to also address LO4 which consists on the identification and characterization of the isolated bacteria by different methodologies, and LO3 to deepen the characteristics of bacteria that allow for selective procedures.
▪ Modifications related to LO4 include: (1) staining and microscopy, (2) bacteriological techniques, and (3) 16sRNA amplification by PCR and sequencing.
Day 4 (30 min–1 h): Bacterial identification through various techniques (LO3 and LO4)
Once the presence of pure culture has been confirmed:
1- Perform a Gram staining (Gamazo et al. 2010) looking for Gram negative bacteria.
2- From isolated luminescent bacteria, inoculate a KIA and incubate at 4°C–8°C. Inoculate a KIA with E. coli ATCC 25922 as a control and incubate at 37°C (Gamazo et al. 2010). Additionally, a cytochrome C oxidase test can be performed. Vibrio genus is expected to be oxidase positive (Supplementary Appendix 1).
3- Extract DNA from isolated luminescent bacteria by resuspending few colonies in sterile water (200 µl) and boiling. Perform a PCR using 2 µl of the sample and primers 16s-27f 5'-AGAGTTTGATCATGGCTCAG-3' and 16s-1492r 5'- TACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3' (Weisburg et al. 1991) amplifying 16sRNA. Perform a Sanger sequencing of the PCR product and analyze the results using bioinformatic tools as BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) (Fig. 5).
Bacteria identification by 16sRNA analysis.
Day 5 (15 min): Interpretation of the results of day 4 (LO4)
Perform the interpretation of the KIA (See Supplementary Appendix 4)
▪ Modifications related to LO3 deal with bacterial growth temperature. Incubations of the cephalopod and plates can be done in parallel at higher temperature (15°C–18°C). With these modifications, students can visualize and remember how bacterial growth is faster at higher temperature (as long as it is below optimal temperature) and how at higher temperatures (15°C–18°C) more bacteria (non-luminescent) are seen and the selective procedure for luminescent bacteria symbionts of cephalopod (that live in cold water) is benefited by the inhibition of a large number of bacteria whose minimum temperature is above 4°C–8°C.
Moreover, optional extensions that allow deep knowledge on LO2 (understanding quorum sensing and regulation) may be undertaken. These activities include: monitor optical density (OD) over time and correlate population density with bioluminescence output (allowing students to directly observe the relationship between cell density and quorum activation); measurement of gene expression of the lux operon via RT-PCR and relating transcriptional activity to both optical density and bioluminescence. Additionally, students could investigate the production and accumulation of autoinducer molecules over the growth curve, by quantifying autoinducer levels (e.g. using biosensor strains or reporter assays). Together, these extensions provide a more integrated understanding of autoinducer production, detection, and quorum-dependent gene regulation, reinforcing the mechanistic basis of bioluminescence in a student-centered, inquiry-based framework.

## Faculty instructions
Prior to the practical session, instructors should engage in a meeting to review the necessary materials (see material listed in Procedure section) and plan their preparation.
In regards to the preparation of the materials, it is important to advise the instructors responsible for this task to prepare the culture media no earlier than one month before the practical to ensure its freshness and efficacy. Additionally, instructors should ensure that the cephalopod is not frozen and is purchased one or two days before the session. For this purpose, cephalopods (squid or cuttlefish) can be acquired at any fish market that guarantees the cephalopod’s freshness (not from fish farming).
It is recommended that all instructors read the introduction section and review the videos listed under pre-requisite knowledge for effective preparation. During the preparatory meeting, instructors should also revisit theoretical concepts (see Supplementary Appendix 1), equipping them to assist students in resolving any queries they may have during the practical session.

## Safety issues
In any laboratory, safety depends on the knowledge and ethical commitment not to harm other people (or oneself) or the environment. Therefore, the most important general safety rule is to think of others and be responsible at work. In this way, before starting the activity, students will be given the safety regulations for the microbiology laboratory, which they must follow at all times during the course. They will have to read and sign the safety instructions document, assuring and promising to understand the regulations.
Since it is a microbiology laboratory, aseptic work standards it is required. For this, the ASM Biosafety Guidelines should be followed https://asm.org/Guideline/ASM-Guidelines-for-Biosafety-in-Teaching-Laborator. This practice includes the use only of BSL1 security level microorganisms.

## Suggestions for determining student learning
Different assessment instruments can be used to assess the development of student skills. First, to collect evidence students can be observed directly. This include evaluating their attitude during the practice and confirming the achievement of LO1 (Table 1). We also propose a report to be completed by students, which comprises two distinct modules. The first module is composed of Likert scale inquiries with questions (related to the student perception of the impact of the practice) and four free text questions (related to the knowledge acquired about quorum sensing, selective growth and enrichment media, bacterial identification, etc). Each of the questions is aligned with the learning objectives proposed in the practice (Table 1). The marking rubric used to assess the four free text questions is included in Supplementary Appendix 2. In this way, in terms of intended learning outcomes, at the end of the practice, the students are expected to be able to analyze and interpret results (see Table 1), activities related to the higher categories of Bloom’s taxonomy (Bloom 1956).
Questions worked in the course.
Instructors may decide to evaluate, in addition to the questions, aspects related to laboratory skills, for example visualizing the quality of streaking, pure cultures, aseptic work, etc.
Finally, the second module involves the creation of a model to illustrate the QS phenomenon in the context of bioluminescence production in cephalopods, along with the isolation of bacteria responsible for light production. This activity requires a higher-order cognitive process, in which students need to be able to develop their own model, using electronic applications such as Biorender or PowerPoint, or opting for a traditional “pencil and paper” approach (an example and the checklist tool outlined are shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Appendix 3, respectively).

## Results and discussion
This activity was developed to increase student engagement with microbiology and facilitate their understanding of notions like bacterial QS (lux operon regulation) and symbiosis. While conducting this practical activity, several general basic microbiology techniques can also be reviewed (streak plate method, gram staining, or aseptic techniques). Moreover, more specific concepts of bacterial physiology are addressed during the execution of the practical such as: role of temperature in bacterial growth (revision of cardinal temperatures), effect of oxygen on bioluminescence process or the importance of bacterial density and genome regulation.
One of the most appealing aspects of this laboratory exercise that engages students is the multidisciplinary nature of the practice (genetics, microbiology, zoology…). Moreover, the fact that the bioluminescence observed in the laboratory is a phenomenon that occurs in nature, together with the showiness of the luminosity on the plates or in the broths, encourage the students to formulate questions other than those posed by the instructors themselves.
To assess this activity, the practice was implemented at the University of Navarra and the study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee from the same University (Institutional Review Board: IRB reference 2023.130). Out of the 42 enrolled students, 28 (66.6%) responded to the voluntary report through google forms application. The Likert scale questions related to the impact of the development of the practice on the understanding/knowledge of the concepts according to student perception was scored as follows: 1 for No Impact; 2 for Low Impact; 3 for Moderate Impact; 4 for High Impact; and 5 Very high Impact. As Fig. 7 shows, most of the students perceived that they acquired valuable tools and concepts, such as quorum sensing and enrichment and selective media (100% scored between 4 and 5). The pure culture and the symbiosis received similar scores, with 88.9% and 94.5%, respectively, indicating a high to very high impact. Finally, the concept that received the lowest scores was bacterial identification, with 22.2% of the students indicating that the impact of this activity on their understanding was moderate.
Impact of the development of the practice on the understanding/knowledge of the concepts according to student perception.
Concerning the results obtained from the evaluation of the four free text questions, the initial cohort (Fig. 8A) revealed that the most accessible questions were number 2 and 3, related to the effect of oxygenation on bacterial bioluminescence and enrichment media. Notably, 86.1% of the students were able to identify and explain the need and utility of pure culture, as well as the techniques used to obtain it (question 1), in agreement with the results shown in Fig. 7 about their own perception of learning. In contrast, question number 4 was the one in which the students found the most difficulties, since 60.7% of them were able to mention some of the characteristics of the KIA, but did not delve into the interpretation, again in agreement with the student perception about the impact of the activity on bacterial identification. These findings indicate that while the activity is effective in achieving LO1, 2 and 3, further refinement is needed to fully support LO4 (bacterial identification), which is part of “Possible modifications and extensions”, and were not so well integrated. This pattern suggests that students more readily assimilate concepts that are directly observable and experimentally reinforced (oxygen dependence of luminescence and selective growth), whereas more abstract or interpretative skills, such as metabolic inference from differential media, require higher-order cognitive processing and prior conceptual integration. Therefore, we recommend that if this extension is carried out, it should be considered to extend the explanation time of days 4 and 5 according to the previous knowledge of Microbiology. Furthermore, applying the activity in subsequent groups will allow continued evaluation of these improvements and contribute to a more comprehensive assessment of its pedagogical effectiveness.
Results of the evaluation of open-ended questions (A) in the initial cohort and (B) in the second independent cohort.
From a pedagogical perspective, this pattern suggests that students more readily assimilate concepts that are directly observable and experimentally reinforced (oxygen dependence of luminescence and selective growth), whereas more abstract or interpretative skills, such as metabolic inference from differential media, require higher-order cognitive processing and prior conceptual integration. This is consistent with constructivist learning theories, in which understanding is supported by linking theory to observable phenomena. Accordingly, the strong performance in question 1, 2, and 3 likely reflects the inquiry-based design of the activity, while the weaker performance in question 4 may indicate insufficient scaffolding for interpretative reasoning, particularly in connecting biochemical tests to bacterial identification frameworks. This indicates that additional guided discussion, explicit modeling of reasoning processes, or formative feedback could improve learning outcomes related to LO4.
To address these limitations, the activity and evaluation were repeated with a second independent cohort of 42 students. The results (Fig. 8B) show a consistent overall pattern, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the initial observations. Notably, questions 1, 2, and 3 again generated the highest proportion of fully correct responses, confirming that these concepts are reliably acquired through the activity. In contrast, question 4 remained the most challenging item, with most students not achieving the highest scores (35.7% and 23.8%, respectively) and responses largely reflecting partial understanding rather than complete interpretation. Additionally, the proportion of zero scores, which was associated with other answers or no answer, was found in less than 15% across both cohorts, supporting the overall accessibility of the activity.
These results indicate that the difficulties associated with bacterial identification persist across the second cohort, despite minor adjustments introduced after the first implementation and highlight the need to further reinforce the explanation and guided interpretation of KIA results in future iterations of the activity. The repetition of results across two cohorts’ validity of the pedagogical conclusions.
Finally, in relation to the second module, the models created by the students obtained generally good ratings, with an average score of 7 out of 10 (following the model correction checklist in Supplementary appendix 3). The highest score achieved was a 9, while the lowest was 3. Approximately 93% of the models contained the image or drawing of a squid, along with the details of isolation conditions and pure culture. Similarly, a substantial percentage of the responses (75%) accurately depicted the connection between the presence of oxygen and the luminescence production, and 71.4% represented the relationship between the population effect and quorum sensing correctly. However, understanding of the lux operon and the bioluminescence reaction involving the luciferase enzyme was less consistent, with rates of 50% and 60.7% of models properly incorporating these concepts, respectively.
Overall, we were encouraged to observe that the student's responses to the laboratory questions reflected their sincere engagement and a solid understanding of the inquiry. In our experience, the implementation of this practice is aimed not only at fostering success in boosting student interest in Microbiology, especially in quorum sensing, but also at providing a valuable tool to illustrate a wide range of concepts that may arise in a general Microbiology course.
