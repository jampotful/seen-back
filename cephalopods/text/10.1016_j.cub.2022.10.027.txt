Abstract
Neurogenesis, the regulation of cellular proliferation and differentiation in the developing nervous
system, is the process that underlies the diversity of size and cell type found in animal nervous
systems. Our understanding of how this process has evolved is limited because of the lack of
high resolution data and live-imaging methods across species. The retina is a classic model for
the study of neurogenesis in vertebrates and live-imaging of the retina has shown that during
development, progenitor cells are organized in a pseudostratified neuroepithelium and nuclei
migrate in coordination with the cell cycle along the apicobasal axis of the cell, a process called
interkinetic nuclear migration. Eventually cells delaminate and differentiate within the boundaries
of the epithelium. This process has been considered unique to vertebrates and thought to be
important in maintaining organization during the development of a complex nervous system.
Coleoid cephalopods, including squid, cuttlefish and octopus, have the largest nervous system of
any invertebrate and convergently-evolved camera-type eyes, making them a compelling
comparative system to vertebrates. Here we have pioneered live-imaging techniques to show that
the squid, Doryteuthis pealeii, displays cellular mechanisms during cephalopod retinal
neurogenesis that are hallmarks of vertebrate processes. We find that retinal progenitor cells in
the squid undergo interkinetic nuclear migration until they exit the cell cycle, we identify retinal
organization corresponding to progenitor, post-mitotic and differentiated cells, and we find that
Notch signaling regulates this process. With cephalopods and vertebrates having diverged 550
million years ago, these results suggest that mechanisms thought to be unique to vertebrates may
be common to highly proliferative neurogenic primordia contributing to a large nervous system.

Introduction

35

Coleoid cephalopods (e.g. squid, cuttlefish and octopus) are charismatic invertebrates

36

known for their expansive behavioral repertoire and large nervous system. One of the striking

37

features of the cephalopod nervous system is their camera-type eyes, which are one of the most

38

acute visual organs among animals (Gagnon et al., 2013; Packard, 1972). This complexity in

39

cephalopods is independently evolved from similar features found in vertebrate species. Although

40

vertebrates have been well studied, the developmental changes that underlie the evolution of

41

large, complex nervous systems are not well understood. Observations in traditional model

42

organisms show that diversity in nervous system size and functionality across animals is a product

43

of diversity in developmental processes (Hartenstein & Stollewerk, 2015). We know that in some

44

cases neurogenesis is highly regulated with invariant numbers of precursors with fixed lineages

45

(i.e. C. elegans), and in others cell lineages are stochastic or plastic (i.e. the vertebrate retina)

46

(Bertrand & Hobert, 2010; Zechner et al., 2020); (Hartenstein & Stollewerk, 2015). Live-imaging

47

has changed our perspective on neurodifferentiation from a primarily genetic process to a cell

48

biological process, defined by orchestrated cell behaviors and tissue architecture. However, in

49

vivo observations of neurogenesis have been limited to vertebrates and Drosophila. To elucidate

50

how changes in neurogenesis may contribute to the evolution of large and complex nervous

51

systems, we sought to understand neurodifferentiation in the cephalopod. The visual system has

52

proven to be a powerful context to learn fundamental aspects of neural development in

53

vertebrates and Drosophila and therefore we have chosen to focus our investigations on retinal

54

differentiation in the squid Doryteuthis pealeii.

55

The adult cephalopod retina is composed of two layers divided by the basal membrane

56

(Figure 1A). Photoreceptor cell bodies are found posterior (further from the lens) to the basal

57

membrane and support cells are found anterior (closer to the lens). The photoreceptor cells

58

extend projections to the anterior of the retina to form the outer segment layer and extend axons

59

out of the posterior retina to synapse directly on the optic lobe (Yamamoto et al., 1965; Young,

60

1974). During development, photoreceptor cell bodies are first identified behind the basal

61

membrane at Arnold embryonic stage 27, and these cells are no longer proliferative (Arnold, 1965;

62

Koenig et al., 2016). This is the earliest evidence of differentiation in the retina. The support cells,

63

in the anterior of the retina, also extend processes into the outer segment (Yamamoto et al.,

64

1965). Although the eye is functional at hatching, the support cells continue to incorporate BrdU

65

(Koenig et al., 2016). The animal continues to grow post-hatching and proliferating support cells

66

may include a long-term retinal stem cell population.

67

Previous observations from fixed tissue suggested that the cephalopod retinal primordium

68

is a pseudostratified epithelium, which is unusual for an invertebrate neurogenic tissue (Koenig

69

et al, 2016; (Koenig & Gross, 2020) (Figure 1A). This type of neuroepithelium is characteristic of

70

central nervous system development in vertebrates, including the retina (Taverna et al., 2014). A

71

pseudostratified epithelium is a monolayer composed of elongated cells with nuclei distributed

72

along the apicobasal axis. Cells in pseudostratified epithelia undergo nuclear movement

73

correlated with the cell cycle called interkinetic nuclear migration (Norden, 2017). This migration

74

is required for proper differentiation and organization in the developing vertebrate nervous system

75

(Strzyz et al., 2016). During nuclear migration, mitosis occurs on the apical surface and in

76

vertebrates this corresponds to the posterior side of the developing retina (Baye & Link, 2008)

77

(Figure 1A). In the cephalopod, fixed time point data suggests that mitosis occurs on the anterior

78

side of the developing retina, inverted relative to vertebrates (Koenig et al., 2016) (Figure 1A).

79

These observations suggest that neurogenesis may be more similar to vertebrate processes than

80

previously observed in other invertebrates and lead us to ask whether cephalopod retinal

81

progenitor cells undergo interkinetic nuclear migration and how cells differentiate within this

82

epithelium.

Results

86

Cell cycle in the developing squid retina

87

Previous work in the squid retina showed that at Arnold stage 23, a three hour BrdU pulse

88

resulted in incorporation throughout the retina (Arnold, 1965; Koenig et al., 2016). This long pulse

89

provided time for cells to move after incorporation, making it difficult to determine if only a subset

90

of cells may be proliferative. To more precisely understand cell cycle state and organization in the

91

early retina, we performed a 10 minute BrdU pulse experiment, fixing immediately, and co-labeling

92

with phosphohistone H3 immunofluorescence (PH3) (Figure 1B). We see that BrdU incorporation

93

in S-phase cells is restricted to the posterior of the retina after 10 minutes. We also find PH3

94

positive labeled nuclei, in late G2 and M phase, on the anterior surface of the retina, supporting

95

previous observations. The lack of BrdU incorporation in the anterior retina suggests that some

96

cells are already post-mitotic.

97

If cells are undergoing interkinetic nuclear migration, we would expect BrdU positive nuclei

98

to eventually migrate apically to divide. To test this hypothesis, we chased our 10 minute BrdU

99

pulse, sampling embryos every ten minutes for 110 minutes, again co-labeling with PH3 (Figure

100

1C). We found that at 80 minutes after the pulse we see BrdU positive/PH3 positive cells in the

anterior retina, suggesting that nuclei are migrating apically to divide (Figure 1D). If proliferative

102

cells in the posterior retina eventually exit the cell cycle and join the cells in the anterior retina, we

103

would expect to find BrdU positive/PH3 negative cells in the anterior retina. Indeed, at 110 minutes

104

we find BrdU positive, PH3 negative cells in the anterior retina (Figure 1E). Together, these data

105

suggest that cells in the cell cycle have nuclei residing in the posterior retina and these nuclei

106

migrate apically to divide. Eventually these cells exit the cell cycle, and their cell bodies migrate

107

to the anterior of the retina. These data are consistent with cells undergoing interkinetic nuclear

108

migration, with post-mitotic cells segregated apically.

110

In vivo observations of nuclear migration and proliferation in the squid retinal epithelium

111

While fixed tissue experiments support our hypothesis, to fully understand the dynamics

112

of these cell behaviors, it is necessary to observe development in vivo. This required us to develop

113

a cell-resolution live-imaging protocol in cephalopods (Figure 2A). We injected fluorescent

114

Dextran to broadly label cell membranes and performed >9 hour developmental time course

115

imaging experiments of the squid retina at stage 23 (Figure 2A). Within these time course

116

datasets, we were able to track individual cells migrating from the basal lamina to the apical side

117

of the retina, where they mitose, and migrate basally again (Figure 2B, 2C, Movie S1, S2, S3).

118

These data confirm the process of interkinetic nuclear migration in the squid retina. From these

119

data we are able to calculate nuclear velocity and were able to assess that this migratory behavior

120

is similar in velocity to movement described in the vertebrate retina (Figure 2D) (Norden et al.,

121

2009).

122

The molecular motors contributing to nuclear migration have been interrogated in a

123

number of developmental contexts and some tissues rely exclusively or primarily on actomyosin,

124

while others require microtubules contribution to promote migration (Kosodo, 2012; E. J. Meyer

125

et al., 2011; Norden, 2017). To understand the contribution of these motors to nuclear migration

126

in the squid retina, we treated stage 23 embryos with either the actin polymerization inhibitor

127

cytochalasin D or the microtubule polymerization inhibitor nocodazole for seven hours and fixed

128

immediately. The impact of these inhibitors on nuclear migration was assessed using

129

immunofluorescence for PH3 (Figure 2E-2H). We find that nuclei positive for PH3 in the

130

cytochalasin D treated retinas are often displaced from the mitotic zone (MZ), defined as the

131

region of the epithelium containing nuclei abutting the most apical membrane. We find a small

132

percentage of PH3 positive nuclei in controls are also outside the mitotic zone, which are likely at

133

the end of G2, migrating toward the apical surface. Quantifying these differences, we see a

134

significant increase in cells outside the mitotic zone in our CytoD treated embryos suggesting

135

actin polymerization is required for nuclear migration. In addition, we see defects in migration in

136

nocodazole treated embryos; a population of PH3 positive nuclei accumulated at the apical

137

surface of the retina. This is what we would expect if microtubules were not required for migration,

138

as cells that have successfully migrated apically would then arrest and accumulate in M phase as

139

microtubules are required for mitosis. However, in addition to this accumulation, we find a

140

subpopulation of PH3 positive cells away from the mitotic zone, suggesting that microtubules play

141

a role in nuclear migration in the cephalopod retina.

142

In addition to nuclear movement, we were also able to assess the angle of cell division in

143

the epithelium. Symmetrical and asymmetrical cell division is an essential aspect of regulating

144

self-renewal, cell cycle exit and cell fate commitment during neurogenesis in multiple organisms

145

(Yu et al., 2006; Zigman et al., 2005); (Dehay & Kennedy, 2007). Overwhelmingly, we find most

146

mitoses observed to be symmetrical relative to the apical surface of the epithelium (Figure 2I,

147

Movie S2). We hypothesize that these divisions are self-renewing. We also observe

148

approximately 10% of cell divisions to be angled relative to the apical surface (Figure 2I, 2J & 2K,

149

Movie S3). We determined that symmetrical cell divisions had a division plane with an angle

150

relative to the apical surface between 60-90 degrees and asymmetrical divisions between 30-60

151

degrees (Figure 2K). We did not observe any cell divisions perpendicular to the apical surface,

152

(0-30 degrees) as have been observed in other systems (Knoblich, 2008; Morin & Bellaïche,

153

2011). We hypothesize that these asymmetric cell divisions may contribute to cells exiting the cell

154

cycle, however we were unable to follow cells long enough during live imaging to confirm this

155

hypothesis.

Molecular identity during retinal neurogenesis

158

To better understand the molecular state correlated with the cell cycle organization

159

observed in our BrdU and live imaging experiments, we sought to identify molecular markers that

160

help define differentiation trajectories in the cephalopod retina. We analyzed spatiotemporal

161

expression of thirteen candidate neurogenesis markers in the retina (Figure 3A, Supplemental

162

Figure 1, Supplemental Figure 2). We identified that in early retinal development, stage 21,

163

DpSoxB1 is homogeneously expressed across the retinal epithelium. At stage 23 the retina is

164

divided across the apical-basal axis with DpSoxB1 found on the basal side of the retina,

165

correlating with BrdU incorporation, and DpEphR is expressed on the apical side. At stage 25 we

166

see the maintenance of segregated DpSoxB1 and DpEphR expression, as well as the first

167

evidence of the terminal differentiation markers DpRetinochrome and DpRhodopsin. At stage 27,

168

the first evidence of the basal membrane is apparent, which is correlated with a loss of strict

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.10.28.466353; this version posted November 18, 2021. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

169

segregation of DpSoxB1 and DpEphR expression. We hypothesize this change is a result of cell-

170

body migration or mixing, with apical cells moving to the posterior of the retina and basal cells

171

moving to the anterior of the retina. We hypothesize that this resorting moves the post-mitotic

172

cells to the posterior where they will become the first photoreceptor cell bodies to migrate behind

173

the basal membrane. This is supported by previous observations of nuclei crossing the basal

174

membrane (Koenig et al., 2016). Supporting this hypothesis, we find that DpSoxB1 expression

175

is never found posterior to the basal membrane. However, DpEphR is found on both sides of the

176

membrane until hatching. At hatching DpEphR is isolated to a subset of cells posterior to the basal

177

membrane. These cells may be maturing photoreceptor cells that have recently migrated to the

178

photoreceptor cell layer during hatching and post-hatching stage growth. Terminal differentiation

179

markers span both sides of the basal membrane at hatching. We hypothesize that DpRhodopsin

180

is expressed exclusively in the photoreceptor cells and is being trafficked to the outer segment

181

and that DpRetinochrome is expressed in both the photoreceptor cells and support cells.

182

This candidate gene survey suggests cell differentiation trajectories in the retina (Figure

183

3B, 3C). Our photoreceptor cell differentiation trajectory starts as DpSoxB1 positive retinal

184

progenitor cells undergoing interkinetic nuclear migration in the pseudostratified epithelium.

185

These cells transition to DpEphR positive cells and become post-mitotic. We hypothesize that this

186

is the result of an asymmetric cell division. Eventually DpEphR expressing cells mature and begin

187

to express terminal differentiation markers, ultimately completing differentiation when they

188

exclusively express DpRhodopsin and DpRetinochrome.

189

We hypothesize a simple differentiation trajectory for the proliferative support cell

190

population (Figure 3B & 3C). These cells start as DpSoxB1 positive retinal progenitor cells,

191

undergoing interkinetic nuclear migration. This self-renewing population is maintained throughout

192

development. During cell mixing at stage 27, DpSoxB1 positive support cells begin to express

193

DpRetinochrome, migrate anterior in the retina and remain in the cell cycle. The

194

DpSoxB1/DpRetinochrome expressing support cells may be a long term stem cell population in

195

the retina.

Notch regulates retinal progenitor identity in the cephalopod

198

Our data show that BrdU incorporation correlates with DpSoxB1 expression and DpEphR

199

expression correlates with post mitotic cells. Our BrdU chase experiments suggest that DpSoxB1

200

cells likely become DpEphR cells over time (Figure 4A, 4B & 4C). Notch signaling is a well-known

201

regulator of cell cycle exit and differentiation. Previous work showed that inhibiting Notch signaling

202

in the cephalopod using the gamma-secretase inhibitor DAPT led to a loss of BrdU incorporation

204

the role of Notch signaling in regulating cell fate trajectories in conjunction with proliferation. We

205

first performed in situ hybridization for Notch signaling pathway members in the retina (Figure 4D

206

and Supplemental Figure 3). If Notch signaling is important for maintaining progenitor fate, we

207

would expect Notch and Hes to be enriched in retinal progenitor cells. We find that DpNotch and

208

DpHes-1 expression is correlated with DpSoxB1 expression and retinal progenitor identity (Figure

209

4D and Supplemental Figure 3). Interestingly, we find that Delta is ubiquitously expressed during

210

these stages (Supplemental Figure 3). We also find DpNotch expressed in a limited number of

211

nuclei on the apical side of the retina. We wanted to know if this expression correlated with nuclei

212

that had migrated apically to divide. We performed in situ hybridization for DpNotch in conjunction

213

with PH3 immunofluorescence and found that apical DpNotch expression is correlated with PH3

214

positive nuclei (Figure 4E). Inheritance of Notch mRNA may be an important part of maintaining

215

progenitor cell identity.

216

To assess the role of Notch in cell fate we used the gamma secretase inhibitor DAPT to

217

inhibit active signaling. Embryos were treated with DAPT or DMSO starting at stage 23 for 24

218

hours. Eyes were dissected and pooled. RNA was extracted from three experimental and control

219

samples and sequenced. 2242 genes were differentially expressed between DAPT and control

220

eyes with a p-value of less than 0.05 (Supplemental Table 1). DpSoxB1 was found downregulated

221

and DpEphR was found upregulated in the dataset, but these changes were not deemed

222

statistically significant. DAPT compared to control eye samples showed both up and down

223

regulation in neural and sensory markers. Of note is the down regulation of Rhodopsin and other

224

G-protein coupled receptor related genes (Figure 4F). In addition, cell cycle related genes were

225

downregulated, which is expected as DAPT treatment leads to cell cycle exit in the squid retina

226

(Koenig et al., 2016) (Figure 4G). Finally, DAPT versus control eyes showed significant changes

227

in cell signaling genes, suggesting that Notch may self-regulate as well as interact with other

228

signaling pathways in the retina, including VEGFR, BMP and Wnt (Figure 4H).

229

To confirm changes in gene expression identified in the RNA-seq analysis, we performed

230

in situ hybridization studies on control and DAPT embryos (Figure 4I and Supplemental Figure 4

231

and Supplemental Figure 5). We confirm trends shown in the RNA-seq results. Specifically, we

232

find a loss of DpNotch expression, a downregulation of DpRhodopsin, and a complete loss of

233

DpSoxB1 in DAPT treated retinas (Figure 4I). We also observe a gain of expression of DpEphR

234

and synaptic transmission related gene, DpSec-1, in the posterior retina (Figure 4I). Interestingly,

235

we see ectopic expression of DpVEGFR in a subpopulation of cells in the DAPT treated retinas

236

relative to control. The unusual placement of ectopic expression of DpVEGFR may be

Discussion

243

In the current study we have characterized the nuclear oscillatory behaviors of retinal

244

progenitor cells in squid. We have defined the molecular markers for progenitor, post-mitotic, and

245

fully differentiated cells in the retina and we have shown that Notch signaling is regulating both

246

cell cycle and molecular identity during retinal development. The importance of these findings is

247

in comparison to what is known about neurogenesis in other systems.

248

Pseudostratified epithelia and interkinetic nuclear migration have been observed in

249

multiple tissue types and across multiple species (E. J. Meyer et al., 2011). In the nervous system,

250

pseudostratification was historically considered a vertebrate specific developmental trait, which

251

was responsible for the large size of the central nervous system. Elaborate tissue-level

252

morphogenesis is has not been observed in invertebrate neural development, where individual

253

cells delaminate or ingress from a simple or stratified neuroepithelium, sometimes divide, and

254

then migrate to their final destination to differentiate (N. P. Meyer & Seaver, 2009; Sur et al.,

255

2020); (Byrne, 2019; Hartenstein & Stollewerk, 2015). However, interkinetic nuclear migration has

256

been described in the Drosophila optic lobe (Egger et al., 2007). In this case, the apicobasal

257

distance of migration is significantly smaller than vertebrate neurogenic tissues and cells

258

delaminate from the edge of the proliferative epithelium and migrate to their final destination to

259

differentiate (Egger et al., 2007; Holguera & Desplan, 2018; Strzyz et al., 2016). This process has

260

been compared to “conveyor belt” neurogenesis found in the zebrafish ciliary and tectal marginal

261

zones, where, during long term growth, cells are progressively added from the edge of a

262

pseudostratified stem cell population (Joly et al., 2016). The behavior described in the squid retina

263

is unusual because of its unique similarity to early neurogenesis in vertebrate species, which is

264

characterized by waves of differentiation within the pseudostratified epithelium (Agathocleous &

265

Harris, 2009; Livesey & Cepko, 2001). In the squid, we show that apicobasal height of the

266

epithelium is similar to vertebrate tissues, cells undergo interkinetic nuclear migration while

267

proliferative, and cells remain within the bounds of the epithelium after delaminating and

268

differentiating. The functional difference between conveyor belt neurogenesis versus within-

269

epithelium differentiation is unclear, but the architecture of the epithelium may provide physical

270

landmarks or signaling information important for proper organization.

Although pseudostratified neurogenesis is found in the cephalopod retina, the cell biology

272

of chordal or central nervous system neurogenesis is still not well described. It has been reported

273

that cells migrate from the lateral lip tissue, an embryonic neurogenic tissue found in the

274

cephalopod embryo to regions within the brain but pseudostratification of the lateral lips is not

275

apparent (Deryckere et al., 2021). Cephalopod central nervous system development may more

276

closely resemble neurogenesis found in other invertebrates or molluscs (Deryckere et al., 2021;

277

Marthy, 1987). With a central nervous system composed of significantly more cells than other

278

Spiralians and requiring long distance cell migration, it is likely that cephalopods have evolved

279

lineage specific mechanisms to manage this process.

280

Interestingly, in the squid, candidate gene expression of neurogenic factors also differs

281

significantly between the developing retina and other brain regions. This includes a lack of

282

expression of the canonical neurogenic ELAV, DpELAV, in the retina, an RNA binding protein

283

commonly found in differentiating neurons across species (Akamatsu et al., 2005; Denes et al.,

284

2007; N. P. Meyer & Seaver, 2009; Nakanishi et al., 2012; Nomaksteinsky et al., 2009). bHLH

285

factors DpNeuroD and DpNeuroG, both with orthologs that have roles in neural specification and

286

differentiation in many animals, are expressed in the lateral lips and brain in D. pealeii and other

287

cephalopods but are completely absent for the retina (Supplemental Figure 2) (Buresi et al.,

288

2013; Deryckere et al., 2021; Shigeno et al., 2015). However, it has been reported in Octopus

289

vulgaris that achaete-scute homolog Ov-ascl1, a third proneural bHLH transcription factor, is

290

expressed in the retina (Deryckere et al., 2021). Furthermore, SoxB1, required to maintain neural

291

precursors cells in vertebrates (Sox1, 2 and 3) and neuroblasts in Drosophila (SoxNeuro and

292

Dichaete), is also a retinal progenitor marker in cephalopods (Buescher et al., 2002; Bylund et al.,

293

2003; Ferrero et al., 2014; Holmberg et al., 2008; Overton et al., 2002; Taranova et al., 2006). We

294

also find that both cephalopod-specific DpELAVL paralogs show expression that suggests a role

295

in neurogenesis in both the retina and brain (Supplemental Figure 2).

296

Finally, we show that Notch signaling is maintaining molecular identity of the progenitor

297

population in the squid retina, and that the loss of Notch signaling leads to expression of the post-

298

mitotic marker DpEphR. In the vertebrate retina, the premature loss of Notch signaling leads to

299

the differentiation of early born cell types (Perron & Harris, 2000). In D. pealeii, we see a decrease

300

in DpRhodopsin expression, suggesting cells may have arrested in this immature post-mitotic

301

state. We also see the maintenance in DpRetinochrome expression, which may be evidence of

302

differing trajectories of these cell types during neurogenesis.

303

The function of pseudostratification and interkinetic nuclear migration during development

304

remains unclear, however our data sheds new light on old hypotheses. In particular,

pseudostratification may aid in intracellular organization, as we see significant mRNA localization

306

in our data, including mRNA transport correlated with nuclear migration. It may also be that the

307

physics and dynamics of pseudostratified epithelia are better at accommodating increased

308

proliferation or provides an organizational scaffold for the differentiating network of neurons. It will

309

be interesting to apply new fluid dynamics approaches used to study vertebrate neurogenesis to

310

the cell biology of this similar tissue (Azizi et al., 2020). Ultimately, the evidence we have

311

generated in cephalopods suggests that vertebrate-like cell behaviors during neurogenesis are

312

not exclusive to the chordate lineage. With greater sampling we may better understand the

313

evolutionary changes that contribute to the diversity and complexity we see.

Methods
Animal Husbandry
Doryteuthis pealeii egg sacks were obtained from the Marine Biological Labs. Egg sacks were
kept at 20 degrees Celsius in 20 gallon aquaria in artificial seawater under a day/night cycle.
Although not required, European guidelines for cephalopod research were followed.
Cloning and in situ Hybridization
Primers were designed using Primer3 in the Geneious software package version 2020.04
(https://www.geneious.com) and primer sequences are reported in Supplemental Table 2. Genes
were cloned into pGEM-T Easy vector and confirmed with Sanger sequencing and DIG-labeled
RNA probes were synthesized as previously reported (Koenig et al., 2016). Embryos were fixed
overnight at 4 degrees Celsius, washed and dehydrated stepwise into 100% ethanol. Embryos
were embedded, paraffin sectioned and in situ hybridization was performed as previously reported
(Neal et al., n.d.). All in situs were replicated in at least three embryos, across multiple separate
in situ experiments. Slides were stained overnight with Sytox-Green 1:1000 overnight, mounted
using ImmunoHistoMount (Abcam) and imaged on a Zeiss Axioskop 2.
BrdU Experiments
Embryos were bathed in BrdU (10 mM) in pen-strep seawater for 10 minutes as previously
described (Koenig et al., 2016). Embryos were fixed immediately, and the remaining embryos
were moved into pen-strep seawater. Groups of 10-15 embryos were fixed every ten minutes for
two hours. Embryos were fixed overnight at 4 degrees and washed out into PBS-Tween. Embryos
were stepped into 30% sucrose and embedded in tissue freezing medium and sectioned as
previously described (Koenig et al., 2016). Antigen-retrieval and immunofluorescence was
performed as previously described. BrdU antibody (Abcam ab6326) at concentration 1:250.
Phosphohistone H3 antibody (Sigma-Aldrich 06-570) was used at concentration 1:300.
Secondary antibodies goat anti-rat Alexa Fluor 488 and goat anti rabbit Alexa Fluor 647
(Invitrogen). Sections were counterstained with SYTOX-Green at 1:1000. Sections were imaged
on a Zeiss LSM 880 or Zeiss LSM 980.

Live-imaging experiments
Embryos were dissected from egg cases. The vitreous space was injected using a pico-liter
microinjector with Dextran Alexa Fluor 488 10,000 MW (D22910) at stage 23. Embryos were
embedded in 1% low melt agarose in seawater and mounted in cover glass bottom dishes
(100503-366). Embryos were immersed in pen strep seawater. Embryos were imaged on a Zeiss
880. Embryos were imaged every ten minutes for at least nine hours.
Imaging analysis
Image analysis was performed in Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2012). Intensity range was adjusted in Fiji
to better identify cell membranes. Drift correction was performed using Fiji plugin CoordinateShift
(written by Housei Wada, https://signaling.riken.jp/en/en-tools/imagej/). Nuclear tracking was
performed both manually and using Fiji plugin Trackmate (Robertson, 2018; Schindelin et al.,
2012). Tracks were visualized and distance and velocity measurements were obtained from
Trackmate and plotted graphically, normalizing to the highest point in migration.
Ex ovo and Drug Treatments
Embryos were treated in 20uM DAPT solution or DMSO control in filter-sterilized, Pen-Strep
seawater starting at stage 23 for 24 hours and fixed immediately as previously described (Koenig
et. al, 2016). Embryos were bathed in 5uM Nocodazole (M1404-2MG, Sigma-Aldrich) and 5uM
Cytochalasin D (C8273-1MG, Sigma-Aldrich) for 7 hours and fixed immediately.
RNA-seq and Bioinformatics
Stage 23 embryos were treated with the gamma-secretase inhibitor DAPT at 20um in filter
sterilized sea water for 24 hours and Control embryos were treated with the equivalent amount of
DMSO, as previously described (Koenig et al., 2016). DAPT and Control eyes were dissected,
pooled and macerated in TRIzol and stored at -80 degrees Celsius. RNA was extracted using a
standard TRIzol (Invitrogen #15596026) chloroform extraction and passed through a gDNA
eliminator mini-spin column (Qiagen #1030958). RNA was precipitated with isopropanol and then
again precipitated with ethanol and checked for quality. Library prep and sequencing was
performed at the Bauer Core at Harvard University. RNA-seq libraries were generated using the
Kapa mRNA-Hyper Prep kit with Poly-A Selection (Roche, Basal) and were sequenced on the
Illumina NovaSeq (>70 million 2x150 bp sequences) (Illumina, San Diego, CA).
Sequence quality control was performed according to the best practice recommendation
on the Harvard FAS Informatics pipeline (https://informatics.fas.harvard.edu/best-practices -forde-novo-transcriptome-assembly-with-trinity.html). Erroneous kmers were removed from the
paired end Illumina dataset using rCorrector. Reads with Ns or other low complexity pairs were
removed using a custom python script provided by the Harvard Informatics GitHub
(FilterUncorrectabledPEfastq.py). Adapters and low quality bases were removed using
TrimGalore! Reads that mapped using Bowtie2 to the rRNA databases SILVA 132 SSURef Nr99
tax and SILVA 132 LSUParc tax were removed. Pseudomapping was performed by Kallisto with
100 bootstraps to a previously published whole embryo transcriptome (Koenig et al., 2016).
Transcript abundances were imported using tximport (https://bioconductor.org/packages/3.7/ b
ioc/vignettes/tximport/inst/doc/tximport.html#use-with-downstream-bioconductor-dge-packages)
into DESeq2 using the Kallisto abundance.h5 files. Differential gene expression was determined

Figure 3: Molecular marker expression suggests a retinal differentiation trajectory.
A. Staged mRNA expression in the developing cephalopod retina: DpSoxB1, DpEphR, DpRhodopsin,
DpRetinochrome. Nuclear stain (sytox) shown in green. Schematic of stages labeled and shown.
Arrowheads and black and white dotted lines identify the basal membrane. Red dotted lines identify the
posterior retinal boundary. Scale is 50um. RP, Retinal Placode; L Lens; OS, Outer Segment; BM, Basal
Membrane; PR, Photoreceptor Cells; V, Vitreous Space; A, Apical; B, Basal. Stage 21, 23, & 25 embryo
anterior is down. Stage 27, 28, & 29 embryo dorsal is up. B. Hypothesis of cell differentiation trajectories.
Color key is used in C. C. High magnification summary of time course gene expression data. At stage 21
the retina is homogenous. At stage 23 the retina is divided into apical and basal regions. At stage 25,
evidence of terminal differentiation markers, DpRhodopsin and DpRetinochrome, are evident. At stage 27,
the first nuclei are found behind the basal membrane and strict segregation of apical and basal expression
is lost. Stage 28, the number of photoreceptor cells behind the basal membrane has increased and gene
expression shows increased regional segregation. Stage 29, differentiation markers are robustly
expressed.
