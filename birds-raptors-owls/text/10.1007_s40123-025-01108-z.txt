The Impact of Disease Activity Criteria on Extending Injection Intervals in Real-World Patients with Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration.

# The Impact of Disease Activity Criteria on Extending Injection Intervals in Real-World Patients with Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration

## Abstract
AbstractIntroductionIn clinical practice, intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) injection intervals for patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) are based on disease activity, with active or recurrent disease requiring more frequent injections. Injection interval modification criteria differ from those used in clinical trials, thereby potentially affecting treatment outcomes. This analysis evaluated the potential impact of applying disease activity criteria from recent clinical trials (TENAYA/LUCERNE; HAWK/HARRIER; PULSAR) on the decision to extend injection intervals in real-world patients commenced on faricimab after the loading phase of treatment and at 12 months.MethodsData were analysed from 105 treatment-naïve patients with nAMD who received anti-VEGF injections at Moorfields Eye Hospital. Disease activity criteria from TENAYA/LUCERNE, HAWK/HARRIER and PULSAR clinical trials were applied to determine the hypothetical impact on the decision to modify injection intervals at week 12 (fourth injection) and 12-month real-world clinic visits.ResultsAt 12 weeks, 79% of patients had injection intervals extended in clinical practice compared to 80% when applying hypothetical TENAYA/LUCERNE disease activity criteria; 77% using HAWK/HARRIER and 96% using PULSAR. There was agreement between clinical practice and all clinical trials in 60% of eyes, and no agreement in 13%. At 12 months, fewer patients were inactive, with 55% of eyes quiescent in clinical practice, 58% when applying TENAYA/LUCERNE criteria and 67% using HAWK/HARRIER. Application of PULSAR disease activity criteria showed 96% of patients were classed as inactive. 34% of eyes showed agreement in disease activity status between clinical practice and all clinical trials at 12 months, with no agreement in 20%.ConclusionsApplying disease activity criteria from clinical trials to clinical practice can have a significant impact on hypothetical anti-VEGF injection intervals. Consideration should be paid to which criteria are used in real-world practice to help achieve treatment burden reductions and optimal treatment outcomes seen in clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-025-01108-z.Keywords: Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF), Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), Optical coherence tomography (OCT), Real-world

## Key Summary Points

## Introduction
Clinical trials test new treatments under controlled conditions to determine their safety and efficacy. These trials often involve strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, leading to a homogeneous patient population and the generation of high-quality data regarding the drug’s efficacy and safety. Participants are monitored closely, which helps to identify potential adverse effects and allows for rigorous statistical analysis of outcomes. In contrast, real-world practice involves a heterogenous population with more diverse ages, ethnicities, comorbidities and socioeconomic status. There may be limitation of funding of treatment, as well as differences in treatment adherence and follow-up. In addition, physicians may use different treatment strategies based on individual patient needs rather than strictly adhering to treatment protocols. This may lead to different outcomes compared to clinical trial data.
Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is one of the leading causes of visual impairment and blindness in Europe [1]. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents are the standard of care for patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Landmark pivotal clinical trials of anti-VEGF agents for nAMD use a range of different dosing strategies in the first year of treatment after a loading phase of 4 weekly injections, including fixed dosing [2]; different extended treatment intervals after consideration of disease activity [3, 4]; or through randomisation at the start of the study [5]. This is in contrast to real-world practice, where patients often receive a series of loading dose injections followed by a treat and extend approach, where the interval between injections is potentially changed at each treatment visit on the basis of disease activity assessment using optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging and clinical evaluation without additional monitoring visits between treatment visits. A Cochrane review in 2020 found treat and extend regimes obtained similar results as monthly injections [6]. In the United Kingdom (UK), a roundtable of retinal experts recommended that an aflibercept treat and extend pathway could be implemented in clinical practice [7]. This proactive approach, compared to an ‘as needed’ protocol, may reduce treatment burden and improve clinic capacity planning.
In clinical trials, disease activity is typically assessed through visual acuity (VA) and/or OCT-derived central subfield thickness (CST) [5, 8, 9]. However, in real-world clinical practice, OCT is primarily utilised to detect qualitative features of disease activity (e.g. intra- or subretinal fluid) throughout the entire macular OCT imaging field. This key difference in disease activity criteria between clinical trials and clinical practice has the potential to result in different durability outcomes between clinical trials and real-world clinical data.
Recently, data from the PRECISE study showed that if extension is based only on the change in CST rather than the presence of macular fluid throughout the macular OCT field, more patients could be extended depending on the cut-off used to define an increase in CST [10].
The definitions of disease activity criteria also vary between clinical trials with differences in specified changes in VA or CST. In the TENAYA/LUCERNE trials, disease activity criteria involved changes in VA or CST [9]. In HAWK/HARRIER, disease status was based on a combination of change in VA or a change in both VA and CST [8]. In the PULSAR study where 8 mg aflibercept arms were randomised at baseline to 12 or 16 weekly dosing, the dose regimen shortening criteria required both a reduction in VA and CST to be classified as active disease [5]. Applying these different criteria may lead to varying outcomes within clinical trials themselves.
This audit evaluated the hypothetical impact of applying disease activity criteria from recent pivotal nAMD clinical trials, namely TENAYA/LUCERNE (faricimab vs aflibercept 2 mg), HAWK/HARRIER (brolucizumab vs aflibercept 2 mg) and PULSAR (aflibercept 8 mg vs aflibercept 2 mg), on treatment intervals for nAMD compared to the actual decisions taken in real-world clinics to shorten, maintain or increase injection intervals in patients with nAMD.

## Methods
This was a retrospective analysis of 105 consecutive treatment-naïve nAMD eyes commenced on treatment with faricimab at Moorfields Eye Hospital, London, UK between 5 September 2022 and 29 December 2022. The study was approved by the Moorfields Eye Hospital Audit Department (Audit number 1319) and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.
Patients were commenced on the Moorfields Eye Hospital treat and extend protocol for anti-VEGF treatment of nAMD. The protocol is similar to treat and extend guidelines developed by a UK expert panel for nAMD treatment [7] and consists of four loading faricimab injections 4 weeks apart, followed by the option of reducing, maintaining or extending treatment intervals depending on the presence of nAMD disease activity, with a minimum treatment interval of 28 days. The first timepoint of assessment of disease activity status and modification of treatment intervals is at the time of the fourth injection (usually 12 weeks from the start of treatment or after assessing the response to three injections). We then compared the injection interval decision made in clinic against the hypothetical effect of applying disease activity criteria from TENAYA/LUCERNE, HAWK/HARRIER and PULSAR trials on the injection interval at the fourth (week 12) injection visit-the earliest time of disease activity assessment in our clinical practice, and at 12 months.

## Data Collection
Patients’ electronic medical records (OpenEyes™) were reviewed and data including demographics, VA, CST, presence of macular fluid and presence of macular haemorrhage were recorded at baseline and at each visit up to the nearest visit to 12 months following the start of treatment. The decision regarding treatment interval was also recorded at the time of the fourth injection and at the appointment closest to 12 months following the start of treatment. VA was assessed in clinic using an Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) Chart with the patient’s habitual correction where available. OCT scans were obtained using either DRI OCT Triton (Topcon Healthcare, Japan), Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg, Germany) or HD OCT Cirrus 5000 (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). Individual patients had scans obtained by the same OCT machine across the clinic visits. The CST was calculated automatically by the machine.

In clinical practice, patients were considered to have inactive nAMD disease if their treatment interval was extended (i.e. there were no signs of nAMD activity based on Moorfields treat-and-extend criteria: Table 1) and active disease if the treatment interval was maintained or reduced. The disease activity criteria (Table 1) from all three clinic trials were then applied to see whether there was agreement between all of the scenarios. In the HAWK/HARRIER trials the first disease activity decision timepoint was at 16 weeks with some disease activity criteria involving comparison to findings at week 12. In this study we compared week 12 VA and CST measurements to that measured at the previous visit (8 weeks) as there was no routine 16-week visit for most patients if their treatment interval was extended.
Decisions made in clinical practice were also compared to the disease activity criteria used by the clinical trials at 12 months (Table 2). In the case of TENAYA/LUCERNE this involved changes in VA or CST compared to the two previous recorded visits or compared to baseline. The HAWK/HARRIER trials used change in VA only (compared to week 12); and PULSAR used changes in VA and CST compared to week 12.
In the TENAYA/LUCERNE clinical trials, patients were started on a personalised treatment interval at 12 months based on disease activity criteria involving an increase in CST (compared to the last two visits or lowest CST recorded) or decrease in VA (compared to either the last two visits or highest recorded visual acuity): to reduce the treatment interval if there was active disease, extend if no disease activity and maintain if neither applies [9]. We compared the results of applying these criteria to decisions made regarding treatment intervals in clinic at 12 months.

## Statistical Methods
Charting of data was performed using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Version 2409). Statistical analysis to determine the significance of differences between clinical practice and clinical trial disease activity criteria was evaluated with Fisher’s exact test. Calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.1). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

## Results
A total of 105 consecutive treatment-naïve eyes commenced on faricimab were included in this analysis. At the fourth injection visit (week 12), 2 eyes were excluded because of lack of OCT scans and treatment decisions of 103 eyes were analysed. At 12 months, 91 eyes were analysed. Ten eyes (10%) were excluded because of loss of follow-up, four had cessation of treatment (including two due to uveitis and two due to patient choice).
Baseline characteristics included a mean age of 78.3 years (range 66–91), with 35 male patients (33%) and 70 female patients (66%) included in this study. Mean VA prior to the start of treatment was 56 ETDRS letters (± 16 letters standard deviation).

## Discussion
This study applied definitions of disease activity criteria used in three recent pivotal clinical trials, to eyes with nAMD receiving anti-VEGF injections in real-world clinical practice to understand the hypothetical impact on injection intervals. In clinical trials, nAMD disease activity is typically assessed through VA and/or CST on OCT. However, in real-world clinical practice, qualitative interpretation of OCT of the entire macular OCT scan for features such as intraretinal or subretinal fluid is used to evaluate disease activity (see Fig. S1 in the electronic supplementary material for examples). Additionally, nAMD disease activity in routine clinical care does not usually use visual acuity change as part of the criteria used to alter injection intervals. This is because vision in real-world clinics is measured without the benefit of optometrist refraction and therefore is not truly ‘best corrected’ leading to more variable visual acuity measurements. Even when standardised refraction is carried out, there is considerable intersession variability in visual acuity in patients with AMD [11]. This audit shows that there are differences in classification in disease activity status both between real-world clinical practice and clinical trials, and between clinical trials themselves.
At the time of the fourth injection (12 weeks, 4 weeks after the third injection) the majority of eyes were classed as having inactive disease, with similar percentages between clinical practice and TENAYA/LUCERNE and HAWK/HARRIER disease activity criteria. However, nearly all of the eyes were classified as having inactive disease by the PULSAR trial disease activity assessment, highlighting the significance of heterogenicity of clinical trial designs. There was consensus between clinical practice and all three clinical trials in 60% of cases.
At 12 months fewer eyes had quiescent disease, both in clinical practice and using the disease activity criteria as per the TENAYA/LUCERNE and HAWK/HARRIER trials. Interestingly, 96% of eyes were classed as not requiring shortening of treatment intervals by the PULSAR trial criteria at both the fourth injection visit (12 weeks) and at 12 months. Of note, the PULSAR criteria used a combination of VA decrease and CST increase compared to the other clinical trials, which classify disease activity based on VA decrease and/or CST increase. A previous analysis has shown that more patients are classified as having quiescent disease when using disease activity criteria based on visual acuity and CST together compared to each individual criterion [12].
At both 12-week and 12-month analyses, there were eyes with agreement between clinical practice and all three clinical trial disease activity scores. There was greater consensus at 12 weeks (60%) compared to that seen at 12 months (34%).
In this study, there were a significant number of patients who maintained their treatment interval at 12 months in clinical practice (i.e. the interval remained the same as the clinic visit preceding the 12-month visit). This was often due to factors such as persistent macular fluid (or new macular fluid not meeting the increase in CST needed to be categorised as active using disease activity criteria); previous reactivation of nAMD when the treatment interval was previously extended; delayed follow-up; or missed appointments. This was in contrast to when the personalised treatment criteria were applied from TENAYA/LUCERNE where nearly all cases would have had their treatment intervals extended or shortened. In cases where application of the TENAYA/LUCERNE criteria resulted in a reduction of treatment interval, this was mainly due to reduction in VA alone rather than on CST increase, or VA and CST findings combined.
Another factor contributing to differences in real-world practice and clinical trial disease activity is that in clinical practice clinicians often consider the presence of retinal fluid within the whole macular OCT when assessing the presence of active disease. Furthermore, in bustling real-world clinical settings, OCT scans may not be properly centred, and clinicians might lack the time to ensure that the scans are accurately centred without any segmentation errors. There have been some studies which have shown greater improvement in VA in patients with resolution of macular fluid and these patients may be able to have their treatment intervals extended rapidly [13, 14]. Additionally, fluctuations in CST have also been shown to be prognostic indicators of poor visual outcome [15–18]. The disease activity criteria for all the clinical trials used in this study only consider changes in the CST rather than the presence of subretinal or intraretinal fluid (with the exception of new/worsening intraretinal fluid in the case of HAWK/HARRIER at week 16) and this may affect outcomes in the long term.
Limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, small size and variation in decisions made by clinicians in practice, despite departmental protocols. In addition, there may be inaccuracies in CST measurements caused by segmentation errors and lack of centration as well as a lack of true ‘best corrected’ VA in real-world settings.

## Conclusion
This study aimed to evaluate how the disease activity criteria utilised in recent pivotal clinical trials for nAMD might influence the injection intervals of anti-VEGF treatments in a real-world clinical setting. The results highlight that long-term real-world studies are needed to evaluate the impact of different disease activity and extension criteria on visual outcome as well as the impact on treatment burden and the need for individualising treatment extension.
