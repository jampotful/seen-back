# Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapy in polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy

## Abstract
The diagnosis and management of polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV), a complex chorioretinal disease which constitutes 60% of neovascular age-related macular degeneration cases among Asians, have evolved significantly in the last decade. SD-OCT biomarkers can reliably diagnose and monitor PCV, restricting the role of invasive indocyanine green angiography to situations like suboptimal responders to therapy where it helps to clarify the diagnosis and explore other treatment options like laser. The introduction of Aflibercept resulted in a paradigm shift in the treatment from combination therapy (photodynamic therapy plus injection of anti-VEGF drugs) to anti-VEGF monotherapy after it was proven noninferior to the former in the PLANET trial. The limited availability of PDT also favored this shift. The longer half-life of Aflibercept led to adoption of proactive treatment regimens like treat and extend guided by fluid markers on OCT, reducing the treatment burden. Newer molecules with more potency and durability like Brolucizumab and Faricimab demonstrated superior drying effects and achieved a longer treatment interval of up to 16 weeks, offering additional options for anti-VEGF monotherapy. HAWK and HARRIER trials proved the noninferiority of Brolucizumab to Aflibercept, though there were safety concerns of inflammation. Interim analysis of the on-going SALWEEN trial for Faricimab in PCV has also shown favorable outcomes. Characterizing the different phenotypes of PCV based on OCT and choosing the appropriate drug and treatment regimen are important to maximize visual outcome while reducing the treatment burden. This article reviews the evolution of anti-VEGF therapy in PCV and provides recommendations for optimal management in the real world.

## Methods of Literature Review
A comprehensive review of the literature was conducted to evaluate the evolution, efficacy, and role of anti-VEGF therapy in PCV. A systematic search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar databases for articles published in English from January 2004 to February 2026. The search strategy included a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords such as “polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy,” “PCV,” “anti-VEGF,” “Aflibercept,” “Ranibizumab,” “Brolucizumab,” “Faricimab,” “photodynamic therapy,” “treat and extend,” and “optical coherence tomography.” RCT, prospective and retrospective clinical studies, real-world evidence, consensus guidelines, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses relevant to the diagnosis and management of PCV were included. Particular emphasis was placed on PCV-specific trials, landmark studies (EVEREST, PLANET, ALTAIR, HAWK/HARRIER), Asia-Pacific consensus statements, and studies evaluating newer anti-VEGF agents and treatment regimens. Case reports and small case series were included selectively when they provided clinically relevant insights, particularly for newer therapies with limited long-term data. Articles focusing exclusively on typical nAMD without specific relevance to PCV were excluded. Specific emphasis was given to treatment efficacy, anatomical and visual outcomes, durability, safety profiles, imaging biomarkers, and real-world applicability. The review also incorporates our clinical experience in managing PCV in routine practice in the context of current evidence-based recommendations.

## Introduction
Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (Anti-VEGF) therapy introduced in early 2000s has revolutionized the treatment of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) with a significant decline in the AMD-related blindness.[1] However, the role of anti-VEGF monotherapy in polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV), the subtype which constitutes almost 60% of AMD among Asians, is not fully established. In the multicenter randomized clinical trial (RCT) EVEREST II, combination therapy which combined anti-VEGF (Ranibizumab) with Verteporfin photodynamic therapy (PDT) was found to be superior to Ranibizumab monotherapy in achieving higher visual acuity gains (8.3 Vs 5.1 letters) and higher rates of polyp closure (69.3% Vs. 34.7%) with fewer injections.[2] Combination therapy also gave periods of quiescence from disease with a treatment free interval of at least 24 months in nearly 50% of patients.[3]
Recently, with the availability of more potent and durable agents, anti-VEGF monotherapy could also achieve polyp closure in 74%, avoiding the need for more expensive Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) and PDT.[4] The significance of polyp closure itself is not well proven as it has been found that good visual outcomes are compatible with unclosed polypoidal lesions (PLs).[5] The sparse availability of PDT across the world is an additional factor favoring a shift to predominantly anti-VEGF monotherapy in the management of PCV. This article aims to review the evolution of anti-VEGF therapy in PCV, elucidating its role in different phenotypes based on clinical and imaging parameters, and to provide recommendations based on real world experience.

## Pathogenesis of PCV
Pathogenesis of PCV is multifactorial, complex, and not yet completely understood. It has been described both as a variant of nAMD and as a choroidal vascular abnormality. Both in nAMD and PCV, some pathogenic mechanisms may be shared, while others could be different, leading to the development of each phenotype. VEGF, inflammation, immune responses, and genetic susceptibility are involved in the pathogenesis of both diseases.[678] Plasma cytokine profiles of patients with nAMD and PCV showed elevated levels of multiple cytokines including monocytic chemotactic protein (MCP)-1, VEGF-A, and VEGF-D, suggesting the role of systemic inflammation and angiogenesis.[9] Genetic studies have shown eight AMD associated gene/loci were associated with PCV pointing to a high correlation between the two conditions.[7]
However, unlike nAMD, drusen atrophy is less common and thick choroid and history of central serous chorioretinopathy is more common in PCV eyes. Not only an increased prevalence but also a more severe morphology of PCV among Asians is another distinguishing feature.[8] Histopathological studies regarding the location of PCV as well as evidence for expression of VEGF in the surgical specimens are conflicting. There are studies reporting strong expression of VEGF in vascular endothelial cells and RPE (retinal pigment epithelium), while lack of VEGF expression is also observed.[10] VEGF levels in aqueous humor in PCV eyes were higher compared to controls but much less compared to nAMD eyes. Despite the variability in VEGF expression, anti-VEGFs do seem to be effective, leading to regression or closure of the PCV lesions to a certain extent.[8]
Histological studies have shown that PCV is fundamentally a disorder centered on Bruchs membrane (BM) pathology. The new vessels arise from the choroidal artery, and its branching arterioles traverse the defects in the BM to reach the sub-RPE space where PL and branching neovascular network (BNN) develop with a connecting vessel in between.[811] Though both PL and BNN correlate with disease activity, PL is more associated with persistent disease activity characterized by exudative recurrences or new hemorrhage while the source of PL is BNN, especially its terminal region.[8] Hence, regression of both PL and BNN are important in PCV management, but maturity of the two lesions differs. OCTA (optical coherence tomography angiography)-based assessment of the morphology of PCV lesions exhibited coil-like loops, focal nodules, and nonhomogeneous flow in PLs, whereas BNN was more mature with fewer branches and fewer small-caliber vessels.[12] This corroborates with immunohistochemical studies which have demonstrated that PLs are composed of thin-walled cavernous vessels with reduced pericytic coverage causing nodular dilatations and the discontinuous endothelial cells make them highly leaky.[8] On the other hand, BNN which is a variant of Type 1 MNV has more complete coverage by mural cells, but it is still immature, fragile, and leaky.[13] Elevated expression of Ang-2 (Angiopoietin-2) is attributed to be the cause for mural cell dropout through the integrin-mediated signaling pathway.
These lesions can become active under certain stimuli like choroidal venule occlusion-induced stasis and ischemia which promote the growth of new-vessels through the defects in BM. Animal studies have shown that degradation of extracellular matrix (elastin and collagen) in the BM and choroidal vasculature is common in PCV which is mediated by HTRA1 (high temperature requirement factor A1) and matrix metalloproteinases -2 and -9.[13] This leads to defects in the BM which facilitate the invasion of neo-vessels into the sub-RPE space, weakening of vessel walls with dilatation, leakage, and rupture and collapse of choroidal arteries. Hence, newer treatment strategies should target BM integrity, vascular leakage, PL regression, and BNN maturation. More large-scale research with reliable animal models and a comprehensive understanding from multiple perspectives may help to completely elucidate the pathogenesis of PCV.[8]

## Evolution of Anti-VEGF therapy in PCV

## Historical perspectives – Role of PDT and combination therapy
Prior to the advent of OCT, polyp detected on ICGA was the main target of treatment as it was the most obvious source of hemorrhage and exudation.[21] Many studies demonstrated the efficacy of PDT by achieving polyp closure in more than 90% of eyes with PCV.[222324] This was translated into resolution of exudative changes and stabilization of vision at 1- year of follow-up. PDT involves intravenous injection of a photosensitive dye (Verteporfin) which is preferentially concentrated in the abnormal vasculature including polyps and BNN. Subsequent activation of the dye with a non-heat-generating laser causes closure or photo-thrombosis of abnormal polypoidal vessels, while reducing collateral damage.[25] However, longer follow-up studies showed persistent BNN and recurrence of polyps requiring multiple PDT sessions and the risk of associated choroidal ischemia due to closure of normal choroidal vessels. Unlike nAMD where the need for re-PDT declined after 1 year, more retreatments were required in years 3 and 4 in PCV.[26] Acute visual loss due to massive subretinal hemorrhage and chronic visual loss due to progressive RPE damage and fibrosis are other serious concerns.[27] Heterogeneous location of polyps especially in the peripapillary area also restricted the accessibility of PDT.
This led to the concept of combining PDT with anti-VEGF agents where the thrombotic property of PDT and the anti-permeability effects of anti-VEGF drugs act synergistically to achieve better polyp regression and reduce exudation. The reduced need for repeated PDTs also helps to protect against the sequelae of RPE and choroidal ischemia.[282930] The EVEREST trial was the first PCV-specific RCT evaluating the safety and efficacy of combination therapy with Ranibizumab (Fab fragment of humanised monoclonal antibody against VEGF-A) and verteporfin PDT versus Ranibizumab monotherapy.[2] Both 1-year and 2-year data revealed combination therapy was superior to monotherapy in visual and anatomical outcomes and it became the popular treatment for PCV.[31] Another RCT, the LAPTOP trial compared PDT monotherapy with Ranibizumab monotherapy and reported superior visual outcomes in the Ranibizumab group at 2 years.[32] Though polyp closure was not evaluated in this study, it proved a point by demonstrating that good visual outcome was possible without PDT and confirming polyp closure.
PCV belongs to the spectrum of pachychoroid diseases, but the relationship between choroidal thickness, dilated vessels, and hyper-permeable vasculature is intriguing as not all eyes with dilated vessels show hyper-permeability on ICGA.[33] Patients with thin choroid are more similar to nAMD spectrum and respond better to anti-VEGF monotherapy, while those with large polyp areas and pachychoroid benefit from combination therapy.[7]

## Change in scenario – shift to anti-VEGF monotherapy
VEGF, an important cytokine involved in angiogenesis, is implicated as a key mediator in the development of CNV in AMD and in PCV, and hence, targeted inhibition of VEGF is a reasonable approach in both conditions.[2934] VEGF overexpression in these eyes induces endothelial proliferation and vascular permeability which is detected clinically as enlarging neovascularization, subretinal hemorrhage, and SRF. Anti-VEGF drugs, by suppressing the activity of VEGF protein, halt the growth of new vessels and reduce exudation in both conditions. Several pivotal clinical trials have proved the efficacy of anti-VEGF monotherapy as the first-line treatment in nAMD.[10] But in most of these trials, ICGA was not performed and patients with PCV were not differentiated.[1] Subsequent studies of anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV reported favorable outcome in PCV also, despite a limited polyp regression rate of 40%.[10] However, abnormal choroidal vascular complexes were not affected by anti-VEGF monotherapy.
The availability of Aflibercept, a recombinant fusion protein with high affinity binding to VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and PlGF (placental growth factor), and thus preventing endothelial proliferation, vascular permeability, and neovascularization offered a superior option for monotherapy in PCV. The efficacy of Aflibercept in PCV either as monotherapy or with PDT only as a rescue therapy was evaluated in the PLANET trial.[5] At 1 year, improvement in visual outcome was seen in >85% and no leakage from the PL in >80% in the monotherapy group, confirming that monotherapy was noninferior to the combination group. However, it has to be noted that though >80% had fluid-free retina (on OCT), only <40% had polyp closure (on ICGA), questioning the significance of polyp closure.[21] These results were maintained at 2-year follow-up concluding that PCV can be treated with monotherapy.[35] Notably, the percentage of patients with polyp regression was similar in both groups at week 96. Though some patients had recurrent polyps in the second year, it did not affect the final visual outcome, implying that not all polyps cause fluid accumulation and activity of polyps should be assessed using both ICGA and OCT. A comparison of 2-year results of EVEREST II and PLANET study showed that Aflibercept monotherapy provided higher visual gain and baseline adjunct PDT in all eyes (EVEREST II) may not offer a real advantage. In fact, only <20% of patients in the PLANET study met the rescue criteria for PDT and authors speculate that rescue PDT may be necessary only when polyps on ICGA are associated with fluid on OCT.

## Choice of treatment regimens
The molecular attributes and longer half-life of Aflibercept offered the additional advantage of extending treatment intervals to beyond 4 weeks in selected cases in the second year.[35] An optional investigator determined T and E (Treat and Extend) regimen adopted in the PLANET study in the second year achieved reduction in the number of visits and treatment without compromising the visual outcome. The mean number of injections was 3.9 and 4.2 in the monotherapy and combinations groups, respectively, when the treatment interval was extended to ≥12 weeks. This led to the comparison of the proactive T and E regimen with fixed dose regimen (requires monthly or bimonthly injections for 1 year) and reactive pro-re-nata (PRN) regimen (treatment on as-needed basis). In the T and E regimen after disease inactivity is achieved with a monthly loading dose of ≥ 3 injections, the treatment interval is gradually extended in increments of 2–4 weeks up to a maximum of 12–16 weeks, but if the disease recurs, the interval is shortened. This is particularly relevant in the Asia-Pacific region where PCV is more common and limited access to health care and financial burden are genuine issues.[36] Subsequently, many studies with Aflibercept T and E in PCV reported encouraging results with extension of treatment interval to ≥12 weeks in 41% to 67% of patients and nearly half had complete polypoidal regression.[35373839] It can be an early T and E after week 12 or late T and E after week 24 (after 3 more monthly doses), in eyes with residual PL detected on ICGA at week 12.[39] PCV subgroup analysis of ALTAIR study reported greater extension of treatment interval to 16 weeks in 51% of patients, substantiating this treatment regimen.[37] A recent 2-year real-world results of T and E regimen of Aflibercept in nAMD and PCV also showed that the treatment interval could be extended to ≥ 16 weeks in 60% of all cases and 79% of PCV cases, with an average of 10.95 injections over 2 years.[40] Fluid-related biomarkers on OCT (SRF and IRF) along with clinical assessment including visual acuity and presence of hemorrhage can guide the treatment decisions. Overall, a higher proportion of PCV subgroup achieved a longer treatment interval and required fewer injections compared to nAMD patients. A systematic review of 27 studies based on T and E regimen in nAMD and PCV by an expert panel of Asia-Pacific Vitreo-retinal society (APVRS) confirmed the benefits of this regimen in providing meaningful visual outcome with less frequent clinic visits and recommended T and E with Aflibercept for long-term management.[36]
However, another systematic review of anti-VEGF regimens involving 636 eyes with PCV concluded that all regimens are similar in efficacy, though T and E may be superior in some patients.[41] There are also few concerns in applying the recommendations of FLUID study (based on nAMD) in PCV as SRF is a prominent marker of disease activity in PCV.[21] The observation that fluctuations in retinal thickness can influence visual outcome and the superior drying effect with combination therapy in EVEREST II study also questions the applicability of FLUID study in PCV management. As PL is the main source of exudation, speed of PL closure also should be considered in titrating the treatment in PCV.

## Newer Anti-VEGF agents – More potency and durability
Potential for more durability in therapy became possible with Brolucizumab, a humanized single-chain antibody fragment, with its smaller size allowing higher molar dosing, greater tissue penetration, and thereby more sustained duration of action. HAWK and HARRIER trials proved the noninferiority of Brolucizumab with Aflibercept in nAMD at week 48 which was sustained at week 96 and it also had a superior drying effect.[4243] Analysis of the subset of Japanese participants with PCV showed that robust and consistent visual gains could be achieved with Brolucizumab injections at 12/8 weeks intervals and 76% of patients could be maintained at 8 weeks dosing after loading to week 48.[44] This can significantly reduce the treatment burden in PCV. However, serious adverse events like intraocular inflammation including retinal vasculitis and vascular occlusion were higher with Brolucizumab (4.7%) compared to Aflibercept (0.6%).[45] Half of the cases experienced the adverse event within 3 months of the first injection and three-quarters within first 6 months.[46] As one key factor identified is the interval between injections, it is advisable that Brolucizumab should not be dosed at intervals <8 weeks.[45] If inflammation is detected, recommendation is wide-field imaging followed by standard anti-inflammatory treatment and withholding further injections. This warrants a cautious approach in selecting eyes for treatment with this drug.[44]
There is only limited literature regarding Brolucizumab in PCV with a follow-up of 1 year or more. In a retrospective study of 17 Japanese eyes with treatment-naïve PCV, Brolucizumab was effective in improving both functional and anatomic outcomes with complete resolution of PL in 93% and dry macula in 86.6% at 1 year.[47] The same authors had previously reported a polyp regression rate of 48% with bimonthly and 53% with PRN regimen of Aflibercept monotherapy. This may also be compared to the 60% regression rate in the combination group in EVEREST II study. This higher rate of polypoidal regression also explains the higher rate of dry macula in eyes treated with Brolucizumab. A similar study from Korea reported a polyp regression rate of 74%.[4] This may be attributed to its smaller molecular size allowing greater penetration into sub-RPE space at higher concentration. Complete polyp resolution reduces the frequency of injections and the risk for recurrence but may not necessarily improve vision. In a study comparing Aflibercept and Brolucizumab in PCV, both achieved similar visual outcomes, but Brolucizumab was more effective in causing regression of PL and caused greater reduction in central retinal thickness (CRT) and sub-foveal choroidal thickness (SFCT).[48] Reduction in SFCT has been observed in other studies also.[4] Long-term studies are needed to know if it could be a risk factor for developing chorioretinal atrophy. Brolucizumab has also been found to be effective as a switch drug, allowing extension of treatment interval from 6.9 to 11.7 weeks, especially in eyes with fewer polyps and smaller lesion sizes.[49] Switching achieves significant reduction in CRT, leakage from polyps, BNN, and choroid, but visual improvement may be less significant in refractory cases compared to treatment naïve cases.[5051]

## Anti-VEGF with dual inhibition
The latest addition in our armamentarium of anti-VEGF drugs is Faricimab, a novel humanized bispecific monoclonal antibody that selectively binds with high affinity to VEGF-A and angiopoetin-2 (Ang-2), the two critical mediators in nAMD and PCV. It suppresses neovascularization and also stabilizes the vascular environment leading to better control of disease.[5253] The 2-year results of TENAYA and LUCERNE trials with Faricimab in nAMD consistently showed that visual gains with Faricimab given at intervals of up to 16 weeks were noninferior to Aflibercept given every 2 months. 59–67% achieved 16 weeks dosing interval, and 74–81% achieved 12 weeks or longer dosing interval.[52] However, patients with macular PCV were under-represented in this study. The only RCT for Faricimab in PCV is the on-going SALWEEN Trial, a Phase IIIb/IV, multicenter, open-label, single-arm study involving 135 treatment-naïve PCV patients across nine Asian countries.[53] In the interim analysis at 16 weeks, 80% was fluid-free, 50% had complete polyp regression, and 86% had inactive polyps. The 12-month data expected later this year might provide more insights into its role in the long-term maintenance of visual gains with less treatment burden offered by the longer dosing interval. Other aspects to be explored are the anti-Ang2 effects and the role of biomarkers to detect vascular stabilization in PCV.[54] A real-world study of Faricimab in refractory PCV (FAR-PEARL) reported that a differential response may be seen depending on the disease type.[53] The eyes were divided into recurrent PCV (>180 days from prior anti-VEGF treatment), suboptimal extension (60–180 days), or recalcitrant PCV (<60 days). Recurrent disease indicates return of symptoms or fluid after an initial positive response to anti-VEGF, whereas recalcitrant cases are those with inadequate response despite ongoing therapy and suboptimal extension is in between the two. The injections were fewer with recurrent than in recalcitrant cases, but all eyes were dry by 4.5 months of switching proving its efficacy even in refractory PCV. Though functional gains were modest, the extended dosing interval reduces the treatment burden. A meta-analysis of the real-world evidence of Faricimab in PCV (five studies with treatment-naïve PCV and two studies switched from other anti-VEGF drugs) showed that in treatment-naïve eyes, Faricimab achieved stable BCVA, a significant decrease in CRT, and polyp closure in 48.7%, but in switch-over cases, the response was variable.[55] MONDEGO is another on-going trial which aims to study the efficacy, safety, and durability of Faricimab in PCV in Caucasian patients (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06709339). Long-term efficacy studies and controlled comparative studies will help to establish the role of Faricimab in PCV.

## Long-Term Results of Anti-VEGF in PCV
There are only limited data regarding the long-term outcomes of treatment in PCV. Evaluation of 90 patients in the EVEREST II trial after 6 years in a recall visit detected good vision in one-third and detectable PL in nearly half of patients in both groups, suggesting that recurrence in PCV is regardless of initial therapy.[56] The combination group had superior anatomical outcome with a dry macula and required less injections, but fibrosis and atrophy were more common post PDT. Hence, the aim of treatment should be controlling any exudation from PL or BNN and not focus only on PL closure. This requires continued monitoring and timely retreatment which can be ensured if reimbursement facilities are made available. In another real-world study of PCV and nAMD with up to 10 years follow-up, it was found that long-term BCVA deteriorated in both groups, mostly due to under treatment with anti-VEGFs, though it was better than that in the pre-anti-VEGF era.[57] Notably, visual outcome was better in the PCV group which is hypothesized to be due to the relatively intact RPE in PCV unlike the more diseased RPE with drusen in nAMD which may account for the difference in the degree of endurance to under treatment.

## Future Directions
Similar to VEGF-A, VEGF-C and VEGF-D of the VEGF family also activate VEGFR2, the main receptor signaling angiogenesis and vascular permeability. They also activate VEGFR3, another important mediator of angiogenesis. Suppression of VEGF-A alone can upregulate VEGF-C/D, and this escape mechanism can contribute to the resistance to anti-VEGF-A monotherapy. A combination of Sozinibercept (2 mg), a novel trap inhibitor of VEGF-C and D with Ranibizumab, achieved improved visual and anatomical outcomes compared with Ranibizumab monotherapy in PCV in a phase IIb study.[58] This has initiated two phase III trials combining Sozinibercept with Ranibizumab or Aflibercept in nAMD, with the primary end point being superiority in the BCVA gains. Additional data on PCV are expected from these trials, but multi-VEGF inhibition specifically in PCV population is warranted in the near future to address this unmet need. Another big step in PCV would be choroid-based phenotyping and choroid-guided treatment strategies.[7] Another promising option is Aflibercept in a higher dose of 8 mg, which was first evaluated in nAMD in PULSAR trial and subsequently found to be effective in PCV also based on the post-hoc subgroup analysis.[5960] Outcomes were comparable with 2 mg every 8 weeks versus 8 mg every 12 to 16 weeks. This higher formulation has the potential to improve PCV management once it becomes available in our country.

## Recommendations for Anti-VEGF Monotherapy in PCV
Initiation Phase: If APOIS diagnostic criteria based on OCT are met, treatment should be initiated with intravitreal injection of anti-VEGF drugs. The three anti-VEGF drugs that have shown efficacy in terms of morphological response on OCT are Aflibercept, Brolucizumab, and Faricimab. In 2021, APVRS recommended initiating treatment with Aflibercept, which was the best option available then.[36] Monthly injections are given until disease is inactive, that is, fluid-free OCT and no new hemorrhage.[36] Newer anti-VEGFs introduced in the subsequent years have proven to be equally effective and also more durable, offering more choices for first-line therapy in PCV. Brolucizumab has the advantages of superior anatomical outcome, higher rates of polyp closure, longer dosing interval, and reduced treatment burden, but cautious selection of patients is important.[4448] The latest molecule, Faricimab, has the added advantage of vascular stabilization apart from extended dosing interval and good tolerance profile.[53] In the recently published PAT (Preferences and Trends) survey by VRSI on the management of PCV by the vitreoretinal surgeons in India, 63% preferred Aflibercept and 23% preferred Brolucizumab to initiate the treatment.[61] While monitoring treatment response, we need to look at both the resolution of fluid and the morphology of PED. One is predominantly BNN ending in polypoidal dilation which is a slow progressing disease and may not require aggressive treatment. They can be maintained on PRN provided regular follow-up can be ensured, to avoid unnecessary injections [Figs. 5 and 6]. But if frequent recurrences are observed over 12–16 months, they should be shifted to T and E regimen. The second is the predominantly polypoidal type with cluster of polyps subfoveally and higher risk of hemorrhage and associated with pachychoroid. This phenotype requires more frequent injections in the initial phase and can have early recurrence during follow-up [Fig. 4]. It would be advisable to move this subtype into an early T and E regimen or combine with PDT, if feasible[36] [Fig. 7]. Initiation Phase:
If APOIS diagnostic criteria based on OCT are met, treatment should be initiated with intravitreal injection of anti-VEGF drugs. The three anti-VEGF drugs that have shown efficacy in terms of morphological response on OCT are Aflibercept, Brolucizumab, and Faricimab. In 2021, APVRS recommended initiating treatment with Aflibercept, which was the best option available then.[36] Monthly injections are given until disease is inactive, that is, fluid-free OCT and no new hemorrhage.[36] Newer anti-VEGFs introduced in the subsequent years have proven to be equally effective and also more durable, offering more choices for first-line therapy in PCV. Brolucizumab has the advantages of superior anatomical outcome, higher rates of polyp closure, longer dosing interval, and reduced treatment burden, but cautious selection of patients is important.[4448] The latest molecule, Faricimab, has the added advantage of vascular stabilization apart from extended dosing interval and good tolerance profile.[53] In the recently published PAT (Preferences and Trends) survey by VRSI on the management of PCV by the vitreoretinal surgeons in India, 63% preferred Aflibercept and 23% preferred Brolucizumab to initiate the treatment.[61] While monitoring treatment response, we need to look at both the resolution of fluid and the morphology of PED. A decrease in the height or absence of polypoidal PED, presence of densely hyper-reflective or homogeneous PED content, and absence of sub-RPE ring are indicators of polyp closure or inactive polyps.[761] An increase in the size of PED, DLS, and heterogeneous internal reflectivity are indicators of activity even in the absence of fluid [Table 2].[1962] Once disease is inactive, subsequent treatment could be either PRN or T and E.[41]

Maintenance Phase:
This phase is for the long-term management of the disease. Current literature recommends extending interval by 2–4 weeks up to a maximum of 16 weeks for Aflibercept.[36] However, this strategy may not be practical in the real world. In this context, it is important to understand the two phenotypes in PCV. One is predominantly BNN ending in polypoidal dilation which is a slow progressing disease and may not require aggressive treatment. They can be maintained on PRN provided regular follow-up can be ensured, to avoid unnecessary injections [Figs. 5 and 6]. This phenotype requires more frequent injections in the initial phase and can have early recurrence during follow-up [Fig. 4]. ## Conclusions
This article summarizes the evolution of anti-VEGF therapy in PCV and provides practical guidelines on its diagnosis and management. The diagnosis should be made on clinical and careful SD-OCT examination by scanning the entire diseased area to identify the diagnostic features. ICGA when available is definitely useful to identify the subtypes amenable to other forms of therapy like thermal laser. Regarding the choice of drug, Aflibercept has the strongest evidence, Brolucizumab has the best drying effect, and Faricimab with its dual action is an emerging option. The less aggressive BNN phenotype may be maintained on PRN regimen, while the more aggressive polypoidal variety will benefit by T and E regimen or even combination therapy. The primary aim is to achieve dry macula and restore vision, and the secondary aim would be to achieve polyp regression. Polyp regression may not always improve vision but helps to achieve longer periods of disease quiescence and reduce the need for frequent injections, thus reducing the treatment burden.
