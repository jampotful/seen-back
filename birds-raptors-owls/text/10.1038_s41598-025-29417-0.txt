# Infrared and visible image fusion using GAN with fuzzy logic and Harris Hawks optimization

## Abstract
AbstractThe fusion of visible and infrared images has gained great importance and attention with the emergence of machine learning techniques for applications such as surveillance and medical diagnosis. This study proposes a novel Fuzzy Generative Adversarial Network (FGAN) integrated with a Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) algorithm to fuse visible and infrared images, addressing the limitations of existing approaches by dynamically optimizing a Mamdani-type fuzzy logic system within the generator. The innovation lies in employing HHO to tune fuzzy rules and output membership functions—using entropy, PSNR, and SSIM as training targets—alongside a Support Vector Machine (SVM) enhanced with Frechet Inception Distance (FID) for discriminator training, justifying the use of multiple models to achieve robust feature extraction and classification. Unlike traditional methods, this hybrid approach ensures superior adaptability to diverse image characteristics. This work achieves remarkable adaptability in infrared and visible picture fusion by incorporating a fuzzy logic system, optimized by HHO, within a GAN framework, therefore pioneering a revolutionary fusion paradigm. Superior visual quality and fusion precision are achieved by the suggested FGAN’s unique integration of a Support Vector Machine and FID in the discriminator, which greatly improves differentiation accuracy. Experimental results on the TNO dataset using MATLAB demonstrate that the proposed FGAN outperforms state-of-the-art techniques, achieving a PSNR up to 55 dB, an SSIM up to 0.99, and SF lower than 10, reflecting enhanced visual quality and information retention. By providing high-quality fused images with better clarity and information retention, this method greatly improves remote sensing, surveillance, and medical diagnostics.Keywords: Infrared and visible image fusion, FGAN fuzzy neural network, Harris hawks optimization (HHO) algorithm, Image sensors, Fuzzy logic system, Support vector machine (SVM) systemSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Computational science, Computer science, Software

## Introduction
The rapid advancement of image sensing technology has significantly expanded the application of numerous image sensors across industries1, driven by the growing demand for efficient information transmission. Infrared and visible image fusion2,3, a key subset of multimodal image fusion, plays a crucial role in creating high-quality fusion images by integrating the most relevant data from infrared and visible sensors4. Infrared images provide valuable thermal radiation information and clear target detection, unaffected by adverse weather conditions, despite their lower resolution and ambiguous features5. In contrast, visible images offer high-resolution details, including rich color and texture, though they are vulnerable to environmental factors such as illumination and fog6. Recent advancements in image fusion and related fields enhance the proposed Fuzzy Generative Adversarial Network (FGAN) framework for infrared and visible image fusion7. introduced RatioVLP to suppress ambient light noise in visible light positioning, improving robustness in dynamic environments, while8 proposed uLiDR, an inertial-assisted system for smartphone-based navigation, aiding in handling spectral disparities9. advanced real-time feature extraction via near-infrared spectroscopy for anesthesia stage classification, informing discriminator training in FGAN10. developed a self-organizing pixel matrix classifier for remote sensing, applicable to optimizing fusion metrics like PSNR and SSIM11. presented a physics-constrained infrared thermography decomposition method, and12 introduced resource-saving binary optical computing, both reducing computational complexity and enhancing thermal detail preservation in GAN-based fusion. This complementary nature underscores the importance of fusing these modalities to produce a single image that retains critical thermal data from infrared sources while preserving the detailed visual information from visible sources, enhancing applications like target detection, intelligent surveillance, and remote sensing13.
Despite significant progress in visible and infrared image fusion techniques, several challenges persist due to the inherent differences between these image types and external environmental influences. Key obstacles include spectral disparities, resolution mismatches (with infrared images typically having lower resolution), computational complexity, geometric misalignments, and information redundancy in methods like the contour let transform14. Recent studies advance infrared and visible image fusion techniques, aligning with the Fuzzy Generative Adversarial Network (FGAN) framework15. proposed a method for detecting weak infrared targets using dual images and areas, enhancing target detection in low-visibility conditions relevant to FGAN’s thermal feature integration16. introduced a guided implicit depth function with scale-aware feature fusion, improving detail preservation in complex scenes, applicable to FGAN’s pixel-level fusion17. developed an adaptive graph frequency location for hyperspectral anomaly detection, aiding in handling spectral disparities similar to those in IR-VIS fusion18. presented MLP-Net, a multilayer perceptron fusion network for infrared small target detection, optimizing metrics like PSNR and SSIM for FGAN’s discriminator training19,20. proposed unified generative frameworks for image emotion classification, leveraging adaptive prompts and feature integration, which can enhance FGAN’s ability to manage diverse image characteristics and improve visual quality. Additionally, sensitivity to conditions such as low light, dust, and humidity can degrade fusion quality. These issues highlight the need for innovative solutions that can effectively manage these variations. Among the most promising approaches, artificial intelligence-based multi-scale transformations and deep learning techniques offer potential to enhance fusion quality and efficiency21. The TNO dataset, which provides paired visible and infrared images, serves as a valuable resource for extracting usable features and testing these advanced methods, addressing the limitations of traditional techniques that struggle with dynamic environmental conditions.
Image fusion is categorized into three primary levels: pixel-level, feature-level, and decision-level, each with distinct advantages and limitations depending on the application context. Pixel-level fusion, the most widely adopted due to its simplicity and computational efficiency, directly combines data from source images without requiring complex feature extraction, preserving all pixel information for rapid processing22. However, it is sensitive to noise and requires precise image alignment, which can lead to poor results under varying contrast or brightness. Feature-level fusion, by contrast, extracts and combines specific image attributes (e.g., edges or regions of interest), reducing data volume and improving noise resilience, though it demands more sophisticated algorithms and may lose some visual details23. Decision-level fusion processes images independently before merging decisions, offering high adaptability and noise tolerance, but it suffers from complexity and potential data loss tied to the quality of initial decisions. The choice of method depends on the image type, fusion objective, and available resources, with ongoing efforts focused on improving time efficiency and fusion performance across these techniques.
The fusion of infrared and visible images has emerged as a critical technique in various applications, such as surveillance, medical diagnostics, and autonomous navigation, due to the complementary information these modalities provide. Infrared imaging excels in capturing thermal signatures, particularly in low-visibility conditions, while visible imaging offers detailed texture and color information. Nevertheless, conventional fusion techniques, such those that depend on wavelet transformations or simple averaging24, frequently have drawbacks, such as decreased contrast, loss of important features, and an inability to adjust to a variety of environmental circumstances. These shortcomings underscore the need for advanced approaches that can effectively integrate the strengths of both imaging types, thereby motivating the current study. This research introduces a novel Fuzzy Generative Adversarial Network (FGAN) enhanced with the Harris Hawks HHO algorithm, justified by the proven efficacy of generative adversarial networks (GANs) in image synthesis25 and the robustness of HHO in optimizing complex systems26. Additionally, the incorporation of a Mamdani-type fuzzy logic system, supported by its success in handling uncertainty27, and a Support Vector Machine (SVM) for classification, validated by its accuracy in pattern recognition28, addresses the dynamic nature of image fusion tasks. By using Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) to precisely differentiate between generated and actual images, a Support Vector Machine (SVM) is incorporated into the discriminator of the suggested Fuzzy Generative Adversarial Network (FGAN) to increase its robustness. This innovative use of SVM guarantees accurate picture authenticity classification, greatly enhancing the fusion quality by maintaining important visual and thermal features under various environmental circumstances. The fusion is guided by distinct loss functions and played with an adversarial game. In the productive section, the generative adversarial network uses a trained fuzzy rules-based fuzzy logic system to execute pixel-level source image fusion, resulting in an initial fused image that includes information about visible textures and infrared thermal radiation. In the distinction section, the network is able to extract more detailed visible images. The HHO algorithm now automatically defines fuzzy fusion rules and designs the degree of differentiation between fusion images using two input photos, eliminating the need for manual definition. Additionally, this method does not require costly multi-scale conversion, which lowers computing costs and complexity. ## Related works
The majority of generative adversarial networks (GANs) use a mixture of two source images as input, which means that the extracted feature is insufficient to preserve context and details. Consequently, a novel GAN-based framework, the DSG-Fusion Filter Network, is proposed in research29. The DSG network generator uses two separate data streams to extract the features of the two images based on the various techniques used for visible and infrared images. The base and detail layers of the source images are obtained by integrating a guided filter into the generator, which solves the issue of obtaining representative background information and concentrating the DSG network on details. While the detail layers take part in the fusion process directly, the base layers are linked to the respective source pictures to extract deeper features.
The study30 suggests a layered fusion method based on the channel attention mechanism and generative adversarial network (HFCA_GAN) to address the issue of edge blurring and non-saliency of prominent targets, which arise from the fact that current visible and infrared image fusion techniques only rely on the representation of the original local or global information. A guiding filter creates a base layer from the infrared picture and a fine layer from the visible image. The second step in improving the contour effect is to integrate the visible light and infrared picture base layers using histogram mapping improvement. Third, the infrared and visible image refining layers are combined using the enhanced GAN algorithm. The local information of the layered fused image is then correlated using the multilayer fusion network with the channel attention mechanism, yielding the final fused image with the contour gradient information and other pertinent features.
Recent research enhances multimodal image processing, supporting the Fuzzy Generative Adversarial Network (FGAN) for infrared and visible image fusion. Article31 proposed image transformation for partial discharge source identification in high-speed train cable terminals, improving feature extraction relevant to FGAN’s thermal data integration. Article32 introduced deformable 3D convolutional group fusion for video super-resolution, and33 enhanced object pose estimation in cluttered RGB scenes, both aiding FGAN’s detail preservation in complex environments. Article34 utilized diffusion-GAN for improved visual perception of marine biology images, while35 developed perceptual information fidelity for industrial image quality estimation, optimizing metrics like SSIM and PSNR for FGAN’s discriminator36. presented a knowledge-embedded diffusion model for infrared image destriping, enhancing thermal detail clarity in FGAN’s fusion process.
Paper37 integrates Fuzzy Lattices and Neural Reinforcement Learning for seizure classification, evaluated using Bonn University and CHB-MIT EEG databases. Features are encoded as fuzzy lattices, with feature vectors derived from Kinetic Energy (K.E.) via the Schrödinger equation, and the top seven K.E.-based lattices are classified using a self-adaptive Neural Reinforcement Learning classifier, distinguishing sub-classes like healthy eyes open, healthy eyes closed, Epileptogenic Zone, hippocampal formation, and epileptic seizure. The method achieves 97.6% and 97.5% accuracy for Bonn and CHB-MIT datasets, respectively, outperforming existing techniques with a computation speed 1.5 times faster than Fuzzy Q-Learning. The goal is to enhance seizure detection and diagnosis accuracy and speed through this hybrid adaptive model, improving patient care with advanced computational techniques.
Electroencephalography (EEG) is a preferred method for detecting and analyzing various epileptic seizure classes. Paper38 introduces a proactive online classification of epileptic seizures using reinforcement learning, proposing novel Genetic Algorithm-assisted Fuzzy Q-Learning and standard Fuzzy Q-Learning classifiers. The method employs the Hilbert-Huang Transform to extract 19 time-frequency domain features during pre-processing. The proposed classifiers achieve classification accuracies of 96.79% and 93.81% for the Genetic Algorithm-assisted and standard Fuzzy Q-Learning approaches, respectively, outperforming other reinforcement learning-based methods and matching contemporary seizure classification techniques. This approach offers medical practitioners an effective tool for analyzing large datasets and accelerating seizure diagnosis.
Through genetic algorithm (GA)-based minimization, paper39 seeks to bring nonlinear optimization into the fuzzy reinforcement learning (RL) technique. By selecting the greedy action or the action with the lowest Q value, an agent in traditional fuzzy reinforcement learning tries to determine the most optimum course of action at each stage. However, Q function is an unknown function and an attempt to find minima of such a function based on a limited set of values, in our view, is inaccurate and insufficient. A more rigorous approach would be to employ a nonlinear optimization procedure for finding minima of the Q function. Rather than using a simple algebraic minimum, we suggest using a genetic algorithm to determine the ideal action value at each algorithm iteration. We employ fuzzy RL control with GA optimizer based on Lyapunov theory to ensure the stability of the developed controller.
For downstream vision tasks, infrared and visible image fusion (IVIF) creates fused images that contain all of the features of both images. Traditional fusion techniques are recognized to have various drawbacks even though they have demonstrated better effectiveness in some areas. (a) Targets in fused images are frequently not conspicuous, and current approaches seldom ever take lighting conditions in low-light scenarios into account. (b) When generating high-quality fused images, they typically need manually chosen feature representations and exact blending rules, which necessitate manual involvement and may impair fusion performance. (c) It can be challenging to create an appropriate overcomplete dictionary when using SR and LRR approaches. As a result, real-time image fusion cannot be accomplished using the related fusion algorithms’ runtime. (d) Because asymmetric feature information overlaps, complex feature extraction and fusion techniques frequently create halos and fuzzy edges.

## Proposed generative adversarial networks

## HHO algorithm
This algorithm, which is used as a key meta-heuristic optimization method in this work, is inspired by the predatory behaviour of Harris Hawks in nature. It solves complex optimization problems by simulating collective hunting strategies, such as random searching in large environments to find prey, sudden attacks from different angles, and dynamic adjustment of positions based on the position of the prey. In this study, HHO is used as a tool to tune the parameters of a fuzzy logic system in a fuzzy adversarial generative network (FGAN), which includes the optimization of fuzzy rules and output membership functions. The process starts with an initial population of solutions and converges to the most optimal settings with successive iterations, which helps improve the system’s resilience to input variability.
The group activity of Harris Hawks hunting rabbits served as the inspiration for the hyper-regression method known as Harris Hawks Optimization. Fast convergence, avoiding local optima, and finding a suitable balance between exploration and exploitation are some of HHO’s benefits. This technique, which comprises two primary phases, is utilized in domains including neural networks, machine learning, and engineering optimization to solve continuous optimization problems:
Exploration: Scanning the issue space at random. Two random techniques are used by the hawks to update their positions during this phase. The first approach uses a random hawk to specify the new position. The second method uses the group’s average position to determine the new position.
Exploitation: To approach the ideal answer, four attack tactics are used: soft, harsh, with and without surprise.
Four tactics are used, taking into account the prey energy (E) and the possibility of surprise (r):

## Image fusion with the generative adversarial neural network (GAN) approach
A generative adversarial neural network (GAN) is a sophisticated technique for merging visual data from two distinct sources in the fusion of visible and infrared images. The objective of this procedure is to combine the complementary and helpful information from both kinds of photos into a single image. Traditional cameras can capture what is known as “visible images,” which include details that can be seen by the naked eye. Images like this are great for capturing natural colours and texture characteristics. Conversely, infrared images are recorded using thermal information and are produced by infrared sensors. They offer information that is not visible to the human eye. These pictures are typically helpful for identifying objects in dimly lit areas or in settings with complicated or cluttered backgrounds.
A generator and a discriminator are the two neural network components that make up a Generative Adversarial Neural Network (GAN)24. The Generator attempts to produce authentic visuals. The Discriminator attempts to ascertain the authenticity of the image produced by the Generator. With the Generator attempting to trick the Discriminator and the Discriminator attempting to make better distinctions, these two networks are engaged in a never-ending competition.
Two visible and infrared images are fed into the network during the image fusion process using a GAN. The goal of the generator network is to create a combined image by utilizing the characteristics of both images. Both sources should be usefully represented in this graphic. The goal of the discriminator network is to determine if the combined picture is authentic or not. Actual visible and infrared pictures are used to train this network. Both the discriminator and the generator undergo ongoing improvement throughout training. Efforts are made by both the generator and the discriminator to improve the quality of the fused images and their realism. By utilizing GAN for picture fusion, the system is able to generate high-quality fused images that incorporate valuable information from both sources, thanks to GAN’s excellent quality. Because of its adaptability, this approach works well with a wide variety of image formats and settings.
The network can automatically integrate and extract significant information from photos using deep learning. But the generative element of this suggested method makes use of a network based on fuzzy logic. The given method uses HHO meta-heuristic algorithm to train fuzzy rules and parameters of the output membership functions, with a reinforcement learning model serving as the basis for the generative phase. One of the primary advancements in this GAN fusion method is this strategy. The primary benefit of this is that, in contrast to the deep learning paradigm, the generative network architecture for GAN is less complicated.
As a whole, GAN-assisted visible and infrared image fusion is an effective and powerful way to combine visual data from many sources, with wide-ranging applications.
As part of the GAN training challenge, the generator is taught the distribution of features for the given data by receiving input of random noise. The discriminator receives both the generator’s output and the actual samples as inputs, checks to see if the data is from the actual or fake sample, and then sends back the judgment result to the generator. These two components are trained alternately. They compete and encourage one another to better in the game of continual conflict. When training is complete, the parameters are fine-tuned until the discriminator and generator are virtually identical, at which point the system reaches the Nash equilibrium. ## FID criterion
Frechet Inception Distance (FID) is used in this work as a key metric for evaluating and training the Discriminator in a Fuzzy Adversarial Generative Network (FGAN). This metric, which is calculated based on the Fréchet Distance between the feature distributions of real images and those generated by the model, measures the visual quality and structural similarity of images fused from infrared and visible images. In this study, FID, by integrating with a Support Vector Machine (SVM), helps the discriminator to detect subtle differences between real images from the TNO dataset and fake images generated by the generator, which leads to improved accuracy and more natural output. The use of FID, especially in this method, plays an important role in dynamic optimization by the Harris-Hock algorithm (HHO) due to its ability to more comprehensively evaluate image quality compared to traditional metrics such as PSNR or SSIM, and enhances the adaptability of the model in diverse environmental conditions.
The quality of images generated by generative models, including generative adversarial networks (GANs), is assessed using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). By comparing their distributions in a high-level feature space, it calculates how similar the produced and real images are. In this study, the integration of HHO algorithm with Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) is designed as an innovative approach to improve the fusion process of infrared and visible images. The HHO algorithm, a meta-heuristic optimization method based on the behaviour of natural predators, is inspired by the search and attack mechanisms of Harris Hawks and is specifically used for dynamic parameter tuning in complex systems. With its ability to explore the search space and converge to global optima, this algorithm is selected as a complementary tool for optimizing the GAN structure. On the other hand, GAN consists of two main parts, Generator and Discriminator, which interact with each other through a competitive process to produce more realistic images. Here, HHO accelerates the optimization by adjusting the parameters of the generative network, including weights and fuzzy membership functions, as well as the discriminator parameters. This combination allows the GAN to not only produce higher quality images, but also to be more resilient to input variability (such as optical or thermal variations). The fusion process is such that HHO acts as an external layer and updates the parameters in each training cycle based on metrics such as entropy, PSNR, and SSIM.
In this method, the coexistence of the terms Reinforcement Learning and Meta-heuristic Optimization plays a key role, especially in the training phase. Reinforcement Learning, inspired by algorithms such as Q-Learning, operates using a Reward Function that penalizes deviations from energy optimization or image quality. This function is designed based on performance metrics (such as FID and PSNR) and allows the network to automatically learn optimal strategies in real time. On the other hand, HHO meta-heuristic optimization explores the parameter space with a random and population-based search approach and uses evolutionary mechanisms to find the best settings instead of relying solely on gradients. In the training phase, these two approaches act as complements: HHO optimizes the initial parameters and the fuzzy structure, while reinforcement learning increases the system’s adaptability to heterogeneous data by dynamically adjusting the weights and fuzzy rules. This coexistence is such that HHO acts as a pre-setter and reinforcement learning as a final adjuster, which ultimately leads to a balance between the convergence speed and the accuracy of the model.
In this work, the standard GAN model is not used and significant changes are made to its structure. In the standard model, the Generator typically uses a De-convolutional Network to generate images, and the Discriminator, with a conventional Convolutional Network, distinguishes real images from fake ones. However, in this study, the Generator is enhanced with a Mamdani-type Fuzzy Logic System that applies fuzzy rules based on the thermal and visual features of the images instead of directly generating pixels. This modification allows the Generator to integrate complementary information from infrared and visible images with intelligent weighting, which is optimized by HHO. The Discriminator is also integrated with a Support Vector Machine (SVM) trained using Frechet Inception Distance (FID) instead of using a simple deep network. This change increases the recognition accuracy in heterogeneous conditions (such as light changes), as SVM is particularly effective for dual classification (real/fake). In addition, the middle layers of the GAN have been replaced with adaptive filters that are tuned by HHO, which improves the flexibility of the model to diverse input data. Overall, these changes have created a hybrid architecture that goes beyond the standard GAN and is specifically designed for multimodal image fusion.

## Integration of SVM, HHO, and RL and justification of model complexity
The suggested FGAN framework tackles the difficulties of fusing visible and infrared images by combining SVM, HHO, and RL in a synergistic manner. The HHO technique dynamically tunes fuzzy rules and membership functions in the generator, which uses a Mamdani-type fuzzy logic system to conduct pixel-level fusion. Motivated by the collaborative hunting habits of Harris Hawks, HHO targets measures like entropy, PSNR, and SSIM to maximize the parameters that define the fuzzy system (such as membership function parameters and rule consequents). By eliminating the need for manual rule definition, this optimization guarantees adaptive fusion that strikes a balance between thermal and visual features while lowering computational cost in comparison to multi-scale transformation techniques.
Real (input visible and infrared images) and created (fused) images are distinguished by an SVM classifier in the discriminator that was trained using Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). By using parameters that have been tuned by HHO to increase discrimination accuracy, the SVM improves the FGAN’s capacity to generate realistic fused images. The discriminator’s resistance to environmental changes like noise or lighting is increased by the FID measure, which guarantees that it assesses high-level feature distributions.
To improve HHO, Reinforcement Learning (RL) refines the fuzzy rules of the generator during training. With RL, weights are dynamically adjusted using a reward function based on fusion measures (e.g., FID, PSNR, SSIM), which allows the generator to respond to heterogeneous data. HHO serves as a pre-setter, exploring the parameter space, while RL functions as a final adjuster, fine-tuning the original settings. Fast convergence and great adaptability are guaranteed by this dual optimization strategy.
Because various models are combined, the integration of SVM, HHO, and RL adds complexity; however, the notable performance improvements make this worthwhile. By reducing the dependence on intricate deep neural architectures, the fuzzy logic system simplifies the generator in comparison to conventional GANs. The population-based optimization used by HHO provides effective parameter adjustment without incurring undue processing costs by avoiding local optima traps. For binary classification (actual vs. fake photos), the SVM-based discriminator is computationally light and incredibly effective with just two adjusted parameters. RL improves adaptability to a variety of picture properties, which is essential for uses such as medical diagnostics and surveillance.
The trained fuzzy system achieves an average fusion time of 30 milliseconds on the TNO dataset, significantly reducing the complexity after training by performing fusion through straightforward operations. The integration of these components is validated by their efficiency and high performance (PSNR up to 55 dB, SSIM up to 0.99), providing a reliable and useful solution for real-time picture fusion applications.

## Criteria for evaluating the efficiency of image fusion methods
Many fusion techniques have been developed, and the topic of information fusion has received a lot of interest in the last few decades. But different fusion techniques have distinct qualities, and the effectiveness of image fusion techniques should be assessed both quantitatively and qualitatively using assessment criteria. A number of criteria have been offered in recent research. Most of these criteria are based on assessments of the quantity of information transferred and features like edges that were present in the original images but not in the new fused image. Several criteria must be used to thoroughly assess various integration techniques because none of them is unquestionably superior to the others. The effectiveness of different integration approaches was evaluated in this study using six quantitative evaluation criteria, including entropy EN, spatial frequency (SF)-based entropy and integration criterion, gradient-based fusion metric QAB/F, and the signal-to-noise ratio and input picture similarity to the SSIM fusion image. The main goal of this innovation is to provide a solution that overcomes the limitations of traditional methods, such as information loss or sensitivity to noise33,34, and is specifically optimized for complex scenarios. ## Adaptability for real-time applications and justification of the criterion selection
When utilizing MATLAB R2019b on a typical PC (Intel Core i5, 2.67 GHz, 3.86 GB RAM), the suggested FGAN model achieves an average convergence time of 30 ms, demonstrating its great potential for real-time applications. This effectiveness results from the post-training fuzzy logic system’s simplicity, which enables pixel-level convergence through straightforward operations without requiring intricate deep neural network inferences at deployment. To adjust the model to real-time situations with fluctuating computational resources, a number of tactics can be employed. HHO optimization can be done offline during training in resource-constrained devices (such embedded systems in security cameras), which lowers the runtime complexity to simple fuzzy assessments. Implementing the model on GPU-accelerated hardware (such as NVIDIA CUDA-enabled systems) can further decrease convergence time for improved performance. By parallelizing the SVM-based and fuzzy discriminant computations, it lowers latency and may achieve convergence speeds of less than ten milliseconds. Moreover, the model can be scaled to edge devices by approximating the HHO iterations using lighter metaheuristic techniques or by reducing the parameters in the SVM (lowering weights while preserving FID accuracy). Full HHO-RL convergence can be maintained for dynamic retraining in resource-intensive situations, including cloud-based medical diagnosis platforms, guaranteeing adaptation to new data streams without sacrificing performance. Because of these modifications, FGAN is ideally suited for real-time applications where low latency is essential, including autonomous navigation or live surveillance.

## Reasons for choosing FID over PSNR/SSIM
The primary metric for discriminator training in FGAN is FID, as opposed to more conventional pixel-based metrics like PSNR or SSIM. This is because FID is better at evaluating the distributional similarity and overall perceptual quality of generated and actual images. Because PSNR and SSIM have a weak association with human perception in complicated circumstances, they frequently miss high-level semantic differences or visual realism in the outputs generated by GANs. PSNR examines the signal-to-noise ratio at the pixel level, whereas SSIM evaluates structure similarity. In contrast, FID provides a more thorough evaluation of image quality, diversity, and fidelity by computing the Fréchet distance between feature distributions taken from a pre-trained starting network. This is crucial for multi-modal fusion, where maintaining thermal and visual meanings is crucial.
When analyzing fused images from the TNO dataset, for instance, a method that is just optimized for PSNR/SSIM may yield outputs with excellent pixel accuracy but artifacts in thermal regions (such as excessively smooth edges in low-light settings), creating strange images. The PSNR-optimized fusion obtained a score of about 30 dB in a comparative trial (inspired by experiments in conditional GANs like MUNIT against pixel-by-pixel). However, it was unable to identify small distributional changes in feature space, resulting in FID values above 50 (showing poor realism). FID-guided training in FGAN, on the other hand, decreased this score to less than 20, guaranteeing more perceptually coherent pictures while preserving competitive SSIM (around 0.92) and PSNR (about 32.5 dB). The benefit of FID in GAN situations is demonstrated by this example, which improves robustness to environmental changes like noise or lighting changes and more closely matches human visual evaluation.

## Conclusion
This study presents a GAN-based approach for fusion of visible and infrared images. The approach is based on fuzzy reinforcement learning and support vector machines, which are improved with the Harris Hawkes Optimization (HHO) algorithm. This technique adds highlights of infrared images while preserving the features of visible images, thus solving the problems of blurred edges and low resolution of conventional fusion approaches. The fusion system consists of two components. In the generator section, a fuzzy logic-based reinforcement learning system is used for the initial fusion of visible and infrared images. The fusion processes are performed on each pixel of the input images, respectively. Then, the discriminator section calculates an optimal separation using an SVM-based technique, as well as the FID similarity measure and other critical fusion measures for the input real images and the generated image output from the generator. The SVM section is used to create the maximum possible discrimination for adversarial treatment of the fusion results in the generator section. The HHO algorithm is used to train both components in order to compare them and create a final fusion image that has more details and contour information for thermal applications. Here, it can enhance the texture information of the visible and infrared composite image details, thereby maintaining high contrast and successfully reducing noise and artifacts. Numerous qualitative and quantitative experiments show that the use of a fuzzy competitive generative network (FGAN) optimized with the Harris Hawk optimization (HHO) algorithm in fusion of infrared and visible images has produced significant improvements in visual quality and performance metrics such as PSNR, SSIM, and entropy. However, there are limitations such as the dependence on the data quality of the TNO dataset, the computational complexity caused by combining multiple models (fuzzy, SVM, and HHO), and the need for initial manual tuning of parameters, which may be challenging on a larger scale. In future work, it is suggested to use more advanced crowd intelligence algorithms to reduce computation time, collect more diverse data from real environments, and explore the possibility of more complete automation of the system with deep learning to increase its flexibility and efficiency.
