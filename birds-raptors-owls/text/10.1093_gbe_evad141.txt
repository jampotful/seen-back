Chromosomal-level Genome Assembly of the Coffee Bee Hawk Moth Reveals the Evolution of Chromosomes and the Molecular Basis of Distinct Phenotypes
Takahiro Yamabe, Rei Kajitani, Atsushi Toyoda, Takehiko Itoh (2023). Genome Biology and Evolution. http://dx.doi.org/10.1093/gbe/evad141. doi:10.1093/gbe/evad141. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Chromosomal-level Genome Assembly of the Coffee Bee Hawk Moth Reveals the Evolution of Chromosomes and the Molecular Basis of Distinct Phenotypes

## Abstract
Abstract Cephonodes hylas, the coffee bee hawk moth is a hawk moth species with unique characteristics, such as larvae feeding on gardenia, overcoming the toxicity of its iridoid glycosides, diurnal adults, and transparent wings. Although C. hylas is a fascinating model for molecular biological research, genome sequence analysis-based genetic approaches to elucidate these peculiarities have not yet been undertaken. We successfully achieved de novo genome assembly at the chromosome level of C. hylas comparable to the Lepidoptera model organism, silkworm. Additionally, 16,854 protein-coding genes were annotated, and the constructed genome sequence and annotated genes were of the highest quality BUSCO completion compared to closely related species. Comparative genome analysis revealed the process of chromosomal evolution from the Bombycoidea ancestral (n = 31) genome and changes in turnover at the chromosome level associated with chromosomal fusion events, such as the rate of repetitive sequence insertion. These analyses were only possible because the genome was constructed at the chromosome level. Additionally, increased the nonsynonymous/synonymous rate (dN/dS) ratios were observed in multiple photoreceptor-related genes that were strongly associated with the acquisition of diurnal activity. Furthermore, tandemly duplicated expanded genes containing many digestive and other enzymes and larval midgut-specific expression were also confirmed. These genes may be involved in the metabolism of genipin, a toxin found in gardenias. Using the genome sequence of C. hylas determined at the chromosome level, we have successfully identified new insights into the chromosomal evolution of Bombycoidea, as well as the relationship between the genome sequence and its characteristic traits.

## Significance
The prior investigations have demonstrated the occurrences of chromosomal scale alterations like chromosome fusions in butterfly genomes. Despite their potential impact on the evolution of various traits of both pollinators and pests, comprehensive comparative analyses of hawk moth genomes have been lacking. In this study, we assembled a chromosomal-level genome sequence of C. hylas. Subsequent comparative genomic analysis of the obtained genome revealed a molecular basis linked to distinct phenotypes, such as gardenia- and coffee-eating larvae, diurnal adults, and possessing a different chromosome structure from two closely related model organisms. This genome will be a valuable resource for examining chromosome evolution not only within the Sphingidae family but also across the Lepidoptera order.

## Introduction
Sphingidae is a family of moths with large and thick-bodied members of Bombycoidea. They are widely used as model organisms in physiology and behavior because of their high speed, hovering, and other excellent flight abilities. Owing to these peculiarities and their practical value, the genomes have been sequenced in several Sphingidae species (Kanost et al. 2016; Pippel et al. 2020; Hundsdoerfer et al. 2022). Cephonodes hylas, the coffee bee hawk moth is a hawk moth species that has a wide distribution range from Africa to East Asia (GBIF Secretariat 2021). The larvae feed on a variety of Rubiaceae plants, including coffee and gardenia. These larvae are also known as coffee pests in West Africa (Crowe 2004). Gardenias contain iridoid glycosides, which are known to slow the growth of lepidopteran insects and are generally repellent to them (Puttick and Bowers 1988). Cephonodes hylas overcomes the toxicity of iridoid glycosides and is a specialist that feeds on gardenia. Unlike other species, the adults are diurnal, shed wing scales upon hatching, and have transparent wings. Nanosized protrusions line the surface of the transparent wings, suggesting its function as an antireflective device for light (Yoshida et al. 1997, 2021). Although C. hylas is a fascinating model for molecular biological research, genome sequence analysis-based genetic approaches to elucidate these peculiarities have not yet been undertaken.
Chromosome-level genome assembly is an important resource for analyzing events associated with chromosome evolution. In Lepidoptera, the ancestral karyotype is n = 31, although smaller chromosome numbers have been reported in some species (d’Alençon et al. 2010; Hill et al. 2019). Chromosome fusions may occur in these species and change the linkage of genes, thereby facilitating adaptation to the environment or other factors (Guerrero and Kirkpatrick 2014; Liu et al. 2022; Mackintosh et al. 2023). Additionally, changes in chromosome number may lead to reduced fertility among individuals with different chromosome numbers (Lukhtanov et al. 2020), which may suppress recombination (Cicconardi et al. 2021). Therefore, the diversification of chromosome numbers may promote speciation (de Vos et al. 2020). Chromosome fusion events are also common in the superfamily Bombycoidea, which includes the Sphingidae family. However, chromosome-level whole-genome sequence resources are scarce, and possible comparative genomic analyses are limited.
Other examples of events observed as traces of evolutionary adaptations in lepidopteran genome sequences include tandem gene duplications such as in NSP genes (Fischer et al. 2008) as well as duplications of chemoreceptor and pheromone receptor genes (Engsontia et al. 2014) in Pieris rapae. Gene duplications have been observed especially in the genomes of polyphagous species (“generalists”) and are associated with adaptation to lepidopteran staple food plants (Gouin et al. 2017; Chen et al. 2019). In contrast, knowledge of gene duplication in “specialist” species (such as C. hylas) with specialized food plants is scarce.
In this study, we aimed to construct a highly continuous chromosome-level genome using PacBio long-read sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding. We then compared the constructed genome sequence with the genome of closely related species at both chromosome and gene levels, focusing on analyzing chromosome evolution and tandem gene duplications. Our results not only contribute new insights into chromosome evolution and host plant adaptation but also provide a useful genomic resource for future evolutionary and ecological research on Sphingidae species.

## Results and Discussion

## Chromosome-level Genome Assembly of C. Hylas
Based on analysis using GenomeScope 2.0, the genome size and heterozygosity of C. hylas were estimated to be 373.9 Mbp and 1.58%, respectively (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). PacBio and Illumina reads were used to assemble C. hylas genome contigs. The genome sequence was assembled to the chromosome level by scaffolding them with Illumina reads from the Hi-C library, consisting of 371.1 Mb, 93 scaffolds, and N50 = 13.6 Mb (Chylas_v1.0, table 1). The obtained genome size was almost equal to the estimated genome size and, as shown in table 1, was the smallest among Bombycoidea species for which genome sequences have already been determined. The contigs (99.76%) were assembled into 29 scaffolds, as observed from the Hi-C contact map, with 29 pseudochromosome-level genome sequences (fig. 1B, table 1), which was consistent with a previous study (Mohanty and Nayak 1983). In these chromosomes, “TTAGG” telomeric repeats were present in at least one end of 18 chromosomes (fig. 2A, supplementary fig. S3, Supplementary Material online). Assessment of completeness using BUSCO showed the highest completion rate (98.7%) compared to related species, whose genomes have recently been determined (table 1). The duplication rate (0.2%) was the lowest, and k-mer analysis using Merqury (Rhie et al. 2020) showed that the specific k-mers in each haplotype assembly were present as a single copy, suggesting that haplotype-derived duplications were successfully removed (fig. 1A, supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online). The availability of a contiguous and comprehensive genome sequence of C. hylas will provide reliable results for downstream analysis and will be a useful genomic resource for future studies on important pest species in the coffee industry.

## Genome Annotation
Repetitive sequences were annotated for C. hylas genome assembly. Overall, 25.59% of the genome was composed of interspersed repetitive sequences, which was the lowest percentage among related species (table 1). This is probably the reason for the small genome size of C. hylas. The distribution of repetitive sequences per chromosome and within chromosomes was examined (fig. 2, Supplementary figs. 4 and 5, Supplementary Material online). The results showed a trend of higher proportion of repeats toward the ends of the chromosomes (fig. 2A). This trend was similar to previously reported trends for Coleoptera (Dam et al. 2021). A large proportion of repeats in chromosomes 27 and 28, which had short chromosome lengths, were found. A negative correlation was observed between the proportion of repeats and chromosome length (fig. 2B, P-value < 3.1e−6). This difference in the proportions of repeats may be explained by the frequency of repeat insertions in recent years. The Kimura distance from the consensus repeat library showed that a lower Kimura distance was more significant in short chromosomes 27 and 28 (fig. 2C, Wilcoxon test and adjusted for Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05). These chromosomes have a higher proportion of repeats and more recent repeat insertions compared to other chromosomes, indicating that they experienced a more recent transposable element (TE) burst.
Next, genes were annotated for the C. hylas genome, and 16,854 protein-coding genes were predicted. 99.1% completeness was achieved using BUSCO completeness evaluation, which predicted a highly comprehensive gene set (table 1). Homology searches against FlyBase, NCBI nonredundant protein sequences (nr), and Swiss-Prot identified at least one hit in 83.3% (14,031) of the genes, and 82.6% (13,923) of the genes were annotated with at least one domain (supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online). Additionally, 157 rRNAs, 88 snRNAs, 94 miRNAs, and 888 tRNAs were annotated (supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online).

## Analysis of Synteny and Chromosome Evolution
In C. hylas, 29 chromosomes were identified, which was more than that in the closely related species, Manduca sexta and Bombyx mori (n = 28). Since the karyotype of the lepidopteran ancestor is thought to be n = 31 (Ahola et al. 2014), the chromosome-level genome sequence of C. hylas provides useful material for understanding genome evolution in these model species. Therefore, we examined the evidence of chromosome fusion based on synteny analysis of three members of the Bombycoidea superfamily: C. hylas, M. sexta, and B. mori. Based on the number of chromosomes of the three species, all three were considered to have undergone chromosome fusion; therefore, comparisons were performed using outgroup species with ancestral karyotypes based on the parsimony method. In this study, a comparative analysis was performed based on the Ectropis grisescens genome, which was confirmed to have a conserved ancestral karyotype (n = 31).
The results showed different traces of fusion in the three Bombycoidea superfamily (fig. 3A and supplementary fig. S6, Supplementary Material online). Two fusions in C. hylas (E22–E24 and E2–E26), three fusions in M. sexta (E2–E26, E22–E24, and E15–E30), and three fusions in B. mori (E11–E29, E23–E30, and E31–E24) were detected. Two of the fusions in M. sexta were also found in the C. hylas lineage, suggesting that the two chromosome fusion events occurred in a common ancestor, and one fusion event occurred after divergence from the C. hylas lineage (fig. 3B). In contrast, B. mori and the Sphingidae lineage had different chromosome fusions, suggesting that chromosome fusions in the B. mori lineage evolved independently from those in the Sphingidae lineage. The E30 and E24 chromosomes were fused to different chromosomes in each lineage. All of the chromosomes in which fusions occurred were commonly short in length, a trend similar to that of previous studies (Ahola et al. 2014).

## Analysis of the Chromosome Fusion Effect
Next, we focused on the fused chromosomes of C. hylas and M. sexta. Focusing on the three fused chromosomes, two were fused in a common ancestor, and only M28 fusion occurred after the divergence of the two species. Therefore, a comparison of M28 and the corresponding C. hylas chromosomes can reveal the effect of fusion on the genome sequence. Chromosome length increases with chromosome fusion. We observed that differences in the proportion of repeat sequences occur in shorter chromosomes. Therefore, we focused on the repetitive sequences of the C. hylas C27 and fused M. sexta M28S chromosomes, which is the shorter part of M. sexta M28 (M28S) and is orthologous to C27, to investigate differences. Significant differences were observed only in the proportion of repeats of chromosome C27 (fig. 4A, t-test P-value < 0.001), and Kimura distance analysis revealed a higher frequency of insertions of young repeat sequences (fig. 4B). In contrast, M28S showed no significant changes in the proportion of repeats compared to the rest of the genomes (fig. 4A). How did the difference in proportions of repeats occur between C. hylas C27 and the corresponding M. sexta M28S? As discussed in the previous section, short chromosomes have more recent TE bursts (see the “Genome annotation” section). In fact, this trend was observed in the short chromosomes, which did not experience fusion (M01 corresponding to C28 and M02 corresponding to C26) of two species (Supplementary fig. 7, Supplementary Material online). Considering the chromosome length before fusion, it can be hypothesized that the proportion of repeats in the M28S region was also high before fusion. However, no clear difference was observed between M28S and other genomic regions as seen in C27 (fig. 4). This suggests that the common ancestral sequence of M28S-C27 underwent chromosome fusion, resulting in M28S with a lower rate of repeat insertion and C27 with a sustained high rate of repeat insertion. This obvious difference in the proportion of repeats between M28S and C27 may also be because shorter chromosomes have a faster sequence turnover rate (high mutation and/or high deletion). This faster turnover of sequences in shorter chromosomes was inferred by the greater sequence differences in the homologous chromosomes (Supplementary fig. 8A and B, Supplementary Material online) and higher gene substitution rate (dS) (Supplementary fig. 8C and D, Supplementary Material online, see the “dN and dS analysis” section). The fast turnover of sequences can be due to difficulty in detecting old repetitive sequences, resulting in a high proportion of repeats of C03. Thus, chromosome fusion alters sequence turnover and can have a significant impact on evolution. Chromosome fusions have been suggested to play an important role in speciation and changes in nucleotide diversity (de Vos et al. 2020; Cicconardi et al. 2021).

## Larval Midgut Expression Analysis
Larvae of lepidopteran insects metabolize plants in their midgut. Cephonodes hylas feeds on gardenias that contain iridoid glycosides. We identified significantly upregulated genes using midgut RNA-seq data from C. hylas larvae and adults. A total of 13,577 genes were expressed in both samples. Comparison of these genes between the two groups was performed using the likelihood ratio test of DESeq2 to detect genes with significant differences in expression (likelihood ratio test, adjusted P-value <0.001). Significant differences were observed in 1,190 genes. The 545 upregulated genes in the larval group were defined as larvae-expressed genes (LEGs) (supplementary fig. S9, Supplementary Material online). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of LEGs showed that many genes with functions such as peptidase were detected in the molecular function instance, and many metabolic processes were detected in the biological process instance (supplementary table S5, Supplementary Material online). The putative functions of these genes included trypsin, carboxylesterase, glutathione S-transferase, and UDP-glycosyltransferase (supplementary table S6, Supplementary Material online). Trypsin is a serine protease that contributes to the digestion of food plants in all Lepidoptera. Carboxylesterase, glutathione S-transferase, and UDP-glycosyltransferase are detoxifying enzymes. UDP-glycosyltransferase transfers glucuronic acid to substrates to form glucuronide conjugates. The gardenia toxin, genipin is metabolized as a glucuronate conjugate in rat experiments (Ding et al. 2013); and the UGT gene may be important in overcoming genipin toxicity in gardenia; however, further investigation is required to understand this mechanism. These results indicate that several genes in LEG may be associated with larval digestion and detoxification.

## Prediction of Orthologous Gene Family and Gain/Loss of Genes
For phylogenetic inference and gene family analysis, ortholog prediction was performed using amino acid sequences of ten lepidopteran species, and 6,143 single-copy orthologs (orthogroup) were detected. Among the 16,894 genes of C. hylas, except for the 6,078 single-copy orthogroup, 2,571 genes belonged to the orthogroup (N:N:N) shared among 10 species, 4,689 genes were in the orthogroup (N:N:0) shared between two or more species, and 3,516 genes belonged to the Ungroup (fig. 5B). The obtained single-copy genes were used to draw a phylogenetic tree and clades were formed within the families Noctuidae, Bombycidae, and Sphingidae. The topology within the Sphingidae family was the same as that previously described (Kawahara et al. 2009, 2019) (fig. 1C). Diurnal species are highlighted in yellow in figure 1C and their phylogenetic positional relationship strongly suggests that the diurnal nature in the C. hylas lineage was independently acquired. Changes in gene family numbers in each lineage were analyzed based on the results of the phylogenetic tree and orthogroup. The results showed expansion and contraction in the gene families of different species. In C. hylas, 181 expanded families and 330 contracted families were observed, which are the smallest numbers from the nearest common ancestor among Sphingidae (fig. 5A). Unlike other species in the Sphingidae family, C. hylas has higher numbers of species-specific contracted gene families than expanded gene families. This higher rate of gene family contraction has also been observed in the Nymphalidae family, which is considered to have undergone host specialization (Breeschoten et al. 2022). These findings suggest that the gain/loss ratio in gene families may be related to host specificity in the specialist C. hylas.

## Tandem-Duplicated Genes
Examination of the distribution of species-specific expanded genes revealed that many of them formed clusters, suggesting that they may be tandemly adjacent to the genome (supplementary fig. S10, Supplementary Material online). Tandem-duplicated genes are paralogous genes that are chromosomally adjacent to each other, and duplicated genes can acquire novel functions through mutations owing to weaker evolutionary constraints. Hence, duplicated genes may be important in “generalists” adapted to a wide range of edible plants in Lepidoptera (Chen et al. 2019). Although C. hylas is a “specialist” without a wide range of edible plants, we investigated the repertoire of tandem-duplicated genes in its genome.
We detected 2,376 tandem-duplicated genes (supplementary table S7, Supplementary Material online), of which 397 genes were identified as C. hylas-specific expanded tandem-duplicated genes. This represents 54% of the 740 genes comprising the 181 gene family that were expanded in C. hylas.
GO enrichment analysis was performed to further examine the function of C. hylas-specific duplicate genes. Several genes were functional hydrolases (hydrase activity, triglyceride lipase activity), serine proteases (serine-type peptidase activity), and metabolic processes (supplementary fig. S11, Supplementary Material online). Serine proteases account for 95% of Lepidoptera's digestive activity (Srinivasan et al. 2006) and may be involved in the processing of toxic plant proteins. Additionally, 91 of the 397 genes were LEGs, that is, genes expressed in the larval midgut. It has been suggested that specialists, which have specialized food plants, such as C. hylas, may have acquired digestion-related genes through a tandem gene duplication process, although acquired gene repertoires are smaller, that other species do not have. This process of newly acquiring functional genes was similar to those of generalists and may use them to digest food plants and detoxify plant secondary metabolites and other substances. In another example, a C. hylas-specific tandem duplication of cytochrome P450 was identified on chromosome 16 (fig. 6). Cephonodes hylas-specific expansion of CYP4 genes was observed in the phylogenetic analysis (fig. 6A), and these genes were located tandemly on chromosome 16 (fig. 6B). Interestingly, a previous study suggested that cytochrome P450 might contribute to the detoxification of plant defense substances, such as chlorogenic acid, in coffee leaves by coffee berry borers (Vega et al. 2015). Further analysis focusing on tandemly duplicated genes, specifically in C. hylas, will advance our understanding of the traits unique to this species.

## Positively Selected Vision-related Genes
Next, positively selected genes were identified using dN/dS analysis for conserved single-copy genes among species. The results revealed that 258 genes had undergone positive selection (FDR < 0.05). GO enrichment analysis was performed for these genes, and functions, such as visual perception, phototransduction, were enriched (supplementary table S8 and fig. S12, Supplementary Material online). These included the blue and long-wavelength opsin (LW-opsin) genes, which encode photoreceptor proteins (fig. 7). In previous studies, a positive selection of opsin genes was observed between nocturnal and diurnal Lepidoptera species in response to differences in light environments (Feuda et al. 2016). In addition, trp and trpl genes, which are cation channels involved in phototransduction (Montell and Rubin 1989) showed a significant increase in dN/dS and are differentially expressed in butterflies and moths (Macias-Muñoz et al. 2019) (fig. 7).

A significant increase in ω was observed in the blue opsin, long-wavelength opsin, trp, and trpl genes.
Next, for each opsin gene, the loci that underwent positive selection were examined (see the “Detection of genes and sites undergoing positive selection” section). Half of the observed positive selected mutations were located in the transmembrane domain. In LW-opsin, a mutation (312 M) was identified near the retinal binding site (321–337) that underwent positive selection (supplementary fig. S13, Supplementary Material online). Opsin binds to retinal to form rhodopsin, and its absorption spectrum changes depending on the amino acids near the binding site. Therefore, mutations in amino acids may alter the absorption spectrum. Another positive selected mutation (194 M) was identified in LW-opsin. Furthermore, the 194 M mutation was a common mutation to Papillio xuthus in a distant lineage of the same diurnal trait. In contrast, comparisons with additional species did not confirm that this mutation is common in diurnal traits (supplementary fig. S14, Supplementary Material online, see the “Interspecies comparison of LW-opsins” section), suggesting that different genetic mutations may have independently arisen in each species to control diurnal activity rather than a shared ancestral mechanism.

## Analysis of the Molecular Basis for the Formation of Transparent Wings
A characteristic phenotype of C. hylas is the formation of transparent wings. C. hylas has scales at the time of hatching, but these scales are shed during hatching as they are no longer required. Therefore, the analysis focused on whether the genes that form scales were modified. Comparing the number of cuticle genes with those of closely related species, C. hylas had the lowest number of cuticle proteins among the three Sphingidae species, with a significant reduction in genes classified as CP-RR2 (supplementary table S9, Supplementary Material online). Two orthogroup gene families that were contracted in C. hylas using Café analysis belonged to CP-RR2 (FamilyID:103, 424). Interestingly, the B. mori homologous gene for one of the contracted gene families was identified as a protein that forms silkworm scales in a previous study (Liu et al. 2021). This may be related to the fact that adult C. hylas does not require scales, but further analysis is required to explore other possibilities.

## Conclusion
This study successfully constructed a novel high-quality C. hylas genome sequence. The obtained genome sequence was fully connected to the chromosome level, and its accuracy, based on BUSCO-based evaluation, was the highest in the lepidopteran genome compared to previous studies. Comparative genomic analysis of the obtained genome sequences also suggests evidence for a potential molecular basis associated with unique phenotypes, such as gardenia- and coffee-eating larvae and diurnal adults; however, further investigations are necessary to fully understand its significance. The high-quality genome sequence of C. hylas, which has a different chromosome structure from two closely related model organisms, whose genomes have already been determined, will be an important resource for investigating chromosome evolution not only in Sphingidae but also in the entire Lepidoptera order. Although this genome analysis alone does not provide insight into the mechanism of the fascinating phenotype of C. hylas, we expect that further experiments and future analyses based on genome analysis of multiple individuals will help us to unravel these mysteries.

## Materials and Methods

## Genome and RNA Sequencing
Cephonodes hylas (supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online) larvae were captured in Tokyo, Japan. The larvae were fed wild gardenia leaves, and genomic DNA and RNA were extracted from hatched male adults. RNA was extracted from the midguts of other adults and larvae with three biological replicates per group (n = 3). Genomic DNA was extracted using Genomic-tip 100/G (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) with a slight modification of the manufacturer protocol (vortexing was not performed to prevent DNA fragmentation). Isolated DNA was quantified with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Total RNA and genomic DNA were extracted from five tissues (leg, wing, midgut, testis, and fat body) of the same sample and the midguts of other adults and larvae. RNA was extracted using the RNeasy protect mini kit (QIAGEN) according to manufacturer protocol. The isolated RNA was quantified using NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
For long reads, the SMRTbell library was constructed using the Sequel© sequencing kit (version 3.0) for the PacBio Sequel II (Menlo Park, CA, USA) sequencer using the standard protocol. One SMRT cell was processed, and approximately 192.8 Gbps subreads (8.7 M subreads with N50 = 35.7 Kbps) were obtained (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). For short reads, an Illumina paired-end library with an average insert size of 600 bp was generated using an Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-free library prep kit (San Diego, CA, USA). A total of 58.2 Gbps with 250 bp paired-end sequences were obtained using HiSeq 2500 (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). Arima Hi-C library was constructed using manufacturer protocol from the same individual male adult muscle. A total of 17.2 Gbps of 150 bp paired-end Illumina reads was sequenced using the HiSeq2500 platform (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online).

## Estimation of Genome Size and De Novo Assembly
Raw Illumina data were trimmed and filtered using Platanus_trim, and half of the trimmed data (27.5 Gb, 73.8x) were used for k-mer analysis and estimation of genome size (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). The 32-mer frequencies were calculated using Jellyfish v2.3.0 (Marçais and Kingsford 2011), and genome size was estimated using GenomeScope 2.0 (Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020). The estimated genome size was used as an input parameter for the de novo assembly. Canu v2.1.1 (Koren et al. 2017) was used to assemble the C. hylas nuclear genome, with PacBio reads as input. The following options were used in the Canu assembly to construct both allele sequences of the diploid genome as distinctly as possible (genomeSize = 380 month, corOutCoverage = 200, correctedErrorRate = 0.035, “batOptions = -dg 3 -db 3 -dr 1 -ca 500 -cp 50”). The low-read-depth regions at the contig end contained in the resulting Canu assembly contigs were trimmed using a custom script. The Illumina reads were mapped to diploid assembly contigs using BWA v0.7.17-r1188 (Li 2013), and Canu contigs were polished three times using Pilon v1.24 (Walker et al. 2014). Haplotigs were then removed using Purge_dups v1.2.5 (Guan et al. 2020) to reduce sequence redundancy due to the separate construction of both haplotypes. Finally, contigs considered as artifacts with extremely high GC content (> 99%, < 1%) and fragmented mitochondrial sequences were removed to obtain the final contig assembly (Chylas_v0.5) for the nuclear genome. We assumed that mitochondrial genome sequences would have sufficiently high sequence coverage; therefore, mitochondrial genome sequences were constructed using GetOrganelle (Jin et al. 2020) with a subset of all reads.
The Hi-C data were used to scaffold the contigs (Chylas_v0.5) up to the chromosome-level assembly (Chlas_v1.0). Hi-C reads were aligned to the Chylas_v0.5 assembly using Juicer v1.6 (Durand et al. 2016), and scaffolding was performed on the contigs to achieve the chromosome-level assembly using 3D-DNA pipeline version 180922 (Dudchenko et al. 2017). Juicebox assembly tools were used to visualize the Hi-C heatmap, and the assembly errors were manually corrected. BLASTN homology search against the NCBI nr database was used to identify contaminant sequences, and sequences that did not hit arthropods were removed. Finally, we obtained a chromosomal-level assembly of C. hylas (Chylas_v1.0). Merqury v1.3 (Rhie et al. 2020) was used to evaluate genome assemblies before and after the purge with k-mers. For this analysis, we applied k = 19 to construct the meryl db from Illumina reads. The completeness of the genome assembly was evaluated using BUSCO v5.0.0 (Manni et al. 2021) with the lepidoptera_odb10 database.

## Genome Annotation
A custom repeat library was created to identify repetitive sequences in the C. hylas genome. Specifically, de novo repeat libraries were constructed from whole-genome sequences using RepeatModeler v2.0 (Flynn et al. 2020) and the LTR structure discovery pipeline. Then, the C. hylas genome was masked using RepeatMasker v4.0.9 (Smit et al. 2015) with a custom repeat library. The Kimura distance from the consensus library was estimated using the RepeatMasker utility script calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl. The annotated repetitive sequences were summarized and plotted using Matplotlib and Circos (Krzywinski et al. 2009) and the RIdeogram (Hao et al. 2020). In the statistical tests, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995) was used to control the FDR as adjust the P-values. Telomeric repeats were identified using tidk v0.2.31 (https://github.com/tolkit/telomeric-identifier). We search “TTAGG” sequences in the ends of the C. hylas chromosomes.
Protein-coding genes were predicted using the combined results of homology-based, RNA-seq-based, and ab initio-based prediction methods. Homology-based predictions were performed using the following amino acid sequences: Drosophila melanogaster, Tribolium castaneum, Helicoverpa armigera, Spodoptera litura, Apis mellifera, Bombyx mandarina, Acyrthosiphon pisum, M. sexta, B. mori, and Plutella xylostella. The sequences were downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database and spliced-mapped to the genome using Spaln v2.3.3 (Gotoh, 2008). For RNA-seq-based prediction, both the de novo assembly and the genome-guided assembly pipelines were used. For the de novo assembly pipeline, filtered RNA-seq reads were assembled using Trinity v2.8.4 (Grabherr et al. 2011) and Oases v0.2.09 (Schulz et al. 2012). The redundant assembled RNA contigs were removed using CD-HIT version 4.8.1(Fu et al. 2012) and then spliced-mapped to the genome sequences using GMAP v.2018-07-04 (Wu and Watanabe 2005). For the genome-guided assembly pipeline, filtered RNA-seq data were mapped to genome sequences using HISAT2 v2.1.0 (Kim et al. 2019) and the transcripts were assembled using StringTie v2.0 (Kovaka et al. 2019) based on the mapping results. The open reading frame (ORF) regions were estimated using TransDecorder v5.5.0 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder) from both de novo assembly pipeline and genome-guided assembly pipeline results. For ab initio-based predictions, AUGUSTUS v3.3.2 (Stanke and Waack 2003) and SNAP v2006-07-28 (Korf 2004) were used. For both programs, gene models were trained from 1,000 genes predicted using the RNA-seq mapping-first methods. Finally, all predicted gene candidates were merged using GINGER pipeline (https://github.com/i10labtitech/GINGER), and the final protein-coding gene set was obtained. The completeness of the annotated genes was evaluated using BUSCO v5.0.0 (protein mode) with the lepidoptera_odb10 database. ncRNAs were predicted by searching against the Rfam_Release 14.6 database (Kalvari et al. 2021) using INFERNAL 1.1.3 (Nawrocki and Eddy 2013) (e-value < 1e−5). tRNAs were predicted using the tRNAscan-SE 2.0.7 (Chan et al. 2021). To exclude short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) from tRNA results, we performed the following: 1) the genome sequence in which SINE was masked was used as input with tRNAscan-SE; and 2) we filtered the results using EukHighConfidenceFilter in the tRNAscan-SE to obtain only the high confidence set. The functions of protein-coding genes were predicted using homology searching against FlyBase (FB2021_01) (Larkin et al. 2021) and Swiss-Prot databases (UniProt Consortium 2021) using BLASTP (Camacho et al. 2009) (e-value ≤ 1e−5), and NCBI nr database using DIAMOND (Buchfink et al. 2021) (e-value ≤ 1e−5). Protein domains and GO terms were identified using InterProScan v5.51-85.0 (Jones et al. 2014).

## Synteny Analysis
Synteny regions were estimated from orthologous gene sets determined from the results of BLASTP search for the protein sequences of each species using MCScanX (Wang et al. 2012). This analysis was performed to compare E. grisescens (GCA_017562165.1), which has 31 chromosomes with a presumed ancestral type of Lepidoptera, and C. hylas, M. sexta, and B. mori. Ectropis grisescens was also compared with P. xylostella (GCA_019096205), which has a different lineage from E. grisescens and maintains its ancestral karyotype (n = 31). The protein-coding gene sequence of E. grisescens was predicted using MetaEuk_v4.a0f584d (Levy Karin et al. 2020) from the protein sequence of C. hylas. The sequences were downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database. The relationship between the estimated synteny regions was plotted using Circos and Rideogram.

## TE Density Analysis
To investigate the effect of chromosomal fusion on TE activities, we compared the distribution of TE density and Kimura distance for the fused chromosome of M. sexta and the corresponding C. hylas chromosome with other genomic regions. We conducted the analysis following the methods of Höök et al. (2023). First, we randomly selected a 100 kb window from the genome excluding the target sequence and generated repeat densities by performing 100,000 rounds of random sampling with replacement. We also measured the repeat densities of the focus chromosomes (C27 and M28S) in 100 kb windows. Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed t-tests. The distributions of Kimura distance were also generated by performing 100,000 rounds of random sampling with replacement. These analyses were also performed on the corresponding short chromosomes of the two species.

## Sequence Differences Between Homologous Chromosomes
Homologous chromosome pairs were detected by aligning the haplotigs removed in the purge_dups process with the assembled genome sequence using minimap_v2.17-r941 (Li 2018). Identity between homologous chromosomes was calculated (match length/alignment length) from the minimap2 results paf file.

## dN, dS Analysis
The dN and dS values of the genes were calculated by comparing them with the homolog of Hyles vespertilio. Protein-coding genes of H. vespertilio were annotated using the same method as for C. hylas annotation, and the C. hylas–H. vespertilio homolog gene pairs were estimated using Sonicparanoid v1.3.5 (Cosentino and Iwasaki 2019). To ensure gene completeness, we only used those genes that had the full-length ORF from the start codon to the stop codon intact and did not have any in-frame stop codons. Multiple alignments of each gene were created using MAFFT v7.475 (Katoh and Standley 2013) after removing the difficult alignment regions using trimAl v1.4. rev15 (-nogap-backtrans) (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009), and then pairwise dN/dS was calculated using the codeml program (runmode = −2), in the PAML 4.9j packages (Yang 2007).

## Expression Analysis
A two-group comparison of expression levels based on RNA-seq was performed between the groups of adult and larval midgut samples (n = 3 each). First, each post-trimmed RNA-seq read was mapped to its genome sequence using STAR v. 2.7.8a (Minh et al. 2020). Read counts of each gene were calculated using RSEM v1.3.3 (Li and Dewey 2011). Based on the expression counts of each sample, differential expression analysis was performed using the likelihood ratio test of DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014). Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.001 and log2 fold change ≥ 1 were designated as LEGs. GO enrichment analysis was performed for the LEGs and all genes using topGO (Alexa and Rahnenfuhrer 2020).

## Orthologous Gene Analysis Between Lepidopteran Species
First, protein-coding gene sequences of 10 lepidopteran species (P. xylostella, P. xuthus, Galleria mellonella, H. armigera, S. litura, B. mori, B. mandarina, M. sexta, H. vespertilio, and C. hylas) were collected. All data were downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database, except for C. hylas and H. vespertilio, which were annotated in the present study. Orthologous gene groups (orthogroups) were predicted using Sonicparanoid v1.3.5. Multiple alignments for each orthogroup were created using MAFFT v7.475 after removing difficult alignment regions using trimAl v1.4. rev15 (nogap-backtrans).
A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was generated using the IQ-TREE v. 2.1.4-beta with the amino acid alignments obtained after trimming. Additionally, the phylogenetic tree was converted to a timescale using the mcmctree program in the PAML 4.9j package. Fossil information and branching ages from previous studies were used as calibration points. Specifically, Mioclanis shanwangiana was set to 15.9 Mya as the lower constraint for branching in the family Sphingidae (Zhang et al. 1994) and Praepapilio colorado was set to 40.4 Mya as the lower constraint for branching in the family Papilionidae (Durden and Rose 1978). The upper constraint was set at 127.3 Mya for the divergence of Yponomeutoidea and Obtectomera, based on previous studies (Kawahara et al. 2019). Finally, Café v5 (Mendes et al. 2020) was used to analyze expansion/contraction in gene family size based on phylogenetic trees and orthogroup combinations.

## Tandem-Duplicated Gene Search
BLASTP homology searches were performed on the gene sequences of C. hylas to identify tandem-duplicated genes, defined as paralogs that are adjacent to each other in the genome. Next, the “Duplicate_gene_classifier” script in MCScanX was used to identify tandem-duplicated genes based on gene annotation and homology analysis results.

## Detection of Genes and Sites Undergoing Positive Selection
The dN/dS values for each gene and site undergoing positive selection were calculated from the multiple alignments and gene tree obtained in the previous section. The sequences were filtered based on the completeness of the entire coding regions, from the start codon to the stop codon. The codeml program with branch models in the PAML 4.9j package was used for calculations. From the results and the null model (which assumes no positive selection), likelihood ratios were calculated and multiple testing corrections were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (FDR < 0.1). Next, to identify the site where the selection was applied, we conducted further analysis using the branch-site model in the PAML package. In the null branch-site model, dN/dS value was fixed at ω = 1. However, the gene of interest (opsin) in this study was highly conserved (ω < 0.5), and no significant positive selection was detected in the branch-site model. Therefore, based on a previous study (Sondhi et al. 2021), the branch-site model was validated using a hyphy program with the Contrast-FEL model (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020). The Contrast-FEL model also detects differences in dN/dS at each site among different lineages but is designed to be more sensitive in detecting positive selection. In this study, the Contrast-FEL model was run on opsin genes, with the C. hylas lineage specified as the foreground. The amino acid sites supported by the branch-site model and the contrast-FEL model were designated as sites undergoing positive selection.

## Interspecies Comparison of LW-opsins
To investigate the commonalities in amino acid mutations of LW-opsin in diurnal species, we added more species for comparison. We obtained transcriptome assemblies of four lepidopteran species that have confirmed LW-opsin from a previous study (Kawahara et al. 2019): Hemileuca maia (Sphingidae), Lymantria dispar (Erebidae), Leucoptera coffeella (Lyonetiidae), and Macrosoma sp. (Hedylidae). We followed the trait of being diurnal or nocturnal described in previous research (Sondhi et al. 2021). TBLASTN searches were performed to detect candidate contigs that encode LW-opsin in transcriptome assemblies, using LW-opsins used in the selection analysis as queries. We then predicted the longest ORF of each candidate contig using TransDecoder (-m 90) to obtain the LW-opsin sequence. We applied the same method described above (see the “Orthologous gene analysis between lepidopteran species” section) to construct a phylogenetic tree of these species using ORFs estimated from transcriptome assemblies.

## Exploring the Molecular Basis for Transparent Wing Formation
Two approaches were used to identify the cuticle protein. First, genes that hit two domains related to cuticle proteins (IPR000618 and IPR033778) were extracted from the functional annotation results using InterProScan. Next, CutProtFam-Pred was used for further classification of the cuticle proteins. This series of analyses was performed for B. mori, M. sexta, and H. vesertilio, in addition to C. hylas, and the results were combined with orthologous gene analysis results to examine expansion/contraction in C. hylas.
