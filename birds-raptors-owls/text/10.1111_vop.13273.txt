Intraocular osseous metaplasia in an older red‐tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis) 24 years following traumatic injury to the globe

# Intraocular osseous metaplasia in an older red‐tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis) 24 years following traumatic injury to the globe

## Abstract
AbstractAn approximately 26‐year‐old, suspected female, red‐tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis) was presented with a history of chronic ocular changes of the left eye likely secondary to blunt trauma resulting in blindness and phthisis bulbi. Ophthalmic evaluation of OS revealed a large intraocular mass, 3+ aqueous flare indicating anterior uveitis, and severely decreased intraocular pressure measuring 5 mmHg. Ocular ultrasound revealed an abnormal, spherical shaped and hyperechoic lens, as well as hyperechoic material or a mass lesion in the posterior segment. An enucleation procedure was performed, and histopathology of the eye revealed no signs of neoplasia but instead intraocular osseous metaplasia with bone marrow and adipose tissue replacing the lens. Similar mass lesions were found in the retina and choroid. Multiple fractures in the scleral ossicle and cartilage correlated the intraocular lesions with a blunt traumatic event. A bioresorbable disk of porcine small intestine submucosa (BioSIS Vetrix®) was incorporated to relieve tension during closure. The graft sloughed during healing process, revealing a healed wound underneath. The BioSIS was not incorporated into the wound as designed but may have facilitated healing. This is the first report of intraocular osseous metaplasia in a raptor and the use of BioSIS Vetrix® with enucleation in an avian species.Keywords: calcification, intraocular mass, neoplasia, raptor, trauma, uveitis

## INTRODUCTION
The term osseous metaplasia, osseous choristoma, and heterotopic bone formation have all been used interchangeably in the literature to describe bone formation in abnormal locations. 1 , 2 , 3 For this report, the term osseous metaplasia will be used. The mechanism for this disease process is still unknown, with existing hypotheses implicating trauma, infection, or inflammation. 4 Depending on the species, other hypotheses have been suggested, such as increased levels of ascorbic acid in the aqueous humor in guinea pigs and rabbits. 3 , 4 Osseous metaplasia in the eye and associated tissues has been described in humans, dogs, horses, rabbits, guinea pigs, and chickens. The disease process has been associated with the uveal tract in humans, dogs, rabbits, and guinea pigs. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Osseous metaplasia has also been found originating from the lens in humans. 8
This case report describes a case of intraocular osseous metaplasia in an older red‐tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis). The formation of intraocular osseous metaplasia was correlated with a traumatic event that had happened 24 years prior to the diagnosis.

## CASE REPORT
An approximately 26‐year‐old, 1.2 kg, suspected female, red‐tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis) was presented for enucleation of the left eye (OS) due to chronic changes secondary to trauma. The bird had been part of an outreach program since November 1999, and was deemed non‐releasable due to a proximal right shoulder injury and visual deficits of OS due to trauma. Over time, OS had become phthisical and at the most recent ophthalmic examination performed by unmasked indicated that OS was blind. Vision status can be challenging to evaluate in raptors, but OS was suspected to be blind due to lack of responses to motion stimuli on the left side as well as a negative menace response and dazzle reflex, even though these neuro‐ophthalmic findings normally are absent in avians. The pupil OS could not easily be evaluated due to corneal opacity but seemed to be miotic, dyscoric, and unresponsive to light. The cornea had diffuse corneal opacity with multifocal pinpoint areas of white crystalline infiltrate. A large intraocular mass and 3+ aqueous flare indicating anterior uveitis were appreciated. Further assessment of the mass, lens, and fundus was not possible due to corneal opacity OS (Figure 1). The right eye (OD) had age‐related changes, including nuclear sclerosis of the lens and an incipient cataract, but was otherwise normal and visual (Figure 1). Tonometry (Canine setting; TonoVet; Icare® Finland Oy, Espoo, Finland) of OS revealed a severely decreased intraocular pressure (IOP) of 5 mmHg and an elevated IOP OD of 44 mmHg (reference range 19.8 ± 4.9 mmHg). 9 The elevated IOP OD was suspected to be secondary to stress and restraint considering the otherwise normal ophthalmic examination findings; however, the decreased IOP OS was believed to be secondary to underlying pathology. Serial complete blood cell counts had changes consistent with inflammation. An increased H:L ratio (heterophils 5.8 × 103/μL, reference interval 35 ± 11%; lymphocytes 0.2 × 103/μL, reference interval 44 ± 9%), and a monocytosis (1.2 × 103/μL, reference interval 6 ± 3%) were found on initial bloodwork and due to these changes, ceftiofur crystalline free acid (20 mg/kg IM every 3 days for three treatments; Excede® Zoetis Inc, Parsippany, NJ, US) was initiated. 9 Three weeks later, an improved H:L ratio (heterophils 2.8 × 103/μL; lymphocytes 0.7 × 103/μL), but worsening monocytosis (1.7 × 103/μL) were appreciated. Given the bloodwork results, concerns for the bird's quality of life, and the possibility for intraocular neoplasia, it was decided to perform enucleation with histopathology OS.

Prior to surgery, it was decided to perform survey radiographs and an ocular ultrasound. On presurgical physical examination, the hawk was in ideal body condition (5 on a 9 scale) and had palpable bony changes within the right shoulder consistent with a previous injury. The bird was premedicated with midazolam (1 mg/kg IM; Akorn Pharmaceuticals, Lake Forest IL, USA), hydromorphone (0.3 mg/kg IM; Hospira Inc, Lake Forest IL, USA), and atropine (0.02 mg/kg IM; MWI, Boise ID, USA), and induced with isoflurane (3%–4%; Dechra Pharmaceuticals, Northwich, UK) in 100% oxygen via facemask, intubated with a 3.5‐mm uncuffed Cole endotracheal tube (JorVet® Jorgensen Laboratories, LLC, Loveland CO, USA) and maintained on 1.5%–1.75% isoflurane for the duration of the procedure. An intravenous catheter was placed in the left basilic vein and crystalloid fluids (10 mL/kg/h IV lactated Ringer's solution, Abbott Labs, Abbott Park IL, USA) were administered.

## Imaging
Survey radiographs revealed a chronic, caudally displaced, and misshapen right coracoid consistent with previous trauma, and moderate chronic degenerative changes to the caudal thoracic vertebrae. The remainder of the radiographs were unremarkable. A presurgical ocular ultrasound was performed of both eyes (OU), which revealed an abnormal, spherically shaped and hyperechoic lens, as well as hyperechoic material or a mass‐like lesion within the posterior segment OS. A focal area of hyperechoic material with hyperreflective foci could be seen within the posterior segment of OD. This finding was probably vitreal degeneration or asteroid hyalosis, an age‐related degeneration process consisting of calcium and phospholipid complexes within the vitreous (Figure 2). 10

## Surgery
The hawk was placed in right lateral recumbency, and the feathers were plucked around the eye before the area was aseptically prepared. In order to keep the globe intact for histopathologic examination, a subconjunctival enucleation approach was performed as described by Spitznagel and colleagues instead of a traditional evisceration procedure. 11 This approach allowed the globe to be removed in toto. An absorbable purified porcine gelatin sponge (Gelfoam®; Pfizer Inc, New York, NY, USA) was placed in the orbit for hemostasis, and the remaining conjunctival tissue was removed using sharp dissection with Westcott tenotomy scissors. The superior and inferior eyelid margins were removed using tenotomy scissors. Due to inadvertent excessive removal of palpebral tissues, apposition and closure of palpebral margins was not possible. Instead, the defect was closed using a 15‐mm bioresorbable disk of porcine small intestine submucosa (BioSIS Vetrix®; Cumming, GA, USA) that was secured to the wound edge of the eyelid skin with braided absorbable suture (6‐0 Polysorb; Medtronic PLC, Minneapolis, MN, USA) in a continuous suture pattern (Figure 3).

(D) The surgical area was healed in 10 weeks.

## Follow‐up
Meloxicam (1 mg/kg SC; OstiLoxTM, VetOne® Boehringer Ingelheim, Ridgefield, CT, USA) was administered perioperatively and recovery from anesthesia was uneventful. The bird was discharged later that day with meloxicam (1.5 mg/kg PO q 24 h × 5 days), gabapentin (15 mg/kg PO q 24 h × 10 days; Pfizer; New York, NY, USA), and the ceftiofur crystalline free acid, as previously described. The surgical site healed without complications and a healthy granulation bed was noted under the desiccated porcine small intestinal submucosa graft at 8 weeks. This wound resolved without complications, and the surgical area was completely healed after 10 weeks post‐surgery (Figure 3).

## Histopathology
The removed globe was submitted for histopathology at Comparative Ocular Pathology Laboratory of Wisconsin. The globe was decalcified prior to histopathologic sectioning. The lens capsule was multifocally ruptured and distorted, and the lens fibers were completely replaced by a large mass of anastomosing trabeculae of woven and lamellar bone surrounding adipose tissue, with multifocal clusters of hematopoietic precursor cells. The pecten was attached to the posterior aspect of the lens blending with the bony mass. The retina was extensively detached, atrophied, and segmentally expanded by similar osseous metaplasia with bone marrow and adipose formation, which multifocally extended into the adjacent choroid. Additionally, there were multifocally areas of retinal lenticular metaplasia characterized by degenerate lens fibers (bladder cells and Morgagnian globules) replacing the retinal tissue. There was a scleral ossicle fracture with prolapse of the adjacent uveal tissue. The mid‐posterior scleral cartilage was multifocally undulant and fractured with fibrosis of the overlying choroid and osseous metaplasia of the tissue between the fractured scleral ends. The optic nerve was within normal limits. A periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) histochemical stain was performed to highlight the remaining lens capsule around the bony mass and Descemet's membrane lesions (Figure 4).

A final diagnosis of intraocular osseous metaplasia with bone marrow and adipose tissue replacing the lens was made. Similar mass‐like lesions were found in the retina and choroid. This lesion, combined with multiple fractures in the scleral ossicle and cartilage, cyclodialysis, lenticular metaplasia in the retina, intraocular and uveal fibrosis, and synechia, as well as breaks in Descemet's membrane, were indicative of a previous blunt traumatic event. 12 , 13

## DISCUSSION
The reported incidence of trauma‐induced ocular morbidity in raptors is up to 90%. 14 Many aspects of the avian eye increase the risk of injury, such as location, size, shape, and rigidity. 14 The avian eye sits narrowly within the orbit, resulting in minimal periocular cushion, and the diameter of the globe often exceeds that of the bony rim, rendering it vulnerable. 14 In some species, protection of the globe originates from the scleral ossicles, which surround the area of the eye that extends beyond the orbit and supraorbital ridge. Fractured scleral ossicles have been described in raptors experiencing head trauma, with the eye eventually becoming phthisical. 15 The hawk in this report was considered nonvisual in the affected eye and was diagnosed with phthisis bulbi.
One proposed mechanism for osseous metaplasia in the uveal tract and retina is secondary delivery of osteoblasts to highly vascularized tissue during inflammation. 5 More recently, osseous metaplasia was linked to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). 7 , 8 This proposed alternative mechanism of action for osseous metaplasia formation is the transdifferentiation of RPE cells, which are known to be multipotential cells with the capacity to differentiate into mesenchymal phenotype cells, including fibroblasts and osteoblasts/osteocytes. The induction of transdifferentiation can be mediated by trauma, inflammation, or anoxia of a detached retina. It is postulated that inflammatory cytokines stimulate RPE cells to release growth factors that stimulate osseous metaplasia. 5 In humans, intraocular osseous metaplasia is a well‐recognized entity most frequently occurring with phthisis bulbi, congenital conditions, or tumors. 8 In chickens, phthisis bulbi has also been connected with osseous metaplasia, secondary to an inherited retinopathy or as a sequela to marked intraocular degeneration. 16 In both humans and chickens, it is thought that chronic inflammation and chronic retinal detachment causes osteoblastic transformation of RPE. 7 , 8 The phthisical eye and a potential chronic retinal detachment in the current case may have led to the formation of osseous metaplasia, especially because it was found expanded within the retina.
Osseous metaplasia in non‐ocular tissue has been described in avian species in relation to trauma and neoplasia. One report described osseous metaplasia in the frontal bones of a gray parrot (Psittacus erithacus), probably secondary to trauma, 17 and another in the soft tissues adjacent to an old fracture caused by gunshot in a golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos). 18 Osseous metaplasia was observed in association with hepatic amyloidosis and myelolipoma in a swan goose (Anser cygnoides), 19 and cutaneous leiomyosarcoma in a budgerigar (Melopsittacus undulatus). 20 Histopathology from the hawk in this case report revealed the scleral ossicle and cartilages were fractured with osseous metaplasia formed between the fractured ends, meaning it could have given rise to the osseous metaplasia seen in this case. Histopathology from the hawk in this case report revealed lesions characteristic of a long‐standing ocular blunt trauma, that along with the clinical history, strongly indicate that the intraocular osseous metaplasia was trauma‐induced. Although the fractured scleral ossicles provide one likely cell source of osseous metaplasia, osseous metaplasia was also identified in multiple noncontiguous ocular tissues in our case, most notably the lens and retina, suggesting that the traumatic event could have induced an osteoblastic transformation of more than one ocular cell type, possibly the lens epithelium and RPE cells, respectively.
A secondary observation from this case report was the use of bioresorbable material to close the surgical wound. Due to tension during closure, a bioresorbable disk of porcine small intestine submucosa disk (BioSIS Vetrix®) was used to close the orbital defect. BioSIS Vetrix® has been used with great success in veterinary ophthalmology as a tectonic support for corneal defects in dogs, cats, and horses. 21 BioSIS supports rapid and robust vascular ingrowth and capillary penetration, confers resistance to infection, and minimizes would contracture. 22 An Acell disk, which is similar to the BioSIS Vetrix® disk but made from porcine urinary bladder instead of small intestinal cells, has been used to close the orbit following enucleation in a snake. 22 The disk sloughed 8 weeks following surgery, revealing a wound with granulation tissue underneath. Though the disk was not incorporated into the wound, its presence may have facilitated healing. The addition of BioSIS Vetrix may have contributed to efficient wound healing and prevention of secondary infection in this hawk. BioSIS Vetrix® may be an alternative to wound closure when primary closure is desired, but not possible due to a lack of tissue.
To the author's knowledge, this is the first case report describing intraocular osseous metaplasia in a hawk due to a traumatic event. This disease process should be considered a differential diagnosis in raptors that have an intraocular tumor and anticipated as a potential sequela in raptors that sustain ocular trauma or develop phthisis bulbi.
