# Impact of Aeroallergen Sensitization on Chronic Rhinosinusitis

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeThis study investigated the impact of aeroallergens on the development and progression of chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), with a focus on the specific associations between aeroallergens and CRS according to allergen type, number, and extent of sensitization.MethodsThe medical records of 256 CRS patients were retrospectively analyzed. All were divided into nonallergic, house dust mite (HDM)-allergic, pollen-allergic, and double allergic groups via specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) testing. Clinical characteristics, computed tomography (CT) scores, olfactory functions, and demographic data were compared. Correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationships between the extent of allergen sensitization and CRS severity. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for hyposmia and anosmia.ResultsThe allergic group exhibited higher total CT scores than the nonallergic group (P = 0.001). Sensitivity to HDM or pollen allergens alone was not significantly associated with increased CRS severity. No significant differences were observed between the effects of HDM and pollen allergens on CRS severity. However, the double allergic group exhibited significantly higher CT scores (P < 0.001, < 0.001, and 0.003) than the other groups. Although the prevalence rates of anosmia and hyposmia were notably higher in the double allergic group, the difference was not statistically significant. The maximum specific IgE levels to HDM and pollen allergens positively correlated with the CT scores (P = 0.001 and 0.001, respectively).ConclusionsAllergen sensitization, particularly to multiple common allergens, contributed to CRS severity. CRS patients sensitized to both HDM and pollen allergens tended to experience the diminished olfactory function. These findings underscore the importance of considering the allergen sensitization pattern when assessing CRS severity and its potential progression.Keywords: Sinusitis; allergens; dust mite allergy; rhinitis, allergic, seasonal

## INTRODUCTION
Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a chronic inflammatory condition involving the nasal cavities and paranasal sinuses that persists for over 12 weeks and is characterized by nasal congestion and discharge, a diminished olfactory sense, and facial discomfort.1,2 The pathophysiology of CRS is thought to involve multiple factors, including nasal anatomical variation, obstruction of the ostiomeatal complex, microbial infections, staphylococcal superantigens, biofilm formation, and impaired mucociliary clearance.3,4,5,6,7
Allergic rhinitis (AR) has been implicated in CRS development and exacerbation; extensive research has been conducted. However, the data are conflicting; no consensus on a link between AR and CRS has emerged.8,9,10 Several hypotheses on how AR contributes to CRS development or exacerbation have been advanced, of which one suggests that AR-induced nasal mucosal edema triggers obstruction of the sinus ostium, creating negative pressure and low oxygen levels within sinus cavities, in turn fostering bacterial overgrowth and accumulation of secretions.11 Also, impaired nasal epithelial barriers have been described in both AR and CRS patients. When AR damages these barriers, allergens and microbes infiltrate the epithelium more readily, triggering submucosal inflammation.12 Baroody et al. 13 found that nasal challenge with grass or ragweed allergen extracts increased the histamine, albumin, and eosinophil cationic protein levels of the maxillary sinus as well as eosinophil counts, indicating that allergen provocation induced paranasal sinus inflammation. Finally, both AR and CRS impede mucociliary clearance. Concurrent AR in CRS patients may increase CRS severity by worsening mucociliary clearance impairment.14
The conflicting data of prior studies on AR-CRS associations may be attributable to different CRS and AR selection criteria, the statistical analyses performed, and variations in disease characteristics by the geographical region or ethnicity.15 The interplay between AR and CRS varies by the CRS subtype. It has been suggested that CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, and central compartment atopic disease exhibit stronger associations with AR than do other conditions.8,16,17,18 Also, the impact of AR on CRS may vary by the type and number of sensitized allergens.19,20,21
This study explored the influence of AR on CRS development and progression, and the various associations between AR and CRS by the types, numbers, and extents of sensitization to common aeroallergens. Specifically, we investigated the effects of house dust mite (HDM) and pollen allergens.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Patients and study design
This retrospective study evaluated medical records. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Incheon St. Mary’s Hospital, the Catholic University of Korea (approval number OC23RISI0028), in 2023, with an exemption from the written consent of a patient. The procedural flow is shown in Figure. The medical records of 338 patients who underwent the multiple allergen simultaneous test (MAST) and paranasal sinus computed tomography between March 2019 and December 2022 and were diagnosed with CRS using the criteria of the 2020 European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps were retrieved.2 We excluded patients under the age of 18 years, with histories of sinus surgery or nasal polypectomy, and with CRS caused by malignant tumors. Ultimately, 256 patients were enrolled.

In the MAST, a serum-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) level ≥ 0.35 mL/L or a class ≥ 1 was considered indicative of sensitization. Patients who tested positive for at least one HDM allergen (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Dermatophagoides farina, and house dust) but negative for all pollen allergens (rye pollen, alder, birch, oak white, short ragweed, mugwort, Japanese hop, hazel, sweet vernal grass, Bermuda grass, orchard grass, timothy grass, reed, redtop bentgrass, sycamore mix, sallow willow, poplar mix, ash mix, pine, Japanese cedar, acacia, hinoki cypress, oxeye daisy, dandelion, English plantain, Russian thistle, goldenrod, and pigweed) were assigned to the HDM-allergic group. Patients sensitized to at least one pollen allergen but not to an HDM allergen were assigned to the pollen-allergic group. Patients exhibiting sensitization to at least one HDM allergen and at least one pollen allergen were placed in the double allergic group. Patients in all 3 groups were defined as allergic. Patients exhibiting no sensitization to either HDM or pollen allergens constituted the nonallergic group.
Demographic characteristics, concurrent asthma status, subjective CRS-related symptoms, nasal polyp (NP) status, serum levels of IgE, eosinophil counts, olfactory function test results, and Lund-Mackay (LM) CT scores were compared between the nonallergic and allergic groups. Among patients whose NP specimen was collected during operation, the proportion diagnosed with eosinophilic polyps on histopathological analysis was recorded. The clinical characteristics were re-evaluated after adjusting for confounders using propensity score matching. The aforementioned clinical characteristics were subsequently compared among the nonallergic, HDM-allergic, pollen-allergic, and double allergic groups. Multinomial regression analysis was conducted to determine the effects of aeroallergens on CRS severity while controlling for confounding factors. To seek the relationships between the allergen-specific IgE reactivities to HDM and pollen allergens and CRS severity, the correlations were analyzed between the highest specific IgE levels to every HDM and pollen allergen, and the LM CT scores. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors contributing to the incidences of hyposmia and anosmia in CRS patients.

## Specific IgE testing
To detect allergen-specific IgE levels, the AdvanSure AlloScreen Inhalant Panel Assay (LG Life Science, Seoul, Korea) with 62 allergens served as the MAST. The levels of IgEs to D. pteronyssinus, D. farina, house dust, rye pollens, alder, birch, oak white, short ragweed, mugwort, Japanese hop, hazel, sweet vernal grass, Bermuda grass, orchard grass, timothy grass, reed, redtop bentgrass, sycamore mix, sallow willow, poplar mix, ash mix, pine, Japanese cedar, acacia, hinoki cypress, oxeye daisy, dandelion, English plantain, Russian thistle, goldenrod, and pigweed were used.

## Olfactory function test
Of all patients, 197 underwent olfactory function tests using either the Korean version of the Sniffin’ Stick test (KVSS-II) or the YSK Olfactory Function (YOF) test. Both the KVSS-II and the YOF test assess the olfactory threshold, discrimination, and identification. In the KVSS-II, each of the 3 tests contributes 16 points (total 48 points); the YOF test allocates 12 points to each item (total 36 points). Anosmia was defined as a KVSS-II total score ≤ 20, hyposmia as a score between 20.25 and 27, and normosmia as a score ≥ 27.25.22 In the YOF test, anosmia was defined as a total score ≤ 14.5, and hyposmia as a score between 14.5 and 21.0.23

## Statistical analysis
IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 28; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.3.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) were used for all data analyses. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations and categorical variables are given as numbers with percentages. The independent-samples t-test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test to compare non-normally distributed continuous variables between 2 groups. The Pearson χ2 test or the Fisher exact test was used when comparing categorical variables between 2 groups. For propensity score matching, nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed with caliper width ≤ 0.1 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Covariates were considered imbalanced between matched pairs if the standardized mean difference > 0.1. Following matching, a paired-samples t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed for continuous variables, and the McNemar test for categorical variables. To compare continuous variables among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post-hoc test or the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized. Pearson χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test with post-hoc Bonferroni adjustment was used to compare categorical variables among multiple groups. Spearman’s and partial Spearman’s correlation analyses of continuous variables were performed. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS
The study enrolled 256 CRS patients of whom 144 were male and 112 were female of average age 52.1 ± 14.8 years. Of all participants, 83 (32.4%) exhibited sensitization to HDM allergen(s), 48 (18.8%) to pollen allergen(s), and 22 (8.6%) to both HDM and pollen allergens; 147 (57.4%) did not exhibit sensitization to either HDM or pollen allergens.
The allergic and nonallergic group are compared in Table 1. The former group had significantly more males and was younger than the latter group (P ≤ 0.001 and 0.010, respectively). The prevalence rates of asthma, NP, eosinophilic NP, and various CRS-related symptoms did not differ between the groups. However, the total LM CT score was significantly elevated in the allergic group (P = 0.001), particularly the subscores for the anterior and posterior ethmoids and sphenoid, as was the ostiomeatal complex score (P ≤ 0.001, 0.003, 0.020, and 0.002, respectively). Olfactory function was similar between the 2 groups.

There were no significant differences in asthma or NP status, subjective CRS-related symptoms, the LM CT scores, or olfactory function between the nonallergic and pollen-allergic groups. However, the double allergic patients included more males (P = 0.001) with higher LM CT scores (P ≤ 0.001) than the nonallergic patients. All LM CT subscores was significantly higher in the double allergic patients than in the nonallergic group (P < 0.05).
The clinical characteristics of the HDM- and pollen-allergic groups did not differ. However, significantly higher LM CT scores were revealed in the double allergic patients compared to the HDM-allergic patients, including the frontal, maxillary, anterior ethmoid, and posterior ethmoid subcategories (P < 0.001 for the total score; P = 0.005, 0.002, 0.001, and 0.002 for the respective subcategory scores). Similarly, it was revealed that the double allergic patients had significantly higher total LM CT scores and subcategory scores for maxillary and sphenoid sinuses (P = 0.003 for the total score; P = 0.002 and 0.005 for the respective subcategory scores) than the pollen-allergic patients.
Although statistical significance was not reached, the double allergic group exhibited a higher rate of subjective smell loss (63.6%), compared to the nonallergic, HDM-allergic, and pollen-allergic groups (39.5%, 39.9%, and 34.6%, respectively, P = 0.138). Similarly, the rates of anosmia and hyposmia + anosmia were notably higher in the double allergic group (45.0% and 50.0%, respectively), compared to the nonallergic, HDM-allergic, and pollen-allergic groups (anosmia: 18.9%, 21.7%, and 10.0%, respectively; P = 0.035. hyposmia + anosmia: 35.1%, 28.3%, and 20.0%, respectively, P = 0.189). Despite the initial identification of a significant difference in anosmia rates among the 4 groups using the Pearson χ2 test, post-hoc Bonferroni adjustment did not reveal any significant pairwise differences.
Multinomial logistic regression analysis, excluding the confounding effects of sex and age, confirmed that the double allergic group had significantly higher LM CT scores compared to the other 3 groups (Table 3).

## DISCUSSION
In South Korea, HDMs are the principal AR aeroallergens, followed by pollen allergens.24,25 The present study explored the impacts of HDM allergens and pollens on CRS. The male-to-female ratios and mean ages of certain groups differed; AR prevalence is affected by sex and age. Kim et al. 26 reported that the male-to-female ratio of Korean AR patients increased from 1.41 to 1.78 from the 1990s to the 2010s; in the latter decade, most AR patients were aged 20 to 29 years. This may explain the significantly younger age of our HDM-allergic group and higher male-to-female ratios of the HDM- and double allergic groups compared to the nonallergic group.
The allergic group exhibited significantly higher total LM CT scores and more elevated scores in certain subcategories compared to the nonallergic group even after propensity score matching, implying that patients sensitized to common aeroallergens experience more severe CRS.
We found that most LM CT scores were more significantly elevated in the double allergic group than in the nonallergic, HDM-, and pollen-allergic groups. The average total LM CT score was significantly higher in the double allergic group (13.8 ± 5.6) than in the nonallergic, HDM-, and pollen-allergic groups (7.5 ± 4.8, 8.7 ± 5.0, and 8.9 ± 5.2, respectively). After adjusting for confounding variables, total LM CT scores remained significantly higher in the double allergic group than in the other 3 groups, highlighting the impact of co-sensitization on CRS severity. However, there were no significant differences in LM CT scores among the nonallergic, HDM-, and pollen-allergic groups. The HDM- and pollen-allergic groups did not demonstrate any evidence of reduced olfactory function compared to the nonallergic group. However, the double allergic group had substantially higher rates of subjective smell loss, anosmia, and hyposmia compared to the other groups, although the differences were not statistically significant. These findings imply that although sensitization to a particular common perennial or seasonal allergen might not strongly impact on CRS severity or olfactory function, co-sensitization to both allergens does.
The maximum serum levels of IgEs to both HDM allergens and pollens exhibited significant positive correlations with total LM CT scores and certain subcategory scores, respectively. Similar results were obtained for partial correlation analysis after controlling for the effects of age. This implies that the extent of sensitization affects CRS severity. Notably, the anterior and posterior ethmoid sinus LM CT scores significantly correlated with the levels of both IgEs. These findings are in line with previous studies which suggested that CRS associated with allergy initially manifests primarily in the central compartment due to exposure of the middle turbinate surface to aeroallergens via nasal airflow, followed by the lateral compartment, which becomes involved later in the disease course.16,17,27
Several similar previous studies examined the relationship between allergy and CRS; the results were consistent. Tan et al. 28 found that AR prevalence was significantly higher in CRS patients than in controls. In a study of patients with recalcitrant CRS undergoing revision sinus surgery, those with inhalant allergies exhibited higher LM scores.29 In contrast, some studies have found no robust association between allergies and CRS. Pearlman et al. 30 reported that atopic status was not significantly associated with the LM CT scores of CRS patients. Likewise, another study found no significant difference in the LM CT scores between allergen-sensitized and non-sensitized CRS patients.31
Other studies have explored the associations between specific CRS subtypes and allergies. Pumhirun et al. 32 and Muñoz del Castillo et al. 33 reported significantly higher skin-prick-test positivity rates in patients with CRSwNP compared to controls. One study that compared patients with CRSwNP and CRS without NPs found more HDM sensitization in the former group.34 Conversely, a prospective study on CRSwNP patients revealed that atopic status was not significantly associated with CRS-related subjective symptoms or endoscopic or imaging findings.35 In our study, no notable difference in NP status by allergen type was apparent.
Other studies similar to ours have explored the effects of different allergens on CRS. Emanuel et al. 36 found that, among patients undergoing surgery to treat chronic or recurrent rhinosinusitis, HDM allergies were more common than seasonal allergies, implicating perennial allergies as potential risk factors for rhinosinusitis. In one study on AR patients, LM CT scores were significantly higher in those with perennial AR than in those with seasonal AR, however, the frequency of rhinosinusitis did not differ.37 In a Canadian study, CRS patients were more highly sensitized to Alternaria alternata, cat, and ragweed allergens than control urticaria patients; the levels of sensitization to HDM or seasonal allergens did not differ between the groups.21 An Asian study grouped CRS patients by the sensitized allergen type (HDM, cockroach, staphylococcal enterotoxin, fungus, and animal); no significant differences in CRSwNP status or LM CT scores were evident.38 We found no significant differences in CT findings or NP status between HDM- and pollen-allergic CRS patients. Emanuel reported no significant difference between the numbers of patients sensitized to single and multiple allergens for any CRS severity grade.36 However, our study implies that CRS is more severe in patients simultaneously sensitized to multiple common allergens.
Our study had two limitations. Any retrospective study is susceptible to selection bias. The semiquantitative MAST assay is less sensitive and accurate than the ImmunoCAP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) test.39 The strength of this study is that we comprehensively analyzed not only the effects of sensitized allergens but also the impacts of allergen types and numbers, and the extents of aeroallergen sensitization on CRS, in a considerable number of CRS patients. Furthermore, our study is useful because it demonstrates that the CRS is significantly more severe in patients simultaneously sensitized to both common perennial and seasonal aeroallergens than in those sensitized to only one allergen type or to no allergens.
In conclusion, CRS patients sensitized to prevalent aeroallergens exhibited more severe disease compared to non-sensitized CRS patients. HDM and pollen allergies alone had little impact on CRS severity, but co-sensitization to both was associated with CRS severity and olfactory function impairment. Significant correlations were evident between the extents of sensitization to common aeroallergens and CRS severity. Our work increases understanding of the complex relationship between aeroallergen sensitization and CRS severity, and will improve the management of CRS patients with concurrent allergic sensitivities.
