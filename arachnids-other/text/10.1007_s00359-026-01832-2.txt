Abstract
Amblypygi (whip spiders) are an order of arthropods (Subphylum: Chelicerata, Class: Arachnida) that navigate primarily
by their keen olfactory abilities. Experiments in the laboratory reveal that the species Phrynus marginemaculatus forms
both short and long-term memories for odors associated with access to a shelter. While bioamine function and location
in the whip spider nervous system remain completely unexplored, comparative anatomy indicates that their olfactory
memory may involve serotonin and dopamine signaling in brain regions involved in processing olfactory input. Preliminary immunohistochemistry detected serotonin and tyrosine hydroxylase immunoreactivity in mushroom body-associated
calycal regions and antenniform primary olfactory glomeruli, supporting future work on monoaminergic modulation of
olfactory circuits. Therefore, if serotonin or dopamine signaling involved in olfactory processing is pharmacologically disrupted, their performance in an associative olfactory learning task should be significantly impaired. Subjects were trained
on an olfactory memory paradigm, subsequently injected with physiological saline, the serotonin receptor antagonist
methiothepin mesylate (MET), or the dopamine receptor antagonist SCH-23390 (SCH), and tested for memory retention
24 h afterwards. Controls injected with saline demonstrated robust associative memory (serotonin antagonism control:
n = 10, p = 0.006 against chance performance on the task, dopamine antagonism control: n = 10, p = 0.039), while treated
groups displayed no such memory (MET: n = 10, p = 0.375, SCH: n = 10, p = 0.892). There was also a significant difference
in performance between the treated and control groups on test day (serotonin antagonism groups: p = 0.048, dopamine
antagonism groups: p = 0.016). Additionally, there were no significant differences in locomotor activity between treatment
and control groups on test day (p > 0.05). Together, these results are consistent with serotonergic and dopaminergic signaling contributing to olfactory memory consolidation without grossly impairing locomotion in Phrynus marginemaculatus.

Amblypygi (whip spiders) are an order of arthropods (Class:
Arachnida). In comparison to other arachnids, their navigational abilities are unusual. Using their specialized antenniform legs, thin, whip-like appendages covered in a vast
number of sensory sensilla that can detect a variety of environmental stimuli (Hebets and Chapman 2000; Santer and
Hebets 2011; Wiegmann et al. 2016), they prowl at night
in search of prey, then return faithfully to their home refuge every morning. Olfactory-sensing multiporous sensilla
located on the distal ends of their antenniform legs (Foelix
1975; Igelmund 1987) appear to be vital for their navigational abilities in the wild and in the lab (Wiegmann et al.
2016; Bingman et al. 2017). Additionally, whip spiders can

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

learn to discriminate between two different neutral odor
cues in olfactory discrimination tasks, and form short- and
long-term memories based on this learning (Wiegmann et
al. 2019). However, little is known regarding the underlying
neural mechanisms that control these memory processes.
Among arthropods, mushroom bodies are strongly associated with olfactory learning and memory, particularly in
insects such as Drosophila melanogaster and honeybees
(Heisenberg 2003; Komischke et al. 2005; Akalal et al.
2006; Blum et al. 2009; Aso et al. 2014; Hige and Turner
2015; Owald et al. 2015; Boto et al. 2020; Anton and Rössler
2021; Giurfa 2025). In these systems, serotonergic and
dopaminergic signaling modulate neural plasticity within
olfactory circuits linked to associative learning (Blenau and
Thamm 2011; Ganguly et al. 2020; Johnson et al. 2011; Lee
et al. 2011; Pech et al. 2013; Sitaraman et al. 2008, 2012;
Verlinden et al. 2015). Amblypygids possess exceptionally
large and elaborately folded mushroom bodies relative to
body size (Strausfeld et al. 1998, 2006; Sinakevitch et al.
2021), and these structures receive both olfactory and visual
input. However, despite their anatomical prominence, the
functional role of mushroom bodies in whip spider learning
and memory remains largely untested.
Existing arachnid serotonin research has primarily
focused on aggression in vivo (Jones et al. 2011; DiRienzo et
al. 2015; Schraft et al. 2023), or identifying serotonin receptor subtypes in vitro (Chen et al. 2004; Sukumar et al. 2018).
Comparative neuroanatomical studies have also identified
serotonin-immunoreactive neurons within the central nervous systems of other chelicerates, including scorpions and
horseshoe crabs (Harzsch 2004), suggesting that serotonergic signaling may be broadly conserved across arthropod
nervous systems. Indeed, serotonin function within the whip
spider brain remains completely unexplored. Based upon
their behavior, capacity for learning, brain organization, and
previous research conducted in our lab, we hypothesized
that the mushroom bodies are innervated with serotonergic neurons and normal serotonin function is required for
olfactory memory in Phrynus marginemaculatus and that
pharmacologically disrupting it with a serotonin receptor
antagonist would result in impaired memory. To test this, we
used antibodies against serotonin to localize serotonergic
neurons in the mushroom body calyces and methiothepin
mesylate (MET), a serotonin receptor antagonist previously
shown to be well-tolerated in arthropods (Thamm et al.
2013; Tierney 2018, 2020). If, as we hypothesized, serotonergic signaling is necessary for olfactory memory consolidation in P. marginemaculatus, then subjects treated with
the serotonergic antagonist should be impaired in learning
an olfactory discrimination task.
Dopamine is another highly conserved bioamine involved
in regulating a range of behavior across species, including

13

reward processing, motivation, locomotion, and learning
(Klappenbach et al. 2013; Søvik et al. 2015; Ryczko and
Dubuc 2017). In arthropods, dopamine plays a prominent
role in memory consolidation and reinforcement learning, including behaviors associated with mushroom body
circuitry. For example, dopaminergic neurons project to
defined subdivisions of the mushroom bodies in Drosophila
and modulate synaptic plasticity, which is essential for the
consolidation of odor-reward associations (Aso et al. 2014;
Hsu and Bhandawat 2016; Waddell 2016). Although dopamine’s role in insect learning has been well-characterized
(Lin et al. 2024; Avishai and Parnas 2025), relatively little
is known about its function in arachnids. Neurochemical
studies have identified catecholaminergic cells in spider and
scorpion brains (Murdock 1971; Meyer and Jehnen 1980;
Auletta et al. 2020), but there are no behavioral data on
dopamine’s possible role in arachnid memory. Given evidence from insects and the extensive olfactory processing
systems present in amblypygids, we hypothesized that the
mushroom bodies are innervated with dopaminergic neurons and dopamine would also be necessary for olfactory
memory consolidation in P. marginemaculatus. To test this,
we used antibodies against tyrosine hydroxylase to localize
dopaminergic neurons in the mushroom body calyces and
the selective dopamine D1 receptor antagonist SCH-23390,
previously shown to impair olfactory memory in mosquitoes
(Vinauger et al. 2018; Wolff et al. 2023). If, as we hypothesized, dopaminergic signaling is necessary for olfactory
memory consolidation in P. marginemaculatus, then subjects treated with the dopaminergic antagonist should be
impaired in learning an olfactory discrimination task.

Materials and methods
Subjects
Seventy-eight adult Phrynus marginemaculatus, collected from National Key Deer Refuge, Florida, United
States (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Permit Numbers:
FFO4RFKD 2015-06, 41581-2018-04, 41580-FY20-03,
and 41580-FY21-12) or purchased from arachnid collector
Sam Floyd of Xeric Bayou, were used for this experiment.
Prior to experiments, subjects were housed individually in
plastic deli containers (diameter 17.1 cm, height 10.8 cm)
partially filled with a coconut fiber substrate and a piece of
Styrofoam, which served as a shelter. Their diet consisted of
6–8 mm crickets, fed once weekly. The inside of their containers were misted daily to increase humidity and provide
access to water. Ambient room humidity was between 50
and 60%, and the temperature ranged from 26 to 28 ℃. The
room was lit by overhead, broad-spectrum fluorescent lights

(400–700 nm) on a 12:12 light: dark cycle (19:00–7:00,
dark).

Immunohistochemistry
Amblypygi (P. marginemaculatus, n = 25) were cold anesthetized and their central nervous systems were removed
and fixed overnight at 4 ̊C in vials of cold 4% paraformaldehyde. The next day, the tissue was washed twice in
phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for twenty minuntes each,
dehydrated in 10-minute steps through a series of 50%,
70%, 90% and 100% ethanol, propylene oxide, and then
rehydrated. Tissue was then embedded in albumin gelatin
blocks, post-fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, and
moved to PBS the next morning. Blocks were sectioned at
60 μm on a Leica VT1000S vibratome and sections were
placed on an orbital shaker in a well plate containing PBS
and 0.5% Triton X-100 (PBS-TX). Sections were washed in
PBS-TX twice for twenty minutes each and blocked in 5%
normal donkey serum before primary antibodies were added
for overnight tissue incubation. The following primary antibodies and concentrations were used: Rabbit anti-serotonin,
1:1000 (Millipore Sigma #S5545); mouse anti-tyrosine
hydroxylase, 1:100 (Immunostar #22941).
The next day, sections were washed six times twenty
minutes in PBS-TX and secondary antibodies, Cy3 donkey
anti-rabbit (Jackson ImmunoResearch #711-166-152) and
Cy5 donkey anti-mouse (Jackson ImmunoResearch #715175-150) were added, each at a concentration of 1:400.
After overnight incubation in secondary antibodies, sections were washed twice for ten minutes in PBS and incubated in DAPI (ThermoFisher Scientific #D3571) for one
hour. Then sections were washed an additional ten times for
ten minutes in PBS and mounted on slides in Elvanol (5 g
Mowiol 4–88 [Millipore Sigma #81381], 20 mL PBS, 10
mL glycerol). Confocal images were acquired on a Leica
Stellaris 5 confocal microscope and maximum projections
were processed in ImageJ.

Behavior experimental apparatus
Previously designed by Wiegmann et al. (2019), olfactory
discrimination training and testing occurred within an opentopped white opaque acrylic arena (29 cm × 14 cm, L × W)
with floor-level holes (3 cm × 1 cm, L × H) centered on each
of the shorter walls, and one on a long wall through which a
subject was released from a black acrylic start box (5 × 7 cm
interior) (Fig. 1). Solid lines drawn in black permanent
marker were present on the semi-transparent underside of
the arena that outlined “goal zones” used on test trials (see
below, 6.5 × 5 cm square areas in front of each dark shelter
visible in Fig. 2). Additional solid black lines divided the

arena into thirds (visible in Fig. 2), and the arena was placed
on a white uniform surface to provide contrast. A compact
fluorescent light source (900 lumens) was positioned at each
short end of the arena. The bulbs were pointed downward
towards the center of the arena at an angle of about 45° so
that the top of each bulb was approximately 15 cm from the
arena floor. The light served as motivation for the subjects
to escape the light and seek refuge within the dark shelters
(whip spiders are nocturnal, and testing occurred during
their sleep cycle).
Two dark shelters (4 × 5 cm interior), constructed of black
acrylic plastic, each contained a clear acrylic cube with a
small well used for odor presentation (2.5 cm x 2.5 cm x
1 cm, L x W x H). These scents were previously shown
not to differ from each other in their spontaneous attraction to whip spiders (Wiegmann et al. 2019). In the training and testing phases, the well of one cube was filled with
10 µl of geraniol (C10H18O, Sigma Aldrich, Product Number 163333), and the other with 10 µl 1-hexanol (C6H14O,
Sigma Aldrich, Product Number 471402). A piece of plastic
mesh positioned at the hole in the arena wall blocked access
to one shelter (CS-) during training, and both shelters (CS+,
CS-) during testing. For training, a small cube-shaped plastic grate was placed over the clear cube in the CS+ shelter,
blocking direct tactile access to the liquids by the subjects,
but not the aerosolized scents themselves. The assignment
of the CS+ scent, and whether the subject was in the control
or treated group, was pseudo-randomly chosen before the
training phase using an online randomization tool for both
the serotonin and dopamine experiments with the constraint
that an equal number of subjects were given geraniol or hexanol as the CS+. All behavioral protocols were performed at
25–26 °C between the hours of 12:00 and 14:00.

Training
Whip spiders were trained in the described experimental
arena (Fig. 1) on an olfactory discrimination task (n = 10
per group) where an open dark shelter was paired with one
of the two pseudo-randomly chosen scents, and a bright
light served to motivate subjects to enter the CS+ shelter.
To begin a training trial, a subject was placed in the start
box for five minutes, then coaxed into the arena using a
paintbrush. Once in the arena, a piece of black acrylic plastic was inserted into the start box to cover the opening and
prevent re-entry. Subjects had five minutes to find the open
CS+ shelter, which was switched between sides pseudo-randomly over seven trials; the same scent was not presented
on the same side for more than two consecutive trials. If a
subject failed to find the open shelter within five minutes, it
was gently moved into the open shelter with a paintbrush.

Serotonin antagonism
Directly after the seventh trial, each of the 20 subjects of the
serotonin experiment were restrained using Parafilm to gently pin them to a small piece of Styrofoam and microinjected
into their opisthosoma (Fig. 1) with 2 µL of either 7.00 × 10− 3
M methiothepin mesylate (MET) (Sigma Aldrich: M149), a
serotonin receptor antagonist, or 0.9% physiological saline,
the control. Saline-injected subjects served as procedural
controls for restraint, injection, and handling. The serotonin
antagonist concentration was informed by previous arthropod pharmacological studies (Thamm et al. 2013; Tierney
2018, 2020) and refined through pilot tolerability observations in P. marginemaculatus (n = 12). The dose used did
not lead to any detectable change in locomotor behavior, a
known side-effect of high-dose serotonin receptor antagonists (Claassen and Kammer 1986; Cabaj et al. 2017; Chiew
and Isbister 2025), and therefore did not interfere with their
ability to participate in the behavioral assay. Pilot observations were conducted in a neutral open-field arena prior to
formal testing to assess gross locomotor effects following
antagonist administration. Subjects were visually monitored for impaired righting behavior, prolonged immobility,
impaired coordination, or inability to locomote normally
within the arena for one hour after injection.
Injection needles were pulled using a pipette puller (Sutter Instrument Company: P-2000) from borosilicate pipettes
(World Precision Instruments: 1B150-3) with 10 μm tips.
Microinjections for each subject were delivered using a
Picospritzer II (Parker Hannifin Corporation), with each
new needle filled with a 10 µL Hamilton syringe. All injection sites were sealed using non-toxic UV dental cement to
prevent bleeding. The fatality rate for this injection protocol
was 16.6% (three control and one treated subject). These
subjects, like those that failed the subject-participation
protocol, were replaced to achieve n = 20 subjects (10 per
group) for analysis. Both the control and treated group were

comprised of seven females and three males. Physiological
saline (0.9% NaCl) was prepared by dissolving non-iodized
sodium chloride in deionized water, followed by autoclave
sterilization prior to use, and stored at 4 °C when not in use.

Dopamine antagonism
The experimental procedures were identical to the serotonin
experiment, except that 2 µL of 1.00 × 10− 6 M SCH-23390
(Sigma Aldrich: D054), a dopamine receptor antagonist,
was administered. This concentration was selected for its
non-toxicity and its proven ability to abolish olfactory memory in Aedes aegypti and Anopheles stephensi (Vinauger et
al. 2018; Wolff et al. 2023). Additionally, this dose did not
result in gross locomotor impairment in an open-field pilot
study (n = 9), a known side-effect of some high-dose dopamine receptor antagonists (Claassen and Kammer 1986;
Reynolds 2021; Büschges and Ache 2025), and therefore
did not interfere with subjects’ ability to participate in the
behavioral assay. The injection protocol fatality rate was
4.8% (one subject in the treatment group). This subject, like
the four subjects that failed the subject-participation protocol, was replaced to achieve a final sample of 20 subjects (10
per group) for the final analysis. At testing, the control group
was comprised of three males and seven females, while the
treated group comprised four males and six females.

Testing
Subjects were memory-tested 24 h after microinjection in
the experimental arena (Fig. 2). After a 5-minute start-box
period, they were released into the arena for 15 min, during which the time spent in the two goal zones (6.5 × 5 cm
squares in front of the two now-blocked dark shelters, containing geraniol and hexanol) was recorded. A subject was
considered to occupy a goal zone if more than half of its
prosoma was within it. An association index, ranging from
0 to 1, was calculated as t1/(t1 + t2), where t1 is the time in
front of the CS+ shelter and t2 is the time in front of the CS–
shelter. Locomotion (side-switching) was measured as the
number of times a subject fully crossed the lines dividing
the arena into thirds during the 15-minute testing period, in
order to rule out gross locomotor impairment in the antagonist-treated groups. The CS+ shelter position was pseudorandomly assigned using an online randomization tool with
the constraint that the left-right position of the CS + was balanced across subjects.

Data analysis
The time each subject spent in each goal zone in a test trial
was scored using video-playback (previously recorded live

13

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

by an overhead Arducam 1080p USB camera) during the
testing phase, where the group to which a subject belonged
was unknown to the scorer. Those times were used to calculate an association index, I = t1/(t1 + t2), where t1 and t2
are the times a subject spent in the goal zone in front of
the CS + and CS- shelter, respectively. Association indices
closer to I = 1 indicate an attraction to the CS+ odor (i.e.,
successful associative learning and memory). Indices near
I = 0.5 indicate no preference for either side (odor) of the
arena. One-tailed independent-samples t-tests were used to
compare the association index means of respective control
and treatment groups. One-sample t-tests were performed to
determine whether association indices exceeded the chance
expectation of I = 0.5 (Glover and Mitchell 2016). For the
control measure of locomotion (side-switching), Wilcoxon
rank sum tests were performed to compare the medians of
line-crossing counts between subjects in the control and
treated groups. All data analyses were performed using R
(4.4.2) and RStudio.

Discussion
This study provides the first evidence that pharmacological
blockade of serotonin or dopamine receptors after training
disrupts olfactory memory consolidation in the whip spider
Phrynus marginemaculatus. Subjects treated with either a
serotonin or dopamine receptor antagonist after training
failed to retain a preference for the conditioned odor 24 h
later, while saline-injected controls retained the association.
Because these impairments occurred without changes in
locomotion and antagonist injections occurred immediately
after training, the deficits likely reflect impaired memory
consolidation. Additionally, this is the first study to show
successful olfactory memory formation in P. marginemaculatus following a single training session, in contrast to previous work in which subjects were trained over multiple
days (e.g. Flanigan et al. 2021; Wiegmann et al. 2019).
This suggests that robust olfactory memory formation may
occur more rapidly in P. marginemaculatus than previously
thought, potentially reflecting ecological pressures associated with navigation in topographically complex environments, alongside the large and elaborated mushroom bodies
characteristic of amblypygids.
The role of serotonin in memory formation has been well
documented in insects such as Drosophila melanogaster,
where pharmacological and gene manipulation in the mushroom bodies consistently disrupts olfactory learning (Johnson et al. 2011; Sitaraman et al. 2012; Ganguly et al. 2020).
Less is known in chelicerates, but existing evidence shows
that serotonergic activity contributes to arthropod navigation (Verlinden et al. 2015; Homberg et al. 2016; Howard et
al. 2019; Gajardo et al. 2023), and amblypygids are notable
navigators (Hebets et al. 2014; Wiegmann et al. 2016; Casto
et al. 2020; Flanigan et al. 2021; Lehmann et al. 2022; Coppola et al. 2024). The mushroom bodies of amblypygids
(Strausfeld et al. 2006; Strausfeld 2012; Wolff and Strausfeld
2015), recently shown to receive both olfactory and visual
input (Sinakevitch et al. 2021), also suggest the possibility
of conserved serotonergic involvement in olfactory memory
processes associated with mushroom body circuitry.
The results reported here support this view, as serotonin
antagonism impaired memory formation following even
minimal training without impacting locomotion, suggesting that serotonergic signaling contributes to the formation
or stabilization of odor-shelter association. Dopamine has
been similarly implicated in associative learning in other
invertebrates, particularly in reward-based memory consolidation (Aso et al. 2014; Waddell 2016). The dopamine D1
receptor antagonist SCH-23390, used here at concentrations
previously shown to impair olfactory learning in mosquitoes
(Vinauger et al. 2018; Wolff et al. 2023), produced comparable deficits in P. marginemaculatus. SCH-treated subjects

13

failed to retain the conditioned odor preference despite no
detectable gross locomotor impairment, reinforcing the idea
that dopaminergic signaling associated with mushroom
body circuitry may represent a broadly conserved mechanism for encoding associative memories, even in phylogenetically distinct arthropods like whip spiders (Sinakevitch
et al. 2021). Moreover, the similar magnitude of impairment
seen with both dopamine and serotonin antagonism suggests
that both neuromodulators contribute to memory formation
in P. marginemaculatus.
We are currently carrying out research aimed at mapping biogenic amine distributions and receptor expression
in the whip spider brain (Wolff et al. in prep.). Sinakevitch
et al. (2021) showed that antenniform leg primary olfactory glomeruli project to the large mushroom body calycal
glomeruli, a subregion linked to olfactory processing and
memory. In line with this architecture, our immunohistochemistry detected serotonin and tyrosine hydroxylase
immunoreactivity in both the antenniform primary olfactory
glomeruli and the large calycal glomeruli (Fig. 3). Together
with the behavioral findings that post-training blockade of
either serotonin or dopamine receptors disrupts 24-hour
retention without affecting locomotion, these results are
consistent with the possibility that monoaminergic modulation of olfactory pathways, potentially including the mushroom body calyx, supports memory consolidation. At the
same time, we cannot determine from our behavioral results
whether the relevant site of action occurs at the primary
olfactory glomeruli, the calycal glomeruli, or elsewhere in
the olfactory circuit. Future work examining receptor localization and more localized antagonist manipulations will be
needed to clarify where and how serotonin and dopamine
act during memory formation in whip spiders.
As with many pharmacological studies, systemic administration prevents localization of drug action; antagonists were delivered diffusely rather than to specific brain
regions. Because hemolymph circulation is shared between
the opisthosoma and prosoma in amblypygids (Weygoldt
2000), systemic distribution beyond the injection site is
biologically plausible, although the present study cannot
directly confirm antagonist concentration within the brain.
However, the behavioral effects seen here are consistent
with monoaminergic modulation of olfactory memory consolidation, potentially involving mushroom body-associated
circuitry, mirroring mechanisms described in other invertebrates (Johnson et al. 2011; Søvik et al. 2015; Kasture et
al. 2018; Ichinose et al. 2021; Rivi et al. 2023; Raza and
Li 2025). Importantly, because antagonists were administered after training, our results indicate that serotonin and
dopamine appear to contribute during the post-training consolidation window for the formation of long-term memory.
Nonetheless, we cannot exclude lingering drug effects on

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

sensory processing of olfactory stimuli at the time of test;
saline controls and locomotion assays helped address this,
but future studies should include additional controls (e.g.,
training after drug administration, or using more localized
injections) to rule out sensory interference. Additionally, the
side-switching assay used here represents a relatively coarse
proxy for locomotor activity and may not detect subtle alterations in movement dynamics, exploratory behavior, or sensory responsiveness.
In summary, post-training blockade of serotonin or
dopamine receptors impaired 24-hour retention of an odorshelter association in Phrynus marginemaculatus without
altering locomotion, indicating a specific role for these neuromodulators in memory consolidation. To our knowledge,
this is the first experimental evidence implicating biogenic
amine signaling in arachnid memory. Together, these findings suggest complementary contributions of serotonergic
and dopaminergic signaling to associative memory formation in P. marginemaculatus.
