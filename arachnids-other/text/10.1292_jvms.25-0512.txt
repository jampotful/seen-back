First record of chigger mites (Acari: Trombiculidae) parasitizing birds in the Ogasawara Islands
Mizue Inumaru, Kazuto Kawakami, Yukita Sato, Yukiko Higa (2025). Journal of Veterinary Medical Science. https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.25-0512. doi:10.1292/jvms.25-0512. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# First record of chigger mites (Acari: Trombiculidae) parasitizing birds in the Ogasawara Islands

## Abstract
AbstractLarvae of trombiculid mites (Acari: Trombiculidae), known as chiggers, are ectoparasites that parasitize various animals and transmit pathogens such as Orientia tsutsugamushi. We report the first record of chigger mites in the Ogasawara Islands, collected from Bonin Greenfinch Chloris kittlitzi and Warbling White-eyes Zosterops japonicus. By morphological and molecular analysis, the chiggers were identified to the genus Toritrombicula which predominantly parasitize avian hosts. Although the mites could not be identified to the species level, this finding provides new insight into the distribution of trombiculid mites of Japan.Keywords: chigger mite, ectoparasite, Ogasawara Islands, Toritrombicula, Trombiculidae
Trombiculid mites (Acari: Trombiculidae) are ectoparasites comprised of over 3,000 species worldwide [10]. The life cycle of these mites consists of 7 stages: egg, prelarva, larva, protonymph, deutonymph, tritonymph, and adult. Among these stages, only the larval stage is parasitic, while all other stages are free-living soil dwellers. Larvae of these mites, commonly known as chigger mites, parasitize various animals including humans and are important vectors of pathogens such as Orientia tsutsugamushi [8]. Because of their medical and veterinary importance, chigger mites and their pathogens have been investigated throughout the world, particularly in what is known as the “tsutsugamushi triangle” which includes Japan [21]. In Japan, over 130 species of trombiculid mites have been recorded, of which only about 10 are known to transmit pathogens [17]. However, many areas and animals have not been investigated.
The Ogasawara (Bonin) Islands, which belong to Tokyo prefecture, are oceanic islands located nearly 1,000 km south of mainland Tokyo, Japan. The subtropical islands support a unique ecosystem comprised of numerous species, including both native and introduced [20]. Meanwhile, there are only few records of ectoparasites in the Ogasawara Islands, including ixodid ticks on domestic mammals [22], haemadipsid leeches on seabirds [9], and hippoboscid flies on land birds [14]. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no record of trombiculid mites in the Ogasawara Islands. Here, we report the first record of chigger mites in the Ogasawara Islands, collected from two bird species.
The birds were captured during a mist-netting survey at four islands of the Hahajima Group (Mukohjima, Meijima, Imotojima, and Anejima) between June 28 to July 2, 2021, with permission from the Ministry of the Environment of Japan (permit number 1051026). Captured birds were removed from the nets, handled briefly for data collection, and released immediately. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Act on Welfare and Management of Animals 1973, Japan. Note that this survey was carried out as part of a separate study in which ectoparasites were not the primary focus. When possible, the chigger mites were carefully collected from the bird using tweezers and placed in 99.5% ethanol. DNA was crudely extracted from the mites using a non-destructive method. Mites were placed in 0.2 mL tubes containing 5 µL of 0.2 M NaOH and incubated at 70°C for 10 min. Following, 20 µL of a buffer mixture containing 90 mM tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane hydrochloride (Tris-HCl) and 1.25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was added to each sample. After DNA extraction, the mite skin was collected into a new tube containing 10 µL of 0.2 M NaOH, incubated at 80°C for 10 min, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 75%, 90%, absolute ethanol), and mounted on a microscope slide with Euparal medium. Mites were observed and photographed using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci, Tokyo, Japan) and Leica MC170 HD camera (Leica Camera, Wetzlar, Germany), respectively. Species were identified using taxonomic keys and descriptions of chigger mites [15, 17, 18]. The specimens were deposited in the Reference Museum at the Department of Medical Entomology, National Institute of Infectious Diseases, Tokyo, Japan.
For molecular analysis, PCR targeting the partial COI gene was performed using a previously described protocol [1]. Briefly, PCR was carried out with the dgLCO1490 and dgHCO2198 primers [7]. Amplification was confirmed by microchip electrophoresis using MutiNA (Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan), and the PCR products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Tokyo, Japan). The purified samples were sequenced at Azenta (Tokyo, Japan), trimmed with the ATGC software (GENETYX, Tokyo, Japan), aligned with GENETYX ver. 13 (GENETYX), and the resulting sequences were confirmed using BLAST [6]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum-Likelihood method and General Time-Reversible model with Gamma distribution and 1,000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA X [5].
During the mist netting survey, chigger mites were found on five birds: two Bonin Greenfinch Chloris kittlitzi (Mukohjima n=1, Anejima n=1) and three Warbling White-eyes Zosterops japonicus (Meijima n=1, Imotojima n=1, Anejima n=1). The mites were found on the lower surface near the shoulder joint (Fig. 1a and 1b). Chigger mites were obtained from four of the five individuals, one mite per individual. For one Bonin Greenfinch, the observed chigger mite could not be safely removed, and no samples were obtained. From morphological observation, all four chigger mites were identified to the genus Toritrombicula, based on the synthetic identification formula (SIF; 7B-N-3-2111.0000), palpal setal formula (fPp; B/B/NNN), scutum shape, eye shape, and presence of trifurcate tibial claws (Fig. 1c and 1d). Due to severe damage and poor condition of all samples, the mites could not be identified to the species level. Two haplotypes (99.6% pairwise identity) were obtained and deposited in GenBank (accession nos. LC894934-LC894935). Phylogenetic analysis placed the obtained haplotypes within a clade containing Toritrombicula densipiliata of Malaysia [12] (Fig. 2), with the sequence identity ranging from 84.9 to 87.2% between the obtained haplotypes and those of Malaysia.

Trombiculid mites have been found throughout Japan, from the northernmost Hokkaido to southernmost Okinawa prefectures [17]. Among islands of Tokyo, trombiculid mites mainly consisting of Leptotrombidium species have been recorded in Izu-Oshima and Hachijo Island [4, 13], which are located roughly 120 and 300 km south of mainland Tokyo. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first record of chigger mites from the Ogasawara Islands, providing new insights into the distribution of trombiculid mites of Japan.
Birds are known to be infested with a wide variety of trombiculid mites, including subgenera that are strictly ornithophilic and others that are generalists [3, 12, 17]. Genera including Leptotrombidium, Euschoengastia, Toritrombicula, Mackiena, Neoschoengastia have previously been recorded from birds of Japan, of which the latter three mainly parasitize birds [2, 18, 19, 23]. The genus Toritrombicula consists of 23 species, including 4 species (T. blumbergi, T. gygis, T. hasegawai, and T. lerdthusneei) that have been recorded in Japan [10, 18]. Unfortunately, due to poor sample conditions, it was not possible to identify the Toritrombicula found in this study to a species. Haplotypes obtained from the mites were placed within a clade containing T. densipiliata of Malaysia. This mite species is distributed in countries of Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines) and Taiwan [16, 18]. Meanwhile, T. densipiliata has not been recorded in Japan thus far, and this could be the first record of this species in Japan. However, Rajasegaran et al. [12] found a considerably high intraspecific genetic diversity of 17.64% in T. densipiliata, suggesting that cryptic speciation may have occurred. In order to clarify the Toritrombicula species found in the Ogasawara Islands, further investigations including both morphological and molecular approaches are necessary. Considering that Toritrombicula mainly parasitize birds and that both host bird species in this study are resident breeders [11], the trombiculid mites are suggested to be established within the Ogasawara Islands. The mites are likely to have arrived via avian hosts, although the timing, route, and host species involved remain unclear. However, note that there are also a few records of Toritrombicula from mammalian hosts [18], and the possibility that these mites arrived via mammals cannot be neglected. No studies have investigated pathogens in Toritrombicula mites and consequently, their pathogenicity and relationship to avian hosts is unknown.
In conclusion, trombiculid mites were found on birds of the Ogasawara Islands for the first time. However, due to a highly limited sample size, the species, prevalence, host range and several other factors are still unclear. Nevertheless, this finding provides important preliminary data for the understanding of the parasite fauna in the region and opens up possibilities of future ecological and epidemiological studies. In the Ogasawara Islands, external parasites considered to be chiggers have also been observed on the Blue Rock Thrush Monticola solitarius and the Bonin White-eye Apalopteron familiare (Kazuto Kawakami, unpublished data). Further investigations are needed to better understand the trombiculid fauna in the Ogasawara Islands.
