Additive Manufacturing of an Airflow Sensor
inspired by the flat Trichobothria of Scorpions
Samuele Martinelli*, Student Member, IEEE, Andrew Reid, Member, IEEE,
James F.C. Windmill, Senior Member, IEEE
Centre for Ultrasonic Engineering, University of Strathclyde, Glasgow, United Kingdom
* Corresponding author: samuele.martinelli@strath.ac.uk
Abstract— Nature has always inspired humans to create
innovative tools. Arachnids show exceptional and functional
sensory receptors at a small scale. Air flow mechanoreceptors,
commonly called trichobothria, are used in different shapes
and sizes by several arachnid species. The goal of this work is
to develop flat hair-like sensors inspired by the adult Buthus
occitanus scorpion. A sensor that responds to airflow has been
developed and realized using multi-material additive
manufacturing (also known as 3D printing). The sensor has
been sputter coated with platinum to add piezoresistive sensing
capabilities. Preliminary results with an unbalanced voltage
divider show a promising response in the mV region.
Keywords— bioinspired, 3D-printed sensor, airflow, sensor,
additive manufacturing, trichobothria, arachnid

I.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years several research themes have taken
inspiration from mechanisms present in the animal kingdom
to engineer tools that can be used by humans. Insects and
arachnids show highly functional miniature systems, the
mechanics of which remain challenging for research
problems [1]. Various authors in the past century have
described the functioning of their mechanoreceptors [2-9].
Trichobothria allow arachnids to sense and react to airflow
and low frequency, near field, sound. Moreover, it is
speculated that this structure was derived from
chemoreceptors [10] and that from this structure infrared
receptors were derived [11]. Therefore, from a change in the
hair structure, insects sense a plethora of physical
phenomena or chemical substances [5, 10-12].
The following study describes the use of digital light
processing (DLP) additive manufacturing (AM) to create a
sensor inspired by the flat trichobothria of the adult Buthus
occitanus scorpions [4], as opposed to cylindrical ones. The
aim of this work is to develop a sensor that responds to
airflow. Similar sensors have been previously produced
using silicon based microelectromechanical systems
(MEMS) techniques [13-15], or by more expensive AM
techniques, such as two-photon polymerization (2PP) [16].
However, the use of DLP AM for sensor production
promises cheaper, faster and less wasteful prototyping
compared to conventional sensor fabrication techniques.
Additionally, it also provides the opportunity to integrate
arrays of sensors within a single structure and adapt it to

II.

METHODOLOGY

The sensors were designed with the computer-aided
design (CAD) software Shapr3D and 3D-printed via an
ASIGA MAX X27 DLP 3D printer. A diagram of the hair
sensor’s structure is shown in Fig. 1. The hair and spring are
3D-printed using two different materials emulating the
stiffness difference shown by mechanoreceptors in
arachnids: a less stiff material for the basal area (responsible
for transduction) and a stiffer material for the hair itself [18,
19]. This maximizes mechanical energy transmission since
the hair deflects the mechanical energy, instead of losing it
while bending, to the material responsible for electric signal
generation [19], the dendrite in arachnids, the piezoresistive
layer in the proposed sensor of this paper.
The hair structure and base are printed using Formlabs’s
grey General Purpose Resin (V4) and the rest of the
structure (the spring) using Formlabs’s Elastic 50A resin
(V1). Previous work used a custom-made PEGDA-based
resin [17], but Formlabs’s elastomer proved to perform
better under frequent and continuous stress, therefore being
more durable and less brittle. Once the sensors were
manufactured, they were immersed in water with dishwash
soap and cleaned in an ultrasonicated bath (Clifton SW3H)
for 30 minutes to remove resin residues. This allowed for
less alteration of the material properties from harsher

chemicals like isopropyl alcohol. The sensors were then left
to air dry for 1 hour before being UV post-cured with an
ASIGA Flash for 5 minutes. Lastly, an Agar sputter coater
(AGB7341) with thickness monitor was used to coat the
spring of the sensor with platinum. The sputter coater was
used for 120 seconds at 40 mA, for an estimated thickness
of 107.5 nm. A microCT x-ray scan of one of the printed
sensors was taken with a Bruker Skyscan 1172 (Fig. 1).
The structure called the spring was printed with a
serpentine design like common strain gauges. This allows
for optimal transduction once coated and for reduced
stiffness of the system. The spring is 0.4 mm thick, and its
height is 1.5 mm. The serpentine design is made of four
vertical beams, the two at the edges are 0.4 mm wide, while
the inner beams 0.3 mm. The spacing between beams is 0.2
mm and they are connected by horizontal beams with a
height of 0.3 mm. The hair is 4x3x1 mm. The printing
settings used in the Asiga for the layers of elastic 50A resin
were the default ones of the Formlabs elastic FLELCL01
taken from Asiga’s material open library. For the remaining
structure (using the grey resin), the specified settings were
modified to print the hair and base accurately due to the
resin being different, while the others remained as default,
light intensity: 19 mW/cm2; exposure time: 2 seconds for the
first four layers, then increased to 3 seconds, Zcompensation: 0.2 mm; wait time after approach and
separation: 5 seconds.
The base has a carved space that works as tracks to add
wires to the sensors. The tracks have a hole with a 0.7 mm
diameter, enough to have a standard breadboard-sized cable
run through. Agar Quick Silver Paint (AGG302) was used
to fill the track while a cable was inserted in the hole. This
ensured proper connection between the cable and the metal
coated spring. Super glue was applied at the top of the track
to ensure the strain of bending cables wouldn’t break the
connection. The resistance of the sensor was measured with
a standard multi-meter at 27.93 Ω.
The sensor was tested in a voltage divider configuration
with a 90.4 Ω resistor (Fig. 2). The reason for using a
slightly higher resistor in these preliminary tests is to ensure
some sort of protection for the sensor from excessive power.
A voltage source of 10 V was applied across the bridge. A
Tektronix DPO2014 oscilloscope was used in AC-coupling

mode to measure changes in resistance from an applied
airflow. AC-coupling was used to be able to see small
voltage changes in the oscilloscope. The VU low-pass filter
of the oscilloscope was applied to the signal at 60 Hz. In all
the experiments a velocity microphone (Knowles NR-3158)
was placed beside the hair sensor as reference to understand
if what was shown on the oscilloscope was an actual
response of the sensor or noise. The screen of the
oscilloscope was photographed, straightened with ScanDoc
(Fig. 3a), the plot points were exported with automeris.io
(Fig. 3b).
The airflow was applied using a Goocher airbrush with
0.3 mm nozzle and its 40 psi compressor. The outlet was set
at the smallest radius. No paint was inserted in the airbrush
as it was used only to create a concentrated airflow. The
outlet of the airbrush was aligned with the hair of the sensor
at different distances. A Sauermann Si-VH3 hot-wire
anemometer was used to measure the equivalent airflow
velocity produced by the airbrush at different distances. Fig.
4 shows the experimental setup.

B. Sensor output’s preliminary results
The preliminary results shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. 6 show
a clear dependency between the airflow strength and the
voltage output of the sensor (therefore with its change in
resistance). When the airbrush is turned on (to the right of
the purple line) there is a sudden negative peak, indicating a
drop in voltage across the sensor, and, consequently in its
resistance. Meanwhile, when the airbrush was turned off one
can notice a positive peak, signaling an increase in voltage
across the sensor, and thus, in resistance.

The transients shown in Fig. 6 cannot be translated to the

and the airflow velocity.
IV.

(b)

FUTURE WORK

The presented results show a promising response of the
sensor to different levels of airflow velocity. Further work
will include using a Wheatstone bridge and a differential
instrumentation amplifier. These experiments will allow for
a deeper understanding of the response of the sensor in terms
of resistance change of the coating of platinum on the 3Dprinted spring. Moreover, increasing the distance of the
airflow source can increase the noise on the airflow.
Therefore, an air compressor with variable pressure at a
short distance will be used as the source of airflow instead.
Direct export of oscilloscope data will be used instead of
automeris.io to improve the output data.
In further experiments an increase in stiffness of the
sensor was observed due to a thicker spring. This warrants
the possibility to tune the sensors for different ranges and
sensitivities of velocities, even in the same building block.

The aim of this work was to show the proof of concept of
a 3D-printed sensor inspired by scorpions’ trichobothria that
could produce an electric signal at different airflow
velocities. The objective has been achieved satisfactorily, as
shown in Fig. 6 and Fig. 7. Future work will include proper
signal conditioning and better circuitry to fully understand
the relationship between airflow and the sensor’s response.
As well as investigating changes in sensor sensitivity due to
changes in sensor stiffness.
