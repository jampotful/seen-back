# The impact of Demodex blepharitis on patient symptoms and daily life

## Abstract
SIGNIFICANCEPatients with Demodex blepharitis have a considerable symptomatic burden that negatively impacts their daily activities and well-being. Despite chronic manifestations of and problems associated with blepharitis that resulted in multiple visits to eye care providers, Demodex blepharitis remained underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed.PURPOSEThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of Demodex blepharitis on patients' daily activities and well-being.METHODSThis prospective, multicenter, observational study recruited 524 patients with Demodex blepharitis from 20 U.S. ophthalmology and optometry practices. Demodex blepharitis was diagnosed based on the presence of the following clinical manifestations in at least one eye: >10 collarettes on the upper lashes, at least mild lid margin erythema of the upper eyelid, and mite density of ≥1.0 mite/lash (upper and lower combined). Patients were asked to complete a questionnaire related to their symptoms, daily activities, and management approaches.RESULTSThe proportion of patients who experienced blepharitis symptoms for ≥2 years was 67.8%, and for ≥4 years, it was 46.5%. The three most bothersome symptoms ranked were “itchy eyes,” “dry eyes,” and “foreign body sensation.” Overall, 77.4% of patients reported that Demodex blepharitis negatively affected their daily life. One-third (32.3%) of patients had visited a doctor for blepharitis at least two times, including 19.6% who visited at least four times. Despite having clinical manifestations of Demodex blepharitis confirmed by an eye care provider, 58.7% had never been diagnosed with blepharitis. Commonly used management approaches were artificial tears, warm compresses, and lid wipes. Among those who discontinued their regimen, 45.9% had discontinued because of either tolerability issues or lack of effectiveness. Among contact lens wearers, 64.3% of the patients either were uncomfortable wearing contact lenses or experienced vision changes “sometimes” or “frequently.”CONCLUSIONDemodex blepharitis results in a significant negative impact on daily activities, creating a psychosocial and symptomatic burden on patients.
Demodex blepharitis is a common ocular inflammatory condition caused by infestation of the human ectoparasite Demodex, which is found on or within the skin, eyelashes, lash follicles, and meibomian glands. The prevalence of Demodex blepharitis has been reported to be 58% among patients visiting eye care practices across the United States.1 Despite the high prevalence of Demodex blepharitis, the condition is often overlooked. This may be due to the substantial overlap of symptoms with dry eye disease (DED), lack of awareness among eye care providers to actively look for collarettes, a pathognomonic sign of Demodex blepharitis, and, until recently, lack of an approved treatment for the disease.2,3 As such, patients with Demodex blepharitis continue to have a considerable symptomatic burden that negatively impacts various aspects of patients' daily life.
Interim results of the Atlas study that included 311 patients with Demodex blepharitis (from 8 eye care practices) demonstrated that Demodex blepharitis is associated with a symptomatic and psychosocial burden that negatively affects daily life in the vast majority of patients with the disease.4 We now report on a larger dataset of patients recruited for the Atlas study from 20 optometric and ophthalmic practices in the United States. The article aimed to validate the interim results of the Atlas study using a larger dataset and provide further insights into the impact that Demodex blepharitis has on patients' daily lives.

## METHODS
In this prospective, multicenter, cross-sectional, observational study, adult patients with Demodex blepharitis from 20 U.S. ophthalmic and optometric practices were examined clinically and asked questions about their symptoms, daily activities, and management approaches. The study was conducted in compliance with the study protocol and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. Institutional review board approval was secured for all sites from Alpha Independent Review Board (San Clemente, CA). All patients provided written informed consent using the institutional review board–approved informed consent forms.
Demodex blepharitis was diagnosed based on the presence of all of the following clinical manifestations in at least one eye: (1) >10 collarettes on the upper lashes, (2) at least mild lid margin erythema of the upper eyelid, and (3) mite density of ≥1.0 mite per lash (upper and lower eyelids combined). Mite density was assessed by selecting and removing two or more lashes from the upper and lower eyelids, with one lash from the medial and lateral half of each lid, using fine forceps. If present, lashes with collarettes were targeted for epilation. Targeted lashes were gently rotated using the Mastrota technique and then extracted.5 The lashes from each lid were individually placed in artificial tear drops on four or more respective separate glass slides with coverslips and examined under a microscope. The number of Demodex mites observed and the number of lashes epilated were recorded, and mite density was calculated as the number of mites per lash. Collarettes were graded for each eyelid using a previously described nonlinear, 0 to 4, grading scale.6 Lid margin erythema was graded on a scale from 0 to 3, as described previously.6 All investigators were trained to perform epilation and grading consistently throughout the trial.
Exclusion criteria included the use of artificial eyelashes within the previous week, presence of lid structural abnormalities (e.g., ectropion, entropion, etc.), acute ocular infection, active ocular inflammation other than blepharitis, severe dry eye, uncontrolled systemic disease, and visual acuity worse than 0.7 logMAR in either eye. Additional exclusion criteria were pregnancy (current or planned), lactation, current participation in an investigational drug or device study, or any other condition that in the investigators' opinion may confound study results, put the patient at risk, or interfere with participation.
Patients who met the study eligibility criteria were recruited and asked to complete a blepharitis questionnaire based in part on the validated BLepharItIS Symptom questionnaire (Appendix 1, available at http://links.lww.com/OPX/A710).7 Symptoms asked about were as follows: eyes that itch, aching from your eyelids, eyelids that are stuck together, red eyes or eyelids, loss of eyelashes, blurred vision, and fluctuating blurring vision. Patients were asked to rank the frequency of the symptoms experienced as “none of the time,” “occasionally,” “frequently,” or “all of the time.” A patient was considered asymptomatic if “none of the time” was chosen for all symptoms. Other questions related to the duration of symptoms, type of doctors visited, number of visits to the eye care professionals, history of diagnosis, and daily activities were also asked (Appendix 1, available at http://links.lww.com/OPX/A710).
Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and range, as applicable) were used to present the results of continuous data. The results of categorical data are presented as counts and percentages. Key study results are presented graphically.

## RESULTS
Overall, 1558 patients were screened for enrollment in the study at 20 different study sites from June 2020 to October 2021. Of these, there were 364 screen failures, 546 patients did not meet the eligibility criteria, and 124 patients declined to fill in the questionnaire form. The remaining 524 patients were included in the study.
The mean ± standard deviation age of the study participants (N = 524 patients) was 66 ± 14 years (median, 69 years; range, 19 to 94 years). The proportion of female participants was higher (56.7%) than male participants (43.3%). Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the study participants.

The analysis of questionnaire responses revealed that less than 1% (4 of 515) of patients were asymptomatic. The proportion of patients with two or more symptoms was 98.3% (506 of 515; Fig. 1A). Nearly two-thirds of patients (352 of 519) had been experiencing blepharitis symptoms for ≥2 years and 46.5% for ≥4 years (Fig. 1B). When asked about the symptoms that bothered the patients over the previous month, the three most common symptoms ranked as bothersome were “eyes that itch,” “dry eyes,” and “foreign body sensation” (Fig. 2). In terms of frequency, “dry eyes,” “eyes that itch,” and “irritated eyes” were experienced “frequently” or “all of the time” by 56.3% (282 of 501), 45.8% (233 of 509), and 39.5% (197 of 498) of patients, respectively (Fig. 3).

When patients were asked about how Demodex blepharitis affected their daily life overall, 77.4% (391 of 505) answered that they had been experiencing negative effects in some way. Stratifying the different aspects of daily life on the basis of psychosocial metrics, 42.4% (214 of 505) of patients reported difficulty with night driving, 42.0% (212 of 505) said that they were conscious of their eyes all day, 30.3% (153 of 505) reported needing additional time for their daily hygiene routine, and 23.2% (117 of 505) reported that their eyes or eyelids gave them an unattractive appearance. Blepharitis symptoms were also found to affect the mental state of a small proportion (6.7% [34 of 505]) of patients. Among women, 33.1% (95 of 287) had difficulty wearing eye makeup, and 2.1% (6 of 287) reported discomfort with artificial lashes or reduced artificial eyelash wear (Fig. 4).
Proportion of patients reporting the impact Demodex blepharitis has on various aspects of daily life (n = 505). Overall, 77.4% of patients reported that Demodex blepharitis negatively affected their daily life.
One-third (32.3% [89 of 275]) of patients said that they had visited a doctor for blepharitis at least two times, including 19.6% (54 of 275) who went to the doctor at least four times. When asked about the type of health care specialist the patients visited for their clinical or patient-reported outcomes of blepharitis, of the total 504 respondents, 54.0% (272 of 504) consulted an ophthalmologist, 38.1% (192 of 504) consulted an optometrist, and 7.9% (40 of 504) consulted a primary care physician.
When asked about any other previously diagnosed lid margin or ocular surface disease, 75.1% (283 of 377) of patients said they had been diagnosed with DED, 4.0% with rosacea, and 13.8% with both DED and rosacea. Although all subjects in the present study had clinical manifestations of Demodex blepharitis confirmed by an eye care provider during the study, 59% (291 of 496) said that they had never previously been diagnosed with blepharitis.
Of the current options for managing Demodex blepharitis, the most commonly used approaches were artificial tears, warm compresses, and lid wipes (Fig. 5). Among patients who stopped using their regimen(s) before this study, half of them (45.9%) discontinued because of either tolerability issues or lack of effectiveness of their selected regimen(s). When asked about in-office procedures, only a small proportion (6.6% [33 of 502]) of patients reported that they had undergone microblepharoexfoliation, thermal pulsation, or other procedures.
Proportion of patients who are currently using or have discontinued/never used various management approaches for Demodex blepharitis symptoms. Among patients who stopped their regimen(s), 45.9% discontinued because of tolerability issues or lack of effectiveness.
Among patients who were contact lens wearers, 64.3% (9 of 14) experienced either discomfort wearing contact lenses or vision changes (“sometimes” or “frequently”; Fig. 6). Among those who experienced vision changes, 50.0% (7 of 14) reported the changes as “intensely notable.”

## DISCUSSION
Demodex blepharitis is a common ocular inflammatory disease characterized most commonly by eye redness and ocular irritation.8 Although the disease shares symptoms with other ocular conditions seen in optometric practice, accurate identification is important.3 Inaccurate diagnosis may prevent or delay appropriate treatment and lead to disease progression, more frequent eye care visits, and increased health care costs.1
Assessment of ocular symptoms in the study showed dryness, itching, and foreign body sensation to be the top three most bothersome symptoms occurring in nearly half of the patients. Such frequent and bothersome symptoms affected patients' daily activities, with more than 40% of patients experiencing difficulty when driving at night and nearly one-third requiring additional time for their daily hygiene routine. These results of the present Atlas continuation study are in alignment with previously reported results from a smaller interim dataset.4
The symptoms of Demodex blepharitis also affected patients' emotional and psychosocial health, with more than 40% of patients feeling conscious of their eyes all day, nearly a quarter (23%) reporting that their eyes or eyelids gave them a negative appearance, and 20% reporting constantly being worried about their eyes. It has been reported that patients with blepharitis symptoms have a 52% higher risk of depression and a 67% higher risk of anxiety compared with those without blepharitis.9 Among woman patients, nearly one-third reported difficulty wearing eye makeup due to the presence of Demodex blepharitis. Also, 2.1% of the female patients reported discomfort with artificial lashes or reduced artificial eyelash wear. Artificial eyelash wear discomfort can be attributed to many factors; allergy to the adhesive used for lash application, altered blink reflex, or altered air flow across the ocular surface should be considered, along with Demodex blepharitis, as potential causes for discomfort and reduced wear time.10
It is believed that contact lens wear may exacerbate microbial accumulation and provide a favorable environment for Demodex mites to proliferate.11 Jalbert and Rejab11 reported that Demodex mites were present in 90% of contact lens wearers compared with 65% of nonwearers. Tarkowski et al.12 found 93% of contact lens–intolerant patients to be positive for Demodex mite infestation, indicating an association between Demodex mite infestation and contact lens intolerance or abandonment. Although there were only 14 contact lens wearers in the present study, 64% of them reported blurred/foggy vision and discomfort while wearing contact lenses at least “sometimes” to “frequently.” Similar to the literature, these findings suggest that the presence of Demodex mites may lead to contact lens discomfort and intolerance. Although it is commonly believed that Demodex infestation affects elderly patients, studies have shown that patients of all ages (including demographic groups more likely to be wearing contact lenses) have Demodex blepharitis.1,13,14 Future studies in younger patient populations with the goal of including more contact lens wearers would be necessary to better understand the impact of Demodex blepharitis on contact lens comfort, vision with contact lenses, wear patterns, and persistence or discontinuation of lenses.
Despite chronic problems associated with blepharitis that often resulted in multiple visits to eye care providers, Demodex blepharitis was likely underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed by providers. Approximately 68% of patients experienced clinical manifestations or symptoms consistent with Demodex blepharitis for more than 2 years (Fig. 1B), 32% of patients consulted an eye care provider at least twice, and 20% visited their eye care provider at least four times. However, nearly 60% of patients never received a diagnosis of blepharitis, let alone a diagnosis of Demodex blepharitis. Accurate diagnosis of Demodex blepharitis depends on a detailed clinical evaluation and an awareness of how common the disease is.15 Direct observation of the lid margin under high magnification at the slit lamp can be helpful in identifying infestation by Demodex. Other methods include in vivo confocal microscopy of the lash base and lateral eyelash tensioning or rotation in situ at the slit lamp.16–18 Collarettes, the pathognomonic sign of Demodex blepharitis, can be easily identified at the slit lamp by instructing the patient to look down or close their eyes to obtain a clear view of the base of the upper lash margin. Incorporating this simple slit-lamp examination into routine clinical practice can facilitate the diagnosis of Demodex blepharitis.
Historically, a number of prescription medications such as topical antibiotics, steroid ointments, oral antibiotics, or anthelmintics, as well as over-the-counter or naturally derived products such as tea tree oil, artificial tears, lid wipes, and warm compresses, were used to manage Demodex blepharitis symptoms.19–26 However, there is uncertainty related to the effectiveness of such treatments. The results of the present study demonstrated that 45.9% of patients who stopped using these products discontinued them because of tolerability, lack of efficacy, or other issues.
This study has some limitations due to its observational nature. Also, the study findings are based on patients' recollection of symptoms, attempted therapies, doctor visits, prior diagnoses, and how they feel about their daily life due to blepharitis, which may introduce recall bias. The symptoms of blepharitis overlap with other comorbid ocular surface conditions. In the present study, we did not assess comorbid ocular conditions or ocular surface clinical characteristics of the subjects. This can also be considered a limitation of the study. Subject to the aforementioned limitations, the findings of the present study should be interpreted accordingly.
To summarize, Demodex blepharitis has a considerable functional and psychosocial impact on patients, affecting daily activities and well-being. Patients are symptomatic and actively seeking relief, as the present study shows, yet they may not be correctly diagnosed. Therefore, earlier identification of Demodex blepharitis, along with safe, tolerable, and effective therapies that have undergone regulatory approval for this disease, is essential.
