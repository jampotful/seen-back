Laser-based 3D printing and optical characterization of optical micro-nanostructures inspired by nocturnal insects compound eyes.
Calin BS, Popescu RC, Tanasa E, Paun IA. (2025). Scientific reports. . doi:10.1038/s41598-025-85829-y. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Laser-based 3D printing and optical characterization of optical micro-nanostructures inspired by nocturnal insects compound eyes

## Abstract
Nature offers unique examples that help humans produce artificial systems which mimic specific functions of living organisms and provide solutions to complex technical problems of the modern world. For example, the development of 3D micro-nanostructures that mimic nocturnal insect eyes (optimized for night vision), emerges as promising technology for detection in IR spectral region. Here, we report a proof of principle concerning the design and laser 3D printing of all ultrastructural details of nocturnal moth Grapholita Funebrana eyes, for potential use as microlens arrays for IR detection systems. Optimized computer-aided design and laser writing parameters enabled us to reproduce the entire complex architecture of moth compound eyes, with submicrometric spatial accuracy. As such, the laser-imprinted structures consisted in ommatidia-like microstructures with average diameter of about 14 μm, decorated with nipple-like nanopillars between 200 and 400 nm in height and average periodicity of around 450 nm. The dimensions of moth-eye inspired structures deviated by less than 10% from the natural corresponding structures. The optical properties of the moth eyes-inspired microlens arrays were investigated in the infrared (IR) range, between 1000 and 1700 nm. The optical transmission of microlens arrays with nanopillars was up to 17.55% higher than the transmission through microlens arrays without nanopillars. Moreover, the reflection of nanopillar-decorated microlens arrays was up to 0.91% lower than the reflection for microlenses without nanopillars. In addition, the focal spot diameter at 1/e2 for nanopillar—decorated microlens arrays was of 7.64 μm, representing and improvement of 16.5% of focal spot diameter as compared to microlens arrays without nanopillars. Similarly with the IR region, the reflection measured in the Visible range was higher for microlense arrays with nanopillars than the reflection through microlenses arrays without nanopillars. In contrast, in the Visible range the transmission of nanopillar-decorated microlens arrays was lower than the one for microlense arrays without nanopillars, which could be, most likely, assigned to diffraction losses on the nanopillars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-85829-y.

## Introduction
Over time, nature has inspired the development of various structures that mimic unique features found in nature that provide solutions for complex technical problems of the contemporary world1. This inspiration from nature opened a new field of research and development, known as biomimetics2. During the last years, along with the evolution of fabrication and characterization techniques, biomimetics reached a refined replication of natural structures, down micro-nanometric scales2,3. By applying the construction principles of natural organisms, scientists have developed new materials and technologies that mimic the micro–nano structures found in living organisms, leading to advances in areas such as controlled cellular adhesion4–7, superhydrophobicity8–10, enhanced mechanical properties11–13, and tailored optical properties10,14. For example, the microstructures from insect wings and plant leaves have inspired the development of structures with self-cleaning15 or anti-reflective10 properties. The lotus effect has been an inspiration for superhydrophobic surfaces mimicking the micro and nano-scaled structures from the lotus flower leaves, which reduced the adhesion of water and contaminants9,16,17. For example, biomimetic structures at micro- and nanoscale can manipulate light in unique ways, enabling innovations such as modulated diffraction18,19 improved light trapping10,20,21, and tailored reflectivity10,14,22.
Biomimetic structures have been obtained using fabrication methods that provide precise control over the arrangement and dimensions of micro/nano-scaled features, such as photolithography11, soft lithography5, electrospinning23, nanoimprint lithography24,25, focused ion beam lithography26, direct laser writing8,27. Among these, Laser Direct Writing via Two Photon Polymerization (LDW via TPP) technique provides unique advantages regarding the fabrication of high spatial resolution (below 100 nm), three-dimensional, arbitrary micro/nanostructures8.
Biomimetic photonic structures are of interest to the scientific community owing to their intricate design, function, highly optimized topology, and optical behavior, all obtained via natural evolution. In this context, arthropod eyes have been a source of inspiration for the construction of specialized imaging systems such as apposition and compound eye cameras28,29. In particular, moth-eye-inspired structures are of special interest for their use as either perfect absorbers or high-efficiency anti reflective coatings, for applications as solar cells30–32, display screens33, light emitting diodes34 and others. Moths have sophisticated compound eyes with superposition optics able to transmit well-resolved images and process colors, even in lightless environments35. For example, the visual system of moth family Ostrinia nubilalis comprises a pair of compound eyes, approximately 1.5 mm in diameter, each containing few thousands ommatidia of a diameter of around 15 μm in diameter, acting as microlenses. Ommatidia’s outer surfaces are covered with nanopillars, known as “corneal nipples”35. Moth compound eyes enhance dim-light vision by overlapping images from different facets /ommatidia that consequently increase the image brightness.
Despite the high interest in mimicking these structures, the existing technologies have not been able to reproduce the entire complex architecture of moth compound eye, only one component being fabricated. More specifically, some of the studies addressed exclusively the fabrication of compound eye-like curved structures (ommatidia-like) i.e. without nipple-like nanostructures ontop36,37. Another part of the existing studies reported only on the fabrication of corneal-nipples-like nanostructures on flat substrates (and not on curved ones, like in natural moth-eyes ommatidia)38–42. The third limitation relates to research conducted for the development of microlens arrays, positioned on curved surfaces, but without having corneal-nipple-like nanopillars on top37,43.
In this study, we overcome the above limitations through an innovative approach regarding the design and single-step fabrication of moth-eye-inspired microlenses arrays, for potential applications as optical filters. The scientific added value of our work consists of the fact that our biomimetic structures mimicked the whole complex architecture of the compound eyes of adult Grapholita Funebrana moths. As such, the artificial biomimetic structures we developed consist in ommatidia-like microstructures decorated by nipple-like nanopillars that followed the curvature of the former. Moreover, the diameters of ommatidia-like microstructures reported in this study, as well as the heights and periodicities of the nipple-like nanopillars on top were in the same range as the corresponding structures from the moth eyes, with less than 10% deviation. The moth-eye-inspired biomimetic structures were fabricated by Laser Direct Writing via Two Photon Polymerization of IP-Dip photopolymeric resin. The ommatidia-like parts of the structures, aimed to act as microlenses, were positioned in a hexagonal lattice on a flat glass substrate, and the nipple-like nanopillars decorating them followed the curved surface of each ommatidium-like structure. Our biomimetic structures have the potential to be used as optical filters in the Near-Infrared spectral range.
The proposed protocol for developing the sub-wavelength moth-eye-inspired structures has three essential advantages that makes this approach unique: (1) The single-step laser direct writing technique employed for fabricating the structures allows for full reproducibility regarding the 3D biomimetic moth-eye inspired structures; (2) The fine tunning of the laser writing parameters enables a high control over the dimensions of the nipple-like nanostructures; (3) The chosen fabrication method allows for fully customizable micro-nanostructures with complex architectures.
Until present, the fabrication and optical characterization of individual components of insect compound eyes has been performed in the visible light region33,34. However, it must not be neglected that the compound eyes of nocturnal insects (such as moths) possess high efficiency for night vision. The development of 3D microstructures, which mimic the complex architecture of nocturnal insects, emerges as promising technology for detection in the IR spectral region44–48. Nowadays, the design and fabrication of compact lightweight optics with a multi-aperture and large field of view in Near-IR is indispensable for growing applications such as robot vision49, autonomous navigation50, detection of gas traces50,51 and multi-target trackin28,52. Until now, the fabrication of IR optical devices derived from the natural structure of nocturnal insects compound eyes, has been limited to reshaping a flexible planar microlens array, which caused morphology distortions due to the non-uniform distribution of strain forces applied53. More recent advances by maskless 3D micro-manufacturing by using lasers with ultrashort pulses resulted in high-quality optics on free-form surfaces, but the obtained structures were in the macroscopic scale54,55.
In this work we report a proof of principle regarding the design, fabrication and optical characterization of bioinspired microlens arrays, similar to compound eyes of nocturnal insects (Grapholita Funebrana moth). In order to provide systematic and relevant data for the development of accurate IR optical detection systems, we evaluated the optical properties of the bioinspired microlens arrays (transmission, reflection and focusing properties) in the near-infrared spectral region.

## Materials and methods

## Structures design
Design was realized using Python programming language, with SciPy and Matplotlib as the main working libraries. Running the Python script resulted in several files containing information appropriate for use with Nanoscribe’s DeScribe software (.gwl files containing laser writing parameters and Cartesian coordinates for all points defining the microstructures, in a specific writing order). Design was verified and validated before fabricating microstructures via Matplotlib’s plotting functions, as well as DeScribe simulation capabilities, which can trace and simulate the whole fabrication process.

## Structures fabrication
The structures discussed in this paper are designed to have multi-scale geometric features similar to the compound eyes of Grapholita Funebrana moth. The unit structure is comprised of a microlens with a parabolic cross-section of the surface, which mimics an ommatidium, on top of which there are nanopillars that follow the surface of the microlens, which mimic the nipple-like nanostructures from the moth eyes.
Nanostructures can be obtained through different methods, among which laser irradiation is often encountered36–38,56. However, many of these methods rely on laser-matter interaction phenomena that are inherently stochastic, such as laser-matter interaction near or over ablation threshold of the target material39,40,56, phenomena which do not allow for a good control over the shape, size and position of nanostructures. While this offers some advantages, such as faster processing, applicable to a wide range of materials, broader selection of laser sources, it also limits the reproducibility and control over structure geometries. The structures discussed in this paper have been fabricated using LDW via TPP technique, whose working principle is shown in Fig. 1a.

Also known as “3D Lithography”, LDW via TPP uses ultrashort laser pulses, usually with an infrared region central wavelength, that are tightly focused inside a photoresist transparent to its central wavelength. The laser intensity in the spatial region close to the focal point reaches above the threshold where two-photon phenomena become the main laser-matter interaction mechanism. The two-photon absorption process initiates a specific chemical reaction such as chain polymerization. Tuning the laser intensity in the focal point enables the effective laser processing volume (volume pixel, or “voxel”) to reach dimensions below the diffraction limit of the focusing optical system, enabling high spatial resolution of the imprinted structures.
LDW via TPP is an additive manufacturing technique that is used to construct arbitrary 3D microstructures with controlled and reproducible geometries, with sub-micrometer spatial resolution41. This resolution is achieved due to laser processing below the diffraction limit, which is achievable through multi-photon interaction. The photoresist is transparent to the incident laser radiation, whose central wavelength is 780 nm. However, photoinitiator molecules show strong optical absorption for the second harmonic of 390 nm. In order to obtain a volume pixel (i.e. “voxel”) below the diffraction limit, in a controllable and reproducible manner, a high repetition rate femtosecond laser source is used (80 MHz, 120 fs, Erbium-doped fiber laser, frequency-doubled). Incident radiation is focused using a photoresist-immersed 100x (N.A. = 1.3) microscope objective, inside a drop-casted transparent photoresist (IP-Dip) on a glass substrate 22 × 22 × 0.17 mm. Incident laser radiation does not interact with the photoresist until volumetric energy density goes above the two-photon absorption threshold. Once this condition is achieved, photoinitiator molecules present in the photoresist interact with the second harmonic, become ionized and free radicals are formed. These ionized photoinitiator molecules form chemical bonds with monomer molecules encountered inside the photoresist. The newly bonded molecules retain active terminations. As such, bonding continues to take place, increasing the polymeric chain, until a chemical bond is formed with another molecule (or polymeric chain) that also has an active termination. This process is known as “chain polymerization” and results in randomly positioned, entangled polymeric chains that form a solid material in the voxel, i.e. the photoresist volume where laser intensity was above two-photon absorption threshold.
In this paper, we used Nanoscribe Photonic Professional, which is a LDW via TPP installation that uses a Er-doped femtosecond fiber laser (80 MHz, 120 fs, diode pumped, frequency-doubled via a periodically poled Li: NbO3 crystal). The photoresist used in this paper is IP-Dip, from Nanoscribe GmbH, which is a UV-curable negative photoresist optimized for high resolution two photon polymerization. The photoresist is a transparent viscous liquid that is drop-casted on a fused quartz glass slide. Laser pulses are delivered via a 100x, N.A.=1.3, polymer-immersed microscope objective. Samples have been fabricated with a writing speed of 90 μm/s and laser powers between 9.6 and 12 mW. After the laser writing process, samples have been developed via immersion in propylene glycol methyl ether acetate (PGMEA) for 13 min, followed by rinsing with Novec 7100 and air drying.

## Structures characterization
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) investigations of structure morphology was performed using a FEI Quanta Inspect F Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (Hillsboro, OR, USA). For accurate quantitative evaluation of the nanopillar heights and periodicity, the samples were investigated by Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), using a commercial system XE100 - Park Systems, Suwon, Republic of Korea. The travel of the AFM needle allowed us to scan 8 × 8 m2 areas on each ommatidium-like microlens.
To check the accuracy of our biomimetic approach, the laser-imprinted (i.e. artificial) moth eye-like structures was tested by comparison with the structure of natural moth eyes. For this, the first generation of Grapholita Funebrana moths were captured using dedicated pheromone traps which were mounted on chemically untreated plum trees in the town of Urlati, Prahova county, Romania, at the end of April – beginning of May 2024. They were enclosed in a small vial with a drop of 2% OsO4 for 24 h, and subsequently air-dried. Preparation of moth samples for scanning electron microscopy investigation was done via fixing the specimens in glutaraldehyde 2.5% in PBS for one hour, at room temperature, followed by dehydration in solutions of ethanol and ethanol: hexamethyl disilazane of increasing concentrations. The specimens were then sputtered with 10 nm gold thin film and examined using FEI Quanta Inspect F Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (Hillsboro, OR, USA).
For optical characterization of the samples, we measured the transmission and reflection spectra, as well as the focal distance and focal point transverse intensity distribution. The incident light source was delivered by a supercontinuum laser system (NKT SuperK Extreme EXW-4). Data acquisition was done using an Optical Spectrum Analyzer (OSA), Ando Aq-6317 C, for IR analysis and Ocean Optics QE65000 for Visible range analysis. We measured the optical transmission between 300 and 750 nm and 1000–1700 nm respectively. Transmission spectrum has been measured using an uniaxial setup (shown in Supplementary Information, Fig. SI.1). The incident light source was collimated and its diameter reduced via an aperture in order to obtain a parallel beam with a diameter that covers the microlens array. For IR spectrum, both transmitted and reflected light were collected using a 5x/N.A. 0.14 Plan Apochromat microscope objective with a working distance of 34 mm (Edmund Optics ELWD 59876), subsequently focused using a 50 mm N-BK7 plano-convex lens with AR coating for 1050–1700 nm, on the ferrule of a multimode fluorozirconate (ZBLAN) fiber optic patch cable with a core diameter of 100 μm, N.A. 0.2. For the Visible range, the transmission and reflection were measured using a QP400-1-VIS-NIR fiber optic patch cable from Ocean Optics with a core diameter of 400 μm, N.A. 0.22. The reflection spectrum was measured using the same optical components, but the incident light source and the light collecting components were positioned at 20 degrees incidence and reflection angles, respectively (shown in Supplementary Information, Fig. SI.1). In both cases, the free beam spectrum has been measured as a reference and experimental data is shown relative to the free beam. Transmission and reflection spectra have been measured for microlens arrays with and without nanopillars.
Focusing characteristics have been measured using a Carl Zeiss Axio Observer camera and a Zeiss LD Plan-Neofluar 63x, N.A. 0.75 microscope objective. Camera parameters were set so that intensity saturation would not occur (i.e. min/max settings). Sample positioning was achieved using the same high resolution positioning system that was used for fabrication. We used a mounted LED system (Thorlabs M625L2, 625 nm) as the incident light source for determining focusing characteristics. In order to determine the focal distance, the system was first focused on the base of the microlens array, using the autofocus procedure of the Nanoscribe system on the surface of the glass substrate next to the microlens array. The objective was then moved away from the microlens system until the focal spot was the smallest. Focus spot characteristics were analyzed using Fiji (ImageJ) software. For image processing, the diameter of a microlens was taken as reference. Fiji was used to generate an intensity (gray levels) surface plot of an isolated focal spot, as well as the transverse intensity profile (gray levels; horizontal), which was fitted with a no-offset Gaussian function. Focusing characteristics have been measured for microlens arrays with and without nanopillars.

## Results and discussions
The application targeted by our study concerns the design and fabrication of compound biomimetic eye for potential use in IR detection systems. For such applications, the biomimetic structures must posses specific optical properties in IR, such as high transmission vs. low reflexivity44. For this purpose, measuring the optical properties of the obtained microlenses in the infrared range rather than the visible spectrum is necessary. For example, Liu et al.44 reported on 3D IR artificial compound eye fabricated form thermoplastic polymers and provided evidence regarding high IR transparency of the optical biomimetic structures. In this case, the functionality of the microlenses must be optimized for IR wavelengths, which makes it crucial to measure the optical properties (such as transmission and reflexivity) in the IR range rather than the VIS spectrum. Also the biomimicry of the natural lenses (the nocturnal moth eye), which are optimized for IR sensing (vision in the dark) rather than VIS sensing (vision in the daytime) suggests that measuring our biomimetic compound eye optical properties in the IR range is more relevant. The group of Zhang57 has designed a morpho butterfly compound eye camera and has employed a NIR light source (850 nm) to prove the efficiency of this device in medical applications such as vascular enhancement imaging. As such, measuring the optical properties of the compound eye biomimetic structures developed in this study in the IR rather than the VIS spectrum range was determined by the specific application targeted by out work, as well as the biomimetic inspiration, which is the nocturnal moth eye. In this context and based on the above arguments, the IR measurements provide the most relevant data for optimizing the design and function of the proposed system. Song et al.43 have developed an apposition camera by fabricating microlenses on an elastomeric surface, where each microlens focused the incident light into an independent photodiode. Hu et al.37 designed a compound eye camera using a standard CMOS detector; the optical system used 3D-printed logarithmic microlens arrays, instead of traditional spherical microlenses; the logarithmic lens profile increased the depth of field and focus range significantly, which allowed each microlens of the curved array to focus incident light on a flat standard CMOS detector.
It is important to mention that the moth-eye inspired structures presented in this work are not strictly biomimetic, but rather bio-inspired. We used the concept of biomimicry in synergy with the advantages of LDW via TPP technique used for structures fabrication, in order to obtain structures that closely resemble the compound eyes of nocturnal insects, both topologically and optically.
We can also observe that the displacement of the nanopillars on the ommatidium surface has a quasi-concentric arrangement (Fig. SI.3), although a close-up inspection reveals various smaller domains where the corneal nipples are locally arranged in a hexagonal pattern. These nanostructures were found to reduce the surface reflection and improve light transmission, ensuring that more light reaches the photoreceptor cells, which enhances the overall sensitivity and efficiency of the ommatidium58.
Belušič et al.35 showed that the visual system of moth family Ostrinia Nubilalis moths comprises a pair of compound eyes, having approximately 1.5 mm in diameter and having the same ultrastructure and physiological properties for males and females. It was found that each compound eye contained approximately 3000 ommatidia having a diameter of about 15 μm. The outer surface of these ommatidia acted as a corneal lens and was decorated with nanostructures known as corneal nipples35. Given that all compound eyes have only few thousands of ommatidia, these have limited spatial resolution. However, the compound eyes are well spread among arthropods species, providing a reasonable modular and panoramic vision. Diurnal arthropods such as butterflies, bees and shore crabs, have a simpler eye structure known as apposition compound eye, where each corneal facet forms the image on a single photoreceptor within the same ommatidium. These eyes provide images with relatively good spatial resolution, but with poor sensitivity, the light being collected within the diameter of one corneal facet acting as a small aperture. On the other side, the dim-light vision characteristic for moths, mayflies, and deep-sea crustaceans, is performed with superposition compound eyes that increase the image brightness by overlapping images obtained from different facets, with the cost of poor image sharpness42.
Back in 1962, it was reported that the face lenses in the moth-eyes consist in arrays of pronounced protuberances named corneal nipples, which were arranged in domains with hexagonal packing59. It was reported that the distance between adjacent nipples varied between 180 and 240 nm, while their heights ranged between 0 and 230 nm. Initially, it was considered that these arrays of corneal nipples had the role of decreasing the reflectance of the facet lens surface of the moth eyes thus increasing their transmittance, in this way enhancing the light sensitivity vision for these insects. This hypothesis was invalidated by experimental results, which indicated that the corneal nipples reduced the reflectance of the facet lens from 4% to only about 1%, thus the transmittance undergoing only a slight increase from 96 to 99%. A second, and more adequate, hypothesis was that the corneal nipple arrays play a role of “camouflage”, by which they decrease the eye’s reflectance during the day to avoid the glittering of moth eyes that would be otherwise easily detected by predators. Although this latter hypothesis seemed valid, it was not demonstrated experimentally. The research that followed showed that the corneal nipple arrays are often encountered among insects. As such, in 1970, Bernhard et al.43 centralized measurements on corneal nipples from 361 insect species and identified three types of corneal nipples, as a function of their height: class I corneas with nipples less than 50 nm in height, class II corneas with “low-sized nipples” i.e. with heights between 50 and 200 nm, and class III corneas with so-called “full-sized nipples”, with heights of about 250 nm.
A recent morphological study of nocturnal moth Daphnis Nerii60 has shown that the surface of moth compound eyes consists of regularly arranged hexagonal facets filled with corneal nipples. It is worth noting that the hexagonal periodicity does not homogeneously extend over the whole surface of the ommatidia, but is rather organized in several independent domains that each have a hexagonal periodicity inside (named “facets”). These domains, along with the hemispherical surface of the ommatidia, determines an overall quasi-circular positioning of the hexagonal facets. A detailed analysis by high-resolution scanning electron microscopy has shown that the corneal nipples within the individual hexagonal facets have an intricate hexagonal i.e. periodical arrangement, with average nipple diameter of 140/165 nm (Male/Female) and average inter-nipple separation of 165 nm. Petros et al.61 reported on the diffractive behavior of moth-eye-inspired nanopillar arrays, i.e. diffraction given by sub-wavelength silicon nanopillar arrays. They have demonstrated that the existience of local domains of hexagonally-packed nanopillar, domains also emphasized by Shamprasad et al.60, exhibits low diffraction intensity and efficient AR properties. As such, the microlens arrays presented in this study have been designed with a spiral-like arrangement of the nanopillars in order to have closely packed nanopillars with lower periodicity, similar to natural moth eyes.
Bio-inspired nipple-like nanostructures on flat substrates have been obtained by Sun et al.41, via Roll-to-Plate ultraviolet nanoimprint lithography, which resulted in antireflective surfaces in the visible range (380–760 nm). In the same line, Kuo et al.42 used a complex multistep fabrication based on Langmuir-Blodgett deposition, oxygen plasma etching, and nanoimprint lithography for obtaining nipple-inspired antireflective glass surfaces, having an average reflectance lower than 1% and a transmittance of approximately 95%, in the visible range.
Nocturnal moth eyes of species such as Philosamia cynthia ricini are characterized by a microstructure of protuberances of 250 nm height and hundreds of nm periodicity (known as corneal nipples), which form a systematic hexagonal array62. Other species of moths and butterflies are also characterized by submicrometric patterned body parts, such as eyes or wings63. The eye surface of nocturnal insects such as moths, featuring nano-patterned structures with nipple-like projections, helps these insects maintain a clear vision in low-light conditions and minimize the light reflection from their compound eyes, which aids to avoiding detection by nocturnal predators62. The nipple-like nanostructures enable a gradual refractive index transition between the air and the eye’s surface, preventing an abrupt shift in the refractive index encountered by the incident light. This smooth transition minimizes the amount of light which otherwise would be reflected because of a sudden change of the optical properties of the medium64.
Refractive index gradient induced by surface nanostructures contributes extensively to antireflective (AR) behavior. A relationship between the effective refractive index and the height of the sub-wavelength structures has been documented, highlighting the importance of this structural parameter in optimizing AR performance62. At the interface between two media we encounter the Fresnel reflection of the incident light, given the separation of refractive indexes of the two media. Insertion of an intermediate index layer or a stepped index multi-layer at the interface would divide the discontinuity into smaller steps, leading to reduced reflectance65.
A more efficient biomimetic antireflective strategy should consider the morphology and dimension of component nanostructures such as the case of the nano-pillars in the moth eye. The height, the shape, as well as the distance between the nano-pillars collaboratively influence the optical properties of the resulted device66. There are many examples in the scientific literature of experimental of theoretical studies assessing the effects of one or two structural factors (e.g. shape, height) on the overall optical characteristics67,68. However, the morphology and dimensions of antireflective structures for the intended functionality have not yet been fully optimized. Most studies focus on non-transparent silicon moth-eye surfaces due to their ease of fabrication and well-established techniques, while only a limited number of studies address the antireflective properties of transparent surfaces, which present challenges in achieving precise nanostructuring66. Transparent materials are characterized by different optical properties compared to semiconductors, which have inherent light absorption properties. It has been proved that the reflectance spectrum of similar structures can be characterized by wavelength modulations, which depend on the nanopillars heights. Similar studies have addressed the ability to tune reflection color of the transparent lens, by modifying the height of the nanopillars. Moreover, it was shown that the flat and low value reflectance spectrum in the visible range wavelength can be modulated with two-sided nanostructuring69,70.
Ji et al.66 have shown that the reflectance spectra characterizes the relationship between the maximum and minimum values of the specific wavelengths and nanopillars heights, respectively: as the height of the nanopillars increases, the maximum and minimum reflectance peaks in the spectra shift towards the infrared (IR) range. These phenomena have been previously found in thin oxidized silicon films having an interference hue71. Put differently, this reflectance characteristic arises from the interference of light reflected at both the top and bottom of the periodic nanopillar array. Despite the nanopillars being made from the same material as the substrate, the moth-eye nanostructuring introduces a heterogeneous optical interface, blending air with the SiO₂ substrate.
Based on the findings regarding height-dependent antireflective properties, the reflectance can be modulated within a specific wavelength range by adjusting the height of the nanopillars. Our experimental approach (using LDW via TPP) allows for the modulation of the morphological parameters at micro-nanoscale66. However, further investigation is necessary in order to experimentally determine the precise influence of nanopillar heights on microlens optical behavior.
In our study, we first designed moth-eye-inspired structures so that they closely replicate the complex structure and dimensions of each part of a moth-eye. As such, Fig. 1b shows the laser path for the microlens i.e. ommatidium-like microstructure as well as the array of nanopillars on top i.e. the corneal nipples. Both structures followed a spiral-like design, chosen specifically to optimize fabrication time and surface quality. These two spiral-like structures, i.e. microlens and array of nanopillars, do not overlap (see Supplementary Information Fig. SI.2). This happens because the spacing of consecutive spirals for the microlens i.e. ommatidium-like structure was chosen so that voxels overlap appropriately, to obtain a mechanically stable surface. In the case of nanopillars, however, the objective was for neighboring nanopillars to not overlap at all, to obtain a spacing between neighboring nanopillars similar to moth-eye corneal nipples. The nanopillar array shows regular but varying spacing between neighboring pillars and followed the curvature of the microlenses (see Fig. 1b).
In order to print the nanopillar array along the surface of a microlens, we first performed a parametric study concerning the two main steps involved in structure fabrication: the design - that theoretically establishes 3D architectures and dimensions of structures - and the laser writing parameters - that experimentally determine the actual fabrication of the structures by LDW via TPP. This was done because the theoretical design accounts for laser paths, i.e. trajectories of the voxel, while the experimental feedback loop takes the energy dose into consideration and, implicitly, voxel size. This is relevant for microstructure design because it has a considerable influence over obtainable nanopillar heights, as well as their diameter. The polymerization process takes place virtually as long as a particular volume is irradiated. Longer irradiation times of a region in space determines an accumulation of polymeric chains, which means a larger resulting voxel, therefore a larger nanopillar (both diameter and height). As such, fine-tuning the geometry of the nanopillar array is done through both design and laser parameters, most notably the energy dose. We changed the theoretical (i.e. defined by laser path) heights of the pillars, in order to experimentally obtain pillar heights of 300 nm. Nanopillars are slim structures (pillar diameter equal to a single voxel diameter) and need to be mechanically stable, as well as to have an appropriate anchoring to the underlying support structure (ommatidium-like microlens). To obtain this, the incident laser beam was focused below the surface, to provide mechanical stability and appropriate adherence to the surface of the microlens.
For samples with low nanopillar height, the irregular surface periodicity is determined mostly by a mismatch between the voxels that describe the support surface of the nanopillars i.e. the fingerprint-like structures, and the nanopillars written on top. This mismatch was present for all samples, but was more obvious for structures having lower heights of the nanopillars due to small differences in height. In previous studies72, we reported that the laser scan speed has no determining influence over structure shapes and dimensions. Therefore, in this work, we emphasized the role of laser power on the height and periodicity of the nanopillars, while the scan speed was kept constant at 90 μm/s.
The irradiation time is correlated with a so-called “polymerization degree”, which indicates the percentage of monomer in the voxel that formed chemical bonds and resulted in a polymer. This polymerization degree directly influences the porosity, mechanical properties and shrinkage phenomena following sample development41. A low polymerization degree results in soft structures that show high shrinkage, which increases the probability of microstructure bending and detaching from the substrate. A high polymerization degree results in structures with higher tensile strength and low shrinkage. In order to obtain a high polymerization degree, an appropriate energy dose is required. In this case, ”energy dose” refers to the overall energy deposited in a unit volume of photoresist. Energy dose is directly determined by both incident laser intensity and irradiation time. A high polymerization degree can be obtained using either lower laser intensities and long irradiation times, or higher laser intensities and short irradiation times. Using lower laser intensities and longer irradiation times can significantly increase the fabrication time for complex microstructures. Using higher laser intensities determines a shorter fabrication time, but also increases the probability of micro-bubble formation. Micro-bubbles form when the laser intensity is high enough to locally produce gaseous products, through a series of mechanisms (thermal, near field intensification, etc.). An expanding micro-bubble can destroy neighboring microstructures and affect laser focusing in the immediate area, therefore hindering the fabrication process. The photoresist we used is IP-Dip (Nanoscribe GmbH), a viscous, liquid, negative photoresist that can be used in a “dip-in” operating mode, where the microscope objective is immersed in the photoresist itself in order to increase the numerical aperture, i.e. obtain a smaller laser spot, and therefore higher resolution73.
Laser interference lithography (LIL) can be an effective method for fabricating high numerical aperture (NA) lenses and complex 2.5D structures due to its ability to create precise, periodic nanoscale patterns over large areas74–77. Regarding the processing time, LIL offers significant advantages regarding the time requested for producing nanostructures over large areas, with process times ranging from only several minutes to few hours, depending on the complexity and on the post-processing needs. The process time of LIL is considerably lower than electron beam lithography or focused ion beam milling, as well as TPP. However, the applicability of the two techniques is different, as LIL is faster and suitable for large-area 2D or quasi-3D structures periodic structures, while TPP is slower, but able to produce much more complex 3D micro-nanostructures. In view of the above considerations, our choice to employ LDW via TPP to fabricate the moth-eye inspired structures is fully justified. Thus, this technique is more suitable to fabricate complex biomimetic structures such as the night-vision lenses that simulate the moth eye than LIL. When it comes to cost effectiveness, however, LIL is more cost-effective in comparison to LDW via TPP when using it for the fabrication of large-area periodic micro/nanostructures because of equipment and materials costs. On the other hand, the precision and flexibility of LDW via TPP can not be matched by LIL in terms of fabricating complex biomimetic 3D micro-nanostructures. LDW via TPP is advantageous in high-precision applications, compared to LIL, which is not suitable for specialized custom-made designs.
The ommatidium, a fundamental unit of the compound eyes in arthropods, exemplifies the intricate and highly efficient natural designs that inspire biomimetic advancements32,33,78. The compound eyes of insects like flies and bees are comprised of numerous ommatidia, each acting as a tiny optical unit (see Supplementary Information Fig. SI.3). It consists of a cluster of photoreceptor cells encircled by support and pigment cells, with the outer layer covered by a transparent cornea. This structure provides these insects with a wide field of view, excellent motion detection, and the ability to function in various lighting conditions35,79. Mimicking the ommatidium’s design through micropatterns has led to significant technological innovations, particularly in the fields of optics and imaging28,33,80.
Our engineered structures, artificial ommatidium-like microlenses, are inspired by the compound eyes of Grapholita Funebrana moths, known for their high efficiency antireflective surface nanostructures. SEM images from Fig. SI.3 illustrates the structure of a moth eye, composed of several hundreds of ommatidia structures of about 11–13 μm in diameter and aligned at approximately 14 μm from one center to the other (Fig. SI.3.c). This arrangement is known to maximize the number of photoreceptive units, thereby increasing the overall resolution and field of view of the compound eye81. Moreover, we can observe the curvature of the cornea, with a dome-like shape covering each individual ommatidium. This favors the focusing of light onto the crystalline cone underneath and the photoreceptor cells respectively35,78,82.
At higher magnifications, the nanostructured surface of the cornea is emphasized. Grapholita Funebrana eyes are decorated with corneal nipples with diameters of about 80 nm, hierarchically positioned at distances of 192 nm (Fig. SI3d). In contrast, Yang et al.83 have measured corneal nipples to be approximately 250 nm in height and 100 nm in diameter for a Grapholita Molesta moth eye, a species with nearly identical bio-morphology. This indicates the Grapholita Funebrana moth we analyzed was young, which corroborates with the moment of capture (first generation, end of April – beginning of May 2024). We can also observe that the displacement of the nanopillars on the ommatidium surface has a quasi-concentric arrangement (Fig. SI.3.c), although a close-up inspection reveals various smaller domains where the corneal nipples are locally arranged in a hexagonal pattern. These nanostructures were found to reduce the surface reflection and improve light transmission, ensuring that more light reaches the photoreceptor cells, which enhances the overall sensitivity and efficiency of the ommatidium43.
Our artificial, laser imprinted, moth eye-like structures from Fig. 2a replicate the ommatidium in the moth eye along with the nipples-like nanopillars ontop, as inspiration to develop optically filtered microlens arrays. With an overall dimension of 8 μm (see Fig. 2a), the structure resembles the average minimum diameters found in smaller species of arthropods. Generally, lower diameter ommatidia result in higher spatial resolution of the moth eye84. The hexagonal packing and periodicity of ommatidia provide a wide and overlapping field of view. Additionally, regular periodicity ensures that light entering the compound eye is efficiently captured and processed.

As discussed above, the size of the voxel depends on the energy dose deposited per photoresist unit volume. Due to the fact that we fabricated nanopillars with geometric characteristics close to the technological limits of the 3D lithography installation we used for Laser Direct Writing via Two Photon Polymerization of the biomimetic structures, the fine-tuning/optimization of the geometry was realized by slowly varying the average power of the incident laser beam, rather than modifying the stage velocity. This provides a better control and reproducibility of the resulting structures, while avoiding potential structural defects induced by the periodic translation stage movement and acceleration, as polymeric structures resulting immediately after irradiation have reduced mechanical strength until complete development and drying. The effects of varying the average incident laser power are presented in Fig. 2a, where the geometry and writing velocity are identical for all samples.
All previous studies aiming to mimic moths-eyes did not fabricate the whole i.e. hierarchical structure of the eye, meaning the ommatidia-like microstructures decorated with nipple-like nanostructures. Instead, all existing studies reported the fabrication of only individual components of the complex structure of moth eyes (i.e. of ommatidia-inspired microstructures but having smooth surfaces and of corneal-nipples-inspired nanopillars but on flat substrates, respectively). Moreover, their optical investigation was performed separately on each of the above individual components. In this context, our study is the first time that moth-eyes-inspired structures replicated all the components of moths’ eyes, specifically ommatidia-like i.e. curved microstructures decorated with nipple-like nanostructures that follow the curvature of the former.
Figure 2b shows the close-up of one Grapholita Funebrana ommatidium (Fig. 2.b.i) decorated with corneal nipples (Fig. 2.b.iv), shown next to the artificial ommatidium-inspired microstructure design (Fig. 2.b.ii), the laser-imprinted biomimetic structure (Fig. 2.biii), as well as close views of the nipple-like nanostructures (Fig. 2.b.v, b.vi). Corneal nipples of the natural ommatidium show a compact quasi-periodic arrangement, with an overall circular periodicity and local hexagonal periodicity. The design of the artificial ommatidium is comprised of a base microlens on top of which nanopillars are disposed over the whole surface. Nanopillars are arranged on a spiral trajectory with slowly varying distances between neighboring pillars to account for natural variations, between 200 and 600 nm.
Figure 2c presents a comparison of moth eye ommatidia and artificial ommatidia. Two principal differences can be noted: the curvature of the natural ommatidium is higher and tends to be spherical, while the corneal nipples orientation seems to follow the direction normal to the surface of the ommatidium, albeit with variation in surface smoothness and less degree of periodicity. The artificial ommatidium, on the other hand, shows lower surface curvature, a parabolic surface shape, while the nanopillars are vertical throughout its surface.

Regardless of laser power and design, measured periodicities were very similar for every structure. Small variations in periodicity are observed for the same reasons as heights variation (see Fig. 2e), i.e. nanopillars were fabricated on top of each microlens surface, which itself resembles a quasi-periodic fingerprint-like structure. Apart from these small standard deviations, measured values obtained for mean periodicity of nanopillars were centered on 450 nm. Periodicity of 450 nm for the middle region of the microlens part was the one set by design, whereas the optimization concerned exclusively the heights of the nanopillars. This validates that measurements made on the 3D AFM images were reliable and correct.

Figure 1b illustrates part of the microstructure design and can be used to debate the reason we chose the central areas of microlens surfaces, when nanopillar measurements were realized. Nanopillars followed the shape of microlens top surface and their height was set relatively to this surface, following a similar spiral-like positioning. Consecutive nanopillars were placed on the spiral-like path, at a constant angular step of 6 degrees, relative to the center of the structure (see Fig. 2.e.i). The spiral-like path from Fig. 1b middle and right panels, together with the constant angular difference from Fig. 1b right panel, determined the overall geometry: a quasi-spiral very close to the center of microlenses due to small distance between consecutive nanopillars, then individual nanopillars around the middle of the radius (where we performed the 2D profiles measurements), and closely packed nanopillars towards the edge. These different morphologies occurred because the angle determined a greater distance between consecutive nanopillars, while the spiral gradient made the nanopillars to overlap in the radial direction.
In natural ommatidia, the corneal nipples are typically arranged in a periodic manner on the surfaces of the corneal lenses, which was replicated in the artificial ommatidia pillars as concentric overlapping swirling patterns of nanopillars on top. This regular arrangement of the nanopillars on the moth eye ommatidia ensures uniformity in optical behaviors such as light diffraction, anti-reflectivity, and enhancement of light transmission efficiency35,81. In the case of the artificial ommatidia we fabricated, the nanotopography of the pillars induces optical effects depending on wavelength, likely due to the slightly different topology/arrangement on the surface of each ommatidium, as well as size relation to the wavelengths of the incident light source. In some wavelength intervals, there is a strong light filtering process. In other wavelength intervals we observed high efficiency transmission, as well as transmission over 100%, likely induced by diffraction and dispersion in the structures (artificial ommatidia induce diffraction, which lengthens the optical path of the incident light, which further determine higher dispersion effects). Optical behavior of the artificial ommatidia-like structures will be detailed further down the section. Sun et al.33 obtained nanopatterned surfaces, covered with an array of pillars of 200–300 nm dimension mimicking the property of the moth eye to reduce reflections and facilitate night vision. This choice of dimensioning is lower than the visible light wavelength range (380–760 nm), facilitating an average reflection of about 1.21% at normal incidence. Similar structures were also replicated by Xu et al.58, who investigated the influence of pillars height and morphology on reflective properties. Hierarchical nanostructured moth-eye-like coatings were obtained using an approach with inductively coupled plasma reactive ion etching (ICP-RIE) using nanodisk-array masks84. These structures enabled uniform antireflection properties of about 1% reflectivity as well as superhydrophobic and anticorrosive properties.

Nanostructures were fabricated via Roll-to-Plate ultraviolet nanoimprint lithography on transparent polycarbonate substrates, using a GP756 UV-curable resin. At 30 degrees incidence angle, a reflectance of 2.70% was measured, and at 50 degrees the average reflectance was 3.20%. Higher incidence angle was reported to induce a significant forward step in the average reflectance, where authors report a reflectance of 8.76% and 10.40% for incidence angles of 60 degrees and 70 degrees respectively. While similar in shape and size, Sun et al.’s nanopillars were fabricated on a flat surface, disposed in a hexagonal lattice and analyzed in the 400–850 nm wavelength interval. The nanostructures we present are fabricated on microlenses that are disposed in a hexagonal lattice, with a different nanopillar periodicity, and reflectance is measured in the 1000–1700 nm interval. Microlens arrays decorated with nanopillars show a similar effect to structures fabricated by Sun et al., i.e. lowering the reflectance, when compared to microlens arrays without nanopillars. Fan et al.88 report on the fabrication of plano-concave microlens arrays for infrared applications fabricated via a combined femtosecond direct irradiation and chemical wet etching methods, in ZnSe. Microlenses are disposed in a hexagonal lattice and have diameters of ~ 30 μm. The paper shows a transmission spectrum for wavelengths between 0.76 and 22 μm, with a transmission of approximately 62% for the wavelength interval between 1000 and 1700 nm. Transmittance results presented in this paper, for the same wavelength interval, are lower. However, the microlens arrays reported in our study are plano-convex (instead of plano-concave as Fan et al.) and fabricated in a single step, in contrast to more complex multistep approach of Fan et al. (substrate treatment, laser irradiation, wet etching, etc.). More recently, Haoran et al.89 reported on the fabrication of microlens arrays using a femtosecond direct laser writing technique in a low shrinkage photoresist (SZ2080), combined with a graphene oxide grating that acts as a linear polariser. In89, microlenses are presented with a diameter of ~ 60 μm, a height of 10 μm, each of them being written on a 12 μm tall cylinder / pedestal. The microlens array was fabricated on a CaF2 substrate in order to minimize optical absorption. Haoran et al. have measured IR transmittance using a Fourier-Transform InfraRed spectrometer in the wavelength interval between 1 and 10 μm and they reported a transmittance of roughly ~ 48% between 1000 and 1700 nm. Similarly, Haoran et al. report higher IR transmittance in the 1000–1700 nm interval, in comparison to our samples. This transmittance difference, in comparison to our experimental results, could be assigned most likely to the transmittance difference of the substrate ~ 10%, (Haoran et al. used CaF2, while our microlenses are fabricated on fused quartz glass slides).

Li et al.87 report on the fabrication of microlens arrays in methyl red-doped polymethyl methacrylate (MR-PMMA) via rear-side picosecond laser swelling. In order to improve fabrication time and ensure the uniformity of resulting microlenses, they used the foci of another microlens array (Thorlabs, 10 × 10 mm2, 30 × 30 lenses, 300 μm pitch, 18.6 mm focal length) to process the MR-PMMA, and a beam shaper to modify the Gaussian intensity distribution of the incident laser beam into top-hat / flat profile. They obtained microlenses of diameters between 40 and 50 μm, with heights between 5 and 15 μm, and focal lengths approximated between 59 and 86 μm, depending on irradiation time and microlens geometry, implicitly. The authors do not present a focal spot diameter in87, but a subsequent image analysis based on the available data indicates a focal spot diameter of ~ 6.9 μm. The fabrication method presented by Li et al.87 is more time efficient than the method presented in this paper for constructing large area microlens arrays, but there are heavy constraints in terms of geometry control (size, shape, resolution, positioning). Moreover, this technique does not allow for submicron resolution, as it can be obtained with LDW via TPP, used in this study. Feng et al.44 report on the fabrication of an artificial compound eye via 3D nano-imprinting technique that uses PMMA replication inside a curved BK7 borosilicate crown optical glass substrate (hemispherical) that is processed using femtosecond laser-assisted wet etching to construct the microlens / compound eye mold. Due to the shape of the mold and the mold-replication process, microlens diameter varies between 140 and 180 μm, and their corresponding focal length between 200 and 150 μm. Focal spot size is reported to be ~ 5 μm. Similarly, in44, a time efficient fabrication method is presented for large area (and in this case curved surface) microlens arrays, but the replication step results in high variations in microlens geometry and their behavior, implicitly. Moreover, these variations indicate significant challenges in obtaining reproducible surface nanostructuring.

For a more accurate evaluation regarding the degree of biomimicry of the moth-eye inspired structures developed in our study, in addition to the optical characterization of Visible and IR transmission and reflectance spectra of our samples presented above, the comparison between the refractive index of the actual mot eyes and the photopolymer used for fabricating the biomimetic moth-eye inspired structures is needed. ## Conclusions
We report on the design and laser-assisted fabrication of moth eye-inspired biomimetic structures, for potential use as microlens arrays in IR detection systems. The biomimetic structures were inspired by Grapholita Funebrana nocturnal moth eyes, which are optimized for night vision. As such, our design included ommatidia-like microlenses that were arranged in a closely packed hexagonal pattern and decorated with nipple-like nanopillars. The design and fabrication parameters were optimized to obtain microlens arrays that closely resembled the topology and geometric characteristics of natural moth eyes. The ommatidia-like parts of the biomimetic structures had average diameters of about 13–14 μm, while the nipple-like nanopillars decorating the ommatidia-like microstructures were between 200 and 400 nm in height and around 450 nm periodicity. These dimensions of moth eyes-inspired structures were deviated from the natural ommatidia’s diameters, corneal nipple heights and spatial periodicities, by less than 10%. The optical performances of the moth-eyes inspired microlens arrays were investigated in 1000–1700 nm wavelength interval. The optical performances of microlens arrays decorated with nipple-like nanopillars were compared to those of microlens arrays without nanopillars, results indicating superior IR optical performance for the former. More specifically, the IR optical transmission of microlens arrays with nanopillars was up to 17.55% higher than the optical transmission through microlens arrays without nanopillars; also, the reflection of nanopillar-decorated microlens arrays was up to 0.91% lower than the reflection of microlens arrays without nanopillars. Moreover, in the Visible spectral range, the reflection followed the same trend as for IR region, being higher for microlense arrays with nanopillars than for microlenses arrays without nanopillars. Interestingly, in contrast with the IR spectral region, in the Visible range the transmission of nanopillar-decorated microlens arrays was lower than the transmission for microlense arrays without nanopillars, most probably because of optical diffraction losses occurring as the size of the nanopillars i.e. height between 200 and 400 nm, were closer to the incident wavelengths. Although the focal distance was the same for both microlens arrays (with and without nanopillars, i.e. of ~ 21 μm), the nipple-like nanopillars improved the focusing properties of the microlens arrays. Namely, their focal spot diameter at 1/e2 was of 7.64 μm, 16.5% higher that the focal spot diameter of microlens arrays without nanopillars. Further investigations are required to determine the cause of these optical effects, as well as to control them. For specific applications, such as optical filtering, an envisaged approach is the fine-tuning of the optical behavior of moth-eye-inspired structures by their geometrical scaling, which will allow a precise adjustment of the absorption and transmission peaks. In all, these results open up novel and promising routes for accurate, low cost and easy-to-use 3D printing technologies for producing 3D biomimetic micro- and nanostructures as bioinspired solutions for challenging issues of the modern world.

(i,ii,iii) Top views; (iv,v,vi) Tilted views (30 degrees); (b) Comparison of Grapholita Funebrana ommatidium (i,iv); moth-eye artificial ommatidium design (ii,v) and LDW via TPP experimental result (iii,vi); (c) SEM micrographs showing a comparison of Grapholita Funebrana moth eyes (i,ii,iii) and LDW via TPP fabricated moth-eye artificial ommatidia (iv,v,vi); (d) Heights of nanopillars on top of ommatidium-like microlens (theoretical values (i.e. set by design) and experimental values (i.e. structures fabricated by LDW via TPP), as measured from AFM images). Laser-imprinted structures fabricated using the specified laser powers and 90 μm/s scan speed. The green rectangles indicate optimized results; (e) 3D images obtained by atomic force microscopy of 8 × 8 µm2 areas of nanopillars from the top of ommatidium-like microlenses, fabricated by LDW via TPP using 90 μm/s scan speed and laser powers indicated on each image. Nanopillar’s height set by design was 500 nm. Experimental values of nanopillar heights are marked on each image. Insets are shown to evidence the nanopillars. The green rectangle is a zoom in to an area where the nanopillar geometry can pe analyzed best.
