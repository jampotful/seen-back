# The Visual Umwelt of primates and Hippocampal Representations of Space

## Abstract
ABSTRACTEvolution sculpts the brain's sensory adaptations. Because these adaptations differ markedly across species, it is challenging for humans to fully comprehend how other animals perceive the world. For a nocturnal mouse, the subjective sensory world—its Umwelt—is dominated by odors, sounds, and textures, with visual input playing a secondary role. In contrast, the Umwelt of a diurnal primate is primarily visual, composed of colors, shapes, and hues, with less emphasis on olfaction, audition, or tactile cues. In mice and rats, hippocampal place cells are activated when the animal occupies specific locations in allocentric space. In contrast, studies in primates have identified hippocampal view cells, which respond when head‐gaze is directed toward certain regions of visual space. The source of this divergence can be traced back to the evolution of mammalian species. Early mammals adapted to nocturnal environments to avoid predation by dinosaurs, becoming heavily dependent on olfaction, audition, touch, proprioception, and vestibular signals for perceiving changes in the environment, while regressing vision. However, one group of mammals started evolving stereoscopic foveal vision to capture insects and fruit in the arboreal environment. Following the mass extinction of dinosaurs, these primate ancestors transitioned to a diurnal niche, re‐inventing color vision, which became their superpower. Stereo high‐resolution color vision enabled primates to locate distant objects and landmarks efficiently, optimizing foraging while minimizing energy expenditure during navigation. The primate hippocampus then evolved to prioritize representations of visual scenes that support landmark‐based navigation.

## Sensory Ecology and Umwelt
Sensory ecology studies how organisms acquire, process, and respond to sensory information in their environments (Raz and Breitkopf 2024). It examines how sensory systems (e.g., vision, smell, hearing) adapt to ecological niches and influence behaviors like spatial navigation for foraging and mating. The Umwelt, a concept introduced by Jakob von Uexküll (Penzlin 2009), refers to the unique perceptual world of a species, shaped by its sensory capabilities and biological needs. It emphasizes that different organisms experience the same physical environment in distinct ways; therefore, there is not a single Umwelt for all animals but a diversity of species‐specific Umwelten (plural). Umwelt and sensory ecology are tightly linked concepts, since the sensory apparatus of a species determines which environmental signals can be sensed, and therefore, the individual's perceptual reality. For example, dogs ( Canis lupus familiaris ) experience the world primarily through smell, supplemented by acute hearing and motion‐sensitive, low‐color vision; their Umwelt is a mosaic of scents, sounds, and movement cues—more like an olfactory‐acoustic landscape than a visual one. A naked mole‐rat's ( Heterocephalus glaber ) Umwelt is a dark, odor‐rich, tactile‐acoustic world made of narrow tunnels, social scent trails, and underground vibrations. Its sensory world is compressed, localized, and multisensory in the non‐visual domains. In contrast, humans ( Homo sapiens ) live in a visually rich and color‐dense world, with heavy reliance on sight. The human Umwelt emphasizes visual form, color, and symbols. Even our dreams are made of visual images (Freud and Brill 1913; Nir and Tononi 2010).
Umwelten vary across species because, over many years of evolution, each species has undergone changes in sensory systems to adapt to the specific demands of its ecological niche. Rats (Rattus norvegicus) are nocturnal and often inhabit urban sewers; naked mole‐rats live in subterranean tunnels; and humans are diurnal creatures who occupy open, light‐filled environments. A compelling question arises: if we were to place individuals from each of these three species in the same physical environment—say, a maze with obstacles in a brightly lit experimental room—how would they perceive that space? Would their sensory systems detect and prioritize the types of information most relevant to their natural habitats, so their Umwelten would not change? For instance, would the rat still rely primarily on odors and sounds, the naked mole‐rat on odors and vibrations, and the human on vision? Or would they recalibrate their senses to prioritize the most informative signals in the new environment—perhaps relying more heavily on vision, as humans do? The answer to this question has profound implications for the use of animal models to study perception and memory, and for extrapolating results to humans.
We argue that such rapid adjustments are highly unlikely. Species have adapted their sensory systems and adjusted their survival strategies within distinct environments over millions—or at least hundreds of thousands—of years. The ontogenetic and phylogenetic histories of a species are encoded in sensory and neural architectures tuned to their respective Umwelten. This must be carefully considered when studying navigation and its neural mechanisms across species, especially when making comparisons or generalizations. Neural representations supporting spatial navigation in hippocampal circuits are not universal but vary across species, and these variations stem from the divergence of sensory systems and behavioral strategies used to navigate different ecological niches.

## Hippocampal Representations During Navigation in Nocturnal Rodents and Diurnal primates
The hippocampus is a phylogenetically ancient structure of the mammalian brain that is thought to have evolved in early vertebrates to support navigation and serial order functions (Murray et al. 2017). Because all mammalian lineages derive from a common ancestor that lived around the time of the dinosaurs, some structural and functional features of the hippocampus have been preserved across many mammalian species (Kaas 2012; Clark and Squire 2013). However, the hippocampus is strongly connected to many brain areas, and mammalian brains have diversified to adapt to different environmental niches; therefore, some of the structural and functional features of the hippocampus have changed across species (Quiroga 2023; Eichert et al. 2024). Here, we will focus on the differences that have evolved through adaptations enabling spatial navigation of diverse ecological niches.
Spatial navigation is the process by which organisms determine a route to a goal and then travel that route (Brodbeck and Tanninen 2012). It is widely believed to rely on cognitive maps—internal representations of the environment that encode landmarks and their spatial and temporal relationships (Eichenbaum 2017). Decades ago, O'Keefe and Dostrovsky identified place cells in the hippocampus of freely moving rats, which fire when the animal occupies specific locations in an environment, representing space in an allocentric frame of reference (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky 1971). These cells were interpreted as neural instantiations of the cognitive map proposed by Tolman (Tolman 1948), thought to support spatial navigation across all mammals (Crish et al. 2006; Moser et al. 2015). To evaluate this idea, numerous laboratories sought to identify place cells in various species. In mice, studies successfully replicated the findings from rats, including in virtual reality (VR) environments (McHugh et al. 1996; Dombeck et al. 2010). However, an early study in rhesus macaques failed to find classic hippocampal place cells, instead reporting neurons that responded to specific views of a visual scene (Robertson et al. 1998; Rolls 1999). This discrepancy in hippocampal representations between rodents and primates remained puzzling for many years, often attributed to methodological differences among studies in different species. Surprisingly, little attention was paid to the fact that rats are nocturnal, and macaques are diurnal, and that these species inhabit fundamentally different Umwelten.
A confounding factor when comparing studies in rodents and primates has been the experimental setup: most studies in macaques are conducted with the animals restrained in primate chairs (Fuster and Alexander 1970; Dubner and Zeki 1971), eliminating vestibular and proprioceptive cues known to be crucial for place cell formation (Stackman et al. 2002). Furthermore, the macaque's advanced manual dexterity allows them to remove head‐mounted recording equipment, complicating electrophysiological studies during free movement. In a series of early influential studies by Rolls and colleagues (Rolls et al. 1997, 1998; Robertson et al. 1998; Georges‐François et al. 1999), macaques were placed inside a primate chair with wheels and allowed to locomote inside a room. Although animals navigated the room reaching velocities of 0.6 m/s, one may ask whether the head‐body movements were natural and therefore comparable to those in freely moving rats.
Subsequent studies using tethered setups also reported mixed results. One study reported place‐selective cells in macaques moving along a linear track. Still, the constrained environment may have conflated spatial position with visual input (Courellis et al. 2019), unlike the open‐field designs typically used in rodents. Some studies explored navigation in VR environments using joysticks (Doucet et al. 2016; Wirth et al. 2017), where macaques learned to navigate mazes rendered by video game engines. These studies reported neurons selective for object–location combinations (Gulli et al. 2020), schema representations (Baraduc et al. 2019; Vericel et al. 2024), and views within the virtual maze (Corrigan et al. 2023). Although some hippocampal neurons showed spatial selectivity, it was often egocentric rather than allocentric (Gulli et al. 2020), and this selectivity failed to generalize across different task contexts.
Similarly, direct hippocampal recordings have been conducted in humans undergoing elective neurosurgery during VR navigation tasks. A study (Ekstrom et al. 2003) recorded human neuronal activity from different frontal and medial temporal lobe structures while participants played a taxi driver computer game and were tasked with finding randomly appearing passengers and delivering them to fixed locations. They identified cells that encoded place (26%), view (12%), and goal (21%). Of the place‐encoding cells, only 11% of the population (24% of hippocampal cells) were not directionally biased. They were referred to as bona fide place cells and were primarily found in the hippocampus. The authors also report cells with significant interaction effects: place × goal (26% of all place‐responsive cells), view × place (~63% of all view‐responsive cells), and view × goal (15% of all goal‐responsive cells).
Later work in virtually navigating humans, under more controlled and customizable environments, also demonstrated that allocentric place encoding was relatively rare (Miller et al. 2013), with only 7.3% of medial temporal lobe (MTL) cells responding to place regardless of direction, supporting the view that truly allocentric hippocampal place responses are less prevalent in humans than in rodents. Another study (Tsitsiklis et al. 2020) further showed that human hippocampal and entorhinal neurons encode the goal location during a VR “Treasure Hunt” task, suggesting that spatial modulation is tightly linked to memory‐guided navigation and goal representation rather than pure allocentric position. Together, these findings suggest a highly malleable spatial code in the human hippocampus, influenced greatly by the visual scene, direction of navigation, and behavioral goals, and tuned less to absolute location in virtual space. These results are consistent with studies in non‐human primates; taken together, they suggest a strong role for vision in primate hippocampal representations. However, the absence of body movements and vestibular input in VR makes direct comparisons to freely moving rodent studies problematic.
More recently, advances in wireless electrophysiology (Hasegawa et al. 2015) and high‐resolution video tracking (Leardini et al. 2007) have enabled hippocampal recordings in freely moving primates. Two recent studies applied these methods in marmosets and macaques as they navigated open mazes. They reported that hippocampal neurons were predominantly selective for combinations of different variables (view, head direction, and spatial position), without the strong bias for allocentric place coding observed in rodents (Mao et al. 2021; Piza et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024). These findings challenge the idea that allocentric place cells found in rodents are universal building blocks of spatial cognitive maps used for navigation in all mammals. Instead, they suggest that hippocampal spatial representations differ across species—even when tested under similar conditions—likely due to differences in how species perceive and prioritize environmental information to construct their corresponding Umwelten.
Interestingly, several studies in rodents have demonstrated that rotating or manipulating distal visual cues can modulate hippocampal place fields—an effect that is amplified during virtual reality navigation and in the absence of vestibular input (Ravassard et al. 2013; Acharya et al. 2016; Aghajan et al. 2017). Despite these influences, hippocampal neurons in rodents still reliably encode spatial location during real‐world navigation. Together, these findings suggest that while vision can modulate hippocampal firing in rodents during freely moving navigation, spatial encoding remains dominant. In primates, however, hippocampal neurons appear to be more fundamentally tuned to visual inputs, reflecting a shift toward visually anchored representations of space (Murray et al. 2017; Rolls and Wirth 2018; Quiroga 2023; Zhu et al. 2023; Martinez‐Trujillo 2025; Xu and Mao 2025).
It is reasonable to state that an individual's behavior generally aligns with its Umwelt. In support of this idea, a comparative study found that during spatial navigation in a maze marmosets made rapid head‐gaze shifts during pauses in locomotion; in contrast, rats made slower, shorter head movements while locomoting. These differences reflect their contrasting sensory priorities: marmosets, inhabiting a vision‐dominant Umwelt, rely on precise head‐gaze saccades to position and stabilize the high‐resolution but small fovea on objects and landmarks to collect precise view information and make navigational decisions. To stabilize gaze and the fovea, marmosets must pause locomotion and additionally engage their powerful head‐eye apparatus and vestibulo‐ocular reflex (Crawford et al. 2003). In contrast, rats rely on olfaction and somatosensation, exploring with their noses and whiskers while remaining in motion, building a map of odors and physical objects in space. Their need for stabilizing gaze is less since they do not have a fovea. These divergent strategies are linked to different Umwelten and are the product of distinct evolutionary adaptations to nocturnal and diurnal ecological niches (Figure 1, top and middle rows diagrams). ## Escaping the Nocturnal Niche Shaped the Primate's Umwelt
Early mammals evolved from reptile‐like synapsids more than 200 million years ago during the Late Triassic period (Kemp 1980). For millions of years, mammals remained small and nocturnal, likely to avoid predation by dinosaurs (Schroeder et al. 2021). To adapt to life in low‐light conditions, they developed an expanded olfactory system (Rowe and Shepherd 2016), high‐frequency hearing (Frost and Masterton 1994), and long sensitive whiskers for tactile exploration (Muchlinski 2010). These sensory specializations were accompanied by cortical expansions supporting olfactory, auditory, and somatosensory processing, while visual regions regressed (Kaas et al. 2022).
Around 77 million years ago, a group of nocturnal mammals began developing adaptations that enabled them to catch insects and reach for fruit among the fine branches of trees. These adaptations included forward‐facing eyes for stereoscopic depth perception and refined hand motor skills for grasping thin branches, grabbing fruit, and catching prey. Their nose retracted to avoid obstructing binocular vision, and the eyes became larger, developing a tapetum lucidum to enhance light capture. The visual processing pathway connecting the retina, lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), and primary visual cortex (V1)—fed by cone photoreceptors and small retinal ganglion cells with high spatial resolution—began to dominate over the older tectal‐pulvinar‐temporal route still common in most vertebrates (Heesy and Hall 2010; Kaas et al. 2022). With these changes, the Umwelt—or perceptual world—of early primates shifted, and vision became a dominant sensory modality. Cortical areas dedicated to visual processing expanded, and a specialized oculomotor system evolved to rapidly and efficiently sample the visual environment through gaze shifts (Felleman and Essen 1991; Crawford et al. 2003; Carvalho et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2017). These early primates became distinct from other mammals, though they remained mostly nocturnal to avoid the predatory dominance of dinosaurs in the daylight niche.
Around 66 million years ago, the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) extinction event occurred. A massive asteroid struck the Yucatán Peninsula, leading to ocean acidification and the extinction of many plant and animal species, including the non‐avian dinosaurs (Henehan et al. 2019). This event opened ecological niches for mammals, enabling them to emerge from the shadows and adapt to diurnal life (Kaas et al. 2022). For the already visually specialized primate, daylight conditions further enhanced their visual capacities. Vision became their evolutionary superpower, enabling them to thrive in diverse habitats. Unlike other sensory inputs, electromagnetic waves reflected by physical objects of varying molecular compositions travel fast and far. In daylight, this information reaches the retina with high fidelity, enabling perception of color, motion, and fine spatial detail. These capabilities allowed primates to better identify food sources, detect predators, and navigate 3‐dimensional space efficiently. The ability to perceive color was especially useful for distinguishing red or blue fruit from green foliage, detecting yellow predators camouflaged in vegetation, or spotting distant resources such as water or fruit‐bearing trees (Carvalho et al. 2017). The primate visual system expanded, and daylight vision fundamentally reshaped the primate's Umwelt, influencing how they explored, perceived, and built cognitive maps of space. Most likely primate memories and dreams that rehearse and consolidate them became vivid visual imageries, like those we humans experience (Nir and Tononi 2010; Rolls 2025).
Not all mammals followed this path. Many species remained in—or later returned to—the nocturnal niche, driven by evolutionary pressures and inter‐species competition (Kaas et al. 2022). Over millions of years, these nocturnal species regressed in their visual capabilities, which offered limited benefits in the dark, and instead sharpened their olfactory and tactile senses. This shift enhanced their ability to recognize objects and detect prey or predators at close range. In nocturnal mammals like rats and mice, small body size and high reproductive rates were sufficient for the species' evolutionary success. Indeed, rats have an enlarged cortical representation of olfactory and somatosensory modalities (Slotnick 2001; Petersen 2007) (Figure 1, right column diagrams), produce a large number of litters, and have short lifespans compared to primates of similar size (e.g., ~2 years in rats vs. > 14 years in common marmosets) (Nishijima et al. 2012; Rothwell et al. 2021). These sensory adaptations for near‐field perception—particularly olfaction and touch—are crucial in low‐light environments, shaping both navigation strategies and hippocampal representations used to construct cognitive maps of space (Piza et al. 2024).
Broadly, according to behavioral patterns for regulating activity over a 24‐h cycle, today mammalian species can be classified into four main categories: diurnal (active during the day, e.g., common marmosets), nocturnal (active at night, e.g., domestic rat), crepuscular (active during dawn and dusk, e.g., pigmy rabbit or Brachylagus idahoensis ), and cathemeral (active at various times during both day and night, e.g., common vole or Microtus arvalis). These different patterns are associated with different sensory adaptations to light or semidarkness shaped by the time illuminated by daylight, moonlight, and/or twilight and between thermal limits suitable for mammal activity (Cuesta et al. 2009; Bennie et al. 2014; Koizumi et al. 2021). These species' behavioral patterns are also associated with different Umwelten and therefore with different hippocampal representations of space during navigation. Unfortunately, we have not found comparative studies focusing on hippocampal representations across species with different behavioral patterns. A recent study in chickadees ( Poecile atricapillus ), a diurnal bird, found that hippocampal representations of space are activated by specific views of remote locations indicating that both place and gaze codes coexist (Payne and Aronov 2025). This strongly supports that in diurnal species with highly developed visual systems, vision strongly shapes Umwelten and representations of space. For the case of diurnal primates, hippocampal representations are shaped by a unique set of specializations.

## Visual Specializations in Primates
The primate neocortex has undergone considerable expansion relative to that of rodents (Hoesen 1982; Amaral et al. 1983). Notably, a substantial proportion of this expansion comprises higher‐order visual association areas that integrate complex features of scenes, such as object identity and spatial layout (Suzuki and Amaral 1994; Epstein et al. 1999; Kravitz et al. 2011). Within the temporal lobe, the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices, both of which provide the hippocampus with rich visual inputs, have enlarged and reorganized to such an extent that the primate hippocampal formation has rotated ventro‐rostrally, lying almost horizontally beneath the floor of the temporal horn of the lateral ventricle. In contrast, the rat hippocampus remains vertically oriented, extending in a C‐curve from just beneath the cortex toward the base of the brain (Figure 1, bottom row diagrams).
Diurnal primates exhibit enhanced visual‐perceptual capabilities compared to other mammals (Felleman and Essen 1991; Kaas 2012; Kaas et al. 2022). Macaque monkeys, for instance, demonstrate a remarkable ability to recognize objects and categorize them based on visual features (Tanaka 1997; Rajalingham et al. 2015). Category‐selective neurons are found in association areas of the primate parietal lobe and prefrontal cortex (Freedman et al. 2001; Freedman and Assad 2006). Primates have also evolved a specialized system for holistic face recognition, enabling them to identify numerous individuals within a group and distinguish friends from foes (Tsao et al. 2006; Tsao and Livingstone 2008; Freiwald and Tsao 2011). In macaques, face patches have been identified on the surface of the temporal lobe and frontal cortex (Perrett et al. 1984; Tsao and Livingstone 2008; Arcaro et al. 2020; Gilbert et al. 2021). Notably, neurons selective for the identity of specific individuals have also been found in the human temporal lobe (Quiroga et al. 2005). This highly developed object recognition system supports the identification of distant visual landmarks, decision‐making about when to approach, selecting optimal paths, and correcting navigational errors in real‐time by visualizing targets.
Primates also display unique adaptations for processing visual motion. Neurons in the middle temporal area (MT) are tuned to the speed and direction of motion (Kohn and Movshon 2003; Born and Bradley 2005; Smith 2012). Downstream, in the medial superior temporal area (MST), neurons integrate visual motion with vestibular inputs to estimate self‐motion speed and heading direction (Britten and van Wezel 1998; Rudolph and Pasternak 1999; Heuer and Britten 2004; Gu et al. 2007; Britten 2008; Sasaki et al. 2019; Wild and Treue 2021). The ability of MST neurons to detect optic flow patterns is comparable to that of human observers (Heuer and Britten 2004; Rodríguez‐Sanchez et al. 2009; Wild and Treue 2021) suggesting they play an important role in optic flow perception during navigation. Although some diurnal species, such as domestic cats, have been found to possess MT‐like motion processing regions, these primarily receive inputs from the SC–pulvinar pathway (Ogino and Ohtsuka 2000; Ohtsuka and Ogino 2001). There is consensus that MT is a primate‐specific specialization (Kaas 2012; Kaas et al. 2022). MT projects to area MST where visual signals are mixed with vestibular and eye position signals (Gu et al. 2007).
The emergence of area MT could have transformed primate navigation by shifting the relative proportion of vestibular, proprioceptive and visual cues integrated by downstream multisensory areas such as MST. The signal mix in area MST and likely other downstream areas may have also enhanced the capacity of primates to use egocentric representations of visual space during visual exploration and navigation (Zhu et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024). Humans can navigate environments solely by vision (Kirschen et al. 1999). Vection, the illusion of self‐motion can be triggered by visual stimuli when a subject is stationary, illustrating the power of visual signals to drive optic flow and recruit the neural machinery engaged in self‐motion and navigation in humans (Palmisano et al. 2015). How multisensory integration under different conditions affects visual and vestibular integration and hippocampal representations remains unclear. Interestingly, rats also use visual cues to navigate specific environments, modulating the responses of grid, border, and speed cells in the entorhinal cortex (Campbell et al. 2018).
A central challenge in landmark‐based navigation is computing the distance to a landmark or goal in depth (i.e., in 3D space). The brain must infer 3D spatial relationships from 2D retinal images. It is widely recognized that binocular disparity cues enable humans to perceive depth (Wheatstone 1843). Neurons selective for inter‐retinal disparity have been described in the primary visual cortex and in area MT of macaques (Cumming and DeAngelis 2001). Interestingly, at least one study has reported disparity tuning of neurons in mouse area V1 in particular for ecologically relevant distances (Scholl et al. 2013). Importantly, MT neurons can integrate motion direction and speed with disparity cues to compute distance to a landmark, approach speed, and time to contact (Born and Bradley 2005). The MT/MST complex (Figure 1, bottom right diagram) may therefore play a key role in planning routes to 3D goals and in using optic flow as feedback for correcting navigation errors online. These visual specializations may have played a role in shifting navigation strategies from path integration (Mittelstaedt and Mittelstaedt 1980), useful in the dark or when vision is poor, to visual landmark‐based navigation (Collett and Graham 2004), used during daylight and when visual information is constantly available.
A major evolutionary advantage of primates is the capacity to perform abstract cognitive operations “in the mind” (Passingham and Wise 2012; Arnsten 2013). This ability is supported by the granular lateral prefrontal cortex (gPFC), a structure that emerged during prosimian evolution and is absent in other mammals, including rodents (Passingham and Wise 2012). The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), a subregion of the gPFC, exhibits expanded cortical layers 2/3 containing neurons that encode representations of objects and spatial locations (Constantinidis et al. 2001, 2018; Constantinidis and Wang 2004; Arnsten 2013; Leavitt et al. 2017; Roussy et al. 2022; Busch et al. 2024). Recent findings suggest that dlPFC neurons encode these representations in an allocentric frame, independent of changes in retinal position due to eye movements (Roussy et al. 2022). Additionally, dlPFC neurons encode the observer's viewpoint during virtual navigation in a manner similar to hippocampal “view cells” (Corrigan et al. 2022, 2023). In macaques navigating a virtual reality maze, dlPFC neurons form neural activation sequences (NAS) during path recall that resemble replay sequences observed in rodents (Busch et al. 2024). It is possible that NASs represent sequences of views along a route to a landmark. Altogether, these findings suggest that primates prioritize visual representations and imagery over other sensory modalities during functions such as spatial navigation, reinforcing the idea that their Umwelt is fundamentally visual.

## The Primate Oculomotor System and Hippocampal Representations
The relatively small size of the primate fovea limits the portion of the visual field that can be sampled at high resolution for detailed object recognition. To compensate, primates have evolved a specialized system for coordinating eye and head movements to rapidly sample the visual scene using multiple saccades per second (Robinson 1968; Tehovnik et al. 2000; Schiller and Tehovnik 2005). Three cortical areas—the frontal eye field (FEF), supplementary eye field (SEF), and lateral intraparietal area (LIP)—encode gaze shift goals and are interconnected with the superficial layers of the superior colliculus (SC), where commands to orient gaze are generated (Crawford et al. 2003). In all these areas, neurons represent both the location of visual targets and the intended gaze position (Bruce and Goldberg 1984; Bruce et al. 1985; Stanton et al. 1988; Colby et al. 1996; Powell and Goldberg 2000; Tehovnik et al. 2000; Bisley and Goldberg 2003; Crawford et al. 2003; Schiller and Tehovnik 2005). These neurons encode goal locations in multiple frames of reference‐eye‐, head‐, and body‐centered (Martinez‐Trujillo et al. 2004; Constantin et al. 2007; Monteon et al. 2010, 2013; Sajad et al. 2015, 2020). This versatile gaze network also encodes priority maps of locations used for route planning during spatial navigation (Fecteau and Munoz 2006; Ibos et al. 2013; Sapountzis et al. 2018; Bisley and Mirpour 2019; Mirpour et al. 2019). By shifting gaze from a fixed, advantageous position, primates can construct spatial maps of the environment without physically visiting different locations—a process known as “exploration without visitation” (Piza et al. 2024). This strategy conserves significant metabolic energy that would otherwise be used to power locomotor muscles.
Saccadic eye movements in primates play a significant role in processing signals in the hippocampus by organizing sensory input into discrete, temporally segmented episodes that facilitate both perception and memory formation (Meister and Buffalo 2016). When primates make saccades to explore visual scenes, these rapid gaze shifts trigger phase resets in hippocampal theta‐band oscillations, aligning neural activity and synchronizing ensemble firing (Doucet et al. 2020). This mechanism is thought to optimize conditions for encoding new sensory information by synchronizing the timing of neuronal spikes, thus supporting memory formation and spatial mapping (Hoffman et al. 2013; Katz et al. 2022; Piza et al. 2024). Additionally, saccade‐related signals can modulate local field potentials and neuronal activity, with stronger phase locking and increased firing rates occurring when saccades coincide with significant visual cues, driving both attention and plasticity processes essential for memory formation and storage in the primate hippocampus networks (Leonard et al. 2015; Leonard and Hoffman 2017).

## Navigation Strategies and Ecological Niche Calibration of Sensory Systems
Evolution by natural selection states that species adapt their anatomy and physiology, and therefore the features of their sensory systems to the ecological niches they live in (Darwin 1951). Clear examples are species that live in extreme conditions. For example, moles have poor eyesight, but have extraordinarily developed somatosensory and auditory systems to recognize objects and subterranean vibrations. The star‐nosed mole ( Condylura cristata ) is quasi‐blind but has a mechanosensory organ (the star) that rapidly and effectively scans the near space with tentacle‐like structures called nasal rays. The star has the highest concentration of mechanoreceptors described in any mammal, matched by a high‐resolution fovea‐like specialization overrepresented in the somatosensory cortical modules (Catania 2012). Many subterranean species show the same extraordinarily developed somatosensory apparatus, including added cortical areas (Catania and Henry 2006). The naked mole rat has regressed its visual apparatus compared to that of their mouse and rat cousins, to the point that they use light mainly for synchronizing circadian rhythms (Crish et al. 2006). Other mammals like nocturnal bats have poor vision but possess an extraordinarily developed auditory system with an echolocation apparatus that allows detecting objects from far (Moss and Sinha 2003). Echolocation is crucial for bats that fly at high velocities and must therefore rapidly and precisely locate prey and other objects. Interestingly, place cell‐like selectivity has been described in the nocturnal Egyptian fruit bat (Yartsev and Ulanovsky 2013). At least one study has shown that a species of nocturnal bat, Kuhl's pipistrelles, uses path integration rather than external cues to estimate flight distance for dozens of meters along linear flyways (Aharon et al. 2017).
Like nocturnal rats, the HPC of nocturnal bats contains place cells, and their entorhinal cortex contains grid cells (Yartsev et al. 2011). Grid and place cells may have likely developed in mammalian species that use path integration as a main navigation strategy. When path integrating from one landmark to another, HPC place cells keep track of position in allocentric space, and ETC grid cells keep track of the moving speed and distance traveled (Moser et al. 2015). Interestingly, place cell selectivity is sensitive to changes in environmental landmarks (Acharya et al. 2016; Purandare et al. 2022) suggesting that the reference frame for place cells may not be purely allocentric but at least to a degree landmark‐centered. The definition of allocentric can be problematic because one could define space depending on how our spatial world or environment is defined (e.g., a room, a house, a neighborhood, a country, the planet or the universe). For a rat or a mouse such abstractions may be off limits and allocentric space may refer to the known, physically explorable, environment that contains a configuration of generally fixed landmarks. In a mouse world, a place cell may provide sufficient information to build a cognitive map that serves navigation (O'keefe and Dostrovsky 1971; Passingham 1979).
On the other hand, diurnal primates with a sophisticated visual system predominantly use landmark‐based navigation in daylight conditions. Using view cells, they can orient themselves, define their position, calculate heading direction and compute distances by using stereo and other visual disparity cues. Interestingly, visual grid cells have been reported in the entorhinal cortex of macaques exploring a visual scene using saccades (Killian et al. 2012). These cells, however, may be considered a visual specialization and would not depend on vestibular signals during locomotion since they were described in head‐restrained macaques. Spatial grid cells in the freely moving primate ETC during navigation have not yet been reported. Vision provides primates with the ability to represent places that have never been visited by assembling different views and reconstructing their spatial relationships in a “mental cognitive map” (Tolman 1948). In visual species such as marmosets, macaques and humans, maps of space may predominantly contain elements of visual scenes, while in species that rely on other senses for exploring the environment such as somatosensory or olfactory, maps may be more “multisensory” or biased toward the sensory modality that individuals use the most. One can hardly imagine a naked mole rat would have a visual representation of the underground tunnels they inhabit. Indeed, studies in blind humans have shown that in the absence of vision they use other senses to build cognitive maps of the environment (Ibrahim et al. 2024). Congenitally blind humans do not develop visual imagery but an Umwelt made of other senses (Cattaneo et al. 2008). Nevertheless, diurnal primates, including humans, may have preserved their nocturnal ancestors' capability to navigate using path integration; therefore under certain conditions, they may be able to form grid and place cell representations of space in the entorhinal cortex and HPC, respectively.
The HPC and the ETC are located far downstream of the various chains of sensory processing and receive inputs from multiple sensory systems, i.e., they are multisensory polyglots (McNaughton et al. 1996; Ravassard et al. 2013; Acharya et al. 2016; Purandare et al. 2022). Sensory inputs can be filtered by multimodal attention according to their behavioral relevance (Mehta et al. 2000), before they arrive at the ETC and HPC (Martinez‐Trujillo 2022). Indeed, attentional filtering has been reported in structures as early in the hierarchy of sensory processing as the thalamus (Halassa and Kastner 2017). Therefore, multimodal representations in the HPC and ETC are built from a mix of sensory signals that come in different proportions and flavors determined by the species adaptation (e.g., predominance of vision in primates) and the behavioral relevance assigned to each signal modality (e.g., attention to visual, auditory, or somatosensory cues) (see Figure 2a,b for overview of rat and marmoset sensory adaptations). During the night, when visual signals are scarce, primates may calibrate their sensory systems using attention to prioritize auditory and somatosensory cues. Supporting this view, blind humans use a combination of auditory, vestibular and haptic cues to navigate environments (Golledge et al. 1996). Blind humans may show specializations in the HPC and ETC like those found in nocturnal rodents, e.g., place cells, or some sort of intermediate representation between view and place cells.

Thus, embedded in evolutionary programs guided by natural selection must be some flexibility to calibrate the relevance of different types of signals in response to changes in environmental conditions. That would allow adjusting navigation strategies to different ecological niches (Figure 2a,b, central diagram). Rats cannot develop the stereoscopic and color vision of primates, nor grow a pathway from LGN to V1 that matches the primate ventral pathway. However, rats can perceive large changes in visual orientation or shape, or shades of gray (Reinagel 2015). When rats are tested in virtual reality environments or using visual stimuli while their bodies are restrained, the number of place cells in the HPC is substantially reduced and visual selectivities emerge (Ravassard et al. 2013; Acharya et al. 2016; Purandare et al. 2022). Neither monkeys nor humans can grow a star like Condylura cristata or large whiskers and a barrel cortex to map with high resolution the somatosensory features of objects like rats. However, suppose primates are tested in conditions where visual inputs are reduced or not informative. In that case, they may use other vestibular, auditory, somatosensory, or olfactory cues for navigation, using path integration, and place cell‐like representations may emerge in the HPC. This prediction will be worth testing in future studies, perhaps using nocturnal primates (e.g., Microcebus murinus or Aoutus trivirgatus) or diurnal rodents (e.g., Sciurus carolinensis ). The most important idea presented here is to bring the perspective of evolutionary biology into the study of HPC representations that serve spatial navigation and cognitive map formation. While the mammalian hippocampus is an evolutionarily ancient structure with conserved features across species, sensory ecology has powerfully shaped species‐specific Umwelten, driving diversification in hippocampal organization and the way space is represented in different brains.

## Conclusions
While prior reviews have highlighted cross‐species differences in hippocampal representations (Murray et al. 2017; Rolls and Wirth 2018; Xu and Mao 2025), our paper advances this discussion in several important ways. First, we synthesize earlier ideas within a sensory ecology framework, introducing the concept of the Umwelt as a unifying principle to explain why nocturnal rodents and diurnal primates diverge in their hippocampal spatial codes. Second, we incorporate recent findings from freely moving diurnal primates—evidence absent from earlier reviews—that reveal a predominance of view‐ and gaze‐related selectivity over “pure” place coding. Third, we link these observations to the evolutionary pressures shaping the primate visual system, including the emergence of cortical specializations such as area MT and the eye movement system. Finally, we put forward the ecological niche calibration hypothesis, proposing that species can adapt their navigation strategies and corresponding hippocampal codes to different environmental contexts, albeit within the constraints imposed by their sensory capacities.
