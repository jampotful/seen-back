SYNCAS based CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing in predatory mites, whiteflies and stinkbugs.

# SYNCAS based CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing in predatory mites, whiteflies and stinkbugs

## Abstract
AbstractDespite the establishment of CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing protocols in a wide range of organisms, genetic engineering is still challenging for many organisms due to constraints including lethality of embryo injection, difficulties in egg/embryo collection or viviparous lifestyles. Recently, an efficient CRISPR-Cas9 method, termed SYNCAS, was developed to genetically modify spider mites and thrips species. The method is based on maternal injection of formulated CRISPR-Cas9 using saponin and BAPC. Here, we investigate whether the method can be used to perform gene editing in other arthropods such as the beneficial predatory mites Amblyseius swirskii and Phytoseiulus persimilis, and the pests Bemisia tabaci and Nezara viridula. For the predatory mites, Antp and SLC25A38 were used as target genes, while the ortholog of the Drosophila melanogaster ABCG transporter white was targeted in B. tabaci and N. viridula. All species were successfully edited with the highest efficiencies (up to 39%) being obtained for B. tabaci. For A. swirskii and P. persimilis no clear phenotypes could be observed, even though SLC25A38 was successfully knocked-out. The lack of a color phenotype in SLC25A38 mutants was confirmed in the spider mite Tetranychus urticae. Disruption of the target gene Antp is likely lethal in predatory mites, as no true null mutants could be recovered. For B. tabaci, KO of white resulted in orange eyes which diverges from the phenotype seen in white mutants of D. melanogaster. In the last species, N. viridula, a single phenotypic mutant could be detected having a patchy white body coloration with wild type eye coloration. Genotyping revealed a single amino acid deletion at the target site, suggesting the creation of a hypomorphic allele. To conclude, the protocols provided in this work can contribute to the genetic study of predatory mites used in biological control, as well as hemipteran pests.Keywords: Gene editing, Microinjection, Knock-out, Amblyseius swirskii, Phytoseiulus persimilis, Bemisia tabaci, Nezara viridula

## Introduction
Arthropods comprise a diverse array of species, including insects and chelicerates such as mites and ticks, and hold immense ecological, agricultural, and medical significance. The manipulation of their genomes has long been a pursuit in the field of entomology, aiming at elucidating fundamental biological processes, improving traits of organisms with economic value and controlling pests (Gantz and Akbari, 2018; Molcho et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2017). Although conventional methods for genetic transformation using transposases (PiggyBac, P-element) and zinc-finger nucleases have been available for several decades (Cary et al., 1989; Miller et al., 1985), it was the development of CRISPR-Cas9 as a gene editing tool that truly revolutionized genetic engineering (Jinek et al., 2012). Since its emergence, CRISPR-Cas9 has been widely used in a vast number of arthropod species (Singh et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2017). Traditional techniques rely on injection of CRISPR-Cas9 components directly in the eggs or embryos of the target organism. This approach, however, is not applicable for every organism due to various constraints such as lethality of injection, eggs/embryos being hard to collect in sufficient numbers, or a viviparous lifestyle (giving live birth instead of laying eggs). Among these hard to edit arthropods are beneficial organisms such as phytoseiid mites, but also pests like spider mites, thrips, whiteflies and stinkbugs (Cagliari et al., 2020; De Rouck et al., 2024; Dermauw et al., 2020; Heu et al., 2020; Presnail and Hoy, 1992).
Biological control is considered the main alternative to chemical based pest control due to its reduced risk for environmental and human health (Knapp et al., 2018; Mcmurtry et al., 2013). Phytoseiid mites are the most important arthropods used as biological control agents (BCA) of phytophagous mite species, thrips and whiteflies as they account for more than 60% of the BCA market (Knapp et al., 2018) with Phytoseiulus persimilis and Amblyseius swirskii belonging to the key species. P. persimilis is specialized in praying upon Tetranychus species and was the first predatory mite to be commercialized (van Lenteren, 2012). A. swirskii, on the other hand, is a more generalist predator able to efficiently control thrips and whitefly species in addition to spider mites in greenhouses (Vangansbeke et al., 2023). Due to the importance that integrated pest management (IPM) has acquired worldwide, interest on BCAs used in augmentative biological control moves forward (van Lenteren et al., 2018). There is increasing focus on genetically improving BCAs (Bielza et al., 2020; Lirakis and Magalhães, 2019), which could provide a strong foundation for successful IPM strategies. Already in 1992, attempts were made to genetically transform predatory mites using a plasmid based technique in Galendromus (Metaseiulus) occidentalis (Presnail and Hoy, 1992), but these were never successfully repeated. Direct embryo injection in predatory mites is very hard due to the limited success of dechorionation according to Presnail and Hoy (1992), which is also considered a critical step in microinjection of ticks (Sharma et al., 2022). The possibility to perform efficient genome editing on these species would facilitate the validation of genes/allelic variants responsible for a trait of interest and facilitate the subsequent selection or introduction of that trait (Leung et al., 2020).
Next to beneficial organisms, gene editing in pests such as whiteflies and stinkbugs is desired to better understand and control them as they are responsible for high economic losses in agriculture (Conti et al., 2021; Sani et al., 2020). The silverleaf whitefly, Bemisia tabaci, is a highly invasive and polyphagous pest species causing damage to various economically important agricultural crops including tobacco, bell pepper, tomato, bean, cotton, potato, eggplant and cassava, as well as ornamental plants such as poinsettia and hibiscus (Defra UK, 2024). Both larvae and adults feed directly on the phloem sap, causing growth distortion, stem weakening, chlorotic spotting and premature leaf drop on their host (Inbar and Gerling, 2008; Zaidi et al., 2017). In addition, secretion of honeydew onto the surfaces of leaves and fruits serves as ideal growth medium for fungi such as black sooty mold, further damaging the host plant (Perring et al., 2018). Last, B. tabaci serves a vector for over 200 plant viruses including begomoviruses which are the leading cause of yield losses in crops, ranging from 20 to 100% with economic losses of millions of dollars (Sani et al., 2020). Pesticides are widely used to control this problematic pest, but intensive employment of these chemicals has resulted in resistance formation as resistance has been reported to over 50 different compounds (Mota-Sanchez and Wise, 2024). CRISPR-Cas9 can play a part in the control of this species, but the small size of whitefly eggs (0.2 mm × 0.08 mm), eggs being anchored in the leaf tissue and high mortality post-injection, make traditional embryo injections technically challenging (Heu et al., 2020; Voigt et al., 2019).
The family of Pentatomidae or stinkbugs harbors several important pest species including Halyomorpha halys, Bagrada hilaris, Piezodorus guildinii, Euschistus heros and Nezara viridula, some of which can be highly invasive. Stinkbugs can cause serious damage to vegetables, fruit trees, nuts and commodity crops such as soybean, corn and cotton (Corrêa-Ferreira and De Azevedo, 2002; Panizzi, 2015; Sosa-Gómez et al., 2020) and are generally hard to control. In order to develop adequate control strategies it is crucial to understand the biology of pest organisms and having a gene editing tool available can aid in this process. Among stinkbugs, the Southern green stinkbug, N. viridula, is frequently used as a model for Pentatomidae (Čokl et al., 2020; de Santana Souza et al., 2013; Lavore et al., 2018; Prado et al., 2009; Rebora et al., 2018). Unfortunately, even though stinkbug eggs are easy to collect, embryo injection proves to be extremely challenging as the eggs are difficult to penetrate and hatching rates drop significantly after being punctured (Cagliari et al., 2020; Siegfried, 2022).
Since traditional embryo injection is not feasible for many arthropod species, various studies have explored CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing through injection of the necessary components in the body cavity of adult females. In Aedes aegypti, for example, successful gene editing was achieved by using a method termed Receptor-Mediated Ovary Transduction of Cargo (ReMOT Control). This approach makes use of a conserved egg maturation process, vitellogenesis, in which yolk is accumulated in developing oocytes through receptor mediated endocytosis. Attaching the yolk ligand recognized by the oocyte receptors to the Cas9 protein, increases its uptake in the eggs and hence increases editing efficiencies (Chaverra-Rodriguez et al., 2018). Later, ReMOT Control was expanded to other species including Anopheles stephensi (Macias et al., 2020), Nasonia vitripennis (Chaverra-Rodriguez et al., 2020), Bemisia tabaci (Heu et al., 2020), Culex pipiens (Li et al., 2021), Ixodes scapularis (Sharma et al., 2022) and Rhodium prolixus (Lima et al., 2024). One drawback of the ReMOT Control approach is the requirement of a specific ligand (and thus a specific recombinant Cas9 protein) that is recognized by the oocytes of the target organism. Fortunately, some species, including Tribolium castaneum, Blattella germanica, Sogatella furcifera, Ramazzottius varieornatus and Orius strigicollis, can be efficiently edited via the maternal injection approach using commercially available Cas9 protein without need for ligands (Kondo et al., 2024; Matsuda et al., 2024; Shirai et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023), also termed Direct Parental CRISPR (DIPA-CRISPR).
Other species, such as Tetranychus urticae, are not easily modified via the DIPA-CRISPR approach (Dermauw et al., 2020). In such cases, a formulation based approach can drastically improve gene editing efficiencies, as was shown for T. urticae where a CRISPR-Cas9 based method termed SYNCAS was able to increase gene editing frequencies up to 40-fold (De Rouck et al., 2024). The SYNCAS formulation exploits a synergism between Branched amphiphilic peptide capsules (BAPC) and Quillaja saponin. The first are self-assembling peptide-based nanoparticles, while the latter are triterpene glycosides extracted from the soap bark tree, Quillaja saponaria. In the present work, the potential of the SYNCAS based approach is further explored in other hard to edit arthropods including beneficial organisms, P. persimilis and A. swirskii, and pests, B. tabaci and N. viridula.

## Materials and methods

## Arthropod rearing
The A. swirskii strain was originally provided by Biobest (Belgium). P. persimilis was obtained from Koppert Biological Systems (Berkel en Rodenrijs, the Netherlands). Both species were maintained on detached leaves of Phaseolus vulgaris cv. “Prelude” on wet cotton. A. swirskii was reared providing pollen (Nutrimite™, Biobest) and T. urticae as prey to boost up the growth. P. persimilis was reared on the CyfR strain of T. urticae (İnak et al., 2024). This spider mite strain was reared on whole P. vulgaris plants. B. tabaci (biotype MED-Q1) was collected in Ierapetra in 2018 and provided by John Vontas (IMBB-Forth). The whiteflies were reared on whole tobacco plants. An in-house culture of N. viridula, present in the lab for over 10 years, originating from individuals collected from different sites in Europe (France, Spain and Italy), was reared on flat bean pods (Phaseolus vulgaris L. cv. ‘Helda’) and sunflower seeds (Helianthus annuus L.). Water was supplied via moistened synthetic cotton. All organisms were kept in a climatic chamber at 25 °C, 60 % relative humidity and 16:8 light:dark photoperiod.

## Target-genes, sgRNA design and phylogenetic analysis
Based on phenotypes observed in spider mites (De Rouck et al., 2024; Huo et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2023), orthologues of T. urticae SLC25A38 (tetur03g01800; sequence available at: bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/orcae/overview/Tetur) and Antp (tetur20g02430) were identified using tBLASTn in transcriptomes of the predatory mites P. persimilis and A. swirskii (Bioproject PRJNA784037 and PRJNA484730, respectively) (Bajda et al., 2022; Paspati et al., 2022). The sequences chosen as target are GHIT01027552.1 (A. swirskii SLC25A38), contig_3656 (P. persimilis SLC25A38; see “final 40 million reads assembly” in Bajda et al., 2022), GHIT01015358.1 (A. swirskii Antp) and contig_14009 (P. persimilis Antp). To validate the phenotype observed in Huo et al. (2021),T. urticae SLC25A38 (tetur03g01800) was also targeted in a red spider mite strain (CyfR).
For B. tabaci and N. viridula, the orthologues of the D. melanogaster white gene (Heu et al., 2020; Souza et al., 2022) were selected as targets. Except for B. tabaci, the sgRNA target sequences were designed using the online tool CRISPOR (Concordet and Haeussler, 2018) and are listed in Table S1. For B. tabaci the sgRNA from Heu et al. (2020) targeting exon 3 was used. Last, a phylogenetic analysis of N. viridula and B. tabaci ABCG transporters involved in pigmentation was performed. The protein sequence of D. melanogaster white was used as query in a BLASTp search against the in silico prediction proteomes of N. viridula, B. tabaci MEAM1 and B. tabaci MED (assemblies PGI_NEZAVIv3, ASM185493v1 and PGI_BMITA_v3, respectively). Only those BLASTp hits with a bit-score higher than 275 and only one isoform per gene was used for further analysis. This selection of N. viridula, B. tabaci MEAM1 and B. tabaci MED ABCG protein sequences was aligned with ABCG transporter protein sequences of H. sapiens, T. urticae and six insect species (Dermauw and Van Leeuwen, 2014). using MAFFT version 7 (Katoh et al., 2019) with “auto” option. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was obtained through IQ-TREE 1.6.12 run with default parameters (Nguyen et al., 2015). The integrated ModelFinder function (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017) indicated LG + F + I + G4 as the best-fit model. Ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) were set at 1000 replicates (Hoang et al., 2018). The layout of the resulting consensus tree was edited using MEGA X (Kumar et al., 2018).

## CRISPR-Cas9 injection mix
Recombinant Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 protein (Alt-R® S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3, #10007808) and sgRNAs (Alt-R™ CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Leuven, Belgium). Cas9 protein was concentrated to 50 μg/μl using an Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter (30 kDa cut-off) while lyophilized sgRNAs were dissolved at a concentration of 10 μg/μl. Branched Amphiphilic Peptide Capsules (BAPC) were obtained from Phoreus Biotech and dissolved at a concentration of 10 μg/μl.
The SYNCAS formulation (De Rouck et al., 2024) was used for the injections of all tested organisms. First, Cas9 and sgRNA, specific for the targeted gene, were incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Then, BAPC and saponin (Merck Millipore, #558255) were added. The mixture was subsequently incubated 30 min on ice and finally centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 20,000 g. The injection mix was kept on ice until the time of injection. The specific composition of each injection mix is shown in Table S2.

## Microinjections
Injections were performed using a Nanoject III programmable nanoliter injector (Drummond Scientific). Glass needles were obtained form 3–000-203-G/X Glass Capillaries (Drummond Scientific) pulled with a P-1000 Micropipette Puller (Sutter Instruments) with following settings: “heat: 500, pull: 60, velocity: 70, delay: 200, pressure: 500, Ramp: 490” and were sharpened with a BV-10 Micropipette Beveler (Sutter Instruments) at a 15° angle. A M205 FCA microscope (Leica) was used during the injections.
Two days post adult emergence (PAE), fertilized female predatory mites were immobilized by placing the dorsal side on double-sided adhesive tape and were injected sideways near the third pair of legs. T. urticae (4 days PAE) and B. tabaci (2 days PAE) were immobilized by placing them with their dorsal side on 2% agarose gel platforms (prepared as described in De Rouck et al., 2024) and were injected ventrally near the third pair of legs and in the center of the abdomen respectively. After injection, mites and whiteflies were removed from the tape/gel using a fine wet brush and transferred to a single arena with detached bean leaf where they were allowed to lay eggs for a total of 48 h with a transfer to a new leaf every 24 h. For the injection of N. viridula, stink bugs of different ages (3-4 d and 6-7 d PAE) were anesthetized by placing them on ice for several minutes after which they were injected ventrally near the center of the abdomen. After injection, stinkbugs were reared until they produced 1 or 2 egg masses (eggs were split per injected mother). Used injection volumes for the various organisms are specified in Table S2.

## Mutant screening
Offspring of injected females were initially visually screened under a stereomicroscope (Leica S8 APO) based on the expected phenotypes described for SLC25A38 (Huo et al., 2021), Antp (De Rouck et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023) and white (Heu et al., 2020; Souza et al., 2022). SLC25A38 mutants of A. swirskii and P. persimilis manifesting an atypical body coloration were first isolated, allowed to lay eggs, and then individually crushed in 20 μl of STE buffer (100 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl,1 mM EDTA,pH 8) with 2 μl of proteinase K (10 mg/ml). DNA of phenotypic mutants of B. tabaci was also extracted using this buffer while DNA of the mutant N. viridula was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Crude DNA extracts were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min followed by 10 min at 99 °C to deactivate proteinase K.
In addition to the screening based on visible phenotypes, offspring with wild type (WT) appearance were screened as well in cases where the phenotype was unclear or hard to distinguish (all cases except B. tabaci). For the predatory mites, injections were repeated and resulting offspring was pooled together after reaching the adult stage, regardless of their phenotype. Mites that developed from eggs laid between 0-24h and 24–48h post injection were pooled separately. DNA extraction was performed on these pools using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit. In one of the injection replicates, 48 random G0 P. persimilis in which SLC25A38 was targeted, were collected and DNA was extracted individually using STE buffer with proteinase K. In the case of T. urticae, 21 G0 daughters from CyfR females injected with the CRISPR-Cas9 injection mix targeting SLC25A38 were randomly isolated. After mating with their first born son and producing G1 daughters, the females were individually crushed in STE buffer with proteinase K. For N. viridula, offspring were pooled in various groups and DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit.
PCR was performed on 2 μl of DNA extract using the GoTaq® G2 DNA Polymerase kit and primers listed in Table S1. The following program was used for amplification: denaturation for 2 min at 92 °C followed by 40 cycles of 30 s at 92 °C, 30 s at 52 °C and 1 min at 72 °C. A final elongation step at 72 °C was held for 5 min. Quality of the PCR products were assessed by running a 2% agarose gel for 30 min at 100 V. PCR product was purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Cycle Pure Kit (Omega Bio-Tek) and sent to LGC genomics (Germany) for Sanger sequencing. All Sanger chromatograms are available in (File S1).

## Cloning and plate sequencing
PCR product obtained from pools (see 2.5) or individual predatory mites was cloned using the CloneJET PCR Cloning Kit (ThermoFisher) and used to transform Subcloning Efficiency™ DH5α Competent Cells (ThermoFisher). Transformed cells were grown on a 96-well plate on LB-agar containing 100 μg/ml of carbenicillin and colonies from each replicate were sent to LGC genomics for Sanger sequencing with the pJET1.2 primers. The obtained sequences were visualized using BioEdit version 7.2.

## Amplicon sequencing
DNA extracts obtained for the N. viridula mutant and offspring without clear phenotype were used in a PCR reaction with an adapted version of the “Nv_white” primer pair (Table S1). Two adaptor sequences, 3′-ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT-5′ and 3′-GACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCT-5′, were attached to the 3′-end of the forward and reverse primer respectively as required by the “NGSelect Amplicons on MiSeq” service provided by Eurofins Genomics (Germany). After purification, PCR samples were sent to Eurofins Genomics where amplicon sequencing was performed providing 60K read pairs per amplicon (2 x 300 bp read mode). Amplicon data was processed using Qiime2 software, version 2024.2 (Bolyen et al., 2019). Obtained FASTQ files were submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive repository (Bioproject: PRJNA1147920).

## Results

## Screening for mutant phenotypes in predatory mites
Gene editing attempts were first performed in A. swirskii due to its ability to grow on pollen as the only food source, convenient for experiments. Three independent injections were performed targeting Antp in A. swirskii and resulting offspring was screened for mutant phenotypes related to leg development during different life stages, previously described in other arthropods species (De Rouck et al., 2024; Khadjeh et al., 2012; Luo et al., 2023), with a final screen after they reached adulthood. For SLC25A38, the second target gene that is believed to be involved in the body coloration of red spider mites (Huo et al., 2021), three independent injections were performed as well. The screening of possible mutant phenotypes related to the body coloration led to the identification of a single mite presenting a lighter/transparent coloration in the idiosomal region (Fig. 1, Fig. S1, Table 1). The mutant was unable to lay eggs after being reared for four days, after which it was homogenized. Sanger sequencing of the mutant revealed the presence of CRISPR events in the region targeted by the sgRNA. The presence of double peaks in the chromatogram around the targeted region suggested multiple events occurred (File S1.1). Cloning of the PCR product indicated that two independent CRISPR knock-out (KO) events, a 88 nt deletion (occurring with a 1 nt insertion) and a 7 nt deletion, likely localized on the two copies of the gene present in the diploid female (Fig. 1). The same approach was used to target SLC25A38 in P. persimilis. A single injection was performed followed by visual screening for the presence of color anomalies in the progeny (Table 1). Four daughters, characterized by a lighter coloration (Fig. 1), were selected and reared separately to allow them to lay eggs. Sanger sequencing revealed the presence of one complete mutant female (File S1.2), but the remaining three daughters appeared to be WT, indicating the lighter coloration is inaccurate to score gene editing. Once more, double peaks were visible in the chromatogram of the mutant around the expected cleavage site of the Cas9 protein, from which it was possible to distinguish two events and no WT sequence. Unlike the A. swirskii mutant, this female was able to lay eggs. The sequencing of the resulting F2 males clearly revealed the presence of two independent events, a 5 nt deletion and a 2 nt insertion (Fig. 1). One mosaic F2 female (5 nt deletion – WT), was also present in the offspring (File S1.2).

## Knock-out of SLC25A38 in T. urticae
Since in P. persimilis WT genotypes were found in three out of four divergent phenotypes, the marker gene is likely not linked to the lighter body coloration and hence is not reliable to phenotypically score gene editing. As this target gene was selected based on phenotypes described in T. urticae upon RNAi silencing (Huo et al., 2021), the effect of SLC25A38 KO was also studied in this species to verify its effect on body coloration. After injection, it was not possible to detect any diverging phenotype in the progeny and therefore the targeted region of SLC25A38 was analyzed in 21 random G0 adult females. Eight mutants, of which six mosaics (based on the presence of double peaks in the Sanger chromatograms), were identified (File S1.7). The G1 male offspring of some of the edited mites was also analyzed, yet no visible phenotype was observed even though sequencing confirmed the KO of SLC25A38. As a final check, since the sensitivity of Sanger sequencing to detect allelic variation is limited (İnak et al., 2024; Ishige et al., 2018; Njiru et al., 2023), a stable line carrying a 8 nt deletion was obtained after repeated backcrossing to ensure only mutant alleles were present. Once more, no difference in body coloration was visible comparing mites from this line and WT individuals, contradicting the study of Huo et al., (2021) (Fig. 1, Fig. S2).

## Verification of gene editing of SLC25A38 and Antp in pooled offspring of P. persimilis and A. swirskii
Since the visual screening of mutant phenotypes was challenging and inaccurate (as WT genotypes were found among divergent phenotypes), PCR products obtained from DNA extracted from pooled G0 offspring was cloned to detect CRISPR-events in the gene pool, regardless of the phenotype.
Three independent injections were performed for SLC25A38 in both P. persimilis and A. swirskii and after extracting DNA of pooled progeny, the PCR amplicon was cloned and individual colonies were sequenced and analyzed for gene editing events (Table 2). For the first SLC25A38 replicate in P. persimilis, offspring generated between 0-24h and 24–48h post injection showed a remarkable higher percentage of edited sequence in the 24–48h group, therefore only the progeny from eggs laid between 24 and 48h after injection was considered in the remaining groups (File S1.3). In two replicates for A. swirskii, multiple distinct gene editing events could be detected while for P. persimilis at least five unique events were found in all three replicates (Table 2, File S1.4).

Before pooling the G0 from replicate 3 targeting SLC25A38, 48 adult P. persimilis mites consisting of 15 males and 33 females were randomly selected and the occurrence of successful genome editing was assessed individually (File S1.5). Sanger sequencing confirmed that two mites (4.2 % of the total), a male and a female, were successfully edited and subsequent cloning confirmed the Sanger sequencing results. The male mite was likely a true null mutant as all ten clones presented a 2 nt deletion, while mosaicism occurred in the female as two colonies out of nine had a 2 nt insertion (File S1.5).
In the case of Antp only one replicate was performed for both species and one unique editing event was found for P. persimilis while two were detected for A. swirskii (Table 2, File S1.6).

## Targeting white in B. tabaci and N. viridula
A phylogenetic analysis of ABCG proteins involved in pigment transport showed that both N. viridula and B. tabaci have a clear ortholog of D. melanogaster white and scarlet, but an ortholog of D. melanogaster brown could not be identified. On the other hand, N. viridula and B. tabaci had 2 and 3 paralogues of Bombyx mori ok (Fig. S3), which encodes a protein belonging to an ABCG subfamily closely related to brown and is only present in Lepidoptera, Hymenoptera and Hemiptera (Khan et al., 2017; Reding and Pick, 2020; Wang et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2018).
For B. tabaci, three independent injections targeting white were performed on mated females. The resulting offspring was screened for red eye color morphs based on the phenotype described by Heu et al. (2020) upon disruption of white. Various mutant phenotypes were observed among the male and female progeny. Some individuals displayed a red eye phenotype, some had a mosaic appearance having one WT eye (brown color) and one orange colored eye, while others had two orange eyes (Fig. 2). Interestingly, all mutants displaying the latter phenotype died during the hatching from the pupal stage and were the only individuals for whom Sanger chromatograms with single peaks could be obtained. These deletions at the CRISPR-Cas9 target site confirm that gene editing was successfully achieved. For the remaining phenotypes, which hatched normally, double peaks appeared in the chromatogram starting from the target site, indicating mosaicism. To validate germline editing, four females with mosaic appearance were separated and allowed to produce offspring. Due to the haplodiploid nature of whitefly reproduction, full KO phenotypes are immediately visible in the haploid male progeny. Three of the four mosaic females successfully produced offspring, all containing orange eyed males (which died during hatching from pupae), hence demonstrating germ cells were edited and the orange eye color to be the true null phenotype. Gene editing efficiencies up to 20.0–39.1% were obtained (Table 3).

N. viridula was the last organism for which a gene editing experiment was performed. A single injection was carried out on twelve fertilized females from varying age (3-4 d and 6-7 d PAE), targeting the stinkbug ortholog of white. Eight females were able to produce offspring after injection. Each progeny was screened for reduced body pigmentation as observed upon RNAi-mediated silencing of white (Souza et al., 2022). Among the offspring of one female, a nymph with divergent pigmentation was found (Fig. 3, Fig. S4, Table 4). Sanger sequencing confirmed a 3 nt deletion at the Cas9 cleavage site resulting in the loss of one amino acid, asparagine, at position 16 in the protein sequence. ## Discussion
Gene editing tools like CRISPR-Cas9 offer significant potential for managing crop pests. They can enhance the effectiveness of natural predators and parasitoids by improving rearing feasibility, environmental tolerance, and predatory efficiency (Leung et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2022). Moreover, gene editing can advance our understanding of pest biology, such as resistance to pesticides (De Rouck et al., 2023; Douris et al., 2020; İnak et al., 2024) or can enable gene drives to control pest populations and disease transmission (Dalla Benetta et al., 2023; Kyrou et al., 2018; Suhag et al., 2021; Yadav et al., 2023). However, practical challenges exist with organisms where direct injections of gene editing components are not always feasible.
Predatory mites, which are among the most used BCA, remain limited in genetic improvement largely due to reliance on phenotypic selection, ignoring the cryptic genetic basis underlying the traits of interest (Leung et al., 2020). However, the identification of genetic factors that underlie a given trait (e.g. pesticide compatibility, resistance to environmental conditions and ability to grow on artificial and factitious food) can facilitate and speed up the trait selection process, for example by exploiting the power of marker-assisted selection (Bielza et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2020). Gene editing tools, such as CRISPR-Cas9, can help validate gene functions and potentially improve BCA breeding.
This study demonstrates a straightforward protocol to achieve CRISPR-Cas9 mediated gene editing in two major predatory mite species, A. swirskii and P. persimilis, based on the SYNCAS method developed in spider mites (De Rouck et al., 2024). Unlike T. urticae, where the phytoene desaturase gene allows easy visual screening via eye pigmentation (Bryon et al., 2017), phytoseiid mites lack common eye pigment markers (e.g., white, scarlet, kmo) due to their eyeless anatomy, complicating screening. Additionally, their pseudo-arrhenotokous reproduction requires fertilization to lay eggs, limiting screening for recessive traits as it prevents to obtaining solely haploid offspring (in contrast to arrhenotokous spider mites).
Given the lack of eyes, promising target genes were selected based on phenotypes observed in other species apart from eye coloration. The first selected gene, Antp, caused leg deformities in T. urticae upon KO (De Rouck et al., 2024) and extra legs in the spider Parasteatoda tepidariorum after RNAi silencing (Khadjeh et al., 2012). In this study, no distinct phenotypes were observed from larval to adult stages when targeting Antp in predatory mites. Noteworthy, the Antp hox gene is conserved (Chen et al., 2013; Grbić et al., 2011; Greenhalgh et al., 2020; Hajirnis and Mishra, 2021; Khadjeh et al., 2012) and results in clear phenotypes upon disruption in other species. Together with only a small fraction of edited clones being obtained from the pooled G0 offspring, this suggests that only mosaic mutants were present and that KO of Antp is lethal in A. swirskii and P. persimilis. Perhaps phenotypes were only visible in the developing embryos, but these were not screened here. Phytoseiid mites have a single copy of Antp, based on transcriptome analyses of P. persimilis and A. swirskii (Bajda et al., 2022; Paspati et al., 2022) and genomes of G. occidentalis (Hoy et al., 2016) and P. persimilis (GCA_037576195.1), while in both T. urticae and P. tepidariorum two copies of the gene are present (Grbić et al., 2011; Schwager et al., 2017). This lack of redundancy in phytoseiid mites may lead to lethality upon KO, similar to the recessive lethality seen in D. melanogaster (Denell et al., 1981). In T. urticae, the second copy (tetur20g02440) might compensate for the loss of the first, though its specific function remains unclear and could be divergent.
The second target gene, SLC25A38, was recently identified via RNAi as potentially influencing red body coloration in Tetranychus species (Huo et al., 2021). Only one A. swirskii female with deviating body coloration was observed, appearing to be a null mutant (with two different KO events) based on Sanger chromatograms. However, the transparent abdomen might result from a lack of visible eggs rather than pigment modification. The fact that only one mutant was visually detected might be related to the recessive nature of the mutation. In P. persimilis, four females had lighter coloration, but only one had mutations at the target site. This species' pigmentation varies with their diet, suggesting the lighter color resulted from different feeding rather than gene disruption. Moreover, all male offspring of the KO female carried one of the mutations, but appeared WT. As males are functionally haploid, they should display a mutant phenotype if there is a causal link between the SLC25A38 KO and the lighter body coloration, even when the trait is recessive. To verify spider mite phenotypes (Huo et al., 2021), SLC25A38 was also targeted in a red T. urticae strain. WT and KO mites were indistinguishable, contradicting prior claims of reduced red coloration post gene silencing, raising doubt about this gene's role in spider mite coloration.
Next, DNA from pooled offspring was sequenced to detect gene editing independently of phenotype. This approach identified several edited sequences, indicating undetected mutant G0 mites were present. However, the percentage of edited sequences found in the PCR clones is unreliable as an indicator of KO efficiency due to PCR amplification bias. It's also unclear if a particular CRISPR event occurred in one or multiple mites, and multiple distinct edits can happen within a single diploid mite. Still, assuming no more than two events per mite occurred, a rough estimate can be made of the minimal percentage of edited mites in the offspring. For example in A. swirskii, two distinct events were found in the DNA pool obtained from 30 G0 mites for SLC25A38 in the first replicate which means at least 3.3% (1/30) of the progeny was edited and using the same reasoning, a minimum of 5.1% was edited in the third replicate. In P. persimilis, at least 2.0%, 3.0% and 6.6% of the offspring showed mutations at the target site in the SLC25A38 gene in the three independent replicates. In addition, for the last replicate 48 mites were analyzed individually before pooling the G0 offspring and 4.2% of them were edited, which is in line with the estimated frequencies. However, it is important to note that germline editing was not systematically evaluated here and offspring was not analyzed per injected mother, hence no information is obtained for the efficiency in individual females. Although further studies are required to accurately determine efficiencies, this study marks the first CRISPR-Cas9 application in predatory mites with promising and repeatable results for future chelicerate research.
Another organisms that has proven notoriously difficult to edit through traditional embryo injection, is the whitefly pest B. tabaci. In 2020, Heu et al. applied ReMOT Control, using a Cas9 linked to an ovary-targeting peptide “BtKV,” achieving variable KO efficiencies with a peak of 12.7% (or 20.7% when only looking at male offspring) in one replicate. In this study, using the SYNCAS formulation with regular Cas9, editing efficiencies mounted as high as 39.1%. Notably, Heu et al. allowed whiteflies to lay eggs for two weeks post-injection, whereas in this study, eggs were only collected up to 48 h post-injection with an interval of 24 h. Our previous work in spider mites and thrips showed that the timing of egg collection is a critical parameter influencing the frequency of mutant offspring (De Rouck et al., 2024). In T. urticae, the highest editing efficiencies were in offspring produced 24–48 h post-injection, while in Frankliniella occidentalis, only the offspring generated within the first 24h was edited. For B. tabaci, the results are less clear as in two replicates high editing rates were observed the first 24h and for one replicate 24–48h post injection, with large differences between the two time intervals.
The observed KO phenotypes are in line with previously described phenotype. However, using ReMOT Control, only red eyed (mosaic) phenotypes were reported among the G0 offspring of injected females and orange eyed (true null mutant) individuals were only found after performing additional crosses (Heu et al., 2020), whereas here orange eyed whiteflies were already present in the progeny of injected females. Whether this is due to the higher dose of Cas9 or the formulation used in our SYNCAS approach remains speculative. Moreover, the study of Heu et al. reported an inhibitory effect of saponin on gene editing efficiencies as no mutants were recovered using formulations containing 8 and 16 ng/μl saponin. The final concentration of saponin in our injection mix amounted 300 ng/μl, resulting in high editing efficiencies. However, saponins are a highly diverse class of molecules and the exact saponin used in the ReMOT Control study was not specified. In addition BAPC was also present in the CRISPR-Cas9 mix of this study and hence any comparison is difficult to make. Last, all true null mutants died during the pupal-to-adult transition, demonstrating the vital function of white in B. tabaci development. Although white is often used as a marker for targeted mutagenesis (Bai et al., 2019; de Souza Pacheco et al., 2022; Heryanto et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020), it can be lethal in some species, such as Helicoverpa armigera, Lygus hesperus and Oncopeltus fasciatus (Heu et al., 2022; Khan et al., 2017; Reding and Pick, 2020).
The high gene editing efficiencies demonstrated in this study are important for the routine implementation of CRISPR-Cas9 in B. tabaci. CRISPR-Cas9 can contribute to the control whiteflies in different aspects such as the evident fundamental research and elucidation of resistance mechanisms as is the case, but it can also be employed for gene drive strategies to either reduce population sizes or inhibit the transmission of viruses (Suhag et al., 2021). Several potential target genes such as genes involved in gamete production, sex determination or host-plant interactions have been reported (Mahmood et al., 2023).
White was also targeted to evaluate gene editing in N. viridula, another hemipteran species. Knockdown of white in N. viridula adults resulted in a clear phenotype and no lethal effects were observed (Souza et al., 2022). However, the strength of knockdown in the offspring was not assessed and perhaps silencing did not reach the lethal level that was obtained previously for L. hesperus (Heu et al., 2022). In this study, a homozygous mutant was found harboring a 3 nt deletion resulting in the loss of asparagine residue at position 16 in the white protein, located outside known functional domains. As the phenotype of the mutant was only divergent in certain parts of its body and mosaicism with WT alleles was not present, as proven by amplicon sequencing, the mutation is likely hypomorphic. Amplicon sequencing of remaining offspring revealed multiple frame-shift events without visible phenotype changes, likely due mosaicism with WT alleles. Since the only recovered mutant had an in-frame mutation and no true null mutants were found, lethality of white KO cannot be excluded and is likely given the recessive lethality observed in O. fasciatus (Reding and Pick, 2020).
The N. viridula female that produced the phenotypic mutant, generated twenty additional larvae where four more distinct events could be detected in the DNA pool, indicating multiple individuals were edited and gene editing frequencies are likely above 10%. In three other DNA pools, assuming no more than two events occur per larvae, an estimated gene editing frequency of 2.6%, 1.0% and 1.6% was obtained in amplicon group 1,3 and 4 respectively. However, the degree of mosaicism in these DNA pools cannot be determined. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of gene editing in N. viridula, hence comparisons with other work in this species is not possible.
Nevertheless, gene editing through embryo injection was obtained for E. heros, another member of the Pentatomidae family, targeting a gene termed yellow (Cagliari et al., 2020). Out of 719 injected eggs, only six larvae hatched among which one individual was confirmed to be edited (Cagliari et al., 2020). Using ReMOT Control to target cinnabar in H. halys, about 2% off the offspring was edited and mutants displayed a divergent cuticle color as well (Terradas et al., 2022).
In a more distinct related species, O. strigicollis (family: Anthocoridae), DIPA-CRISPR was performed obtaining up to 52% gene editing frequencies in the cinnabar gene (Matsuda et al., 2024). It was demonstrated that in O. strigicollis, the age of the injected mothers is a crucial parameter influencing efficiencies. Since only a limited number of injections was performed here, further optimization is recommended for N. viridula using more individuals in more different age groups with a narrower age interval. In addition, it would be advisable to include a different target gene such as the cinnabar gene (GenBank accession: CAH1402338.1; in some species referred to as kynurenine 3-monooxygenase or kmo) used in H. halys, O. strigicollis and L. hesperus (Heu et al., 2022; Matsuda et al., 2024; Terradas et al., 2022).
Notably, none of the B. tabaci mutants had white compound eyes, a typical trait in insect white mutants. Instead, they exhibited orange eyes. However, this aligns with most observed white mutant phenotypes in other hemipterans, including O. fasciatus (Reding and Pick, 2020), N. lugens (Xue et al., 2018) and Peregrennis maidis (Klobasa et al., 2021), and resembles the red-eye phenotype of white mutants in F. occidentalis (De Rouck et al., 2024). Although, in the latter thysanopteran species white-like, a paralogue of white, is present and needs to be knocked-out as well to obtain pigmentless compound eyes. Based on our phylogenetic analysis (Fig. S3), such white-like gene is not present in hemipteran species. Remarkably, B. tabaci, N. viridula, O. fasciatus, and N. lugens lack a brown homolog but have multiple paralogs of ok, an ABCG subfamily related to brown. These ok proteins might (self)dimerize to form functional ABCG transporters that transfer orange pigment in B. tabaci as in H. armigera ok transporters are clearly involved in transport of yellow pigment (Khan et al., 2017).
In conclusion, using SYNCAS, successful gene editing was achieved in beneficial predatory mites (A. swirskii and P. persimilis) and hemipteran pests (B. tabaci and N. viridula). The obtained efficiencies should make screening feasible enough to allow routine applications in these species, although for N. viridula further optimization would be recommended. Potentially, the SYNCAS approach can contribute to gene editing in other hemipterans as well, as editing in this insect order remains challenging (Pacheco et al., 2022). While only gene KO was performed, this foundational step is essential for introducing specific mutations via homology-directed repair.
