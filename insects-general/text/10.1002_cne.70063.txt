# Neural Repertoire Behind the World's Most Complex Retina: Neuroanatomy of the Stomatopod Lamina

## Abstract
ABSTRACTThe lamina is the first optic neuropil and visual information integration station in crustaceans and insects, containing synaptic connections between photoreceptors and first‐order interneurons. The lamina circuitry in mantis shrimp (stomatopods) is both interesting and complex, as there are 16 different types of photoreceptors contained within the mid‐band region of the eye. Using serial block‐face scanning electron microscopy, we have reconstructed photoreceptor terminals and lamina interneurons in two major superfamilies with different visual ecologies. Neurons follow the same general pattern as other crustaceans but with notable differences in gross anatomy from insects. The photoreceptors form bulbous terminals in the lamina, following the same overall connectivity pattern in all lamina cartridges across species examined and eye regions. The photoreceptor terminals themselves appear to be complex, with many large mitochondria, a notable difference between insects and stomatopods. Connectivity between photoreceptors and interneurons, as well as cross‐cartridge connections, is estimated based on neuronal overlap. Lamina monopolar cells follow previous research in stomatopods and crustaceans, with a set of common neuron types that may provide the beginning of an opponency circuit. Additionally, neurons that extend beyond their parent cartridge in the midband show a preference for branching between rows within the same visual column of cells, as well as some connections within the same row. This added complexity suggests that the stomatopod lamina performs a unique processing of visual signals versus other crustaceans and insects and provides further evidence for the emerging hypotheses around the processing of information by the scanning visual system of stomatopods.

## Introduction
The first neural structure behind the arthropod retina is the lamina, a neuropile assumed to be conserved in structure and function across the phylum. In many arthropods, the lamina summarizes visual input from the retina to reduce noise and decrease redundancy in photoreceptor information (Borst 2014; Laughlin 1987; Meinertzhagen and Sorra 2001; Sinakevitch et al. 2003; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981). However, recent works have suggested an expanded role for the lamina: in flies and butterflies, the lamina is involved in achromatic motion processing and may compare color information via opponent processing (Chen et al. 2019; Hamanaka et al. 2013; Schnaitmann et al. 2013, 2020). These discoveries raise questions about how the lamina may contribute to visual processing in different ways across the phylum.
Stomatopod crustaceans, also known as mantis shrimp, have the most complex retina of all animals investigated, with up to 20 information channels of visual input based around 16 anatomically different photoreceptor types, most of which are in a specialized midband area (Figure 1) (Cronin and Marshall 1989; Marshall et al. 2007). Despite having at least 12 different spectral sensitivities, they have relatively poor color discrimination and may be using a different method to interpret color information rather than through opponency (Marshall and Arikawa 2014; Thoen et al. 2014). They also exhibit a visual behavior called scanning, which is unique among arthropods and distinct from object tracking or optokinetic saccades (Cronin et al. 1988; Land et al. 1990; Marshall et al. 2014). The scanning motion involves animals moving their eyestalk at right angles to their midband and is thought to act similar to a barcode or multispectral scanner (Land et al. 1990; Wolpert 2011). Though the structure of the stomatopod retina is well described, the visual signaling pathways and how they lead to the sophisticated behaviors stomatopods show are poorly understood (Dingle and Caldwell 1969; Green and Patek 2018). It is not known what information the stomatopod lamina processes, and here, we provide new observations on retina–lamina and within‐lamina connectivity in an attempt to fill this knowledge gap. Because the stomatopod retina is so specialized, the lamina must either contain structures to process the information from the many different channels or selectively filter or condense it. Previous research has characterized some cell structures (Kleinlogel et al. 2003; Kleinlogel and Marshall 2005; Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017), but the overall structure of each cartridge and how cartridges are connected has not been described. Further, the information may be processed differently in stomatopods with varying ecological demands, prompting us to investigate multiple species.

The single retinal units of the arthropod compound eye are the ommatidia, which are comprised of multiple (often eight) photoreceptor cells, which extend their light‐sensitive microvilli into a central rhabdom. Mantis shrimp have at least eight different types of ommatidia, which are located in different parts of the eye. The stomatopod eyes contain three regions: a dorsal and a ventral hemisphere and a central, highly specialized midband (Figure 1C). Hemisphere ommatidia resemble those found in the eyes of other crustaceans and are primarily sensitive to linearly polarized light. The enlarged midband ommatidia are separated into six rows, four specialized for color vision and two for sensing elliptical (often circular) polarized light (Figure 1D) (Cronin and Marshall 1989; Marshall 1988; Marshall et al. 2007, 1991a). Due to the separation between information types, mantis shrimp offer the opportunity to understand if the pathways to compare and process color and polarization information use similar mechanisms or if this information is treated differently.
Within a single ommatidium, individual photoreceptor cells can be divided into short visual fibers (SVF), which terminate in the lamina, and long visual fibers (LVF), which extend through the lamina and terminate in the medulla (Thoen et al. 2018). The SVF cells can be divided by the direction of their microvilli, with two groups of photoreceptors having orthogonal microvilli to receive linearly polarized light separated by 90° (Marshall et al. 1999, 1991b). In stomatopods, these groups are R1/4/5 and R2/3/6/7, and R8 is the LVF (Figure 1E) (Cronin and Marshall 1989; Marshall 1988; Marshall et al. 2007, 1991a). The photoreceptor types perform different functions in the hemispheres and midband. In both the hemispheres and in Rows 5 and 6 of the midband, SVF photoreceptors process polarization information, with R1/4/5 and R2/3/6/7 containing orthogonal microvilli. Rows 5 and 6 have a specialized LVF distal to the SVF cells, which acts as a quarter‐wave retarder, allowing for the processing of elliptically polarized light (Templin et al. 2017). Within the color midband rows (1–4), the SVF groups are sensitive to different wavelengths of light instead of different polarization angles or handedness (Figure 1D).
Photoreceptor axons exit the retina through the basement membrane and send axons into the lamina, where the first step of information processing takes place. The lamina is separated into retinotopic cartridges, with all photoreceptors from a single ommatidium projecting into the same lamina cartridge. Each cartridge has two layers, external plexiform Layers 1 and 2 (epl1 and epl2). Previous descriptions of photoreceptors described the general terminal structure of SVFs and the branching of the LVF (R8) in the lamina, but questions remained about their relative location due to the high tortuosity of the axons (Kleinlogel et al. 2003; Kleinlogel and Marshall 2005). The major connections in the arthropod optic lamina are between the terminals of SVF photoreceptors and the first‐order interneurons, lamina monopolar cells (LMCs). Other lamina interneurons include tangential cells, which are wide‐field and project into multiple cartridges, as well as local amacrine cells and narrow‐field centrifugal cells (Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017).
Though the cell types and general structure for the lamina interneurons have been identified in stomatopods, their relative locations within a cartridge, their connectivity, and their abundance could not be established with the stochastic staining of the Golgi method (Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017). Studies on the crayfish and crab visual systems have provided some information about the lamina structure in crustaceans, but the diversity of Malacostraca prompts further investigation into the similarity of this structure across species and ecosystems (Hafner 1973; Nässel 1976b; Stowe et al. 1977; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981; Sztarker et al. 2009). We hypothesized that the overall structure of the lamina would follow the general crustacean structure, as was found previously, and the midband would show specializations not seen in the hemispheres, especially the LMCs (Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017).
Using new methods, we describe the structure of the mantis shrimp lamina in two ecologically distinct stomatopod families, which can also be identified by their raptorial appendages (Figure 1A,B): a gonodactyloid “smasher,” Haptosquilla trispinosa, and a lysiosquilloid “spearer,” Alachosquilla vicinia (Van Der Wal et al. 2017). Both families have a six‐row midband with color specializations and are found in different habitats with similar light availability (Cronin et al. 1993). H. trispinosa lives in small holes in coral rubble, while A. vicinia digs burrows in sand flats (Caldwell and Dingle 1975). We hypothesized that H. trispinosa would have a more complex lamina organization than A. vicinia due to the greater variability of its environment. Using Serial Block‐Face Electron Microscopy (SBEM), we show that the lamina cartridge structure across all ommatidia types and between species is conserved, and the lamina interneuron structures allow for the beginning of an opponent processing system. Additionally, we show that the majority of inter‐cartridge connections in the midband lie along a single column instead of along a row, which is likely related to the unique scanning motion of stomatopod eyes.

## Methods
We obtained retinas from one specimen each of Haptosquilla trisponsa and A. vicinia in August 2019 from Lizard Island Research Station (GBRMPA Permit no. G17/38160.1). These individuals were of similar size, at 2.5 cm in length, and were collected within approximately 100 m of each other, H. trispinosa from coral rubble and A. vicinia from nearby sandbanks. The animals were processed using the methodology detailed in Templin (2017). Animals were cooled on ice, and their eyestalks were removed into chilled 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 4% paraformaldehyde in PEMS buffer (0.1 M PIPES, 0.01 M EGTA, 0.0005 M MgCl2, 0.15 g/mL sucrose, pH 7.1) (Chiou et al. 2005). Following fixation, the samples were stained with a sequence of heavy metals for electron microscopy using a microwave regimen (Pelco Biowave, Ted Pella, USA): 2% osmium tetroxide with 1.5% potassium ferricyanide (2 min × 2 min on/2 min off/2 min on, 80 W, vacuum on), 1% thiocarbohydrazide (20 min on bench), 2% osmium tetroxide (2 min × 2 min on/2 min off/2 min on, 80 W, vacuum on), 1% uranyl acetate (2 min × 2 min on/2 min off/2 min on, 150 W, vacuum on), and lead aspirate (60 min in 60°C oven) (Tapia et al. 2012; Templin 2017). Samples were rinsed with ultrapure water between all steps at least three times, the last two in the biowave (2 s × 40 s, 80 W, vacuum on). After staining, the samples were dehydrated with acetone and embedded in Durcupan resin, left to set in a 60°C oven for two days, and were then mounted on aluminum stubs with two‐part silver epoxy. We cut down to the basement membrane on an ultramicrotome using a glass knife, with particular care to ensure the correct orientation of the midband within the sample. The sample was only cut to the base of the retina in order to later identify the photoreceptor numbering (Figure 1E). The stub was transferred to the SBF‐SEM Volume Scope (Thermo Fisher Apreo—High‐Resolution FESEM) at the UQ Centre for Microscopy and Microanalysis.
Each sample was first aligned to view the entire midband, with approximately 10 ommatidia per row to allow for possible alignment drift in the sample mounting due to sectioning a flat surface on a curved eye. Samples were imaged in a low vacuum at 2.0 kV, with a pixel dwell time of 2–3 µs and a 1.0–2.0 nA beam current, to compensate for any sample charging. They were cut at 100 nm with a 50 nm × 50 nm pixel resolution from the basement membrane to the start of the lamina (approximately 50 µm or 500 sections, depending on the sample). After reaching the lamina, the sections were then cut at 50 nm through the depth of the lamina (approximately 30 µm or 600 sections). With the Volume Scope, a region of interest can be targeted for high‐resolution imaging at 15 nm × 15 nm pixel size for H. trispinosa and 10 nm × 10 nm pixel size for A. vicinia. We targeted about four cartridges per midband row for high‐resolution imaging. A separate sample of the dorsal hemisphere from each species was imaged at 10 nm × 10 nm × 50 nm.
Additionally, TEM images were taken of H. trispinosa to confirm the presence of synapses. Briefly, samples were fixed in a similar manner as above, then incubated in 2% osmium tetroxide before ethanol dehydration and embedded in EPON resin. Thin sections of the lamina were cut at 60 nm with a Diatome diamond knife into a water bath and placed on copper grids. They were stained with 5% uranyl acetate in 50% ethanol and Reynolds lead citrate and imaged with a Hitachi HT 7700 TEM (Templin 2017).

## Reconstructions
Raw images were first loaded into FIJI (RRID: SCR_002285) (Schindelin et al. 2012) by reading each set of images as a stack. Individual stacks were first denoised with the Gaussian Blur function (sigma 1.0) and contrast‐enhanced using the CLAHE (contrast limited adaptive histogram equalization) filter. Stacks were imported into TrakEM2 (RRID: SCR_008954) (Cardona et al. 2012) and aligned, and the high‐resolution stacks registered within the coordinate frame of the larger low‐resolution stacks (Saalfeld et al. 2010, 2012).
Photoreceptors were identified using the description from Marshall et al. (1991a) (Figure 1E), and their axons were manually segmented to the lamina. Two adjacent cartridges from each of the six rows and from the dorsal hemisphere in each species were reconstructed for a total of 28 cartridges and 224 photoreceptors. Axon areas were manually outlined approximately every 20 slices, depending on changes in location, and interpolated (filled in) between z sections. Within the lamina, terminals were manually outlined every 5 or 10 sections and interpolated.
Interneurons in one cartridge from each row and two from the hemisphere in both species, for a total of 16 cartridges, were chosen for reconstructions based on whether their axons entered or exited the lamina cartridge along with the photoreceptor bundle or if there were large axons within the lamina. Many of the axons and branches were, therefore, not reconstructed, which amounted to approximately half of the overall cartridge volume. Axons were segmented to just below the lamina boundary, above the first optic chiasm, due to limitations in the region size of the microscope.
The photoreceptor terminals of two lamina cartridges were reconstructed in the hemisphere and six midband rows of each species, and interneurons were reconstructed in two hemisphere cartridges and a single ommatidium of each midband row. We attempted to do semiautomatic segmentation using Amira, but it was not successful with these scans (Streets 2021).
Volume analysis was done with Amira 2020.2 (RRID: SCR_007353) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each photoreceptor reconstruction was normalized by creating a subvolume of the axon and photoreceptor terminal. The beginning of the axon was defined by the first section, where the axon is fully surrounded by the basement membrane, and ending with the section where the axon passes the thin membrane surrounding the lamina, which is where the photoreceptor terminal subvolume begins.
In order to find differences between rows within and between species, we measured the distance travelled by the axon versus the depth (tortuosity, or curved:chord length), the maximum axon radius, and the total volume of the photoreceptor terminal within the lamina. These measurements were chosen due to observations made during reconstruction, as the axons were not consistent in radius or curvature.
Further analysis on the hemisphere interneurons was performed using Amira. Because the scans were not of sufficient resolution for synapse identification, we used volume overlap as a proxy for connectivity. The lamina cartridge subvolume of each neuron was first dilated by approximately 50 nm to ensure overlap of neighboring membranes, and the label interfaces function was used to determine the volume of overlap between each cell type segmented within a cartridge. While membrane proximity does not definitely indicate synaptic connectivity, it is still a necessary precondition for its existence (Braitenberg and Schüz 1991; Takemura et al. 2015).

## Results
Overall, the lamina cartridges were consistent across all ommatidia types and between species (Figure 2). Photoreceptors terminate in two major layers, with R2/3/6/7 in epl1 and R1/4/5 in epl2. The first layer, epl1, can further be divided by terminal endings, with R2/6 above R3/7. Small differences in the volume of photoreceptor terminals and cartridges varied with the photoreceptor group and location within the eye. The major interneurons (LMCs) are consistent with previous research, and the five LMC1‐3 are found in every cartridge (Figure 2).

## Color Photoreceptor Axons Contain Additional Pigment Reservoir
At the base of the retina, the photoreceptors narrow into axons to penetrate the basement membrane. Directly proximal to this membrane, some axons, especially in the midband, enlarge and form bulbous sacks (Figure 3). This enlarged area was previously described in mantis shrimp and serves as a screening pigment reservoir: screening pigments travel distally during the day to protect the rhabdom from excess sunlight and return proximally to allow for more light penetration at night (Marshall et al. 1991b).

The pigment reservoirs are particularly obvious in the midband color rows, Rows 1–4, where the proximal tier appears to have a larger diameter of axons (R2/3/6/7 in Rows 1, 3, and 4; R1/4/5 in Row 2), perhaps to further screen these bottom layers from longer wavelengths that penetrate through the distal tier in order to sharpen color sensitivity (Figure 3E,F). Differences in pigment between individuals are likely based on variations in the time of day of capture and dissection.
Additionally, H. trispinosa midband axons spiral towards the lamina with high tortuosity (Figure 3C), though this is not seen in the hemisphere or anywhere in A. vicinia (Figure 3D).

## Photoreceptor Terminal and Cartridge Volumes Vary Between Species
Photoreceptor terminals enter the lamina and rotate approximately 180° from their location relative to each other at the basement membrane. All photoreceptor terminals show a similar pattern of orientation (Figure 2A, Figures S1–S3). This is different from previously described patterns: Kleinlogel et al. 2003; Kleinlogel and Marshall 2005) state that R1/4/5 form epl1 and R2/3/6/7 form epl2. With our methods, we were better able to trace the axons from the basement membrane to the lamina, where we found the opposite termination pattern (Figure 2A). Additionally, there is symmetry along the length of the cartridge: R2 and R7 form bulbs at the top of the lamina, while R3 and R6 extend around and beneath them. R4 and R5 terminate below both of these layers, also curving behind and beneath R3 and R6. R1 and R8 extend through the center of the cartridge, and R1 branches laterally in epl2 towards R4 and R5. Here, we define the “back” of the cartridge as the R8 position, and the “front” is towards R1 (Figure S1).
The volume of photoreceptor terminals in the hemisphere columns is much smaller than that in the midband, but when corrected based on total cartridge size, this difference decreases (Figure 4E, Figures S2 and S3). The total cartridge volume in midband rows of H. trispinosa is about twice that of the hemispheres, with Rows 5 and 6 being the largest (Figure 4E). Interestingly, the photoreceptor terminals have the smallest volume fraction in this row. The cartridge volumes of A. vicinia are more varied: Rows 1, 5, and 6 are at least twice the volume of the hemisphere, but Rows 2–4 are smaller and closer to the volume of the hemisphere cartridges (Figure 4E). In both species, Row 2 appears to have the smallest cartridge volume of any of the midband rows. This row is also different in the retina, with inverted tiering, though the photoreceptor termination pattern is still consistent with all other cartridges (Figure 1E).

The smallest volumes are the R8 cells, as they are LVFs and have little to no branching in the lamina, except in row 1 of A. vicinia. Previous research showed they have extensive branching in the first four rows of the midband, no branching in rows 5 and 6, and some small branches in the hemisphere (Kleinlogel and Marshall 2005). The small amount of branching in our data may be due to difficulties tracing very small branches between serial sections, but it is not clear if the R8 branching pattern is consistent across all cartridges in a single row or was only seen in some Golgi stains, as cartridge identification is not always possible with Golgi sections (Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017). We observed extensive branching only in Row 1 R8 of A. vicinia, where all branches appear to be towards R1, and R1 also branches towards R8 throughout both epl layers (Figure 4C, Figure S1). This is also the only instance where a photoreceptor terminal has branches from the general bulb. Also, one hemisphere, R8, in A. vicinia has small branches, which appear to be more lateral. These were the only R8 cells where branches could be segmented in these reconstructions. Additionally, in H. trispinosa, the R8 axon in Rows 2 and 3 branches just below the lamina. This branch did not appear to extend very far and did not noticeably contact any other cartridges.
The most obvious difference between species is the relatively large size of R1 in the hemisphere of A. vicinia, which was present in both cartridges. R8 in Row 1 is also large and has a large amount of branching, which is not seen in H. trispinsa. There is also a trend in Rows 1, 3, and 4, where the proximal tier bulbs are smaller than the distal tier in both species (Figure 4C,D).

## LMC Form Multiple Circuits
The five integral LMCs (LMC1‐3) were identified in all cartridges (Figure 2B, Figures S2–S5). The naming conventions for LMCs vary across arthropods. Here, we use the numbering from Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. (2017), which more closely resembles the traditional insect numbering scheme. LMC1‐3 was identified in all cartridges by their branching structure and location within a cartridge, even if their axons could not be followed to the cell body above the lamina, especially LMC1 (Figures S4 and S5). An additional cell, presumably LMC4, could be identified in most cartridges, but the cell body was not able to be confirmed in many of them. Cells matching the description of LMC5 were not identified (Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017).
LMC1 axons are unilateral; they branch on either side of the cartridge (Figures 2B and 5). There are often two LMC1 cells per cartridge: LMC1a, whose axon weaves through and branches along the terminal bulbs of R2/3/4, and LMC1b, which is associated with R5/6/7. The axon is beaded or does not have a consistent diameter through the lamina as well as beneath it. We found a beaded axon that only extended through one side of each cartridge in all cartridges and both species. The cell bodies that were able to be reconstructed are spherical and sit directly above the lamina.

LMC2 branches broadly into all parts of the cartridges (Figure 5). The axon of LMC2 in both species is very large and can often be identified in cross‐section as being central in the cartridge. It is close to the R8 axon, behind the LMC3i axon (Figure S1). In most midband reconstructions, LMC2 branched in both epl1 and epl2; in the instances where not many branches were able to be segmented, the cell was identified by the axon's position. The cell body of LMC2 is in the upper cell body layer, either in line with or above the LMC3 cell bodies. LMC2 has the most overlap with all other interneurons within the cartridge of the hemispheres, as well as the largest relative volume in all species (Figures 4F and 6).
LMC3 outer (LMC3o) is defined by its axon in the front of the cartridge, outside of R1 (Figure S1). It branches along its axon to R2/3 and R6/7, often forming a hook shape due to the branches coming off of the main axon at slightly different depths (Figure 5A,D, Figures S4 and S5). LMC3o branches only in epl1, with the major putative connections in the hemisphere being R2/3/6/7 (Figure 6A,B). In the midband, the two side branches separate from the main axon near the base of the terminal bulbs in the lower epl1, with branches extending towards the back of the cartridge. The cell body is often in line with LMC3i. In the hemisphere of both species, LMC3o has a larger volume than LMC3i, though the relative volume of this cell in H. trispinosa is larger (Figure 4F).
LMC3 inner (LMC3i) branches primarily in epl2 and can be identified by its axon behind R1 but in front of LMC2 (Figure 5). The majority of branching is towards R1/4/5 (Figure 6A,B). There are some small branches in epl1, but these are only at the back of the cartridge and contact the upper terminals of R1/4/5 (Figures S4 and S5: H. trispinosa row 1, 4, 5, 6; A. vicinia row 4, 5, 6, both hemisphere cartridges). The side branches are not as large as LMC3o.
The cell bodies of LMC2 and LMC3 are in the outer cell layer but are not completely spherical like the cell bodies of LMC1. Instead, there are often small horns or fingers on the cell body structure that appear near the axon bundle before it enters the cartridge (Figure S1A). This is especially evident in Rows 5 and 6 of A. vicinia.
LMC4 could be identified with its cell body in a small number of cartridges in A. vicinia based on the cell body location and cross‐cartridge connectivity. Otherwise, an additional interneuron in all other A. vicinia and most H. trispinosa cartridges with an axon extending above the lamina is assumed to be LMC4, as described below (Figures S4 and S5). This cell appears to only make a few inconsistent branches within the hemisphere cartridges (Figures 5A,D and 6). LMC4 cells with cross‐cartridge connections were found in one H. trispinosa hemisphere cartridge and rows 1, 3, and 6, and both hemisphere cartridges of A. vicinia (Figure 6, Figures S4 and S5). These branches appeared to only extend to one or two adjacent cartridges, but there appears to be no pattern of connectivity either within or between rows (Figure 6C,D). The cell body appears between those of LMC1 and LMC2&3 within an intermediate layer. The cross‐cartridge branches appeared mainly in epl2.
If there was no cell body and inconsistent branching, LMCs were identified by the position of their axon. However, some of these may represent different varieties of LMCs, as explained in Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. (2017). A small number of cell identities could not be fully confirmed. Instead, these cell identities were deduced based on other identified cells in the cartridge: for example, if a cartridge had confirmed LMC1‐3 cells, an unidentified cell was assumed to be LMC4 (Figures S2 and S3). A cell with branches in epl1, similar to LMC3o, was reconstructed in H. trispinosa Row 3 and A. vicinia Rows 2 and 4; however, unlike LMC3o, the axon was close to R8 in the back of the cartridge. Also, an axon extending only along one side of the cartridge, similar to an LMC1, was found in H. trispinosa Rows 1 and 5 and A. vicinia Row 5 (Figures S4 and S5). If a cell could not be identified as LMC1‐3, it was assumed to be a type of LMC4, which was not otherwise identified in the relevant cartridges. It is unlikely that these cells were the sparse LMC5 cells, as they were only found in the hemispheres in Golgi staining (Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017).
Synapses were not visible in the SBEM scans, and thus, cell connectivity and synaptic directionality could not be confirmed. Darkened membranes, which are indicative of synaptic connectivity, could be seen at a resolution of 30 × 30 nm pixel in TEM (Figure S8A), but no such membrane specialization could be seen in SBEM. Photoreceptor synapses included invaginations into the cells from other interneurons (Figure S8C), as well as a putative triad synapse with multiple synaptic partners (Figure S8B).

## Tangential Cells and Some Other Interneurons Form Inter‐Cartridge Connections
Other interneurons were found in most cartridges, though none could be conclusively identified, as scans did not extend far enough beneath the lamina to locate cell bodies above the medulla. Instead, cells were described and identified based on the location and size of their axon and if they extended beyond the parent cartridge (Figures S6 and S7). Here, we also describe cells as native or non‐native, referring to whether the main axon joins the bundle of LMC and R8 axons as it leaves the cartridge and into the optic chiasm. Interneurons are classified based on the descriptions in Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. (2017).
Tangential cells were found to be associated with almost all cartridges (Figure 5, Figures S6 and S7). They were identified by their inter‐cartridge spread and thick, horizontal axons either below or above the lamina. Within the hemispheres, tangential cells had the most intra‐cartridge connections, second to LMC2 (Figure 6A,B). One A. vicinia tangential cell had extensive branching (Figure 6D) and connected to all surrounding cartridges (Figures 5D and 6D). The hemisphere tangential cells all had a thick vertical primary axon directly below the cartridge, with one or two thick vertical secondary axons extending up to the cartridge. In H. trispinosa, the hemisphere tangential cells also extended above the lamina, with a branch along the top of the neuropile. In the midband, tangential cells appear to branch primarily to cartridges in other midband rows within the same column instead of within rows (Figure 6C,D). Some tangential cells also branched to a single cartridge within the same row.
Most H. trispinosa cartridges have at least one native tangential cell, except Rows 1 and 5, though these had non‐native cells (Figure S6). Row 2 had two native tangential cells, and Row 5 had two non‐native ones. The Row 6 native tangential cell is very thick, with two claw‐like vertical branches surrounding the cartridge, and no inter‐cartridge branching could be found (Figure S6). Most tangential cells found in A. vicinia were non‐native, found in Rows 1–4, 6, and one hemisphere cartridge. Rows 1, 2, and 4 also had a native tangential cell, along with both hemisphere cartridges (Figure S7).
Amacrine cell identity could be inferred based on their lack of an axon, as they are local interneurons, as well as by their inter‐cartridge connections and beaded branches (Figures S6 and S7). No amacrine cell bodies were identified, so the cell identity could not be confirmed, as the cell bodies were outside of the field of view of the microscope scans. The branching of amacrine cells is not as extensive as the tangential cells, with many only connecting either to an adjacent cartridge within the same row or in the closest adjacent row (Figure 6C,D).
Putative narrow‐field centrifugal cells were identified based on the lack of inter‐cartridge connections and beading along the axon, as described in Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. (2017). As with their other interneurons, they could not be conclusively described due to the lack of cell body, and their thin axons and branches prevent a more complete reconstruction.

## Discussion
In this work, we have reconstructed photoreceptors and principal lamina interneurons using SBEM, allowing for a more accurate and complete description of the layout of the lamina. First, we have shown that lamina cartridges consistently contain the major LMC cells, LMC1‐3, as predicted by Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. (2017). These possess the expected putative connections with relevant photoreceptor terminals, at least within the hemispheres (Figure 6A,B, Figure S2–S5). We were surprised to find that the general structure is conserved across the two major superfamilies despite their ecological differences. However, the lamina cartridges of H. trispinosa have a larger volume than those of A. vicinia, and the midband cartridges are at least twice as large, which may reflect an adaptation for faster neural processing (Figure 4). The cross‐cartridge connectivity in the midband appears to be between different rows in the same vertical column, often directly dorsal or ventral to the parent cartridge (Figure 6C,D). This may contribute to the unique scanning behavior of many stomatopod eyes, including both species in this study, in which the midband is thought to create a one‐dimensional field of view, similar to a multispectral scanner (Land et al. 1990; Marshall et al. 2014; Wolpert 2011).

## Lamina Structure Is Conserved Across Crustacea and Distinct From Insecta
Photoreceptor terminals are described as bulbous and terminate in two layers, depending on the type of photoreceptor sensitivity, in both stomatopods and decapods: crayfish (Hafner 1973; Nässel 1976b), crab (Stowe et al. 1977; Sztarker et al. 2009), and review (Strausfeld and Nässel 1981). The photoreceptor terminal location also follows the pattern of other crustaceans, where four photoreceptors terminate in the upper layer and three in the lower layer, presumably of the same cell identities (Hafner 1973; Nässel 1975, 1977). In contrast, photoreceptor terminals in insects are typically columnar with branching neurites, terminating in a small point, and often span the length of the cartridge: bee (Ribi 1975), butterfly (Ribi 1987), and fly (Meinertzhagen and Sorra 2001).
LMC structure is generally the same across both crustaceans and insects, with differences in the branching patterns. Both laminae studied have monopolar cells that branch in all layers, but the separation of layers is consistent only within crustaceans (Greiner et al. 2004; Matsushita et al. 2022; Stöckl et al. 2016; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981). Additionally, there is a characteristic hook shape of the LMC3o in all stomatopod cartridges, which is similar to the cook‐neck variant of the crab LMC3, which also branches only in the upper lamina (Figure 5, Figures S4 and S5) (Sztarker et al. 2009). It is unclear if this shape serves a functional purpose or if it is simply the most spatially efficient wiring.
The morphology of the crustacean lamina is generally less organized than the insect lamina, as well as less well studied, making the identification of neuron types in sections more challenging (Strausfeld and Nässel 1981). Do the differences found in stomatopods reflect unique specializations in this taxon, or are they more general features of a crustacean eye? Further study into crustacean visual systems and their differences from the more well‐studied insects is needed.
However, the closer resemblance of stomatopod neural structure to that of other decapods may end in the visual system. In the lateral protocerebrum, the mushroom body in stomatopods more closely resembles that seen in insects, where it is known as a learning and memory center. Decapods, on the other hand, have a reduced neuropile, called the hemiellipsoid body, which is thought to be a loss of function (Wolff et al. 2017). More proximally in the neural system, the central complex of stomatopods appears to be anatomically much closer to the central complex of insects than other decapods (Thoen, Marshall, et al. 2017).

## LMC Morphology Creates Two Circuit Types
The layout of photoreceptors and LMC1‐3 shown in Figure 2 is consistent across all parts of the eye and between the two species (Figure 1E, Figures S4 and S5). Some cells have branches confined to a single layer, which only contact terminals in that layer and thus likely form the beginning of complementary opponent channels. Other cells have branches in both layers and likely are involved in a brightness feedback circuit, or motion vision, which is separate from the color and polarization opponent channels (Borst 2014; Melnattur et al. 2014; Paulk et al. 2008).

## LMC3 May Form the Beginning of an Opponency Circuit
Photoreceptors terminate in two layers, and all of the photoreceptors terminating in the same layer have the same polarization or spectral sensitivity. The LMC3 neuron is the most likely candidate for color or polarization processing, as it separates the opposing SVF types. This type of monopolar cell is most similar to M3 and M4 in decapods (Nässel, 1976b; Stowe et al. 1977; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981; Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017).
Previous work used a combination of light and electron microscopy to describe SVF types as terminating in different layers than we found here (i.e., R1/4/5 terminating in epl1 and R2/3/6/7 in epl2) (Kleinlogel et al. 2003). Our revision of this layout is more accurate because our study uses a much higher resolution and smaller distance between serial sections than is possible with light microscopy. This change in termination pattern would mean that the information processed in each layer, as well as cell identification in electrophysiological recordings, would be different than expected using the previous layout.
The opposing LMC3 cells branch as predicted and are generally separated into the different epl layers (Figures 2B and 5A,D). These cells, thus, will have opposing spectral or polarization sensitivities. However, visual opponency additionally requires not only activation from one type of stimulus but also inhibition from another. For example, if LMC3 forms an opponency circuit, LMC3o in midband Row 1 would be more sensitive and hyperpolarize to a shorter wavelength, approximately 420 nm in H. trispinosa, but would also have a depolarized response to the longer wavelength that Tier 2 is sensitive to at 470 nm (Thoen, Chiou, et al. 2017). This is consistent with preliminary electrophysiological recordings in H. trispinosa, where four LMCs showed both hyperpolarizing and depolarizing responses to different stimuli (Wang 2023).

## LMC1, LMC2, and LMC4 Are Likely Part of a Brightness or Motion Circuit
Brightness and motion circuits require the comparison of inputs from multiple photoreceptors and cartridges. Because the other main LMCs, LMC1 and LMC2, span both lamina layers, we propose that they are part of a brightness and/or motion circuit. These two LMC cells, which are present in most crustaceans (Hafner 1974; Hamori and Horridge 1966; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981; Sztarker et al. 2009), are most similar to L1 and L2 in insects, as described in Thoen, Strausfeld et al. (2017).
In insects, L2 both receives input from SVF photoreceptors and also feeds back onto these cells (Matsushita et al. 2022; Meinertzhagen and Sorra 2001; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981). This is likely most similar to LMC2 in stomatopods, as it has branches and putative connections to all photoreceptor cells as well as most other interneurons (Figures 2 and 6A,B). It is unclear if this cell synapses back onto photoreceptor cells, as synapses could not be identified with the available magnification. They may also be involved in brightness regulation across the eye via tangential cells, with which they likely connect (Figure 6A,B).
The two LMC1 cells most likely perform a similar function, as they do not differentiate between SVF types, though they are much smaller and would theoretically receive approximately half the input that LMC2 does. The function of LMC4 is still unclear, but it does extend branches primarily in epl2 into adjacent cartridges. This is also similar to the L4 of some insects, where the cell branches in the lowest part of the lamina (Greiner et al. 2004; Meinertzhagen and Sorra 2001; Ribi 1987). The function of these cells is not fully described among insects (reviewed in Borst 2014; Strausfeld 2010).
Additionally, there is a separation within epl1 between R2/7 and R3/6, creating three lamina layers total. This was also previously identified in another mantis shrimp, Squilla mantis (Strausfeld and Nässel 1981), and can be seen in other crustaceans (Hafner 1973; Nässel 1977; Sztarker et al. 2009). The separation into three layers potentially would allow for functional specificity in each layer, with a middle layer of photoreceptors dedicated to brightness. In the hemisphere, R1 has the most overlap with LMC2 and thus the most potential for synaptic connection, and R3 and R6 have more overlap than R2 and R7 (Figure 6A,B). The putative middle layer, consisting mainly of R3 and R6, and the larger R1 receptor, may be preferentially involved in the brightness calculations that LMC2 likely performs than the other photoreceptors. The upper and lower lamina layers could then be preferentially involved in opponent processing.

## Cross‐Cartridge Connections Provide a Neural Basis for Scanning Behavior
The cross‐cartridge connectivity in the midband appears to be mainly within a single column and spanning multiple rows instead of between cartridges of the same row (Figure 6C,D). This type of connectivity suggests that the midband views a narrow band of space, where each row in a column samples the same point in space but at different wavelengths or polarization types. This is supported by inter‐ommatidial angle measurements and is consistent with the hypothesis that the midband acts as a multispectral line scanner during scanning behavior (Land et al. 1990; Marshall 1988; Wolpert 2011).
Tangential cells, similar to LMC2, have branches to all other neurons within the cartridge (Figure 5). While LMC2 likely forms a local, intra‐cartridge intensity circuit, tangential cells branch into multiple adjacent cartridges to form a larger intensity feedback circuit, as they likely do in insects (Meinertzhagen and Sorra 2001; Strausfeld 2010).
Additionally, the larger pigment reservoirs in the color midband rows allow for greater functional separation of each midband row column by isolating the rhabdom in bright light (Figure 3E,F) (Marshall et al. 1991b).

## Ultrastructure
In insects, photoreceptor terminals can be identified by their mitochondria, which are much more numerous compared to those in surrounding interneurons, as well as their slightly more electron‐dense cytoplasm (Greiner et al. 2005; Meinertzhagen and O'Neil 1991; Stöckl et al. 2016; Takemura and Arikawa 2006). These morphological differences between the photoreceptors and the interneurons are exaggerated in mantis shrimp. The photoreceptor terminals in mantis shrimp have dark profiles, and the mitochondria are relatively large compared to those seen in insect TEM images. They are circular with thick cristae and sometimes fill the majority of the cell's cross section (Figure 5, Figure S8A). The purpose of these larger and more numerous mitochondria is unknown, but they may be associated with the specialized vision of mantis shrimp or possibly a difference between insect and crustacean laminae, as similar traits are seen in some decapods (Hafner 1973; Hamori and Horridge 1966; Nässel 1975; Strausfeld and Nässel 1981). Synapses could be identified using TEM but not SBEM, even though the resolution and tissue processing are comparable to other work in insects (Matsushita et al. 2022; Scheffer et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2018). Darkened membranes, typically associated with synaptic specialization, could be viewed in TEM samples at approximately 30 nm × 30 nm pixel size (Figure S8A). To our knowledge, this was the first SBEM scan of crustacean neural tissue, and our inability to detect synapses may illustrate the need for a protocol more specifically tailored to crustaceans, possibly with an increased concentration of heavy metals or a longer incubation time. As in insects and crustaceans, most photoreceptor connections were invaginating, with interneurons forming processes into the terminals (Figure S8) (Hafner 1974; Nässel 1976a; Nässel and Waterman 1977). There are many dark membrane areas, which are likely synaptic specializations, but the bar‐shaped synaptic ribbon terminal could not be identified in most of these areas (Hafner 1973; Leitinger et al. 2012).

## There Are Minor Species Differences Between Structure Volumes
In general, photoreceptor terminals have a larger volume in the midband than in the hemispheres, especially in H. trispinosa. This may allow for faster information processing due to the larger axon radius and/or greater connectivity due to a larger surface area. In H. trispinosa, midband cartridges are generally double the volume of those in the hemisphere, but this was notably not seen in A. vicinia, which exhibits smaller cartridges (Kleinlogel et al. 2003; Thoen, Strausfeld, et al. 2017). Instead, all rows except for Row 1 are much smaller in A. vicinia (Figure 4D).
Another species difference included the spiral shape created by axons in the midband photoreceptors of H. trispinosa. The high tortuosity may simply be due to a different developmental pattern or is possibly needed to conserve retinotopy while still fitting the axons in a small space (Figure 3C,D). Additionally, the photoreceptor terminals in midband Rows 1–4 are more spread out, with more space between them, in H. trispinosa, which may allow for greater contact with interneuron branches (Figure 4, Figures S2 and S3). Differences in species may be more evident in small branching structures, which would have required a higher resolution to follow, or synaptic connectivity, which we were not able to visualize in SBEM using our staining protocol. Other differences could also exist in support cells or other interneurons that were not part of the axon bundle above or below the lamina.
We hypothesized that the lamina structure in these two species might differ. They were collected within 100 m of each other and share the same macrohabitat and light environment, but they are divided by microhabitats and over 150 million years of evolution (Van Der Wal et al. 2017). Their larger neuron and axon volumes of H. trispinosa support our hypothesis that they need faster processing for their visually complex habitat and intraspecific contests for habitat space (Figure 4) (Caldwell and Dingle 1975; Dingle and Caldwell 1969; Green and Patek 2018).
Our data show that the lamina cartridge structure is conserved across the entire eye and in two very different species. This structure is also likely conserved across all crustaceans and would imply that the lamina of crustaceans has the neural structures to perform opponent processing. Our reconstructions can inform future electrophysiological recordings of the stomatopod lamina, especially in confirming cell identities with dye fills. Additionally, cells will be easier to identify in electron microscopy, as each photoreceptor and main LMC axon are consistently in the same location.
