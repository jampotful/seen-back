Revealing the light-regulated circadian rhythm in the vector mosquitoes, Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus.

# Revealing the light-regulated circadian rhythm in the vector mosquitoes, Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus

## Abstract
Light serves as a primary zeitgeber for regulating circadian rhythms in mosquitoes. As important vector insects, the diurnal Aedes albopictus and the nocturnal Culex quinquefasciatus are not only nuisance biters, but also transmit viruses such as dengue, Zika, and West Nile. We conducted a comparative analysis of the oscillating molecular rhythms in these two mosquito species. Light-blocking paint application experiments reveal species-specific photic pathways. CRISPR/Cas9 knockouts of the Aalopsin1, Cxqopsin6, and Cry1 genes revealed that Ae. albopictus relies principally on the compound eye pathway, whereas Cx. quinquefasciatus depends more on the non-visual ocelli-like pathway for circadian rhythm regulation. In addition, different light conditions can affect the lifespan of adult mosquitoes. These findings reveal the light-regulated circadian rhythm in diurnal and nocturnal vector mosquitoes.

## Introduction
Many organisms have evolved stable biological clock mechanisms to cope with the alternating changes in light intensity between day and night, thereby maintaining their physiological and behavioral rhythms1,2. This circadian clock is referred to as the circadian rhythm, an endogenous rhythm with a periodicity of ~24 h that can be synchronized by external environmental cues3,4. The endogenous rhythm in the vinegar fly, Drosophila melanogaster, is regulated by a transcriptional-translational feedback loop (TTFL) formed by core clock genes comprising key genes such as period, timeless, clk, cycle, and cry playing critical roles in regulating the loop1,5,6. However, other insects, such as mosquitoes and butterflies, possess six core clock genes, including Cry1, which is homologous to Drosophila cry, as well as an additional Cry27,8. Similar TTFL-based circadian regulatory mechanisms have been found in other insects and mammals, providing evidence that the TTFL loop is highly conserved across species3,4,6.
Synchronizing the internal biological clock with the ever-changing environment is crucial for most organisms. The environmental factors that can regulate endogenous rhythms are called zeitgebers, and light is the most important for synchronization of circadian rhythms9–11. Many organisms use different photoreceptors sensitive to variations in intensity, wavelength, and even the direction of sunlight to synchronize their circadian rhythms3,12. In mammals, rhodopsin and melanopsin help synchronize daily rhythms with the regular light-dark (LD) cycle13–16. Similarly, D. melanogaster uses multiple photoreceptors to synchronize its biological clock17,18. In addition to six types of rhodopsins located in their photoreceptive organs (compound eyes, ocelli, and Hofbauer–Buchner (HB) eyelets), light can penetrate directly through the exoskeleton of D. melanogaster and act on the photoreceptor protein CRY in the clock neurons of the brain to synchronize rhythms19–22.
Aedes albopictus, a diurnal mosquito originating in Southeast Asia, breeds in tree holes and natural water bodies and serves as an important vector of arboviruses such as dengue, Zika, and Chikungunya23,24. It has invaded regions in Africa, Europe, and the Americas in recent years25–27. Culex quinquefasciatus, a nocturnal mosquito, primarily inhabits tropical and subtropical areas where humans live and can transmit encephalitis viruses and West Nile virus28–30. The recent increase in the populations of these two mosquito species has led to the spread of more pathogens, posing serious public health risks31,32. Studies on the relationship between light and circadian rhythms in mosquitoes have reported that different light and temperature conditions can affect the diurnal activity and core clock gene expression in Aedes aegypti and Cx. quinquefasciatus, with Ae. aegypti being more sensitive to temperature and Cx. quinquefasciatus more sensitive to light33. The core clock gene period in the ovaries of Ae. aegypti exhibit oscillatory expression under LD cycles and are regulated by light conditions34. Furthermore, the diurnal Ae. aegypti are attracted to ultraviolet light, while the nocturnal Anopheles gambiae displays avoidance behavior towards ultraviolet light, both of which are regulated by circadian rhythms35. However, the molecular mechanisms by which light regulates the circadian rhythms of Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus remain unclear. Since Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus exhibit contrasting circadian behaviors such as mating, blood-feeding, and oviposition36,37, understanding the mechanisms by which light regulates their circadian behaviors could be crucial for the control of these mosquitoes and the diseases they transmit.
To investigate the regulatory mechanisms by which light affects mosquito circadian rhythms, we first established a laboratory platform for monitoring the circadian rhythms of Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus. We then conducted transcriptome sequencing to perform a comparative analysis of the circadian oscillatory molecular rhythms between the two mosquito species. Subsequently, we applied light-blocking paint to specific regions of the head of both species to assess the roles of the compound eyes and ocelli-like pathways in circadian regulation. CRISPR/Cas9 technology was deployed to knock out and functionally analyze the Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6 genes in the compound eyes pathway and the Cry1 gene in the ocelli-like pathway to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which light regulates mosquito circadian rhythms. Finally, we explored the effects of light exposure on mosquito growth, development, and lifespan.

## Results

## The circadian rhythms and oscillating transcript abundance profiles of clock genes in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus
To characterize the flight activity patterns of Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus, individual mosquito locomotor activity was recorded continuously using a locomotor activity monitor under LD cycles followed by dark/dark (DD) conditions (Fig. 1A). Both Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus exhibited pronounced rhythmicity in flight activity under both LD and DD conditions. Under LD conditions, Ae. albopictus was active mainly during the day. The flight pattern displayed a narrow activity peak at the start of the light phase (ZT0) and a significant activity peak towards the end of the light phase (ZT6-12). There was little flight activity observed during the dark phase (Fig. 1B, top row, ZT12–ZT24, U = 340.5, p < 0.0001, r = 0.807, Fig. 1C, left column). In contrast, the nocturnal Cx. quinquefasciatus was active mainly during the night, showing a prominent peak of activity shortly after the start of the dark phase. Culex quinquefasciatus remained active throughout the night until it became inactive towards the start of the light phase. Unlike Ae. albopictus, little Cx. quinquefasciatus flight activity was observed during the light phase (Fig. 1B, top row, ZT0–ZT12, U = 699, p < 0.0001, r = 0.776, Fig. 1C, left column). The flight activity of both species still exhibited robust rhythmicity under DD conditions. Ae. albopictus showed a free-running period of 23.7 ± 0.22 h (mean ± SEM), while Cx. quinquefasciatus exhibited a free-running period of 23.8 ± 0.03 h (mean ± SEM). Furthermore, 55.8% of the total Ae. albopictus individuals displayed rhythmic activity, compared to 94.4% of Cx. quinquefasciatus (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, even in constant darkness, Ae. albopictus remained primarily active during the subjective daytime, whereas Cx. quinquefasciatus exhibited a primary activity during the subjective nighttime at the overall level of several DD cycles. To explore the molecular basis underlying their distinct diurnal and nocturnal rhythmic behavior, transcriptome analysis of rhythmic gene expression was performed in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus under a 12-h:12-h LD cycle. For this analysis, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) samples were collected from adult female Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus mosquitoes. Samples were collected under a 12-h light and 12-h dark cycle (LD 12:12 h). Before collection, newly eclosed female and male mosquitoes were housed together for 2–5 days to allow for mating. Subsequently, the mated females were separated and housed individually before being collected as samples. Whole female mosquitoes, 3–6 days post-eclosion, which had mated but not blood-fed, were selected as samples. Sampling occurred every 3 h over a 24-h period, with specific collection time points at Zeitgeber Time (ZT) 0, ZT3, ZT6, ZT9, ZT12, ZT15, ZT18, ZT21, and ZT24. RNA samples were examined using RNA-seq, and data were statistically analyzed using the JTK_CYCLE algorithm38. A substantial number of rhythmic transcripts were identified in both Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus, accounting for 9.29% (1183/12,740) and 43.88% (5952/13,563) of the total expressed gene sets in the two mosquito species, respectively (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 1). To characterize the relationship between the functions of rhythmically expressed genes and the diurnal/nocturnal activity preferences of mosquitoes, rhythmic genes were classified by their peak phase (LAG, a value given by JTK_CYCLE, which representing the time of peak transcript abundance as calculated by JTK_CYCLE38) as either during the light phase (0 ≤ LAG < 12 or LAG = 24) or the dark phase (12 ≤ LAG < 24), followed by Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. We found that genes with rhythmic transcription abundance profiles peaking during the light or dark phases represent diverse biological functions, including vision, sleep, memory, emergence, immunity, and metabolism, and these differed between Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus. For example, rhythmic transcript profiles in Ae. albopictus related to sleep and long-term memory functions, had peak abundance during the dark phase, whereas the profiles in Cx. quinquefasciatus peaked during the light phase. The core clock genes in D. melanogaster, Anopheles gambiae, Ae. aegypti, and Cx. pipiens have been reported previously39,40. We identified the canonical core clock genes in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus and confirmed them by amino acid sequence alignment with the reported core clock genes. To explore whether there is a difference in the molecular clock between the diurnal Ae. albopictus and the nocturnal Cx. quinquefasciatus, we compared the expression patterns of the core clock genes in both species. Most core clock genes were found to be rhythmically expressed under LD conditions in both Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus, among which period (per), timeless (tim), cycle (cyc), and cryptochrome 2 (Cry2) exhibited similar rhythmic expression patterns with amplitudes ranging from 1.23-fold to 4.52-fold (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 2). The clock genes per, tim, and Cry2 peaked at similar phases, while cyc peaked in the antiphase, consistent with the predicted model for the molecular clock from other insects1,6,7,41. Meanwhile, Clock (Clk) was found to oscillate in Cx. quinquefasciatus, while cryptochrome 1 (Cry1) was rhythmically expressed in Ae. albopictus. The amplitude of per in Ae. albopictus was higher than that in Cx. quinquefasciatus, whereas the amplitude of tim was higher in Cx. quinquefasciatus.

## Different photoreception pathways affect the circadian rhythms and clock gene transcript abundance in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus
Adult mosquitoes have a pair of compound eyes as their photoreceptive organs and lack ocelli. Studies have reported that the eye structures in mosquitoes, similar to the HB eyelets in D. melanogaster, are degenerate and have not been found to have photoreceptive functions42–44. To explore the pathways through which mosquitoes perceive light and regulate their circadian rhythms, we used a light-blocking paint application method to investigate the photoreceptive pathways and potential mechanisms in mosquitoes. In Drosophila, there are three ocelli arranged in a triangular pattern located between the pair of compound eyes at the top of the head45–49. Since mosquitoes lack these ocelli, we defined the area on the mosquito head where photoreceptive structures might exist, based on the location of ocelli in Drosophila, as the ocelli-like area (OL), and the area where the compound eyes are located as the compound eyes area (CE). On the day following the paint application, we assessed mosquito flight by gently shaking the rearing cage. Mosquitoes that displayed normal flight were considered to be in good condition. The paint-treated mosquitoes in good condition were selected for activity monitoring experiments. Activity monitoring results show that Ae. albopictus is primarily active during the day, while Cx. quinquefasciatus is mainly active at night (Supplementary Fig. 2). Under LD conditions, the daytime activity of the CE-blocked and CE + OL-blocked groups of Ae. albopictus was significantly lower than that of the control group (U = 175, p < 0.0001, r = 0.563; U = 205, p < 0.0001, r = 0.500), whereas there was no statistical difference between the OL-blocked group and the control group (U = 480, p = 0.858, r = 023, Fig. 3B). This indicates that the compound eyes are involved principally in regulating daytime activity in Ae. albopictus, while the ocelli-like area does not play a significant role in light perception during the day. During the subjective day under DD conditions, the activity of the CE-blocked group was significantly lower than that of the control group (U = 305.5, p = 0.006, r = 0.344), while the activity of the OL-blocked and CE + OL-blocked groups showed a downward trend compared to the control group, but the differences were not significant (U = 404.5, p = 0.222, r = 0.154; U = 465.5, p = 0.704, r = 0.048, Fig. 3B). These results suggest that the ocelli-like area in Ae. albopictus is not the primary structure for light perception during the day; instead, the compound eyes are the main pathway for regulating daytime activity in this diurnal species. However, the results under dark and subjective day conditions indicate that the ocelli-like pathway may play a role in regulating activity in Ae. albopictus during the night or in the absence of light. Under LD conditions, the activity level of the CE-blocked group in Cx. quinquefasciatus was significantly lower than that of the control group during the day (U = 282.5, p < 0.001, r = 0.423). The OL-blocked group showed significantly reduced activity levels at night (U = 236.5, p < 0.0001, r = 0.481), while the CE + OL-blocked group exhibited significantly lower activity levels than the control group during both the day and night (U = 109.5, p < 0.0001, r = 0.689; U = 380, p = 0.025, r = 0.273, Fig. 3C). This suggests that the compound eyes and ocelli-like pathways can jointly regulate the diurnal and nocturnal activity of Cx. quinquefasciatus, likely through a synergistic interaction. Under DD conditions, the activity of the OL-blocked group was significantly lower than that of the control group during the subjective night (U = 323.5, p = 0.005, r = 0.343, Fig. 3C). This indicates that the ocelli-like pathway may play a primary role in regulating the endogenous rhythms of Cx. quinquefasciatus. We observed that under DD conditions, Cx. quinquefasciatus exhibited only a moderate decrease in activity in the CE + OL-blocked group, without a significant decline (Fig. 3C). This suggests that when both the compound eye and ocelli-like pathways are blocked, other pathways (such as the antennal region) may compensate for the lack of light under DD conditions in Cx. quinquefasciatus.
In previous studies, the TTFL formed by core clock genes (period, timeless, clk, cycle, cry) has been identified as a key mechanism regulating the rhythms of D. melanogaster, playing an important role in behaviors such as flight activity, mating, and eclosion6,50. To investigate whether the circadian rhythm of core clock genes in mosquitoes is altered after compound eye paint application, RT-qPCR was used to detect the transcript abundance profiles of six major core clock genes in the control and painted groups of both mosquito species. The results showed that the transcript abundance profiles of Aalcyc and AalCry1 were significantly lower in the CE-blocked group of Ae. albopictus compared to the control group (mean difference = 0.47, 95% CI = [−0.92, 0.92], p = 0.041; mean difference = 0.38, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.69], p = 0.017), while the transcript abundance profiles of AalClk and AalCry2 were significantly higher in the OL-blocked group (mean difference = −0.82, 95% CI = [−1.63, −0.007], p = 0.049; mean difference = −1.26, 95% CI = [−2.11, −0.43], p = 0.008, Fig. 3D, left panel). This supports the conclusion that the compound eyes and ocelli-like pathways may differentially regulate the transcription of different core clock genes in Ae. albopictus. In Cx. quinquefasciatus, the transcript abundance of Cxqtim, CxqClk, and Cxqcyc was significantly lower in the CE-blocked group compared to controls (mean difference = 0.46, 95% CI = [0.21, 0.70], p = 0.002; mean difference = 0.45, 95% CI = [0.25, 0.66], p = 0.001; mean difference = 0.37, 95% CI = [0.18, 0.55], p = 0.002), while the abundance of all six core clock gene transcripts was significantly lower in the OL-blocked group (Cxqper: mean difference = 0.65, 95% CI = [0.22, 1.08], p = 0.008; Cxqtim: mean difference = 0.34, 95% CI = [0.10, 0.58], p = 0.011; CxqClk: mean difference = 0.60, 95% CI = [0.39, 0.81], p < 0.001; Cxqcyc: mean difference = 0.58, 95% CI = [0.40, 0.76], p < 0.0001; CxqCry1: mean difference = 0.56, 95% CI = [0.26, 0.86], p = 0.002; CxqCry2: mean difference = 0.48, 95% CI = [0.08, 0.87], p = 0.024, Fig. 3D, right panel), indicating that the ocelli-like pathway might play a more critical role in the transcriptional regulation of core clock genes in Cx. quinquefasciatus.
The compound eyes of mosquitoes contain numerous photoreceptor cells with different amounts and types of opsins. The opsins combine with retinal to form rhodopsin, a key photopigment involved in the light transduction pathway51–55. Previous studies have reported that Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus possess 10 and 13 opsin genes, respectively56,57. RT-qPCR was used to measure opsin gene transcript abundance in control and painted mosquitoes in both mosquito species. Aalopsin1 showed the highest transcript abundance in the control Ae. albopictus and its abundance was notably lower in CE-blocked and CE + OL-blocked groups (t = 3.06, df = 4, p = 0.037, d = 1.88; t = 4.95, df = 4, p = 0.007, d = 3.02, Fig. 3E, left panel). Cxqopsin6 exhibited the highest transcript abundance in control Cx. quinquefasciatus group and its abundance was significantly lower in all three painted groups (t = 3.20, df = 4, p = 0.032, d = 2.16; t = 5.39, df = 4, p = 0.005, d = 3.39; t = 8.45, df = 4, p = 0.001, d = 5.07, Fig. 3E, right panel). These results indicate that the compound eye pathway in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus may act primarily to regulate the expression of core clock genes and circadian activity rhythms through Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6, respectively.
Overall, our findings support the conclusion that the compound eye pathway is most likely the main pathway regulating circadian rhythms in Ae. albopictus, whereas Cx. quinquefasciatus may rely on multiple pathways to regulate its circadian rhythms.

## Cas9/guide RNA-mediated knockout of the Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6 genes affects the circadian rhythm and the expression of clock genes in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus, respectively
The compound eyes are the primary photoreceptive organs in adult mosquitoes42,44. After light-blocking paint was applied to the compound eyes, the main phototransduction pathways mediated by opsins were disrupted, leading to diminished light sensitivity and altered circadian rhythms. To investigate whether opsins play a crucial role in the regulation of mosquito circadian rhythms, we selected the opsin genes with the highest transcript levels in Ae. albopictus (Aalopsin1) and Cx. quinquefasciatus (Cxqopsin6) and performed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout and functional analyses of these two genes. One homozygous Ae. albopictus knockout strain was established for Aalopsin1 (and Aalop1Δ119) and one Cx. quinquefasciatus homozygous knockout strain (Cxqop6Δ1) was recovered (Supplementary Figs. 3–5).
To determine whether the knockout of the Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6 gene affects their activity rhythms, the flight activity rhythms of Aalop1Δ119 and Cxqop6Δ1 were assessed under LD and DD conditions, respectively. The activity of wild-type (WT; Aalop1+ and Cxqop6+) and knockout strains were monitored using a Drosophila Activity Monitoring (DAM) system (Supplementary Fig. 6). Under LD conditions, the Aalop1Δ119 strain showed significantly increased activity during both day and night (U = 2697.5, p < 0.0001, r = 0.350; U = 2321.5, p = 0.043, r = 0.182, Fig. 4A, left and right panel). Core clock genes in D. melanogaster are essential for controlling activity peaks in the morning and evening, as well as phase shifts60–63. To investigate whether the knockout of Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6 affects the transcript abundance of six downstream core clock genes under LD and DD conditions. RT-qPCR was performed to examine the temporal expression profiles of core clock genes in both wild-type and mutant strains. The overall transcript abundance profiles of AalClk, Aalcyc, and AalCry2 in Aalop1Δ119 under LD and DD conditions were also similar to those of the controls (Fig. 4C). However, Aalper, Aaltim, and AalCry1 transcript levels were significantly reduced compared to the wild-type, indicating that mutation of Aalopsin1 affected the expression of certain clock genes (Fig. 4B, C). The transcript abundance profiles of the six core clock genes in Cxqop6Δ1 under LD conditions were not significantly different from those of the wild-type controls (Fig. 5B, C). However, apart from CxqCry1, the abundance profiles of the other core clock genes under DD conditions showed phase delays or advances relative to the wild-type, suggesting that mutation of Cxqopsin6 has a certain impact on the endogenous circadian rhythm in Cx. quinquefasciatus (Fig. 5C).
The process of transmitting signals from the compound eyes to the circadian clock neurons in the brain involves the conversion of light, electrical, and chemical signals, with histamine playing a critical role as a neurotransmitter in signal transduction and transformation55,64–68. It has been reported that in D. melanogaster, light signals received by the compound eyes are converted into chemical signals through phototransduction pathways, which are then relayed to intermediate neurons and subsequently to the core clock neurons69,70. The pigment-dispersing factor (PDF) is another important neuropeptide neurotransmitter and acts as a circadian rhythm regulator, effectively synchronizing PDF neurons with other clock neurons’ daily rhythms71–73. Transcript abundance profiles of the histamine-gated chloride channels genes (HACls) and pdf were measured using RT-qPCR in the head tissues of wild-type, Aalopsin1 knockout, and Cxqopsin6 knockout strains to assess the role of histamine and pdf in signal transmission. The results showed that HACls transcript abundance was significantly higher in Aalop1Δ119 mutant strains relative to wild-type controls (t = −6.42, df = 4, p = 0.003, d = −4.68, Fig. 4D, left panel). These results suggest that the important neurotransmitters histamine and pdf play crucial roles in the regulation of circadian rhythms in diurnal Ae. albopictus.
Our findings indicate that the regulatory effect of the Aalopsin1 on the circadian rhythms of Ae. albopictus is significantly stronger than that of Cxqopsin6 on the circadian rhythms of Cx. quinquefasciatus. This suggests that the compound eye pathway plays a more critical role in the regulation of circadian rhythms in diurnal mosquitoes compared to nocturnal mosquitoes.

To determine whether the disruption of the Cry1 gene affects their activity rhythms, the flight activity rhythms of AalCry1Δ13 and CxqCry1Δ44 were assessed under LD and DD conditions, respectively. The results showed that under LD conditions, both AalCry1Δ13 and the wild-type controls exhibited significantly higher daytime activity compared to nighttime activity (WT; AalCry1+, U = 7, p < 0.0001, r = 0.843; AalCryΔ13, U = 13, p < 0.0001, r = 0.830, Fig. 6A left and right panel, and 6B). The mRNA levels of AalClk in the AalCry1Δ13 strain were significantly lower during the daytime (ZT3-6) and late night (ZT24) under LD conditions, while the transcript levels were reduced during the subjective daytime (CT3-6) and increased during the subjective nighttime (CT12-18) under DD conditions (Fig. 6D). In addition, transcript abundance of AalCry2 genes were lower compared to wild-type controls at ZT0 or CT0 and during nighttime (under LD condition) or subjective nighttime (under DD condition), respectively. Under DD conditions, however, the transcription levels of AalCry2 and CxqCry2 genes were higher than their wild-type controls around midday (near CT12). Notably, the upregulation of CxqCry2 mRNA levels in the CxqCry1Δ44 mutant strain was more pronounced compared to that of AalCry2 in the AalCry1Δ13 strain (Figs. 6D and 7D). The Cry1 gene mutation affected the circadian rhythms of locomotor activity and molecular clock expression patterns in mosquitoes. For Cx. quinquefasciatus in particular, the absence of the Cry1 gene led to the disruption of rhythmic behavior under both LD and DD conditions. These results suggested that the Cry1 pathway plays a more critical role in the regulation of circadian rhythms in nocturnal mosquitoes compared to diurnal mosquitoes.

## Effects of different light conditions on growth and development in mosquito larval and adult stages
Studies in Drosophila have demonstrated that continuous light exposure leads to shortened lifespan and reduced survival rates79. In Aedes aegypti, constant light conditions have been shown to impair embryonic development and compromise reproductive capacity80. To examine the effects of light conditions on lifespan variation across developmental stages, we conducted comparative experiments between the diurnally active Aedes albopictus and the nocturnally active Cx. quinquefasciatus. The experimental design was based on our hypothesis that differential photoperiod responses in these species, potentially mediated by distinct light perception mechanisms, may drive both their temporal activity patterns (diurnal vs. nocturnal) and survival characteristics. Larvae were reared under LD, LL, and DD conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10A), and their pupation and eclosion rates were recorded. Comparing the growth and development of Ae. albopictus larvae under LD, LL, and DD conditions, the pupation rates were 0.93 ± 0.06, 0.87 ± 0.06, and 0.90 ± 0.05, respectively (LD vs LL, χ2 = 2.92, p = 0.088; LD vs DD, χ2 = 0.84, p = 0.412, Supplementary Fig. 10B and Table 7). All pupation rate and eclosion rate values are mean ± SEM unless indicated otherwise. The eclosion rates were 0.94 ± 0.05, 0.97 ± 0.04, and 0.93 ± 0.06, respectively (LD vs LL, χ2 = 0.05, p = 0.813, LD vs DD, χ2 = 1.91, p = 0.166, Supplementary Fig. 10C and Table 7). Similarly, comparing the growth and development of Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae under LD, LL, and DD conditions, the pupation rates were 0.83 ± 0.03, 0.80 ± 0.10, and 0.80 ± 0.06, respectively (LD vs LL, χ2 = 0.36, p = 0.548; LD vs DD, χ2 = 0.36, p = 0.548, Supplementary Fig. 10D and Table 7). ## Discussion
The selection of temporal niches reflects the co-evolution of mosquito behavioral rhythms and environmental factors. Different mosquito species have evolved distinct diel activity patterns: for example, Culex and Anopheles mosquitoes are typically active during dusk or nighttime, while Aedes mosquitoes are primarily diurnal. This temporal divergence facilitates the partitioning of resources over time and may reduce interspecific competition and predation risk81–83.
The Aalop1Δ119 mutant strain exhibited significantly altered activity compared to the wild-type Aedes albopictus, while the Cxqop6Δ1 strain showed no substantial difference in activity relative to wild-type Culex quinquefasciatus; neither mutant showed obvious changes in rhythmicity compared to their respective wild-type controls. In contrast, both the AalCry1Δ13 and CxqCry1Δ44 strains displayed pronounced alterations in activity relative to their respective wild types, although only the CxqCry1Δ44 strain exhibited a significant reduction in rhythmicity (WT; CxqCry1⁺, 90.3%; CxqCry1Δ44, 41.4%, Supplementary Table 6). These results suggest that both the compound eye and ocelli-like pathways play important roles in circadian rhythm regulation in mosquitoes. However, based on the overall findings, we propose that the diurnal Aedes albopictus predominantly relies on the compound eye pathway, while the nocturnal Culex quinquefasciatus preferentially uses the ocelli-like (Cry1-mediated) pathway. Furthermore, these pathways appear to differentially influence the transcriptional regulation of core clock genes (Supplementary Fig. 12).
In Drosophila, photoreceptor cells of the compound eye convert light signals into chemical signals using histamine as a neurotransmitter, and the widely distributed PDF-positive neuronal fibers are thought to play essential roles in signal reception and coordination among clock neurons64,84. Previous studies have reported that Hr38 can regulate PDF expression75. Based on these findings, we hypothesize the existence of a regulatory cascade in the mosquito compound eye pathway, wherein light signals are converted into histaminergic signals, which in turn modulate Hr38 to regulate PDF expression and ultimately coordinate clock neuron activity. This regulatory pathway is likely to exist in the compound eye of the diurnal Aedes albopictus, whereas alternative mechanisms may operate in the nocturnal Culex quinquefasciatus (Figs. 4D and 5D, Supplementary Fig. 12).
Our transcriptome analysis confirmed that core clock genes, such as period (per), timeless (tim), cycle (cyc), and cryptochrome 2 (Cry2), are expressed in both Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus, and their rhythmic expression patterns are largely consistent with the known TTFL model from other insects4,50. For example, the expression peaks of per, tim, and Cry2 were close in phase, while cyc exhibited an antiphasic rhythm, supporting the existence of a conserved core clock oscillatory mechanism in both mosquito species.
Although the core TTFL mechanism shows a high degree of conservation between species, our data also revealed potentially significant species-specific differences in light perception pathways and rhythmic outputs. GO analysis indicated that while many similar biological processes (such as vision, sleep, memory, eclosion, immunity, and metabolism) are under circadian control in both mosquito species, the specific gene sets involved and the peak phases of their rhythmic regulation differ between the two species. This suggests that even if the upstream core clock is similar, the downstream rhythmic output networks and the ways environmental signals (especially light) are perceived and integrated may have evolved unique characteristics adapted to their respective diurnal (Aedes albopictus) and nocturnal (Culex quinquefasciatus) ecological niches. This also provided a molecular background for our subsequent functional studies of specific photoreceptive proteins (e.g., Aalopsin1, Cxqopsin6) and Cry1 using gene-editing technology, where we found they play different roles in the two mosquito species.
One of the most striking findings from this RNA-Seq analysis was that the proportion of rhythmically expressed genes in the nocturnal Culex quinquefasciatus (43.88%, 5952/13,563) is significantly higher than in the diurnal Aedes albopictus (9.29%, 1183/12,740) (Fig. 2A). We speculate that this substantial difference reflects a more profound and extensive molecular adaptation of Culex quinquefasciatus to its nocturnal lifestyle. Nocturnal organisms primarily operate in an environment devoid of the main zeitgeber (light) during their active period, which may necessitate a greater reliance on the clock for the precise temporal regulation of nearly all physiological processes to ensure behavioral timing and optimize energy use. Therefore, a larger proportion of the genome being under rhythmic control might aid Culex quinquefasciatus in accurately initiating and maintaining complex nocturnal activities, such as host-seeking, blood-feeding, and predator avoidance in the dark. It might also conserve energy and reduce survival risks by strictly suppressing non-essential gene expression during the daytime. In contrast, the activity of Aedes albopictus is more synchronized with light availability, and its reliance on direct environmental light cues might be relatively stronger, thus potentially resulting in a smaller proportion of genes being strictly regulated by the endogenous clock, although these genes still precisely control key physiological functions required for its daytime activities. As indicated by our GO analysis, although rhythmic genes in both mosquito species are involved in diverse biological functions (e.g., vision, sleep, memory, eclosion, immunity, and metabolism), the number of genes participating in these functions is clearly much larger in Culex quinquefasciatus (Supplementary Fig. 1). This finding not only reveals strategic differences in how these two mosquito species adapt to different temporal niches at the gene expression level but also offers valuable perspectives on how nocturnal insects maintain robust endogenous rhythms and underscores the importance of investigating species-specific circadian regulatory mechanisms in future studies.
Earlier studies have reported the use of light-blocking paint applications to study effects on mosquito and fruit fly activity57,85–87. These studies consistently demonstrated that this smearing method provides effective visual occlusion without significantly impacting flight activity. In our experiments, we meticulously confined the paint application to the compound eyes or ocelli-like areas and ensured uniform and consistent smearing. We believe that this approach, while serving our experimental objective (investigating optical pathways), minimized any potential adverse effects on the mosquitoes’ flight balance and overall motor activity. Therefore, we are confident that under both LD (light/dark cycle) and DD (constant darkness) conditions, the mosquitoes’ activity patterns are not significantly influenced by this smearing treatment. Furthermore, the acrylic paint dries quickly after application, forming a rubber-like membrane on the surface of the compound eyes88,89. This membrane prevents further penetration of the paint into mosquito tissues, thereby minimizing any potential harmful effects on the mosquitoes.
Previous studies have reported a close relationship between the evolution of different opsins and the regulation of circadian rhythms in Drosophila and other insects90,91. To better understand the opsin gene repertoire and potential evolutionary trends in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis of opsin genes from Ae. albopictus, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and D. melanogaster. The results showed that Aalopsin1, Aalopsin2, Cxqopsin5, and Cxqopsin6 clustered closely together, indicating a high degree of homology between Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6 (Supplementary Fig. 11). Moreover, these opsins were found to be relatively close to Drosophila Rh6, which has been reported to be a long-wavelength-sensitive opsin92,93, suggesting that Aalopsin1 and Cxqopsin6 may function as long-wavelength-sensitive opsins as well.
In the absence of light, normal rhodopsin consists of retinal and opsin. Upon absorbing light, retinal undergoes structural isomerization, which causes opsin to lose its ability to recognize the isomerized retinal, leading to the destabilization of its binding. Consequently, the opsin structure changes and enters an activated state, where it can bind to G proteins and initiate downstream signaling cascades94–96. The Aalopsin1 protein in the Aalop1Δ119 mutant strain shows weakened light sensitivity due to the loss of retinal-binding sites. However, the activity levels of the Aalop1Δ119 mosquitoes are significantly higher than those of the wild-type controls during the day and under DD conditions, both in subjective day and subjective night. The Aalop1Δ119 strain has a frameshift mutation that results in the opsin protein losing C-terminal amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 7). The C-terminal domain contains multiple serine (S) and threonine (T) residues similar to what is found in D. melanogaster rhodopsin Rh195,97. The Rh1 multiple serine and threonine residues in the C-terminal domain can be phosphorylated by G-protein-coupled receptor kinase 1 (GPRK1), a process that modulates the amplitude of visual responses measured with electroretinograms98. An increase or decrease in GPRK1 activity leads to weaker or stronger visual responses, respectively95,98. We expect that the Aalopsin1 in the Aalop1Δ119 strain cannot be phosphorylated normally by GPRK1 as a consequence of the loss of the C-terminal domain. The lack of phosphorylation regulation of Aalopsin1 in the Aalop1Δ119 mutant strain is expected to result in aberrant amplified autonomous retinal potential signals, resulting in significantly increased activity during the day and during subjective day and subjective night.
According to the current TTFL theory in Drosophila1,5,6, alterations in the expression of any clock gene can potentially affect the expression of other clock genes. Based on our experimental results, we believe that Aalopsin1 most likely regulates the downregulation of AalCry1, which in turn leads to the reduced expression of Aalper and Aaltim. The downregulation of AalCry1 expression may lead to a slower degradation rate of the PER-TIM heterodimer. As a result, the PER-TIM complex remains bound to the CLK-CYC heterodimer for a prolonged period, thereby preventing the CLK-CYC complex from binding to downstream E-box elements and initiating the transcription of target genes such as Aalper and Aaltim. This hypothesis may be consistent with our experimental results. The transcription of clk is mainly regulated by PDP1 and VRI, which implies that opsin deletion in Aalopsin1Δ119 may not directly affect this regulatory pathway99,100. The transcription of Aalcyc may be modulated by an alternative regulatory pathway. This mechanism aligns best with the theoretical foundation of the transcriptional feedback loop. However, we do not rule out the possibility that Aalopsin1 may also directly regulate Aalper, especially given the significant reduction in Aalperiod temporal transcript abundance profiles. Clearly, the TTFL model alone is insufficient to fully explain the complexity of these regulatory mechanisms, and further investigation into deeper regulatory layers is warranted.
In contrast, in the Cxqop6Δ1 strain, a frameshift mutation near the 5′ end of the Cxqopsin6 gene leads to the loss of all functional domains of the Cxqopsin6 protein, including the G-protein and retinal-binding sites (Supplementary Fig. 8). As a result, the final translated product in the Cxqop6Δ1 strain is a short peptide with virtually no functional capacity, whereas the Aalop1Δ119 strain retains some functional domains, thus potentially conferring partial functionality (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8)94,96. Under DD conditions, the temporal transcript abundance profiles of clock genes in the Cxqop6Δ1 strain, except for CxqCry1, show varying degrees of phase delays or advances. These findings suggest differences in how Cxqopsin6 regulates clock gene expression compared to Aalopsin1, though we cannot exclude the possibility that this difference arises from the distinct mutation sites in the genes.
CRY1 is a light-responsive circadian photoreceptor that has been reported to be involved in regulating light-regulated behaviors in insects101,102. The CRISPR-mediated mutations of the Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus cryptochrome1 (Cry1) gene homologs have different effects on the circadian locomotor activity rhythms of the respective species. The AalCry1 mutation affected the activity patterns of diurnal Ae. albopictus under LD and DD conditions, showing a trend of increased activity during the daytime and significantly enhanced activity during the subjective daytime, with the activity peak shifting earlier under DD conditions. However, a robust locomotor activity rhythm was still present under both LD and DD conditions. The impact of the AalCry1 mutation on the locomotor activity rhythm is remarkably consistent with that of the Aalopsin1 mutation, supporting the conclusion that there is a close connection between both genes and the regulation of circadian rhythms in diurnal Ae. albopictus. In contrast, the Cx. quinquefasciatus CxqCry1 mutation significantly disrupted the nocturnal rhythmic activity under the same LD and DD conditions, leading to aberrant nighttime and subjective nighttime activity and the disruption of rhythmic activity under constant darkness. This supports the conclusion that the products of the Cx. quinquefasciatus Cry1 gene plays a key role in maintaining nocturnal circadian locomotor activity in these mosquitoes. Cry1 mutations cause circadian clock resetting defects in D. melanogaster but allow them to maintain rhythmicity under both LD and constant darkness conditions78,103. Cry1 mutant strains of the monarch butterflies maintain daytime flight activity under LD conditions, but the rhythmic pattern is damped compared to wild-type butterflies104. Therefore, while Cry1 appears to be essential for regulating circadian locomotor rhythms of mosquitoes, the characteristics of the rhythms vary among different species.
The rhythmic behavior of diverse animals is controlled by an internal circadian clock that is regulated by specific genes (clock genes) that form one or more transcriptional feedback loops to control the expression of output genes throughout the body, thereby temporally regulating behavior105,106. In the TTFL, CRY1 acts as a light-responsive circadian photoreceptor. Upon light stimulation, CRY1 resets the clock by binding to TIM and promoting its degradation107. The AalCry1Δ13 and CxqCry1Δ44 mutant strains used in this study contain a premature stop codon, and such transcripts can be removed through the NMD pathway, which degrades aberrant mRNAs with a premature stop codon58,59. The absence of Cry1 transcripts under LD conditions likely results in an inability to degrade TIM mRNAs during the daytime, thereby extending the transcriptional repression of PER/TIM complexes on CLK/CYC and impacting downstream clock gene expression. Surprisingly, while the transcription products of the core clock genes in both AalCry1Δ13 and CxqCry1Δ44 mutant strains mainly reduced abundance levels, there were no significant phase shifts under LD cycles, supporting the conclusion that CRY1 is not the only photoreceptor involved in light-regulated behavior. The transcript abundance profiles of core clock genes under DD conditions in both AalCry1Δ13 and CxqCry1Δ44 strains were affected, and this is consistent with changes in the locomotor activity rhythms of both mutant strains under constant darkness, indicating that Cry1 is essential for maintaining the endogenous circadian clock in these mosquitoes. In AalCry1Δ13, we observed extremely low expression levels of period (per) and cycle (cyc) mRNA. Given that the CLK/AeCYC complex is the primary activator of AePer transcription, a drastic reduction in AeCyc mRNA would almost inevitably lead to exceptionally low levels of AePer. This suggests that AeCRY1 may play an essential, and potentially light-independent, role. This role could be crucial for the stability or functional potency of the CLK/AeCYC transcriptional machinery, or it might even directly influence the basal expression of the AeCyc gene itself. Although such a dramatic phenotype, characterized by the near-complete disappearance of per and cyc transcripts, has not been previously reported in cry1 mutants of other insects, evidence for the broad involvement of CRY1 in core clock regulation is supported by studies in other species. For instance, knocking out cry1 in the silkworm (Bombyx mori) leads to a disruption of eclosion rhythms and the impairment of photoperiodic diapause induction, directly indicating that Bm-cry1 participates in the circadian feedback loop regulating behavioral rhythms108. In the monarch butterfly, cry1 also causes similar disruptions to eclosion rhythms109. These studies suggest that insect CRY1 proteins, including AalCRY1 and CxqCRY1, may be more deeply integrated into the regulation of core clock gene function than previously recognized. They might influence the activity or stability of CLK/CYC, even though the specific molecular mechanisms and the extent of their impact may vary among different species.
Furthermore, we observed that in AalCry1Δ13 mutants, even with a significant reduction in the expression of the clock genes Aalper and Aalcyc, the flight activity of these mutants, while affected, still exhibited a degree of rhythmicity under both LD and DD conditions. This finding suggests that within the mosquito circadian regulatory system, there might be, on one hand, other compensatory rhythmically expressed genes that enable the endogenous core oscillator to maintain robust rhythmicity. On the other hand, it could be related to the multi-level regulation of the circadian clock. Specifically, post-transcriptional (e.g., mRNA stability, translation efficiency) and post-translational (e.g., protein phosphorylation, degradation rate regulation) mechanisms can effectively buffer fluctuations in upstream gene expression levels, ensuring the production of sufficient quantities and activity of functional clock proteins even when mRNA levels are low. Concurrently, signal amplification in downstream output pathways might also allow the core oscillator to drive clear behavioral rhythms, even if it generates a relatively weak initial signal. This also highlights the complex regulatory mechanisms of circadian clock systems across different insect species.
Notably, the transcript levels of Ae. albopictus AalCry1 were significantly lower in the Aalop1Δ119 mutant strain under both LD and DD conditions, while the Aalopsin1 levels in AalCry1Δ13 mosquitoes were significantly higher under the same conditions, showing a compensatory increase in the absence of Cry1. Increased Aalopsin1 transcript levels are consistent with the increased daytime activity levels observed in the AalCry1Δ13 strain. Furthermore, mutations of Ae. albopictus Aalopsin1 or the AalCry1 led to the reduction of Aalper and Aaltim transcript levels. These results support our conclusion that the compound eye pathway plays a crucial role in the regulation of circadian rhythms in diurnal mosquitoes and further suggest the existence of an indirect relationship between Aalopsin1 and AalCry1 in the circadian behavior regulatory pathway.
Studies have shown that specific knockout of pdf can transform the diurnal insect Pyrrhocoris apterus into a nocturnal species110. Our results implicate Hr38 and PDF as important components in the circadian behavior regulatory pathway. In contrast, the CxqCry1 mutation under the same conditions used to evaluate these gene products had significantly lower transcript levels of various clock genes, including Cxqopsin6, and disrupted rhythmic locomotor activity. Compared to the Cxqopsin6 mutation, the CxqCry1 mutation had more severe consequences for Cx. quinquefasciatus, further supporting the critical role of the Cry1 pathway in maintaining circadian rhythmicity in nocturnal mosquitoes. However, the complete molecular regulatory mechanisms of both input pathways and how they influence downstream behaviors require further investigation.
Light is a crucial factor influencing insect growth and development. It plays an essential role in regulating circadian rhythms, which in turn influence numerous aspects of mosquito behavior and physiology, including oviposition, flight activity, mating, metabolism, immune response, and olfaction111. Our findings display that LL does not affect the Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus pupation and eclosion rates. We speculate that this is because mosquito larvae inhabit aquatic environments, where their circadian rhythms are influenced less by light exposure. As a result, light has a minor impact on these stages of their growth and development. Consistent with this conclusion, no differences in locomotor behavior were observed in larval zebrafish, Danio rerio, under LD or DD condition112. In contrast, adult mosquitoes exhibit a significant sensitivity to light as a zeitgeber (time cue) for their circadian rhythms. Diurnal Ae. albopictus experience a severe disruption of their circadian rhythm under DD conditions, leading to alterations in locomotor activity, metabolism, and other life cycle rhythms, ultimately shortening their lifespan. In contrast, nocturnal Cx. quinquefasciatus are more susceptible to LL conditions, which disrupt their circadian rhythms and impact locomotor activity and metabolism and leading to a shortened lifespan. We attribute this difference to the distinct light-sensing pathways regulating circadian rhythm in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus.

## Methods

## Mosquitos strains
The wild-type strains of Ae. albopictus (Foshan strain) and Cx. quinquefasciatus (Guangzhou strain) were provided by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Guangdong Province, China. The Aalopsin1, Cxqopsin6, and Cry1 gene mutants (Aalop1Δ119, Cxop6Δ1, AalCry1Δ13, and CxqCry1Δ44) were established and maintained by our laboratory. Larvae (200 larvae/L water) were reared in plastic trays containing dechlorinated water and were fed daily with turtle food (INCH-GOLD®, Huizhou City, China). After eclosion, the mosquitoes were placed in cages (23 × 23 × 35 cm) and provided with a 10% glucose solution. All mosquitoes were maintained under standard insectary conditions of 27 ± 1 °C, 70 ± 10% relative humidity, and a LD cycle of 12 h/12 h (500 lux, LED light).

## Sample collection and RNA-seq library construction
For RNA-seq, mosquitoes were collected at 3 h intervals under LD conditions (lights on at 9:00 am (ZT0) and lights off at 9:00 pm (ZT12)) and paced in liquid nitrogen. Twenty mosquitoes were collected as pools from each time point, and three biological replicates were made. Total RNA was extracted from adult female Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) followed by TURBO DNase (Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific) digestion to remove contamination with genomic DNA. The concentration and the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of total RNA were determined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer using the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit. Only RNA with RIN > 7.0 and OD 260/280 ~2.0 was used for library construction and sequencing. mRNA was enriched by oligo(dT)—attached magnetic beads from total RNA and fragmented at an appropriate temperature. The fragmented RNA was reverse transcribed using PCR (RT-PCR) and N6 random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. Subsequently, A-Tailing Mix (Enzymatics, USA) and RNA Index Adapters were added to perform end-repair, and the resulting cDNA was amplified by PCR. The PCR products were purified by Ampure XP Beads, and then heat-denatured and circularized by the splint oligo sequence to obtain the final library. The single-stranded circular DNA was amplified using a single oligo and phi29 (Thermo Scientific, USA) to generate DNA nanoballs (DNB). The DNBs were loaded into the patterned nanoarray on the BGISEQ-500 platform (Beijing Genomics Institute, China) to generate single-end 50 bp reads. The raw reads were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive database with accession numbers of PRJNA1172286.

## Analysis of next-generation sequencing (NGS) and oscillator gene identification by JTK_Cycle
Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus reference genomes and annotation files were downloaded from Vectorbase113. Adapter sequences were removed from all reads with Cutadapt (v3.3)114 and quality checks performed with FastQC (v0.11.9)115. The clean reads were aligned to respective reference genomes with Hisat2 (v2.2.1)116. Alignment files were transferred from sam to bam, and the reads count quantified for each gene with Rfeaturecount of Rsubread R package (v2.8)117. Reads counts were normalized to TMM with DESeq2 (v1.34.0). Circadian rhythmicity was analyzed with JTK_CYCLE analysis using MetaCycle (v1.2.0)118 implemented in R (x64 v3.0.2). Genes were considered rhythmically expressed if they met the following least stringent cutoff values: adjusted P value < 0.05, period length between 18 and 28 h, and median expression ≥1119.

## Aalopsin1, Cxqopsin6, and Cry1 gene knockout strains generated using CRISPR/Cas9
A dual sgRNA knockout strategy was used in Ae. albopictus and Cx. quinquefasciatus. Pairs of sgRNAs were designed for the exons of Aalopsin1, Cxqopsin6, and Cry1 using the CRISPOR online tool (http://crispor.tefor.net). The template DNA for transcription in vitro of sgRNAs was prepared using Q5 HighFidelity DNAPolymerase and purified using MiniBEST DNA Fragment Purification Kit Ver.4.0 (TaKaRa) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The sgRNA templates were transcribed in vitro using the T7 RiboMAX Express Large Scale RNA Production System kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA transcripts were purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation. The injection mixes contained 300 ng/μL NLS-Cas9-NLS Nuclease (Genscript, NJ, USA), 100 ng/μL purified sgRNA1, and 100 ng/μL purified sgRNA2120. The injection mixtures were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C to reconstitute active ribonucleoproteins before microinjection. The mixtures were then injected into mosquito eggs using the FemtoJet 4i microinjector in combination with the InjecTMan NI 2 micromanipulator121.

## DNA extraction, mutated gene detection, and screening
All injected embryos were incubated for 3–5 days in insectary conditions for hatching, and the hatched larvae and emerged adults were designated G0. Genomic DNA samples from all G0 adults were extracted from the legs of individual adults using the Extract-N-Amp™ Tissue PCR Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Mosaic G0 mosquitoes were screened by gene amplification (polymerase chain reaction, PCR) using Maxima Hot Start Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific). Each mosaic G0 virgin male was mated with five wild-type virgin females. Meanwhile, each mosaic G0 virgin female was mated with five wild-type virgin males. All mated female mosquitoes were fed blood to lay eggs, and the G1 generation was obtained. PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target band was excised from the gel and purified by using MiniBEST Agarose Gel DNA Extraction Kit Ver.4.0 (TaKaRa). The purified DNA was then sequenced by the Sanger method to obtain the complete gene sequence. The sequence analyses were performed with Chromas. After detection of the mutations, the G1 heterozygous adult male and female mosquitoes with the same mutation type were screened and crossed with each other to produce the G2 generation. The Aalopsin1, Cxopsin6, and Cry1 mutants were mass-crossed to establish homozygous mutant strains.

## Locomotor activity analysis
Individual 3–5-day-old, non-blood-fed, mated female mosquitoes were recorded using the Locomotor Activity Monitoring System (TriKinetics, LAM25) in the presence of a 10% sucrose solution for nutrition. The monitors were located inside an incubator with a constant temperature of 27 °C and an LD cycle of 12 h/12 h (500 lux light). The LD cycle refers to lights on at ZT0 (zeitgeber time, 9:00 am) and lights off at ZT12 (21:00 pm). The mosquitoes were kept in LD conditions for 7 days, after which they were released into DD (constant dark) for an additional 5 days. Data acquisition was performed using the DAMSystem310 (TriKinetics). Actograms or histograms under LD conditions were generated from activity data recorded on days 4–7. Histograms and circadian period analyses under constant darkness (DD) were based on data from the final 4 days of the DD phase. The circadian period analyses were performed on the online website ShinyR-DAM (https://karolcichewicz.shinyapps.io/shinyr-dam)122.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR analysis
Heads of ten non-blood-fed, mated adult females (3–5 days old) at different zeitgeber times on day 4 under 12:12 LD cycles or on day 2 under DD for temporal transcript abundance profiles. 15 female adult mosquito heads of different mutant strains were collected for the detection of the pdf, HACls, and Hr38. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. 5 μg total RNA was digested using the TURBO DNA-free Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to remove genomic DNA following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized with GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. All quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analyses were performed using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgene, Beijing, China) and Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each sample was analyzed with three biological replicates and normalized using the reference gene rpS7 (ribosomal protein S7). All gene transcript abundance profiles assays were performed with three biological replicates. RT-qPCR results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. All primers for quantitative PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

## Light-blocking paint applications on mosquito heads
Light-blocking paint applications were performed using a method described previously for mosquitoes and vinegar flies (50,72). The opaque black acrylic paint (793, Marie’s) was diluted and applied to glass slides. A photometer (OHSP-350C, Hopoocolor, China) was used to measure the light flux in the painted area until it decreased to 0 lux, indicating that the paint was ready for application. We classified the mosquito head area into two parts, designated the CE (Compound eyes area) and OL (Ocelli-like area). Three-to-five-day-old adult female mosquitoes were anesthetized with CO2, and the paint was applied using a brush to the different head areas. After painting, the mosquitoes were synchronized for 24 h in a climate chamber under an LD cycle of 12 h:12 h with other conditions kept constant. On the second day, a subset of the paint-treated mosquitoes was used for activity monitoring, while another subset was used for gene expression analysis. Five adult mosquitoes were collected at the same time point for total RNA extraction, and qRT-PCR was performed to measure the expression levels of opsins and core clock genes in Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus.

## Larval growth and development
The experimental subjects included larvae of Aedes albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus. Fifty newly hatched first-instar larvae were placed in 300 ml plastic bowls containing 150 ml of dechlorinated tap water. Larvae were reared until all individuals pupated. After pupation, individual pupae were transferred to 2.0 ml centrifuge tubes (cut off the lid) and monitored until emergence. The number of pupae were recorded daily until all larvae died or emerged subsequently as adults. The experiments were conducted in a climate chamber maintained at 26 °C and 70–80% humidity. Three replicates were done for each treatment group: a constant light (LL) group with 24 h light and light intensity of ~1000 lux, an LD group with a 12:12 h LD cycle, and a constant dark (DD) group.

## Comparing the adult life spans
Pupae nearing emergence were transferred individually to 2.0 ml centrifuge tubes. Upon emergence, the females and the males were separated and placed in paper bowls (14 × 11 × 10 cm) covered with mesh netting, with ~40 individuals per group. Cotton wool was placed in each bowl, and a 10% glucose solution was added to the mesh netting daily. The experiments were conducted under three light regimes: a constant light (LL) group with 24 h light and a light intensity of ~1000 lux, an LD group with a 12:12 h light: dark cycle, and a constant dark (DD) group. The experiments were conducted in a climate chamber maintained at 26 °C and 70–80% humidity.

## Statistics and reproducibility
All assays, including gene transcript abundance profiles assays, locomotor activity analysis, larval growth and development, and comparison of adult life spans, were performed with three biological replicates. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics). All data were first tested using a Shapiro-Wilk normality test (α = 0.05) to determine if they followed a normal distribution. The relative mRNA abundance levels between the groups at different time points were compared using the Student’s t-test. The differences in the total daily activity counts between the two groups or strains were assessed using the Mann–Whitney test. The differences in the relative abundance of mRNA of the all opsins genes between the WT and different paint application groups were analyzed using the Student’s t-test. The differences in the relative abundance of mRNA of the clock genes among the different paint application groups were compared using a one-way ANOVA. Differences in pupation and eclosion rates were determined using a Chi-square test. Differences in adult survival time between treatments were determined using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
