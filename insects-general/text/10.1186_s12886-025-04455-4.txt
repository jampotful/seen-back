# A rare case of fungal endophthalmitis caused by an entomopathogen

## Abstract
PurposeTo report the first known case of exogenous fungal endophthalmitis caused by Corniculantispora psalliotae, managed successfully with intraocular antifungal therapy and adjunctive cryotherapy.Case presentationA 56-year-old woman presented with pain and progressive vision loss in the left eye, six months after an insect bite–related ocular injury. She had previously received topical and oral antifungal therapy for a presumed fungal corneal ulcer. On examination, the left eye showed corneal edema, focal stromal infiltrates, hypopyon, and a lens abscess. B-scan ultrasonography revealed moderate vitreous echoes suggestive of endophthalmitis. Pars plana vitrectomy and lensectomy were performed, along with intravitreal injections of vancomycin, ceftazidime, and voriconazole. Vitreous biopsy smear showed septate fungal filaments, and culture yielded significant growth of Corniculantispora psalliotae, confirmed by DNA sequencing. The patient received additional intravitreal antifungals and systemic therapy. Due to persistent anterior segment inflammation and a cyclitic membrane seen on ultrasound biomicroscopy, targeted cryotherapy was performed. Clinical improvement followed, with resolution of infection and improvement in visual acuity to 20/125.ConclusionThis case highlights Corniculantispora psalliotae as a rare ocular pathogen and underscores the importance of comprehensive microbiological workup in atypical endophthalmitis. Adjunctive cryotherapy may be beneficial in cases with poor initial response and cyclitic membrane formation.

## Introduction
Fungal endophthalmitis is a vision-threatening intraocular infection that can arise via exogenous routes, such as trauma or surgery, or less commonly, through endogenous hematogenous spread [1]. The clinical presentation often overlaps with non-infectious uveitis or chronic postoperative inflammation, contributing to diagnostic delays and poorer outcomes. Filamentous fungi are a common cause of exogenous fungal endophthalmitis in tropical regions, particularly following corneal trauma or fungal keratitis.
Corniculantispora psalliotae is an entomopathogenic fungus belonging to the phylum Ascomycota. Formerly classified as Lecanicillium psalliotae, this species is primarily known from soil and insect-associated environments and is used in agricultural biocontrol [2]. Human infections with C. psalliotae are exceedingly rare, with only a few reports describing extraocular involvement, including orthopedic infections [3, 4], and to our knowledge, no prior reports exist describing ocular involvement. We report the first documented case of exogenous fungal endophthalmitis caused by Corniculantispora psalliotae in a previously healthy woman with a history of ocular trauma. The case was managed with pars plana vitrectomy, intraocular and systemic antifungal therapy, and adjunctive cryotherapy, with eventual clinical and visual recovery.

## Case report
A 56-year-old woman presented with complaints of ocular pain and gradually diminishing vision in the left eye. She gave a history of trauma involving an insect falling into her eye approximately six months prior. She had been diagnosed elsewhere with a corneal ulcer and had been initiated on topical therapy, including fortified voriconazole, amphotericin B, and moxifloxacin (Vigamox), along with oral ketoconazole for the past 2–3 months. The patient had a known history of hypertension but was otherwise immunocompetent. HIV testing was performed and was negative. On examination, her best-corrected visual acuity was 20/30 in the right eye and hand movements in the left eye. The right eye was unremarkable apart from early senile cataract. The left eye, however, exhibited corneal edema, a focal anterior chamber exudates adjacent to the iris, hypopyon, and a lens abscess (Fig. 1A). B-scan ultrasonography of the left eye revealed moderate-intensity vitreous echoes with an attached retina, findings, the clinical picture is more suggestive of exogenous endophthalmitis (Fig. 1B), likely secondary to fungal keratitis with intraocular extension. The presence of a lens abscess, hypopyon, and stromal infiltrates near the iris further supports this diagnosis. At the initial presentation, the presence of corneal edema, hypopyon, and a lens abscess made it difficult to apply the SUN classification for vitreous haze. There was no wound site of ocular injury. Accordingly, empirical treatment was initiated in line with established guidelines for the management of exogenous fungal endophthalmitis. The patients’ hematological and biochemical parameters, including complete blood count, liver function tests, renal function tests, ECG, and HIV serology were within normal limits. The patient underwent pars plana vitrectomy and pars plana lensectomy on the same day, along with intravitreal administration of empirical antibiotics—vancomycin (1 mg/0.1 mL) and ceftazidime (2.25 mg/0.1 mL). Due to the presence of whitish exudates in the vitreous cavity, intravitreal voriconazole (100 µg/0.1 mL) was also administered.

A diagnostic vitreous biopsy of the left eye was performed and subjected to smear, culture, and molecular analyses. On direct smear, Gram staining demonstrated few septate fungal filaments (Fig. 2A), Culture on Potato dextrose agar (PDA) showed white velvety colonies which couldn’t be identified by routine conventional method due to lack of sporulation (Fig. 2B). Upon complete ITS gene sequencing, the isolate was identified as Corniculantispora psalliotae (GenBank accession number – PV541759). Consensus was generated using the obtained forward and reverse sequences using SeqMan Lasergene version 7.0.0 (DNAstar, Madison, WI). The final sequences were compared with the GenBank DNA database using the BLAST tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and GenBank accession number is obtained (PV541759). Antifungal susceptibility was performed following CLSI M38 (3rd edition) guidelines against natamycin, voriconazole, posaconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, caspofungin and amphotericn B. The Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) were read visually as the lowest drug concentration showing complete inhibition of growth. Amphotericin B and posaconazole showed MIC of 2 µg/ml, MIC of caspofungin, voiconazole, ketoconazole and itraconazole was variable from 4 to 8 µg/ml and natamycin indicated high degree of resistance with MIC of 32 µg/ml. An anterior chamber (AC) membrane was also obtained and cultured but no fungal growth was observed after 7 days of incubation.

The patient subsequently received two additional intravitreal antifungal injections (amphotericin B − 5 µg/0.1mL), along with systemic antifungal therapy (Oral Ketoconazole 200 mg twice daily) for 1 month. Follow-up B-scan ultrasonography showed significant improvement in vitreous clarity, although persistent anterior segment inflammation and a membrane behind the iris were noted. Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) revealed the presence of a cyclitic membrane in all quadrants (Fig. 3). To address this, anterior retinal cryotherapy was performed targeting the ciliary body region, in conjunction with another dose of intravitreal antifungal. The intraocular pressure (IOP) before cryotherapy was 13 mmHg. On postoperative day 1 it dropped to 2 mmHg, increased to 15 mmHg by postoperative day 3, and subsequently remained stable. Over 3 weeks, the infiltrates and infection resolved, with progressive improvement in the clinical picture of endophthalmitis (Fig. 4). Her best-corrected visual acuity improved to 20/125 with a + 10.00 D correction. Media clarity improved sufficiently to visualize third-order retinal vessels, and the optic disc and macula appeared healthy. At the final postoperative follow-up, however, all retinal details were clearly visible, there was no vitreous haze, the intraocular pressure of the affected eye was stable at 17 mmHg and the corrected visual acuity had further improved to 20/60.

## Discussion
Fungal endophthalmitis is a rare but serious ocular infection that may occur following ocular trauma, surgery, or by hematogenous spread of fungal organisms, most commonly Candida species [1]. Its clinical presentation often mimics chronic uveitis, which may lead to delayed diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy. In this case, the isolated organism was Corniculantispora psalliotae, is known to infect insects and nematodes and has been reported in the United Kingdom and India. Certain species are utilized as biocontrol agents in agriculture. Human infections are exceedingly rare and typically occur in severely immunocompromised individuals. The fungus can potentially be introduced through inhalation or direct skin contact. However, due to its specific parasitic adaptations and the substantial differences between its natural hosts and the human internal environment, it has previously been considered non-pathogenic to humans [5, 6]. The isolation of C. psalliotae from vitreous biopsy and its identification by DNA sequencing confirmed the organism’s pathogenic role. This represents the first documented case of ocular infection by C. psalliotae.
A comprehensive literature review revealed only a few isolated reports of C. psalliotae being implicated in human infection, with no previous case reports involving ocular tissue. One previously reported case involved identification of Corniculantispora psalliotae in a patient who developed an infection following knee replacement surgery [7]. Fungal elements were isolated from a synovial fluid sample obtained during debridement. The patient underwent multiple drainage procedures and received antibiotic therapy, which resulted in clinical improvement. The authors hypothesized that direct exposure of the surgical wound to air may have facilitated fungal entry, and that broad-spectrum antibiotic use postoperatively may have suppressed normal bacterial flora, thereby allowing overgrowth of antibiotic-resistant fungi.
A limitation of our report is that histopathological examination of the excised lens was not performed. Demonstrating fungal elements within the lens capsule or cortex would have provided stronger pathological confirmation of intraocular spread from the anterior segmentIn another case series, Lecanicillium species (L. lecanii and L. attenuatum) were isolated from tissue samples obtained during orthopedic procedures [3, 4, 7]. These isolates were later attributed to a pseudo-outbreak caused by environmental contamination rather than true infection. Cryotherapy has been reported as a useful adjunct in managing recalcitrant or non-resolving fungal lesions [8–10]. Chen et al., in an experimental rabbit model, demonstrated that cryothermal treatment inhibited fungal proliferation by disrupting fungal cellular structures, as confirmed by scanning and transmission electron microscopy [8]. Their work showed that typical cryotherapy was effective in curing fungal corneal ulcers, with varying susceptibility among organisms — Candida albicans responding most favorably and Aspergillus fumigatus being relatively resistant.
Further, Pathangey and Kandaswamy et al. reported favorable outcomes when cryotherapy was applied as an adjunct to conventional antifungal therapies in cases of recalcitrant infection [9, 10]. The proposed mechanism is direct destruction of fungal elements in the treated area, reduction of fungal load, and improved host ability to control infection.
In our case, due to the poor initial therapeutic response and the presence of a cyclitic membrane, identified via ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), targeted cryotherapy was employed as an adjunct to antifungal treatment. Procedurally, cryotherapy was performed trans-sclerally using a double freeze–thaw technique, typically 1 mm from the limbus and extending one clock hour on either side of the affected uveal tissue. The treatment was applied in a contiguous manner to cover the involved area, while care was taken to avoid excessive application that could otherwise exacerbate inflammation or cause retinal breaks. By combining its antifungal effect with its ability to suppress fibrovascular proliferation, anterior retinal cryotherapy served as a valuable adjunct in our treatment strategy.
This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with rare pathogens such as Corniculantispora psalliotae. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for uncommon etiologies in cases of atypical endophthalmitis, particularly in patients who are unresponsive to conventional therapy.
