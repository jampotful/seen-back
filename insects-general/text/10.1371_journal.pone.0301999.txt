Abstract

3

To study how the nervous system processes visual information, experimenters must record

4

neural activity while delivering visual stimuli in a controlled fashion. In animals with a nearly

5

panoramic field of view, such as flies, precise stimulation of the entire visual field is

6

challenging. We describe a projector-based device for stimulation of the insect visual system

7

under a microscope. The device is based on a bowl-shaped screen that provides a wide and

8

nearly distortion-free field of view. It is compact, cheap, easy to assemble, and easy to operate

9

using open source software for stimulus generation. We describe the general principle and test

10

the system in a series of experiments measuring the membrane potentials of visual interneurons

11

in the optic lobe of Drosophila melanogaster.

13

Introduction

14

Neural responses are inherently variable. The visual system is no exception in this respect, as

15

repeated presentations of the same stimulus tend to elicit different neural responses. To

16

decipher the influences of visual stimulation on neuronal responses, experimenters must be

17

able to deliver light in a controllable and reliable manner. An ideal visual stimulation device

18

would provide precise temporal and spatial control over every retinal photoreceptor cell. In

19

recent years, research on the visual system of insects, in particular that of Drosophila

20

melanogaster, has produced a rich collection of stimulation devices that take many shapes and

21

forms. Fueled by technological advances, most of them either repurpose devices originally

22

designed for the human visual system [1–5], or are custom-made from highly specialized

23

components [6–9] rendering their implementation almost prohibitively expensive. To tailor a

24

stimulation device to the demands of an insect's visual system, while keeping it versatile,

25

compact and affordable, poses a formidable technical challenge.

26
27

In contrast to the human eye, the compound eye of Drosophila is tiled by hundreds of

28

ommatidia arranged in a hexagonal grid. Each ommatidium of the convex retina collects light

29

from a visual angle of approximately 5° [10] using a miniature lens with an aperture of 16 µm

30

and a focal length of 20 µm [11,12]. These dimensions, for the most part, obviate the need to

31

accommodate, as any object beyond a few millimeters appears at infinity focus. With an inter-

32

ommatidial angle of ~5°, the spatial resolution of Drosophila is low, but the convex geometry

33

of its eyes provides for a nearly panoramic field of view [13,14].

34

Dynamic visual environments for head-restrained insects that allow experimenters to record

35

behavioral and neural responses to visual stimuli are commonplace in insect neuroethology

36

[15]. Relicts of long-gone manufacturing constraints, most displays used to create these virtual

37

environments have either a flat or a cylindrical geometry neither of which matches the shape

38

of the fly eye. Hence, they all bear at least three caveats: First, they cover only a small fraction

39

of the fly's field of view. Second, the brightness of the display varies across visual space as a

40

function of the viewing angle; and third, distortions in the periphery of the visual field

41

complicate the correspondence between actuator (pixels) and sensor (ommatidia) (Fig 1A–1C).

42

While the latter two can be compensated for by computationally costly pre-adjustment and pre-

43

distortion the displayed images, the first one is unavoidable.

44
45

Here, we describe an inexpensive projector-based visual stimulation device that caters to many

46

of the demands of the insect visual system. It provides a wide and nearly distortion-free field

47

of view with uniform brightness, but is compact enough to fit under a microscope. The crux of

48

our system lies in its unconventional screen geometry, which simplifies both software and

49

hardware development. Our open-source Python code allows any user with access to a

50

computer and a 3D printer to create a bowl-shaped screen that suites the specifications (i.e. the

51

throw ratio) of their projector. The temporal, spatial and spectral performance of the system

52

can be adjusted, according to experimental requirements and budget constraints, by resorting

53

to different commercially available projectors. We provide Python code to generate the screen

54

design and to export it for 3D printing, but also to display a wide repertoire of visual stimuli

55

commonly used by researchers, teachers, and students that study the insect visual system.

Comparing screen geometries

59
60

We use a polyhedron as a simplified and general model of an invertebrate compound eye as a

61

spherical approximation. As a simplified and general model of an invertebrate compound eye,

62

we use a polyhedron as a spherical approximation. The screen capture from each projected

63

polyhedron face can be understood as the number of projector pixels required to stimulate a

64

certain fraction of the visual field, for example, one ommatidium or one spatial receptive field

65

of a neuron. To allow direct comparison of different screen geometries, a Goldberg

66

polyhedron—containing 272 almost identical faces—served as a model observer in front of a

67

flat, a cylindrical, or a bowl-shaped virtual surface.

Observing the grid of a polyhedral projected onto a flat surface (Fig 1A), only the hexagon at

69

the center of the visual field appeared undistorted. With increasing distance from the center,

70

the hexagons grew rapidly in surface area and were increasingly distorted. At an angular

71

distance of 50° from the center, hexagonal projections were on average 3.3 times longer and

72

1.9 times wider, after correcting for the hexagon's original aspect ratio; their area increased by

73

a factor of 5.5. On a cylindrical surface (Fig 1B), hexagons remained undistorted along the

74

equator but were subject to heavy distortions along the elevation axis. At an angular distance

75

of 50° from the center, the average hexagon grew by a factor of 2.9 in elevation and 1.6 in

76

azimuth. The area of a hexagonal projection increased by an average factor of 4.6. The bowl-

77

shaped surface (Fig 1C) featured no distortion along the elevation and only slight azimuthal

78

distortions in the periphery of the screen. At an elevation angle of 50° from the pole, the

79

azimuthal distortion factor amounted to 1.2; at 100° from the pole, this factor was 2.4. The area

80

of the hexagonal projection increased by the same factor of 1.2 at 50° and 2.4 at 100° from the

81

center. Aside from robustness against distortions, a bowl-shaped screen features another

82

advantage: It covers a substantially larger fraction of the visual field compared to a flat or a

83

cylindrical screen of the same surface area. A cylindrical shape would require 44% of the area

84

to cover the same visual field as a flat screen. The bowl-shaped screen can stimulate the same

85

field of view with only 24% of the screen surface.

Screen design and evaluation

87
88

Our display system consists of an image projector and a screen. Like in any virtual reality

89

system, the image must be pre-distorted in order to be accurately perceived from the position

90

of the observer. Both the observer and the projector are stationary, which allows us to account

91

for any perspectival distortions in a single step. The correction is not based on a

92

computationally intensive software solution, as is usually the case, but on a hardware solution

93

that uses a specific, pre-calculated screen geometry at no computational costs. To determine

94

the ideal shape, we created simplified models of both the observer and the projector. A

95

spherical model with a polar grid served as an approximation of the compound eye, where the

96

optical axis of each ommatidium was represented by a radial vector connecting the center of

97

the sphere with the center of the respective face. Taking advantage of the rotational symmetry

98

of the sphere, we reduced the model of the observer to a two-dimensional cross section

99

(Fig 1D I) (Equation 1). The projector model was simplified to the same extent by considering

100

only two dimensions: one along the optical axis of the projector and a second one along the

101

elevation (Fig 1D). Commercially available projectors are designed to project images with

102

uniform pixel size and uniform luminance onto a planar screen (Fig 1D II) (Equation 2). To

103

guarantee uniform brightness across the visual field of the insect, it is necessary to maintain a

104

constant ratio of pixels (photons) per ommatidium of the convex retina (Fig 1D I). A precise

105

correspondence between computer-generated pixels and the retinotopy of the insect visual

106

system can only be established if this ratio is constant across the field of view. We implemented

107

this by intersecting the optical axis vector of each ommatidium along the elevation plane with

108

the modeled photon vector originating at the projector. The trajectory connecting the resulting

109

intersection points defined the screen curvature along the elevation (Fig 1D) (Equation 3). The

110

curvature of the screen along the azimuth was obtained by rotating the trajectory about the

111

polar axis and interconnecting the trajectories (Equation 4). The resulting concave surface was

112

predicted to be of uniform brightness, from the insect's point of view, provided that the screen

113

surface features incidence angle-independent diffuse Lambertian reflectance (Fig 1D III). To

114

achieve this property and to avoid artefacts caused by a cascade of specular reflection (Fig 2A),

115

we coated the inner surface of the 3D-printed screen with different combinations of white paint

116

and varnish. In order to test the optical properties of the surface, we illuminated square spots

117

(5° × 5°) along the elevation and recorded the reflected light with a panoramic 360° camera

118

(Fig 2B and 2C). One of these combinations showed almost no reflection along the elevation

119

and was used in all subsequent experiments (Fig 2B and 2C).

123

124

125

Stimulus design and validation

142
143

In order to create an immersive, panoramic virtual reality for the animal, we take the insect

144

observer to be at the center of a fictive unit sphere from where it observes an equidistant

145

azimuthal projection of the sphere. Owing to the special geometry of the screen, this projection

146

is—conveniently— identical to the image that must be displayed by the projector (Fig 1F). In

147

other words, the screen geometry allows the direct projection of a spherical texture. The

148

equidistant azimuthal projection is formally equivalent to a polar-transformed version of an

149

equi-rectangular projection, the latter of which might be more familiar to the reader, for

150

example, as a Cartesian map of the Earth. These interpretable equi-rectangular projections

151

served as spherical textures and were chosen as the input format. In order to avoid subsequent

152

interpolation, the projectors polar coordinate system was transformed into the cartesian

153

coordinate system of the input texture using two pre-calculated transformation matrices: one

154

for the x- and another for the y-coordinate of each pixel. The size of each matrix, and therefore

155

the runtime, depends on the spatial resolution of the projector. Since the output image size, the

156

transformation matrices, and the input image size were known (and constant) during runtime,

157

we used just-in-time compilation within the JAX (Google) framework to calculate the

158

transformations on the computer's graphics processing unit (GPU). On a standard laptop with

159

a GTX1060 (Nvidia) graphics card, the transformation of a color image of 1280 × 720 pixels

160

took on average 2-3 ms. Ahead of the transformation, to speed up processing times, equi-

161

rectangular representations were cropped to the field of view covered by the screen (here, 180°

162

× 125° in azimuth and elevation, respectively) and stimuli with a rotational component were

163

pre-rotated (Fig 3B). To illuminate only the screen and to minimize stray light, a clipping mask

164

was applied, which could be adapted to incorporate additions to the setup, such as time-stamp

165

signals or cameras for monitoring behavior.

170

171

172

173

174

176

177

the accuracy of the transformation and the correct, undistorted display of images on the 3D-

180

Experimental validation

192
193

The large, nearly distortion-free field of view and the uniform illumination of the bowl-shaped

194

screen make it possible to stimulate the visual system in an identical manner at various spatial

195

positions. This is essential, for instance, when spatiotemporal receptive fields of neurons at

196

different spatial locations in the visual system are to be measured precisely. Before testing our

197

bowl-shaped screen on real neurons, we measured the receptive field of a reference photodiode

198

placed at different locations in the arena by projecting a 60 Hz spatiotemporal binary white-

199

noise stimulus onto our screen. We cross-correlated the photodiode signal with the luminance

200

at each screen position (Fig 4A) and corrected the resulting equirectangular image of the

201

receptive field by rotating the position to the undistorted center of the cylindrical texture (Fig

202

4B). The result demonstrates that the perspective correction yields identical receptive field

203

sizes, even in cases where the receptive field is located more than 50° off the center. The

204

occurring deviations of about 8% were below the resolution of the binary white noise grid

205

(Fig 4C).

After the technical part, the biological validation was performed using in vivo patch clamp

208

recordings of medulla intrinsic Mi9 neurons in tethered flies. First, we presented a 300-s binary

209

white noise stimulus with a solid angle resolution of 2.8 degrees. The spatial center of mass of

210

each receptive field was determined and corrected by inverse rotation, whereby the largest

211

distance from the center was 38° in azimuth and 41° in elevation. We estimated the time-

212

averaged spatial receptive fields at two different time intervals (0 to 0.1 s and 0.2 to 0.6 s) and

213

the temporal linear kernel at the center of the spatial fields (Fig 5A). The full width at half

214

maximum was ~10° (–3.5° to +6.5°) in azimuth and ~10° (–5.5 to +4.5°) in elevation, along

215

both time intervals, and is consistent with previous measurements [17]. The temporal linear

216

kernel shows a strong negative contrast correlation with a kernel density of ~–7 at 0.05 s and a

217

weak positive correlation a with kernel density of ~1 at 0.57 s, which corresponds to a weak

218

bandpass characteristic (Fig 5A). In addition, we tested a battery of commonly used visual

219

stimuli: square-wave gratings and bright and dark edges moving at various velocities. The

220

responses to moving gratings showed the expected frequency- and velocity-depended

221

amplitude modulation (Fig 5B). The responses to moving bright and dark edges were aligned

222

based on the location of the receptive fields position (Fig 5C). They show a mirror symmetric

223

negative step response with respect to the stimulation contrast. In summary, the membrane

224

potentials recorded from Mi9 neurons of flies stimulated using the bowl-shaped screen are

225

consistent with those from an already established stimulation device [17,18].

Discussion

228
229

The screen geometry of visual stimulation devices has important implications for the

230

acquisition, the analysis, and the interpretation of functional data from the visual system. In

231

many aspects, a cylindrical surface is superior to a flat one, but observer-related perspective

232

distortions still remain. Super Bowl provides a virtual spherical texture, displayed on a

233

spherical screen, virtually devoid of any projection-related distortions, which are otherwise

234

difficult to overcome. The bowl-shaped screen geometry allows for a larger part of the visible

235

space to be covered with far smaller distortions than in commonly used geometries.

All large system components, including the screen, can be 3D printed. The overall cost of the

238

system depends on the type of projector used, which gives scientists a maximum of cost control.

239

The system is compact and allows for recording neural responses under a microscope as well

240

as for behavioral experiments. (Fig 1E and 6A). The distance of the projector and the size of

241

the screen can be adjusted to the needs of the experiment. Thus, a complete immersive virtual

242

environment can be created (Fig 6A). Alternatively, if required, a subset within the field of

243

view can be stimulated selectively (Fig 6B). The system was designed to be easy to

244

manufacture and assemble by scientists. The provided software generates vector graphic files

245

that are readable by a broad range of programs and can be used as a design basis for 3D printing

246

and laser cutting. The processes of filling and painting the screen require neither special

247

equipment nor special practical skills. Projectors with identical throw ratios can be exchanged

248

seamlessly, making it easy to incorporate the latest advances in spatial and temporal resolution.

249

It should be mentioned, however, that the matte Lambertian surface is crucial for operation and

250

the brightness distribution.

The included software for stimulus generation allows researchers and students to create visual

253

be generated based on input from a game engine, a video, or a simple numeric array. The

254

255

transformation allow for the system to be used in closed-loop virtual reality settings. The self-

256

257

258

260

Projector-based systems are compatible even with noise-sensitive electrophysiological

261

experiments such as whole-cell patch clamp recordings (Fig 5). The only local source of

262

electromagnetic noise, the power supply unit or the projector itself, can be easily shielded.

263

Some projectors can be operated in battery-powered mode, increasing the versatility even

264

further. Additionally, measuring spatiotemporal receptive fields in electrophysiological

265

experiments is difficult in flat and cylindrical arenas. Receptive fields located in the periphery

266

of the screen tend to be distorted and overestimated in size (Fig 1A and 1B) and moreover,

267

because of their proximity to the edge, they often do not fully capture the surround of the

268

receptive field. Therefore, it is common practice to include in the analysis primarily neurons

269

with a receptive field at the center of the screen, which introduces a spatial picking bias and

270

reduces the amount of analyzable data.

271
272

To investigate mechanisms of contrast normalization, especially the contribution of the visual

273

surround on a neuron’s receptive field [18–20], it is necessary to control luminance and contrast

274

precisely across as much of the visual surround as possible. While the brightness of rear-

275

projection screens [1,3,9] and conventional LED displays [5] depends on the viewing angle

276

and requires adjustment at the cost of dynamic range, the bowl-shaped screen features nearly

277

uniform brightness and only minimal perspective distortions. This allows for precisely

278

measuring receptive fields of visual interneurons without the need to correct them post hoc, as

279

is the case when using cylindrical or flat arenas.

280
281

282

possible field of view and as little distortion as possible, in order to fully understand its

283

neurobiology. Although spherical wide-field displays have been used in behavioral

3D modeling and printing

310
311

The screen shape along the elevation axis was calculated using a custom written script in

312

Python v.3.7 (Python Software Foundation), using NumPy v.1.21.5, SymPy v.1.10.1, and

313

Cairo v.1.16.0, based on the projector- and user-dependent input variables, such as distance,

314

throw ratio, resolution, and field of view. Here, we specified a field of view ranging from 15°

315

to 140° along the elevation axis. The automatically generated scalable vector graphics (SVG)

316

file was imported into the computer-aided design program SketchUp (Trimble), where a 180°

317

rotational extrusion along the optical axis of the path generated the final screen shape as a 3D

318

surface mesh. After adding mounting connections to the drawing, a surface tessellation lattice

319

(STL) file was exported for 3D printing. The screen was printed from black polylactide using

320

fused filament fabrication on a Replicator 3D printer (MakerBot). Where a 3D printer is not

321

available, online services such as Hubs (www.hubs.com), Materialise (www.materialise.com),

322

Shapeways (www.shapeways.com), or Sculpteo (www.sculpteo.com) can be used to create the

323

screen at competitive prices. Grooves in between filaments were filled using 2K General

324

Purpose Body Filler (3M) to create a smooth texture. The surface was sanded and painted with

325

three layers of white paint (585036, Dupli Color). To reduce specular reflection and associated

326

artifacts, a final layer of matte varnish (Matt varnish 850, Marabu) was applied which is a

327

critical step to achieve near-Lambertian reflective properties.

Stimulus generation

342
343

A custom build Python software was used to generate and project the stimuli, as well as to

344

control the timing. For ease of use, the creation of stimuli is mainly done using the NumPy

345

v.1.21.5 Python package, for rotating textures a “just in time compiled” function was written

346

using the JAX package, to increase performance. As mentioned above, the projection function

347

was also written using the JAX package. Software based image processing and presentation

348

was realized using the OpenCV v.4.6.0 package. Timing was realized using the Python build

349

in time module. After initialization, the software operates in a loop, which is run periodically

350

with a stimulus dependent rate of 80 to 120 frames per second. Within each iteration, the

351

elapsed time is determined and, based on this, a rotation or recalculation of the input texture is

352

executed. The resulting texture is then projected, masked and displayed on the projector. This

353

procedure continues until the stop time point is reached. The entire procedure can be processed

354

in one thread; multithreading is not necessary, but would lead to additional speed-up. For

355

closed-loop experiments, behavioral feedback can also be implemented in the stimulus

356

generation or rotation pipeline.

357
358

The white noise stochastic stimulus used to determine the receptive fields, is updated online at

359

60 frames per second. The individual pixels correspond to a visual solid angle on a virtual equi-

360

rectangular cylindrical texture. For example, using a solid angle of 5°, the field of view of the

361

arena was divided into 36 × 28 pixels grid and the updating stimulus was displayed over 8

362

minutes. The intensities of the individual pixels were drawn randomly and were either 0% or

363

100% of contrast. Using a sliding reverse correlation, the impact of each stimulating pixel on

364

the signal could be reconstructed. Each reconstructed image thus corresponds to an equidistant

365

cylindrical projection of the receptive field.

Evaluation of screen geometries

380
381

To visualize the effect of screen geometry on the ratio of pixels per ommatidium, we generated

382

computer-aided designs of a flat [2–4], a cylindrical [1,5], and a bowl-shaped screen (this

383

paper). In place of the insect, we added a Goldberg polyhedron model of the observer. The

384

polyhedron contained 260 hexagonal faces of equal area (±1%) and, like any Goldberg

385

polyhedron, 12 pentagonal faces, each of which with an area of approximately 66% of that of

386

the largest hexagon. On average, one hexagonal face covered a solid angle of 14°, equivalent

387

to the angle covered by ~9 ommatidia of Drosophila melanogaster, a necessary approximation,

388

given that Goldberg polyhedron cannot assume any arbitrary number of faces. Twenty of the

389

hexagonal faces were regular, the rest were irregular hexagons with aspect ratios ranging from

390

0.87 to 1.07.

391
392

The edges of the faces were projected radially onto the screen surfaces, using the open-source

393

physically-based render engine LuxCoreRender (https://luxcorerender.org). The resulting

394

renderings were vectorized and analyzed in Python v.3.7.13. Hexagonal projections were

395

color-coded based on their screen surface area relative to that of a central, undistorted

396

projection. To allow for a rigorous comparison between the different structures, a field of view

397

covering 105° in azimuth and elevation was considered for all screen shapes (Fig1A, B, C).

398

For visualization purposes, we used an orthographic view of the flat screen and a flattened

399

orthographic projection of the cylindrical screen. Additional perspective distortions that would

400

occur in the cylindrical setting under real projection conditions were neglected. The projection

401

of the bowl-shaped screen was already a perspective projection, which did not require

402

additional image distortion.

Patch-clamp recordings

415
416

Whole cell in vivo patch clamp recordings were performed as recently described [17]. In this

417

procedure, female flies were cold-anesthetized 2-24 hours after hatching and fixed with soft

418

thermoplastic wax on a special polyoxymethylene mount. The left dorsal optic nerve was

419

exposed by surgical removal of the cuticle, adipose tissue, and trachea. Green fluorescent

420

somata were visually identified by bright-field and epifluorescence microscopy. Using a small

421

incision in the perineural sheath, access was provided to the cell membranes. Signals were

422

recorded at room temperature (21-23°C) using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier, low-pass filtered,

423

and sampled at 10 kHz. The data were corrected for the liquid junction potential and analyzed

424

using custom-written Python software. The resting potential was determined to be the most

425

negative membrane potential. Cells with a resting membrane voltage greater than -25 mV were

426

excluded.
