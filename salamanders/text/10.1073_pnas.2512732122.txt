# Subterranean salamanders lean into mechanical sense following vision loss
Extreme environments pose unique challenges to living organisms—think intense heat and aridity of many deserts, freezing temperatures of polar seas, high temperature and excessive pressure of hydrothermal vents, and the perpetual darkness and scarce resources of underground caves. The wide array and novelty of evolutionary responses of organisms found in those environments may seem bizarre to us as humans, but adaptation is not a beauty contest; it is only survival that matters. In effect, extreme environments are laboratories for evolutionary experimentation in which the same natural experiment is repeated over and over by both closely related and distantly related taxa and on the same and different continents. Moreover, analysis of such systems can yield important insights into basic evolutionary patterns and processes that are common to organisms generally (1–4). Subterranean aquifers and the many air- and water-filled cavities associated with them have served as a particularly fruitful substrate for evolutionary studies of animals, including fishes, salamanders, and a variety of invertebrates (5, 6). In response to the complete absence of light, cold temperatures, and limited food resources due to lack of photosynthesis, a taxonomically wide range of cave-adapted animals, or troglobionts, have converged on a suite of shared phenotypes. Yet, the detailed historical pattern of cave adaptation by which such phenotypes evolved is known for few if any of these groups, and an understanding of how underlying genetic and developmental mechanisms were perturbed to effect such change is known for even fewer. In this issue of PNAS, Tovar et al. report a comprehensive analysis of evolutionary adaptation to belowground cave environments by Texas Eurycea, salamanders that have undergone extensive diversification in species number, adult anatomy, and ontogenetic development (7). Their study is highlighted by the serendipitous discovery of novel tissue expression of a regulatory gene typically associated with eye development, which in turn reveals a fascinating instance of sensory compensation between the visual system and lateral line mechanoreceptors that accompanied the evolution of cave adaptation (Fig. 1).

Traditionally, cave adaptation has been viewed as an instance of regressive evolution, in which ancestral structures are reduced, simplified, or even lost over time (8). Characteristic features of troglobionts—the two most conspicuous ones are reduction or loss of both external pigmentation and eyes—were assumed to be the result of unidirectional change from a non-cave-adapted ancestor; one, for example, with fully developed eyes and robust pigmentation. The study by Tovar et al., however, benefits from analytical, technical and statistical advances in comparative methods. Such methods include those that rigorously assess phylogenetic relationships among populations and species based on gene-sequence data (thereby avoiding the potentially misleading results based on morphology); infer the amounts, rates, and directions of morphological character change from a “reconstructed” ancestral state(s); and estimate the degree of character correlation vs. independence during an evolutionary transition. With such methodology, one no longer assumes unidirectional, regressive evolution. Instead, this assumption becomes a testable hypothesis. Tovar and colleagues also apply powerful technology for three-dimensional digital imaging of ocular anatomy and take advantage of the ongoing development of ever-more-facile procedures for studying the embryonic development of nonmodel organisms to quantify evolution of the lens and retina during cave adaptation and begin to assess the genetic basis of observed changes.
Tovar et al. focus on a closely related group of salamanders, clade Paedomolge, within the larger North American genus Eurycea. The 15 species are restricted geographically to the Edwards-Trinity aquifer system of west-central Texas, where they occur mostly as discrete populations found in belowground caves or surface spring habitats (9, 10). Unlike many salamander species in which a free-living, aquatic larval stage metamorphoses into a terrestrial adult, nearly all Paedomolge retain a larval morphology throughout life and are permanently aquatic. Several surface populations and species have fully developed skin pigmentation and well-developed eyes—the surface ecotype—while obligately subterranean species all exhibit the suite of features—the subterranean ecotype—that is typically associated with cave adaptation: reduced pigmentation, vestigial eyes, etc. Importantly, remaining species are somewhat intermediate between these two extremes, comprising both surface and subterranean populations that vary extensively in the degree to which they exhibit troglobiont features. While all subterranean populations are ultimately derived from non-cave-adapted surface salamanders, the former represent a series of repeated, independent colonizations of subterranean habitats, although the exact number, relative timing, and sequence of colonizations have not been known with confidence. Together, these features make Paedomolge a nearly ideal system for testing hypotheses regarding the selective and genetic bases of cave adaptation, for revealing the possible limits to evolutionary parallelism and convergence, and for documenting the way that organisms overcome physiological challenges posed by cave environments.

The phylogenetic results of Tovar et al. confirm with high statistical confidence the repeated invasion of subterranean environments by surface salamanders: Cave-adapted phenotypes were acquired independently at least six times. Moreover, this evolutionary change occurred relatively recently—within the past few million years. There is, however, considerable morphological variation among these troglobionts, and by applying “stochastic character mapping” (11), a statistical technique for quantifying the frequency of change in character state along branches of a phylogeny, the study documents numerous instances of morphological convergence and reversal. In fact, separate instances of reversal in two species resulted in the reacquisition of metamorphosis and the ancestral biphasic life history in some individuals.
The most exciting discovery reported by Tovar et al. is parallel sensory compensation between the visual system and the mechanoreceptive lateral line during the evolution of cave adaptation in Texas Eurycea (“parallel,” in that the same basic phenomenon is shared by the several lineages of troglobionts that independently evolved from surface forms). Sensory compensation is a well-known phenomenon in neuroscience in which the loss or impairment of one sensory modality leads to the development of heightened acuity in a remaining sense (12). While widespread among animals, sensory compensation has been studied mostly during the ontogeny of a given species and largely in a biomedical context. Although augmentation of particular sensory modalities as adaptations for specialized environments characterizes the evolution of many animal taxa, few studies evaluate sensory compensation among closely related populations or species (13–15).
Tovar et al.’s study may represent the first detailed investigation of the evolution of sensory compensation at the species level within vertebrates, although this was not their original intent. Their morphological analysis had revealed significant differences in eye, lens, and retina size between surface and subterranean ecotypes, as well as variation in these dimensions among species within each ecotype. In the blind cavefish, Astyanax mexicanus, eye reduction and loss in cave-adapted populations is associated with downregulation of the paired-box 6 gene (pax6) during embryonic development (16), but when Tovar et al. assessed pax6 labeling in the developing eyes of Eurycea embryos, they saw no significant difference between surface and subterranean ecotypes. There was, however, unexpected pax6 labeling in neuromasts of the anterior lateral line (ALL), a chain of mechanoreceptors that extends along the surface of the head of many fishes and larval amphibians, and the labeling was much more intense in the subterranean ecotype. This led Tovar et al. to count the number of ALL neuromasts at 1-mo postoviposition, and subterranean animals had significantly more neuromasts than surface animals.
The lateral line is a mechanosensory system found in all fishes and larval amphibians. It is typically resorbed during amphibian metamorphosis but is retained in some species that remain aquatic as adults (17). By functioning to detect water currents and other mechanical stimuli in aquatic environments, including those generated by potential prey and predators, the lateral line system confers an important sensory ability—a “sixth sense”—that is lacking in terrestrial vertebrates. The neuromast, its primary functional component, converts mechanical vibrations to nerve impulses that are then directed to the brain. Tovar et al. document that eye reduction and lateral line augmentation evolved together in multiple lineages that independently colonized subterranean habitats. Moreover, they suggest that evolutionary increase in the number of neuromasts in troglobionts may be a direct response to the lack of a visual sense, with consequent reduction and ultimately loss of eyes, in permanently dark cave environments. This combination of both regressive and constructive adaptations, along with similar findings by other authors (1, 18, 19), defies the traditional view of cave adaptation as entirely regressive and unidirectional. Interestingly, the possibility of mechanosensory augmentation during cave adaptation by Texas Eurycea was likely enhanced by loss of the ancestral biphasic life history and adoption of a permanently larval adult morphology at the base of the Paedomolge clade, given that the lateral line is typically absent following metamorphosis. Such instances of paedomorphosis, in which late-appearing stages of an ancestor are lost from the ontogeny of its descendants, are a dominant feature of animal evolution but are especially prominent in salamanders (20).
