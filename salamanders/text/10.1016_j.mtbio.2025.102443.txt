Axolotl-derived decellularized skin ECM as a pro-regenerative scaffold for attenuating fibrotic wound healing.
Wang MH, Kuan CH, Ma H, Cheng NC. (2025). Materials today. Bio. . doi:10.1016/j.mtbio.2025.102443. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Axolotl-derived decellularized skin ECM as a pro-regenerative scaffold for attenuating fibrotic wound healing

## Abstract
AbstractConventional wound healing in mammals often results in fibrosis, leading to permanent scarring and compromised skin function. Inspired by the remarkable regenerative capacity of axolotls, we developed an axolotl skin decellularized extracellular matrix (A-dECM) as a pro-regenerative scaffold for mammalian wound healing. A-dECM retained its native ECM composition, including a glycosaminoglycan-rich and collagen-dense profile, while effectively eliminating cellular and immunogenic components, as confirmed by histological staining and DNA quantification. Whole scanning electron microscopy revealed a highly organized ultrastructure in intact A-dECM. To improve biocompatibility, potentially cytotoxic ∼40 kDa protein fractions were removed from A-dECM via size-exclusion chromatography, and the fractionated A-dECM promoted enhanced proliferation of HaCaT keratinocytes. In a murine burn model, A-dECM significantly enhanced re-epithelialization, dermal remodeling, and collagen organization compared to murine dECM and PBS controls. Moreover, A-dECM reduced expression of fibrotic markers (α-SMA, TXNDC5), suppressed inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α), and inhibited M1 macrophage polarization. These molecular changes correlated with improved restoration of key skin features, including epidermal rete ridge formation and partial hair follicle regeneration. This work demonstrates that decellularized axolotl ECM can guide mammalian tissue regeneration, highlighting its potential as an evolution-inspired biomaterial for clinical translation.Keywords: Axolotl, Decellularized extracellular matrix, Fibrosis, Wound healing, Immunomodulation

## Introduction
Human wound healing often culminates in fibrotic scar formation, with significant functional and psychosocial consequences [1,2]. Millions of patients annually develop scarring following trauma or surgery, despite the use of tension-free suturing, silicone dressings, and fractional lasers, no existing therapy reliably restores normal skin architecture in adult humans [1]. While scarless regeneration is elusive in adult mammals, fetal skin wounds heal with minimal or no visible scar, a capacity attributed to a unique wound environment characterized by reduced inflammation, elevated hyaluronic acid, rapid fibronectin deposition, and a distinct ECM composition [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Fetal mammals exhibit minimal immune cell infiltration and reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines during healing [[7], [8], [9]]. This regenerative capacity is progressively lost during the third trimester, as the immune system matures and fibrotic signaling pathways begin to dominate [3,5]. The fetal wound ECM displays elevated levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) relative to their natural inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), resulting in a high MMP-to-TIMP ratio, which facilitates controlled matrix remodeling and prevents excessive scar tissue accumulation [4,5,7,10]. Furthermore, fetal fibroblasts demonstrate resistance to myofibroblast differentiation, a cellular process central to wound contraction and scar formation. This resistance has been attributed to reduced responsiveness to transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling and to the presence of a softer, less fibrotic ECM environment in fetal skin [3,7,11,12].
Scarless healing in certain species demonstrates that fibrosis is not an inevitable outcome of injury. These regenerative models exhibit wound microenvironment that differ fundamentally from those found in adult mammals. In many of these species, tissue repair takes place within the context of an attenuated immune response. For instance, adult African spiny mice (Acomys cahirinus) are capable of regenerating large skin wounds, with minimal fibrosis and restoration of dermal appendages [13]. The axolotl (Ambystoma mexicanum) presents one of the most remarkable examples of adult regenerative healing, exhibiting the ability to heal full-thickness skin wounds and regenerate limbs without fibrotic scarring [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Axolotls exhibit limited neutrophil and macrophage infiltration following injury, and the resulting muted inflammatory phase supports tissue preservation and regenerative remodeling [16,18]. In contrast, adult mammalian wounds typically trigger a robust immune response, which precedes fibroblast activation and fibrotic ECM deposition. This process establishes a self-sustaining cascade that perpetuates scar formation [[19], [20], [21]].
The ECM is increasingly recognized not only as a structural scaffold but also as a dynamic microenvironment that modulates cellular responses during tissue repair [[22], [23], [24]]. In regenerating axolotl skin, ECM components such as tenascin-C, decorin, and specific collagen isoforms are expressed, and these molecules are associated with tissue patterning and suppression of myofibroblast activation [15,[24], [25], [26]]. Amphibian ECM is also enriched in heparan sulfate proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), which regulate the availability of growth factors and cellular behaviors important for morphogenesis [[25], [26], [27]]. These cues, together with a permissive immune environment, create a regenerative niche that favors enhanced tissue organization and reduced fibrotic scarring.
Native tissue ECM has been shown to regulate the recruitment and polarization of immune cells, thereby modulating inflammation responses and fibrotic progression [18,19]. Macrophages, in particular, respond to ECM stiffness and composition, which influence their polarization toward either a pro-inflammatory (M1) or pro-regenerative (M2) phenotypes [4,19,28]. In addition to immune cells, the ECM also interacts with fibroblasts, regulating their differentiation into myofibroblasts through the integration of mechanical cues and biochemical signals such as TGF-β and integrin-mediated signaling [12,21]. In chronic or fibrotic wounds, aberrant ECM remodeling perpetuates a pathological cycle of inflammation, matrix disorganization, and functional impairment [12,20,21].
Decellularized ECM (dECM) biomaterials are designed to recapitulate key regenerative cues by preserving matrix architecture while eliminating immunogenic cellular components [29]. Clinically, dECM products derived from human or animal tissues, such as artificial dermis and porcine small intestinal submucosa, have demonstrated improved healing outcomes in burns and chronic wounds [30,31]. However, these matrices are typically sourced from scar-forming adult tissues and may lack key pro-regenerative cues necessary for optimal tissue regeneration [32,33]. Accordingly, there is a pressing need to identify alternative ECM sources that possess intrinsic scarless healing properties as next-generation regenerative biomaterials capable of promoting re-epithelialization, supporting organized collagen deposition, and suppressing myofibroblast activation, thus partially recapitulating features of fetal regenerative healing [10,15,34].
Besides dECM, a wide range of biomaterial-based approaches have recently been explored for scarless or scar-reduced wound management, including immunomodulatory and mechanoregulative hydrogels, mechanically tuned scaffolds, and bioactive composites that attempt to recreate regenerative microenvironments. For example, an immunomodulatory hydrogel capable of fibroblast subtype regulation could contribute to almost imperceptible scars in infected mouse skins and rabbit ear wound models [35]. Similarly, a mechanoregulative hydrogel with stage-adaptive control of the wound niche facilitated rapid scar-reduced healing [36]. It was emphasized that biomaterial-based mechanical regulation can even enable regeneration of skin appendages [37], and that immune-instructive scaffolds can reprogram wound healing toward regeneration [28]. Although these approaches seem promising, they often require synthetic modifications or delivery of exogenous factors. This highlights the rationale for exploring axolotl skin ECM (A-dECM), which derive from species with intrinsic scarless regenerative capacity, as an alternative source of regenerative cues. Unlike synthetic systems, A-dECM may intrinsically retain cues associated with reduced scarring, providing a compelling basis to investigate its therapeutic potential in mammalian wound repair.
Herein, we investigated the therapeutic potential of A-dECM as a bioactive scaffold for mitigating fibrosis and supporting regenerative wound healing in mammals. We developed and optimized a decellularization protocol that effectively removed cellular components while preserving the ultrastructural and biochemical integrity of A-dECM. Potentially harmful low-molecular-weight protein fractions in crude A-dECM were removed via size exclusion chromatography (SEC), resulting in a purified A-dECM product with improved biocompatibility. Using a murine burn model, we evaluated the regenerative efficacy of A-dECM in comparison with mouse skin dECM (M-dECM). A-dECM treatment led to accelerated wound closure, enhanced epidermal and dermal architecture, decreased inflammatory and fibrotic markers such as TXNDC5 and α-SMA, indicating attenuation of myofibroblast activation. Concurrently, A-dECM modulated the immune microenvironment by downregulating M1 macrophage markers and increasing M2-associated signals both in vitro and in vivo. These findings highlighted the dual antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects of A-dECM, supporting its potential as a next-generation regenerative biomaterial for mitigating fibrosis and promoting functional skin regeneration.

## Materials and methods

## Isolation and decellularization process
Animal procedures for skin tissue collection, approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of National Taiwan University College of Medicine (Approval No. 20220292), were conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines. Full-thickness dorsal skin tissues were surgically harvested from 6 to 10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, while full-thickness lateral tail skin was collected from 3-year-old male axolotls (Ambystoma mexicanum) under 0.1 % MS-222 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) anesthesia. Immediately after excision, the skin samples were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C. A portion of each sample was subsequently lyophilized for use as untreated controls in downstream assays, while the remaining tissues underwent decellularization process.
Decellularization of lyophilized skin was performed as previously described with slight modifications [33]. Briefly, rehydrated tissues were incubated in 0.25 % Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 12 h at room temperature, followed by three 30-min washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Samples were then treated with 1 % Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 12 h, washed again, and subsequently incubated with 500 U/ml DNase I (Qiagen, Germany) for 24 h. All steps were carried out at 4 °C on an orbital shaker, with 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) added to prevent microbial contamination. The resulting dECM samples were collected using sterile transfer pipettes. Following decellularization, the dECM samples were lyophilized and stored at −80 °C until use for further experiments (Fig. 1A).

## Decellularization verification

## Biochemical analysis
Biochemical characterization was performed using ∼10 mg of lyophilized skin and skin dECM from each sample. All measurements were normalized to dry weight. For collagen quantification, samples were digested in 1 ml of papain digestion buffer (125 μg/ml papain, 0.1 M sodium phosphate, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM cysteine HCl, pH 6.5) at 65 °C overnight. Total soluble collagen content was then measured using the Sircol™ Collagen Assay Kit (Biocolor, UK), with absorbance read at 540 nm and concentrations calculated from a standard curve (0–50 μg/ml soluble collagen). For glycosaminoglycan (GAG) quantification, samples were dissolved in 1 ml of sodium acetate–citric acid buffer, and GAG content was measured using the Blyscan™ GAG Assay Kit (Biocolor). Absorbance was measured at 656 nm, with chondroitin sulfate used to generate the standard curve. For DNA quantification, genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were measured by absorbance at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Scanning electron microscopic analysis of dECM
Lyophilized dECM samples were rehydrated in distilled water and subjected to a graded ethanol dehydration series. Dehydrated samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using carbon adhesive tape and sputter-coated with a 5 nm layer of gold using a Leica ACE600 sputter coater (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Images were obtained using a Hitachi X-650 microscope (Hitachi, Japan) under secondary electron imaging mode, with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, working distance of 12 mm, spot size 30, and magnification up to 5000 × .

## Skin dECM solubilization
Decellularized skins were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to remove any residual detergents. The cleaned tissues were then lyophilized and completely digested in a porcine pepsin solution (pH 2.0; Sigma-Aldrich) at a final concentration of 3 mg/ml. To neutralize the acidic dECM solution and irreversibly inactivate pepsin, 0.1 M NaOH was gradually added until the pH reached neutrality. The neutralized dECM solution was then subsequently filtered through 100 μm and 40 μm cell strainers (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) to remove residual tissue debris and insoluble impurities. Further purification was conducted using Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter units with a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MilliporeSigma) to achieve desalting and concentration. The final solubilized dECM was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further use.

## Size exclusion chromatography for A-dECM fractionation
SEC was performed using a Pharmacia C column (1.6 × 100 cm) packed with Sephacryl S-300 gel matrix (Cytiva, USA). The column was equilibrated by slowly adding Buffer A-150, allowing the gel matrix to fully settle until it occupied 70–80 % of the total column volume. Solubilized A-dECM was carefully loaded onto the column, with the sample volume not exceeding 3 % of the total column bed volume to ensure optimal resolution. The sample was gently dispensed along the inner wall of the column to minimize matrix disturbance. Elution was performed using Buffer A-150 at a constant flow rate of 0.1 ml/min, and 3 ml fractions were collected sequentially according to elution time. Protein concentrations in the collected fractions were quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with absorbance measured at 562 nm. Fractions containing the highest protein concentrations were selected for subsequent processing.

## HaCaT keratinocyte culture and proliferation assay
HaCaT keratinocytes (AddexBio, T0020001) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2. For in vitro experiments involving solubilized dECM, HaCaT cells were first seeded in complete DMEM for 24 h, followed by serum starvation in serum-free DMEM overnight. Solubilized dECM derived from axolotl or mouse skin, or collagen I, was added to serum-free medium at final concentrations of 50, 5, 0.5, or 0.05 μg/ml.
To assess the proliferative response, HaCaT cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured in the dECM-supplemented media for up to 7 days. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Alamar Blue assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 % (v/v) Alamar Blue reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence was measured at 560 nm excitation and 590 nm emission using a microplate reader (TECAN, Spark 10M, TECAN, Switzerland). Untreated cells cultured in serum-free medium served as the control group.

## In vivo burn wound model
All procedures related to the mouse burn wound model were approved by the IACUC of National Taiwan University College of Medicine under protocol number 20220292. Male C57BL/6 mice, aged between 6 and 8 weeks, were anaesthetized with isoflurane (1.5 %–2.5 %), and the dorsal skin was shaved, depilated, and cleaned using a commercial hair removal cream. Full-thickness thermal injuries were induced bilaterally away from the dorsal midline using an electronic empyrosis device (Bio-Cando Co., Ltd., Taiwan). A cylindrical metal rod (8 mm diameter), pre-heated to 100 °C, was applied to the skin surface for 8 s to create standardized deep dermal burns. Immediately after burn induction, wounds were gently cleansed with a 2 % chlorhexidine gluconate disinfectant solution (PBF Biotech Co., Taiwan) and mice were randomly assigned to three treatment groups. Each wound received a single intradermal/subdermal injection of 100 μl PBS (control), A-dECM, or M-dECM directly beneath the burn site. Injections were performed using a 29G insulin syringe and administered slowly over several seconds to minimize tissue damage. The selected injection volume (100 μl) was within the recommended limits for mice according to institutional animal care guidelines, which specify a maximum of ∼50 μl per site and a total maximum of 0.1–0.15 ml per 20–25 g mouse [38]. The entire burn-to-injection procedure was completed within 5–10 min per mouse. Treatments were performed once daily for five consecutive days (day 0–4 post-burn).
To maintain a moist wound environment, wounds were initially covered with a semi-occlusive dressing (Tegaderm; 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA). Because daily injections required dressing removal, dressings were replaced at each injection for the first 5 days, and subsequently changed every 2 days until day 14. Postoperative analgesia was provided with carprofen (5 mg/kg, s.c.) once daily for 5 days following burn induction.
Wound healing progression was monitored by standardized digital photography on days 0, 4, 7, 11, 14, 18, 21, 25, and 28. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software (version 2.1.0), and the percentage of residual wound area relative to the wound area on day 0 was calculated. In addition, wound healing on days 7, 14, and 21 was semi-quantitatively scored (0–4 scale) by four blinded investigators, based on wound discoloration and scab formation (Supplementary Table 1) [39].

## Histomorphometric analysis of wound tissues
The axolotl/mouse skin ECM and dECM samples, as well as mouse burn wound skin samples, were fixed overnight in 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin blocks were sectioned into 6-μm-thick slices for histological analysis. For skin ECM and dECM samples, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and 4′’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) to visualize general morphology and nuclear content. For murine skin tissues, consecutive sections were prepared perpendicular to the anterior-posterior axis and the wound surface. Sections were subjected to H&E and Masson’s trichrome (MT) staining. Regions near the center of the wound were selected for quantitative analysis of granulation tissue and fibrotic area. Re-epithelialization was quantified in H&E-stained sections by measuring the distance between the two epithelial tongues at the wound edges (epithelial gap) relative to the original wound width, and expressed as percentage closure using the formula: (original wound width-epithelial gap)/original wound width × 100. All histological images were captured using the EVOS M7000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed using ImageJ software (version 2.1.0).

## Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining
For both immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining, murine wound tissue sections were first deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval using Tris-EDTA buffer (95 °C for 1 h). After cooling to room temperature, the sections were washed three times with PBS.
For immunohistochemical staining, the slides were treated with a peroxidase block reagent (BioTnA Biotech, Kaohsiung, Taiwan) for 10 min, followed by incubation with a protein block solution (BioTnA, Taiwan) for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD68 (Abcam, ab125212), iNOS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA1-036), CD163 (Abcam, ab182422), K5 (Abcam, ab52635), K10 (Abcam, ab76318), and Loricrin (Elabscience, Wuhan, China; E-AB-92323). The next day, slides were washed and sequentially incubated with TnAlink Post Primary reagent (BioTnA) for 10 min and TnAlink Polymer (BioTnA) for 45 min. Chromogenic development was performed using diluted DAB substrate (BioTnA) for 2 min. Finally, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin for 15 s and rinsed three times with distilled water.
For immunofluorescence staining, after antigen retrieval and PBS washing, sections were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min and blocked with 3 % bovine serum albumin for 1.5 h. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against α-SMA (Abcam, ab7817), TXNDC5 (Proteintech, IL, USA, 19834-1-AP), and CD31 (Abcam, ab281583). After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h: Alexa Fluor 488 (green) and Alexa Fluor 568 (red). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (blue). Slides were washed again and mounted using Aqua Mount (BioTnA).
Both immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence images were acquired using an EVOS™ M7000 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Default LUT settings were applied (DAPI in blue, Alexa Fluor 488 in green, Alexa Fluor 568 in red). For each antibody, exposure time and gain were kept constant across all experimental groups. Brightness and contrast were adjusted linearly and applied uniformly without nonlinear enhancement. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 2.1.0).

## Dermal fibroblast culture
The adult human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs; ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA, SC-2300) are cultured in Fibroblast Medium (FM; ScienCell Research Laboratories) supplemented with 2 % FBS, 1 % fibroblast growth supplement (FGS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin solution. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 and 95 % air. To induce TGF-β1 stimulation in fibroblasts, HDFs were cultured in serum-free medium and treated with TGF-β1 (10 ng/ml; PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) for 48 h prior to downstream experiments.

## Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total mRNA was extracted from murine wound tissue harvested at 14 and 28 days post-burn using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) with mouse-derived cDNA templates. Gene expression was quantified using the CFX Manager™ software (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences used for qPCR analysis of mouse and human target genes are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was isolated from the wound tissues of different groups. Tissues were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Abcam), supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10 % SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked using blocking buffer (Abcam) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against collagen I (Col I; Abcam), TXNDC5 (Proteintech), α-SMA (Abcam), and GAPDH (GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA, GTX100118). Subsequently, the membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA, HAF018 and HAF008). Protein bands were visualized using the Western Lightning® Plus-ECL reagent (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) and imaged with the UVP BioSpectrum® 810 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (version 2.1.0).

## Macrophage polarization assay
Human monocytic cell line THP-1 was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10 % FBS (HyClone) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. Every three to five days, cells were passaged by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min) and resuspended in fresh medium at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2. For macrophage differentiation, THP-1 monocytes were seeded and treated with 10 ng/ml phorbol myristate acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h to induce M0 macrophage polarization. M1 macrophages were generated by stimulating M0 macrophages with 50 ng/ml interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and 15 ng/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h. To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of dECM, M0 macrophages were treated with either M-dECM or A-dECM, and macrophage phenotype distribution was subsequently analyzed. Moreover, to assess whether A-dECM can reverse M1 polarization and promote a shift toward the M2 phenotype, A-dECM was co-administered with IFN-γ and LPS during the 48-h M1 induction period at varying concentrations.
Inflammatory cytokine levels in macrophage culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacture’s instructions. THP-1-derived macrophages were cultured to approximately 90 % confluence and then stimulated with LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) in the presence of either M-dECM or A-dECM (50 μg/ml) for 48 h. Following stimulation, culture media were collected and analyzed for tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels.
To evaluate macrophage polarization, surface markers associated with M1 and M2 phenotypes were assessed by flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were harvested and washed twice with cold PBS. For PMA-induced adherent macrophages, detachment was performed using a cell scraper, followed by resuspension in BD™ Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, USA). For surface staining, cells were incubated separately with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD86-FITC (M1 marker; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA, 326506) and CD163-PE (M2 marker; BioLegend, 374204) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Each marker was analyzed in single-stained samples. After staining, cells were washed twice with Stain Buffer and resuspended in 500 μl of buffer for flow cytometric acquisition. Fluorescence data were acquired using a FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Unstained cells controls were used to set gating thresholds and eliminate non-specific binding.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple-comparisons test for group-wise comparisons, using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For all figures, statistical significance is consistently indicated by asterisks: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Comparisons without annotations were not statistically significant.

## Results

## Decellularization removes cells and retains the skin ECM
The effectiveness of skin tissue decellularization was validated by histological staining and DNA quantification. H&E staining and DAPI staining showed near-complete removal of cellular nuclei in both axolotl and mouse skin tissues (Fig. 1B). Quantitative analysis showed that DNA content was from 89.3 ± 20.0 to 0.4 ± 0.2 μg/mg in axolotl tissues, and from 37.6 ± 7.2 to 5.2 ± 1.6 μg/mg in mouse tissues (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C). Major ECM components were preserved after decellularization. Collagen levels in axolotl were 18.9 ± 2.3 μg/mg (native skin) and 21.7 ± 4.6 μg/mg (dECM), and in mouse, 8.2 ± 2.2 μg/mg and 8.4 ± 2.4 μg/mg, respectively, with no significant differences between native and decellularized tissues (Fig. 1D). Similarly, GAG content remained unchanged: 2.5 ± 0.3 vs. 2.6 ± 0.4 μg/mg in axolotl, and 0.8 ± 0.0 vs. 0.8 ± 0.1 μg/mg in mouse (Fig. 1E). Interspecies comparison revealed that axolotl tissues possessed higher baseline levels of DNA, collagen, and GAGs than mouse tissues (p < 0.05). SDS-PAGE analysis revealed a protein band around 40 kDa, present in native skin samples of both species, which was substantially diminished after decellularization (Fig. 1F). In the range of 15–40 kDa, A-dECM and M-dECM displayed distinct protein profiles. Scanning electron microscopy illustrated ultrastructural differences between species: M-dECM exhibited a dense, disordered fibrous network, whereas A-dECM showed a highly aligned and woven structure (Fig. 1G).

## Characterization of the fractionated A-dECM
To determine the optimal concentration of A-dECM for in vitro applications, dose–response assays were conducted using solubilized A-dECM at increasing concentrations. A-dECM promoted HaCaT proliferation at lower concentrations but resulted in a reduced proliferation index at 50 μg/ml. To examine whether specific components were associated with this concentration-dependent response, solubilized A-dECM was subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) to separate protein fractions by molecular weight (Fig. 2A). Individual SEC fractions were collected, quantified using BCA assay (μg/ml), desalted, and reconstituted in PBS (Fig. 2A). Fractions with protein concentrations exceeding 1000 μg/ml were selected, concentrated, and tested in HaCaT proliferation assays. HaCaT cells were cultured in low-serum medium (1 % FBS) supplemented with individual fractions at concentrations of 0.05, 0.5, 5, and 50 μg/ml (Supplementary Fig. 1). At 0.05 μg/ml, fraction No. 41 showed a lower proliferation index compared to other fractions. No significant differences were observed at 0.5 μg/ml. At 5 μg/ml, fraction No. 40 showed the lowest proliferation index. At 50 μg/ml, both unfractionated A-dECM and fraction No. 40 resulted in marked reductions in proliferation, while fraction No. 41 showed a moderate decrease. In contrast, fractions No. 42–49 maintained proliferation indices greater than 1.
Fractions with proliferation indices close to or above 1 were regarded as relatively non-inhibitory and were pooled into two groups (No. 40–41 and No. 42–49) for re-quantification and additional testing. At 0.05 μg/ml, unfractionated A-dECM showed higher proliferation indices than fractions No. 40–41. At 0.5 μg/ml, M-dECM showed higher values than fractions No. 40–41. At 5 μg/ml, A-dECM showed higher proliferation indices than M-dECM and both pooled fractions (No. 40–41 and No. 42–49). At 50 μg/ml, proliferation indices of unfractionated A-dECM and fractions No. 40–41 decreased to nearly 0, while M-dECM and fractions No. 42–49 remained close to 1 (Fig. 2B). SDS-PAGE of individual fractions showed prominent protein bands around 40 kDa in fractions No. 40–41, whereas fractions No. 42–49 displayed a different banding pattern (Fig. 2C). For subsequent testing, pooled fractions No. 42–49 were used, and fractions No. 40–41 were excluded.

## Restoration of epidermal stratification and dermal architecture in the A-dECM-treated wounds
On days 2, 7, and 14, H&E staining (Fig. 4A) revealed dynamic changes in epithelial coverage and dermal integrity. At day 2, all groups exhibited partial loss or fragmentation of the epidermis at the wound center, accompanied by dense inflammatory cell infiltration and pronounced disruption of the underlying dermal architecture. Remnant epidermis remained visible at the wound margins. By day 7, the wound bed in all groups remained incompletely re-epithelialized. In both M-dECM and A-dECM groups, epithelial tongues extending from the wound edge were observed, indicating partial initiation of re-epithelialization. Hair follicles within the burn zone appeared shrunken or atrophic, especially in the PBS group. In PBS-treated tissues, the epidermal layer was often discontinuous or fragmented, suggestive of delayed coverage or tissue instability. On day 14, the A-dECM group showed extended, but not complete, neo-epithelialization coverage at the wound center. A portion of the wound surface remained occupied by a lightly eosinophilic structure, likely representing incompletely detached eschar or fibrinous material, rather than newly formed epidermis. In the PBS group, a thick scab remained over a majority of the wound bed, indicating delayed re-epithelialization. The M-dECM group showed partial epithelial coverage with variable thickness. Consistently, quantitative analysis of re-epithelialization at day 14 (Fig. 4B) demonstrated significantly greater epithelial closure in the A-dECM group (40.9 ± 6.1 %) compared with PBS (32.1 ± 3.7 %) and M-dECM (31.4 ± 2.6 %) groups (p < 0.05).

## Discussion
This study provides the first experimental evidence that A-dECM attenuated fibrosis and promoted regenerative features, partly through immunomodulatory ECM cues. We observed that A-dECM accelerated re-epithelialization, enhanced collagen remodeling, and reduced α-SMA and TXNDC5 expression in both wound tissue and TGF-β1–stimulated fibroblasts. Moreover, A-dECM suppressed pro-inflammatory M1 polarization while increasing CD163-positive macrophages and reducing TNFα, IL-1β, and CD86 expression both in vivo and in THP-1 macrophage assays. These findings collectively support A-dECM as a multifunctional immuno-instructive scaffold capable of redirecting mammalian wound repair from fibrosis toward regeneration. Nevertheless, it should be noted that our study employed daily subcutaneous injections of 100 μl beneath the wound, and this volume and frequency may themselves influence wound mechanics, local inflammation, or healing kinetics. While this potential confounder does not detract from the observed differences between groups, it highlights the need for future work using alternative delivery strategies (e.g., topical application, hydrogel embedding) to confirm the robustness of A-dECM’s effects.
Notably, re-epithelialization is a critical hallmark of functional skin regeneration, requiring coordinated keratinocyte migration, proliferation, and stratification. In our model, quantitative analysis of H&E sections at day 14 showed that A-dECM-treated wounds exhibited significantly greater epithelial coverage compared to PBS and M-dECM groups (Fig. 4B). By day 28, immunohistochemistry further demonstrated re-establishment of the typical layer-specific marker distribution, with K5 in the basal layer, K10 in the suprabasal/spinous layer, and Loricrin in the superficial granular/cornified layers (Fig. 5E). This sequential pattern resembles key aspects of the physiological architecture of normal epidermis, supporting the role of A-dECM in facilitating timely epithelial coverage and more organized epidermal stratification [40].
The ECM composition plays a pivotal role in tissue regeneration, as demonstrated in highly regenerative species such as axolotls, fetal mammals, and the African spiny mouse (Acomys) [8,13,15,31,34,41]. While SEM revealed that intact A-dECM exhibits an organized collagen network, our in vivo application employed solubilized A-dECM, which lacks this native ultrastructure. Thus, the observed bioactivity is more likely attributable to preserved biochemical components (e.g., collagen isoforms, GAGs, and regeneration-associated proteins) that support keratinocyte proliferation, modulate inflammation, and enhance dermal remodeling. Ultrastructural features such as lattice-like collagen architectures have been described in intact axolotl skin [42], but our study indicated that the role of ultrastructure may not be essential. Future work using biomaterial formats that preserve both structure and composition—such as advanced hydrogels with adhesive, self-healing, or inflammation-responsive properties [43,44] may further clarify this issue.
Emerging evidence suggests that ECM derived from regenerating tissues may harbor stronger pro-regenerative cues than those obtained under homeostatic conditions. Single-cell transcriptomics of axolotl limb regeneration revealed profound differences between homeostatic and regenerating states, with blastema fibroblast-like progenitors and wound epidermis expressing regeneration-specific genes that likely shape a pro-regenerative niche [45]. Similarly, in zebrafish, decellularized cardiac ECM obtained from injured hearts supported mammalian heart regeneration more effectively than ECM from uninjured controls, in part through reactivation of ErbB2/NRG1 signaling [46]. Considering the recent findings in fetal mammals and regenerative rodents showing lineage-specific fibroblast programs and unique ECM composition favoring scarless repair [1,13,47], the regenerative advantage of A-dECM may not only derive from species-specific composition but could be further potentiated if sourced from regenerating axolotl tissues.
Indeed, axolotl ECM was found to contain higher levels of collagen and GAGs, including hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate, than murine ECM. These components are known to create a hydrated microenvironment and modulate immune responses [15,25,27,48]. Moreover, tenascin-C, decorin, fibronectin, thrombospondins, and other proteoglycans—present in axolotl ECM—play key roles in regulating fibroblast behavior and inflammation [15,[25], [26], [27],42,49,50]. These regenerative ECM traits are not unique to axolotls; similar features are also found in fetal mammalian tissues, which are well-known for their scarless wound healing potential [3,5,8,34]. Fetal skin exhibits lower inflammatory cytokine expression and a higher TGF-β3 to TGF-β1 ratio, promoting a regenerative, immune-tolerant microenvironment [3,5,6]. Moreover, fetal fibroblasts demonstrate lineage-specific programs that limit myofibroblast differentiation and collagen hyperproduction, further reducing the risk of fibrosis [51,52]. The transition from regeneration in fetal life to fibrotic repair in adulthood is further influenced by changing ECM composition, cellular signaling, and increasing mechanical tension during tissue growth [12,52].
This contrasts with the less defined or fragmented expression patterns observed in PBS- and M-dECM-treated groups during earlier phases, supporting the role of A-dECM in facilitating coordinated epidermal maturation. Histological analysis also revealed that collagen fibers within A-dECM-treated wounds appeared more aligned and organized than in controls. Such organized remodeling is associated with reduced fibrotic healing, as supported by studies in reduced scarring wound models such as fetal mammals and marine mammals. In particular, Fraser’s dolphins demonstrate the ability to restore normal collagen architecture after transient hypertrophic-like scarring, ultimately removing excess collagen and re-establishing dermal integrity [28,53]. Likewise, fetal wound healing exhibits low collagen density and a balanced type III/I collagen ratio during early remodeling, contributing to reduced fibrosis and scar formation [3,5,6,35]. In addition to histological features, macroscopic wound characteristics, such as scab thickness and discoloration, were recorded as part of the healing index. These visual parameters have been used in murine burn models as indirect indicators of healing kinetics and epithelial restoration, with early normalization of wound appearance often correlating with improved dermal regeneration [39].
While the regenerative potential of A-dECM is promising, concerns regarding potential residual bioactive and toxic peptides remain significant. Amphibian skin secretions, including salamanders, contain various antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and bioactive molecules such as tylotoin and pleurocidin-like peptides [21,43,49,[54], [55], [56]]. Although AMPs and other secreted peptides contribute positively to tissue regeneration by modulating microbial populations and inflammation, some can induce cytotoxicity at higher concentrations or in different biological contexts [49,54,55]. Indeed, our in vitro assays revealed concentration-dependent cytotoxic effects of A-dECM on HaCaT cells. SDS-PAGE and fractionation via SEC identified toxic protein components around 40 kDa, which could be effectively removed through fractionation to improve safety. These findings highlight the importance of purifying xenogeneic ECMs to eliminate cytotoxic residuals while retaining bioactive fractions. Such concerns echo broader challenges in hydrogel-based regenerative materials, where safe fabrication methods and controlled release strategies are essential to translate biomaterials from bench to bedside [43,44].
Comparatively, current clinical ECM scaffolds, such as porcine-derived acellular dermal matrices and small intestinal submucosa, offer structural support and limited biological activity, often resulting in incomplete regeneration and persistent scarring [30,33,[57], [58], [59]]. In contrast, A-dECM provides distinct immunomodulatory advantages due to its inherently regenerative ECM composition, including bioactive GAGs and specific collagen isoforms [15,21,48]. Regenerative species such as axolotls, fetal mammals, and the African spiny mouse generally exhibit milder immune responses post-injury, though not completely lacking immune activity [16,18,19,[60], [61], [62]]. For example, axolotl limb regeneration requires specific macrophage subsets that regulate immune responses, inflammation, and create an environment conducive to regeneration [60,61]. Conversely, adult mammals typically mount stronger and more prolonged inflammatory responses post-injury, promoting fibrosis and scar formation [19,21,63]. Thus, the difference in regenerative capability stems not from the complete absence of immune reactions but rather from differences in immune response intensity, duration, and macrophage function regulation [16,[60], [61], [62]]. Our immunohistochemical analyses confirmed that A-dECM significantly reduced iNOS expression and elevated CD163-positive macrophage populations in wounds, indicating a phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 macrophages. Gene expression analysis further showed reduced IL-1β and moderately increased IL-4 levels, supporting a balanced immune modulation rather than suppression. These immunophenotypic and transcriptomic findings collectively suggest that A-dECM modulates macrophage behavior toward a pro-regenerative M2-like profile. In vitro, THP-1 macrophages treated with A-dECM exhibited significantly decreased CD86 and TNFα expression under inflammatory conditions. Meanwhile, without LPS stimulation, A-dECM elicited minimal effects on macrophage polarization, suggesting that its immunomodulatory activity is context dependent, with more pronounced effects in inflamed environments. Notably, while in vivo analyses revealed increased CD163-positive macrophages in A-dECM-treated wounds, in vitro assays using THP-1 cells did not show significant increase of CD163. This divergence can be attributed to both model and species differences. Murine wound tissues provide a complex stromal and cytokine milieu that favors CD163 upregulation, whereas human THP-1 macrophages generally require IL-10 or glucocorticoid stimulation for robust CD163 induction [[64], [65], [66]].
Although A-dECM accelerated re-epithelialization and dermal remodeling, CD31-positive vessel density was not higher than controls at late endpoints (days 14–28). This is consistent with longitudinal wound-healing studies showing that structural density metrics can return toward baseline during remodeling, whereas healing outcomes correlate more strongly with perfusion and vessel maturation (e.g., pericyte coverage, α-SMA+ arterioles, luminal patency) rather than CD31 area alone [[67], [68], [69]]. For example, in a diabetic wound model, a decellularized dermal matrix derived from thrombospondin-2–null mice improved tissue integration and increased α-SMA+ mature vessels at late time points, while total CD31-positive vessel counts remained unchanged [70]. Taken together, the absence of a late CD31 difference does not preclude vascular involvement; rather, A-dECM’s benefits likely act predominantly through immune modulation and matrix reprogramming, with vascular effects reflected in function/maturation instead of density [[67], [68], [69], [70]].
Fibrosis, marked by myofibroblast activation and excessive ECM deposition, is commonly indicated by the presence of α-SMA [12,71]. Recent research highlights TXNDC5, an ER-resident protein involved in collagen folding and secretion, as another critical mediator in fibrogenesis. TXNDC5 enhances TGF-β signaling, further activating myofibroblasts, increasing α-SMA expression, and promoting excessive collagen synthesis across multiple tissues, including skin, heart, lung, and kidney [[72], [73], [74], [75]]. Our study demonstrated that A-dECM treatment significantly reduced α-SMA and TXNDC5 expression in wound tissue and TGF-β1-stimulated fibroblasts (Fig. 5), underscoring its direct antifibrotic effect. These results suggest that regenerative ECMs not only passively prevent scarring but may actively downregulate fibrotic signaling pathways. This observation aligns with current literature supporting TXNDC5 as a novel therapeutic target in fibrosis management [73,75]. Taken together, these findings not only validate the therapeutic efficacy of A-dECM but also indicate novel mechanistic pathways that may formulate future antifibrotic strategies.
Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the intrinsic limitations of the murine wound healing model. Unlike human skin, which primarily depends on re-epithelialization for closure, murine wounds contract rapidly due to the panniculus carnosus, a subcutaneous muscle layer absent in humans [[76], [77], [78]]. This contraction-driven mechanism can accelerate closure and potentially obscure subtle differences in epithelial regeneration [79]. Single-cell and transcriptomic profiling revealed species-specific gene expression dynamics in ECM remodeling and immune signaling, underscoring that murine models may not fully capture the complexity of human regenerative response [80,81]. Thus, while our findings demonstrated improved epithelial coverage and stratification in the A-dECM group, they should be interpreted with consideration of these model-specific constraints.
This study fabricated axolotl-derived ECM for enhancing mammalian wound healing, representing a significant conceptual advance in biomaterials development [15,31,82]. Our results show that A-dECM accelerates wound closure with improved tissue organization and modulates immune and fibrotic responses. Despite its promising regenerative properties, several challenges remain toward clinical translation. Scalable and standardized production of A-dECM, thorough removal of potential xenogeneic antigens or toxic peptides, and rigorous regulatory scrutiny must be addressed before clinical implementation [57,[83], [84], [85], [86], [87]]. Future advances in recombinant technologies, synthetic analogs, and biomimetic engineering could help recapitulate the regenerative attributes of axolotl ECM in more scalable and safer formats, facilitating wider clinical adoption [[86], [87], [88]]. Ultimately, the clinical potential of A-dECM extends across diverse applications, such as deep burn injuries, chronic diabetic wounds, and complex surgical wounds. With continued research and development, A-dECM could significantly advance regenerative medicine, bridging the gap between biological understanding and clinical application.

## Conclusion
This study provides the first evidence that A-dECM can serve as a potent bioactive scaffold to promote antifibrotic wound healing and regeneration in mammals. A-dECM preserves key regenerative features of amphibian skin—namely, its enriched GAG content, structurally organized collagen network, and the ability to modulate immunomodulatory capacity, while minimizing adverse effects through optimized decellularization and fractionation. Our results demonstrate that A-dECM-treated wounds exhibit accelerated closure, reduced inflammation, attenuated fibrosis (indicated by downregulation of TXNDC5 and α-SMA), and enhanced dermal architecture restoration. Collectively, these findings support the concept that regenerative ECMs derived from species with reduced-scarring forms of wound healing can actively instruct the host healing response. By shifting wound repair from a fibrotic toward a regenerative trajectory, A-dECM represents a promising next-generation biomaterial platform for clinical wound management, particularly in challenging conditions such as burns, chronic ulcers, and complex surgical injuries.
