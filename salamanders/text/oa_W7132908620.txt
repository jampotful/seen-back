Abstract
Usher syndrome (USH) is the most common cause of hereditary deaf blindness. Vision
loss in USH is poorly understood. We used zebrafish models to investigate the role of the Usherassociated gene protocadherin-15 (Pcdh15) in photoreceptors. Pcdh15 localized to the inner
segment (IS) and apically to the synapses of photoreceptors, with sporadic expression apical to
the outer limiting membrane (OLM). This apical Pcdh15 sometimes co-localized with calyceal
processes (CPs), and potentially the IS-OS boundary. Pcdh15b mutants showed morphological
abnormalities in the red cone OS, mitochondria, and filamentous actin. Visual background
adaptation (VBA) and visual motor response (VMR) assays show a potential visual deficit in
pcdh15b mutants, but the optokinetic reflex (OKR) assay did not. My findings suggest that
Pcdh15b plays a role in maintaining the structure of photoreceptors, potentially links this role to
vision loss in pcdh15b mutants and validates zebrafish as a model to study USH-retinopathy.

Chapter 1: Introduction
1.1 Retinal neurogenesis
The vertebrate retina develops out of a single-layer retinal neuroepithelium (Stenkamp
2015). The major neuronal and glial cells of the retina are derived from cells called retinal
progenitor cells (Wang and Cepko 2016). The different cell types of the retina differentiate in a
specific order, though with overlap between development of different cell types, which varies
between species, but usually has retinal ganglion cells differentiating first and Müller glia and
rod photoreceptors differentiating last (Stenkamp 2015). The precise spatial and temporal
coordination of retinal development is necessary to achieve the highly ordered structures of some
vertebrate species’ retinas (Raymond and Barthel 2004).
The retina has a highly conserved basic structure in most vertebrates, with five major
classes of neurons (and one major type of glial cell, Müller glia) arranged into multiple layers
(Figure 1 A). The so-called “first order” neurons in the retina are the photoreceptors, the cells
that detect light, whose nuclei form the outer nuclear layer (ONL) (Kolb et al. 2001; Hoon et al.
2014). Being the primary focus of this thesis, they will be discussed in depth below. At the outer
plexiform layer (OPL), photoreceptors form synapses with horizontal cells and bipolar cells
(Kolb et al. 2001). These two cell types, as well as amacrine cells and Müller glia, have their cell
bodies located in the inner nuclear layer (INL), and bipolar and amacrine cells form synapses
with retinal ganglion cells at the inner plexiform layer (IPL) (Kolb et al. 2001; Balasubramanian
and Gan 2014; Hoon et al. 2014). Ganglion cells, whose cell bodies form the ganglion cell layer
(GCL) project their axons to form the optic nerve which carries visual signals to the brain
(Brown et al. 2001).

2

Numerous intrinsic factors (such as transcription factors) and external factors (such as
extracellular growth factors) are required to achieve the spatial and temporal coordination
involved in retinal development (Stenkamp 2015). Although retinal progenitor cells are
multipotent, it appears that upregulation of particular genes enables them to produce specific cell
types as daughter cells (Wang and Cepko 2016). For instance, zebrafish retinal progenitor cells
expressing the gene thrb (a known cone marker) were found to have daughter cells that were
exclusively longer wavelength cones and never rods (Wang and Cepko 2016). Genes involved in
determining retinal cell fate tend to come from the homeodomain, basic-helix-loop-helix, and
nuclear hormone receptor families (Stenkamp 2015). Often, multiple genes work together to
determine cell fate, such as how expression of the genes Crx, Rx, and NeuroD are all necessary
for a cell to become a photoreceptor (Stenkamp et al. 2015). Knockdown or mutation of these
genes results in a lack of photoreceptors (Stenkamp 2015). Studies on mouse models show genes
such as Nr2e3 and Rorα are specifiers of rod cell fate, while zebrafish models have been used to
demonstrate that the gene tbx2b is required for UV cone development, and trβ2 and rxrγ promote
development of medium to long wavelength cones (Stenkamp 2015). The interplay of these
intrinsic and extrinsic factors combines to produce the mature morphology of cells of the retina,
including photoreceptors.
Though many advances have been made studying photoreceptor development, there are
still many aspects that remain unknown, such as the mechanism behind how various intrinsic and
extrinsic factors affect the development of photoreceptors (Raymond and Barthel 2004).

1.2 Photoreceptors
Photoreceptors are the cells in the vertebrate retina that detect and respond to the stimulus
of light and begin the transduction of this stimulus into signals that can be sent to downstream

3

neurons, eventually reaching the brain (Goldberg et al. 2016). They are a specialized type of
neuron that, like the other neurons and glial cells of the retina, derive from retinal progenitor
cells (Wang and Cepko 2016; Malhotra et al. 2021). Vertebrates possess two broad classes of
photoreceptors: rods and cones (Goldberg et al. 2016). Rods are very sensitive, able to respond to
single photons, and are thus responsible for vision in low light conditions (Hoon et al. 2014).
Cones, on the other hand, are less light-sensitive but have higher visual acuity and come in
multiple subtypes that each respond to differing wavelengths of light thus allowing for colour
vision (Hoon et al. 2014). The precise arrangement of photoreceptors in a species’ retina has
important implications for its vision (Raymond and Barthel 2004). For instance, in the retina of
the zebrafish, the photoreceptors are organized into alternating rows of paired red and green
cones and rows of paired blue and UV cones (Raymond and Barthel 2004).
Photoreceptors are highly polarized. Rods and cones share a basic structure, and the
maintenance of this polarized structure is essential to their proper functioning (Malhotra et al.
2021). Both rods and cones possess an inner segment (IS) and an outer segment (OS) (Figure 1
B), joined by a structure called the connecting cilium (Goldberg et al. 2016). The IS contains
organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and mitochondria (Figure 1 A;
Molday and Moritz 2015). The mitochondria are concentrated in an apically located subsection
of the IS called the ellipsoid (Burnside and King-Smith 2009). The IS and the nuclear region are
continuous, with the nucleus being located basal to the IS (Molday and Moritz 2015). Basal to
the photoreceptor nuclei is a layer called the outer plexiform layer (OPL), which is where the
photoreceptors form synapses with other retinal neurons (Figure 1 A; Mercer and Thoreson
2023). Just apical to the nuclear region, along the boundary between it and the IS, is a structure
called the outer limiting membrane (OLM), composed of a series of adherens and tight junctions

4

between photoreceptors and the processes of Müller glia, the main type of glial cell in the retina
(West et al. 2008; Omri et al. 2010). The OLM is thought to provide structural stability to the
photoreceptors, and act as a semi-permeable membrane that controls the movement of molecules
in the extracellular fluid into and out of the area containing the photosensitive OS (West et al.
2008; Omri et al. 2010).
The OS, located apically to the IS, is composed of a series of membranous discs stacked
atop one another (Goldberg et al. 2016; Malhotra et al. 2021). These discs contain light-sensitive
proteins such as opsins that begin the transduction of light into cellular signals (Kennedy and
Malicki 2009). In rods the OS discs are contained inside the plasma membrane and are thus not
exposed to the extracellular environment, whereas in cones the discs are in effect indentations of
the plasma membrane and therefore are exposed to the extracellular environment (Kennedy and
Malicki 2009). In many vertebrates, a series of actin-based processes called calyceal processes
(CPs) extend from the IS to cover the basal end of the OS (Figure 1 C). These CPs are thought to
play a role in supporting the structure of the OS and keeping it attached to the IS (Noel et al.
2022; Sharkova et al. 2024). The OS continuously sheds its apical-most discs (Xu et al. 2024).
Membranous discs migrate from the basal to the apical end of the OS whereupon they are shed
and phagocytosed by cells of the retinal pigmented epithelium apical to OS (Delmaghari and ElAmraoui 2022; Xu et al. 2024). Therefore, to maintain its structure, proteins and lipids produced
in the IS must constantly be trafficked to the OS and assembled into new discs to replace those
which have been lost (Kennedy and Malicki 2009). It is therefore essential that a connection
between the IS and OS is maintained, otherwise this renewal of the OS cannot happen, often
resulting in abnormal morphology of photoreceptors (Xu et al. 2024). This is one example of the

5

fact that disruptions to the structure of photoreceptors can often lead to severe consequences, and
since photoreceptors are crucial for vision these include vision diseases (Malhotra et al. 2021).
Studying photoreceptors and their structure is crucial to understanding vision impairment
diseases, as damage to photoreceptors or abnormalities in their development are leading causes
of vision impairment and blindness (Newton and Megaw 2020). Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is the
leading cause of inherited vision impairment and is characterized by the progressive loss of
photoreceptors over time (Newton and Megaw 2020). Other leading causes of vision impairment
in humans include cone-rod dystrophy (where both cones and rods are lost or damaged) and
macular degeneration (where the cones of the macula are lost) among others (Noel et al. 2021).

1.3 Usher Syndrome
Usher Syndrome (USH) is the most common form of hereditary combined vision and
hearing loss (Mathur and Yang 2015). Symptoms are exemplified by hearing deficits, retinopathy
(usually RP), and varying levels of vestibular areflexia which often leads to impaired balance
(Castiglione and Möller 2022; Delmaghari and El-Amraoui 2022). There are several types of
USH, of which Usher Syndrome Type I (USH1) has the most severe symptoms (Ouyang et al.
2005). USH1 patients typically have severe congenital hearing loss and usually experience vision
loss by ten years of age (Mathur and Yang 2015).
There are nine canonical genes that have been well-established to be linked with USH
(Castiglione and Möller 2022; Delmaghari and El-Amraoui 2022). The protein products of these
genes are thought to form a complex with one another in the inner ear (El-Amraoui and Petit
2014). These genes were identified from within previously established genetic loci that were
linked to USH (El-Amraoui and Petit 2014). One of these loci, one linked specifically to USH1,
is known as USH1F (Mathur and Yang 2015). The causal gene of the USH1F locus is known to
be a gene called PCDH15 which encode the protein protocadherin-15 (PCDH15) (Ahmed et al.
2001). PCDH15 protein is a member of the cadherin superfamily, a broad class of proteins
involved primarily in intercellular adhesion (Mathur and Yang 2015; Yusuf et al. 2022).
Specifically, it is a cadherin-related protein (Yusuf et al. 2022). PCDH15 is composed of 11
extracellular cadherin repeats, an extracellular linker domain, a transmembrane domain, and an
intracellular domain (Alagramam et al. 2001; Araya-Secchi et al. 2016; Yusuf et al. 2022). One
study on the frequencies of various USH-linked mutations found that 11% of USH1 patients had
mutations in PCDH15 (Ouyang et al. 2005).

8

The mechanism of hearing loss in USH, and specifically the role of PCDH15 in this
mechanism, is fairly well understood (Mathur and Yang 2015). Numerous USH-linked genes are
known to be necessary for the proper functioning of hair cells in the cochlea of the inner ear
(Cosgrove and Zallocchi 2014). Two USH-linked cadherin-superfamily proteins are involved:
PCDH15 and another cadherin-related protein called cadherin-23 (CDH23) (Kazmierczak et al.
2007). These proteins form a structure called a “tip link” (Figure 2 A, B), which connects
stereocilia on the hair cells to one another (Cosgrove and Zallocchi 2014). The proteins of the tip
link are anchored to the actin cytoskeleton of the stereocilia via a complex of other USH1
proteins (Figure 2 C; El-Amraoui and Petit 2014). For example, Cdh23 is connected to the actin
cytoskeleton via the USH1 protein Harmonin, which was found in mice to co-localize with both
Cdh23 and to filamentous actin and was mis-localized when its actin binding domains were
deleted (Grillet et al. 2009). Myosin VIIa (Myo7A) and Sans, two other USH-related proteins,
were also found by immunohistochemistry and tracking of GFP-tagged versions of these
proteins, to co-localize with Cdh23 and form a complex with each other and Harmonin (Grati
and Kachar 2011). Similar studies have shown co-localization between Pcdh15 and Myo7A in
the inner ear, and mis-localization of both proteins when the other is absent (Senften et al. 2006).
Given the known ability of Myo7A to bind to actin, it has been suggested that Myo7A links
Pcdh15 to the actin cytoskeleton in the inner ear (Senften et al. 2006). When sound waves enter
the inner ear and bend the stereocilia, the tip link proteins are deformed, in turn opening
mechanically gated ion channels that are physically associated with pcdh15 and depolarizing the
hair cell (Maeda et al. 2014). In zebrafish, Pcdh15, specifically the cytoplasmic domains of
Pcdh15, was found to interact with subunits of these channels, and overexpression of these
subunits caused Pcdh15 to be expressed across a wider area of the hair cells than was typically

9

seen in controls, indicating that Pcdh15 localization in the inner ear was tied to that of the ion
channels it associates with (Maeda et al. 2014). Thus, loss of function of Pcdh15 or any of the
other USH1 proteins would have a negative impact on this channel-gating mechanism that could
lead to hearing loss.

In contrast, the mechanism of vision loss in USH is less well-understood. Unlike hair
cells, photoreceptors do not rely on mechanotransduction to send signals to the brain (Cosgrove
and Zallocchi 2014). This makes it less obvious what role USH-linked proteins might play in the
sensory cells of the eye that makes them necessary for proper vision. A further difficulty in
understanding the specifics of USH-mediated vision loss is that most detailed research on USH
thus far has been done using mouse models (Ahmed et al. 2008). While mouse models of USH
are useful in studying the disease in the ear, they fail to show morphological evidence of
significant retinal degeneration, and only display modest symptoms related to vision loss,
particularly at higher light intensities (Ahmed et al. 2008; Calabro et al. 2019; Sethna et al.
2021). This lack of strong retinal phenotype in mice could be due to the morphological
differences between mouse and human photoreceptors. For example, mouse photoreceptors lack
CPs (Mathur and Yang 2015). Given that previous studies have established that certain USH1
proteins, including protocadherin-15, appear to localize to the CPs in multiple species, this might
explain why mice which lack CPs show a milder phenotype (Sahly et al. 2012).

1.4 Non-mammalian models of retinal disease
Given the difficulties in using mice as a model to study USH-related retinal disease,
alternate model organisms might present a useful alternative. Xenopus laevis have been used to
investigate how mutations to structural proteins, including cadherin-related proteins, lead to
morphological abnormalities in photoreceptors (Carr et al. 2021). Further studies using Xenopus
tropicalis as a model for USH-related retinopathy have shown that pcdh15 localizes to CPs, and
that knockdowns of pcdh15 result in functional deficits in photoreceptors as seen in
electroretinograms (Schietroma et al. 2017). Zebrafish are another commonly used model
organism, owing to their fast development time, large clutch sizes, optical transparency of larvae,

12

and approximately 70% of their genes sharing an orthologue with those of humans (Veldman and
Lin 2008; Choi et al. 2021). Zebrafish genetic mutant models have been used to study numerous
retinal diseases (Noel et al. 2021). The photoreceptors of zebrafish also share key structural
similarities to those of humans, including the presence of CPs (Noel et al. 2022). Furthermore,
zebrafish, as vertebrates, possess a retina that has a similar basic structure to that of humans
(Hoon et al. 2014). For these reasons, zebrafish are well-suited as a model to study retinal
disease, potentially including USH.
In zebrafish, there are two paralogues of the human PCDH15 gene, called pcdh15a and
pcdh15b (Seiler et al. 2005). Mutations to the paralogue pcdh15a are associated with hearing loss
(Seiler et al. 2005). Furthermore, zebrafish Pcdh15a specifically was found to interact with
TMC1 and TMC2a, thought to be subunits of the mechanotransduction ion channel in the inner
ear by using two-hybrid assays (Maeda et al. 2014). Recent evidence from our lab found that
pcdh15b mutants appear to have retinal phenotypes, namely defective growth in photoreceptor
outer segments (Figure 2 A), loss of calyceal process attachments to outer segments (Figure 2 B),
and in many cases the outer segment was detached from the inner segment (Miles et al. 2021).
Furthermore, there is evidence that pcdh15b mutant zebrafish larvae show a disorganized
morphology at the photoreceptor ribbon synapse (Miles et al. 2021). These morphological
phenotypes in pcdh15b mutants lend further support to the idea that mutation of pcdh15b in
zebrafish can serve as a useful model to investigate the mechanisms of retinopathy related to
USH1F.

1.5 Research objectives
There are still several unanswered questions regarding the role of Pcdh15 in the retina.
For instance, the subcellular localization of Pcdh15 is not well characterized. Studies of Pcdh15
protein localization in primate photoreceptors suggest a prominent role at the outer segmentinner segment boundary as well as the connection between calyceal processes and the outer
segment, which is supported by evidence from loss of function phenotypes in zebrafish and
Xenopus (Carr et al. 2021; Miles et al. 2021; Verschueren et al. 2022). However, it is not known
for most of the subcellular compartments of photoreceptors mentioned in section 1.2 whether
Pcdh15 is present or not. There are also many subcellular compartments for which it is unknown
what happens in the context of mutations to one of the paralogues of Pcdh15. For example, while
it has been shown that photoreceptor OS in general show a phenotype in pcdh15b mutants, it is
less clear what effects there may be on specific subtypes (Miles et al. 2021). However, there is
still a lack of a more complete analysis of morphological changes in the various subcellular
compartments of photoreceptors in pcdh15b mutants. Moreover, there is limited information on
how defects in photoreceptors in pcdh15b mutants might impact visual behaviour, if at all.
Therefore, the main objective of my project is to investigate and clarify the role of Pcdh15 in
photoreceptor morphology and its correlation to visual behaviour. I hypothesize that loss of
function of Pcdh15 leads to broad defects in photoreceptor structure that are correlated to
functional deficits. To test this hypothesis, my thesis has three experimental aims: (1) to examine
the wildtype expression patterns of Pcdh15 protein in larval photoreceptors; (2) to examine
changes in photoreceptor structures in pcdh15b mutants; and (3) to assess whether pcdh15b
mutants have vision defects.

Chapter 2: Materials and Methods
2.1 Zebrafish husbandry
All the animals that were used were housed in the Aquatics Facility at the University of
Toronto. Wildtype animals, unless otherwise stated, were generated from in-crosses of the AB
strain of zebrafish. Again, unless otherwise stated, pcdh15b mutants were generated from incrosses of homozygous adults of the maternal zygotic pcdh15b17ins line. Transgenic animals,
specifically the Tg [sws-1:eGFP] line, were generated from in-crossing as well and screened
using a fluorescent dissecting microscope.
Larvae were raised in an incubator kept at 28 degrees Celsius. They were stored in the
incubator in glass or plastic petri dishes and raised to either 6 days post fertilization (dpf) or 10
dpf depending on the assay. Upon reaching 5 dpf, larvae were fed twice daily using size 0 food
(Gemma Micro 75 zebrafish, Skretting). The water in the petri dish was replaced after each feed
to remove detritus. After behavioural assays, larvae were euthanized by cold shock. The animals
were raised according to an approved protocol from the University of Toronto Animal Care
Committee.

2.2 Immunohistochemistry
Larval samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 degrees Celsius.
After this, at room temperature, they were washed three times with PBS and then fixed in a
series of sucrose solutions of increasing concentrations (first a 5% solution, then a 2:1 ratio of
5%:30% solution, then a 1:1 ratio, a 1:2 ratio, and finally a 30% solution). The samples were
then embedded in a 2:1 mixture of 30% sucrose and OCT, put in a plastic mould, and frozen on
dry ice. Samples were stored at -80 degrees Celsius.

16

The blocks were sectioned on a Leica CM3050S cryostat. Coronal sections of the eye, 20
microns thick were placed onto slides for staining. Slides were stored at -20 degrees Celsius.
For staining, slides were thawed for 1 hour at room temperature. A hydrophobic boundary
was drawn around the edges of the slide using a PAP pen, after which the slides were rehydrated
with PBST for 30 minutes. Slides were incubated for 1 hour in a blocking solution composed of
97.8% PBS, 2% normal goat serum (NGS), and 0.2% Triton. The slides were next incubated with
primary antibodies dissolved in this same blocking solution overnight at 4 degrees Celsius. The
next day, the slides were rinsed four times with PBST for 10 minutes each at room temperature.
They were then incubated at room temperature with secondary antibodies dissolved in a blocking
solution composed of 97.8% PBS, 2% NGS, and 0.2% Tween for 2 hours. After four ten-minute
washes with PBST, the slides were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 dye for 30 minutes, then
washed twice again for five minutes each with PBS. Finally, the samples were mounted in 40
microlitres of 70% glycerol, and a glass coverslip was placed on top and sealed with nail polish.
Stained tissue slides were stored at 4 degrees Celsius.

2.3 Microscopy and imaging
Retinal tissue samples were imaged on a Leica TCSSP8 confocal microscope. Tissue
slides were covered in Leica Type F Immersion liquid prior to microscopy, as this was a
requirement for confocal lenses. Imaging was performed with the assistance of the program
Leica Application Suite X (LAS X). All images were collected exclusively using the Hybrid
Detector (HyD) channels. Hoechst staining of nuclei was detected using the UV (405 nanometre)
laser set to an intensity of 0.20. Secondary antibodies were detected using the laser that aligned
most closely to their excitation wavelength, 552 nanometres for Cy3 and 638 for Cy5. Phalloidin
conjugated to AlexaFluor 488 was detected with the 488-nanometre laser. For Cy3, Cy5, and
AlexaFluor 488, the laser intensity was set to 2.00 when imaging at 63X magnification, and to
4.00 when imaging at 100X magnification. When making Z-stacks, Z-step size was set to 0.3
microns.
Images generated in LAS X were formatted and analyzed using ImageJ. ImageJ was used
to add scale bars to images, make final adjustments for clarity, and take measurements of
structures in the images such as red cone OS or the distance from the OLM to the OPL. 3D
renders of images were produced using the program Imaris, using the surfaces tool to make
renders of each individual channel. Images generated in ImageJ and Imaris were used to generate
figures using Inkscape, where all further labelling, cropping and formatting of images was done.

2.4 Visual background adaptation assay
Larvae at 6 dpf were placed in a cardboard box to dark-adapt for 30 minutes. After dark
adapting, 3 larvae at a time were placed in an imaging slide and anaesthetized with one drop of
25X tricaine. The anaesthetized larvae were then embedded in 0.8% low-melt agarose, oriented

18

with the dorsal side facing the top of the slide, with a layer of water on top. Images of darkadapted larvae were taken of the larvae on the confocal microscope. Larvae were then gently
pried out of the agarose with forceps and placed in a 25 mL beaker of water. Once in the beaker,
the larvae were then light-adapted by shining a light down onto the beaker for 15 minutes. The
light-adapted larvae were then re-embedded in agarose and imaged again.

2.5 Movement tracking
Tracking the movements of 6 dpf larvae was performed on the movement tracking arena
with the support of the Calarco Lab. The set up consists of an elevated glass platform with a
camera overhead in a dark room that records stop motion videos of the arena below. The arena is
illuminated by infrared (IR) light. Zebrafish larvae were placed into six-well plates, of which
each well was filled with 2 mL of fish water. This six-well plate was then placed in the arena. A
sheet of paper was placed underneath the six-well plate. Stop motion videos at 20 frames per
second (fps) were recorded. For the light-switch assay, a white light source was placed
underneath the glass platform to illuminate the arena and turned on halfway through the recorded
period. The recordings were then analyzed in the software FIMTrack (Risse et al. 2017).

2.6 Optokinetic reflex assay
The OKR assay was performed with the setup of, and with the assistance of the Lin Lab.
At 6 dpf, larvae were immobilized, embedded, and placed in a petri dish in an arena with a
moving visual stimulus. The stimulus used was an image projected below the larva consisting of
a rotating disc with a black and white sunburst pattern. The eye movements of the larvae while
tracking this stimulus were recorded with a camera and then analyzed in the program
DeepLabCut.

2.7 Data analysis
Statistical analyses were performed in R. Analyses performed were Student’s t-test and

Chapter 3: Results
Aim 1: Examining the wildtype expression pattern of Pcdh15 in larval

photoreceptors.
My first aim was to examine the expression of Pcdh15 in the retina of wildtype larval
zebrafish. I examined both the protein localization patterns of Pcdh15 within photoreceptors of
the retina by immunohistochemistry and used a publicly available transcriptomic dataset to
conduct a preliminary quantitative investigation of the transcript expression levels of the two
Pcdh15 paralogues across the different photoreceptor subtypes. Overall, the aim was to improve
our understanding of the cellular distribution of Pcdh15 in the retina, on the basis that knowing
this distribution would aid in attempting to understand its functional role. I focused on wildtype
individuals here because the aim was to understand the role of Pcdh15 in a properly functioning
retina.
3.1.1 Subcellular localization of Pcdh15 in photoreceptors
I wanted to determine which subcellular compartments of photoreceptors showed a
presence of Pcdh15. To accomplish this, I conducted a series of immunohistochemistry (IHC)

20

assays in which I labelled histological sections of 10 dpf larval retinas with both an anti-Pcdh15
antibody and antibodies raised against several markers of specific subcellular compartments of
photoreceptors. I then imaged these sections with confocal microscopy and Imaris image
analysis. Co-localization between Pcdh15 protein expression and that of a given marker could be
taken as evidence of the presence of Pcdh15 in the subcellular compartment labelled by that
marker. In this way the localization of Pcdh15 within photoreceptors could be clarified. The antiPcdh15 antibody that is used in our lab is thought to detect both Pcdh15a and Pcdh15b (Miles et
al. 2021; Phillips et al. 2023). Therefore, the aim here was not to distinguish between the
localization of the two paralogues, but to determine where Pcdh15 proteins are localized
generally. Ultimately, I examined larvae at 10 dpf. I chose this age because at 10 dpf most cone
cell subtypes have differentiated, and rods have begun to differentiate, though not fully matured
(Tsujikawa and Malicki 2004).
I labelled sections with both Pcdh15 and Zpr-1, an antibody that labels Arrestin3a and is
used as a marker for the inner segments (IS) of red and green cones (Ile et al. 2010). Zpr-1
labelling in the inner segment of photoreceptors at 10 dpf encompasses most of the cell body, but
it is absent from the outer segment. Samples with both Pcdh15 and Zpr-1 labelling, showed
overlap in the areas where these two antibodies were expressed (Figure 4 A, B). This overlap
between Zpr-1 and Pcdh15 indicates that Pcdh15 is most likely found in the inner segments of
red and green cone cells in the retina of 10 dpf zebrafish larvae. Pcdh15 was found around the
periphery of the cells labelled with Zpr-1, rather than diffused across their entire area. The
peripheral expression of Pcdh15 is consistent with a plasma membrane localization.
Furthermore, the Zpr-1 antibody does not label all photoreceptor types, only the IS of red and
green cones. Therefore, it is possible that the Pcdh15 expression seen in the images in Figure 4

21

could be associated with the IS of other cone cell subtypes not labelled by Zpr-1 but closely
adjacent. Overall, the apically extending peripheral expression of Pcdh15 appear to be localized
principally to the IS, as demonstrated by the frequent overlapping with Zpr-1 labelling. There
were limited examples of Pcdh15 expression extending apically beyond the area labelled by Zpr1, and thus it appears that there is little to no expression in red or green cone outer segments.

I also examined retinal sections that had been co-labelled with both Pcdh15 and
phalloidin, which labels F-actin (Figure 5A). F-actin was found to form two bands at the
photoreceptor layer. One is found along the OPL, the region containing the synapses between
photoreceptors and bipolar and horizontal cells (Sharkova et al. 2024). The other band is found at
the OLM which is involved in maintaining the structural integrity of the retina (Omri et al.
2010). Apically extending F-actin expressing projections emerge from the OLM band, which
could represent the F-actin found in the calyceal processes surrounding the outer segments
(Figure 5A). The identification of the apically extending actin filaments as potentially being CPs
is due to both their location matching that of the CPs, and the fact that the core of the calyceal
processes is composed of actin (Verschueren et al. 2022). This is the pattern typically seen in
phalloidin staining in the zebrafish retina, consistent with the findings of previous studies
(Sharkova et al. 2024). Based on the co-labelling I observed, there appears to be some
association between Pcdh15 and F-actin. The basal expression of Pcdh15 in photoreceptors often
lies just apical to the OPL band of F-actin, though not directly overlapping (Figure 5 B, C).
Furthermore, while most of the apically extending domains of Pcdh15 do not extend beyond the
OLM, there are sporadic examples that do (Figure 5 B, C). The minority of Pcdh15-positive
filament-like structures that do extend apically beyond the OLM are frequently found to be
adjacent to or directly overlapping with the apically extending actin filaments extending from the
OLM (Figure 5 B, C). If the apically extending actin filaments are indeed CPs, this could
indicate associations between Pcdh15 expression and CPs, which would align with previous
findings (Sahly et al. 2012).

When considering the results of the immunohistochemistry data presented above, it is
important to consider that only one antibody was used to label Pcdh15. This antibody was
previously used for this purpose by Miles et al. (2021) and showed a very similar expression
pattern as seen by them. The same antibody was used by Phillips et al. (2023), and they observed
a similar overall expression pattern except that they detected expression more consistently at the
OS-IS boundary and somewhat more diffuse (or small punctate staining) within the IS. There
may be differences in fixation or antigen retrieval protocols, or imaging parameters, that explain
some of the differences in Pcdh15 localization observed between these studies. However, others
have used different antibodies to label Pcdh15 and have found different expression patterns than
what I have seen, finding it mostly in the region of the OS-IS boundary with little expression
elsewhere (Patel et al. 2024). I attempted to use the same antibody used by Patel et al. (2024) but
was unable to optimize the staining protocol to this antibody and therefore was not able to
produce any usable images that showed a pattern resembling that seen by Patel et al. (2024) or
any discernable pattern at all. The pattern observed by Patel et al. (2024) is what might be
expected if Pcdh15 is in fact associating with calyceal processes.
It seems, therefore, that using different antibodies to label Pcdh15 can produce different
staining patterns. One potential reason why different antibodies might yield different results has
to do with the different epitopes targeted by these antibodies. The different epitopes of the
antibodies might be differentially susceptible to being blocked or otherwise rendered inaccessible
in certain subcellular regions. It might, for instance, be the case that the epitope for the antibody I
used, found in the extracellular linker region near the transmembrane region of the protein (Ge et
al. 2018; Miles et al. 2021) is less accessible to that antibody in the OS-IS boundary region. It is
also possible that different antibodies differentially detect isoforms of Pcdh15 that are poorly

36

characterized in zebrafish photoreceptors but that are known to exist in mammals (HaywoodWatson et al. 2006).
Nevertheless, some preliminary IHC data using pcdh15b mutant retinal tissue appears to
validate the antibody that I have used here. The results of these assays appear to show that
Pcdh15 is reduced in pcdh15b mutants based on staining intensity using the same imaging
parameters as wildtype control tissue (Figure 11). This is consistent with previous findings
showing reduced levels of Pcdh15 protein and pcdh15b mRNA (Miles et al. 2021), although
replicates of this analysis using different antibodies would aid in further validating the expression
pattern. A reduction in Pcdh15 staining in pcdh15b mutants suggests that the antibody that I have
used is indeed labelling Pcdh15. There is, however, some labelling of Pcdh15 even in the
pcdh15b mutants (Figure 11). The residual Pcdh15 labelling in the mutant context is primarily
found in basal regions of the photoreceptors, and the apically oriented expression appears mostly
absent. Given the fact that the antibody used to detect Pcdh15 has its epitope in the extracellular
linker domain, and that the pcdh15b17ins mutant line is predicted to be truncated such that the
extracellular linker domain is missing (Miles et al. 2021), it seems unlikely that the antibody is
detecting Pcdh15b expression in pcdh15b mutant samples. It is possible that the Pcdh15 staining
that remains in the pcdh15b mutants represents Pcdh15a expression. As will be discussed below,
there is growing evidence of Pcdh15a being expressed in photoreceptors (Figure 12 A).

3.1.2 Transcript levels of Pcdh15a and Pcdh15b
To get a rough idea of the quantitative expression levels of Pcdh15 in photoreceptors, and
whether there were variations in expression levels between different photoreceptor subtypes, I
used a publicly available bulk RNA-seq dataset from Angueyra et al. (2023). The dataset was
generated from pooled samples of specific photoreceptor subtypes taken from adult zebrafish
(Angueyra et al. 2023). Therefore, from this dataset I was able to compare the fragments per
kilobase million (FPKM) values for the two Pcdh15 zebrafish paralogues across all the
photoreceptor subtypes (Figure 12 A, B).
I found that Pcdh15b transcript levels were relatively low, and that there were no
significant differences between the different photoreceptor subtypes (Figure 12). While not
statistically significant, there was some variation in the FPKM values of Pcdh15b in the different
photoreceptor subtypes. Green cones had the lowest transcript level, with FPKM = 4.86, and red
cones showing the highest with FPKM = 8.84. On the other hand, Pcdh15a transcript levels were

39

very low in some photoreceptor subtypes (Rods at FPKM = 0.13, and green cones at FPKM =
0.41), and somewhat higher in the others, though still lower than even the lowest level seen for
Pcdh15b (Figure 12 A). As a comparison, the transcripts for arrestin3a, which as mentioned
above is detected by Zpr-1 antibodies, were expressed at levels that were 3-4 orders of
magnitude higher than either paralogue of Pcdh15 (Figure 12 C). This indicates that transcript
levels of both paralogues of Pcdh15 are expressed at relatively low levels in zebrafish
photoreceptors. It would also appear that Pcdh15b is more uniformly expressed in all zebrafish
photoreceptors, whereas Pcdh15a may only be expressed in a specific subset of cones. A caveat
to the data shown here is that it is based on separate pooled samples of adult zebrafish
photoreceptors, which may be different in their mRNA expression patterns from larval zebrafish
from which the IHC data was collected. No comparable dataset for larval photoreceptors was
available at the time of my analysis but it would be of interest in the future to further analyze
Pcdh15 paralogue expression in photoreceptors to better understand its function during

Aim 2: Examining changes in compartment markers in pcdh15b mutants.
In Aim 2, I wanted to examine if any of the markers analyzed above were altered in their
localization patterns when endogenous Pcdh15b expression was reduced. I looked at the
localization of F-actin, Cox IV, and red cone opsin in the maternal zygotic pcdh15b17ins mutant
context to see if there were any disruptions that could be attributed to loss of Pcdh15b. This was
done on larvae that were 10 dpf to match their ages to the wildtype larvae that had been used in
the data obtained for Aim 1.
3.2.1 Altered shape and reduced size of red cone OS in pcdh15b mutants.
In the pcdh15b mutants, there were significant changes to the staining pattern observed
for red cone opsin (Figure 13 A). This indicates morphological defects in the red cone OS in
pcdh15b mutants. Mutant larvae tended to show a more disordered configuration of red cone
opsin staining. The shapes of the red cone OS were less similar from cell to cell, and they varied
in their orientation as well. Some appeared to have a bent shape, with the apical most part of the
OS at a perpendicular angle to the basal portion. Some red cone outer segments seem to not be
aligned with any photoreceptor nuclei below them, which could be an example of a “floating”
OS, resembling those observed by Miles et al. (2021). These could therefore be examples of
outer segments that have detached from the inner segments of their photoreceptors. To further
substantiate these observations, I quantified the length and area of the red cone OS in wildtype
and mutant larvae. This was done using the image analysis program ImageJ on confocal images.
I measured the lengths of all the cone OS present across a span of 20 photoreceptor nuclei in the
central retina, then calculated the mean length across this 20-photoreceptor span. I defined OS
length as the distance between the two farthest apart points of the OS. I did the same for area,
taking the average OS area across a 20-photoreceptor span of the central retina. I found that both

42

average OS length (Figure 13 B) and average OS area (Figure 13 C) were significantly reduced
in pcdh15b mutants normalized to the same number of cells. Therefore, it appears that in
pcdh15b mutants, the OS of red cones shows both abnormal shape and reduced size.
The phenotype seen in the red cone OS in pcdh15b mutants is somewhat puzzling given
the clear lack of localization of Pcdh15 to the red cone OS (Figure 7). It seems that Pcdh15b is
nonetheless exerting an influence on the morphology of the red cone OS. Figure 4 shows that
Pcdh15 does localize to the IS of red and green cones. Furthermore, Figure 12 indicates that
Pcdh15b transcript is expressed in red cones, and indeed at the highest average level of any
photoreceptor subtype (at least in the adult retina). It could therefore be that Pcdh15 is playing a
role in the IS of the red cones that is necessary for the OS to assume its proper morphology. This
could be related to the known role of the photoreceptor IS in maintaining the OS due to
treadmilling (Delmaghari and El-Amraoui 2022; Xu et al. 2024). Pcdh15, which is known to
associate with cytoskeletal elements in the inner ear ((El-Amraoui and Petit 2014) could be
implicated in trafficking of materials from the IS to the OS along the cytoskeleton, and the loss
of Pcdh15b could lead to a disruption in this traffic and therefore distort the morphology of the
red cone OS.

3.2.2 Change in localization of Cox IV and F-actin.
I observed changes in the localization of Cox IV in the pcdh15b mutants as well (Figure
14 A, B). In wildtype larvae, Cox IV localized almost entirely apically to the photoreceptor
nuclei, at the apical end of the inner segment, mirroring the tightly localized distribution of
mitochondria to the ellipsoid. This can be seen when co-labelling with phalloidin to mark
structures such as the OLM. In retinal sections of wildtype tissue co-labelled with Cox IV and
phalloidin, the Cox IV staining is very rarely found basal to the OLM. However, in pcdh15b
mutants, there is a significantly higher presence of Cox IV basal to the OLM (Figure 14 B, C). I
measured this by counting the number of discrete CoxIV-positive punctae that were found basal
to the OLM across a span of 20 photoreceptor nuclei at the central retina. This data suggests that
either the size of the inner segment is reduced such that the ellipsoid is found more basally than
in wildtypes and/or a potential mis-localization of mitochondria themselves in pcdh15b mutants.
The latter pattern could be the result of a failure of the mitochondria to traffic apically to their
proper position in the ellipsoid. This potential failure of trafficking within the cell is like the
possibility that Pcdh15b is somehow required for the proper trafficking of materials from the IS
to the OS as an explanation for the phenotype seen in the red cone OS (Figure 13).

Furthermore, I measured the distance between the OLM and the OPL along a 20photoreceptor span of the central retina in wildtype and mutant larvae. In ImageJ I took the

qualitatively, it appears that the apically projecting actin filaments are less orderly in pcdh15b
mutants than in wildtypes. In wildtype larvae, the filaments are nearly parallel to one another,
and perpendicular to the OLM. The apically extending actin filaments, in pcdh15b mutants,
sometimes appear to be somewhat deflected, rather than being perpendicular to the OLM as is

seen in wildtypes, though this has not been quantified here. These observations of abnormal

morphology of CPs.
Overall, it appears that reduction of Pcdh15b affects the morphology of larval
photoreceptors in several ways. The phenotype seen in pcdh15b mutants with respect to red cone
outer segments suggests a role for Pcdh15 in establishing and/or maintaining the morphology of
the OS in red cones. This could potentially be achieved via the previously postulated association
between Pcdh15 and calyceal processes. This is possible if the actin processes which appear to
be associated with Pcdh15 expression are indeed the calyceal processes. The CPs themselves aid

47

in regulating the morphology of the OS by tethering it to the IS. However, it could also be the
result of Pcdh15b playing a role in trafficking materials needed to maintain the OS along the
cytoskeleton in the IS. This might better fit the data observed above that showed Pcdh15 to be
present in the red cone IS but not the OS (Figures 6 and 9). Mitochondria appear to be mislocalized in the pcdh15b mutant context, with what seems to be a pooling of mitochondria basal
to the OLM. This could indicate that Pcdh15 has a role in trafficking mitochondria to the elipsoid
region of the IS, and thus that reducing the expression of Pcdh15b results in failure of
mitochondria to migrate. This mis-localization of mitochondria could also potentially result in
downstream effects on the metabolism or even the light sensitivity of photoreceptors as both
depend in part on the proper funcitoning of mitochondria (Giarmarco et al. 2020; Ball et al.
2022). This could potentially have consequences for the visual function of pcdh15b mutants. The
question of whether the morphological phenotype seen in pcdh15b mutants is matched by vision
deficits will be addressed below.

Aim 3: Assessing whether pcdh15b mutants have vision defects.
The pcdh15b mutant displays morphological defects in the outer segments and synaptic
terminals of photoreceptors, and my recent data further expand on these observations to suggest
possible morphological deficits in the inner segments. The experiments conducted in Aim 3
investigate whether these phenotypes are linked with visual impairment. These examinations of
the functional impact of loss of Pcdh15 can be used to further validate our pcdh15b mutant
model.

3.3.1 Visuomotor response phenotypes in pcdh15b mutants.
I examined differences in visuomotor reflexes between wildtype and pcdh15b mutant
larvae. This was done because it was anecdotally observed that pcdh15b mutants were much less
active than wildtype larvae. Furthermore, and possibly contributing to this lack of activity,
pcdh15b mutant larvae often failed to inflate their swim bladders. Therefore, I conducted a series
of assays to attempt to characterize and quantify this lack of movement activity in pcdh15b
mutants. To do this, I recorded stop motion videos, using the setup in the Calarco Lab, of 6 dpf
larvae both wildtype and pcdh15b mutants in six-well plates over five minutes (Figure 15 A).
The purpose was to record their movements over this span of time and compare the activity level
of the wildtypes to those of the mutants. The recordings were analyzed using the software
FIMTrack. This program can track the movements of small animals from stop-motion videos and
return readings on several parameters of their movements (Risse et al. 2017). I ran two assays.
The first had the larvae entirely in the dark for the whole five minutes, with only infrared light
used to make the recordings. In the second assay, halfway through the video, a light underneath
the platform was turned on (Figure 16 A). This was done to see whether the larvae responded to
the change in lighting conditions by altering their locomotory behaviour. The response of the
larvae to the change in light conditions was recorded. I analyzed two parameters of movement as
proxies for the larvae’s movement activity. The first was total distance travelled by the larva. The
second was the number of times larvae initiated movements over the course of the assay.
With lighting conditions kept constant (only infrared light), wildtype larvae showed
significantly higher activity than pcdh15b mutants across both parameters (Figure 15 B, C, D).
Wildtypes initiated movement significantly more times than mutants and travelled on a
significantly greater distance on average over the course of five minutes. This lowered activity in

49

the pcdh15b mutants may be a result of vision loss, as movement in zebrafish is partially directed
by responses to visual stimuli (Chen and Engert 2014). However, alternative explanations for the
decreased activity in mutants cannot be discounted. For instance, vestibular defects are a
symptom of USH1 (Ouyang et al. 2005). It is therefore possible that the lower activity of the
pcdh15b mutants is due to an insult to the vestibular system, such as that which is observed in
the pcdh15a mutant (Seiler et al. 2005). As well, the pcdh15b mutants often fail to inflate their
swim bladders, which may also affect their locomotion.

The next tracking assay was to assess whether the larvae responded to changes in lighting
(Figure 16 A) by altering their locomotory behaviour, referred to as a visual motor response
(VMR) and to see if there was a difference in responses between wildtype and mutant larvae
(Emran et al. 2008). Wildtype larvae showed a clear response to changes in illumination. The
wildtype larvae became much more active after the light was turned on. They initiated movement
significantly more frequently after the light was turned on compared to before (Figure 16 C) and
travelled greater distances in the 150 seconds when the light was on compared to the prior 150
seconds when the light was off (Figure 16 B, D). Overall, this suggests that wildtype zebrafish
larvae will alter their locomotory patterns in response to a visual stimulus, such as a change in
lighting conditions. This matches previous work that has reported similar results of larval
zebrafish exhibiting higher activity under bright light conditions (Tuz-Sasik et al. 2022).
The pcdh15b mutants, when subject to the same change in lighting, did not alter their
locomotory behaviour. There was no significant difference in the number of movement
initiations (Figure 16 C), or the total distance travelled (Figure 16 B, D) for mutant larvae before
and after the light was switched on. This lack of responsiveness could be the result of a lack of
detection of the visual stimulus in pcdh15b mutants, caused by vision deficits. The caveat here is
that, as discussed above, the pcdh15b mutants could be less active than wildtype counterparts
due to reasons unrelated to vision such as a vestibular defect or a consequence of their uninflated
swim bladder. Nevertheless, the mutants not only failed to match the wildtypes’ activity levels
after the change in lighting conditions, but they also failed to display any change at all in their
locomotion in response to light.

3.3.2 Optokinetic reflex assay.
To further assess the potential vision impairment in pcdh15b mutants, I performed an
optokinetic reflex (OKR) assay with the assistance of Daniel Liu from the Lin lab. This assay
tracks the movements of larvae’s eyes as they follow a moving light stimulus. Thus, it allows one
to measure the ability of a larva’s eyes to respond to a visual stimulus. When tracking the
stimulus, a larva will smoothly move its eyes across the field of view, then when the next cycle
starts, very quickly dart its eyes back to their starting position at the opposite end of the field of
view (Figure 17 A). This is called a saccade (Figure 17 B). The frequency, amplitude, and speed
of saccades can be measured and used as a proxy for an animal’s ability to visually track
movement.
First, I conducted this assay using pcdh15b mutants generated from in-crossing maternal
zygotic pcdh15b homozygote individuals and comparing them to wildtypes generated from an
in-cross of wildtype individuals. This was the same way the pcdh15b mutant and wildtype
control groups were generated in the experiments discussed above. The results showed that
across all three saccade parameters mentioned above, saccade rate, amplitude, and speed, there
was no significant difference between the pcdh15b mutants and the wildtypes (Figure 17 B-E).

3.3.3 Visual background adaptation in pcdh15b mutants
I conducted a Visual Background Adaptation (VBA) assay. This assay makes use of a
physiological response of zebrafish to changes in light conditions to assess impairment of light
detection in photoreceptors. Zebrafish larvae have pigmented cells called melanophores on the
dorsal side of their heads. The pigment in the melanophores is contained in granules called
melanosomes (Neuhauss et al. 1999). When zebrafish larvae are placed in a dimly lit
environment, the melanosomes are distributed in a diffuse manner making the dorsal side of the
head appear darker in colour. When the larvae are placed in a brightly lit environment, and it
detects this bright light via its photoreceptors, a signal is sent from the photoreceptors to the
hypothalamus which results in the release of pituitary hormones that cause the melanosomes to
cluster more tightly around the centre of the melanophore (Neuhauss 2010). This results in
smaller patches of dark colour, and thus an overall lighter appearance to the zebrafish and is
thought to be a natural camouflage response.
Because the VBA response is dependent on the ability to detect changes in light via
action of the retina, analyzing whether VBA is affected in mutant lines can serve as a screen for
identifying probable visual impairment (Mueller and Neuhauss 2014). To assess whether this was
true of pcdh15b mutants, I performed a VBA assay in which I compared wildtype and pcdh15b
mutant larvae’s ability to contract their melanosomes in response to light exposure. Images were
taken of each 6 dpf larva before and after being exposed to a bright light (ranging from 3368 to
3522-lux) from an LED light source (Figure 19A). I analyzed the images of the larvae taken
before and after light-adaptation in ImageJ. Specifically, I compared the percentage of the area of
the dorsal side of the larva’s head that was darkly pigmented before and after light exposure. This
was to see if the percentage of the area of the head that was darkly pigmented decreased after

58

light exposure, as would be expected to happen, or if the larvae failed to reduce the darkly
pigmented area, which would indicate a failure of their photoreceptors to detect light.
The images showed that wildtype larvae had a significantly reduced area of dark
pigmentation after light exposure, while pcdh15b mutants did not (Figure 19 A, B). This suggests
that photoreceptors in pcdh15b mutants have a reduced ability to detect increases in light. It also
therefore provides evidence that mutation to pcdh15b results in visual impairment in zebrafish.
Overall, there is evidence for visual impairment in pcdh15b mutant larvae from the
results of the VBA assay, which shows that pcdh15b mutants fail to respond to changes in
brightness. The results of the VMR assay also appear to suggest an impaired ability to respond to
changes in brightness, albeit with the important caveat that the pcdh15b mutants’ failure to alter
their locomotory activity could be due to a motor rather than a visual deficit. However, there is
no evidence from the OKR assay of impairment in the ability of pcdh15b mutants to track a
moving visual stimulus. This could imply a partial vision impairment where some, but not all
aspects of vision suffer deficits in pcdh15b mutants. This will be further discussed below.

Chapter 4: Discussion
Overall, a picture is emerging of Pcdh15 having an important function in photoreceptors
in zebrafish. Details of its localization patterns, as well as both morphological and visual
phenotypes in pcdh15b mutants point to Pcdh15 being of similar importance in the retina as it is
known to be in the inner ear. My work showed that Pcdh15 localizes to the IS of red and green
cones, is found in basally oriented regions just apical to the synapses, and occasionally extends
apical to the OLM. It also demonstrated that red cone OS, mitochondria, and F-actin show
disruptions to their localization in pcdh15b mutants, indicating a possible reliance on Pcdh15b
for their proper localization pattern. I also found evidence for a deficit in some but not all vision
tests in pcdh15b mutants, which might be linked to the morphological phenotypes observed in
these mutants.
It seems that Pcdh15 is important in maintaining the polarized structure of
photoreceptors, and that this maintenance of their unique structure is crucial to their proper
functioning. While much of the details concerning the precise ways that Pcdh15 accomplishes
this function remain mysterious, this thesis suggests several potential areas of future study by
clarifying which subcellular compartments of photoreceptors Pcdh15 is localized to and by
identifying certain photoreceptor structures that are affected by mutations to pcdh15b and thus
which are plausible candidates to interact with Pcdh15.

4.1 Pcdh15 expression pattern
In terms of the localization patterns of Pcdh15, there are two major patterns to consider:
the peripheral and somewhat discontinuous expression along the basal end of the inner nuclear

61

layer, and the apically extending expression which is mostly confined to the cell body and IS but
sometimes extends beyond the OLM.
The apically oriented expression is found mostly along the peripheries of the nucleus or
inner segment (Figures 4, 6). This pattern might be expected if Pcdh15 is found in the plasma
membrane and in cell-cell adhesion between photoreceptors. Zebrafish photoreceptors are
connected to one another and to Müller glia by adherens junctions which are found along the
OLM (West et al. 2008; Omri et al. 2010; Kujawski et al. 2019). These adherens junctions are
important in maintaining the structure of the retina, and in its development, particularly in
establishing apical-basal polarity in photoreceptors (West et al. 2008; Omri et al. 2010; Kujawski
et al. 2019). Though not as well understood, it is possible that Pcdh15 is involved in cell-cell
adhesion between photoreceptors via adherens junctions in the IS, or the cell body, and that this
is the function of the preponderance of Pcdh15 expression which is found basal to the OLM.
Further testing of this idea, including the possible use of specific plasma membrane markers in
co-labelling assays with Pcdh15, would further clarify whether this is indeed the case.
The small Pcdh15-positive structures that were found to extend across the OLM (Figure 5
B, C) imply that Pcdh15 may have a role at or near the adherens junctions between
photoreceptors and Müller glia found at the OLM. A role for Pcdh15 at the OLM, as one of the
proteins that helps maintain the structural integrity of the photoreceptors might also be a reason
why pcdh15b mutants show a mis-regulation of photoreceptor morphology. Expression of
Pcdh15 apical to the OLM, and seemingly often adjacent to actin filaments, appear to support the
conclusions of Miles et al. (2021) that Pcdh15 is also found at the calyceal processes. The fact
that Pcdh15 structures were found directly adjacent to, or overlapping with F-actin filaments is
consistent with the idea that Pcdh15 may function in adhering CPs to the OS, thus supporting the

62

connection between the OS and the IS and helping to maintain the structure of photoreceptors.
The localization of Pcdh15 adjacent to CPs is also consistent with the findings of several
previous studies (Sahly et al. 2012; Schietroma et al. 2017; Miles et al. 2021; Patel et al. 2024)
However, the potential localization of Pcdh15 to CPs was observed only sparingly
(Figure 5 B, C), and the reason why most of Pcdh15 expression does not co-localize with CPs is
currently unknown. It could be that Pcdh15 apical to the OLM is localizing to structures other
than CPs that are less densely distributed in sectioned tissue, thus explaining the only sparing
expression of Pcdh15 apical to the OLM. One such structure could be the accessory outer
segment (AOS), a membranous protrusion that originates from the connecting cilium and is
positioned parallel and adjacent to the OS (Noel et al. 2022). The function of this structure is not
well known, but it is known to be enriched in expression of myo7a, another USH-associated
protein (Hodel et al. 2014). Another possibility is that the actin filaments overlapping with
Pcdh15 represent processes of the Müller glia. More investigation into structures such as the
AOS is needed to clarify the precise nature of Pcdh15 expression found apical to the OLM, and
what structures these Pcdh15-positive structures associate with.
The basally oriented expression appears to be apical to the OPL, and to the region of SV2
expression. This implies that Pcdh15 might associate with structures in the synaptic terminal, or
perhaps the axon leading to the synaptic terminal. Furthermore, Pcdh15 expression appeared to
extend basally towards the synaptic pedicles of UV cones (Figure 8 B), though not to overlap
directly with the pedicles. This appears to mimic the outline of the UV cone’s axon (Nelson et al.
2022). It is therefore a possibility that the basal bands of Pcdh15 are primarily or partially
present in the axons of photoreceptors. This could explain the consistent pattern of Pcdh15

63

appearing apical to the synapses but never overlapping them directly. As above, further
investigation into the possible role of Pcdh15 in the axons of photoreceptors is needed.
There was little direct overlap seen between Pcdh15 and Cox IV (Figure 9 A). This would
suggest that there is limited direct interaction between Pcdh15 and the mitochondria in the
ellipsoid region of the IS, and indeed that Pcdh15 does not frequently localize to the ellipsoid.
However, there was limited examples of Pcdh15-positive structures that extended into the
ellipsoid (Figure 9 B). Furthermore, Pcdh15 expression that extends apically beyond the OLM
would more likely than not extend into and past the ellipsoid. This largely mirrors the situation
seen with actin filaments, where most Pcdh15 structures do not extend apically enough to
overlap with them, but a minority subset does. Despite the limited direct overlap between Pcdh15
and mitochondria, there is evidence discussed below that Pcdh15 still has an influence on their
localization.
More work needs to be done to further clarify the localization patterns of Pcdh15, and
especially to figure out in more detail its functional role within photoreceptors. Potential binding
partners of Pcdh15 in photoreceptors have not yet been identified. Testing to see if cadherin-23,
its binding partner in inner ear tip links, or other cadherin-superfamily proteins overlap with
Pcdh15 in the retina could help elucidate this point. Furthermore, the nature, if any, of the
associations between Pcdh15 and the cytoskeleton of photoreceptor cells is also not well
understood. Pcdh15 is known to be connected, via other USH1 proteins, to the actin cytoskeleton
of inner ear hair cells (El-Amraoui and Petit 2014). This is consistent with observations of
Pcdh15 expression overlapping with actin filaments (Figure 5 B, C). Further work would need to
be done to establish whether a similar relationship exists between Pcdh15 and the cytoskeleton in
photoreceptors as is found in the inner ear.

4.2 Morphological phenotypes in pcdh15b mutants
Several subcellular compartments of photoreceptors showed morphological phenotypes
in pcdh15b mutants. Some of these were consistent with previous findings, while others were
more novel.
Reduced levels of Pcdh15 have been shown to result in abnormal OS morphology in both
zebrafish (Miles et al. 2021) and Xenopus (Schietroma et al. 2017). These results align with the
findings of abnormal morphology of red cone OS in pcdh15b mutants (Figure 13). It is thought
that the shape of the OS is in large part established by the CPs and by neighbouring
photoreceptors, which surround and hem in the OS as it grows (Miles et al. 2021). The irregular
morphologies of red cone OS in pcdh15b mutants may therefore be due to the lack of Pcdh15
which means that CPs are unable to attach to the OS. However, this idea is complicated by the
fact that there was no direct overlap of Pcdh15 with red cone OS. This implies that even if
Pcdh15 does overlap with CPs around the OS of some photoreceptor subtypes, it does not extend
apically enough to overlap with those CPs that cover the red cone OS. Therefore, it might be
more likely that the phenotype seen in the red cone OS in pcdh15b mutants is due to indirect
effects that reduction in Pcdh15b has at the IS-OS boundary or between adjacent photoreceptors,
rather than the loss of a protein that directly interacts with the red cone OS itself.
The red cone OS also showed a reduction in size compared to those seen in wildtypes.
One possible explanation for this smaller size is that the renewal of the OS in pcdh15b mutants is
slowed or prevented in some way. Miles et al. (2021) showed that the same pcdh15b mutant line
as used in this study had a significant number of detached OS that had “floated” away from their
photoreceptors. There were some OS that appeared to potentially be detached from their IS in the
mutants (Figure 13 A). It could be that a lack of sufficiently strong connection between the IS

66

and OS is occurring in pcdh15b mutants, and that this can lead to either deficient replenishment
of the OS membranes and thus reduced size, or in some cases even a detachment of the OS, and
that it is the reduced Pcdh15b levels that are responsible. If so, this would suggest a role of
Pcdh15b in red cones, perhaps at the IS-OS junction rather than in CPs. Or a potential role of
Pcdh15 in the trafficking of materials from the IS to the OS. Given the known association
between Pcdh15 and the cytoskeleton in inner ear cells, a possible function for Pcdh15 in
trafficking materials along the cytoskeleton is worthy of future exploration.
Trafficking of materials to their proper location may also help explain the pattern seen
with mitochondria in pcdh15b mutants. Mitochondria, as marked by Cox IV, were found to be
disproportionately common in the region below the OLM in pcdh15b mutants when compared to
wildtypes (Figure 13 B, C). Indeed, in wildtype larval zebrafish retinas, nearly all mitochondria
are located apical to the OLM, as this is where the ellipsoid region is found (Burnside and KingSmith 2009). The seeming pooling of mitochondria more basally than they are supposed to be
could indicate that they are failing to migrate to the ellipsoid. Once again, trafficking along the
cytoskeleton is a possibility for how Pcdh15 could be influencing the localization of
mitochondria, though there are other possibilities. Recent work has shown that in mice, the
localization of mitochondria in photoreceptors is partly dependent on the mitochondria being
tethered to the plasma membrane, though the proteins involved in this tethering are unknown
(Meschede et al. 2020). I observed that Pcdh15 only sporadically co-localizes with mitochondria
in the ellipsoid region, where these tethers were observed in mice (Meschede et al. 2020), so it is
unclear if such a mechanism exists in zebrafish. It would be useful to establish whether these
tethers are present in zebrafish, or any other vertebrate species. If so, given Pcdh15 is a
transmembrane protein known to function primarily in adhesion, looking into whether Pcdh15

67

has any role in this tethering of mitochondria to the photoreceptor plasma membrane could be a
fruitful object of future research. Mitochondria are crucial for photoreceptors in terms of meeting
their high energy demands, and there is new evidence that the tightly packed arrangement of
mitochondria in the ellipsoid region contributes to photoreceptor function by helping to direct the
movement of light into the OS (Ball et al. 2022). Therefore, the mis-localization we see in
pcdh15b mutants, specifically the seemingly more random and diffuse distribution of
mitochondria throughout the cell, may interfere with this function of mitochondria and contribute
to vision impairment in pcdh15b mutants as discussed below.
The changes to the distribution and localization of F-actin seen in pcdh15b mutants
included a slight disorganization of the apical actin filaments as well as a significant reduction in
the distance between the OPL and the OLM (Figure 14 B, D). These changes provide further
evidence of a potential link between Pcdh15 and the actin cytoskeleton of photoreceptors.
Indeed, this direct change in the localization patterns of actin when Pcdh15b is the strongest
evidence for a connection between Pcdh15 and actin in the retina.
Overall, each of the subcellular compartments we examined showed some sort of
morphological abnormality in pcdh15b mutants. The aggregate of this evidence would therefore
suggest that Pcdh15b is required for establishing and/or maintaining many aspects of the
structure of photoreceptors. As discussed above, the structure of photoreceptors, with each
subcellular compartment being found in its proper place and with its proper morphology, is
crucial to the functioning of photoreceptors in detecting light. Below, the implications of the
defects in structure seen in pcdh15b mutants on the functioning of photoreceptors will be
elaborated.

4.3 Vision deficits in pcdh15b mutants
The various behavioural and physiological assays conducted only partially support the
idea that reduction of Pcdh15b leads to vision impairment in zebrafish. The VBA assay seems to
clearly show a vision impairment. Movement tracking seems to show a lack of response to
changing light conditions, but with an important caveat to consider. Meanwhile, the OKR assay
appears to show no visual deficit in pcdh15b mutants given the parameters used in the assay.
The movement tracking assay shows that pcdh15b mutants display lower activity than
wildtype larvae. More significantly, pcdh15b mutants fail to respond to changes in light
conditions by altering their locomotory VMR patterns, unlike wildtype larvae which consistently
show higher activity in response to a change in lighting. It must be remembered, however, that
the pcdh15b mutants showed very low activity when light conditions were kept constant as well.
This could be due to effects of loss of pcdh15 in other tissues and cell types required for
locomotion, such as the vestibular system, musculoskeletal system, or other parts of the central
nervous system besides the retina. Vestibular deficits are a symptom seen in USH1, and therefore
it is possible that pcdh15b mutants would display this symptom (Delmaghari and El-Amraoui
2022). However, one might still have expected a transient increase in activity in mutants in
response to the light being changed, even if they were still less active than wildtypes. The fact
that mutants showed no change at all lends support to the idea that they lack the ability to
perceive the change and therefore lends support to the idea that they have a vision impairment.
The result of the VBA assay also shows a significant difference between the responses of
wildtype and pcdh15b mutant larvae to changes in light conditions. The wildtype larvae
consistently demonstrate an ability to undergo the VBA in response to light exposure. The
pcdh15b mutant larvae fail to undergo the VBA in response to this stimulus. Because the VBA

70

response is dependent on detection of changing light conditions by photoreceptors, it serves as a
proxy for proper visual function. A lack of VBA indicates a lack of proper functioning of
photoreceptors (Fleisch and Neuhauss 2006). This is most likely the result of disruptions to
photoreceptors in the retina. There is another population of photoreceptors in zebrafish, namely
the pineal photoreceptors (Li et al. 2012). However, studies on zebrafish mutant larvae that show
morphological defects in the retina (Viringipurampeer et al. 2014; Letelier et al. 2023) as well as
on wildtype larvae that had their eyes removed (Shiraki et al. 2010) all show a loss of VBA
response, indicating that the retina is necessary for this response. The VBA pathway also
includes many steps downstream of photoreceptors, including the optic nerve, hypothalamus, and
pituitary gland (Neuhauss 2010). It is therefore possible that the differences between wildtype
and pcdh15b larvae in the VBA assay are due to an effect in these other tissues. Nevertheless,
given the knowledge that photoreceptors in the retina show morphological defects in pcdh15b
mutants, it seems most plausible that it is this population of cells that is at least a major
determining factor behind the VBA assay results.
The OKR assay seems to contradict the former two tests of vision impairment. It appears
that loss of Pcdh15b did not significantly impair the ability of larvae to track a moving stimulus.
While this seems discordant with the results seen in the VBA and VMR assays, these assays
measure different aspects of vision. The OKR assay specifically measures the ability of an
animal to track a moving visual stimulus. The other two are primarily measures of the larvae’s
ability to respond to changes in brightness in their surroundings. Furthermore, the VMR is
heavily based on a motor component, which cannot be discounted as an explanation for the
results observed in this assay. The OKR also includes a motor component, the movement of the
larva’s eyes. It could be that the pcdh15b mutants suffer from a motor defect that affects

71

swimming but not eye movements. Alternatively, pcdh15b mutants may have a partial vision
impairment which does not severely affect their perception of movement. Perhaps only
perception of brightness is affected. This could potentially be due to differential effects on
different photoreceptor subtypes with different functional roles in pcdh15b mutants, where some
subtypes are more severely affected by reduced Pcdh15 than others. But this needs to be more
thoroughly characterized before firm conclusions are drawn.
Since there are two paralogues in zebrafish, Pcdh15a and Pcdh15b, it could be that there
is some compensation for some of the phenotypes observed in pcdh15b mutants by Pcdh15a. For
example, compensation in the retina by Pcdh15a could potentially explain why the pcdh15b
mutants appear to not show a vision impairment phenotype in the OKR assay. Repeating the
experiment on pcdh15a/pcdh15b double mutants could clarify whether this is the case. However,
it is the case that many morphological phenotypes and some visual phenotypes are still quite
severe in pcdh15b mutants, so if Pcdh15a is compensating for some phenotypes it would need to
be determined why it is not able to compensate for all of them. Furthermore, USH is a
degenerative disease, in which symptoms worsen over time (Mathur and Yang 2015). It would be
interesting to see whether pcdh15b mutants showed a more severe phenotype in such assays as
the OKR assay at older time points than the ones tested above.
Taken together, the three tests of vision impairment seem to suggest that there is a partial
impairment to vision in pcdh15b mutants. Future tests could attempt to tease apart why it seems
that this only extends to perceiving brightness and not movement. As well, future research could
attempt to further validate and quantify vision impairment in pcdh15b mutants by using
electroretinography to directly measure activity of photoreceptors in vivo (Allwardt et al. 2001).
The likely vision impairment in pcdh15b mutants provides a good reason to continue to use the

72

zebrafish model to study USH1. It also allows for the possibility of rescue experiments where
one could attempt to restore not just the morphology of photoreceptors but also function. Future
researchers could, after attempting rescue of Pcdh15b, see if VBA responses and locomotion
have been restored to match wildtype larvae. Rescue experiments such as these could have
clinical significance down the line.

4.4 Implications for USH1 retinopathy
A picture of the role of Pcdh15 in photoreceptors is beginning to emerge. As would be
expected for a protein of its family, and with its other known functions, it appears that the
evidence points to Pcdh15 playing a role in maintaining the structure of photoreceptors. This
likely includes tethering the CPs to the OS to keep it in place. Despite not directly contacting red
cone OS, Pcdh15b nevertheless appears to be necessary for them to develop a proper shape and
size. Pcdh15b also seems to be required for the proper localization of mitochondria. A function
of Pcdh15b in trafficking may explain these two phenotypes. Its localization to the inner segment
is still somewhat mysterious, as is its basal localization to the area apical to the synapse. This
latter pattern may be explained by a presence in the axon. Reductions in Pcdh15 lead to
morphological phenotypes in multiple different subcellular compartments, many of which have
potential implications for the proper functioning of the photoreceptors. This appears to have a
correlation with what looks like partially diminished visual capabilities in pcdh15b mutants.
Overall, it seems that Pcdh15 is required to maintain the structure of photoreceptors, and that its
absence leads to morphological abnormalities and vision impairment. Given the known role of
Pcdh15 in Usher Syndrome type I, learning more about the role it plays in photoreceptors, the
cell type whose degeneration is the cause of the vision loss associated with this disease, could
prove useful in understanding the mechanisms behind vision loss in USH1.
