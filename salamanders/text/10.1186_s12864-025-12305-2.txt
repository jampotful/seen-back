A comparative transcriptomic analysis provides new insights into the ecological adaptations in Ichthyophis bannanicus.
Lai J, Wang Z, Li G, Meng S, Bai L, Zhao F, Bei Y. (2025). BMC genomics. . doi:10.1186/s12864-025-12305-2. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# A comparative transcriptomic analysis provides new insights into the ecological adaptations in Ichthyophis bannanicus

## Abstract
BackgroundBanna caecilian (Ichthyophis bannanicus) was the first species of Gymnophiona to be discovered in China, with unique lifestyles and characteristics. Although there have been some studies on distribution and morphological characteristics in I. bannanicus, few have focused on the molecular level. A comprehensive analysis of gene expression profiling across tissues can provide necessary information for an in-depth understanding of their biological functions.ResultTo provide new insights into molecular mechanisms of ecological adaptation in I. bannanicus, we generated a comprehensive and high-resolution transcriptomic atlas, which covers 19 major organs for I. bannanicus. We identified 5,092 housekeeping genes (HKGs), 2,169 tissue-specific genes (TSGs), and conducted weighted gene co-expression network analysis to determine the modules most relevant to each tissue. Functional enrichment indicated that HKGs support basic cellular activities, while TSGs and the module genes are associated with tissue differentiation and specific physiological processes. And the cross-species comparison of HKGs, TSGs, and co-expression modules highlighted an enhanced and unique immune capacity in I. bannanicus. Notably, the comparative genomic analysis between I. bannanicus and Xenopus tropicalis revealed genes potentially lost in I. bannanicus, including RCVRN.1, GNAT2, SAG, GRK7, SMURF1, NOG, and MAPK14, which may be associated with degeneration of its visual system and limb structures.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that HKGs, TSGs, and co-expression module genes offer valuable perspectives on tissue specialization and evolutionary divergence. The comparative transcriptomic analysis across multiple species and diverse tissue types provides valuable clues into the molecular features that may contribute to ecological adaptation in I. bannanicus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12305-2.

## Introduction
Among diverse environments, the soil, presents several restrictive conditions, including low levels of light, high resistance to locomotion, low airborne transmission and low oxygen (O2), high carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, and many microorganisms, diverse pathogenic invertebrates [1]. Despite these challenges, the caecilian amphibians are still well adapted to life in soil [2]. Caecilian (Gymnophiona), along with frog (Anura) and salamander (Caudata), is one of the three living orders of the Class Amphibia and less known because of their secretive habits. They mainly live in the burrows as adults, not frequently observed in the wild [3]. Caecilians are an old group, with a separate evolution at least 300 million years from their sister-group, the frogs and salamanders [4]. Although this group is smaller in size and hard to observe, caecilians are intriguing to scientists because they have many traits that readily distinguish them from frogs and salamanders. For example, they have elongate bodies segmented by annular grooves, which is consistent with their fossorial lifestyle. They also lack limbs, have reduced visual systems and paired bilateral sensory tentacles on the snout [5, 6]. Although the caecilians have a very important evolutionary position and many unique amphibian traits, many aspects of their biology, particularly at the molecular level, remain poorly be studied.
Banna caecilian (Ichthyophis bannanicus), belonging to the family Ichthyophiidae, order Gymnophiona, was the first caecilian species discovered in China, sporadically distributed in China (Guangdong, Guangxi, and Yunnan), Vietnam, Laos, and Thailand. Following its discovery, two additional species were described from Yunnan province: Ichthyophis yangi in 2024 [7] and Ichthyophis moustakius in 2025 [8] respectively. Their bodies are slender, annulated, worm-like, and brown or black in color, with a yellow longitudinal band on each side. They lack any trace of limbs, have a short tail, strong, heavily ossified skulls and sensitive olfactory system. Eyes are also greatly degenerated into spots with no vision function and only the sensation of light, seemingly compensated for by a stretchable and putative chemosensory pair of tentacles on the snout. In summary, all the above features are consistent with their burrowing lifestyle.
Several studies revealed that I. bannanicus has a biphasic life history that enables it to switch from aquatic in larval stage to subterranean in post-metamorphic stage. The larvae are aquatic and primarily diurnal, grazing on algae or aquatic invertebrate, taking shelter in macrophytes or stone cracks. By contrast, the adults are terrestrial and nocturnal, grazing on earthworms and other insect larvae, spending the day in the soil caves, and crawling out to feed at night. I. bannanicus mainly inhabits streams, creeks, puddles, ponds, swamps, stone crevices, soil caves. Sometimes, they have also been found in harsh environment, such as muddy ponds or sewers, suggesting that I. bannanicus may have a strong immune system that protects them against the invasion of pathogenic bacteria.
Despite its unique lifestyle and characteristics, only a few studies have focused on distribution and morphological characteristics, and little attention has been paid to the molecular level. As the first caecilian species named in China, I. bannanicus represents a unique evolutionary lineage within Gymnophiona. Its specialized burrowing lifestyle and adaptation to subterranean habitats make it an ideal model to study the molecular basis of ecological adaptation in amphibians. It was reported that the genomic sequence of I. bannanicus evolved comparatively slowly [9]. In recent years, a study on transposable elements (TEs) estimated the I. bannanicus genome size to be approximately 12.2 gigabases (Gb) using second-generation sequencing [10]. And the genome has since been released, revealing a total of 23,143 protein-coding genes [11]. Despite these advancements, systematic studies on gene expression profiles across multiple tissues remain scarce. RNA-seq has become a powerful complement of de novo genome sequencing, particularly helping with functional annotation and gene expression assessment [12]. Transcriptome data have been reported in several caecilian species including Rhinatrema bivittatum, Caecilia tentaculata Linnaeus, Typhlonectes compressicauda, Microcaecilia unicolor, Microcaecilia dermatophaga [13]. Although a reference-free transcriptome including heart, brain, liver and tail of I. bannanicus has been assembled and numerous autonomous and non-autonomous TEs were identified [10], this study is of limited help for investigating the relationship between the gene expression patterns and ecological adaptation in I. bannanicus.
To reveal more information about the ecological adaptation of I. bannanicus, we performed the transcriptomic analysis in adult multi-tissues including skin, blood, spleen, liver, kidney, lung, heart, stomach, testis and ovary, which are crucial for metabolism, reproduction, growth, and immunity, and carried out the in-depth comparative transcriptome analysis among I. bannanicus, M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis to identify regulatory pathways and genes potentially involved in sensory perception and immunity that likely related to ecological adaptation such as fossorial lifestyle. Our results provide more and new perspectives for understanding the ecological adaptation of I. bannanicus.

## Materials and methods

## Sample processing
This study was performed in strict accordance with the guidelines developed by the China Council on Animal Care and Use. We collected some freshly road-killed I. bannanicus specimens during the rainy season in Beiliu (22° 48′ 9.9′′ N, 118° 17′ 43.93′′ E), Guangxi, China. The specimens of ten tissues (skin, blood, ovary, liver, kidney, lung, heart, stomach, spleen and testis) were collected, washed by sterile water three times, and dissected immediately. Each tissue had three replicate samples. All tissue samples were soaked in RNAlater stabilization solution (Qiagen), incubated at 4 ℃ overnight (to allow the solution to thoroughly penetrate the tissue) and stored at −20 ℃. After all tissue samples were disrupted and homogenized, RNA was isolated with the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Library preparation and sequencing
The purity, concentration and integrity of RNA samples are tested using advanced molecular biology equipment to ensure the use of qualified samples for transcriptome sequencing. A total amount of 1 μg RNA per sample was used as input for the construction of sequencing libraries, using NEBNext®Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®(NEB, USA) and following manufacturer’s recommendations. And the index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Briefly, the mature mRNA was purified using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5X). First strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities. After adenylation of 3’ ends of DNA fragments, NEBNext Adaptor with hairpin loop structure were ligated to prepare for hybridization. To select cDNA fragments of preferentially 240 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Then 3 μL USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was used with size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min followed by 5 min at 95 °C before PCR. Then PCR was performed with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers and Index (X) Primer. At last, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system) and library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq 2000 platform and paired-end reads were generated.

## The collection of public transcriptomic data
Details of samples were included in Supplementary Table S1. To comprehensively capture the tissue-specific expression landscape and species-specific features of I. bannanicus, we integrated our newly generated data with publicly available multi-tissue transcriptome datasets and the reference genome of this species. Additionally, reference genomes and transcriptome data of three other published species (M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis) were also retrieved for comparative analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

## The processing pipeline of transcriptomic data
The raw transcriptome sequencing data from the four species analyzed in this study were first subjected to quality control and filtering using FASTP-v0.24.1 [14]. In this step, clean data (clean reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapter, reads containing poly-N and low-quality reads from raw data. At the same time, Q20, Q30 [15], GC-content and sequence duplication level of the clean data were calculated. All the downstream analyses were based on clean data with high quality. The index of all four species’ reference genome was constructed using the hisat2-build module of HISAT2-v2.2.1 [16]. Clean reads were then aligned to the respective reference genomes using HISAT2. Samtools-v1.21 [17] was used to sort and convert the SAM files to BAM format. FeatureCounts-v2.1.1 [18] was used to quantify the number of reads mapped to each gene. Gene expression levels were then estimated in terms of transcripts per million (TPM).

## Gene expression pattern across tissues
To ensure the reproducibility of our sequenced samples, gene expression levels were log-transformed [log2(TPM + 1)] and subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) for tissue clustering. Additionally, Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to assess the relationships between samples, and hierarchical clustering was performed using the R package pheatmap (v1.0.12, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap). To further investigate gene expression patterns across 19 different I. bannanicus’ tissue types, additional public datasets were integrated, and similar PCA and correlation analyses were conducted.

## Identification of tissue-specific expressed genes
To assess tissue specificity of gene expression, a tissue specificity index (TSI) was calculated for each gene based on transcript abundance across multiple tissues. TPM values from biological replicates were first grouped by tissue type, and average expression levels for each gene within each tissue were computed. Genes with maximal average TPM values ≥ 1 were retained for further analysis. Based on previously published functional annotations of I. bannanicus genes [11], gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to characterize the biological functions associated with each organ system.

## Housekeeping genes identification and categorization
Housekeeping genes (HKGs) are defined as constitutively expressed genes that are present in all tissues and are essential for maintaining fundamental biological processes and cellular functions [20]. To obtain reliable HKGs, genes with TPM values below 1 in any sample were excluded from further analysis. In I. bannanicus, TSGs were excluded to yield a final set of 5,092 HKGs. HKGs were classified based on their coefficient of variation (CV), calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean, to assess the variability of gene expression across tissues. The calculation formula was as follows: CV = σ/μ, where CV represents the variation degree for genes between tissues; σ represents the standard deviation; μ represents the mean. Based on the CV distribution quartiles, HKGs were grouped into three categories: low variability expression (CV ≤ first quartile), moderate variability expression (first quartile < CV < third quartile), and high variability expression (CV ≥ third quartile). To explore the functional differences among HKGs with low, medium, and high expression variability, GO enrichment analysis was performed on each group of HKGs. To identify stably expressed genes within low variability HKGs, additional criteria were applied, requiring TPM values to satisfy log2 (TPM_max/TPM_min) < 4 [21]. These stably expressed genes were further categorized by TPM value range into three expression classes: low expression (TPM 1–10), moderate expression (TPM: 10–100), and high expression (TPM > 100). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between gene expressions across tissues, and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using the R package pheatmap (v1.0.12, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap).

## Construction of co‑expression network
For the transcriptome data from the four species (I. bannanicus, M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis), gene expression levels were normalized using log2(TPM + 1). For tissues with biological replicates, expression values were averaged to obtain a representative expression level. Genes with an average TPM below 1 across all tissues were excluded from further analysis. The variance of gene expression across tissues was then calculated, and the genes with the top 25% of high variance were selected for co-expression network construction using the WGCNA R package (v1.71) [22]. As a result, 4,777, 4,310, 4,593, and 5,254 genes were included in the WGCNA analysis for I. bannanicus, M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis, respectively. And the soft-thresholding powers of 6, 20, 12, and 20 were selected based on network topology model fit and mean connectivity. These thresholds enabled the construction of hierarchical clustering trees for each species, which were used to assess the correlations between gene co-expression modules and tissues. For I. bannanicus, due to the inclusion of a large number of tissue types, all modules with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.4 for a given tissue were considered as candidate tissue-associated modules. For the other three species, only the module with the highest correlation to each tissue was selected, provided the correlation coefficient exceeded 0.6. Moreover, GO enrichment analysis was conducted for genes within each tissue-specific module to explore their associated biological functions. The total network connectivity (kTotal) and the intra-module connectivity (kWithin) were calculated, and the genes with the top 5% of high kWithin were defined as hub genes (HUBGs).

## Comparative transcriptome analysis
To investigate functional conservation across species, orthologs of I. bannanicus genes were identified in M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis, using ortholog detection results from Orthofinder-v2.2.7 [23]. The HKGs, TSGs, and genes from WGCNA modules were mapped to their orthologs in the other three species. The identification of HKGs, TSGs, and WGCNA module genes in M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis was performed using the same procedures as those described above for I. bannanicus. Species-specific genes and modules unique to I. bannanicus were further characterized to elucidate its distinct biological features. To investigate genes potentially lost in I. bannanicus during evolution related to eye and limb development, single-copy genes present in X. tropicalis but absent in I. bannanicus were identified. Functional enrichment analysis of these X. tropicalis-specific genes was performed using KOBAS-V2.0 [24], revealing several genes associated with eye and limb development. To further confirm whether these genes were completely lost or partially retained (on the genome) during evolution, we performed a genome-wide alignment between the I. bannanicus and X. tropicalis genomes using minimap2 v2.28-r1209 [25]. Syntenic regions in I. bannanicus were identified based on this alignment. Candidate genes annotated in X. tropicalis but lacking alignment or showing only partial and fragmented matches within the syntenic regions in I. bannanicus were considered potentially absent. Manual inspection of these syntenic regions and their flanking sequences was conducted to validate the presence or absence of genes.

## Results

## Statistics summary of sequencing dataset
A large-scale transcriptome dataset of I. bannanicus was generated, comprising both newly sequenced data (Supplementary Table S1) and publicly available datasets (Supplementary Table S2). The dataset includes 19 tissue types covering ten major organ systems, namely the nervous, cardiovascular, hematopoietic, digestive, urinary, respiratory, reproductive, immune, skeletal, and muscular systems (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). A uniform library construction strategy (described in Methods) was adopted for all tissue samples to minimize the impact of batch effects on gene expression levels. After quality control of the raw data, approximately 603 million reads (~180.35 Gb) were generated, with an average of 22 million reads (~6.68 Gb) per sample, and a Q30 base percentage of no less than 92.89% for each sample (Supplementary Table S1).

## Evaluation of sample repeatability
Using gene expression data, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted after removing three low-quality samples: OV02, KI01, and TE03 from the original set of 30, resulting in 27 high-quality samples. The analysis revealed that the three samples from the same tissue clustered distinctly, indicating good repeatability among the three replicated samples in each group (Supplementary Figure 1A). This observation was further supported by the hierarchical clustering heatmap based on Pearson's correlation coefficients, which showed strong correlations among biological replicates and consistent clustering patterns (Supplementary Figure 1B).
Additionally, after incorporating publicly available transcriptome data of I. bannanicus, the same analyses were performed on all 48 samples. The PCA results showed that, despite uncertainty regarding individual sources and growth conditions of the public samples, tissues from the same organ (whether from our own dataset or the public data) tended to cluster together (Supplementary Figure 2A), which indicates that the transcriptional differences behind functional differences among organs are greater than those within different tissues or sampling conditions within an organ. The heatmap based on Pearson’s correlation coefficients largely supported this observation, except for a few outlier samples. Furthermore, we observed that tissues from the organs with similar physiological functions tended to group together, such as intestine, gallbladder, and stomach tissues, all of which are components of the digestive system (Supplementary Figure 2B). These results also suggest that the observed divergence in gene expression profiles among tissues may be closely related to tissue-specific differentiation and functional specialization.

## Detection and expression profiling of housekeeping genes
Housekeeping genes (HKGs) are defined as constitutively expressed genes present in all tissues, maintaining essential biological processes and cellular functions, such as cell transport and the cell cycle [20]. In I. bannanicus, a total of 5,092 HKGs were identified across the 25 tissues. Although HKGs are expressed in all tissues, their expression levels vary among different tissues (Fig. 1A). Based on expression variability measured by the coefficient of variation (CV), the HKGs were classified into three groups: 1,273 low-variability genes, 2,546 moderate-variability genes, and 1,273 high-variability genes (see Materials and Methods for details) (Fig. 1B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional characteristics for each group. The low-variability HKGs were predominantly enriched in fundamental transcription and translation processes, including mRNA splicing and RNA processing, suggesting that these genes are essential for basic cellular maintenance shared by all tissues. Moderate-variability HKGs were mainly involved in energy metabolism, protein catabolic processes, and RNA stability regulation, indicating their role in supporting tissue-specific functions and the regulation of regeneration and differentiation. Among these 1,273 low-variability HKGs, 575 (11.29%) continuously expressed genes were identified by applying a threshold of Log2(Max/Min) ≤ 4 across tissues (Fig. 1B). After further filtering out genes with low expression levels (TPM < 100), 88 HKGs with high expression levels and low expression variability across tissues were obtained, which may serve as valuable experimental controls in gene expression experiments (Supplementary Table S3). Among the 88 candidates, HSP90AB1 and YWHAE were identified as consistently and highly expressed genes with low variability across tissues [26, 27]. Both genes have been widely reported as stable reference genes in vertebrate studies, supporting their potential utility in I. bannanicus. In addition, several other candidates, such as PHB2, PARP3, and BCCIP, also showed consistent expression and may serve as internal controls, although further experimental validation is needed.
To explore the potential functional contributions of 5,092 HKGs, pearson correlation analysis was performed on pairwise tissue comparisons (Fig. 1D). The results showed that, unlike the clustering of overall transcriptional trends, tissues with similar physiological functions did not always cluster closely, which is also consistent with the fact that most HKGs have little relationship with functional specialization of organs. However, the complexity of living organisms is reflected in the fact that there are often exceptional cases. Consistent with the full gene expression correlation patterns, spine (spn) and muscle (mu) grouped into one cluster, whereas blood (BL) formed a separate branch. This may indicate that some HKGs have already developed unique expression patterns and possible functional specialization in spine, muscle, and blood.

## Tissue-specific gene expression profiles
Due to potential differences in developmental stages and sampling criteria between the newly generated and publicly available I. bannanicus transcriptome datasets, samples from the same tissue were not merged across the two data sources. Moreover, the identification of tissue-specific genes (TSGs) can be influenced by anatomical subregions and developmental timing, which further justified analyzing the two datasets separately. Following this strategy, 2,169 TSGs were identified across 25 tissues (see Materials and Methods for details). Among these, the brain exhibited the highest number of TSGs (n = 429), followed by the ovary (n = 267). The number of TSGs in other tissues ranged from 8 to 256.
To assess lineage-associated genes involved in organ development, all tissues were classified into ten major organ systems. The reproductive system contained the greatest number of TSGs (n = 702), while TSG numbers in the remaining systems ranged from 8 to 603 (Fig. 2A). GO enrichment analysis of TSGs within each organ system revealed that most of these tissue-specific genes were associated with the functional specialization of corresponding organs (Fig. 2B). Immune-related terms dominated in the spleen, blood, skin, and lung, suggesting strong immune contributions from both classical and unconventional immune organs. In the digestive system (intestine, liver, stomach, gallbladder), enriched GO terms such as digestive system process (GO:0022600), lipid digestion (GO:0044241), and organic anion transport (GO:0015711) were associated with nutrient processing and metabolic activity. TSGs from the respiratory system, which includes lung and skin in amphibians, were enriched in pathways related to mast cell activation involved in immune response (GO:0002279), glycoprotein biosynthetic process (GO:0009101), and lung epithelium development (GO:0060428), reflecting both gas exchange and immune defense functions (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S5). ## Gene co-expression network and hub gene analysis
To explore the biological relationships and potential functions of core driver genes across tissues, a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted for I. bannanicus. The network was constructed using a total of 4,777 genes that passed expression filtering (average TPM > 1 across tissues). Using the network topology model fitting degree and mean connectivity, a soft threshold of 6 was chosen, ensuring that the gene connections followed a scale-free network distribution (Supplementary Figure 3A, B). A hierarchical clustering tree was constructed to group genes with similar expression patterns into modules (Supplementary Figure 3C). Due to the inclusion of numerous tissue types, some of which are functionally similar or anatomically related, modules with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.4 with any tissue were retained as candidate modules. As a result, 14 co-expression modules were identified (Fig. 3A). Among these, all modules except “brown”, “lightcyan”, and “lightgreen” were found to be highly correlated with a single tissue or with multiple tissues within the same organ system, indicating potential tissue-specific regulatory programs (Supplementary Figure 3D and 4). Expression and GO enrichment analyses were performed for the selected key modules. The expression analysis revealed that genes in modules highly specific to a single tissue or organ system exhibited significantly higher expression levels across corresponding tissues compared to those in less correlated modules (Fig. 3A). Functional annotations of genes in tissue-specific modules corresponded to the functions of those tissues. Modules associated with the nervous system were enriched in synaptic regulation, neurogenesis, and behavioral processes. Immune and hematopoiesis related terms dominated the modules correlated with blood, spleen, skin, and lung. Modules linked to metabolic organs such as the liver and intestine were enriched for pathways related to protein processing, small molecule metabolism, and detoxification. Network connectivity (kTotal) and intramodular connectivity (kWithin) were calculated for each gene. For each module, the genes with top 5% of kWithin were defined as hub genes (HUBGs) and a total of 142 HUBGs were identified across 14 tissue-associated modules (Supplementary Table S8). Collectively, these results highlight the modular organization of gene expression in I. bannanicus, with distinct co-expression modules reflecting tissue or system-specific biological functions, and HUBGs potentially serving as key regulators of these processes.

## The conservation of gene expression patterns across species
To investigate species-specific gene expression patterns in I. bannanicus, transcriptome data from three additional amphibian species (M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis) were obtained for comparative analysis. HKGs, TSGs, and co-expression modules in these species were identified using the same analytical pipeline as applied to I. bannanicus. Orthologous genes between I. bannanicus and the three other species were identified to ensure valid cross-species comparisons. This approach allowed for a systematic comparison of gene expression patterns among species.
For HKGs, a total of 6,615, 8,760, and 8,877 genes were identified in M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis, respectively. After ortholog filtering, I. bannanicus shared 3,900, 4,457, and 4,488 orthologous HKGs with the three species, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). These results indicate a substantial degree of conservation in core gene expression across species. Although the number of I. bannanicus-specific HKGs was relatively small compared to the other species, these genes still reflect species-specific expression characteristics. A comparative analysis of the I. bannanicus-specific HKGs against all three species yielded a core set of 205 HKGs uniquely present in I. bannanicus but absent in the orthologous HKG sets of M. unicolor, R. bivittatum, and X. tropicalis (Fig. 4A). Using the same strategy, TSGs were identified in the three additional species. TSGs were first determined for each tissue within each species (Supplementary Table S4). To enable cross-species comparison, tissues that were shared between I. bannanicus and at least two of the other three species were selected. Orthologous gene filtering was then performed for these matched tissues. As a result, a total of 83, 31, 65, and 27 I. bannanicus-specific TSGs were identified in the kidney, liver, skin, and lung, respectively (Supplementary Figure 6A-D). GO enrichment analysis of these species-specific TSGs revealed several biological processes potentially underlying the unique physiological traits of I. bannanicus. Kidney-specific TSGs were enriched in xenobiotic transport, tissue remodeling, and immune-related processes. Liver-specific TSGs were associated with lipid metabolism, glucose regulation, and liver regeneration. In the skin and lung, enriched terms were primarily related to immune responses and tissue development, suggesting enhanced immune defense and respiratory adaptation in I. bannanicus (Fig. 4C).
To extend the comparison to gene regulatory networks, co-expression modules were also constructed for the three other species (Supplementary Figure 5A-C). Modules corresponding to those associated with specific tissues in I. bannanicus were identified, and orthologous genes were filtered. This revealed 39, 47, 172, and 72 I. bannanicus-specific module genes in kidney, liver, skin, and testis-related modules, respectively (Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Figure 6E-H). Functional enrichment analysis of the species-specific co-expression module genes in I. bannanicus revealed significant involvement in immune response, metabolic processes, skin development, and reproductive functions across kidney, liver, skin, and testis, respectively (Fig. 4D). Notably, immune-related pathways were enriched in both kidney and skin modules, consistent with TSGs enrichment results. This overlap highlights a robust and specialized immune capacity in I. bannanicus, underscoring its unique adaptation and biological characteristics among amphibians.

## Identification of gene loss related to limbs and eyes
Given the long-term subterranean lifestyle of I. bannanicus, this species exhibits characteristic regressive traits, including the loss of limbs and degeneration of the eyes. In contrast, X. tropicalis possesses well-developed limbs and functional vision. A comparative genomic analysis was conducted between the two species to identify single-copy genes present in X. tropicalis but absent in I. bannanicus. These genes were considered potential candidates, lost during the evolutionary history of I. bannanicus, and possibly contributing to its defects in the development of limbs and eyes. And the results of functional enrichment analysis of these genes also confirmed this: several pathways associated with eye development (e.g., phototransduction, VEGF signaling) and limb development (e.g., BMP signaling regulation, cell adhesion molecules) reached significant levels, highlighting possible molecular mechanisms underlying the regressive traits observed in I. bannanicus (Supplementary Table S9).
Seven genes associated with limb and eye development were identified as candidates lost in I. bannanicus (Supplementary Table S10). To further evaluate their genomic presence, sequence alignment was performed against the I. bannanicus genome. Among them, SMURF1, NOG, RCVRN.1, GNAT2, SAG, and GRK7 showed no alignment matches in the entire gene bodies or flanking regions within the I. bannanicus genome, suggesting possible complete deletions. In contrast, MAPK14 exhibited a large fragment deletion (Fig. 5C). ## Discussion
I. bannanicus was the first species of Gymnophiona to be discovered in China, which has been living on the earth for hundreds of millions of years, so it plays an extremely important role in studying the evolution of amphibians. The development of modern genomics technology provides a powerful means to analyze the genetic basis of phenotype and living habits of special species from the genome level. However, the genome of I. bannanicus is large and rich in repetitive sequences [10, 28], and the advancement of third-generation sequencing technologies has recently enabled the successful assembly of a chromosome-level genome [11]. The assembled genome spans approximately 12 Gb and includes the annotation of 23,143 protein-coding genes. With the availability of a high-quality reference genome, transcriptome-based analysis of gene expression patterns in I. bannanicus has become more accurate and informative. In this study, we performed transcriptome sequencing on 10 tissues (skin, blood, ovary, liver, kidney, lung, heart, stomach, spleen and testis; Supplementary Table S1), and additionally incorporated publicly available data from other tissues (brain, gallbladder, intestinal, lower jaw, muscle, spinal cord, spine, and upper jaw; Supplementary Table S2) for a comprehensive analysis. Our results will provide important basic data and important reference for the molecular mechanism research of I. bannanicus.

## Visual gene loss or ectopic expression is consistent with the burrowing adaptation in I. bannanicus
The cave environment is generally dark, and many burrowing animals have degenerated eyes. At present, the mainstream view is that the degeneration of the eye is related to energy, that is, maintaining vision requires a lot of energy. Torres-Sanchez et al. attributed the adaptation of other Gymnophiona species to low-oxygen cave life to the up-regulated expression of genes related to Oxidation–reduction (redox) processes [13]. In this study, some genes were also enriched in this pathway (Fig. 1C). Since energy consumption is important for many growth and development processes, it seems more in line with biological logic that the degeneration of the eyes leads to the reduction of energy consumption rather than the other way around. Therefore, our study also focused on the number and expression patterns of genes related to eye development in I. bannanicus. Comparative analysis revealed the potential loss of key phototransduction genes such as RCVRN.1 [29], GNAT2 [30], SAG [31], and GRK7 [32], which are essential for retinal signal transduction. The absence of these genes may contribute to the severe reduction of visual capacity observed in this species (Fig. 5C). Additionally, vision-related genes were unexpectedly enriched in non-visual tissues of I. bannanicus, including the eye-development regulator GPR98 in blood [33], as well as several vision-related genes such as DMBX1 [34], CRYBB2 [35], SLC24A1 [36], PDE6H [37], RHO [38], OPN1LW [39], LRIT3 [40], PDE6A [41], PRPH2 [42], USH2A [43], RBP3 [44], RS1 [45], KERA [46] and RAX2 [47] in the upper jaw tissue (Fig. 5A). The presence of such genes in non-visual tissues suggests possible ectopic expression, potentially reflecting remnants of ancestral developmental programs or regulatory shifts during adaptation. Together, visual gene loss, and ectopic expression in skeletal tissue, together with the enrichment of redox-related genes, provide transcriptomic clues to eye degeneration and hypoxia adaptation in I. bannanicus. However, it should be noted that the degeneration of eyes in I. bannanicus may involve additional genetic mechanisms, such as copy number variations, regulatory element mutations, or protein-coding sequence changes, which are not captured by transcriptome analysis alone. Future functional studies are needed to comprehensively understand the molecular basis of eye degeneration.

## Constitutive and/or tissue-specific expression of immune-related genes may underlie the robust immune traits in I. bannanicus
Transcriptomic analysis revealed widespread and tissue-enriched expression of immune-related genes in I. bannanicus. Several immune-function genes were identified among the HKGs, such as GSTP1 [48], CD109 [49], RNASEL [50], GCA [51], and RETN [52], all of which showed consistent expression across tissues, suggesting their fundamental roles in baseline immune function (Fig. 5B). Like other amphibians, the skin of I. bannanicus is an important innate immune organ and first line of defense against pathogens in the environment [53]. Amphibian skin is multifunctional with additional roles in respiration, water regulation, and in defense against predators and pathogens [54]. Torres-Sanchez et al. found that caecilian skin shows upregulation of biochemical genes, suggesting adaptations to burrowing distinct from frogs and salamanders [55]. In TSGs, MRGPRX2 [56] was highly expressed in skin, supporting its role in mast cell–mediated defense. Co-expression modules also revealed skin-enriched immune genes such as PIGR [57], PTX3 [57], and RAB3D [58], which are involved in mucosal immunity and antimicrobial responses. In other tissues, JAK2 [59] was specific to blood, LY86 [60] and PRSS2 [61] to spleen, and PSMB10 [62] and VIP [63] were found in modules associated with multiple digestive organs, highlighting diverse immune specializations across organs (Fig. 5B). These features together suggest that the composition and spatial expression of immune genes in I. bannanicus may contribute to its ability to cope with diverse pathogens and environmental challenges associated with a burrowing lifestyle.

## Loss of limb-related developmental genes may contribute to limb degeneration in I. bannanicus
Ovchinnikov et al. analyzed the genomes of Geotrypetes seraphini and Microcaecilia unicolor and found that the degradation of limbs was associated with missing the zone of polarizing activity regulatory sequence (ZRS) enhancer of Sonic Hedgehog [64]. Recent studies have suggested that the loss of limbs in I. bannanicus may be associated with the absence of key developmental genes such as GREM1, as well as the convergent degeneration of conserved noncoding regulatory elements involved in limb patterning. These elements are typically conserved in limbed vertebrates and active during limb development, indicating that both gene loss and regulatory decay may have contributed to the morphological specialization of limb reduction in caecilian [11]. In this study, several genes potentially lost in I. bannanicus, including SMURF1, NOG, and MAPK14, which are known to play important roles in limb development through the regulation of BMP signaling and related pathways (Fig. 5C). SMURF1 encodes a ubiquitin ligase that targets SMAD proteins in the BMP pathway [65]. NOG encodes a cystine-knot protein that functions as a BMP antagonist [66]. MAPK14 is a signaling kinase involved in cellular differentiation and developmental processes [67]. The absence of these genes may suggest a link to limb degeneration in I. bannanicus. Similarly, limb reduction may be influenced by multiple levels of genetic regulation beyond transcriptomic changes, including enhancer deletions, copy number variations, and sequence mutations. Our study can only provide transcriptomic insights, and further integrative genomic analyses will be necessary to fully elucidate the genetic mechanisms underlying limb degeneration.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study investigates the transcriptome profile of multi-tissues in I. bannanicus by integrating 27 newly generated RNA-seq datasets with 21 publicly available transcriptomes, aimed to explore the transcriptome atlas and provide new insights into ecological adaptation. A total of 5,092 housekeeping genes (HKGs) and 2,169 tissue-specific genes (TSGs) were identified. HKGs, TSGs, and co-expression module genes were found to be involved in tissue specialization and evolutionary divergence. The comparative transcriptomic analysis across multiple species and diverse tissue types reveals a list of genes related to sensory perception, limb loss and immunity. These findings preliminarily provide valuable clues into the molecular features that may contribute to ecological adaptation in I. bannanicus at a transcriptomic level and need to be further verification.
