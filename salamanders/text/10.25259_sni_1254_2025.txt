# Decompression alone for 62 isolated L1-2 lumbar disc herniation

## Abstract
Background: Upper lumbar disc herniations (ULDHs) are rare and present significant technical challenges due to the inability to retract the conus medullaris. Here, we reviewed our outcomes treating 62 patients with L1-2-disc herniations with open laminectomy/microdiscectomy (OLM) without fusion.Methods: Between 2005 and 2022, we performed 62 OLM procedures at the L1–2 level without fusion. Our analysis included demographic characteristics and outcomes assessments (Visual Analog Scale leg pain, Modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association score), plus 1-year postoperative dynamic X-rays to confirm stability.Results: L1-2-disc herniations occurred in 0.9% out of a larger series of lumbar discs we reviewed. Patients’ presenting symptoms typically included back (95%)/leg pain (82%), cauda equina symptoms/signs (64%), and neurogenic claudication (45%); more than 50% of patients had been symptomatic for over 1 year. Postoperatively, no discs recurred, and patients exhibited neither clinical nor radiological symptoms/signs of instability.Conclusion: The OLM technique for removal of L1-2 lumbar disc herniations resulted in adequate outcomes without evidence of spinal instability.


## INTRODUCTION
Upper lumbar disc herniations (ULDHs) are rare and constitute just 1–11% of all lumbar discs. At the L1-2 level, the thoracolumbar junction, one has to be careful to avoid retraction/compression of the conus medullaris. Here, we safely and successfully 62 L1/L2 limited open laminectomy with microdiscectomy (OLM) without fusions.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
Patients with L1-2 discs represented 0.9% of the total of 6643 one-level discectomies performed in our institution (2005 and 2022). Following IRB approval and utilizing multiple inclusion and exclusion criteria along with preoperative magnetic resonance (MR) scans, we prospectively reviewed electronic medical records for 62 patients with L1-2 disc herniations [Figure 1 and Table 1].

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria.
Patients averaged 60.4 years of age and included 40 (64.5%) females and 22 (35.5%) males [Table 2]. Almost all had back pain (95%) and/or leg pain (82%) but variably described; attendant symptoms/signs of cauda equina signs (64.5%); neurogenic claudication (45.1%); and sphincter dysfunction (25.8%). All patients underwent open laminectomy, medial facetectomy, and microdiscectomy without fusion and were followed for a minimum of 1 postoperative year. Patients with recurrent postoperative symptoms were routinely evaluated with MR ımaging, computed tomography, and dynamic radiography. Postoperatively, they were mobilized within 12–24 h and wore lumbosacral orthoses for 1–1.5 months. In addition, they had 1-year postoperative radiographs to document stability [Figures 1 and 2]. Preoperatively and postoperatively patients’ outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and Modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association Scale (mJOA).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics. Pre and postoperative clinical and pain scores were compared using paired t-tests. Univariate analyses were conducted using independent t-tests to evaluate the effects of pain duration and trauma history on clinical scores. Assumptions of normality for continuous variables were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

## RESULTS
The postoperative mJOA scores improved in 55 patients (meanΔ1.75 ± 1.03; P < 0.001); only 6 were unchanged, and 1 was worse. Leg pain VAS also decreased significantly in nearly all patients (n:60), with VAS scores decreasing from 7.50 ± 0.91 to 2.41 ± 0.91 (mean Δ 4.66 ± 1.69; P ≤ 0.0001); unchanged in only two patients [Table 2]. These results indicate generally favorable functional recovery and pain relief following OLM.
Radicular symptoms persisted for longer than 1 year in 36 patients; postoperative mJOA scores improved in 32 of these cases, while 4 patients remained unchanged. The mean increase in mJOA scores was 1.73 ± 0.96; however, this did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). All patients with symptoms >1 year showed postoperative leg pain VAS reduction, though duration did not significantly affect outcomes statistically (P > 0.05). Likewise, trauma history, present in 16 (25,8%) patients, showed no significant impact on postoperative improvement in either mJOA (P > 0.05) or VAS scores (P > 0.05) [Figure 3].
The distribution of patients’ symptoms.
Minor complications were observed in 6 cases (9.6%). 2 patients occurred dural tears due to severe adhesions. The remaining 4 complications included wound infections and temporary dysesthesias. No major complications were observed and no patients presented recurrence and evidence of increasing instability was found during the entire follow-up period.

## DISCUSSION
Owing to reduced segmental motion and increased posterior longitudinal ligament and laminar thickness, ULDH is less common, and no optimal surgical approach has been standardized.[12] Our findings demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes and no radiographic instability following OLM without fusion for L1–2 disc herniations. The low complication rate and absence of recurrence further support the safety and effectiveness of this technique.
Advanced age has been identified as a contributing factor for ULDH, with patients typically presenting at an older than those lower segments, consistent with the mean age of 60.4 years in our cohort.[4,5] Although Echt et al. reported a male predominance (50–93%) in their meta-analysis, the proportion of males in our cohort was lower (35.5%), suggesting population-based variation.[2] Although trauma-related structural changes (ligament injury and/or endplate disruption) have been proposed as contributors to ULDH, Karaaslan et al. reported that trauma is not a significant risk factor.[5] In our cohort, trauma history (25%) did not influence postoperative outcomes.
In the ULDH, the most common clinical features are back/leg pain, motor and/or sensory losses, reflex changes, and sphincter dysfunction.[2,3,11] Back pain has been reported in 75–95% of cases, consistent with our results, which we believe reflect greater accuracy due to the exclusion of patients with other spinal pathologies.[4,5] Leg pain has been reported at a comparable rate in previous studies, consistent with the 82.3% in our cohort. Although L2 root compression is typically expected to present as anterolateral thigh pain, a single herniation may affect multiple roots, resulting in variable clinical.[6,7] Therefore, variable radicular leg pain should raise suspicion for ULDH. In addition, the reported incidence of cauda equina findings in ULDH varies considerably (2.4–75%), likely due to differing definition criteria and position of the conus medullaris relative to the disc level.[5,6] In our study, cauda equina signs were identified in 64% of patients, a rate that may be related to our assessment of sacral root symptoms and/or bladder dysfunction as cauda equina findings. Although sphincter and multiple nerve root dysfunction are associated with worse reported outcomes, this finding did not affect postoperative mJOA scores in our series.
Although Lurie et al. reported that ULDH demonstrated the greatest benefit from surgery compared with nonoperative,[9] surgical management is often challenging. While unilateral partial hemilaminectomy with discectomy procedure has been used effectively for years, the main problem encountered is a narrow canal and conus medullaris, which cannot be safely retracted at the L1-2 level[4-6,10,13] [Table 3]. Therefore, sufficient exposure is key to performing safe and effective surgery at this level. Increased lateral exposure, and particularly full iatrogenic facetectomy, may result in instability often warranting fusion.[8] Lin et al., stated that the fusion group had a greater satisfaction than only the decompression; intraoperatively, fusion was necessary for more than 50% of laminectomy or with full facetectomy.[8] In contrast, the favorable postoperative improvements observed in our cohort suggest that OLM without fusion can achieve satisfactory results when compared favorably with studies focusing on L1–2 ULDH.[7] Lee et al., compared the lateral retroperitoneal approach, percutaneous discectomy, and OLM, reporting the worst outcomes in the percutaneous discectomy.[7] Ahn et al. noted that percutaneous discectomy might yield better results in younger patients and in cases of lateral disc herniations.[1]

This study has limitations inherent to single-center, low-incidence pathology research. The rarity of L1–2 disc herniations prolongs case accumulation, and expanding inclusion criteria to multilevel or recurrent cases would have increased sample size at the expense of bias. Future studies comparing particularly endoscopic discectomy, with OML, would provide valuable guidance.

## CONCLUSION
In our series of 62 single-level L1–2 disc excisions performed with open laminectomy, microdiscectomy, medial facetectomy, and foraminotomy, without fusion, as no significant morbidity, reoperation, or postoperative instability was identified during follow-up.
