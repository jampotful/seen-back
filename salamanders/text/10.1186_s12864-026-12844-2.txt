# A transcriptome analysis revealing the molecular mechanism of light intensity-driven color variation in the skin of Chinese giant salamander (Andrias davidianus)

## Abstract
The body color of juvenile Chinese giant salamander (Andrias davidianus) exhibits a lighter hue in a dimly lit environments and a darker hue in well-lit environments, and this variation follows a circadian rhythm. To determine the effect of light on the body color of juvenile salamanders, three treatments were arranged in this study, including: natural light (C, control light, 12 h day/night), high light (H, 24 h day, 220 Lux), and complete darkness (B, 24 h night, 0 Lux). Skin samples were collected for RNA sequencing after 12 days, and skin, blood, liver, brain, and eyes were gathered on days 0, 4, 8, and 12 for skin density measurement, tissue sectioning, enzyme activity detection, and qPCR validation to analyze the effect of light on the characterization and molecular regulation of the body color. The results showed that with constant lighting, the skin color significantly darker, exhibiting the lowest average gray level and highest average optical density. HE staining showed a significant increase and clustering of melanin particles between the epidermis and glands. The activity of tyrosinase in the skin was highest on the days 8 in group H. Both continuous light and darkness significantly increased the activity of CAT in the liver and serum. qPCR validation showed that key genes involved in various melanin biosynthesis pathways (such as Tyr, Tyrp1, Pmel) were generally upregulated in the group H. In summary, light can activate the pigment synthesis pathway, promote melanin deposition and gene expression, and enhance antioxidant enzyme activity. However, continuous exposure to light and darkness can disrupt the circadian rhythm in the giant salamander, inducing feedback changes in certain genes. The giant salamander adapts to its environment by coordinating melanin deposition and antioxidant defense, achieving dynamic changes in body color while clearing free radicals to mitigate oxidative damage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12844-2.

## Introduction
Coloration of animal's body, especially for aquatic animals, is a crucial phenotypic trait that aids in camouflage [1], UV protection [2], and social interaction [3], and is affected by genetic [4], environmental [5], and hormonal influences [6]. The body color of aquatic animals is primarily determined by pigment cells located on their skin or scales, with factors such as the number of these cells, their distribution area, and the spread and contraction of pigment granules influencing the color formation [7]. There are six main types of pigment cells in aquatic animals, which include erythrophores, xanthophores, melanophores, iridophores, leucophores, and rare cyanophores [8]. Melanophores are the most extensively researched and commonly found pigment cells in animals. Melanin is crucial for the color patterns seen on aquatic animals’ surfaces and is significantly linked to their growth and survival [9]. Melanogenesis is driven by intracellular melanogenic enzymes, including tyrosinase (TYR), tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TYRP1, also known as DHICA oxidase), and dopachrome tautomerase (DCT/TYRP2). Tyrosinase catalyzes the hydroxylation of L-tyrosine to L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) and the subsequent oxidation of L-DOPA to dopaquinone. In addition to these enzymatic reactions, melanogenesis is further regulated by α-MSH, endothelin-1, and UV radiation [10].
Light plays a crucial role in the environment of aquatic animals, influencing their survival [11], growth [12], metabolism [13], hormone release [14], and adaptability [15]. The light absorption of melanin leads to different skin pigmentation [16]. In amphibians, body color is not only related to camouflage and predation, but also closely related to temperature regulation, social behavior, and ecological adaptation [17–19]. Studies conducted recently have indicated that many amphibians can quickly and reversibly change their body color through pigment cells in response to environmental light signals. Its molecular basis involves the dynamic regulation of pigment particle movement and the pathway for melanin synthesis [20–22]. An elevated concentration of melanin in animals’ results in the production of darker body coloration, which is crucial for physiological defense mechanisms. Additionally, melanin effectively absorbs ultraviolet radiation, thereby mitigating DNA damage and reducing cellular oxidative stress induced by such radiation [23]. A close association between melanin-based pigmentation and antioxidant defense, which is often reflected by changes in CAT and SOD activities [18]. In tadpoles of (Hyla arborea), background-driven pigmentation adjustment has been linked to shifts in antioxidant defense and oxidative status [18], whereas in tadpoles of Western spadefoot toad (Pelobates cultripes), color change is rapid and reversible, is largely melanin-based, and accompanies measurable changes in redox status [24]. In vertebrate melanocytes, melanogenesis is also sensitive to redox balance, with the thioredoxin and glutathione systems implicated in shaping melanogenic responses under oxidative stress and UV-related ROS signaling [25].
Transcriptome sequencing serves as a robust tool for studying the molecular network that influences body color [2, 26]. RNA seq can be used to identify genes with differential expression in skin tissue under varying environmental conditions, facilitating the screening of key factors in melanin synthesis, transport, and upstream regulation. Previous research has revealed that environmental factors, including light, can modulate the body color of various aquatic animals by affecting the expression of genes related to pigmentation [27, 28]. In the latest transcriptome research on amphibians, the classic melanin biosynthesis pathway was investigated, revealing new regulatory genes associated with pigment plasticity, including those involved in metabolic remodeling and stress response [29, 30].
Andrias davidianus, the Chinese giant salamander, is the largest extant amphibian globally and is crucial for scientific, ecological, and economic [31]. With an ancient evolutionary history, it shows marked body coloration variation: most individuals are brown or black, while some display uniform or patchy yellow or pale coloration. These natural color morphs make it a useful non-model system for investigating pigment regulation and environmental adaptation [32, 33]. The rapid expansion of artificial breeding of A. davidianus in China has highlighted the practical importance of understanding phenotypic traits like body coloration for industry development. The rare whole-yellow (albino phenotype) is often regarded as having greater ornamental and educational value, which increases the demand for stable and predictable coloration in captive populations [26, 33]. Elucidating the molecular processes underlying these phenotypes is important not only for basic biology, but also for practice: it can support selective breeding and early screening of desirable traits, and it can inform husbandry optimization (including light-regime management) to improve the stability and consistency of coloration during captive rearing.
Currently, studies on the body color of giant salamanders have primarily focused on individual candidate genes, such as Tyr [33, 34], Mc1r [35], Agouti [36] and Mitf genes [37]. To date, no research has been conducted on the impact of environmental elements, especially light, on the body color of giant salamanders. Meanwhile, the RNA sequencing was used to investigate the regulatory mechanisms of skin pigmentation in albino individuals [26]. In the previous studies, we have explored the diurnal variation of body color in juvenile A. davidianus, under different photoperiods, juvenile A. davidianus displayed circadian rhythmicity with a changed period length, and the period of body-color oscillation differed across photoperiod regimes [38]. That indicated light and its periodic changes may be one of the key factors regulating the color depth of A. davidianus. Therefore, in this study, we conducted transcriptomic sequencing of skin that exposed in different light condition, analyzed the expression profiles of mRNA in skin, and further studied the skin sectioning, enzyme activity, antioxidant, and gene expression to elucidate the regulatory effect of light on the body color and healthy of A. davidianus. This investigation will contribute to the understanding of amphibian body color regulation and molecular adaptation networks, offering essential theoretical and practical implications for the conservation of endangered species and aquaculture production.

## Materials and methods

## Ethics statement
Experimental animals in this study were handled in accordance with the New International Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals (https://iclas.org/cioms-and-iclas/). The study was approved by the Animals Research and Ethics Committee of Jishou University (Approval code is: JSDX-2023–0038).

## Experimental larval and sampling
The same hatching batch larval A. davidianus were purchased from the Yuanhui Chinese giant salamander Breeding Cooperative, Zhangjiajie, China. Before experiment, the larval were reared in plastic breeding pot (53 × 39 × 15 cm) for one week under controlled conditions: constant temperature (19 °C) and natural light exposure (5–12 Lux during daytime, complete darkness at night). The larval fed bloodworm provision once every 48 h at 08:00, with uneaten residues removed after 2 h to prevent water contamination.
A total of 180 larval giant salamanders with similar body weight (4.5–5.0 g) and comparable baseline body coloration (visually screened by the same operator under identical conditions) were selected and randomly divided into three groups: control group (natural light, group C, 12-h light/12-h dark cycle), darkness group (0 Lux, group B, 24-h continuous darkness), and high-light group (220 Lux, group H, 24-h continuous illumination). Light intensity was measured using a handheld lux meter (Model S8608; Xiamen Meidemei Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). Artificial daylight conditions in group H were maintained using overhead 6500 K LED strips, providing spectral characteristics comparable to natural sunlight. Three replicate pots (20 individuals per replicate) were established for each treatment group in the 12-day experiment. Samples were collected on days 0, 4, 8, and 12 from each experimental group. Following MS-222 anesthesia (200 mg/L bath), cervical blood was collected into RNase/DNase-free tubes (Eppendorf, 1.5 mL), 4 °C overnight storage, centrifugation (3,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and supernatant collection. Skin tissue samples were divided into three aliquots: two replicates preserved in RNA stabilization reagent for transcriptome sequencing and gene expression analysis; one replicate fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for histological sectioning. Liver tissue for enzymatic activity assays, and brain, eye tissues was immediately immersed in BioSample Stabilizing Reagent (AG21015; Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, China) for gene expression analysis All specimens were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

## Quantification of skin melanin via average optical density and histological analysis
Skin melanin analysis was conducted as previously described [39]. Briefly, dorsal surfaces were photographed under controlled lighting using a camera. Melanin content was quantified as area-normalized absorbance optical density (AOD), where higher AOD values indicate greater pigment deposition and darker skin coloration. Area normalization was applied to reduce bias caused by inter-individual differences in body size and shape.
Tissue samples fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) were processed through graded ethanol dehydration series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned consecutively at 5–7 μm thickness. Following hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, histological sections were examined under a stereomicroscope (M205C; Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) to characterize pigment granule distribution patterns in skin. Further evaluate the differences in pigment deposition on the skin of A. davidianus under different treatment conditions.

## Tyrosinase and antioxidant enzyme activity assays
Tyrosinase activity in skin samples (Days 0, 4, 8, and 12) was measured using the Solarbio Acidic Tyrosinase Assay Kit (BC4055; Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities in serum and liver tissues were determined with CheKine™ kits (KTB1030 for SOD, KTB1040 for CAT; AbbKine, Wuhan, China) employing WST-1-based 96-well microplate colorimetry and colorimetric methods, respectively. All assays were performed with triplicate biological and technical replicates.

## RNA isolation, library preparation and sequencing
At day 12, nine skin samples, with three biological replicates per group, were subjected to total RNA extraction Using Trizol reagent. The integrity of the extracted RNA was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and its purity was checked with the NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The cDNA libraries were generated from poly(A) + mRNA enriched by oligo(dT) magnetic beads, which targets poly(A) tails through A-T complementary base pairing. The mRNA was fragmented using a fragmentation buffer to generate short segments, followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis employing random hexamers (6-mer primers) with the fragmented mRNA as template. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized with buffer, dNTPs, and DNA polymerase I, followed by purification using AMPure XP beads. The purified dsDNA was subjected to end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, followed by size selection using AMPure XP beads and PCR amplification to generate the final cDNA library. Library quality was verified by Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Subsequently, nine cDNA libraries were generated and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp reads) by Frasergen Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

## De novoassembly
Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled using SOAPnuke (v2.1.0) to remove adapter contaminants and low-quality sequences (Q20 < 90%), yielding clean reads. Due to no A. davidianus reference genome, these reads were assemble using Trinity software. A separate de Bruijn graph was constructed for each contigs set and then the reads were aligned back to remove the sequencing error path and use the dynamic programming scoring algorithm to obtain the final transcripts.

## Analysis of differentially expressed genes
The genes' expression level was calculated by RSEM after HISAT2 alignment and transcript reconstruction with Stringtie. Subsequently, gene differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software following FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase Million) normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of Genes with |log2(fold change) |≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered as the differentially expressed genes for further analysis.

## Analysis of functional annotation and pathway enrichment
To annotate all genes function, the following databases were employed: Nr (NCBI nonredundant protein sequence database; accessed on 2023/01/29), SWISS-PROT (a non-redundant protein sequence database that is manually curated; accessed on 2021/10/08), GO (Gene Ontology database; accessed on 2021/09/01), COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins database; accessed on 2021/06/29), KOG (Clusters of Protein homology database; accessed on 2021/06/29), and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database; accessed on 2021/12/15).
The clusterProfiler R package was employed to carry out Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment and KEGG pathways analysis of DEGs. Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were identified based on a BH-FDR-adjusted q-value < 0.05. Multiple-testing correction for GO (and KEGG) enrichment p-values was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) procedure, and the adjusted p-values are reported as q-values.

## qPCR validation for mRNA expression
To verify the mRNA expression levels of selected genes. eight DEGs, including associated with pigmentation were selected for a qRT-PCR test. Total RNA was extracted from skin, brain, and ocular tissues of A. davidianus at 0, 4, 8, and 12 days using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (ER501, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Reverse transcription was carried out following the protocol of the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (M16325, Thermo Scientific). Primers were designed by Primer-BLAST in NCBI signal.
The qPCR reaction was performed on the QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with PerfectStart™ Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, AQ601-02-V2, AQ601, Beijing, China), Gapdh was employed as the internal reference control [33–35]. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and the primer information is shown in Supplementary Table 1. Gene relative expression levels were analyzed using the 2−△△ct method, and all experimental groups were analyzed in triplicates.

## Statical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple-range test. The results are presented as the mean ± SD, and P value < 0.05 was considered as a significant difference. All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism v10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and RStudio (R v4.3.1) with the pheatmap v1.0.12 and ggplot2 v3.4.0 packages.

The highest activity in Day 12 has no significant difference between two groups (P > 0.05). On Day 4, hepatic CAT activity in Group B was the highest among the three treatments (14.35 ± 0.22 nmol min⁻1 mg⁻1 protein), which was significantly higher than Group H and Group C (P < 0.05). Across the four sampling points, hepatic CAT activity showed a similar temporal pattern in all three groups, with the lowest values on Day 8 and the highest values on Day 12 (Fig. 2A). ## Discussion
Amphibians alter their body color to adapt to light and background changes, primarily to enhance crypsis and reduce photodamage [40]. Under strong light, skin darkens to improve protection against ultraviolet and visible radiation, while lighter coloration minimizes energy expenditure in matching the surroundings [24]. As the world’s largest amphibian, the giant salamander exhibits semi-cave-dwelling habits and diverse coloration, yet the role of light in regulating its pigmentation remains poorly understood. In this study, we elucidated the impact of light intensities on pigment deposition and antioxidant activity in the skin of the A. davidianus by employing transcriptome sequencing, histological analysis, enzyme activity assays, and gene expression profiling.

## Effects of different light conditions on color phenotype and enzyme activity of A. davidianus
Under intense light exposure, giant salamander skin exhibited maximal average optical density, with histological analysis revealing significant melanin granules accumulation- a photo-adaptive strategy analogous to observed in P. cultripes [24]. Tyrosinase (TYR), the rate-limiting enzyme in melanin biosynthesis, catalyzing the initial steps of tyrosine conversion to DOPA and dopaquinone [41]. High levels of tyrosinase activity supports melanin synthesis and deposition. Abnormal lighting conditions disturb the regular production of melanin and raise oxidative stress levels in the body. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation under extreme light conditions may activate the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway, which exerts negative feedback on the melanin synthesis pathway, reducing late-stage TYR activity to prevent excessive pigment deposition and mitigate oxidative damage [42, 43]. The slight elevated CAT levels in serum in the high light groups at 12 days likely reflects sustained ROS-induced stresses on the antioxidant system, consistent with observations in the H. arborea [44]. Notably, CAT and SOD activities in liver and serum showed tissue-specific and time-dependent responses to light stress, reflecting differential regulation of antioxidant defenses across compartments. This aligns with reports in aquatic invertebrates such as Litopenaeus vannamei where CAT activity changed significantly in hepatopancreas but not gills under chronic ammonia-N exposure, while SOD activity increased in both tissues [45].

## Comparative analysis of enriched signaling pathways of DGEs in skin under different light conditions
RNA-seq results revealed that down-regulated genes in the group H and group B compared with group C showed similar KEGG pathway enrichment patterns, primarily involving signal transduction, metabolism, and immunity pathways (e.g., cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and glycine, serine and threonine metabolism). Disruption of circadian rhythm by extreme light conditions may induce oxidative stress and impair cellular redox homeostasis, as molecular clocks play a critical role in regulation antioxidant defenses [46]. Notably, immune-genes and tyrosinase activity were down-regulated on Day 12, contrasting with previous studies finding that A. davidianus upregulates energy metabolism and antioxidant system under starvation stress, [47]. This discrepancy suggests that extreme light conditions (long-term darkness or continuous light) may redirect energy allocation from immune defense to basic metabolism processes (e.g., energy storage and repair), thus inhibiting the immune pathway activation.
In group H, the melanogenesis pathway was significantly up-regulated compared with group C and group B. This pathway directly regulates melanin synthesis, melanosome maturation, and pigment transport within melanocytes [48]. Long-term darkness specifically inhibits melanin production and tyrosine metabolic, reducing key genes expression and metabolic substrates supply, ultimately leading to lighter body color. In contrast, light exposure upregulation tyrosine metabolic pathway to supplies precursors for melanin synthesis and generate ROS-scavenging intermediates (e.g., dopaquinone), synergistically accelerating melanin deposition in the skin of A. davidianus [24]. While MAPK and Wnt signaling pathways have been implicated in aquatic vertebrate pigment cell development [49, 50], they were not significantly enriched in this study, possibly because MAPK primarily regulates early melanoblast differentiation rather than adult pigmentation maintenance [48]. Up-regulated genes in group B and group H Compared with group C were both significantly enriched in antigen processing and presentation, and proteasome pathways. In terms of energy maintenance and hormone levels, the long-term light group was more dependent on protein digestion and absorption and steroid hormone biosynthesis to maintain normal physiological functions, while the long-term dark group was more prone to glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, adrenergic, and thyroid hormone to adapt to the dark environment. This result implied that there might be differences in the response mechanism of A. davidianus to different light.
It is worth noting that the calcium signaling pathway was significantly enriched in both the group H and B, with greater marked enrichment in group B. Previous studies have demonstrated an association between Ca2+ signaling and pigmentation in vertebrates. In pigment cells, Ca2+ can influence melanosome transport and intracellular distribution, and it can also modulate melanogenic activity. In teleost fish, nonvisual photoreception in the skin has been shown to drive rapid light-dependent body color change and to involve Opsin 3 and Ca2+ related responses [51]. In medaka (Oryzias latipes), light induces a pituitary hormone-release mechanism, and Ca2+ imaging supports light evoked Ca2+ responses in pituitary endocrine cells that are associated with hormone release, including melanocyte-stimulating hormone, and subsequent pigmentation changes [14]. In African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), light modulates melanophore responses to alpha-MSH through signal-transduction crosstalk that includes steps dependent on Ca2+ [22]. In zebrafish (Danio rerio), signaling dependent on Ca2+ has been discussed in relation to pigment granule behavior, and regulation related to NFAT2 provides a mechanistic example connecting Ca2+ signals to pigmentation-related processes [52]. In alpaca (Vicugna pacos) melanocytes, Ca2+ influx through TRPM1 has been linked to melanogenesis [53]. Given that oxidative indices such as SOD were measured, calcium signaling as a second messenger may represent a plausible node linking changes in melanocyte function with changes in antioxidant enzyme activity, providing a potential explanation for their coordinated variation under different light conditions.

## Effects of light factors on the expression patterns of body color genes in different tissues of A. davidianus
Based on functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs, we identified 26 pigment-related genes in A. davidianus associated with tyrosine metabolism, melaninogenesis pathway, and functional annotation as pigments. Subsequently qPCR analysis the expression levels of body color-related genes, including Tyr, Tyrp1, Pmel, Mitf, Mc1r, and Kit, in the brain, eyes, and skin at 0, 4, 8, and 12 days. Compared with group C, Sox7s, Tnk2, Ada and Kit were down-regulated both in group H and group B, while Ednrb, Il12b, Adamt3, Wnt11 were up-regulated. Notably, 12 pigment-related genes (including Oca2, Dct, Tyr, Tyrp1, Pmel, Mlana, Gpnmb, etc.) were significantly higher in group H than in group B.
SOX family comprises approximately 20 transcription factors, each containing a high-mobility-group (HMG) domain that binds to downstream DNA sequences [54]. SOX family genes can both activate and inhibit melanin synthesis. Sox10 has been reported to co-activate the expression of Mitf alongside Pax3, and Sox10 can bind to the regulatory genes involved in melanin synthesis, including Tyr [55], Dct [56], and Tyrp1[57]. While Sox5, another member of the SOX family, can competitively bind to target genes of Sox10, such as Mitf and Dct [58]. In this study, Sox7s was significantly down-regulated in both groups B and H, consistent with previous findings that Sox7 is up-regulated in wild-type A. davidianus compared with albino individuals [26]. Disruption of circadian rhythm may contribute to this down-regulation, as Sox2 and Sox9 expression is known to be circadian-dependent [59, 60], and we also observed reduced expression of circadian genes (Per1, Vri, Nr1d1) in groups B and H. This result suggests that the disruption of circadian rhythm may affect gene expression.
Kit regulates melanocytes migration, survival, proliferation and differentiation [61] and interacts with Mitf, a crucial gene that is implicated in the formation of melanin and further regulates the development of melanocytes [62]. In Rex rabbits, Kit overexpression significantly increased melanogenic genes (Tyr, Mitf, Pmel, Dct) and promotes melanocyte proliferation and inhibits apoptosis [63]. The melanocortin 1 receptor (Mc1r) is recognized as a crucial regulator of pigmentation across various species [64]. The activation of Mc1r by its ligand, α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-Msh), promotes the production of eumelanin, resulting in darker pigmentation via the activation of intracellular cAMP/PKA signaling pathways [65]. Glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein b (Gpnmb), a proteomics analysis revealed that Gpnmb is also a melanosome protein [66], and it is regulated by Mitf as is Tyr, Tyr1, Dct, Pmel17, and Mart-1[67]. In this study, no significant enrichment of the cAMP/PKA pathway was detected at the transcriptome level, suggesting that Mc1r signaling to Mitf via this route is limited under our conditions. Although Kit has been reported to promote melanin synthesis, our study found that after the normal light–dark cycle was disrupted (i.e., continuous light or darkness), the expression of Kit on skin was significantly lower than that of the normal circadian rhythm group on days 4, 8, and 12. Similarly, the differentially expressed Mitf and Mc1r genes, which were not detected in the transcriptome, exhibited an expression pattern consistent with that of Kit following the cessation of the light–dark cycle. This suggests that circadian rhythm disruption may have a significant impact on the expression of these genes.
Pigmentation regulation is complex and involves neuroendocrine signaling and direct light perception [68]. The skin-brain axis enables bidirectional communication, where the central nervous system's response to stress leads to changes in skin function. Simultaneously, the skin itself can transmit stress signals to the brain, which is also reflected in the regulation of pigmentation [69]. Stress signals activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis to produce corticotrophin-releasing hormone (CRH), which leads to the production of proopiomelanocortin (POMC), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and increased cortisol. CRH directly inhibits melanogenesis but also indirectly promotes it by inducing histamine release from mast cells and through POMC and ACTH. Cortisol triggers the release of Substance P and nerve growth factor (NGF) from keratinocytes, both enhancing melanogenesis. This indicates that the dual regulation of the pigment system by the neural center reflects the complexity of the pigmentation mechanisms. Therefore, it is challenging to fully elucidate the response mechanisms of the body color of the giant salamander to light/dark factors solely through the expression of a single gene. Additionally, melanocytes respond directly to light, showing pigment aggregation or dispersion upon light stimulation, indicating they may contain visual pigments [70]. Visual opsins and phototransduction components are present in fish skin during pigmentation remodeling [71], with dermal opsin such as Short-Wavelength Sensitive 1 gene (SWS1) localized beneath chromatophores in a rapid color-changing fish, where chromatophore state alters the short-wavelength light reaching the opsin [72]. Opsin 3 in fish skin also mediates nonvisual light-driven body color change [52]. these findings suggest that skin photoreception may provide additional light input to pigmentation control alongside ocular vision. The giant salamander's eyes are adapted for low light, with many rod cells and few cone cells, suitable for its cave-dwelling, nocturnal lifestyle [73]. In aquaculture production, even in complete darkness, there are still diurnal variations in body color, but this stops with extended light exposure, indicating a possible internal color regulation influenced by light [38].
Among the selected genes, Tyr, Typr1, and Pmel were highly expressed in the brain, eyes, and skin across experimental stages. The main melanogenesis-related genes Tyr and Tyrp1 ware continuously enhanced under high light conditions, while Pmel showed a significant response to group H only in the mid-to-late stages. Mitf, Mc1r, and Kit expression were reduced or maintain a certain level in groups B and H with prolonged light/darkness exposure. As upstream regulator, Mitf, Mc1r, and Kit promote the expression of downstream Tyr and Tyrp1 expression via signal transduction cascade of [39]. Skin tyrosinase activity correlates with Tyr and Pmel expression in the group H and with Tyr and Tyrp1 expression in group B. It is speculated that Tyr may be the most direct responsive factor in the giant salamander body color adaptation. Furthermore, photoperiod not only affects the animal's coloration, circadian rhythms, and antioxidant capacity, but the organism's response to light is multifaceted. Although current research findings struggle to clarify the relationships among these three aspects, they suggest that a single phenotypic change is the result of multiple complex physiological processes.

## Conclusion
This study employed skin histology, tyrosinase activity assays, serum/liver antioxidant enzyme measurements and skin transcriptomics to investigate light effects on A. davidianus. Disrupted circadian rhythms under prolonged light or darkness altered body color and antioxidant performance, with distinct molecular responses between the two conditions. While constant light deepened body color, it also elevated free radical levels and activated the antioxidant system, whereas constant darkness may trigger an endogenous body color regulation mechanism. Pigment-related genes Tyr, Tyrp1, and Pmel exhibited dynamic expression across the brain, eyes, and skin in groups H and B, while Mitf, Mc1r, and Kit remained relatively stable, indicating that the response of tyrosinase synthesis to light factors is more pronounced. The findings provide a theoretical basis for subsequent research on amphibian body color, antioxidant capacity, and circadian rhythms interactions.
