ABSTRACT: The European blind cave salamander Proteus anguinus, or olm, is an endangered
species endemic to the Dinaric Karst with specific ecology, biology and reproduction. Despite its
importance in sustainable conservation, the reproductive biology of the olm is still poorly understood due to the absence of sexual dimorphism, sex chromosomes being homomorphic, gonadal
anomalies, a prolonged reproductive cycle and drawbacks of the current methodology. To gain
insight into the sex identification and reproductive cycles of the olm, we combined visual observations and histological examinations of the gonads with in vitro analyses of vitellogenin, sex
hormones and their metabolites. The applied approach revealed the hormonal oscillations during
the reproductive cycles and the variability of sex-related traits and allowed a comparison of the
methods used. Detection of cloacal swelling and observable oocytes has proven to be straightforward, yet only moderately reliable, and is limited to specific periods of the reproductive cycle.
In contrast, analysis of vitellogenin and sex steroids in blood plasma provided insights into the
dynamics of gametogenesis, while the ratio of testosterone to 17-b-estradiol was shown to be a
reliable marker for sex identification. Analysis of sex steroid metabolites in fecal samples proved
to be a promising non-invasive approach for long-term monitoring of hormonal oscillations. The
advantages, disadvantages and reliability of methods for identification of sex and phases of the
reproductive cycle, which are essential for further conservation efforts of this endangered
amphibian, are discussed.

1. INTRODUCTION
The European blind cave salamander Proteus anguinus Laurenti, 1768, also known as olm, is a fully aquatic
and neotenic cave salamander and the only European
representative in the family Proteidae. The olm is also
the only troglobitic tetrapod in Europe and one of the
largest cave animals in the world. The species is currently divided into a white (P. a. anguinus) and a black

(P. a. parkelj) subspecies (Sket & Arntzen 1994) (Fig. 1).
However, recent studies have recognized individual
populations of the olm as deeply divergent phylogeographic lineages, most likely representing distinct species (Recknagel et al. 2023). The white subspecies inhabits the subterranean waters of the Dinaric Karst in
Italy, Slovenia, Croatia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina
(Sket 1997), while the range of the highly endemic
black subspecies is restricted to about 3 km2 in Bela

Krajina, SE Slovenia (Gorički et al. 2017). The species is
protected by national laws in all countries of its range,
classified as Vulnerable with a decreasing population
trend on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species
(Miaud et al. 2024) and listed as a priority species in
Annexes II and IV of the European Union’s Habitats
Directive (92/43/EEC; EC 2004). Due to its obligate
neoteny, its extreme longevity (reaching lifespans of
>100 yr) (Voituron et al. 2011), its low reproductive rate
(Juberthie et al. 1996) and its strictly aquatic lifestyle
confined to subterranean waters beneath permeable
limestone, the olm is highly vulnerable to changes in its
environment, induced predominantly by anthropogenic activities. These include direct destruction of the
subterranean habitats, overexploitation of underground waters and their pollution by toxic chemicals,
pesticides, heavy metals and organic pollutants from
sewage and agricultural activities (summarized in Kostanjšek et al. 2023). Recently identified threats include
opportunistic infections, emerging pathogens (Kostanjšek et al. 2023) and pollution of subterranean waters
by microplastics (Balestra et al. 2022). Considering the
wide range of threats, the vulnerability of wild populations of olms is evident, emphasizing the urgent need
for conservation measures, including the establishment
of long-term captive populations.

The reproductive biology of the olm, as observed in
captive animals, is very specific, compared to other
amphibians. Studies have shown that they reach
sexual maturity between 11 and 15 yr of age (Juberthie
et al. 1996) and can also reproduce at the age of 80 yr
(Ipsen & Knolle 2017). Females have very long intervals between egg-laying events (on average, every 6 to
12.5 yr) (Juberthie et al. 1996), while the frequency of
mating in males remains unknown. However, research
based solely on the histology of testes of wild-caught
animals indicates a duration of a spermatogenic
period of about 2 yr (Bizjak Mali 2017). Due to stable
cave environments, olms do not reproduce seasonally;
however, it has been observed that in males, gametes
tend to mature during winter (Bizjak Mali 2017) and
females lay eggs with a slight preference for wintertime
(Juberthie et al. 1996). Considerable morphological
variation and several anomalies have been identified
in the olm’s gonads. Viable diplotene oocytes were
present in testes (testis-ova) of one-third of studied
males (Bizjak Mali 2017), while in females, numerous
degenerating oocytes (follicular atresia) can be commonly observed (Bizjak Mali & Bulog 2011). In addition, an apparently functional testis and ovary were
also recorded in a single individual (Bizjak Mali 2017).
The combination of the above-listed features of olms,
their highly specific environmental requirements,
conservation status, and consequently, the limited
number of animals available for research, considerably
limits experimental approaches on these amphibians
and observations of their reproductive cycle in controlled environments.
Despite extensive experience in keeping olms in
captivity, reproductive success and research of their
reproductive biology are considerably impaired by
the limited possibilities of sex identification. Olms are
not sexually dimorphic, and sex identification based
on external morphology is therefore unreliable and
limited to a relatively short period just before reproduction, when maturing oocytes can be observed
through the abdominal wall in females, while males
have swollen cloaca (Briegleb 1962). It is also not possible to identify the sex based on sex chromosomes,
as these are homomorphic and have identical appearance in both sexes (Sessions et al. 2016). The estimated genome size of the olm ranges between 48.4
and 51.5 pg (Gregory 2004) and is therefore one of the
largest among vertebrates and is still being sequenced (Kostanjšek et al. 2022). In addition, no sexspecific markers have been identified so far; moreover, sex-specific genetic markers in amphibians are
species-specific (Yoshimoto et al. 2008, Smith & Voss
2009, Hu et al. 2019). Ultrasound imaging to visual-

ize the gonads of olms has been explored, but the
results showed an unexplained bias towards females
and only 1 animal has had its sex confirmed after
necropsy (Höltze et al. 2017). In general, ultrasonographic visualization of the reproductive organs in
amphibians is considered difficult, especially in
males (Schildger & Triet 2001, Ruiz-Fernández et al.
2020). To accurately identify the sex of olms, more
recent methods have focused on identifying females
at the stage of active egg-yolk deposition by detecting the protein vitellogenin (Vtg) in their blood
plasma (Gredar et al. 2019). Apart from females in the
later stages of oogenesis, necropsy therefore remains
the only accurate method for sex identification.
In the field of endocrinology, including in amphibians, blood stands as the prevailing sample used for
evaluation of endocrine activity and studies of reproductive cycles (Bolafﬁ & Callard 1979, Licht et al.
1983, Tanaka & Takikawa 1983, Lynch & Wilczynski
2005, Galligan et al. 2021). A non-invasive alternative
to sampling blood hormones is the analysis of their
metabolites in feces, which is a well-established tool
in the research of mammal reproduction and welfare
(Palme 2019). While fecal metabolites of stress hormones have been measured in several amphibian species (Cikanek et al. 2014, Nagel et al. 2019), the analysis of fecal metabolites of sex steroids (e.g. estradiol,
testosterone [T], progesterone) have rarely been
characterized and are so far limited to Anurans (Szymanski et al. 2006, Hogan et al. 2013), despite the
potential of new insights into the reproductive biology of Urodelans, including the olm.
The reproduction of olms in captivity has so far
relied on chance and the presence of several adult
animals in an aquarium. For successful captive breeding, however, it is crucial not only to know the sex of
the animals but also to understand their reproductive
cycle and detect possible gonadal abnormalities.
Research is also limited by the number of animals
available, as the species is endangered and protected.
Therefore, systematic breeding would not only contribute to conservation efforts but would provide animals for scientific research, as this species is particularly important for understanding evolutionary
developmental mechanisms, especially adaptation to
the cave environment, regeneration and longevity.
Moreover, accurate sex identification would also contribute to the understanding of the structure and
dynamics of wild populations.
The main objective of our study was to improve the
current knowledge of the reproductive biology of the
olm in terms of reproductive cycles and hormonal
oscillations during gametogenesis and to develop a

reliable methodology for sex identification combining external morphology, in vitro analyses of Vtg and
sex hormones in blood plasma and analysis of hormonal metabolites in feces.

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Animals
The study was performed using a captive population
of 13 animals kept in the cave laboratory of the Chair of
Zoology, Department of Biology, Biotechnical Faculty,
University of Ljubljana. The animals included in the
study comprised 12 individuals of the white subspecies
of the olm Proteus anguinus anguinus (11 individuals
from the population in the Planina cave in SW Slovenia
and a single individual from the population in the
Stobe cave in SE Slovenia) and a single individual of the
black subspecies P. a. parkelj (collected in Bela Krajina
in SE Slovenia). Details on the animals, the observation
periods (from 2 mo to 6 yr) and the analyses performed
are given in Table S1 in the Supplement at www.intres.com/articles/suppl/n057p453_supp.pdf.
All animals were housed in separate aquaria in filtered, UV-treated, aerated and dechlorinated tap
water, in constant darkness and at a temperature
between 11 and 13°C, replicating the conditions of
their natural environment. During the study, Male 2
and Female 2 were identified as a potential breeding
pair based on external morphology (swollen cloaca in
the male and visible oocytes >3 mm in diameter in the
female) and subsequently housed in the same aquarium. The animals were fed weekly with enchytraeid
worms only. The exact age of the animals was not
known. Based on their total body size (ranging between 21 and 28 cm) and body mass (ranging between
15.7 and 41.6 g), all animals were considered adults
(Durand & Delay 1981). All animals appeared healthy
and exhibited normal behavior during the study.
The capture and handling of the animals was carried out under permits No. 35601–8/2016–4 and
35601-27/2021-8 issued by the Ministry of the Environment and Spatial Planning and U34401–25/2013/
11 of the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Food
of the Republic of Slovenia.

2.2. External morphology
The external morphology of the animals was documented by photographs every few months. Particular attention was paid to the potential swelling of the

cloacal region in males (indicating active spermatogenesis) and the visibility of growing oocytes in the
body of the females (indicating active oogenesis).

2.3. Histology of gonads
The sex of 8 animals was confirmed by necropsy
performed for other research purposes and the
gonads were examined histologically to validate the
analysis of plasma hormones and fecal metabolites
(Table S1). To remove the gonads, animals were
euthanized with a 1 % aqueous solution of 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate (MS-222) buffered with
sodium bicarbonate to a neutral pH, and then the
gonads and other organs (liver, intestine, etc.) were
removed. The removed organs were fixed in 10 %
buffered formalin and preserved in 70 % ethanol in the
archive collection of the Department of Biology of the
Biotechnical Faculty, University of Ljubljana. These
are the standard procedures used in the department’s
collection for further studies of these endangered animals, reducing the need for animal sacrifice.
For histology, the fixed gonads were dehydrated
through graded alcohol, cleared in xylene, and
embedded in paraffin. Serial 8 μm thick sections were
stained with hematoxylin-eosin (Presnell & Schreibmann 1997). Slides were examined by light microscopy. Meiotic cells were staged according to Bizjak
Mali (2017) and Dumont (1972).

2.4. Blood sampling
Blood samples from 5 males and 6 females were collected as described in Gredar et al. (2018). Briefly,
animals were anesthetized in a 0.03% MS-222 solution
buffered with sodium bicarbonate to a neutral pH,
and blood was sampled by cardiac puncture using a
heparinized needle and syringe. The blood sample
was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to obtain
plasma, which was stored at –73°C until analysis.

2.5. Liquid chromatography-tandem
mass spectrometry
Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry
(LC-MS/MS) was used for quantification of T, 17-bestradiol (E2), 5a-dihydrotestosterone (5a-DHT) and
androstenedione (A4) in blood plasma.
Sample preparation involved liquid-liquid extraction with methyl-tert-butyl ether (MTBE) (Sigma-

Aldrich, #1634-04-4). First, stock solutions of unlabeled steroid were prepared gravimetrically, followed
by dissolution in methanol (Honeywell/Riedel-de
Haen; #34966). A working profile calibration solution
containing all monitored steroids was prepared by
mixing the appropriate volumes of each steroid stock
with methanol at a final steroid concentration of 1 μg
ml–1. Ten-point calibration curves (except zero and
blank) were prepared in 0.1 % bovine serum albumin
(BSA; Karl Roth; #2834.2) (w/v) in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, #D8537-500).
Then, 20 μl of plasma, or calibrator was diluted with
400 μl of PBS and an internal standard mixture containing d2-E2 (Sigma-Aldrich, #E4260; Chemical
Abstracts Service [CAS]: 53866-33-4) and [13C3]-T
(Sigma-Aldrich, #730610; CAS: 327048-83-9) was
added to each sample. The mixture was incubated for
15 min at room temperature. After incubation, 750 μl
of MTBE was added, and the samples were vortexed
at 1400 rpm for 10 min to facilitate phase separation.
The upper organic layer was transferred to a clean
tube. This extraction process was repeated 2 more
times to maximize recovery. The combined organic
extracts were dried under vacuum in a concentrator
(Savant SPD 31 DDA-230; Thermo Fisher Scientific).
The dried residue was reconstituted in 60 μl of 70 %
methanol containing 0.2 mM ammonium fluoride
(NH4F; Sigma-Aldrich, #338869; CAS: 12125-01-8)
prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.
LC-MS/MS analysis of E2 (CAS: 50-28-2; #142406;
Steraloids) was performed as described previously
(Pavlič et al. 2021). LC-MS/MS analysis of A4 (CAS:
63–05–8; #A6030–000; Steraloids), T (CAS: 58–22–
0; #B6500; Sigma Aldrich) and 5a-DHT (CAS: 521–
18–6; #A2579–000; Steraloids) was performed as previously described (Gjorgoska & Rižner 2024). For
both methods, chromatographic separation was performed on a Shimadzu Nexera XR HPLC system
using a Kinetex 2.6 XB-C18 (100 × 4.6 mm) column
(#00D-4496-E0, Phenomenex) and mobile phases A
(5 % methanol in H2O with 0.2 mM NH4F) and B
(methanol with 0.2 mM NH4F). For E2, a linear gradient from 30 % to 96 % B was applied at 1.0–3.0 min at
a flow rate of 0.5 ml min–1, followed by a wash with
96 % B until 8 min and equilibration at starting conditions until 15 min. For A4, T and DHT, a linear gradient from 30 % to 96 % of mobile phase B was applied
for 10 min at a flow rate of 0.5 ml min–1, followed by a
wash step with 96 % B until 12 min and equilibration at
starting conditions from 12.01 to 15 min.
The eluate was then injected into a Sciex 3500 triple
quadrupole mass spectrometer operated in negative
electrospray ionization (ESI) mode for E2 and in posi-

tive ESI mode for A4, T and DHT. The MS parameters
for E2 were as follows: curtain gas, 50 psi; collision
gas, 8 psi; ion spray voltage, −4500 V; source temperature, 600°C; and ion source gas 1 and 2, 40 and
80 psi, respectively. The MS parameters for A4, T and
DHT were as follows: curtain gas, 45 psi; collision gas,
8 psi; ion spray voltage, 5000 V; source temperature,
700°C; and ion source gas 1 and 2, 60 and 40 psi,
respectively. Data processing and quantification were
performed using Analyst 1.6 software (Sciex), which
utilizes the peak area ratio of analyte to internal
standard for quantification. The lower limit of quantification for all analytes was 10 pg ml–1.

2.6. Detection of Vtg
We examined the presence of Vtg, a female-specific
protein, in blood samples. The presence of Vtg was
analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide
gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), as described previously (Gredar et al. 2019). Briefly, 2.5 μl of blood
plasma was denatured and loaded onto a 7 % resolving
gel. After electrophoresis, the proteins were stained
with a commercially available dye (SimplyBlue Safe
Stain, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the presence of
Vtg was confirmed by detecting a band with a molecular mass of 190 kDa, specific for this protein (Gredar
et al. 2019).

2.7. Fecal sample collection
The feces of 3 males and 4 females were collected.
The aquaria were inspected daily, and fecal samples
were collected in 1.5 ml tubes. The water was
removed as much as possible. All fecal samples were
stored at –20°C until further processing. A different
number of samples (from 4 to 30) were analyzed for
each individual animal (Table S1).
In addition to the captive adults, fecal samples of 5
captive juveniles of total body length between 10 and
13 cm were collected for a comparative analysis of
fecal hormone metabolites with those of adult olms.
The juveniles had hatched in captivity in June 2016
from eggs laid in February in the aquarium of the
Postojna tourist Cave (SW Slovenia) (Bizjak Mali
2017). Juveniles were kept in separate tanks with filtered and UV-treated underground water at 11°C and
fed enchytreaeid worms (1 worm wk–1). Fecal samples of the juveniles were collected monthly over a
period of 2 yr (from January 2019 to December 2020)
(Table S1).

2.8. Determination of fecal metabolites
Prior to analysis, fecal samples were thawed, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min and the upper water
phase was removed. These water phases were kept
for detection of the potential presence of hormone
metabolites, which would reduce the amount of
measured metabolites. The sedimented fecal samples were weighed and 0.5 ml of 90 % methanol was
added, followed by shaking for 30 min. The mixture
was centrifuged at 2500 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was then transferred into a new tube, and
the steps of methanol extraction, shaking and centrifugation were repeated. The supernatants were
combined and dried using a vacuum centrifugal
concentrator (MiVac). The dried extracts were
reconstituted in 1 ml of 100 % methanol, sonicated
for 10 min and divided into 2 equal parts (one for
fecal estrone [fE1m] and testosterone [fTm] metabolite analysis, and the other for epiandrosterone
[fEpiAm] metabolite analysis). The samples were
dried again and reconstituted in 200 μl of the assay
buffer from the corresponding assay.
The considerably smaller fecal samples (in range
between 2.6 and 26.2 mg) obtained from juveniles
were reconstituted in 100 μl of the assay buffer to
ensure detectable concentration of the metabolites.
fE1m, fTm and fEpiAm were quantified by an
enzyme immunoassay (EIA) using a commercial DetectX kit from Arbor Assays (K031, K032 and K063,
respectively). Assay analyses were performed in
duplicate, with acceptance criteria for intra-assay and
inter-assay coefficient of variation (% CV) below 15 %.
The % CV was calculated from the obtained concentrations (pg of metabolite ml–1).
Measurements were performed using Cytation5
and concentrations were determined using the Four
Parameter Logistic (4PL) Curve (http://www.myas
says.com/four-parameter-logistic-curve.assay). For all
assays, results were excluded if the ratio between the
percentage of bound steroid and the maximum binding (% B/B0) was at either end of the standard curve,
i.e. <20 % or >80 %.
To assess the hormonal cycles of the olms, hormone metabolites were collected and analyzed over
a period of up to 4 yr. The frequency of defecation
of the individuals varied, ranging from 1 feces per
4 mo to 6 feces mo–1, and ideally 1 fecal sample
mo–1 was analyzed for each of the total of 7 animals. Due to the uneven defecation and the expected slow reproductive cycles of the olm, we
averaged the concentration of hormone metabolites
obtained over 3 mo.

3.4. External morphology
Fig. 4 shows the abdominal view of 2 females and
2 males. In 6 of the 8 females examined in this study,

observation of oocytes to oviposition in Female 1

female olms. This female also had a swollen cloaca
about 8 mo before oviposition (Figs. 3 & 4D). An even
longer period of visible oocytes lasting 33 mo was
observed in Female 2 (Fig. 2, Table S1), after which
the eggs degenerated. Oviposition was also not observed in the remaining females in which the oocytes
were underdeveloped, or the oocytes degenerated
(Fig. 2, Table S1).
In 4 out of 5 examined males, the swollen cloaca
was observed at least at some point during longterm observation (up to 6 yr) (Table S1, Fig. 4B).
The period of observable swollen cloaca varied between the males, ranging from 2 to 14 mo (Table S1).
In Male 1 and Male 3, the cloaca appeared to be
swollen twice (2 yr and 3 mo apart, and 5 mo apart,
respectively). However, cloacal swelling in males
did not correlate with increases in hormone metabolites (Fig. 3).

3.5. Gut passage time
After feeding the glitter-marked enchytraeid
worms to 3 individual olms, the glitter was observed
in the first feces thereafter. The frequency of defecation varied from animal to animal, and the time interval between feeding and observation of glitter in the
first feces was 7 d, 16 d, and 2 mo, respectively.

4. DISCUSSION
This is the first study combining visual observations, histology, vitellogenesis and endocrinology for
sex identification and study of reproductive cycles in
olms Proteus anguinus. Playing a central role in the
development of sex-related traits and in reproduction, sex steroids were the first target of the study.
In male amphibians, T and 5a-DHT are considered
the major plasma androgens, while the concentration
of A4 is generally lower (Kime & Hews 1978, Garnier
1985, Canosa & Ceballos 2002). The same pattern was
observed in the olm. Compared to other neotenic salamanders, the concentrations of circulating androgens in male olms were in the range of the axolotl
Ambystoma mexicanum (Eisthen & Krause 2012) and
lower than in the common mudpuppy Necturus maculosus, the only other known genus of the family Proteidae (Bolafﬁ & Callard 1979) (Table 2). The concentration of circulating E2 in female olms was also
within the range of that previously reported for A.
mexicanum (Eisthen & Krause 2012) and N. maculosus
(Bolafﬁ & Callard 1979), while T and 5a-DHT concentrations were lower in female olms compared to N.
maculosus (Bolafﬁ & Callard 1979). Despite the variability in measured hormone concentrations in the
blood of olms, the ratios of androgens to estrogens
have proven useful for distinction between sexes. We

identified the T/E2 ratio as an accurate hormonal sex
indicator, with significantly lower values in females
(<0.2) compared to males (>1.6). The reliability of the
T/E2 ratio obtained from a single blood sample as a
sex marker was confirmed by anatomical observations of the gonads at necropsy and/or by observable
oocytes in the body cavity of live females.
Hormone profiles during the female reproductive
cycle reflect the major stages of gametogenesis, and
their relationship to blood sex steroids has been studied in several amphibians. In general, oocyte follicles
produce low concentrations of androgens and estrogens in the early stages of oogenesis, while their
concentration increases during oocyte maturation.
High concentrations of E2 induce the synthesis of Vtg
in the liver, whose presence in the blood marks the
beginning of the vitellogenic phase of oogenesis (Fortune 1983, Iela et al. 1986). The observed variations in
sex steroids between individuals are therefore most
likely due to different stages of gonadal maturation
(Table 1). For example, 2 consecutive measurements
of sex steroids in Female 2, taken 19 mo apart
(Table 1), revealed higher concentrations of E2 and
the presence of Vtg in the second blood sample. This
indicates progressive oogenesis and an accumulation
of egg yolk proteins, which was further confirmed by
the coincidence of the observable oocytes in the
ovaries.
In male urodeles, elevated T concentrations coincide with increased activity of the cloacal glands,
leading to swelling of the region around the vent
(Norris et al. 1985, Woodley 1994). In male olms,
cloacal swelling can last between 4 and 6 wk, as
reported by Briegleb (1962), while our observations
of Male 1 confirmed swelling for up to 14 mo. An
exact determination of the duration of cloacal swelling is difficult, due to the gradual and slow changes
in cloacal morphology at the beginning of the process and the subjective assessment of cloacal morphology. In addition, cloacal swelling can occasionally occur in females, as shown by our observations
of Female 1, in which swelling persisted for 8 mo
prior to oviposition. Despite this discrepancy, the
visibility of oocytes through the abdominal wall of
females remains a clear indicator of the sex of the
olm. The presence of observable oocytes for 17 mo
and even 33 mo in Female 1 and Female 2, respectively, and the swelling of the cloaca in Male 1 for
14 mo indicate considerably longer reproductive
cycles in olms compared to other amphibians. This
is especially the case when one considers that the
reproductive cycles go far beyond the recognizable
external morphological features. The presence of

testis-ova in Male 3 represents yet another unusual
feature of the reproductive biology of the olm. This
phenomenon is relatively common in olms, with
translocation of the sex chromosomes as a suggested
underlying mechanism (Bizjak Mali 2017). As observed in Male 3, testis-ova in olm commonly comprise only individual oocytes in otherwise normal
testes (Bizjak Mali 2017). In that view, a significant
interference of oocytes with the hormonal profile of
males with testis-ova is not very likely, as has also
been shown by the T/E2 ratio of Male 3.
Despite established protocols for safe blood sampling in olms (Gredar et al. 2018, 2024), non-invasive
methods are still preferable. To avoid the potential
stress induced by blood sampling, we introduced the
analysis of sex steroid metabolites in olm’s fecal samples. In addition to the previously identified challenges associated with the small amounts of the biological material (Millspaugh & Washburn 2004), the
accuracy of metabolite measurements in olm fecal
samples was further challenged by a limited number
of specimens and by irregular and infrequent defecations, which ranged from 6 occurrences mo–1, to 1
every 4 mo. Measurement of sex steroid metabolites
in olm’s feces by EIAs included fE1m, fEpiAm and
fTm, which were selected based on preliminary tested
responsiveness of different antibodies and the efficiency of sample preparation techniques (e.g. drying
of fecal samples, different extraction protocols and
solutions, use of a sample pool or individual samples).
Juveniles generally showed low concentrations of the
measured metabolites, which correspond to their
underdeveloped gonads and previously reported late
sexual maturity in olms (Juberthie et al. 1996). In
adult olms, the concentrations of metabolites were
higher, yet their variability and overlap between individuals, regardless of sex, considerably reduced their
sex-identifying potential. Furthermore, the elevated
concentrations of fEpiAm and fTm were not consistent with the observations of swollen cloacae in the
males. In addition to the already mentioned subjective assessment of cloacal swelling, this could also be
due to slow gut passage and infrequent defecation,
leading to considerable delays between the highest
concentrations of sex steroids in the blood and their
detection in the feces. Another possible explanation
for the discrepancy between the appearance of cloaca
and the elevated concentrations of fecal metabolites
would be suboptimal specificity of the anti-fEpiAm
and fTm antibodies in the assays used in the study.
Although all 3 assays detected fluctuations in metabolite concentrations in both sexes over a relatively
long observation period (Fig. 3), it has been shown

Gredar et al.: Reproductive biology of the olm

that the sensitivity of the EIA and its predictive power
for sex identification can be influenced by the choice
of antibodies (Grebe et al. 2023).
On the other hand, in the monitored female olm
(Female 1), fE1m concentrations showed a stronger
correlation with oocyte maturation, with the highest
concentrations of fE1m coinciding with visible
oocytes and the presence of Vtg. The highest
estrogen concentrations in amphibians are usually
associated with the most active vitellogenesis or just
before oviposition (Fortune 1983, Itoh et al. 1990).
However, in Female 1, the concentrations of fE1m
decreased several months before oviposition. In
addition to the aforementioned delay in the detection
of fecal metabolites due to irregular defecation of
olms, it can be assumed that the lag time of fecal
metabolite detection in olms is much longer than in
mammals (Schwarzenberger et al. 1996) due to slow
gut passage and slow metabolism in olms (Hervant et
al. 2000). In addition, it is not uncommon for amphibians, including urodeles (Sever et al. 1996), to store
mature oocytes in the posterior part of the oviducts
prior to oviposition, which may further explain the
delay between hormone drop and oviposition.

Various methods and approaches have been proposed for identifying sex and the reproductive cycle
in olm, including those in the present study. Their
advantages, disadvantages and reliability are summarized in Table 3. In short, observation of external morphology is non-invasive and easy to perform, but subjective and only moderately reliable. Approaches
using blood, such as measurement of sex steroids or
detection of Vtg, are reliable and objective, but blood
sampling requires brief sedation of the animals. Analysis of fecal metabolites of sex steroids offers a promising, non-invasive approach for long-term reproductive cycle monitoring, although the lack of data in
olms makes interpretation of the results challenging.
The use of ultrasound imaging, while useful to some
extent, is prone to misinterpretation and gender bias
and can lead to errors if not interpreted carefully.
Genetic tests based on sex chromosomes or sexspecific sequences are not yet available.
Studying the complex reproductive biology of the
olm is challenging due to its biology, ecology and
genetics. Unambiguous identification of the sex of
the olm is often obscured by the morphological variability and anomalies of its gonads (Bizjak Mali 2017).
