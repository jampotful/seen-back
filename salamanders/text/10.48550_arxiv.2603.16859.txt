Abstract
Evaluating omni-modal large language models (OLMs) in multi-party dialogue requires more than answer correctness on pre-segmented inputs. We introduce SocialOmni, an offline diagnostic benchmark
that separates three turn-level decisions: identifying who is speaking, deciding when a designated participant should enter at an annotated query time, and determining how that participant should continue the
dialogue. SocialOmni contains 2,000 perception items and a quality-controlled core split of 200 interactiongeneration items, including naturally occurring speaker-visibility mismatches. Each item is independently
checked by three human annotators. Evaluated systems receive only query-time-bounded multimodal evidence, while manually verified reference continuations are reserved for response judging. A complete
three-judge ensemble scores every eligible response, with leave-one-judge-out and family-sensitivity audits. Across 11 OLMs, rankings vary substantially by axis, and coverage-adjusted scores reveal when high
conditional response quality depends on selective turn entry. The protocol does not measure persistent
streaming state or wall-clock latency.

Introduction

Current OLM evaluation under-specifies the integrated decisions required for social dialogue. Omni-modal large language models combine audio, vision, and text within a unified generation interface [5, 7, 11, 17, 36, 37], but dialogue
success also requires recognizing dynamic cues, selecting an appropriate entry point, and producing a socially coherent response. As summarized in Table 1, most benchmarks remain centered on static, accuracy-oriented understanding
tasks [10, 15, 18, 41], leaving the integrated interaction decision largely unmeasured.
The missing unit of analysis is the integrated conversational turn. At a single dialogue state, a model must identify
the active speaker, determine whether a designated participant should enter, and produce a grounded continuation.
SocialOmni targets this unit directly rather than introducing another static comprehension leaderboard; the Related
Work section contrasts it with prior benchmark families and motivates our interaction-centered protocol.
This missing turn-level evaluation matters because semantic correctness does not guarantee successful interaction. A
delayed entry, premature interruption, or incoherent continuation can degrade user experience even when the propositional content is accurate [28, 30]. Correctness-only evaluation can therefore over-reward offline comprehension while

SocialOmni: Dimensions of Social Interactivity
Who: identify the active speaker in a marked interval.
When: decide whether the target should speak now.
How: generate an appropriate reply for the target role.
SocialOmni organizes the integrated turn as a diagnostic sequence from audio-visual grounding to turn-entry decision and contextual continuation. Who requires fine-grained speaker–content alignment, when integrates prosodic, lexical, and visual cues at an annotated state, and how tests whether the resulting continuation remains role-consistent
and contextually appropriate. The benchmark contains 2,000 perception items and a quality-controlled core set of 200
interaction-generation items across 15 dialogue categories in four broad domains, including naturally occurring speakervisibility mismatches. It does not reproduce a persistent full-duplex session or measure system latency.
Evaluation across 11 OLMs supports a diagnostic profile rather than a single competence score. Model leadership
changes across speaker attribution, turn-entry classification, gold-state generation, and coverage-adjusted response
quality, so gains on one measure do not establish robustness on others. These descriptive rank reversals show that
an understanding-centric score alone cannot characterize interaction behavior across these coupled decisions.
Our contributions are threefold:
i) Diagnostic Benchmark. We evaluate 2,200 items across the who, when, and how diagnostics.
ii) Auditable Response Evaluation. Three judges score every response, with judge/family sensitivity audits.
iii) Grounding Analysis. Visibility strata diagnose attribution failures without causal claims.

Related Work

Omni-Modal Large Language Models. OLM development has broadened multimodal generation faster than
interaction-focused evaluation. The field has progressed from perception-centric paradigms such as CLIP [27]
and instruction-tuned VLMs such as Flamingo and LLaVA [1, 21, 22] to systems that couple text, vision, and
audio [5, 7, 11, 17, 34, 36, 37]. Architectures now range from modular dispatch to native cross-modal generation [9, 23, 35, 38, 40], yet evaluation remains dominated by isolated request–response trials. Speaker grounding, turn
entry, and socially appropriate continuation therefore remain comparatively under-tested.
Answer-Centric Benchmarks for OLMs. Answer-centric benchmarks establish perception and reasoning quality but
do not operationalize conversational entry. Broad-coverage omni suites [10, 15, 18, 41] and modality-specific benchmarks [6, 14, 16, 24, 31, 39, 42] evaluate question answering or retrieval over pre-segmented stimuli. Video benchmarks
add temporal reasoning while retaining isolated prompt–response trials. Consequently, these suites do not test whether
a designated participant should enter at a particular dialogue state or produce a fitting continuation.

Behavior-Centric Benchmarks for OLMs.
Behavior-centric benchmarks move toward interaction, but typically
isolate only part of the integrated turn. Social reasoning datasets [13, 25] target multi-speaker inference, while spokendialogue and full-duplex benchmarks [2, 12, 19, 20, 29, 32] emphasize turn-taking or interruption detection, often
from audio alone. Multimodal interaction benchmarks [3, 4, 26, 33, 43] broaden audio-visual coverage. SocialOmni
complements these efforts with one offline protocol spanning speaker attribution, fixed-time turn-entry classification,
and conditional response generation. Its purpose is controlled cross-model diagnosis under a shared task definition and
evaluation contract, not persistent-state or wall-clock latency evaluation.

3

SocialOmni

SocialOmni evaluates an integrated conversational turn through three offline diagnostics. The tasks identify the active
speaker (who), decide whether a designated participant should enter at an annotated query time (when), and generate
an appropriate continuation after a correctly detected opportunity (how). Every model receives query-time-bounded
evidence under a common task definition, with a disclosed adapter when an API cannot accept joint audio and visual
input. The protocol does not measure persistent streaming state, wall-clock latency, or deployment eﬀiciency.

3.1 Benchmark Construction
Benchmark construction prioritizes dialogue clarity and reproducible item definitions. We compile search terms across
15 dialogue categories in four broad domains: entertainment, professional, daily life, and narrative. From more than
3,000 reviewed source videos, we retain 10–30 s segments with clear audio, visible conversational context, and licenses
compatible with reconstruction. A source video may yield a clip, whereas a benchmark item denotes one evaluated
query constructed from a retained clip. The resulting benchmark contains 2,000 perception items and 200 interactiongeneration items, with a mean clip duration of 25.0 s.
Three-stage human verification defines every benchmark label. Each item is independently checked by three annotators
drawn from a trained pool, covering speaker identity, spoken-content options, visibility, target-participant descriptions,
query times, YES/NO turn-entry labels, and reference continuations. Cross-review and senior adjudication then resolve
disagreements and verify the textual artifacts. The supplementary material reports annotation roles and agreement.

3.2 Data Stratification
The perception split stratifies speaker attribution by naturally occurring visibility conditions. It contains 1,725 speakervisible items, where the active speaker is visually attributable at query time, and 275 speaker-visibility-mismatch items,
including reaction shots, off-screen speech, partial occlusion, and weak identity cues. These cases are neither artificial audio swaps nor matched counterfactual interventions. The split therefore diagnoses grounding diﬀiculty without
supporting a causal estimate of controlled audio-visual conflict.
The interaction-generation split is a controlled aggregate diagnostic rather than a per-domain leaderboard. Each of its
200 items must provide an identifiable target participant, an unambiguous query state, suﬀicient multimodal context, and
a human-written continuation. We consequently report aggregate results with uncertainty and treat language-, culture-,
and domain-specific conclusions as out of scope.

3.3 Task Design
Who: speaker attribution. The who task separates speaker grounding from content selection. At query interval t, a
model receives video V , audio A, and four options formed by crossing speaker identity with spoken content. The options
contain the correct identity–content pair and three distractors: wrong identity with correct content, correct identity with
wrong content, and both wrong.
When: fixed-time turn-entry classification. The when task evaluates turn entry at a single controlled dialogue state.
For item i, annotators identify target participant Xi with a visible description and select query time ti . The model
receives only prefix (Vi,≤ti , Ai,≤ti ) and answers whether Xi should begin a substantive turn using A (YES) or B (NO).
Repeated prefix queries, first-response timing, and system latency remain outside the protocol.
How: conditional response generation. The how task isolates continuation quality after a correctly detected opportunity. A model that correctly answers YES on a gold-positive item generates the next utterance for the same target
participant Xi . Fillers and backchannels count as full turns only when the annotation targets that pragmatic function.
The evaluated model never sees the human-authored reference continuation, which is reserved for response judging.

3.4 Evaluation Protocol
Speaker-attribution metrics quantify both aggregate accuracy and visibility-conditioned diﬀiculty. Non-parsable outputs count as incorrect; we report top-1 accuracy, macro-F1 over the four answer positions, and accuracy within the
speaker-visible and visibility-mismatch strata. The gap ∆vis = Accvisible −Accmismatch is descriptive; naturally occurring,
unmatched strata cannot identify causal visibility effects across models and dialogue settings.
Turn-entry metrics characterize classification at the same annotated query states. We report accuracy, precision, recall,
and macro-F1 over 200 binary decisions, with non-parsable outputs counted as incorrect. These metrics avoid imposing
a universal timing window across dialogues.
Response-generation metrics separate conditional quality from opportunity coverage. Let P = {i : yi = YES} denote
gold-positive opportunities and Rm ⊆ P the items on which model m both predicts YES and returns a non-empty

Experiments

The experiments test both cross-model performance and the failure patterns hidden by aggregate scores. We ask how
current OLMs compare across the three diagnostics and which conclusions change after coverage, modality, visibility,
and judge sensitivity are exposed explicitly.

4.1 Setup
Models. The evaluation covers eleven commercial and open-source systems: GPT-4o [11]; Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash [5]
and Gemini 3 Flash/Pro Preview [8]; Qwen3-Omni, Qwen3-Omni-Thinking, and Qwen2.5-Omni [35, 36]; OmniVinci [37]; VITA-1.5 [7]; and Baichuan-Omni-1.5 [17]. All use the common task contract through disclosed
interfaces, with every attempted item retained in the strict denominator.
Inputs. Input handling preserves the query-time boundary while exposing interface differences. Native omni interfaces consume truncated audio-video media directly. GPT-4o uses a disclosed cascade: gpt-4o-transcribe processes the truncated audio, and the chat endpoint receives its text with sampled frames. The Gemini relay accepts
sampled frames but not raw audio, so its rows are visual-only baselines. Released metadata records these differences
for cross-model interpretation. Evaluated models never receive the manually verified context or reference continuation
reserved for judges.
Metrics. The metrics preserve each diagnostic’s interpretation. We use accuracy and macro-F1 for who; accuracy,
positive-class precision/recall/F1, and macro-F1 for when; and QEns , Cov.+ , and QEns
joint for how. Table 2 reports point
estimates; the supplementary material provides uncertainty and failure accounting for every evaluated system.

4.2 Main Results
Performance is strongly axis dependent. Table 2 assigns the best speaker attribution, turn-entry classification, conditional
response quality, and coverage-adjusted response quality to different systems. These rank changes justify a diagnostic
profile rather than a single social-competence score, but they do not establish causal independence among the axes.
Coverage adjustment materially changes the interpretation of response quality. The GPT-4o cascade obtains the highest conditional score (76.50) but responds to only 30.47% of positive opportunities, reducing its joint score to 23.31.
Qwen3-Omni-Thinking reaches 64.86 conditional and 30.40 joint quality, whereas VITA-1.5 combines 49.93 conditional quality with 88.28% coverage to obtain the highest joint score (44.08). Conditional quality alone therefore
overstates systems that respond selectively at test time.

4.3 Diagnostic Analysis
Speaker attribution. Speaker attribution degrades under visibility mismatch for most, but not all, systems. Against
a 25% uniform-random baseline, several models remain near or below chance after failures count as incorrect. Table 3
shows gaps from −4.58 to +10.06 points across the naturally occurring strata. These gaps diagnose diﬀicult grounding
conditions without estimating a causal audio-visual conflict effect; supplementary results report macro-F1 and Wilson
intervals for every system and visibility stratum.
Turn-entry classification. Turn-entry models exhibit distinct conservative and permissive policies. Because 128 of
200 items are positive, always predicting YES reaches 64.00% accuracy but only 39.02 macro-F1. Reporting both
metrics prevents prevalence from making a permissive policy appear strong; neither metric measures real-time latency.
Simple pause and transcript-endpoint controls do not solve fixed-time turn entry. Table 4 shows that the strongest fixed
pause rule reaches 63.50% accuracy and 57.15 macro-F1: it improves over always-YES macro-F1 but remains below
the best OLM accuracy. We report every threshold to avoid selecting a pause duration on the test set.
The GPT-4o cascade illustrates how interface and decision policy jointly shape a score. It predicts YES on 49 of 200
items, including 39 true positives, and reaches 50.50% accuracy, 49.84 macro-F1, and 30.47% positive-opportunity
coverage. Because the cascade converts truncated audio to text, these conservative-policy results characterize the adapter
as well as the underlying model being evaluated.
Input-channel ablations expose interface dependence without identifying causal modality contributions. Table S5 keeps
the primary denominators fixed while comparing audio-only and video-only settings. Their model- and task-specific
variation is interpreted as an interface effect.

The ablation shows model-specific channel sensitivity: audio-only inputs are stronger for most native omni models on
who, while video-only inputs generally reduce attribution and gold-state generation quality. GPT-4o shows a similarly
large generation gap between its transcript-only and frames-only adapters. The complete per-model ablation table is
provided in the supplementary material.
Judge reliability. Response judging is complete and auditable, but its members are not interchangeable. All three
judges score every non-empty true-positive response, and the audit reports pairwise agreement, leave-one-judge-out
rankings, and same-family removal. The primary leaderboard is defined only by this fixed, fully observed judge protocol.
Individual judges correlate with the ensemble while retaining distinct lower-tier orderings. Table 5 reports responselevel Spearman correlations of 0.76–0.90 against the complete ensemble and model-level Kendall correlations of 0.75–
0.93. GPT-4o and Qwen3-Omni move any model by at most one rank, whereas Gemini 2.5 Pro moves one model by
five ranks. On independent controls, each judge separates gold from shuffled continuations with AUC 0.93–1.00; FPR
denotes shuffled candidates scored at least 75.
Judge removal confirms that the ensemble ranking is not controlled by one evaluator. Each leave-one-out ranking has
Kendall τ ≥ 0.93 and changes any model by at most one position; same-family removal preserves the complete ordering.
We retain the complete ensemble and report these shifts explicitly rather than claiming that family preference is absent.
Full per-model differences appear in the supplementary material for independent inspection.
Score distributions still show evaluator differences. Pairwise response correlations range from 0.51 to 0.73, and pairwise MAE ranges from 17.22 to 28.27 points. Requiring all three scores fixes the response set, but averaging cannot
remove rubric-interpretation differences. The ensemble is thus a reproducible surrogate rather than evidence of judge
equivalence at the response level.
The coverage-adjusted generation results remain stable under resampling. GPT-4o retains the strongest conditional quality, whereas VITA-1.5 retains the strongest coverage and joint utility; Qwen3-Omni-Thinking combines the secondhighest conditional quality with a joint score of 30.40. 4.4 Robustness and Interpretation
The results support a diagnostic, rather than scalar, interpretation of SocialOmni. Leadership reverses across all three
axes: Qwen3-Omni leads speaker attribution, VITA-1.5 leads fixed-time turn entry and joint response quality, and
GPT-4o leads gold-state and conditional generation. Moreover, QEns measures produced-response quality, whereas
QEns
joint incorporates missed opportunities. Their separation reveals whether progress comes from stronger continuation
generation or a more effective entry policy.
Cross-channel comparisons reinforce the importance of speech-bearing evidence for continuation generation. Among
configurations that return valid outputs, audio- or transcript-based inputs yield higher QGold than video-only inputs:
GPT-4o scores 78.97 with transcript evidence versus 26.11 with frames, Qwen3-Omni scores 59.51 with audio versus
18.75 with video, and VITA-1.5 scores 51.24 versus 22.14. These interface comparisons diagnose evidence sensitivity
without asserting causal modality independence.
Metric triangulation exposes policy differences hidden by accuracy. The always-YES control obtains 64.00% turn-entry
accuracy because 128 of 200 items are positive, yet its macro-F1 is only 39.02. OmniVinci reaches 62.03 macro-F1 at
similar accuracy, indicating stronger balance across entry and non-entry states. VITA-1.5 achieves the highest accuracy
at 65.50%; together, the metrics separate balanced discrimination from opportunity capture in this split.
Bootstrap intervals support the response-policy distinction. VITA-1.5’s positive-opportunity coverage interval is
[81.56, 92.77], compared with [23.16, 38.92] for GPT-4o and [38.45, 55.48] for Qwen3-Omni-Thinking. Its joint-score
advantage therefore reflects broad opportunity capture rather than a small set of favorable responses.
Visibility strata provide an independent grounding stress test. The visible-to-mismatch gap reaches 10.06 points for
Gemini 3 Pro and 9.39 for Gemini 3 Flash, while Qwen3-Omni remains strongest in both strata at 75.25% and 70.91%.
The sign reversal for VITA-1.5 further shows that the partition is not a uniform diﬀiculty rescaling. Reporting both strata
therefore reveals model-specific grounding behavior hidden by aggregate speaker accuracy.
Independent judge checks support the central comparisons while exposing lower-tier sensitivity. Individual judges
achieve model-level Kendall τ = 0.75–0.93 against the complete ensemble; the Gemini judge produces the largest
single-judge shift of five ranks, but every leave-one-out ensemble changes models by at most one position. Together

5

Conclusion

SocialOmni reframes omni-model evaluation around the conversational turn: grounding who is speaking, deciding when
a designated participant should enter, and determining how that participant should respond. Its 2,200-item benchmark,
fixed-prefix protocol, gold-state generation track, and coverage-aware response metrics turn these coupled decisions into
an auditable diagnostic profile. The accompanying visibility, modality, baseline, and judge-sensitivity analyses make
the profile interpretable rather than reducing social interaction to a single opaque score.
Evaluation of 11 OLMs yields three actionable findings. First, leadership changes across speaker attribution, turn entry,
and response generation, so static understanding accuracy does not determine interaction quality. Second, gold-state and
coverage-adjusted scores separate continuation ability from the policy that decides whether to speak. Third, visibility
strata and input-channel ablations expose dependence on scene evidence and interfaces, while the judge audit quantifies
open-ended evaluation sensitivity. Together, these results make SocialOmni a practical foundation for socially interactive
omni-model development and comparison.
Because each score maps to a distinct failure mode, the resulting profile gives developers concrete targets for improving speaker grounding, entry policy, and response quality while keeping progress across the three interaction stages
separately measurable under a common evaluation contract.

A.1 Human Annotation and Agreement
Every benchmark item is independently checked by three trained human annotators before adjudication. For who, annotators verify the active speaker, spoken content, four answer options, and speaker-visibility stratum. For when, they
verify the target-participant description, query time, and YES/NO label. For how, they verify the target role and write
the reference continuation. Disagreements are cross-reviewed and resolved by a senior annotator. The reported preadjudication raw agreement is 94.2% for perception annotations and 91.8% for interaction-generation annotations. We
distinguish these construction-stage figures from the adjudicated gold labels distributed with the benchmark; chancecorrected agreement cannot be reconstructed from the public adjudicated labels alone.

A.2 Domain Definitions and Size Rationale
The 15 dialogue categories are grouped into four broad domains: entertainment, professional, daily life, and narrative.
Entertainment covers interactive media such as talk shows and podcasts; professional covers interviews, debates, and
task-oriented settings; daily life covers everyday conversations; narrative covers scripted movie and drama scenes. These
labels support stratification, but the 200-item interaction-generation split is not large enough for stable per-category
leaderboards.
The interaction-generation split is a manually curated diagnostic set. Scaling open-ended continuation indiscriminately
can add ambiguity in target-role identification, query-state selection, and reference quality. Each retained item therefore
requires a clearly described target participant, an agreed YES/NO entry label at the query time, suﬀicient audio-visual
context, and a human-written continuation. This quality criterion motivates the 200-item core design; it does not imply
that 200 items exhaust the diversity of social interaction.

A.3 Speaker-Visibility Stratification
The perception split contains 1,725 speaker-visible and 275 speaker-visibility-mismatch items. Labels are based on
whether the active speaker can be attributed from the visible scene at the query time. Mismatch cases include off-screen
speech, reaction shots, partial occlusion, and weak identity cues. They are naturally sampled rather than created by
replacing audio or pairing unrelated clips. The visibility gap is therefore descriptive and may also reflect shot type,
composition, or domain.

A.4 Perception Option Balance
The correct-option distribution over 2,000 who items is A: 569, B: 561, C: 453, and D: 417. We retain this natural
imbalance and report macro-F1 in addition to accuracy. A non-parsable output counts as incorrect and as a false negative
for its gold class; because it has no predicted class, it contributes no class-specific false positive.

A.5 Fixed-Time Turn-Entry Protocol
Each interaction item provides an annotated query time ti , target participant Xi , and binary label yi . The target is
identified by a concrete visual description such as clothing, location, or a visible action. Every model receives evidence
bounded by the same prefix ending at ti and answers whether Xi should start a substantive turn; disclosed adapters
preserve this temporal boundary when an API lacks a joint media endpoint. The task does not repeatedly query earlier
prefixes, estimate a first-YES timestamp, or include inference latency in the score.
This fixed-time formulation avoids imposing a universal asymmetric timing window on heterogeneous conversations.
It also makes the protocol interface-agnostic across APIs and open-source checkpoints. Native streaming evaluation,
persistent dialogue state, first-token latency, and acceptable-entry intervals remain separate extensions.

A.6 Response Scoring and Coverage
For the policy-conditioned evaluation, a response is generated on a gold-positive item only when the model predicts YES.
Only non-empty true-positive responses enter conditional quality QEns . Missed opportunities and empty responses are
excluded from this conditional mean but contribute zero to QEns
joint over the fixed 128 positive opportunities. Conditional
quality is always reported with positive-opportunity coverage and the joint score. In the separate gold-state evaluation,
QGold forces generation at all 128 positive query states regardless of the model’s entry prediction; failed or missing
responses contribute zero to that fixed-denominator mean.

MAC-AutoML

Each eligible non-empty response is scored independently by GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Qwen3-Omni on
{0, 25, 50, 75, 100}. The scale is interpreted as follows:
• 100: contextually grounded, role-consistent, coherent, and pragmatically natural;
• 75: appropriate overall, with minor genericness or missing detail;
• 50: partly relevant but with a noticeable grounding, role, or coherence problem;
• 25: weakly relevant, contradictory, or pragmatically inappropriate;
• 0: unrelated, wrong-role, or incompatible with the dialogue state.
Judges receive the manually verified dialogue context ending before the annotated continuation, the continuation itself as
a reference, and the evaluated response. The reference is withheld from evaluated models. The rubric explicitly permits
valid paraphrases and alternative continuations rather than treating lexical similarity as the objective.
Every non-empty true-positive response requires all three judge scores, whose arithmetic mean defines its ensemble
quality. Failed judge calls are retried rather than averaged over a smaller pool. The evaluation artifact stores raw outputs,
parsed scores, retries, model versions, and invocation dates. Pairwise agreement, leave-one-judge-out rankings, and
same-family removal test whether the main conclusions depend on one judge.

A.7 Judge Sensitivity
Single-judge comparisons and leave-one-judge-out checks use different rankings: the former compare one judge with
the complete ensemble, whereas the latter compare the remaining two-judge mean with that ensemble. Thus the fiveposition shift for the Gemini judge is compatible with a maximum one-position shift in each leave-one-out ranking.
Same-family removal preserves the complete ordering. These checks reveal sensitivity within the fixed reference-aware
protocol but cannot eliminate reference-style or model-family bias.

A.8 Model and Inference Details
Commercial model identifiers cover GPT-4o [11], Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash [5], and Gemini 3 Flash/Pro Preview [8].
Open-source systems use Qwen3-Omni Instruct/Thinking [36], Qwen2.5-Omni [35], OmniVinci [37], VITA-1.5 [7],
and Baichuan-Omni-1.5 [17]. For GPT-4o, the already truncated query prefix is converted to 16-kHz mono audio and
transcribed by gpt-4o-transcribe; GPT-4o receives this machine transcript together with sampled prefix frames.
The Gemini relay used in this evaluation accepts sampled frames through its OpenAI-compatible endpoint but does
not expose the media audio track. We therefore retain Gemini as an explicitly interface-limited baseline rather than
labeling it native audio-video inference. No adapter receives content after the query time, and evaluated models never
receive the human reference continuation. Decoding uses temperature 0.3 and top-p 1.0 where exposed; judge decoding
is deterministic where supported. Exact identifiers, invocation dates, request envelopes, preprocessing, timeout, retry
settings, and interface metadata are retained with the versioned evaluation snapshot.

A.9 Reproducibility and Release Scope
The supplementary package provides adjudicated annotations, frozen evaluation results, and offline analysis scripts. The
original video media are not included in the package. The released evaluation manifest fixes 2,000 perception items and
200 interaction-generation items (video_0001–video_0200), matching every denominator reported in this paper.
The public annotations include item IDs, media paths, labels, dialogue metadata, and the visible/mismatch field used in
our stratified analysis. The public code repository is available at https://github.com/MAC-AutoML/Social
Omni, and the public dataset, including video media, is available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/
alexisty/SocialOmni. These public resources are distinct from the frozen evaluation snapshot described below.
We distinguish the public dataset artifact from the versioned evaluation snapshot. The latter retains raw model text,
parsed predictions, parse failures, response presence, judge scores, interface type, and run metadata; tables and figures
are generated from that single snapshot. Automated checks verify fixed item IDs, model means, score support, and
coverage identities. Source hashes, original download dates, and per-source licensing fields are not currently present in
the public dataset repository, so we do not claim that those provenance fields can be reconstructed from the release.

B.3 Blind Three-Annotator Calibration
The blind three-annotator calibration assesses response quality without redefining the primary leaderboard. Three annotators, blinded to source-model identity, independently score 200 final responses from ten systems on the same
{0, 25, 50, 75, 100} support as the ensemble; the analysis uses their per-response mean. GPT-4o is absent from this
frozen subset, so the calibration does not define a complete model ranking.
Human and ensemble assessments agree most clearly at the capability-tier level. Response-level Pearson correlation is
0.619 (95% bootstrap CI [0.492, 0.726]), model-level Pearson correlation is 0.817, and eight of ten systems share the
same descriptive Poor/Moderate/Strong tier (80%; κ = 0.655). The top two systems preserve their order, while most
rank changes occur in the compressed low-quality region. Human ratings are internally reliable (ICC(2,k)= 0.925; 94%
within 25 points). VITA-1.5 is the principal model-specific discrepancy, supporting continued human auditing rather
than a claim of judge equivalence. Table S9 gives all model-level means.

Reliability, Bias, and Scope

C.1

Three-Annotator Verification

Eight trained annotators participate in benchmark construction, with three independent checks assigned to every retained
item before cross-review and senior adjudication. All task labels, dialogue text, and reference continuations are humanauthored or manually verified. Raw pre-adjudication agreement is 94.2% on perception and 91.8% on interactiongeneration annotations; Section A.1 details the annotation roles.

C.2

Target Identity and Visibility Labels

The target participant in when is specified by a visible description rather than by the phrase “your turn.” For who,
speaker identity and spoken content are independently varied across the four options. Visibility labels distinguish a
clearly attributable on-screen speaker from naturally occurring off-screen, reaction-shot, occluded, or weak-cue cases.
These labels support error stratification but do not constitute synthetic conflict pairs.

C.3

Judge Alignment and Residual Bias

The scalable how protocol requires complete GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Qwen3-Omni scores for every eligible
response. Pairwise agreement, leave-one-judge-out rankings, same-family removal, and the blind three-annotator calibration in Section B.3 support broad quality stratification, but do not establish human equivalence or eliminate relatedfamily preference.
Judges see one human continuation to clarify role and intent, whereas evaluated models do not. The reference-free and
held-out sanity controls are supplementary sensitivity analyses, not replacements for the fixed three-judge referenceaware scores in the main results. Reliability conclusions are therefore specific to that primary protocol and do not
establish reference-free judging reliability. Continued blind three-annotator calibration remains necessary as judges
and response distributions change.

C.4

Benchmark Scope

The 200 manually reviewed interaction-generation items support aggregate diagnosis rather than fine-grained perdomain ranking. Dense verification strengthens target identity, query-state, and response-quality labels, but does not
remove uncertainty from set size, category coverage, or source diversity. Claims therefore remain limited to the evaluated clips, models, and protocol.
The offline fixed-prefix protocol does not measure persistent state, deployment latency, interruption recovery, or multiturn adaptation. Its short, mostly third-person clips also leave multilingual and culture-specific norms, first-person interaction, backchannels, and longer dialogue for future evaluation. Interface gaps require similar care: the GPT-4o cascade
preserves query-bounded transcript semantics but loses raw prosody, while the evaluated Gemini relay is visual-only.
Native streaming and broader interaction settings are complementary extensions of this controlled cross-interface diagnostic.
