ABSTRACT
Studying the interaction between core-collapse supernova remnants (SNRs) and their surrounding
environments is essential to understanding the mechanism for energy transfer to the interstellar medium
(ISM) and the intrinsic physical properties of these remnants. In this paper, we focus on G309.8−2.6.
Our new observations reveal that this object includes an SNR shell with a relic pulsar wind nebula
(PWN) that extends well beyond the emission that has been previously observed in X-rays. We
present new radio continuum and polarization images of G309.8−2.6 from the Evolutionary Map of
the Universe (EMU) and Polarization Sky Survey of the Universe’s Magnetism (POSSUM) surveys
with the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP). The images reveal the complex and
peculiar morphology of G309.8−2.6. The linear polarization displays an atypical S-shaped morphology
and a highly ordered magnetic field. The rotation measure (RM) map shows a large-scale gradient or
possible sign reversal, depending on the foreground RM. We reprocessed archival X-ray observations
from Chandra and eROSITA, and retrieved archival Hα and infrared observations. We performed a
joint analysis of the multiwavelength data and proposed scenarios to explain the unusual shape. Our
results place new constraints on the magnetic field of G309.8−2.6, including its environment, and
demonstrate the power of polarization observations in probing the properties of SNRs.

1. INTRODUCTION

As a supernova remnant (SNR) evolves, any embedded pulsar wind nebula (PWN) will interact with the
reverse shock from the remnant, leading to distorted
PWN morphologies. In some cases, the pulsar escapes
from the explosion site, leaving behind a “relic” PWN
and forming a new compact nebula near its current
position (see B. M. Gaensler & P. O. Slane 2006; R.
Kothes 2017; B. Olmi 2023, for reviews). The relic PWN
G309.8−2.6 provides a unique opportunity to understand how PWNe evolve and interact with their parent
SNRs.
G309.8−2.6 was initially identified as an SNR candidate by A. R. Duncan et al. (1997) in the Parkes 2.4
GHz survey (A. R. Duncan et al. 1995), on the basis of
its extended features and polarization signal. However,
the object has many unusual characteristics, such as its
lack of shell-type morphology and flat spectrum, indicating a potential classification as a PWN rather than
a typical SNR ( H. E. S. S. Collaboration et al. 2011).
The main features discussed above and below are displayed in Figure 1, including the pulsar associated with
an X-ray PWN and extended X-ray emission, the HESS
and Fermi sources, and the extended PWN emission that
is observed in radio and is offset from the X-ray PWN.

On the eastern edge of the radio PWN, PSR
J1357−6429 was discovered during the Parkes Multibeam Survey (F. Camilo et al. 2004). Its pulsations
have been detected in X-rays with XMM–Newton and
in GeV γ-rays with Fermi (P. Esposito et al. 2007; M.
Lemoine-Goumard et al. 2011; C. Chang et al. 2012).
The characteristic age of ∼7.3 kyr, combined with its
spin-down luminosity of Ė = 3.1× 1036 ergs s−1 , characterizes it as a young and energetic Vela-like pulsar. PSR
J1357−6429 has a dispersion measure (DM) of ∼127 pc
cm−3 , which places it at a distance of ∼3.1 kpc using
the Galactic electron density model of YWM16 (J. M.
Yao et al. 2017). While the previous distance estimate
by F. Camilo et al. (2004), based on the Galactic electron density model of J. M. Cordes & T. J. W. Lazio
(2002), places the source at a closer distance of ∼2.5 kpc.
This value is further supported by the optical extinction
based distance of 2.3 ± 0.2 kpc toward G309.8−2.6 reported by S.-S. Shan et al. (2019). There are two previous measurements of the pulsar RM: −47±2 rad m−2 (S.
Johnston & J. M. Weisberg 2006) and −43±1 rad m−2
(A. Kirichenko et al. 2015).
Observations with Chandra and XMM-Newton revealed a 2′ tail-like extended emission around PSR
J1357−6429 (V. E. Zavlin 2007; C. Chang et al. 2012).
Spectral analysis suggests that this tail-like structure is
an associated PWN, designated as PWN G309.92−2.51.
This analysis also shows that the spectrum softens with
increasing distance from the pulsar, although it is still
unclear whether the PWN is part of a pulsar jet or of
a pulsar tail (C. Chang et al. 2012). No counterpart
or tail-like structure at radio wavelengths has been reported. A broader field of view (FOV) X-ray study with
ROSAT, XMM-Newton, and Suzaku ( H. E. S. S. Collaboration et al. 2011; M. Izawa et al. 2015) revealed
faint extended emission with a size of ∼10′ . The peak
of the extended emission is slightly offset from the pulsar and partially overlaps with the radio emission from
G309.8−2.6. No significant spectral variation as a function of distance from the pulsar was found (M. Izawa
et al. 2015). NuSTAR has observed this region (K. Mori
et al. 2022), with a detailed X-ray and multi-wavelength
SED study forthcoming (I. Sander et al. 2026, in preparation).
One outstanding feature of the G309.8−2.6 field is
the spatial connection with the TeV source HESS
J1356−645 ( H. E. S. S. Collaboration et al. 2011). X.
Liu et al. (2023) analyzed over 13 years of Fermi-LAT

3
data and detected significant GeV γ-ray emission. The
GeV to TeV spectrum is well described by a leptonic
model with a broken power law. The γ-ray properties
of HESS J1356−645 resemble those of several known
PWNe. However, because of limited spatial resolution,
it cannot be determined whether the high-energy emission arises from a single source or from multiple components. Notably, its γ-ray emission varies with energy, which is similar to the Vela-X PWN, supporting
a possible connection between these different emission
regions (X. Liu et al. 2023).
The system comprising G309.8−2.6 and X-ray PWN
G309.92−2.51 exemplifies a potential relic PWN scenario (C. Chang et al. 2012; X. Liu et al. 2023; I. Sander
et al. 2026, in preparation). A key parameter for understanding the evolution of extended emission regions
is the energy loss rate of relativistic electrons, which is
strongly influenced by the magnetic field. In radio observations, polarization measurements serve as a valuable
tool for probing magnetic structures in complex systems.
Because G309.8−2.6 is located relatively far from the
Galactic plane, its polarization measurements are less
affected by foreground contamination. The Parkes 2.4
GHz Survey (A. R. Duncan et al. 1997) shows polarized
emission aligning with the northeastern region of the
Stokes I candidate. This is consistent with the largescale polarization, although the survey was constrained
by limited angular resolution.
Recent observations from the Evolutionary Map of
the Universe (EMU, A. Hopkins et al. 2025) and Polarization Sky Survey of the Universe’s Magnetism (POSSUM, B. M. Gaensler et al. 2025) surveys conducted by
ASKAP show a highly polarized S-shaped source (B. D.
Ball et al. 2025). The observation provides insights into
the local magnetic field and raises questions about the
origin of this system. Here we present a radio polarimetric and multiwavelength study of this source. The
source has been named “Salamander” based on its X-ray
emission near the pulsar PSR J1357−6325, which resembles a salamander (discussed in a forthcoming paper
by I. Sander et al. 2026, in preparation). Its polarization morphology displays an S-shaped feature that also
strongly resembles a salamander on a larger scale, and
thus we use the name here to refer to the entire object
observed in radio. In Section 2, we describe the observations and data reduction procedures, while Section 3
provides the results. In Section 4, we present a distance
estimate and polarimetry analysis. Section 5 discusses
the results, and Section 6 provides the conclusions.

2. DATA ACQUISITION AND REPROCESSING

We present new radio observations of G309.8−2.6 with
ASKAP. We also reprocess or retrieve archival infrared,
optical and X-ray observations for a multiwavelength
analysis. Information of all the observations is listed in
Table 1.
2.1. Radio
2.1.1. ASKAP
The field containing G309.8−2.6 is located near the
center of the ASKAP scheduling block ID 53310 (A.
Hopkins et al. 2022). Observations were carried out for
∼10 hours on 2023 September 30 in the continuum averaged mode. In this mode, the total 288 MHz bandwidth
centered at 943 MHz is divided into 288 frequency channels, each 1 MHz wide. ASKAP simultaneously formed
36 independent beams in the standard “closepack36”
configuration, covering ∼30 deg2 (A. W. Hotan et al.
2021).
We obtain the calibrated visibilities and image products from the CSIRO ASKAP Science Data Archive
(CASDA)19 . The available products include multifrequency synthesis (MFS) mosaicked images of Stokes I
and V , as well as 1 MHz resolution image cubes for all
four Stokes parameters (I, Q, U , and V ). The mosaicked images are convolved to a uniform resolution of
15′′ for all beams, while the resolution of the spectral
cubes varies with frequency, reaching up to 18′′ at the
lowest frequencies. For spectral and polarization analysis, we convolve all cubes to a common resolution of
18′′ .
To improve sensitivity to extended structures, the full
288 MHz bandwidth is re-binned into coarser sub-bands
of both 144 MHz and 72 MHz for spectral analysis and
18 MHz for polarization studies. For each case, we image and mosaic the nine beams covering the G309.8−2.6
region and convolve all resulting sub-band images to a
common resolution of 18′′ .

Since no single-dish observations are available at the
same frequency, short spacing data in Stokes I are derived using a power-law extrapolation to 943 MHz with
the closest match from the Southern Twenty-centimetre
All-sky Polarization Survey (STAPS) in the frequency
range 1.3−1.8 GHz (X. Sun et al. 2025). For the extrapolation, the power law spectral index α, defined as
Sν ∝ ν α , with Sν being the flux density at frequency
ν, was obtained from fitting the STAPS data. The uncertainty of the extrapolated flux density at 943 MHz is
∼ 2%.
We apply the feather function in CASA ( CASA
Team et al. 2022) to combine the Parkes and ASKAP
data, ensuring a consistent flux scale across overlapping
spatial frequencies (see A. Plunkett et al. 2023, for a
more detailed explanation). Additionally, we test the
short-spacing correction method of S. Faridani et al.
(2018) in the image domain. These two approaches yield
similar results, with intensity levels for G309.8−2.6 being consistent to within 1%, and we adopt the feather
combination for the following analysis. The comparison
of the original EMU observation image and the com-

bined image is shown in Figure 2. After combination,
the added flux densities range from about 2 mJy beam−1
to 4 mJy beam−1 (Figure 3).
The combined image contains both the Galactic background extended emission (Ibkg ) and the emission from
G309.8−2.6 itself (Isnr ). We use Aegean (P. J. Hancock
et al. 2012, 2018) for fitting and subtracting the background and point-like sources. The final image in total
intensity at 943 MHz is shown in Figure 4. Note that
the point source subtraction procedure was not able to
remove all the sources, due to the extensive diffuse emission in this region.
The same procedure cannot be applied to Stokes Q
and U . Due to Faraday rotation and depolarization, the
large-scale polarized emission from STAPS at a higher
frequency and from ASKAP at a lower frequency are
different. Therefore, the extrapolation from STAPS to
ASKAP, as has been done for I, is not reliable for Q and
U . However, since Q and U generally show more smallscale variations and complex structures than Stokes I,
this limitation may not be a significant issue.

When the electron-density-weighted line-of-sight averaged magnetic field points toward the observer, ϕ is positive; otherwise, it is negative.
As shown by B. J. Burn (1966) and M. A. Brentjens
& A. G. De Bruyn (2005), the observed complex polarized intensity P(λ2 ) = Q(λ2 ) + iU (λ2 ) as a function of
λ2 and the complex polarized intensity F (ϕ) as a function of ϕ, called Faraday dispersion function or Faraday
spectrum, are a Fourier transform pair. By applying
the RM synthesis (M. A. Brentjens & A. G. De Bruyn
2005) technique, F (ϕ) can be derived. The limited observation bandwidth with some channels flagged due to
Radio Frequency Interference (RFI) introduces a sample
function in λ2 domain, which results in a spread function, called the RM spread function (RMSF), in the ϕ

domain. Similarly to the clean procedure in imaging,
RM CLEAN (G. Heald 2009) was developed to estimate
the true F (ϕ).
The Faraday dispersion function F (ϕ) reflects the
emission structure of a polarized source as a function of
Faraday depth. In the case of a Faraday-thin (M. A.
Brentjens & A. G. De Bruyn 2005) polarized source
behind a rotation only magneto-ionic medium, |F (ϕ)|
will manifest as a single peak at ϕpeak = RM with
|F (ϕpeak )| = P , where P is the polarized intensity of the
source. The width of the peak is equal to the full width
at half maximum (FWHM) of the RMSF. Therefore, the
FWHM determines how precisely the RM can be measured. In more complicated cases, such as Faraday-thick
components or multiple emission components, |F (ϕ)|
will show broadened multiple peaks.
We use rmsynth3d and rmclean3d in the RM-Tools
package (C. R. Purcell et al. 2020; C. Van Eck
et al. 2025, submitted) to apply RM synthesis and
RM CLEAN on both 1-MHz channel and 18-MHz
channel images to obtain the Faraday spectra. The
FWHM of the RMSF is 59 rad m−2 . We then use
rmtools peakfitcube in RM-Tools package to find the
fitted ϕpeak and |F (ϕpeak )|, corresponding to RM and
P . The noise in the Faraday cubes has a mean rms σP
of ∼13.5 µJy beam−1 RMSF−1 .

2.2.1. Chandra
To compare the radio data with X-ray diffuse emission, we present the Chandra ACIS-S observation of
PSR J1357−6325 (ObsID: 10880). The observation was
taken on August 8, 2009, with a total exposure time of
69.22 ks. The global properties of the X-ray emission
were previously presented by C. Chang et al. (2012).
We reprocessed the data using CIAO version 4.15 (A.
Fruscione et al. 2006) and calibration files (CALDB version 4.10.7), including additional steps to enhance the
visibility of diffuse emission.
The wavdetect tool in CIAO utilizes a Mexican-hat
wavelet to identify discrete sources, which are then employed for aligning images and subsequently removed
to analyze diffuse emissions. A diffuse (discrete sourceexcised) X-ray intensity image was produced with a
smoothing optimized for comparison with the radio data
following the approach of N. Klingler et al. (2016). Note
that the final diffuse image has undergone significant
processing, and caution is advised when interpreting its
scientific properties20 . This research employs a list of
Chandra datasets, obtained by the Chandra X-ray Observatory, contained in DOI: 10.25574/cdc.519.
2.2.2. eROSITA
We extracted public data from the eRASS:1 release
of the SRG/eROSITA All-Sky Survey (P. Predehl et al.
2021; A. Merloni et al. 2024). The region containing
G309.8−2.6 is covered by four eROSITA tiles (205153,
204156, 212153, and 211156). These data were processed by the eSASS (eROSITA Standard Analysis Software) pipeline (H. Brunner et al. 2022, processing number #020), and have an average angular resolution of
26′′ . For data analysis, we use the standard tasks available in eSASS.
We created intensity maps for the two energy bands
0.5−2.3 keV (soft) and 2.3−7 keV (hard), used erbox
to detect the point-like sources, and masked all those
sources. The 0.2–0.5 keV band image was excluded here
as there was no significant emission. The final image was
smoothed to a resolution of 4′ to enhance the visibility
of the diffuse emission.

900 s each. It achieves a sensitivity of ∼5 Rayleigh and a

3. RESULTS

3.1. Overall radio morphology
Figure 4 presents the combined EMU and STAPS total intensity image of the G309.8−2.6 field, as described
in Section 2. Based on its morphology, we divide the
field into several regions, as shown in Figure 4. Here,
Region II represents the relic PWN, Region I represents
the shell, and these two together represent the SNR.
At the center of the image is PSR J1357−6429 (RA =
13h 57m 02.43s , Dec = −64◦ 29′ 30.2′′ ; at the center of the
yellow diamond). Based on the flux density measurements of 417 ± 58 µJy at 1376 MHz and 309 ± 86 µJy
at 2496 MHz by A. Kirichenko et al. (2015), we make
a power-law fitting and obtain a flux density of 0.56 ±
0.18 mJy at 943 MHz. However, the pulsar exhibits an
integrated flux density of 1.46 ± 0.40 mJy, significantly
higher than the fitted value. This discrepancy is attributed to the blending of the pulsar with its associated
compact PWN, as discussed in C. Chang et al. (2012).

In the vicinity of the pulsar, various filamentary structures and protrusions are observed (see Figure 8). However, none of these features spatially coincide with the
X-ray PWN G309.92−2.51 (see Section 3.5 for details).
The most prominent radio feature is a bridge-like structure extending southward from the pulsar. Its surface
brightness initially increases with distance along the

bridge, peaks at about 1.′ 5 from the pulsar, and then
gradually fades as it merges into the broader emission
region. This morphology suggests a physical connection

This alignment suggests a well-ordered magnetic field,
likely shaped by particle acceleration or compression
processes, such as those found in relic PWNe, reverse

3.4. Hα
The higher-resolution SuperCOSMOS Hα survey image (Q. A. Parker et al. 2005) reveals faint filamentary
features along the southeastern edge of the radio shell.
A rotated cutout of this region is shown in Figure 7,
alongside the ASKAP 943 MHz and WISE 12 µm images.
The Hα filaments closely follow the ASKAP radio contours but have no obvious counterpart in the 12 µm infrared image. The lack of infrared emission may imply a
relatively cold ISM, supporting the interpretation that
the SNR is currently in a radiative phase. Notably, the
WISE 12 µm image shows a faint filamentary feature
near the southeastern edge of the shell (see Figure 7
second panel, indicated by grey arrows), slightly ahead
of the radio boundary. The 12 µm emission primarily
traces stochastically heated small dust grains. It is possible that this emission arises from dust grains heated
by the SNR shock front or swept-up ejecta. A previous
study by A. J. Walker et al. (2001) reported numerous
non-radiative Hα filaments across this region. However,
no obvious radio counterparts to these sharp Hα filaments have been detected. A. J. Walker et al. (2001)

also noted that images obtained using the MSSSO
Wide Field CCD Hα Imaging Survey (M. Buxton
et al. 1998) reveal emission in both Hα and [S II]. As
the original data are unavailable and the [S II]/Hα ratio cannot be assessed, it is thus difficult to determine
whether the emission arises from shock-heated gas.
Additionally, we also notice a nearby pulsar,
PSR J1401−6357, located near the terminus of both
the radio and Hα shell, as illustrated in Figure 7. This
pulsar lies at a distance of ∼1.8 kpc based on its DM
of 97.74 cm−3 pc. The associated Hα emission may indicate the proper motion of the pulsar, as fast-moving
pulsars can produce a bow shock or trailing tail structure
in Hα (S. Brownsberger & R. W. Romani 2014). However, the radio filament does not directly connect to the
pulsar, no associated X-ray PWN and no proper motion
have been reported. These facts argue against a causal
relationship between the filament and PSR J1401−6357.
Toward the relic PWN, no large-scale Hα emission is
detected. However, a faint patch of Hα emission can be
marginally seen near the western edge of the radio relic.

4.2. Polarimetry
One of the most interesting features of this object is
the prominent S-shaped polarization signal and the sign
reversal and gradient in RM. As shown in Figure 5, the
RM distribution follows an S-shaped morphology with
a symmetry about its centre: the northern arc shows
predominantly negative RMs while the southern arc displays positive RMs. Since the sign of RM reflects the
direction of the electron-density-weighted average lineof-sight magnetic field, such polarization observations
of SNRs can provide valuable constraints on the propagation of turbulent magnetic fields in SNR and the

isotropic component of turbulence in the plane of the
sky.

Faraday rotation through the magneto-ionic medium,

up by the expanding SNR. To investigate the magnetic
field structure intrinsic to the PWN, it is necessary to
estimate and, if possible, remove the foreground RM
component.

estimate the large-scale Galactic foreground RM. We use

4.2.2. Polarized emission at other Faraday depths
Besides the main peak in the Faraday spectrum, faint
secondary components are visible in several subregions.
We extract a 2◦ ×2◦ region centered on G309.8−2.6 from
ϕ = −240 to +120 rad m−2 , and integrate |F (ϕ)| using

of Faraday depth is shown in Figure 12.
Panels (d) through (h) for the Faraday depth from
−120 to +80 rad m−2 show emission that is unambiguously related to the PWN. Panels (a) to (c) show the
secondary components in |F (ϕ)| from −240 to −120 rad

to the S-shaped PWN based on morphology. The secondary component is well separated from the primary
component in Faraday depth. The separation of the order of 100 rad m−2 is larger than the FWHM of the
RMSF, indicating that this emission is real.
To produce such a two-component |F (ϕ)| structure, at
least two synchrotron-emitting regions must exist along
the line of sight. It is difficult to conclude the origin of
the secondary components. If the secondary components
correspond to physically distinct structures within the
SNR, they could be indicative of internal shocks, density
variations, or interaction zones with the ambient ISM.
However, producing the observed large RM separation
requires a thermal Faraday screen with large RM between the emission components. Such a Faraday screen
is expected to have a very strong magnetic field or a very
high electron density. We suggest that the secondary
component is more likely from the Galactic ISM.
5. DISCUSSION

Our multiwavelength results and polarization studies
reveal a region with significant morphological and environmental complexity. One of the favored explanations is an evolved or relic PWN associated with PSR
J1357−6429.

As the SNR evolved, the reverse shock propagated inward through the ejecta and interacted with the PWN.
This interaction, combined with the pulsar’s proper motion and the ambient density gradient, probably disrupted the original, more extended PWN. As a result,
a relic PWN was left near the pulsar birth site, while a
new, smaller, and possibly more irregular PWN formed
around the energetic pulsar currently offset from the
centre (B. M. Gaensler & P. O. Slane 2006). At the
same time, the forward shock of the surrounding associated SNR continued to expand and became distorted
by the inhomogeneous environment, potentially causing the partial shell structure observed around the relic
PWN. This scenario is consistent with previous studies
of the G309.8−2.6 and shares key features with other
relic PWNe interacting with their host SNRs and surrounding medium, such as Vela (P. Slane et al. 2018),
“Boomerang” (G106.3+2.7, R. Kothes et al. 2006), CTB
87 (R. Kothes et al. 2020), the “Snail” PWN (Y. K. Ma
et al. 2016), and the “Goose” PWN (N. Klingler et al.
2022).
The pulsar PSR J1357−6429 has a characteristic age
of ∼7.3 kyr and still lies within the remnant, suggesting a middle-aged system in the reverberation phase (B.
Olmi 2023). If the eastern shell (Region I in Figure 4)

The S-shaped structure in Region II (Figure 4) exhibits strong linear polarization and clear RM gradient.

the imprint of RT instabilities as the relic PWN is compressed and reshaped by the reverse shock. These instabilities can also lead to mixing between hot thermal gas
and relativistic fluid, further complicating the observed

Toward the pulsar, the RM measured in our ASKAP
943 MHz data is −49 ± 2 rad m−2 , consistent with
previous measurements at higher frequencies: −47 ±
2 rad m−2 at 1.369 GHz (S. Johnston & J. M. Weisberg
2006) and −43 ± 1 rad m−2 from 1.376 to 2.358 GHz (A.
Kirichenko et al. 2015). Both the ASKAP and the pulsar RM measurements consist of the entire foreground
RM and a fraction of the PWN RM. For the ASKAP
measurement, the fraction depends on how the thermal
and nonthermal electrons are mixed inside the PWN; for
the pulsar measurement, the fraction depends on where
the pulsar is located in the PWN. Using the foreground
model in Section 4.2.1, the RM of the PWN can be estimated to be ∼−120 rad m−2 . Here, the fraction of 0.5
was used, which means the thermal and non-thermal
electrons are uniformly mixed or the pulsar is located
at the center of the PWN. However, it is impossible to
estimate the magnetic field strength because the thermal electron density and line-of-sight path length are
unknown.
An alternative interpretation of this radio tail is that
it might represent part of a jet. The jet extends toward
the northeast, where only polarized emission is observed
(Figure 8). It is intriguing that the jet is curved in the
opposite direction to the curved X-ray emission. Further
investigation is needed for this interpretation.

5.2.1. Region II-1: the northern part
In Segment 1, a double-peaked structure is evident
in the Stokes I and P profiles (Figure 14), with the
primary peak at R ≈ 110′′ and the secondary peak at
R ≈ 160′′ , possibly marking the compressed boundaries
of the PWN. There is also a weak excess of P beyond
these two peaks, which corresponds to an excess of I
over a much broader radius range. The double-peaked
structure possibly traces the reverse shock and moves
toward the center of the arc. The sharp inner edge is an
unusual feature and is not consistent with the typical
morphology expected from the outer shell of an SNR,
as can be seen in Figures 13 and 14 (Segment 1). The
fractional polarization is higher because the turbulent
magnetic field is stronger for the already shocked material. The reverse shock heats the gas, which explains
the soft X-ray emission outside the two peaks, as can be
seen in Figure 9.
5.2.2. Region II-2: the southern part
Region II-2 in Figure 4 contains both the southern arc
of the S-shaped structure and diffuse emission. Segment
3 in Figure 13 is a zoom-in of part of the arc, and the
corresponding radial profiles in Figure 14 show strong
I and P and high relative fractional polarization. Segment 2 in Figure 13 is farther out, and the profiles show
a broad weak peak in P . Segment 4 contains most of

the Region II-2. In addition to showing similar characteristics of Segments 2 and 3, the profiles show an
enhancement of I that covers a wide range extending
from the bright arc of the PWN.
The enhancement in P shown in both Segment 3
(R ∼200′′ ) and Segment 4 (R ∼500′′ ) marks the inner
boundary of the relic PWN and indicates a tangential
magnetic field, as expected for a relic PWN compressed
by reverse shocks from the SNR (J. M. Blondin et al.
2001). The polarization peak in Segment 2 (R ∼750′′ )
and Segment 4 (R ∼900′′ ) may reflect the forward shock
of the SNR. The Region II-2 thus contains both forward
shock and reverse shock, which accelerate electrons to
produce radio emissions.
The fractional polarization in the southwestern part of
Region II-2 is relatively low, as can be seen in Segment
4 (R = 500′′ − 900′′ , Figure 14), implying depolarization. The depolarization might arise from the enhanced
turbulent magnetic field due to the interactions between
shocks and nearby interstellar clouds (B.-I. Jun & T. W.
Jones 1999). The presence of weak Hα emission (Figure
6) supports the existence of such a cloud.

The system, which has an age of ∼104 –105 yr, has
likely evolved into the reverberation phase, and the reverse shock from the surrounding SNR collides with the
PWN, compressing it and changing its internal magnetic and particle structure. As demonstrated by D. C.
Ellison et al. (2005), the reverse shock can produce considerable radio emission depending on the ambient or
ejected magnetic field, implying strong amplification of
the magnetic field. The reverse shock as well as its
interaction with the PWN may strongly influence the
observed morphology and synchrotron emission in the
radio and X-ray bands.

The morphology can originate from the irregularities
of the interstellar medium, such as cavities and clouds.
If there is a pre-existing cavity (illustrated in Figure 15
inset (A)), which was not produced by the progenitor’s
stellar wind but by other massive stars, the interaction
between the cavity wall and the RS could result in the
asymmetry in Region II-1. We searched for a surrounding cavity in the Hα and WISE infrared data, but no
clearly related structure was found. We also searched
for nearby O/B-type stars (B. A. Skiff 2014; B. Q. Chen
et al. 2019), but we did not find any definite associations. However, the absence of a cavity does not rule
out complex prior stellar wind interactions.
There might exist a cloud in Region II-2 (Figure 15,
inset (B)), which is coincident with the Hα emission in
Figure 6. A dense cloud can interact with the FS and
still remain within the SNR interior, and then interact
with the RS (B.-I. Jun & T. W. Jones 1999). The interaction could have also amplified the turbulent magnetic
field, which contributed to the enhanced radio emission
and caused depolarization, as observed in both cases.
The overall morphology can also be shaped by the evolution of the SNR. If the pulsar has a large kick velocity,
or if the progenitor of the SN is a runaway star (e.g.
D. M.-A. Meyer & Z. Meliani 2022), the ejected material in the same and opposite directions of the pulsar will
have different speeds and thus experience different compressions. This will generate a shear force (Figure 15,
inset (C)) that laterally displaces the two arcs and produces the S-shape in the relic PWN. The shear force
will also drag the magnetic field and cause a reversal

of the field direction, as indicated by the observed RM
variations.
6. CONCLUSION

We conduct a detailed study of G309.8−2.6 (the
“Salamander”). We present new radio continuum and
polarization images from ASKAP EMU/POSSUM observations. We reprocess archival X-ray observations
to obtain images, which trace PWN or gas heated by
reverse shock. We retrieve archival Hα images, which
can trace radiative shock. We also retrieve infrared images, which indicate properties of the ambient interstellar medium.
Combining multi-wavelength data, including infrared,
Hα and X-ray, we identify a complex system composed
of an X-ray PWN powered by PSR J1357−6429, a relic
radio PWN, and a faint partial shell likely tracing the
forward shock. These features, taken together, indicate
that the source is of SNR origin.
The relic PWN shows a highly polarized S-shaped
structure with an ordered magnetic field and a largescale RM gradient or sign reversal across the PWN.
We estimated the foreground RM to be 13±17 rad m−2
based on the nearby pulsars. We also detect a radio
tail extending from the pulsar, offset from the X-ray
nebula, which likely traces the pulsar’s past proper motion. Assuming a distance of 1.8 kpc, the relic PWN
spans 12−18d1.8 pc, and the full system reaches Rs =
20–42 d1.8 pc in diameter, consistent with an evolved
system of age 104 –105 yr.
The morphology, polarization signatures, and RM
structure indicate that G309.8−2.6 is a middle-aged
composite remnant in the reverberation phase, where
the relic PWN is being reshaped by interactions with
both the forward and reverse shocks. We also discuss several possible scenarios for the local environment.
This system demonstrates the diagnostic power of radio
polarimetry in tracing the evolution of PWNe and their
host SNRs and highlights G309.8−2.6 as a valuable laboratory for studying the coupling between pulsar activity, shock dynamics, and the magneto-ionic interstellar
medium.
