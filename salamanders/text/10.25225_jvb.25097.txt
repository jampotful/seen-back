Abstract. Chemical communication is important in amphibians, contributing to various functions such as
social interactions, foraging, parental care, mate recognition, and predator deterrence. Unlike most anurans
(frogs and toads) and caudatans (salamanders and newts), most gymnophionan amphibians (caecilians) lead
a secretive, burrowing life and have reduced visual systems; hence, they are expected to make substantial use
of chemical communication. In addition, caecilians uniquely possess a protrusible pair of little-studied and
poorly understood chemosensory tentacles that connect to the vomeronasal organs, suggestive of reliance
on (vomer-)olfaction communication. However, current knowledge of the behaviour of (vomer-)olfaction
and chemical communication in caecilians is almost non-existent, having been studied briefly in only two
species previously. We investigated the role of olfactory cues in self-recognition in a terrestrial Indian caecilian
Gegeneophis sp., using a Y-maze behavioural choice experiment. We analysed choices made under self vs.
blank chemical-cue conditions. Individuals expressed a significant preference for self-cues over no cues. This
is only the second study on self-recognition in caecilians mediated by olfactory cues, and the first to test it in
a terrestrial, soil-dwelling species with highly reduced eyes.
Key words: burrowing, chemical communication, spatial orientation, tentacle, vomerolfaction, Y-maze

Introduction
Chemical communication has been an important part
of the ecomorphological diversification of modern
amphibians throughout their 250 million years of
evolution (e.g. Iglesias-Carrasco & Wong 2023).
Amphibians employ specific chemicals secreted from
their highly glandular skin, cloaca, oviducts, and
those found in faeces (e.g. Jaeger & Wise 1991, Lee &
Waldman 2002, Iglesias-Carrasco & Wong 2023) for
a variety of functions including foraging, parental

and locating suitable habitats, shelter, and oviposition

or on non-volatile molecules detected on surfaces,
including soil and on individuals (Weiss et al. 2021,
Iglesias-Carrasco & Wong 2023).

and cues (sensu Iglesias-Carrasco & Wong 2023:
signals being “the chemical compounds released
intentionally by an individual to transfer certain
information that alters the behaviour of the receiver
in an advantageous way for the sender”, and
cue “a chemical compound that is not released

receiver as a guide to modify their behaviour to their
advantage”) are received by the sensory neurons in
the nasal cavity in the main olfactory system and/or
accessory vomeronasal organ (Wyatt 2017, IglesiasCarrasco & Wong 2023), mediating primary olfaction
and vomerolfaction, respectively.
Intraspecific chemical communication involved in
complex social interactions in amphibians has been
investigated in larvae and adults of some species.
Larval aggregations with conspecifics are aided by
waterborne odour cues (Waldman 1985, Blaustein &
Waldman 1992), with possible genetic self-referent
markers such as major histocompatibility complex
(MHC) (Villinger & Waldman 2008, Iglesias-Carrasco
& Wong 2023). The adults of the aquatic, viviparous
South American typhlonectid caecilian Typhlonectes
natans (Fischer, 1880) secrete chemical markers in their
shelters, facilitating the aggregation of conspecifics
(Warbeck et al. 1996). Also, adults of T. natans
use chemical cues for potential mate recognition
(Warbeck et al. 1996, Warbeck & Parzefall 2001).
Laboratory experiments on the oviparous, biphasic
southeast Asian ichthyophiid caecilian Ichthyophis cf.
kohtaoensis Taylor, 1960 showed that the terrestrial,
soil-burrowing adults use chemical cues to locate
prey and for olfactory orientation in their burrows
(Himstedt & Simon 1995). Several frogs also likely
use chemical cues for locating nearby mates, though
in general their primary mode of mate attraction is via
acoustic signals (e.g. Byrne & Keogh 2007, IglesiasCarrasco & Wong 2023) and/or by vision (e.g. de Sá
et al. 2016), given that frogs in general have relatively
very large eyes (Thomas et al. 2020).
Current knowledge of chemical communication in
amphibians is based almost entirely on studies of
anurans (frogs and toads) and caudatans (salamanders
and newts). In contrast, chemical communication
in gymnophionan amphibians (caecilians) is littlestudied and extremely poorly known (e.g. Woodley
2010, Jared et al. 2019, Iglesias-Carrasco & Wong
2023), as are most aspects of their ecology and natural
history (e.g. Taylor 1968, Wake 1980, Himstedt 1996).
Almost all of the 231 extant caecilian species (Frost
2025), including the few species that are fully aquatic

Also, the degree of surface activity and fossoriality
in species that are terrestrial at least as adults differs
across genera, with some being more dedicated
burrowers than others (see Kupfer et al. 2005, Prakash
et al. 2024). Considering their regressed visual system,
elusive, nocturnal and burrowing habits, it is highly
likely that chemical communication plays a vital role
in caecilians (Wiedersheim 1879, Fox 1985). Along
with the olfactory system, caecilians uniquely possess
a pair of laterally placed tentacles that supplement
the reception of chemicals by acting as feelers
collecting volatile molecules and transferring them to
the vomerolfactory system (Schmidt & Wake 1990).
Caecilians are diverse in their ecologies, including
species with aquatic, semiaquatic and terrestrial
adults, and they have a wide range of life-history
strategies with biphasic and direct-developing
oviparous species as well as viviparous species
(San Mauro et al. 2014, Kupfer et al. 2016). There is
also diversity in the morphology of the visual and
olfactory sensory systems, with species having eyes
covered by skin and sometimes also bone (Wilkinson
1997), and species having tentacles that are relatively
filamentous and protrusible and positioned at the tip
of the snout, between the nares and eyes, or adjacent
to the eyes and seemingly less protrusible (e.g.
Taylor 1968, O’Reilly et al. 1996, Gower et al. 2004).
Among terrestrial species, there is diversity in the
extent to which leaf litter or deeper, more compact
soils are utilised, though this is little-studied and
poorly known for most species (Bardua et al. 2019).
Substantial variation in eco- and sensory morphology
across caecilians is generally considered adaptive,
but details have been difficult to interpret or infer
because of scant data on aspects such as burrowing
performance, diet, jaw mechanics, and behaviour
(e.g. Lowie 2022).
The only previous studies of behaviour associated
with caecilian (vomer-)olfaction and chemical
communication have been in the adults of the fully
aquatic T. natans (Warbeck et al. 1996, Warbeck &
Parzefall 2001, Warbeck 2002) and in the terrestrial
adults of Ichthyophis cf. kohtaoensis (Taylor 1968,
Himstedt & Simon 1995, Himstedt 1996). Both of
these species have externally visible eyes covered
by translucent skin; the former species has short
tentacles positioned a short distance behind the nares,
and the latter species has more protrusible tentacles
closer to the eye and just above the upper lip (Taylor

Y-maze apparatus
A wooden Y-maze with three arms positioned at
120° angles apart was used. The maze comprised
three identical cubical chambers (one start chamber
and two choice chambers), one start arm, and two
identical choice arms (Fig. 2A). The dimensions of the
maze were as follows: cubical chambers 10 × 10 × 3 cm
(L × B × H), start arm 10 × 3 × 3 cm, and choice arms
each 5 × 5 × 3 cm. The start chamber had a transparent
glass gate that restricted the animals to within the start
chamber during the initial acclimatisation period. The
top sections of the maze were closed with separate,
removable transparent glass lids. The wooden floor
of the maze was covered with moist black paper
during the trials to provide a smooth surface for the
movement of animals. The experimental setup was
placed in a dark room within the husbandry facility,
and the experimental trials were recorded using
a night-vision camera (Yale HD1080) positioned 1 m
above the maze. A red LED bulb (0.5 W) was placed
near the camera for visibility when handling animals
between trials.
Cue collection
Chemical cues, consisting of glandular skin secretions
and possibly urine, were collected by moving each
individual to an earthworm- and coir-free (ventilated)
plastic box (13.7 × 10.5 × 6.3 cm) lined with six fresh,
moist tissue papers (11 × 9.5 cm) for 24 hours, using
a method modified from Rantala et al. (2002). The
substrate was moistened with 15 ml of purified water.
To limit potential cue deterioration, the substrate
was used within 5 min of the animal’s removal and
transferred into a 50 ml plastic container, then covered
with soft mesh secured in place using a rubber band
(Fig. 2B). The substrate was presented in a container
such that only volatile cues were accessible to the
animal. We discarded the substrate if the animal
had defecated on it (or anywhere in the collection

Behavioural trials
Animals were tested under the choice conditions: self
vs. blank, where animals were given a choice of two
substrates: one collected from its box (self) and the
other from an equivalently moistened ‘blank’ substrate
with which no animals had been in contact. During
each trial, the test animal was released at the centre
of the start chamber with the gate closed, allowing it
to acclimatise for 2 min. During this period, a cue and
a blank container were placed in the choice chambers.
After 2 min, the gate was opened, allowing the animal
to move into the start arm (with the gate remaining
open). A choice was recognised when the whole or at
least the anterior one-third of the test animal entered
either of the two choice chambers. Trials where the test
animals did not enter either of the choice chambers
after 30 min were considered ‘no choice’. The animals
were tested once more to determine if the response
was observed as ‘no choice’. Each of the eight animals
was tested twice during the course of the experiment,
a total of 16 trials.
After each trial, the maze was cleaned with water and
70% ethanol and dried to eliminate all chemical traces.
The room in which the Y-maze trials were conducted
was kept undisturbed during the trials to minimise
stress and distraction. There was a minimum of two
days’ gap between consecutive trials of the same
animals, and this interval ranged from two to 35 days.
All Indian caecilians investigated thus far are
nocturnal (at least in their surface activity: Prakash
et al. 2024), and all trials were conducted after 6 p.m.
Because the sex and the age of the live specimens
could not be determined externally, we did not
control for these two factors.

Data recording and analysis
The effect of olfactory cues on the spatial orientation
of caecilians was tested by analysing their choices in
each trial and the time taken to reach the choices. The
choices are categorised as ‘self’ and ‘blank’. The time
to reach the choice was measured as the time taken
for the head of the animal to enter the choice chamber
after entering the start arm. The choices and the time
taken to choose were assessed manually from video
recordings using the VideoLAN Client (VLC) media
player (version 3.0.16).
A generalised Linear Mixed Model (GLMMs)
was fitted using the glmmTMB function from
the glmmTMB package (Brooks et al. 2017) with
a binomial distribution and logit-link function. The
response variable was the choice (self/blank) made by
the animal in each trial. To account for the repeated
measure of choice from the animals (two replicates
per animal), individual animal ID was included as

a random effect. We used an intercept-only model
with no predictor variables, which evaluates whether

using the DHARMa package in R (Hartig 2025)

A linear mixed-effects model (LMM), fitted using the

to analyse the time data as the ‘time to choice’ data
is normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk test: W = 0.95,

with each animal tested twice.
All statistical analyses were conducted using the R
software environment (version 4.2.2), considering
P-values < 0.05 to be statistically significant and
visualised with the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

Results
Self-recognition in Gegeneophis sp.
Out of the 16 trials, animals chose to enter the self-cue
chamber in 13 trials (81.3%) and the blank chamber in
three trials (18.7%). Of the eight individuals studied,
five oriented towards the self-cue in both trials
(replicates), and three showed a mixed response
by entering the self-cue chamber in one trial and
the blank cue in the subsequent trial. The GLMM
revealed a statistically significant preference for the
self-cue over the blank cue by the animals (GLMM:
nself = 13, nblank = 3, z = 2.28, P = 0.02; Fig. 3A).
Time to choice
The Linear mixed effects model showed no significant
effect of choice on the time taken to enter each choice
chamber (estimate (self) = –5.9 ± 10.7 SE, t(8.2) = –0.5,
P = 0.59). The estimated marginal means indicate that
the mean choice shows animals took on average 62.4
± 11.8 s (95% Cl: 37.2-87.7) to choose the blank cue,
and choosing the self-cue was marginally faster,
estimated marginal mean from the model is 56.4 ± 7.5
s (95% Cl: 38.9-74) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion
This study is among the few behavioural studies on
the functional significance of olfaction in caecilian
amphibians (order Gymnophiona). The sampled
Gegeneophis sp. showed a strong preference for their
own cues compared to a blank substrate, which
suggests a likely use of airborne cues to recognise
shelters, microhabitats, or previously explored trails
or tunnels. The cues received by the experimental
animals in this study must be volatile and airtransmitted, because the cue source was restricted
to a physically separate chamber, and the choice
arms, thoroughly cleaned before each trial, were
free of cues. Our observations of Gegeneophis sp. are
consistent with a previous study (Warbeck et al. 1996,
Warbeck 2002) on the aquatic caecilian T. natans,
wherein the animals exhibited a strong preference for
their own (self) chemical stimulus.
Self-recognition using chemical cues, enabling
various forms of spatial orientation and social
communication, has been documented in anuran
and caudatan amphibians as well as in T. natans.
Adult anurans discriminate between self and nonself, with responses varying based on the degree
of spatial overlap of the natural home ranges of an
individual. In the anuran species Leiopelma hamiltoni,
co-occupying home ranges failed to discriminate

Chemical signals found in scent marks often facilitate
intraspecific communication, and this is a key aspect
of social behaviour in many caudatan species (e.g.
Gillette 2002). At least some salamanders mark their
territory through the deposition of pheromones
in faecal pellets, and individuals can distinguish
between their faecal pellets and those of conspecifics
(Horne & Jaeger 1988).
A notable limitation of the present study was the
limited sample size and lack of information on the
sex of each animal (because of an absence of obvious
external sexual dimorphism) or on the breeding
season(s) of the study species. Thus, the effect of sex
on the self and conspecific recognition in caecilians is
one of several topics worthy of further research. Sexbiased behavioural responses to chemical cues have
been reported in other amphibians (Byrne & Keogh
2007, Graham et al. 2020). For example, studies on
conspecific recognition through chemical cues in
anurans have shown varied results depending on the
sex of the individual. Some studies show conspecific
attraction (Belanger & Corkum 2009), while others
demonstrate avoidance (Schulte & Rossler 2013) or
no response (Gonzalo et al. 2006). The strength of
the response also varies between sexes, with some
species showing stronger responses by males (Hamer
et al. 2011) and others by females (Pearl et al. 2000).
Although very little is known about chemical
detection and communication in caecilians, there
is clear reason to believe this topic warrants much
greater study. The 231 extant species of caecilians
(Frost 2025) are diverse in ecology and life history,
and in the morphology of their sensory systems (see
Introduction). It is known that, like other amphibians,
caecilians have highly glandular skin (Jared et al.
2018) that is the site of substantial and diverse gene
expression (Torres-Sánchez et al. 2020), and there is
some interesting but poorly understood intra- and
interspecific variation in the skin and its glands and
papillae, especially around the external opening of
the cloaca (e.g. Giri et al. 2004, Maciel & Hoogmoed
2011, Mailho-Fontana et al. 2024), that might be
expected to play a role in chemical communication.
In addition, caecilians are known to bite conspecifics
(e.g. Teodecki et al. 1998), potentially permitting
transdermal delivery of chemical signals. Given the
