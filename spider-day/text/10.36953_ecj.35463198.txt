ABSTRACT

Received : 04 August 2025
Revised : 15 January 2026
Accepted : 08 February 2026

Crab spiders (family Thomisidae), particularly the genus Thomisus Walckenaer,
1805, are known for their cryptic coloration and ambush predation strategy, often
utilizing flowers as hunting grounds. Despite their global diversity, local populations,
like those within the Indian subcontinent are often under-documented. This study
was conducted over a two-year period (2023-2025), explored the presence and ecology
of Thomisidae on the science campus of the University of Allahabad, Prayagraj.
Using direct visual encounter surveys and opportunistic sampling across diverse
seasonal flowering plants (Tagetes, Dahlia), a total of 168 specimens were collected
and documented. Identification was performed based on morphological
characteristics, eye arrangement and standard taxonomic literature. The survey
recorded 10 species belonging to 4 genera (Thomisus, Camaricus, Mastira, and
Misumena), with Thomisus spectabilis emerging as the most abundant taxon. The
diversity indices indicate a healthy and relatively even distribution (Shanon index
H’=2.1809; Simpson’s Index 1-D = 0.8861) among the observed species with a strong
microhabitat preference for host plants in the Asteraceae family. The findings
highlight the importance of managed green spaces in the university campuses,
particularly those free from pesticides and rich in floral resources, in supporting the
populations of these ecologically significant predators.

Introduction

The spider family Thomisidae Sundevall 1833,
commonly known as crab spiders or flower spiders
due to their crab-like appearance and hunting habits,
represents a significant component of global
arachnid diversity with 170 genera and 2172 species
(WSC, 2025), Thomisidae ranks as the seventh
largest spider family. Ecologically, these spiders are
vital secondary consumers that regulate insect
populations, particularly pollinators, thereby
influencing plant reproductive success and
community dynamics (Morse, 2007). Their unique
role as “ sit-and-wait ” predators make them
excellent indicators of habitat health and floral
diversity. However, India's recorded fauna accounts
for only about 9.7% of this global total (WSC,
2021), with initial descriptions dating back to
Stoliczka (1869). While the diversity of Thomisids
has been extensively documented in certain regions

Thomisus (Walckenaer, 1805) is particularly noted
within this family.
The colourful, vibrant fragrance of the flower plants
captures our attention, along with insects and bees.
The science campus of the University of Allahabad,
Prayagraj, U.P., India, provides a suitable habitat for
these spiders. Its landscape features seasonal
flowers, such as Marigolds (Tagetes spp.), which
attract a variety of insects, serving as a vital food

source during winter. Crab spiders thrive in this
environment, yet often go unnoticed due to their
exceptional cryptic coloration, blending seamlessly
with flowers and foliage (Singh et al., 2023).
Typically measuring 3 to 12 mm in body length,
these spiders possess a distinctive short, broad body
with a clear cephalothorax and abdomen. Their
eight eyes are arranged in two rows of four. A key
characteristic is their robust front two pairs of legs,
larger than the others, used laterally to grasp prey
before delivering a venomous bite.
Crab spiders are ambush predators, employing a ‘sit
-and-wait’ strategy rather than active pursuit. They
patiently lie in wait within flowers, on leaves,
amidst foliage, or in leaf litter, capturing
unsuspecting prey that comes within reach.
Significant sexual dimorphism is common, with
males often being considerably smaller than
females. While many hunt among vegetation, some
species utilize crevices in tree trunks for shelter or
as hunting posts (Cardoso et al., 2011). These
spiders play an essential role in maintaining the
ecological balance of the environment. Despite the
ecological importance of these predators, no
systematic study has previously evaluated the
species composition and microhabitat preferences
of Thomisidae within the campus of the University
of Allahabad. This study aims to fill this knowledge
gap by assessing the species richness and diversity
Indices of Crab spiders in the campus ecosystem;
documenting their specific host plant associations,
and highlighting the role of pesticide free urban
green spaces in conserving “unseen” arthropod
diversity. The novelty of this work lies in its focus
on a managed Institutional landscape as a refuge for
cryptic predators amidst an expanding urban
environment.
The specific chromatic cues
employed by invertebrate predators, such as spiders,
in the detection and response to mimicry are not yet
fully elucidated. Existing research shows that
spiders can learn to associate signals with aversive
outcomes (Huang et al., 2011), but the precise color
patterns mediating their responses to mimicry
require further investigation. The study sites
focused on areas within the campus known for
potential crab spider diversity, particularly those
rich in flowering plants.

study area comprises approximately 10 hectares of
diverse urban microhabitats, including manicured
ornamental gardens, dense shrubbery, seasonal
flower beds, and semi-wild patches with secondary
vegetation. The climate is typical of the Indo-

Gangetic plains, characterized by three distinct
seasons: a hot-dry summer (March-June), a humid
monsoon (July-October), and a cool winter
(November-February). A notable feature of the
selected areas is the absence of chemical pesticide
application, fostering higher insect diversity.
Sampling technique
Field surveys were conducted weekly throughout the
two -year study period to ensure full seasonal
coverage. Spiders were located using direct visual
encounter surveys (Tikader, 1980). Each sampling
session lasted approximately 3-4 hours, conducted
during peak spider activity periods (08:00 - 11:00
AM and 04:00 - 06:00 PM). While visual search on
flowers (especially Tagetes and Dahlia spp.) was the
primary method, it was supplemented by the ‘handpicking’ method and ‘branch shaking’ over a white
cloth for spiders inhabiting denser foliage. No pitfall
traps or sweep nets were used to avoid damaging the
ornamental floral beds and to focus specifically on
the flower-dwelling Thomisid guild.
Data Collection
Data were gathered through direct observation and
photographic documentation. Recorded information
included the presence of crab spiders, their specific
microhabitat (type of flower, leaf surface), observed
hunting
behaviours
and
morphological
characteristics. While the majority of specimens were
observed and photographed in-situ to minimize
ecological impact, representative voucher specimens
(n=2 per species) were collected for laboratory
verification. Collected specimens were euthanized
using ethyl acetate and preserved in 70% ethanol in
glass vials, properly labelled with date, locality, and
host plant information.
Identification
Identification to the family (Thomisidae) and genus
level (-Thomisus) was based on characteristic
morphological features described in taxonomic
literature, including body shape, relative leg size
(robust front pairs), eye arrangement. Detailed
taxonomic keys from Tikader (1980, 1987), Barrion
& Litsinger (1995), and the World Spider Catalog
(2025) were consulted. Size measurements were
estimated visually or from photographs using a scale.
Ethical Permission and Specimen Deposition: This
study was conducted in accordance with the ethical
guidelines for invertebrate research. No endangered
or protected species were harmed during the survey.
Specimens
have
been
deposited
in
the
Arachnological Collection of the Department of
Zoology, University of Allahabad for future
reference and taxonomic validation.

commonly accepted ecological indices. The
calculations were performed as follows:

The Shannon Diversity Index (H′) was calculated to
quantify species richness and evenness.
The formula was used
H′=−∑i=1S (pi ln pi)
The calculated Shannon diversity index for this study
was H′=2.1809.
Simpson's diversity index
To gain a comprehensive understanding of species
diversity, we calculated several forms of Simpson's
Diversity Index. This index essentially tells us the
likelihood that any two individuals picked at random
from a community will be of the same species. It was
calculated using the formula.
D=∑i=1S(pi2)
where pi is the proportion of individuals belonging to
species i. The calculated value for D was 0.1139.
Simpson's Index of Diversity (1-D)
This index represents the probability that two
randomly selected individuals will belong to
different species. It was calculated as 1−D. For this
study,
1−D=1−0.1139=0.8861.
Simpson's Reciprocal Index (1/D)
This index provides a measure of the "effective
number of species" indicating the number of equally
abundant species required to produce the observed
diversity. It was calculated as 1/D. In this study, 1/
D=1/0.1139=8.78.
Statistical analysis
Experimental data underwent statistical analysis
using Excel 2016 and SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS

Result and discussion

The ecological observations conducted at the
University of Allahabad science campus confirm its
role as a vital urban microhabitat supporting a
diverse population of Thomisidae crab spiders.
During photographic documentation in a flower
field, an area noted for its insect richness due to the

lack of pesticides, a significant predator-prey
interaction involving a crab spider was observed
(Sidheek, 2021). ,,The primary hunting strategy
observed was " sit-and-wait " ambush predation
(Nentwig et al., 2022), typically executed from
flowers or leaves. Spiders were documented
successfully capturing insect visitors, such as
bumblebees.
A total of 168 individuals representing 10 species
and 4 genera were recorded (Table 1). The
assemblage was dominated by the genus Thomisus,
which accounted for 60% of the total species
richness.
Quantitative analysis of diversity
The community structure was analyzed using
multiple diversity indices to ensure a robust
ecological profile (Table 2). The Shannon Diversity
Index (H' = 2.1809) indicates a moderately high
diversity for a localized urban campus, suggesting a
complex niche partitioning among the spiders. The
Simpson’s Index of Diversity (1 - D = 0.8861) and
the high Simpson’s Reciprocal Index (1/D = 8.78)
further demonstrate that the community is not
dominated by a single species. Instead, the
‘effective number of species’ (8.78) is remarkably
close to the actual observed species richness (10),
proving high species evenness (Pielou’s Evenness J'
~ 0.94). Thomisus spectabilis was the most abundant
(n=30, relative abundance 17.8%), while Camaricus
formosus was the rarest (n=6, relative abundance
3.5%).

specific floral architecture of Asteraceae
plants.Habitat Preference and Floral Association: A
significant positive correlation was observed
between Asteraceae density and Thomisid
abundance. Unlike generalist hunting spiders, these
crab
spiders
showed
‘substrate-matching’
preferences; yellow-bodied Thomisus species were
predominantly
found
on
Tagetes
and
Chrysanthemum. This suggests that floral choice is
not random but is an evolutionary adaptation for
maximizing ‘cryptic efficiency’ against avian
predators and improving capture rates of pollinators
like Apis florea and Bombus spp. (Heiling et al.,
2003).
Seasonality and Conservation Significance: The
population peaks coincided with the winter
blooming season (November to February), which
provided both maximum camouflage and a high
influx of prey. The absence of chemical pesticides
on the science campus is likely the primary driver
for this healthy population. In contrast to ‘urban
sinks’ where heavy pesticide uses leads to local
extinction of sensitive predators, this campus acts as
an ‘urban refuge’. Our findings provide credence to
the "Passive Conservation" theory, which holds that
institutional green spaces preserve high levels of
biodiversity that are frequently lost in Prayagraj's
surrounding urban structure.

Conclusion
In conclusion, the science campus of the University
of Allahabad ‘Diversity and distribution’ of spider,
as evidenced by the documentation of 10 distinct
Thomisidae species representing a significant
portion of regional crab spider richness. This study
confirms that the campus serves as a critical urban
microhabitat, where the intricate relationship
between floral diversity and cryptic predators
remains intact.
The findings underscore the high conservation value
of institutional green spaces; by maintaining
pesticide-free zones and a high density of
Asteraceae plants, the campus functions as a
functional ecological corridor for specialized
arthropod guilds. These results provide a baseline
for future biodiversity assessments and suggest that
urban landscape management should prioritize
floral heterogeneity to support higher trophic levels.
Ultimately, this research highlights that even small,
managed environments can mitigate the loss of
biodiversity in urbanizing landscapes. Maintaining
these ‘pesticide-free refuges’ is essential for
preserving not only charismatic predators like crab
spiders but also the complex predator-prey
dynamics and pollination services they indirectly
support. Further systematic surveys over a larger
geographical scale could provide deeper insights
into the long-term population dynamics and the
resilience of these spiders to urban environmental
stressors.
