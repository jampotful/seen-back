Motion enhances camouflage: Retinal evidence for flicker fusion camouflage in jumping spiders
Min Tan, Alex M. Winsor, Long Yu, Eunice J. Tan, Elizabeth M. Jakob, Daiqin Li (2026). iScience. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13520617/. doi:10.1016/j.isci.2026.117225. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Motion enhances camouflage: Retinal evidence for flicker fusion camouflage in jumping spiders

## Abstract
SummaryCamouflage is often assumed to fail once prey move, yet theory predicts that rapid motion can reduce detectability through flicker fusion camouflage (FFC), where high-contrast patterns blur into perceptual uniformity. We tested this prediction using retinal tracking in the jumping spider Menemerus bivittatus, a predator with a highly modular visual system. Using computer-generated stimuli varying in pattern, speed, size, and contrast, combined with anterior lateral eye (ALE) masking, we show that small, fast-moving thin stripes strongly suppressed retinal responsiveness. Low-contrast stripes further impaired retinal tracking compared to high-contrast stripes, indicating that signal salience modulates motion processing. ALE-masking validated the motion-sensitivity of ALEs and their critical role in FFC. Together, these findings establish FFC as an ecologically plausible strategy and reveal how prey pattern, movement, and predator sensory architecture jointly shape visual detectability. Our results challenge traditional viewpoints of camouflage and highlight the dynamic nature of visual concealment in predator-prey coevolution.

## Introduction
Motion is essential to survival—animals must move to forage, find mates, and evade predators. While camouflage is well understood,1 the mechanisms by which animals maintain concealment while moving remain poorly explored.2,3,4,5,6 This gap has fueled a long-standing debate: does motion inevitably break concealment by revealing prey,7,8,9,10 or can certain visual patterns exploit the sensory constraints of predators to maintain concealment during movement?11,12,13,14
Flicker fusion camouflage (FFC) is hypothesized to support concealment during motion.13,15,16 When high-contrast patterns with stripes oriented perpendicular to the direction of motion move sufficiently rapidly, the alternating light and dark elements may exceed the receiver’s spatiotemporal resolution limits. Beyond this threshold, temporally modulated signals are no longer perceived as discrete events but instead blur into a uniform field, reducing pattern visibility.13,15,16 Thin, high-contrast stripes moving at high speeds would theoretically blur effectively, while thicker stripes remain more detectable.4,16,17 Despite indirect evidence,17,18,19,20 direct empirical tests of FFC remain surprisingly scarce, with only one behavioral study providing support to date.21
Jumping spiders (Araneae: Salticidae) provide an ideal system to address this gap. Their modular visual system partitions visual tasks between different eye types: the large principal anterior median eyes (AMEs) resolve fine details, while the anterior lateral eyes (ALEs) specialize in wide-field motion detection.22,23 Thus, masking different eye types can reveal how patterns and movement are processed both separately and in tandem. Critically, infrared retinal eye-tracking enables real-time, non-invasive quantification of retinal movements, providing a direct window into sensory detection at the input stage.24 Unlike behavioral assays that capture downstream motor responses, this approach reveals whether FFC genuinely impairs detection and tracking.
Here, we combined high-resolution retinal eye-tracking with selective eye-masking in the jumping spider Menemerus bivittatus to test whether FFC reduces pattern detectability across four experiments (Figure 1). Menemerus bivittatus spiders were first presented with five computer-generated stimuli (uniform gray, uniform black, background-matching, thin-striped, and thick-striped) moving horizontally across a textured background at three speeds. If FFC were to occur, the black-and-white stripes should blur into a uniform gray when moving at sufficiently high speeds, thereby eliciting reduced detectability similar to that for the gray stimulus.16 The black stimulus served as a high-contrast control, while the gray stimulus provided a benchmark for perceptual blurring at high speeds, a key FFC prediction.21 By varying stripe width, we tested how the spatial resolution of the stripes influences the blurring effect; thick stripes should blur less effectively and be more detectable than thin stripes.21 We also explored the effects of stimulus size and stripe contrast on FFC. Both factors have been shown to influence detectability and depth perception,25,26,27 and may potentially affect the blurring effectiveness on moving stripes. Specifically, small or low-contrast striped stimuli are expected to be less detectable than large or high-contrast striped stimuli. ## Results

## Small, thin-striped stimuli were less detectable at high speeds
Using high-resolution retinal eye-tracking, we first tested the predictions of FFC by manipulating stimulus pattern and speed (experiment 1; Figure 1; Video S1) and quantifying retinal responses in M. bivittatus. As stimuli were presented in both directions (i.e., right-to-left and left-to-right), movement direction was included as an additional factor in the analysis. Model selection based on Akaike’s information criterion corrected (AICc) for small sample sizes28 identified stimulus pattern, speed, and movement direction as significant predictors of both retinal response and response latency (defined as the time from stimulus appearance to first retinal movement; Tables S1–S4). Retinal response probability (as a binary response) was used as a measure of stimulus detectability, whereas response latency reflects the speed of detection. Both retinal response probability and response latency declined with increasing speed (retinal response: unstandardized β = –0.292, standardized β = –0.89, SE = 0.028, p < 0.0001; response latency: unstandardized β = –0.056, standardized β = –0.110, SE = 0.006, p < 0.0001; Tables S2 and S4), indicating that rapid motion impaired stimulus detection. Direction of stimulus motion had a weaker but significant effect: right-to-left motion elicited higher retinal responses (unstandardized β = 0.698, standardized β = 0.7, SE = 0.165, p < 0.0001; Table S2) and longer latencies (unstandardized β = 0.245, standardized β = 0.14, SE = 0.035, p < 0.0001; Table S4), likely reflecting asymmetries in spider orientation within the eye-tracking setup.

Among all patterns, the black stimulus consistently evoked the strongest retinal responses and shortest latencies across speeds (Figures 2A and 2B), confirming high conspicuity. In contrast, gray and background-matching stimuli consistently produced lower response probabilities (Figure 2A) and longer response latencies compared with the black stimulus, particularly at low speeds (Figures 2B and 2D), indicating reduced detectability. Thin- and thick-striped stimuli were readily detectable at low speeds, with the thick-striped stimulus being significantly more detectable than the gray stimulus (Figure 2C). However, both striped stimuli elicited longer response latencies than the black stimulus, regardless of speed (Figures 2D, 2F, and 2H), suggesting slower visual processing or reduced perceptual clarity, consistent with FFC predictions. Post-hoc comparisons revealed speed-dependent pattern effects (Figures 2C–2H). At low speed, only the background-matching stimulus elicited significantly lower responses than the black stimulus, suggesting relatively similar detectability among most patterns (Figure 2C). However, at medium and high speeds, all patterned stimuli, apart from the black stimulus, showed reduced detectability (Figures 2E and 2G). At high speed, response probabilities to all patterned stimuli converged and were indistinguishable from one another, except for the black stimulus, which remained highly detectable (Figure 2G). Although thick stripes tended to elicit higher response probabilities and longer response latencies than thin stripes, these differences were not significant (Figures 2C–2H). Notably, thick stripes remained significantly more detectable than gray even at high speed (Figure 2G), indicating that stripe width modulates pattern visibility under rapid motion. Both striped stimuli also elicited significantly longer response latencies than the black stimulus at the highest speed (Figure 2H), suggesting that fast-moving stripes impaired visual processing. Together, these results provide direct support for FFC hypothesis: thin stripes moving at high speeds lose visual definition and become less detectable.

## Retinal torsion reflected stimulus engagement
We quantified retinal torsion, which refers to the mean angular change over time, as a measure of eye movement engagement across stimulus conditions. Torsion varied significantly with stimulus pattern, speed, and direction in both retinas (Figure 3; Tables S5 and S6). Post-hoc comparisons further revealed that differences in torsion among pattern types were speed-dependent for both left and right retinas (Figure 3). At low and high speeds, gray and background-matching stimuli induced greater torsion in the right retina (Figures 3B and 3F), whereas the black stimulus induced greater torsion in the left retina at the medium speed (Figure 3C). ## Small stimuli were harder to detect than large stimuli
We tested whether FFC is constrained by stimulus size (small versus large; experiment 2; Figure 1; Video S2). Small stimuli consistently elicited weaker retinal responses (unstandardized β = –3.926, standardized β = –3.93, SE = 0.55, p < 0.0001; Tables S7 and S8; Figure 4A) and longer latencies than large stimuli (unstandardized β = 0.636, standardized β = 0.36, SE = 0.044, p < 0.0001; Tables S9 and S10; Figure 4B). For large stimuli, detection remained uniformly high across all patterns and speeds (Figure 4A), indicating that FFC was ineffective at this stimulus size. As responses to large stimuli approached saturation (99.3% detection, 876/882 responses; 91.3% tracking, 797/873 tracking responses), we quantified retinal tracking duration to assess subtler effects. Sliding-window cross-correlation analysis showed that speed, rather than pattern, and the interaction between pattern and speed had the most consistent influence on tracking duration (left retina: unstandardized β = –0.397, standardized β = –0.865, SE = 0.007, p < 0.0001; right retina: unstandardized β = –0.395, standardized β = –0.37, SE = 0.007, p < 0.0001; Tables S11 and S12). Slower stimuli were tracked for longer simply due to extended visibility, while pattern effects were negligible. Thus, while FFC operates under size constraints, once stimuli are large enough to be conspicuous, the pattern has little effect on detection.

## Low-contrast stripes were more difficult to track than high-contrast stripes
To test whether stripe contrast affects stimulus detectability, we compared spider response latency across thin- and thick-striped stimuli presented in high versus low contrast (experiment 3; Figure 1). Overall, response rates were near saturation (99.4%, 339/341), likely due to the use of large, highly visible stimuli. Stimulus speed, not stripe contrast or pattern, was the primary factor influencing response latency (unstandardized β = –0.043, standardized β = –0.08, SE = 0.009, p < 0.0001; Table S13); fast-moving stimuli elicited significantly shorter latencies.
Although stripe contrast did not influence latency, retinal tracking revealed subtle effects (Figure S1; Tables S14–S16): low-contrast stripes were tracked for shorter durations than high-contrast stripes by the left retina (unstandardized β = –0.075, standardized β = –0.05, SE = 0.032, p = 0.021; Table S16), but not the right retina (unstandardized β = –0.042, standardized β = –0.042, SE = 0.039, p = 0.281; Table S16). Thus, while stripe contrast does not affect the likelihood or speed of initial detection, it may potentially influence the effectiveness of stimulus tracking.

## ALE-masked spiders failed to detect moving stimuli
Masking the ALEs (experiment 4; Figure 1) resulted in a dramatic reduction in spider responses to all stimuli and speeds (Figure 4C; Tables S17 and S18). Although the black stimulus elicited slightly higher response rates than other patterns, these differences were not statistically significant (Tables S17 and S18). ALE-masking elicited the strongest effect (unstandardized β = −5.505, standardized β = −5.51, SE = 0.374, p < 0.0001; Table S18), confirming the crucial role of ALEs in motion detection. These results demonstrate that while AMEs contribute to visual processing, they are insufficient to support reliable detection of moving stimuli.

## Discussion
This study provides empirical support for FFC and reveals how stimulus size, stripe contrast, and visual system architecture interact to shape the detectability of moving patterns. FFC was most effective for small, thin-striped stimuli at high speeds, where detectability converged with uniform gray controls. This outcome aligns with core FFC predictions that rapid motion can exceed a predator’s critical flicker fusion frequency, causing high-frequency patterns to blur into uniformity and reduce their salience.15,16
Across all experiments, stimulus speed consistently emerged as the dominant predictor of both detection probability and response latency. Rapid motion reduced detection probability and response latency, regardless of pattern, size, or contrast, underscoring speed itself as a potential camouflage mechanism. Furthermore, rapidly moving stimuli triggered shorter response latencies. One possible explanation is that spiders exhibiting longer response latencies at low speeds may fail to detect or respond to very fast-moving stimuli altogether. Alternatively, rapid motion may capture perceptual attention more effectively, resulting in faster responses once detected. The mechanisms behind motion perception and attentional processing therefore warrant further investigation. Even large, high-contrast striped stimuli became harder to detect at high speeds, consistent with findings in other invertebrates, such as mantids and spiders, showing that prey moving quickly are intrinsically more difficult to track or capture.21,25 Thus, these findings suggest that speed is not merely a confounding factor, but an essential component of motion-based camouflage.
The interaction between pattern and speed—a hallmark of FFC—was most evident in our first experiment. Although small thick-striped stimuli tended to be more detectable than small thin-striped stimuli, this difference was not significant, suggesting that a broader range of stripe widths should be tested to fully resolve the role of spatial frequency on FFC. In contrast, stimulus size imposed a critical constraint: large stimuli remained conspicuous across all conditions, whereas small thin-striped stimuli were disproportionately affected. This trend supports the prediction that effective stripe spatial frequency, determined by stripe size and viewing distance, governs the onset and strength of FFC.16 Compared with the conspicuous black stimulus, prolonged response latencies to both thin- and thick-striped patterns at high speeds (Figure 2H) further suggest that processing rapidly moving stripes is visually demanding. Rather than conferring camouflage, such conditions may instead generate motion dazzle, whereby stripes disrupt estimates of speed and trajectory.16,29,30,31 Alternatively, increased response latencies may reflect hesitation in attack decisions, consistent with proposed behavioral effects of flicker fusion.16 By demonstrating that jumping spiders are susceptible to these perceptual limits, our results extend the camouflage mechanism of FFC beyond vertebrate receiver systems and highlight its broader ecological relevance in arthropod predator-prey interactions.
Retinal torsion results provide further insights into how jumping spiders engage with stimuli. Gray and background-matching stimuli elicited heightened torsion at high speeds (Figure 3F), potentially reflecting delayed detection and increased attentional investment once visually cryptic stimuli were perceived.32 In contrast, large stimuli induced robust but less pattern-specific retinal tracking, suggesting that high visibility reduces visual demand for pattern discrimination, specifically for focused tracking.
Stripe contrast also modulates the visual processing of moving stimuli. Although low- and high-contrast stripes were both highly detectable, large low-contrast stimuli were tracked for shorter durations, particularly in the left retina. This suggests that stimulus contrast influences sustained tracking, rather than initial detection, consistent with predictions of FFC that low-contrast patterns blur more readily in motion. However, empirical support remains limited as our tests were restricted to large stimuli at two contrast levels. Future research should systematically manipulate both stripe contrast and stimulus size to better resolve their joint effects on FFC.
Our eye-masking experiment confirms the critical role of the ALEs in motion detection. ALE-masked spiders displayed near-complete loss of responsiveness across all patterns and speeds, consistent with prior findings.24 Without ALE input, the AMEs alone were insufficient for reliable detection of rapid motion. These results reinforce the functional modularity of the jumping spider visual system—AMEs are specialized for high-acuity pattern vision, while ALEs are employed for motion detection—and highlight the importance of temporal rather than spatial acuity in detecting motion-blurred patterns.22,23
Stripes are among the most widespread and repeatedly evolved animal patterns across taxa.33 Our results show that even conspicuous, high-contrast stripes can function effectively in motion-based camouflage. This may appear counterintuitive, as background-matching body coloration is traditionally considered the most effective form of concealment.1 While such cryptic color patterns may conceal the animal effectively, they lack flexibility. In contrast, striped patterns can contribute to concealment through disruptive coloration,1,34 while also serving multiple functions, including sexual signaling, social dominance, prey attraction, antipredator defense, and thermoregulation.11,29,35,36,37,38 In the context of antipredator defense, stripes provide behaviorally and ecologically versatile solutions: animals can potentially switch between concealment, deception, or warning depending on their behavior, the environment, and predator sensory constraints.13,15,31,38,39,40
Our use of eye-tracking was crucial for uncovering these mechanisms, offering mechanistic insights into how motion and camouflage are processed in real time. Unlike behavioral assays that reflect downstream motor output, retinal tracking directly captures sensory detection. Small, fast-moving stimuli often failed to elicit retinal responses, likely because they traversed the visual field too quickly to be processed. Conversely, large stimuli were tracked more readily but with increasing delays at higher speeds, suggesting that sensory lag, rather than motivation or attention, constrains real-time object tracking. Comparable sensory bottlenecks have been documented in other taxa,21 suggesting broad limits on motion perception across taxa. Future research could quantify the specific velocity thresholds beyond which motion detection collapses.
The ecological relevance of FFC hinges on whether animals can attain speeds sufficient to exceed the visual limits of their viewers. The speeds tested here (<10 cm/s) are well within the escape speeds reported for insects and spiders (approximately 60–99 cm/s), which constitute both predators and prey of M. bivittatus.41,42 As the discrimination distance of M. bivittatus extends to approximately 12.5 cm,43 the relatively short viewing distance in the experimental setup (approximately 2.5 cm) may influence the perceived speed and spatial frequency of visual patterns, critical determinants of FFC.16,43 Since stripe spatial frequency depends jointly on stripe width and viewing distance,4,16 our experimental conditions may be limited in capturing real-world perceptual geometry. Although the multi-lens optics of the eye-tracker can project stimuli at virtual distances beyond 2.5 cm from the spider,44 some deviation from the natural interaction distances of M. bivittatus with predators or prey is unavoidable.
In summary, our results provide robust empirical support for FFC in an invertebrate predator, showing that rapid motion, small size, high-frequency patterns, and visual system architecture jointly shape whether stimuli remain distinct or fuse perceptually. These findings broaden the conceptual framework of camouflage by demonstrating that motion can, under specific conditions, conceal rather than reveal. More broadly, our results highlight the value of integrating sensory biology, behavioral ecology, and neuroethology to understand how dynamic camouflage strategies operate in real time.

## Limitations of the study
Several caveats merit consideration. First, while the refresh rates of our display exceeded reported flicker fusion thresholds in jumping spiders, rapidly moving striped stimuli may induce temporal aliasing (e.g., the wagon-wheel effect), thus potentially biasing motion perception.45 Future investigations of FFC using higher refresh rates could help minimize such artifacts. Second, while we used eye masking to isolate the role of specific visual pathways, a sham control (e.g., paint applied to the carapace) would further exclude potential side effects of the manipulation, although the controlled arrangement of the spider on the eye-tracker setup likely reduces this concern. Finally, FFC is inherently context-dependent, with its efficacy shaped by viewing distance, background complexity, and ambient illumination. Testing under more naturalistic conditions will therefore be key to establishing the ecological validity of FFC.

## Experimental model and study participant details
Adult Menemerus bivittatus were reared under controlled laboratory conditions (25 ± 1°C, 16:8 h light: dark cycle). Spiders were housed individually in plastic enclosures (18 × 13 × 10 cm) with green wooden sticks for enrichment. Spiders were fed twice weekly with crickets (Acheta domesticus) or fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster) and provided with continuous access to water.

## Method details

## Background and stimuli
For all the experiments, spiders were presented with computer-generated stimuli moving horizontally across a textured background (Figure 1). The background, adapted from Tan et al.,25 was constructed from layered photographs of the rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis) trunks—where M. bivittatus can be found in the field. Five stimulus patterns were adapted from Umeton et al.21: uniform gray, uniform black, background-matching, vertically thin-striped, and vertically thick-striped. The background-matching stimulus was cropped from the central region of the background image, whereas the remaining patterns were generated in ImageJ v1.5446 and MATLAB R2022a.47
Stimulus size varied across experiments: “small” (3 × 1.5 mm; 12 × 6 pixels at 96 ppi) in Experiments 1 and 4, and “large” (7.6 × 4.1 mm; 30 × 15 pixels at 96 ppi) in Experiments 2 and 3. To determine if the patterns were discernible to the spiders, the AcuityView package48 was used to simulate how small, patterned stimuli were perceived when stationary (Figure S2). The spatial acuity of Phidippus sp. (i.e., 0.13°)23 was used as proxy for that of M. bivittatus. Experiment 3 included low-contrast versions of thin- and thick-striped stimuli. For small stimuli, the widths of the thin and thick stripes were 0.33 mm and 0.6 mm, respectively, whereas for large stimuli they were 0.84 mm and 1.52 mm. All stimuli were luminance-matched (average ∼102 cd/m2) to the background (101 cd/m2). High-contrast stripes (Experiments 1 and 2) ranged from 0 to 255 cd/m2 whereas low-contrast stripes (Experiment 3) ranged from 52 to 177 cd/m2.
Stimuli animations were compiled in PowerPoint and projected using Processing v2.2.1 via a projector (AAXA P300 Neo, 60 Hz refresh rate, California, USA) onto a Roscolux frosted diffusion filter screen (4.9 × 4.9 cm; Gel/116, 9% transmission). This setup exceeded the flicker fusion threshold of jumping spiders.49 Stimulus presentations and retinal responses were recorded using the image acquisition toolbox in MATLAB and displayed simultaneously on a secondary monitor using the Open Broadcasting Software (OBS).

## Mounting and calibrating spiders to the eye-tracker
Spiders were restrained between Parafilm sheets, leaving only the AMEs and ALEs exposed. The Parafilm was coated with blackboard paint (Americana Chalkboard Paint) to minimize reflections. Each spider was mounted on a fiber-coupled LED (Thorlabs infrared 940 nm w/intensity modulator) positioned 2.5 cm from the eye-tracker lens for optimal retinal focus. AME retinas were aligned using Processing v2.2.1, following Jakob et al.24 Spiders were acclimated for 5 min before trials began.

## Experimental design
All experiments were conducted in a darkened room. The sequence of stimulus presentations was randomized, with 1-min intervals between trials.

## Experiment 1 (pattern and speed; small stimuli)
47 spiders (29 females, 18 males) were presented with five small-sized, stimulus patterns at three speeds (2.45, 4.9, 9.8 cm/s; corresponding to 1.3, 0.7, 0.3 s across the ALEs’ field of view) in both directions (Video S1).

## Experiment 2 (stimulus size)
22 spiders (13 females, 9 males) viewed the same patterns as in Experiment 1, but in the large size and at five speeds (0.63, 0.92, 1.84, 3.68, 7.31 cm/s; corresponding to 6.6, 4.4, 2.2, 1.1, 0.5 s across the ALEs’ field of view) in both directions (Video S2).

## Experiment 3 (stripe contrast)
10 females viewed low-contrast thin- and thick-striped stimuli at the same speeds as in Experiment 2.

## Experiment 4 (ALE-Masking)
21 spiders (10 females, 11 males) with >50% response rate in Experiment 1 were tested under ALE-masked conditions. Note that the exterior lens of the eye is part of the spider’s carapace and does not move. Spiders were presented with the same stimulus patterns and speeds as in Experiment 1. Prior to masking, they were presented with moving black stimuli to confirm responsiveness. Masking followed Jakob et al.24: two layers of orange acrylic paint (Apple Barrel “Pumpkin”) were applied to the ALEs with a micro brush. Paint color was chosen for its high visibility against black eyes. Trials began 15 min after application. Paint was easily removed after trials by swiftly detaching it with a fine probe.

## Response scoring and annotation
Stimulus and retinal videos were overlaid (30% opacity) in Adobe Premiere Pro (2025). For each trial, we scored binary responses (1 = retinal movement, 0 = no response) and response latency (time for stimulus onset to first retinal movement). Only trials with <25% responses to the black control were excluded for analyses, resulting in the removal of four trials. Responsive trials were subsequently analyzed frame-by-frame at 10 fps. Using ImageJ v1.5446 with MTrackJ, five landmarks were tracked per frame: the centers (inner elbow) and tips of the left and right retinas, where visual acuity is highest, as well as the stimulus center. Retinal torsion—defined as the mean angular change between consecutive frames—was calculated separately for the left and right retinas.

## Quantification and statistical analysis

## Statistical analysis
All analyses were conducted in R v4.4.2.50 Model selection followed Akaike’s information criterion for small sample sizes (AICc)28 using the MuMIn package.51 Model fit was assessed using residual diagnostics from the DHARMa package52 for mixed models, and multicollinearity was checked via variance inflation factors.

## Experiment 1 (speed and pattern)
Eight generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) with binomial error structures and a logit-link function were constructed using the lme4 package.53 The binary response variable (0 = no response, 1 = response) was modeled with fixed effects of speed, pattern, and direction; spider ID was included as a random intercept. Sex was excluded due to lack of significance in preliminary models. Competing models comprised: (i) the null model (1), (ii) single-factor models (3), (iii) additive and interaction terms for speed and pattern (2), and (iv) full models with and without interaction terms (2). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons of stimulus patterns at each speed level were conducted using the emmeans package54 with Tukey’s correction. Response latencies were analyzed using eight linear mixed models (LMMs) on log-transformed values. Response latency was included as the response variable while the same predictors were used. To assess the effects of pattern and speed on retinal torsion, we fitted six LMMs. Left and right retinal torsion values were analyzed separately as response variables, as they occasionally differed in movement magnitude. Stimulus pattern, speed, and direction were included as fixed effects. Retinal torsion was quantified across frames extending up to 10 frames following stimulus offset.

## Experiment 2 (stimulus size)
Nine GLMMs and LMMs tested spider binary responses and response latencies, respectively, with stimulus size as an additional fixed effect. Retinal tracking duration was quantified with a sliding-window cross-correlation function (CCF). Stimuli presented at five speeds (2–10 s durations) were grouped into fast (two highest speeds) and slow (three lowest speeds). For each trial, tracking windows of 8 frames (fast) and 10 frames (slow) were evaluated in steps of two frames. Windows with CCF ≥ 0.7 were flagged as potential tracking periods. To reduce false positives, tracking was confirmed only if ≥ 2 (fast) or ≥ 4 (slow) consecutive windows met this threshold. Tracking durations for left and right retinas were extracted for each trial. To determine whether retinal tracking duration was influenced by pattern, speed, and direction, seven linear models tested log-transformed tracking duration against pattern, speed, and direction.

## Experiment 3 (stripe contrast)
To test contrast effects, seven LMMs evaluated the fixed effects of pattern, speed, and stripe contrast (high vs. low) on log-transformed response latency. Additional linear models tested contrast effects on retinal tracking duration, with pattern excluded due to convergence issues.

## Experiment 4 (ALE-Masking)
Eleven GLMMs compared unmasked (Experiment 1) and ALE-masked (Experiment 4) data, testing the fixed effects of masking treatment, speed, pattern, direction, and the interactions between speed and pattern.
