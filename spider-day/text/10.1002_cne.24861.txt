Abstract

36

Some animals have evolved task differentiation among their eyes. A particular example is

37

spiders, where most species have eight eyes, of which two (the principal eyes) are used for

38

object discrimination, whereas the other three pairs (secondary eyes) detect movement. In

39

the spider species Cupiennius salei these two eye types correspond to two visual pathways in

40

the brain. Each eye is associated with its own first and second order visual neuropil. The

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

41

second order neuropils of the principal eyes are connected to the arcuate body, whereas the

42

second order neuropils of the secondary eyes are linked to the mushroom body. However,

43

eye size and visual fields are considerably different in jumping spiders. We explored the

44

principal- and secondary eye visual pathways of the jumping spider Marpissa muscosa. We

45

found that the connectivity of the principal eye pathway is the same as in C. salei, while there

46

are differences in the secondary eye pathways. In M. muscosa, all secondary eyes are

47

connected to their own first order visual neuropils. The first order visual neuropils of the

48

anterior lateral and posterior lateral eyes are further connected with two second order visual

49

neuropils, whereas the posterior median eyes lack second order visual neuropils and their

50

axons project only to the arcuate body. This suggests that the posterior median eyes probably

51

do not serve movement detection in M. muscosa. Furthermore, the second order visual

52

neuropil (L2) in Marpissa muscosa potentially integrates information from the secondary eyes

53

and might thus enable faster movement decisions.

1 Introduction

61

In some animal species with multiple eyes, different eyes serve different specific tasks, such

62

as compound eyes and ocelli in insects or the rhopalia in box jellyfishes (Garm et al., 2007;

63

O’Connor et al., 2009; Paulus, 1979). Spiders vary strongly in their visual abilities, as well as in

64

number, arrangement and size of their eyes (reviewed in Morehouse et al. 2017). Most

65

species possess four pairs of eyes, of which one pair differs from the three other pairs in its

66

anatomy and developmental origin (Land, 1985a; Morehouse et al., 2017). The principal eyes

67

(or anterior median eyes) possess a movable retina and their spectral sensitivities allow for

68

color vision (Barth et al. 1993; Land, 1985b; Schmid, 1998; Yamashita & Tateda, 1976, 1978).

69

The other three pairs of eyes are the so-called secondary eyes (posterior median, anterior

70

lateral and posterior lateral eyes). Their morphology and anatomy differs considerably among

71

spider families (Homann, 1952, 1950), but they generally do not have a movable retina. In

72

several groups of cursorial hunting spiders, the secondary eyes are used for movement

73

detection (Land, 1985a; Morehouse et al., 2017; Schmid, 1998). Most secondary eyes possess

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

74

a light-reflecting tapetum (but absent in e.g. jumping spiders), which led to the assumption

75

that they play a major role for night or dim light vision (Foelix, 1996).

76

Jumping spiders (Salticidae) are cursorial hunters with excellent vision. Their eyes have been

77

studied in much greater detail compared to any other spider group (e.g. Harland et al., 2012;

78

Land, 1985a; Morehouse et al., 2017). The principal eyes of jumping spiders are large and face

79

forward; their field of view is small, but their spatial acuity is one of the highest among

80

invertebrate eyes (Harland et al., 2012; Land, 1985b, 1985a). The secondary eyes of salticids

81

possess only one photoreceptor type and thus cannot discriminate colors (Yamashita and

82

Tateda, 1976). Among the secondary eyes, the anterior lateral eyes (ALE) and posterior lateral

83

eyes (PLE) are large, able to detect motion, and elicit an orienting response of the principal

84

eyes towards the moving object (Duelli, 1978; Jakob et al., 2018; Spano et al., 2012; Zurek et

85

al., 2010; Zurek and Nelson, 2012a, 2012b). The posterior median eyes (PME) of most salticids

86

(except for the non-salticoids) are very small and positioned between ALE and PLE (Land,

87

1985b; Maddison and Hedin, 2003). Since the fields of view of ALE and PLE overlap, the PME

88

do not have an exclusive field of view (Land, 1985b).

89

While the eyes of jumping spiders have been studied in detail, less is known about the brain

90

structures that process visual information (Duelli, 1980; but see Hanström, 1921; Hill, 1975;

91

Nagata et al., 2019; Steinhoff et al., 2017; Strausfeld, 2012). Our knowledge on the visual

92

processing pathways in the brain of spiders is mostly based on investigations of the spider

93

model species Cupiennius salei (Keyserling, 1877) (Babu and Barth, 1984; Becherer and

94

Schmid, 1999; Schmid and Duncker, 1993; Strausfeld et al., 1993; Strausfeld and Barth, 1993).

95

C. salei possesses two separate visual pathways, one for the principal eyes and one for all

96

secondary eyes (Strausfeld et al., 1993; Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). In C. salei, every eye is

97

connected to its own first and second order visual neuropil (Barth, 2002). While the second

98

order visual neuropils of the principal eyes are connected to the arcuate body (Strausfeld et

99

al., 1993), the second order visual neuropils of the secondary eyes are associated with the

100

mushroom bodies (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). Additionally, the first order visual neuropils

101

of the secondary eyes are directly connected to the arcuate body (Babu and Barth, 1984).

102

In C. salei, similar to salticids, the principal eyes are used for object recognition, while the

103

secondary eyes serve the purpose of movement detection (Fenk, Hoinkes, & Schmid, 2010;

104

Schmid, 1998). The secondary eyes of C. salei have a high spectral sensitivity, similar to their

105

principal eyes (Barth et al., 1993) and similar to jumping spiders are capable of acute motion

106

detection (Fenk & Schmid, 2010; Zurek & Nelson, 2012b). However, they are unable to detect

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

107

color in moving objects (Orlando and Schmid, 2011) and the relative size of different eyes and

108

thus fields of view in C. salei differ considerably from jumping spiders (Land, 1985b; Land and

109

Barth, 1992). Furthermore, Steinhoff et al. (2017) showed that the structure and arrangement

110

of the secondary eye visual neuropils in the jumping spider Marpissa muscosa (Clerck, 1757)

111

differ from those in C. salei. We thus hypothesize, that the secondary eye visual pathways of

112

jumping spiders also differ from that in the model species C. salei. Here, we describe the

113

principal and secondary eye visual pathways in the brain of the jumping spider M. muscosa.

114

We use paraffin histology, immunohistochemistry, and microCT analysis to visualize tracts in

115

the visual system. We compare our findings in M. muscosa with results of earlier studies and

116

new immunohistochemical data on the principal and secondary eye pathways in the brain of

117

C. salei. We discuss the similarities and differences in the principal and secondary eye

118

pathways of these two spider species in the light of different functional roles of their eyes.

2.1 Animals

123

Adult female Marpissa muscosa were collected in and near Greifswald (Germany). Spiders

124

were fed on Drosophila sp. weekly and kept individually in plastic boxes of 145 x 110 x 68 mm

2.2 Paraffin-histology

128

The prosomata of two females of M. muscosa were fixed in Gregory’s artificially aged Bouin

129

solution (Gregory, 1980). After four days in fixative, the prosomata were dehydrated through

130

a graded ethanol series (80%, 90%, and 3x 96% ethanol for 20 minutes each) and were then

131

transferred for two hours into a 1:1 solution of 96% ethanol:tetrahydrofuran (Carl Roth,

132

CP82.1) at room temperature. Samples were kept for 24 hours in pure tetrahydrofuran

133

followed by 24 hours in a solution of 1:1 tetrahydrofuran and paraffin (Carl Roth, 6643.1) at

134

60°C. Afterwards, samples were transferred to 100% paraffin at 60°C for 48 hours and then

135

embedded in fresh paraffin. Sagittal and transversal sections (5 μm) were cut with a

136

motorized rotary microtome (Microm HM 360). Sections were stained with Azan according to

137

Geidies (Schulze and Graupner, 1960), and mounted in Roti-Histokitt II (Carl Roth, T160.1).

2.5 Western Blot

198

In western blots of Drosophila melanogaster Meigen, 1830 head homogenates (Klagges et al.,

199

1996), the monoclonal antibody mouse anti-Drosophila synapsin ‘‘Synorf 1‘‘ (provided by E.

200

201

Transferase(GST)-synapsin fusion protein) recognizes at least 4 synapsin isoforms (ca. 70, 74,

202

80, and 143 kDa). In western blot analyses in Crustacea (Harzsch and Hansson, 2008; Sullivan

203

et al., 2007) and Chilopoda (Sombke et al., 2011), isoforms in the same range (75-90 kDA)

204

were recognized. Furthermore, the Synorf 1 antibody labels synaptic neuropil in taxa as

205

diverse as Araneae (Fabian-Fine et al., 1999; Nagata et al., 2019; Steinhoff et al., 2017;

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which

Widmer et al., 2005), Chaetognatha (Harzsch and Müller, 2007) and Plathelminthes (Cebrià,

3.1 General organization of the protocerebrum

259

In Marpissa muscosa, the protocerebrum comprises bilaterally paired neuropils of the visual

260

system and the mushroom body as well as the unpaired arcuate body and the protocerebral

261

neuropil (Figs. 1, 2; for details see Steinhoff et al., 2017). All neuropils of the visual system are

262

recognizable in paraffin sections (Fig. 3) and microCT analysis (Fig. 4; supporting information,

263

Fig. S1), and are also characterized by a strong synapsin-immunoreactivity (Figs. 1; 5; 6; 7a,

264

b). Tracts and individual neurites exhibit prominent tubulin-immunoreactivity (Figs. 1; 2; 5; 6;

265

7). Furthermore, the optic nerves (between retinae and first order visual neuropils) and the

266

corresponding first order visual neuropils display histamine-

267

which is known to consistently label retinula cell axons and their terminals in the visual

268

systems of arthropods (Battelle et al., 1991; Harzsch et al., 2006; Harzsch, Wildt, Battelle, &

269

Waloszek, 2005; Nässel, 1999; Schmid & Duncker, 1993; Sombke & Harzsch, 2015). A soma

270

cortex including three discernable protocerebral clusters surrounds most of the central

271

nervous system (CNS) (Figs. 2; 5a; 6a-b, d; 7a–d). Soma cluster 1 (SC1) is located between the

272

first order visual neuropils of the lateral eyes and the second order visual neuropil of anterior

273

lateral and posterior lateral eyes (AL2/PL2), cluster 2 (SC2) medially to the AL2/PL2, and

274

cluster 3 (SC3) anteromedially to the mushroom body bridge (MBbr) (Fig. 2).

3.2 The principal eye pathway

277

The retina of the anterior median eyes (AME) is connected to its first order visual neuropil

278

(AM1), which consists of at least two, but most likely four tightly adjoining subunits (Fig.

279

1a,e,f). The AM1 is situated close to the second order visual neuropil of the AME (AM2) and

280

connected to it via parallel neurites (Fig. 1e,f). The AM2 is large, oval shaped and its posterior

281

margin within the protocerebral neuropil is not clearly recognizable in anti-synapsin labeling

282

(Fig. 1). Anti-tubulin labeling reveals a tract of neurites that connects the AM2 with the lateral

3.3 Connectivity of the first order visual neuropils of the anterior and posterior lateral eyes

287

The retinae of the anterior lateral eyes (ALE) and posterior lateral eyes (PLE) are connected

288

with their respective first order visual neuropils (AL1 and PL1) via short, thick optic nerves

289

(Figs. 3b, c; 4a-b; 5c). A subpopulation of histaminergic neurites, which are interpreted here

290

as retinula cell axons, terminates in distinct distal layers of these neuropils (AL1x and PL1x;

291

Fig. 8a–c). Both the AL1 and PL1 are connected to the second order visual neuropil of the

292

lateral eyes (L2) and the AL2/PL2 via prominent tracts of parallel ordered neurites that exhibit

293

strong tubulin-immunoreactivity (Figs. 2; 5c; 6a-b, d; 7a-b). This connectivity is also evident in

294

both, histological sections (Fig. 3) and microCT analysis (Fig. 4). The tracts are predominantly

295

formed by neurites that originate in SC1, which surrounds this region of the anterior

296

protocerebrum laterally, dorsally and medially (Figs. 2; 6a-b; 7b, d). The L2 and the AL2/PL2

297

are bi-lobed, which corresponds to the separate inputs from AL1 and PL1 (Figs. 3a; 3b, d; 5a;

298

6a, C; 8a, b). In histological sections, a thin but conspicuous tract is detectable that passes

299

between L2 and AL2/PL2 and likely terminates in the arcuate body (AB) (Figs. 3b, c; 5a, b; 6d;

300

7b). The neurites forming this tract might originate in SC1 and further innervate the AL1x and

301

PL1x, as well as the PM1 (see below; Figs. 3; 5a, b; 6d).

302
303

3.4

Connectivity of the first order visual neuropils of the posterior median eyes

304

The posterior median eyes (PME) are much smaller than all three other pairs of secondary

305

eyes, and so is their retina (Fig. 4c, e). Because of the small retina, only a thin but long nerve,

306

which enters the protocerebrum between the AL1 and PL1, connects the PME to its first order

307

visual neuropil (PM1) (Figs. 4e; 6c, c’). The PM1 is situated between AL1 and PL1 (Figs. 3a-b;

308

4e, 5b; 6c-d; 7b; 8a). A prominent tract projects from the PM1 to the dorsoventral part of the

309

protocerebrum and seems to terminate in the anterolateral apices of the AB (Fig. 3b; 5b; 6d).

3.5

The second order visual neuropil of ALE and PLE and the mushroom body

312

The second order visual neuropil of ALE and PLE (AL2/PL2) is of glomerular organization and

Visual neuropils and the mushroom body in Cupiennius salei

334

The dorsalmost neuropils in the protocerebrum of C. salei are the AM1 and AM2, which are

346

347

348

4 Discussion

355

Comparative neuroanatomical studies on different spider species show that while general

356

brain anatomy is similar, the structure of different neuropils especially within the

357

protocerebrum varies (Hanström, 1921; Long, 2016; Steinhoff et al., 2017; Weltzien and

358

Barth, 1991). The previous investigation by Steinhoff et al. (2017) on the general brain

359

anatomy of Marpissa muscosa suggested that the brains of jumping spiders and the spider

360

species Cupiennius salei differ in structure and arrangement of neuropils. Although the

361

general architecture is similar, we have shown here that the connectivity of neuropils in the

362

secondary eye pathways differ between these two species, which suggests differences in

363

information processing and function of secondary eye visual neuropils.

364

The relative size and position of the secondary eyes differs between M. muscosa and C. salei.

365

While in M. muscosa the PME is very small and oriented sideways, it is the largest eye in C.

366

salei and oriented forward (Morehouse et al., 2017; Fig. 11). The PLE are similar in relative

367

size and position, but the ALE are considerably smaller in C. salei, albeit also oriented forward

368

(Fig. 11). The relative size of the retinae as well as the brain is smaller in C. salei, which is why

369

the optic nerves associated with the eyes are much longer in C. salei (Fig. 11).

370
371

4.1 Connectivity of neuropils in the principal eye pathway

372

The connection of the AME retinula cell axons to the AM1 has recently been described in

373

detail by Nagata et al. (2019) for the jumping spider Hasarius adansoni. These authors

374

revealed that there are four photoreceptor layers in the retina, which each connect to one of

375

four subregions in the AM1 (Nagata et al., 2019). We show here, that in M. muscosa the AM1

376

is further connected to the AM2, which is the same as described for C. salei (Strausfeld et al.,

377

1993). In C. salei, two types of neurons have been identified whose axons connect the AM2

378

with the flanges and the inner part of the AB (Strausfeld et al., 1993). In M. muscosa this

379

pattern is likely the same, although we were only able to unambiguously detect neurites that

380

connect the AM2 with the flanges of the AB. There are, however, neurites that project from

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

381

the AM2 into the protocerebral neuropil and some of those may well proceed towards the

382

inner part of the AB.

383
384

4.2 Connectivity of the first order visual neuropils of the anterior and posterior lateral eyes

385

In M. muscosa, all retinula cell axons from ALE, PLE and PME seem to terminate in their

386

associated first order visual neuropils (Fig. 8). This is in accordance to what has been found in

387

other Araneae (Becherer and Schmid, 1999; Duelli, 1980; Schmid and Duncker, 1993;

388

Strausfeld et al., 1993; Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). Nevertheless, this pattern is different in

389

all other investigated taxa of Chelicerata (e.g. Scorpiones, Xiphosura, or Pycnogonida), where

390

a subset of retinula cell axons also projects into the second order visual neuropils (Battelle,

391

Sombke, & Harzsch, 2016; Lehmann & Melzer, 2013, 2018). It should be noted, however, that

392

this discrepancy between spiders and all other arachnids still needs to be scrutinized using

393

cobalt fills, which have not been employed in spiders yet (Lehmann and Melzer, 2018). In C.

394

salei, a few histaminergic neurites terminate in all second order visual neuropils, and have

395

been interpreted as interneurons (Schmid and Duncker, 1993). However, as long as no

396

neuronal tracings have been carried out, it cannot be ruled out that these neurites might

397

instead represent retinula cell axons. In M. muscosa, we did not detect any histaminergic

398

neurites that terminate in the second order visual neuropils. There are, however, numerous

399

histaminergic neurites that terminate in AL1x and PL1x and we interpret those as retinula cell

400

axons.

401

In our histological sections, it appears as if some neurites that contribute to the tract between

402

AL1, PL1 and the AB, arise in AL1x and PL1x respectively (Fig. 3b, c; 5a-b). Hill (1975) described

403

in Phidippus sp. that neurites from the AL1x domain contribute to a tract projecting towards

404

the posterior protocerebrum. However, Hill (1975) did not mention terminations of retinula

405

cell axons in the AL1x and PL1x, but detected neurites connecting the AL1 with the AL1x. Thus,

406

it is possible that Hill (1975) either misinterpreted retinula cell axons as interneurons, or that

407

we missed connecting neurites between AL1 and AL1x. In our immunohistological

408

investigations, we found that synapsin-immunoreactive substructures of first order visual

409

neuropils are also present in C. salei, the PM1x and the PL1x. In both, M. muscosa and C. salei,

410

this region is additionally characterized by strong tubulin-immunoreactivity. This can be

411

explained by the tight packing of neurites that converge at the level of the AL1x and PL1x in

412

M. muscosa and the PM1x and PL1x in C. salei to form the tracts to the respective second

413

order visual neuropils. A subdivision of PM1 and PL1 is also visible in histamine-

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

414

immunolabeled material from the study by Schmid & Duncker (1993; compare Figs. 2c,d and

415

3a,b), although not discussed by the authors. It remains open whether retinula cell axons or

416

interneurons terminate at the synapsin immunoreactive part of the PL1x and PM1x in C. salei

417

and the AL1x and PL1x in M. muscosa.

418

In M. muscosa, the convoluted first order visual neuropils of the ALE and the PLE are both

419

connected with the AL2/PL2 and the L2, as well as with the AB, which is only partially

420

consistent with Hill’s (1975) study on the nervous system in Phidippus spp., where he found

421

that axons of the AL1, and not the PL1, join the tract that projects towards the AB. Duelli

422

(1980), did not detect connections between PL1 and L2 in the salticids Evarcha arcuta (Clerck,

423

1757) and Pseudamycus validus (Thorell, 1877). He described “large field horizontal neurons,”

424

closely bypassing the AL2/PL2 and L2 and connecting the PL1 with the posterior

425

protocerebrum (Duelli, 1980). Judging from their structure and position, these neurons might

426

be identical with the neurites described in our study which contribute to the tract that

427

connects the first order visual neuropils of the secondary eyes with the arcuate body. This

428

discrepancy might be due to incomplete staining in the earlier studies where reduced-silver

429

staining was employed. Our approach combines specific and broader markers with specific

430

staining techniques. It should also be noted that the studies on the secondary eye pathways

431

in jumping spiders were conducted on different species. However, it seems unlikely that the

432

secondary eye pathways differ considerably within salticoid jumping spiders (Maddison and

433

Hedin, 2003; Morehouse et al., 2017).

434

Based on our results, AL2/PL2 and L2 are shared second order visual neuropils of both AL1

435

and PL1 in M. muscosa. The situation in C. salei is, however, different. Here, the first order

436

visual neuropils of all three secondary eyes are connected to individual second order visual

437

neuropils (compare Fig. 11a and b) (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). Each of these second order

438

visual neuropils (AL2, PL2, PM2) is composed of small neuropilar glomeruli (compare Fig. 11).

439

An unstructured second order visual neuropil (without glomeruli), such as the L2, is absent in

440

C. salei (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). Similar to the “giant interneurons” described for jumping

441

spiders by Duelli (1980), Strausfeld & Barth (1993) detected “lamina wide-field neurons” that

442

connect all first order visual neuropils with a region close to the AB. It is very likely that these

443

neurons correspond to the axons forming the tract between first order visual neuropils and

444

the AB that we described in M. muscosa.

445
446

4.3

First order visual neuropils of the posterior median eyes

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

447

The PM1 is by far the smallest first order visual neuropil of all secondary eyes in M. muscosa.

448

This correlates with the much smaller overall size of the PME (compared to ALE and PLE). The

449

PME have previously been considered vestigial (Land, 1985a, 1972), but since there is an optic

450

nerve and a corresponding first order visual neuropil, we conclude that these eyes are

451

functional. Previously, Steinhoff et al. (2017) considered the PM1 to be a second order visual

452

neuropil (“PM2”), based on its close spatial relationship with the AL1x. However, we now

453

demonstrate that the PM1 is innervated only by retinula cell axons originating in the PME,

454

and connections between PM1 and AL1x seem to be absent. This coincides with Hill’s (1975)

455

description for Phidippus spp., although he described additional neurites connecting PL1 and

456

PM1. It is likely that these neurites contribute to the tract that connects the first order visual

457

neuropils with the AB (compare Fig. 11a), instead of actually forming synapses in the PM1.

458

The PM1 differs from all other eyes by being connected to the AB only. Consequently, the

459

pathway of the PME lacks a second order visual neuropil. Information from this eye is not

460

processed within the mushroom bodies, but exclusively in the arcuate body.

462

4.4

Second order visual neuropils of the ALE and PLE

463

Visual neuropils with a glomerular structure have been found in the protocerebrum of several

464

spider species (e.g. Long, 2016). They have been termed “lame glomérulée” by Saint-Remy

465

(1887), “glomeruli” by Hanström (1921) and Babu & Barth (1984), “spherules” by Strausfeld

466

& Barth (1993) and “microglomeruli” by Steinhoff et al. (2017). The presence and

467

arrangement of these glomerular neuropils differs between spider species (Hanström, 1921;

468

Long, 2016) and some authors have considered them as a substructure of the MB (Babu and

469

Barth, 1984; Hill, 1975), whereas others have considered them to be separate second order

470

visual neuropils (Long, 2016; discussed in Steinhoff et al., 2017; Strausfeld and Barth, 1993).

471

Our results demonstrate, that in M. muscosa, the AL2/PL2 represents two closely adjoining

472

second order visual neuropils of the ALE and PLE, respectively. In M. muscosa, the AL2/PL2

473

was also found to react less plastically to different environmental conditions than the MBp

474

(Steinhoff et al., 2018), which was interpreted as further evidence for their function as

475

discrete visual neuropils. Within the AL2/PL2, each glomerulus seems to consist of numerous

476

synaptic complexes and Duelli (1980) estimated roughly 400 synapses per glomerulus in the

477

jumping spider Evarcha arcuta. Studies in insects used anti-synapsin labeling to detect

478

presynaptic sites, and phalloidin (which labels f-actin) to highlight postsynaptic sites of

479

synaptic complexes (Groh and Rössler, 2011). To investigate the AL2/PL2 of M. muscosa, we

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

480

have employed the same double-labeling approach (Fig. 9d-e), but the anti-synapsin and

481

phalloidin labeled areas in each synaptic complex were not clearly differentiated, as is the

482

case in social hymenopterans (Groh and Rössler, 2011), despite co-localization being minimal.

483

This might indicate that phalloidin does not consistently label postsynaptic sites in M.

484

muscosa, or that the organization of the pre- and postsynaptic sites within the AL2/PL2 of M.

485

muscosa differs compared to the glomeruli in mushroom body calyces of investigated insect

486

species. To clarify synaptic connectivity within the AL2/PL2 in M. muscosa and spiders in

487

general, a detailed transmission electron microscopic study is needed.

4.5

Mushroom body pedunculus, haft and bridge

490

The somata within SC2 of the neurons that connect the second order visual neuropils to the

491

MBp have been referred to as “globuli cells” (Hanström, 1921; Steinhoff et al., 2017) and the

492

neurons including these somata as “T-cells” (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). In C. salei, globuli

493

cells have a smaller diameter of the soma and nucleus than other cell bodies in the

494

protocerebrum (Babu and Barth, 1984). We could not find such size differences in M.

495

muscosa. In fact, all three nuclear markers revealed that somata of the three identified

496

clusters as well the somata of the surrounding cortex exhibit a similar size (Figs. 2; 3; 5; 6; 7).

497

Thus, according to their definition (Kenyon, 1896; Richter et al., 2010), somata of SC2 are no

498

globuli cells in M. muscosa. The long neurites that connect the SC2 with the AL2/PL2 and the

499

L2 are probably primary neurites (Richter et al., 2010), from which dendrites branch into the

500

AL2/PL2 and L2 respectively, and axons project into the MBp, MBh and MBbr. A similar

501

arrangement has been described by Babu and Barth (1984) for C. salei and by Hill (1975) for

502

two Phidippus species.

503

The structure and organization of the MB differs between M. muscosa and C. salei, as already

504

reported by Steinhoff et al. (2017). The MBh is a separate structure of the mushroom body in

505

M. muscosa, connected to the MBp by neurites (Steinhoff et al., 2017), whereas in C. salei, it

506

is fused with the MBp (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993). Furthermore, the mushroom body shaft,

507

which in C. salei gives rise to the MBbr (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993) is absent or fused with

508

the MBp in M. muscosa.

4.5 Functional considerations

511

The jumping spider Marpissa muscosa and the wandering spider Cupiennius salei differ

512

strongly in their lifestyle. While M. muscosa is a diurnal hunter that actively pursues its prey

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

513

and relies on vision for prey capture, C. salei is an exclusively nocturnal sit-and-wait predator,

514

which detects its prey based on vibratory cues (Barth & Seyfarth, 1979). Although attack

515

behavior of C. salei can be elicited by visual cues under experimental conditions (Fenk et al.,

516

2010), mechanosensing is the major modality for hunting prey, since C. salei also captures

517

prey when its eyes are occluded (Hergenröder and Barth, 1983). Given these obvious

518

differences in lifestyle and relative importance of sensory modalities, it is surprising how

519

similar the general brain anatomy of M. muscosa and C. salei is. Apart from differences in size

520

and structure of neuropils, the visual pathway of the principal eyes is identical in C. salei and

521

M. muscosa. Parts of the visual pathways of the secondary eyes in the jumping spider

522

Marpissa muscosa differ, however, from those in the wandering spider Cupiennius salei.

523

These differences might reflect the differences in the visual fields and corresponding

524

functions of their secondary eyes (Land, 1985b; Land and Barth, 1992). The most conspicuous

525

difference between the visual systems in the brains of M. muscosa and C. salei is the pathway

526

of the PME. While the two larger secondary eyes (ALE and PLE) are connected to the

527

mushroom bodies in M. muscosa, the PME are not (Fig. 11). This suggests that the PME do

528

not play a role in motion detection (Duelli, 1978; Land, 1971; Zurek et al., 2010). In C. salei,

529

however, the PME are important movement detectors (Schmid, 1998) connected to the

530

mushroom bodies (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993).

531

Another interesting difference between the two species is the presence of the L2 in M.

532

muscosa (Steinhoff et al., 2017), which is absent in C. salei. The structure of this neuropil

533

differs from the AL2/PL2 and all second order visual neuropils in C. salei, since the L2 in M.

534

muscosa lacks a glomerular organization. Glomeruli might ensure a retinotopic organization

535

of neurites within the second order visual neuropils. This has been hypothesized for both C.

536

salei (Strausfeld and Barth, 1993) and for the PLE pathway of jumping spiders (Duelli, 1980),

537

where no lateral connections between individual glomeruli of the AL2/PL2 were found. We

538

hypothesize that while a retinotopic organization is kept in the AL2/PL2, it is lost in the L2.

539

Information from the movement-detecting ALE and PLE in M. muscosa might be integrated

540

not only in the MB, but also in the L2, and enable faster movement decisions. Alternatively, a

541

glomerular organization might be the most space-saving structure for the processing of input

542

from differentially tuned groups of axons (Eisthen, 2002; Hildebrand and Shepherd, 1997),

543

and although the L2 is not composed of glomeruli, information might still be processed in

544

parallel without lateral connections. This would also indicate that a higher number of neurites

545

synapse in the AL2/PL2 than in the L2 in M. muscosa.

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/640706; this version posted December 13, 2019. The copyright holder for this preprint (which
was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

546

Some early-branched off salticid species possess a large PME that has its own field of view

547

(e.g. Portia; Land, 1985b; Maddison and Hedin, 2003). Land (1985b) suggested that in jumping

548

spiders with a small PME, such as M. muscosa, the fields of view of ALE and PLE have expanded

549

to fill the space not covered by the PME. The evolutionary transformation to a smaller PME

550

and larger ALE and PLE might have led to visual pathways that also incorporated the L2. Thus

551

in early-branched off jumping spiders with large PME, it could be hypothesized that the L2

552

might serve as a second order visual neuropil of the PME. Future studies should thus analyze

553

the brain architecture of early-branched off salticid species.
