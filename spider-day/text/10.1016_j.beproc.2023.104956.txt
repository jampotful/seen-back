Abstract
Many flies and moths mimic the frontal appearance of jumping spiders. This type of mimicry, which we
term as Partial mimicry, can be distinguished from Batesian mimicry since the mimic has spider
resembling patterns only in certain parts of the body, and not the entire body. The presence of spider like
patterns is obvious only at certain angles suggesting that the mimic is frequently targeted by its
predators from particular angles. We tested this hypothesis using Deep Convolutional Neural Networks
(DCNNs). First, we trained the network on images of forward facing jumping spiders, where features such
as the large principal eyes, small lateral eyes and outstretched legs were evident. Then we tested the
classifier on images of jumping spider mimicking flies and moths. A probability value according to the
likelihood of the image being a jumping spider or not was assigned by the classifier. We show that the
classifierwas more likely to misidentify mimicking flies and moths as jumping spiders, but that this
probability varied according to the species tested. We further tested it on images of flies from different
angles and by taking into consideration the visual acuity of potential predators. Our results suggest that
1. Neural networks can be efficient toolsfor testing evolutionary hypotheses, 2. partial mimicry may be a
result of the effect of the signaling angle and orientation of the mimics in combination with the likelihood
that predators may depend on cognitive shortcuts to identify insects as prey. Further experiments
incorporating the properties of the visual system of predators (such as vision in ultraviolet) would result
in a better understanding of the evolution of partial mimicry.

Introduction
Visual signals are widely used in animal communication between conspecifics (e.g., courtship displays,
male aggression (Stevens 2013)) and heterospecifics (e.g., warning signals, misdirection (Ruxton et al.
2004)). For a visual signal to be effective, the relative positioning of the sender and the receiver is critical.
The correct geometrical alignment of the receiver with respect to the sender allows for the evolution of
strategies such as private channels of communication and inhibition of eavesdropping by predators
(Cummings et al. 2003).
Visually hunting predators use the physical appearance of their prey to detect, recognize and attack their
prey. Due to the different types of predation strategies, prey need to optimize their defense mechanisms
to survive attacks from different predators. One of the many defensive strategies used by prey is the
evolution of body coloration and appearance that mimic the species that have physical or chemical
defenses (Batesian mimicry; (Ruxton et al. 2004)). While examples of Batesian mimicry are widespread
(e.g., Monarch butterflies, Coral snakes), little attention has been paid to the effect of predator cognition
and vision with respect to their geometrical alignment with the prey (Echeverri et al. 2021). If there is
selection pressure by a predator that approaches a prey at a relatively consistent orientation, we would
expect to see the evolution of body markings only on certain parts of the body. In a sense, this is what we
see in strategies that have been termed visual deflection or misdirection (Ruxton et al. 2004; RobledoOspina and Rao 2022). In these cases, the prey has distinctive and conspicuous markings in non-vital
parts of the body that invite an attack, such that the prey can escape without too much damage as a
Page 2/12

result of the attack (Chotard et al. 2022). Predators are usually in motion before and during an attack and
often have to make decisions very quickly leading to speed-accuracy tradeoffs (Ings and Chittka 2008;
Chittka et al. 2009). Prey body patterns that result in a mis-categorization of the identity of the prey by the
predator or misdirect the attack trajectory take advantage of the cognitive and visual biases of the
predator's visual system.
Another strategy used by many prey species is the mimicking of the appearance of other species but only
on certain parts of the body. The canonical example for this strategy is seen in jumping spider-like leg
patterns on the wings of some tephritid flies and moths (Eisner 1985; Greene et al. 1987; Mather and
Roitberg 1987; Hasson 1995; Rota and Wagner 2006; Rao and Díaz-Fleischer 2012). This mimicry doesn't
extend to the whole body of the prey, it is only at certain angles and distances does the mimicry become
apparent. We designate this as Partial Mimicry and define it as the occurrence of patterns and colors on

only certain body parts of the mimic that reflect some salient component of their model. We distinguish
this from traditional Batesian mimicry by the fact that the mimic does not resemble the whole body of the
model. Furthermore, we can distinguish partial mimicry from imperfect mimicry (McLean et al. 2019),
since the component being mimicked has high fidelity but only to the specific salient body component.
Jumping spider like markings are widely present across many insects, with the most common being the
wing markings of tephritid flies and metalmark moths. There is a wide diversity of insects that possess
jumping spider like patterns, specifically large frontal eyes, outstretched front pairs of legs, and general
stance (Hill et al. 2019). Tephritid flies (e.g., Ceratitis capitata, Rhagoletis pomonella, Zonosemata

vittigera, Anastrepha ludens) perform a wing display that deters attacks from jumping spiders (Eisner
1985; Greene et al. 1987; Mather and Roitberg 1987; Hasson 1995; Rota and Wagner 2006; Rao and DíazFleischer 2012). This mimicry has been termed ‘predator mimicry’, suggesting that the prey mimics the
appearance of its own predator, but other studies have shown that the motion component of the display
is probably more important due to the movement biases of the jumping spider's visual system (Rao and
Díaz-Fleischer 2012; Rao et al. 2022).
However, in these examples, mimicry has been defined by human observers. Since humans are very good
at attributing pattern where there is none (Pareidolia (Liu et al. 2014)) and given the logistical difficulties
in testing animal perceptions of mimicry at scale, there is a need for an objective method to categorize
the degree of resemblance. An earlier study showed that Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs)
are a useful method to approach to characterize protective resemblance in snakes (Solan et al. 2020). In
this study, we used a DCNN to classify jumping spider faces with images obtained from citizen science
platforms such as iNaturalist (https://www.inaturalist.org). We then tested it on reported jumping spider
partial mimics and tested the effect of insect prey orientation on the probability of being categorized as a
jumping spider. We hypothesize that 1. Partial mimics of jumping spiders contain sufficient resemblance
to jumping spiders and result in a mis-categorization by the DCNN, and 2. Mis-categorization only occurs
at certain angles and 3. Mis-categorization is dependent on the visual acuity and identity of the predator.

Methods
Page 3/12

Image Acquisition for training
We used 1022 images to train and validate the neural net classifier. The database was divided into two
sets, one containing 273 images of jumping spiders and one containing 749 images of an assortment of
small land arthropods to represent the range of body shapes and patterns a jumping spider might
encounter. Images of jumping spiders were obtained by downloading a dataset from GBIF (Global
Biodiversity Information Facility; GBIF.org) which included all occurrence data which had an iNaturalist
research grade observation of a species within the family Salticidae. We then obtained a random sample
of 500 images and manually selected the ones showing a clear frontal view of the spider, and then
cropped the images to remove most of the background. Images for the non-spider set were obtained from
iNaturalist with a search for research grade observations of non-crustacean arthropods, with a time
window of one week to constrain the number of results to a manageable size. We curated this dataset by
removing orders with less than 20 records as well as Endeostigmata (mites) because of their size, and
spiders (Araneae). Of the remaining records, we selected a random sample of 500 images. To avoid a
bias towards over-represented species (such as bees or butterflies) we selected a maximum of 50 images
per order. These images were then cropped to remove most of the background and any images where the
organism was not in focus or partially covered by vegetation were discarded.

Neural net training
While training a DCNN for image classification usually requires a much larger than the one used in this
study, a technique called transfer learning can dramatically reduce the number of images required to
obtain a high accuracy classifier (Körschens et al. 2018; Norouzzadeh et al. 2018; Solan et al. 2020). We
used this technique on a pretrained network (EfficientNet; Tan and Le 2019) to classify images into two
categories corresponding to jumping spider or not jumping spider. All training and analysis were done
using Wolfram Mathematica Version 13 (Wolfram Inc, Champaign, USA). The code, images and details of
the training process are available online
[https://github.com/CarlosErnestoMA/PartialMimicryWithMachineLearning].

Test 1: Proof of concept
As a proof of concept, we tested the DCNN against a randomly chosen jumping spider (Maripanthus
draconis). As a control we tested the classifier against an ant mimicking jumping spider (Myrmarachne

sp.) in two orientations (side view and frontal view). We also tested the classifier against two spider
mimicking tephritid flies (Rhagoletis pomonella and Ceratitis capitata) and a non-mimicking fly (Musca
domestica).

Test 2: Other mimicking insects
We chose 6 insects (Pscocoptera sp, Olethreutes arcuella, Brenthia sp., Acentropinae sp, Gelastopsis
insignis, Rhotana maculata) that have been reported to mimic jumping spiders (Hill et al. 2019). These
were run through the DCNN classifier.

Test 3: Mimicking fly from different angles
Page 4/12

We chose a single species of tephritid fly for this test. Ceratitis capitata has been previously studied as a
mimic of jumping spiders (Hasson 1995). We selected photographs of C. capitata taken at different
angles.

Test 4: Incorporating visual acuity of a model predator
Based on the results of the earlier test, we selected the orientation with highest probability of being
identified as a jumping spider. We transformed the image accounting for the visual acuity of two
potential predators (Vespa vulgaris, a predatory wasp with a visual acuity value of 0.5 cycles per degree,
(Feller et al. 2021) and Phiddipus audax, a jumping spider of visual acuity value of 3.86 (Rao et al. 2022))
using the QCPA plugin (Berg et al. 2019) in ImageJ (Schnieder et al., 2012. The images were resized for
uniformity but retained spatial acuity information according to the transformation as seen from 3
behaviorally relevant distances (2 cm, 5 cm, 10 cm). These images were then tested using the classifier.

Looking inside the net
We used an algorithm based on gradient ascent (Nguyen et al. 2014) to generate images which
maximized the output of one of the neurons which encodes the classification result (Wolfram 2023). We
started with an image with random pixel values, and iteratively modified it in a way which increases the
activation of the target neuron. After a few iterations, an image was obtained which was classified either
as a spider or non-spider with a high confidence.

Results

Test 1: Proof of concept
The jumping spider image was assigned a probability value of 0.99 of being identified as a spider by the
DCNN classifier. The ant-mimicking jumping spider had only a 0.62 probability of being a spider whereas
the same spider from the frontal perspective gave a probability value of 0.88 of being a spider (Fig. 1A).
Two of the jumping spider mimicking flies were assigned probability values of 0.41 and 0.71 respectively,
whereas the non-mimicking fly was assigned as non-spider with 0.99 probability (Fig. 1B).

Test 2: Other insects
Except for Rhotana maculata, all other jumping spider mimics obtained probability values of higher than
0.5 from the DCNN classifier.

Test 3: Orientation and predator acuity
The orientation of a selected spider mimicking fly affected the probability value assigned by the DCNN.
The probability value of the fly being mis-categorized as a jumping spider was seen in only one
orientation (Fig. 2A, first image). When run through images that were simulated according to the visual
acuity of a model predator, probability values decreased with distance but stayed above the 0.5
probability mark for the wasp predator, but stayed consistent for the spider predator.

Discussion
In this study, we proposed a new subcategory of mimetism that we designate as Partial Mimicry and
used DCNNs to evaluate the concept using images of insects and spiders. We distinguish this type of
mimicry from Batesian mimicry since only a part of the model is mimicked. Natural selection due to
predator pressure could lead to the evolution of partial mimicry if we assume that most attacks occur at
particular angles. For examples, in the case of Scarlet snakes that mimic coral snakes, the ventral side of
the snake does not possess the characteristic patterns of the coral snakes (Kikuchi and Pfennig, 2010).
Even if there is no attack, predators may be dissuaded from approaching palatable prey if they come
across them at these angles. Furthermore, the presence of these patterns may also serve to misdirect
predator attention and consequent attacks away from vital body parts such as the head. Different
predators may respond to the mimicry at different distances according to their visual acuity. From a
sexual selection point of view, there is some evidence that males may incorporate patterns and
colouration that resemble predators into their courtship repertoire, perhaps in order to sustain the
attention of females (Harris and Morehouse 2020) but this needs to be tested further.
An interesting implication of partial mimicry is that it leads to the idea that predators use some sort of a
gestalt view (~ cognitive template, but see (Sung et al. 2021) ) while hunting. Just the mere suggestion
that the insect in front of the predator may be something else, perhaps something dangerous, may be
enough to dissuade them from carrying out the attack. Predators may use these cognitive shortcuts to
recognize their prey, and since they usually need to decide on an attack while in motion, they may be
prone to making speed-accuracy tradeoffs. Another implication is that the direction of attack, or the
approach angle is of greater importance in governing predator prey interactions. The effect of geometric
alignment, in conjunction with visual acuity of the predator has hitherto been neglected in the design of
predator-prey interaction studies, and promises to be a fruitful avenue of research (Echeverri et al. 2017).
Predator prey interactions need to be placed in an explicitly geometry-aware visual ecology context.
There are several caveats to this study. We found that the orientation of the insect in the image greatly
influences the probability values of the DCNN categories, which inhibits the use of multiple images to test
the network unless the photos are taken of different individuals in a fairly standardized manner. The
inherent problem with using these networks is that often the 'reasoning' behind the DCNN's assignment of
probability values is a mystery. To address this, we generated an artificial image that hints at the possible
cues used by the DCNN to categorize jumping spiders (Fig. 3), but the result was inconclusive. The main
difference between the jumping spider artificial image and the non-spider image is that the former seems
to have a distinct spot perhaps representing the principal eyes of the spider, while the non-spider image is
missing this feature. Another caveat is represented by the low probability score assigned to a debrid
planthopper (Fig. 1C, last image), which to human eyes clearly resembles a spider. We expect newer
techniques in the future may enable us to pinpoint what features are used by the DCNN, but for now we
are unable to elaborate further.

The use of citizen science images along with computer vision techniques has see a boom in recent years,
and while it may not adequately substitute controlled ethological experiments, there is potential for using
these techniques to rapidly test concepts that can then be followed up with rigorous experimentation. By
incorporating more aspects of the predator's visual system (such as UV, polarization vision etc) along
with the geometry of alignment and motion components, human biases in the design and execution of
predator prey interaction studies can be controlled.
