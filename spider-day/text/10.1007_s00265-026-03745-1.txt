Abstract
Signals function in intra- and inter-specific communication across several different contexts, such as predation, foraging,
competition and mate choice. Static signals including shape or color are well-studied, whereas locomotion-related signals
remain less understood despite being particularly intriguing as they can be controlled by the animal’s perception of danger
or competition. In this study, we explore the potential function of a highly conspicuous dynamic behavior—abdomen bobbing—exhibited by the diurnal jumping spider Heliophanus cf. cupreus during locomotion. Due to the high conspicuousness, we hypothesized that abdomen bobbing could function as a visual signal, specifically as an anti-predatory signal.
To test this hypothesis, we first analyzed the behavior and found that bobs occurred almost exclusively during gait stops
(98.5%) with 83.5% of gait stops entailing abdomen bobbing. We then examined its context-dependency, measuring the
prevalence of abdomen bobbing under five conditions: (1) control, (2) in darkness, (3) during prey encounters, (4) during
simulated conspecific encounters (i.e. mirror), and (5) during predator encounters. Abdomen bobbing was virtually absent
in darkness and during prey encounters, but significantly increased during conspecific and predator encounters, suggesting
active control. The increase in abdomen bobbing when confronted with a predator supports an anti-predatory signaling
function, potentially a form of locomotion mimicry. We equally found support for an intra-specific signaling function,
which might indicate the co-option of this signal from one context (predatory) to another (conspecific). Our study opens
new avenues for investigating the role of motion in spiders for both inter- and intraspecific signaling.
Significance statement
Animals often use visual signals to avoid being eaten, but most research has focused on static characteristics like color or
shape. This study reveals that a dynamic movement—abdomen bobbing—in a jumping spider is actively controlled and
increases in the presence of predators and spiders of the same species. These findings suggest that movement itself can
function as a visual signal, potentially mimicking the motion of defended species like ants or wasps. The behavior may
have a role in both a predator deterrence and social communication, highlighting the flexibility of motion-based signaling.
By experimentally identifying the contexts in which spiders bob their abdomen, our work broadens the understanding of
how animals use movement not just to navigate their environment, but perhaps also to communicate within it.

Introduction
Most animals move in search of food, shelter or mates.
While in motion, their visibility to unintended viewers, such
as predators, may increase (e.g. by breaking their camouflage or crypsis) and may make them more vulnerable, creating a trade-off between the benefits and costs of locomotion
(Tan et al. 2024). There are morphological and behavioral
traits that can alleviate this trade-off, such as warning signals, visible forms of defense (e.g. stings or spines) or the
mimicry of such static morphological traits (Stevens and
Ruxton 2018). It is not surprising, from a practical perspective, that static morphological anti-predatory traits are
often studied in great morphometric detail (e.g. Kelly et al.
2025). While less well understood than static morphological
traits, animals can counteract the increased visibility during
movement by adjusting locomotion and even using motion
to conceal, dazzle and deceive predators (Stevens and Ruxton 2018; Tan et al. 2024). For example, praying mantids
(Watanabe and Yano 2009) and stick insects (Bian et al.
2016) sway their bodies in an apparent mimicry of trembling leaves. By increasing the swaying in wind conditions
their detection by predators may be lowered.
In locomotor mimicry, the classical morphological
resemblance of a mimic extends to how the model moves
(Huang and Caro 2023) with evidence from mimetic butterflies (e.g. Kitamura and Imafuku 2010), wasp-mimicking clear-winged moths (Skowron Volponi et al. 2018)
and wasp-mimicking hover flies (Penney et al. 2014). Antmimicking spiders also exhibit complex morphological
(myrmecomorphy) and behaviorally mimicry of ants. For
example, myrmecomorphic spiders move at speeds matching those of ants (Subramaniam et al. 2022), and in typical ant-like stop-go fashion including winding trajectories,
with their first legs raised in resemblance of antennae when
stopping (Shamble et al. 2017). What is particularly compelling about mimetic behavior in myrmecomorphs is the
ability to modulate the behavior in different contexts. For
example, Myrmarachne ant-mimics increase behavioral
mimicry of ants when moving more and thus be potentially
more exposed to predators, but abandon ant-like behavior
when confronted with immediate danger (Ceccarelli 2008).
It would also be of advantage to reduce behavioral ant
mimicry when in proximity of ant specialists (e.g. Servaea
jumping spiders), as these readily attack ant mimics at similar rates to ants (Pekár et al. 2017). Such context‑dependent
behavioral mimicry reveals how these animals actively
balance multiple, sometimes conflicting, selective forces.
The ability to flexibly adjust their behavior indicates that
mimicry is not a static trait but a decision‑based strategy.
This plasticity provides insight into how animals integrate ecological information, compensate for imperfect

A particularly intriguing behavior that has been described
in myrmecomorphic spiders, as well as in non-mimicking
spiders (see Table 1) is abdomen bobbing (or twitching)
where the spider lifts and lowers the abdomen as it walks.
In particular, abdomen bobbing in myrmecomorphic spiders
has been linked to how some ants also lift and lower their
gaster during locomotion (Ceccarelli 2008), suggesting that
abdomen bobbing in spiders may be a form of behavioral
mimicry of ants and wasps, functioning as an anti-predator
signal (Table 1). There are a number of non-mutually exclusive alternative hypotheses for the function of abdomen
bobbing. The up-down movement of the abdomen might aid
ventilation or, alternatively, the twitching abdomen may create seismic signals for intra-specific communication, either
as a form of courtship signal or a threat display between
competitors (Table 1). As these interpretations are based
almost exclusively on observations, and abdomen bobbing
itself has never been quantified or its function experimentally tested, we are currently unable to evaluate and discern
between alternative functions.
Here, we aimed to experimentally test a potential visual
function of abdomen bobbing in the non-myrmecomorphic
jumping spider Heliophanus cf. cupreus (Supplemental
Video S2). Our choice of a spider that was not morphologically resembling ants, reduced the potential of a synergetic
effect between morphology and behavior in generating
a convincing mimetic signal in a predatory context (see
McLean and Herberstein 2021).
First, we described the gait of Heliophanus cf.
cupreus and compared it to that of two other common
salticids (Marpissa muscosa and Salticus scenicus) to
understand if abdomen bobbing is a general trait across
jumping spiders. Second, we recorded spiders of different sexes and maturity levels to test whether bobbing
was sex or developmentally specific, which would have
supported a potential sexual signal. We then experimentally manipulated the context in which the spiders moved
to test if abdomen bobbing is hard-wired to the spider’s
gait or whether it is under active control. If abdomen
bobbing had a visual signaling function, we predicted
that this diurnal spider would engage in abdomen bobbing under daylight conditions, when bobs are visible
to potential receivers, while it should cease to do so in
the dark. In further experiments we tested the prediction that abdomen bobbing decreases in a foraging context, as these conspicuous movements during stalking
of prey could hamper prey capture. We then examined
a potential intra-specific signaling function, predicting
that abdomen bobbing would increase in the presence of

Materials and methods
Study species and animal husbandry
In this study, we used jumping spiders (Salticidae),
which are known for their excellent vision and high
visual acuity (Foelix 2010), allowing us to conduct visual
experiments. The study species, Heliophanus cf. cupreus
(Walckenaer and Fabricius 1802) (hereafter Heliophanus), is distributed across Europe, Northern Africa, and
parts of Asia and can usually be found on low vegetation
(World Spider Catalog, 2025). The opisthosoma is dark
with bright spots, covered in iridescent hairs. The adult
female measures about 4.6–5.8 mm in body length with

bright yellow pedipalps in contrast to their darker body
coloration. Adult males are smaller, ranging from 3.6 to
4.0 mm in body length, and have dark-colored pedipalps
(Bellmann 2016; Metzner 2024; Wesołowska 1986).
All individuals of Heliophanus, as well as individuals
of two other salticids, Marpissa muscosa and Salticus
scenicus, used in the experiments were wild-caught animals collected in Constance, Germany (47° 41’ 28.2264”
N 9° 11’ 10.3164” E) between 2021 and 2023. We transported the spiders to the laboratory where we kept them
separately in clear plastic boxes (5.5 × 5.5 × 8.5 cm). We
enriched each box with a plywood board (5.4 × 5.4 cm)
to provide structure and shelter and fed the animals with
flightless Drosophila melanogaster twice a week. The
spiders had access to ad libitum water provided in an
Eppendorf tube (2 ml) filled with water and stoppered
with a cotton ball. Animals were housed under a constant
temperature of 22 °C (+/- 2 degrees Celsius) and under a
12:12 light: dark cycle.

Experimental design
In total, we placed 102 spiders (juveniles = 31, females = 52,
males = 29) on a 3D-printed horizontal runway and subsequently filmed them moving along the runway. Runways
were mounted on a 12.8 cm pole in front of a camera and
the length and width of the runway was adjusted for each
treatment to optimize stimulus encounter, resulting in
three different runway sizes (a shorter runway to allow
detailed observations during control and darkness, a longer runway to allow more natural and not sudden encounter of the objects in foraging and predator trials, as well
as an intermediate sized runway to increase chances of
the spider to spot its mirror image, see Fig. 1). In all trials
we covered the runway with white filter paper, which was
removed and the runway cleaned with ethanol (98%) after
each trial to remove any potential silk and/or chemical
traces left. To maximize sample size in each treatment,
we reused some individuals, resulting in repeated measures, which we accounted for in the statistical analyses
(see Video scoring and data analysis below). Reusing
individuals became necessary with shifting availability of

spiders over the seasons. Spiders were randomly reused,
but only in treatments they had not participated in before
and with at least one week between test days. In total, of
the 102 spiders, 66 were only used once, 29 were used in
two different treatments and 7 spiders were used in three
different treatments.
We filmed all daylight trials with a Nikon D7500 (Nikon,
Tokyo, Japan) under LED light strips (daytime eco110.2
Ultra White 7.000 K). For trials run in darkness, we used
infrared lights (INSTAR IN-907) and a full-spectrum modified DSLR camera (Nikon D7200, Tokyo, Japan) for infrared video recording.
During the control and light/dark context trials, we started
the scoring once the spider entered the runway. Each trial
lasted a maximum of three minutes. We manually transferred
spiders from a plastic vial using a piece of paper or wood and
encouraged them to jump onto the runway by holding the
paper close to it. If this failed, we gently tapped the spiders
out of the vial from above and placed them onto the runway.
A trial ended earlier when a spider jumped off. For the foraging-, conspecific-, or predator trials, the scoring started once

Control
We quantified a baseline (control) of bobbing behavior of Heliophanus during daylight conditions and in the
absence of a stimulus, testing 55 spiders (juveniles = 12,
females = 27, males = 16) in a randomized order on an 8 cm
× 1.5 cm runway platform (Fig. 1A). To demonstrate that
bobbing is not a general salticid trait, we additionally filmed
two other jumping spider species, Marpissa muscosa and
Salticus scenicus, as species-controls using the same experimental setup. We tested 10 female Marpissa muscosa and
17 female Salticus scenicus.
We then tested Heliophanus under different treatments
and compared abdomen bobbing to the control trials (Supplemental Video S1).

Light context (darkness)
To investigate if abdomen bobbing represents a visual signal, we tested whether bobbing behavior persisted in the
absence of visible light, using the same setup as the control
treatment, but with infrared lights (Fig. 1A). A total number
of 35 spiders (juveniles = 6, females = 16, males = 13) were
chosen at random and tested in a randomized order. We
conducted all trials during the daytime, when spiders were
active, and created a dark environment by switching off the
lights once the spider started to walk on the platform.

Foraging context
To test whether the abdomen bobbing might function as a
signal in prey capture, we observed spiders in the presence
of tethered flightless Drosophila melanogaster. For each
trial, we fixed a live fly to one end of the runway (Fig. 1B)
using double sided sticky tape which held the flies in place
while allowing them to move as they tried to free themselves.
The movement reliably triggered stalking and ultimately an
attack response in the spiders. We placed the spider at one
end of the runway with the fly at the opposite end. The trial
commenced when the fly was visually detected by the spider, indicated by a freeze behavior, while eyes were directed
at the fly (see Table 2). A total of 25 spiders (juveniles = 4,
females = 11, males = 10) were chosen at random and tested
in randomized order.

Conspecific context
To test whether abdomen bobbing behavior functions as an
intra-specific signal, we presented the test spider with a mirror (2 × 2 cm) at the end of the runway (Fig. 1C). We chose a
mirror to create size- and sex-matched conspecific encounters, a method frequently used in jumping spiders (e.g.
Cross et al. 2007), who readily interact with their mirror
image. Once the spider detected its mirror image, it moved
towards it. A total of 9 spiders (juveniles = 5, female = 4)
were randomly chosen and tested in randomized order (at
the time of these experiments, we no longer had male spiders in the lab).

Predator context
To test whether abdomen bobbing functions as an antipredatory signal, we exposed test spiders to 3D-printed
models (11 mm in length) of a larger predatory jumping
spider (3D prints based on microCT scans of Phidippus audax, stl-file available from Zenodo: Rößler and
Shamble 2022). Jumping spiders readily recognize and
respond to models of larger predatory spiders, which
trigger a freeze and subsequent escape response (see also
Plate and Rößler 2024; Rößler et al. 2022). The eyes of
the models were painted with black acrylic paint resulting in a shiny and realistically eye-like surface (Revell).
We placed the models at one end of the runway (Fig. 1D)
and released the test spider at the opposite end. A total
of 18 spiders (juveniles = 8, females = 9, males = 1) were
randomly chosen and tested in randomized order.

Video tracking
In order to illustrate the movement of the abdomen during a
walking sequence (Fig. 2), we used the pose estimation software SLEAP (Pereira et al. 2022) to track spinnerets and the
anterior-superior edge of the abdomen. Trajectories were
processed with the animovement R package (Roald-Arbøl
2024), and spiders were determined to be walking when
moving at > 20 mm/s.

Video scoring and data analysis
We scored all videos manually using BORIS (Friard and
Gamba 2016) according to the observed behaviors defined
in Table 2. The movement of the abdomen was measured by
recording each time the abdomen reached its highest and/or
lowest position, as well as whether the spider was walking
or had stopped.
To account for the difference of trial length, we randomly
selected three stops for each trial, from which abdomen bobs
per stop were calculated. For trials with less than three stops
we used the maximum number of stops for that trial. Stops
that lasted longer than 15 s were not included in the analysis, as we could not reliably interpret these as gait stops.
Statistical analyses were carried out in R 4.3.1 (R Core
Team, 2024). We used generalized linear mixed models
(GLMM) with the package glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017) to
test whether treatments or group (i.e. maturity/sex) had significant effects on the dependent variables (abdomen bobbing
or stop duration). We then applied an analysis of deviance to

Discussion
The aim of our study was to quantify abdomen bobbing
in Heliophanus jumping spiders and to test the hypothesis that abdomen bobbing may serve as a visual signal
in intra-and/or inter-specific contexts. The gait we quantified in Heliophanus cf. cupreus contained significantly
shorter stop durations than the other two tested salticids (Marpissa muscosa and Salticus scenicus) (Fig. 3).
Such rapid stop-and-go gait has been described in other
salticids (Jackson, 1986 b, 1986 c; Jackson and Hallas
1986), however in Heliophanus abdomen bobs almost
exclusively occurred during gait stops (98.5%), contrasting for example with Cosmophasis micarioides (Jackson, 1986 b), where bobbing continued when the spider
resumed walking (Fig. 2). Additionally, in contrast to C.
micarioides, bobbing in Heliophanus generally started
with a downward motion, rather than upward. While

Our experimental framework tested abdomen bobbing
under different contexts, resulting in context-dependent
increases and decreases of bobbing up to the point of
seizing it entirely. These patterns indicate that abdomen
bobbing was under voluntary control diminishing the possibility that abdomen bobbing is a mechanical consequence
of the spider’s gait or functions in ventilation as proposed in
earlier studies (Jackson and Hallas 1986). In fact, Jackson
and Hallas (1986) do not provide an explanation of how,

of abdominal segments (also absent in spiders) (e.g. ants:
Kuusik et al. 2004). Thus, in the absence of any supporting
arguments for these physiological functions, it seems reasonable to dismiss this functional explanation.
By contrast, we obtained stronger support for the idea
that bobbing has a visual function, especially in the context
of the light environment, as bobbing was entirely absent in
non-visual conditions (sudden darkness). Of course there is

the possibility that the lack of light might have resulted in
the cessation of bobbing for alternative reasons, such as spiders entering a resting phase. However this seems unlikely
since the spiders kept actively moving in the dark. The
results of the light-context experiments are even more compelling when interpreted together with the results of remaining experiments that presented visual stimuli (e.g. predator,
prey, conspecific).
Our predator-context experiment supports a possible
visual anti-predator signal function for the abdomen bobbing in Heliophanus due to the increase of bobbing when
confronted with a predator. It would be remiss of us not to

13

Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology (2026) 80:68

question why bobbing in Heliophanus was present even in
the absence of immediate danger, and whether this could be
a counter argument against an antipredatory function. Yet,
bobbing by walking Heliophanus, is reminiscent of signals
on display when animals are more detectable, as moving
increases predation risk. For example, displaying predator
deterrents during movement, such as the bright red aposematic soles of harlequin toads, address the intrinsic risk of
predation (Rößler et al. 2019).
While we did not test direct fitness impacts of bobbing
behavior, there is evidence that similar bobbing behaviors in
other invertebrates reduce predation. Male stalk-eyed flies
(Teleopsis dalmanni) increase their chances of survival by
abdomen bobbing (Worthington and Swallow 2010) and the
Asian honeybee (Apis cerana) uses abdomen wagging as a
form of an “I See You Signal”, deterring predator attacks
(Tan et al. 2012). Finally, even though the study did not
measure mortality, it is worth noting that even Drosophila
display abdomen lifts in response to the presence of a salticid predator (Parigi et al. 2019).
The protective mechanisms of abdomen bobbing reported
in the studies above may indeed be behavioral mimicry of
a defended model, such as wasps and ants. Body oscillations, including abdominal wagging, have been reported for
various wasp species (Savoyard et al. 1998) and abdominal
wagging in fire ants (Cassill et al. 2016). While Heliophanus is not considered a morphological ant mimic, the very
pointy tip of the abdomen together with the overall glossiness of the spider’s exterior is reminiscent of the glossy,
smooth abdomen that characterizes many ant-mimicking
spiders (Cushing 1997; Nelson 2010).
Bobbing significantly increased in another visual context—the conspecific context, where the presence of a
conspecific (in our case a mirror image) increased bobbing. These results do not diminish the results from the
predator context as conspicuous inter-specific signals,
such as aposematic signals, are often co-opted into an
intra-specific sexual signaling context where they may
indicate male or female quality (see Rojas et al. 2018).
However, we can not conclude that abdomen bobbing is
a sexual signal, since we found no difference of bobbing
in the control condition between juveniles, females and
males. Nonetheless, abdomen bobbing in Heliophanus
may contain relevant information about individual quality that may determine the outcomes of intra-specific
interactions, an intriguing prospect for further experimental exploration testing both sexes as well as juveniles to explore the function of bobbing in intra-specific
communication. Such experiments would be particularly
useful to disentangle species recognition from anti-predator signals; since intraguild predation as well as cannibalism is common in salticids (Okuyama 2007) and

Page 11 of 14 68

has been observed in Heliophanus (pers. obs.) the lines
between recognizing a conspecific, who could also be a
potential predator, and a hetero-specific predator, might
be blurry. Therefore, testing actual conspecifics as well
as actual predators would be crucial to determine if bobbing responses differ.
The only visual context involving another animal that
reduced bobbing was the prey encounter. Once the prey
item was detected, the spiders crouched down to the
ground, snuck up to the prey, and attacked. It is likely
that conspicuously bobbing would otherwise alert the
prey to the stalking spider, hampering successful prey
capture. This finding distinguishes bobbing behavior in
Heliophanus from jumping spider species where bobbing does occur during prey encounters such as in Siler
(see Table 1). Since Siler feeds on ant broods and interacts directly with ants, bobbing behavior may be favored
even in ant encounters. This implies that there may not
be one universal function of bobbing that holds true
across species but rather bobbing seems to have been coopted into different species-specific adaptive functions,
with an anti-predator function being one of them.
Our experimental work not only provides support for
abdomen bobbing as a visual anti-predatory and/or intraspecific communication signal, but also generates further
hypotheses that can be tested experimentally in the lab as
well as the field by quantifying the bobbing rate across
different microhabitats. For example, bobbing could be
increased in more exposed sites (i.e. on top of vegetation) and decreased when the spider is obscured by dense
vegetation. Another important step would be to experimentally test receiver response of conspecifics as well
as of potential predators to establish whether bobbing
actually results in a fitness benefit by startling, pausing
or fully preventing predator attacks. Potential predators
that occur in similar habitats could include other visually-guided spiders such as jumping spiders, wolf spiders or crab spiders as well as mantises (see Parigi et
al. 2019). Further, there may be nuanced differences and
variation in the bobbing behavior that we were not able
to identify with the manual scoring methods applied, but
that might emerge in future work applying even more
standardized conditions (e.g. a trackball, see De Agrò et
al. 2021) and using neural network-based video tracking
methods to analyze bobbing motions and frequencies in
greater detail. Additionally, testing newly emerged spiderlings could yield insight into whether abdomen bobbing is an innate or learned behavior. Finally, a broader
comparative analysis of bobbing behavior among salticids, and perhaps even other spider families, may help
understand interspecific differences and identify ecological correlates for this intriguing trait.
