The semi-rigid structure of bodies forces animals to move in rhythmic patterns shared by all
creatures with skeletons, exoskeletons, or limb connections. This pattern, known as biological
motion, is instantly recognizable and conveys "animacy," even when body shape is removed and
only a cloud of moving dots is shown. The motion alone is so informative that some animals can
reconstruct the original shape. Jumping spiders, highly visual arthropods, divide motion detection
and shape recognition between their four pairs of eyes. Previous studies showed that they can
distinguish biological from non-biological motion using only their anterior lateral eyes, yet seemed
unable to extract shape from motion. In this study, we examined how the anterior medial eyes of
jumping spiders—which are used for shape recognition—respond to dot clouds depicting biological
or non-biological motion. Using a custom eye tracker, we monitored retinal movements during
presentation of static and moving stimuli. We found spiders change their retinal shifting pattern
based on both the target’s motion (biological or not) and implied structure (i.e., whether dots
suggest a coherent shape). These results reveal that jumping spiders analyze motion with more
complexity than previously thought, suggesting a deeper integration of motion and form processing
within their minuscule, modular brains.

Vision is an amazing biological feat—by capturing light from highly organized sensors, animals can
meaningfully interpret a scene; segregating objects from the background, and categorizing and
recognizing these objects. Most notably, however, even in the absence of a recognizable shape,
much can be understood about objects in the environment through motion alone. This motion-based
vision is particularly useful when trying to detect other animals in the environment, be they
conspecifics, predators or potential prey. For example, animals are “self-propelled”, meaning that
they are the only objects in the environment that can initiate, modulate, and cease locomotion
without the intervention of an external agent [1], [2], [3]— thus, movement, alone, can provide
important clues about object identity

However, this is not to say that object (or creature) shape is irrelevant. On the contrary, specifically
due to their construction, animal bodies are spatially constrained, and therefore conform to semirigid motion patterns—for example, a wrist may never change its distance from an elbow, and can
even only move so far from a knee. Even in an octopus arm, which completely lacks rigid skeletal
elements, these constraints are at work, as sections of the body move coherently with neighboring
sections. This characteristic structure, and consequently this semi-rigid, idiosyncratic motion
pattern is shared across most animals, and is typically referred to as “biological motion” [4].

The ability to recognize biological motion in the absence of any structural information was first
described in humans by Gunnar Johansson in the 1970s [4], [5], [6]. The author produced stimuli
composed of just 11 dots, moving in correspondence with the major joints of a human body during
locomotion. These deeply impoverished stimuli, termed point-light displays, were capable of
eliciting the impression of a walking human in the observers. The effect does not limit to the
recognition of a conspecific: observers can guess from the moving points of light the kind of
animals they are looking at, which action is being performed, the direction of motion, or even
subtler differences like the individual’s sex [7], [8], [9].

Curiously, even point-light displays in which the initial location of each point is “scrambled”, but
preserve the same local trajectory, observers perceive “something alive”, even if no recognizable
identity can be inferred [7], [10], [11], [12]. Animals seem capable to infer whether an object is
moving biologically, and use this information as a form of “life detector” [13], [14]. Only then, the
motion pattern can be used to extract the object shape (i.e., “shape from motion”), by virtually
connecting the dots through their synchrony. Finally, subtleties of the dots’ motion and
directionality can be used to infer characteristics of the depicted action or agent.

The ability to use biological motion as a life detector has been demonstrated to be innate and
widespread across a plethora of vertebrate taxa [15], [16], [17], [18]. However, the reconstruction of
shape from motion has been more sparsely studied [7], [19]. In invertebrates, some indirect
evidence is available on the use of general motion cues to detect liveliness [20]. However, the
ability to perceive biological motion has been described before only in jumping spiders [21], [22].

This family of arachnids has recently become a powerful model for the study of visual perception
[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. Their highly developed visual system [30] is modularly
organized in 4 separate eye-pairs, each with its own dedicated brain region and each pair specialized
for a given visual task. The anterior medial eyes (AME), or principal eyes, are the largest pair and

are characterized by the highest visual acuity [31], [32]—far surpassing the resolution of any insect,

eyes are movable [33], [34], allowing for the coverage of around 60 degrees of visual span directly
in front of the animal. The other three pairs of eyes, anterior lateral, posterior medial and posterior
lateral (ALE, PME, PLE) are collectively named secondary eyes. These are characterized by a
poorer resolution with respect to AMEs and cannot be moved. However, their visual field is quite
large, and combined it spans across almost 360 degrees all around the spider [35].

The different visual computations are thought to be segregated across different eye pairs, with the
secondary eyes specialized in motion perception, and the principal eyes in figure recognition. This
is shown by the spiders’ behavior: when a moving target shifts across the secondary eyes visual
field, the animal suddenly pivots its body in order to face the object frontally [36], [37], [38];
thereafter, the AMEs’ muscles are engaged and the small visual fields start to move across the
contours of the object, seemingly reconstructing its shape, in a process dubbed “scanning” [33],
[39], [40]. The movements of the AMEs’ visual fields during scanning are stereotyped, and can be
divided into two types (even if they can happen simultaneously) [33]: (i) torsional, where the retinas
are rotated around the center of the two fields; (ii) translational, where the retinas move back-andforth across the target. Horizontal movement can be semi-independent for the two eyes, which may
move further apart or near each other during the scanning process.

Secondary eyes do not indiscriminately trigger a body pivot for any moving object. Rather, they are
selective, often having to choose between many possible targets, not all worthy of attention [41],
[42], [43], [44]. Such a selective responding requires the ability to not only detect moving objects
but to also discriminate different types of motion. Indeed, recent work has shown that jumping
spiders can discriminate biological from non-biological motion [21], and do so using only their
ALEs [22]. However, unlike in other systems, the spiders’ discrimination ability seems to work only
as a “life detector”, without any figure (i.e., structure from motion) recognition, as they performed
full body pivots to point-light displays depicting a walking spider and scrambled stimuli [21]
equally.

As stated above, the figure and motion interpretation are split across separate eyes in these
creatures, with ALEs only focused on the former task. This may explain why the secondary eyes
could discriminate biological from non-biological motion while remain unable of recognizing to
which creature they belonged to. The motion-specific eyes may be unable to extract “shape from
motion” from the point-light displays, as the neural substrate required for shape perception is not
present in the network of these eyes. However, it has been demonstrated that AMEs’ scanning
movements are directed by ALEs [39], [40], most likely using object motion to inform the required
shifting of principal eyes. This suggests that ALEs may be capable of extracting shape information
from biological motion stimuli, but this difference may not be relevant for orientation behavior
though it may be crucial for AMEs scanning patterns. Observing the shape from motion ability in
jumping spiders—along with the previously demonstrated life detector—would provide an
immensely powerful model species for studying the two aforementioned levels of biological motion
perception. In all other species ever tested for the ability, exclusion of one of these levels is virtually
impossible, exclusion made for ablation studies that anyway would require targeting a specific, yet
unknown exact brain region. Jumping spiders instead would offer the unique possibility of testing
every module of the visual system alone by simple eye coverage, and allow for deep inferences on

3 Discussion
In this paper, we described the AMEs movement pattern when presented with biological and nonbiological point light displays. We observed many significant differences in these eyes’ activity, as
driven by the differences in behavior. While the data we collected goes much in depth into the
activity over the full presentation time, to avoid overextending interpretations we will focus only on
the main and evident results.
3.1 Spiders attend to static stimuli according to their structure

When considering the overall gaze displacement, spiders show a significant activity with respect to
the no-stimulus control. It appears evident that the animals are shifting their gazes to fixate on the
newly appeared stimulus, increasing the eye movement speed from their previous state of
quiescence. These quick movements towards a target are termed “saccades” in the literature, and
has been long described as the typical response of the AMEs upon detection of an appearing target
by the ALEs [34], [36], [40]. This initial AMEs shift is identical for all 5 stimuli, suggesting that the
nature of the target in our paradigm is irrelevant for triggering this behavior. Thereafter, the spiders
maintain an activity significantly higher than the no-stimulus control only for the silhouette and
ellipse, while the three-point light displays drop quickly down to baseline. Fittingly, the first two are
the only stimuli with a discernible shape, and as such they are the only one for which the spiders
engage in scanning behavior [33], [45]. Indeed, for all three measures we collected of AMEs
movement, we observed no differences between the three point-light displays between the
appearance and the initiation of motion. This is in agreement with the literature, as the points cannot
be connected into their discernible structure unless already in motion [7]. Without motion, the three
stimuli are identical sets of scattered dots.

When considering the change in distance between the eyes, we still see an effect for the stimulus
appearance but the difference between stimuli is much weaker. This may suggest that the two
retinas change their distance, getting near and far repeatedly during the saccade, but instead remain
generally constant during scanning, hence why no difference is appreciable between shaped stimuli
and point light displays. The average distance between eyes themselves instead does not change
from the baseline for the whole static section, suggesting that across saccade and scanning the
average distance between eyes remains constant.

Upon the initiation of motion, for all three considered measures the biological display appears to be
different from the static ellipse control, as well as the random one. When considering the gaze
movement speed, the spiders show a significantly slower velocity when compared to all the other
stimuli. When considering the distance between the two retinas, this significantly decreases as soon
as the biological display starts moving, similarly to what happens for the scrambled display and the
silhouette. For the random display instead, the retinas get much nearer. For the speed of change in
retinal distance, the biological motion display sits again at the middle, between the slow speed
observed for the static ellipse and the faster speed of the other stimuli.

These results confirm the ability of jumping spiders to distinguish biological from non-biological
types of motion, and that such distinction is influential to their subsequent scanning pattern. This
represents the first level of biological motion perception, described in the introduction as the “life
detector”. It is likely that this distinction is operated by ALEs alone, as previously demonstrated
[21], [22]. As we know from previous literature, the AMEs movement pattern is driven by the ALEs
[40], which may operate the biological vs non-biological distinction before the first AMEs fixation.

In this experiment, we observed a previously non-described behavior of the AMEs retinas in
response to moving stimuli: the average distance between the two centers decreases. It is crucial to
point out that the two retinas still move near and away from each other during the scanning process,
but their average distance appears to be lower with respect to static stimuli. The reason this specific
behavior went unnoticed for so long is probably connected to the type of stimuli we used:
historically, eye tracking experiments on jumping spiders always used static targets, or at most
translating object but with no limbs’ movement. Indeed, we observed absolutely no difference with
the no-stimulus control for the appearance of any of the stimuli presented, with the convergence

3.4 Responses to the scrambled display as evidence for shape-from-motion extraction
Scrambled point light displays fully maintain the biological motion attribute as their original
counterpart, but do not depict any discernible shape. As such, they are treated the same by animal
operating a discrimination between biological and non-biological motion (i.e., the life detector)
[13], but they instead appear different if the animal attempts to “connect the dots” as based on their
motion pattern, in order to extract a discernible shape (i.e., the figure from motion). When
previously tested, jumping spiders responded equally to the biological and the scrambled displays,
suggesting that at least their secondary eyes are only acting as life detectors [21]. This had to be in
some way expected, as figure discrimination is a task of the AMEs in their modular visual system.

Here, we observed that in terms of distance between the two retinas, the values observed for the

other hand, both in terms of the visual field shifting speed and the distance change between the
eyes, the scrambled displays present values initially similar to the biological display, but seem to
remain at a higher level for a longer time, eventually becoming significantly higher than the
biological, even though never at the level of the random. As stated above, the visual field shifting
speed here measured is representative of the spiders’ saccades and subsequent scanning behavior.
According to the literature, it is exactly during the scanning that jumping spiders extract the shape
information from a target picture, useful for subsequent decision making [30], [33]. We argue that
the different scanning speed observed for the biological, the scrambled, and the random point light
displays reflect the different availability of structural information in the stimulus, that is extracted
(or attempted to be) over time through the AMEs scanning behavior. This would suggest that
jumping spiders also possess the second level of biological motion perception: with ALEs acting as
life detector and AMEs extracting the figure from motion. On the other hand, average retinal
distance seems to be connected only to the motion type observed. As such, the scrambled and
biological display do not appear to elicit a different response.

The observed responses to the ellipse and silhouette stimuli are hard to interpret for the purpose of
this experiment. The latter especially appears for some of the measures to be equal to the random
point light display, while for others it settles at the same level of the biological. Indeed, the
silhouette stimulus moves biologically, following the same locomotion patterns that the point-light
display presents. However, the presence of a trackable contour of the picture constitutes a
confounding factor. We know from previous literature in fact that during scanning the AMEs retinas
follow the contour of the target object [33], which will definitely affect the translation speed as
calculated in our first model. We observed in fact that the translation speed of the retinas upon
stimuli appearance remains high for both silhouette and ellipse (the two stimuli with a contour) for a
long time, while it drops back to baseline for the point-light displays sooner. Upon movement
initiation, the ellipse is now uninteresting, but the silhouette instead jumps back up to the highest
speed level, being both shaped and biologically moving. A very similar pattern is observed for the
speed in retinal distance change.

The effect we observed for retinal convergence further solidifies this hypothesis. As stated above,
we hypothesize that this type of behavior is fully linked to the movement of the target being
observed, independently from its shape. Here, the continuously static ellipse remains at baseline
level, while the silhouette drops to the biological motion level: the shape is irrelevant and the
behavior only depends on the motion characteristics.

3.6 Conclusions
In this paper, we tested jumping spiders’ AMEs’ retinal movement upon presentation of biologically
and non-biologically moving stimuli. We identified two main behavioral categories, being retina
movement speed and their average distance. We observed that the former is more linked to the
scanning process, and as such to the object shape, while the latter dynamically changes depending
on the target motion. The biological and random point-light displays elicit different responses,

On the other hand, biological and scrambled motion appear different for retinal speed (i.e., may be
interpreted to be of different “shape”), but not for retinal convergence (i.e., may be interpreted to
moving the same way), suggesting that jumping spiders reach the second level of biological motion

The unprecedented level of detail in the spiders’ discrimination abilities observed here opens up
many new possibilities for vision research. Firstly, it brings back the AMEs into the task of motion
perception: generally thought to be solely used for figure discrimination, the change in scanning
pattern must have a function that needs to be explored. Perhaps more importantly, these results
suggest a fineness in biological motion discrimination generally observed in “higher” animals [7],
here performed by a modular visual system contained in a brain the size of a pinhead.

4.1 Subjects

368
369
370
371

We collected Phidippus audax in the fall of 2022 from buildings, fences, and other artificial structures in Amherst, MA,
USA. Spiders were housed individually in plastic boxes (18 × 13 × 10 cm) in a temperature-controlled room set to 25°C
with a 16L:8D light cycle. Each enclosure contained a stick, a hollow black tube, and plastic foliage for enrichment.
Spiders were fed one Acheta domesticus cricket per week and had access to water ad libitum.

372

374

For the experiment, we randomly selected 31 individuals from the lab colony using a random number generator. Each
spider was isolated and starved for seven days prior to testing. On the day of the experiment, spiders were anesthetized
on ice for 10 minutes, secured in Parafilm around a Styrofoam ball with only the anterior eyes exposed, and painted
black (Stuart Semple Black Paint) to minimize IR glare during our experiment. The mounted spider was then aligned
and calibrated in the eye tracker (see section 4.3).

378
379

Each spider completed two trials on the same day. Before each trial, we presented a blank screen for 300 seconds for
habituation. Trials consisted of 10 stimulus bouts, each about 35 seconds long and separated by 30-second breaks (see
section 4.2). Stimulus identity and order were randomized in Processing (version 3.5.4).

4.2 Stimuli

381

For a complete description of the experimental stimuli used in this experiment, please see [21]. In brief, we employed 5
different stimuli, 3 of which were point light displays, and 2 were complete figures.

383

The first stimulus was a “biological motion” point-light display, depicting a spider walking, as viewed directly from the
side. To construct it, we collected 72 frames of a 60fps video of a Salticus scenicus walking on a twig, where three full
steps were visible. Frame by frame, we located the coordinates of the three major joints of the 8 legs, plus the frontal
eyes position, the pedicel and the spinneret, totaling 27 points. These coordinates were extracted, rescaled, and shifted
such that the points were centered around the pedicel location. This produced a point cloud depicting a spider walking
“on the spot”, allowing for seamless video looping.

random position in the very first frame. The new location was selected to be inside the general area occupied by the
points in the original stimulus. Thereafter, the points moved with the exact same trajectory that they followed in the
original stimulus. We produced 4 different versions of the scrambled stimulus, each with a different randomized
position of dots in the first frame. Each subject was randomly assigned to one of the 4 versions.

4.4 Experimental procedure
First, the spider was appropriately positioned in the eye-tracker setup. The projector was left blank, showing a white
background for 300 seconds, to allow the animal to habituate and revert back to normal behavior after manipulation.
Then, the first stimulus appeared. This was one of the 5 stimuli described above, chosen at random. The image appeared
as static, and remained so for a total of 12 seconds (or 360 frames). After this time, the stimulus would start moving (all
but the ellipse, which instead remained as static) for the following 12 seconds. After this second block elapsed, the
stimulus stopped, remaining static for another 12 seconds before disappearing completely. After 30 seconds of blank,
the next stimulus (again, randomly assigned) would appear, following the same pattern just described. During the full
trial, the spiders were exposed to all 5 types of stimuli, one time oriented right and one time oriented left (left and right
being arbitrary, as scrambled and random displays don’t necessarily have a “direction”), for a total of 10 stimuli
presentation. The order was randomized, as well as the stimulus version for the scrambled and random point-light
displays.

4.5 Scoring
Once the videos were collected, we used the software DeepLabCut [46] to automatically track the position of both
AMEs retinas in each frame. Due to the eye-tracker construction, the exact location of the stimulus relatively to the
position of the retina cannot be determined. Similarly, the angular shift of the retina in terms of degrees cannot be
precisely determined. For this reason, the coordinates measured via DeepLabCut were not standardized in terms of real
dimension nor centered on the stimulus position. Instead, we collected relative measures that could be compared across
all videos. Specifically, we re-scaled all videos so that the recorded field of view was exactly 1000 pixels. Then, for
every frame, we derived the center point between the two retinas, and measured how many pixels it moved in respect to
the previous frame. This was set as the eyes shifting speed. We also the distance between the two retinas centers (pix) in
every frame. Lastly, we measured how much this distance changed between one frame and the next, recorded as the
speed of retinal distance change (pix/frame). We were unable to measure eye torsion, as the marker fidelity achieved
with DeepLabCut was high for the retinas’ centers, but very low for the retinas’ edges. This was probably due to the

4.6 Statistical analysis

451
452
453
454
455

All analysis were performed in R, with the libraries data.table, glmmTMB, emmeans, car, DHARMa. We also used the
library ggplot2 for fast plotting, and the library parallel to accelerate the analysis. We started by focusing on the time
frame between stimulus appearance and motion initiation, sub setting our full data frame to only these first 12 seconds
of every subject and every stimulus presentation. We also selected for every trial 10 more 12 seconds bouts where no
stimulus was presented. This acted as a baseline with which compare the eye movement to the various stimuli.

456
457
458
459
460
461
462
463
464
465
466

We performed a time series, sliding window GLMM analysis. We divided the full 12 seconds into partially overlapping
30 frames-long sections, taken every 5 frames. For each, we performed 3 different generalized linear mixed model, one
for each dependent variable we selected (eye displacement, inter-eye distance, eye-distance change speed). The stimulus
identity (including no stimulus) was used as independent variable as well as the random slope, while individual identity
was used as a random intercept. We performed an analysis of deviance for all of the generated models, and recorded the
p-values linked with the stimulus identity. We then applied a Bonferroni correction, adjusting for the number of models
performed. We then selected only the windows where the p-value was <0.01, considering them sections where a true
observable difference between the stimuli was appreciable. As a last preparatory step, we dropped any significant
section shorter that 5 consecutive windows, to avoid discussing possible spurious correlations. Only on the selected
significant windows, we proceeded with a post-hoc analysis, comparing each stimulus with the no-stimulus (intended to
test the difference with rest state) and with the biological motion stimulus (as the focus of the experiment).

468
469

We followed the same exact procedure for the next 24 seconds of stimulus presence, from the initiation of motion, to
stopping, to then disappearance. This time however, we did not include the no-stimulus data, using instead the ellipse
stimulus as a baseline (as it is the only one that does not start moving).
