Agroecological intensiﬁcation aims to increase farm productivity, reduce
dependency on external inputs, and enhance biodiversity conservation. However,
the contribution of recent sustainable agricultural practices to arthropod
assemblages and their ecosystem services remains underexplored. Through
a 2-year multilocation trial, we investigated the response of above-ground
arthropod assemblages and their ecological functions in cowpea and maize
grown under monocropping systems and diversiﬁed systems (pure and
intensiﬁed push-pull technologies). Using three sampling methods (visual
observations, pitfall traps, sticky traps), a total of 65,307 arthropod specimens
were collected. The most dominant arthropod taxa were Hemiptera (true
bugs; 47%), followed by Thysanoptera (thrips; 17%), Diptera (true ﬂies; 16%),
Hymenoptera (ants; 9.7%), Trombidiformes (mites; 3.5%), Coleoptera (beetles;
2.2%) and Araneae (spiders; 1.5%). Familywise, grass spiders were most dominant
in diversiﬁed systems, while wolf spiders and jumping spiders were more
dominant in monocrops. Regarding the beetles, the highest abundance of
ladybird beetles was observed in diversiﬁed systems, while ground beetles and
rove beetles showed a high preference for monocrops. Tachinid ﬂies showed a
high preference for monocrops. Carpenter ants were more abundant in cowpea
monocrops, while harvester ants were more abundant in diversiﬁed systems.
Families of honeybees (pollinators), hoverﬂies (pollinators, predators) and wasps
(parasitoids, predators, pollinators) occurred frequently in diversiﬁed systems.
Regarding herbivores, noctuid moths/larvae were the most dominant pests in
maize monocrops, while leaf miners, aphids, spider mites, thrips and leaf beetles
were the most abundant pests in cowpea monocrops. The highest natural
enemy-to-herbivore ratios were reported in diversiﬁed systems compared to
monocrops. Diversiﬁed systems increased the crop yield by about two-fold
compared to untreated monocrops. Our study demonstrates that diversiﬁed
systems increase overall arthropod population and create a more balanced
ecosystem in which beneﬁcial arthropods are not dominated by destructive ones,
which may have resulted in improved ecological resilience and crop productivity.

Although previous studies have shown that cereal-based
PP attracts more natural enemies than cereal monocultures
(Cook et al., 2006; Midega et al., 2008), little is known about
how multi-component agroecological systems like VIPP might
inﬂuence above-ground arthropod community structures, their
ecosystem services and functional interactions. Most past research
has focused on taxonomic biodiversity (e.g., abundance, species
richness, diversity indices), yet functional composition provides
deeper insights into how biodiversity supports ecological processes
(Birkhofer et al., 2017; Lamarre et al., 2016; Potapov et al., 2022).
Addressing this gap is critical for evaluating the ecological
sustainability of such systems. Therefore, this study aimed to
evaluate how cereal- or vegetable-integrated push-pull systems
inﬂuence the structure and functional composition of aboveground arthropod communities, with implications on sustainable
pest management and agroecosystem resilience.

For decades since the Green Revolution, agriculture has
relied on intensive but ecologically simpliﬁed production systems
characterized by lower biodiversity and overreliance on often
harmful synthetic agrochemicals (Pingali, 2012). While this
approach can signiﬁcantly increase yield, its sustainability has
been a source of societal concern globally, given the detrimental
eﬀects such as soil degradation, eutrophication, greenhouse gas
emissions, and biodiversity loss (John and Babu, 2021; Pingali,
2012). Of concern is how the multifunctionality of agricultural
landscapes, biodiversity, and ecosystem services provisioning
under such cropping systems, coupled with the ongoing climate
change, compromise the resilience and sustainability of agricultural
production systems (Duru et al., 2015; Landis, 2017). Therefore,
agroecosystems form part of socio-ecological transitions which
integrate biodiversity conservation, ecosystem services and
agricultural productivity to meet both current and future food
needs (FAO, 2016).
Arthropod biodiversity is crucial to agroecosystems by
providing key ecosystem services such as pest regulation,
pollination, insect-plant mutualisms, and nutrient cycling, which
directly impact crop performance (Blaise et al., 2022). Arthropods
also play an important role in food webs (serving as food sources for
other animals), acting as environmental indicators and producing
useful substances such as honey and silk (Sollai et al., 2024).
However, the sustainability of these services is being undermined
by conventional agricultural practices (Quandahor et al., 2024).
These developments call for ecologically grounded alternatives to
sustainable agricultural productivity while preserving biodiversity.
Crop diversiﬁcation, such as intercropping, relay cropping, crop
rotations, and mixed cropping, promotes resource use eﬃciency
and ecosystem services through supporting diverse beneﬁcial
arthropods. Understanding how arthropod communities respond
to diﬀerent plant ﬂora is crucial in determining what vegetation
is required to enhance these ecosystem services. Enhancing the
eﬃcacy of beneﬁcial arthropods may involve providing them
with one or more of the following resources: shelter, nectar,
alternative prey and pollen (Gurr et al., 2017). These resources
may not be available in main crops during critical periods,
which may require beneﬁcial arthropods to obtain them from
neighboring plants.
Crop diversiﬁcation may supply resources needed to increase
the population of beneﬁcial arthropods, such as natural
enemies; nevertheless, this does not necessarily eliminate
pests but may improve pest suppression on primary crops
(Almdal and Costamagna, 2023). The changes in below-ground
arthropod and nematode communities due to crop diversiﬁcation
have also been reported (Kiriga et al., 2026; Omuse et al., 2026).
In sub-Saharan Africa, push-pull (PP) technology is an example
of a promising agroecological approach that relies on functional
biodiversity for pest management. It involves intercropping cereals
with perennial forage legumes such as Desmodium species while
perennial forage grasses such as Napier grass or Brachiaria grass
are planted around the ﬁeld (Cook et al., 2006; Khan et al., 2011).
Desmodium releases volatiles that “push” (or repel) Lepidopteran
pests such as stem borers and fall armyworm away from the

2 Materials and methods
2.1 Study sites
The study was conducted in three counties in western Kenya:
Homa Bay, Busia and Kisii counties (Figure 1). In Homa Bay
County, the study was conducted at the International Center
of Insect Physiology and Ecology (icipe), Thomas Odhiambo
Campus in Mbita [0◦ 5’6 S, 34◦ 2’8 E; 1,146 m above sea level
(asl)]. The study was also conducted at two Kenya Agricultural
and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) ﬁeld stations in
Busia County [0◦ 9 1 N, 34◦ 7’9 E; 1,158 m asl] and Kisii County
[0◦ 0’9 S, 34◦ 7’2 E; 1,768 m asl]. Mbita (in Homa Bay) is subject
to Lake Victoria basin climatic conditions, which are warm with
low to moderate rainfall. The area receives annual rainfall ranging
from 1,200 to 1,700 mm with monthly temperatures ranging from
23.2 ◦ C to 24.9 ◦ C (https://en.climate-data.org). KALRO Busia
experiences average monthly temperatures of between 20.9 ◦ C
and 23.4 ◦ C and annual rainfall ranging from 480 mm to
1,700 mm (https://en.climate-data.org). KALRO Kisii experiences
mean monthly temperatures of between 17.8 ◦ C and 19.7 ◦ C with
annual rainfall ranging from 1,500 mm to 2,000 mm. All three study
sites experience bimodal rainy seasons, with the Long Rainy (LR)
season commencing from March to May and the Short Rainy (SR)
season occurring from August to October in Busia, October to
December in Mbita, and November to December in Kisii.

2.6 Data analysis
2.6.1 Analysis of high arthropod taxa: order
The catches in the yellow and blue sticky traps were pooled
and expressed as abundance per trap, while pitfall trap catches
were expressed as abundance per trap. For physical observation,
the number of arthropods was expressed as abundance per 10
plants. Before analyses, the spatial autocorrelation across plots
was assessed using Moran’s I method with moran.test function
in the spdep package (Bivand, 2022). Moran’s I test whether
nearby locations have similar values or spatially independent
values. Moran’s I test revealed a weak and non-signiﬁcant spatial
autocorrelation, indicating randomness of values across plots in
the three study sites. Overdispersion was detected when a linear
mixed-eﬀects model (LMM) was used to analyze abundance data,
and therefore, the data were subjected to a generalized linear
mixed-eﬀects model (GLMM) with a negative binomial error
distribution using the lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015). In the
model, cropping systems, study sites, cropping seasons, sampling
methods, arthropod orders and their interaction were ﬁxed eﬀects,
with blocks serving as both grouping structure (replication) and
source of variability (random variables) in treatment response.
The Anova function from the car package (Fox and Weisber,
2019) was used to summarize the signiﬁcance of the model’s
ﬁxed eﬀects for diﬀerent response variables (p values of type II
Wald χ 2 tests).

2.4 Arthropod identiﬁcation and functional
groupings
We sorted the sample specimens in the laboratory
and identiﬁed them to higher-taxa (class/order) levels
and lower-taxa (family and/or subfamily) levels. We
employed various morphological keys listed in literature
(Husemann and Hawlitschek, 2026; Garcia et al., 2013; Kioko et al.,
2021; Klimaszewski and Watt, 1997) and online platforms (https://
keys.lucidcentral.org). We then counted the number of individuals
represented in the lower taxa of arthropods. Dermaptera was the
only order that could not be identiﬁed at the family/subfamily
level. Major groups within the order Hymenoptera, such as
ants, bees and wasps, were examined separately. The two major
groups (crickets and grasshoppers) in the order Orthoptera
were also examined separately. Furthermore, we grouped these
arthropods based on their trophic levels within ecosystems as
herbivores, predators, parasitoids, pollinators, omnivores, and
detritivores. Grouping of arthropods into predominant ecological
functional groups was based on the literature (Balzan et al., 2014;
Potapov et al., 2022; Sattler et al., 2010; Sforza, 2021), followed by
conﬁrmation by experts. We placed arthropods in their respective
functional groups, with some families occupying more than one
group. In this study, the predators and parasitoids were analyzed
together as natural enemies due to their ecosystem service of
pest regulation.

2.6.2 Analysis of lower arthropod taxa: families
and subfamilies
The families and subfamilies of arthropods sampled using
various techniques and from study sites and cropping seasons
were combined for ease of interpretation before descriptive
analyses. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) in
the vegan package using the Bray-Curtis similarity index
(Oksanen et al., 2025) was used to explore arthropod taxa
composition (families/subfamilies) across cropping systems. In
NMDS plots, the lower taxa were displayed using non-overlapping
text labels. Further, the mean abundance of the families/subfamilies
within the corresponding orders of arthropods was computed
and presented graphically. Due to lower taxonomic resolutions
(for our case, family and subfamily levels), we represented
total counts and number of individual families as a measure
of abundance and richness rather than using diversity indices
such as Shannon’s Index, which are most meaningful at the
species level. Lower taxonomic resolutions contain uneven
and ecologically heterogeneous units, according to previous
studies, which increase bias and decrease the sensitivity and
accuracy of biodiversity patterns in community composition

2.6.3 Analysis of functional groups, natural
enemy—Herbivore ratios and crop yield
Arthropod communities sampled using diﬀerent methods were
pooled by summing up at the plot level. Irrespective of taxa,
arthropods that share similar functional groups were summed at
the plot level and analyzed using GLMM with a negative binomial
residual distribution. The overall abundance of natural enemies was
computed by the summation of the abundance data of predators
and parasitoids at the plot level. The enemy–herbivore (NE–H)
ratio was then calculated by dividing the abundance of natural
enemies by that of herbivores at the plot level. Fresh cowpea
leaves (fresh weight) and maize grains (dry weight) were used
as measures of crop productivity. Both NE–H ratios and crop
yield data were analyzed using LMM using the lme4 package.
In both GLMM and LLM for analysis of functional groups and
NE–H ratios, the ﬁxed variables were sites, cropping seasons,
cropping systems and their interactions, while blocks served as
both replications and random variables. Separation of means
was performed using the lsmeans package (Lenth, 2016), and pvalues were adjusted according to the Sidak test. All statistical
analyses were performed in R statistical software version 4.5.0
(R Core Team, 2025).

3 Results
3.1 Summary of arthropod community
The total number of arthropods sampled using physical
observations, sticky traps and pitfall traps was 14,817, 43,289,
and 7,201, respectively. The most dominant higher taxa from all
sampling methods combined were Hemiptera (47%), followed by
Thysanoptera (17%), Diptera (16%), Hymenoptera (ants; 9.7%),
Trombidiformes (3.5%), Coleoptera (2.2%) and Araneae (1.5%).
The least occurring taxa (<1%) were Orthoptera (Gryllidae;
0.9%), Neuroptera (0.8%), Lepidoptera (0.6%), Hymenoptera
(wasps; 0.4%), Orthoptera (Acrididae; 0.3%), Dermaptera (0.2%),
Hymenoptera (bees; 0.2%), Isopoda (0.1%), and Diplopoda (0.1%).
CpM had the highest arthropod abundance (10,687), followed

Cp; 3,794) > MzM + Ag (3,689) > MzM (3,431) > PP (Mz;
3,097). The highest arthropod abundance was reported in Kisii
(31,246), followed by Busia (19,023), while Homa Bay recorded
the least abundance (15,038). There were more arthropods in the

were collected.

4.1.4 Hymenoptera

Among families of Dipterans, such as Tachinidae, are the
dominant larval parasitoids of Lepidopteran and Coleopteran
pests, making them, alongside parasitic Hymenoptera (wasps),
the most common insect parasitoids. Tachinids are eﬀective
parasitoids of a wide range of herbivorous insects, particularly
in agroecosystems with high host availability (Weber et al., 2021),
and their high presence in monocropping systems in this
study may be attributed to the presence of a diverse range
of hosts. Similarly, the relatively high abundance of Syrphidae
across both monocropping and diversiﬁed systems reﬂects their
dual ecological function as pollinators and predators (in larval
stages), often associated with ﬂowering resources and aphid
populations (Dunn et al., 2020). The low abundance of Tabanidae
may be attributed to their preference for more humid or
livestock-associated habitats rather than crop ﬁelds. Concerning

4.1.5 Lepidoptera

4.1.7 Orders Thysanoptera, Orthoptera,
Neuroptera, Dermaptera, Trombidiformes and
Oniscidea and class Diplopoda

The dominance of Noctuidae (noctuid moths) in maize-based
monocropping systems suggests that simpliﬁed agroecosystems
favor herbivorous pest populations. Members of Noctuidae are
well-known generalist herbivores, many of which are major crop
pests (e.g., armyworms, stalk borers, cutworms, and bollworms)
that thrive in homogeneous cropping environments where food
resources are continuous and natural enemy pressure is often
reduced. In contrast, the lower abundance of lepidopteran
families such as Noctuidae, Crambidae, Bombycidae, Erebidae,
and Nymphalidae in diversiﬁed systems may reﬂect the inﬂuence
of increased plant diversity, which can disrupt host-ﬁnding
and enhance biological control through greater predator and
parasitoid activity. This pattern is consistent with ecological theory
and empirical studies showing that crop diversiﬁcation reduces
herbivore pressure by increasing habitat complexity and promoting
natural enemies (e.g., Letourneau et al., 2011; Ratnadass et al.,
2012). Additionally, the low representation of other Lepidopteran
families, including Psychodidae and unidentiﬁed larvae, may
indicate either the limited suitability of the habitat or sampling
bias. These ﬁndings support the notion that monocropping systems
can facilitate the proliferation of dominant herbivore groups such
as Noctuidae, whereas diversiﬁed systems contribute to fewer
Lepidopteran populations.

The dominance of herbivorous groups such as Tetranychidae
(red mites) and Thripidae (thrips) in cowpea monocropping
compared to diversiﬁed systems likely reﬂects the higher suitability
and resource concentration of cowpea as a host, which can enhance
pest colonization and reproduction under simpliﬁed cropping
conditions, consistent with the resource concentration hypothesis
(Wenda-piesik and Piesik, 2020). Increased pest abundance in
monocultures has been widely documented, particularly for mites
and thrips, which respond rapidly to favorable microclimatic
conditions and host availability (Sunderland and Samu, 2000).
In contrast, diversiﬁed cropping systems often promote natural
enemy activity and disrupt pest population build-up. The relatively
low abundance of Orthopteran families (Acrididae, Gryllidae,
Rhaphidophoridae, and Tettigoniidae) suggests limited herbivory
pressure from chewing insects, possibly due to habitat structure
or seasonal dynamics. Predatory taxa such as Chrysopidae (green
lacewings) and Dermaptera (earwigs), which were consistently
observed across cropping systems, are known generalist predators
that contribute to natural control by feeding on soft-bodied pests,
including aphids, mites, and thrips (Sforza, 2021). Additionally,
the presence of detritivores such as Diplopoda (millipedes)
and Oniscidea (woodlice), along with omnivorous groups of
Orthopterans such as Rhaphidophoridae and Tettigoniidae,
highlights the role of these systems in supporting decomposition
processes and nutrient cycling, which are essential for maintaining
soil health and ecosystem functioning (Potapov et al., 2022). These
patterns further emphasize the ecological importance of diversiﬁed
cropping systems in regulating pest populations while supporting
diverse arthropod functional groups.

food sources and oviposition substrates (Van Der Kooi et al.,

quality when selecting the host plant (Döring and Kirchner,
2022). Therefore, this visual detection may interfere with
crop diversiﬁcation. Some phytophagous arthropods rely

locate their host plant. Monocropping encourages resource
concentration, making the cues from the resource (crop plant)
easily recognizable to herbivores. Hence, herbivorous insects
are more likely to locate and eﬃciently attack the host crop
in monocropping systems (Ratnadass et al., 2012). Conversely,
diversiﬁed cropping systems such as intercropping result in
resource dilution due to the mixing of cues of host and nonhost plants, which disrupts the pests from locating hosts, and
consequently reduces the build-up of specialist pest populations

crops with non-host plants, in our case Desmodium, possibly

with repellent properties, therefore preventing the pests from
reaching their preferred resource (the crop) (Pickett and Khan,

insect pests, but becomes unﬁt for the development of the
progenies of the pests (Cheruiyot et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2016;
Van den Berg and Van Hamburg, 2015). Lastly, the “dead-end”
trap plant may enhance the action of the natural enemies on the
pests (Cook et al., 2006). This study showed low abundance and
richness of herbivorous insects in push-pull plots when compared
to non-push-pull plots.

4.3 Natural enemy—Herbivore (NE–H)
ratios
Crop diversiﬁcation has been known to recruit more
natural enemies, potentially reducing herbivorous insect pests
(Jaworski et al., 2023). However, assessment of NE–H ratios sheds
more light on the temporal and antagonistic interactions of natural
enemies and insect pests. A higher NE–H ratio could indicate
that there is a stronger top-down control of arthropod pests. This
study showed that the diversiﬁed cropping systems enhanced the
NE–H ratios, which can be linked to improved resource availability
(e.g., alternative prey, nectar, shelter and pollen) and habitat
heterogeneity that supports predator and parasitoid populations.
This potentially emphasize the contribution of diversiﬁed cropping
systems such as PP and VIPP to the recruitment of natural
enemies and pest suppression. Also, there was high variability
of NE–H ratios in the three study sites, which can be attributed
to local environmental conditions, landscape structure, and
surrounding habitat composition, which can modulate biological
control potential (Tscharntke et al., 2007). The absence of seasonal
variation suggests that NE–H dynamics in this system may be
stable, either due to the persistence of both herbivores and their
natural enemies or compensatory ecological processes that buﬀer
temporal ﬂuctuations (Welch and Harwood, 2014). Compensatory
ecological processes are mechanisms that stabilize ecosystem
function by compensating for the decline of one species or
functional group with the increase of another, usually in response
to environmental variations, resource scarcity, or disturbances.
For example, generalist predators such as ladybirds, lacewings,
and syrphid larvae buﬀer against aphid ﬂuctuations by moving

4.4 Overall summary and study limitations
These ﬁndings oﬀer compelling evidence that the VIPP
delivers synergistic beneﬁts for ecological pest regulation and
pollinator support, advancing sustainable crop protection and
insect pollination. Our ﬁndings indicate a two-fold yield increase
in the diversiﬁed systems compared to the monocrop. These
increments can be partially attributed to the beneﬁcial ecosystem
services of arthropod biodiversity. However, some trade-oﬀs merit
further consideration. While increased natural enemy abundance
supports biological control, it may also aﬀect non-target or
beneﬁcial arthropods, warranting careful assessment. For instance,
arthropods can cause ecosystem disservices by preying on beneﬁcial
arthropods (intraguild predation) by ants and spiders (Gontijo,
2019; Katayama et al., 2015; Toro et al., 2012) or by ants protecting
pests such as aphids from predators and parasitoids (Billick et al.,
2007). Additionally, the population of predators, for instance, ants
and spiders, can be negatively correlated due to niche overlaps
and relatively few prey (Katayama et al., 2015). Even though there
was weak and non-signiﬁcant spatial autocorrelation, fast-moving
insects (e.g., Dipteran ﬂies, wasps, bees) can readily disperse
between crop types to seek resources, such as nectar, pollen,
or prey. The proximity of our experimental ﬁelds may have
allowed relatively higher spillover rates of these arthropods between
adjacent plots compared to landscape-scale cropping systems,
which often limit the movement of arthropods (Inclán et al., 2016).
Moreover, scaling these practices to landscape or regional levels
requires alignment with policy frameworks, extension services, and
farmer capacity-building programs. Although the ﬁndings oﬀer
valuable insights into above-ground biodiversity under diversiﬁed
cropping, they are constrained by two-season data and site-speciﬁc
conditions. Future studies should evaluate year-to-year variability,
below-ground dynamics, parasitism rates, predation rates, and the
eﬀects of surrounding landscapes on these diversiﬁed cropping
systems with species-level data. Such longer-term monitoring and
species-level data are required to conﬁrm resilience of functional
outcomes of PP and VIPP on pest suppression, pollination
eﬃciency, and crop yield.
