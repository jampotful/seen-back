Abstract
Physiological or genetic assays and computational modeling are valuable tools for understanding animals’ visual discrimination capabilities. Yet sometimes, the results generated by these methods appear not to jive with other aspects of an animal’s
appearance or natural history, and behavioral confirmatory tests are warranted. Here we examine the peculiar case of a male
jumping spider that displays red, black, white, and UV color patches during courtship despite the fact that, according to
microspectrophotometry and color vision modeling, they are unlikely able to discriminate red from black. To test whether
some optical or neurological component could have been missed using these methods, we conduct mate choice experiments.
Some females are presented with a choice between males with their red leg coloration painted over with either red or black
paint, while other females are presented with a choice between males with the same coloration painted over by either red or
white paint. This latter pairing of red and white males should have been easily distinguishable to the spiders and served as
a control to ensure our experimental setup was conducive to natural mating behavior. Red males were more likely to mate
than white males (P = 0.035), whereas red and black males had identical mating success (P = 1.0). This suggests that previous physiological and computational work on these spiders was correct in concluding that they are unable to discriminate
between red and black. Any functional significance of displaying both colors, rather than only black, remains unresolved.
Keywords Spectral sensitivity · Color vision · Salticidae · Mate choice · Sexual selection · Color pattern evolution

Introduction
A combination of physiological or genetic and computational methods is typically employed to determine what
colors are discriminable to animal eyes (Kelber et al. 2003).
Physiological methods include microspectrophotometry and
electrophysiology, both of which involve stimulating photoreceptors with different colors of light to determine the
wavelengths of their peak sensitivities. Genetic methods can
provide the same information, but require knowledge of the
gene sequences and spectral sensitivities of opsins of related
taxa, as well as the spectral properties of any optical filters
within the eye (Kelber et al. 2003). Computational methods

can then be used to calculate chromatic contrasts between
different colors (Gawryszewski 2018). Alternatively, behavioral methods can be employed, typically by training animals
to associate particular colors with a reward or punishment.
However, pinpointing an animal’s peak spectral sensitivities using behavioral trials requires an enormous number of
training and testing sessions and is rarely done, especially in
animals that are difficult to train or to keep in the laboratory
(see, for example, Christa Neumeyer’s classic experiments
on goldfish and turtles: Arnold and Neumeyer 1987; Neumeyer and Arnold 1989; Neumeyer and Jäger 1985; Neumeyer 1985; Neumeyer 1984).
Although physiological and genetic techniques can produce results more rapidly than behavioral tests, they do have
limitations. Physiological techniques can, for example, miss
photoreceptor classes that occur only sparsely in the retina,
and genetic assays will overlook the presence of optical filters. When such techniques produce a result that seems surprising next to other aspects of an animal’s natural history,
behavioral tests can be a useful tool to help corroborate (or
refute) the surprising result. That said, behavioral techniques

have their own limitations, as one can argue that the animals
were not properly motivated or that they only discriminate
between certain stimuli under specific conditions — i.e., not
the ones tested in the experiment. Some aspect of the experimental design can also prevent animals from being able to
demonstrate an ability that they would have under natural
conditions. This latter point is well illustrated by research
on cephalopod vision. Over the past century, behavioral,
physiological, and genetic evidence have all pointed towards
the majority of cephalopods being color blind (Hanke and
Kelber 2019). This has long baffled scientists, since many
cephalopods exhibit colorful intraspecific displays as well
as camouflage that they can dynamically change to match
a variety of backgrounds. How they do this without color
vision has been difficult to imagine. Eight years ago, Stubbs
and Stubbs (2016) proposed a novel mechanism for color
vision in cephalopods that would use optics (specifically,
chromatic aberration) rather than differently-tuned photoreceptors to discriminate colors. In order for the mechanism
to work, the target would have to have fine-scale structure
for the animal to focus on. Since targets in previous behavioral experiments lacked fine-scale structure, it is difficult
to evaluate the correctness of their conclusions; thus, it is
still unclear whether the majority of cephalopods might have
some form of color vision. This shows how useful it is to
design behavioral experiments that ask animals to perform
tasks or make decisions that closely mimic those they perform in nature. For example, if Messenger et al. (1973) had
trained Octopus vulgaris to discriminate between different
colors of naturalistic targets, like models of prey or corals,
rather than large homogeneous targets, then their results
could, all these years later, be used to either corroborate or
refute the optics-based mechanism proposed by Stubbs and
Stubbs (2016).
Recent work has uncovered yet another animal that, like
cephalopods, has an apparent mismatch between its visual
capabilities and its natural history. The jumping spider Saitis
barbipes is highly sexually dimorphic, with males displaying
legs ornamented with red, black, white, and UV patches on
their forward-facing surfaces during courtship and agonistic
displays (Glenszczyk et al. 2022; Wearing et al. 2014). Yet
microspectrophotometric measurements and examination
of the retina for ocular filters suggest that these spiders are
likely UV-green dichromats and lack a red photoreceptor
class. Color contrast calculations show they are unlikely
able to discriminate red from black (Glenszczyk et al. 2022).
Why they should produce both red and black coloration,
rather than only black, is unclear. One recent study tested the
hypothesis that red patches may actually be a better match to
their leaf litter background, which consists of various shades
of low-chroma long-wavelength colors. Multispectral imaging of the spiders in their natural environment supported
this hypothesis. However, a field predation experiment, in

The Science of Nature (2025) 112:5

which posed freeze-dried spiders, either unmanipulated or
with their red leg patches painted black, found unmanipulated spiders to be more heavily predated upon (Gerfen and
Tedore 2024). Any functional significance of the spiders’
red coloration therefore remains unclear. Another explanation could be that red and black have similar production
costs in these spiders, and that, in the absence of strong
predation pressure, both colors are equally likely to evolve
and be maintained by sexual selection as a mechanism to
produce achromatic contrast with the spiders’ background
and white color patches. Saitis barbipes’ red coloration is
at least partially structural (Gerfen and Tedore 2024) but
could also contain ommochromes, while their black coloration is likely either imparted by ommochromes or eumelanin
(Hsiung et al. 2017). The relative costs of these different
mechanisms are unclear. It is also possible that red reflects
heat better than black does, while producing equivalent achromatic contrast, thus serving the dual purposes of visual
signaling and thermoregulation (Glenszczyk et al. 2022). Yet
another possibility is that something was missed in previous
microspectrophotometric measurements. The spiders may
possess a red photoreceptor class that occurs only sparsely
in the retina or a different mechanism altogether for distinguishing red from black. Saitis barbipes could, for example,
exploit the chromatic aberration of its lens and the tiered
layout of its retina (Blest et al. 1981; Land 1969; Nagata
et al. 2012) to use the most proximal layer of photoreceptors
(layer 1) to receive in-focus red light, and the layer distal to
it (layer 2) to receive in-focus green light, and compare the
output of photoreceptors from each layer.
If these spiders do, indeed, have a mechanism for distinguishing red from black, then contexts in which they
would probably use this ability would be courtship and/
or agonistic interactions. As mentioned above, this seems
likely given that red appears only on the forward-facing
surfaces of the males, i.e., on the face and on the third pair
of legs which are waved during courtship and agonistic
interactions. We therefore designed a behavioral experiment to test whether or not males with red coloration experience an advantage in such contexts. Specifically, we set
up mating trials with one female and two males, with each
male manipulated to have its red leg color patches painted
over by a different color. Every trial included one male
with its red leg color patches painted over with a similar
red and another male with its red leg color patches painted
over with either black or white. Red, black, and white paint
colors were custom-mixed to match the spiders’ natural
red, black, and white coloration. Since red should be easily
discriminable from white to these spiders, the red-white
pairing served as a control to ensure that our experimental setup was conducive to natural visual signaling and
mating behavior. We chose to use a white similar to the
one in the male display, rather than some other color, to

The Science of Nature (2025) 112:5

mitigate the possibility that females might actually prefer
the non-red male, since novel display elements have been
shown to enhance attractiveness in various animals, from
jumping spiders to fish to birds (Basolo 1990; Burley and
Symanski 1998; Elias et al. 2006). To ensure the spiders’
surroundings were as naturalistic as possible, we designed
our arena to be made up of colors similar to those of their
natural habitat. Our expectation was that red and black
males should experience similar mating success, whereas
white males should experience lower mating success. If,
counter to expectation, red males were more successful
than black males, this would suggest that something was
missed in previous physiological measurements.

Materials and methods
Spider collection and husbandry
Saitis barbipes were collected in Osp, Slovenia
(45°34′47.7″N 13°51′20.0″E) in May 2022 and 2023 in an
Aristolochio luteae-Quercetum pubescentis (Poldini, 2008)
submediterranean forest. In the laboratory, they were kept
individually in plastic containers (14.5cm x 11.5cm x 7cm)
outfitted with a folded piece of cardboard as enrichment.
Spiders were exposed to a light:dark cycle of 15:9 with a
combination of a daylight-mimicking LED strip (6500K
ABSOLUTE SERIES™ LED Flexible Strip, Waveform
Lighting LLC, Vancouver, USA) with supplementary
UV at 365 nm (realUV™ LED Strip Lights, Waveform
Lighting LLC, Vancouver, USA) and 395 nm (DT5UV,
Onforu Technology Ltd, Shenzen, China). The relative
intensities of the LEDs were adjusted to induce similar
relative quantum catches (i.e., photons absorbed) in the
spiders’ eyes as the ASTM standard solar spectrum. Spiders were sprayed with water daily and fed five to ten fruit
flies (Drosophila melanogaster) twice a week. In the room
where they were housed, the temperature varied from 23 to
29 °C and the humidity between 50 and 60% depending on
outdoor weather conditions. All spiders whose colors were
measured to guide our paint selection (see below) were
collected as adults. But spiders used in behavioral trials
were collected as juveniles to ensure they had not already
mated. To determine their date of maturation, spider enclosures were checked for molts daily. Female maturation was
determined by the appearance of genitalia visible under a
microscope. To enable size matching in experimental trials, male body length and mass were measured within 24 h
of them molting to maturity. Mating trials were conducted
with females 7–10 days post maturation (final molt) and
males seven or more days post maturation.

Color measurement and paint and arena design
Spider color patches were too small to be measured with
traditional spectrometry equipment, so instead, we generated coarse reflectance spectra using a custom multispectral
imaging setup. Spiders were photographed through each of
15 bandpass filters with center wavelengths ranging from
325 to 675 nm with full width half maximums (FWHM)
of 50 nm (Edmund Optics Hard Coated 50 nm Bandpass
Filters with OD 4.0 blocking, Mainz, Germany). Each filter was spectrally spaced apart by 25 nm. The photography
setup included a macro lens (Jenoptik CoastalOpt UV-VISIR 60 mm 1:4 Apo Macro, Jupiter, FL, USA), 36 mm and
12 mm extension tubes (Kenko Extension Tube Set DG,
Kenko Tokina Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), a custom-fabricated infrared blocking filter (Tedore and Nilsson, 2019),
and monochrome camera (JAI CM-140 GE-UV, San Jose,
CA, USA). Spiders (N = 10) were euthanized with C
­ O2 at
4 °C overnight, and photographs were taken the following
day. The spider prosoma was glued to the head of a nail
and the right foreleg was positioned, using insect pins, as it
would be during a courtship display; i.e., extended upwards
at a 45° angle, with the front surface perpendicular to the
camera’s perspective. The nail was inserted into a piece of
black-painted foam in front of a vertically-oriented Spectralon white standard evenly reflective across the UV-VIS
spectrum (WS-1, Ocean Insight, Dunedin, FL, USA). The
spider and white standard were illuminated, in an otherwise
darkened room, by a xenon light source (XE-140BF, Seric
Ltd., 196 Tokyo, Japan), oriented 45° from vertical, whose
spectrum closely mimics that of natural daylight (Glenszczyk et al. 2022).
Images at two different exposures were acquired through
each filter to enable the generation of high dynamic range
images. Dark noise, acquired from several columns on the
camera sensor not exposed to any light, was subtracted
from all image pixels. Since the posed leg often moved
slightly over the course of taking 30 photographs, we used
MATLAB’s ‘imregister’ function to align same-channel
images and ‘registrationEstimator’ function to align different channel images. Before aligning the different-channel
images, they were cropped to encompass only the leg segment of interest, which enabled more accurate alignment.
Also before alignment, images were checked for over- and
under-exposed pixels in the regions of interest, and none
were found. Once aligned, images were combined to form
high dynamic range images.
Patches for the three colors of interest (red, white,
and black), as well as the white standard, were manually
selected using MATLAB’s interactive “roipoly” function.
For red coloration, a dense patch of red hairs on the tibia was
selected, as the hairs are brighter than the red cuticle (Glenszczyk et al. 2022) and therefore have the highest chance

5

Page 4 of 10

of being distinguishable from black. For black coloration,
a combined selection of black cuticle and dense black hairs
protruding from the metatarsus was selected, as this best
represents what a theoretical black tibia covered with black
hairs would look like. For the white coloration, a dense area
of white hairs protruding from the tarsus was selected. The
reflectance for each color patch through each of the 15 filters
was then calculated by dividing the median pixel value of
the patch by the median pixel value of the white standard.
Custom paint colors were mixed to match the reflectance
spectra of the spiders’ colors as closely as possible (Fig. 1a).
Iron oxide pigments were used to create red and black (Dark
Red Ochre (73.107) and Natural Iron Oxide (74.115),
respectively, Vallejo Pigments, Fantasy-In, Hannover, Germany), and finely ground chalk was used to create white.
Pigments were mixed with a binding solution of 20% acrylic
(Acrylbinder, Gerstaecker, Eitorf, Germany) to water (v/v).
To create full coverage paint, 400 mg iron oxide pigment
or 700 mg chalk were mixed into 1 mL binding solution for
each of the three colors. Paint samples were painted onto
black cardboard and measured with a spectrophotometer
(QE Pro, Ocean Optics, Ostfildern, Germany) with a pulsed
xenon light source (PX-2, Ocean Optics, Ostfildern, Germany). The fiber optic cable emitting the light was angled
at 45° relative to the measurement probe leading back to the
spectrophotometer, which was oriented vertically. The white
standard used for calibration and the paint samples were all
measured at the same distance from the light source and the
measurement probe.
Each male was painted one day before his first experimental trial. To immobilize them, males were trapped between
a soft foam and a piece of pantyhose stretched taut over
the spider. Then, small scissors were used to cut a single
strand of pantyhose and the leg to be painted was pulled
through the hole. The red portions of the front of the femur,
patella, and tibia were carefully painted with a miniature
brush (da Vinci MICRO-NOVA Series 170, Size 20/10, da
Vinci Künstlerpinselfabrik DEFET GmbH, Nuremberg, Germany) under a microscope in the desired color, excluding the
joints so as not to impair movement.
To design a naturalistic color environment for S. barbipes’
behavioral trials, reflectance spectra were taken of leaf litter
(the upper and lower sides of each of three dead leaves) and
calcite rock from the spiders’ habitat, which are both substrates the spiders are commonly found on. Undyed brown
paper (Braunes Packpapier, Baier and Schneider GmbH and
Co. KG, Heilbronn, Germany) had a spectrum similar to leaf
litter, and a gray granite had a spectrum resembling that of
the calcite rock (Fig. 1b). Undyed brown paper was therefore used to line the walls of the arena, and grey granite was
used as the floor. Granite had the additional advantage that
it is a poor conductor of vibratory cues (Hebets, 2005) and
should have forced the spiders to attend more to the visual

cues we were interested in rather than vibratory ones. For a
qualitative sense of the similarity between the two environments, Figure 1c shows a male S. barbipes situated in his
natural habitat with an inset showing a male situated in the
experimental arena.

Experimental set‑up and process
A paired experimental design was used to determine whether
S. barbipes differentiate between red and black in a threeway behavioral interaction. In each trial, a female was either
exposed to a red male and a black male or a red male and a
white male. This setup allowed us to determine the overall
effect of the different colors in both male-male interactions
and female-male interactions, as both types of encounters
involve gestures of the red third leg pair (Wearing et al.
2014). Such three-way interactions may be common in
nature, as S. barbipes occurs in dense populations where
two males have been observed simultaneously courting a
female (pers. obs., Jasmin Laura Gerfen).
Males in each pair were randomly assigned either the
treatment (white or black) or the control (red). Male spiders
in the same experimental trial were matched as closely as
possible in mass, body length, and days post maturation. In
2022, the treatment (white or black) was randomly selected
for each trial with a 50% chance of either treatment being
selected. In 2023, the treatment was again randomly selected
for each trial, but this time, with a 90% chance of the treatment being black. We made this adjustment in 2023 because
of the low mating rate in 2022 and the especially low sample
size of black trials in which mating occurred (9 black trials versus 14 white trials). If the difference in mating rate
between treatments were to persist in 2023 (which it did
not), this should have yielded a similar total number of trials
with mating in the black and white trial groups.
Experiments were conducted in a circular arena 24 cm in
diameter with walls made of 0.75 mm thick polycarbonate
sheeting wrapped in undyed brown paper to provide a single
consistent background color. The inside of the polycarbonate cylinder was sealed with glass coating (Clean Glas, Brip
International Inc., Las Vegas, USA) to make the walls slippery and difficult to climb, and a line of Vaseline was applied
at a height of five centimeters to further discourage climbing
and to prevent spiders from leaving the arena. The granite
floor was sealed with transparent varnish so that it could be
cleaned between trials without the cleaning solution soaking
into the porous rock. Between each trial, the polycarbonate
wall and sealed granite were washed with isopropyl alcohol
to remove silk and pheromones left from previous trials. The
entire arena was set in a cardboard box lined with acoustic
foam to dampen any noise from adjacent rooms. In an otherwise darkened room, the experimental arena was lit from

to the female at the beginning of the trial (to her left or right
side) was randomized. The three spiders were allowed to
interact for 1 h. If spiders were copulating after the hour
was up, they were allowed to finish; otherwise, spiders were
separated and the trial ended. All trials were filmed from
above using one of three cameras, depending on availability
(iPhone SE 2020 (Apple Inc., Cupertino, USA), Xperia XZ
(Sony Inc., Tokyo, Japan), or BRIO 4K Ultra HD (Logitech

Each female was run in only a single trial, but some males
were re-used once. To minimize any learning effects, e.g.,
a winner or loser effect (Mesterton-Gibbons et al. 2016), or
any effects of having mated recently, such males were only
paired with males that had had similar experiences in previous trials. This meant that spiders were matched by the outcome of the experiment (mating or no mating). If mating had
taken place in a male’s first trial, and he had been the one to
mate, he was paired with another male who had also mated
in his first trial. Conversely, if a male had not mated (but his
rival had) in his first trial, he was paired with another male
who also had not mated (but whose rival had). Males were
also paired with the same color of opponent in both trials.
Before spiders were reused, their paint was checked under
a microscope the day before running. If some of the paint
had chipped away, the spider was touched up with additional
paint. Males had at least one day between trials, and at most
19 days. The median time between trials was 4 days (first
quartile: 3 days, second quartile: 7 days). As far as was possible, we attempted to minimize the discrepancy between
males in the number of days since their last experimental

Results
In total, 79 usable mating trials were conducted, 51 in the
black group (of which 20 mated) and 28 in the white group
(of which 15 mated). The overall mating rate by females
was 44%. Two black trials were excluded because one of the
males escaped the arena before mating occurred. In another
black trial, a male who had previously mated three days previously was accidentally paired with a naïve male; however,
no winner effect seems to have influenced the result as the
male who mated was the naïve one. Spiders showed no difficulty in moving their painted third leg pair, and we observed
no case of cannibalism. The two males were generally very
active in moving about the arena and attempting to court the
female. There was a lot of variation in the relative amounts
of time allocated to different combinations of two- and threeway interactions between spiders. In some trials, males spent
relatively more time competing directly for the female in
three-way interactions, whereas in other trials, males mostly
courted the female individually while the other male was
wandering about the arena, presumably unaware of the
female’s whereabouts and/or avoiding direct confrontation
with the other male. Although males commonly postured
at and chased each other, physical attacks were rare. While
being courted, females alternated between facing the male,
turning and moving away, and turning to the side and raising
the abdomen, advancing towards the male as she did this.
Unlike in Maratus volans (Girard et al. 2015), there was no
obvious correspondence between female abdomen-raising
behavior and males’ mating success, as females raised their
abdomens to males they did and did not ultimately mate with
(e.g., Suppl. Videos 1–2). Most females were courted by
both males at least once before they allowed one of the males
to mount and copulate with them. The proportion of trials in
which mating occurred did not significantly differ between
the white and black groups (two-tailed Fisher exact test, p
= 0.24). In trials in which mating occurred, given a choice
between white and red (trial color: white), the probability
that the female mated with the red male was 80% (i.e., in 12
of 15 trials), which was significantly different from the null
hypothesis expectation of 50% (two-tailed binomial exact
test, p = 0.035; Fig. 2). When given the choice between
black and red (trial color: black), the probability that the
female mated with the red male was 50% (i.e., in 10 of 20
trials), which was identical to the null expectation of 50%
(two-tailed binomial exact test, p = 1.0; Fig. 2)). Although

Discussion
The results of our study suggest that red coloration confers
no mating advantage over black in male S. barbipes. Thus,
previous physiological measurements were probably correct
in concluding that these spiders do not possess a mechanism for discriminating red from black. This conclusion is
strengthened by the naturalistic design of our experiment,
in which spiders were asked to make decisions similar to
those they make in nature while interacting against a naturalistic background. That said, it is impossible to completely
exclude the possibility that the spiders could, in fact, discriminate between red and black color patches, but simply
had no preference for one over the other.
An advantage of our study’s design was that it allowed for
coloration to play a role in both inter- and intra-sexual encounters, enabling us to test for effects of both inter- and intrasexual selection simultaneously (albeit without being able to
distinguish between the two). Three-way encounters like those
in our experiment may be common in the spiders’ natural
habitat, as the spiders occur in high densities, and one such

Page 7 of 10 5

encounter has been observed in nature (pers. comm., Jasmin
Laura Gerfen). Even if such encounters are uncommon, however, the arena was large enough, and females choosy enough,
that each male generally had the opportunity to court the
female individually while the other male explored other parts
of the arena, apparently unaware of the whereabouts of the
other two spiders. Most females took long enough to accept
a male that they were courted by both males, and there were
plenty of opportunities for both two-way and three-way interactions between the three spiders before copulation occurred.
Another strength of our study was the care we took in
matching spider and habitat colors to paint and experimental
arena colors. Inspired by the approach of Taylor and McGraw
(2013), which used black liquid eyeliner to occlude Habronattus pyrrithrix’s red patches on the face, we measured the
spectral reflectance of a large number of liquid eyeliners,
but their spectral shapes of reflectance were all markedly
different from those of the spiders’ red and white coloration.
In the end, we found iron oxides and chalk to be ideal pigments, not only because their spectral shapes matched those
of the spiders, but also because, when mixed with an acrylic
binding solution, they produced paints that were similar in
consistency and dried with a matte appearance, exhibiting
little to no specular reflection, similar to the spiders’ natural
coloration. As iron oxide pigments come in a variety of different long-wavelength colors, as well as black, they may be
useful to other researchers interested in manipulating longwavelength animal colors. Chalk powder may also be useful
to other researchers, as its spectral shape closely mimics the
slow drop-off in reflectance at short wavelengths characteristic of many biological whites (Laczi et al. 2022; Mäthger
et al. 2008; Santos et al. 2007). This is in contrast to pigments more commonly used in commercial paints, such as
titanium dioxide and zinc oxide, which absorb strongly in
the ultraviolet, and barium sulfate, which reflects ultraviolet and visible light equally well (Grum and Luckey 1968;
Kühn 2012; Laver 2012). The matches between the granite
floor and calcite rock and between the brown paper wall and
leaf litter were also quite close. Matching the visual properties of the arena to those of the natural habitat is uncommon
in behavioral experiments, despite the strong effect this has
on the color and achromatic contrast of an animal with its
background. Some studies, for example, have used unnatural
white or transparent walls through which the varied furniture
and equipment of the surrounding laboratory can be seen
(Lietzenmayer et al. 2019; Taylor et al. 2014; Tedore and
Johnsen 2012; Vickers and Taylor 2020). Other studies have
displayed naturalistic backgrounds using computer screens
without adjusting the colors for animal viewers (Uetz et al.
2011, 2024); thus, although they look naturalistic to human
eyes, they almost certainly do not look naturalistic to animal
eyes (Tedore and Johnsen 2017). These poor matches could
have an effect on the outcome of experiments. A white wall

5

Page 8 of 10

could, for example, enhance the achromatic contrast of a dark
pigment against its background, making it appear more conspicuous than it would in a natural environment. A white wall
could also raise the adaptation level of the eye to a brighterthan-normal scene, making dark colors more difficult to discriminate from black or from each other (Davis et al. 2022).
Fortunately, in recent years, more studies are considering the
effect of visual background and designing naturalistic arenas;
Cook et al. (2021), for example, surrounded their transparent
experimental arena with sand and leaf litter from the natural microhabitat of their study subjects, the jumping spiders
Habronattus pyrrithrix and Phidippus californicus.
Although many male jumping spiders, like S. barbipes, display red coloration to females during courtship dances, it is
unclear how many species are actually able to discriminate red
from black or dark green. Electrophysiological, microspectrophotometric, and genetic studies have typically not found strong
evidence for red photoreceptors in jumping spiders (Blest et al.
1981; De Voe 1975; Glenszczyk et al. 2022; Nagata et al.
2012), with the exception of Habronattus pyrrithrix (Zurek
et al. 2015). This species appears to use a red filter over a small
patch of green photoreceptors to enable them to see red as a
distinct color from black or green (Zurek et al. 2015). This
discrimination seems to be possible only when the spiders are
illuminated by very bright light (Taylor and McGraw 2013),
however, which makes sense given the tiny range of wavelengths over which the green photoreceptor’s sensitivity and
the red filter’s transmission overlap. An electrophysiological
study also found preliminary (due to small sample size) evidence for red photoreceptors in Menemerus confusus (Yamashita and Tateda 1976). Other behavioral studies have provided
only equivocal evidence for red photoreceptors in jumping
spiders. For example, Phidippus regius, previously reported
from intracellular recordings to possess only UV and green
photoreceptors (De Voe 1975), was recently shown to be able
to successfully capture prey under red light (Raška 2023). However, the emission spectra of the red lights used overlapped with
the long-wavelength tail of their green photoreceptor. Rather
than evidence of them having a red photoreceptor, this instead
could be interpreted as showing that this spider can catch prey
under dim illumination. In the peacock spider Maratus volans,
whose spectral sensitivities are unknown, males have red elements in the colorful abdomen that they raise over the head
when displaying to females, but it is unclear whether females
attend to these elements, as they are equally receptive to males
when most of the light reflected by these elements is blocked by
a filter in front of the illuminant, so long as the overall intensity
of the illuminant is increased (Girard et al. 2018). This suggests
that Maratus, like Saitis, may have evolved to use red coloration to generate achromatic, rather than chromatic, contrast.
Nakamura and Yamashita (2000) showed that when Hasarius
adansoni are trained to avoid red when given a choice between
red and white, these spiders generalize from this training to

The Science of Nature (2025) 112:5

also avoid red when given a choice between red and black. This
suggests that these spiders use chromatic cues to discriminate
red from black, although it is possible they instead learned to
avoid a specific brightness rather than a color. Indeed, Tedore
and Johnsen (2016) found that, when presented with a range
of different gray printed rectangles, the jumping spider Lyssomanes viridis prefers to perch on one specific shade of gray,
indicating adeptness at brightness discrimination. It would be
interesting and more conclusive to test whether H. adansoni
or S. barbipes can be trained to discriminate red from not only
black, but saturated dark greens of varying brightness.
In conclusion, our study provides behavioral corroboration
of previous physiological measurements suggesting that these
spiders cannot discriminate red from black. It leaves open the
question as to why these spiders have evolved both red and
black ornaments rather than only black ones. Future work
should attempt to train S. barbipes to discriminate red from
black and saturated dark greens of varying brightness, as well
as explore the thermoregulatory hypothesis outlined in the
Introduction, i.e., that red coloration reflects heat better than
black does. If black cuticle and hairs do indeed absorb more
heat than red cuticle and hairs, it would be interesting to follow
up by examining whether males’ red coloration varies with
climate over their geographic range (Glenszczyk et al. 2022).
