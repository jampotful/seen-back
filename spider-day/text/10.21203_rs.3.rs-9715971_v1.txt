Abstract
Most sit and wait predators use camouflage to avoid detection by their prey. However, lynx spiders have
bright green body colouration and inhabit highly contrasting flowers suggesting that they are visible to
approaching insects. One way to resolve this paradox is by evaluating the appearance of the spider from
the perspective of insects and using their flight patterns as a proxy for their ability to detect these
predators. We tested the response of free flying insects such as hoverflies to the presence of lynx
spiders on yellow and white flowers under natural conditions. We used psychophysical colour modelling
to simulate the appearance of the spider and the flower background as seen by hoverflies. We recorded
the approaches of hoverflies in high speed and reconstructed their flight trajectories in 3D. Both the
flower and the spider absorb UV, effectively making the spider undetectable in the UV range which
contributes to their overall crypsis. We show that the detection of spiders by hoverflies is highly
dependent on the colour of the flower and the distance from which they are perceived. Insects were able
to detect the spiders only at a very close range, performing evasive manoeuvres at distances as close as
5 cm, suggesting that the colour perception and visual acuity of the hoverflies along with flight control
drives predator detection.

Introduction
Spiders insert themselves into the everyday movement of insects in various ways. Orb web spiders
intercept foraging insects that are en route to their destination (Craig, 1986; Rao et al., 2008; Eberhard,
2020), whereas crab spiders (Family Thomisidae) wait for pollinating insects to land on flowers (Morse,
1986). To make a successful attack on unwary prey, spiders, like other predators, need to avoid
detection. Predators conceal themselves from their prey using strategies such as background matching,
where the form and colour of the predator blends into the background environment, or disruptive
colouration, where the patterns and colouration are ambiguous to the prey such that the prey is unable to
recognise the presence of the predator (Ruxton et al., 2004; Smith and Ruxton, 2020).
For prey that are intent on their task, such as finding a suitable flower for nectar or pollen, detecting a
predator may be an added cost to their evaluation of the scene (Ings and Chittka, 2008). Insects are at
their most vulnerable when they are about to land within reach of a hitherto invisible predator. By
exploiting a multimillion year old relationship between flowers and insects (Peña-Kairath et al., 2023),
spiders can gain a fairly reliable source of nutrition without expending the energy required for active
pursuit. Detecting a predator during landing depends on the prey’s visual system (Cronin et al., 2014); i.e.,
the insect’s visual acuity, (a measure of the resolving power of its eyes), its spectral sensitivity (the ability
to discriminate different colours) and finally temporal resolution (the time taken to see details in the
complex scene unfurling below).
Some crab spiders (Araneae: Thomisidae) can slowly adjust their body colouration to match the flower
they are on to appear undetectable not only to potential prey but also to their predators (Théry and
Casas, 1993; Anderson and Dodson, 2015; Rodríguez-Gironés and Maldonado, 2020). For other crab
Page 2/17

spiders such as Mecaphesa sp., a mottled appearance may be sufficient to avoid detection (RodríguezMorales et al., 2021). However, green lynx spiders (Peucetia viridans; Oxyopidae) are often found perched
on highly contrasting (to human eyes) yellow and white flowers awaiting their prey. These spiders are
also sit and wait predators and are generally larger than the crab spiders. They are found on trichomedense plants but are also seen on the flowers of several species that are visited by pollinating insects
(Neto et al., 2007).
There are studies suggesting that certain conspicuous colours in spiders may attract pollinators,
particularly in the ultraviolet (UV) spectrum where the colours of the spider may be miscategorised as a
nectar guide by approaching pollinators (Heiling et al., 2003; Heiling et al., 2005; Vieira et al., 2017).
Though the green lynx spider on a white flower may appear conspicuous to human eyes, it is necessary
to evaluate this scene from the perspective of the target insect itself (Cronin et al., 2014; Caves et al.,
2018). In contrast to the blue, green, and red trichromatic vision of humans, some insects such as bees
possess UV, blue and green photoreceptors that allow them to perceive the same spider on a flower
differently (Briscoe and Chittka, 2001). A previous intent at quantifying the colouration of this spider did
not consider the UV component nor the visual acuity of potential observers (Rodríguez et al., 2022).
Due to the logistical complexity of carrying out these interaction experiments under natural conditions,
studies have often opted for a simplified model, such as with trained prey and simulated predation (Ings
and Chittka, 2008; Ings et al., 2012). Bumblebees that approached flowers with previous experience of
simulated predation respond to the presence of cryptic spiders by slowing down their inspection flights
in comparison to flights approaching conspicuous spiders, suggesting that the benefit of crypsis for the
spiders may be rendered ineffective by bees’ ability to control their flight as well as learning (Ings and
Chittka, 2008). Bees were also shown to specifically use shape cues to inform their decision to avoid or
land on flowers (Ings et al., 2012). In a field experiment with cryptic crab spiders, pollinating dune wasps
were shown to adapt their approach both in terms of speed as well as the shape of the trajectory in
response to presence of the spider and especially when the flower itself was in movement (RodríguezMorales et al., 2021). These studies emphasise the importance of inspection time in the ability of the
insect to avoid a predator (Chittka et al., 2009).
Here, we studied the response of flying flower seeking insects to the presence of a colourful predator in
natural conditions. We used the green lynx spider as the predator and hoverflies as the prey. By
combining psychophysical visual modelling of the appearance of the spider on the flower with highspeed filming and 3D reconstruction of flight trajectories, we asked the following questions.
1. 1. Are green lynx spiders conspicuous from the perspective of their potential prey?
2. 2. Does the flower colour (i.e., background) influence predator detection?
We expect that insects reduce the speed of their trajectories when approach to flowers, and the decision
to avoid or land on the flowers depends on the evaluation of the scene at short distances (Ings and
Chittka, 2008; Rodríguez-Morales et al., 2021).

Material and methods
Study species
The study was conducted through February-July 2023 in the Instituto de Biotecnología y Ecología
Aplicada, Universidad Veracruzana. We collected six adult female green lynx spiders (Araneae:
Oxyopidae; Peucetia viridans) from cloud forest areas surrounding the institute. P. viridans is a sit and
wait predator often found on flowers with contrasting colours, such as yellow, orange, white or violet
(Arango et al., 2000; Neto et al., 2007; Jiménez-Salinas and Corcuera., 2008; Rodríguez et al., 2022). In
our field site, the spider was found perched on yellow and white daisies (Asteraceae: Bellis sp; pers. obs.,
Fig. 1). Before filming, the spiders were housed in plastic containers (15 x 15 x 10 cm) and were fed
twice weekly with small crickets (Acheta domesticus) with water provided ad libitum. After to the
experiments, the spiders were released near their original collection sites.
Interactions between insects and flowers were often brief, making it difficult to capture individuals
without disrupting their natural behaviour. We therefore focused on the most common group of floral
visitors, the hoverflies (Syrphidae), which were identified to the family level. We were unable to
distinguish male and female hoverflies in the field. Although sexual dimorphism in eye structure in
Eristalis tenax has been recorded (Straw et al., 2006), this difference has been attributed to the detection
of conspecifics during sexual behaviour rather than for foraging.
Visual modelling
We used digital multispectral photography (Stevens et al., 2007) to simulate how hoverflies might
perceive the spider on the flower. We photographed five P. viridans females positioned at the center of
the two flowers (white and yellow) with natural sunlight as a light source. We used a Nikon D7100
camera converted to full spectrum (LifePixel.com), with a UV-VIS transmitting 105 mm Jenoptik Macro
lens attached. We placed the camera ~ 1 m facing downwards above the flower with the still spider and
took two photos for each spider and treatment: a photo corresponding to the visible spectrum (~ 400–
700 nm) using a Baader UV/IR-Cut/L filter (420–685 nm); and a photo for the UV spectrum (~ 320–400
nm) using a Baader U filter. All images included a scale bar and two reflectance standards of 70% and
10% (Zenith PTFE). We saved the photos in RAW format and processed them using the Multispectral
Image Calibration and Analysis (MICA) toolbox (Troscianko and Stevens, 2015) in ImageJ (v 1.54j) to
obtain multispectral files containing reflectance values for the spiders and the flowers. The spectral
sensitivity for this combination of camera, lens, and filters was available in the toolbox. From the
multispectral images, we identified two regions of interest (ROI) comprising: 1. The cephalothorax and
the abdomen of the spider, and 2. The flower (excluding the spider). We converted each multispectral file
to a quantum catch visual model for the hoverflies, as detailed below.
We used Eristalis tenax (Syrphidae) as a model since there is little information available on visual
systems in other species. The ommatidium of E. tenax contains multiple photoreceptors split up into two
subsystems: A) 6 photoreceptors (R1-6) for achromatic vision and motion detection, and B) 4
Page 4/17

photoreceptors for colour vision (R7 and R8), comprised of 4 spectral classes (R7p, R7y, R8p, and R8y)
with ommatidia housing either R7p/R8p tandem or a R7y/R8y tandem (Hannah et al., 2019). The spectral
sensitivity peaks are as follows: R7p 330nm, R7y 340nm, R8p 460nm, and R8y 540nm (Horridge et al.,
1975), with sensitivity curves reconstructed from data available (Lunau, 2014).
First, we created false colour images to have a representation of the spiders as ‘perceived’ through the
hoverfly visual system. The colours yellow, red, blue, and green were assigned to the photoreceptors R7p,
R7y, R8p, and R8y, respectively. Using these false colour images, we simulated the view from the
perspective of a hoverfly at different distances (5, 10, 15 cm) using the MICA toolbox for ImageJ
(Troscianko and Stevens, 2015; Caves et al., 2018) and the visual acuity of 1.8 cpd for E. tenax (Straw et
al., 2006; Feller et al., 2021).
For this putative visual system, and based on Troje's (1993) categorical visual space model for the
tetravariant visual system of flies, we estimated quantum catch values for the ROIs in the photoreceptors
R7p, R7y, R8p, and R8y, and converted them to the colour-stimulus loci (X, Y) for each ROI. We then
projected the colour stimuli onto the fly colour space, using the equations for visualisation available in
pavo package for R (Maia et al. 2013).
We calculated the chromatic contrast (ΔS) between the spiders and the flowers in which they were
photographed using the Euclidean distance. Additionally, we calculated achromatic (luminance) contrast
(ΔSL) using the quantum catch values for the R1-6 photoreceptors, as the ratio of the sum of the spider
and flower values to the difference between the two ROIs (Fleishman et al., 2009). Positive values of this
ratio indicate that spiders might be perceived as brighter than flowers. In contrast, negative values
indicate that spiders might be perceived as darker according to the dipteran visual system.
Finally, to compare the perceived chromatic and achromatic contrast of the spider in the flowers, we
conducted an independent t-test after normality and homogeneity tests. Additionally, we estimated
Cohen's d as a measure of effect size in the comparison between groups (White and Kemp, 2016).
Flight trajectories
We filmed hoverflies approaching Bellis sp. flowers using two synchronised high-speed cameras with 50
mm Zeiss lenses attached and recording at 500 fps (Chronos 1.4, Krontech.com). The cameras were
positioned ~ 1.5 m in front of the flowers (yellow or white) at an angle of approximately 45º from each
other, with a focal flower always centred. Cameras were synchronised with a cable and were calibrated
with a 2 cm square checkerboard. We placed each spider on the top of the focal flower. We waited five
minutes to allow the spider to adapt to the flower. We recorded hoverflies approaching the focal flower
and observed whether they avoided or landed on the flower, or whether there were any attacks. In total,
we filmed 27 hoverfly flights (yellow: 12; white: 15). Furthermore, we used yellow and white flowers
without a spider (control, white: 5; yellow: 3) and recorded each landing on a flower.

We digitised the position of the head and abdomen of each hoverfly in the paired videos, as well as the
position of the cephalothorax and the abdomen of the spider, using the application DLTdv8a (8.2.10;
Hedrick, 2008) in MATLAB (Ver. 2024a, Mathworks Inc.). The x,y coordinates thus obtained were
converted into stereo-calibrated 3D coordinates using the pose3d application in MATLAB (Sheshadri et
al., 2020). For the analysis, we only considered the trajectory up to the first approach to the flower.
We focussed on two variables (speed and persistence velocity) that contribute substantially to the flight
dynamics of the insects and may serve as proxies for insect detection of predators. We calculated the
distance to the spider and speed of the hoverfly at each frame. We fitted a linear regression to the data
and compared the slopes of hoverflies approaching a spider occupied flower and a control flower. The
data were smoothed with a moving average method to minimize autocorrelation effects.
To identify possible changes in the flight trajectory, we calculated the persistence velocity of the insect
at each frame. Persistence velocity is a measure of the propensity of the insect to continue along its
path (Gurarie et al., 2009). It combines the speed of the insect at every given frame with the angle of its
movement. We calculated the orthogonal components of velocity by converting x,y,z coordinates of
insect positions from cartesian coordinates to spherical coordinates for the angle calculations and then
converted them back to obtain the persistence velocity values using the following equation (Qureshi et
al., 2023):
Pi = vi sin(Φi) cos(Θi)
Where v is the instantaneous velocity at each frame
Φ is the change in horizontal angle
Θ is the change in vertical angle
Persistence velocity values can be positive (moving in the same direction), negative (moving in the
opposite direction) or zero (stationary or, in this case, hovering). We analysed persistence velocity values
obtained for hoverflies approaching a white or yellow flower and analysed them with respect to the
distance from the flower using a Smooth Density histogram to generate a heat map. We also compared
time spent in inspection by hoverflies across the treatments with a Mann Whitney test. All trajectory
analyses were carried out in Wolfram Mathematica ver 14.3. (Wolfram Research Inc 2024).

Results
Image Analysis
The multispectral false colour images incorporated both UV and visible (i.e., to hoverflies) parts of the
spectra. They show the spider against the flower from the perspective of the hoverfly visual system
(Fig. 1A). The visual acuity simulation shows that spiders are indistinct from distances of 15 cm and
become discernible at close distances (Fig. 1B).
Page 6/17

The chromatic and luminance contrast of the spider differed significantly between flowers (Fig. 2A, B).
Spiders on white flowers showed higher chromatic contrast (t = 6.828; df = 8; p < 0.0005), with a large
effect size (Cohen's d = 4.58), indicating that flower colour has a strong influence on spider contrast by
hoverflies chromatic perception. Similarly, the luminance contrast was significantly higher in spiders on
white flowers than on yellow flowers (t = -11.7803; df = 8; p < 0.0001; Cohen's d = -7.451) (Fig. 2B).
Trajectories
We documented only one instance of successful attack (Supplementary video S1); all other hoverflies
approached flowers with spiders and turned away (see Fig. 3 for sample trajectories). There was no
significant difference in the closest distance between hoverflies approaching yellow or white flowers
(Mann-Whitney Test; Mean ± S.D = 4.5 ± 2.22 (yellow), Mean ± S.D = 7.3 ± 6.46 (white), Test statistic = 76,
p = 0.48). Hoverflies spent a longer time inspecting the spider on a white flower than a yellow flower, but
this difference was not significantly different (Mean ± SD seconds; White flower = 0.53 ± 0.33, Yellow
flower = 0.36 ± 0.23; Mann-Whitney test statistic = 59, p = 0.125). Hoverflies reduced their speed as they
approached the spider occupied flowers (Fig. 3C,F).
Hoverflies approached yellow and white flowers differently (Linear Regression: yellow flower: R2 = 0.39,
Estimate = 5.83 ± 0.15 SD, t-statistic = 39.49, p < 0.001; white flower: R2 = 0.52, Estimate = 6.97 ± 0.12 SD,
t-statistic = 56.28, p < 0.001, Fig. 4); the reduction in speed was significantly related to their distance from
the spider, but hoverflies decreased their speed towards white flowers (Slope = 6.9) at a steeper slope
than to yellow flowers (Slope = 5.8).
There was no significant difference in the slopes of speed of hoverflies approaching a control yellow
flower and a yellow flower with a spider (Control flower slope = 5.56, Spider flower slope = 5.83; T test; t
= 1.40, p = 0.16). However, hoverflies approaching white flowers had a significantly different rate of
descent (Control flower slope = 3.18, Spider flower slope = 6.97; T test; t = 27.08, p < 0.001).
When approaching yellow flowers, hoverflies modified their flight at 6–8 cm distance from the flower
(Fig. 5A). The persistence velocity analysis showed two peaks for hoverflies approaching white flowers,
a smaller one at around 12 cm distance to the spider and a bigger one at 5 cm distance (Fig. 5B). Both
peaks occur with persistence values at zero suggesting that the fly was hovering or carrying out an
inspection of the flowers.

Discussion
According to visual modelling, green lynx spiders themselves might be hard to detect for hoverfly visual
systems, as indicated by the proximity of the spider loci to the achromatic centre (neutral stimulation) in
the fly colour space (X, Y 0,0). The detection depends on the colour and luminance contrast between the
spider and the flower. The decision to avoid the flower or land on it is thus informed by controlling their
flight to slow down and evaluate the scene (see supplementary video S1).
Page 7/17

The chromatic and achromatic contrasts between the spider and the background were higher in the
white flower compared to the yellow flower in the visual systems of hoverflies, suggesting that spiders
could be less detectable on yellow flowers. Hence, foraging on white flowers suggests that the spider
might have a higher chance of being detected by hoverflies. P. viridans has also been shown to have
considerable colour variation, but do not change colour according to their background (Vickers et al.,
2025). In an analysis of (human visible) colour in P. viridans, the variation found was not related to
background colour but rather to small areas on the abdomen (Rodríguez et al., 2022) which is unlikely to
play a significant role in detection by pollinating insects (see Fig. 1B).
Hoverflies reduced their speed as they approached the target flowers. The relation between speed and
distance has been considered a critical parameter that drives many visual tasks such as recognition and
avoidance of obstacles, initiation of landing and gap estimation (Breugel and Dickinson, 2012; Baird and
Dacke, 2016; Baird 2020). We found that hoverflies have a less steep slope in speed reduction when they
fly towards spider occupied white flowers, suggesting that the possibly are detecting the spider earlier
on white flowers, because of high chromatic and achromatic contrast. Bees use achromatic cues while
approaching a target and then switch to chromatic cues at a very close distance (Giurfa et al., 1996).
Something similar may occur in hoverflies as well. Further indication that the distance at which the
insects evaluated the scene is seen in the persistence velocity results, where we show a definite change
in flight parameters for the two flowers. Spiders on white flowers triggered a change in instantaneous
speed as well as the propensity to continue in the same angle at an earlier point compared to yellow
flowers (Fig. 5B). Since we found no difference in the slopes of the speed of descent of hoverflies
approaching yellow flowers with or without spiders, we can infer that hoverflies are less discriminating
when they approach yellow flowers. This may be because hoverflies have a pronounced innate
preference for yellow flowers (An et al., 2018; Matoušková et al., 2023).
We recorded only one instance where a spider caught a hoverfly (Supplementary video S1). This attack
was instructive in that the spider jumped at the insect when it was hovering less than a centimetre away
from the flower. This distance was substantially smaller than the average in our study (4.7–5.3 cm). The
spider's large spines on the forelegs touched one of the wings, which caused the hoverfly to drop and be
captured. Contrary to the predation strategy of crab spiders, where they wait for the insect to land on the
flower (Heiling et al., 2005, Anderson et al., 2015), lynx spiders seem capable of capturing insects in midair. If this pattern of predation is typical, then it implies that the effective attack reach of the lynx spider
extends beyond the confines of the flower itself. Future work should reveal whether lynx spiders can
detect approaching prey through airborne vibrations (similar to other groups such as jumping spiders
(Shamble et al., 2016) and orient themselves accordingly even before the insects get close to striking
range.
In this study, we explored the interaction between flying insects approaching a concealed predator. While
we focussed on the visual system, insects use a wide variety of senses for predator detection including
odour, vibrations and perhaps even the static electricity field (England and Robert, 2022). Future studies
could consider evaluating the multimodal integration of sensory cues in predator-prey interactions.
Page 8/17

Nevertheless, the visual system probably operates at a greater range compared to the other modalities
and may be the primary source of information about possible dangers, since colour perception and
visual acuity in insects is greatly enhanced by ultrafast photomechanical photoreceptor movements
especially in conjunction with flight (Juusola et al., 2025). Our study is one of the few to use natural
experiments with untethered spiders and free flying insects, which while providing more understanding
of the types of interactions that can occur also brings with it significant logistical challenges especially
with respect to variation in insect flights. It is necessary to evaluate visual detection from the perspective
of other flying insects, such as bees, wasps and bumblebees, across different coloured substrates, given
that lynx spiders are known to perch on orange and violet flowers as well as on green leaves (Arango et
al., 2000; Neto et al., 2007; Rodríguez et al., 2022). Focussing on biologically relevant flight parameters
can help in pinpointing the diverse factors that influence predator detection and avoidance.
