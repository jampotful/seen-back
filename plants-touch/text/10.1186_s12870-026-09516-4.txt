Functional characterization of Acyl-CoA Synthase 1 in tomato branch angle development.

# Functional characterization of Acyl-CoA Synthase 1 in tomato branch angle development

## Abstract
Branch angle is a crucial determinant of plant architecture, which is an important agronomic trait that contributes greatly to plant yield. In this study, we reported that SlACOS1 (Acyl-CoA synthase1) was related to regulate the branch angle in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum). Knockout of the SlACOS1 gene through CRISPR/Cas9 editing system resulted in a reduced lateral branch angle. Histological analysis revealed that editing SlACOS1 stimulated asymmetric growth of the branch base caused by the curve growth of vascular cylinder, parenchymatous tissue incrassation, cell elongation and expansion in abaxial side (AB) of the branch. Further analyses revealed that SlACOS1 responds to auxin and influenced its asymmetric distribution, leading to higher auxin concentration in the AB sides of the branch and subsequent differential growth of the two branch sides. In addition, SlACOS1 interacted with two lignin biosynthesis genes SlCCR5 and 6 to regulate lignin content in the branch. The Slacos1 mutants bent upward more rapidly than WT in the stem gravitropism assay. However, the amyloplast sedimentation rate of statolith cells showed no significant difference between Slacos1 and WT, suggesting SlACOS1 functions independently of gravity. In conclusion, the SlACOS1 contributed to asymmetric growth of branch to regulate the branch angle by modulating the asymmetric distribution of auxin and biosynthesis of lignin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09516-4.

## Background
Branch (or tiller) angle, defined as the angle between the main culm and its side branch, is one of the most important agricultural traits [1, 2]. Branch angle is determined by genetic and phytohormonal pathways, and also modulated by environmental factors such as light, gravity and planting density [3–6]. In Arabidopsis, LAZY1 is the first identified gene that regulates the branch angle and promotes upward shoot orientations [7]. lazy1 (la1) mutant exhibits wide-angled branch phenotype due to disrupted shoot gravitropism and altered auxin transport [7]. TAC1 and LAZY1 both belong to IGT gene family that regulate lateral shoot orientation in plants [8]. TAC1 promotes outward orientations via establishing wide branch angle in response to light. Meanwhile, TAC1 and LAZY1 show opposing functions in regulating lateral branch angle [9]. In rice (Oryza sativa), OsTAC1 is isolated by map-based cloning and participates in the regulation of rice tiller angle [10]. The Ostac1 mutant displays a smaller tiller angle, resulting in a more compact plant architecture that contributes to rice yield substantially and is broadly used in the breeding of Asian cultivated rice [10, 11].
Phytohormones act as plant growth regulators that modulate the size of branch angle, especially auxin, brassinosteroid, cytokinin and strigolactone [3]. Asymmetrical auxin distribution within plant organs explains the mechanism of tropic growth of branch [12, 13]. Auxin transport has been thought to be a central component of branch angle signaling [7]. Loss of HSFA2D function enhances polar auxin transport (PAT) and impairs lateral auxin transport, resulting in tiller-spreading phenotype [12]. Suppressing of rice PIN1 (PIN-FORMED1) or over-expression of rice PIN2 (PIN-FORMED2), two auxin efflux transporters, both alter PAT and increase tiller angles [14]. Additionally, the maize LAZY1 protein can directly bound to the promoter of a nuclear-localized Aux/IAA auxin signaling repressor gene ZmIAA17 to regulate lateral organ angle [15]. Strigolactone (SL) acts in regulating branch angle indirectly by suppressing auxin biosynthesis [16]. Brassinosteroid (BR) is also involved in the regulation of both tiller and leaf angle. The rice dwarf61 (d61) mutant indicates the role of BR signaling: d61 exhibits erect leaves owing to the impaired development of the lamina joint [17]. And rice Leaf and Tiller Angle Increased Controller (LAX) gene regulates tiller and leaf angle through BR signaling [18].
There is a growing body of evidence that vertically oriented branch architectures have been shown to enhance crop productivity in horticultural crops, as observed in cereal crops. For the fruit tree apple (Malus domestica), a trait called columnar or Co, which has narrow branch angles and higher apical dominance, has been bred for integration into high-density orchard systems [19]. The Co locus has been shown to contain a Ty3-type transposable element that is not inserted within a known gene but may act, in part, through altered expression of a neighboring 2-ODD gene [20]. Peach (Prunus persica) trees carrying the “Pillar” or “Broomy” trait have erectly oriented branches and narrower canopy caused by loss-of-function mutations in TAC1 gene [21]. In rapeseed (Brassica napus), genome-wide association mapping (GWAS) has revealed genetic control of branch angle, with positional variation in branch angles evaluated across individual plants [22]. Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is one of the most important vegetable crops cultivated worldwide and a commonly used model species for plant biology [23]. However, little was known about the tomato lateral branch angle.
In current study, a tomato Acyl-CoA synthase 1 gene, SlACOS1, was identified. ACOS, encoding a fatty Acyl-CoA synthetase, belongs to a supergene family 4-coumarate: CoA ligase (4CL) [24, 25]. Our study suggested that editing SlACOS1 using CRISPR/Cas9 system resulted in a phenotype with a smaller branch angle in tomato line PI 114,490. Histological analysis revealed that the curve branch was caused by the cell elongation and expansion on the abaxial side (AB) of the branch base. Further analysis demonstrated that the curved branch was caused by the asymmetric auxin distribution. Besides, SlACOS1 was involved in regulating lignin content by interacting with two lignin biosynthesis genes, SlCCR5 and 6, supporting the branch bending. In a word, our result provided novel insights into the function of SlACOS1 in regulating branch angle and underlying mechanisms of this physiology process.

## Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum var. cerasiforme accession PI 114490) was used as the plant material for all experiments in this study. All plant materials were transplanted into the greenhouse 45 days after sowing with consistent water and fertilizer management.

## Generation of mutant plants
The CRISPR/Cas9-editing system [26] was used to generate mutants of Slacos1 in tomato. Target sites (Single-guide RNAs, sgRNA) for the SlACOS1 gene were designed using the released tomato reference genome (SL 2.5) from CRISPR-P 2.0 (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/; Fig. 1A). The sgRNA fragments inserted into a recombinant CRISPR/Cas9 vector pCBC-DT1T2 with tomato U6 promotor according to the methods of the previous description [27]. The tomato genetic transformation was performed using the previous methods [28].

## Histological analysis
Longitudinal and transverse sections of lateral branches were observed via paraffin sectioning. FAA solution and 0.1% Toluidine Blue O were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co. (Beijing, China). Experimental procedures were performed referring to Xie et al. [29] and reagent instructions with some modifications.

## Amyloplast sedimentation analysis
For amyloplast sedimentation analysis, a Periodic Acid Schiff Stain kit (Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd) was used according to the manufacturer’s directions. Briefly, tomato plants were reoriented by 180° for 10, 20, 30 and 40 min, and lateral branches were cut into 0.5 cm fragments and fixed with FAA solution. Samples were embedded in paraffin and cut into 10 μm sections using the method described as above. Then, cross-sections were oxidized for 10 min in 0.5% periodic acid in 0.3% nitric acid, rinsed in running water for 1–2 min with a final rinse in distilled water, stained for 15 min in Schiff’s reagent, washed three times in 0.5% sodium metabisulfite for 2 min each, rinsed for 5 min in running water and then transferred to distilled water, dehydrated with graded ethanol and sealed with neutral balsam. Stained sections were observed microscopically.

## Hormones treatment and measurement
To investigate the relationship between SlACOS1 and hormones, we tested the induction of SlACOS1 by IAA, CTK and BR. WT seedings were treated with individual phytohormones when plants have 5 fully expanded true leaves. Three separate plants groups were sprayed to leaves of the whole plant with only one phytohormone, 20 µM IAA (Indole-3-acetic acid), 50 µM 6-BA (Cytokinin) and 50 µM BR (Brassinosteroid). After treatment for 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h, three simple leaves at the same stem node were detached, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until further use. The experiments were repeated three times with a total of 9 tomato plants for each treatment.
To detect the phytohormones content of lateral branch, tomato plants at the nine-leaf stage were used. The 3rd, 4th and 5th lateral branches (numbered acropetally from the first true leaf) were excised from each plant. Based on previously description [30], we defined the half of upper/lower side of the branch named adaxial side (AD)/ abaxial side (AB; Fig. S1). The lateral branches were transversely and horizontally divided into two equal parts as AD and AB samples. Tissue from three individual plants were pooled together to form one biological sample, and three independent biological replicates were prepared. The extraction and quantification of IAA, 6-BA and BR were performed using the procedure as described previously [31, 32], and the experiments were performed by the Wang Baomin’s laboratory (Center of Crop Chemical Control, China Agricultural University, Beijing, China).

## Gene transcript analysis
All the analysis of gene expression was performed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) in the current study. Total plant RNA extraction and first-strand cDNA synthesis were performed using Quick RNA Isolation Kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China) and PrimerScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio Inc., Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. RT-qPCR was performed in 10 µl reactions containing 5 µl of SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio Inc.), 0.5 µl of 1.0 µM of each primer, 1 µl of cDNA templates and 3 µl of sterile distilled H2O, using the Applied Biosystems® QuantStudio™ 6 Flex real time-PCR machine (Bio-Rad, California, USA). DNA amplification was conducted using the following thermocycling program: 40 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 30 s. After amplification, the samples were maintained at 55 °C for 1 min, and the temperature was gradually increased by 0.5 °C every 10 s to perform the melting curve analysis. The tomato gene EF-1α (Solyc06g0050600) was used as the internal control [33]. And reactions for the reference genes were included in each plate. Each sample had three biological and technical replicates, and the gene relative expression was calculated via 2^-△△Ct method [34]. The standard deviation was calculated from three biological replicates. RT-qPCR primers (Table S1) for each gene were designed by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/).

## Gene interaction analysis
For yeast two-hybrid screening, SlACOS1-BD fragment was amplified by PCR using primers (Table S1). The SlACOS1-BD plasmid was used to screen the yeast library of tomato leaf in the pGADT7-AD vector. And the yeast strain AH109 was transformed via Takara Matchmaker yeast two-hybrid kit (Takara Bio Inc.) following official protocols.
For Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC), PCR products generated using primers SlACOS1-YN and SlCCR5/6-YC (Table S1) were connected to pSPYNE and pSPYCE vectors digested with Bam HI and Kpn I restrictions enzymes to generate SlACOS1-pSPYNE and SlCCR5/6-pSPYCE recombinant vectors, respectively. Tobacco leaf inoculation and microscopic observation were conducted following the method described by Xie et al. [29].

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., California, USA; http://www.graphpad.com/). A paired Student’s t-test was adopted for data significance testing using SPSS (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics). All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on no fewer than three biological replicates. Significant differences compared with the control group were marked as *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

## Results

## Mutation of SlACOS1 influences the branch angle in tomato
In order to obtain clues on the function of SlACOS1 in the development of tomato, the transcript accumulation of SlACOS1 gene in different tissues, including root, stem, axillary bud, young branch, mature branch, young leaf, mature leaf, flower, mature green fruit and ripening fruit were detected by quantitative RT-PCR. RT-qPCR results showed that SlACOS1 transcripts were present in all tissues tested and abundant in stem, axillary bud and young branch (Fig. S2). The highest transcripts levels were in axillary bud, where they were 8.2-fold higher than in the reference (root) tissue (Fig. S2). The expression parents of SlACOS1 in different tissue suggested that it maybe play roles in the branch growth.
To verify the function of SlACOS1 in tomato, CRISPR/Cas9 editing system was applied to knock out SlACOS1 in PI 114,490 (Fig. 1A). The phenotype of WT and Slacos1 mutants were showed in Fig. 1B and C. The lateral branch of Slacos1 lines were more erect and upward growth compared with WT. The angle between lateral branch and main stem was defined as the branch angle denoted by alpha (α, Fig. 1C). Statistical analysis of branch angle indicated that the average branch angle was 45° and 27°, respectively in WT and Slacos1. The Slacos1 branch angle was significantly (p < 0.01) smaller than that of WT (Fig. 1D). The results revealed that SlACOS1 gene played roles in the growth angle of lateral branch in tomato.

## SlACOS1 regulates the asymmetric growth of the branch
In order to investigate how SlACOS1 influenced the branch angle, we monitored the microstructure of branch using histologic sections. First of all, the magnified surface of the branch bases was observed by stereo-microscope. In WT, the branch was growth obliquely but straightly with a larger angle. Oppositely, the branch of Slacos1 was curve upwardly, resulting in a smaller angle (Fig. 2A). A vertical section was used to divide the branch bases into two to observe the interior tissue structure (Fig. 2A). As we can see, all the branch tissue structure, from outside to inside, including epidermis, cambium, phloem, xylem and pith grew in normal direction and parallel orient along the branch, and there was little macroscopic difference between the two sides of the WT branch (Fig. 2A). And the diameters of vascular cylinder composed by cambium, phloem, xylem and pith was 2.8 mm with almost the same length in every observed position (Fig. 2B). However, the Slacos1 branch tissue showed extremely difference with that of WT, where the vascular cylinder was upwardly curve with a changed thickness (Fig. 2A). And the smallest thickness of vascular cylinder was 1.6 mm which was significantly reduced compared to the 2.7 mm in WT (Fig. 2B). In Slacos1, the mean cell length, which was measured approximately in the most curve position about 3–5 mm from the branch–stem junction in the abaxial side (AB) was 280 μm and significantly (p < 0.01) longer than the adaxial side (AD,167 μm) and the AB (176 μm) of WT, while there was no difference (ns) between the AD (189 μm) and AB (176 μm) in WT (Fig. 2C and D).

In transverse sections, the AB side of the branch was elongated and thicker than AD, and the cell size was also bigger than AD in Slacos1 branch, whereas the WT branch had no evident difference in everywhere in the branch (Fig. 3A and D). Data statistics were found that the branch transverse semidiameter of the AB in Slacos1 (1.9 mm) was significantly (p < 0.05) bigger than AD (1.5 mm) with almost the same length with AD (1.7 mm) and AB (1.8 mm) in WT (Fig. 3B). The thickness of the parenchymatous tissue (from endodermis to phloem; signed in Fig. S1) in AB was 489 μm and significantly (p < 0.01) thicker than AD (351 μm) in Slacos1 or AB (364 μm) and AD (338 μm) in WT (Fig. 3F). After examining parenchymatous tissue cells number and size, we confirmed that this morphology phenotype was caused by the increase of cells size instead of number. Expectedly, comparison of the parenchymatous tissue cells number and size showed no significant difference between the AD and AB in WT (Fig. 3C and E). However, the Slacos1 cell size in AB (4376 µm2) was more than 4.3 times larger than AD (998 µm2), whereas the cells number remained unchanged (Fig. 3C and E). Those above results revealed that the curved branch in Slacos1 was caused by the cell elongation and expansion in AB side of the branch.

## SlACOS1 is involved in auxin signaling pathway
It has been reported that plant hormones such as auxin, cytokinin and brassinosteroid played a crucial important role in regulating branch angle [3]. Hence, we measured the content of IAA, CTK and BR in WT and Slacos1 branch. The results showed that all the three hormones content in the whole branch was significantly reduced in Slacos1, 20 ng/g for IAA, 1.3 ng/g for BR and 1.8 ng/g for CTK compared to 22.2 ng/g for IAA, 1.5 ng/g for BR and 2.3 ng/g for CTK in WT (Fig. 4A, B and C). In WT, the IAA content was higher in AD (23.08 ng/g) than AB (20.25 ng/g), while the content in AD decreased to 17.98 ng/g and was significantly lower than AD (21.29 ng/g) in Slacos1 (Fig. 4A). The BR and CTK content were significantly higher in AD than AB whatever in WT or Slacos1 with the same trend (Fig. 4B and C). Moreover, in AD samples, BR and CTK content in WT were also higher than in Slacos1 with no difference in AB (Fig. 4B and C). The results suggested that mutation of SlACOS1 disrupted the original content of hormones, especially auxin and resulted in a new asymmetric distribution in branch to regulate the asymmetric growth.

To further study the relationship between SlACOS1 and those hormones, we determined whether IAA, CTK and BR could induce the expression of SlACOS1. The WT plants were treated by exogenous IAA, CTK and BR hormones for 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h to detect the transcript levels of SlACOS1. The RT-qPCR results showed that SlACOS1 expressed significantly increased (2.4-fold) after only 0.5 h of IAA treatment compared to 0 h (Fig. 4D). The peak expression level was obtained after 2 h treatment, with approximately 6 times higher than control. Afterward, its expression gradually decreased with time extension (Fig. 4D). On the whole, the expression of SlACOS1 increased at first and then decreased after treatment by IAA. However, BR and CTK treatment only resulted in a slightly difference at 1, 4 and 8 h which meant that those two exogenous hormones cannot induced the expression of SlACOS1 (Fig. 4D). The results revealed that SlACOS1 only responded to IAA instead of BR and CTK.
Based on the above results of the altering auxin content and the sensitivity of SlACOS1 to auxin, we detected the expression of auxin biosynthesis, polar transport and response-related core genes to verify the effect of SlACOS1 to auxin. The RT-qPCR results showed that a number of IAA signaling pathway genes were suppressed in Slacos1 mutants. Among them, the core components of IAA biosynthesis SlFZYs genes, including SlFZY1, SlFZY2 and SlFZY4, were significantly suppressed in Slacos1 (Fig. 5A). The expression of several SlPINs (SlPIN3, SlPIN4 and SlPIN5), the key genes of polar auxin transport, were also strongly inhibited in Slacos1. Meanwhile, the expression of SlPIN1 was upregulated for more than 1.6 times compared to WT (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the IAA transcriptional regulator gene SlIAA20 was detected with significantly decreased expression in Slacos1. An auxin influx carrier-like gene SlLAX4 expressed with about 1.5-fold more abundant than WT with significantly downregulation of SlLAX2/3. What’s more, we also found the transcripts of an auxin response factor SlARF8 accumulated more than 2-fold lower than WT (Fig. 5C). Other genes (SlFZY5/6, SlPIN2/6/7, SlIAA15 and SlLAX5) detected by RT-qPCR did not present significant difference between WT and Slacos1 (Fig. 5A, B and C). The results showed that editing SlACOS1 influenced the expression of IAA-related genes, and the changes in gene expression partly explained the asymmetric auxin distribution.

## SlACOS1 is involved in lignin pathway by interacting with SlCCR5 and 6
In order to detect the signaling pathway that SlACOS1 participated in, SlACOS1-interacting proteins were screened by yeast two-hybrid cDNA library of tomato. Two SlCCR proteins encoded by Solyc03g097170 and Solyc08g076790 were identified to interact with SlACOS1 (Fig. 6A). CCR gene encoded cinnamoyl-CoA reductase which was the first committed enzyme of the lignin-specific branch in the phenylpropanoid metabolic network. Phylogenetic analysis showed that those two SlCCRs were highly homology with alfalfa (Medicago truncatula) MtCCR1-5/6 and formed a distinct clade (Fig. S3). According to the homologous MtCCR1-5 and 6, Solyc08g076790 and Solyc03g097170 were respectively named as SlCCR5 and SlCCR6 (Fig. S3). A bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) analysis was performed to further confirm the interaction between SlACOS1 and SlCCR5/6 in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaf cells. When SlACOS1-nYFP and SlCCR5/6-cYFP were transiently co-expressed, green fluorescence was observed in the plasma membrane, revealing the interaction of SlACOS1 and SlCCR5/6 in plants (Fig. 6B). It has been reported that CCRs were lignin synthase enzymes and controlled the lignin biosynthesis [35] and lignin regulated the branch angle [36]. Thus, we detected the lignin content in WT and Slacos1 to determine whether the alter branch angle in Slacos1 was related to lignin. The lignin content in Slacos1 branch was about 4.91 (OD/280 FW) which was significantly higher than 3.68 (OD/280 FW) in WT branch (Fig. 6D). Lignin staining revealed that vascular bundles and mechanical tissues in the internode of Slacos1 had darker red color and larger stained region than WT (Fig. 6C). It meant that Slacos1 had higher lignin content and was intensely lignified, whereas WT was relatively weakly lignified. Taken together, these results demonstrated that the change of Slacos1 branch angle was related to the increased content of lignin, and SlACOS1 regulated the lignin biosynthesis by interacting with two lignin synthase enzymes SlCCR5/6.

## SlACOS1 is involved in gravitropic response but not gravity perception
Previous studies have suggested that shoot gravitropism contributes greatly to the control of branch or tiller angle [4, 37]. To explore whether SlACOS1 was involved in the perception of gravity, we horizontally placed WT and Slacos1 plants to 90° inverted gravity processing to observe the degree of stem bending (Fig. 7A). After 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 12 and 24 h of inversion, the angle of the stem deviating from horizontal direction was recorded by camera and measured (Fig. 7A). The Slacos1 plants clearly bent upward slightly at 4 h, while no visible bending was observed in the WT plants. After 6 h treatment, a significant difference in bending angle was observed between the Slacos1 and WT plants. After 8 h of inversion, the bending angles reached maximum difference between WT (34°) and Slacos1 (54°). In addition, the bending angle differences in WT and Slacos1 were dying out gradually after 12 h (Fig. 7C). The results indicated that SlACOS1 may play a role in the response to gravitropism in tomato plants.

To confirm the role of SlACOS1 in gravitropism, Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was used to detect amyloplasts in statolith cells and examine the starch–statolith hypothesis in Slacos1 mutants [38]. Amyloplasts normally localize at the bottom of statoliths and sediment toward the morphological upper end when plants are inverted (Fig. 8A). We observed amyloplast sedimentation in the basal region of branches after whole-plant inversion. Each cell was divided into four equal parts (positions 0 to 3 from top to bottom), and amyloplast distribution was quantified at different time points. At 0 min, PAS-stained amyloplasts were exclusively located at position 0 in both WT and Slacos1. Under gravity stimulation, amyloplasts gradually sedimented toward position 3. At 10 min, the proportions of amyloplasts at positions 0–3 were 38.33%, 46.67%, 10.00%, and 5.00% in Slacos1, versus 48.33%, 31.67%, 13.33%, and 6.67% in WT, with no significant difference (Fig. 8B). Similarly, no significant differences were observed at 20, 30, or 40 min. These results indicate that SlACOS1 does not affect gravity perception or amyloplast sedimentation in tomato. Although Slacos1 plants bent more rapidly than WT in the gravitropism assay, it may be caused by the higher degree of lignification of to facilitate plants upward growth.

## Discussion
Branch angle shapes plant architecture into a loosen or dense structure, and thus influences the light interception and competition between neighboring plants in natural settings, ultimately contributing to the plant yield and productivity [2]. Due to its important contribution to agronomic trait, lots of genes controlling branch or tiller angle have been identified in different plants [11]. For example, Tiller Angle Control is one of major controlling genes, and loss-of-function in plants contribute to erect and compact growth. In Arabidopsis, tac1 mutants show significantly narrower lateral branch angles than the wild-type [39], and Ppetac1 mutants “br” also show small branch angle and have a more erect plant architecture in peach (Prunus persica) [40]. In this study, SlACOS1 gene was knockout by CRISPR/Cas9, and the Slacos1 mutant demonstrated an evident erect growth phenotype with small branch angle in tomato (Fig. 1). The Slacos1 phenotype of branch angle was consistent with those tac1 mutants in diverse plant species. However, Y2H assays showed no direct interaction relationship between SlACOS1 and SlTAC1 (Fig. S4). Those results indicated that SlACOS1 performed function in setting the orientation of lateral branches, but may be independent on TAC1.
Plant development is determined by a combination of three major cell biological processes: cell division (proliferation), differentiation and growth (expansion and elongation) [41]. A notable example is hypocotyl elongation and apical hook formation caused by cell elongation which is driven by asymmetric auxin distribution across the hypocotyl [42]. The rice Loose Plant Architecture 1 mutant lpa1 shows larger tiller angle and leaf angle due to faster and enhancer elongation on the adaxial side of the lamina joint [6]. The Slacos1 branch grew bending upward caused by the increased parenchymatous tissue elongation on the abaxial side (AB) of branch base with larger cell length (Fig. 2C and D). Moreover, scanning the transverse sections of Slacos1 branch base was found that the cell size was significantly larger on AB than AD and both sides of WT with little change in cell number (Fig. 3A, D and E). The cell expansion resulted in the unequal growth rapid on the two sides of Slacos1 branch and a more thickness semidiameter of AB than AD (Fig. 3), which contributed to the branch bent upward.
Almost all the process of plant growth and development are controlled by the plant hormones. In sweet oranges (Citrus sinensis), CsTAC1 regulates the branch angle by modulating the endogenous hormone content with lower auxin contents and increased cytokinin levels [43]. However, in this study, auxin, cytokinin and brassinosteroid contents were all reduced in the whole branch of Slacos1 mutant lines (Fig. 4A, B and C), disagreed with the results of Dutt et al. [43]. BR related mutants, such as dwarf61 (d61), Oslic, Osdwarf4–1 and m107 (a gain-of-function mutant in BR biosynthetic gene D11), showed altered tiller or leaf orientation by disrupting the BR biosynthesis or signaling [17, 18, 44, 45], which were partly similar to the upward growth of branch with the decrease of BR content in Slacos1 (Fig. 4B). Especially for auxin, the branch angle is regulated by not only the relative auxin content but also the asymmetric distribution. The rice lazy1 (la1) mutant, which shows wide-angled branch phenotype, reveals that LA1 gene acts on the polar auxin transport stream to redistribute auxin preferentially to the lower side of the shoot. Consistently, the unequal distribution of auxin was detected with an increase of relative content on AB of the branch than AD in Slacos1 compared with WT (Fig. 4A). It meant that knockout SlACOS1 altered the redistribution of auxin in branch, where it triggered growth in AB and in turn led to bending.
Auxin distribution patterns and polar transport are directly driven by different auxin transmembrane carriers, including the auxin influx carrier (AUX1)/LIKE AUX1 (LAX1) protein family [46], the auxin efflux-facilitating PIN-FORMED (PIN) family [47] and a number of the p-glycoprotein ABC transporters [48]. This progress is also involved in a trans-organ differential in the degradation of AUX/IAA proteins by the auxin receptor-E3 ubiquitin ligases, and subsequent gene expression changes promoted by ARF proteins such as NPH4/ARF7 [49, 50]. For instance, rice LA2 regulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle through the asymmetric expression of OsIAA20 and auxin redistribution [37]. In Slacos1, most SlPINs and SlAUXs were down-regulated, whereas other genes such as SlPIN1 and SlLAX4 showed higher expression (Fig. 5A-C). The results could partly interpret the redistribution of auxin in Slacos1 branch. These apparently “contradictory” gene expression patterns may be caused by conditional gene function, exactly as over-expression of OsPIN2 enhances auxin transport and leads to increased rice tiller angle [51], while the OsPIN1b RNAi mutant plants show a larger tiller angle [52]. Thus, disrupted expression of auxin biosynthetic and transport genes contributes to the exaggerated asymmetric auxin gradient in Slacos1, though the molecular intermediates connecting SlACOS1 acyl-CoA metabolism to these transcriptional shifts remain unclear, and only a correlative relationship can be established at present, which demands further research.
According to the Cholodny-Went hypothesis, gravity-induced asymmetric auxin distribution results in differential cell elongation and organ bending in higher plants [53]. A number of studies have been verified that the theory applied to the regulation mechanisms of tiller or branch angle. Gravitropism analysis showed that Slacos1 plant stems bent upward more quickly than WT guided by gravity (Fig. 7). Amyloplast sedimentation is believed to be a primary and symbolic event in gravity sensing and auxin redistribution [54]. However, the amyloplast sedimentation rate showed no significant difference between Slacos1 and WT in statolith cells (Fig. 8), indicating that gravity perception by amyloplast sedimentation is not affecte in Slacos1. But asymmetric auxin redistribution was still observed during the bending process (Fig. 7). These observations suggest that SlACOS1 does not function in the gravity perception or early auxin redistribution steps, but rather acts downstream in a process that might modulate the efficiency of the bending response.
Lignin is a major structural component of the cell walls that provides mechanical strength for the vascular systems thereby facilitating plants upward growth [55–58]. In bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon L.), the tiller angle and stem growth direction are changed by lignin in erect-growing lines [36]. We also found the lignin content of Slacos1 was higher than WT (Fig. 6C and D), which might support the erect growth of branch. Acetyl-CoA has been identified as the donor substrate to feruloyl-CoA monolignol transferases ρHBMTs that acylate monolignols [59], suggesting that acetyl-CoA biosynthesis may directly participate in the biosynthesis pathway of lignin. SlACOS1 could interact with two tomato cinnamoyl-CoA reductase genes, SlCCR5/6 confirmed by Y2H and BiFC (Fig. 6A and B), and the total lignin content was increased in Slacos1 branch (Fig. 6C and D). Furthermore, in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L. cv. Microtom), two CCR genes (LeCCR1 and 2), are identified and characterized to regulate lignin biosynthesis, and reduced LeCCR1 expression leads to growth defect, leaf morphological changes, and decreased lignin content [60]. In addition, the LeCCR1 greatly influences the growth of vascular bundles and the xylem ring with a partly similar function to SlACOS1 (Figs. 2 and 3). Thus, we speculated that the Slacos1 could influence the lignin biosynthesis to supply more lignin for the formation of secondary cell walls in the progress of cell elongation and expansion. Notably, the faster upward bending of Slacos1 is not purely mechanical nor a simple hyper-gravity response (Figs. 7 and 8). Auxin redistribution sets the direction and triggers differential cell elongation, while elevated lignin strengthens cell walls and reduces tissue resistance, thereby amplifying the bending magnitude induced by the same auxin gradient. Auxin might determine the growth asymmetry, whereas lignification modulates the final bending speed and angle. SlCCR5 and 6 were considered as the putative genes in the signaling pathway of SlACOS1 regulating branch angle. However, additional experiments need to be performed to prove this speculation.

## Conclusions
In this study, we identified and characterized a tomato Acetyl-CoA synthetase gene named SlACOS1 which was relative to regulate the branch angle. Editing SlACOS1 using CRSPR/Cas9 resulted in smaller branch angle in tomato line PI 114,490. Slacos1 mutants disrupted the asymmetrical auxin distribution to enhance the cell elongation and expansion and the asymmetric growth in the abaxial side (AB) of the branch. SlACOS1 interacted with two lignin biosynthesis gene SlCCR5/6 and altered the lignin content and the degree of lignification of the branch to support the branch bending. Taken together, SlACOS1 regulated branch angle by modulating the asymmetric distribution of auxin and lignin biosynthesis in tomato.
