Homoeologous exchanges contribute to branch angle variations in rapeseed: Insights from transcriptome, QTL-seq and gene functional analysis.
Sun C, Wu J, Zhou X, Fu S, Liu H, Xue Z, Wang X, Peng Q, Gao J, Chen F, Zhang W, Hu M, Fu T, Wang Y, Yi B, Zhang J. (2024). Plant biotechnology journal. . doi:10.1111/pbi.14292. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Homoeologous exchanges contribute to branch angle variations in rapeseed: Insights from transcriptome, QTL‐seq and gene functional analysis

## Abstract
SummaryBranch angle (BA) is a critical morphological trait that significantly influences planting density, light interception and ultimately yield in plants. Despite its importance, the regulatory mechanism governing BA in rapeseed remains poorly understood. In this study, we generated 109 transcriptome data sets for 37 rapeseed accessions with divergent BA phenotypes. Relative to adaxial branch segments, abaxial segments accumulated higher levels of auxin and exhibited lower expression of six TCP1 homologues and one GA20ox3. A co‐expression network analysis identified two modules highly correlated with BA. The modules contained homologues to known BA control genes, such as FUL, YUCCA6, TCP1 and SGR3. Notably, a homoeologous exchange (HE), occurring at the telomeres of A09, was prevalent in large BA accessions, while an A02‐C02 HE was common in small BA accessions. In their corresponding regions, these HEs explained the formation of hub gene hotspots in the two modules. QTL‐seq analysis confirmed that the presence of a large A07‐C06 HE (~8.1 Mb) was also associated with a small BA phenotype, and BnaA07.WRKY40.b within it was predicted as candidate gene. Overexpressing BnaA07.WRKY40.b in rapeseed increased BA by up to 20°, while RNAi‐ and CRISPR‐mediated mutants (BnaA07.WRKY40.b and BnaC06.WRKY40.b) exhibited decreased BA by up to 11.4°. BnaA07.WRKY40.b was exclusively localized to the nucleus and exhibited strong expression correlations with many genes related to gravitropism and plant architecture. Taken together, our study highlights the influence of HEs on rapeseed plant architecture and confirms the role of WRKY40 homologues as novel regulators of BA.Keywords: Rapeseed, Branch angle, Homoeologous exchange, WGCNA, QTL‐seq, WRKY40

## Introduction
Rapeseed (Brassica napus L., 2n = 38, AACC) is a crucial oilseed crop that comprises approximately 13%–16% of global vegetable oil production. As the fifth largest crop in China and the country's primary source of edible vegetable oil, continuous growth of rapeseed production is essential to support the growing population. To enhance rapeseed production, high‐density planting along with techniques such as mechanized harvest and controlled‐release fertilizer have been advocated (Hu et al., 2017). Gradually, growers have been being adopting these strategies, however, high‐density planting poses challenges due to shading by neighbouring plants. Branch angle (BA), which refers to the adaxial angle between the branch and the stem, plays a critical role in shaping plant canopy architecture. Plants with adequately small BAs are better suited for high‐density planting. At later growth stages, when siliques replace leaves as the main photosynthetic organs (Wang et al., 2014), a compact canopy architecture becomes even more critical, as it improves light capture under high‐density planting.
Regulation of BA primarily involves shoot gravitropism, which proceeds through four conserved steps: gravity perception, signal transduction, asymmetric auxin redistribution and organ bending growth (Strohm et al., 2012). To date, numerous genes that influence BA have been identified in model plants and rapeseed. For example, IGT family members LAZY1, TAC1 and LAZY4/DRO1 have been widely reported to regulate BA in both monocots and dicots (Dardick et al., 2013; Guseman et al., 2017; Yoshihara et al., 2013). In rapeseed, multiple genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) of BA also identified LAZY1 or TAC1 homologues as strong candidate genes (Li et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2016). In Arabidopsis, a series of sgr1–sgr9 mutants were defective in shoot gravity perception with aberrant endodermis, which led to attenuated shoot gravitropism and enlarged BA (Hashiguchi et al., 2013). Auxin content and auxin response have also been linked to BA regulation. Mutants with higher levels of auxin (yucca1‐1D) or a more sensitive auxin response (axr3, arf10, arf16 and axr3) exhibited vertical branches, whereas those with lower levels of auxin (taa1 and tar2) or attenuated auxin response (tir1, afb4 afb5, arf7 and arf19) had more horizontal branches (Roychoudhry et al., 2013). In addition to its noted role in fruit development, the MADS‐domain transcription factor (TF) FUL positively regulated BA by repressing expression of an early auxin‐responsive gene, SAUR10, in abaxial branches (Bemer et al., 2017). In rapeseed, overexpressing BnaYUC6a or disrupting the conserved motif of BnaA3.IAA7 interfered with the normal auxin‐related pathways and led to smaller BA phenotypes (Hao et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019). Also in rapeseed, CRISPR mutagenesis of BREVIPEDICELLUS (BP) copies caused a semi‐dwarf phenotype and vertical branch growth (Fan et al., 2021).
In allopolyploids, homoeologous chromosomes come together in a single genome. Thus, disomic inheritance occasionally fails in these species, leading to homoeologous exchanges (HEs) (Schiessl et al., 2019). HEs occur as a result of mispairing between ancestral chromosome segments that swap pieces of DNA and have been conclusively linked to phenotypic changes like flowering time, seed acid detergent lignin, glucosinolate content and flower colour in rapeseed and other species (Bertioli et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2018; He et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2012). These exchanges, which result in deletions, duplications, and translocation of harbouring genes, are widely present in natural and synthetic allopolyploids, such as rapeseed, wheat, strawberry and allotetraploid rice (Edger et al., 2019; Hurgobin et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Research showed that 30 out of 53 rapeseed accessions were observed with HE‐related genomic structural variations (Hurgobin et al., 2018), and analysis of the commonly used rapeseed reference genome Darmor‐bzh revealed the presence of 17 large HE events scattered throughout the genome, ranging in length from 70 to 860 kb (Chalhoub et al., 2014).
To investigate the regulatory mechanism of BA in rapeseed, we conducted a comprehensive analysis utilizing 115 data sets, including 109 RNA‐seq data sets for 37 rapeseed accessions with divergent BA phenotypes and six DNA re‐sequencing data sets for extreme accessions and bulks. We found multiple instances confirming that several HE events indeed cause changes in BA, utilizing a combination of analyses such as weight correlation network analysis (WGCNA) and QTL‐seq. Validation through overexpression, knockdown, and knockout approaches confirmed that BnaA07.WRKY40.b and BnaC06.WRKY40.b are functional genes underlying the impact of the A07‐C06 HE on BA. Overall, our study highlights the impact of HEs on BA differentiation in rapeseed and reveals the role of WRKY40 homologues as novel regulators of rapeseed BA.

## Results

## Auxin and transcriptome differences between adaxial and abaxial branch segments
The Cholodny‐Went hypothesis proposes that auxin accumulates on the lower side of gravi‐stimulated organ, resulting in differential growth rates that cause the organ to curve (Evans, 1991). To test this hypothesis in rapeseed, we selected 34 accessions with divergent BAs, ranging from 23.2° to 56.5°, and measured the auxin contents in both adaxial and abaxial branch segments (Figure 1a, Additional file 1: Figure S1, Additional file 2: Table S1). Despite the lack of a clear correlation between auxin levels and BA phenotype, abaxial segments generally accumulated higher levels of auxin compared with the adaxial segments, with an average difference of approximately 18.2% (Figure 1b). These results confirmed the asymmetrical distribution of auxin in rapeseed branches, which is essential to support the upward growth of branches.

To characterize the transcriptional differences associated with BA control, we conducted RNA‐seq analysis on the adaxial and abaxial segments of three accessions, X5, X77 and X80. Despite the large number of active genes expressed in the branches with transcripts per million (TPM) ≥ 0.5 (54 006), only 12, 13 and 9 were differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Of these DEGs, seven were consistently identified in all three accessions (Figure 1c, Additional file 2: Table S2). Six of the common DEGs encode the TCP family transcriptional activator, TCP1, a homologue of which controls tiller angle in wild rice (Zhang et al., 2019). These genes were moderately expressed in the adaxial segments, with an average TPM of 8.1, but were nearly silent in the abaxial segments (Figure 1d). The remaining common DEG, BnaC03G0557200ZS (BnaC03.GA20ox3), which encodes a gibberellin 20‐oxidase 3, was also up‐regulated in the adaxial segments (TPM: 13.9 vs. 2.8). Interestingly, despite the importance of auxin in shoot gravitropism, no DEGs related to auxin biosynthesis, transport, or signalling were identified. Taken together, these results suggested the potential role of TCP1 and GA20ox3 homologues in maintaining the angled growth of branches.

## Expression patterns of known BA control genes in branches
To analyse the expression pattern of known BA control genes in branches, we performed RNA‐seq analyses of the young branches of 17 accessions with small BAs (23.2–36.3°) and 20 accessions with large BAs (45.1–56.5°), generating 97 data sets with a total size of ~700 Gb (Additional file 1: Figure S2). These data revealed distinct expression patterns of BA control genes in branches. A total of 27 IGT family members have been identified in rapeseed (Sun et al., 2021). Among them, two LAZY1 homologues on chromosomes A03 and C03, as well as all TAC1 homologues, displayed relatively high expression levels in branches, which confirmed their critical role in BA regulation, especially the latter. However, in all accessions, most LAZY2–LAZY6 homologues exhibited low or no detectable expression (Figure 2a). A comprehensive set of 34 SGR series genes were identified in rapeseed genome. With the exception of individual copies of SGR7 and SGR9, the majority of SGR series genes exhibited expression in branches across a broad range of accessions, with particularly high levels of expression of several SGR3 and SGR4 homologues (Additional file 1: Figure S3). Similarly, the majority of the auxin pathway genes, including homologues of YUCCA6, ARF7, ARF10, ARF16, ARF19, IAA7, IAA17, TIR1, AFB4 and AFB5, were expressed in branches, with the four copies of IAA7 exhibiting particularly high expression levels (average TPM: 41–100). FUL has been reported to control the expression of the auxin‐responsive gene SAUR10 in abaxial branches, thereby influencing BA in Arabidopsis (Bemer et al., 2017 ). Out of the six FUL homologues in the rapeseed genome, five displayed relatively high expression levels in branches, with the exception being the C01 homologue (Figure 2b).

Correlation analysis of gene expression levels and the BA phenotype revealed that only a small subset of known BA control genes exhibited significant correlations (Additional file 2: Table S3). Notably, BnaA02G0391500ZS (BnaA02.FUL) was highly expressed in all small BA accessions with an average TPM of 38.7, which was nearly twofold higher than that in large BA accessions, indicating a strong negative correlation with the BA phenotype (Pearson correlation coefficient of −0.62, P = 6.3E‐12) (Figure 2c). In our earlier GWAS study, the chromosome segment containing BnaA02.FUL was not anchored to the chromosome (using the Darmor‐bzh reference genome); consequently, it was inadvertently overlooked in the analysis at that time (Sun et al., 2016). Upon referencing the ZS11 genome, we found that a significant locus associated with BA, Bn‐A02‐p25736307, was located within the genic region of BnaA02.FUL (Figure 2d, Additional file 2: Table S4), which further confirmed the pivotal role of BnaA02.FUL in the regulation of rapeseed BA. Furthermore, the expression levels of BnaC07.YUCCA6, BnaC01.SGR7, BnaC09.SGR9, BnaA02.SGR3 and BnaA03.ARF16 were also significantly correlated with the BA phenotype (Figure 2e, Additional file 1: Figure S4). On the other hand, a large number of genes, including LAZY1, TAC1, SGR1, SGR2, SGR4 and IAA7 homologues, showed no significant variation in expression level between small and large BA accessions, which suggested that modest changes in their expression levels might not lead to significant alterations in the BA phenotype.

## Two modules correlated with BA phenotypic variations
To investigate the transcriptional networks correlated with the BA phenotype, we conducted a correlation network analysis using the WGCNA package. This analysis clustered 34 461 genes into 55 modules, which were labelled with different colours (Figure 3a, Additional file 1: Figure S5, Additional file 2: Table S5). The module‐trait association analysis revealed that two modules, represented in darkred and darkolivegreen, exhibited the highest correlation with BA (|r| ≥ 0.68), and eigengenes of both modules displayed differential expression patterns between small and large BA accessions (Figure 3b,c). The hub gene networks of both modules were visualized in Cytoscape (Additional file 1: Figure S6). The darkred module, which comprised 237 genes, was enriched in 76 gene ontology (GO) terms, including GDP‐L‐fucose biosynthetic process, glucan biosynthetic process and cellulose biosynthetic process (Figure 3d, Additional file 2: Table S6). These terms were related to plant cell wall biosynthesis, membrane trafficking, protein signal transduction, etc. The darkolivegreen module consisted of 147 genes and was enriched in 17 GO terms, including 3′‐tRNA processing endoribonuclease activity, intracellular membrane‐bounded organelle, cytosolic large ribosomal subunit (Figure 3e, Additional file 2: Table S7).

A considerable number of BA‐related pathway genes were assigned to the darkred and darkolivegreen modules, including the aforementioned genes BnaA02.FUL, BnaC07.YUCCA6 and BnaA02.SGR3. Moreover, BnaA02G0165200ZS (BnaA02.TCP1) was also assigned to the darkred module and was expressed at moderately higher levels in small compared to large BA accessions (TPM 2.1 vs. 1.4) (Figure 3f). Another darkred module member BnaA01G0135700ZS (BnaA01.ARF9) was homologous to the Arabidopsis auxin signalling pathway gene ARF9, which has a reported role in stem gravitropic response mechanisms (Roberts et al., 2007). The darkolivegreen module gene, BnaA02G0215800ZS (BnaA02.WAT1), encodes a vacuolar auxin transport facilitator that is required for secondary wall formation and cell elongation, and thus plant height and shoot branching. It exhibited significantly higher expression in small than large BA accessions (TPM 98.7 vs 52.4) (Figure 3g). Furthermore, both modules included homologues of other known gravitropism‐ and plant architecture‐related genes, such as phyE, ROP6, COL12, RCN1, IAA11 and IAA13. As expected, a large number of known BA control gene homologues, such as LAZY1, TAC1, SGR1, SGR4 and IAA7, were assigned to modules with minimal correlations to BA, and different homologues of these genes were often categorized into distinct modules.

## HEs explained the formation of hub gene hotspots
When analysing the distribution of darkred module genes, we observed an interesting phenomenon. More than 40% of the darkred module genes were located on chromosome A09, while the other chromosomes had 1–16 genes (Additional file 2: Table S8). Furthermore, two adjacent module gene hotspots that included all hub genes were identified at the telomeric regions of A09. The first spanned 408 kb (64.2–64.6 Mb) and consisted of 47 module genes, while the second spanned 484 kb (65.4–65.8 Mb) and consisted of 48 module genes. Notably, genes within these two hotspots displayed opposite expression patterns. Relative to small BA accessions, genes within the first hotspot exhibited markedly lower expression levels in most large BA accessions, whereas expression of genes within the second hotspot were nearly twice as high (Figure 4a,b).

To investigate the underlying causes of this phenomenon, we re‐sequenced a representative large BA accession, X23 (56.5°), and a small BA accession, X80 (23.2°), with 30‐fold depth of coverage (Figure 4c). In comparison to X80, X23 had very few reads that aligned within the first hotspot, but had approximately two‐fold that number of reads aligned within the second (Figure 4d). Chromosomal synteny analysis revealed that a large region containing the first hotspot was homologous to the adjacent region containing the second hotspot, as well as the telomeric regions of A10 and C05 (Figure 4e). Accordingly, these results confirmed that an HE rearrangement substituted the first darkred hotspot with the second in X23 and other large BA accessions, which led to a spurious correlation between BA and these hotspot genes (Figure 4f, Additional file 2: Table S4). In our previous GWAS study, a significant locus associated with BA, Bn‐A09‐p36803825, was identified within the first darkred hotspot, which was one of the top loci in both the mixed linear model (MLM) and general linear model (GLM) (Figure 4d, Additional file 2: Table S4) (Sun et al., 2016). This result further confirmed that this HE was indeed associated with the observed BA phenotypic variations. Similarly, we found that the two hub gene hotspots of the darkolivegreen module on A02 (13.24–13.40 Mb) and C02 (26.80–27.09 Mb) were created by a C02 to A02 HE rearrangement in the majority of small BA accessions (Figure 4g, Additional file 2: Table S9).

## QTL‐seq analysis identified a large HE affecting BA
QTL‐seq analysis provided further evidence of HE's impact on the BA phenotype. Two rapeseed accessions with divergent BA phenotypes, X38 (29.6°) and X73 (52.4°), were crossed to generate an F2 population comprising 654 individuals, of which 40 individuals from each extremity were sampled to generate the small and large pools (Figure 5a,b). Both parents and two pools were re‐sequenced with 30× and 120× depth, respectively, which generated 307.6 Gb of 150 bp paired‐end reads. A total of 1.40 million high‐quality variations, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and InDels, were called between the two parents (Figure 5c).

When using a significance threshold of 95%, the ∆(SNP‐index) analysis identified four adjacent quantitative trait loci (QTLs), namely BA.C06‐1–BA.C06‐4, located on the long arm of C06 (Figure 5d, Additional file 1: Figure S7, Additional file 2: Table S10). Interestingly, a remarkable HE was detected in X38, where a 14.6 Mb region at the terminal end of C06 was replaced with an 8.1 Mb homologous segment derived from A07 (Figure 5e). This rearrangement was also observed in the small BA pool and encompassed the two BA QTLs, BA.C06‐3 and BA.C06‐4, confirming that these QTLs and thus the narrow BA phenotype could be attributed to the A07‐C06 HE (Figure 5f). Compared with the previous two HEs, the A07‐C06 HE was relatively rare, occurring only in three out of the 17 small BA accessions (X12, X38 and X65), and X65 had the least BA values among all accessions (23.2°).

## WRKY40 homologues positively regulated BA in rapeseed
To elucidate the underlying functional genes determining BA in the QTLs, we analysed the annotated genes located within their confidence intervals. No known BA control genes were found near the peak of BA.C06‐2 at 35.6 Mb, nor near the peak of BA.C06‐4 at 52.4 Mb. Interestingly, we observed that one copy of the WRKY family member WRKY40, BnaC06G0246700ZS (BnaC06.WRKY40.a), was located at the peak of BA.C06‐2, and another copy of WRKY40, BnaC06G0453200ZS (BnaC06.WRKY40.b), was also situated within the confidence interval of BA.C06‐4 (Additional file 1: Figure S8). Several prior studies indicated that WRKY40 is expressed in response to simulated gravity stress in Arabidopsis (Soh et al., 2011, 2012). The X38 allele of BnaC06.WRKY40.a harboured four SNPs in its exons, resulting in three amino acid transitions (M55I, E114D and D214E) (Additional file 1: Figure S9). Due to the HE rearrangement in X38, BnaC06.WRKY40.b was substituted by its counterpart BnaA07G0385400ZS on A07 (BnaA07.WRKY40.b). The X38 allele of BnaA07.WRKY40.b had six SNPs in its exons, resulting in two amino acid transitions, one of which occurred in its critical WRKY DNA‐binding domain (S180A) (Figure 6a).

To investigate the putative role of WRKY40 homologues in BA control, overexpression (OE) rapeseed plants were developed by introducing the X73 allele of BnaA07.WRKY40.b under the control of a CaMV35S promoter. The qRT‐PCR analysis showed that BnaA07.WRKY40.b was overexpressed in the transgenic lines, exhibiting a 2‐ to 4‐fold increase in expression compared to the wild‐type (Figure 6b). Notably, the OE lines showed an obviously loose plant architecture, with a remarkable increase of up to 20° in BA values (Figure 6c, Additional file 1: Figure S10). This phenotype remained stable across the T3 and T4 generations (Figure 6d, Additional file 1: Figure S11). Notably, the OE lines did not exhibit significant changes in other agronomic traits, including other aspects of plant architecture, yield and seed quality traits, which suggested the specific role of BnaA07.WRKY40.b in regulating rapeseed BA (Additional file 2: Table S11).
We then generated homozygous CRISPR‐mediated knockout mutants (CRI‐1 and CRI‐2) possessing two editing types in both BnaA07.WRKY40.b and BnaC06.WRKY40.b (Figure 6e,f). CRI‐1 carried a 1‐bp insertion at both targets 1 and 2 of BnaA07.WRKY40.b, and an 8‐bp deletion at target 1 of BnaC06.WRKY40.b. CRI‐2 carried a 1‐bp insertion at target 1 of BnaA07.WRKY40.b, an 8‐bp deletion at target 1 and a 1‐bp insertion at target 2 of BnaC06.WRKY40.b. These mutations led to frameshifts in both genes, thereby disrupting the structure of their WRKY DNA‐binding domains. Additionally, we developed two RNAi‐mediated knockdown mutants (RNAi‐2 and RNAi‐9), which had 40% to 60% lower expression of both genes (Figure 6g). Both types of mutants exhibited a more compact architecture, with knockout mutants showing a more significant reduction in BA, by up to 11.4° (Figure 6h,i). Additionally, they had a similar number of branches as the wild‐type (Additional file 1: Figure S12). The subcellular localization experiment revealed that BnaA07.WRKY40.b localizes exclusively to the nucleus of Arabidopsis protoplasts, which is consistent with its role as a TF (Figure 6j). Taken together, our study provided the first evidence of the critical role played by WRKY40 in regulating BA in plants.

## WRKY40 homologues showed strong correlations with BA‐related genes
A total of six WRKY40 homologues were identified in rapeseed (Additional file 2: Table S12). In the WGCNA analysis, two low‐expressing WRKY40 homologues were filtered out. BnaA07.WRKY40.b, BnaC06.WRKY40.b and two other homologues were assigned to the yellow module, and three of them were identified as hub genes.
The strong expression correlation observed among the four homologues (average edge weight: 0.35) indicates coordinated expression, which suggests their potential to form protein complexes that could regulate downstream gene expression (Additional file 1: Figure S13, Additional file 2: Table S13). Although the yellow module was weakly correlated with BA, expression of a considerable number of its genes were strongly correlated with that of the four WRKY40 homologues. These genes, including APD5, IAA1, IAA19, GH3.5, SAUR60, NAC036, BIR1, BAK1, ADR1‐L1, XTH22, WRKY18, WRKY33 and WRKY46 homologues, are related to gravitropism, plant architecture and cell wall biosynthesis (Additional file 2: Table S7). Motif analysis showed that 59.4% (1172) of the yellow module genes contain at least one W box sequence in their promoters (Additional file 2: Table S14), indicating the WRKY40 protein complexes could regulate their expression, thereby affecting BA.

## Discussion
On Earth, gravity is a constant stimulus governing a plant's growth and orientation of its organs. Different from the vertical growth of the primary stem, which can be explained by a classical auxin‐dependant plant gravitropic response, lateral branches of higher plants are maintained at specific angles under long‐term gravitational stimulation. There must be additional mechanisms involved in regulating this process; however, they had yet to be clearly elucidated. Roychoudhry et al. (2013) proposed an antigravitropic offset mechanism in the adaxial stem segments that operates antagonistically with gravitropic responses in the abaxial segments to generate angled growth. In this study, by comparing the transcriptome difference between both sides of branches in three accessions, we identified seven common DEGs, including six TCP1 and one GA20ox3 homologues, which collectively exhibited significantly higher expression in the adaxial branches. In rice, the TCP1 homologue, TIG1, is primarily expressed on the adaxial side of the tiller base, which promotes cell elongation and enlarges the tiller angle (Zhang et al., 2019). Further studies revealed that TCP1 modulates the expression of a key brassinosteroid (BR) biosynthetic gene, DWARF4, thereby regulating BR biosynthesis in Arabidopsis (Guo et al., 2010). GA20ox3 encodes a gibberellin 20‐oxidase that functions in later gibberellic acid (GA) biosynthetic stages (Plackett et al., 2012). Since GA and BR are known to promote cell elongation, the TCP1 and GA20ox3 homologues could participate in the antigravitropic offset mechanism by modulating adaxial cell elongation to prevent branch over‐bending. Further research can be conducted to verify their role in this plausible mechanism.
WGCNA has proven to be superior in identifying functionally associated gene pairs by examining pairwise gene expression in many species (Du et al., 2017; Feng et al., 2021; Niu et al., 2020). Based on the 97 branch transcriptome profiles, we established a gene co‐expression network and found that the darkred and darkolivegreen modules had the highest correlation with BA. After removing genes related to HE, a considerable number of genes from both modules showed functional relevance to BA and shoot gravitropism, including homologues of FUL, YUCCA6, TCP1, SGR3, phyE, ARF9, ROP6, COL12, IAA11 and IAA13. Particularly noteworthy was the homologue of the well‐known MADS‐domain TF FUL, BnaA02.FUL, which had significant association signals with BA in its the genic region and displayed a very strong expression correlation with the BA phenotype (R = 0.63). Furthermore, a ChIP‐Seq experiment in Arabidopsis identified 939 potential FUL target genes, and two darkolivegreen and five darkred module genes were homologous to some of these FUL targets (Additional file 2: Table S8 and S9). BnaC03G0122900ZS is homologous to the Arabidopsis gene SEF, which exhibits a pleiotropic phenotype characterized by shortening inflorescence internodes and a bushy canopy in loss of function mutants (March‐Díaz et al., 2006). BnaA05G0010000ZS is homologous to the Arabidopsis gene PIL1, which encodes a novel Myc‐related bHLH transcription factor that positively regulates plant photomorphogenesis (Luo et al., 2014). These genes could function downstream of BnaA02.FUL to affect plant architecture and BA. Apart from the aforementioned genes, the majority of genes within both modules, as well as those from the grey (|r| ≥ 0.57), tan (|r| ≥ 0.36) and darkorange2 (|r| ≥ 0.35) modules, which include various types of TFs, gravitropic response genes, auxin pathway genes, cell wall biosynthesis enzymes and transmembrane protein kinase, have not been previously reported to be associated with BA. These genes can be valuable candidates to explore for BA control in future studies.
Contrary to our initial expectations, the expression levels of certain BA genes, such as homologues of LAZY1, TAC1, SGR1–SGR2, SGR4, SGR6, SGR8 and IAA7, showed no significant variation between small and large BA accessions. Additionally, there was no significant correlation in expression, observed either within these genes or among different homologous copies. For example, BnaA03.LAZY1 and BnaC03.LAZY1 have been identified as strong candidates in multiple GWAS studies of BA (Li et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2016). However, in the present study, they both exhibited similar expression in small and large BA accessions (P > 0.14), and were assigned to different modules. Similarly, a previous transcriptome comparison between two rapeseed accessions with distinct BA phenotypes also did not detect these genes as DEGs (Cheng et al., 2017). A recent study in Arabidopsis revealed that overexpressing the wild‐type allele of TAC1 and LAZY1 did not result in major phenotypic variation (Hollender et al., 2020). Taken together, these results suggest that LAZY1, TAC1, IAA7 and SGR series homologues lack involvement in BA control, at least at the transcriptional level. Besides, unless essential mutations occur in their conserved domains, mild variations in the expression level of these genes are unlikely to cause large phenotypic changes in rapeseed.
WRKY proteins comprise a large family of TFs that play important roles in many physiological processes and environmental (Liu et al., 2018; Rushton et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2022). Several WRKY members are implicated in the regulation of plant gravitropism. In the inflorescence gravity persistent signal experiment, significant changes in expression were observed for WRKY18, WRKY26 and WRKY33 (Cook et al., 2015). The previous studies of WRKY40 mainly focused on its roles in biotic and abiotic stress responses (Abeysinghe et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2010; Schweizer et al., 2013). However, in this study, we observed, for the first time, that WRKY40 plays a positive role in regulating plant BA. Overexpression of BnaA07.WRKY40.b in rapeseed led to an increase in BA by up to 20°, whereas RNAi‐ and CRISPR‐mediated mutants (BnaA07.wrky40.b BnaC06.wrky40.b) exhibited decreases in BA of up to 11.4°. Considering that the rapeseed genome contains six copies of WRKY40, and at least four of them exhibited coordinated expression with each other, breeders could selectively knock out one to six copies to obtain plants with the desired level of compactness in plant architecture. The WGCNA results indicated strong expression correlations between four WRKY40 homologues, along with genes related to gravitropism, plant architecture and cell wall biosynthesis. Future studies can refer to this information to find downstream targets that function in BA control.
HE is a specific type of genomic structural variation that has been linked to important agronomic traits in polyploid crops (Schiessl et al., 2019). For example, an A02‐C02 HE event replaced the flowering gene, BnFLC.C2, with its counterpart on A02, BnFLC.A2, generating an early‐flowering phenotype in rapeseed (Chen et al., 2018). In tobacco, HE caused the substitution of low‐expressing SAR8.2 genes with high‐expressing homologues, resulting in enhanced resistance to soil‐borne pathogens (Shi et al., 2022). In this study, we present evidence that HEs contribute to phenotypic variations in BA through the analysis of WGCNA and QTL‐seq results. We observed that the hotspot regions of darkred module genes, generated by a ~400 kb HE in the telomeric regions of A09, were present in most large BA accessions but absent in all small BA accessions (Chi‐square test, 9.8E‐7). Similarly, the first hotspot region of the darkolivegreen module on A02 was substituted by the second on C02 through HE (Chi‐square test, 1.6E‐3). Additionally, QTL‐seq analysis revealed that a large A07‐C06 HE generated two contiguous BA QTLs with long confidence intervals that resulted in a more upright branch phenotype. Through gene functional analysis, we verified that BnaA07.WRKY40.b was the key gene contributing to the phenotypic variations in BA following the occurrence of the A07‐C06 HE. However, considering that this HE affected over one third of the A07 and C06 genes, causing extensive copy number variations, allele substitutions, and subsequent dosage changes, we cannot exclude the possibility that other genes may also play a role in this process. On the other hand, the A09 and A02‐C02 HE regions were absent of any known BA regulatory genes. However, genes such as ARL1, GIF2, FKGP and IQD31 homologues exhibit functional associations with the BA pathway and could serve as valuable candidates. Further works are needed to pinpoint the specific functional genes within these regions.
Although HEs play a vital role in generating genomic diversity in polyploids, they also pose challenges for genomic studies. Current genotyping approaches based on next‐generation sequencing platforms, like RNA‐seq, ATAC‐seq, ChIP‐seq and Hi‐C, require the alignment of short reads to the reference genome to obtain their physical positions. However, if the reference genome has undergone several HE events, reads from the duplicated regions could be misclassified as multi‐mapped and filtered from further analysis, and reads from the deleted regions could be flagged as unmapped. This could result in false‐negative mapping results and hinder further analysis in these HE‐related regions. If the accessions being analysed contain HEs, the duplicated regions could gain an average of one‐fold more aligned reads, leading to an overestimation of transcription, chromatin accessibility, histone modification and chromatin interaction levels. Conversely, the deleted regions would be short of reads, causing an underestimation of these signals, which is what we encountered in this research. Without awareness of the impacts of HE, we may have mistakenly concluded that all genes from the HE‐derived regions showed actual differential expression levels between the small and large BA accessions. Hence, future genotyping analysis in allopolyploids should be carried out with caution when interpreting any biased patterns between homologous regions before eliminating the effect of HEs as a possible cause.

## Conclusion
Taken together, in this study, we generated large‐scale transcriptome data of rapeseed accessions with varying BA phenotypes to analyse the expression pattern and regulatory networks of BA control genes. First, we observed that six TCP1 and one GA20ox3 homologues exhibited higher expression in the adaxial segments. Second, our results emphasized the indispensable contribution of HEs in influencing BA variations in rapeseed. Third, we verified the role of WRKY40 homologues as novel regulators of rapeseed BA.

## Materials and methods

## Plant materials and tissue collection
A collection of accessions with varying BA values was gathered and cultivated on the experimental farm. At the BBCH 59 growth stage (bolting period), representative plants were selected from the middle of each plot, and the top three branches, each measuring between 5 and 10 cm in length, were excised and collected in a pooled sample. The fourth and fifth branches were longitudinally sectioned, and the adaxial and abaxial portions were pooled separately. All the samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use.

## Measurement of free IAA content
About 150‐mg adaxial and abaxial branch samples were extracted twice with cold methanol (80% [v/v]) by sharking overnight at 4 °C with two biological replicates. Indole‐3‐acetic‐2,2‐d2 acid (IAA; Sigma‐Aldrich) were added to each sample as an internal standard. The quantification of IAA was performed on an ABI 4000 Q‐Trap system (Applied Biosystems) according to a method described previously (Liu et al., 2012).

## RNA‐seq and WGCNA analyses
Total RNA was isolated from samples using an RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, DP441). Libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Kit, following the manufacturer's recommendations. Paired‐end 150‐bp sequencing was performed for 37 accessions on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten system (Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China), and the majority of them (91.9%) had at least two biological replicates (Additional file 2: Table S15). The adaxial and abaxial segment mRNA sequencing were performed for three accesions (X5, X77 and X80) with two replicates.
Trimmomatic was employed to remove adaptors and trimming low‐quality bases (Bolger et al., 2014). The clean reads were aligned against the B. napus var. ZS11 genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2019; Song et al., 2020). Only uniquely mapped reads were retained for further analysis. Raw counts for each rapeseed gene were calculated using featureCounts and were then normalized to TPM values (Liao et al., 2014). Correlation analysis between biological replicates and DEG analysis was performed using DESeq2 in R (Love et al., 2014). GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the OmicShare webtool (http://www.omicshare.com/tools).
Co‐expression networks were constructed using the WGCNA package in R (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). Genes with very low expression (averaged TPM <1) or a low coefficient of variation of TPM (CV <0.3) among all samples were discarded. The co‐expression modules were obtained using the automatic network construction function (blockwiseModules) with default settings. The hub genes in this study were defined as those with |K ME| > 0.80 within the assigned module. The network of modules was visualized in Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003).

## DNA re‐sequencing and HE detection
Total DNA was extracted from leaves with a TIANGEN Plant Genomic DNA Kit. At least 1.5 mg of genomic DNA was used to construct the library with a TruSeq Library Construction Kit. DNA re‐sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten system to obtain paired‐end 150‐bp reads. After removing adaptors and trimming low‐quality bases, the clean data were mapped to the ZS11 genome using BWA‐MEM (Li, 2013). Aligned reads with MAPQ >30 were sorted using SAMtools (Li et al., 2009), PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/command‐line‐overview.html), and mapped reads were retained in BAM format. The HE events between ancestrally duplicated regions were detected with reference to a previously described protocol (Chalhoub et al., 2014). Visualization of HEs was performed in IGV (Thorvaldsdóttir et al., 2012), and the visualization of specific syntenic blocks were performed in JCVI (Tang et al., 2008).

## QTL‐seq analysis
We pool DNA from 40 individuals from each extreme, and sequenced the two parents and two pools with 30× and 120× genome coverage, respectively. The trimming and mapping procedures were the same as described in the DNA re‐sequencing analysis. Then, SNP and InDel calling for each sample were performed using GATK (McKenna et al., 2010). Bi‐allelic variations with quality values ≥100 were retained using Vcftools (Danecek et al., 2011). The delimited file containing bulk read depth information was generated as an input file by using GATK VariantsToTable. The ∆(SNP‐index) analysis with 95% confidence intervals was implemented to identify QTLs in QTLseqr (Mansfeld and Grumet, 2018).

## Vector construction, genetic transformation
The BnaA07.WRKY40.b gene was cloned from the cDNA library of X73 using BnWRKY40‐cDNA‐F/R primers (Additional file 2: Table S16), and the fragment of BnaA07.WRKY40.b was then subcloned into PMDC83 using Spe I + Asc I double digestion, generating a 35S:BnaA07.WRKY40.b vector.
We used an intron‐containing hairpin vector (pMDC83‐ihpRNAi) as the backbone for constructing the RNAi vector for BnaA07.WRKY40.b and BnaC06.WRKY40.b (Wu et al., 2022). Primers pairs BnWRKY40‐RNAi‐F/R were designed to amplify the common 327 bp sequences of both genes. The correct PCR‐amplified and sequenced correct segment was then separately ligated in forward and reverse orientations into the pMDC83‐ihpRNAi vector via homologous recombination.
Two sequence‐specific sgRNAs were designed using the online software CRISPR‐P 2.0 (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/cgi‐bin/CRISPR2/CRISPR). A CRISPR/Cas9 construct containing two sgRNA cassettes was generated using the pRGE32 multiplex genome targeting vector, kindly provided by Prof Kabin Xie at Huazhong Agricultural University.
These vector constructs were then introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 for genetic transformation. The susceptible rapeseed line, J9712, was used as the receptor. Genetic transformations were performed according to a previously established experimental procedure (Liu et al., 2021). Total DNA was extracted from the leaves of transgenic plants using the CTAB method, and PCR analysis was performed to identify positive transformants with the specific primers (Additional file 2: Table S16). For CRISPR lines, we amplified and sequenced the target regions of BnaA07.WRKY40.b and BnaC06.WRKY40.b with gene‐specific primers.

## Traits measurement
After selfing for 3–4 generations, we measured BA and other agronomic traits, including other aspects of plant architecture, yield and seed quality traits, for J9712, as well as the homozygous OE, RNAi and CRISPR mutants (Additional file 2: Table S11).

## qRT‐PCR analysis
Total RNA of J9712, OE and RNAi mutants was extracted and reverse transcribed into first‐strand cDNA with a HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). qRT‐PCR was carried out using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) in the ABI Step One Plus real‐time PCR system. The relative expression of each gene was calculated using the 2−▵▵Ct method. BnaACTIN7 and BnUBC9 in rapeseed were used as reference genes (Additional file 2: Table S16). All qRT‐PCR experiments were performed with three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates.

## Subcellular localization
The BnaA07.WRKY40.b CDS was inserted into the N‐terminus of eGFP to construct a recombinant plasmid pUC‐35S:WRKY40‐eGFP. A nuclear marker, pUC‐35S:NLS‐mCherry, containing a nuclear localization signal of SV40, was used. Two plasmids were co‐transfected into Arabidopsis protoplasts. Following a transfection period of 12–16 h, a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP8) was used for capturing the fluorescence signal using specific excitation/detection wavelengths for GFP (488 nm/505–540 nm) and mCherry (590 nm/600–650 nm).
