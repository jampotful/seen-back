Passive acoustic monitoring is an emerging survey method in freshwater environments. Unidentified ticking
sounds appeared to be a major contributor to freshwater soundscape. These unidentified ticking sounds in the
soundscape may be related to aquatic plant primary production in the freshwater environment as such ticking
sounds have previously been described in the aquatic plant Elodea canadensis. This plant emits sounds linked to
gas bubble trails in response to exposition to light. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the sound
features of different aquatic plant species, and their daily acoustic cycles under natural sunlight. To achieve this,
the sounds of four species (Callitriche sp., Elodea nuttallii, Myriophyllum spicatum and Potamogeton lucens) were
recorded in field mesocosms over 13 summer days. Ticking sounds were emitted by each species during the day.

ticking sounds was best identified by the Acoustic Complexity Index over the 0–2 kHz frequency band (ACI02kHz). Similar daily curves with sound peaks between 17:00 and 19:00 were observed in all species, which was

To test these hypotheses, we used acoustics recorders to monitor the
sound emissions by four different submerged aquatic macrophyte spe­
cies in a field mesocosm study. We first compared the acoustic features,
computed four commonly used acoustic indices to evaluate their suit­
ability to indicate plant sounds, and then utilized the suitable acoustic
indices to investigate daily cycles.

Over the past decade, recording underwater sounds has gained
popularity as a non-invasive and user-friendly monitoring method to
survey freshwater environments (Linke et al., 2018; Desjonquères et al.,
2020a). By using passive acoustic monitoring (PAM), it is possible to
detect acoustic signals underwater with hydrophones (underwater mi­
crophones). Freshwater environments can harbour a high diversity of
sounds produced by at least four animal groups, including amphibians,
fish, insects, and crustaceans (Linke et al., 2022). Consequently, PAM
may have the potential to monitor variations in aquatic animal biodi­
versity (Linke et al., 2018). Recently, it has been shown that calls from
these aquatic animals can be masked by whistling and ticking sounds
(Van der Lee et al., 2020). These sounds could be emitted by plants when
they produce and expel oxygen as bubbles into the water during
photosynthesis (Felisberto et al., 2015; Desjonquères et al., 2020a).
So far, these sounds have been detected in the field using acoustic
indices (Greenhalgh et al., 2023; Van der Lee et al., 2020) — mathe­
matical functions allowing to quantify certain aspects of the variability
of the sounds in the soundscape (Alcocer et al., 2022; Bradfer-Lawrence
et al., 2023). Greenhalgh et al. (2023) found that certain acoustic indices
responded best to invertebrate sounds (e.g. Normalized Difference
Soundscape Index NDSI), whilst others responded best to daily cycles (e.
g. Acoustic Entropy Index H). An increased acoustic activity was
observed during the day at sites with macrophytes compared to sites
characterized by more open water suggesting that plant sounds may be
responsible for this difference (Greenhalgh et al., 2023). In line with
these findings, various acoustic indices were associated with the daily
fluctuation in dissolved oxygen saturation, further suggesting there is a
link with aquatic plant sounds (Van der Lee et al., 2020). The acoustic
daily cycles in these studies were, however, based on whole soundscape
recordings in which cycles could be driven by other confounding factors
than plants, including non-biological sounds. Acoustic indices thus
appear as a convenient tool to measure meaningful variations between
recordings and allow comparing acoustic activity (Bradfer-Lawrence
et al., 2023).
In laboratory experiments, sound production by aquatic plants has
been reported for Elodea canadensis (Kratochvil and Pollirer, 2017) and
for a marine macroalgae (Freeman et al., 2018). More recently, sago
pondweed (Stuckenia pectinata) was recorded in shallow waters and in
aquaria in the laboratory (Campbell et al., 2025). In an aquarium,
E. canadensis produced ticking sounds associated with gas bubble trails
only when the light was on (Kratochvil and Pollirer, 2017). These sounds
covered a wide frequency band from 0.5 to 10 kHz with pulses lasting
between 1 and 3 milliseconds. The number of pulses emitted was
correlated with light intensity. Macroalgae produce similar ticking
sounds, but with a dominant frequency around 13 kHz (range 2–20 kHz)
(Freeman et al., 2018). The three studies suggest a link between
photosynthetic activity and sound production (Kratochvil and Pollirer,
2017; Freeman et al., 2018). However, to our knowledge, these are the
only aquatic autotrophs for which sound emission has been reported,
and the sound features have not been compared between different
species.
Here we asked whether other submerged aquatic macrophyte species
(than E. canadensis and sago pondweed) emit sounds. We also wanted to
assess the acoustic space that plant sounds may occupy in the freshwater
soundscape. The aim of this study was thus to investigate the sound
features of different aquatic macrophyte species, and their daily acoustic
cycles under natural sunlight. It was hypothesized that 1) different

2. Material and methods
2.1. Study design
On the 9th of July 2021, 18 plastic mesocosms were constructed and
installed outside on the ‘De Sinderhoeve’ experimental station in
Renkum, the Netherlands (Fig. 1A). The external and internal di­
mensions (length * width * height) of the mesocosms were 120 * 100 *
79 cm and 110 * 91 * 61 cm, respectively. The mesocosms were filled to
55 cm with groundwater from the Sinderhoeve and 50 mL Duo GH/KH
plus (VijverTechniek, the Netherlands) to enrich the water with minerals
to support plant growth (total water volume: 555 L). Each mesocosm
contained one of the four different submerged macrophyte species that
are commonly found in stagnant and slow flowing waters in the
Netherlands, including Callitriche sp. (12 spatulate leaves in a rosette;
Fig. 1B), Elodea nuttallii (tiny, narrow leaves in whorls of three; Fig. 1C),
Myriophyllum spicatum (feather shaped leaves in whorls of four to six;
Fig. 1D), and Potamogeton lucens (large, broad leaves; Fig. 1E), replicated
four times (total 16 of the mesocosms). The first two species were
collected from the artificial ditches at the experimental station and the
latter two species were collected from water bodies in proximity of
Wageningen University Campus, Wageningen. All the plant individuals
of the same species were chosen to have approximately similar sizes. The
plants were rinsed and then planted in plastic pots containing fine sandy
clay sediment originating from dry floodplains of the River Waal, The
Netherlands with an organic matter content of approximately 3 %. The
remaining two mesocosms contained controls: one contained an empty
plastic pot (Fig. 1F) and the other one a pot solely filled with the sandy
clay sediment (Fig. 1G). This allowed controlling for any sound poten­
tially emanating from the surroundings and the sediment.
2.2. Monitoring acoustics and dissolved oxygen
From the 13th of July, 1 min of underwater audio every 10 min for
13 days was recorded in each mesocosm at 16 bits and 48 kHz sampling
frequency. For that purpose, a H2A hydrophone (Aquarian Audio &
Scientific, Anacortes, WA, USA, sensitivity: − 180 dB re: 1 V/μPa) con­
nected to an Audio Moth recorder (v1.2.0; Open Acoustic Devices, Ox­
ford, UK; Hill et al., 2018) was set up in each mesocosm. The Audio
Moths recorders were placed in ziplog bags under a bucket by the
mesocosms. The resonant frequency according to Akamatsu et al.’s
(2002) calculation was: 1.7 kHz. The hydrophones were placed
approximately 1 cm from the plants as sounds were difficult to capture
at further distance. This resulted in 26,785 recordings amounting to
almost 450 h. During the same period, dissolved oxygen (DO) concen­
trations (mg/L) and water temperature (◦ C) were measured with optical
HOBO® Dissolved Oxygen loggers U26–001 protected by the antifouling
protective guard U26-GUARD-2 (Onset Computer Corporation, Bourne,
Massachusetts). Measurements were taken every 10 min. The percent
DO saturation was then derived from the DO concentrations and tem­
perature, using the DOTABLES developed by the U.S. Geological Survey
(2011), assuming 0 ‰ salinity and 1 atm barometric pressure. DO

To test our second hypothesis that the acoustic activity of the four
species of aquatic macrophytes follows similar daily cycles, we assessed
acoustic activity with acoustic indices. First, we tested the relationship
between our four acoustic indices and the number of ticks using Pearson
correlation tests with the function cor.test in R package stats (R Core
Team, 2023; version 4.3.1). To meet assumptions of linearity, number of
ticks were log10(x + 1)-transformed. Second, we compared the daily
cycle in the acoustic indices between species and the controls. For that,
we calculated the median of the four acoustic indices (RMS, NP, Hf, and
ACI) per sampling time of all the recording days. To account for any
autocorrelation in our daily data, the daily cycle for each mesocosm was
modelled using Functional Data Analysis (FDA), which allows repre­
senting a set of temporal data points as a single continuous mathemat­
ical function (Ramsay et al., 2009; using R package FDA version 5.5.1).
To obtain a representation of the daily cycle for each test system, 144
spline bases (measurements every 10 min over 24 h) were used with
order four (fourth-degree polynomial function). A smoothing parameter
of 104 was chosen to optimise the trade-off between degrees of freedom
and generalised cross validation (Ramsay et al., 2009). Then, using a
functional linear model (function fRegress of the package fda) enabled to
assess whether there was a difference in daily acoustic cycle between the
controls and plant mesocosms (predictor: control vs plants). To assess
whether daily cycles were specific to the different macrophyte species,
the two levels of the factor (one plant species against the other three)
were permuted and the explained variance of the initial model was
compared to the explained variance of the permuted models. Because
functional linear models’ theoretical null distribution of test statistics is
unknown, the significance of the predictors was tested with permutation
tests (Ramsay et al., 2009). This resulted in a total of five permutation
tests with each 1000 permutations (one for the control vs. plants and one
for each of the four plant species comparing it to the other three plant
species). To account for multiple testing, a Bonferroni correction was
applied resulting in p < 0.01.

2.3. Acoustic analyses
To process the recordings, our dataset was cleaned up by removing
recordings during rain events based on the meteorological data from
weather station Deelen located 10 km from the experimental station
(obtained from the Royal Netherlands Meteorological institute KNMI
www.knmi.nl). In total 3 days (13–15/07/2021) with precipitation re­
cordings exceeding 25 % of the day were removed. Additionally, 1 day
(16/07/2021) was removed because of sounds of heavy machinery
working at the experimental station. In total, we thus analysed 9 days of
recordings (17–25/07/2021) resulting in a total of 23,004 recordings.
A preliminary analysis was carried out to explore in which band­
width acoustic activity of plants occurred. To do so, we listened to
samples of recordings of 2 different days from all 18 mesocosms and 6
times (00:00, 4:00, 8:00, 12:00, 16:00, 20:00), totalling 216 recordings,
which were visualized with spectrograms in the software Audacity
(https://www.audacityteam.org/). Most of the acoustic activity was
present between 0 and 2 kHz.
To measure the acoustic features of the plant sounds, we selected and
analysed one recording per day for each mesocosm containing clear
ticking sounds based on the spectrogram. This resulted in 144 analysed
recordings. The analysis consisted of manually measuring the ticking
rate, tick duration, tick frequency, tick regularity using Audacity. This
analysis confirmed that the 0–2 kHz frequency band previously identi­
fied was the band of interest for aquatic plant sounds. For each
recording, we selected 10 ticks from which we calculated tick rate (10/
time between first and last tick) and tick regularity (standard deviation
of the 9 intervals between ticks). Next, we measured tick length of the
loudest tick among the ten and the number of zero crossing for frequency
estimation as well as using the meanspec function from R package See­
wave (Sueur et al., 2018, version 2.2.3). We also measured peak to peak
amplitude of the ticks using the region of interest function in the python
package scikit-maad (Ulloa et al., 2021) to detect ticks and then
measuring the average of these peak to peak values over each recording.
To assess the performance of acoustic indices as indicators of plant
acoustic activity, we selected 216 recordings (at 00:00, 4:00, 8:00,
12:00, 16:00 and 20:00 on the 19th and 23rd of July in all 18 meso­
cosms). In these recordings, we counted the number of plant ticks as a
proxy of plant acoustic activity. We then compared this proxy to acoustic
diversity indices. Specifically, the following four complementary indices
were computed: the root mean square (RMS) which quantifies the
amplitude within a recording, number of peaks (NP) which quantifies
the number of frequency peaks in a recording (Gasc et al., 2013),
acoustic entropy index (Hf) which assesses the amount evenness in the
mean frequency spectrum (Sueur et al., 2008), and acoustic complexity
index (ACI) which quantifies amplitude variations over time and fre­
quency in the spectrogram (Pieretti et al., 2011). All indices were
calculated over the 0–2 kHz frequency band with the default parameters
in the R package Seewave: RMS, NP and Hf were calculated on a mean
spectrum with a Hanning window of 512 samples and no overlap. The
ACI was calculated with a Hamming window of 512 samples and no
overlap. Next, the acoustic diversity indices were computed for all re­
cordings to quantify acoustic activity at each moment in time.

3. Results
Listening to recordings and visualising spectrograms revealed ticking
activity during the day in all the mesocosms with plants (example in
Figs. 2 and S1). The performance of the discriminant analysis based on
plant features was low (accuracy of 25 % and false predictions rate over
75 % for all the plant species) suggesting that there were no differences
in tick features between plant species (Fig. S2, Table 1). The ticks had an
average duration of 9.1 ± 6.4 ms (mean ± standard deviation) at 1.5 ±
0.7 kHz. The mean ticking rate was quite variable and ranged from less
than a tick per second to 32 (4.2 ± 5.1 ticks.s− 1). The space between the
ticks ranged from very regular with a standard deviation of the space
between ticks as low as 0.01 s to very irregular with a maximum of 3.3 s.
The median amplitude of ticks was 111 dB re 1μPa which is equivalent to
49 dB re 20 μPa (the equivalent in air, which corresponds to a quiet
conversation).
Evaluating the different acoustic indices, ACI0-2kHz most strongly
correlated with the number of ticks (Fig. S3; r = 0.58, p < 2.2.10− 16).
Moreover, ACI0-2kHz showed daily activity patterns consistent with the
sounds observed in spectrogram visualisation and did not have daily
patterns in the two control mesocosms contrary to the other acoustic
indices (Figs. S4–S7). Accordingly, the linear model revealed highly
significant differences between the FDA of the control and plant meso­
cosms for ACI0-2kHz (permutation test: p-value<0.001; Fig. 3). For the
other indices, there were no significant differences between control and
plant mesocosms (all permutation tests: p-value >0.14), suggesting
some other factor than the plants influenced the acoustic activity
captured by these indices. Therefore, the ACI0-2kHz results were used for
subsequent analyses.

4.1. Potential mechanisms behind sound production of aquatic plants
There have been no studies providing hypotheses on how aquatic
plant sounds are produced. In this paragraph, we propose three possible
hypotheses for sound production mechanisms. These hypotheses are the
most likely according to our experience in the field. Submerged mac­
rophytes could produce sound due to several aspects of the release of gas
bubbles. Sound could be generated by flow of gas from the internal
medium of the plant to the water, this would be like whistling and ex­
pected to produce tonal sounds (personal observations, Campbell et al.,
2025). It is therefore an unlikely mechanism for the ticking sounds we
recorded, even though such sounds have been observed in the field
(personal observations, Campbell et al., 2025). Alternatively, sound may
be produced by the bubbles detaching from the plants (personal obser­
vations), but this would likely be difficult to capture using a hydrophone
as it may be low amplitude. Another option is that the bubbles are
hitting the hydrophone as they ascend to the surface, but this would
require the hydrophone to be placed right above the bubble trail and the
resulting sound would be a “pseudo” sound, a by-product of the presence

that started to increase around 8:00, reaching a maximum between
17:00 and 19:00, and decreasing until 22:00 to 24:00, although the ACI02kHz peaks varied. Generally, photosynthesis rates measured by oxygen

4.4. Implications for future studies on freshwater acoustics
The present study provided evidence that several submerged mac­
rophytes produce sounds underwater. These sounds resemble sound
types that often dominate the underwater soundscape (Van der Lee
et al., 2020), which may have important implications for the study of
freshwater soundscapes. Specifically, the soundscape in freshwater en­
vironments is formed by sounds from a variety of sources, including
various animal groups, as well as physico-chemical and hydrological
processes (Desjonquères et al., 2020a). It needs to be further assessed
whether the sound emitted by the release of bubbles by submerged
macrophytes can be filtered from the whole soundscape, and if they can
be distinguished from other ticking sounds, such as the sound emitted
when methane is expelled during anaerobic organic matter decompo­
sition (Linke et al., 2018). If the ticking sounds can be directly linked to
the photosynthetic activity of plants, it may be possible to quantify
relative primary production rates with passive acoustic monitoring
(Kratochvil and Pollirer, 2017; Freeman et al., 2018; Campbell et al.,
2025).
Moreover, the sounds produced by plants may influence the rest of
the soundscape. For instance, the bubbles could potentially influence the
speed of sound underwater as they form many small, resonant cavities in
the water column that can both scatter and absorb sound (Felisberto
et al., 2015; Erbe and Thomas, 2022). These changes in the soundscape
by submerged macrophytes may in turn influence the behaviour of
aquatic animals. Mullet et al. (2017) hypothesized that sounddependent species require specific acoustic and physical conditions
suitable for their signals to be produced and detected. Some species,
such as the micronectid Micronecta scholtzi, may avoid vegetated sites for
calling because of masking from plant sounds or conditions of sound
propagation that are less favourable (Desjonquères et al., 2020b).
Moreover, ambient noise may also impact the ability of aquatic animals
to find prey or provide an acoustic refuge from species that use an
acoustic signal to detect their prey (Wilson et al., 2013; Roca et al.,
2020). Future research has yet to reveal whether aquatic animals are
actively changing their behaviour in relation to the changes in the un­
derwater soundscape due to aquatic plants.

5. Conclusions
In conclusion, ticking sounds were detected for four common sub­
merged macrophyte species during the day using passive acoustic
monitoring. These ticking sounds had similar acoustic features in the
four species. The daily variation in ticking sounds was best described by
the Acoustic Complexity Index over the 0-2 kHz frequency band, and the
ACI was highest for all plants in the late afternoon. Further research will
be required to better assess mechanisms behind the sound production.
We will also need to evaluate the detection performance of acoustic
indices or other detection methods such as ROI or machine learning
models in more complex soundscapes typical of freshwater environ­
ments. Finally estimating the impact of these sounds on the rest of the
underwater soundscape will further our understanding of what cues are
available for underwater species.
