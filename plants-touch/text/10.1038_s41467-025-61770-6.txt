A mechanosensitive ion channel controls touch-triggered stigma movement through manipulation of calcium signature in Torenia.

# A mechanosensitive ion channel controls touch-triggered stigma movement through manipulation of calcium signature in Torenia

## Abstract
AbstractRapid movements of plant structures triggered by mechanical force have been documented for centuries; however, how plant cells sense mechanical stimuli and generate rapid response remains unknown. Here we show the central role of a mechanosensitive channel gene in touch-triggered rapid stigma movement of Torenia fournieri. T. fournieri possesses a bilobed stigma sensitive to mechanical stimuli. Using live-cell calcium imaging, we detect that touch induces a wave of cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+]cyt) essential for stigma movement. Transcriptomic analysis reveals distinct gene expression profiles between the stigma and style, leading to the identification of the MscS-like gene JUE1, predominantly expressed in stigmas. Stigmas of jue1 mutants exhibit complete loss of touch-triggered movement, confirming its role as a key regulator in sensitive stigma movement. JUE1 is required for rapid propagation of the touch-induced [Ca2+]cyt, as cell-to-cell spreading of the [Ca2+]cyt wave is impaired in the jue1. To conclude, we report a plant mechanosensitive ion channel involved in touch-triggered organ movement by modulating calcium dynamics.Subject terms: Plant transporters, Plant signalling, Plant reproduction

## Introduction
Unlike animals, most plants are sessile and incapable of locomotion. Instead, as an adaptation to escape injury or to benefit growth, plants have evolved various strategies to perform certain organ movements in response to external stimuli, e.g., light, chemical compounds, water and touch1–4. Rapid thigmonastic movements exist widely in the plant kingdom for various essential functions, such as defense (the leaflet movement of Mimosa pudica)1, nutrition (the trap closure of Dionaea muscipula and Utricularia)2, pollination (the snapping movements of stamens in Berberis and Sparmannia)5, and outcrossing (the rapid closure of stigmas in Mimulus and Torenia)6. Touch-triggered movements of plant organs have been documented over centuries since Linnaeus7, and studies have addressed the adaptive significance and physical basis involved in these movements8–11. However, the molecular networks underlying touch-triggered plant movements remain elusive.
The whole process of a touch-triggered rapid plant movements can be divided into different phases, including perception of mechanical force, change of mechanical force into a cellular signal, transfer of signal between cells, and response through hydraulic movements. By converting membrane tension variations into ionic and electrical signals, mechanosensitive (MS) ion channels have been proposed as prime candidates in mechanosensation and mechanotransduction12,13. In plants, MS channels from five distinct families have been identified, including Piezo, a family of mechanical sensors in the plasma membrane of animal cells, localized to the tonoplast of plant cells14; reduced hyperosmolality-induced [Ca2+]cyt increase channel (OSCA), a type of MS channel conserved across eukaryotes15; Mid1-Complementing Activity (MCA), a Ca2+ permeable channel family sensitive to membrane tension16; Two-Pore K+ channels (TPK), vacuolar K+ channels that also function as osmosensors17; and MS channel of small conductance-like (MSL) family, mechanically gated channels found across bacteria, archaea, fungi and plants18,19.
The Arabidopsis genome encodes ten MSL homologues named MSL1–10, and phylogenetic analysis of the ten MSLs indicates that they can be divided into two classes: MSL1-3 belong to Class I and MSL4-10 fall into Class II MSLs12,13. MSL1 is crucial for mitochondrial membrane potential and regulating mitochondrial redox homeostasis under abiotic stress20. Mutations in both MSL2 and MSL3 show abnormalities in plastid shape, size and division, supporting similar osmotic stress signalling pathways in mitochondria and plastids18. MSL8 is specifically accumulated in pollen and is required for tuning osmotic forces during pollen hydration and negatively regulates pollen germination21. Patch clamp experiments showed that MSL10 is a stretch-activated ion channel with a preference for anions such as Cl−19. MSL10 is accumulated in root tips, vascular tissues and at the junction between roots and shoots, functioning as a molecular component for oscillatory perception in plants22,23. In addition, MSL10 is also involved in programmed cell death induction and in response to cell swelling, and these functions are possibly separable from its MS channel activity24,25. Recently, homologues of MSL10 were identified from carnivorous Droseraceae plants, with high expression in trigger hairs of D. muscipula and tentacles of Drosera capensis26. Nevertheless, mutants of Arabidopsis msl10 or mslΔ5 (a quintuple mutant with T-DNA insertions in MSL4/5/6/9/10) have no detectable defects in morphology, and double mutants of Dionaea MSL10 homologues are still sensitive to touch stimuli, though exhibiting reduced responses to ultrasound stimulation, suggesting the existence of other proteins acting redundantly as a mechanical sensory module in these plants27,28.
Calcium serves as an important second messenger in many cell types29–32. Cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+]cyt) is maintained at a low concentration at rest, and stimulus can induce calcium influx from the extracellular space or the intracellular organelles, which in turn activates the cellular signalling32. In plants, Ca2+ transport across cell membranes is mediated by different types of ion channels, including OSCAs that function as mechanically activated calcium channels, cyclic nucleotide-gated channels (CNGCs), glutamate receptor-like proteins (GLRs), two-pore channels (TPCs), annexins (ANNs) and several types of noncanonical ion channels formed by nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptors (NLRs)33,34. Once entering the cytosol, calcium ions can be rapidly decoded by a number of proteins such as calmodulin (CaM), calmodulin-like proteins (CML), calcineurin B-like proteins (CBL), CBL-interacting protein kinases (CIPK) and calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPK)34. Many stimuli including mechanical force, induce increases in [Ca2+]cyt concentrations35–37. In Arabidopsis, mechanical forces, like raindrops, induce Ca2+ waves starting from trichomes and trigger immune response36; in M. pudica and D. muscipula, similarly, touching induces leaf movements mediated by rapid changes in [Ca2+]cyt concentrations35,37. Despite these findings, the molecular hub linking mechanosensation and rapid Ca2+ signalling remains unclear.
In this study, we investigated T. fournieri which possesses a touch-sensitive bilobed stigma in its flower (Fig. 1a). The stigma is sensitive to mechanical stimuli and can close rapidly within seconds. Using live-cell calcium imaging and pharmacological treatment, we demonstrated that mechanical stimuli triggered a rapid wave of [Ca2+]cyt that is necessary for rapid stigma closure. We further identified two mechanosensitive ion channel genes preferentially expressed in the stigmas. CRISPR mutants of jue1 rather than jue2 showed complete loss of touch-triggered stigma movement, supporting that JUE1 functions as a key player in the process. Finally, we found that the transmission of touch-induced [Ca2+]cyt wave relies on function of JUE1. Our study is a good illustration of how plant cells transduce mechanical signals and rapidly generate a response.

## Results

## T. fournieri has a touch-sensitive bilobed stigma
Since the stigma of T. fournieri is covered by the corolla, it is difficult to observe directly. Hence, we compared the sensitivity of stigmas using the in vivo (the whole flower) or in vitro (dissected style with stigma) systems. No difference was detected between the two systems, and we therefore use the in vitro system to evaluate stigma sensitivity in this study (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). We mechanically stimulated each lobe of stigma and found that the mechanical stimulus did not transmit from one lobe to the other as only the touched lobe moved (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Movie 1). We further compared the sensitivity between two faces (outer/abaxial and inner/adaxial) of a stigma lobe and concluded that only the inner face is sensitive to touch (Fig. 1c). To compare the relationship between the degree of deformation (reflecting the strength of stimuli) and the degree of movement of the lobe (indicating the degree of response), we applied different stimuli on different stigma lobes (Fig. 1d). We observed a positive correlation between deformation and movement (Sigmoidal, R2 = 0.8659), and defined three different levels (L1/2/3) of touch stimuli: L1 refers to Δdeformation <0.05, where no obvious movement was detected; L2: 0.1 < Δdeformation <0.3, where movement was observed in partial lobe; L3: Δdeformation >0.4, where movement was observed in the whole lobe.
Next, we investigated morphogenesis and cellular architecture of the Torenia pistil. The pistil developmental process is divided into nine stages (Supplementary Fig. 3a): two carpel primordia initiate and fuse together (stage 1), followed by differentiation of stigma and ovary (stage 2); style emerges (stage 3) and elongates (stage 4) until forming a hook (stage 5); the whole flower bud grow fast accompanied by rapid elongation of the style (stage 6); the flower bud opens prior to separation of two stigma lobes (stage 7), which completely separate (stage 8) and wilted after pollination (stage 9). Using paraffin sections and scanning electron microscopy, we characterized morphologically different types of cell layers (Fig. 1e, f), including papilla cells, subpapillar cells, vascular bundles, parenchyma cells and abaxial epidermis. Various sorts of epidermal cells including papilla (large part of the inner surface) and tentacle cells (boundary of the inner surface) were also characterized (Supplementary Fig 3b).

## Touch induces a sudden rise of cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+]cyt)
Cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+]cyt) is indispensable for plant growth, development, and adaptation. To observe the spatiotemporal [Ca2+]cyt changes in stigma, we transformed a CamelliA [Ca2+]cyt sensor38 into T. fournieri and obtained five hygromycin-resistant plants. Two independent lines (1# and 2#) showed increased [Ca2+]cyt fluorescence in leaf veins after wounding (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Movies 2and 3). Since the two calcium reporter lines show similar wound-triggered calcium wave, line 1# was used for subsequent experiments. We further examined the spatiotemporal dynamics of [Ca2+]cyt in stigmas. Strong (L3 level) mechanical stimuli caused rapid [Ca2+]cyt increase in the touched region, while stronger [Ca2+]cyt signals were detected in the deformed region, where movement was initiated (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Movies 4 and 5). We noticed that touch-induced [Ca2+]cyt increase preceded stigma movement, and the [Ca2+]cyt wave propagated from the deformed region to the stigma-style junction area after the end of movement (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Movies 4 and 5). The region showing increased fluorescence intensity is not continuous as the touched lobe is partially obscured by the unstimulated lobe in front (Fig. 2a). In the side view, we found that the [Ca2+]cyt wave started from the inner face of the lobe, and then spread to large part of the lobe (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Movie 5). Consistent with the transmission of mechanical stimuli, the fluorescence did not propagate from one lobe to the other (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Movies 4 and 5).

We further compared the average [Ca2+]cyt wave intensity of the whole lobe induced by three different levels of touch stimuli, and found that all levels of stimuli could trigger [Ca2+]cyt increase, and stronger stimuli triggered stronger [Ca2+]cyt increase (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, Supplementary Movies 6 and 7). As the [Ca2+]cyt wave was only induced in a partial region of the lobe when applying L1 or L2 level of mechanical stimulus, we calculated the average [Ca2+]cyt wave intensity specifically in the region where a [Ca2+]cyt elevation was observed and found that both of the two levels induced similar calcium dynamics, with differences in intensity and transmitted area (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6, Supplementary Movies 6 and 7).

## The [Ca2+]cyt wave is necessary for rapid stigma movement
To elucidate the causal relationship between [Ca2+]cyt wave and lobe movement, we pre-treated pistils with a Ca2+ chelator, EGTA. Compared with the control (mock), 50 mM EGTA-treated stigmas failed to respond to strong (L3 level) mechanical stimuli (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Movies 8 and 9). We further pre-treated pistils with a Ca2+ channel blocker, LaCl3, and similar results were observed, showing that 50 mM LaCl3 successfully blocked touch-triggered stigma movement (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Movies 10 and 11). To evaluate the effect of EGTA or LaCl3 treatment on [Ca2+]cyt wave generation, we compared the average [Ca2+]cyt wave intensity of the lobe under different treatments. Both EGTA and LaCl3 successfully blocked the generation of [Ca2+]cyt wave, consistent with the stigma behaviour in response to touch (Fig. 3e–h, Supplementary Movies 12–15). Therefore, we concluded that the rapid [Ca2+]cyt increase in the stigma tissue is required for touch-induced lobe movement.

## Transcriptomic landscapes of style and stigma tissues
To identify the molecular mechanisms involved in touch-triggered organ movement, we carried out transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) using the style and stigma tissues in untouched conditions. We identified 5979 differentially expressed genes [log2 fold changes (log2FC) > 1, FDR < 0.05] (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b, Supplementary Data 1, 2). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of molecular function revealed a striking enrichment in GO terms such as protein kinase activity, transporter activity and ion binding activity (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Data 3). We focused on top 300 stigma-expressed genes and many of them are associated with channels, calcium signalling, cell wall, phytohormones and transporters (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 2). Among the 6 channel-encoding genes, we identified five candidates preferentially expressed in stigmas, including two genes (Unigene0011542 and Unigene0027138) encoding MSL channels, two (Unigene0025703 and Unigene0025704) encoding plasma membrane aquaporins, and one Unigene0017954 that encodes a depolarization-activated Stelar K+ outward rectifier (SKOR) channel.

Mechanosensitive (MS) ion channels are good candidates as force sensors of membrane as they show responses to increased membrane tension in previous studies12,19,21,22. It has been demonstrated that gadolinium ion (Gd3+) blocks MS channels, making Gd3+ suitable for testing the role of MS ion channels in plant thigmotropic responses39. Therefore, we pre-treated pistils with 50 mM GdCl3, and we observed that GdCl3 successfully blocked touch-triggered stigma movement as well as the generation of [Ca2+]cyt wave (Supplementary Fig. 8a, d, Supplementary Movies 16 and 19). These results indicate that stretch-activated MS channel(s) are likely required for touch-triggered stigma movement.
We therefore focused on the two MSL candidates and designated them as JUE1 (Unigene0011542) and JUE2 (Unigene0027138), respectively. JUE originates from the pronunciation of two Chinese homophones that can either refer to sense, indicating the gene’s function; or two pieces of jade joined together, representing the bilobed stigma. Strikingly, JUE1 is expressed 77-fold higher in stigma tissue (FPKM = 3663.2) than in style (FPKM = 47.4); by contrast, JUE2’s expression was only 3.8-fold higher in stigma tissue (FPKM = 635.3) than in style (FPKM = 164.0).
To carry out phylogenetic analysis of Torenia MSL proteins, we performed a genome-wide search for MSLs using BLASTP in Phytozome (http://www.phytozome.net/) and Torenia database (http://dandelion.liveholonics.com/torenia/). Different numbers of MSLs present in different plant lineages. The phylogenetic tree indicates that class II MSL proteins are further divided into two clades, or four subclades (Fig. 4c). JUE1 and JUE2 are in different clades: JUE1 belongs to the same clade as Arabidopsis MSL9/10; whereas JUE2 is a member of another clade, with Arabidopsis MSL4/5/6/7/8 (Fig. 4c).

## JUE1 functions as a key regulator in sensitive stigma movement
We further compared the expression of JUE1 and JUE2 using reverse transcription (RT)-PCR and RNA in situ hybridization assays. RT-PCR assays showed that JUE1 is highly expressed in the stage 5 and stage 8 stigmas, while JUE2 exhibits lower expression in stage 8 stigmas (Supplementary Fig. 9). RNA in situ hybridization assays demonstrated that JUE1 is highly expressed in the inner faces of stigma as well as the stigma-style junction from stage 5; its mRNA was not detected in papillae cells by in situ hybridization (Fig. 5c). We failed to detect the expression of JUE2 in the pistil by in situ hybridization possibly due to its low expression. Since the expression region of JUE1 is identical to the sensitive region, we infer that JUE1 is a potential candidate in mechanosensation or mechanotransduction of stigma.

Previous studies have shown that Class I MSLs are localized to plastids and/or mitochondria, while Class II are localized on the plasma membrane or the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)18,19,21,22. We further examined the subcellular localization of JUE1 by transient expression of eGFP-fused proteins in tobacco leaf epidermal cells (Fig. 5b, c). We found that GFP signals partially co-localized with a plasma membrane marker, LTI6b-mCherry (Fig. 5b). We further detected that the JUE1-GFP signal largely co-localized with an ER marker, mCherry-HDEL, supporting that JUE1-eGFP proteins are predominantly located in the ER or the ER–plasma membrane contact sites (Fig. 5c).
To understand the functions of JUE1 and JUE2, we obtained loss-of-function mutants using the CRISPR/Cas9 method. Plasmids coding for four guide RNAs each were constructed and transformed into T. fournieri. Mutations of JUE1 were detected in two independent transgenic lines, jue1-6 and jue1-10, resulting in frame shifts and premature termination (Supplementary Fig. 10a). We also identified two jue2 mutants with 1 bp insertion or 2 bp deletion at target site 2 (Supplementary Fig 10b). We compared the stigma behaviours of wild-type (WT), jue1 mutants (jue1-6 and jue1-10) and jue2 mutants (jue2-10 and jue2-30) after touch. Interestingly, two jue1 CRISPR mutants showed complete loss of touch-triggered stigma movement and there was no difference between jue1-6 and jue1-10, whereas jue2-10 was still sensitive to touch (Fig. 5d, e, Supplementary Fig 11). These results demonstrate that JUE1 rather than JUE2 acts as a key factor controlling touch-triggered stigma movement.
Previous studies have shown that except for touch, wound, electrical or osmotic shock can also trigger stigma closure35. To investigate if loss of response to touch in jue1 mutants is caused by failure in mechanotransduction or merely by defects in cell contraction, we further observed stigma behaviours under different treatments. Electrical shock or wound could trigger stigma movement in jue1-10 (Supplementary Fig. 12, Supplementary Movies 20 and 21), and similar to WT, stigmas of jue1-10 closed under hyperosmotic shock and reopened in hypoosmotic solution (Fig. 5f, g), demonstrating that loss of touch-triggered movement in jue1 mutants may be caused by failure in touch sensation or signal transduction.

## JUE1 is necessary for propagation of the touch-induced [Ca2+]cyt wave
We next investigated the relationship between [Ca2+]cyt change and mechanotransduction by analysing [Ca2+]cyt dynamics in the jue1 mutant. The transgenic [Ca2+]cyt marker line was crossed with the jue1-10 mutant and we identified a marker line with homozygous mutation in jue1-10. To compare the differences of [Ca2+]cyt dynamics between WT and the jue1-10 mutant, we touched the stigmas with a variety of force (Fig. 6a–h, Supplementary Movies 22–27). Strong (L3) and medium (L2) mechanical stimuli could trigger increases of fluorescence in both WT and the jue1-10 mutant within 0.3 second, whereas the fluorescent intensity was much higher and duration of the [Ca2+]cyt wave also lasted longer in WT stigmas (Fig. 6a–f, Supplementary Movie 22–25). We also found that the [Ca2+]cyt wave propagated concentrically away from the deformed region of WT stigma but failed to propagate in the jue1-10 mutant (Fig. 6d–f, Supplementary Movies 24 and 25). We further touched several papillae cells with a fine needle (Fig. 6g, h, Supplementary Movies 26 and 27). In WT, it triggered an [Ca2+]cyt wave that propagated away from the touched papilla cells downward to the subpapillar cells and then to the neighbouring papilla cells (Fig. 6g, Supplementary Movie 26); in the jue1-10 mutant, however, increases in fluorescence were only detected in the touched papilla cells without propagation to the subpapillar cells or other neighbouring papilla cells (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Movie 27). Our data support that JUE1 is required for the local and fast propagation of [Ca2+]cyt wave, suggesting that plant MSLs are probably not the initial mechanical sensor, but are rather important for distal cell-to-cell signalling, in line with previous studies in Arabidopsis and Dionaea22,40,41.

In Arabidopsis, application of glutamate solution to the leaf distal region triggered a sudden increase of [Ca2+]cyt wave propagating to the whole leaf, and the [Ca2+]cyt wave was completely inhibited in the loss-of-function mutant of glutamate receptor-like proteins (GLRs)42. In the leaf of T. fournieri, application of 0.4 μl 200 mM glutamate solution to the leaf tip induced transmission of [Ca2+]cyt wave to the proximal region of the leaf, while the control solution only induced local transmission of [Ca2+]cyt wave surrounding the leaf tip possibly due to surface tension of the liquid drop (Supplementary Figs. 13and 14, Supplementary Movies 28 and 29). We also observed that glutamate-induced leaf [Ca2+]cyt wave transmitted to other untreated leaf and thus potentially induced leaf-to-leaf systemic signals in the whole plant (Supplementary Fig. 15, Supplementary Movies 30). We further tested if the glutamate and wounding treatments can induce [Ca2+]cyt waves in the leaves of the jue1-10 reporter line, and the results showed that, similar to the WT, both treatment successfully induced [Ca2+]cyt waves spreading to the leaf distal regions (Supplementary Figs. 16–18, Supplementary Movie 31–33), indicating that JUE1 may not be a key regulator involved in systemic spreading of [Ca2+]cyt waves after glutamate or wounding.
Nevertheless, in the stigma of T. fournieri, application of glutamate or control solution only triggered a weak generation of [Ca2+]cyt wave without inducing movement (Supplementary Fig 19a, Supplementary Movie 34 and 35). Quantification of the [Ca2+]cyt wave intensity suggested no difference between control and glutamate treatment (Supplementary Fig 19b). We identified 9 GLR genes in T. fournieri from our transcriptome, however, none of them is highly enriched in the stigma tissues (Supplementary Fig 20). These results suggest that, unlike in the leaf, GLR may not be the major component responsible for the generation of [Ca2+]cyt wave in the stigma.

## Discussion
Sensitive stigmas have been recorded by Linnaeus, Darwin and Newcombe, and the movement of sensitive stigmas is thought to be a mechanism to promote outcrossing and pollination efficiency8–11. When a pollinator without pollen on its body enters a flower with sensitive stigma, it touches the stigma lobes that rapidly close within seconds, and thereby prevent self-pollination (Fig. 1). However, once it leaves the flower and enters a new flower, attached pollen on its back will be delivered onto the stigma of the new flower, which will be thus cross-fertilized. However, it has been poorly understood from the molecular perspectives.
In this study, we characterized the function of a Torenia MSL, JUE1, which regulates sensitive stigma movement through integration of cytosolic calcium signature. When the stigma lobe is touched by a pollinator, mechanical force generated by the pollinator is applied to the adaxial face of stigma lobe, leading to changes in membrane tension of the papilla and subpapillar cells (Fig. 7). The membrane tension–gated MS ion channel, JUE1, is thereby activated through a conformational change, followed by the movement of selected ions down their electrochemical gradient (Fig. 7). Since orthologues of JUE1 such as Arabidopsis MSL10 and Dionaea FLYC1 display a preference for Cl− ions19,26, we propose a similar mechanism of JUE1 by quick release of Cl− ions through its channel pore and subsequently activating unidentified depolarization-activated Ca2+ channels (DACCs) to initiate a [Ca2+]cyt wave that further triggers cell contraction (Fig. 7). However, proteins with DACC function have not been identified in plants, and it is necessary to elucidate the function of JUE1 by electrophysiological methods in the future.

## A working model for touch-triggered stigma movement in Torenia fournieri.
Papilla cells (pa) and subpapillar cells (spa) are shown; F represents mechanical force. In the rest state, JUE1 is inactivated (green); when the stigma lobe is touched by a pollinator, mechanical force generated by the pollinator applies onto the adaxial face of stigma lobe, leading to activation of JUE1 (orange).
While we cannot exclude the possibility that JUE1 functions redundantly with other mechanosensitive ion channels for touch sensation, our results are most consistent with the conclusion that JUE1 would be the key molecule that link mechanotransduction and Ca2+ signal in plant cells (Fig. 6). In Arabidopsis, MSL10 is required for wound-triggered electrical signals in the leaf vascular bundles41. The amplitude and kinetics of the [Ca2+]cyt wave are also impaired in the distal leaves of msl10-1 mutants, indicating that MSL10 mediates long-range transmission of Ca2+ signals41. Our study demonstrates that JUE1 is required for fast propagation of local [Ca2+]cyt wave (Fig. 6). Different levels of mechanical stimuli triggered a sudden rise of [Ca2+]cyt in the touched/deformed regions of both WT and mutant, however, propagation of the [Ca2+]cyt wave was abolished in the mutant (Fig. 6). These results also prove that propagation of the [Ca2+]cyt wave to the neighbouring cells is required for lobe movement.
Structural analyses of Arabidopsis MSL10 and FLYC1 unveil that each protein assembles as a heptameric channel43,44. Each subunit of the heptamer contains a transmembrane (TM) domain with six helices and a β-rich C-terminus, and the hydrophobic residues (V549 and F553 in MSL10, and V568 and F572 in FLYC1) in the pore form a hydrophobic barrier in both channels43,44. Interestingly, the gating mechanism for MSL10 differs from that for FLYC1: the TM4-TM5 linker of MSL10 is in a down conformation, while the TM4-TM5 linker of FLYC1 can be in either up or down configuration44. Structural modelling reveals that JUE1 shares 46.84% sequence identity with MSL10 and 42.47% identity with FLYC1, with low similarity in the N-terminal domain and flexible loops between TM helices, where structures have not been resolved (Supplementary Fig 21). Multiple alignments of Arabidopsis MSL4-10, FLYC1-like and JUE1/2 suggest that the equivalent hydrophobic phenylalanine (F553 in MSL10, and F572 in FLYC1) is conserved in all proteins (such as F504 in JUE1), while the valine (V549 in MSL10, and V568 in FLYC1) always shifts to a more hydrophobic leucine in many other MSL (such as L500 in JUE1), such subtle difference may cause modification of mechano-sensitivity in different MSL channels (Supplementary Fig 22). Future mutational analyses are required to confirm roles of these amino acids.
To summarize, our study provides a good illustration of how plant cells transduce the mechanical force and rapidly generate a response. It is of interest in the future to reveal how the MSL ion channel affects [Ca2+]cyt dynamics from the perspective of structure and electrophysiology, and to answer the Darwin’s questions of sensitive stigmas by comparing the mutants to wild-type plants in a pollination assay.

## Methods

## Plant materials and transformation
Plants of T. fournieri and Nicotiana benthamiana were maintained at 25 °C under a 16:8 light-dark cycle. For plant transformation of T. fournieri, leaf discs of T. fournieri were collected from sterile plants grown on 1/2 MS culture medium. The cytosolic Ca2+ sensor plasmid (Camellia_CytG) was a gift from Prof. Zhenbiao Yang (University of California, Riverside)38. The CRISPR/Cas9 binary plasmids were produced according to the published method called CRISPR-GE (http://skl.scau.edu.cn/)45. Specifically, four target sequences with a protospacer adjacent motif (NGG) were designed within the specific regions of JUE1 and JUE2. Transformation of T. fournieri was performed as previously reported46. Briefly, the plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and transformed into T. fournieri using a leaf-disc transformation protocol. Hygromycin-resistant seedlings were subsequently identified by PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Data 4. Genotyping of CRISPR/Cas9 mutants was performed by amplifying the target-containing genomic fragments from plants. The PCR products were purified before sequencing. Eleven jue1 and five jue2 transgenic plants with heterozygous or biallelic mutations in T0 generation were obtained. Homozygous mutants without CRISPR transgenes were generated by self-fertilization and genotyping. Homozygous T1 mutant lines, jue1-6, jue1-10, jue2-10 and jue2-30, analysed in this study were selected from different T0 plants.

## Paraffin sectioning
The paraffin sectioning was performed according to an established protocol47. Briefly, pistils were fixed in FAA fixative (10% formalin solution, 5% acetic acid and 50% ethanol). The tissues were then embedded with Paraplast-plus (Sigma‒Aldrich) after treatment with a series of ethanol and xylene solutions. Sections were further stained with 0.5% Fast Green Stain solution prior to observation under a Nikon ECLIPSE Ni microscope.

## Scanning electron microscopy
To observe the epidermal cell morphologies of T. fournieri, stigmas were rapidly covered by ImprintTM II Garant (3 M ESPE) impression materials and filled with 2-Ton Epoxy adhesive (Devcon Ltd.) to produce the epoxy replica as previously reported48. The epoxy replicas were then directly imaged under a scanning electron microscope (VHX-D510, Keyence).

## Transcriptomic analysis
Stage 8 stigmas and styles were collected from wild-type (WT) plants. We pooled tissues from ten flowers as one biological replicate, and three biological replicates for each tissue were prepared. Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Vazyme). The cDNA libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation kit (Illumina). Sequencing, assembly and annotation of contigs were carried out as previously reported49. Specifically, the libraries were sequenced with Illumina novaseq 6000, and clean reads were trimmed using fastp (version 0.18.0). Transcriptome de novo assembly was carried out with short reads assembling programme: Trinity. The unigene expression was quantified and normalized to RPKM (Reads Per kb per Million reads). To annotate the unigenes, we performed BLASTx analysis (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST/) with an E-value threshold of 1e−5 to NCBI non-redundant protein (Nr) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), the Swiss-Prot protein database (http://www.expasy.ch/sprot), the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg), and the COG/KOG database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/COG).

## Protein localization assay
To generate the constructs for subcellular localization, the eGFP protein was fused to the C-terminus of JUE1, and mCherry was fused to the C-terminus of TfLTI6b or the ER retention signal peptide (HDEL), under the control of the CaMV35S promoters in pCAMBIA1302. The constructed plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105 and then introduced into leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana. After 24-36 hrs, transfected leaves were placed on a piece of slice glass, and the fluorescence was examined under appropriate channels of a confocal microscope (A1R + , Nikon), equipped with a 405/488/561/640 dichroic beam splitter, 60× objective lens (NA 1.42, CFI Plan Apochromat Lambda D 60X Oil, Nikon) and a sCMOS camera (Digital Sight 10, Nikon). The enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) and red fluorescent protein (mCherry) were excited by 488 and 561 nm diode lasers. The eGFP and mCherry signals were detected within the 500–550 nm and 570–616 nm ranges, respectively. For ER localization, Z-stacks were taken with a 2 μm slice for 15-25 slices, and then converted to a maximum-intensity projection. Images were analysed in an imaging software (NIS-Elements, Nikon). All the primers used here are listed in Supplementary Data 4.

## Phylogenetic analysis and homology modelling
To determine the phylogenetic relationship of MSL and GLR proteins in Arabidopsis and T. fournieri, we retrieved homologous gene sequences from Arabidopsis genome database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The search for plant MSL proteins was carried out using BLASTP and 50% identity was taken as the threshold. The sequences were retrieved from Phytozome (http://www.phytozome.net/) and Torenia database (http://dandelion.liveholonics.com/torenia/). Alignments of amino acid sequences were performed in MEGA X50. The maximum-likelihood or neighbour-joining trees of proteins under 500 bootstrap iterations were also generated by MEGA X. Structural modelling of JUE1 was performed by Swiss-Model (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/), and the Cryo-EM structure of the wild-type MSL10 (8tdj.1.A) was used as a template.

## Expression analysis
For RT‒PCR analysis, total RNA was extracted by FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC401-01; Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), and cDNA was further generated using the TReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (FSQ-301, TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). β-Actin (TfACT3) expression was used as an internal control. All the primers used here are listed in Supplementary Data 4.
For RNA in situ hybridization, pistils were fixed in FAA fixative (10% formalin, 5% acetic acid, and 50% ethanol). The tissues were then embedded with Paraplast-plus (Sigma‒Aldrich) after treatment with a series of ethanol and xylene solutions. The sense and antisense probes were labelled with digoxigenin-UTP. Briefly, partial cDNA sequences were amplified and cloned into the pTA2 plasmid (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) that contains T7 and T3 RNA polymerase promoters for in vitro transcription. The probes were labelled with DIG RNA Labelling Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and purified by LiCl-based precipitation. Hybridization procedures were performed as described51. Briefly, microtome sections of 10 µm thickness were fixed onto 0.1% poly-L-lysine pre-treated glass slides. Slides were treated with 0.125 mg/ml of proteinase at 37 °C for 10 min after being deparaffinized and rehydrated. Slides were further incubated in 4% paraformaldehyde solution and acetic anhydride solution (0.5% acetic anhydride, 100 mM triethanolamine), each for 10 min, followed by incubation in a series of ethanol solutions for dehydration. The hybridization process was carried out in hybridization buffer with probes at 50 °C overnight. Slides were further washed in SSC (Saline Sodium Citrate), RNase A (0.02 mg/ml) and NTE (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 M NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA) buffers to remove free probes. For immunological staining, slides were incubated in DIG buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.15 M NaCl, 1% bovine serum albumin, 0.3% Triton X-100) containing 0.1% Anti-Digoxigenin-AP (Cat. no. 11093274910, Roche) at 25 °C for 2 h. The slides were washed and stained by NBT/BCIP (4-nitro blue tetrazolium chloride/5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate) solution (100 mM Tris-HCl pH9.5, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM MgCl2, 0.15 mg/ml NBT, 0.075 mg/ml BCIP) up to 48 h according to the darkness. Slides were mounted by Neutral Balsam prior to observation. All the primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 4.

## Calcium imaging
Fresh or treated pistils were cut with razor blades and taped onto glass slides covered by proper solutions to maintain humidity. Movies were captured with a fluorescence stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus) equipped with a 1× objective lens (SDFPLAPO1XPF, Olympus) and a sCMOS camera (DP74, Olympus). For stigma imaging, white LED illumination was used. For calcium imaging, the GFP-based Ca2+ indicator was excited using a mercury lamp, a 470/40 nm excitation filter, and a 500 nm dichroic mirror42. The fluorescence signals were captured at 1.5–4× magnification with intervals of 8–16 frames/s using CellSens imaging software (Olympus). The fractional fluorescence changes (ΔF/F) were calculated according to the equation ΔF/F = (F − F0)/F0, where F represents fluorescence at a certain time and F0 denotes the average baseline fluorescence determined by the average of F over the first 10 frames of the recording before touch. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc.). For whisker plotting, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test was applied for statistical analysis; for measurement of calcium fluorescence, two-way ANOVA was used.

## Treatments
For pharmacological treatments, LaCl3·7H2O and GdCl3·6H2O were dissolved in water to a final concentration of 50 mM. EGTA was dissolved in water to a final concentration of 50 mM, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 with NaOH and HCl. The pistils of T. fournieri were dipped into these solutions in for 2.5 h for La3+ and Gd3+ treatment, 5 h for EGTA treatment. Pistils were transferred to a piece of filter paper to remove excess liquid prior to observation. For electric shock treatment, a piezoelectric lighter was used to create a spark and stigmas were placed between two metal electrodes. For osmotic shock treatments, freshly cut pistils were dipped into tubes with different concentrations of Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal salts with vitamins (M519, PhytoTech) solutions, 0.05×, 0.5×, and 5 × MS solutions were used as hypoosmotic, control and hyperosmotic treatment, respectively. After 30 min, pistils were transferred to a piece of filter paper to remove excess liquid prior to observation.
