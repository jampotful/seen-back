Physiological and transcriptomic assessment of Arabidopsis identifies two distinct gravity signaling systems.
Canaday ELJ, Meyers A, Scinto-Madonich NJ, Wyatt SE, Wolverton C. (2026). Plant physiology. . doi:10.1093/plphys/kiag095. Licence: cc-by. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Physiological and transcriptomic assessment of Arabidopsis identifies two distinct gravity signaling systems

## Abstract
AbstractPlants employ multiple sensing and signaling mechanisms to inform their growth. Earth's gravity is a constant force, which plants perceive and use to direct growth. Despite the ubiquity of gravitropism in plants, the mechanism for signal initiation remains a point of debate. The starch statolith hypothesis suggests that dense amyloplasts sediment within perceptive cells to initiate signaling. However, the persistence of gravitropism in starchless pgm-1 mutants of Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) suggests that plants still sense gravity even without amyloplast sedimentation, hinting toward a second mechanism by which plants can perceive gravity. In an attempt to isolate this mechanism, we exposed seedlings to a range of fractional gravities from 0.003 to 1 g; this showed that plants without starch require a much larger force to induce gravitropic signaling than those with starch-filled statoliths. We used the difference in final root angle between genotypes after simultaneous application of blue light and gravity to estimate the relative contributions of the two systems to gravity sensing, demonstrating that starchless signaling can produce 51.7% of the wild-type response. Transcriptomics across the gravity gradient showed a distinctive shift in RNA regulation coinciding with the force required for starchless response. Mutants of these highly regulated genes showed gravity-specific defects and were largely involved in cell-to-cell communication and extracellular signaling. These data provide molecular evidence for both starch-dependent and starch-independent gravity signaling within a vascular plant, as well as the molecular components used in the starch-independent response.

## Introduction
Plants, like all life on Earth, have evolved under a consistent gravitational force of 1 g or 9.8 m s−2. This force acts on plants as a persistent and ubiquitous cue that directs the growth of axial organs, along with other less persistent directional cues such as light, temperature, moisture gradients, and variation in the concentration of mineral ions. Gravity influences plants in numerous ways, but primarily through regulating the adaptive growth and developmental response pathways that give rise to the architecture of the plant body. The physical force of gravity appears to exert its influence on plant primary organs predominantly by inducing the sedimentation of dense, starch-filled plastids (amyloplasts), found in specialized cells known as statocytes (Sack 1997). The identification of the dense statoliths in the root caps of various plant species (Ciesielski 1872; Darwin and Darwin 1880) and the ensuing characterization of these sensitive cells (Noll 1892; Némec 1900) led to the proposal of the Starch–Statolith theory of gravity perception, which continues to gain experimental support.
The identification of starch biosynthesis mutants in Arabidopsis provided a noninvasive means of testing the contribution of amylopast sedimentation to gravity sensing. Depending on the assay method employed, starchless mutants show slightly or significantly reduced gravity responses (Caspar and Pickard 1989; Kiss et al 1989), and intermediate-starch mutants show an intermediate gravitropic response (MacCleery and Kiss 1999). This difference in response appears to be specific to amyloplasts functioning as statoliths, as the magnitude of amyloplast sedimentation varies as a function of starch content and is correlated with the magnitude of gravitropic response (MacCleery and Kiss 1999). Experiments that displaced amyloplasts within the gravisensitive cells of hypocotyls or roots using a high-gradient magnetic field resulted in differential growth responses similar to those of a gravitropic response, further supporting the link between amyloplast position and directional growth (Kuznetsov and Hasenstein 1996; Kuznetsov and Hasenstein 1997). Taken together, these experiments provided strong evidence for the role of statolith sedimentation in a plant's ability to sense the direction of gravitational force.
Along with the reduced response of starchless mutants in gravitropic reorientation experiments, starchless and other gravitropic response mutants have shown enhanced phototropic response (Okada and Shimura 1992; Vitha et al 2000; Kimura et al 2018). In these experiments, roots were grown vertically along an agar surface with unilateral light perpendicular to the gravity vector. The root tip angle was measured, as degrees deviation from the gravity vector, after 1 to 3 d. Mutants with a final tip angle closer to the vector of light than that of gravity were interpreted as having reduced ability to sense gravity. Later experiments demonstrated that plant roots rapidly degrade statoliths when provided a hydrotropic stimulus, dampening their gravity perception to allow preferential growth toward the perceived moisture (Nakayama et al 2012). While it is difficult to definitively prove a causal relationship between attenuated statolith sedimentation and the augmentation of other tropic responses, the experimental evidence supporting that relationship is compelling. There does remain an alternative hypothesis that this phenotype was the result of increased phototropic response, independent of gravity sensing (Vitha et al 2000). Addressing this hypothesis would require a comparison of phototropic response in the absence of a strong gravity signal, which was not possible given the limitations in technology when these publications were released.
The rate of gravitropic response also varies as a function of the inclination of the organ in the gravitational field away from its gravitropic setpoint angle. This relationship can be described by a sine function approximating the best fit curve representing the response rate as a function of angle and holds for both shoots and roots across a wide range of plant species and measurement methodologies (Sachs 1882; Larsen 1969; Perbal and Perbal 1976; Iino et al 1996; Mullen et al 2000; Chauvet et al 2016; Roychoudhry et al 2025). However, the relationship between tip angle and gravitropic response rate is uncoupled in roots of the starchless mutant pgm-1 (Wolverton et al 2011). In these experiments, when root tips were constrained at a constant angle relative to the gravity vector using a vertical rotating stage, wild-type roots showed the characteristic dependence of response rate on tip angle, with a maximum response rate approaching 30° h−1, while pgm-1 roots showed a constant 8° h−1 regardless of their angle of inclination (Wolverton et al 2011). This persistent gravity response in starchless mutants occurred in the absence of an auxin gradient across the root cap as indicated with both the DR5::GFP reporter (Wolverton et al 2011) and the DII-VENUS degradation-based auxin reporter (Band et al 2012). In addition, root tip inclination angle, statolith sedimentation, and differential auxin transport were recently shown to be highly correlated with each other and with the relocalization of specific members of the PIN family of auxin transporters (Roychoudhry et al 2025). This link is abolished in pgm-1 roots where statoliths did not sediment (Roychoudhry et al 2025).
The mechanism by which statoliths position within the cell may be acting as a polarity signal was shown to involve the translocation of a LAZY family protein from the outer envelope of the plastid to the plasma membrane (Chen et al 2023; Nishimura et al 2023). This translocation was observed within 15 min of root tip reorientation, well after the 3 min necessary for statolith sedimentation and preceding the visible auxin gradient as reported by the R2D2 reporter system, which was observed between 16 and 30 min. While the role of the LAZY proteins in regulating gravity responses remains open, they appear to be required for regulating asymmetric auxin transport via the recruitment and physical interaction with RLD proteins (Furutani et al 2020).
The reduced and constant gravitropic response with a lack of tip angle dependence coupled with the lack of auxin gradient formation in starchless roots suggest the contribution of a gravity-sensing mechanism that functions independently of the well-characterized statolith mechanism. The objective of the present study was to characterize this hypothetical gravity-sensing mechanism and the well-known statolith system with respect to their activation threshold(s) and capacity for differential growth regulation across a range of simulated gravity conditions at the level of the whole seedling using image analysis. These physiological response data were then used to guide the analysis and interpretation of gravity-induced gene expression changes in distinct organ regions grown under a range of simulated gravity treatments using RNA-seq methods.

## Results

## Root phototropism in microgravity reveals the contribution of the nonplastid gravity sensing system
Roots of most plant species show phototropic growth away from a unilateral blue light source, resulting in a growth trajectory on Earth that is presumed to represent the combined effects of the gravity and light vectors. These competing responses were used to quantify the influence of light and gravity on organ orientation. On Earth, plants were grown for 72 h under constant white light from above, then stimulated with unilateral blue light perpendicular to the gravity vector. Roots responded by orienting at an angle between the light and gravity vectors (Fig. 1e), as has been shown in previous studies (Okada and Shimura 1992; Vitha et al 2000; Pang et al 2023). Wild-type roots reached an average of 42.7°, while roots of the pgm-1 mutant averaged 57.8°, a statistically significant difference (Fig. 1e), (Student's t-test, t(104) = 4.26, adjusted P < 0.001) and in agreement with previous studies (Vitha et al 2000).

The difference observed between the final angles of Col-0 and pgm-1 roots, with the starchless roots reaching an angle more highly diverged from vertical, has been attributed to the reduced gravity sensitivity of these mutants leading to a larger contribution of phototropism to the final growth angle (Okada and Shimura 1992; Vitha et al 2000). This was tested directly by exposing roots to directional light stimulation in the microgravity environment of the International Space Station (ISS). Seedlings were first grown with roots directed by simulated 1 g and directional white light using the European Modular Cultivation System (EMCS), a centrifuge supporting plant growth aboard the ISS (Fig. 1a to 1d). Seedling reorientation was achieved by a 12 h unilateral blue light treatment, resulting in root tips oriented approximately perpendicular to the previously present simulated gravity vector, with both genotypes attaining an average tip angle that was almost parallel to the new light vector. Roots of Col-0 and pgm-1 attained average angles of 78.3° and 82.8°, respectively (Fig. 1e), a statistically insignificant difference between the two genotypes (Student's t-test, t(478) = 1.59, adjusted P = 0.56). These results support the interpretation that the difference in final angles between wild-type and starchless roots following phototropic stimulation is due to the reduced gravity sensing and not an enhancement of phototropism in starchless roots.
To directly test the interaction between light and gravity, seedlings were simultaneously exposed to unilateral blue light and simulated gravity between 0.526 and 1.0 g, depending on the position of the seedlings along the radius of rotation on the centrifuge arm of the EMCS (Fig. 1a and 1b). Under this treatment, roots of each genotype attained final angles almost identical to those attained on the ground under Earth 1 g, again showing a statistically significant difference between genotypes (Student's t-test, t(76.9) = 5.25, adjusted P < 0.001) but no difference within the Col-0 or pgm-1 genotypes when comparing between the artificial gravities approach 1 g in flight and Earth 1 g (Student's t-test, t(106) = −2.29, adjusted P = 0.12, t(96.4) = 0.61, adjusted P = 1). The more vertical angles attained by wild-type roots in simulated gravity indicate that they are more sensitive to gravity than starchless roots, consistent with previous interpretations from studies on Earth. The difference in tip angle observed between starchless mutants grown in microgravity and those grown in either real or simulated gravitational environments indicates that gravity sensing in these mutants remains intact and consistently reduced relative to wild-type roots. Taken together, these results demonstrate that gravity attenuates the phototropic response of roots and that roots lacking statolith sedimentation have reduced but persistent gravity sensing. Under the conditions of these experiments, the contribution of statolith-independent gravity sensing resulted in root tips oriented approximately 21° more toward the gravity vector than in microgravity, and the addition of the functioning statolith-based system in wild-type roots provided another 20° of gravity-directed orientation. The impact of the presence of statoliths on gravity sensing can also be inferred by comparing the effect size, a statistical measure of the biological importance of any difference in the response of the two genotypes. Cohen's d for tip angles between Col-0 and pgm-1 in Earth 1 g conditions was 0.82, in the simulated 1 g environment on the ISS, Cohen's d between the genotypes was 1.06, a similarly large effect indicating preserved importance of statoliths for directing growth in both gravity treatments.

## Fractional gravity treatments reveal thresholds for signal activation
To characterize the sensory properties of statolith-dependent and independent gravity sensing, seedlings were grown in a range of simulated gravity conditions using centrifugation in microgravity through the use of the EMCS on the ISS. All seedlings were grown for 3 d under simulated 1 g conditions with continuous white light from the top of the seed cassette (Fig. 1a to 1b). Seedling reorientation was achieved by a 12 h unilateral blue light treatment in microgravity, resulting in root tips oriented approximately perpendicular to the simulated gravity vector as detailed previously (Fig. 1c). Simulated gravity treatments were then carried out in darkness. Each root tip was qualitatively scored on a binary scale for gravity responsiveness (showing a change in tip angle toward the gravity vector) and evidence of growth (showing an increase in length between 84 and 120 h experiment times). Scoring was carried out independently by two individuals and analyzed using a binomial logistic regression.
More than 50% of wild-type roots showed gravity responsiveness at the lowest acceleration treatment achievable by the centrifuge (0.003 g) and continuing across all treatments carried out (Fig. 2a, Fig. S1). In contrast, starchless mutants are projected to require 0.090 g of force before showing a consistent ability to respond to gravity (Fig. 2a, Fig. S1). Based on these results, 12 intensities of gravity were selected for a thorough phenotypic and transcriptomic analysis to pair the phenotypic intensity of statolith-dependent and -independent responses with the molecular foundation during this response.

## Wild-type and starchless roots require district force to achieve full gravitropic responses
Gravitropic responses are often represented by plotting the angle of the responsive organ as a function of time, but the constraints of the flight hardware coupled with the desire to maintain darkness throughout the period of simulated gravity application limited image capture to the start and end of the treatments. In lieu of time-course analysis, only the final root tip angle was recorded, and each result was compared to the simulated 1 g treatment for the respective genotype. Gravity intensities with phenotypes equivalent to the 1 g treatment were considered capable of a full response. Only roots having an initial tip angle greater than 20° after direction by blue light were included in this phase of analysis as 20° was the minimum angle necessary to induce a visible gravitropic response in roots (Mullen et al 2000).
As multiple centrifuge runs were required to achieve all the fractional gravities, it was first confirmed that neither the run nor cassette position had a significant effect on starting angle and that all gravity intensities had an equivalent starting angle (Type III Anova, F(3, 299) = 0.49, P = 0.75, F(4, 299) = 0.47, P = 0.70, and F(1, 299) = 0.46, P = 0.50 respectively). This consistency validates that any difference in final tip angle is a function of the gravity applied after stimulation and that there were no differences in starting angle between each treatment group.
Consistent with the binary observations, wild-type roots showed some alignment toward the gravity vector across all treatments, with increasing accelerations resulting in decreasing variance and greater convergence toward vertical. Wild-type roots reached a full 1 g response at only 0.011 g (Fig. 2b, Table S1). In contrast, starchless roots showed limited directional growth toward the gravity vector in treatments below 0.29 g, with root tips reaching a phenotype consistent with 1 g at intensities of 0.38 g and above (Fig. 2b, Table S1).
When combined, the binary response shows that a force of less than 0.003 g is necessary for statolith-dependent signal initiation and a force of 0.011 g and can produce a full 1 g response. In the starchless pgm-1 line, a force of 0.09 g is necessary for roots to begin showing any consistent gravity sensitivity and increasing the gravity to 0.38 g can produce the 1 g response. These data provide a range of gravities required for statolith-based initiation in wild-type roots, as well as the greater force required for statolith-independent signaling in starchless pgm-1 mutants.

## Sequential transcriptome analysis
Twelve representative levels were selected from the 25 gravity intensities based on the results of the gravity response phenotyping. At each of these gravities, plants were dissected into root tips, mature roots without tips, hypocotyls, and cotyledons. mRNA was extracted separately from each tissue for analysis of changes in transcription across a gradient of gravity intensities. Root tips in particular showed strong transcriptional shifts in correlation with phenotypic changes in gravity response (Fig. 2c). An impulse analysis found that a large group of genes abruptly increased in root tip expression (z score >1) between 0.29 and 0.526 g, the range in which pgm-1 began showing a phenotypic response to gravity (Supplementary Dataset S1).
Ontological analysis of the genes found in the Col-0 root tips identified an enrichment for DNA and RNA-based regulation (Table 1). This included multiple transcription factors and RNA nucleotide-binding and modifying regulatory proteins. In the pgm-1 background, where plants begin showing gravity responsiveness, the RNA environment showed major shifts in the regulation of the extracellular space (Table 1). The assembly and regulation of cell-to-cell junctions were highly enriched in the pgm-1 root tips at 0.38 g. Several genes transcribing extracellular proteins, membrane proteins, and cell wall modification proteins saw higher expression at 0.38 g as well. At 0.526 g, the six most significantly regulated ontologies are all related to oxygen levels and reactive oxygen species (ROS) stress (Table 1). Starchless mutants upregulated multiple transcripts associated with the removal of ROS, especially extracellular ROS through the action of class III peroxidases.

Col-0 root tips showed changes in transcriptional regulatory processes around 0.38 g, while pgm-1 saw changes in major cell remodeling processes. The contrast between these genotypes demonstrates that while both are responding to the changes in gravity at 0.38 g, the response is different between Col-0 plants responding to a change in the intensity of gravity and pgm-1 plants only starting to show gravitropic growth. The pgm-1 transcriptome showed a sudden activation of cell wall remodeling and cell-to-cell communication, as well as the modification of the extracellular space. While many of these processes are possibly a result of the changes in growth as gravity signaling results in remodeling of root architecture, it also points to the potential that statolith-independent gravity signaling involves cell-to-cell signaling and could be closely tied with membrane and extracellular proteins. The single time point of transcription and phenotype data makes it impossible to disentangle signaling and response with computational approaches alone, but this list of genes does provide targets for mutant analysis to investigate the specificity of these processes to gravity signaling.

## Identification of additional gravity mutants
Using a selection of the genes identified during the flight experiment, knockout mutants were identified and tested for gravitropic response to validate that these genes are specific to gravity signaling and not only activated by general growth changes during response. Genes from either genotype, which showed a marked change in expression at or above 0.38 g, a point of significant change in gravity response in starchless mutants, were selected to confirm that this gene block represented a response to gravity. The 0.38 g gravity level enables an initial disentanglement of statolith-independent gravity signaling from starch-based mechanisms, though a deeper analysis of multiple mutants and signaling interactions will be needed to assemble a mechanistic pathway. These genes do represent components of a gravitropic response at statolith-independent activation levels.
Homozygous mutant lines in the Col-0 were screened for the ability of roots to respond to gravity after a single reorientation stimulus or under continuous reorientation. Single rotation stimulation resolved differences in total response between wild-type and mutants over 3 h, while continuous rotation assessed the ability of seedlings to respond to constant gravity signal initiation and quantified the net curvature as plants responded to repeated changes in their position relative to gravity (Fig. S2). Mutants that deviated from the wild-type response were considered gravity mutants. Of the gravity mutants found through this process, a striking number act in cell-to-cell communication. These mutants are defective in direct cell-to-cell connections as well as regulation of the broader extracellular environment.
At the barrier of cells, two mutants in predicted lipid transferases (AT4G23670 and AT4G33550) were defective in gravity response, with both showing reduced response after gravity stimulation (Fig. 3). One of these, AT4G23670, has been reported previously as up-regulated within 2 min of gravistimulation in Arabidopsis roots (Kimbrough 2004). A knockout of the steroid binding protein MSBP1, which was highly downregulated at 0.38 g, showed increased response to gravity under continuous reorientation (Fig. 4), which complements previous reports of overexpression lines of MSBP1 having altered gravitropic response (Yang et al 2008). The mRNA for this gene is also believed to be cell-to-cell mobile (Thieme et al 2015), pointing to the possibility for gravity to be sensed not only within a single cell, but through a combination of extracellular signals both via steroid sensing and extracellular mRNA transfer.

The importance of cell-to-cell mobile products can also be seen as multiple peptide transporters in the UMAMIT family were identified in the flight data and confirmed via mutant screening. Both umamit17 and umamit28 mutants were found to have reduced gravitropic responses after a single gravity stimulation (Fig. 3). UMAMIT17 (AT4G08300) was previously shown to be up-regulated during weight-induced secondary growth in Arabidopsis stems (Ko et al 2004), which may suggest a role for it in mechanical signaling. On the other hand, mutants in PRX40, an extracellular peroxidase that is highly expressed in the pericycle of the distal elongation zone of the root (Brady et al 2007; Winter et al 2007), were faster to respond to gravity under continuous stimulation (Fig. 4). All of these mutants demonstrate the importance of cell-to-cell communication in gravitropic signaling, especially in the second wave of signaling observed at 0.38 g (Fig. 5).

## Discussion
The work reported in this study supports a gravity perception threshold in plants with intact statoliths at or around 0.003 g. This is the highest sensitivity assay for plant gravity perception carried out to date, and it tested the limits of perception in a microgravity environment. Previous studies using the same hardware applied greater accelerations and did not resolve a minimum force necessary to induce directional growth (Herranz et al 2019; Vandenbrink et al 2019). The force identified here is in the same range as previous estimates for roots in ground-based experiments using clinostat-centrifuges (Shen-Miller et al 1968; Laurinavicius et al 1998). In contrast, roots lacking starch-filled statoliths failed to respond to such a low acceleration treatment, but showed directional growth responses beginning at or around 0.09 g and above (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). Given previous experiments have shown that starchless plastids fail to sediment under 1 g conditions (Roychoudhry et al 2025) or in centrifugation treatments (MacCleery and Kiss 1999; Fitzelle and Kiss 2001), these results imply a mechanism of gravity perception separate from statolith sedimentation or position.
Previously observed changes in cell growth, proliferation, and organization of the nucleus between 0.17 and 0.38 g provide additional evidence for a gravity-sensing threshold in this range (Matía et al 2010; Manzano et al 2018). These results were obtained in the same flight hardware as used in the present study through the application of simulated gravity by centrifugation. Similar results were also reported in ground-based studies using a clinostat or random positioning machine to simulate partial gravity conditions (Boucheron-Dubuisson et al 2016). These intensities of gravity might be of particular interest considering their relevance to space research and exploration with lunar and Martian gravity at 0.16 and 0.38 g, respectively.
To understand the contributions toward the full gravitropic response provided by statoliths at the first threshold and the statolith-independent response at the second threshold, the gravitropic responses of Col-0 and pgm-1 were compared under simultaneous treatment with unidirectional blue light and simulated gravity. Given that wild-type and starchless roots both attained an equivalent tip angle of approximately 80° when light was applied under microgravity, any difference in tip angle when plants are treated with both unilateral blue light and gravity can be concluded to be the result of gravity response alone and is not the result of differences in phototropic response as was previously postulated. These results indicate that roots lacking starch-filled statoliths can achieve approximately 51.7% of the wild-type root curvature response after 12 h of treatment with simulated gravity (a change of 21.8° vs 42.2° in final root angle). This suggests that the statolith-independent mechanism of gravity perception, which has heretofore gone largely unrecognized, plays a significant role in the overall growth and orientation of roots.
As a first step toward identifying the molecular and cellular mechanisms involved in this system of gravity perception, RNA-seq analyses were used to profile gene expression across the gravity treatments. Distinct gene expression profiles were identified in the root tips of both genotypes approaching the levels of force required to activate the statolith independent gravity response (Fig. 2). Pathways involved in cell to cell communication and extracellular connections were highly enriched (Table 1), suggesting that statolith independent gravity signaling relies on an extra- or multicellular response instead of the subcellular signaling associated with statolith positioning (Chen et al 2023; Nishimura et al 2023). A reverse genetic approach was used to test the contributions of genes from several of the gene ontology groups represented. Results indicate that genes encoding extracellular peroxidases, putative amino acid transporters, and membrane regulators all have unique gravity-altered mutant phenotypes and suggest extracellular processes could represent a key component in statolith independent gravity signaling (Fig. 5). As substrates, regulators and transported molecules which these proteins regulate are yet to be fully described, the mechanisms by which these genes regulate gravitropic growth remains an open field for investigation.
These data represent molecular evidence for gravity signaling, which is initiated and carried out without starch statoliths and presents a fundamental mechanism that would allow plants to respond to gravity when starch stores are low. As starch formation is reliant on photosynthetic output, plants could be limited in starch due to low light or energy storage limiting conditions and links between gravitropic response and photosynthetic capability have already been found (Hanba et al 2025). As plants have multiple mechanisms for initiating and directing phototropic growth (Christie and Murphy 2013; Haga and Sakai 2023), it should not be surprising that gravitropic growth could also have more than one method of signal initiation. The idea of statolith independent signaling has been proposed previously in both vascular and non vascular plants (Caspar and Pickard 1989; Wayne et al 1992; Hashimoto-Sugimoto et al 2025), the overwhelming evidence for statoliths in gravity signal initiation (Kuznetsov and Hasenstein 1996; Blancaflor et al 1999; Jiao et al 2021; Chen et al 2023) has often overshadowed the possibility of an additional mechanism when the initially identified mechanism has yet to be fully described (Wyatt and Kiss 2013; Kiss 2025).
The results presented here support the starch statolith mechanism of gravity perception in plants and identify its activation threshold at ∼0.003 g, as well as the presence of a previously uncharacterized mechanism of gravity perception activated between 0.09 and 0.38 g that operates independently of statolith position. A pattern of expression changes in the transcriptomic data coincides with the gravity-directed growth response observations, and mutant phenotypes support the involvement of extracellular pathways in gravity perception.

## Materials and methods

## Plant materials and experimental hardware for spaceflight experiments
Spaceflight experiments and ground controls were conducted in hardware designed for the EMCS, a cell culture chamber with stacked centrifuges that was installed on the ISS. Experiments were carried out in specialized hardware used previously for experiments with young seedlings of Arabidopsis thaliana and described in detail elsewhere (Correll et al 2005; Millar et al 2010). The hardware enabled the preparation and assembly of seed cassettes containing dry, dormant seed in advance of launch and their automated hydration once installed in the cell culture centrifuge facility in orbit.
Seeds of Arabidopsis Col-0 wild-type and pgm-1 mutant were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (Table S1). The pgm-1 starchless phenotype was validated by staining root tips of 4-d-old seedlings with I2KI and confirming the absence of starch with a microscope. In addition, the genotypes of the mutant and wild-type were confirmed using allele-specific PCR to ensure the presence of the homozygous mutant allele in the mutant and the lack of the mutant allele in wild-type seed stock. A single nucleotide polymorphism (TGG to TGA) gives rise to the pgm-1 mutation at position 1306 at the PGM locus (Periappuram et al 2000). Primers designed to exploit this single base substitution produce PCR extension products of different sizes by anchoring the 3′ base of the oligonucleotide at the substitution site and introducing a base-pair mismatch 3 to 4 bases 5′ to this site (Gaudet et al 2009) (Table S2). This strategy produces highly allele-specific extension products in a reaction that includes all three primers. By adding a random 10-bp tail to the 5′ end of the wild-type-specific primer, the difference in product size (146 or 136 bp) can be visualized on a 4% (m/v) agarose gel (Fig. S3).
After confirmation of the genotype of stocks, seeds were surface sterilized for 5 min in 70% EtOH (v/v) containing 0.01% Triton-X 100 (v/v) (Sigma), followed by two 1 min rinses in 100% EtOH (v/v), after which seeds were aspirated onto sterile blotter paper and air dried in a laminar flow hood for 1 h. Dry seeds were affixed to polyethersulfone gridded membrane (Whatman) using a 1% (m/v) guar gum suspension and allowed to air dry for 30 min, as described in greater detail elsewhere (Meyers et al 2022). Seeded membranes were attached with guar gum to blotter paper that had previously been saturated with Arabidopsis growth media (½ MS salts, 1 mM MES buffer, pH 5.7) and allowed to dry completely. After affixing the seeded membrane to the blotter paper, seed cassettes were capped and sealed with aluminum foil tape (3M). Cassette caps included ventilation holes covered in activated carbon cloth to filter any volatile organic compounds, including ethylene, and Gore-Tex tape for gas permeability without water loss.

## Spaceflight growth conditions and experimental design
Experiments were initiated by hydrating each seed cassette in experiment containers mounted in the EMCS facility. Following hydration, imbibing seeds were exposed to continuous centrifuge rotation at and approaching 1 g equivalent to 1 g and white LED light (approximately 20 to 30 µmol m−2 s−1) until experiment hour 12, unilateral red LED light (approximately 10 to 20 µmol m−2 s−1) to promote germination between hours 12 and 24, then continuous white light until hour 72 (Fig. 1a). After 72 h of growth, the centrifuge rotors were stopped and seedlings were stimulated with unilateral blue LED illumination (approximately 10 to 30 µmol m−2 s−1) oriented perpendicular to the simulated gravity vector and original growth direction of the seedlings. This allowed the seedlings to experience a saturating unilateral blue light treatment under the free-fall microgravity environment of Earth orbit. For some experiments, the unilateral blue light treatment was combined with centrifugation to produce an interaction between the light and simulated gravity vectors for 12 h. For these experiments, the combined treatment was followed by a unilateral blue light stimulation in microgravity conditions for 12 h. Immediately following the unilateral blue light stimulation, centrifugation resumed to produce simulated gravity conditions from a minimum of 0.003 g to a maximum of 1.0 g across different experimental runs (Fig. 1a, Fig. S4).

## Spaceflight sample image acquisition and analysis
Partially overlapping images of each seed cassette were obtained throughout the experiment by a CCD camera mounted within each centrifuge using the ambient light from the white or blue LED arrays or an infrared LED array mounted near the camera. Image captures for response analysis were 720 × 480 pixels, with an effective resolution between 14.43 and 15.8 pixels per mm. All image analyses were carried out in ImageJ (Rasband 1997). Time-ordered sequences of images were loaded in ImageJ as a virtual stack and image registration was performed using the “Translation” feature extraction model and the “Translation—no deformation” registration model in the “Registration” plugin. Root tip angles were measured using the angle measurement tool. Visual inspection using the 3 mm × 3 mm grid lines printed on the growth membranes ensured alignment to the orientation of the gravity vector produced by centrifugation.

## Dissection and RNA extraction
Following the 5 d growth experiment in the EMCS aboard the ISS, seed cassettes were removed from experiment containers, stowed in a −80 °C onboard freezer (MELFI) and returned to Earth aboard the SpaceX CRS-14 Dragon capsule on 5 May 2018. Samples were shipped on dry ice within 2 days of return to Earth and were maintained at −80 °C until processing. Prior to downstream tissue processing, intact frozen seedlings on growth membranes were flooded with RLT buffer from the Qiagen Rneasy RNA extraction kit (Qiagen) and dissected in place into four tissue sections, consisting of cotyledons, hypocotyls, root tips (apical 3 mm), and the remainder of the root. The tissue sections from individual seedlings were pooled with 7 to 14 other sections from the same treatment and genotype and RNA extraction was carried out following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA libraries were generated using the Takara SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit, with indexing accomplished with the Nextera DNA Flex Library Kit. RNA sequencing was completed using Illumina NovaSeq S2 flowcells at Nationwide Children's Hospital (Columbus, OH, USA).

## Transcriptomic analysis
Primers and low-quality sequences were removed from reads using Trim Galore! 0.6 with a quality cut-off of 26 to account for the low RINs, all other parameters were left as the default. Paired reads were aligned using version 2.6 of STAR and the Araport11 genome annotations. Gene Counts were normalized and sequentially analyzed using ImpulseDE2 version 0.99.10. Full results, including z-scores for each gene at each intensity of gravity is shown in Supplemental data S1. Ontological analysis and subcellular localization were performed on the output gene lists using g:Profiler2 version 0.2.3 and SUBA5.

## Plant materials for mutant identification
T-DNA insertional mutants (Alonso et al 2003) for selected genes were identified and ordered from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (Table S2). PCR primers for genotyping were designed using the Salk Institute Genomic Analysis Laboratory (SIGnAL) primer design tool (http://signal.salk.edu/tdnaprimers.2.html). To confirm mutants contained knockouts, expression was also measured using RT-PCR, with primers designed against the 5-prime end of the predicted mRNA (Table S3). PCR experiments were performed using purified DNA (GeneJET Plant Genomic DNA Purification Kit, Thermo Fisher) and the GoTaq Green PCR MasterMix (Promega Corp) or the Phire Plant Direct PCR MasterMix (Thermo Fisher). RNA was purified using the Monarch Total RNA MiniPrep Kit (New England Biolabs) and cDNA was synthesized using the iScript Reverse Transcription SuperMix (BioRad). PCR was carried out as described above.
Phenotyping experiments were carried out under standard conditions (½ strength MS basal salts, 1 mM MES buffer, pH 5.7 to 5.8, and 1% agar (m/v)). Seeds were surface sanitized with 70% EtOH (v/v) (5 min) followed by two brief 3 min rinses with 95% EtOH (v/v), after which they were aspirated onto sterile filter paper in a laminar flow hood, allowed to dry, and placed into 60 mm Petri plates with growth media. Plates were wrapped with micropore tape (3M) and refrigerated at 4 °C for 1 to 5 nights before use. To initiate germination, plates were removed from the refrigerator and placed vertically under white LED lamps (80 to 100 µmol m−2 s−1) with a 16 h light and 8 h dark photoperiod. Plates were oriented such that roots elongated along the surface of the agar. Seedlings were used for experiments 4 d after initiation of germination.

## Mutant phenotypic analysis
Growth response experiments were carried out by mounting plates, in the same vertical orientation in which they had been grown, onto holders attached to a stepper motor to control rotation treatments. For free response experiments, plates were rotated 90° after a 30 min rest period. For continuous rotation experiments, plates were rotated 90° after the same rest period, then subjected to small rotations equaling 15° h−1 to approximate the stimulus required to maintain the root tip at a highly stimulated angle over a long time period, as in Mullen et al (2000) and Wolverton et al (2011). Under both conditions, plants were illuminated with white light perpendicular to the gravity vector to allow for clear photography. Time-lapse images were captured with a Raspberry Pi HQ camera and an Arducam 8 to 50 mm C-Mount Zoom Lens giving a capture resolution of 65 to 70 pixels per mm. Root tip angles were analyzed using the FIJI distribution of ImageJ (Rasband 1997).

## Statistical analysis of phenotypic measurements
For phototropic growth and mutant phenotyping, seedlings met the assumptions of normality and equal variance. A Student's t-test was used with the Bonferroni method to account for the repeated testing. Both the t-test and correction were completed using the base stats package in R (version 4.4.1). The logistic regression was calculated using the glm function and the binomial family to account for the binary response data. The gravity variable was log-transformed before generating the model to account for the skew toward low intensities of gravity in the experimental design. Functions were in the base stats package of R (version 4.4.1). Validation that starting conditions had no significant effect on final root tip angle was confirmed by type III Anova using the car package (version 3.1-3). Comparison of flight phenotypes within genotypes and across the gravity gradient had higher levels of variance requiring a Welch's t-test for comparisons to 1 g (stats version 4.4.1).
Results of each test were recorded with P-values and where appropriate, differences of means, degrees of freedom, and test statistics (Table S1).

Dry seeds affixed to a membrane substrate were hydrated at hour 0 and grown under the indicated conditions. c) seedlings growth is directed by the combination of directional light (hν) and gravity (g) from hours 0 to 72. After directional blue light, fractional gravities are returned without light, and gravitropic curvature can be measured. d) Image of plants grown on the EMCS hardware experiencing directional blue light and demonstrating phototropic curvature. e) Circular histograms showing the final angle of roots after 12 h of perpendicular blue light.
