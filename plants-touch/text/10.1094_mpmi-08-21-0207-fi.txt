ABSTRACT

17

Plants sense and respond to molecular signals associated with the presence of pathogens and

18

their virulence factors. Mechanical signals generated during pathogenic invasion may also be

19

important, but their contributions have rarely been studied. Here we investigate the potential role

20

of a mechanosensitive ion channel, MscS-Like (MSL)10, in defense against the bacterial

21

pathogen Pseudomonas syringae in Arabidopsis thaliana. We previously showed that

22

overexpression of MSL10-GFP, phospho-mimetic versions of MSL10, and the gain-of-function

23

allele msl10-3G all produce dwarfing, spontaneous cell death, and the hyperaccumulation of

24

reactive oxygen species. These phenotypes are shared by many autoimmune mutants and are

25

frequently suppressed by growth at high temperature in those lines. Here, we found that the same

26

was true for all three MSL10 hypermorphs. In addition, we show that the SGT1/RAR1/HSP90

27

co-chaperone complex was required for dwarfing and ectopic cell death, PAD4 and SID2 were

28

partially required, and the immune regulators EDS1 and NDR1 were dispensable. All MSL10

29

hypermorphs

30

Pto DC3000, Pto DC3000 expressing the avirulence genes avrRpt2 or avrRpm1, but not Pto

31

DC3000 hrpL, and showed an accelerated induction of PR1 expression compared to wild-type

32

plants. Null msl10-1 mutants were delayed in PR1 induction and displayed modest susceptibility

33

to infection by COR deficient Pst. Finally, stomatal closure was reduced in msl10-1 loss-of-

34

function mutants in response to Pst COR−. These data show that MSL10 modulates pathogen

35

responses and begin to address the possibility that mechanical signals are exploited by the plant

36

for pathogen perception.

INTRODUCTION

47

Plant immune receptors achieve a two-tiered response to pathogens (reviewed in (Chiang and

48

Coaker 2015; Saijo et al. 2018; Jones et al. 2016)). The first line of defense relies on plasma

49

membrane-localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which facilitate molecular recognition

50

of microbe- or damage-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs or DAMPs, respectively) and

51

subsequently trigger pattern-triggered immunity, or PTI (reviewed in (Saijo et al. 2018)).

52

However, pathogens evade PTI responses by delivering molecular virulence factors or effectors

53

that subvert basal defenses into host cells (reviewed in (Toruño et al. 2015)).

54

55

To counter such pathogens, plants have in turn evolved cytosolic receptors which constitute the

56

second layer of plant defense (reviewed in (Jones et al. 2016)). These intracellular receptors

57

contain nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeats and are thus known as NB-LRRs or NLRs. Both

58

TIR-NLR and CC-NLRs detect the presence of intracellular effectors and activate effector-

59

triggered immunity or ETI (reviewed in (Cui et al. 2015). Some NLRs recognize and bind

60

effectors directly, while others recognize the host proteins that effectors modify (reviewed in

61

(Khan et al. 2018)). It has recently been discovered that ZAR1 NLRs oligomerize to form a large

62

complex that serves to detect the activity of several bacterial effectors and is capable of

63

channeling calcium and triggering defense responses (Bi et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2019).

64

65

As described above, the molecular perception of pathogens and their effectors by PRRs and

66

NLRs is central to the plant immune response. However, there is growing evidence that

67

mechanical signals generated during invasion also elicit plant defense responses. For example,

68

Arabidopsis, strawberry, and bean plants acquire transient immunity to necrotic or wounding

69

pathogens following mild mechanical stimulus (reviewed in (Coutand 2020)). It is not yet known

70

if pathogens create detectable mechanical perturbations, if the mechanoperception of pathogens

71

utilizes the same pathways as defenses do, nor how such signals would be detected.

72

73

One mechanism by which the plant may recognize pathogen-associated mechanical signals is the

74

activation of mechanosensitive (MS) ion channels. Multiple families of MS ion channels mediate

75

the flux of ions across a membrane in response to increased membrane tension in plants

76

(reviewed in (Basu and Haswell 2017; Guerringue et al. 2018)). Genetic and biochemical data

77

support roles for several proteins that are homologs of known MS ion channels in the response to

78

pathogens. Plants lacking the AtPIEZO channel are unable to fully suppress long-distance

79

movement of viruses (Zhang et al. 2019), and OSCA1.3 is required for immunity-based stomatal

80

closure (Thor et al. 2020). The putative MS ion channel MscS-Like (MSL) 4 contributes to basal

81

immunity and MAMP-induced responses (Zhang et al. 2017).

82

83

MSL10 is a ubiquitously expressed homolog of MSL4 and is known to form a mechanically

84

activated ion channel both in root cells and in Xenopus oocytes (Maksaev and Haswell 2012;

85

Haswell et al. 2008) making it an intriguing candidate for a sensor of pathogen invasion. It is

86

directly activated through membrane tension and is largely non-selective, with a slight

87

preference for anions (Maksaev and Haswell 2012). Based on homology to other MscS-like

88

channels, the MSL10 channel is likely a homoheptamer (Bass et al. 2002; Deng et al. 2020),

89

though it may form multimeric complexes with its closest homolog, MSL9 (Haswell et al. 2008)

90

91

Plants constitutively overexpressing MSL10-GFP display severe

growth

retardation,

92

hyperaccumulation of H2O2, induction of genes associated with stress-response and cell death,

93

and ectopic cell death (Veley et al. 2014; Basu et al. 2020). These phenotypes are reproduced in

94

plants harboring an EMS-induced gain-of-function point mutation in the soluble C-terminus

95

(S640L; msl10-3G) and in transgenic lines expressing an untagged version of MSL10 containing

96

seven Ser/Thr-to-Ala mutations in the soluble N-terminus from the native (or genomic) context

97

(MSL10g7A) (Basu et al. 2020). Plants expressing wild-type MSL10 (MSL10g) or a phospho-

98

mimetic version of MSL10 (MSL10g7D), do not share these phenotypes, suggesting that

99

dephosphorylation of N-terminal Ser/Thr residues leads to channel activation (Basu et al. 2020).

100

Because MSL10-GFP overexpressors, msl10-3G mutants, and plants expressing MSL10g7A all

101

show the same gain-of-function phenotypes, we collectively refer to them here as MSL10

102

hypermorphs. While the molecular mechanism by which MSL10 hypermorphs are dysregulated

103

has not yet been established, we have speculated that phosphorylation of the seven Ser/Thr

104

residues in the MSL10 N-terminus keeps it in an inactive signaling state, and that either

105

preventing phosphorylation with Ser/Thr-to-Ala mutations or overexpression of the GFP-tagged

106

protein circumvents this regulation (Veley et al. 2014; Basu et al. 2020). We also recently

107

proposed that an interaction between the soluble N- and C-termini of MSL10 monomers

accompanies MSL10 activation, and that the msl10-3G point mutation promotes or recapitulates

110

111

Dwarfism and spontaneous lesions are also observed in two previously characterized, partially

112

overlapping, classes of Arabidopsis thaliana mutants. Autoimmune mutants are unable to

113

properly suppress immune responses in the absence of pathogens (reviewed in (Wersch et al.

114

2016), while lesion mimic mutants exhibit uncontrolled programmed cell death on the leaf

115

surface (reviewed in (Bruggeman et al. 2015)). Prompted by these similarities between

116

autoimmune and lesion mimic mutants and MSL10 hypermorphs, our previous characterizations

117

of MSL10 as a sensor of membrane tension, and by the intriguing possibility that plants could

118

sense mechanical signals of pathogen invasion, we investigated the potential role of MSL10 in

119

plant defense responses against Pseudomonas syringae.

Growth at high temperatures suppresses the phenotypes associated with overexpression of

124

MSL10-GFP, expression of MSL10g7A, and the msl10-3G mutant

125

Growth at elevated temperatures (28-30°C) can suppress the dwarfing and the localized ectopic

126

cell death in autoimmune mutants (Zhu et al. 2010; Huot et al. 2017; Hammoudi et al. 2018), and

127

we speculated that the same might be true for MSL10 hypermorphs. As shown previously (Veley

128

et al. 2014), MSL10-GFP overexpressors were dramatically smaller than the WT when grown at

129

21°C (Figure 1A, B). However, when grown at high temperature, MSL10-GFP overexpressing

130

lines were nearly indistinguishable from wild-type, msl10-1, or msl9-1 msl10-1 mutant plants in

131

terms of rosette size and fresh weight. Elevated temperature did not substantially alter the level

132

of transcripts from the endogenous MSL10 gene in wild-type plants, msl10-1 null mutants, or

133

MSL10-GFP overexpression lines (Figure 1C), and MSL10-GFP protein abundance was

134

unaffected (Figure 1D). Furthermore, MSL10-GFP was localized at the periphery of the cell at

135

both normal and high temperatures in all three transgenic lines (Figure 1E). Growth at high

136

temperature also suppressed dwarfing in msl10-3G mutants and in plants expressing the

137

MSL10g7A transgene but did not affect the growth of plants expressing MSL10g or MSL10g7D.

138

High temperature did not appreciably affect transcript levels of endogenous or transgenic MSL10

139

in these lines (Figure S1). Thus, growth at elevated temperatures relieved dwarfing in all three

140

MSL10 hypermorphs but did not do so by altering transcript levels, protein abundance or

141

subcellular localization. Growth at elevated temperature also suppressed previously documented

142

cellular phenotypes of MSL10 hypermorphs, including the hyperaccumulation of reactive oxygen

143

species, ectopic cell death, and increased transcript levels of four genes previously established as

144

hallmarks of MSL10 activity (Veley et al. 2014). We assessed these phenotypes in rosette leaves

145

from three-week-old wild-type, msl10-1 and msl10-3G mutant plants; three independent

146

transgenic lines overexpressing MSL10-GFP (previously described in (Veley et al. 2014)); and

147

msl10-1 plants harboring MSL10g, MSL10g7A or MSL10g7D transgenes (which contain a genomic

148

149

MSL10-GFP Overexpression Phenotypes Require SGT1b, RAR1, and HSP90.1

166

Most NLRs require SUPPRESSOR of G2 ALLELE of SKP1 (SGT1) for function (Kadota et al.

167

2010). SGT1 is part of a co-chaperone complex that includes REQUIRED for MLAI2

168

RESISTANCE (RAR1), and HEAT SHOCK PROTEIN 90 kD (HSP90) (Siligardi et al. 2018;

169

Takahashi et al. 2003; Liu et al. 2004). SGT, RAR1 and HSP90 interact directly with NLRs and

170

are required for their accumulation and subcellular trafficking (reviewed in (Borrelli et al.

171

2018)). Thus, in the absence of any known NLR associated with MSL10-induced downstream

172

autoimmune response or any evidence of MSL10 acting as a target for a pathogen effector

173

protein, we tested if silencing of SGT1, RAR1, and HSP90 by Virus Induced Gene Silencing or

174

VIGS (Baulcombe 1999) could abolish the cell death triggered by transiently overexpressed

175

MSL10-GFP in Nicotiana benthamiana.

176

177

Leaves were agroinfiltrated with VIGS constructs (TRV2:SGT1, TRV2:RAR1, TRV2:HSP90, or

178

empty vector). After ten days, transcript levels of each target gene were reduced by ~80%

179

(Figure S2A). After another ten days, leaves were subjected to a second agroinfiltration with

180

35S:MSL10-GFP, 35S:MSL107D-GFP, or 35S:MSL107A-GFP. Cell death was quantified 5 days

181

later, using dual FDA/PI staining as described (Veley et al. 2014). As expected, in leaves that

182

had been pre-infiltrated with an empty VIGS vector, transient expression of wild-type MSL10-

183

GFP resulted in the death of ∼27% of N. benthamiana epidermal cells, and expression of

184

MSL107A-GFP triggered even more cell death (~39%), while expression of MSL107D-GFP

185

resulted in the death of just 11% of cells (Figure 3A). However, we found that silencing of either

186

SGT1, RAR1, or HSP90 almost completely prevented cell death in leaves that were transiently

187

expressing MSL10-GFP (~10%) or MSL107A-GFP (~16%). Immunoblotting showed that these

DISCUSSION

363

Considerable progress has been made in identifying and characterizing plant receptors involved

364

in detecting bacterial effectors and MAMPs, but less is known about non-canonical players

365

involved in plant defense. Here we investigated the role of the mechanosensitive ion channel

366

MSL10 in plant immunity. We found that growth at elevated temperature, loss of the

367

SGT1/RAR1/HSP90 chaperone complex, or disruption of SA biosynthesis fully or partially

368

alleviated all the phenotypes triggered by MSL10 hypermorphic alleles that we tested. These

369

phenotypes, most of which are associated with enhanced basal defenses, included dwarfing,

370

ectopic cell death, hyperaccumulation of ROS, and a characteristic gene expression profile

371

(Figure 1-3, 5, Figure S1-3). However, the NLR signal transducers EDS1 and NDR1 were not

372

required (Figure 6). MSL10 hypermorphs exhibited enhanced disease resistance against both

373

virulent and avirulent strains of Pseudomonas syringe, but not hrpL (Figure 6), and msl10 null

374

mutants showed increased susceptibility to infection by coronatine-deficient Pto (Figure 7). We

375

also found that MSL10 promotes precocious PR1 induction and is required for normal stomatal

376

closure in response to Pto DC3000 (COR-) infection (Figure 7).

377

378

MSL10 hypermorphs compared to lesion mimic and autoimmune mutants. MSL10

379

hypermorphs strongly resemble a category of mutants called Lesion Mimic Mutants (LMMs).

380

LLMs display spontaneous cell death in the absence of any pathogenic infection, mechanical

381

stress, or abiotic stress (Moeder and Yoshioka 2008; Bruggeman et al. 2015). Like MSL10

382

hypermorphs, LMMs often share other phenotypes, including dwarfing and the constitutive

383

activation of defense responses. Also similar to MSL10 hypermorphs, the phenotypes of some

384

LMMs are temperature-sensitive (Huang et al. 2010; Yang and Hua 2004; Yang et al. 2010).

385

Some LMMs are autoimmune mutants--that is, their phenotypes result from the loss of

386

repression of pathogen responses (Wersch et al. 2016; Chakraborty et al. 2018). Autoimmune

387

effects can result from the overactivation of PRRs and NLR proteins, the loss of negative

388

regulators of NLRs, and/or the disruption of NLR-guardee interactions and mis-regulation of SA

389

biosynthesis. In this study, we provide evidence that the LMM-like phenotypes exhibited by

390

MSL10 hypermorphs are due to the induction of autoimmune responses (see discussion

391

below). However, unlike canonical/known autoimmune mutants, MSL10 hypermorphs did not

392

exhibit increased basal expression of the PR1 gene (Figure 7A). Instead, they might more

appropriately be considered constitutive priming mutants, poised to respond to the presence of a

394

pathogen more quickly or to a higher degree than the wild type (Mauch-Mani et al. 2016)

395

396

MSL10 and R gene-mediated immunity. The SGT1b/RAR1/HSP90 co-chaperone complex is

397

known to function as an integral component of ETI (Wersch et al. 2019). Silencing of

398

SGT1/RAR1/HSP90 components alleviates autoimmune responses in several autoimmune

399

mutants. For example, SGT1b is a positive regulator of the autoimmune response displayed by

400

chs3 mutants (Xu et al. 2015), HSP90.3 is required for the constitutive activation of defense

401

response in the saul1-1 mutant (Liang et al. 2020) and topp4-1 autoimmune phenotypes require

402

RAR1 and HSP90.2 (Yan et al. 2019). Similarly, the spontaneous cell death and

403

hyperaccumulation of ROS exhibited by plants overexpressing MSL10-GFP required SGT1b,

404

RAR1, and HSP90 in both tobacco and Arabidopsis (Figure 3, S2 and S3).

405

406

As we were unable to detect a physical interaction between MSL10 and SGT1b, RAR1 or

407

HSP90.1 (Figure S4), MSL10 is unlikely to be a client of the co-chaperone complex. Rather,

408

these data suggest that MSL10 acts upstream of R gene-mediated immune responses. However,

409

most NLR-mediated signaling requires either EDS1 or NDR1, and we observed that disruption of

410

EDS1 and NDR1 did not alleviate the autoimmune-like phenotypes associated with MSL10

411

hypermorphs. Furthermore, while autoimmunity can be caused by defects in guardee proteins,

412

msl10 null mutants do not show an autoimmune response under unstressed conditions, indicating

413

that MSL10 is unlikely to function as a guardee. A few scenarios fit the data presented here: 1)

414

MSL10 could function upstream of a pathway that involves R proteins that are EDS1- and

415

NDR1-independent. There is at least one other example of an immune response that requires

416

SGT1 but not EDS1 (Sang et al. 2020); 2) the MSL10-induced autoimmunity pathway could

417

involve non-NLR R proteins; or 3) MSL10 could function in a pathway that is entirely

418

independent of R genes. For the latter to be the case, the SGT1 complex would have to be

419

involved in an R-gene independent function as well, and both HSP90 and SGT1b have been

420

shown to interact with the receptors for plant hormones auxin and jasmonic acid (Wang et al.

421

2016; Zhang et al. 2015).

423

MS ion channels in pathogen perception. Cation fluxes are immediate and essential parts of the

424

host immune response (Moeder et al. 2018), and it was recently reported that the OSCA1 cation

425

channel is required for defense-induced stomatal closure (Thor et al. 2020). While MSL10 is

426

unlikely to transport calcium directly (Maksaev and Haswell 2012), the release of anions through

427

MSL10 could depolarize the plasma membrane, thereby indirectly activating calcium channels

428

(Guerringue et al. 2018). Indeed, MSL10 is closely associated with and required for the

429

cytoplasmic calcium transient associated with cell swelling (Basu and Haswell 2020). However,

430

the ability to trigger dwarfing and programmed cell death may be triggered independently of ion

431

flux through MSL10 (Veley et al. 2014; Maksaev et al. 2018). Thus, it is possible that MSL10

432

serves as a sensor of membrane integrity that is triggered by changes in membrane tension

433

resulting from Pseudomonas infection, either due to the insertion/assembly of the type III

434

secretion apparatus within the plasma membrane, or indirectly to events triggered by cytoplasmic

435

effectors such as water-soaking (though water-soaking is not seen with avirulent strains (Xin et

436

al. 2016)). Activation of MSL10 could then lead to calcium transients or to non-conducting

437

interactions with components of R gene-mediated immunity, eventually leading to PR1 induction

438

and/or stomatal closure. Interestingly, the putative MS ion channel MSL4, a close homolog of

439

MSL10, interacts with the defense regulator ACD6 and is required for PRR-mediated basal

440

immunity responses (Zhang et al. 2017), and AtPIEZO1 is required for long distance virus

441

translocation (Zhang et al. 2019). Taken together, these results suggest that MS ion channels play

442

diverse, yet-to-be-discovered roles in plant defense.
