Abstract
Sessile organisms within the kingdom Plantae utilize decentralized signaling networks to
navigate environmental stress, yet the capacity of non-neural systems to exhibit predictive
behavioral adaptations remains a critical area of investigation. This study evaluates the
presence of an acoustic-thigmonastic coupling mechanism in Mimosa pudica L.,
investigating whether airborne acoustic frequencies can serve as a predictive "prime" to
modulate rapid tactile reflexes. Utilizing a custom-engineered, vibration-isolated bioacoustic
attenuation chamber, uniform cohorts of M. pudica (𝑛 = 45) were exposed to continuous,
normalized acoustic pressure gradients (75 dB SPL) across two frequency bands (250 Hz
and 2500 Hz) for 60 minutes prior to standardized mechanical stimulation. High-speed video
tractography (240 fps) and computational motion analysis were deployed to track the
kinematics of leaf closure.
We introduce the Acoustic Priming Factor ( ), a novel, dimensionless biophysical metric, to
isolate and quantify changes in pulvinar sensitivity relative to mechanical energy input.
Quantitative analysis revealed that pre-treatment at 250 Hz induced a highly significant
40.8% reduction in the mechanical kinetic energy threshold required to gate the thigmonastic
cascade ( < 0.001), while accelerating the downstream response to a peak angular velocity
( max ) of 3.78 ± 0.19 rad/s (compared to 2.92 ± 0.14 rad/s in controls). Conversely, the
2500 Hz cohort demonstrated zero statistical variance from the control baseline ( =
−0.013, > 0.05). These data suggest a highly selective, narrow-band mechanical tuning
window wherein low-frequency pressure waves induce sub-threshold membrane
depolarization via mechanosensitive ion channels. This research provides concrete,
empirical evidence of anticipatory priming, offering profound implications for our sensory
gating, and aneural cognition in plants.

Introduction
The capacity to perceive, process, and adaptively respond to fluctuating environmental
stimuli is a fundamental prerequisite for organismal survival. While metazoans rely on
localized central nervous systems to orchestrate rapid behavioral adaptations, sessile
organisms within the kingdom Plantae have evolved complex, decentralized networks
capable of sophisticated non-neural information processing (Calvo, 2026). Among these,
Mimosa pudica L. serves as a classic model organism for the study of rapid epiclinic
movements. The plant's characteristic thigmonastic response, the rapid folding of pinnules
and subsequent dropping of the petiole upon mechanical disruption, is driven by a
specialized asymmetric motor organ known as the pulvinus (Hagihara & Toyota, 2020).
Mechanistically, physical deformation of the plant's mechanoreceptive structures, such as
tactile trichomes, gates stretch-activated ion channels. This induces a rapid influx of
cytosolic calcium ions ( 2+ ), triggering a propagating depolarization event akin to an animal
action potential ( ). Upon reaching the pulvinus, this bioelectrochemical cascade drives a
massive, rapid efflux of potassium ( + ) and chloride ( − ) ions from the abaxial motor cells
into the apoplast, resulting in an instantaneous loss of turgor pressure and subsequent
hydraulic collapse (Volkov et al., 2011). While the physical and electrical kinetics of this
tactile reflex are well-documented, the capacity of M. pudica to modulate these threshold
dynamics via predictive environmental cues remains poorly understood.

Emerging paradigms in plant bioacoustics—or phytoacoustics—demonstrate that airborne
acoustic frequencies act as potent environmental vectors capable of eliciting distinct
physiological modifications (Nakade & Dhadse, 2024). Sound waves travel as localized
acoustic pressure gradients that exert mechanical stress upon the plant’s cellular
boundaries. Recent investigations indicate that specific, ecologically salient frequencies can
prime vegetative defenses, altering phytohormone synthesis and gene expression profiles
prior to direct physical contact (Kim et al., 2021). Despite these advances, a significant
quantitative gap persists regarding how acoustic waves interact with immediate behavioral
kinematics. Specifically, it remains unverified whether acoustic pre-treatment serves as a
predictive behavioral "prime," lowering the mechanical energy threshold required to gate the
thigmonastic reflex.
This study investigates the presence of an acoustic-thigmonastic coupling mechanism in
Mimosa pudica. We hypothesize that exposure to targeted acoustic frequencies lowers the
mechanical stimulation threshold and accelerates the kinetic execution of leaf closure,
functioning as a form of aneural anticipatory priming. To evaluate this, we introduce a
standardized quantitative framework—the Acoustic Priming Factor ( )—to map the
logarithmic relationship between acoustic frequency ( ) and changes in the angular
velocity ( ) of pinnule movement. By isolating frequency as an independent variable within a
highly controlled, vibrationally isolated hygrothermal environment, this paper aims to
delineate the operational boundaries of sound perception in M. pudica, offering new insights
into the evolutionary ethology of non-neural systems.

Materials and Methods
1. Plant Material and Phytotron Cultivation
Specimens of Mimosa pudica L. (𝑛 = 45) were propagated from certified organic seed stock
within an automated phytotron chamber to ensure genetic and phenotypic uniformity. Plants
were grown in identical 10 cm diameter polypropylene vessels containing a standardized
substrate composition of 50% sphagnum peat moss, 30% perlite, and 20% vermiculite.
Cultivation parameters were strictly maintained at a diurnal temperature profile of 28∘ C ±
0.5∘ C daytime and 22∘ C ± 0.5∘ C nighttime, under a 14-hour photoperiod
(150 mumolcdotm−2 ⋅ s−1 photosynthetic photon flux density supplied via full-spectrum LED
arrays). Relative humidity ( ) was regulated continuously at 65% ± 3%. All specimens
received 25 mL of distilled water systematically every 48 hours to preserve uniform cellular
turgor pressure.

2. Acoustic Isolation and Environmental Stabilization
To completely eliminate confounding anthropogenic noise and external mechanical
oscillations characteristic of an urban laboratory environment, a custom bioacoustic
attenuation chamber was constructed.
The chamber walls were lined with 50 mm high-density open-cell polyurethane acoustic
absorption foam (NRC rating ≥ 0.85). The entire apparatus was mounted on a pneumatic
vibration-isolation table to damp low-frequency structural micro-seisms. Prior to any
experimental trials, specimens were relocated into the quiescent chamber for a 72-hour
stabilization period to allow full recovery from translocation-induced ethylene spikes and
electrical noise. The baseline ambient noise floor within the sealed chamber was verified at
≤ 32 dBA using a calibrated Type 1 sound level meter.

4. Standardized Quantitative Mechanostimulation Protocols

To eliminate the subjective errors associated with manual tactile stimulation, this study
developed a multi-tiered mechanical threshold framework. Initial boundary calibration
attempted to utilize a standardized Von Frey Tactile Assay (Figure 1, Right), wherein
calibrated micro-filaments were applied orthogonally to the adaxial surface of individual
leaflets in an ascending force progression (measured in milli-Newtons, mN).
However, systematic trials revealed that Von Frey filaments were highly ineffective for
mapping thigmonastic thresholds in Mimosa pudica. The static, slow-pressing force vectors
of the filaments routinely caused localized tissue compression without triggering the stretchactivated ion channels, resulting in high false-negative rates and premature mechanical
habituation.
To overcome this limitation and accurately capture the dynamic kinetics required to gate the
pulvinar cascade, two high-velocity mechanical assays were deployed:

Discussion
1. Biophysical Mechanisms of Frequency-Dependent Gating
The core finding of this study is that pre-exposure to a low-frequency acoustic tone (250 Hz)
significantly reduces the mechanical energy required to trigger thigmonastic closure, yielding
a highly positive Acoustic Priming Factor ( = 0.436). Conversely, high-frequency
stimulation (2500 Hz) produced an effect statistically indistinguishable from the control
baseline. This selective sensitivity suggests that Mimosa pudica does not merely register
sound as generalized environmental noise; rather, its mechanosensitive infrastructure is
finely tuned to a specific acoustic spectrum.
At the cellular level, this phenomenon can be explained by the gating kinetics of
Mechanosensitive Ion Channels (MSCs) located within the plasma membranes of the
parenchymal cells. Acoustic waves travel as rhythmic mechanical pressure fluctuations. A
250 Hz wave exerts oscillatory shear stress on the cell wall at a frequency that likely
matches the resonant properties of the plant's structural proteins or tactile trichomes (plant
hairs). This structural resonance causes micro-deformations in the lipid bilayer, inducing a
state of sub-threshold mechanical tension.
While this acoustic pressure is insufficient on its own to fully open the channels, it acts as a
mechanical "bias." It partially depolarizes the membrane by allowing a minor, localized influx
of cytosolic calcium ions ( 2+ ). Consequently, when the subsequent tactile stimulus is
applied via the pendulum actuator, the energy barrier required to open the voltage-gated
channels and initiate a full-scale Action Potential (AP) is significantly lower (8.4 muJ vs.
14.2 muJ in the control). At 2500 Hz, the rapid compression-rarefaction cycles likely occur
too quickly for the physical inertia of the lipid bilayer or channel proteins to respond, resulting
in no mechanical deformation or priming effect.

2. Kinematic Acceleration and Turgor Efflux Dynamics
The significant increase in peak angular velocity ( max = 3.78 rad/s) observed in the 250 Hz
cohort indicates that acoustic priming does not just make the plant more sensitive—it
accelerates the downstream hydraulic mechanism itself.
Once the tactile threshold is crossed, the velocity of the thigmonastic fold is governed by the
rate of water efflux from the abaxial motor cells into the apoplast within the pulvinus. This
rapid water movement is driven by an explosive outflux of potassium ( + ) and chloride ( − )
2+
ions. Because the acoustic pre-treatment has already stimulated sub-threshold
signaling, the downstream activation of aquaporins (water channel proteins) and ion pumps
occurs with higher synchronicity. Instead of a staggered cellular collapse, the motor cells
dump turgor pressure simultaneously, resulting in the accelerated rotational kinematics
captured by our high-speed video tractography.

3. Evolutionary Significance and Aneural Cognition
From an evolutionary standpoint, maintaining a state of high tactile sensitivity is
metabolically expensive. Rapid leaf closure halts photosynthesis, requires significant ATP to
pump ions back into the cells during the recovery phase, and renders the plant temporarily
incapable of responding to subsequent threats. Therefore, a permanent state of hypersensitivity is an Evolutionary Unstable Strategy (EUS).

Aacoustic priming allows Mimosa pudica to navigate this trade-off by utilizing predictive
environmental cues. In nature, low-frequency vibrations between 100 Hz and 300 Hz are
highly salient; they correspond to the wingbeats of heavy insect pollinators, the movement of
herbivorous predators, or the approach of heavy rainfall. By "listening" to these low
frequencies, the plant can transition into a temporary state of alertness. This capacity to use
a non-threatening, predictive stimulus (sound) to optimize a response to a secondary
threatening stimulus (touch) provides compelling empirical support for theories of aneural
cognition and ecological decision-making in non-neural organisms (Calvo, 2026).

4. Experimental Limitations and Future Outlook
While the acoustic attenuation chamber minimized anthropogenic noise, complete isolation
from ultra-low frequency micro-seisms in a dense urban environment like New Delhi remains
challenging. Future iterations of this protocol should utilize an anechoic chamber to map a
broader, higher-resolution frequency sweep (e.g., testing at 50 Hz increments from 100 Hz to
1000 Hz) to identify the exact peak resonant frequency of the pulvinus. Furthermore,
integrating fluorescent intracellular 2+ imaging would allow researchers to visually confirm
the sub-threshold ion gating hypothesized in this paper, turning a macro-behavioral
observation into a confirmed molecular mechanism.
Furthermore, because thigmonastic responsiveness is heavily tied to individual cellular
turgor dynamics and genetic factors, the absolute values of the Acoustic Priming Factor ( )
may vary across different plant accessions and developmental stages. Future multigenerational studies are required to map how individual metabolic baselines influence the
scale of this acoustic-thigmonastic coupling.

Conclusion
This study successfully demonstrates that Mimosa pudica exhibits a frequency-specific
acoustic priming mechanism that directly modulates its rapid thigmonastic defense system.
By employing a rigorous biophysical framework and standardized mechanical actuators, we
confirmed that low-frequency acoustic waves (250 Hz) systematically lower the energy
threshold required for leaf closure while accelerating the subsequent rotational kinematics.
The absence of any observable response at a higher frequency (2500 Hz) under identical
sound pressure levels confirms that this behavior is a targeted physiological reaction rather
than a generalized response to acoustic energy.
Ultimately, this capacity to interpret airborne vibrations as predictive indicators of imminent
physical contact represents a highly sophisticated form of non-neural information processing.
By transitioning into a temporary state of "alertness" only when specific, ecologically relevant
frequencies are detected, M. pudica successfully balances the high metabolic costs of rapid
motor movement with the evolutionary necessity of anti-herbivore defense. This discovery
expands our understanding of plant sensory capabilities, demonstrating that plants do not
merely react to the present, but computationally prepare for the immediate future.
