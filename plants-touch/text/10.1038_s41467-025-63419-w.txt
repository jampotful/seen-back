MSL10 is a high-sensitivity mechanosensor in the tactile sense of the Venus flytrap

# MSL10 is a high-sensitivity mechanosensor in the tactile sense of the Venus flytrap

## Abstract
The tactile sense is essential for living organisms. However, the molecular mechanisms of mechanosensing in plants remain unclear. The Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula), a carnivorous plant, possesses mechanosensitive organs called sensory hairs. After sensing a touch stimulus, the hair generates an action potential (AP) and a long-range calcium signal that propagate to the leaf blade, triggering leaf movement to catch prey after two successive calcium signals. Here, we show that the MECHANOSENSITIVE CHANNEL OF SMALL CONDUCTANCE–LIKE homologue (DmMSL10/FLYC1) in the Venus flytrap acts as a high-sensitivity mechanosensor. Using simultaneous intracellular recordings of calcium and electrical signals, we identify a two-step mechanical reception system in the sensory hairs: mechanosensing, limited to the stimulated cell, followed by threshold-dependent AP firing and subsequent calcium signal propagation. In dmmsl10 knockout mutants, long-range calcium and electrical signal propagation are impaired, leading to defective prey detection in planta. Our findings demonstrate that DmMSL10 is crucial for mechanosensing, facilitating AP firing by generating a receptor potential (RP) amplitude.

## Introduction
Plants are exposed to various mechanical stresses, ranging from abiotic factors such as wind and rain to biotic interactions such as herbivore attacks and physical contact with other organisms1–6. Stimulated plants exhibit clear responses through leaf movements, morphogenesis, and defence responses2–6. Furthermore, several stretch-activated channels have been described as proteins responsive to mechanical stimuli7–12, such as MECHANOSENSITIVE CHANNEL OF SMALL CONDUCTANCE–LIKE 8 (MSL8), which is involved in pollen hydration and germination10; MSL10, which mediates cell swelling in seedlings and wound-induced calcium and electrical signals in systemic leaves13,14; MID1-COMPLEMENTING ACTIVITY (MCA) and PIEZO, which are required for root penetration into the soil8,15; and the HYPEROSMOLALITY-GATED CALCIUM-PERMEABLE CHANNEL (OSCA) family proteins, which are responsible for sensing osmotic stress11,12,16. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the instantaneous sensing of touch stimuli in plants, analogous to tactile sensations in animals, are poorly understood. While trichomes serve as specialized organs for mechanosensing in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana17, visualising and characterizing cellular-level mechanosensing events in planta remains technically challenging.
The Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula) is a carnivorous plant that catches prey by closing its leaf blade18. The plant senses mechanical stimuli through specific organs called “sensory hairs”, six of which are formed on the leaf blade19. When the sensory hair senses a mechanical stimulus, it typically generates a single action potential (AP), with multiple APs being generated in some cases, depending on the angle and angular speed of the stimulus20. These APs propagate from the sensory hair to the leaf blade21–23. Recent technical advances have enabled the visualisation of the cytosolic calcium ion concentration ([Ca2+]cyt). The results revealed that a mechanical stimulus triggers an increase in the levels of [Ca2+]cyt as the calcium signal at the basal part of the sensory hairs propagates to the leaf blade within approximately one second6. In these sensory hairs, DmMSL10, a stretch-activated chloride ion (Cl−) channel, is enriched in the outermost epidermal cells in the basal part, called indented cells24,25. However, its physiological function remains obscure24,25. These characteristics make the Venus flytrap an ideal plant for mechanosensing analysis.

## Results

## Two-step calcium signal propagation
To investigate how the Venus flytrap detects mechanical stimuli using its sensory hairs, we manually stimulated sensory hairs under a fluorescence stereomicroscope, and changes in the [Ca2+]cyt were observed using the calcium-sensor protein GCaMP6f expressing line6,26. A strong touch stimulus (a deflection of approximately 0.157 rad at 3.91 rad/s to the hair) induced a [Ca2+]cyt increase in the sensory hair and leaf blade (Supplementary Fig. 1a, c and Supplementary Movie 1). The sensory hairs were subjected to two consecutive mechanical stimuli, inducing sequential calcium signals that triggered leaf movement, as described previously6. Calcium signals were detected in both the epidermal and subepidermal layers on the adaxial side, as well as in the abaxial epidermal layers of the leaf blade (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, a weak touch stimulus (a deflection of approximately 0.0715 rad at 0.595 rad/s to the hair) caused elevation in [Ca2+]cyt in the basal part of the sensory hair, with no propagation to the leaf blade (Supplementary Fig. 1b, d and Supplementary Movie 2).
These results led us to hypothesise that the magnitude of the given physical quantities determines whether the calcium signals propagate over a long range. We applied mechanical stimuli to the same location on the sensory hair, where each stimulus moved at a constant speed but travelled different total distances to test this hypothesis (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The results showed that stimuli with long-distance deflections were more likely to trigger calcium signal propagation than stimuli with short-distance deflections (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Similarly, high-speed stimuli were more likely to induce calcium signal propagation than low-speed stimuli (Supplementary Fig. 3c). These results indicate that sensory hairs perceive stimulus intensity based on both the magnitude and speed of deflection, generating long-range calcium signals, as in AP firing previously described20.
Sensory hairs possess mechanosensitive sensory cells in their basal indentation zones27. We observed an initial [Ca2+]cyt elevation in cells around the indentation zone using a two-photon microscope, with mechanical stimuli at the apical part of the sensory hairs (Supplementary Fig. 4). The observed hair region consisted of multiple layers of outermost epidermal cells (indented cells) and inner cells (central cells) (Fig. 1a)28. In response to a small deflection (approximately 0.009 rad, 0.0945 rad/s), [Ca2+]cyt increased at the centre of the indented cell on the stretching side of the sensory hair and propagated both apically and basally to adjacent cells with slight intensity changes (Fig. 1b, d and Supplementary Movie 3). In contrast, a large deflection (approximately 0.02 rad, 0.131 rad/s) triggered an initial [Ca2+]cyt increase in the indented cell, similar to the response to small deflections, followed by [Ca2+]cyt increases in adjacent and surrounding cells more than two cells apart from the indented cell (red arrowhead in Fig. 1c, e; Supplementary Movie 4). We mechanically stimulated the sensory hairs from two different directions and found that the initial calcium signals were always triggered at the indented cells on the stretched sides (Supplementary Fig. 5). In indented cells, the amplitude of calcium signals increased with both the deflection distance and speed (Supplementary Fig. 3d and e), and the peak of the signal was reached after the fast-speed (0.245 rad/s) deflection (Supplementary Fig. 3d). These observations indicate that the indented cells detected the magnitude of displacement as the amplitude of the calcium signals.
To evaluate the traits of calcium signals at the subcellular level, we developed a program to calculate the rise slope, decay slope, duration, and signal arrival time from the trajectory of temporal fluorescence changes using an approximation with a general formula for an exponentially modified Gaussian function (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Movie 5). The analysis revealed that the [Ca2+]cyt elevation in indented cells had a significantly shorter duration than that in adjacent and surrounding cells because of fast signal decay (Supplementary Fig. 6c and d). The time course changes in the rise slope suggest that calcium signals initiated from the indented region of the indented cell and were propagated toward the apical and basal sides (Supplementary Fig. 6a, e and Supplementary Movie 5). In adjacent and surrounding cells, calcium signals propagated synchronously along the outline of the cells and, subsequently, toward the cell interior (0.250 s in Supplementary Fig. 6a and the green-coloured area in Supplementary Fig. 6e, f; Supplementary Movie 5), supporting the idea that calcium influx into the surrounding cells begins at the plasma membrane before being released from intracellular Ca2+ storage. Consistent with these findings, observations from optical sections perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the sensory hairs also showed that weak stimuli triggered calcium signals only in the indented cell layer, whereas strong stimuli triggered signals in both the indented and central cell layers (Supplementary Fig. 7). Based on these results, we conclude that the basal part of the sensory hair consists of two cell types with distinct calcium dynamics: indented cells, which are the sensory cells, and their adjacent and surrounding cells. In indented cells, physical-mechanical stimuli are converted into calcium signals, which trigger long-range calcium signal propagation through adjacent and surrounding cells only when the physical stimuli are strong enough.

## Calcium signals are initiated by indented cells
We conducted single-cell destruction analysis using laser ablation to determine the roles of indented cells and their adjacent cells. When an indented cell on the stretched side of the hair was disrupted, the sensory hair lost its ability to trigger calcium signals in response to mechanical stimuli (Fig. 2a–d; Supplementary Movie 6). However, a long-range calcium signal was triggered when the same laser-ablated sensory hair was stretched from the opposite side (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Movie 7). In contrast, calcium signal propagation was not impaired in a central cell-disrupted sensory hair in response to mechanical stimuli, although the signal was impaired in the disrupted cell (Supplementary Fig. 9; Supplementary Movie 8). High-speed imaging results (226.55 frames/s) showed that calcium signal propagation starts from the adjacent cells on the basal side of the indented cell and continues to the apical side through the central cells (Supplementary Fig. 10; Supplementary Movies 9 and 10). A sharp increase in signal intensity was observed at the basal end of the indented cells before the onset of long-range calcium signal propagation (Supplementary Fig. 10, arrowhead). We cannot rule out the possibility that the calcium signal could also be triggered from apical side adjacent cells, but we did not observe signals from the apical side in our high-speed imaging. Our findings and the laser ablation results revealed that long-range calcium signals originate from the terminal ends of indented cells, not from the central cells.

## AP propagation with calcium signals
Electrical potential changes are another rapid signal generated in response to mechanical stimuli. To understand the relationship between calcium and electrical signals, we established a simultaneous calcium and electrical signal recording system by combining two-photon microscopy and electrophysiological devices for intracellular voltage recording (Supplementary Fig. 4). The electrode insertion into the indented cell did not eliminate the calcium signal upon mechanical stimuli, suggesting that the indented cell maintains sufficient cellular integrity and homeostasis to generate and propagate the calcium signal during this operation (Supplementary Fig. 11). An electrode inserted into the indented cell detected a typical resting potential of −115.94 ± 7.60 mV (mean ± SEM, n = 10 from independent hairs; Supplementary Fig. 12a). In response to the mechanical stimulus, an AP with an amplitude of 150.20 ± 10.10 mV (mean ± SEM, n = 10 from independent hairs) was detected alongside long-range calcium signal propagation (peak2 in Fig. 2e and f; Supplementary Fig. 12b). However, when the applied stimulus was not strong enough, only small potential changes were detected, with the [Ca2+]cyt elevation limited to the stimulated indented cells (peak1 in Fig. 2e and f) and their adjacent cells (peak1 in Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). In simultaneous recordings, small potential changes were detected after the initial calcium signals (Fig. 2g, arrowheads), and APs were detected before long-range calcium signals (Fig. 2h, arrowheads). In addition, small potential changes were observed before the waveforms of the APs (left slope of the orange arrowhead in Fig. 2h), and all small potential changes, APs, and long-range calcium signals were impaired by treatments with calcium channel inhibitors, which diminished initial calcium signals (Supplementary Fig. 14; Supplementary Movies 11 and 12)6,29. Therefore, these events occur sequentially: the initial local calcium signal triggers electrical potential changes, followed by long-range calcium signal propagation.
Analysis of electrical signals and fluorescence intensity changes revealed two distinct categories (Supplementary Fig. 13d and 15): small potential changes that were positively correlated with small calcium signal amplitudes (r = 0.7136, blue dots in Supplementary Fig. 13d) and large amplitude APs with large calcium signal amplitudes that showed no correlation (r = −0.0422, red dots in Supplementary Fig. 13d). These differences between the two categories are more likely due to the different dynamics of the signals, although the possibility that large calcium signals are out of the dynamic range of GCaMP6f sensitivity cannot be ruled out. APs were detected in conjunction with long-range calcium signal propagation in the leaf blades (peak1 in Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Movie 13), whereas small potential changes were not detected (peak2 in Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Movie 13). In addition, logistic regression analysis identified a threshold for the amplitudes of small potential changes that trigger APs (Supplementary Fig. 17). These results indicate the existence of “receptor potentials (RPs)” as small potential changes that only propagate around the sensory tissue, and APs serve as the primary signal for long-range propagation, which has a putative threshold for the voltage level, as in animals30.

## DmMSL10 is necessary for long-range signals and prey detection
Cl− efflux is considered the main component of the AP amplitudes in the Venus flytrap31,32. To confirm the role of anion channels in signal propagation, we applied anthracene-9-carboxylic acid (A-9-C), which is known as a Cl− channel blocker in animals33, to sensory hairs. Both calcium signal propagation and APs were impaired, and only slight calcium signals and RPs were recorded (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Movie 14). We focused on DmMSL10 as a stretch-activated Cl− channel enriched in indented cells24,25. Using the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method and the CRISPR-Cas9 system6,34, we successfully generated GCaMP6f-expressing DmMSL10 knockout lines (Supplementary Fig. 19).
The dmmsl10 leaves exhibited no defects in trap closure, as recently described35, or long-range calcium signal propagation when their hair was strongly stimulated by human hands (Supplementary Fig. 20a–d). In addition, laser ablation stimuli applied to the basal surrounding cells of the sensory hair triggered AP-like electrical signals with large amplitudes in the indented cells (Supplementary Fig. 20e). These results indicate that dmmsl10 can trigger long-range signal propagation in response to strong stimuli, such as fast-large deflections or cell destruction. We applied mechanical stimuli to dmmsl10 sensory hairs at various speeds to elucidate the mechanical sensitivities that trigger touch-responsive long-range signals. The peak amplitudes of the electrical potential changes were classified into two categories: small amplitudes with only RPs and large-amplitude APs accompanied by RPs (Supplementary Fig. 21a). The rates of AP generation in dmmsl10 were lower than those in wild-type plants, and both AP and long-range calcium signal propagation were impaired in dmmsl10, even under conditions that typically trigger APs in wild-type plants (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Movie 15). AP generation rates decreased with reduced stimulation speed, and no APs were observed below 0.1 rad/s in dmmsl10; however, wild-type plants continued to generate APs under reduced stimulation speeds (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 21b). Amplitudes of RPs in response to the constant speed mechanical stimuli (0.1 rad/s) in dmmsl10 were significantly smaller than those in wild-type plants (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 21c), and the average amplitudes in dmmsl10 (#1; 7.42 ± 3.75 mV [mean ± SEM, n = 4], #2; 5.72 ± 0.60 mV [mean ± SEM, n = 8]) were lower than the 95% confidence intervals of the threshold value of the RP to trigger APs (14.72–19.26 mV) calculated from logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 17b). In contrast, the AP amplitudes were not significantly different between the wild-type and the two independent dmmsl10 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 21d). These results indicate that DmMSL10 contributes to the generation of RP amplitudes necessary to trigger APs.
To investigate the contribution of DmMSL10 to prey capture, we constructed a microecosystem and observed the interactions between traps and ants, one of the most common prey species for the Venus flytrap in its natural habitat (Supplementary Movie 16)36. The ants rarely triggered calcium signals when visiting the dmmsl10 traps (Fig. 4a–f; Supplementary Movie 17 and 18). A 3-h observation of ant activity in the microecosystem showed that the probability of calcium signals occurring in response to ant visits was significantly reduced by 18.5% in the dmmsl10 traps (Fig. 4g). The dmmsl10 traps also showed a tendency to close 13.3% less frequently in response to ant visits; however, this difference was not statistically significant at the 5% level (Fig. 4h). ## Discussion
We developed a single-cell level simultaneous recording system for calcium and electrical signals and a microecosystem to detect plant-insect interactions. A combination of novel techniques enabled us to visualise the moment physical external stimuli are converted to biological intracellular signal propagation; this has facilitated the discovery of a two-step response system for mechanical stimuli in sensory hairs, i.e., the mechanosensing and signal firing steps. Furthermore, knockout mutant analysis showed that DmMSL10 contributed to the mechanosensing step to fire AP propagation from the sensory hair to the leaf blade by generating an RP amplitude. Microecosystem analysis demonstrated that DmMSL10 contributes to the mechanosensitivity of sensory hairs under natural conditions, maintaining their high sensitivity to micro-newton scale stimuli from insects37.
The complete impairment of signal propagation in dmmsl10 with weak stimuli ( ≤ 0.1 rad/s; Supplementary Fig. 21b) indicates that DmMSL10 primarily contributes to sensing small mechanical stimuli. Furthermore, genes other than DmMSL10 expressed in sensory hairs, such as other MSL10 homologues and OSCA homologues, may play additional roles in ensuring mechanosensitivity across various stimulus intensities24, as well as other trigger-hair expressed calcium channels, such as GLUTAMATE RECEPTOR–LIKE homologues, may contribute to the calcium signals in hairs38. This hypothesis is consistent with the fact that dmmsl10 plants exhibit weak calcium signals in indented cells in response to mechanical stimuli (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Movie 15). The RP threshold for triggering APs resembles the AP firing system observed in animals, leading to the hypothesis that voltage-dependent channels are responsible for AP firing in a manner similar to that in animals30. In addition, the high correlation between the amplitudes of the calcium signals and RPs explains how initial calcium signals may have a threshold for triggering depolarization, as previously proposed (Supplementary Fig. 13d)31,39.
We propose the following mechanism for long-range calcium and electrical signal generation based on our findings (Fig. 5): (1) A mechanical stimulus activates mechanosensitive ion channels, including DmMSL10, leading to an increase in RP with an increase in [Ca2+]cyt in the indented cells and their adjacent cells. (2) The RP surpasses the threshold to trigger the AP. (3) Subsequently, the long-range calcium signal is triggered and propagates with AP to the leaf blade from the terminal end of the indented cells along the contours of the surrounding cells. (4) The increase in [Ca2+]cyt propagates toward the centre of the cells where calcium and electrical signals pass. (5) Finally, two successive calcium signals trigger leaf movement, enabling the plant to catch prey.Fig. 5Model of the mechanosensing system in the Venus flytrap.a Schematic illustration of cell types in the sensory hair, showing an indented cell with stretched side (blue), its adjacent cells (green), and its surrounding cells (yellow). DmMSL10 is indicated as a stretch-activated anion (A−) channel. b Schematic of signal propagation triggered by mechanical stimuli applied to sensory hairs. Both weak and strong mechanical stimuli trigger receptor potentials in the indented cells, to which DmMSL10 contributes. Weak stimuli only generate subthreshold receptor potentials, whereas strong stimuli produce high-amplitude receptor potentials that are sufficient to trigger action potentials. These action potentials and the subsequent long-range calcium signals propagate through surrounding cells to the leaf blade.

Further research using our novel recording systems will illuminate how plants have evolved tactile sensing systems using the mechanosensor gene DmMSL10, which is lacking in animals.

## Methods

## Plant materials and culture conditions
Previously reported aseptic wild-type and GCaMP6f overexpression Dionaea muscipula plants were cultivated on nitrogen-reduced half Murashige-Skoog medium (NRHMS; 2.58 mM NH4NO3 [Wako], 2.35 mM KNO3 [Wako], 2.35 mM KCl [Wako], 625 µM KH2PO4 [Wako], 750 µM MgSO4·7H2O [Wako], 1.5 mM CaCl2·2H2O [Fujifilm], 50 µM Fe-EDTA [Wako], 50 µM H3BO3 [Wako], 50 µM MnSO4·5H2O [Wako], 15 µM ZnSO4·7H2O [Fujifilm], 2.5 µM KI [Wako], 500 nM Na2MoO4·2H2O [Wako], 50 nM CuSO4·5H2O [Wako], and 50 nM CoCl2·6H2O [Wako]) with 2% (w/v) sucrose (Fujifilm), 1× Gamborg’s vitamins (Sigma-Aldrich), antioxidant mixture (0.15 mg mL−1 ascorbic acid [Wako] and 0.1 mg mL−1 citric acid [Fujifilm] at pH 5.8), 0.1% (w/v) 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (Fujifilm), and 0.3% (w/v) Gellan gum (Fujifilm) at pH 6.1 in a tissue culture dish (SPL-310100; SPL Life Sciences)6. The aseptic plants were cultivated under 16 h light/8 h darkness conditions at 25 °C. Aseptic cultures of wild-type and dmmsl10 expressing GCaMP6f were transferred to wet sphagnum moss in plastic pots and cultivated in the greenhouse at 25 °C with 5-h supplemental lighting from a metal halide lamp (M400DL/BUDP, Iwasaki Electric Co., Ltd.).

## Fluorescence stereoscopic microscope imaging
Leaf blades from the GCaMP6f overexpression line were attached to glass slides using double-sided tape. Sensory hairs on the leaves were manually stimulated with forceps under a stereoscopic fluorescence microscope (SMZ25, Nikon) equipped with a 2× objective lens (P2-SHR PLAN Apo 2×, Nikon). Imaging was performed using an sCMOS camera (ORCA-Flash4.0 V2, Hamamatsu Photonics) in conjunction with a fluorescent filter cube (P2-EFL GFP-B, Nikon) and an excitation light source (X-Cite XYLIS XT720L, Excelitas Technologies). The fluorescence emission passing through a 535/50-nm filter was captured at 99.93 frames per second (fps) using imaging software (NIS-Elements Advanced Research, Nikon). To evaluate whether wild-type and dmmsl10 leaves showed any defects in mechanosensing when roughly touched, the leaves of potted plants expressing GCaMP6f were manually stimulated under the fluorescence microscope system described above, except a 1× objective lens was used instead (P2-SHR PLAN Apo 1×, Nikon). The touch probe was fabricated by pulling a glass microcapillary (GD-1.5, Narishige) with a puller (PC-100, Narishige) and heat-polishing its tip. Sensory hairs on wild-type and dmmsl10 leaves were touched twice using a probe attached to a manipulator (M3301R, World Precision Instruments). The movies were recorded at 49.99 fps.

## Confocal imaging of the leaf blades
Leaf blades from the GCaMP6f overexpression line were cut using a razor blade, placed on a glass slide (Fisher Scientific), and immersed in extracellular fluid (ECF; containing 0.1 mM KCl [Wako], 10 mM CaCl2 [Wako], 20 mM sorbitol [Wako] and 5 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid [Dojindo], adjusted to pH 6.0 with 2-amino-2-hydroxymethyl-1,3-propanediol (Tris) [Wako])6,40. GCaMP6f fluorescence was observed on the adaxial and abaxial sides of the leaf blades using an upright confocal microscope (A1R, Nikon) equipped with a 20× objective lens (S Plan Fluor ELWD 20XC, Nikon) and an inverted confocal microscope (A1R, Nikon) equipped with a 20× objective lens (Plan Apo VC 20x DIC N2, Nikon). To image the adaxial side of the leaf blade, GCaMP6f was excited by a 488 nm laser, and fluorescence emission was detected at 499–544 nm using a GaAsP detector. To image the abaxial side of the leaf blade, GCaMP6f was excited by a 489 nm laser, and fluorescence emission was detected at 500–550 nm wavelength in a GaAsP detector. All images were acquired using imaging software (NIS-Elements Advanced Research, Nikon). The section perpendicular to the midrib was cut from the tissue-cleared leaf blade using a razor blade. The sections were observed under an upright confocal microscope (A1R, Nikon) equipped with a 4× objective lens (Plan Fluor 4X, Nikon). Direct Red 23 was excited by a 561 nm laser, and fluorescence emission was detected at 570–620 nm using a GaAsP detector. The tissue clearing process was performed as follows: Leaves were fixed in 20% (v/v) acetic acid (Wako) dissolved in ethanol (Wako) with four 15-min rounds of decompression to a gauge pressure of −0.08 MPa at room temperature and then kept overnight at 4 °C. Fixed leaves were rehydrated using a dilution series of ethanol (90%, 70%, 50%, 30%, and 0%) and cleared in a solution consisting of 8 g of chloral hydrate (Wako), 1 mL of glycerol (Wako), 0.1% (w/v) Direct Red 23 (Sigma), and 2 mL of distilled water for 3 days at room temperature under continuous dark conditions6.

## Two-photon microscope imaging of sensory hairs
The leaf blades were cut using a razor blade along the midrib and perpendicular to the midrib to isolate intact sensory hairs. The isolated hairs were fixed on a dish (CK-1, Narishige) using Vaseline (HPC) and immersed in ECF. Images were captured using an upright two-photon microscope (AX R MP, Nikon) equipped with a 16× (CFI75 LWD 16X W, Nikon) or 25× objective lens (CFI75 Apochromat 25XC W 1300, Nikon) in multiphoton mode (AX MP NDD mode) using imaging software (NIS-Elements Advanced Research, Nikon). To detect the spatial-temporal dynamics of [Ca2+]cyt, GCaMP6f was excited by a two-photon laser at 920 nm, and emission fluorescence was detected at 500–550 nm wavelength using a GaAsP detector with resonant scanner mode at 15.06–226.55 fps for time course imaging or with galvano scanner mode for capturing high-resolution snapshots. Mechanical stimuli were applied to sensory hairs using an electric micromanipulator (µMP-3, Sensapex) with a heated, drawn glass capillary (GC150F-10, Harvard Apparatus), either manually by a human operator (Figs. 1 and 2e–h; Supplementary Fig. 6, 10, 13, 15, and 16), or automatically with programmed parameters (Other experiments). The precision of mechanical stimulation varied between manual and automated operations due to differences in the control methods. The tip of the glass capillary was placed 200 µm apically from the centre of the central cell of the sensory tissue and stimulated at various speeds and distances perpendicular to the apical-basal axis of the sensory hair (Supplementary Fig. 3a). For single-cell destruction, a 920 nm laser was repeatedly applied using a two-photon microscope (AX R MP, Nikon) until the laser-ablated cells died, as indicated by the disappearance of autofluorescence.

## Imaging analysis
Several ROIs were analysed using imaging software (NIS-Elements Advanced Research, Nikon). The fractional fluorescence changes were calculated according to the equation ΔF/F0 = (F − F0)/F0, where F denotes GCaMP6f fluorescence at any given time and F0 denotes the averaged background fluorescence defined as the average of F over 30 frames before the stimulus, except for the optical sections perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the hairs, for which we used the average of F over 3 frames before the stimulus.

## Simultaneous recording of calcium and electrical signals
Sensory hairs, a manipulator for mechanical stimuli, and a microscope were prepared as described in the “Two-photon microscope imaging of sensory hairs” section. To detect electrical signals, glass capillaries (GC150F-10, Harvard Apparatus) were pulled to resistances between 15 − 25 MΩ using a micropipette puller (P-1000, Sutter Instruments), filled with 100 mM KCl (Wako) containing 0.1% (w/v) Rhodamine B (TCI), and set into the Ag/AgCl (⌀ 0.3 mm) wire-inserted electrode holders (1-HL-U, Axon Instruments) to be used as recording electrodes. The recording electrodes were inserted into the recording cells using an electric micromanipulator (µMP-3, Sensapex) under a microscope, and reference electrodes (HLA-003) were immersed in the ECF. The electrical potential difference (PD) between the recording and reference electrodes was set to 0 mV before inserting the recording electrode into the recording cell, which was sampled at 10 kHz and low-pass filtered at 10 kHz using a head stage (CV-7B, Axon Instruments), a microelectrode amplifier (Multiclamp 700B, Axon Instruments), a digitiser (Digidata 1550B, Axon Instruments), and data acquisition software (Clampex11, Axon Instruments). Simultaneous recordings of microscope imaging and electrical signals were triggered using microscope imaging software (NIS-Elements Advanced Research, Nikon) via an external trigger cable (S-AX-EXTG, Nikon).

## Amplitude measurements in potential changes and calcium signals
To measure the APs, the difference in the electrical potential between the pre-stimulus level and the peak value after the stimulus was calculated as the amplitude of the electrical potential. For the correlation calculations in Supplementary Fig. 13d, an electrode was inserted into the single cell adjacent to the indented cell, and the same difference in electrical potential was calculated for the amplitude. Similarly, a single cell adjacent to the indented cell was set as the ROI, and the difference in the mean intensity in the ROI between the pre-stimulus level and the peak value after the stimulus was divided by the pre-stimulus level to calculate the amplitude of the GCaMP6f signals. To compare the wild-type and dmmsl10 plants (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Fig. 21a, c, and d), the electrical potential changes were detected using data analysis software (Clampfit11, Axon Instruments). Some of the detected potential changes, the start times of which matched the stimuli in the imaging, were used.

## Logistic regression analysis
The RP amplitudes were manually obtained for the explanatory variables at the time point when the slope of the membrane potential changed (Supplementary Fig. 17a). As dependent variables, samples showing APs with high amplitudes were scored as 1. In contrast, samples without APs were scored as 0. For the likelihood-ratio test, we set a null hypothesis that AP firing is independent of explanatory variables and tested it using the parametric bootstrap test with 10,000,000 bootstrap replicates.

## Inhibitor experiment
A-9-C (Nacalai Tesque) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (Wako) to make a 1.5 M stock solution. The 2 mM A-9-C was prepared by mixing the stock and ECF. The 500 µM A-9-C was prepared by diluting the 2 mM A-9-C supernatant 4-fold with ECF. The 2 mM LaCl3 and GdCl3 solution was prepared by dissolving LaCl3·7H2O (Wako) and GdCl3·6H2O (Wako) in ECF, respectively. Calcium and electrical signals were recorded from dissected sensory hairs following the method described in the “Simultaneous recording of calcium and electrical signals” section, where the hairs were immersed in inhibitor solution substituted for ECF.

## Vector construction
Plasmid vectors for genome editing, which were used to measure electrical potential, were modified from pSB11Cas11-ELP1A5. hCas9 between the XbaI and AscI sites was replaced with zCas9i, which was amplified from pAGM55273 (Addgene, Plasmid #153211), with the XbaI site replaced by the SmaI site. An expression cassette composed of the 35S promoter, CAT1 intron, EGFP, and NOS terminator between the AgeI and PmeI sites was replaced with a cassette composed of the CmYLCV promoter43,44, GCaMP6f26 (Addgene, Plasmid #40755), and the Ps rbcS 3 A terminator45. The spacer guide RNA sequence between the AtU6 promoter and scaffold sequence was replaced with the sequence 5ʹ–GGGACAGCTGGTAGCAGACG–3ʹ targeting the first exon in DmMSL10. The final vector was named pSB11_pCmG6_zCas9i_dmmsl10. To measure the electrical potential and perform calcium imaging, GCaMP6f of pSB11_pCmG6_zCas9i_dmmsl10 was replaced with GCaMP6f conjugated to mScarlet-I46 connected by a T2A linker sequence47. The final vector was named pSB11_pCmG6 T2A_zCas9i_dmmsl10.

## Generation of transgenic Venus flytraps
Transgenic Venus flytraps were generated using a previously described method with the modifications as follows6. The superbinary intermediate vector pSB11 was introduced into LBA4404 Agrobacterium harbouring the superbinary acceptor vector pSB1. Agrobacterium was cultivated on solid LB medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar (Nacalai Tesque) and 50 µg mL−1 (w/v) of hygromycin-B (Life Technologies) for 48 h at 30 °C. A single Agrobacterium colony was inoculated into 5 mL of liquid LB medium with 25 µg mL−1 (w/v) of hygromycin-B and precultured at 28 °C for 24 h with shaking at 180 r.p.m. A portion of Agrobacterium was diluted to a concentration of optical density OD600 0.15 with 40 mL of liquid LB medium supplemented with 25 µg mL−1 of hygromycin-B in a 200-mL baffled flask and further cultivated at 28 °C with shaking at 180 r.p.m. until an OD600 of ~0.55 was reached. After centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 25 °C, the supernatant was removed and precipitated Agrobacterium cells were resuspended to OD600 0.3 in the cocultivation medium (half-strength basal MS salts [Wako] medium with 2% (w/v) sucrose, 1× Gamborg’s vitamins [Sigma-Aldrich], 0.1% (w/v) 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid [Dojindo], and antioxidant mixture, at pH 6.1) with 40 µg mL−1 of acetosyringone (Wako) after washing with the cocultivation medium once. Green leaves were cut at the middle of the petiole, submerged in 10 mL of the Agrobacterium suspension and placed twice successively under a vacuum (a gauge pressure of −0.08 MPa) for 10 min. The infected leaves were submerged in the cocultivation medium with fresh Agrobacterium and cultivated under continuous darkness at 25 °C for 72 h. After cocultivation, Agrobacterium-infected leaves were transplanted onto NRHMS medium supplemented with 150 µg mL−1 of cefotaxime sodium salt (SANOFI) and 15 µg mL−1 of hygromycin-B (Nacalai Tesque) and cultivated in continuous darkness at 25 °C. The NRHMS medium was exchanged once a week for at least a month, until transgenic shoots formed. One month after infection, the leaves were transferred to the medium plates lacking cefotaxime sodium salt. Transgenic shoots were cultivated under 8 h light/16 h darkness at 25 °C until green leaves formed. Plants were acclimatised by cultivating them in sphagnum in the greenhouse at 25 °C with 5 h of daily supplemental lighting from a metal halide lamp (M400DL/BUDP, Iwasaki Electric Co., Ltd.). Transgenic plants were selected based on the fluorescence of mScarlet-I (pSB11_pCmG6_T2A_zCas9i_dmmsl10) and GCaMP6f (pSB11_pCmG6_zCas9i_dmmsl10), using a stereoscopic fluorescence microscope M165FC (Leica) equipped with fluorescence filters for GCaMP6f (ET GFP LP 480/40 510LP M, Leica) and mScarlet-I (ET DSR 545/30 620/60 M, Leica). The resulting transformants were named dmmsl10 #1 (pSB11_pCmG6_T2A_zCas9i_dmmsl10; for calcium and electrical signal measurements) and dmmsl10 #2 (pSB11_pCmG6_zCas9i_dmmsl10; for electrical signal measurements), respectively.

## Genotyping
Each dissected single leaf was placed in a 2 mL microtube (Sarstedt) with 5 mm diameter zirconia beads (Nikkato) and 100 µL lysis buffer (100 mM Tris, Wako) at pH 9.5 adjusted with HCl (Wako), 1 M KCl (Wako), and 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, Dojindo). Leaves were homogenised at room temperature using a TissueLyser II (QIAGEN) at 20 Hz for 3 min. The homogenised sample was centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and 80 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Equal volumes of 2-propanol (Fujifilm) were added to the supernatant, vortexed, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min to obtain a pellet. The pellet was washed with 80 µL of ethanol (Fujifilm) and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min. The obtained pellet was dried for 30 min and then resuspended in 50 µL of TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA). A 0.5 µL aliquot was used for PCR, which was performed using KOD One PCR Master Mix Blue (Toyobo) and primers 5ʹ–GCAGCTACCTCCATGAGTCAC–3ʹ and 5ʹ–TCTAACCCAATCACCGTGTACC–3ʹ. The PCR products were sequenced using the same primers.

## Feeding experiment
Japanese pavement ants (Tetramorium tsushimae) were collected from the field and used as prey for the Venus flytrap in subsequent experiments. Each potted plant expressing GCaMP6f was placed in a plant box (CUL-JAR300, AGC Techno Glass). To prevent ants from escaping during the experiments, the gap between the pot and the plant box was filled with sponge tape, and the inner surface of the plant box was coated with baby powder. Twenty ants were simultaneously weighed using an electronic balance (GR-202, A&D Company) before the experiments. To monitor the interactions between ants and leaves (Fig. 4a–f), twenty ants (12.0 ± 0.9 mg [mean ± SEM], n = 4 independent experiments) were released on each wild-type or dmmsl10 #1 plant. Movies were captured at 5 fps using the fluorescence stereo microscope system described in “Fluorescence stereoscopic microscope imaging” section. Bright-field images of the selected leaves were obtained using a compact digital camera (Olympus Tough TG-5, Olympus). We developed a camera-type fluorescence imaging system for whole-plant Ca2+ imaging-based feeding assays (Fig. 4g and h). This novel system can capture GCaMP6f fluorescence from an extremely wide area, more than 50 × 50 cm, thus enabling fluorescence imaging at a whole-plant scale. An sCMOS camera (ORCA-Fusion, Hamamatsu Photonics) was equipped with a manual focusing lens (24 mm F1.4 ED AS IF UMC, Samyang) via a mount adapter (CANON EOS-C, Kipon) and an emission filter (ET525/50 m, Chroma). The camera was mounted on a tripod (Light Carbon E84, Kenko Tokina). Excitation light was emitted to the plants using an LED light source (SOLIS-470C, Thorlabs) through an excitation filter (ET470/40x, Chroma) and a ring fibre illumination unit (P2-FIR, Nikon). Twenty ants (12.7 ± 0.3 mg [mean ± SEM], n = 5 independent experiments) were released on each wild-type or dmmsl10 #1 plant. Movies were captured using the system and imaging software (NIS-Elements Advanced Research, Nikon) at 5 fps for 3 h after the ants were placed. The probability of calcium signal propagation was calculated by dividing the number of visible increases in GCaMP6f fluorescence intensities in the leaf blades by the number of ant-leaf interactions that occurred before the leaves were moved. The probabilities of leaf movement were calculated by dividing one by the number of ant-leaf interactions that occurred before the leaves moved. From the movies, the probabilities were manually evaluated for each leaf.

## Figure Fig. 5: Model of the mechanosensing system in the Venus flytrap.a Schematic illustration of cell types in the sensory hair, showing an indented cell with stretched side (blue), its adjacent cells (green), and its surrounding cells (yellow). DmMSL10 is indicated as a stretch-activated anion (A−) channel. b Schematic of signal propagation triggered by mechanical stimuli applied to sensory hairs. Both weak and strong mechanical stimuli trigger receptor potentials in the indented cells, to which DmMSL10 contributes. Weak stimuli only generate subthreshold receptor potentials, whereas strong stimuli produce high-amplitude receptor potentials that are sufficient to trigger action potentials. These action potentials and the subsequent long-range calcium signals propagate through surrounding cells to the leaf blade.
