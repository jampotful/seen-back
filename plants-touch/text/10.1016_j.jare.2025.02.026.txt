An auxin response factor regulates tiller angle and shoot gravitropism by directly activating related gene expression in rice.

# An auxin response factor regulates tiller angle and shoot gravitropism by directly activating related gene expression in rice

## Abstract
Graphical abstract

## Introduction
Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is a cereal food crop that feeds billions of people worldwide [1]. Plant architecture significantly affects grain yield in rice, which is determined by factors such as leaf angle, the number and angle of tillers, panicle type, and plant height [2], [3]. Tiller angle refers to the angle between the tillers and the vertical axis, which impacts plant density, resource utilization, and grain yield in rice. Consequently, gaining insights into the regulation of tiller angle will establish a theoretical basis for rice breeding to improve plant architecture [2].
Shoot gravitropism is a pivotal factor controlling the angle of tillers in rice. Seedlings with deficiencies in shoot gravitropism usually exhibit an enlarged tiller angle. Gravitropism refers to the gradual adaptation of plant growth in response to gravity, facilitating upward growth of the shoot (negative or shoot gravitropism) and downward growth of the root (positive or root gravitropism) [2]. Auxin plays a key role in promoting cell division and expansion, thereby regulating plant growth. The asymmetric distribution of auxin leads to the occurrence of shoot gravitropism. When a plant is placed horizontally, the lower side of the hypocotyl accumulates more auxin, causing the organ to bend upwardly [4], [5].
A few genes related to the control of tiller angle have been isolated in rice [2]. LAZY 1 (LA1) was the first isolated gene regulating tiller angle in rice. The asymmetric distribution of auxin in the shoots enlarges the tiller angle of la1 [6], [7], [8]. Oryza sativa Brevis Radix-Like 4 (OsBRXL4) and Heat Stress Transcription Factor 2D (HSFA2D) act as regulators of LA1 to manage tiller angle [9], [10]. Additionally, Oryza sativa Homeobox gene 1 (OsHOX1) and OsHOX28 reduce the auxin concentration in planta and suppress the HSFA2D-LA1 pathway [11]. OsmiR167a is involved in tiller angle control through targeting OsARF12, OsARF17, and OsARF25, which induces the differential enrichment of WUSCHEL-related Homeobox 6 (WOX6) and WOX11 transcripts on the upper and lower sides of coleoptile in rice [12]. Mutations of a-1,3-Fucosyltransferase (FucT), Plant Architecture and Yield 1 (PAY1) and Tiller Angle Control 4 (TAC4) impact auxin transport and distribution, thereby changing the tiller angle in rice [13], [14], [15].
Starch granules play a vital role in sensing the gravistimulation signal [16]. LAZY2 (LA2) interacts with rice plastidic phosphoglucomutase (OspPGM) and LAZY 3 (LA3) to regulate the tiller angle in rice. The la2 mutant completely loses the starch granules in the gravity-sensing cells, showing a spread-out tiller phenotype [17], [18], [19]. Rice ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGP)-Like 1 (OsAGPL1) and its homolog gene OsAGPL3, which encode large subunits of AGP (an enzyme involved in starch biosynthesis), impact the sensing of gravity [20], [21], [22]. Loose Plant Architecture 1 (LPA1), encoding an Indeterminate Domain (IDD) domain-containing protein, modulates the amyloplast sedimentation and gravity sensing in rice shoots [23], [24], [25]. A plant-specific NAM/ATAF1/ATAF2/CUC2 (NAC) transcription factor ONAC106 is physically associated with the LPA1 promoter and represses its expression, thereby affecting the tiller angle development in rice [26]. Although advanced progresses have been achieved, the regulatory networks manipulating shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice remain to be fully understood.
In this study, we identified a mutant with an enlarged tiller angle, named bigger tiller angle 3–1 (bta3-1), by screening for negative regulators of tiller angle in rice. The BTA3 gene was cloned with the positional cloning approach, which encodes Oryza sativa Auxin Response Factor 5 (OsARF5), a member of the ARF transcription factor superfamily whose biological functions have not been reported before. OsARF5 binds directly to the cis-acting elements of OsAGPL1, HSFA2D, LA1, and OsPIN1a, promoting their expression and regulating tiller angle in rice. Additionally, we discovered that OsARF5 physically interacts with Indole Acetic Acid 7 (OsIAA7), OsIAA11, and OsIAA30 in rice. High-throughput mRNA sequencing showed that the osarf5-1 mutation and gravistimulation treatment leads to the differential gene expression. Our findings reveal the roles of OsARF5 in controlling tiller angle and provide us with gene resources for genetic improvements of plant architecture in rice.

## Materials and methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
The japonica rice variety SN9816 was used as the wild-type (WT) reference for bta3-1. The bta3-1 mutant was obtained during screening for the negative regulators of tiller angle in rice. Rice seedlings with four leaves were transplanted to the paddy field in Shenyang, China.

## Analysis of shoot gravitropism
Following germination, seeds were sown in the silica sand and grown at 28℃ for 4 days in either light or dark conditions prior to applying gravity. Then, the seedlings were positioned horizontally for gravistimulation in either dark or standard long-day conditions (14 h of light and 10 h of darkness) [17]. The curvature angle of shoot was examined every 12 h over a period of three days, by using 30 seedlings at each time point. Three biological replicates were performed.
To investigate the response of shoot gravitropism to auxin, an auxin polar transport inhibitor, and other phytohormones, four-day-old seedlings were treated with gravity and naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA, 100 μM), the auxin polar transport inhibitor 1-naphthylphthalamic acid (NPA, 50 μM), gibberellin (GA, 10 μM), brassinolide (BR, 10-5 μM), abscisic acid (ABA, 200 μM), jasmonic acid (JA, 100 μM), and salicylic acid (SA,100 μM) simultaneously for 72 h. Distilled water (ddH2O) was used as a mock control. The shoot curvature angle of 30 seedlings was measured for each treatment, and three biological replicates were conducted.

## Positional cloning of the BTA3 gene
The bta3-1 was crossed with SN9816 to create an F2 population for genetic analysis. To isolate the BTA3 gene, we crossed bta3-1 (a japonica mutant) with Habataki (an indica variety) to generate an F2 segregation mapping population [27]. The simple sequence repeat (SSR) and insertion/deletion (InDel) markers evenly covered the entire genome of rice were developed. From the F2 mapping population, 36 individuals exhibiting bta3-1 phenotypes were selected to make a DNA pool for initial mapping using bulked segregant analysis (BSA). The exons of the genes in the mapped region were amplified with PCR and sequenced by using Sanger sequencing technique.

## Phylogenetic tree and protein structure of OsARF5 homologs
The homologs of OsARF5 in the rice genome were identified from the Rice Annotation Project Database (RAP-DB, https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/) and the Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP, https://rice.uga.edu/) database. An unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method with MEGA X software (https://www.megasoftware.net/), with a bootstrap value of 1000. The domains of OsARF5 and its homologs was characterized through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database.

## Plasmid construction and genetic transformation
To carry out the complementary experiments, a 3420 bp coding sequence of OsARF5 fused with Green Fluorescence Protein (GFP) driven by the native promoter of OsARF5 (OsARF5p) were ligated in the pCambia1300 binary vector, generating a plasmid OsARF5p:OsARF5-GFP. To produce overexpression transgenic lines, a plant expression vector 35S:OsARF5-GFP was developed. To assess the promoter activity of OsARF5, a construct containing β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene driven by OsARF5p was constructed generating a plasmid OsARF5p:GUS.
The OsARF5p:OsARF5-GFP and 35S:OsARF5-GFP plasmids was transformed into bta3-1 and WT, while the OsARF5p:GUS and 35S:GFP (used as an empty vector control) were transformed into WT using an agrobacterium (Agrobacterium tumefaciens) mediated mature embryo transformation approach [28], [29]. The primers for plasmid construction are shown in Table S1.

## Subcellular localization
The 35S:OsARF5-GFP and 35S:GFP plasmids in agrobacterium strain GV3101 was expressed in the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana through infiltration [30]. After 72 h, the signals of 4′, 6-Diamidino-2′-phenylindole (DAPI) and GFP were visualized under a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM780), with excitation wavelengths of 358 nm and 488 nm, respectively.

## Gene expression analysis
Gene expression was examined with the previously described method [31]. Total RNA was extracted from the seedlings and organs of adult rice plants with an RNA isolation kit (Takara, #9108). The extracted RNA was treated with DNase I for 30 min (Promega, #M6101). The cDNA synthesis, or reverse transcription (RT), was performed according to the procedure of the kit (Thermo Scientific, #K1621). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was implemented with Ex Taq (Takara BioInc., #RR420A) and a PCR facility (Applied Biosystems, QuantStudio 5). The OsACTIN 1 (OsACT1, Os03g0718100) was used as the endogenous control. The relative gene expression compared to OsACT1 was calculated using the following formula: relative gene expression = 2−ΔCT.
To analyze the expression pattern of OsARF5, WT (SN9816) was utilized as the material. The shoot apical meristem (SAM) were collected from the seedlings at the three- or four-leaf stage. The flag leaves (FL), the second uppermost leaves (2nd UL), the third uppermost leaves (3rd UL), stem, sheath, and young panicle were harvested from the rice plants at booting stage. To examine the expression profile of OsARF5 throughout the development stages, the leaves were sampled every 10 days starting from 50 days after sowing.
To study the inducible expression of OsARF5 in the response to auxin and gravistimulation, 10-day-old WT seedlings were transferred to a culture medium supplemented with the 100 μM NAA and subjected to gravity treatment. The seedlings treated with ddH2O mixed with 2 ml of anhydrous ethanol per liter and no gravity treatment were used as the control (Mock). Leaf samples were collected at various time points: 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h after treatment.
For histochemical GUS staining analysis, samples including leaves, stems, sheaths, SAM, young panicles, seeds, and roots were picked from the transgenic plants harboring the OsARF5p:GUS construct. These samples were soaked in chilled 90% acetone for 15 min and then immersed in a GUS staining solution in the dark at 37 °C for about 12 h. After staining, the samples were transferred to a gradient of 70% to 100% ethanol to decolorize them until they became transparent [30]. Photographs of the stained organs were captured with a SONY camera.

## ChIP assay
The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was conducted following the previously established protocol [32]. In brief, the 20-day-old seedlings of WT and a complementary transgenic line 1 (Com-1) containing the OsARF5p:OsARF5-GFP construct were used as materials. After harvesting, the samples were subjected to cross-linking with 1% (m/v) formaldehyde. Then, nuclei were precipitated by centrifuging at 3000 g. The chromatin was fragmented into pieces ranging from 100 to 300 bp using sonication. Anti-GFP antibodies was utilized to immunoprecipitate (IP) the OsARF5-GFP protein-DNA complex (Abcam, #ab290) with Protein A/G agarose resin (Pierce, #20421). Following dissociation and DNA purification, the resulting DNA was used as templates for quantitative PCR (qPCR). The primers used for the ChIP assay are listed in Table S2.

## DLR assay
The method described before was used for the dual luciferase reporter (DLR) assay [33]. The 1.5 kb promoters of HSFA2D and OsAGPL1 was ligated into the pGreenII-0800 vector, resulting in firefly luciferase (LUC) reporter constructs HSFA2Dp:LUC and OsAGPL1p:LUC. The constructs 35S:OsARF5-GFP and 35S:GFP, developed for transgene in this study, served as the effector vectors. These effectors were introduced into the N. benthamiana leaves together with the reporter vector mediated by agrobacterium. The N. benthamiana plants with infiltrated leaves were then grown for 3 days. Afterward, we examined the activities of both LUC and renilla LUC (REN) through an analysis kit (Promega, #E1910). The relative activities of LUC to REN was calculated.

## Yeast two-hybrid assays
For yeast two-hybrid experiments, the 3420 bp coding sequence of OsARF5 were inserted into the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors with Bgl II and Sal I restriction enzymes. To screen the partner of OsARF5, we generated a yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) cDNA library in pGADT7 vector of rice. Then, the construct of OsARF5 in pGBKT7 vector was applied as a bait to screen the library, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Clontech, PT3024-1) [33]. To verify the interaction between OsARF5 and OsIAA7, OsIAA11 or OsIAA30, these three OsIAAs were inserted into the pGBKT7 vector using EcoR I and Sal I enzymes. Subsequently, the OsIAA7, OsIAA11 or OsIAA30 construct in pGBKT7 was transformed along with OsARF5 in pGADT7 into the yeast strain, Y2H Gold. The transformed yeast strains were then grown on a selective medium that lacked tryptophan and leucine (SD/-Trp-Leu) or on a medium that lacked tryptophan, leucine, histidine, and adenine (SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade), at 30°C for about three days.

## BiFC assays
To perform bimolecular florescence complementation (BiFC) assays, OsARF5 was ligated to the N-terminal of YFP (nYFP) in the pSYNE vector with Xba I and Bgl II restriction enzymes, while OsIAA7/11/30 were ligated to the C-terminal of YFP (cYFP) in the pSYCE vector with Xba I and BamH I restriction enzymes [33]. The resulting plasmids, including 35S:OsARF5-nYFP and 35S:OsIAA7/11/30-cYFP, were co-infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana. After 72 h of expression, the YFP fluorescence was visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscopy with a 513 nm wavelength (Zeiss, LSM780).

## High-throughput mRNA sequencing
The high-throughput mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was carried out on the seedlings of WT and osarf5-1 before and after 72 h of gravitropic stimulation, respectively. Two biological replicates were performed. TopHat was used to align the reads to the rice genome (IRGSP-1.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined based on the criteria of an absolute value of log2 (fold change) > 2 and a Q value < 0.05. To further elucidate the functional significance of these DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis utilizing PlantGSEA [6]. The RNA-seq data has been submitted to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), with the accession number available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE269550.

## Statistical analysis
Data shown in this study were means ± standard deviation (s.d.). A two-tailed unpaired t-test was employed for statistical analysis. Significant differences were marked with one asterisk (*) for P < 0.05 and two asterisks (**) for greatly significant differences at P < 0.01. The abbreviation “n.s.” denoted no significant differences (P > 0.05). The experiments were conducted with three biological replicates.

## The bta3-1 mutation confers an enlarged tiller angle phenotype in rice
To explore the regulatory factors of tiller angle formation, the bta3-1 mutant was obtained in rice. The bta3-1 exhibited a spread-out tiller phenotype throughout its entire developmental period. Both gravitropism and phototropism affect the upright growth of shoots [24], [34], [35]. To determine whether shoot gravitropism is disrupted by bta3-1 mutation, the response of WT and bta3-1 to gravitropic stimulation was examined. The bta3-1 seedlings showed a reduced response to gravistimulation under various light conditions, including light, light to dark, dark, and dark to light. After 72 h of gravity treatment, the curvature angle of the shoots against the horizontal axis in bta3-1 was smaller than that in WT. The curvature angle of the shoots was unaffected by light, suggesting that the response of bta3-1 to gravitropic stimulation was less sensitive to light (Fig. 1C–H, Fig. S1A and B). These findings reveal that the bta3-1 mutation disrupts shoot gravitropism in rice.
Phytohormones, particularly auxin, are crucial in regulating shoot gravitropism of rice. We initially assessed how auxin influences shoot gravitropism in WT and bta3-1 seedlings. The seedlings were subjected to gravistimulation along with the synthetic auxin analog NAA or the polar auxin transport (PAT) inhibitor NPA. The curvature angle of WT seedlings under NAA and NPA treatment was smaller than that of the ddH2O control, suggesting that a 100 µM NAA treatment disrupts auxin homeostasis and blocks the shoot gravitropism; NPA treatment blocked PAT and further reduced the gravitropic response. In contrast, the application of exogenous NAA had no effect on the gravitropic response of bta3-1; however, NPA treatment further diminished its shoot gravitropism (Fig. 1I and Fig. S1C). These results imply that BTA3 affects auxin signaling. The bta3-1 mutant showed reduced sensitivity to exogenous auxin, indicating that the bta3-1 mutation interferes with the gravitropic response to auxin. Nevertheless, the curvature angle of the bta3-1 seedlings treated with NPA was smaller than that of untreated seedlings, suggesting that NPA further blocked PAT in the bta3-1 mutant and suppressed shoot gravitropism (Fig. 1I and Fig. S1C).
Furthermore, we examined the gravitropic response of both WT and bta3-1 to various phytohormones. The external application of GA enhanced the gravitropic response of WT, while it diminished the gravitropic response ability in bta3-1. Conversely, the application of ABA, BR, SA, and JA, reduced the gravitropic response in WT and bta3-1 (Fig. 1J and Fig. S1D). These findings imply that the application of exogenous GA elevates shoot gravitropism in rice, a process that is impaired by the bta3-1 mutation.

## BTA3 is a novel gene controlling tiller angle development and encodes auxin response factor 5 in rice
To isolate the BTA3 gene, we first conducted a genetic analysis of the bta3-1 mutation through a cross to WT. The resulting F1 plants exhibited a normal tiller angle phenotype. In the F2 population, the ratio of plants with normal tiller angles to those exhibiting the bta3-1 phenotypes was 3.48 : 1, χ2 = 3.22 < χ2 = 3.84 (P = 0.05，df = 1), indicating that bta3-1 was a single recessive mutation (Table S3). The bta3-1 mutation was initially mapped to chromosome 2, located between the G2-1 and B2-4 markers by using the BSA method (Fig. 2A). Ultimately, the bta3-1 mutation was pinpointed to a 480 kb region between G2-3A and G2-7, containing 49 open reading frames (ORFs). Then, we performed a complementary test by delivering the BTA3p:BTA3-GFP construct into the bta3-1 mutant. All the transgenic lines with BTA3p:BTA3-GFP construct exhibited a normal tiller angle phenotype (Fig. 2C and D, Fig. S2A). The shoot response of the complementary transgenic lines to gravistimulation was more pronounced than that of bta3-1, yet comparable to the transgenic lines harboring empty vector (EV) (Fig. 2E). These findings indicate that the BTA3 gene recovers the larger tiller angle and weakened shoot gravitropism in bta3-1. Additionally, the plant height and tiller number defects in bta3-1 were also rescued by transforming the BTA3 gene (Fig. S2B and C). The BTA3 gene encodes an auxin response factor that has been previously identified as OsARF5 through genome-wide analysis in rice [36]. Therefore, the BTA3 and bta3-1 were renamed as OsARF5 and osarf5-1, respectively.
To gain a better understanding the roles of OsARF5 in regulating tiller angle, we created overexpression transgenic lines (OE lines) of OsARF5 by transforming the 35S:OsARF5-GFP construct into WT (Fig. S3A and B). The tiller angles of the OE1, 2, and 3 lines were smaller than that of WT, resulting in a more compact architecture (Fig. 2F and G, Fig. S3C). The shoot curvature angle of the OE lines in response to gravistimulation was greater than that of the EV controls (Fig. 2H).
Twenty-five ARF homologs were identified in the rice genome, which were categorized into seven subfamilies, referred to as Group I–VII, through phylogenetic analysis (Fig. S4A). OsARF5 was classified in Group I alongside OsARF19, OsARF21, and OsARF16. The proteins in Group I–IV or V–VII featured either three or two conserved domains, which include the B3 domain, auxin response domain, and PB1 domain (Fig. S4B).

OsARF5 belongs to the ARF superfamily, which may play a role in auxin signaling, potentially being activated by auxin itself. An auxin response element (AuxRE, TGTCTC) at 612 bp upstream of ATG in OsARF5 gene was identified. We then treated 14-day-old WT seedlings with 100 µM NAA, which resulted in the induction of OsARF5 expression two hours after NAA application (Fig. 3D). In addition, the expression of OsARF5 was also triggered by gravistimulation (Fig. 3E).
The OsARF5 protein consists of 1139 amino acids and includes three functional domains (Fig. S4B). To observe its subcellular localization, the 35S:OsARF5-GFP and 35S:GFP constructs were transiently expressed in the leaves of N. benthamiana through an agrobacterium-mediated infiltration method. The GFP signal from the infiltrated leaves carrying the 35S:GFP plasmid served as a positive control. The GFP signal from the N. benthamiana leaves expressing the OsARF5-GFP protein was exclusively located in the nucleus, coinciding with DAPI signals (Fig. 3F).

## The osarf5-1 mutation and gravistimulation treatment affect genome-wide gene expression
To further explore the roles of OsARF5, an RNA-seq was performed on two sets of seedlings: 7-day-old WT and osarf5-1 seedlings grown vertically, which served as controls without gravistimulation, referred to as WT-C and osarf5-1-C; WT and osarf5-1 seedlings that were vertically grown for 4 days and then horizontally grown for an additional 3 days, representing the gravistimulation treatment, labeled as WT-G and osarf5-1-G. The Pearson correlation coefficients (R2) for two biological replicates of WT-C, osarf5-1-C, WT-G, and osarf5-1-G were all above 0.93. The R2 values for samples within the same group and between the same samples treated with or without gravistimulation were greater than 0.90 (Fig. S5). These results suggest that the sampling for transcriptome analysis was reliable. The quality of the RNA-seq was analyzed (Table S4), and the mapping results were evaluated, suggesting that the quantity was adequate and the data quality met the standards required for further experimental analysis (Table S5).
To study the effects of the osarf5-1 mutation and the application of gravity on genome-wide gene expression, a thorough analysis of the RNA-seq data was carried out to identify the DEGs. Compared with WT, the osarf5-1 mutation led to changes in gene expression under normal conditions. A total of 2071 DEGs were found between osarf5-1-C and WT-C, with 819 genes being upregulated and 1252 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A). Gravistimulation induced more DEGs in the WT (3755 genes, accounting for 13.04 % of all expressed genes) than that in the osarf5-1 mutant (3221 genes, making up 11.13 % of all expressed genes). ## Discussion
The angle of tillers is a key factor regulating the plant architecture and grain yield in rice. Shoot gravitropism is closely associated with the control of tiller angle. Auxin is a major factor in managing both shoot gravitropism and the development of tiller angles [2]. However, the underlying mechanisms of auxin signaling in the regulation of tiller angle are not fully understand. In this study, we found that OsARF5, a transcription factor associated with auxin signaling, was involved in the tiller angle formation of rice (Fig. 10).Fig. 10A working model for illustrating how OsARF5 controls the angle of tillers in rice. OsARF5 interacts with OsIAA7, OsIAA11, and OsIAA30, which suppress the activity of OsARF5. Once the OsIAAs are degraded, OsARF5 is released and binds to the promoter of target genes, including HSFA2d, OsAGPL1, LA1, and OsPIN1a, to control shoot gravitropism and the angle of tillers in rice.

## A novel regulator OsARF5 modulates tiller angle through auxin signaling in rice
In this study, we isolated the BTA3 gene, which encodes the OsARF5 protein with the B3, auxin response, and PB1 domains belonging to the ARF transcription factor family. A missense mutation in the B3 domain of OsARF5 disrupted its function, resulting in an enlarged tiller angle phenotype in rice (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. S1 and S2). The B3 domain in ARFs acts as a DNA binding domain [37]. Overexpression of OsARF5 conferred a more compact plant type (Fig. 2, Fig. S3). These results reveal that OsARF5 plays a negative role in regulating the rice tiller angle. Shoot gravitropism is a pivotal factor in determining the tiller angle [2]. We found that the osarf5-1 mutant exhibited a partial loss of shoot gravitropism, which further increased the tiller angles (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1).
Auxin is an important phytohormone that manipulates shoot gravitropism. The asymmetric distribution of auxin, caused by lateral auxin transport (LAT) and PAT, is related to shoot gravitropism, further controlling the angle of tillers [2]. The expression of OsARF5 was activated by both auxin and gravistimilation treatments (Fig. 3), suggesting its potential role in auxin signaling and the control of shoot gravitropism in rice. As expected, the response of shoot gravitropism to exogenous application of NAA, a synthesis analog of auxin, was reduced in osarf5-1 mutant (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1). These findings imply that the osarf5-1 mutation affects auxin signaling rather than auxin biosynthesis, highlighting the role of OsARF5 in auxin signaling pathways.
In addition to auxin, GA is a hormone involved in many growth and development processes [38], [39]. The interaction between GA and auxin regulates agronomic traits of crops has been reported. Overexpression of rice Growth Regulating Factor 7 (OsGRF7), encoding a plant specific transcription factor, reduces leaf angle and leads to a more compact plant architecture. The osgrf7 mutant exhibits contrasting phenotypes. OsGRF7 binds to the promoters of rice Auxin Response Factor 12 (OsARF12) and OsCYP714B1, a cytochrome P450 gene, thereby regulating auxin signaling and GA biosynthesis [12], [40], [41]. The asymmetric expression of OsGA3ox1, a gene for an enzyme in GA biosynthesis pathway, is induced by gravity treatment, causing a gradient of GA and differential expression of Xyloglucan Endotransglycosylethylene (XET), an enzyme related to cell wall formation, at the base of the tillers, which alters the tiller angle [42]. Consistently, the osarf5-1 mutation affected the response of shoot gravitropism to GA (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1), implying OsARF5 mediated crosstalk between auxin and GA in controlling shoot gravitropism. Further insights into these mechanisms will be revealed in the future.

## OsARF5 acts as a transcription activator to control shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice
Proteins in the ARF superfamily usually act as transcription activators or suppressors to manage gene expression and mediate the response to auxin [43]. Twenty-five ARF proteins have been found in the rice genome (Fig. S4) [36], [44]. Previous studies have shown that the overexpression of OsmiR167a represses the expression of OsARF12, 17, and 25 and results in a spread-out tiller phenotype. These OsARF proteins function redundantly to control the angle of tillers in rice [12]. In this study, the osarf5-1 mutation caused differential gene expression on a genome-wide scale. The number of DEGs in the response to gravistimulation in the osarf5-1 was lower than that in WT, suggesting that the osarf5-1 is less sensitive to gravistimulation, which aligns with its diminished shoot gravitropism (Fig. 4). Almost all the known genes affecting tiller angle formation display differential expression between the WT and osarf5-1, highlighting the significant roles of OsARF5 in regulating the tiller angle development (Fig. 5).
The genes that control tiller angle, such as HSFA2D, OsAGPL1, LA1, and OsPIN1a, showed reduced expression in the osarf5-1 mutant (Fig. 5). Mutations of HSFA2D, OsAGPL1, LA1, OsPIN1a, and OsPIN1b resulted in an enlarged tiller angle and weakened shoot gravitropism in rice [8], [9], [10], [21], [45]. OsARF5 directly interacts with their promoters to activate their expression and further control shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice (Fig. 6). These findings reveal that OsARF5 is a typical transcription factor capable of binding to DNA and activating gene expression. Besides the regulatory interactions among OsAGPL1, HSFA2D, LA1, and OsPIN1a [2], OsARF5 also fine-tunes their expression during the tiller angle formation.
Furthernore, the osarf5-1 mutation changed the expression of the known genes affecting the tiller angle development in rice (Fig. 5). The RAN-seq data revealed that auxin response genes were altered in osarf5-1 when compared to WT. The down-regulated DEGs were enriched in pathways related to the starch and sucrose metabolism and plant hormone signal transduction (Figs. 4, S6). These findings indicate that OsARF5 regulates almost every step of gravitropic response, including starch biogenesis of amyloplast, signal transduction, and shoot curvature, which in turn controls tiller angles in rice [2].

## OsIAA7 and OsIAA11 have opposite functions against OsARF5 to control tiller angle in rice
AUX/IAA proteins are involved in regulating plant development, with 31 members in the entire rice genome [46]. OsIAA11 has been reported to regulate lateral root initiation [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], callus initiation, and the uptake of manganese (Mn) and cadmium (Cd) [52], [53]. However, it remains unclear whether the Aux/IAA proteins control the angle of tillers in rice. In this study, we discovered that OsARF5 was physically associated with OsIAA7, OsIAA11, and OsIAA30 in yeast and plant systems (Fig. 7). Supporting this, the four proteins were found to colocalize in the nucleus (Fig. 3, Fig. 8). Moreover, the tiller angles of CRISPR/Cas9-engineered mutants of OsIAAs, such as osiaa7s and osiaa11s, were smaller than those of WT, similar to the OsARF5-OE lines, suggesting that OsIAA7 and OsIAA11 positively regulate tiller angle in rice (Fig. 9). Our results indicate that OsIAA7 and OsIAA11 function antagonistically to OsARF5. Consistently, the expression of the OsARF5-target genes, including OsAGPL1, LA1, HSFA2D, and OsPIN1a, was elevated in osiaa7-1 and osiaa11-1 mutants (Fig. 9). Overall, these two AUX/IAA proteins inhibit the OsARF5′s activity to control shoot gravitropism and tiller angle through their physical interaction. This agrees with previous studies showing that AUX/IAA proteins normally interact with ARF, suppress the activation activity of ARF transcription factors and are degraded through the 26S proteasome pathway upon auxin signaling, thereby releasing ARF activity [43], [54].

## Conclusion
Taken together, our findings demonstrate the roles of OsARF5 in controlling the formation of the tiller angles in rice. OsIAA7, OsIAA11, and OsIAA30 interact with OsARF5 to repress its activation activity and prevent auxin signaling. Once the OsIAA7/11/30 are degraded upon sensing auxin signals, OsARF5 is freed. The released OsARF5 is directly associated with cis-acting elements located in the promoters of HSFA2D, OsAGPL1, LA1, and OsPIN1a, triggering their transcription and ultimately controlling shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice (Fig. 10). These results shed light on the insights into the mechanisms behind tiller angle formation and molecular breeding for ideal plant architecture, while also raising new questions for future study in rice.

## Figure Fig. 10: A working model for illustrating how OsARF5 controls the angle of tillers in rice. OsARF5 interacts with OsIAA7, OsIAA11, and OsIAA30, which suppress the activity of OsARF5. Once the OsIAAs are degraded, OsARF5 is released and binds to the promoter of target genes, including HSFA2d, OsAGPL1, LA1, and OsPIN1a, to control shoot gravitropism and the angle of tillers in rice.
