# Cellular and physiological functions of SGR family in gravitropic response in higher plants

## Abstract
Graphical abstractKeywords: Gravity sensing, SGR family, Shoot Gravitropism, Amyloplast sedimentation, Cell division, Vacuole formation, Endodermis

## Introduction
The phenomenon of tropism is observed in plants as a response to external stimuli; these include phototropism, hydrotropism, and gravitropism. Phototropism refers to the directional movement of plants towards a source of light, which facilitates the absorption of photons from sunlight for photosynthesis [1]. Equally, the movement of roots toward water to acquire mineral nutrition is known as hydrotropism [2]. While, gravitropism refers to the plant's response to the gravitational force. The gravitropism leads to a distinctive pattern of plant growth, wherein the shoot exhibits negative gravitropism, i.e., growing in a direction opposite to that of gravity. In contrast, the root exhibits positive gravitropism, i.e., growing in the same direction as gravity [3]. Gravitropism reveals the bipolar growth movement exhibited by plants. The shoot's gravitropic response facilitates photosynthesis in plants, while the roots aid in the uptake of water and nutrients from the soil and provide structural support to tissues and organs, as well as provide firm anchorage.
Since shoot gravitropism affects plant architecture, gene families engaged in gravitropism are related to crop production. For example, in plants with wide shoot canopy and loose architecture, the branches are spread out in the field; this reduces the number of net crop plants that can be grown per unit area. It may become vulnerable to attacks from many external factors, which lowers the quality of crops. On the other hand, compact plant architecture with narrow plant branch angles increases the crop density grown per unit area. On the other hand, too much of density could have the disadvantage of increasing the humidity in the field due to lack of space between branches. Higher humidity can cause outbreaks of various diseases, such as fungi or insect attacks. Therefore, SGR and other families could facilitate the generation of commercial crop cultivars with ideal branch angles through research on gravity response for enhanced crop yield and quality [4].
The phenomenon of gravitropism encompasses three primary mechanisms: the detection of gravity, the transmission of gravitational signals, and the subsequent response, which is mediated by the transport and redistribution of auxin (Fig. 1). Upon perceiving the gravitational force, the amyloplast containing starch particles initiates sedimentation in the direction of gravity [5]. Subsequently, the process of signal transduction and cascade effect occurs, wherein the participation of secondary messengers, namely Calcium ions (Ca2+), proton (H+), and inositol-1,4,5-triphosphate (InsP3), has been observed [6], [7]. Subsequently, the plant tissues respond as the shoot's endodermal cells and the root's columella cells commence to redistribute auxin through auxin transporters, such as PIN proteins. The concentration and ratio of auxin are reoriented towards the lower end of the cell, with the aid of PIN proteins that promote the accumulation of auxin in the direction of gravity [8].

Various gene families, namely LAZY, PGM, TAC1, SGR, and PIN, are implicated in distinct stages of shoot gravitropic responses (Table 1). In brief, the SHOOT GRAVITROPISM (SGR) family and phosphoglucomutase (PGM) have been identified as critical players in the process of gravity sensing [9], [10]. LAZY genes are implicated in the process of signal transduction. PIN genes are activated in response to the force of gravity. According to Mano et al., the phosphoglucomutase (PGM) typically operates in the process of starch biosynthesis for amyloplast [10]. Further, the gravity signaling process involves the participation of the LAZY family and TAC1 [11], [12]. Additionally, it has been established that PIN proteins function as auxin efflux proteins [8].
Some known genes that play significant role in gravitropism of higher plants.
Here, we focus on illuminating the role of the SGR family in gravitropic responses in plants at cellular and organism levels. The SGR gene family exhibits a range of functionalities in gravitropism perception. For example, the proteins SGR1, SGR5, and SGR7 have been identified as transcriptional regulators. The regulation of asymmetric cell division is jointly governed by SGR1 and SGR7 [13], [14], [15], [16]. On the other hand, the organization of amyloplasts is mediated by SGR2, SGR3, SGR6, and SGR8, which was demonstrated by several studies [17], [18], [19], [20]. Additionally, the SGR3, SGR4, and SGR8 proteins are involved in the vacuolar pathway [6], [20], [21]. SGR5 can perceive gravitational stimuli and modulate auxin production [13], [22]. Moreover, it is found that a mutation in SGR family members in plants results in functional impairments in gravity perception. It is evidenced by a decrease in gravitropism in the plant's stem, root, and hypocotyl (Table 2). Although there are prominent examples of SGR family members affecting plant growth and development in response to gravity, there is a lack of complete understanding regarding the involvement of the SGR gene family in shoot gravitropism, particularly in the context of gravity perception. Therefore, this review aims to present insights into recent discoveries concerning the SGR protein family and its function in gravity perception in plant cells. The information synthesized in this review would be useful to plant scientists, researchers, and students to understand the significance of the SGR family in gravity response.

## Conserved domains in SGR families
Proteins are known to contain specific domains, a unique sequence of amino acid residues that have been conserved throughout evolution. The classification of protein families is commonly based on the presence of a defined domain due to the significant impact of the combination of domains on the protein's function. Notably, the SGR family does not share a conserved domain among its members [23]. However, they harbor domains that are shared with non-SGR proteins, viz. SGR1 and SGR7 harbor GRAS family domain, SGR5 possesses the IDD family domain, SGR2 has iPLA1 domain, SGR3 carries VAM3 domain, and SGR4 possesses ZIG/ATVI11 domain (for details, see Fig. 2). Each SGR protein's domain was identified to comprehend the mechanisms underlying shoot gravitropism regulation. Further, numerous orthologs of the Arabidopsis SGR family have been identified in various plant species, such as soybean, tobacco, rice, maize, and tomato (Table 3). The key domains harbored by SGR family members are discussed in the following sections (Fig. 2).

The GRAS transcription factors are denoted after the initial three members, namely GIBBERELLIC-ACID INSENSITIVE (GAI), REPRESSOR of GAI (RGA), and SCARECROW (SCR), and they possess a specific GRAS domain. The C-terminal of GRAS proteins is typically composed of multiple discrete motifs, including LHRI, VHIID, LHRII, PFYRE, and SAW [24], [25]. The SGR1 and SGR7 families share a common GRAS domain [25]. The functions of the GRAS domain, such as the regulation of movement and intercellular trafficking, rely on various regions within it. It is proposed that multiple motifs of the GRAS domain regulate the movements of SGR1 and SGR7. The SGR7 movement was impeded in the stele, and the endodermis did not exhibit the presence of either protein upon deletion of its VHIID or PFYRE domain [24].
Furthermore, it has been observed that the SGR1 can interact with the SGR7/SHR, thereby leading to the formation of complexes via their LRI-VHIID-LRII motifs; in turn, it causes an interruption in the movement of SGR7 and its subsequent confinement within the nucleus by SGR1/SCR [25]. The findings suggest that the genetic mutants of SGR1 and SGR7 have the potential to induce anomalous endodermis, thereby hampering gravitropism. The SGR7/SHR exhibits a hydrophobic groove, and several studies confirm that a subset of the INDETERMINATE DOMAIN (IDD) family, specifically IDD1 through IDD13, possesses an SHR-binding motif that is highly conserved. This motif is characterized by a Zinc Finger 4 (ZF)-specific sequence, namely R(K/R)DxxITHxAFCD, which facilitates binding to the groove mentioned above of the SHR [26]. The SGR5 gene is responsible for encoding IDD15, a member of the IDD family. However, due to the absence of a crucial Phe (F) residue, SGR5 cannot bind to SGR7. The SGR5 protein comprises four ZF domains: a C2H2-type ZF and a coiled-coil domain located at the C-terminus.
Morita et al. identified the role of the C2H2-type ZF domain in both DNA or RNA binding and protein–protein interaction [13]. Additionally, the investigation revealed that the SGR5 gene exhibits two splice variants. There are two variants of the SGR5 protein: the full-size SGR5 transcription factor and a truncated form of SGR5 that does not possess functional ZF motifs. The splicing variants forms of SGR5 over-expression line in Arabidopsis resulted in a decrease in gravitropic response in the inflorescence stem. Based on the findings, the ZF domain may be crucial in the gravitropic response [27], [28].
Tani et al. noticed that SGR2 belonged to the iPLA1 family of intracellular phospholipases A1 [29], where the DDHD domain is prevalent in this particular family, which is known for its high degree of conservation and contains four amino acid residues that are conserved across species (i.e., three residues of aspartic acid, D and a residue of histidine, H). Furthermore, the domain is believed to be significant in the formation of a site for binding metal ions [29], [30]. Moreover, the lipase consensus sequence GXSXG is present in the iPLA1 family, which is shared with the SGR2 protein [29], [31]. A site-directed mutagenesis study in SGR2, wherein the amino acid Arg (R) was substituted with the final Gly (G), showed a significant impairment in the ability to respond to gravity [32], indicating the crucial role of the lipase consensus GXSXG sequence in SGR2 for a gravitropic response.
The proteins SGR3 and SGR4 share SNARE (soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) protein domains, which play a crucial role in the vacuolar and vescicular transport pathway. The genes SGR3 and SGR4 encode the Qa-SNARE AtVAM3/SYP22 and Qb-SNARE AtVTI11, respectively [20], [33]. Syntaxins, such as SGR3, are a constituent of the SNAREs, a highly significant structural group. The syntaxins possess a conserved coiled-coil domain known as a SNARE-helix proximal to a C-terminal transmembrane domain or lipid anchor. The protein complex SGR3/SYP22 can identify and establish a binding with either one or two additional SNAREs located on the membrane of the target. The v-SNARE present on the membrane of the vesicle is subsequently identified [34]. As a result, the endodermal cells can create an SNARE complex [20]. The interactions between SGR4 (ZIG/AtVTI11) and AtVAM3 have been previously documented [20], [34]. According to a study, the zig-3 mutant, characterized by a substituted amino acid in the conserved residue of the coiled-coil domain, exhibited the most negligible impact on the gravitropic response compared to other zig mutants. The coiled-coil domain plays a crucial role in the interaction with a partner SNARE, and it has been observed that this interaction may experience a reduction in zig-3 [32]. The observations mentioned above imply that the transportation of vesicles to the pre-vacuolar compartment or vacuole in the endodermal cells is facilitated by a distinct SNARE complex that comprises AtVAM3 and AtVTI11. In addition, it appears that the gravitropism process necessitates cargo conveyance facilitated by the ZIG-dependent pathway, as opposed to ZIG itself [20], [32].
Next, the SGR6 protein carries the characteristic HEAT repeat motifs, with no other conserved domain detected in this member [17], [35]. The nomenclature of the HEAT motif is derived from the constituent proteins: Huntingtin, elongation factor 3 (EF3), the PR65/A subunit of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), and the lipid kinase TOR [36]. The C-terminal region of SGR6 is characterized by predicted six HEAT repeats. The study has postulated that the protein–protein interaction associated with gravitropism function may be influenced by the two HEAT repeats located at the C-terminal end of the protein [17].
The SGR8 gene has been reported to comprise a solitary Dna-J domain and four IWN repeats [35], [37]. It bears a resemblance to C. elegans RME-8, a protein that performs the task of sorting other proteins to the vacuole via receptor-mediated endocytosis [37]. The interaction between the Dna-J domain and Heat Shock Protein 70 (HSP70) has been established, and studies have revealed that proteins containing Dna-J facilitate the proper folding of substrate proteins by promoting their interaction with HSP70 [37], [38]. Especially, Heat Shock Cognate Protein 70 (HSC70), which belongs to the HSP 70 family, interacts with Dna-J domain in the Drosophila homolog of RME-8 [37], [39]. Binding affinity between HSC70 and substrate could be enhanced by Dna-J by rising ATPase activity [39], [40]. The multifunctional proteins HSC70 and HSP70 involve various processes, such as protein transportation and dynamin binding through the effect of the clathrin-coated vesicle formation [37], [38]. These functions aid in separating vesicles from the membrane of the donor organelle [38]. In this manner, the vacuolar membrane system may influence gravitropism via SGR8. Additionally, the IWN repeats of C. elegans RME-8 have been reported to control the conformation of RME-8, aiding in the exposure of Dna-J domain. However, not much study has been conducted on the IWN repeats of SGR8 [41].
In the SGR9 protein, a RING finger protein of the C3H2C3 type is present and is characterized by a distinctive N-terminal half and a RING finger domain of the C3H2C3 type located at the C-terminal region [19], [42]. It has been established that specific RING finger proteins act as ubiquitin E3 ligases and that the RING finger domains are crucial for E3 activity [42]. Further findings indicate that the RING finger domain of SGR9 exhibits ubiquitin E3 ligase activity in vitro, implying its involvement in the ubiquitin–proteasome system [42], [43]. The regulation of the interaction between amyloplasts and actin filaments is a crucial aspect of gravitropism, which is mediated by the ubiquitin E3 ligase [19].
Despite the absence of shared conserved domains among the SGR family, individual genes play a significant role in gravitropic responses at the cellular level. Each SGR possesses a critical domain shared with non-SGR protein families and is found to exert a functional role in shoot gravitropism in plants. This represents the availability of molecular diversity of each SGR protein family controlling cellular responses.

## Nucleolar localization
The SGR1 and SGR7 proteins are crucial in Arabidopsis for asymmetric cell division and play a significant role in the specification of quiescent center (QC) cells, maintenance of peripheral stem cells, and radial pattern formation of roots. SGR1 is expressed in the ground tissue, where it autonomously regulates asymmetric cell division. On the other hand, SGR7 initiates its activity in the central region and moves towards the QC and ground tissue cells. SGR1 is induced by SHR and maintains this induced state, requiring the transcriptional activation of SCR. SGR1 and SHR interact with each other, controlling the periclinal division in the endodermal cells. This process plays a crucial developmental role in plants, particularly in regulating the development and structure of ground tissue by controlling periclinal division in endodermal cells [42], [43].
Additionally, it was observed through GFP-tagged expression and GUS analysis that both genes were co-localized within the nucleus. Moreover, it was observed that SGR7 exhibited expression in both the cytoplasm and endosomes. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the localization of SGR7 orthologs in various crops, including rice, soybean, maize, and potato, was restricted solely to the nucleus (Fig. 3A and B) [42], [43]. Furthermore, the nuclear localization of SGR5, which plays a role in regulating gravity signaling through the management of starch accumulation, was confirmed. In order to examine the localization of SGR5, the researchers utilized a fused GFP that was linked to the C-terminus of SGR5, and it was expressed under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter in Arabidopsis protoplasts (Fig. 3C) [13]. Furthermore, these proteins are thought to predominantly localize in the nucleus, given their commonality in contributing to the regulation of transcription.

## Plasma membrane and vacuole
SGR2, SGR3, SGR4, SGR6 primarily affect gravity sensing, particularly influencing amyloplast sedimentation and transportation, with a significant impact on vesicle formation and movement [17], [20], [32]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, it is reported that SGR2 and SGR6 are linked to the vacuole membranes, as well as involved in the process of amyloplast sedimentation [17], [44]. However, the orthologs of these proteins expressed in other crops appear localized to the cytoplasmic region. The regulation of amyloplast sedimentation is facilitated by transporting SGR3 and SGR4 proteins from the vacuole. SGR3 is predominantly restricted to the vacuolar and cellular membranes and is present in both the SNARE complex and the endomembrane. The gene SGR3, which resembles the yeast Vam3p, is responsible for encoding the Syntaxin AtVAM3 in Arabidopsis. This protein exhibits localization within the pre-vacuolar compartment and vacuole, indicating its potential involvement in facilitating the transport of vesicles toward the vacuole (Fig. 3E) [18]. It is widely acknowledged that SGR2 exhibits localization to vacuoles. An investigation aimed to examine the subcellular distribution of SGR2i-GFP in the root structures of transgenic seedlings, the results indicated the fluorescence signal was primarily detected on membranes that are presumably linked to vacuoles and minor organelles, as opposed to plasma membranes. It is commonly recognized that SGR2 is found in vacuoles; a study on the localization of SGR2i-GFP in the roots of genetically modified seedlings revealed that the fluorescent signal was mainly observed on membranes associated with vacuoles and smaller organelles rather than on plasma membranes (Fig. 3D) [18]. The transgenic plants exhibited similar localization patterns and bright dot presence, which was previously observed in protoplasts. The findings presented in this study offer compelling support for the proposition that SGR2 is localized explicitly on the membranes of vacuoles and minor organelles across diverse tissue types. Additionally, it is established that the SGR4 protein, which performs a comparable function, is localized to the vacuoles (Fig. 3G) [18]. Finally, it is demonstrated that SGR6 (Fig. 3F) [17], which shares a similar function with the genes mentioned earlier, is predominantly localized on the vacuolar membrane.

## ER and Golgi network
Despite being primarily linked to plasma membranes, the SGR4 plays a significant role in vesicular transport. SGR4 has been identified as a participant in various cellular processes, including ER-to-Golgi transport, Golgi apparatus function, vacuole regulation, SNARE complex formation, trans-Golgi network activity, and vacuole membrane dynamics. SGR4 is predominantly confined to the Golgi apparatus and the trans-Golgi network, which are linked to cellular transport. The SGR8 protein is crucial in regulating vacuole formation and trafficking, endosome formation, vacuole protein sorting, and embryonic growth axis determination. Specifically, it is recognized to participate in protein translocation from the Golgi apparatus to the post-Golgi compartment. SGR8 plays a crucial role not only in the transport of vacuolar contents but also in the trafficking of vacuolar membrane components essential for vacuolar dynamics. The subcellular localization of SGR8 in Arabidopsis found to multiple organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, endosome membrane, Golgi apparatus, intracellular membrane-bounded organelle, late endosome, peroxisome, trans-Golgi network, vacuolar membrane, and vacuole (Fig. 3H) [38], [39]. This signifies the multifaceted functions and interactions of SGR8 in gravitropic response.

## Amyloplast
The regulation of amyloplast dynamics and sedimentation is governed by SGR9, which controls the interactions between amyloplasts and actin filaments (AFs) in the endodermal cells (Fig. 3I) [19]. A study demonstrated that AFs are predominantly localized in amyloplasts [19]. Additionally, in the phenotypes of plants with sgr2, sgr3, sgr4, and sgr8 mutants, the amyloplasts were seen in the upper and lower sides of the endodermal cells, which is known to localize outside of the vacuole lying in proximity to cellular boundaries [20], [38], [44]. In mutants of sgr2 and sgr4, when amyloplasts were stained with potassium iodide, they formed less starch granules than wildtype plants [44]. Overall, the analysis showed amyloplast localization is affected in sgr mutants, causing altered gravitropic responses in plants.

## Functional role of SGR/cellular role of SGR protein in shoot gravitropism
The gravitropic reaction exhibited by plants can be grouped into three distinct stages: the perception of gravity, the transduction of signals, and the response of asymmetric growth. The involvement of the endodermis is crucial in the gravity perception process [44]. The perception of gravity in shoots is facilitated through amyloplast sedimentation in the endodermis's vacuolar membrane [21]. The mutation of the SGR proteins in Arabidopsis impedes the development of functional endodermis or vacuolar membranes, leading to anomalous amyloplast deposition that impacts the shoot's gravity. The following text will highlight various biological functions of SGR family members in plants.

## SGR1 and SGR7 regulate endodermis differentiation
Asymmetric cell division is required for appropriate amyloplast sedimentation and normal gravity response in plants. SGR1 and SGR7 perform this function in the cortex and endodermis primary cell and endodermal cell lineages of both tissues in the shoot and root [16]. With AtSGR7, AtSGR1 governs shoot axial organ radial structure and root radial patterning [44], [45].
Upstream of AtSGR1 (SCR) is AtSGR7 (SHR) [15], the level of AtSGR7 expression was unaltered in the scr-1 mutant. Furthermore, the SHR::GFP expression pattern in the scr-3 mutant GFP was revealed to be independent [14]. Despite this, SGR1 transcript levels were reduced in all shr mutant alleles, indicating that SHR expression is necessary for SCR transcriptional activation [16], [45]. A recent study discovered that SGR1 acts autonomously in ground tissue to govern asymmetric cell division. SGR1 gene expression is regulated by autoregulation. SGR1 inhibits SGR7 movement, and transitory SGR1 activities are sufficient to distinguish endodermis and cortical fates via asymmetric cell division [46]. SGR7 proteins are transported from stele to QC and ground tissue cells. SGR1 expression in QC and ground tissue stem cells is triggered by SHR and maintained by autoregulation [46]. SGR1 expression reduces SHR's inductive capacity. In ground tissue stem cells, QC inhibits SGR1-induced periclinal cell division [46]. SGR1 causes periclinal division in the daughters of ground tissue cells [47]. The SGR1 is in charge of regulating fast endodermal (SGR7) and cortical destiny separation. SGR7 and SGR1 residues are degraded in the outer cell [46]. SHR in WT::GFP could not travel in phloem partner cells or epidermal cells, which were found to aid in the migration of other proteins. GFP was detected in the endodermis of SHR::GFP/scr plants. It suggests that SGR1 inhibits SHR migration to nearby cortical cells, inhibiting further periclinal division [46]. Furthermore, histone deacetylase (HDA19) was discovered to interact with SGR1 and influence root epidermal cell patterning via cortical cell fate regulation. HDA19 regulates SGR1 target gene expression by binding to upstream DNA sequences of SGR1 and DNA sequences of numerous SGR1 target genes [48].
Hypocotyls, inflorescence stems, roots, and the endodermis are all aberrant in sgr1 and sgr7 mutant plants. Hypocotyls and inflorescence stems in these mutant plants do not respond to gravitropic stimulation [49]. The loss of endodermis in both mutants' hypocotyls and inflorescence stem results in the absence of sedimented amyloplasts [15]. However, both mutants have sedimented amyloplasts in the root cap's core columella cells [44] and normal gravitropic responses in roots [49]. As a result of the aberrant endodermis, the loss of gravitropism phenotypes in sgr1 and sgr7 in Arabidopsis aerial organs was found.

## SGR proteins are involved in the biogenesis of vacuolar membranes
The vacuolar membrane encloses amyloplasts that settle in the direction of gravity; as the amyloplasts settle, gravity stimulation is identified, and signals are created in the endodermal cells, where numerous SGR components are implicated in gravity response. SGR2, for example, encodes a phospholipase A1-like protein that changes membrane fluidity and detects signals in the vacuolar membrane. Similarly, SGR3, which encodes AtVAM3, encodes a syntaxin-related protein involved in vacuolar assembly. Furthermore, SGR4, which encodes AtVTI11, recognizes the vesicle and its target membrane and forms a central vacuole that governs the positioning and movement of amyloplasts during membrane trafficking [20]. SGR3 and SGR4 are SNARE proteins that are classified as Qa-SNAREs (Syntaxins), Qb-SNAREs, Qc-SNAREs, R-SNAREs (VAMPs), and SNAP-25. A collection of Qa-, Qb-, and Qc-SNAREs on the target membrane engage with R-SNAREs in the vesicle to create a complex, resulting in membrane fusion [33]. To participate in a number of processes, including vacuolar transport, the Qa-SNARE protein SGR3/VAM3 and the Qb-SNARE protein SGR4/VTI11 form membrane-anchored SNARE complexes with particular Qc-SNARE proteins and interact with specific R-SNARE proteins [50].
SGR6 is only known to be involved in creating and maintaining the vacuole membrane structure, which is crucial for amyloplast deposition in the gravity direction [17]. SGR8 (KATAMARI2 or GRAVITROPISMDEFECTIVE2), on the other hand, is a homolog of RME8 (RECEPTOR-MEDIATED ENDOCYTOSIS-8), which is known to play an essential role in endocytosis in animal cells, suggesting that SGR8 is similarly involved in intracellular trafficking in Arabidopsis [6]. Furthermore, the sgr8 (Kam2) mutant showed a more straightforward vacuolar membrane structure than the wild type because it lacked features like vacuolar strands and bub-like structures [38]. As a result, SGR8 is critical in the trafficking of not only vacuolar contests but also vacuolar membrane components required for vacuolar dynamics.
SGR2, SGR3, SGR4, SGR6, and SGR8 all have an effect on vacuole formation and vesicular trafficking into endoderm cells, which are involved in gravity sensing. The phenotype found in the mutants indicates that they play an aberrant role in the shoot gravity response. Furthermore, unlike wildtype plants, the amyloplasts do not settle in the direction of gravity and lack regular inflorescence stems [17], [20], [32], [37], [51].

## Role of SGR5 in gravity perception
SGR5 may be implicated in gravity perception and/or the signaling cascade following amyloplast sedimentation as a potential transcription factor in gravity-perceptive cells. SGR5 is a new gene that participates in post-amyloplast sedimentation but only appears in gravitational crustal cells [13]. SGR5 deficiency affects the gravity response of Arabidopsis inflorescence stems. The sgr5 mutants generate an endoderm layer on inflorescence stems and practically all amyloplast sediment. According to molecular and cellular studies of this mutant, The sgr5 mutation reduces gravity responsiveness in the inflorescence stem while maintaining root and hypocotyl gravity. Therefore, The shot-out development angle is influenced [13], [52]. Along with these findings, SGR5 may be involved in the early stages of gravitropism, such as gravity perception or signaling events during amyloplast sedimentation. In addition, the alternative splicing of SGR5 generates two protein variants (SGR5a and SGR5b), with SGR5b forming non-DNA-binding heterodimers to inhibit the function of SGR5a. Transgenic plants overexpressing SGR5b (35S:SGR5b) exhibit a reduction in gravity response in inflorescence stems, similar to the SGR5-deficient sgr5-5 mutant. Notably, SGR5 alternative splicing is accelerated at high temperatures, leading to the accumulation of SGR5b transcripts. When exposed to high temperatures, while gravity curvature decreases in Col-0 inflorescence stems, it remains unchanged in the inflorescence stems of 35S:SGR5b transgenic plants and sgr5-5 mutant. Therefore, the thermoresponsive alternative splicing of SGR5 is proposed as an adaptive strategy by which plants protect shoots from heat stress in natural habitats [52]. Overall, this provides evidence that alternative splicing of SGR5 contributes to the modulation of gravity response in inflorescence stems under high-temperature conditions.

## SGR9 modulates the interaction between amyloplasts and actin filaments
The Arabidopsis sgr9 mutant plants, like other sgr mutants, display reduced shoot gravitropism and amyloplast sedimentation [19], [53]. However, SGR9 appears to be engaged in amyloplast sedimentation independent of vacuoles, as the vacuolar membrane of SGR9 Arabidopsis did not change from wildtype plants [19]. Amyloplast sedimentation was reduced in sgr9 mutant endodermal cells because the amyloplast motions were less dynamic before and after gravity stimulation by reorientation; also, the starch granules were not placed near the bottom of the cells [19]. Furthermore, sgr9 mutant endodermal cells had aberrant interactions with actin filaments (F-actins) and amyloplasts. The cytoskeleton's F-actin is an essential component of the gravity signal transduction cascade and a factor in gravity perception [53], [54]. F-actins stimulate saltatory movement in endodermal statocytes, influencing amyloplast mobility [55]. Latrunculin B (Lat-B) treatment stopped the amyloplast saltatory motions. Lat-B can disrupt F-actin in sgr9 endodermal cells [19], [55]; this disruption resulted in decreased interaction between AFs and amyloplasts in sgr9 mutants [19]. These findings suggest that SGR9 interacts with AF in plant gravitropism, can influence the interaction between amyloplasts and AFs [19], and regulates amyloplast sedimentation in the direction of gravity.

## SGR family's function in other plants
SGR proteins are found in various plant species and serve non-overlapping roles in gravitropism (Table 3). Arabidopsis SGR1 (SCR) promotes the development of asymmetric radial root tissues in rice, maize, and tomato via cortex and endodermis development [56], [57]. Furthermore, SGR1 is thought to be involved in producing rice stomata and ligules by creating cytoplasmic polarization [58]. SGR1 was discovered to impact photosynthetic growth in maize [59]. In maize and tomato, sgr7/shr mutant seedlings exhibit different symptoms, such as the defective cortex and endodermis development in the roots and asymmetric radial disruptions [60], [61]. As a result, SGR7/SHR, like Arabidopsis, may be implicated in root endodermis development in maize and tomato [62]. In rice, two orthologs of Arabidopsis, AtSHR, OsSHR1, and OsSCR, assist in root endodermis development through cooperative networking [63]. Recent studies on rice have revealed that the SGR7/SHR protein interacts with OsIDD12 and OsIDD13 proteins, playing a vital role in the formation of minor veins and the differentiation of ground cells. Additionally, it has been discovered that these IDD proteins directly interact with putative regulatory elements within the promoter of the auxin transporter gene PIN5c [56]. This finding is expected to enhance our understanding of the relationship between SGR7 and PIN in the gravity response mechanism in rice.
OsFSE1, an ortholog of AtSGR2, functions in amyloplast formation and has in vivo phospholipase activity in rice. Furthermore, fse1 mutants had lower starch content and higher lipid content in the seed, but the developing fse1 endosperm had lower galactolipid content and, as a result, aberrant amyloplast formation [64].
Another SNARE protein is SGR3, which is also known as VAM3. It has been demonstrated to participate in the vacuole deposition stage during the final transport of Rab7 vacuole cargo, inducing vacuole transport in tobacco [65].
SGR5′s ortholog, Loose Plant Architecture1 (LPA1), has been demonstrated to govern plant architecture in rice. Plant structure can influence it by determining the angle, size of the tiller, and leaf in rice and maize [66]. Furthermore, in the case of rice, LPA1 regulates the sedimentation rate of amyloplasts, allowing them to feel gravity and engage in signal transmission [67].
Although the functions of SGR family proteins in other plants have not been well investigated, they appear to play similar roles to those in Arabidopsis. In the future, advanced research on this topic in other plants will be crucial to improving agricultural output.

## Functional divergence of the SGR family
In addition to gravitropism, the SGR family is engaged in various processes in Arabidopsis thaliana, including plant development, hormone signaling pathways, and stress responses. The first SGR protein discovered, AtSGR1/SCR, is known to govern root development [46], [49], [57], [58]. SGR1 and SGR2 are required for seed development and maturity [59]. SHR, an allelic variation of SGR7, works upstream of SCR to generate a complex that aids in root formation [14], [57]. SCR and SHR also involve hormone pathways and stress responses [60], [63]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the SHR is also implicated in cytokinin homeostasis [61], [62], [63]. VAM3, an allelic form of SGR3, operates as a t-SNARE in Arabidopsis and is involved in Golgi trafficking and myrosin cell development [64], [65], [66]. VAM3 also involves salt stress [67]. SYP22, allelic to SGR3, has been shown to control flowering time [68]. SYP22 is also engaged in the BRs' hormone pathway and defense response [69], [70]. VTI1a, like the VAM3 protein, participates in Golgi trafficking [64], [71]. SYP22 and VTI11 (VTI1a) interact and produce important complexes in plant development [50], [64], [72]. VTI11 has been reported to be engaged in the defense response as a vesicle for virus infection [73]. SGR5 contains a zinc finger domain and has been linked to transcriptional control [74]. SGR5 controls floral growth by regulating auxin production and transport [75], [76]. KAM2 is an SGR8 allele known to be involved in the vacuolar network [38], [77]. Because the activities of SGR6 and SGR9 have been discovered to be limited to gravitropism, more research is needed to understand their other roles in plants.

## Other protein families in gravitropism
In this review, we have concentrated on the involvement of the SGR family in gravity sensing, signaling, and responses (such as auxin transport) [3]. Although SGR is the most crucial gene family, other gene families are involved in gravitropism. The LAZY family, for example, is known to be involved in gravity signaling and contains the IGT domain. The lazy1 mutant lacks gravitropism traits such as lower gravity response and a more prominent branch angle than Col-0 [12], [78]. Similarly, lazy2, lazy3, and lazy4 mutants exhibit aberrant gravitropism while exhibiting normal starch accumulation [79]. TILLER ANGLE CONTROL (TAC1) has a narrower branch angle than Col-0 and Lazy1 due to its conserved IGT domain [11]. Because only the LAZY family contains CCL domains with LxLxL EAR motif repressor domains at the C terminal, the CCL domain of the AtLAZY1/LAZY1-LIKE (LZY) gene and the BRX domain of the RCC1-like domain (RLD) interact to recruit RLD from the cytoplasm to the protoplasmic membrane in a gravitational study of the lateral root. PIN3 trafficking is regulated by RLD proteins [80].
The PIN family, also engaged in gravitropic response and curvature angle determination, is primarily involved in auxin transport. It had two conserved hydrophobic domains with five transmembrane helices each [8]. Auxin transporters are reallocated in the direction of gravity upon gravity signaling [81]. PIN3 and PIN7 relocate to the plasma membrane's base, and PIN2 is found on the proximal side of the plasma membrane in the lateral root cap and epidermis, where it participates in the basal transport of auxin from the root cap's bottom side to the renal region [82]. Plant cells include two types of PINs: long PINs and short PINs. ER-localized PIN proteins maintain auxin homeostasis within cells by regulating auxin flow between the cytosol and the endoplasmic reticulum lumen. Long PIN proteins localize to the PM and are asymmetrically polarized to certain sections of cells, determining the direction of auxin transport between cells and regulating auxin distribution within tissues [8].

## Technical challenges in analysis of shoot gravitropism responses in plants
Gravity perception plays a key role in plant development and acclimation to environment by adjusting plant’s architecture and anchorage. Although diverse research has been conducted on the gravity for a long time, there are still some limitations. Therefore, to deepen our understanding of shoot gravitropism, specifically on the morphological functions of SGR family active research in field crops must be pursued.
Unlike other gene families, there is a lack of correlation studies between SGR family members. In particular, most SGR proteins do not have a shared domain. Domains give us the information, including general function, evolutionary mechanisms, and specific binding sites, but SGR family lacks a common domain that could be targeted. Usually, proteins of the same family and homologous proteins have similar functions, as they share conserved domains [83]. Based on this, we can predict the unreported functions or classify newly identified proteins into specific groups. However, study of SGR family based on the domain has been challenging for identifying its functions, biological relevance, and the relationship between them in gravitropism.
Since each SGR family functions in a unique organelle of the plant, further research on the relationship will open up the opportunity to predict how gravity response is regulated in each organelle and will help to establish interconnectedness. Additionally, expanding research at the transcriptional level, for example, exploring the downstream, upstream, and interactive partners of SGR1, SGR5, and SGR7, which are transcription factors involved in the gravity perception process. This will reveal the multifaceted functions of SGR family members beyond gravity response. Further, discovering the role of SGR proteins under diverse stress conditions that can induce gravity response is vital to determine how these proteins mediate between gravity response and stress.
Applications of large-scale experimental methods like single-cell transcriptomics [84] and proteomics with SGR mutants will demonstrate the networks of genes involved in gravity sensing. As these approaches lead to a generation of big data, complex mathematical calculations, and statistical models are necessary to resolve the relationship among various SGR members. Recently, a study where a mathematical model was explored to understand shoot bending and shoot curvature change in Arabidopsis [85]. To expedite the experiment and corroborate the impact of experimental findings on mutants, detailed studies emphasizing quantitative and statistical approaches are needed to analyze SGR family members.

## Conclusion and perspectives
As the world's population expands, so do food scarcity and energy demand concerns. Crop density could be used to benefit humans by increasing crop yields per unit area. Crops with a lower gravity response, on the other hand, tend to branch out laterally, allowing for fewer crops to be sown per unit area. Such crops are more susceptible to infections and pests, which reduces output. As a result, understanding gravitropic principles and reactions can aid in designing the most productive plant architecture. Furthermore, gravitropic studies will help plants survive by ensuring that their roots are appropriately anchored in the soil and that their stems span out to photosynthesize and respire efficiently.
The SGR family is crucial in plant gravity-sensing activities. Each member has its sequence, chemical characteristics, and functional involvement in shoot gravitropism. SGR1 and SGR7, for example, regulate endodermis differentiation, which is required for normal amyloplast localization. The plant reveals functional problems due to aberrant endodermis in sgr1 and sgr7 mutants, which lowers the gravitropic response [49]. SGR2, SGR3, SGR4, SGR6, and SGR8 proteins also function in the vacuole and at endodermal cell vacuolar membranes [17], [20], [38]. The knockout mutants have anomalous amyloplast localization; for example, the sgr9 mutant has reduced amyloplast sedimentation but is independent of the vacuoles [19]. Amyloplasts are dynamic in SGR9 mutant plants and help to minimize sedimentation [19]. Further, the mutants of sgr4-1, sgr5-1, and sgr6-1 show 59.6 %, 83.8 %, and 89.2 % reduction of growth rate in inflorescence stem compared to wildtype plants [86]. Additionally, sgr1, sgr2, and sgr4 mutants exhibit unique morphological effect such as small leaves, thin and short stems; while sgr2 and sgr4 show twisting and zigzag stem, respectively [49], [87]. There is a need to perform experiments on phenotypic impact of these mutants in agronomic crops.
As previously stated, the SGR family members encode TFs involved in plant shoot gravitropism among the multiple transcription factor families. The SGR family may have direct or indirect interactions with gravity-signaling-related genes. The regulators of SGR1 and SGR7 have been examined among SGR family members, but the upstream, downstream, and interactors of additional genes have yet to be found. SGR5 is a transcription factor involved in post-amyloplast sedimentation and is active in gravity-perceptive cells [5], [13]. SGR5 modulates the gravity response via modulating the transcription of gravity response genes. Because SGR5 contains a DNA binding site, it is likely to interact with the LAZY family or TAC1, both of which have been implicated in gravity signaling and additional interactors [11]. Furthermore, in earlier research, SGR5 has been shown to influence auxin biosynthesis; it may interact with auxin biosynthesis-related genes such as YUCCA proteins and tryptophan aminotransferase (TAA). SGR5 is an IDD family member, and the direct targets of IDD14, SGR5/IDD15, and IDD16 were discovered to be YUCCA5, PIN, and TAA1 [22]. Furthermore, SGR4 modulates vacuole formation, whereas SGR2 may be involved in vacuole membrane fluidity, signal production, and gravity signaling mechanisms [18]. The SGR8 protein contains a Dna-J domain and interacts with HSC70 [37]. HSC70 has been previously studied for its interaction with auxilin, a putative tyrosine-protein phosphatase [40], [88]. In the presence of ATP, auxilin can influence clathrin-mediated trafficking, subsequently affecting PIN protein trafficking [37], [89], [90]. Therefore, it is conceivable that SGR8 may relate with PIN proteins.
In this way, each SGR family functions by localizing to a unique organelle. Studying the gravitational response mechanism in each organ will serve as a jumping-off point for new gravitational response research. Additionally, it has been revealed that vacuole and actin bundle play an essential role in amyloplast sedimentation, and this should be studied in more detail. Transcriptome and proteome analysis is a powerful tool for the identification of gravity regulation networks of the SGR family.
An alternate strategy is to analyze protein families that bind to each SGR family member and study their function via common binding or interaction using proteomics analysis. Consequently, investigating how the function of SGR proteins is affected when these signal transduction proteins are either inhibited or activated can provide valuable insights into elucidating the pathway bridging signal recognition and signal transmission in the context of gravity response. For example, LAZY1-CCL domain and RLD-BRX domain interaction studies have recently uncovered pathways of gravity signaling and PIN3-dependent auxin transport [80]. The black box between gravity sensing and signaling processes will be disclosed if we investigate the control mechanism of gravity sensing by the SGR family.
Similarly, studies involving large-scale biological experiments with big data need focused applications to develop a mathematical model to assess cellular-level differential growth during the bending in shoots quantitatively. At the initial bending stage, the relative elongation growth rates of the outer and inner tissues vary significantly. Therefore, a model reflective of wildtype and SGR mutants' morphologies impacts shoot gravitropism, emphasizing differences in maximum curvatures while their extension ratios remain similar. Such a model, fitted to bending events, reveals key parameters that will allow the exploration of mechanical information associated with stretching, bending, and gravity. These mechanics underlying shoot gravitropic response are highly dynamic and cumulative effects of gravitropic bending vs compensating force. More studies focused on shoot growth-induced bending vs deformation and the interplay of these actions will be helpful for a dynamic understanding of shoot morphology in economically essential field crops.
Currently, food shortages are emerging around the world due to changes in climate and adverse effects on field crops. If the study of gravity response in various plant organs of the SGR family is applied to commercial crops such as tomatoes, potatoes, wheat, maize, and rice, an increase in crop yield per unit area can be expected. The elucidation of the plant's gravitropic pathway will help to increase crop productivity by improving plant architecture and shoot canopy.

## Figure Fig. 1: Process of gravity response in cells of higher plants. When plants sense gravity, first, (1) the amyloplasts are sedimented to the lower side of plant cells. Next, (2) signal transduction begins with secondary messengers such as Ca2+ and InsP3. As a result, (3) auxin transporter relocalized to the lower side of a plant cell causes differentiation auxin flow, eventually leading to plant organ curvature development.
