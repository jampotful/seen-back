# Ultrasound-driven mechanophore activation in living plants

## Abstract
SignificancePolymer mechanochemistry offers a powerful strategy for remote molecular control, yet its application in plants remains unexplored. Existing activation methods often interact with plants’ intrinsic sensing and signaling pathways, underscoring the need for minimally disruptive control strategies. Here, we use tomato leaves as a model to show that mechanoresponsive nanoparticles can be noninvasively activated under biocompatible conditions. By combining focused ultrasound (FUS) with gas vesicles (GVs) as cavitation amplifiers, we achieve localized mechanophore activation through selective bond cleavage while preserving tissue integrity and photosynthetic performance. This work establishes ultrasound-triggered mechanochemistry as a minimally invasive platform for molecular activation in planta. It further lays the groundwork for precise, on-demand release of signaling molecules that may help guide plant homeostasis and improve crop resilience.Keywords: polymer mechanochemistry, plant biotechnology, nanoparticles, focused ultrasound, gas vesicles
Achieving precise control of molecular events within living organisms is a cornerstone in chemical biology, nanomedicine, and bioengineering (1, 2). The ability to trigger specific chemical transformations—such as bioconjugation (3), bond cleavage (4), or molecular release (5)—in situ and without genetic modification or invasive manipulation would unlock powerful new tools for modulating biological function. However, this goal is significantly impeded by the complexity of multicellular organisms. Issues like limited tissue accessibility, structural heterogeneity, and physiological fragility pose major barriers to targeted molecular intervention.
Existing stimulus-responsive strategies—including light (6)-, heat-(7), pH-(8), and redox-based systems (9)—each have major limitations. Light-based methods suffer from poor tissue penetration (10), while others often lack the precise spatiotemporal control or tissue compatibility needed to effectively probe regulatory mechanisms (11). In contrast, ultrasound is a highly promising modality. Widely used for medical imaging and therapy (12, 13), its proven biocompatibility, deep tissue penetration, and ability to deliver tunable mechanical stimulation make it ideal for molecular control in live systems.
Ultrasound’s mechanical effects (14, 15) are particularly useful for activating mechanophores—molecular motifs that undergo covalent transformations in response to mechanical force and deformation (16, 17). This force-induced rupture can be harnessed for applications like fluorescence imaging (18), free radical generation (19–21), or drug release (22–24). Recent work has begun to translate these concepts into in vivo contexts, including ultrasound-triggered release of nucleic-acid cargo from polynucleotide assemblies (25), and azo mechanophore activation in nanoparticles (19). However, a major hurdle remains: most covalent mechanophores require forces (26) that exceed what is safely achievable with tissue-compatible ultrasound (15, 27). While more labile mechanophores, like azo-based motifs (19, 20), have lower activation thresholds (28, 29), they often lack thermal stability (30). This fundamental mismatch between the required mechanical force and the organism’s tolerance (15, 27) has severely limited the application of mechanophore chemistry in vivo.
To bridge this gap, recent studies have explored acoustic cavitation enhancers like microbubbles (31–33) and gas vesicles (GVs) (23). These gas-filled microstructures dramatically amplify the localized mechanical effects of ultrasound (34). By creating localized shear and pressure spikes through stress accumulation (31, 32) or implosion (23, 34), they can trigger mechanophore activation under much milder global u ltrasound conditions (23, 31). While this cavitation-enhanced approach has been successfully demonstrated with mammalian cells (23), its broader applicability and robustness in more complex, biologically distinct systems like plants remain unexplored.
Plants present a stringent and demanding model system for validating this technology. Their dense extracellular matrices and complex vascular structures make delivery challenging (35). Moreover, light plays a central role in regulating plant physiology (36), making it difficult to employ light as an orthogonal stimulus without perturbing endogenous biological processes (37). Traditional methods like genetic transformation are often inefficient and species-specific (38, 39). These challenges make plants an ideal testbed for a robust remote activation strategy. Success in this context would not only confirm the versatility of ultrasound-based mechanochemistry but also open the gates for methods to study endogenous and exogenous signals/regulators of plant systems.
In this study, we introduce a noninvasive platform that combines GV–amplified focused ultrasound (GV-FUS) with fluorogenic mechanophore nanoparticles (FMNPs) to achieve spatially confined molecular activation in the leaves of tomato plants. FMNPs were constructed by incorporating Anthracene–Maleimide mechanophores (40) onto silica nanoparticles, enabling force-triggered blue fluorescence at 440 nm that minimizes interference from the chlorophyll, the dominating source of autofluorescence in leaves (41). We demonstrate that while low-frequency ultrasound (25 kHz) can activate the mechanophores, it causes significant tissue damage. Conversely, a higher frequency (550 kHz) focused ultrasound (FUS) is well tolerated but insufficient to mechanophore activation on its own. Critically, we show that by involving GVs as cavitation amplifier, the robust, localized activation within the plant’s vasculature without any observable damage can be achieved, proving the feasibility of ultrasound-driven, biocompatible, and spatially precise mechanochemical manipulation in intact living systems (Fig. 1).

## Results

## Synthesis, Characterization, and In Vitro Activation of Fluorogenic Mechanophore-Embedded Nanoparticles (FMNPs).
The preparation process of FMNPs is illustrated in Fig. 2A, while a detailed description of mechanophore small molecule synthesis can be found in SI Appendix. Briefly, amine-functionalized silica nanoparticles were first prepared and subsequently connected with the mechanophore molecules via an amidation reaction. This was followed by an esterification step to introduce an initiator group for surface-initiated radical polymerization. Polymerization of a PEG-functionalized methyl acrylate monomer yielded the final FMNPs used throughout this study. The polymer molecular weight was controlled by adjusting the polymerization duration. Due to the presence of PEG oligomers, the resulting FMNPs were enveloped in extended, brush-like hydrophilic chains that improved their colloidal stability in aqueous media (19). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, SI Appendix, Fig. S1) revealed a significant weight loss in polymer-conjugated samples, indicating successful polymer attachment. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) profile following butylammonium fluoride (TBAF) treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) confirmed dissociation of the polymer from the silica core. Together, these results confirm successful polymer grafting onto the nanoparticle surface.

Under interfacial ultrasound conditions, mechanical stress is concentrated at the junction between the rigid SiO2 core, and the well-solvated and extended poly-acrylate chains with PEG sidechains, due to their substantial mass difference (19, 42). This asymmetric force distribution directs tension toward the embedded mechanophore, which undergoes a retro-Diels–Alder (rDA) reaction upon activation (Fig. 2B). This polymer mechanochemistry has been well characterized and thoroughly studied in previous work (18, 43–45). After the activation of mechanophore, the resulting leftover structure on nanoparticle features an extended conjugated system with an anthracene moiety that emits blue fluorescence centered at 440 nm (Fig. 2C).
To examine the successful incorporation and evaluate activation efficiency, 10 mg mL−1 FMNPs were suspended in water and subjected to probe sonication at pulsed mode, 25 kHz and 120 W with various treatment times. A distinct fluorescence signal centered at 440 nm emerged posttreatment, confirming successful incorporation and mechanochemical activation of the fluorogenic mechanophores. The fluorescence intensity increased progressively over the first 40 min and reached a plateau by 60 min, indicating completion of the activation process under these conditions (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Based on experimental results, FMNPs treated for 40 min were used as the positive control for calculating relative activation efficiency in all subsequent experiments.
Interestingly, in addition to increasing intensity at the emission peak (440 nm), we observed a concurrent decrease in signal intensity at wavelengths outside the main emission range (≤420 nm and ≥500 nm) as sonication time increased (Fig. 2C). This spectral narrowing suggests a reduction in broadband fluorescence readout, potentially attributable to ultrasound-induced changes in particle size and associated scattering of excitation beam (46).
To explore this possibility, we performed dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements before and after sonication. The FMNPs exhibited a broad hydrodynamic diameter centered around 1.62 ± 0.42 μm in aqueous suspension, consistent with partial aggregation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). After 5 min of low-power bath sonication, the size distribution shifted into two populations (564 ± 233 nm and 3.76 ± 0.95 μm), indicating a combination of partial disaggregation and secondary aggregation. Notably, after 60 min of probe sonication (the activation condition), complete disaggregation was observed in fluorescence microscopy assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The absence of measurable scattering before US treatment is consistent with a colloidally stable PEGylated dispersion. Upon sonication, however, transient destabilization may occur, for example via depletion interactions (47), leading initially to agglomeration and subsequently to a reduction in agglomerate size under prolonged sonication. This reduction in particle size correlates with decreased light scattering and may explain the narrowing of the fluorescence spectrum. Zeta potential measurements showed no significant difference before and after activation (−4 mV, SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), suggesting that ultrasound did not affect the surface charge of the particles.
The structural morphology of FMNPs was further confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which revealed a clear core–shell architecture consisting of uniform silica cores (98 ± 11 nm) and polymer shells 17 ± 5 nm thick (Fig. 2D).
The fluorescence from FMNP after ultrasonic activation can also be observed under both fluorescence microscope (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) and macroscale imaging. To exhibit imaging performance, activated FMNPs were loaded into an “I”-shaped polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold and imaged under fluorescence microscopy using the DAPI channel. A well-defined fluorescence pattern was observed (Fig. 2E), demonstrating the visibility of the activated FMNPs. These findings suggest that FMNP-derived fluorescence contrast can be potentially harnessed to resolve fine-scale anatomical structures in living plants.

## Biocompatibility of Ultrasound Exposure in Plant Tissue.
Having established that FMNPs can be efficiently activated by ultrasound with strong fluorescence contrast, we next assessed their biocompatibility in living tissues. Tomato was chosen as the model species because its plant and leaf size are well suited for controlled ultrasound exposure, and it serves as a widely used model in plant biology (48, 49). Our objective was to identify ultrasound parameters that enable mechanophore activation while minimizing tissue damage, thus allowing noninvasive, spatially resolved molecular control in planta.
To compare acoustic biocompatibility, we tested two ultrasound modalities: low-frequency probe sonication (25 kHz) and high-frequency (FUS, 550 kHz), as shown in Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6. In both cases, pulsed ultrasound was employed to minimize undesired thermal effects, consistent with common practice in ultrasound applications (32, 50, 51). For 25 kHz exposure, the transducer probe was submerged in a water tank along with the tomato leaf, positioned ~1 cm from the transducer tip and stabilized using a glass bottle. During exposure visible debris were released from the leaf surface. After the treatment, the leaves displayed increased translucency and, in some cases, complete perforation in the exposed area (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Despite this acute local damage, no systemic symptoms such as wilting or yellowing were observed, suggesting preserved plant viability within the observation window. In contrast, 550 kHz FUS was applied using a vertically oriented transducer placed beneath a coupling bag that supported the leaf in water, aligned to the acoustic focus ~1 cm above the transducer. Similar ultrasound condition with FUS has been used in previous related work (19) and proved to be biocompatible for cells and tumors. Based on experimental results, the treatment of pulsed FUS (10 s on/10 s off, 6 cycles, 4.7 W cm−2) caused no visible changes to the leaf surface or color (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Treated plants were returned to growth chambers and monitored for 24 h to further examine the biocompatibility of this ultrasonication process.

To assess internal tissue integrity, treated leaves were examined by fluorescence microscopy. Brightfield imaging revealed that 25 kHz-treated samples exhibited widespread mesophyll disruption, visible as porous or eroded structures (Fig. 3C). In contrast, leaves treated with 550 kHz FUS (Fig. 3B) were indistinguishable from untreated controls. Chlorophyll autofluorescence (emission ~680 nm) (52) was used as a physiological indicator of tissue health. Far-red channel imaging (Ex 650 nm/Em 673 nm) showed substantial fluorescence loss in 25 kHz-exposed areas, corresponding to regions of physical damage. No significant change was detected in the 550 kHz and control groups. Conversely, imaging under the green channel (Ex 488 nm/Em 509 nm) revealed increased signal intensity in 25 kHz-treated areas, likely due to greater light transmittance through thinned or perforated tissues. Ring-like artifacts in the far-red images were attributed to localized photobleaching (53). All imaging parameters were held constant for comparability.
To quantify tissue damage, we analyzed pixel intensity distributions from far-red chlorophyll autofluorescence (52) images across treatment groups (n = 5) (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). All groups exhibited a primary fluorescence peak centered near 2,000, corresponding to healthy mesophyll tissue. This peak was well fitted by a Gaussian distribution (R2 > 0.95), and statistical comparison of its mean and variance using F-tests and unpaired two-tailed t tests revealed no significant differences between the control, 550 kHz, and 25 kHz groups, indicating that intact regions remained unaffected. However, only the 25 kHz group showed an additional low-intensity peak centered around 400, consistent with pigment loss in damaged areas. This secondary population was statistically distinct from the main healthy peak, confirming the presence of ultrasound-induced damage.
To further quantify the extent of injury, we defined “low fluorescence” regions as those with pixel intensities below a threshold set at the Gaussian center of the control group minus one full width at half maximum (FWHM), yielding a cutoff value of 1,193. Based on this threshold, the 25 kHz-treated leaves exhibited 37 ± 18% low-fluorescence area, significantly higher than both the 550 kHz group and untreated controls, which showed no statistical difference from each other (Fig. 3E).
To further investigate physiological compatibility, we focused on the 550 kHz FUS system, as the 25 kHz low-frequency ultrasound was found to cause significant tissue damage and was thus deemed unsuitable for in planta activation. While 550 kHz FUS preserves tissue integrity, its mechanical effect is weaker than that of low-frequency ultrasound (14) and was insufficient to activate FMNPs as we shall show below. To overcome this limitation, we leveraged GVs as mechanical amplifiers to enhance local stress during FUS exposure.
To confirm that the combined GV+FUS treatment does not adversely affect plant physiology, we assessed photosynthetic performance following FUS and GV treatments. Mechanical wounding is known to induce rapid suppression of photosynthesis, usually within hours and almost invariably within 24 to 48 h after injury (54–56). Therefore, photosynthetic performance was measured on Day 1 and Day 4 following treatment to evaluate both the immediate and prolonged effects. The maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (Fv/Fm, where Fm is the maximum possible yield of fluorescence and Fv is the variable fluorescence), a well-established indicator of plant health (57), remained ~0.8 across all groups, consistent with healthy, unstressed leaves (Fig. 3F). No significant changes were observed in Fv/Fm values over 4 d following either FUS alone or FUS+GV treatment. Also, maximum efficiency of PSII photochemistry in the light (Fv′/Fm′) showed no difference between treated and control plants (Fig. 3G), confirming that neither FUS nor FUS+GV introduction disrupted photosynthetic efficiency. These results also show that although water immersion is not a native condition, brief submersion (≤5 min) during sonication process did not affect leaf performance.
In summary, while 25 kHz ultrasound compromises tissue structure and pigment retention, 550 kHz FUS, with or without GV administration, maintains physiological normalcy and is well suited for downstream mechanophore activation studies in live plants. Based on this conclusion, the high frequency biocompatible ultrasonic condition was used for further mechanical activation.

## GV-Enhanced Mechanophore Activation.
To evaluate whether ultrasound-triggered mechanophore activation could be achieved under biocompatible conditions, we first tested FMNP activation in a 96-well plate using 550 kHz FUS. However, the activation efficiency was limited (Fig. 4), suggesting that high-frequency FUS, though biocompatible, might be too weak to efficiently activate the FMNP and additional cavitation enhancement was necessary. Inspired by previous work (23), we introduced GVs as acoustic cavitation nuclei to amplify the mechanical effects of ultrasound. It is worth noting that some azo-type mechanophore-incorporated nanostructures were reported to be activatable by solely FUS condition (19). Those extremely labile properties under ultrasound can be attributed to the vulnerability of aliphatic azo motifs (58), which will also lead to the poor thermostability of those nanostructures and limit their further applications. In this study, we select a more robust mechanophore (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) to ensure thermostability (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) and functional performance. Consequently, a cavitation amplifier (GV) was required to achieve activation of the mechanophore-incorporated nanostructure (59).

GVs were produced using genetically engineered E. coli following a modified version of a previously reported procedure (60, 61). After cell lysis and purification, we performed a series of characterizations and calibrations. TEM revealed characteristic spindle-shaped vesicles with an average length of 276 ± 43 nm (Fig. 4A). DLS analysis showed a hydrodynamic diameter of 136 ± 30 nm upon preparation, which modestly increased to 151 ± 39 nm after 21 d of storage, indicating moderate colloidal stability (Fig. 4B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) further confirmed a mean diameter of 111.9 ± 37.0 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). Quantification assays further validated consistency: BCA protein measurements scaled linearly with optical density at 500 nm (OD500), and NTA established a direct correlation between OD500 and GV particle count (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B). Critically, when exposing to 550 kHz FUS (4.7 W cm−2, 1 min), GVs rapidly collapsed (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), confirming their ultrasonic response.
We then evaluated the combined effects of GVs and FUS on FMNP activation. FMNP suspensions in 96-well plates were treated with 550 kHz FUS, and changes in fluorescence were monitored. As shown in Fig. 4C, purified GVs alone had no effect on FMNP fluorescence, as they exhibited no fluorescence in the tested spectral range and remained spectrally unchanged before and after sonication. FMNPs treated with FUS alone showed a slight decrease in emission at 400 to 420 nm and a very mild increase beyond 440 nm. This pattern is consistent with the behavior observed during low-frequency activation (Fig. 2C) and is likely due to reduced particle aggregation. In contrast, a combination of GVs and FUS led to a pronounced fluorescence enhancement, indicating synergistic activation.
To quantify activation efficiency, all fluorescence spectra were normalized to the 420 nm signal, which served as a nonresponsive internal reference to correct for baseline scattering. FMNPs treated with 25 kHz ultrasound at 120 W for 60 min were defined as maximum activation can be achieved based on ultrasound activation (theoretically 100%), and freshly prepared, untreated FMNPs were taken as 0%. The activation percentage was determined by linear interpolation of the normalized intensity at 440 nm. In the absence of GVs, 550 kHz ultrasound alone yielded only 1.0 ± 0.6% activation. With the addition of GVs, activation efficiency increased significantly to 14.9 ± 1.3%, demonstrating that GV-mediated cavitation can greatly enhance mechanophore activation under mild, biocompatible ultrasound conditions (Fig. 4D).

## In Vivo Activation of FMNP in Plant Tissues.
Having demonstrated that GVs enhance FMNP activation under biocompatible 550 kHz FUS in vitro, we next investigated whether this strategy could be extended to induce mechanochemical activation within live plant tissue.
To verify that activated FMNPs can be effectively delivered into leaf vasculature and retain fluorescence in planta, we injected preactivated FMNPs directly into the major veins of tomato leaves. A 10 μL aliquot of activated FMNPs (10 mg mL−1) was introduced using a fine needle to minimize mechanical disruption. Representative wide-field fluorescence images (Fig. 5A) show that intravascular delivery produced a bright, spatially confined signal that colocalized with the major vein architecture. In addition to the primary veins, enhanced fluorescence was also observed in surrounding regions, indicating that the particles not only accumulated within large veins but also spread through the vascular network into finer structures. By contrast, saline controls displayed only punctate autofluorescence associated with trichomes and other microstructures on the leaf surface.

Fluorescence microscopy was further performed before and after injection to assess changes in signal intensity (Fig. 5B). Images were acquired using the blue fluorescence channel (Ex 353 nm/Em 465 nm) to detect emission from FMNPs, and the red channel (Ex 592 nm/Em 614 nm) to visualize plant pigments and autofluorescence. Prior to injection, due to the natural distribution of autofluorescence, major veins appeared slightly dimmer in the blue channel compared to surrounding mesophyll tissue. Following injection, a notable increase in blue fluorescence was observed, particularly along the injected vein. Quantitative column-wise averaging of pixel intensity revealed a 1.6-fold global increase and a 2.8-fold localized enhancement within the vein region (Fig. 5C), confirming successful vascular delivery and fluorescence signal retention of FMNPs in planta.
To assess whether mechanophore activation could be induced in planta under biocompatible conditions, we coinjected FMNPs (10 mg mL−1) and GVs (3 × 109 particles/mL) into the tomato leaf vein, followed by 550 kHz FUS exposure (4.7 W cm−2, 1 min). To rule out potential confounding effects and dissect the role of each component, we designed four experimental groups using all pairwise combinations of FMNPs (NP), GVs, and ultrasound: NP only, NP+GV, NP+FUS, and NP+GV+FUS.
We observed relatively wide variability in fluorescence signals across groups, which we attribute to biological heterogeneity inherent to leaf tissue—such as differences in thickness, maturity, and positional identity along the stem. To mitigate this variability, we selected leaves of similar size (length 4 to 7 cm) and used sample size n = 30 per group. Among all conditions, only the NP+GV+FUS group showed a statistically significant increase in blue-channel fluorescence (61 ± 7 a.u.) compared to baseline, indicating successful mechanophore activation within live plant tissue under mild ultrasound exposure (Fig. 5D).

## Discussion
In this study, we explored the feasibility of using FUS-GV as a noninvasive strategy to activate FMNPs in living plant tissue. By systematically evaluating ultrasound biocompatibility and delivery efficiency, we identified a successful combination—550 kHz FUS assisted by GVs—that enabled in planta mechanochemical activation of Anthracene–Maleimide mechanophores in tomato leaves.
This study provides a demonstration of mechanophore activation in intact plant systems using an externally applied physical stimulus. Further optimization of ultrasound parameters—including frequency, intensity, and duty cycle—as well as GV dosage, may further enhance activation efficiency and spatial precision. Notably, the current proof-of-concept employed a fluorogenic mechanophore to provide an optical readout of activation events. Future work may build upon this platform to incorporate mechanophores that liberate biologically active signaling molecules, thereby enabling remote, noninvasive modulation of plant homeostasis. For instance, abscisic acid (ABA)-releasing mechanophores could enable spatially localized hormone activation to modulate stomatal conductance and drought physiology, with functional outcomes quantified through leaf-level gas-exchange measurements and ABA-responsive gene expression analysis.
Importantly, the current delivery approach—vascular introduction via the petiole—naturally defines the application space of this technology. Rather than broadacre agricultural deployment, GV-assisted mechanochemical activation is best suited to precision plant-biotechnology contexts, such as controlled laboratory studies, molecular pharming, or targeted delivery of micronutrients and biostimulants. Within these settings, localized activation offers a distinct advantage by minimizing systemic exposure while enabling site-specific molecular control.
Achieving mechanophore activation in fully intact, photosynthetically active leaves introduces plant-specific constraints—nonphysiological coupling media, vascular transport, stomatal function—that are absent in typical mammalian or benchtop surrogates. Our results indicate that brief water coupling (≤5 min) is tolerated and that vein-confined activation can be obtained without impairing PSII, suggesting a path to other broad-leaf species with similar biomechanics, while thinner leaves may require alternative approaches. While this study focused on leaves, future work could explore roots as an especially promising testbed. Their natural residence in water or porous media would simplify the process by allowing for gel-free acoustic coupling in hydroponic or agar systems.
Although the GV-assisted strategy successfully bridged the gap between mild, biocompatible acoustic input and the relatively high activation threshold of retro-DA mechanophores, the overall activation efficiency (~15%) remains modest. However, modest activation yields do not necessarily limit practical utility. Many plant signaling pathways exhibit high sensitivity to bioactive molecules in the low-micromolar to nanomolar range, such that only a small fraction of the total molecular pool is sufficient to initiate amplified downstream responses (62). Therefore, activation efficiencies on current order already fall within a biologically meaningful regime.
It is also worth discussing the mechanistic underpinnings of activation efficiency differences observed in this work versus previous studies. In earlier work, nanoparticles embedded with azo-type mechanophores (19) (using the same SiO2 core and similar polymeric linkers) could be activated solely by high-frequency FUS, without requiring GVs. This contrast stems from the intrinsic mechanochemical properties of different mechanophore classes. Azo groups are among the most labile mechanophores (29, 63), readily cleaved under modest mechanical force due to the release of nitrogen gas as a byproduct (64). However, their low activation barrier also compromises thermal stability. For example, azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN), a well-known and widely used azo-type radical generator, also included as the mechanoresponsive motif of azo-type mechanophores, begins decomposing at 30 to 70 °C in bulk or solution (58). Such limitations may pose safety and stability challenges in real-world applications.
In contrast, Anthracene–Maleimide mechanophores possess high mechanical thresholds but excellent thermal stability (44), making them appealing candidates for robust and controllable activation—provided that sufficient mechanical input can be delivered. Thermal dynamic analysis results of FMNP solution indicate that the nanoparticle has no observable decomposition at 37 °C for a week or at 60 °C for 2 d, pointing to the excellent thermostability of FMNP in solution (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Our data suggest that GVs, as biogenic cavitation nuclei, offer a versatile solution to this challenge by amplifying ultrasound-induced mechanical stress. Meanwhile, as evidenced by simulation studies (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), the mechanical robustness of retro-DA scaffolds is much higher than azo-type mechanophore and typically exceeds the threshold of biologically compatible ultrasound, underscoring the need for auxiliary amplification.
Mechanophore chemistry offers substantial flexibility for tuning force responsivity through molecular design. For the retro-Diels–Alder–type mechanophore used in this work, prior studies have demonstrated that mechanical activation efficiency can be modulated through rational structure optimization, such as varying the polymer attachment handle, introducing electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents, or incorporating heteroatoms to tune bond polarization and transition-state energetics (65, 66). However, the key bottleneck for biological translation is not solely the development of increasingly labile mechanophores. Highly labile mechanophores often exhibit limited thermal stability, which may lead to premature activation or degradation under physiological conditions. Therefore, we believe that strategies enabling biocompatible ultrasound to reliably activate thermally stable, mechanically labile mechanophores will ultimately be more impactful than pushing molecular fragility to the extreme. In this context, retro-Diels–Alder–type mechanophores are known to exhibit relatively high rupture force thresholds (67), and their successful activation under biocompatible GV-FUS conditions supports the feasibility of activating mechanophores with lower force thresholds using the same strategy.
In summary, our findings establish a mechanochemical framework for plant applications. We have successfully bridged the gap between mild, biocompatible acoustic input and the high activation threshold of robust mechanophores by using GVs as a critical amplifying element. This proof-of-concept in live plants not only validates our GV-assisted FUS strategy, but also fundamentally expands the design space for biointegrated nanostructures. Our work demonstrates that we are no longer limited to low-threshold motifs; instead, we can now consider a diverse array of thermally stable mechanophores to enable remote-controlled, ultrasound-triggered functionalities in fields ranging from plant biotechnology to synthetic biology.

## Materials and Methods

## Synthesis and Physicochemical Characterization of FMNPs.
Mechanophore-functionalized silica nanoparticles (FMNPs) were synthesized via a multistep protocol; full synthetic procedures and characterization of intermediates are provided in the Supplementary Information.

## TEM.
Dispersions (10 mg mL −1) were negatively stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate and deposited on carbon-coated copper grids. Images were acquired on a Thermo Fisher FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN. Core diameter and polymer shell thickness were quantified from TEM images (n = 20 particles) using ImageJ.

## DLS and Zeta Potential.
Hydrodynamic diameter and ζ-potential were measured at 25 °C using a Litesizer 500 particle sizing system (Anton Paar).

## Fluorescence activation assay.
Fully activated FMNPs (activation conditions described under “Ultrasound exposure”) were embedded in PDMS molds (SYLGARD™ 184; base:catalyst = 10:1) cured at 60 °C for 2 h in custom 3D-printed “I”-shaped channels. Fluorescence imaging was performed on a BioTek Cytation 5 hybrid imaging reader using the DAPI channel (Ex/Em = 353/465 nm). Exposure time and gain were held constant across samples. Signal intensities from activated and nonactivated FMNPs were compared to confirm mechanochemical activation.

## Ultrasound Exposure and Acoustic Coupling.
Ultrasound was applied to induce FMNP activation in excised tomato leaves.

## 550 kHz FUS.
Leaves were positioned 1 cm above the acoustic focus of a custom FUS system and treated at 4.7 W cm−2 using a pulsed protocol (10 s on/10 s off, 6 cycles). Ultrasound intensity is calculated peak refraction pressure that is measured from the hydrophone.
Posttreatment, brightfield and fluorescence images were acquired. Chlorophyll fluorescence was collected in the far-red channel (Ex/Em = 650/673 nm) to assess plant health, distinct from FMNP fluorescence. Tissue damage was quantified in MATLAB/ImageJ using a threshold of 1193, determined from control full width at half-maximum (FWHM; R2 > 0.95). Acoustic calibrations are detailed in the SI Appendix.

## GV production, purification, and quantification.
GV were produced in E. coli BL21(DE3) (Thermo Fisher) transformed with pBAD-bARGSer-AxeTxe (Addgene #192473). Cultures were grown in LB at 37 °C for 2.5 h to OD600 = 0.6 to 0.8, induced with 1% (w/v) arabinose (Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated at 26 °C for 20 h. After lysis, buoyant GVs were enriched by repeated low-speed centrifugation (600×g); the top fraction was collected with a 26-gauge needle, diluted into PBS (pH 7.4), and washed. ## In-Plant Injection and Imaging.
US treated FMNPs (10 mg mL−1) were injected into tomato leaf veins using a 31G needle. Fluorescence images were recorded before and after injections using DAPI (Ex/Em = 353/465 nm) and Texas Red (Ex/Em = 592/614 nm) channels on a BioTek Cytation 5 with fixed exposure/gain. For image quantification, pixel intensities were averaged column-wise across the image height to generate intensity profiles.

## Plant Growth and Photosynthesis Measurement.
Beefsteak tomato (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Beefsteak; Bonnie Plants) were grown under 12-h daily photoperiods using LED grow lights and watered every 2 to 3 d before soil desiccation. Ultrasound and imaging experiments were performed on mature leaves from 8 to 10-wk-old plants.
Photosystem II (PSII) activity was measured using an LI-6800 Portable Photosynthesis System (LI-COR, Nebraska) at CO2 = 420 ppm, 24 °C, 50% relative humidity, and flow = 500 μmol s−1 at 1- and 4-days postinjection/treatment (n = 3 to 4). Measurements were conducted according to Murchie and Lawson (57). Briefly, a saturating pulse was applied to dark-adapted leaves, followed by actinic illumination at 75 μmol m−2 s−1 representative of growth conditions until stabilization, then a second saturating pulse and immediate far-red illumination to drain electrons from PSII. The maximum quantum efficiency (Fv/Fm, dark-adapted measurement) and the maximum efficiency in the light (Fv′/Fm′) were determined.

## Image Analysis and Statistics.
Image analysis was performed in ImageJ and MATLAB as described above. Unless otherwise stated, statistical comparisons used two-tailed unpaired t tests in R (v4.2.0), with P < 0.05 considered significant. Exact n values and replicate definitions are reported in the figure legends.

## Figure Fig. 1.: Schematic representation of mechanophore activation mechanisms in vitro and in planta. (A) Activation of fluorogenic mechanophore-embedded nanoparticles (FMNP): The diagram illustrates the ultrasound-induced mechanochemical activation of FMNPs, where mechanical force triggers a retro-Diels–Alder reaction in the anthracene-maleimide mechanophore (blue structure) embedded within a silica nanoparticle (SiO2 NP) core and polymer shell. This activation shifts the fluorescence spectrum (orange line) to emit blue fluorescence at 440 nm (black line), as shown by the wavelength-dependent fluorescence intensity plot. (B) Activation in plant tissue: The schematic depicts the activation of FMNPs within the vasculature of a tomato leaf using biocompatible 550 kHz focused ultrasound (FUS). Gas vesicles (GV) (blue diamond shapes) act as cavitation nuclei, amplifying localized mechanical stress (depicted by arrows and cavitation burst) to activate FMNPs (dark blue circles) from their inactive state (gray circles). Excitation at 400 nm and emission at 440 nm (FL) enable fluorescence detection, highlighting spatially confined mechanochemical reactions without significant tissue damage.
