Reversible phosphorylation of NPH3/RPT2-like proteins regulates phototropin receptor signaling.

# Reversible phosphorylation of NPH3/RPT2-like proteins regulates phototropin receptor signaling

## Abstract
AbstractPhototropin receptor kinases (phot1 and phot2) enhance photosynthesis by coordinating light responses such as phototropism and chloroplast repositioning under low blue light conditions. These processes rely on NON-PHOTOTROPIC HYPOCOTYL 3 (NPH3)/ROOT PHOTOTROPISM 2-like (NRL) proteins. The Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) NRL protein NPH3 is phosphorylated by phot1 at S744, a modification that alters its localization and promotes hypocotyl phototropism. Although reversible phosphorylation of S744 is necessary for reassembling an active phot1–NPH3 complex at the plasma membrane, the mechanisms driving NPH3 dephosphorylation remain unclear. Here, we show that clade L TYPE 2C PROTEIN PHOSPHATASES (PP2C19 and PP2C35) regulate S744 phosphorylation and NPH3 function. Mutants lacking PP2C19 exhibit sustained S744 phosphorylation and reduced phototropism, with greater defects observed in pp2c19 pp2c35 double mutants, indicating functional redundancy between these phosphatases. Moreover, clade L pp2c mutants display altered RPT2 phosphorylation and abundance and modulate the phosphorylation status of NRL PROTEIN FOR CHLOROPLAST MOVEMENT 1 (NCH1), suggesting broader regulatory control over phot1–NRL targets. Consistent with this, phototropin-dependent chloroplast accumulation is impaired in both Arabidopsis and Marchantia pp2c19 mutants. These findings identify clade L PP2Cs as key regulators of auxin-dependent (phototropism) and auxin-independent (chloroplast accumulation movement) light responses, which, together with their additional role in regulating hypocotyl gravitropism, are particularly important for seedling emergence and establishment from beneath the soil surface.

## Introduction
Phototropins (phot1 and phot2) are plasma membrane-associated serine/threonine kinases that autophosphorylate in response to blue light, regulating key physiological processes in Arabidopsis, including hypocotyl phototropism, chloroplast accumulation movement, leaf positioning, and leaf flattening. These responses collectively optimize light capture for photosynthesis and promote growth (Takemiya et al 2005; Inoue et al 2008; Gotoh et al 2018). Phot1 serves as the primary photoreceptor for phototropism, functioning over a wide range of fluence rates of blue light in etiolated seedlings (Sakai et al 2001).
Light scattering through intercellular air spaces in the hypocotyl (Nawkar et al 2023) creates a gradient of phot1 activation, with maximal activity occurring on the irradiated side (Salomon et al 1997). This gradient drives asymmetric auxin distribution, leading to accumulation on the shaded side and promoting differential growth (Fankhauser and Christie 2015), a mechanism long proposed to underlie phototropic curvature (Christie and Murphy 2013). Two families of auxin transporters are implicated in this process: PIN-FORMED efflux carriers (Willige et al 2013) and ATP-BINDING CASSETTE B (ABCB)-type multidrug resistance transporters (Christie et al 2011). However, the signaling pathways linking phot1 activation to auxin transport remain poorly defined.
PHYTOCHROME KINASE SUBSTRATE (PKS) proteins play prominent roles in auxin signaling and are required for phototropism (Kami et al 2014). PKS4 is phosphorylated by phot1 (Demarsy et al 2012), and members of the PKS protein family have been shown to form a plasma membrane complex with phot1 and NON-PHOTOTROPIC HYPOCOTYL 3 (NPH3) (Lariguet et al 2006; de Carbonnel et al 2010), which is essential for establishing the lateral auxin gradient required for phototropic curvature (Motchoulski and Liscum 1999; Haga et al 2005). NPH3, along with ROOT PHOTOTROPISM 2 (RPT2), belongs to the NPH3/RPT2-Like (NRL) family, comprising 33 members in Arabidopsis (Christie et al 2018). NPH3 contains an N-terminal BTB (bric-a-brac, tramtrack and broad complex) domain, a central NPH3 domain and a C-terminal coiled-coil domain (Christie et al 2018), and is proposed to function as part of a CULLIN 3-based E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that controls the abundance or subcellular localization of protein targets associated with auxin transport (Roberts et al 2011), although information on such targets is currently lacking.
Phot1 phosphorylates NPH3 at S744, within a conserved C-terminal motif (Sullivan et al 2021). This medication promotes 14-3-3 binding and relocalization of NPH3 into intracellular condensates, transiently attenuating its interaction with phot1 (Haga et al 2015; Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021). S744 phosphorylation also triggers dephosphorylation of N-terminal residues (S213, S223, S233, S237) (Kimura et al 2021), which can be detected by increased electrophoretic mobility (Pedmale and Liscum 2007; Haga et al 2015; Sullivan et al 2019). These dynamic phosphorylation events may serve to desensitize phot1 signaling, with reversible phosphorylation required to recycle NPH3 to the plasma membrane and sustain directional light signaling under continuous irradiation.
The phosphatase(s) responsible for dephosphorylating NPH3 at S744, as well as the kinase(s) that re-phosphorylate its N-terminal residues, remain poorly characterized. RPT2, a phototropin kinase substrate (Waksman et al 2023), plays a role in modulating NPH3 phosphorylation dynamics and promoting reassembly of the phot1-NPH3-PKS signaling complex at the plasma membrane (Kimura et al 2020). In addition, RPT2 attenuates phot1 autophosphorylation, thereby limiting phototropic signaling under high light conditions (Kimura et al 2020). As a result, phototropic defects in rpt2 mutants become more pronounced under elevated blue light intensities (Sakai et al 2000), consistent with its expression profile, which increases upon exposure to red or blue light (Tsuchida-Mayama et al 2010). Phot1 also stabilizes RPT2 by preventing its proteasomal degradation in blue light (Kimura et al 2020). RPT2 also functionally overlaps with other phototropin-NRL targets, including NRL PROTEIN FOR CHLOROPLAST MOVEMENT 1 (NCH1), which together regulate chloroplast repositioning toward the upper cell surface under low blue light (Suetsugu et al 2016). Mutations in ROOT CURLING IN N-NAPHTHYLPHTHALAMIC ACID 1 (RCN1), a subunit of PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2A (PP2A), impair chloroplast accumulation movement (Sztatelman et al 2016), although the involvement of dynamic RPT2/NCH1 phosphorylation in this process remains to be elucidated.
Roles for serine/threonine protein phosphatases in phototropism have been limitedly reported. Mutations in RCN1 and PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 6 (PP6) show minimal effects on phototropic responses under continuous blue light (Tseng and Briggs 2010; Haga and Sakai 2018). However, TYPE 2C PROTEIN PHOSPHATASES from the clade D subfamily (Ren et al 2018) have been implicated in auxin-induced cell expansion. Etiolated seedlings lacking PP2C.D2, PP2C.D5, and PP2C.D6 exhibit elongated hypocotyls and defects in phototropism and apical hook development (Ren et al 2018). This prompted us to re-examine whether clade D PP2Cs and members from neighboring PP2C clades displayed alterations in NPH3 phosphorylation status. Genetic profiling revealed a prominent role for PP2C clade L subfamily members, PP2C19 and PP2C35, in regulating S744 phosphorylation and NPH3 function. Moreover, these phosphatases also modulate the phosphorylation status of RPT2 and NCH1, thereby influencing chloroplast accumulation movement under low blue light in both Arabidopsis and Marchantia. Together, these results suggest that reversible phosphorylation by clade L PP2Cs represents an evolutionarily conserved mechanism among phototropin-NRL targets, coordinating diverse light-directed movement responses pivotal for plant development and environmental adaptation.

## Results

## Clade L PP2Cs regulate NPH3 S744 dephosphorylation
Arabidopsis contains 80 PP2C-type phosphatases, which are classified into 12 clades (A-L) (Bhaskara et al 2019). Clade D comprises 9 subfamily members, whereas Clade E has 12 (Fig. 1a). Members of both these clades have been reported to regulate cell expansion (Bhaskara et al 2017; Ren et al 2018). These include PP2C.D2, PP2C.D5 and PP2C.D6 from clade D, as well as PP2C33 and CLADE E GROWTH-REGULATING 2 (EGR2) from clade E (Fig. 1a). To investigate potential roles in phototropic signaling, we examined whether mutants in these PP2Cs exhibit alterations in NPH3 phosphorylation status (Fig. 1b). In addition, we analysed mutants in the adjacent clade L (Fig. 1a). Etiolated seedlings were exposed to a 15-min pulse of blue light to induce S744 phosphorylation by phot1, then returned to darkness for up to 30 min. Protein extracts were isolated at various time points and S744 phosphorylation assessed using a phospho-specific anti-pS744 antibody (Sullivan et al 2021). In wild-type (WT) seedlings, dephosphorylation of S744 was evident within 15 min after returning to darkness (Fig. 1b, d). No statistically significant difference in S744 dephosphorylation kinetics was observed in the pp2c.d2/5/6 triple mutant or in the clade E mutants pp2c33-1 and egr2-3 (Fig. 1d). Notably, mutants lacking the clade L member PP2C19 exhibited a more pronounced delay, with S744 phosphorylation significantly higher after 15 min in darkness (Fig. 1b, d). These findings indicate that clade L members contribute to S744 dephosphorylation.

Clade L consists of two subfamily members, PP2C19 and PP2C35 (Bhaskara et al 2019). Both proteins contain a PP2C catalytic domain (Fig. 1c). However, PP2C19 also harbors a cyclic nucleotide binding (CNB) followed by an AGC kinase-like domain (Fig. 1c), which is predicted to be catalytically inactive due to the absence of key residues required for kinase activity (Taylor and Kornev 2011). No significant difference in S744 dephosphorylation was observed between WT and pp2c35-1 mutant seedlings (Fig. 1d). In contrast, the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant exhibited a further delay in S744 dephosphorylation compared to the pp2c19-1 single mutant, with S744 phosphorylation still detectable after 30 min of darkness (Fig. 1d). In all cases, the kinetics of S744 dephosphorylation closely mirrored the timing of NPH3 re-phosphorylation at N-terminal sites and the associated electrophoretic mobility shift, as detected using an anti-NPH3 antibody (Fig. 1b). This regulatory effect appears to be specific to NPH3, as clade L PP2Cs were not required for phot1 dephosphorylation following blue light activation (Figure S1). Collectively, these results indicate that PP2C19 and PP2C35 function cooperatively to regulate the dephosphorylation of NPH3 at S744.

## Effect of clade L PP2Cs on light-dependent relocalization of NPH3
To investigate the role of clade L PP2Cs in regulating NPH3 relocalization, we co-expressed PP2C19-GFP or PP2C35-GFP with RFP-NPH3 in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. As previously reported (Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021), repeated excitation with the 488 nm laser to activate endogenous phot1 triggered relocalization of RFP-NPH3 from the plasma membrane into cytosolic condensates in dark-adapted leaves (Fig. 2a). In contrast, co-expression of RFP-NPH3 with either PP2C19-GFP or PP2C35-GFP inhibited this light-dependent relocalization (Fig. 2b, c), resulting in constitutive plasma membrane localization of NPH3, resembling the localization phenotype of the S744A mutant (Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021). Consistent with these observations, co-expression of RFP-NPH3 with either PP2C19-GFP or PP2C35-GFP promoted dephosphorylation of NPH3 at S744 in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Fig. 2d, e). However, co-expression of RFP-NPH3 with the clade E PP2C, PP2C33-GFP, had no significant effect on light-dependent phosphorylation of S744 or NPH3 relocalization (Figure S2), indicating functional specificity among clade L PP2Cs in regulating NPH3 dynamics.

## Clade L PP2Cs promote hypocotyl phototropism and gravitropism.
We next investigated the phototropic responses of pp2c19, pp2c35, and pp2c19/35 mutants to assess whether disruption of S744 phosphorylation affects NPH3 function. Etiolated seedlings were exposed to unilateral blue light at fluence rates of 0.05, 0.5, or 20 µmol m−2 s−1, and their curvature kinetics were recorded using time-lapse imaging. pp2c19 mutants exhibited reduced responsiveness compared to WT seedlings at all fluence rates tested (Fig. 3; Figure S3). In contrast, pp2c35 mutants displayed phototropic responses comparable to WT across all light conditions (Fig. 3; Figure S3). pp2c19/35 double mutants showed a more pronounced reduction in hypocotyl curvature at lower fluence rates (0.05 and 0.5 µmol m−2 s−1) (Fig. 3; Figure S3).

To confirm the subcellular localization and functionality of clade L PP2Cs, pp2c19-2 mutants were transformed with a GFP-tagged version of PP2C19 driven by the constitutive Cauliflower Mosaic Virus 35S promoter (35S::PP2C19-GFP), while the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant was transformed with a construct encoding 35S::PP2C35-GFP. Two independent homozygous lines with comparable expression levels were selected for further analysis (Figure S4). Both PP2C19-GFP and PP2C35-GFP are localized to the plasma membrane in epidermal cells of etiolated seedlings, as demonstrated by colocalization with the lipophilic dye FM4-64 (Fig. 4a, b). Expression of 35S::PP2C19-GFP complemented the hypocotyl phototropism defects observed in pp2c19 mutants (Fig. 4c; Figure S5a, b), with a slight increase in the kinetics of curvature observed during irradiation with 20 µmol m−2 s−1 of unilateral blue light (Fig. 4c). Transgenic pp2c19/35 seedlings expressing 35S::PP2C35-GFP also exhibited restored phototropic responsiveness to WT levels (Fig. 4d; Figure S5c, d). Furthermore, expression of 35S::PP2C19-GFP and 35S::PP2C35-GFP rescued the delayed dephosphorylation of NPH3 at S744 observed in the pp2c19 mutant (Figure S6a, c) and pp2c19/35 double mutant seedlings (Figure S6b, d), with overexpression of PP2C19 resulting in accelerated NPH3 S744 dephosphorylation upon return to darkness compared to wild-type control seedlings (Figure S6a, c) These findings therefore demonstrate that the reduced phototropism in clade L PP2C mutants correlates with the role of these phosphatases in regulating NPH3 dephosphorylation at S744 (Fig. 1b, d).

PP2C.D2, PP2C.D5, and PP2C.D6 have been reported to inhibit cell expansion and overall plant growth (Ren et al 2018). As a result, pp2c.d2/5/6 triple mutants exhibit elongated hypocotyls in darkness, a phenotype that could indirectly affect phototropic responsiveness (Ren et al 2018). To determine whether clade L PP2C mutants exhibit similar growth alterations, we assessed hypocotyl elongation in etiolated seedlings. No significant differences in hypocotyl length were observed across the clade L PP2C genotypes examined (Fig. 5a), indicating that these mutations do not affect general hypocotyl growth in darkness. However, pp2c19/35 double mutants displayed a clear defect in hypocotyl gravitropism (Fig. 5b, c), which was fully rescued by expression of 35S::PP2C35-GFP in the pp2c19-1/35-1 mutant background (Figure S7). This gravitropic impairment appeared to be specific to the hypocotyl, as root gravitropic responses remained unaffected in all clade L PP2C mutants tested (Figure S8). These findings suggest that pp2c19/35 double mutants exhibit a selective defect in hypocotyl gravitropism, without broader disruptions to gravitropic signaling.

Given the role of clade L PP2Cs in regulating hypocotyl phototropism and gravitropism in etiolated seedlings, we investigated whether pp2c19/35 double mutants were less efficient than WT in penetrating the soil following germination. To examine this, we adapted a protocol recently used to show that KINESIN-LIKE CALMODULIN-BINDING PROTEIN INTERACTING PROTEIN KINASES (KIPKs) are required for efficient seedling emergence (Xiao et al 2025). WT and pp2c19/35 double mutant seeds were either sown directly on the soil surface or buried 1 cm below the soil surface as a test condition (Fig. 6a). Germination rates were comparable between genotypes when seeds were placed on the soil surface (Fig. 6b). However, when buried, pp2c19/35 mutants exhibited a significant reduction in seedling emergence relative to WT (Fig. 6b). To further investigate this phenotype, we measured hypocotyl straightness in both emerged and non-emerged seedlings germinated below the soil surface (Fig. 6c). In each case, pp2c19/35 double mutants showed impaired directional growth toward the soil surface, with the most pronounced defects observed in non-emerged seedlings (Fig. 6d). These data therefore indicate that clade L PP2Cs are required for efficient seedling emergence, likely by promoting proper hypocotyl orientation during soil penetration.

## Clade L PP2Cs regulate RPT2 phosphorylation and abundance
RPT2 is the second founding member of the NRL protein family and functions alongside NPH3 to regulate phototropism (Sakai et al 2000). Like NPH3, RPT2 is phosphorylated by phot1 at a conserved serine residue (S591) within its C-terminal region (Waksman et al 2023). Mutation of S591 to alanine compromises RPT2 function in phototropic signaling (Waksman et al 2023). To investigate whether clade L PP2Cs regulate S591 phosphorylation, we first examined phosphorylation dynamics in light-grown plants, where RPT2 expression is more abundant (Tsuchida-Mayama et al 2010). Plants were dark-adapted for 16 h, then either maintained in darkness or exposed to a 15-min blue light pulse to induce S591 phosphorylation, followed by a return to darkness for up to 30 min. Consistent with previous findings (Waksman et al 2023), blue light-induced RPT2 phosphorylation at S591 was detected in WT seedlings using a phospho-specific anti-pS591 antibody (Fig. 7a), which also cross-reacts with a higher molecular weight protein (potentially another NRL protein) in light-treated samples expressing phototropins (Figure S9a). Dephosphorylation of S591 occurred rapidly in darkness, with the signal becoming undetectable within 5 min, much faster than the dephosphorylation kinetics observed for NPH3 at S744 (Fig. 1b, d). In contrast, the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant showed significantly elevated levels of S591 phosphorylation throughout the time course (Fig. 7b and c). However, this was restored in pp2c19-1/35-1 mutants expressing 35S::PP2C35-GFP (Figure S10a, c) and in pp2c19-2 mutants expressing 35S::PP2C19-GFP (Figure S10b, d), respectively. These results indicate that clade L PP2Cs regulate the phosphorylation status of other NRL proteins besides NPH3.

In addition to changes in RPT2 phosphorylation status, clade L PP2C mutants exhibited changes in RPT2 protein abundance. Both pp2c19-1 and pp2c19-1/35-1 mutant plants had significantly increased RPT2 protein levels, while RPT2 abundance in pp2c35-1 plants was comparable to WT (Fig. 8a, b). This increase in RPT2 protein levels was fully complemented in pp2c19-2 mutants expressing 35S::PP2C19-GFP (Figure S11a, c) and partially complemented in pp2c19-1/35-1 mutants expressing 35S::PP2C35-GFP (Figure S11b, d). To determine whether the changes in RPT2 protein levels in the clade L PP2C mutants were dependent upon prior light exposure, we assessed RPT2 abundance in etiolated seedlings either maintained in darkness or following a red or blue light exposure. RPT2 is expressed at lower abundance in WT etiolated seedlings, a 2 h exposure to red or blue light was sufficient to increase RPT2 protein levels (Fig. 8c, e), consistent with previous findings that these light conditions promote RPT2 transcript accumulation (Tsuchida-Mayama et al 2010) (Fig. 8g). In pp2c19-1 and pp2c35-1 single mutants, both RPT2 transcript accumulation and protein levels were comparable to WT (Fig. 8c, e). However, pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutants exhibited elevated levels of RPT2 in darkness and red light (Fig. 8d, f), without a corresponding increase in transcript abundance compared to WT seedlings (Fig. 8g). These findings indicate that both PP2C19 and PP2C35 are required to promote RPT2 protein turnover in etiolated seedlings. Supporting this conclusion, treatment of dark-grown seedlings with the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib led to an increase in RPT2 protein levels to amounts comparable to those observed following blue light exposure (Fig. 8h). Together, these results suggest that clade L PP2Cs facilitate proteasome-mediated degradation of RPT2, thereby maintaining appropriate protein levels in the absence of blue light.

## Clade L PP2Cs control NCH1 phosphorylation status
RPT2 functions together with NCH1 to mediate chloroplast accumulation movement to the periclinal cell surface under low light conditions (Suetsugu et al 2016), a process that enhances photosynthetic efficiency and supports seedling establishment (Gotoh et al 2018). Although NCH1 contains a conserved phototropin phosphorylation site consensus sequence at its C-terminus, it has not previously been confirmed as a substrate target. To address this, we tested whether NCH1 is a direct phosphorylation target of phot1 using a gatekeeper-engineered version of the kinase (phot1GK), which can utilize the bulky ATP analog N6-benzyl-ATPγS as a thiophospho-donor (Schnabel et al 2018). NCH1 was co-expressed with phot1GK in a cell-free expression system and subjected to in vitro kinase assays in the presence of N6-benzyl-ATPγS. Light-induced thiophosphorylation of NCH1 was detected by immunoblotting with an anti-thiophosphoester antibody following chemical alkylation of the incorporated thiophosphates (Fig. 9a). While light-dependent phosphorylation was still observed in the S604A mutant, substitution of S602 to alanine abolished phosphorylation, indicating that phot1 phosphorylates NCH1 at S602, an equivalent residue to S591 in RPT2 and S744 in NPH3.

To assess NCH1 phosphorylation at S602 in vivo, we raised a phospho-specific antibody targeting this site (pS602). In dark-adapted, light-grown WT plants, a 15-min blue light pulse induced phosphorylation at S602, which was not detected in phot1-5 phot2-1 mutant plants (Figure S9b). Light-dependent increases in S602 phosphorylation were observed in both WT and the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant (Fig. 9b, c). As with RPT2 S591 (Fig. 7a), S602 phosphorylation was rapidly reversed in darkness in WT plants, with dephosphorylation occurring within 5 min (Fig. 9b). However, this dephosphorylation was substantially delayed in the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant (Fig. 9c, d), as well as pp2c19-2 single mutant (Figure S12a, c) plants. Transgenic lines expressing 35S::PP2C19-GFP or 35S::PP2C35-GFP in the pp2c19-2 or pp2c19-1/35-1 mutant backgrounds, respectively, restored WT levels of S602 dephosphorylation (Figure S12a–d), indicating that clade L PP2Cs dynamically regulate NCH1 phosphorylation at S602.

## Light-induced chloroplast accumulation movement is reduced in clade L PP2C mutants of Arabidopsis and Marchantia
The observed effects of clade L PP2Cs on the phosphorylation status of RPT2 and NCH1 (Figs. 7 and 9), as well as on RPT2 protein abundance (Fig. 8a) in light-grown plants, prompted us to examine light-induced chloroplast movement responses in pp2c19 and pp2c35 mutants. Under low blue light conditions, chloroplasts accumulate at the upper cell surface, reducing red light transmittance through the leaf. In contrast, under high-intensity blue light, chloroplasts relocate to the side walls (avoidance response), increasing red light transmittance. To assess these responses, we measured red light transmittance through leaves to monitor the kinetics of chloroplast movement in clade L pp2c mutants.
In wild-type (WT) plants, chloroplast accumulation in response to 0.01 or 1 µmol m−2 s−1 blue light was clearly detectable, whereas this response was absent in the phot1/2 double mutant (Fig. 10a, b). pp2c35-1 mutants exhibited chloroplast accumulation responses comparable to WT under both light conditions (Fig. 10a, b, d, e). In contrast, pp2c19-1 mutants showed reduced accumulation responses, which were further diminished in the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant (Fig. 10a, b, d, e). These defects were alleviated by expression of 35S::PP2C19-GFP and 35S::PP2C35-GFP in pp2c19-2 and pp2c19-1/35-1 mutant backgrounds, respectively (Figure S13). These findings support a role for clade L PP2Cs in regulating the phosphorylation dynamics of NCH1 and RPT2, both of which are involved in the chloroplast accumulation response. Importantly, NCH1 and RPT2 are not required for the chloroplast avoidance response, which is mediated exclusively by phot2 (Kagawa et al 2001; Suetsugu et al 2016). Accordingly, this response remained unaffected across all clade L PP2C genotypes tested (Fig. 10c, f), highlighting the specificity of clade L PP2Cs in modulating components involved in the chloroplast accumulation pathway.

To investigate the conserved role of clade L PP2C proteins in regulating phototropin-mediated chloroplast movement across land plants, we searched for homologs of Arabidopsis PP2C19 in the liverwort Marchantia polymorpha. A single ortholog, designated MpPP2C19 (Mp4g20380), was identified (Fig. 11a). MpPP2C19, like Arabidopsis PP2C35, encodes a protein containing only a PP2C domain. To investigate its function, we generated MpPP2C19 loss-of-function mutants using a CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing approach. Multiple independent mutant lines (designated Mppp2c19ge) were isolated, and three representative lines (Mppp2c19ge−1 to Mppp2c19ge−3) were selected for further analysis. Mppp2c19ge-1 and Mppp2c19ge-2 harbored a 1-bp deletion and a 1-bp insertion in the MpPP2C19 coding sequence, resulting in a predicted premature stop codon (Fig. 11a). Mppp2c19ge-3 harbored a 9-bp deletion, resulting in a 3-aa deletion (Fig. 11a). Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed a significant reduction in MpPP2C19 transcript levels in the mutant lines compared to wild-type plants (Figure S14a). To assess chloroplast photorelocation responses, 3-d-old white-light-grown gemmalings from wild type, Mpphotko, and the three Mppp2c19ge lines were dark-adapted for 3 d and then either maintained in darkness or exposed to weak (10 μmol m−2 s−1) blue light for 2 h. In dark-adapted wild-type gemmaling, chloroplasts were mostly localized along the anticlinal cell walls, avoiding the outermost walls of marginal cells, a typical dark positioning pattern in Marchantia (Komatsu et al 2014) (Fig. 11b). Upon weak blue light exposure, wild-type chloroplasts accumulated at the cell surface (accumulation response). In contrast, Mpphotko mutants lacked both responses, displaying random chloroplast distribution regardless of the light conditions (Komatsu et al 2014). Mppp2c19ge lines exhibited normal dark positioning (Fig. 11c) and retained the avoidance response under strong blue light (Figure S14b). However, they failed to show the accumulation response under weak blue light (Fig. 11b, c). These findings suggest that MpPP2C19 is specifically required for the chloroplast accumulation response and that its function is evolutionarily conserved among land plants.

## Discussion

## Clade L PP2Cs dephosphorylate S744 in NPH3 to promote phototropism
Functional characterization of clade L PP2Cs has been limited, though a rice ortholog of PP2C19 has been implicated in hormone responses to salt (Shen et al 2019). In this study, we uncovered a role for clade L PP2Cs in regulating phototropism through modulation of NPH3 phosphorylation. While preparing this manuscript, Sakai et al (2025) independently reported that PP2C19 regulates NPH3 S744 dephosphorylation and phototropic responses under continuous light. Their findings align with our results, which show that pp2c19 mutants exhibit reduced phototropism (Fig. 3; Figure S3) due to impaired dephosphorylation of NPH3 at S744 (Fig. 1b, d). We further demonstrate that PP2C35 acts redundantly with PP2C19 to regulate the phosphorylation dynamics of NPH3, acting in an additive manner with PP2C19. In the pp2c19/35 double mutants, S744 dephosphorylation is further reduced compared to pp2c19 single mutants (Fig. 1b, d), leading to diminished re-phosphorylation of NPH3 at N-terminal sites (Fig. 1b). This would impair the reassembly of the active phot1-NPH3 complex at the plasma membrane, a process necessary for maintaining directional light signaling under continuous irradiation. Consequently, pp2c19/35 double mutants exhibit more pronounced phototropic defects (Fig. 3; Figure S3). These findings support a model in which reversible phosphorylation of S744 is important for NPH3 function (Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021).
De-etiolation has previously been shown to enhance phototropism by modulating NPH3 phosphorylation dynamics (Sullivan et al 2019), raising the possibility that clade L PP2Cs may be upregulated or activated during this developmental transition. Supporting this hypothesis, constitutive expression of PP2C19 under the control of the 35S promoter in the pp2c19 single mutant background led to modest enhancements in both S744 dephosphorylation (Figure S6a, c) and phototropic curvature (Fig. 4c), suggesting that increased PP2C19 activity can positively influence phototropic responsiveness. In contrast, PP2C35 overexpression in the pp2c19/35 double mutant did not accelerate phototropism (Fig. 4d; Figure S5c, d) and had a weaker effect on NPH3 S744 phosphorylation (Figure S6b), possibly due to differences in genetic backgrounds. Phosphorylation of S744 by phot1 promotes 14-3-3 binding and triggers NPH3 relocalization from the plasma membrane into intracellular condensates. Co-expression of NPH3 with PP2C19 or PP2C35 blocks this light-induced relocalization (Fig. 2b, c), thereby reversing the impact of S744 phosphorylation and mimicking the constitutive plasma-membrane localization of the S744A mutant (Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021). We did not observe any marked relocalization of PP2C19 or PP2C35 from the plasma membrane (Fig. 2b, 2c). These findings suggest that the phosphorylation status of NPH3 at S744 is governed by the relative activities of plasma membrane-localized phototropin kinases and clade L phosphatases. The clade L PP2Cs would act to attenuate S744 phosphorylation status, facilitating the formation of a signaling gradient across the hypocotyl required for a robust phototropic response (Sullivan et al 2019; Nawkar et al 2023). Further investigation is required to identify the specific kinases and phosphatases responsible for modulating phosphorylation at NPH3 sites within the N-terminus, and to elucidate the functional significance of clade L phosphatase localization at the plasma membrane. Similarly, as NPH3 S744 dephosphorylation is not completely abolished in pp2c19/35 double mutants, it is likely that additional phosphatases, perhaps other PP2C family members, participate in regulating NPH3 signaling and remain to be identified. Further analysis of clade D PP2Cs is warranted, as the pp2c.d2/5/6 triple mutant showed minor, though not significant, effects on NPH3 dephosphorylation (Fig. 1b, d).
NPH3 membrane association is phospholipid-dependent, particularly on PI4P (Reuter et al 2021). Depletion of this interaction, or replacement of the phot1 phosphorylation site with a synthetic 14-3-3 binding peptide (R18), displaces NPH3 from the plasma membrane (Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021). Mutations that prevent or constitutively bind 14-3-3 s severely compromise NPH3 function in phototropism, further highlighting the importance of S744 phosphorylation (Reuter et al 2021; Sullivan et al 2021). Interestingly, a myristoylated version of GFP-NPH3 (mGN3) that remains membrane-bound under light conditions retains full phototropic function, despite still undergoing changes in S744 phosphorylation (Zhu et al 2024). This suggests that membrane localization alone may be sufficient to support phototropism if S744 phosphorylation dynamics are preserved.

## Clade L PP2Cs regulate multiple phot-NRL targets
The phot1 phosphorylation site is conserved across several other NRL proteins, including RPT2 (Sullivan et al 2021; Waksman et al 2023) and NCH1 (Fig. 9a). Mutation of the conserved serine residue in RPT2 (S591) does not affect its plasma membrane localization but compromises its function in phototropism and chloroplast accumulation movement (Waksman et al 2023). Here, we show that S591 is rapidly dephosphorylated upon transferring light-grown seedlings to darkness, a process that involves PP2C19 and PP2C35 (Fig. 7; Figure S10). Our findings also reveal that clade L PP2Cs promote RPT2 protein turnover. In light-grown seedlings, RPT2 protein levels are elevated in both pp2c19 and pp2c19/35 double mutants (Fig. 8a, b; Figure S11). In etiolated seedlings, however, RPT2 turnover requires the combined action of PP2C19 and PP2C35 (Fig. 8d, f; Figure S11b, d). Notably, elevated RPT2 levels are already present in dark-grown pp2c19/35 mutants prior to light exposure, suggesting that this regulation is independent of phot1-mediated S591 phosphorylation. This is consistent with previous findings that mutation of S591 to alanine had no impact on the accumulation of RPT2 protein in response to blue light (Waksman et al 2023).
RPT2 is thought to modulate photosensory adaptation of the phot1-NPH3 complex by influencing NPH3 phosphorylation (Haga et al 2015). While RPT2 overexpression enhances phototropism under continuous blue light in etiolated seedlings (Haga et al 2015), pp2c19/35 mutants, despite elevated RPT2 levels, exhibit reduced curvature responses (Fig. 3; Figure S3). The pp2c19 single mutants also display reduced phototropism (Fig. 3) without significant changes in RPT2 protein levels (Fig. 8e). RPT2 also attenuates phot1 autophosphorylation via interacting with its light-sensing region (Kimura et al 2020). Yet phot1 autophosphorylation remains unaffected in the pp2c19/35 mutant (Figure S1b), suggesting that differences in RPT2 phosphorylation status may underlie these phenotypic discrepancies.
We also demonstrate that NCH1 is a direct substrate of phot1 kinase activity. Like NPH3 and RPT2, NCH1 is phosphorylated by phot1 at a conserved C-terminal serine (S602) (Fig. 9a). Phosphorylation at S602 is detectable in light-grown seedlings using phospho-specific anti-pS602 antibody and is rapidly reversed in darkness by the presence of clade L (Fig. 9b, d). Loss of PP2C19, and more so of both PP2C19 and PP2C35, impair chloroplast accumulation movement in Arabidopsis (Fig. 9c, d), indicating that reversible phosphorylation regulates NCH1 function. In contrast, chloroplast avoidance movement under highlight remains unaffected in pp2c19/35 mutants (Fig. 10c, f), indicating that this response, solely mediated by phot2, is independent of clade L PP2C activity. Similarly, MpPP2C19 is specifically required for chloroplast accumulation movement in Marchantia (Fig. 11; Figure S14), suggesting that the regulatory role of clade L PP2Cs in chloroplast positioning is evolutionarily conserved among land plants. Mppp2C19 mutants failed to show the accumulation response under weak blue light (Fig. 11b, c), phenocopying the Mpnch1ko mutant (Suetsugu et al 2016). This is consistent with previous findings that avoidance movement does not require NRL proteins (Christie et al 2018).

## Hypocotyl gravitropism is regulated by clade L PP2Cs
Reversible protein phosphorylation is central to modulating auxin-dependent physiology and development, primarily through the action of AGC kinases such as PINOID (PID) and D6 PROTEIN KINASE (D6PK) family members (Tan et al 2021). Mutants deficient in clade L PP2Cs exhibit impaired hypocotyl gravitropism (Fig. 5b, c), suggesting that these phosphatases influence auxin signaling beyond phototropism. PID kinases contribute to the red light-mediated enhancement of hypocotyl phototropism in Arabidopsis (Haga et al 2014), a response that concurrently suppresses hypocotyl gravitropism (Lariguet and Fankhauser 2004). Similarly, both hypocotyl phototropism and gravitropism are disrupted in d6pk mutants (Willige et al 2013). Clade L PP2Cs may therefore modulate the phosphorylation status and activity of NRL proteins involved in PID/D6PK signaling.
PID and D6PKs activate auxin efflux by phosphorylating the intracellular hydrophilic loop of PIN auxin efflux carriers (Zourelidou et al 2014). Several protein phosphatases, including PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2A (PP2A) (Michniewicz et al 2007), PP1 (Guo et al 2015), and PP6 (Dai et al 2012) have been implicated in PIN dephosphorylation. Currently, there is no direct evidence linking clade L PP2Cs to PIN phosphorylation. The NRL protein MACCHI-BOU 4 (MAB4), also known as ENHANCER OF PID (ENP) and NAKED PINS IN YUCCA 1 (NPY1), has been shown to act together with PID to facilitate PIN polar localization at the plasma membrane by restricting lateral diffusion of the PID-PIN-MAB4 complex (Glanc et al 2021). MAB4 is also phosphorylated by phot1 at its C-terminal region (Sullivan et al 2021). Further work is now needed to elucidate the role of reversible phosphorylation in MAB4 function and determine whether clade L PP2Cs are involved in this regulatory process.

## Materials and methods

## Plant material and growth
Arabidopsis thaliana Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used as the wild-type (WT) control in all experiments. The mutants egr2-3 (Ding et al. 2019), phot1-5, phot2-1 (Sakai et al. 2001) and pp2c.d2/5/6 (Ren et al 2018) have been described previously. T-DNA insertion lines for pp2c19-1 (SALK_108016), pp2c19-2 (GABI-KAT_918D02), pp2c35-1 (SALK_027747), pp2c35-2 (SALK_021793), and pp2c33-1 (SALK_017280) were obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC, Nottingham, UK). Double mutants pp2c19-1 pp2c35-1 and pp2c19-1 pp2c35-2 were generated by crossing. All T-DNA lines were genotyped for homozygosity using primers listed in Table S1. Unless otherwise stated, seeds were surface sterilized and sown onto half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 0.8% agar (w/v) or directly onto soil (Levington Advance Seed and Modular F2S), followed by stratification at 4 °C for 2 to 5 d. Soil-grown seedlings were transferred to a controlled environment room (Fitotron, Weiss Technik) with LED illumination (C65NS12, Valoya) under long-day conditions (16 h 22 °C light: 8 h 18 °C dark) and 100 μmol m−2 s−1 white light. For etiolated seedlings, seeds on MS medium were exposed to 80 μmol m−2 s−1 white light for 6 to 8 h to induce germination, then grown vertically in darkness for 3 d. For light-grown plants, seeds were sown on half-strength MS agar medium supplemented with 0.5% sucrose and grown for 2 weeks under constant white light (50 μmol m−2 s−1) at 22 °C. Nicotiana benthamiana plants were grown on soil under long-day conditions (16 h 22 °C light: 8 h 18 °C dark) and 100 μmol m−2 s−1 white light for 3 to 4 weeks before plants were used for Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration. Light treatments were performed by filtering white light through a Moonlight Blue filter (No. 183, Lee Filters) or a Deep Golden Amber filter (No. 135, Lee Filters) for blue or red light, respectively. Light fluence rates were measured with a Li-250A light meter and quantum sensor (LI-COR). The male accession Marchantia polymorpha Takaragaike-1 (Tak-1) was used as the wild-type strain. A phototropin knockout line (Mpphotko) was previously described (Komatsu et al 2014). Gemmalings were grown on 1% agar medium supplemented with half-strength Gamborg's B5 at 22 °C under continuous white light (∼50 μmol m−2 s−1).”

## Immunoblot analysis
Total proteins were extracted from etiolated seedlings by grinding 50 seedlings in 100 µl 2×SDS sample buffer (125 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], 2% [w/v] SDS, 20% [v/v] glycerol, 10% [v/v] β-mercaptoethanol, 0.004% [w/v] bromophenol blue) on ice, under red safe light illumination. Extracts were clarified by centrifugation at 13,000g for 5 min and boiled for 4 min. For light-grown plants, plants were dark-adapted for 16 h prior to extraction. Samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground in a mortar and pestle on ice in GTEN buffer (10% [v/v] glycerol, 25 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 1% [v/v] Triton X-100) supplemented with 0.4% SDS, 10 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Complete EDTA-free; Merck). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C, mixed with SDS sample buffer, and boiled for 4 min. Protein concentrations were determined using Protein Assay Dye Reagent (Bio-Rad). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF or nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad) using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Immunodetection was performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-GFP (H39, Chromotek), anti-GST (Cytiva, 27-4577-01; Merck), anti-thiophosphoester (TPE; clone 51-8, Abcam), anti-phot1 (Cho et al 2007), anti-RPT2 (Waksman et al 2023), anti-NCH1 (Suetsugu et al 2016), anti-NPH3, and anti-NPH3 pS744 phospho-specific (Sullivan et al 2021) antibodies. Phospho-specific antibodies against phot1 S350, RPT2 S591, and NCH1 S602 were raised against the peptides KRPRALpSESTNL, TKPRRRRFpSIS, and KPPKDRRHpSIS, respectively (Eurogentec), where pS denotes phosphorylated serine. Blots were developed using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Promega), Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Merck), and visualized with a Fusion FX imaging system (Vilber) or X-ray film (Carestream), followed by digitization using an Epson Perfection V700 flatbed scanner. Quantification of band intensities from immunoblots was performed using Fiji software (Schindelin et al 2012).

## Bortezomib treatment
Bortezomib (Santa Cuz Biotechnology, sc-217785) was prepared as a 10 mM stock solution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Hypocotyl segments of etiolated seedlings were dissected immediately below the cotyledonary node and above the root-shoot junction using microscissors (Fine Science Tools) under red safelight illumination. Segments were transferred to six well plates containing one-quarter-strength MS medium and 50 µM Bortezomib or 0.5% (v/v) DMSO as a solvent control. Seedlings were vacuum infiltrated for 5 min, followed by incubation in darkness for 3 h with gentle agitation. Following treatment, seedlings were either maintained in darkness or irradiated with 20 µmol m−2 s−1 blue light for 2 h prior to protein extraction. Proteins were extracted by grinding in GTEN buffer (10% [v/v] glycerol, 25 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 0.2% SDS, 10 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and a protease inhibitor mixture (Complete EDTA-free; Merck).

## In vitro kinase assay
The coding sequence of DOT3 was removed from the GST-DOT3-pSP64 vector (Sullivan et al 2021) using BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites and replaced with the NCH1 coding sequence, amplified from cDNA and inserted via Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs). Site-directed mutagenesis was used to generate the NCH1 S602A and S604A variants. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. In vitro kinase assays were performed by co-expressing the substrate together with a gatekeeper engineered phot1 (phot1GK) (Schnabel et al 2018) using the TnT® SP6 High-Yield Wheat Germ Protein Expression System (Promega). Each 20 µl cell-free expression reaction contained 2 µg of pSP64 poly(A) vector encoding phot1GK and 2 µg of substrate-encoding vector, which was incubated with 10 µM flavin mononucleotide (FMN) for 2 h at room temperature in darkness. Thiophosphorylation reactions were prepared under a red safe light. For each 20 μl reaction, 10 μl of the expression mixture was incubated in the presence of 500 μM N6-benzyl-ATPγS (Jena Bioscience) in phosphorylation buffer (37.5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 5.3 mM MgSO4, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EGTA). Samples were either mock-irradiated or exposed to white light (60,000 µmol m−2 total fluence) for 20 s. Reactions were allowed to proceed for 5 min before the addition of EDTA (pH 8.0) to a final concentration of 20 mM. Thiophosphorylated molecules were alkylated with 2.5 mM p-nitrobenzyl mesylate (PNBM, Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature in darkness, then mixed with SDS sample buffer and boiled for 4 min.

## Transformation of Arabidopsis
To generate 35S::PP2C19-GFP, a 4415 bp genomic fragment containing the 14 exons and 13 introns of PP2C19 was amplified from WT genomic DNA and inserted into the pEZR(K)-LN binary vector (Christie et al 2002) using KpnI and BamHI restriction sites via Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs). For 35S::PP2C35-GFP, the PP2C35 coding sequence was amplified from cDNA and inserted into the same vector using EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites, also via Gibson Assembly. All primers are listed in Table S1. The pp2c19-2 mutant was transformed with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying the 35S::PP2C19-GFP construct, while the pp2c19-1/35-1 double mutant was transformed with the 35S::PP2C35-GFP construct, following the floral dip method (Davis et al 2009). Briefly, a 500 mL saturated A. tumefaciens culture was grown in YEBS medium at 28 °C with shaking, diluted 1:1 with 5% (w/v) sucrose, and supplemented with 0.01% (v/v) Silwet® L-77. Flowering plants were dipped into the solution and sealed in a plastic bag overnight. A second dip was performed 3 to 5 d later. Independent homozygous T3 lines were selected for analysis based on segregation of kanamycin resistance.

## Transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana
To generate the NPH3::RFP-NPH3 construct, the NPH3::GFP-NPH3 binary vector (Sullivan et al 2019) was digested with HindIII and BamHI to remove the GFP-NPH3 fragment. The coding sequences for RFP (amplified from the pB7WGR2 binary vector) (Karimi et al 2005) and NPH3 were inserted via Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs) using primers listed in Table S1. For 35S::PP2C33-GFP, the PP2C33 coding sequence was amplified from cDNA and inserted into the pEZR(K)-LN binary vector using EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites via Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs). A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying either 35S::PP2C19-GFP, 35S::PP2C35-GFP, 35S::PP2C33-GFP or NPH3::RFP-NPH3 was resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES-KOH [pH 5.6], 200 mM acetosyringone) and incubated at room temperature for 2 to 3 h. For co-infiltrations, bacterial cultures were mixed prior to inoculation and diluted to a final OD600 of 0.4. Agrobacterium suspensions were infiltrated into the abaxial side of leaves of 3 to 4-week-old N. benthamiana plants using a needleless syringe. Plants were dark-adapted for 16 h, and 1 cm leaf discs were collected 2 d post-infiltration for confocal microscopy.

## Confocal microscopy
Confocal imaging was performed with a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with HC PL APO 20×/0.75 or 40×/1.30 objectives. For localization of GFP-tagged PP2C19, PP2C35, or PP2C33 and RFP-tagged NPH3 in N. benthamiana leaves, GFP was excited with the 488-nm laser and emission collected between 500 and 530 nm. RFP was excited with the 552-nm laser and emission collected between 600 and 630 nm. In each case, sequential scanning was performed between lines. Images were acquired at 1,024 to 1,024-pixel resolution with a line average of two. Z-stacks were captured at 5 min intervals, with samples kept in darkness between scans. Maximum projection images were generated from z-stacks using Fiji software version 2.14.0 (Schindelin et al 2012). For localization of GFP-tagged PP2C19 or PP2C35 in transgenic Arabidopsis, etiolated seedlings were stained with 25 μM FM4-64 (Invitrogen, T3166) for 1 min prior to imaging. GFP was excited at 488 nm with emission collected between 500 and 530 nm, and FM4-64 was excited at the same wavelength with emission collected between 680 and 710 nm. Images were acquired from 1,024 to 1,024-pixel resolution with a line average of three. Fluorescence intensity profiles were measured from single optical sections along a 20 μm line using Fiji software.

## Hypocotyl and gravitropism measurements
Seedlings were grown vertically on half-strength MS medium for 3 d in darkness before being placed on a white light transilluminator and photographed. Hypocotyl lengths as well as hypocotyl and root angles relative to the vertical axis were measured using Fiji software (Schindelin et al 2012). Box-and-whisker plots and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used to assess statistical significance.

## Phototropism
Phototropic responses were measured using free-standing etiolated seedlings as described (Sullivan et al 2016). Seeds were sown on a layer of silicon dioxide (Honeywell, Fluka) in transparent plastic entomology boxes (Watkins and Doncaster), watered with one-quarter-strength MS medium, and stratified at 4 °C for 2 to 5 d. To induce germination, seeds were exposed to 80 µmol m−2 s−1 white light for 6 to 8 h, then grown in darkness for 64 to 68 h. Seedlings were exposed to unilateral blue light, and images were captured every 10 min for 4 h using a Retiga 6000 CCD camera (QImaging) connected to a PC running QCapture Pro 7 software (QImaging), with supplemental infrared LED illumination. Hypocotyl curvature was measured using Fiji software (version 2.14.0; Schindelin et al 2012) (Schindelin et al 2012) from at least two biological replicates, with approximately 10 seedlings per replicate. At each time point, significant differences compared to the WT control were calculated by two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test (GraphPad Prism, version 10.2.3).

## Chloroplast movement
Arabidopsis plants were grown for 4 weeks in a controlled growth chamber under a 12 h light:12 h dark photoperiod, approximately 100 μmol m−2 s−1 of light from fluorescent tubes, and 80% relative humidity. Measurements were performed on detached leaves from plants that had been dark-adapted overnight, using a custom-built photometric setup. Chloroplast responses were induced with blue actinic light (peak at 455 nm, M455L4 LED; Thorlabs). Photon irradiance (Braslavsky 2007) at the sample plane was measured with a LI-190R sensor (LI-COR) connected to a picoammeter (Keithley 6485). The red measuring beam (660 nm; M660L4 LED, Thorlabs) was modulated at 1,033 Hz. Actinic and measuring beams were collimated and combined using a dichroic mirror (DMLP550; Thorlabs), then directed toward a leaf mounted in front of an integrating sphere (IS200-4; Thorlabs) to measure total transmittance. Transmitted red light was detected using a photodiode detector (DET100A2; Thorlabs) positioned at another port of the sphere. For Marchantia, 3-d-old gemmaling were dark-adapted for 3 d and then irradiated with blue light supplied by an LED source (DS757-RGB, MRT Corporation). Chloroplast distribution and movement were observed using a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus) equipped with a UPlanXApo 20×/0.80 objective. Chloroplast coverage was quantified as the percentage of cell surface area occupied by chloroplasts using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/). Statistical comparisons among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test.

## Seedling emergence
Seeds were surface sterilized and sown onto half-strength MS medium containing 0.8% agar (w/v), then stratified at 4 °C for 2 to 5 d. To induce germination, seeds were exposed to 80 μmol m−2 s−1 white light 4 h before being transferred to soil (5:1 mix of compost and horticultural silver sand). Seeds were either covered with 1 cm of soil or, as a control, sown directly on the soil surface. Plants were grown under long-day conditions (16 h 22 °C light: 8 h 18 °C dark) with 100 μmol m−2 s−1 white light for 7 d. For each treatment, 50 seeds per genotype were sown, and seedling emergence at the soil surface was recorded from five biological replicates. Seedlings that germinated beneath the soil surface were removed, rinsed with water, transferred to agar plates, and imaged. Seedling straightness was quantified by using Fiji software version 2.14.0 (Schindelin et al 2012) by measuring the straight-line distance between the base and apex of the hypocotyl (LC) and dividing by the total hypocotyl length (LH). Bar charts and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3), with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test.

## Transcript analysis
Total RNA was isolated from three biological replicates of 3-d-old, etiolated seedlings maintained in darkness (D) or following 2 h of irradiation with 20 µmol m−2 s−1 of red or blue light, using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) followed by DNase treatment (Turbo DNA-free; Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using oligo(dT) and SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed using Brilliant II SYBR Green qRT-PCR Master Mix (Agilent) with ROX passive reference dye on a StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). IRON SULFUR CLUSTER ASSEMBLY PROTEIN1 was used as the reference gene (Bordage et al 2016). For M. polymorpha, Total RNA was extracted from 10-d-old gemmaling using RNA Premium Kit (Nippon Genetics). cDNA was synthesized from 400 ng of total RNA using the PrimeScript FAST RT Reagent Kit (Takara). Quantitative PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on a Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System II (Takara). The MpELONGATION FACTOR 1 (MpEF) gene (Mp3g23400) was used as an internal reference for normalization, as described (Inoue et al 2019). Primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Table S1. Bar charts and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3), with two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's post hoc test.

## Generation of Marchantia polymorpha mutants
A guide RNA (gRNA) targeting the MpPP2C19 coding region was designed using CasFinder (https://marchantia.info/tools/casfinder/). The gRNA-coding DNA oligomer, generated by annealing primers gRNA_1F and gRNA_1R, was cloned into the BsaI site of the pMpGE_En03 entry vector (Sugano et al 2018). The gRNA cassette was then transferred into the pMpGE010 CRISPR/Cas9 binary vector (Sugano et al 2018) using LR Clonase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting construct, pMpGE010-MpPP2C19-gRNA1, was introduced into M. polymorpha Tak-1 thalli via A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation (Kubota et al 2013). Hygromycin-resistant transformants were screened, and genomic DNA was extracted for PCR amplification of the MpPP2C19 coding region using primers A and B. Amplicons were sequenced to confirm genome editing. Confirmed genome-edited lines were propagated and maintained via gemmae. Primers for vector construction and sequencing are listed in Table S1.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis by t-test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 software. All statistical results are provided in Supplementary Data Set S1.
