SUPPRESSOR OF LAZY QUADRUPLE 1 acts at ER-plasma membrane contact sites to control a gravitropism pathway in the <i>Arabidopsis</i> stem.

# SUPPRESSOR OF LAZY QUADRUPLE 1 acts at ER–plasma membrane contact sites to control a gravitropism pathway in the Arabidopsis stem

## Abstract
SignificanceLand plants use molecular pathways to convert the gravity vector into a signal that guides the growth of organs. The pathways appear more elaborate with each new finding. Currently we know that LAZY family proteins feature in pathways that guide stem growth upward, roots downward, and branches outward at an intermediate angle. We conducted a genetic screen that identified a protein (SLQ1) in a gravity signaling pathway that operates independently and oppositely of LAZY pathways. SLQ1 functions in endodermal cells at sites where the ER contacts the plasma membrane. The evidence indicates that independent gravity signaling pathways combine in opposition to create a shoot architecture adapted for the conditions, which affects fitness in nature and productivity in crops.
Gravitropism assists a plant’s search for resources such as water and light by orienting growth using the gravity vector as a reference (1, 2). The differential growth across the organ that produces the correct orientation is due to a redistribution of auxin as first proposed by Cholodny and Went (3). PIN-FORMED (PIN) auxin efflux carriers create the auxin gradient when they are trafficked to the plasma membrane (PM) at the lowest face of gravity-sensing cells called statocytes (4, 5), which are located in the cap of the root and the endodermis of the stem (6, 7). How plants determine which side of a statocyte is lowest, and hence the direction of the gravity vector has long been thought to depend on to which cellular face some amyloplasts referred to as statoliths, being denser than cytoplasm, come to rest (1, 8). According to the current model for gravity sensing in root caps, settling statoliths bring LAZY proteins with them due to an association with TOC proteins in the outer plastid membrane (9–11). The LAZY proteins then shift from the surface of the statolith to the PM, apparently stabilized there by basic hydrophobic domains in the protein that have an affinity for acidic headgroups of lipids on the cytoplasmic face of the PM (9, 10, 12). In the case of the Arabidopsis inflorescence, an interacting protein called BRXL4 determines how much LAZY1 remains at the PM versus delivered to the nucleus where it decreases its own expression in a mechanism that determines branch angles (12). In the root cap, the RCC1-like domain protein and the D6 protein kinase (D6PK) participate in converting the cellular polarization of LAZY proteins into polarized auxin efflux from the statocytes (13–15). These relatively recent results outline an elegant statolith-based, LAZY-dependent, gravity-directed auxin efflux mechanism that may be the core of a gravitropism pathway in both stems and roots if not all plant organs (16).
LAZY proteins play a crucial role in a major if not the principal gravitropism mechanism of land plants, but a LAZY-independent mechanism for aligning an auxin gradient according to the gravity vector is also apparent. Arabidopsis possesses five complete LAZY genes (17). The inflorescence stem of a quintuple lazy mutant lacking these five genes is prostrate (18), growing along the surface of the soil like the original maize lazy mutant (19), as if it were completely agravitropic. However, reorientation assays demonstrated that the quintuple lazy mutant responds to gravity, albeit weakly and in reverse—its stem bends downward. Likewise in roots, a reverse form of gravitropism occurs in mutants lacking three LAZY genes due to the formation of a reversed auxin gradient (20, 21). The reverse form of gravitropism has also been called antigravitropism (22), and may serve to produce a smooth, well controlled bend as it opposes the stronger, normal gravitropism mechanism.
The reverse gravitropism mechanism in the atlazy1atlazy2atlazy3atlazy4 mutant (hereafter atlazy1;2;3;4) is probably distinct from any LAZY pathway because atlazy1;2;3;4 consists of three null alleles of atlazy1, atlazy2, and atlazy4 (13, 17, 23) and an atlazy3 allele that has its fourth exon disrupted with a T-DNA (21, 24) upstream of the highly conserved and functionally necessary region V (18). Thus, the quadruple mutant probably does not produce any functional LAZY protein in the inflorescence stem. To investigate a phenomenon that appears to be the result of LAZY-mediated and LAZY-independent processes, we conducted a genetic screen for mutations that could suppress the prostrate phenotype of atlazy1;2;3;4. The results of the screen and follow-up experiments generated evidence of a LAZY-independent gravitropism signaling pathway that involves protein complexes localized to ER–PM contact sites in gravity-sensing cells of the Arabidopsis inflorescence stem.

## Results

## Suppressor Screen for Recovery of Gravitropism in atlazy1;2;3;4.
We screened for suppressor mutations that produced upright inflorescence stems in atlazy1;2;3;4 plants. SI Appendix, Fig. S1 shows the posture of such a suppressor mutant in screening conditions. Its primary inflorescence stem was upright, though its branches drooped like lazy mutants (Fig. 1 A and B). The suppressor mutant had recovered some ability to perform typical negative gravitropism (upward bending) instead of the weak positive gravitropism of the atlazy1;2;3;4 plant (Fig. 1 C and D), which was previously characterized (18).

## slq1, a Missense Mutation That Suppresses the atlazy1;2;3;4 Phenotype.
A mapping strategy identified a missense mutation (S149F) in the At3g08570 gene (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) as the probable cause of the atlazy1;2;3;4 phenotype suppression. Mutating At3g08570 with the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing method in an atlazy1;2;3;4 plant (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) reproduced the suppressor mutant’s upright phenotype (Fig. 1F), confirming that the correct suppressing mutation had been identified. We named this mutation slq1, for suppressor of lazy quadruple mutant 1. Expressing At3g08570 (SLQ1) fused with eGFP under the control of its native promoter in the atlazy1;2;3;4;slq1 plant eliminated the suppression caused by slq1 (Fig. 1G). Both of the edited slq1 alleles in the atlazy1;2;3;4 background restored negative gravitropism comparable to that of the original atlazy1;2;3;4;slq1 inflorescence (Fig. 1 D and H). The slq1 alleles are recessive. A T-DNA insertion allele, slq1-1, and the two gene-edited slq1 mutants in a wild-type background did not alter the gravitropism response to a 90-degree reorientation apart from a possible delay in its onset (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Light cues did not influence the reverse gravitropism of the lazy quadruple mutant, its suppression (reversal) by the slq1 mutation, or the ability of the wild type to reorient completely within 8 h as shown by experiments performed in total darkness (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).
SLQ1 was previously classified as NRL10 in the RPT2 clade of the NPH3/RPT2 Like (NRL) family (25), which is named after the NONPHOTOTROPIC HYPOCOTYL 3 (26) and ROOT PHOTOTROPISM 2 (27) genes. No reports of a biological or biochemical function for NRL10 could be found. Because the name SLQ1 connotes some functional information, we prefer to use it over NRL10 in this work. SLQ1 possesses all three consensus domains typical of a NRL protein: a BTB/POZ domain that has been shown to participate in protein–protein interactions (28, 29), an NPH3 domain, and a C-terminal coiled-coil domain (UniProtKB protein Q9C9Z7).

## SLQ1 Functions in the Starch Sheath.
Gravity sensing in inflorescence stems is believed to begin in the endodermis, or starch sheath cells (30). Expressing SLQ1 predominantly in the starch sheath of an atlazy1;2;3;4;slq1 plant with the Actin Depolymerizing Factor 9 promoter (pADF9:SLQ1), a method Taniguchi et al. (13) previously demonstrated, reversed the suppressing effects of the slq1 mutation (Fig. 1I). This indicates that SLQ1 acts in the gravity-detecting cells.

## The slq1 Mutation Partially Restored the Gravity-Induced Auxin Gradient.
We tested the hypothesis that slq1 restored a normally oriented auxin gradient (more auxin on the lower side) to restore gravitropism in atlazy1;2;3;4 by measuring the expression of IAA5, an auxin-inducible gene that can be used to quantify a gravity-induced auxin gradient across the inflorescence stem (18, 31). A large difference in IAA5 mRNA levels between the upper and lower sides of the stem was observed in the wild type after 1 h of 90° gravistimulation (Fig. 1J). In atlazy1;2;3;4, the basal expression of IAA5 is only 20 to 25% of the wild-type level (18). Gravistimulation did not shift its distribution (Fig. 1J). However, introducing either of the two edited slq1 alleles caused a significant gravity-induced auxin gradient to form in the wild-type direction (more on the lower side) despite the lower levels (Fig. 1J). These results indicate that SLQ1 downregulates a process that is required for the establishment of a gravity-directed auxin gradient.

## SLQ1 Expresses in the Shoot Endodermis.
SLQ1 is expressed in the vasculature of the shoot and the root in light- and dark-grown seedlings (Fig. 2 A and E). Expression outside of the vasculature occurred in the upper hypocotyl (Fig. 2 A and E). SLQ1 expression was detected in the root cap (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) but not in the columella cells that roots use to detect the direction of the gravity vector (7). SLQ1 protein was localized at the ends of cells in the etiolated hypocotyl, particularly in cells forming the inner side of the apical hook (Fig. 2C). It was not possible to determine whether the protein was at the apical or basal ends, or both, of cells in a file. In cross-sections of inflorescence stems, the protein was observed in the gravity-detecting endodermis and occasionally in vascular tissues (Fig. 2 F–H), consistent with the results of an inflorescence cell-type transcriptome analysis (32). SLQ1 was also expressed in lateral root primordia (Fig. 2 I–K).

## SLQ1 Localization and Its Function.
The molecular construct used to show the expression patterns in Fig. 2 C–K (pSLQ1:SLQ1-eGFP) restored agravitropism to an atlazy1;2;3;4;slq1 plant (Fig. 1G), indicating SLQ1-eGFP is functional. Although it was not possible to study subcellular SLQ1-eGFP patterns in intact inflorescence stems, because the native expression level is too low and the endodermis too deep within the stem for confocal microscopy to resolve the fluorescence, some information could be obtained in hypocotyl cells. There, in focal planes that bisected the cell, SLQ1-eGFP typically appeared as small bodies that apparently crossed the cell wall (Fig. 3A), in some ways resembling plasmodesmata-marking proteins (33–35). The S149F suppressor mutation drastically changed the subcellular pattern to a diffuse distribution in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3B). Transient expression of wild-type SLQ1-eGFP in leaf epidermal cells of Nicotiana benthamiana also showed small bodies in the periphery of the cell (Fig. 3C) that, like in Arabidopsis, the S149F mutation converted into a diffuse signal in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3D).

SLQ1-eGFP consistently resided near the endoplasmic reticulum marker, ER-mk (36), but precise colocalization was not convincingly observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). To test for association between SLQ1 and plasmodesmata (PDs), we created an N-terminal translational fusion between eGFP and the PD-b-Glucanase1 (PdBG1) that Benitez-Alfonso et al. (37) previously used to create a fluorescent PD marker. Our N-terminal fusion, unlike their C-terminal fusion, labeled the ER lumen rather than PD when transiently expressed in N. benthamiana (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). SLQ1 speckles or bodies were consistently observed to overlap ER regions marked by mRFP-PdBG1 in optical planes near and parallel to the PM (Fig. 3 E and F). The SLQ1 bodies were largely absent from regions lacking ER. The results in Fig. 3 A–F indicate that SLQ1 may occur where the ER is very close to or contacting the PM, as is the case near PDs. Aniline blue (AB) binds to callose deposited in the cell wall at the neck region of a PD and is often used as a PD marker (38). Spots of AB were like SLQ1 bodies in size and number, but the two may not have overlapped or only occasionally overlapped (Fig. 3G). Thus, SLQ1 may accumulate at or near PDs but not all PDs. The legitimate PD marker (C-terminally tagged PdBG1) produced spots like SLQ1 spots, but the two did not precisely overlap (Fig. 3H). The callose binding protein PDCB1 has also been used to mark PDs (39). PDCB1-DsRed2 produced spots similar in size and number to AB, PdBG1, and SLQ1 but they also did not precisely overlap with SLQ1, or only rarely (Fig. 3I). These results do not support consistent colocalization of SLQ1 and PDs.

## SLQ1 Interactions and Effects on Subcellular Structures.
An optical plane through the cytoplasm parallel to the face of an epidermal cell in an Arabidopsis hypocotyl expressing pUBQ10:SLQ1-eGFP (an overexpression scenario) shows many large, bright SLQ1 spots (Fig. 4A). Orthogonal views created by compiling a Z-stack of parallel optical planes showed structures that appear to cross the cell wall and widen in the cytoplasm in a manner reminiscent of funnel-like PDs. However, the diameter of the SLQ1 structures could exceed 1 µm, which is an order of magnitude larger than the opening of funnel-like PDs (40). Movies S1 and S2 show a compiled Z-stack of these structures recorded from Arabidopsis hypocotyls stably expressing eGFP-tagged SLQ1. A similar result was obtained in N. benthamiana cells transiently expressing SLQ1 (Movie S3). We conclude that diffracted fluorescence emanating from highly concentrated SLQ1-eGFP sources near the PM is shaped by the point spread function (PSF) of the microscope optics (41) to produce the observed flare (Fig. 4B) and concentric circles (Movies S1–S3).

A computer model predicts that the N and C termini of SLQ1 are flexible (Fig. 4C) and therefore could be translationally tagged without disturbing the central conserved domains. We tagged SLQ1 proteins for bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) tests of the hypothesis that SLQ1 oligomerization produced the concentrated spots of fluorescence. We did not observe SLQ1–SLQ1 interaction when the two halves of the split Venus reporter were attached to the C-terminus (Fig. 4E). Instead, physical interaction (complemented fluorescence) occurred when one SLQ1 was tagged on the N terminus and another on its C-terminus. The head-to-tail orientation of the interaction could support oligomerization and condensation (42), which could produce the high-density source of fluorescence inferred from the confocal microscopy analysis. The S149F mutation nearly abolished the interaction when it was in the C terminally tagged (tail) protein (Fig. 4G), but not when it was only, or also, in the N terminally tagged (head) protein (Fig. 4 H and I). This result indicates that heterologous interfaces mediate the interaction, which may produce linear or cyclic structures (43). We propose that disrupting the head-to-tail SLQ1 oligomerization prevents the formation of SLQ1 condensate-like bodies (Fig. 3 A and B).
In other NRL family proteins such as NPH3, RPT2, NCH1, and potato NRL1, the BTB/POZ domains can mediate isologous homodimerization or heterodimerization (43–47). We examined the binding of three other NRL proteins to SLQ1. SI Appendix, Fig. S6 shows that C terminally tagged SLQ1 interacted with NPH3 and NCH1 regardless of which end of these proteins was tagged. The directional indifference of their interactions with SLQ1 is consistent with internal BTB domains mediating an isologous interaction rather than a head-to-tail heterologous arrangement (43). A third NRL protein, SETH6, was selected because coexpression analysis with ATTEDII software (48) showed a relationship between its gene and PDLP6, a PD-localized protein (49). This association seemed noteworthy because SLQ1 formed PD-like spots at the cell periphery (Fig. 3). C terminally tagged SLQ1 interacted with SETH6 regardless of which ends of the proteins were tagged (Fig. 4 J and K).

## SETH6 Is Functionally Homologous to SLQ1.
SETH6 (pSETH6:SETH6-venus) was observed in patches of the shoot epidermis (cotyledon and hypocotyl), in the root stele, and in guard cells of Arabidopsis seedlings (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). According to Shi et al. (32), SETH6 is expressed in the endodermis of inflorescence stems but at a level only 22% that of SLQ1 (SI Appendix, Table S1), which may be why a seth6 mutation did not suppress the agravitropism of atlazy1;2;3;4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8) like slq1. When we expressed SETH6 in the endodermis of atlazy1;2;3;4;slq1 plants, the prostrate phenotype of atlazy1;2;3;4 was restored (Fig. 5A). Thus, SETH6 can perform the function of SLQ1 when placed in the appropriate biological context. The normally straight pedicels that hold wild-type flowers at a 45° angle (Fig. 5B) drooped when SLQ1 was expressed under the control of the endodermis-specific promoter, pADF9:SLQ1 (Fig. 5C), and when SETH6 was generally overexpressed with the pUBQ10 promoter (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, SLQ1 and SETH6 produced similar overexpression phenotypes in seedlings, namely increased hypocotyl length and hyponasty that could be quantified by measuring cotyledon petiole angle (Fig. 5 E and F). Together, the results in Fig. 5 indicate that SETH6 functions similarly to SLQ1 or somehow exerts similar effects on a common pathway.

At the subcellular level, SETH6 formed many closely spaced small spots or specks in focal planes that pass through the cytoplasm, parallel to and near the PM in transfected N. benthamiana (Fig. 6A) cells. The same subcellular distribution was observed in stably transformed Arabidopsis hypocotyls and cotyledons (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Movies S4 and S5). The SETH6 specks did not colocalize well with the ER marker (Fig. 6A) or resemble the SLQ1 pattern (Figs. 3 E and F and 4A). However, when coexpressed, SLQ1-eGFP and SETH6-mRFP perfectly overlapped in a novel pattern (Fig. 6B). The SLQ1-SETH6 bodies were more irregularly shaped and less uniformly bright than bodies formed by SLQ1-GFP expressed alone. Together with the BiFC results in Fig. 4, these results indicate that SETH6 directly interacts with head-to-tail SLQ1 structures, and the interaction modifies the organization of a larger supramolecular entity.

Next, the relationship between the apparent SLQ1-SETH6 complex and the ER was investigated. SETH6-Venus, untagged SLQ1, and the ER lumen marker (35S:eGFP-PdBG1) were coexpressed in N. benthamiana (Fig. 6C). In focal planes through the cytoplasm parallel to the face of the PM, the ER morphology changed to match that of the SLQ1-SETH6 bodies (Fig. 6C). Because SLQ1 was not tagged in this experiment its expression could not be directly visualized, but its presence was evidenced by the SLQ1 effect on the SETH6 pattern (compare Fig. 6 A and C). Because ER is a core component of PDs, we tested whether the PD marker PDCB1-DsRed2 would colocalize with SLQ1-SETH6 complexes. Projections of Z-stacks revealed that every occurrence of transcellular PDCB1-DsRed2 signal (i.e. PDs) colocalized with SLQ1-SETH6 bodies (Fig. 6D). Movie S6 also shows this result. Thus, SLQ1-SETH6 bodies colocalize with ER near the PM, or ER–PM contact sites, including PDs.

## Discussion
Mutating multiple LAZY genes unmasks a weaker LAZY-independent gravitropism, which causes roots to bend up and shoots to bend down (18, 20, 24). An additional mutation in the SLQ1 gene restores a normally oriented inflorescence stem, even in a plant lacking four (Fig. 1) or even all five complete LAZY genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). No gene like SLQ1 had previously been associated with shoot gravitropism. The S149F amino acid change in SLQ1 that flips LAZY-independent gravitropism prevents head-to-tail binding of SLQ1, prevents a SLQ1-containing supramolecular complex forming at ER–PM contact sites including PDs, and disrupts its function. The SLQ1 complex probably also contains the SETH6 homolog, which interacts with SLQ1. If the direction-agnostic interaction between SLQ1 and SETH6 is mediated by their BTB/POZ domains, as has been shown for other BTB/POZ-containing proteins (29) including other NRL protein pairs (44–47), then SETH6 may decorate head-to-tail SLQ1 oligomers. If so, SETH6 subcellular distribution should adopt the shape of SLQ1 bodies when the two proteins are coexpressed. This was observed (Fig. 6B).
The diagram in Fig. 7A depicts SLQ1-SETH6 complexes functioning at ER–PM contact sites and adjacent to PDs. This interpretation is consistent with several results but is also subject to a caveat. Some of the data were acquired in the heterologous N. benthamiana system because fluorescence from the tissue in which they naturally function (endodermis) is too weak and distorted by the optical path length to resolve. In the heterologous system, the SLQ1 and SETH6 proteins were almost certainly expressed at much higher levels and in a different ratio than is natural. This could cause misleading or artifactual localization patterns. Also, some results were obtained in hypocotyl cells (e.g., Fig. 3 A and B), which may not be fully relevant to the natural sites of action, i.e., the statocytes in the inflorescence stem where SLQ1 functions (Fig. 1I). These potential pitfalls apparently did not greatly confound studies of LAZY1 that relied on the same methodologies (12) so we believe Fig. 7A depicts a reasonable hypothesis about the subcellular arrangement of SLQ1 and its interactors. Future studies may test the hypothesis and functional relevance of the SLQ1-SETH6 complexes by determining the effects of overexpressing SLQ1, SETH6, and both proteins together in the endodermis with the pADF9 promoter.
Interpretations of SLQ1 subcellular localization and signal pathway architectures that explain the phenotypes. (A) Images of plant cells expressing fluorescently tagged SLQ1, SETH6, and various markers show bodies containing SLQ1-SETH6 complexes apparently only at ER–PM contact sites, including plasmodesmata. (B) A parsimonious pathway model includes a LAZY-mediated mechanism for creating a normal auxin gradient (higher auxin at lower side of stem) that is negatively regulated by BRXL4 and TAC1, and a parallel, weaker, LAZY-independent pathway that SLQ1 inverts to move auxin in the opposite direction. An alternative model features the same LAZY-dependent pathway, a reverse pathway that directs auxin in the opposite direction, and a weaker normally oriented pathway that SLQ1 negatively regulates. (C) The pathways in B are redrawn in a cellular context using oval symbols as PM polarity tokens—PIN auxin efflux facilitators would be an example. The thickness of the arrows indicates the strength of the pathway. The Top panel shows that a LAZY-dependent pathway directs the polarity tokens to the lowest face of the cell following a reorientation of the organ. In a parallel pathway, SLQ1 prevents the token from reaching the lowest face of the cell by redirecting it to the uppermost. The Lower panel shows a variation in which two LAZY-independent pathways competitively move the tokens to opposite faces of the cell, with SLQ1 functioning to block the weaker pathway that is oriented in the direction of the gravity vector. These architectures are consistent with loss of LAZYs causing a weak reverse gravitropism in stems, and a weak normal gravitropism resulting from the additional loss of SLQ1.
ER–PM contact sites are increasingly being associated with physiological control and signaling mechanisms (50–55). Recently, ER–PM contact sites associated with PDs, resembling those demonstrated here, were shown to regulate the intercellular movement of small molecules (56), presumably including auxin. These findings raise the possibility that SLQ1-SETH6 complexes may influence the effect of PDs on intercellular auxin transport (57). The top diagram in Fig. 7B shows that LAZYs convert a gravity signal (stem displacement) into an auxin gradient that causes upward bending growth. BRXL4 and TAC1 negatively regulate this pathway (12, 58). Accordingly, neither tac1 nor brxl4 mutations altered the lazy quadruple mutant phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Parallel to the LAZY pathway is a weaker LAZY-independent pathway that SLQ1 reverses to produce an oppositely oriented auxin gradient and bending growth. When LAZYs and SLQ1 are both absent, the signal inversion does not occur and the upward bending (suppressor) phenotype develops.
The upper diagram in Fig. 7C uses cellular polarity tokens (for example, PIN auxin efflux proteins) to show how the two parallel pathways in a wild-type context combine to produce a net downward auxin efflux. The lower diagrams in Fig. 7 B and C depict a variation in which the reverse process (thin upward arrow) is separate from a second normally oriented pathway that SLQ1 inhibits (thicker downward arrow). In summary, Fig. 7 B and C show that SLQ1 may either switch the polarity of a signaling step upstream of the auxin redistribution process (upper diagrams) or inhibit a LAZY-independent pathway that operates parallel to a separate reverse pathway (lower diagrams). In either scenario, quantitative integration of the pathways would shape the time course of the reorientation response to determine the endpoint inclination of an organ.
Bending growth that determines an angle can generate tension in membranes, cell walls, and tissues. Tension can be sensed (proprioception) and initiate the reversal of growth differentials to produce organ straightening (59, 60). Therefore, a role for SLQ1-SETH6 complexes at ER–PM contact sites in proprioception could explain their effects on bending growth. Such a role would be consistent with the observed expression of SLQ1 and SETH6 in locations other than gravity-sensing statocytes such as the hypocotyl apex (Fig. 2) and apical layers of the root cap (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). SLQ1-dependent proprioception in these regions may integrate membrane tension into a growth rate sensing mechanism. Overexpression may affect this hypothetical sensing mechanism, leading to the observed long hypocotyls (Fig. 5 E and F). A SLQ1-dependent intracellular tension sensor could also be important at the very beginning of gravity vector detection, as settling statoliths may compress the ER according to older theories (61, 62), or somehow determine an angle of inclination (63) that guides bending growth.
The results summarized in Fig. 7 may be relevant only to the main inflorescence stem because the slq1 mutation did not restore altered branch angles in the atlazy1;2;3;4 mutant. The mechanisms that set branch angles may differ significantly from the mechanisms that align the main inflorescence stem because BRXL4 was shown to control the former but not the latter (12). If the mechanisms that guide growth of branches and the main stem both use NRL proteins, tests of RPT2 (At2g30520), NCH1 (At5g67385), NRL2 (At1g30440), and NRL28 (At5g48800) may be fruitful because our analysis (SI Appendix, Table S1) indicates that these four NRL family members are in the top 10 in terms of similarity to SLQ1 and endodermal expression level. While SETH6 is the fifth most like SLQ1 in terms of protein sequence (43% identical), its expression level is below the median of all 33 NRL genes in the dataset we used (32).
Mutating multiple LAZY genes made it possible to study an SLQ1-dependent pathway in apparent isolation. The reciprocal approach of mutating multiple NRL genes may make it possible to isolate the opposing LAZY-dependent pathway for deeper study. If successful, this approach may show that the great variation in stem, branch, and flower postures in nature is due to the relative strengths of the pathways in Fig. 7 being set differently across organs and species.

## Materials and Methods

## Plant Materials and Growth Conditions.
The wild type used in this study was Columbia-0 obtained from Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre. The mutants for Arabidopsis LAZY homologs were obtained as described in refs. 18 and 20. atlazy1;2;3;4;slq1, atlazy1;2;3;4;6;slq1, atlazy1;2;3;4;seth6 and atlazy1;2;3;4;brxl4 were created by CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing (64) in atlazy1;2;3;4 or atlazy1;2;3;4;6. The T-DNA insertion line, GK_699F04, for SLQ1 (At3g08570) that we designate as slq1-1 in SI Appendix, Fig. S2 was obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center.

## Suppressor Screen.
The seeds of atlazy1;2;3;4 mutagenized with ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) were planted and selfed to obtain M2 population. About 4000 M1 lines were screened, and seven lines showed recovery of gravitropism in inflorescence stems. The BC1S1 mapping population was obtained by backcrossing these lines with atlazy1;2;3;4 and subsequent selfing. Tissue samples were collected and pooled from the more than 80 BC1S1 plants showing the suppressor phenotype. DNA samples prepared from the samples using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN) were subjected to next-generation sequencing. Whole genome sequencing was done by UWBC DNA sequencing facility (Madison, WI) using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina). 10 million paired-end reads with 150 bp length were aimed. The average coverage achieved was 21.4. Analysis with artMAP software (65) identified putative candidate causal mutations in the suppressor on the short arm of chromosome 3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). In this region, there were only two missense mutations, one in gene model At3g06530 and the other in At3g08570. These two genes were separately tested by mutating each with the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing method in atlazy1;2;3;4 plants. The resulting mutations in At3g08570, described in SI Appendix, Fig. S1C, reproduced the upright phenotype in atlazy1;2;3;4 (Fig. 1F).

## Inflorescence Gravitropism Measurement.
Gravitropism of inflorescence stems was examined as described before (18) with minor modifications. An apical 9-cm section was obtained from the inflorescence stem grown up to 9 to 14 cm and the bottom end was placed in a 0.2-mL PCR tube containing half-strength MS media supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) Sucrose. The stem section and media were sealed in the tube by wrapping Parafilm M (Pechiney Plastic Packaging). The prepared samples were stood upright in a custom tube rack and left for 2 h in dim red light (4 to 5 µmol m−2 s−1; λ max 660 nm). The rack with samples was rotated by 90° and placed in front of a CCD camera (Marlin; Allied Vision Technologies) with its infrared filter removed and furnished with an 8-mm lens (M0814-MP; Computar). The imaging started right after the rotation. The samples were illuminated from the backside with infrared light-emitting diodes (λ max 940 nm). The tip angle of the inflorescence stem with a horizontal line was measured by using ImageJ software (66).

## Other Plant Measurements.
To measure hypocotyl length and cotyledon angle, seedlings were grown on full-strength MS agar plates (4.3 g/L MS salts [Sigma-Aldrich], and 1.2% bacto-agar, pH 5.7) containing 1% sucrose oriented vertically under 80 to 100 μmol m−2 s−1 constant white light at 22 °C. After growing for 7 d, the plates with seedlings were scanned on a flatbed scanner (Perfection V600; Epson). The scanned images were analyzed by using ImageJ software. The petiole angles of cotyledons were measured by drawing tangent lines at the upper ends of petioles. The averaged angle of the two tangent lines from a horizontal line were used as a petiole angle for the seedling. For the analyses of pUBQ10:SETH6-Venus T2 population, each individual seedling was genotyped to check for the pUBQ10:SETH6-Venus T-DNA by PCR. The primers used were shown in Dataset S1. Seedlings found to contain at least one copy of T-DNA were labeled pUBQ10:SETH6-Venus.

## Plasmid Construct and Generation of Transgenic Plants.
All promoters and genes were obtained by PCR with Phusion High Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) and subcloned into pGEM-T-Easy (Promega). The clones in which the sequences were confirmed were used in the following steps. The primers used are listed in Dataset S1. The plasmid constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens, GV3101. Arabidopsis plants were transformed using the floral dip method (67). Each plasmid construct was prepared as follows.
For promoter-reporter analysis, the pSLQ1:GUS construct was created. The promoter region (1.0 kb) of SLQ1, the entire intergenic region upstream of SLQ1, was amplified by PCR from Arabidopsis genomic DNA. The primers which amplified 5′ and 3′ terminals contained restriction sites for SalI and BamHI, respectively. The resultant fragments were subcloned and recloned into the SalI/BglII site in the binary vector, pBI101 (68).
To examine whether SLQ1 functions in the starch sheath, the pADF9:SLQ1 construct was created. The promoter region of ADF9 was amplified with primers containing KpnI site at 5′ end and genomic SLQ1 was amplified with the primers containing BglII site at 3′ end. pADF9 and genomic SLQ1 were fused by overlap extension PCR and recloned into the KpnI/BamHI site of pCAMBIA1300 (Cambia, Canberra, Australia).
To examine subcellular localization of SLQ1 and SETH6, the pSLQ1:SLQ1-eGFP and pSETH6:SETH6-Venus constructs were created. For the pSLQ1:SLQ1-eGFP construct, the SLQ1 genomic fragments and eGFP were cloned from Arabidopsis genomic DNA and from pEGAD (69), respectively. The SLQ1 genomic fragments from the promoter to right before the stop codon, the eGFP sequence, and 3′UTR of SLQ1 were amplified separately with the primers designed to have overlaps with each other and these fragments were fused by overlap extension PCR. The primers which amplified 5′ and 3′ terminals contained restriction sites for KpnI and BamHI, respectively. The resultant fragment was recloned into the KpnI/BamHI site in the binary vector, pCAMBIA1300. For the pSETH6:SETH6-Venus construct, the SETH6 genomic fragment from 5′ intergenic region (entire intergenic region, ca 1.3 kb) to 3′ end just before stop codon was amplified with KpnI site at 5′ end. The Venus sequence was amplified from pDR5:3xVenus-N7 (70). The entire 3’UTR was amplified with BamHI site at 3′ end. All three fragments were fused by overlap extension PCR. The resultant PCR product was recloned into the KpnI/BamHI site of pCAMBIA1300.
To examine subcellular localization in N. benthamiana by transient expression, the 35S:eGFP-SLQ1, pUBQ10:SLQ1-eGFP, pUBQ10:SLQ1-mRFP, 35S:eGFP-PDBG1, 35S:PDBG1-eGFP, 35S:mRFP-PDBG1, 35S:PdBG1-mRFP, pUBQ10:SETH6-Venus, and 35S:SETH6-mRFP constructs, were created. For the 35S:eGFP-SLQ1 construct, the genomic SLQ1 fragment was recloned into the EcoRI/BamHI site of pEGAD. For the pUBQ10:SLQ1-eGFP construct, the UBQ10 (At4g05320) promoter, genomic SLQ1, and eGFP amplified from pEGAD vector were fused by overlap extension PCR and recloned into the StuI/BamHI site of pEGAD. The pUBQ10:SLQ1-mRFP construct was also created in the same way. The mRFP fragment was amplified from pSAT6-RFP-C1 vector. The fused fragment was recloned into the PacI/BamHI site of pEGAD. The subcellular marker, 35S:eGFP-PdBG1, was created by inserting the genomic PdBG1 into the EcoRI/BamHI site of pEGAD. For the 35S:mRFP-PdBG1 and 35S:PdBG1-mRFP constructs, mRFP and PdBG1 were fused and recloned into the AgeI/BamHI site of pEGAD. For the pUBQ10:SETH6-Venus construct, the SETH6-Venus was amplified from the pSETH6:SETH6-Venus construct and the promoter UBQ10 was amplified as stated above. These fragments were fused and recloned into the PacI/BamHI site of pEGAD. For the 35S:SETH6-mRFP construct, the genomic SETH6 and the mRFP from pSAT-RFP-C1 were fused, and recloned into AgeI/BamHI site of pEGAD. To see the effect of SLQ1, the 35S:SLQ1 construct was also used. The genomic SLQ1 was amplified and recloned into AgeI/BamHI site of pEGAD.
To test whether SETH6 can function in the starch sheath, the pADF9:SETH6-Venus construct was also created. The promoter ADF9 and SETH6-Venus were amplified as stated above with KpnI site at 5′ end and BglII site at 3′ end and fused. The resultant fragment was recloned into the KpnI/BamHI site of pCAMBIA1300.
To generate the CRISPR/Cas9-edited lines, the gRNA adaptors (Dataset S1) were inserted into the egg cell-specific promoter-controlled CRISPR/Cas9 vector, pHEE401E (64). The sequences of the gRNA adaptors were selected using CRISPR-PLANT v2 (71) so that Cleaved Amplified Polymorphic Sequences assay can be applied for screening of the targeted mutations.
For the BiFC assays, the plasmid vectors, pDOE-05 and pDOE-06 (72), were used. The genomic SLQ1 was amplified with the NcoI and SpeI sites and recloned into NcoI/SpeI site of pDOE-05 and pDOE-06 to express SLQ1-NmVn210. The genomic SLQ1, NCH1, and NPH3 were also amplified with primers enabling In-Fusion seamless cloning (Takara Bio USA Inc, San Jose, USA), and the fragments were recloned into SanDI (KflI) site of pDOE-05 and pDOE-06 by using In-Fusion® Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara Bio USA Inc.), according to the manufacturer’s instruction to express fusion proteins with CVen210.

## Relative Gene Expression Level.
The expression level of IAA5 was analyzed mostly as described in Yoshihara and Spalding (18). Inflorescence stems grown to 8 to 11 cm were used. After 1 h of gravistimulation as in the time course imaging experiments described above, a stem section ~0.5 to 4.0 cm below the apical meristem was cut into upper and lower halves along the longitudinal axis under a dissection microscope. 10 to 11 tissue samples were pooled per genotype and time point. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and RNase-free DNase (Qiagen). RT-qPCR was carried out using the 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with the Luna® Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (New England BioLabs). Triplicate RT-qPCR assays were performed for each of four independent biological replicates. ACTIN8 (ACT8) was used as an internal control. Relative expression of IAA5 was calculated using Pfaffl’s model (73). The primers used were as described by Taniguchi et al. (31).

## Site-Directed Mutagenesis.
To introduce S149F mutation in the SLQ1 sequence, site-directed mutagenesis was conducted using the Phusion site-directed mutagenesis kit (Finnzymes), according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The primer sets used are in Dataset S1. The subcloned fragments in pGEM-T-Easy obtained as stated above were subjected to the site-directed mutagenesis and recloned into the same binary vectors used for the wild type of counterpart.

## Histochemical Staining.
Seedlings were stained in the solution [100 mM sodium phosphate buffer pH 7.0, 0.1% (v/v) Triton-X-100, 1 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 1 mM potassium ferricyanide, 1 mM X-gluc], vacuum infiltrated for 20 min, and incubated at 37 °C for overnight. Chlorophyll was removed in 70% (v/v) ethanol at 4 °C.

## A. tumefaciens–Mediated Transient Expression.
N. benthamiana were injected with A. tumefaciens strains GV3101 harboring expression vectors as described previously (17). Bacterial cells of overnight cultures were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in deionized distilled water. The resuspended cells were injected with a needleless syringe into the abaxial side of the leaf. For coexpression of multiple genes, equal volumes of resuspended cells were mixed for injection. The injected leaves were left attached to the plant until observation (42 to 48 h). For aniline blue staining, aniline blue was injected into the leaves about 15 min before observation.

## Microscopy for Localization and Colocalization.
Confocal microscopy was performed on a Zeiss LSM 780 laser scanning confocal microscope. Optics employed were a Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 M27 lens, and an LD C-Apochromat 40×/1.1 W Korr M27 water immersion lens. The wave lengths of the excitation laser lines were 405 nm (for aniline blue), 458 nm (for mTurquoise2), 488 nm (for eGFP and chlorophyll), 514 nm (for Venus and mVenus), 543 nm (for mRFP and DsRed2), and 561 nm (for PI). Channel mode detection was used and the detection windows were 410 to 524 nm (for aniline blue), 463 to 520 nm (for mTurquoise2), 500 to 550 nm (for eGFP), 519 to 588 nm (for Venus), 520 to 620 nm (for mVenus), 582 to 754 nm (for mRFP and DsRed2), 600 to 721 nm (for chlorophyll), and 578 to 718 nm (for PI). GUS staining was observed on an Olympus BX60. To quantify overlapping fluorescence of different wavelengths, the Mander’s overlap coefficient was calculated as previously described (74).

## PI Staining.
3-d-old seedlings were incubated in water containing 10 μg/mL PI (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 to 10 min for root observation and about 30 min for hypocotyl observation.

## Statistics and Reproducibility.
Gravitropism of inflorescence stems was presented as the mean ± SEM. For IAA5 expression, two-sided paired Student’s t tests were performed using Excel (Microsoft 365). Welch’s t tests were performed using R (4.3.2.) on RStudio. Statistical significance was considered as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

## Structural Modeling.
The structural modeling of SLQ1 protein was performed using AlphaFold2 with default setting (75). The model was colored by pLDDT score from blue (high confidence) to yellow, and to red (low confidence) using UCSF ChimeraX (76).

## Figure Fig. 7.: Interpretations of SLQ1 subcellular localization and signal pathway architectures that explain the phenotypes. (A) Images of plant cells expressing fluorescently tagged SLQ1, SETH6, and various markers show bodies containing SLQ1-SETH6 complexes apparently only at ER–PM contact sites, including plasmodesmata. (B) A parsimonious pathway model includes a LAZY-mediated mechanism for creating a normal auxin gradient (higher auxin at lower side of stem) that is negatively regulated by BRXL4 and TAC1, and a parallel, weaker, LAZY-independent pathway that SLQ1 inverts to move auxin in the opposite direction. An alternative model features the same LAZY-dependent pathway, a reverse pathway that directs auxin in the opposite direction, and a weaker normally oriented pathway that SLQ1 negatively regulates. (C) The pathways in B are redrawn in a cellular context using oval symbols as PM polarity tokens—PIN auxin efflux facilitators would be an example. The thickness of the arrows indicates the strength of the pathway. The Top panel shows that a LAZY-dependent pathway directs the polarity tokens to the lowest face of the cell following a reorientation of the organ. In a parallel pathway, SLQ1 prevents the token from reaching the lowest face of the cell by redirecting it to the uppermost. The Lower panel shows a variation in which two LAZY-independent pathways competitively move the tokens to opposite faces of the cell, with SLQ1 functioning to block the weaker pathway that is oriented in the direction of the gravity vector. These architectures are consistent with loss of LAZYs causing a weak reverse gravitropism in stems, and a weak normal gravitropism resulting from the additional loss of SLQ1.
