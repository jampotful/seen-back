Modulation of Root Hydrotropism and Recovery From Drought by MIZ1-like Genes in Tomato.
Wexler Y, Kiere Y, Sobol G, Nuriel R, Azoulay-Portal S, Cohen A, Toporik H, Pasmanik-Chor M, Finkler A, Shkolnik D. (2025). Plant, cell & environment. . doi:10.1111/pce.15260. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Modulation of Root Hydrotropism and Recovery From Drought by MIZ1‐like Genes in Tomato

## Abstract
ABSTRACTDrought limits crop performance worldwide. Plant roots' ability to grow toward moisture, termed hydrotropism, is considered one strategy for optimizing water recruitment from the growth medium. Based on the sequence of the hydrotropism‐indispensable MIZ1 protein in Arabidopsis thaliana, we identify hydrotropism and drought‐responsive genes in tomato. We utilized CRISPR/Cas9 genome‐editing technology for targeted mutagenesis of three hydrotropism‐associated loci (MIZ1‐like) in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum). We show that the three tomato MIZ1‐like genes are drought‐responsive and two of them are hydrostimulation‐responsive. Examination of the root hydrotropic response of triple and double mutants indicated the gene SlMIZ1‐1 as indispensable for tomato root hydrotropism. Moreover, expression of the SlMIZ1‐1 gene in the Arabidopsis miz1 mutant effectively complemented the lost MIZ1 functionality, including root hydrotropic bending and generation of hydrotropic Ca2+ signals. Transcriptome analysis of hydrostimulated tomato root tips under control gravity and continuous clinorotation conditions was performed to identify gravitropism‐ and hydrotropism‐responsive genes. This analysis suggested the involvement of ethylene and ABA signalling in modulating the interplay between hydrotropism and gravitropism. Unveiling the molecular mechanisms that underlie hydrotropism and drought response holds great potential for improving crop performance under limiting water availability due to global climate changes.

## Introduction
Drought is a major constraint in agriculture, limiting crop growth, development, yield and product quality in arid and semiarid regions (Dietz, Zörb, and Geilfus 2021; Santini et al. 2022). The frequency, duration and intensity of drought episodes are on the rise, and their seasonal timing is becoming less predictable due to global climate change (IPCC 2023). As the major interface with the soil, the root system plays a vital role in maintaining plant growth and development under various conditions, including response and adjustment to drought (Amtmann, Bennett, and Henry 2022). The architecture of the root system is profoundly affected by moisture distribution in the soil (Dinneny 2019; Fromm 2019). Our knowledge regarding the signalling pathways and genes that function to modulate root growth and development in response to spatial drought conditions and optimize the plant's water recruitment is fragmented.
Under natural soil conditions, roots encounter heterogeneous water distribution that requires adjustments in root growth and development to optimize the recruitment of water. Root parts that face a damper soil develop more lateral roots and root hairs in the direction of the water source, a phenomenon termed hydropatterning (Bao et al. 2014; Feng et al. 2016; Giehl and von Wirén 2018). A subclass of root hydropatterning is a phenomenon termed xerobranching, by which lateral root formation in root parts that are exposed to dry soil or air patches is repressed by the activity of the abiotic stress‐related hormone abscisic acid (ABA) (Orman‐Ligeza et al. 2018). Moreover, to optimize water recruitment when the root faces water deficiency in the top soil layers, deeper growth is enhanced, at a reduced angle with respect to the gravity vector, a phenomenon termed xerotropism (Feng et al. 2016; Rellán‐Álvarez, Lobet, and Dinneny 2016). In rice, this deeper root growth has been described as a drought‐avoidance strategy that is modulated by the growth‐related hormone auxin, which negatively regulates the expression of the DEEPER ROOTING 1 (DRO1) gene (Uga et al. 2013). DRO1 has been suggested to adjust root growth downwards by promoting cell elongation, which is attenuated at auxin‐accumulation sites, as demonstrated in the lower root side of gravistimulated roots (Uga et al. 2013). Additional evidence that auxin signalling functions in xerotropic growth was provided by the lack of a xerotropic response in the tir1 mutant, in which the auxin receptor F‐Box protein TRANSPORT INHIBITOR 1 (TIR1) does not function (Dharmasiri, Dharmasiri, and Estelle 2005; Rellán‐Álvarez, Lobet, and Dinneny 2016). Additional potential xerotropism‐related genes in rice were found among quantitative trait loci that were associated with deep root growth under water‐deficit conditions (Courtois et al. 2009; Lou et al. 2015; Wade et al. 2015). Together, xerotropism and xerobranching can be considered complementary features of the plant's strategy to optimize water recruitment under conditions of spatial drought in the growth medium, by promoting deeper rooting into damper soil, while adjusting the root branching accordingly.
Another trait that roots utilize to optimize water recruitment from the soil is their ability to change their growth angle with respect to the water potential gradient to grow toward a moisture source, a phenomenon termed hydrotropism (Shkolnik and Fromm 2016; Dietrich 2018; Fromm 2019). To achieve hydrotropic growth, roots are often compelled to resist the strong default growth toward the earth's centre of gravity, an auxin‐governed tropic growth termed gravitropism (Kaneyasu et al. 2007; Shkolnik and Fromm 2016; Shkolnik et al. 2016; Wexler, Schroeder, and Shkolnik 2024). The interplay between hydrotropism and gravitropism is modulated by crosstalk between the signalling pathways of hormones, including auxin, ABA and cytokinins, as well as secondary messengers such as the calcium ion (Ca2+) and reactive oxygen species (Krieger et al. 2016; Dietrich et al. 2017; Shkolnik et al. 2018; Chang et al. 2019; Wexler, Schroeder, and Shkolnik 2024). Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms in the root that underlie the hydrotropism‐related gravitropism‐resisting capabilities remain obscure.
Arabidopsis thaliana has been used as a major model plant to elucidate the mechanisms underlying root hydrotropism (Shkolnik and Fromm 2016; Dietrich 2018; Wexler, Schroeder, and Shkolnik 2024). In Arabidopsis, the DUF617‐containing protein MIZ1 (At2g41660) has been found indispensable for root hydrotropism (Kobayashi et al. 2007). In this context, MIZ1 was later shown to be required for generating phloem‐conveyed root tip‐to‐elongation zone cytosolic Ca2+ ([Ca2+]cyt) signals upon hydrostimulation, achieved by direct inhibition of Ca2+ influx from the cytosol to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen by the Ca2+‐ATPase isoform ECA1 (Shkolnik et al. 2018); this was consistent with previous research demonstrating the involvement of Ca2+ signalling in root hydrotropism in pea (Takano et al. 1995). Analysis of the Arabidopsis genome revealed 12 genes containing the DUF617 domain, but only MIZ1 was characterized as indispensable for root hydrotropism (Kobayashi et al. 2007). Furthermore, a miz1‐1 mutant was reported to be drought‐sensitive, while MIZ1 overexpression improved drought tolerance in Arabidopsis (Iwata et al. 2013). In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), 14 MIZ1‐like DUF617‐containing genes were identified, 3 of which gave the highest identity scores to MIZ1: Solyc04g016060, Solyc10g080060 and Solyc10g080050 (Li et al. 2023). Furthermore, Li et al. (2023) demonstrated induction of the ABA biosynthesis‐related gene ABA4 in the side of the root that experiences lower water potential and suggested that ABA accumulation on this side promotes cell wall acidification and subsequent enhanced cell division and expansion, leading to root tip bending toward moisture. Nevertheless, the involvement of MIZ1‐like genes in tomato roots' hydrotropic response has yet to be fully investigated. Here we used the Arabidopsis miz1 mutant to examine the possible function of tomato MIZ1‐like genes in root hydrotropism and further characterize the relationship to the tomato drought response. We present transcriptome analysis of tomato root tip transcriptomes in response to hydrostimulation under control gravity conditions and under clinorotation to identify genes that play a role in the interplay between hydrotropism and gravitropism.
It is predicted that global climate change will cause more frequent and prolonged drought periods. Therefore, elucidation of the identity and function of genes and signalling components that maintain integrative modulation of root growth and development under drought conditions has great potential to provide efficient approaches and technologies for improving crop performance under these less‐than‐optimal conditions.

## Materials and Methods

## Plant Materials, Growth and Hydrostimulation Assays
Tomato (S. lycopersicum) M82 and A. thaliana Col‐0 plants were used in this research. The miz1 mutant (SALK_076560) was obtained from the Arabidopsis Stock Centre in Columbus, OH. For root‐hydrostimulation assay and seedling imaging, Arabidopsis seeds were surface‐sterilized as previously described (Shkolnik and Bar‐Zvi 2008) and tomato seeds were washed with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 2 min, treated with 3% (v/v) household bleach supplemented with 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 for 15 min, and washed thoroughly with sterile deionized water. Seedlings were germinated and grown on 1% (w/v) agar‐solidified 0.25× MS salts (Murashige and Skoog 1962) medium. CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing was used to mutagenize three MIZ1‐like genes in tomato (Solyc04g016060, Solyc10g080050 and Solyc10g080060) to create two allelic Slmiz1 x 3 plants. CRISPR‐P v2.0 (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) was used to design gene‐specific guide RNAs (gRNAs). A triple gRNA‐expression cassette was introduced into the BsaI sites of pSAT4 vector (Tzfira et al. 2005) followed by PCR amplification and introduction into AvrII sites of pMR284 vector (Sugano et al. 2020). The pMR284‐Slmiz1 x 3 vector was introduced into 14‐day‐old M82 tomatoes via Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 as described previously (McCormick 1991). Root‐ and shoot‐system‐generating plants grown from calli on kanamycin‐selection plates were moved to soil and genotyped using primers for Cas9 and for the gRNA‐expression cassette. T1 seeds were collected and self‐crossed, and T3 plants were back‐crossed with wild‐type M82 plants to obtain mutant plants without Cas9. F1 seeds were collected and self‐crossed, and F2 seedlings were genotyped and further used for hydrotropism and drought‐recovery assays. Root‐hydrostimulation assays using a CaCl2/dry chamber and sorbitol were conducted in square Petri dishes (120 mm × 120 mm × 17 mm) as described previously (Takahashi et al. 2002; Krieger et al. 2016). A silica gel/dry chamber was constructed by placing 100 mm × 50 mm × 5 mm agar‐solidified H2O gel (control) or 3 g of silica gel on the bottom of the chamber. Root tip images were captured using a Nikon D7100 camera equipped with an AF‐S DX Micro NIKKOR 85 mm f/3.5 G ED VR lens (Nikon). Solyc04g016060 (SlMIZ1‐1)‐, Solyc10g080050 (SlMIZ1‐3)‐ and Solyc10g080060 (SlMIZ1‐2)‐expressing (separately) miz1 Arabidopsis under the regulation of its native promoter and terminator (miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1/SlMIZ1‐2/SlMIZ1‐3) were created by amplifying the genes' coding sequences from the M82 genome (the genes' genomic structure is a single exon; Supporting Information S6: Figure S2a), MIZ1 1926‐bp promoter and 1284‐bp terminator regions, respectively, from the Col‐0 genome using Q5 DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs). The MIZ1‐like tomato genes were introduced into BglII and KpnI sites of pSAT4, the MIZ1 promoter into AgeI and BglII sites, and the MIZ1 terminator into KpnI and NotI sites. To create SlMIZ1‐1‐overexpressing Arabidopsis plants, the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter was introduced into NcoI and KpnI sites of SlMIZ1‐1 harbouring pSAT4. Constructs were digested from pSAT4 using I‐SceI and introduced into pPZP‐RCS2 bialaphos‐resistance gene (BAR)‐harbouring vector (Goderis et al. 2002), followed by introduction into A. tumefaciens GV3101, and floral dip transformation into Arabidopsis (Clough and Bent 1998). BAR‐tolerant seedlings were selected and genotyped using gene‐specific primers (Supporting Information S5: Table S5), and T2 and T3 seeds were used in the described experiments. Root tip curvature was measured using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/). Root waviness was quantified by measuring the waviness index using ImageJ Fiji software with the SNT plugin (https://imagej.net/plugins/snt/). The software returns a value of ‘straightness’, therefore, the ‘waviness’ value was calculated as waviness = 1 − straightness. NES‐YC3.6‐expressing miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1 plants were obtained by crossing with NES‐YC3.6 expressing Col‐0 (Krebs et al. 2012). NES‐YC3.6 signal was visualized using the Leica SP8 confocal microscopy system with a 10× objective Acquisition parameters were as follows: master gain was always set between 670 and 720, with a digital gain of 1, excitation at 458 nm (15%) and emission at two channels: 473–505 nm and 526–550 nm. Bright‐field imaging was performed with the TPMT module. Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis was conducted with Fiji (ImageJ 1.51 C) software. FRET/CFP values were calculated using raw region of interest intensity values and RGB pseudo colour images were created using PixFRET 1.5.0 ImageJ plugin.

## Protein Structure Analysis
Protein sequences were obtained from the Sol Genomics Network (https://solgenomics.net/) and secondary structure was analyzed using the UCSF ChimeraX programme (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax/).

## Drought‐Recovery Assay
Wild‐type (M82) and Slmiz1 x 3‐1 tomato seedlings were germinated and grown in soil‐filled pots (8 cm × 8 cm × 5 cm) for 3 weeks, followed by normal irrigation (control) or no irrigation for 2 weeks before re‐irrigation. Plant imaging and quantification of root and shoot weights were performed after 16 h of re‐irrigation.

## RT‐qPCR Analysis
Total RNA was isolated from the control, and 4 h‐hydrostimulated 5‐mm primary root tips of 5‐day‐old tomato seedlings using the ZR Plant RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research), and total cDNA was synthesized using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The reaction mixture was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions with random primers, and supplemented with 1 µg of total RNA. PCR mixtures (10 µL), containing 5 µL TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Clontech; Takara), 500 nM forward and reverse primers designed to amplify 80–120 bp of the genes of interest, and 20 ng cDNA were subjected to the Rotor‐Gene Q 5‐Plex HRM real‐time PCR system (Qiagen) using the default programme. The SlActin2 (Solyc11g005330) gene was used as an endogenous control. Relative quantification data were analyzed by Rotor‐Gene Q Pure Detection software V2.3.5 (Qiagen).

## RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis
RNA was isolated from 5‐mm root tips (from apex to elongation zone) of 5‐day‐old tomato seedlings that were control‐treated or hydrostimulated for 4 h under control gravity conditions (static) or clinorotation conditions (three independent replicates, total n = 30) for 3.5 h (see Section 2.1). Isolation was performed using the ZR Plant RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research). RNA sequencing was conducted at The Mantoux Institute for Bioinformatics of the Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Centre for Personalized Medicine, Weizmann Institute of Science, Rehovot, Israel. Illumina RNA sequencing was performed in triplicate (Illumina Hiseq. 2500; Illumina TruSeq RNA library Preparation Kit v2), resulting in 50‐bp reads. Sequencing libraries were prepared using LIB_PREP_PROTOCOL. READ_TYPE reads were sequenced on NUM_LANES lane(s) of an Illumina ILLUMINA_INSTRUMENT. The output was ~18 million reads per sample. Mapping and alignment were performed using TopHat version 2.1.1 and Bowtie2 version 2.5.1 (reference genome SL4.0 and annotation ITAG4.0), followed by gene counting performed by HTseq‐count version 2.0.3. Values above 10 were used for normalization and gene‐expression analysis (DESeq. 2 version 3.17). Partek Genomics Suite was used for further analysis (version 7.20.083). Venn diagrams were created with Venny tool (http://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html; Oliveros 2015). Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org) (Wickham, 2016). Genes were plotted based on their log2 fold change (FC) in expression and their −log10 p value. The annotated data points represent the 10 genes with the largest distance from the origin of axes based on their Manhattan distance (|ΔX| + |ΔY|). Gene name annotations represent the name of the Arabidopsis gene with the highest protein similarity according to the TAIR website Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Functional classification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed with the DAVID Gene Functional Classification tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The tool was used with multiple database sources of functional annotations. Enriched significant classifications were selected based on p ≤ 0.05. Enrichment of the upregulated and downregulated DEGs was classified by biological process, molecular function and cellular component. Tomato gene annotations were extracted from the Sol Genomics Network (https://solgenomics.net/).

## Statistical Analysis
Data were analyzed using JMP software version pro16 and Microsoft Excel 2019 ToolPak.

## Results

## Identification of MIZ1 Ortholog From Tomato
To identify orthologs of Arabidopsis MIZ1 in tomato, we subjected the complete Arabidopsis MIZ1 amino acid sequence to the Sol Genomics Network protein BLAST (BLASTp) (https://solgenomics.net/). Fourteen MIZ1‐like proteins were identified as encoded by the tomato genome (Supporting Information S6: Figure S1). Among them, three had the highest identity scores (% ID), Solyc10g080060 (62.29%), Solyc04g016060 (59.83%) and Solyc10g080050 (51.03%) (Table 1; Figure 1 and Supporting Information S6: Figure S1), as previously reported (Li et al. 2023). Notably, Kobayashi et al. (2007) demonstrated that a Gly235Glu mutation positioned in the hydrotropism‐indispensable domain of MIZ1, DUF617 (Figure 1a), completely arrests the root hydrotropic response. Hence, to identify the tomato MIZ1 ortholog that is most functionally related to MIZ1 from Arabidopsis, we utilized the BLASTp analysis for DUF617 amino acid sequence comparison. Interestingly, according to this analysis, DUF617 of Solyc04g016060 had the highest score, followed by Solyc10g080050 and Solyc10g080060 with less significant scores (Table 1). We validated these data with the Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform (MAFFT) programme (version 7) (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/index.html) for searches based on the full MIZ1‐like or DUF617 amino acid sequences. Solyc04g016060 consistently showed the highest identity score for both the complete protein and its DUF617, whereas Solyc10g080050 and Solyc10g080060 showed lower values for both the complete protein and DUF617 (Table 1). Collectively, according to amino acid sequence homology, Solyc04g016060 was more similar to Arabidopsis MIZ1 than the other two MIZ1‐like proteins and harboured a DUF617 that could be more functionally related to DUF617 of Arabidopsis MIZ1. We classified the MIZ1‐like protein‐encoding genes as SlMIZ1‐1 (Solyc04g016060), SlMIZ1‐2 (Solyc10g080060) and SlMIZ1‐3 (Solyc10g080050).

Finally, we compared the tertiary structure of the proteins encoded by the three MIZ1‐like genes and MIZ1 from Arabidopsis using the UCSF ChimeraX molecular visualization programme (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax/). Interestingly, DUF617 of all proteins formed a soluble β‐barrel structure (Figure 1c), also known as outer‐membrane β‐barrel, which is found on the periphery of gram‐negative bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts (Franklin et al. 2018; Day, Inoue, and Theg 2019; Solan et al. 2021). Notably, all proteins also contained various unstructured soluble domains (Figure 1c). Assessing these interesting structural features offers a new viewpoint and approach that could help in elucidating the elusive biochemical functions of DUF617 and MIZ1.

## Mutagenesis of MIZ1‐like Genes in Tomato Impairs Root Hydrotropism
To further assess the involvement of the three MIZ1‐like genes in the tomato root hydrotropic response, we utilized the CRISPR/Cas9 system to inactivate them (Supporting Information S6: Figure S2 and Section 2), creating Slmiz1 x 3 triple mutants. No visible morphological differences between 5‐day‐old triple mutant and wild‐type (M82) seedlings were observed (Figure 2a). Next, we examined the hydrotropic phenotype of roots of wild‐type and two allelic Slmiz1 x 3 seedlings using the common CaCl2/dry chamber root‐hydrostimulation assay (Figure 2b) (Takahashi et al. 2002; Shkolnik et al. 2016; Krieger et al. 2016). Roots of 5‐day‐old Slmiz1 x 3‐1 and Slmiz1 x 3‐2 seedlings achieved curvatures of (mean ± SD) 29.7 ± 4.2 and 27.6 ± 5.1 degrees, respectively, 5 h post‐hydrostimulation, whereas roots of wild‐type seedlings bent 44.24 ± 13.5 degrees after the same treatment duration, and achieved a mean curvature of 78.9 ± 7.2 degrees after 7 h of treatment, appearing fully bent (Figure 2c,d). No difference in root growth rate was measured among the three genotypes (Figure 2e). These data indicate the involvement of at least one of the three MIZ1‐like genes in the root hydrotropic response. By back‐crossing the mutant with the wild‐type, we were able to isolate a double mutant in which both SlMIZ1‐3 and SlMIZ1‐2 were mutated (Slmiz1 x 2), and further examine its root hydrotropic response. Roots of Slmiz1 x 2 had a curvature of 81.8 ± 13.08 degrees after 5 h of hydrostimulation, which did not differ significantly from the wild‐type (Supporting Information S6: Figure S3), strongly suggesting that SlMIZ1‐1 is indispensable for root hydrotropism in tomato.

In addition, we analyzed the relative transcript levels of the tomato MIZ1‐like genes in control and hydrostimulated root tips of wild‐type seedlings. The transcript levels of both SlMIZ1‐1 and SlMIZ1‐2 rose in response to hydrostimulation by 1.52 ± 0.15‐fold (normalized to the control, which was set to 1) and 2.85 ± 1.04‐fold, respectively (Figure 2f). The induced expression of SlMIZ1‐2 in response to hydrostimulation was consistent with a previous report (Li et al. 2023). Interestingly, the transcript level of SlMIZ1‐3 was not induced in response to hydrostimulation (0.9 ± 0.17‐fold) (Figure 2f). Collectively, these data suggest that both SlMIZ1‐2 and SlMIZ1‐1 are involved in the molecular mechanism that underlies the tomato root hydrotropic response.

## Arabidopsis miz1 Root Phenotype Is Rescued by Expression of SlMIZ1‐1

## Root Hydrotropism
Arabidopsis MIZ1 is indispensable for root hydrotropism (Kobayashi et al. 2007; Shkolnik et al. 2018). To examine the effect of the expression of each tomato MIZ1‐like gene on the ahydrotropic Arabidopsis miz1 phenotype, we expressed each gene separately in the miz1 mutant and tested the hydrotropic response. Hydrostimulation of roots of three lines of miz1 expressing SlMIZ1‐1 under the regulation of the native MIZ1 promoter (miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1.1/2/3) or the constitutive CaMV 35S promoter (miz1/35S::SlMIZ1‐1) showed full restoration of miz1 hydrotropic bending, reaching 87.7 ± 8.3, 87 ± 3.6, 88.7 ± 2.4 and 89.3 ± 7.2 degrees after 7 h of treatment, respectively (Figure 3a,b and Supporting Information S6: Figure S4d). In contrast, roots of miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐3 and miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐2 seedlings failed to bend hydrotropically, achieving curvatures of 20.2 ± 6.4 and 16.52 ± 7.3 degrees, respectively (Supporting Information S6: Figure S4a,b). Roots of miz1 completely failed to bend as previously demonstrated (Kobayashi et al. 2007), and wild‐type (Col‐0) roots achieved a curvature of 80.3 ± 7.4 degrees (Figure 3a,b). Seedlings of all genotypes displayed similar primary root growth rates (Figure 3c and Supporting Information S6: Figure S4c). To reinforce these data, the hydrotropic phenotypes of miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1.1 and miz1/35S::SlMIZ1 seedlings were examined using the sorbitol/split agar hydrostimulation assay as described previously (Takahashi et al. 2002; Krieger et al. 2016) with a few modifications (see Section 2). Briefly, seedlings were grown by allowing the roots to penetrate the agar‐solidified medium (0.5%) to promote a non‐wavy growth pattern of the primary root (Supporting Information S6: Figure S5a), as occurs when the seedlings are grown on the medium surface. Wavy growth reduces the reproducibility of root hydrotropic bending angles in the hydrostimulation assay due to the inconsistency of root tip orientation. Moreover, to avoid attenuation of root hydrotropism due to the counteracting gravitropic growth, we performed the hydrostimulation under continuous clinorotation, where the seedlings were exposed to zero mean gravity forces at each stimulation time point. Clinorotation of seedlings has been found to effectively induce root hydrotropism by impairing the root tip's perception of gravity and nullifying the gravitropic response (Takahashi et al. 1996; Mao et al. 2022). Roots of miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1.1 miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1.2 and miz1/35S::SlMIZ1‐1 seedlings consistently bent toward the higher potential (43.1 ± 9.78, 42.9 ± 9.81 and 43 ± 9.67 degrees, respectively), similar to the wild‐type roots (44.36 ± 7.75 degrees), while miz1 roots failed to bend (0.54 ± 3.64 degrees) (Supporting Information S6: Figure S5d,e). This assay further confirmed the ability of SlMIZ1‐1 to complement the Arabidopsis MIZ1‐modulated root hydrotropic response.

## Primary Root Waviness
Under control conditions, wild‐type Arabidopsis roots display a wavy growth pattern that is associated with hydrotropism (Takahashi et al. 2002), consistent with the substantially reduced waviness of the miz1 root phenotype (Kobayashi et al. 2007). To examine the possible effect of expression of tomato SlMIZ1‐1 on root waviness, we quantified the root waviness of vertically grown 5‐day‐old wild‐type and miz1 seedlings, and two lines of miz1/MIZp::SlMIZ1‐1 seedlings. We imaged the seedlings and further analyzed root waviness levels using the ImageJ SNT plugin (https://imagej.net/plugins/snt/). This analysis provides a straightness index value for each root, with perfect straightness determined as 1; this was converted to a waviness index value by subtracting the straightness value from 1. Using this tool, roots of wild‐type seedlings had waviness index values of 0.152 ± 0.034, whereas miz1 showed a substantially lower value of 0.043 ± 0.011, as reported previously (Kobayashi et al. 2007). Roots of the two miz1/MIZp::SlMIZ1‐1 lines gave a waviness index value of 0.116 ± 0.011 and 0.118 ± 0.026, which was not significantly different from the wild‐type (Figure 4). These data clearly showed that SlMIZ1‐1 can restore root waviness in the miz1 mutant.

## Generation of Root Tip Hydrotropic Ca2+ Signals
Ca2+ signalling modulates root hydrotropism in Arabidopsis and peas (Takano et al. 1995; Shkolnik et al. 2018). These hydrotropic Ca2+ signals are absent in miz1 (Shkolnik et al. 2018). Therefore, we examined whether the expression of SlMIZ1‐1 in miz1 would affect [Ca2+]cyt in response to 1 h of hydrostimulation using the cytosol‐targeted FRET‐based Ca2+ sensor Cameleon (NES‐YC3.6) (Krebs et al. 2012). Roots were hydrostimulated using the CaCl2/dry chamber system, and NES‐YC3.6 signal was visualized under a confocal microscope (Section 2). The FRET‐to‐CFP ratio was quantified in the meristem and elongation zones as indicated in Figure 5a. Remarkably, hydrostimulation of the two lines of miz1/MIZp::SlMIZ1‐1 roots resulted in the generation of hydrotropic Ca2+ signals similar to those in the wild‐type roots, with FRET/CFP values of 2.14 ± 0.4, 2.29 ± 0.22 and 2.24 ± 0.34, respectively; signal was not observed in roots of the miz1 mutant (1.06 ± 0.31) (Figure 5a,b). These findings strongly suggest that SlMIZ1‐1 functions as a component in the mechanism that generates the shootward hydrotropic Ca2+ signalling, similar to MIZ1 in Arabidopsis.

## Transcriptome Analysis of Hydrostimulated Tomato Root Tips Under Control Gravity and Continuous Clinorotation Conditions
Transcriptome analysis was conducted to identify genes that are involved in the tomato root response to hydrostimulation and potentially function in the interplay with the opposing gravitropic growth. Lists of DEGs were identified in response to hydrostimulation under control conditions (static) and continuous clinorotation. RNA was isolated from wild‐type (M82) tomato root tips (5 mm from the apex), which had been hydrostimulated using the silica gel/dry chamber to allow rotation of the hydrostimulation system (see Section 2; Supporting Information S6: Figure S6). Expression reads higher than 10 were filtered for differential expression using Partek Genomics Suite v7.20.083. Lists of DEGs for hydrostimulated versus control treatments under control conditions (static) or clinorotation (cutoff FC ≥ |1.25| and p ≤ 0.05) were extracted (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).
Venn diagrams were created to compare the DEG lists (cutoff FC ≥ |1.25| and p ≤ 0.05; Supporting Information S2: Table S2) and identify hydrostimulation‐responsive DEGs under control gravity force (static) or clinorotation conditions. Using these selective cutoffs, 227 (static) and 184 (clinorotation) exclusively upregulated hydrostimulation‐responsive genes, and 233 (static) and 184 (clinorotation) downregulated hydrostimulation‐responsive genes were identified (Supporting Information S6: Figure S7). Furthermore, the analysis revealed 65 upregulated and 38 downregulated DEGs that were commonly expressed under both control gravity and clinorotation conditions (Supporting Information S6: Figure S7). Both SlMIZ1‐1 and SlMIZ1‐2, but not SlMIZ1‐3, were identified as upregulated in response to hydrostimulation under static and clinorotation conditions (Supporting Information S2: Table S2), consistent with the RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure 2f). Next, based on these Venn diagram‐derived DEG lists, volcano plots were created to identify hydrostimulation‐responsive genes (cutoff FC < 1.25 and p > 0.05) and provide comparable factors, termed ‘Manhattan distance’ (Supporting Information S6: Figure S8). Under static hydrostimulation, several ABA‐related genes were identified as hydrostimulation‐responsive, including the downregulated ABA receptor PYL6 (Solyc05g052420) with a Manhattan distance of 6.58 and the upregulated AIW2 (Solyc01g109120; 6.36), LEA29/LEA76 (Solyc03g116390; 6.28) and HVA22D (Solyc03g097420; 6.15) (Supporting Information S3: Table S3). In addition, the upregulated cytokinin dehydrogenase CKX6 (Solyc01g088160; 6.69) and the pectin methyl esterase that also contains a methyl esterase inhibitor domain PME44 (Solyc06g009190; 6.11) were identified (Supporting Information S3: Table S3). Under clinorotation conditions, we identified, among others, the downregulated early auxin response gene SAUR59 (Solyc08g150103; 7.44), the cell wall modification‐related gene CEP2 (Solyc03g111730; 5.73), the upregulated cytokinin response regulator gene EXO70D1 (Solyc06g062990; 6.52) and the ABA and osmotic stress‐responsive gene EDL3 (Solyc09g064820; 5.97) (Supporting Information S3: Table S3). Notably, it appears that ABA signalling pathways function in response to hydrostimulation under both static and clinorotation conditions by involving osmotic stress and drought‐related genes such as HVA22D (Guo and David Ho 2008) and EDL3 (Koops et al. 2011). Interestingly, hydrostimulation was found to induce the expression of the ethylene‐regulated transcription factors ERF F4 (Solyc07g053740; 2.7) under static conditions, and PTI6 (Solyc06g082590) and ERF E1 (Solyc09g075420) under both static and clinorotation conditions (Manhattan distances of 2.3 and 3 for PTI6, and 2 and 1.6 for ERF E1, respectively) (Supporting Information S4: Table S4). Taken together, these data suggest a possible interplay between ABA and ethylene during hydrotropism, as previously demonstrated in the regulation of root growth in maize (Spollen et al. 2000).
Functional analysis of these upregulated and downregulated genes (hydrostimulation vs. control, under static or clinorotation conditions) was conducted using the DAVID Functional Classification tool (http://david.ncifcrf.gov/) (Sherman et al. 2022) with multiple sources of functional annotations according to DAVID knowledgebase v2023q4 (http://david.ncifcrf.gov/content.jsp?file=update.html). Prominent classifications are depicted in Figure 6, selected genes are listed in Table 2, and the full data are available in Supporting Information S4: Table S4. The two MIZ1‐like genes, SlMIZ1‐1 and SlMIZ1‐2, were identified as upregulated in response to hydrostimulation (Table 2), reinforcing the RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure 2f). The expression of protein kinase activity‐associated DEGs was identified as downregulated under static (7.4%) and both clinorotation and static (23.53%) conditions (Figure 6; Supporting Information S4: Table S4), consistent with a recent report demonstrating modulation of hydrotropism by phosphor‐regulation (Chang et al. 2024). Transcription regulators were identified as upregulated under static (8.3%), clinorotation (2.85%) or both conditions (9.75%), and downregulated under clinorotation (2.75%) (Figure 6; Supporting Information S4: Table S4). The expression of aquaporins was identified as downregulated under clinorotation (1.83%) and under both conditions (11.76%) (Figure 6; Supporting Information S4: Table S4), suggesting a role for these proteins in modulating the cell water balance required for differential cell expansion to achieve curvature. Interestingly, under clinorotation conditions, downregulation of starch biosynthesis‐associated DEGs was identified (Figure 6; Supporting Information S4: Table S4), consistent with the previously described process of columella cell amyloplast starch degradation in response to hydrostimulation (Takahashi et al. 2003), consistent with recent report of amyloplast role in MIZ1‐modulated root hydrotropic response (Hong et al. 2024).

## Slmiz1 x 3‐1 Develops Fewer Lateral Roots and Displays Hypersensitivity to Drought
In Arabidopsis, young miz1 seedlings have been reported to develop more lateral roots than the wild‐type (Moriwaki et al. 2011). Under our conditions, 12‐day‐old wild‐type and miz1 seedlings were found to develop lateral root numbers (mean ± SD) of 7.9 ± 2 and 3.8 ± 1.2, respectively (Figure 7a,b). A curve in the root is a potential site for enhanced auxin accumulation in pericycle cells, initiating cell divisions that lead to the formation of a lateral root primordium (Laskowski et al. 2008). Hence, the miz1 phenotype of fewer lateral roots is consistent with its ‘straight’ primary root growth pattern (Figure 4). Moreover, roots of miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1 were found to develop 7.8 ± 1.71 lateral roots (Figure 7a,b). These data further show that SlMIZ1‐1 rescues the miz1 phenotype in Arabidopsis and strongly suggests the requirement of a functioning MIZ1 for modulation of normal root architecture. In view of these data, we examined the effect of the triple mutation of the three MIZ1‐like genes on primary root branching of young tomato seedlings. Eleven‐day‐old Slmiz1 x 3‐1 seedlings developed 9.8 ± 2.1 lateral roots, substantially less than the wild‐type seedlings (14.2 ± 1.7 lateral roots) (Figure 7a,b). Both the wild‐type and the Slmiz1 x 3‐1 mutant exhibited similar primary root elongation rates (Figure 7c). To assess the possible function of MIZ1‐like genes in later stages of development and in response to drought treatment, we quantified their relative transcript levels in the roots of a 5‐week‐old wild‐type tomato. The expression of all three genes rose in response to 2 weeks of withheld irrigation compared to controls, 10.8 ± 3.8‐fold for SlMIZ1‐1, 6.47 ± 1.56‐fold for SlMIZ1‐3 and 6.24 ± 1.05‐fold for SlMIZ1‐2 (Figure 8a–c). These data strongly suggest the involvement of these three MIZ1‐like genes in the tomato response to drought at this developmental stage. Withholding irrigation for 2 weeks from 5‐week‐old pot‐grown wild‐type and Slmiz1 x 3‐1 seedlings resulted in substantial wilting of both genotypes with no apparent difference. Remarkably, upon restoration of irrigation, only ~0.2% of the drought‐stunned Slmiz1 x 3‐1 mutants were able to recover, whereas ~98% of the wild‐type seedlings fully recovered (3 independent experiments, 15 seedlings each) (Figure 8b). Root and shoot fresh weight were measured at the end of the experiment. Wild‐type and Slmiz1 x 3‐1 drought‐recovered plants gained 2.85 ± 0.47 and 1.73 ± 0.43 g root fresh weight, respectively (Figure 8c), and 3.43 ± 0.55 and 1.65 ± 0.28 g shoot fresh weight, respectively (Figure 8d). Wild‐type and Slmiz1 x 3‐1 control plants gained root fresh weights of 2.85 ± 0.47 and 1.87 ± 0.44 g, respectively (Figure 8c), with no significant difference in shoot weight (Figure 8d). Together, these data suggest that the impaired ability of Slmiz1 x 3‐1 to recover from the drought event is related to its less‐developed root system, which might reduce its ability to recruit sufficient water upon re‐irrigation compared to the wild‐type.

## Discussion
We assessed the involvement of three MIZ1‐like genes in modulating root hydrotropism and drought response in tomato, and further profiled the transcriptome in hydrostimulated tomato root tips under natural gravity and clinorotation conditions. CRISPR/Cas9‐derived triple mutation of the three MIZ1‐like genes in tomato resulted in an ahydrotropic response for Slmiz1 x 3 roots (Figure 2c,d). Comparative sequence analysis between Arabidopsis MIZ1 and the three tomato MIZ1‐like amino acid sequences revealed the highest similarity with both SlMIZ1‐1 complete protein and DUF617 (Figure 1 and Supporting Information S6: Figure S1), in contrast to previous research suggesting SlMIZ1‐2 as the closest homologue of MIZ1 (Li et al. 2023). Expression of each tomato MIZ1‐like gene in the ahydrotropic Arabidopsis miz1 mutant revealed that only SlMIZ1‐1 could complement MIZ1 function in the hydrotropic response (Figure 3 and Supporting Information S6: Figure S4). Together with the hydrotropic phenotypes of Slmiz1 x 3 and Slmiz1 x 2 (Figure 2 and Supporting Information S6: Figure S3), SlMIZ1‐1 appears to be indispensable for root hydrotropism in tomato. This conclusion was reinforced by examination of additional miz1‐related traits, including restoration of root waviness and generation of [Ca2+]cyt signal in response to hydrostimulation (Figures 4 and 5) (Kobayashi et al. 2007; Shkolnik et al. 2018). To date, the biochemical function of MIZ1 is unknown. Nonetheless, we know that it is associated with the ER membrane (Yamazaki et al. 2012), where it functions to inhibit ECA1‐facilitated Ca2+ import into the ER lumen (Shkolnik et al. 2018). Together with the annotated DUF617 soluble β‐barrel structure (Figure 1c), which has been associated with bacterial Ca2+‐binding activity (Solan et al. 2021), MIZ1 could be considered a cellular Ca2+‐sensing component that is required for the hydrotropic response and general root growth and development.
According to the transcriptome analysis, both SlMIZ1‐1 and SlMIZ1‐2 were identified as upregulated under control gravity and clinorotation conditions (Table 2), consistent with the RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure 2f). Hence, at least at the transcriptional level, both SlMIZ1‐1 and SlMIZ1‐2, but not SlMIZ1‐3, are involved in hydrotropism. Several cell wall modification‐ and cell division‐associated DEGs were identified as upregulated under both conditions, including the pectin methylesterase PME44 (Solyc06g009190) which functions in modulation of cell wall loosening (Wen et al. 2020) (Supporting Information S3: Table S3) and the AAA‐type ATPase CDC48 (Solyc11g069720) (Park, Rancour, and Bednarek 2008) (Table 2), suggesting that the hydrotropic response involves cell wall processing, possibly required for cross‐root differential cell expansion, cell division or both. Interestingly, ethylene‐regulated transcription factors, including PTI6 (Solyc06g082590), ERF E1 (Solyc09g075420) and ERF F4 (Solyc07g053740) (Gu et al. 2002; Liu et al. 2016), were identified as upregulated under static or both conditions but not under solely clinorotation (Table 2). These data suggest that these transcription factors function in the ethylene‐mediated gravitropism‐attenuating effect during hydrotropic root bending. In maize primary root, negative regulation of ethylene synthesis by ABA under low water potential promotes root elongation (Spollen et al. 2000). Together, these data suggest the involvement of ethylene signalling in hydrotropism, which could explain the described role of ABA in modulating differential cross‐root elongation zone cortical cell expansion, while no differential ABA accumulation is observed in hydrostimulated Arabidopsis roots (Dietrich et al. 2017). Aquaporins are known as facilitators of water molecule transport across the membrane under control and various stress conditions (Reuscher et al. 2013; Afzal et al. 2016; Kapilan, Vaziri, and Zwiazek 2018), as well as uptake of nutritionally important metalloids, including silicon (Takano et al. 2006; Deshmukh and Bélanger 2016) and boron (Ma et al. 2006), which are required for cell wall maintenance (Sheng et al. 2024). The aquaporins XIP1.4 (Solyc10g054800) and XIP1.1 (Solyc10g054840) were identified as downregulated under both static and clinorotation conditions, whereas PIP1.3 (Solyc12g056220) and PIP2.9 (Solyc10055630) were found to be downregulated under solely clinorotation conditions (Table 2). This altered hydrostimulation‐responsive expression of aquaporins suggests their involvement in the modulation of cellular turgor pressure and nutrient uptake, required for cell expansion, division or both during root hydrotropic bending.
The interplay between gravitropism and hydrotropism is partly modulated by phosphor‐regulation, which is mediated by the activity of kinases and phosphatases that converge in the signalling pathways of Ca2+, ABA, auxin and reactive oxygen species (Wexler, Schroeder, and Shkolnik 2024). Our functional classification analysis indicates upregulation and downregulation of kinase‐encoding genes under control gravity conditions but not under clinorotation (Figure 6; Table 2), which might indicate their function in attenuating gravitropism during root hydrotropism.
A correlation between a particular root system architecture—more or less branched—and responses to various drought conditions and heterogeneous water distribution in the soil has yet to be characterized and differs among plant species (Dinneny 2019). A more developed and branched root system might be disadvantageous under drought conditions as a result of the larger interface with the dry matrix; however, upon the improvement of water availability, a more developed root system could enhance the plant's ability to recover by recruiting water more rapidly. The transcript levels of the three tomato MIZ1‐like genes increased substantially under drought conditions (Figure 8a), strongly suggesting their involvement in modulating the response to drought. The impaired recovery from drought of the Slmiz1 x 3‐1 mutant (Figure 8b), in which reduced root branching was observed (Figure 8c), might indicate that at least for tomato cv. M82, a more developed root system, modulated by MIZ1‐like genes, enhances the plant's ability to recover from a relatively long drought episode. In response to hydrostimulation, the expression of SlMIZ1‐1 and SlMIZ1‐2 was induced by 50% and 250%, respectively (Figure 2f), but only the expression of SlMIZ1‐1 was found to effectively restore miz1 hydrotropism (Figure 3 and Supporting Information S6: Figure S4). Collectively, MIZ1‐like genes are suggested as important players in determining tomato root architecture in response to water scarcity and directing root growth toward a moisture source.
With today's decreasing availability of water suitable for agriculture worldwide due to global climate changes, adjusting molecular approaches, as presented herein, could improve crop performance under limited irrigation conditions.

Slmiz1 x 3 seedlings were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome‐editing technology to mutate the MIZ1‐like genes SlMIZ1‐1, SlMIZ1‐2 and SlMIZ1‐3 (see Section 2 and Supporting Information S6: Figure S2). Scale bar, 1 cm. (b) Schematic representation of the CaCl2/dry chamber used for root hydrostimulation. (c) Root‐hydrostimulation assay of 5‐day‐old M82 and Slmiz1 x 3 seedlings in CaCl2/dry chamber system; g, gravity vector; Ψ, water potential gradient. Following 7 h of hydrostimulation, M82 and Slmiz1 x 3‐1 achieved curvatures of 78.9 ± 7.2 and 29.7 ± 4.2 degrees, respectively. Scale bar, 1 mm. (d) Quantification of root tip curvature of hydrostimulated M82 and two allelic Slmiz1 x 3 triple mutant seedlings. Root curvature was measured at 1‐h intervals for 7 h of hydrostimulation. Error bars represent mean ± SD (three independent biological experiments, 10 seedlings each). (e) Quantification of growth rate of hydrostimulated roots of the indicated genotypes determined by measuring the lengths at the beginning and end of the experiment. Error bars represent mean ± SD (3 independent biological experiments, 10 seedlings each). In (d) and (e), different letters above the bars indicate significantly different values by Tukey's HSD post hoc test (p < 0.001). (f) RT‐qPCR analysis for transcript level quantification of the three indicated tomato MIZ1‐like genes. RNA was extracted from 5‐mm root tips of 5‐day‐old control or 4‐h hydrostimulated M82 seedlings. Data are means ± SD (4 biological experiments, 10 root tips each). Asterisks indicate Student's t‐test (hydrostimulation vs. control); for SlMIZ1‐1, P = 6.85E−3; SlMIZ1‐2, P = 3.75E−4.

## Figure Figure 3: Expression of tomato SlMIZ1‐1 rescues miz1 root hydrotropic response in Arabidopsis. (a) CaCl2/dry chamber root‐hydrostimulation assay of 5‐day‐old Arabidopsis seedlings: wild‐type (Col‐0), miz1, miz1 expressing the tomato SlMIZ1‐1 gene under the regulation of Arabidopsis MIZ1 native promoter (miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1), and miz1 overexpressing SlMIZ1‐1 under the regulation of the viral constitutive CaMV 35S promoter (miz1/35S::SlMIZ1‐1). Scale bar, 1 mm; g, gravity vector; Ψ, water potential gradient. (b) Quantification of root tip curvature of hydrostimulated seedlings of miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1 (three lines) and miz1/35S::SlMIZ1‐1 (image of additional lines in Supporting Information S6: Figure S4d). Root curvature was measured at 1‐h intervals for 7 h following hydrostimulation. Error bars represent mean ± SD (3 independent biological experiments, 10 seedlings each). *p < 0.001 (Student's t‐test) when comparing the curvature at each time point versus Col‐0. (c) Quantification of growth rate of hydrostimulated roots determined by measuring the lengths at the beginning and end of the experiment. Error bars represent mean ± SD (3 independent biological experiments, 10 seedlings each). No significant difference in growth rate was found among the various genotypes according to Tukey's HSD post hoc test (p < 0.001).

## Figure Figure 4: Expression of tomato SlMIZ1‐1 restores Arabidopsis miz1 waviness. (a) Five‐day‐old wild‐type (Col‐0), miz1, and two lines of SlMIZ1‐1‐expressing miz1 (miz1/MIZ1p::SlMIZ1‐1.1/2) Arabidopsis seedlings that were germinated and grown vertically on 0.25× MS medium. Scale bar, 1 cm; g, gravity vector. (b) Quantification of root waviness index of seedlings using ImageJ Fiji software with the SNT plugin (https://imagej.net/plugins/snt/) (see Section 2) where a perfectly straight root growth pattern is set to 1 and waviness index is calculated by subtracting the straightness value from 1. Bold lines within each box represent mean values (3 independent biological experiments, 10 seedlings each). Top and bottom sides of the boxes represent the third and first quartiles of the value distribution, respectively. Lines extending from the boxes denote SD values. Different letters above the bars indicate significantly different values by Tukey's HSD post hoc test (p < 0.001).

## Figure Figure 6: Functional classification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to hydrostimulation and continuous clinorotation.
