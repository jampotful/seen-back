OsAux1 is required for nutritropism in rice roots.

# OsAux1 is required for nutritropism in rice roots

## Abstract
SUMMARYNutritropism is a directional root growth response to nutrient gradients, and rice (Oryza sativa L.) roots show nutritropism toward sources containing ammonium. Here, we report characterization of an EMS‐mutagenized rice plant that shows a complete defect in nutritropism. Genetic analysis of the mutant identified a nucleotide substitution as a candidate for the causal mutation; this mutation leads to an amino acid substitution (E111V) in an auxin influx carrier protein, OsAUX1 (LOC_Os01g63770, Os01g0856500). We generated novel allelic mutants using CRISPR/Cas9, and these independent mutant lines showed defects in nutritropism. We concluded that OsAUX1 is the gene required for nutritropism based on this result. Analysis of RFP fluorescence in root tips expressing the DR5::RFP reporter suggested an asymmetric distribution of auxin in nutritropic root bending, and the asymmetry was similar to that in gravitropism. Nutritropism of aux1 mutants was recovered by exogenous application of membrane‐permeable 1‐naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) but not by impermeable 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid, indicating that passive diffusion of NAA into the cytosol from the medium is sufficient to imitate the OsAUX1 functions. Our results establish the involvement of auxin in nutritropism, indicating the similarity to gravitropism but not to hydrotropism. This provides insights into the mechanisms for integration of the complex tropic stimulations from environments.

## INTRODUCTION
Tropism is a directional growth response to or away from a directional external stimulus. Tropisms are important for adaptation of sessile plants to their environments. The major tropisms—phototropism, gravitropism, and hydrotropism—enable plants to efficiently acquire growth resources such as light and water in heterogeneous environments (Darwin, 1897; Gilroy, 2008). However, tropism toward nutrients, which are also present heterogeneously in soil (Giehl & von Wirén, 2014) has not been proved experimentally. We recently identified a directional growth response of rice roots (Oryza sativa L.) under ammonium gradient and rice root bending toward the nutrient sources (Yamazaki et al., 2020; Yamazaki & Fujiwara, 2022). Although our discovery specifically revealed a directional growth response toward ammonium nutrients in rice roots, we designate this phenomenon as nutritropism, referring broadly to directional growth toward any nutrient gradient, by analogy to phototropism, which denotes the stimulus category “light” rather than a specific wavelength such as blue light. There are no experimental results demonstrating nutritropism in other plant species yet. It was estimated that rice roots exposed to an ammonium gradient sense a sub‐μM increase in ammonium concentration generated in agar medium (containing 412 μM ammonium). It has been reported that rice primary roots, but not lateral roots, exhibit helical growth in high ammonium hydroponic solution (2.5 mm) due to a loss of gravitropism (Jia et al., 2020). Nutritropism is independent of gravitropism (Yamazaki et al., 2020; Yamazaki & Fujiwara, 2022). Therefore, nutritropism differs from the helical growth in the effective concentrations, the root types involved, and the dependence on gravitropism. Ammonium regulation of root development has been extensively studied in plants (e.g., Liu & von Wirén, 2017; Motte et al., 2019). In the cases of rice roots, ammonium inhibits elongation of seminal and lateral roots (Hirano et al., 2008). Ammonium supply also promotes root hair elongation compared with nitrogen‐free conditions in rice (Rajendran & Kim, 2022) and lateral root branching (Pélissier et al., 2024). It is known that local ammonium supply to a single crown root enhances branching and elongation of the lateral roots (Wu et al., 2022). This series of root responses toward local ammonium appears to be beneficial for rice in efficiently acquiring ammonium and other co‐existing nutrients by gathering lateral roots around ammonium hotspots.
The mechanisms of the major tropisms have been studied extensively. In root gravitropism, gravitational stimulation is sensed by amyloplasts in columella cells, causing their gravity‐dependent movement (e.g., Jiao et al., 2021). This movement induces polar localization of plasma membrane auxin transporters, such as PIN‐FORMED family proteins, leading to an asymmetric auxin distribution in root tips. This asymmetry causes differential cell elongation between the downward and upward sides, resulting in root tip bending in the direction of gravity (Kong et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2025). In hydrotropism, stimulation seems to be sensed by unknown factors in cortex cells and leads to the asymmetric cytokinin distribution in root tips (Chang et al., 2019; Dietrich et al., 2017). This asymmetry causes differential cell division between the wet and dry sides, resulting in root tip bending toward higher moisture. Mutant analysis has played a crucial role in understanding the molecular mechanisms of tropisms, as comprehensively reviewed in previous studies (Baldwin et al., 2013; Blancaflor & Masson, 2003; Christie, 2007; Dietrich, 2018; Liscum et al., 2014; Miyazawa & Takahashi, 2020). The effects of nutrient conditions (Yamazaki & Fujiwara, 2022) and transcriptome responses in the root tips (Yamazaki et al., 2024) have been reported on nutritropism. However, the molecular mechanisms remain largely unclear owing to the lack of nutritropic mutants for analysis.
Here, we report a rice EMS mutant, #633, which is defective in nutritropism. We describe the genetic analysis of #633 and characterize CRISPR/Cas9 mutants of a candidate causal gene of #633 phenotypes. Then, we identify the essential gene and the involvement of auxin in nutritropism. In addition, auxin distribution in nutritropic roots is observed. From these results, we discuss a hypothetical model for the physiological events in this tropism.

## RESULTS

## Defect in nutritropism in the #633 mutant from an EMS‐mutagenized population
The #633 mutant was isolated from an EMS‐mutagenized population in the Hitomebore background (Fekih et al., 2013; Tanaka et al., 2016). This mutant showed little or no nutritropic response of lateral roots in our nutritropic bioassay (see Materials and Methods, Figure 1A,B). In WT, some lateral roots gathered around the nutrient sources but not the mock sources 24 h after source insertion, whereas lateral roots of #633 did not gather around the nutrient sources (Figure 1A,B). Root bending toward nutrient sources was quantified using the bending curvature score (see Materials and Methods). In WT, a significantly greater bending curvature was observed toward the nutrient sources (score 0.33 ± 0.11, n = 11, P < 0.001) than toward the mock sources (−0.06 ± 0.09, n = 11), while the lateral root elongation after the source insertion was similar around each source. This indicated positive nutritropism toward the nutrient sources (Figure 1C). Bending curvature toward the nutrient sources was significantly lower in #633 (0.00 ± 0.30, n = 12, P < 0.001) than in WT, but that toward the mock sources did not differ significantly (P = 0.855) between #633 (0.00 ± 0.16, n = 13) and WT. In #633, no significant difference (P = 1.000) was observed in bending curvature scores between the mock and nutrient sources. These results indicate that #633 is defective in nutritropism. The variation of bending curvature scores toward the mock and nutrient sources was higher in #633 than in WT. This difference was more obvious when the bending curvature scores of individual lateral roots were compared (Figure S1A); some lateral roots of #633 showed scores around 1 toward the mock source, while others showed scores lower than −1 toward 10× MS. This suggests that the upward and downward bending of #633 lateral roots is independent of nutritropic and gravitropic stimulation (Figure S1B). However, we never observed lateral roots gathering and coiling around 10× MS sources in the #633 mutant, as observed in WT (Figure 1A,B); thus, the observation of the gathering‐to‐coil behavior around the nutrient sources is a more reliable criterion for judging nutritropic potential in a single plant than the bending curvature score. Upon further characterization, we found that the root systems of #633 were clearly distinct from those of WT Hitomebore: the main roots of WT elongated downward, whereas those of #633 elongated upward and coiled in some portions (Figure 1A,D). The root pattern of #633 suggests that the mutant had an abnormal gravitropic response. In a gravitropic assay, primary roots of WT and #633 seedlings were subjected to a 90° gravistimulation by reorienting them horizontally (Figure S2A). Following this gravistimulation, bending angles of primary roots were measured over time (Figure S2B). The bending angles of #633 were significantly lower than those of WT (n = 9–10, P < 0.01, Student's t‐test). These results indicate that gravitropism of #633 primary roots was impaired.

## Next‐generation sequencing‐based bulked segregant analysis of the nutritropism defect
To identify chromosomal regions responsible for the defect observed in #633, we performed bulked segregant analysis with the SIMPLE pipeline (Wachsman et al., 2017) on a F2 population derived by crossing Hitomebore and #633. Before that we found F1 plants with lateral roots gathering around nutrient sources (Figure S3). This indicates that the defect of #633 is a recessive phenotype; failure of crossing could be excluded because #633 was used as the seed parent. In addition to the recovery of the nutritropic defect, gravitropism was recovered in F1 plants, because their main roots elongated only downward. In the evaluation of F2 phenotypes for the bulked segregant analysis, lateral roots gathering around the nutrient sources were judged as the WT‐F2 phenotype showing obvious nutritropism. The bending curvature scores of some WT lateral roots toward the nutrient sources were similar to those toward the mock sources (Figure S1), suggesting a possibility where some of many WT‐F2 plants do not show clear nutritropic bending. This means that we might not be able to accurately screen F2 plants defective in nutritropism based on root bending observations or curvature scores. Because all the 20 F2 plants selected as the WT‐F2 bulk showed normal gravitropism (downward elongation of the main roots), these two tropisms clearly co‐segregated. In contrast, all the 20 F2 plants selected by obviously gravitropism‐defective root elongation similar to that in #633 (Figure 1D) showed no obvious bending toward nutrient sources, that is, no lateral roots gathering around nutrient sources, also supporting co‐segregation. So, we decided to identify the causal mutations/genes responsible for the gravitropic defect in #633 by bulked segregant analysis and then to confirm the gene contribution to nutritropism by further mutant analysis. In SIMPLE analysis using the two bulked samples, a peak of the moving average curves of the allele ratio was observed within a 35‐Mb region on chromosome 1 (Figure 2A). For recessive mutant phenotypes, the theoretical value of the allele ratio for the causal mutation should be around 0.67, and the top peak of the ratio in our analysis was nearly 0.6. Thus, the peak indicates a promising region for the causal mutation. We also screened causal mutations using two criteria: (1) mutations leading to an amino acid substitution, stop gain, or frameshift, or mutations at splicing junctions, and (2) mutations present in all aligned reads of the #633‐type bulk. Six mutations in five genes across the genome satisfied both criteria (Table S1), and only one mutation in OsAUX1 (MSU ID, LOC_Os01g63770; RAP‐DB ID, Os01g0856500) was located within the 35‐Mb region of chromosome 1. Therefore, OsAUX1 is a promising candidate for the causal gene for the gravitropic defect of #633. Sanger sequencing analysis demonstrates that #633 carries a nucleotide mutation within the OsAUX1 coding sequence (CDS) (332 A > T in the third exon, Figure 2B,C). This mutation causes an amino acid substitution at position 111 (glutamate to valine; E111V). To investigate the possible effects of E111, we examined the spatial position of E111 and its potential interacting residues on the AlphaFold‐predicted 3D structure of OsAUX1 (UniProt ID: Q5N892) (Figure S4). E111 is located on the intracellular side of the helix forming the second transmembrane domain (Figure S4A), and this positioning is consistent with that of the corresponding residue E103 in the cryo‐EM structure of AtAUX1 recently reported (Jing et al., 2025). To screen potential hydrogen bond partners of E111, donor atoms of the carboxyl groups of the E111 and E103 side chains were examined using the 3.5 Å cutoff (Kajander et al., 2000). The backbone nitrogen of an alanine residue (A419 or A411) in the fifth intracellular loop (IL5) was the only atom found within 3.5 Å in both structures (Figure S4B,C). E111 and IL5 appear to be located on the side opposite the central cavity containing IAA (Figure S4D, Movie S1), which opens toward the intracellular side (Jing et al., 2025). These results suggest that E111 may contribute to the overall structural changes via an interaction with IL5, rather than to gating the cavity.

## OsAUX1 mutations responsible for nutritropism deficiency
OsAUX1 is an auxin influx transporter composed of 492 amino acids, with 11 transmembrane domains (Singh et al., 2018). To obtain the null mutants for the analysis of the OsAUX1 contribution to nutritropism, we edited the OsAUX1 CDS of the Nipponbare genome using the CRISPR/Cas9 and obtained three independent transgenic mutant lines. Two of them (aux1‐2 and aux1‐3) carried the same G deletion at position +651 of the CDS, causing a frameshift (Figure 3A), which changed the amino acid sequence from W217 in the 5th transmembrane domain and generated a premature stop codon after the 286th amino acid. Thus, in the aux1‐2 and aux1‐3 mutants, OsAUX1 lacked the 5th to 11th transmembrane domains, thereby abolishing its function. In the aux1‐4 line, the codon for W217 was deleted. These three lines showed an abnormal root system similar to that of #633 (Figure 3B). In the gravitropic assay of primary roots, the bending angles of these osaux1 mutants toward the gravity vector were significantly lower than those of the WT (n = 13–24, P < 0.001, Dunnett's multiple comparison test), indicating impaired gravitropism in their primary roots (Figure S2C,D). Then we examined nutritropism of these lines in the bioassay (Figure 3C,D). The bending curvature scores of the osaux1 mutants toward 10× MS sources (−0.01 ± 0.05, n = 6 for aux1‐2; 0.06 ± 0.05, n = 4 for aux1‐3; 0.05 ± 0.04, n = 5 for aux1‐4) were significantly lower than that of the WT Nipponbare (0.30 ± 0.02, n = 7, P < 0.001), while the curvature of the mutants toward mock sources (−0.05 ± 0.06, n = 6 for aux1‐2; 0.04 ± 0.12, n = 4 for aux1‐3; −0.02 ± 0.06, n = 5 for aux1‐4) was not significantly different from that of WT (−0.04 ± 0.05, n = 7, P = 0.994–1.000). The mutants showed no significant difference (P = 0.910–0.994) between 10× MS and mock sources, as observed in #633 (Figure 1D). These results indicate that all osaux1 mutants were defective in nutritropism. Therefore, OsAUX1 is required for nutritropism.

## Auxin distribution in root tips of WT plants during nutritropism
The requirement of OsAUX1 suggests the involvement of auxin transport in nutritropism. To observe changes in auxin distribution in bending root tips, we used WT rice plants carrying the DR5::RFP reporter and the tips of nutritropic lateral roots that coiled around the 10× MS sources (Figure 4A). In the tips of nutritropic lateral roots, the RFP fluorescence was stronger on the inside of the bend than on the outside, even in the root cap region (Figure 4B, arrowheads). In roots around the mock sources, the RFP signal was evenly distributed between the two sides. In root tips of plants carrying no DR5::RFP (negative control), little or no autofluorescence was observed (Figure 4B). From the results of the RFP fluorescence quantification, relative RFP fluorescence in both epidermis and cortex was significantly weaker on the outside and stronger on the inside of the bending roots than that of roots around the mock (Figure 4C). Our observations suggest that auxin accumulated more on the inside of the bending roots than on the outside in response to nutritropic stimulation.

## Effect of exogeneous synthetic auxins on nutritropism
We examined whether application of a membrane‐permeable synthetic auxin, NAA, would complement the defects of #633 and the CRISPR/Cas9 mutant aux1‐2. NAA was added to the growth medium at 10 nm before sowing seeds. We tested the nutritropism of their lateral roots under this condition (Figure 5A–C). The bending curvature scores of both mutants toward the mock sources (−0.09 ± 0.08, n = 9 for #633 and − 0.06 ± 0.07, n = 11 for aux1‐2) were not significantly different from those of their respective WTs (−0.09 ± 0.09, n = 11, P = 0.998 for Hitomebore and − 0.04 ± 0.06, n = 11, P = 0.947 for Nipponbare). The scores of #633 (0.11 ± 0.10, n = 9) and aux1‐2 (0.11 ± 0.05, n = 11) toward 10× MS sources were significantly higher than those toward the mock sources (P < 0.001 and <0.001, respectively), indicating that their nutritropic defects were recovered by NAA. The bending curvature score of #633 was recovered to a level similar to that of Hitomebore (0.17 ± 0.08, n = 11, P = 0.487). That of aux1‐2 was partially recovered compared with that of Nipponbare (0.30 ± 0.10, n = 11, P < 0.001). To exclude the possibility that the recovery by NAA was due to an increase in auxin concentration in the apoplast rather than to an increase in auxin influx, we supplied a synthetic impermeable auxin, 2,4‐D, at 10 nm instead of NAA (Figure 5D–F). The bending curvature scores of both mutants toward the 10× MS sources (0.00 ± 0.10, n = 9 for #633 and 0.04 ± 0.06, n = 11 for aux1‐2) were not significantly different from those toward the mock sources (0.01 ± 0.06, n = 9, P < 0.314 for #633 and 0.00 ± 0.12, n = 11, P < 0.841 for aux1‐2). In the WTs, the scores toward the 10× MS sources (0.17 ± 0.08, n = 11 for Hitomebore and 0.35 ± 0.07, n = 10 for Nipponbare) were significantly higher than those toward the mock sources (−0.03 ± 0.12, n = 11, P < 0.001 for Hitomebore and − 0.01 ± 0.06, n = 10, P < 0.001 for Nipponbare), indicating that WTs showed nutritropism in this condition. Overall, we conclude that NAA influx into the root cells is sufficient to complement the nutritropic defects of both mutants.

## DISCUSSION
This is the first report of mutants defective in plant nutritropism. The three independent transgenic CRISPR/Cas9 osaux1 mutant lines were defective in nutritropism, demonstrating that OsAUX1 is required for nutritropism. Because lateral root elongation around both the mock and 10× MS sources was similar in each osaux1 mutant, the disruption of nutritropism was not attributable to hypersensitivity to the 10× MS treatment. It was already reported that T‐DNA insertion mutants of OsAUX1 are defective in gravitropism (Giri et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2015). Although the genetic background of the T‐DNA insertion mutants (Dongjin) is different from those of our osaux1 mutants (Nipponbare for the CRISPR/Cas9 mutants and Hitomebore for #633), the reported gravitropic defect of the T‐DNA insertion mutants seems to be similar to that in our mutants (Figure S2). This indicates that the function of OsAUX1 is similarly disrupted in both the T‐DNA insertion and our mutants. Therefore, our results demonstrated that OsAUX1 contributes to both nutritropism and gravitropism. It is known that AtAUX1 and OsAUX1 also mediate root hair elongation on the roots exposed to low phosphorus conditions (Bhosale et al., 2018; Giri et al., 2018). In these previous reports, the authors concluded that phosphorus‐deficit conditions elevate IAA levels in roots exposed to low phosphorus, and it was proposed that AtAUX1 contributes to the root hair elongation via shootward auxin transport from the root apex to the elongation and differentiation zones (Bhosale et al., 2018). Therefore, auxin distribution in roots via AUX1 and the other auxin transporters regulates root morphology to acquire phosphorus by root hairs, ammonium by nutritropism, and water by gravitropic deep rooting. Genetic variations affecting OsAUX1 functions, such as changes in expression levels and auxin transport activity, may underlie part of the diversity in these responses among rice varieties. However, OsAUX1 variation alone cannot explain the nutritropic diversity reported in a World Rice Core Collection (Yamazaki & Fujiwara, 2022). For example, Nipponbare exhibited weaker nutritropism but normal gravitropism in the primary roots. The actual molecular mechanisms that generate this nutritropic diversity remain to be elucidated.
Determination of the causal gene for the nutritropic defect of #633 may require more detailed classical gene mapping to narrow the causal region on chromosome 1 or genetic complementation tests. At this point, it remains uncertain whether the causal gene is OsAUX1. However, OsAUX1 is the most likely candidate because only OsAUX1 satisfied the criteria in our bulked segregant analysis (Figure 2 and Table S1) and because the nutritropic defect, gravitropic defect, and the effect of NAA supplementation observed in #633 were identical to those in aux1‐2 mutants (Figures 1, 3 and 5; Figure S2). OsAUX1 encodes a plasma membrane‐localized auxin influx transporter. It is expressed in the root cap and epidermis of root tips (Yu et al., 2015). If the causal gene in #633 is OsAUX1, the amino acid substitution E111V found in #633 OsAUX1 is critical for auxin transport function. This speculation does not conflict with the amino acid conservation; the E111 residue corresponds to E103 in Arabidopsis AtAUX1 encoded by the orthologous gene At2g38120, and E103 has been shown to be one of the completely conserved residues among the AUX1 homologs in the land plants investigated (Singh et al., 2018). E111 is located in TM2, which belongs to the transport core domain (Chen et al., 2025; Jing et al., 2025). Our structural analysis suggests that the E111 side chain is positioned close enough to interact with IL5 via a hydrogen bond (Figure S4). Such an interaction could influence the conformational changes of the transport core domain during IAA transport, while this residue does not appear to be directly involved in auxin binding or cavity gating. In aux1‐4, OsAUX1 with the W217 deletion also resulting in a loss of nutritropism (Figure 3); W217 corresponds to W209 of AtAUX1, and W209 is also completely conserved among the land plants (Singh et al., 2018).
Based on transcriptome data from our previous study (Yamazaki et al., 2024), auxin was suggested to be involved in nutritropism. In the previous study, genes associated with GO terms related to auxin responses, including seven AUX/IAA genes, were significantly enriched in the inside tissues of bending roots under nutritropism. The current genetic analyses provide the first direct evidence that auxin influx and signaling are essential for nutritropism. Asymmetric auxin distribution was also visualized with the DR5::RFP reporter (Figure 4), and this distribution pattern is consistent with the previous transcriptome results. The lack of bending in osaux1 mutant roots toward the nutritropic stimulus indicates that the asymmetric auxin distribution required for bending is impaired. This inference is supported by a previous study showing that asymmetric auxin distribution alone is sufficient to drive root bending even without tropic stimulation (Joo et al., 2001). Notably, nutritropism in rice was observed only in response to ammonium and not to other tested nutrients, including other nitrogen forms (such as nitrate and urea) and non‐nitrogen essential nutrients (Yamazaki et al., 2020). Differential cell elongation in nutritropism was observed in epidermal cells within the apical region (<150 μm from QC) (Yamazaki et al., 2020). On the side opposite the nutrient source, cells were more elongated than those on the side facing the nutrient source and cells of non‐bending roots. Taken together, we conclude that an ammonium gradient induces asymmetric auxin distribution, with higher auxin accumulation toward the higher ammonium side of the apical region, causing differential cell elongation (Figure 6A). This sequence of events is similar to that in gravitropism (Su et al., 2020). Thus, the well‐established molecular models of gravitropic bending help us understand the molecular mechanisms of nutritropic bending.
A hypothetical model for nutritropism.
(A) Root bending toward an ammonium source. When a root tip is exposed to an ammonium gradient, it senses the gradient by an unknown mechanism (Yamazaki et al., 2020). Sensing induces preferential auxin transport to the side closer to the ammonium source (red arrows). An asymmetric auxin distribution was observed in the root cap region (Figure 4), suggesting the possibility that the cells responsible for ammonium sensing may be located in the root cap. Then, cell elongation on the opposite side of the source is promoted (Yamazaki et al., 2020). Finally, the root tip bends toward the ammonium source.
(B) Generation of polar auxin transport in a root cell for nutritropism. Because asymmetric auxin distribution is sufficient for bending in plant roots, the nutritropic defect of osaux1 means that there is no or insufficient asymmetric auxin distribution in its root tips. This defect was recovered by exogenous NAA supplementation (Figure 5), meaning that NAA supplementation resulted in generation of polar auxin transport and subsequent asymmetric auxin distribution, similar to those in WT. This suggests that polarity of auxin influx is not required for establishing polar auxin transport. Therefore, auxin efflux transporters need to be polarized to generate the polar auxin flow. Further studies are needed to prove the involvement of polarized auxin efflux transporters in nutritropism.
Our experiments with exogenous auxin supplementation demonstrated that OsAUX1 functions for nutritropism were complemented by the exogenous NAA treatment but not by 2,4‐D (Figure 5), and these results resemble previous studies on NAA effects on gravitropism of aux1 mutants in Arabidopsis and rice (Marchant et al., 1999; Zhao et al., 2015). These results suggest that polarized auxin influx is not required to generate asymmetric auxin distribution in nutritropism and gravitropism because NAA uptake into root cells probably occurs non‐polarly. Molecular models of root gravitropism therefore suggest that the asymmetry is controlled by the polarized auxin efflux transporters (Su et al., 2017; Tiwari et al., 2023). We propose a similar model for the establishment of asymmetric auxin distribution in root tips for nutritropism (Figure 6B). The critical inhibition of nutritropism by treatment with N‐1‐naphthylphthalamic acid (NPA), an auxin efflux inhibitor, observed in our previous study further supports this idea (Yamazaki et al., 2024). Further studies are needed to prove the involvement of polarized auxin efflux transporters in nutritropism.
Nutritropism implies the presence of an ammonium gradient–sensing mechanism in rice roots, which likely triggers asymmetric auxin distribution upon ammonium perception. Since asymmetric RFP fluorescence was already observed in the root cap region (Figure 4B), the cells responsible for ammonium sensing might be located in the root cap. However, to the best of our knowledge, no ammonium sensor protein has been reported in plants, and the mechanisms of ammonium sensing and subsequent auxin redistribution remain unknown. At least, OsAUX1 is not involved in ammonium sensing, as osaux1 mutants still responded to the ammonium gradient under NAA treatment (Figure 5).
In natural environments, plant shoots and roots perceive directional stimuli, such as light, gravitation, soil moisture, and nutrients, and there are complex interactions among tropisms (Iino, 2006; Takahashi et al., 2003). In Arabidopsis roots, a moisture gradient for hydrotropism leads to asymmetric cytokinin distribution, with higher cytokinin levels on the side opposite the bending direction (Chang et al., 2019). Auxin accumulates on the bending direction side in gravitropism and nutritropism, opposite to the cytokinin distribution in hydrotropism. Because auxin and cytokinin signaling pathways antagonize each other (Kurepa & Smalle, 2022), the distributions of these hormones may contribute to the interactions between different tropisms. When gravitropic/nutritropic and hydrotropic stimuli induce bending in opposite directions, the distributions of auxin and cytokinin overlap, which may result in antagonism. When both stimuli induce bending in the same direction, the distributions of auxin and cytokinin become opposite, potentially generating additive curvature by promoting cell elongation and division in the same tissue. This antagonism and synergism might underlie the interaction mechanisms between gravitropism/nutritropism and hydrotropism. In contrast, the interaction between gravitropism and nutritropism may result from the competition for auxin flow. The interaction mechanisms among the three tropisms and other root behaviors should be investigated in further studies. Understanding these interactions will elucidate how the environmental cues are integrated to determine the optimal growth direction of each root in soil.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant materials and growth conditions
We used japonica rice cultivars Hitomebore and Nipponbare (Oryza sativa L.). The mutant #633 was isolated from an EMS‐mutagenized population in the Hitomebore background; this population was kindly provided by Dr. Ryohei Terauchi and was used previously (Fekih et al., 2013; Tanaka et al., 2016). The mutation of #633 in OsAUX1 (MSU ID, LOC_Os01g63770; RAP‐DB ID, Os01g0856500) was determined as follows: genomic DNA was extracted from Hitomebore and #633, and the genomic region containing the mutation was amplified by PCR with the forward primer 5′‐TACGCACTCACGCAGCTGAC‐3′ and the reverse primer 5′‐CAAACGATCATCAGTCACTG‐3′. The amplicons were pre‐treated with a cleanup reagent, ExoSAP‐IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and then Sanger‐sequenced with the primer 5′‐CCAGGTATAACTCATCCTGC‐3′. CRISPR/Cas9 mutant lines were generated in the Nipponbare background. To visualize auxin accumulation or signaling activity in the lateral root tips, the line harboring the RFP gene under the control of an auxin‐responsive DR5 promoter (DR5::RFP reporter) in the Nipponbare background was used. The line was generated by transformation with the Agrobacterium strain EHA101 carrying the DR5rev∷mRFPer construct, which was kindly provided by Dr. David Jackson (Gallavotti et al., 2008).
Rice plants were grown on agar plates as described previously (Yamazaki & Fujiwara, 2022). Sterilized seeds were sown and seedlings were grown on agar plates with growth medium at a vertical angle of 60° under continuous light at 28°C in a growth chamber. This angle lets roots grow on the bottom of the plate, and this is helpful for obtaining clear images. The growth medium was 1:200‐diluted MS medium (pH 5.7–5.8, (Murashige & Skoog, 1962)) containing 2% (w/v) sucrose (guaranteed reagent; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), 0.05% (v/v) Plant Preservative Mixture (PPM) (Plant Cell Technology Inc., Washington, DC, USA), and 1.5% agar (Agar Purified, powder, Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and 60 ml of the medium was added to a square plate (sterile Schale No. 2, Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Stock solutions (50 μM) in dimethyl sulfoxide were prepared for 1‐naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA, a membrane‐permeable synthetic auxin; 148–00092, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and 2,4‐dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4‐D, a membrane‐impermeable synthetic auxin, first grade; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). In the supplemented conditions, stock solutions were added to a final concentration of 10 nM to the growth medium before autoclaving.

## Preparation of nutrient sources
Nutrient sources containing 10× MS salts were prepared as reported previously (Yamazaki et al., 2020). We used 10× MS sources rather than sole ammonium sources due to slightly stronger bending toward the 10× MS sources. The ammonium concentration in the 10× MS sources is ~ 206 mM. A plastic pipette tip (1–200 μl; Watson Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was filled with 10 μl of 1.5% (w/v) agar (Agar Purified powder, Nacalai Tesque) gel using a micropipette before the agar solidified. The gel also contained 0.05% (v/v) PPM to prevent microbial contamination. The mock sources contained the same contents except MS salts.

## Nutritropic bioassay
A nutritropic bioassay was established previously (Yamazaki et al., 2020). Lateral roots generated from seminal roots of 6‐ or 7‐day‐old seedlings were tested. Some of the crown roots were removed from agar media if they elongate near the lateral roots to avoid physical interference with lateral root bending. Nutrient or mock sources described above were inserted ~1 cm away from the seminal root. The bending curvature of lateral roots toward each source was measured at 12 or 24 h later. As described previously, only the lateral roots whose tips at 0 h were located within 1 cm of the inserted source were analyzed to quantify nutritropic bending. Lateral roots whose tips were located above the inserted sources at 0 h were excluded from analysis, because it would be difficult to distinguish their downward bending toward nutrient sources from gravitropism. The bending of each lateral root toward the nutrient source was evaluated with θ 1/θ 2 values on the root image in ImageJ software (v. 1.53t, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) (Yamazaki et al., 2020). No lateral root initiated after the source insertion was measured. In the present study, the θ 1/θ 2 values were referred to as “bending curvature score.” Higher values of this score indicate larger bending toward the source, that is, stronger nutritropic response. Because lateral roots from primary roots in WTs grew approximately horizontally due to the downward elongation of the primary roots, the nutritropic bending direction is upward and opposite to the gravitational direction in our quantification as described above. This orientation is important for reliably quantifying nutritropism. To apply the same measurement to osaux1 mutants, including #633, we selected plants that exhibited relatively downward primary root elongation before these nutritropic bioassays. A median value of all measured lateral roots (≥3) per seedling was used as the biological replicate for this quantification.

## Image stacking for analysis of root bending curvature
Grayscale images of seedlings on agar plates were acquired at 400 dpi resolution using an image scanner with a positive film transparency unit (Epson GT‐X970; Seiko Epson Corp., Nagano, Japan). The plates were scanned at 0 h and at the indicated time points after the insertion of nutrient or mock sources. Images of a seedling were stacked in ImageJ software and aligned by Rigid Body using the StackReg plugin (Thevenaz et al., 1998). The new roots that grew after source insertion were highlighted in red in the stacked images by manipulation with the “Stack to RGB,” “Make Composite,” and “Arrange Channel” functions in ImageJ.

## Bulked segregant analysis to identify loci responsible for the nutritropic defect of #633
F1 plants were obtained by crossing #633 with Hitomebore as the pollen parent, and F2 seeds were harvested from the F1 plants. In the nutritropic bioassay, F2 plants with lateral roots gathering around 10× MS sources (normal nutritropism) and main roots elongating downward (normal gravitropism) were considered as the WT type. F2 plants with a gravitropism‐defective root system, that is, upward elongation and coiling, were considered as the #633 type. Genomic DNA was extracted from 20 F2 seedlings from the WT bulk and #633 mutant bulk using the DNeasy Plant Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The DNA was subjected to next‐generation sequencing (NGS) and analyzed by the SIMPLE pipeline to obtain a candidate mutation list (Wachsman et al., 2017). In this pipeline, NGS reads were aligned to the O. sativa cv. Nipponbare reference genome (IRGSP‐1.0 in RAP‐DB; https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp) (Kawahara et al., 2013; Sakai et al., 2013) in BWA (Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner) software (Li & Durbin, 2009). Alignment files were converted to SAM/BAM files in SAMtools (Li et al., 2009) and Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard), and nucleotide variants were called by GATK HaplotypeCaller (DePristo et al., 2011; McKenna et al., 2010; Van der Auwera et al., 2013). The “ratio” score was defined and calculated in the SIMPLE pipeline for each nucleotide variant; this ratio indicates the bias in allele frequency for each variant between the two bulk NGS data. All nucleotide variants were annotated for their potential effects in SnpEff 4.3 t software (Cingolani et al., 2012).

## CRISPR/Cas9‐based genome editing
To generate osaux1 CRISPR/Cas9 mutant lines for OsAUX1, we designed the target sequence for the guide RNA, 5′‐CTTGGTACCTCGCCATCGCC‐3′, with CRISPR‐P (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/). Two primers with the target sequences, 5′‐GTTGGGCGATGGCGAGGTACCAAG‐3′ and 5′‐AAACCTTGGTACCTCGCCATCGCC‐3′, were annealed, resulting in oligo dimers with protruding ends. The dimer was inserted into a BbsI‐linearized cloning vector, pU6gRNA (Shan et al., 2013). The resulting plasmid was digested with AscI and PacI. The excised fragment was recloned into the AscI‐ and PacI‐digested binary vector pZDgRNA_Cas9 ver.2_HPT (Endo et al., 2015). Sequences of the inserts of both constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Agrobacterium strain EHA101 carrying the binary vector was used to transform Nipponbare according to Nishimura et al. (Nishimura et al., 2006). To identify mutations around the CRISPR/Cas9 target sequences of OsAUX1, genomic DNA was extracted from the transgenic plants at T1 or later generations, and the genomic region containing the target sequence was amplified by PCR with the same primers described above. The amplicons were pre‐treated by ExoSAP‐IT and then Sanger‐sequenced with the primer 5′‐CCACGGTGTTCATCCTGC‐3′. Finally, three independent transgenic mutant lines were established: aux1‐2 and aux1‐3 carried the same mutation, c.651delG, and aux1‐4 carried c.650–652delGGT.

## RFP fluorescence observation
Nipponbare plants carrying the DR5::RFP reporter or WT Nipponbare as a control (to estimate the background autofluorescence) were grown on agar plates as described above. At 48 h after the insertion of nutrient and mock sources, lateral roots coiling around the nutrient sources or elongating near the mock sources were cut and immediately placed into 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in PBS for fixation. The samples were incubated with gentle agitation for 1 h at room temperature, rinsed twice with PBS and transferred to ClearSee solution (Kurihara et al., 2015). After 24 h at room temperature, the samples were transferred to fresh ClearSee solution containing 0.1% (v/v) Calcofluor White (Fluorescent Brightener 28 disodium salt solution (25%), Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated for 30 min to stain cell walls. The samples were rinsed once in fresh ClearSee solution without Calcofluor White, transferred to fresh ClearSee solution and incubated for >1 h until observation.
The root samples were mounted in ClearSee solution on slides. Root tips were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope FV3000 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). RFP fluorescence was detected using excitation at 561 nm and emission at 570–670 nm. Calcofluor White fluorescence was detected using excitation at 405 nm and emission at 430–470 nm. Mean RFP fluorescence intensity (intensity per area) and RFP fluorescence intensity (the mean intensity × measured area) in epidermis (single cell layer) and cortex (two cell layers adjacent to epidermis) on either side were measured and calculated using ImageJ. Then, total “mean RFP fluorescence intensity” in the four tissues of a root tip was calculated from the total measured area and total intensity of the four tissues. Finally, relative RFP fluorescence intensity of each tissue was calculated as follows: RelativeRFPfluorescence intensitytissue=MeanRFPfluorescence intensitytissueMeanRFPfluorescence intensitytotal

## Figure Figure 6: A hypothetical model for nutritropism.(A) Root bending toward an ammonium source. When a root tip is exposed to an ammonium gradient, it senses the gradient by an unknown mechanism (Yamazaki et al., 2020). Sensing induces preferential auxin transport to the side closer to the ammonium source (red arrows). An asymmetric auxin distribution was observed in the root cap region (Figure 4), suggesting the possibility that the cells responsible for ammonium sensing may be located in the root cap. Then, cell elongation on the opposite side of the source is promoted (Yamazaki et al., 2020). Finally, the root tip bends toward the ammonium source.(B) Generation of polar auxin transport in a root cell for nutritropism. Because asymmetric auxin distribution is sufficient for bending in plant roots, the nutritropic defect of osaux1 means that there is no or insufficient asymmetric auxin distribution in its root tips. This defect was recovered by exogenous NAA supplementation (Figure 5), meaning that NAA supplementation resulted in generation of polar auxin transport and subsequent asymmetric auxin distribution, similar to those in WT. This suggests that polarity of auxin influx is not required for establishing polar auxin transport. Therefore, auxin efflux transporters need to be polarized to generate the polar auxin flow. Further studies are needed to prove the involvement of polarized auxin efflux transporters in nutritropism.
