# ROPGAP3 interacts with PIN2 and modulates its clustering and trafficking in Arabidopsis

## Abstract
SignificanceSmall guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-hydrolyzing enzymes (GTPases) are pivotal in various essential cellular processes in eukaryotic cells. GTPase activating proteins (GAPs) have traditionally been known as components of the small GTPase signaling module, inactivating GTPases by catalyzing the hydrolysis of GTP. In our quest to identify intracellular modulators of PIN-FORMED (PIN) auxin transporters, we found that Arabidopsis ROPGAP3 directly interacts with the central hydrophilic loop of PIN, modulating its clustering, endocytosis, and polarity. Our study unveils a molecular and cellular function of GAPs in eukaryotic cells, beyond their canonical role in Rho small GTPase Of Plants (ROP) signaling, and introduces a modulator for PIN polarity, which is crucial for auxin-mediated plant growth and development.Keywords: auxin transport, clustering of membrane protein, GTPase activating protein (GAP), PIN-FORMED (PIN), Rho of plant (ROP)
Small guanine nucleotide-binding proteins belonging to the Ras superfamily (hereafter, small GTPases) serve as versatile molecular switches in various biochemical and cellular processes, such as signal transduction and membrane trafficking, and these roles are evolutionarily conserved in eukaryotic cells (1, 2). Small GTPases undergo a guanine nucleotide-dependent conformational change between the GTP-bound active form and the GDP-bound inactive form. Among the Ras superfamily members, the Rho (Ras homology) family is involved in diverse cellular signaling processes, including cytoskeletal reorganization, polarity, cell proliferation, differentiation, endocytosis, exocytosis, and gene expression (1, 2). Plant Rho members form a unique subfamily called ROP (Rho Of Plants) (3). ROPs perform most of the aforementioned Rho functions and plant-specific signaling processes (4). Phytohormone auxin inhibits the endocytosis of recycling plasma membrane (PM) proteins, such as PIN-FORMED (PIN) auxin efflux transporters (5, 6). In Arabidopsis leaf pavement cells and root epidermal cells, ROP2 and ROP6 signaling mediate this process for PIN1 and PIN2, respectively (7–9).
GTP hydrolysis and GDP/GTP exchange of small GTPases are catalyzed by GTPase activating proteins (GAPs) and Guanine nucleotide Exchange Factors (GEFs), respectively, indicating that GAPs play a negative role and GEFs play a positive role in GTPase signaling (10). Plants have two types of GAPs, distinguished by the additional motif/domain to the conserved GAP domain: CRIB (Cdc42/Rac-Interactive Binding) type and PH (Pleckstrin Homology) type (11–13). The CRIB-type GAP (ROPGAP) is unique to plants, and the CRIB motif is required to interact with ROP and for subcellular localization of ROPGAP (11, 14, 15). Conversely, the PH domain of PH-type GAP (RENGAP, ROP1 Enhancer GAP) interacts with phosphoinositides and does not participate in the subcellular localization of RENGAP (12). There are 6 ROPGAPs and 3 RENGAPs in Arabidopsis.
Studies on GAPs have predominantly focused on their interaction with ROPs (15, 16) and subcellular localization (11, 12, 14, 17, 18). Several recent studies reported the biological functions of RENGAPs by observing their mutant phenotypes, such as cell division pattern, leaf pavement cell shaping, and moss tip growth (19–22). All these studies focused on the role of GAPs in association with ROP signaling.
PIN auxin efflux carriers play critical roles in plant growth and development by forming local auxin gradients, which are enabled by PIN’s polar localization in the PM (23, 24). The central hydrophilic loop (HL) domain of PIN proteins is a key cis-acting factor for PINs to exert polar endomembrane trafficking and thus localize asymmetrically in the PM. The HL domain includes a motif for clathrin-mediated PIN endocytosis (25), ubiquitylation sites for PIN turnover (26), and various phosphorylation codes for modulating PIN polarity and activity (27). The vast physiological spectrum of auxin action implies that PINs are subject to modulation by diverse developmental and environmental cues, and the HL domain of PINs could carry various cis-acting elements to accommodate those cues.
Recent efforts to search for trans-acting factors interacting with the PIN-HL domain (called PIN-interacting proteins) led to discoveries of further cis-acting elements in the HL domain and their trans-acting PIN-interacting proteins, such as calcium-dependent protein kinase (CPK29) that facilitates PIN’s recycling exocytosis by phosphorylating HL (28) and a phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate 5-kinase (FAB1C) that modulates PIN’s lytic trafficking in the HL phosphorylation-dependent manner (29).
Here, we identified Arabidopsis ROPGAP3 as a PIN-interacting protein that directly interacts with PINs and modulates PIN’s clustering, lateral diffusion, endocytosis, polarity, and lytic trafficking independently of the canonical ROP signaling. This study provides insight into a molecular function of GAPs in eukaryotic cells beyond typical small GTPase signaling.

## Results

## ROPGAP3 Interacts Directly with PIN-HL.
Protein pulldown of protein extracts from Arabidopsis seedlings using the PIN3-HL domain as bait, followed by mass spectrometry analysis, identified ROPGAP3 as a PIN interactor. The in vitro pulldown assay confirmed that ROPGAP3 interacted with the HL domains of PIN1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 1A). The yeast two-hybrid assay demonstrated that PIN2- and PIN3-HL interacted with ROPGAP3, with PIN3-HL showing a stronger interaction (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). To identify the specific HL region interacting with ROPGAP3, the HL domain of PIN3 was divided into three regions (HL1, 2, and 3; SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). The in vitro pulldown assay revealed that HL1 and HL2 regions interacted with ROPGAP3, but the HL3 region did not (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). To determine whether the interaction of PIN-HL with ROPGAP3 affects its GAP activity, the ROPGAP3-catalyzed GTPase activity of ROP6 was tested with increasing PIN2-HL protein concentrations. The GAP activity of ROPGAP3 was not significantly compromised by the interaction with PIN2-HL (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

Next, we tried to approximately confine the region of ROPGAP3 that is required for the interaction with PIN-HL. ROPGAP proteins include a CRIB motif and a GAP catalytic domain (Fig. 1B). The CRIB motif interacts with ROP, and the GAP domain catalyzes ROP-mediated GTP hydrolysis (11). We divided ROPGAP3 into three regions, the N-terminal region including the CRIB motif, the GAP domain, and the rest C-terminal region, for the in vitro pulldown assay with PIN2-HL (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). The assay exhibited that these three regions were all capable of interacting with PIN2-HL (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). GalaxyWEB (30) was used to predict the putative residues or motifs of ROPGAP3 involved in interaction with PIN-HL. HL domains from different PINs and regions of the HL domain were used to predict the interaction with the whole sequence of ROPGAP3. Predictions with the HL1 region of PIN3-HL identified putative interacting residues within a small region between 415 and 438 amino acids near the C-terminus of ROPGAP3 with the best scores in different models (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). We termed this region the putative “PIN-Interacting motif (PI).” Most residues of PI were predicted to be located in the random coiled region by AlphaFold (31) 3-dimensional modeling (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). We experimentally tested the function of PI by conducting in vitro pulldown assays with intact and PI-deleted (ΔPI) ROPGAP3 to determine whether the PI region is required for interacting with PIN-HL. PIN2 was selected as a model among other PINs because its subcellular behaviors are readily observable in root epidermal cells. The pulldown assay revealed that the deletion of PI significantly reduced ROPGAP3’s interaction capability with PIN2-HL, suggesting that PI is at least partially required for the in vitro interaction (Fig. 1C). Testing the GTPase activity of ROP6 with intact ROPGAP3 and ΔPI-ROPGAP3 showed that the deletion of PI did not affect the GAP activity of ROPGAP3 (Fig. 1D).
To confirm the interaction between ROPGAP3 and PIN-HL and the function of PI in vivo, we conducted Förster Resonance Energy Transfer by Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging (FRET-FLIM) analysis using cotransgenic plants expressing ProROPGAP3:RFP:ROPGAP3 or ProROPGAP3:RFP:ΔPI-ROPGAP3 and ProPIN2:PIN2:GFP constructs (RFP or GFP, Red or Green Fluorescent Protein). ProROPGAP3:RFP:ROPGAP3 showed considerable expression in root epidermal tissue, overlapping with ProPIN2:PIN2:GFP expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). ROPGAP3 mainly localized to the PM region, with some distribution in the cytoplasm of epidermal cells in the meristem and transition zones (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The sharp colocalization of RFP and GFP signals at the PM region suggested that ROPGAP3 and PIN2 colocalize at the PM (Fig. 2A). The subcellular localization of ΔPI-ROPGAP3 was similar to that of intact ROPGAP3, indicating that PI deletion does not affect ROPGAP3’s subcellular localization (Fig. 2A). FRET-FLIM analysis showed a considerable decrease in the fluorescence lifetime of PIN2:GFP in cotransgenic plants with RFP:ROPGAP3 and PIN2:GFP, indicating resonance energy transfer and physical interaction in vivo. In contrast, PIN2:GFP lifetime was not decreased in plants with RFP:ΔPI-ROPGAP3, suggesting that PI is essential for the interaction in vivo (Fig. 1F). Subcellular FRET-FLIM analysis showed strong interaction between ROPGAP3 and PIN2 in both PM and cytoplasm, while ΔPI-ROPGAP3 marginally interacted with PIN2 in the cytoplasm (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–D).

Next, we tested whether the interaction with PIN-HL is general among ROPGAP homologs. In Angiosperms, at least three groups of CRIB-type ROPGAP are conserved; ROPGAP2 clade, ROPGAP1,4,5 clade, and ROPGAP3 clade (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We chose ROPGAP1 and tested its in vitro and in vivo interaction with PIN2-HL. ROPGAP1 localized mainly in the cytoplasm (Figs. 1G and 2A). On the other hand, exogenous auxin (1-naphthaleneacetic acid, NAA) induced a weak but statistically significant localization of ROPGAP1 at the PM region, while the PM localization of ROPGAP3 was unchanged by auxin treatment (Fig. 2B). Unlike ROPGAP3, ROPGAP1 did not show significant in vivo interaction with PIN2-HL in either PM or cytoplasm (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E), nor in the in vitro pulldown assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F). Despite high overall homology among CRIB-type ROPGAP paralogs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), local homology around the PI region was conspicuous only among ROPGAP3 orthologs in Angiosperms, barely found in other Arabidopsis paralogs and outgroup orthologs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). This suggests that the PI region endows ROPGAP3 with the specific capability of interacting with PIN-HL.

## ROPGAP3 Reinforces PIN Polarity.
Given the involvement of ROP signaling in intracellular PIN trafficking and polarity (7–9, 32), we investigated the role of ROPGAP3 in this context, focusing on PIN2. We first observed the polarity changes of PIN2 in the loss-of-function ropgap3 and ropgap1 mutant backgrounds (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). The apical polarity of PIN2 in root epidermal cells was significantly compromised in two ropgap3 mutant alleles, as well as in the ropgap1 mutant. In these mutants, the PIN2:GFP signal extended more to the lateral PM sides, with the polarity defect being more severe in the ropgap3 mutants compared to ropgap1 (Fig. 3 A and B).

To complement the mutant results, we examined PIN2 polarity in overexpression (ox) lines of ROPGAP3, ROPGAP1, and ΔPI-ROPGAP3. In contrast to the loss-of-function mutants, ROPGAP3 overexpression (ROPGAP3ox) dramatically enhanced apical PIN2 polarity by significantly reducing the lateral localization of PIN2. However, ROPGAP1ox did not significantly affect PIN2 polarity (Fig. 3 A and C). This suggests that ROPGAP3 and ROPGAP1 might operate differently in establishing PIN2 polarity. Additionally, the deletion of the PI region from ROPGAP3 (ΔPI-ROPGAP3) almost completely eliminated the enhancement of PIN2 polarity seen in ROPGAP3ox lines, indicating that the interaction of ROPGAP3 with PIN2 via the PI region is crucial for reinforcing PIN2 polarity (Fig. 3 A and C). The lack of PIN2 polarity enhancement in ROPGAP1ox and ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox lines was unlikely due to lower expression levels of these transgenes, as their protein levels were comparable to those of the ROPGAP3ox line (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C).
In addition to influencing PIN2 polarity, ROPGAP3 also played a role in establishing the polarity of PIN3 in root pericycle cells, whereas the polarity of PIN1 in root vascular cells did not appear to be affected by ROPGAP3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

## ROPGAP3 Enhances PIN2 Clustering in the PM.
While investigating the role of ROPGAP3 in establishing PIN2 polarity, we observed a significant effect of ROPGAP3 on the uneven distribution of PIN2 proteins along the PM Fig. 3 D and E). To assess the degree of uneven distribution, the fluorescence signal intensity of PIN2:GFP was scanned along the apical PM of root epidermal cells, and the degree of uneven distribution was expressed as the SD from the mean value of the PIN2:GFP signal intensity along the PM (Fig. 3 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). We first tested whether ROPGAP3-induced uneven PIN2 distribution in the PM was due to functional clustering or simple protein aggregation. Unlike aggregation, clustering is a reversible process, and treatments with the sterol-binding agent filipin can reduce PIN clustering (25). Filipin reduced ROPGAP3-induced uneven PIN2 distribution to the WT level within 0.5 h after treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This result indicates that ROPGAP3 enhances PIN2 clustering rather than aggregation.
When ropgap mutants were observed, PIN2 clustering in the ropgap3 mutant was reduced by 18% compared to the WT level, whereas the clustering level in the ropgap1 mutant was not significantly reduced (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–C). In contrast, ROPGAP3ox greatly enhanced PIN2 clustering in the PM by 48% (Fig. 3 D, E, and G). However, ROPGAP1ox and ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox did not increase PIN2 clustering (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D and E). Furthermore, when the colocalization between PIN2 and intact ROPGAP3 or ΔPI-ROPGAP3 in the PM (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B) was compared, the colocalization correlation between the two proteins was higher with intact ROPGAP3 than with ΔPI-ROPGAP3. The correlation slope and the coefficient of correlation were higher with intact ROPGAP3 than with ΔPI-ROPGAP3 (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). These results suggest that ROPGAP3, unlike ROPGAP1, specifically enhances PIN2 clustering, and its PI-mediated physical interaction with PIN2 in the PM is necessary for this process.
Next, we investigated whether ROPGAP3-mediated PIN2 clustering depends on conventional ROP signaling. PIN2 clustering was assessed through two complementary approaches: 1) mutations in the ROP-interacting CRIB motif of ROPGAP3, and 2) loss- and gain-of-function modifications of ROP6, a major ROP isoform involved in PIN2 trafficking. The CRIB motif, particularly a conserved histidine (H113), is essential for ROP–ROPGAP interaction and GAP activity (11). In our in vitro protein pulldown and GTPase assays, both an H113Y substitution and deletion of the CRIB motif significantly reduced ROPGAP3’s interaction with ROP6 and compromised its GAP activity, as measured by ROP6 GTP hydrolysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B). To evaluate whether these CRIB mutations affect PIN2 clustering, we compared PIN2 distribution in ROPGAP3 overexpression lines carrying either CRIB mutation with wild-type ROPGAP3 overexpression lines. Notably, these CRIB-mutated ROPGAP3ox lines did not significantly affect PIN2 clustering (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A–D), suggesting that GAP activity may be involved in ROPGAP3-mediated PIN2 clustering.
However, because the CRIB-containing N-terminal region of ROPGAP3 also interacts with the HL of PIN2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E), we further tested whether these mutations affect the ROPGAP3–PIN2 interaction. In vitro pulldown assays showed that the CRIB mutations reduced ROPGAP3’s binding to PIN2-HL (SI Appendix, Fig. S13E). In vivo expression of the CRIB-mutated ROPGAP3 in root epidermal cells revealed no notable changes in subcellular localization (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 F and G). However, FRET-FLIM analyses demonstrated that the CRIB mutations impaired ROPGAP3–PIN2 interaction at the PM, while the cytoplasmic interaction remained unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 H and I). These findings suggest that the CRIB motif may interact with PIN2 either without ROP or simultaneously with ROP at the PM.
To further assess the role of ROP signaling in PIN2 clustering, we analyzed PIN2 distribution in loss-of-function rop6-2 mutant, constitutively active gain-of-function rop6-ca mutant, and ROP6 overexpression (ROP6ox) lines. ROP6 is a well-characterized regulator of PIN2 trafficking in the Arabidopsis root (9, 33). As expected, apical PIN2 polarity was reduced in both rop6-2 and ROP6ox backgrounds compared to the wild type (Fig. 3 H and I), consistent with disrupted ROP-regulated PIN2 trafficking (7, 8, 32). However, PIN2 clustering levels were not significantly altered in any of these ROP6 mutant or overexpression lines (Fig. 3J and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 F–I). Together, these findings indicate that canonical ROP signaling activity, as mediated by ROP6, is not required for ROPGAP3-mediated PIN2 clustering.

## ROPGAP3 Inhibits Lateral Diffusion of PIN2 in the PM.
PIN clustering is implicated in PIN polarity establishment by restraining the lateral diffusion of PIN in the PM (25). To determine whether ROPGAP3-mediated PIN clustering is linked to the inhibition of lateral diffusion of PIN, we compared the lateral diffusion of PIN2 in WT, ROPGAP3ox, ROPGAP1ox, ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox, and ropgap mutant lines using Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) analysis. The FRAP rate of PIN protein can be affected by both protein exocytosis and lateral diffusion in the PM (28, 34). To eliminate the impact of PIN exocytosis during the FRAP assay, the seedling samples were treated with cycloheximide (to inhibit de novo PIN synthesis) and brefeldin A (BFA; to inhibit exocytosis of PIN).
In the presence of BFA, the FRAP rate of PIN2 was considerably reduced in all lines, indicating the contribution of PIN2 exocytosis to the recovery. However, a certain level of FRAP remained, suggesting that lateral diffusion of PIN2 also contributed to the recovery (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Therefore, this FRAP rate under BFA treatment was considered the lateral diffusion rate of PIN2. ROPGAP3ox significantly inhibited PIN2 lateral diffusion by 41% 10 min after photobleaching compared to the WT level, whereas ROPGAP1ox and ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox scarcely affected PIN2 lateral diffusion (Fig. 4 A–C). The difference in FRAP rates between the WT and ROPGAP3ox lines was more pronounced upon BFA treatment, indicating that ROPGAP3 considerably suppresses the lateral diffusion of PIN2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A–C). In addition, PIN2 lateral diffusion was slightly (~17.8%) increased in the ropgap3 mutant but not changed in ropgap1, rop6, rop6-ca, and ROP6ox lines compared to WT (Fig. 4 D–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 D–K), suggesting that native ROPGAP3 specifically inhibits PIN2 lateral diffusion and this does not rely on canonical ROP activity.

## ROPGAP3 Shows Dual Functions in PIN2 Endocytosis.
The inhibition of PIN2 endocytosis is associated with PIN2 clustering in the PM (35, 36). To investigate the role of ROPGAP3 in PIN2 endocytosis, we used the BFA-body formation system to estimate the PIN2 endocytosis rate. BFA treatment inhibits PIN exocytosis and lytic trafficking, resulting in the formation of internal vesicles called “BFA bodies” (37, 38). Because endocytosed PIN proteins recycle back to the PM via recycling exocytosis or target to the vacuole via lytic trafficking, the accumulation of BFA bodies correlates with the PIN endocytosis rate when de novo PIN synthesis is blocked by cycloheximide (37, 38). On the other hand, auxin-mediated ROP signaling inhibits PIN endocytosis (9, 33; SI Appendix, Fig. S15 A and B). Given that ROP signaling inhibits PIN endocytosis and ROPGAPs switch off ROP activity, ROPGAPs are thought to play a positive role in PIN endocytosis.
We tested whether ROPGAP3 follows this rule during PIN2 endocytosis under different ROP-signaling strengths in the ropgap3 mutant and ROPGAP3ox lines, comparing them with ROPGAP1 counterparts. We hypothesized that ROPGAP3 and ROPGAP1 might show different behaviors in terms of PIN2 endocytosis. This hypothesis is based on the fact that both ROPGAP3 and ROPGAP1 participate in ROP activity (Fig. 1D; 17), but only ROPGAP3 interacts with PIN2 and enhances its polarity, inhibits lateral diffusion, and enhances clustering (Figs. 3 A and G and 4 A–E). While the former fact enhances PIN endocytosis, the latter fact should inhibit it.
In terms of ROP signaling, the loss of ROPGAP will enhance ROP signaling and thus decrease PIN2 endocytosis (i.e., BFA body formation). The loss of ROPGAP1 decreased PIN2 endocytosis accordingly to this hypothesis, whereas the loss of ROPGAP3 did not decrease PIN2 endocytosis (Fig. 4I). This could result from ROPGAP3’s dual functions; increased ROP signaling (i.e., enhanced PIN2 endocytosis) and reduced PIN2 clustering (i.e., decreased PIN2 endocytosis) could be balanced under the loss of ROPGAP3. Conversely, auxin (NAA) treatment decreased PIN2 endocytosis even in the ropgap3 mutant most likely due to auxin-induced high ROP signaling (9, 33), suggesting a balance shift to high ROP signaling-mediated inhibition of PIN2 endocytosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S15C).
Overexpression of ROPGAP3 and ROPGAP1 showed opposite effects on PIN2 endocytosis: ROPGAP3ox suppressed, while ROPGAP1ox enhanced PIN2 endocytosis dramatically (Fig. 4J). If a ROPGAP is involved only in inhibiting ROP signaling, it should always stimulate PIN2 endocytosis, as seen in ROPGAP1ox. In contrast, the inhibition of PIN2 endocytosis by ROPGAP3ox likely results from ROPGAP3-mediated PIN2 clustering in the PM, which exceeds ROPGAP3ox-mediated inhibition of ROP signaling (i.e., stimulation of PIN2 endocytosis). Additionally, comparing the correlation between ROPGAP3 dose and PIN2 endocytosis under different ROP-signaling strengths (+ or -NAA) showed that increasing ROPGAP3 doses significantly decreased PIN2 endocytosis, a tendency lessened by auxin-induced strong ROP-signaling conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S15 D–G).
These results suggest that ROPGAP1 participates only in the inhibition of ROP signaling (i.e., stimulation of PIN2 endocytosis), whereas ROPGAP3 exhibits dual functions: stimulation and inhibition of PIN2 endocytosis through inhibiting ROP signaling and enhancing PIN2 clustering in the PM, respectively.

## ROPGAP3 Exhibits Distinct Biological Functions in Root Gravitropism and Lateral Root Development.
Given ROPGAP3’s involvement in the endocytosis and polarity establishment of PIN, we investigated its role in auxin-related biological processes. Overexpression of ROPGAP3 significantly compromised root gravitropism, whereas overexpression of ROPGAP1 or ΔPI-ROPGAP3 did not affect root gravitropism (Fig. 5A). This indicates that ROPGAP3 specifically operates in root gravitropism, requiring the PI-mediated interaction with PIN. PIN2 degradation in the upper epidermal cells of the horizontally gravity-stimulated root is critical for root-gravitropic responses, and PIN2 clustering is linked to the endocytosis and vacuolar degradation of PIN2 (39). Therefore, we examined this PIN2 trafficking behavior during root gravitropism in these transgenic lines. The ratio of PIN2 protein at the apical PM between upper and lower epidermal cells was significantly decreased in WT, ROPGAP1ox, and ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox lines, whereas the ratio did not change in the ROPGAP3ox line after gravity stimulation (Fig. 5 B and C). Similarly, while the upper epidermal cells of WT, ROPGAP1ox, and ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox lines accumulated endocytosed internal PIN2 vesicles, ROPGAP3ox did not form internal vesicles after gravity stimulation (Fig. 5 B and D), consistent with the observation that ROPGAP3ox decreased PIN2 endocytosis (Fig. 4J). These results align with their gravitropic responses, suggesting that ROPGAP3 specifically modulates root gravitropism by interacting with PIN2 and inhibiting its endocytosis in the epidermal cells of ROPGAP3ox plants.

(E) Vacuolar-trafficked PIN2 in root epidermal cells of WT and ropgap mutants. PIN2:GFP signals were observed 12 h after dark treatment. Bar = 10 µm. (F) The signal intensity ratio of internalized vs. PM PIN2:GFP as shown in E (n = 35 to 130 cells from 7 to 26 seedlings). Different letters indicate significant differences (ANOVA test, P < 0.05). (G) The canonical ROP signaling model in PIN2 endocytosis. ROPGAP3 enhances PIN2 endocytosis by inhibiting ROP in auxin-mediated ROP signaling. (H) The direct interaction model of ROPGAP3 with PIN2. ROPGAP3 interacts with, enhances clustering, and inhibits lateral diffusion and endocytosis of PIN2, resulting in the reinforcement of PIN2 polarity in the PM. The ROPGAP3-mediated PIN2 clustering in endosomes may facilitate the lytic trafficking of PIN2.
The ropgap3 loss-of-function mutants also exhibited gravitropic defects in the root, which were rescued by reintroducing ROPGAP3 into the mutant, indicating that ROPGAP3 is required for normal root gravitropism (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A). In contrast, the ropgap1 mutant did not show significant defects in root gravitropism (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A). The defective root gravitropism in ropgap3 mutants is unlikely due to altered PIN2 degradation in the upper epidermal cells, as the ratios of PIN2 protein at the apical PM and endocytosed PIN2 protein in the mutant were not significantly different from those of WT plants (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 B–D). This result is aligned with the observation that endocytosis activities were similar between WT and the ropgap3 mutant (Fig. 4I). The root gravitropic defect in ropgap3 mutants could result from the partially compromised apical polarity of PIN2 as shown in Fig. 3 A and B.
Lateral root (LR) density was greatly (~50%) increased by ROPGAP3ox compared to WT plants, while ROPGAP1ox and ΔPI-ROPGAP3ox did not affect LR density, suggesting that ROPGAP3 again specifically functions during LR development and requires the interaction with PIN for this process (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 E and G). On the other hand, ropgap3 mutants did not show significant alterations in LR development (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 F and H).
The distinct biological roles of ROPGAP3 from ROPGAP1 in the root are unlikely due to differential expression patterns, as the transcript levels of ROPGAP3 and ROPGAP1 were comparable in root tissues (SI Appendix, Fig. S17).

## Other Biological Roles of ROPGAP3.
To gain further insight into the biological roles of ROPGAP3, we investigated additional auxin-related developmental processes. The hypocotyl gravitropism was mildly impaired in ropgap3 mutants, and this defect was rescued by reintroducing ROPGAP3 into the mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S18A). The relocalization of PIN3 in starch-sheath cells is crucial for hypocotyl gravitropism (40). In WT, the PIN3 level at the upper lateral PM of upper starch-sheath cells significantly reduced upon gravity stimulation, whereas in ropgap3 mutants, this reduction was less pronounced (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 B and D). Correspondingly, the auxin level, measured by DR5:reporter expression, was greatly decreased in the upper part of the WT hypocotyl but not significantly in the ropgap3 mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 C and E). Therefore, the gravitropic impairment in ropgap3 mutants is likely due to defective PIN3 relocation in the upper starch-sheath cells, leading to the failure of auxin redistribution in the upper part of the gravity-stimulated hypocotyl (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 B–E).
Arabidopsis roots exhibit a wavy growth pattern on an agar medium surface, with greater amplitude on an inclined medium surface (41). Auxin plays a key role in this wavy root growth (42). We compared the wavy root growth of WT and ropgap3 mutants. A 45° inclination of the agar medium increased root waviness, which was more pronounced in ropgap3 mutants than in WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S18F). Root wavy growth is linked to root twisting along the root axis (43), and the root twist in ropgap3 mutants was increased compared to WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 G and H).
Phyllotaxis, regulated by PIN-mediated polar auxin transport, also exhibited defects in ropgap3 mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 I–K).
However, similar phenotypic effects were observed in ropgap1 mutants, such as impaired hypocotyl gravitropism, increased root waviness and twisting, and defective phyllotaxis (SI Appendix, Fig. S18 A and F–K). These results suggest that ROPGAP3 is likely mediating these biological processes through ordinary ROP signaling, similar to ROPGAP1.
Overall, while ROPGAP3 exhibits unique functions in PIN2 endocytosis, clustering, and polarity, it also plays roles in broader auxin-related processes, potentially in concert with or similarly to ROPGAP1 through ROP signaling pathways.

## Cytoplasmic ROPGAP3 Could Be Involved in the Lytic Trafficking of PIN2.
ROPGAP3 is localized not only in the PM but also in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2A), and the PI-dependent interaction of ROPGAP3 with PIN2 also occurs significantly in the cytoplasm (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and D). This led us to examine the role of cytoplasmic ROPGAP3 in intracellular PIN2 behavior. Therefore, we tested whether ROPGAP3 affects the vacuolar lytic trafficking of PIN2, which is one of the major intracellular trafficking events for PIN.
To evaluate the lytic trafficking rate, we utilized dark-mediated visualization of GFP-fusion proteins in the vacuole (39). When observing vacuolar-targeted PIN2:GFP levels in the root epidermis of dark-treated WT, ropgap3 mutant, and ropgap1 mutant seedlings, the level was significantly reduced in the ropgap3 mutant but unchanged in the ropgap1 mutant compared to the WT level (Fig. 5 E and F). This suggests that ROPGAP3 is specifically required for the native vacuolar trafficking of PIN2.

## Discussion
Plant Rho small GTPases (i.e., ROPs) are instrumental in endomembrane trafficking, similar to their counterparts in other eukaryotes (44, 45). Notably, ROPs mediate the inhibition of clathrin-dependent endocytosis of PIN auxin transporters by responding to extracellular auxin signals (5–9). The actin cytoskeleton, influenced by ROP signaling, plays a crucial role in vesicle trafficking, with ROPs interacting with RICs to regulate cytoskeletal organization (7–9, 46–49). Traditionally, GAPs, which catalyze the inactivation of ROPs by converting them from their GTP-bound active form to their GDP-bound inactive form, have been understood to enhance PIN endocytosis by inhibiting ROP signaling. Our study, however, uncovers a role for Arabidopsis ROPGAP3, which diverges from this traditional view by inhibiting the endocytosis of PIN-including vesicles, unlike its paralog ROPGAP1.
The focus of studies on ROP-mediated vesicle trafficking has predominantly been on ROPs themselves, with less attention given to their signaling components, such as GAPs. This has limited the understanding of GAPs to their roles in Rho or ROP signaling. In our exploration of PIN-interacting proteins, we identified ROPGAP3 and found its direct interaction with the central HL domain of PIN. This interaction restricts PIN’s lateral diffusion on the PM, inhibits its endocytosis, and reinforces its polarity, independently of traditional ROP signaling.
Our findings consistently demonstrate that ROPGAP3, but not ROPGAP1, can interact with PIN, inhibit PIN lateral diffusion and endocytosis, reinforce PIN clustering and polarity in the PM, and mediate PIN lytic trafficking (Figs. 1, 3–5). These specific functions of ROPGAP3 are mainly attributable to its PI motif, essential for interacting with PIN and conserved among ROPGAP3 orthologs in Angiosperms (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S5), though other regions in ROPGAP3 may also participate in PIN interaction cooperatively with the PI motif (SI Appendix, Figs. S1E and S13 E and H). The unique role of ROPGAP3 and its PI motif is evident in molecular and cellular processes and biological processes such as root gravitropism and lateral root formation (Fig. 5 A–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S16G).
A significant difference in subcellular localization between ROPGAP3 and ROPGAP1 was observed, with ROPGAP3 localizing to the PM while ROPGAP1 did not (Fig. 2A). PM localization of ROPGAPs typically depends on the active status of ROPs (50). Although auxin has been implicated in ROP activation (7–9), direct evidence of auxin-induced PM localization of ROPGAPs was lacking. Our results show that auxin enhances ROPGAP1’s PM localization (Fig. 2B), suggesting its recruitment to active ROPs. In contrast, ROPGAP3’s PM localization was unaffected by auxin and the defects in the CRIB motif did not deter ROPGAP3 from localizing to the PM (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S13 F and G), indicating that PM localization of ROPGAP3 does not require ROP activation. Although the PI defect hampers ROPGAP3-PIN2 colocalization in the PM (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), the PM localization of ROPGAP3 per se does not seem to solely depend on PI-mediated PIN2 interaction, as PI-motif deletion did not significantly affect its PM localization (Fig. 2 A and B). This implies that ROPGAP3 might interact with additional PM proteins or membrane components.
Clustering is a universal mechanism regulating the polarity of membrane proteins across eukaryotic lineages (51). PIN clustering in the PM restricts lateral diffusion and helps maintain polarity (25). This clustering is regulated by phosphoinositides, cytoskeletal structures, and cell-wall components (52). Salicylic acid, through REMORIN, also influences PIN clustering (36, 53). Clustering of cargo proteins in endosomes is thought to play a role in their lytic trafficking (54–56). Our data support this, showing ROPGAP3’s role in normal lytic trafficking of PIN2 (Fig. 5 E and F). Given ROPGAP1’s lack of significant effect on lytic trafficking and ropgap3 mutant’s similar endocytosis level to but less active lytic trafficking than WT (Figs. 4I and 5 E and F), we speculate that the interaction of ROPGAP3 with PIN2 might be crucial for PIN2 clustering in endosomes. Direct PIN-interacting proteins affecting PIN clustering have not been identified previously. We propose ROPGAP3 as a PIN-clustering modulator through direct interaction with PIN. Future research should investigate whether ROPGAP3 collaborates with other clustering factors to mediate PIN clustering.
Several lines of evidence from our study support the idea that ROPGAP3 regulates PIN2 clustering and its downstream effects—such as polarity reinforcement, reduced lateral diffusion and endocytosis, enhanced vacuolar trafficking, and associated developmental outcomes—via a mechanism that is independent of canonical ROP signaling. First, ROPGAP1, a close paralog of ROPGAP3, and a PIN-interaction–deficient ROPGAP3 variant (ΔPI), both retain normal GAP activity but fail to induce PIN2 clustering or its related phenotypes (Figs. 1, 3–5). Second, ROP6, a key ROP isoform previously shown to regulate PIN2 trafficking, when either mutated or overexpressed, does not significantly alter PIN2 clustering or lateral diffusion (Figs. 3J and 4F). These findings suggest that ROPGAP3 exerts its function through a noncanonical mode, likely involving direct interaction with PIN2.
Notably, our data show that the CRIB motif of ROPGAP3, classically known for its role in ROP binding and GAP activity, also contributes to interaction with PIN2 and to clustering at the PM, while this role is not observed in the cytoplasmic compartment (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13). Based on this, we propose two possible scenarios: 1) the CRIB motif may mediate clustering independently of ROP interaction, via direct binding to PIN2 at the PM; or 2) it may facilitate clustering through dual interaction with both PIN2 and ROPs, possibly involving a yet unidentified ROP isoform. Moreover, given that the PI motif in ROPGAP3 is critical for PIN2 interaction and clustering, and that ROPGAP1, despite containing a CRIB motif, cannot interact with PIN2, it is possible that the CRIB motif in ROPGAP3 functions cooperatively with the PI motif to facilitate PIN2 interaction. These possibilities raise an interesting question as to whether other ROP homologs participate in a noncanonical signaling mechanism that cooperates with ROPGAP3 in regulating PIN2 clustering—a possibility that warrants further investigation.
Diverse trans-acting factors interact with PINs, including protein kinases and phosphatases for PIN activity and trafficking (27, 28), adaptor proteins for PIN endocytosis (57), ubiquitin ligases for PIN ubiquitination and degradation (26), phospholipid kinases for lytic trafficking of PINs (29), and phospholipases for PIN endocytosis (58). Our study introduces a functional category of proteins: ROP GAPs, which modulate PIN clustering (Fig. 5 G and H).
In conclusion, ROPGAP3 emerges as a multifaceted regulator of PIN dynamics, diverging from the traditional role of GAPs in ROP signaling. By directly interacting with PIN, ROPGAP3 influences PIN clustering, lateral diffusion, endocytosis, and lytic trafficking, thereby contributing to crucial auxin-mediated developmental processes. This study broadens our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying PIN regulation and opens avenues for exploring the roles of GAPs in plant cell biology.

## Materials and Methods

## Plant Materials and Growth Conditions.
Arabidopsis thaliana Columbia ecotype (Col-0) plants were used as WT and for transformation with transgene constructs unless otherwise stated. The ropgap3-1, ropgap3-2, and ropgap1-3 mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Stock Center (http://www.Arabidopsis.org/). The rop6-2 mutant was from Zhenbiao Yang, and rop6-ca and ROP6ox were from Prof. Martiniere Alexandre (33, 59). Seeds were cold-treated at 4 °C for 4 d before germination and grown at 22 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod using fluorescent illumination (FHF 32SS-EXD; Kumho Electric, Korea) with a light intensity of 130 μmol m–2 s–1. The seedlings were grown on agar plates containing 2.15 g L−1 Murashige and Skoog (MS) nutrient mix (Duchefa, the Netherlands), 1% sucrose, 0.5 g L−1 MES (pH 5.7 with KOH), and 0.8% agarose (SeaKem, Cambrex BioScience Rockland). Arabidopsis plants were transformed using the floral dip method with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain C58C1, and transformants were selected on agar plates containing hygromycin (30 μg mL−1) or phosphinothricin (30 μg mL−1) and cefotaxime (10 μg mL−1). Unless otherwise stated, analyses were performed with 4-d-old seedlings.

## Transgene Constructs.
To construct yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) vectors, the complementary DNA (cDNA) for the full-length (FL, 1 to 456 amino acid [aa] as seen in Fig. 1B) ROPGAP3 protein was obtained from Arabidopsis seedling mRNA by PCR using the primer sets listed in SI Appendix, Table S1 (hereafter, all PCR primers are listed in this Table) and cloned into the EcoRI/SalI sites of pGBK-T7 vector (Clontech, USA). The PIN-HL parts from pGEX-4 T-1 vector (60, 61) were transferred into the EcoRI/SalI sites of pGAD-T7 vector.
For bacterial protein expression, which was used for in vitro protein pulldown assays, cDNAs for PIN-HLs (PIN1, 167 to 460 aa; PIN2, 167 to 485 aa; PIN3, 167 to 479 aa) and FL (1 to 456 aa), N (1 to 153 aa), GAP (154 to 332 aa), C (333 to 459 aa), and ΔPI (1 to 415, 439 to 456 aa) parts of ROPGAP3 were cloned into the BamHI/SalI sites of pGEX-4 T-1 vector (GE Healthcare, Inchon, Korea) for generation of fusion proteins with glutathione S-transferase (GST) at their N termini. cDNAs for PIN2-HL (167 to 485 aa) and the fragments (HL1, 167-286 aa; HL2, 267-386 aa; HL3, 360-479 aa) of PIN3-HL were cloned into the BamHI/SalI sites of pET-30a-c(+) (Novagen) so as to include a histidine (His) tag at their N termini.
Binary vectors pCAMBIA1300-NOS and pCAMBIA3300-NOS, with modified cloning sites (62), were used to assemble transgenes for Arabidopsis transformation. The ProPIN1:PIN1:GFP (61), ProPIN2:PIN2:GFP (63), and ProPIN3:PIN3:GFP (60) constructs were as previously described. To construct ProROPGAP3:RFP:ROPGAP3 (ROPGAP3ox), the 2,858-bp ROPGAP3 promoter and 2,552-bp ROPGAP3 coding regions (with stop codon removed) were amplified from Arabidopsis genomic DNA and sequentially inserted into the SalI/XmaI and BglII/SpeI sites of the binary vector, respectively. The RFP coding region was obtained from ProE7:PGP4:RFP (64) and inserted into the MluI site of the binary vector. To construct Pro35S: RFP:ROPGAP1 (ROPGAP1ox), the 2,286-bp ROPGAP1 coding region (with the stop codon removed) was amplified from the genomic DNA and inserted into the BglII/SpeI sites of the binary vector. Pro35S was obtained from Pro35S:PGP4:YFP (64) and cloned into SalI/XmaI and sites of the binary vector. Deletion constructs (ProROPGAP3:RFP:ΔPI-ROPGAP3; ΔPI-ROPGAP3 and ProROPGAP3:RFP:ΔCRIB-ROPGAP3; ΔCRIB-ROPGAP3) and a point-mutant construct (ProROPGAP3:RFP:H113Y-ROPGAP3; H113Y-ROPGAP3) were generated by mutagenesis using the In-Fusion cloning method (Takara, Japan). The PCR products were cloned into the BglII/SpeI sites of the ProROPGAP3:RFP vector. For the ProDR5:ntdTOMATO construct, the ProDR5v2 and ntdTOMATO coding regions were PCR-amplified using the pGreenIIM DR5v2:ntdTomato/DR5:n3GFP vector (65) as a template and inserted into the AscI and XabI site, respectively.

## Bacterial Protein Expression and In Vitro Protein Pulldown Assays.
Plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21-DE3 Rosetta competent cells (Novagen), and protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 4 h. Cells were lysed using Thermo Scientific Pierce B-PER Bacterial Protein Extraction Reagents, which consisted of 1 mL B-Per, 2 µL DNaseI (5 units µL–1), 20 µL lysozyme (10 mg mL–1), and 1 x protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche).
For the pulldown experiment to obtain PIN-HL-interacting proteins, 40 µL glutathione sepharose beads 4B (GE Lifesciences) were incubated first with approximately 10 μg of GST or GST: PIN3-HL fusion protein at 4 °C on a shaker (10 rpm) for 4 to 6 h to allow binding (28). To obtain plant protein extracts, 7-d-old Col-0 seedlings were homogenized in a mortar using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton-X 100, 0.5% NP-40, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF], and 10% glycerol). The homogenate was then centrifuged twice at 10,000× g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. For the pulldown of PIN-interacting proteins, 100 μg of plant protein extracts were added to the GST- or GST: PIN3-HL-bound sepharose beads, and the mixtures were incubated at 4 °C for 3 h. The beads were then washed six times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20), after which proteins were eluted with 100 µL of glutathione elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 30 mM reduced glutathione pH 8.0). Elution fractions were separated using 10% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and distinct bands were excised from the gel and used for trypsin digestion and mass spectrometry analysis to obtain PIN-interacting protein candidates
For in vitro pull-down experiments between ROPGAP3 and PIN1-, PIN2-, and PIN3-HL, His-tagged ROPGAP3 (His: ROPGAP3) and GST:PIN-HLs were expressed in the E. coli cells as described above. Fifty μL glutathione sepharose beads 4B was incubated first with ~10 μg GST or GST:PINs-HL for 3 h at 4 °C on a rotator (15 rpm) to allow binding. After removing the supernatants, the beads were incubated with ~10 μg His: ROPGAP3 for 2 h at 4 °C on a rotator (15 rpm) for interaction. The beads were then washed five times with 1× Tris-buffered saline (TBS, including 0.1% [v/v] Tween-20) to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. Bound proteins were isolated from the beads by adding 1× SDS loading dye, separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes (GE Lifesciences), and probed with 1:3,000 diluted anti-His (MBL, cat. # D291-3) and anti-GST antibodies (Abcam, cat. # AB3416). Chemiluminescence detection was performed with ECL (LPS solution) protein-gel-blotting substrate in a chemiluminescence imaging system (Davinch-chem). Full immunoblot images are provided in SI Appendix, Fig. S20.

## Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) Analysis.
LC–MS/MS analysis was used to identify PIN-interacting protein candidates as described previously (28). Proteins were digested with trypsin in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer overnight at 37 °C. LC–MS/MS using high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) fragmentation was performed on Orbitrap Q-Exactive or Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a nanoACQUITY UPLC (Waters) instrument equipped with an in-house packed capillary trap column (150 μm internal diameter, 3 cm) and an analytical column (75 μm internal diameter, 100 cm) with 3 μm Jupiter C18 particles (Phenomenex) at a flow rate of 300 nL min−1. The acquired dataset was searched using the MS-GF+ (ver. 2017-Jan) algorithm at 10 ppm of precursor ion mass tolerance with variable phosphorylation modification parameter (+79.966 Da) on Ser/Thr/Tyr residues against the A. thaliana UniProt proteome database with 1% protein false discovery rate. ROPGAP3 was identified as one of PIN-interacting proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S19).

## Yeast Two-Hybrid Assay.
The yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay was conducted using the Matchmaker Yeast Two-Hybrid System (Clontech, Palo Alto, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s manual. Yeast strain AH109 was employed for transformation, and the transformed yeast cells were cultured on SD2− (-Leu and -Trp) plates at 30 °C for 4 d. One colony from each plate was selected and resuspended in liquid SD2− broth. Each cell culture was then diluted to a specific optical density (OD) at 600 nm. The yeast suspensions were subsequently plated on SD2− and SD3− (-Leu, -Trp, and -His) containing 1.0 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole and incubated at 30 °C for 6 d before observation.

## GTPase Activity Assay.
GST-tagged ROPGAP3 variants and ROP6 were prepared from E. coli cells as described above. GTPase activity assay was conducted as described previously (11). Enzcheck TM Phosphate Assay Kit (E-6646, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to estimate the change of ROP6 from the GTP-form to the GDP-form in the presence of ROPGAP3. The assay followed the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. The reaction mixture contained 5 μL of 20× reaction buffer, 20 μL of 2-amino-6-mercapto-7-methylpurine riboside (MESG), 1 μL of purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP), 6 μL of GTP solution (100 mM, R0461, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and approximately 10 μg of GST-ROP6 protein. The mixture was preincubated at 22 °C for 30 min to allow GTP binding. Subsequently, ~10 μg of GST-ROPGAP3 variant proteins and 10 μL of GAP assay buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.6, 100 mM NaCl, 0.2 mg/mL bovine serum albumin, and 5 mM MgCl2) were added to initiate the GTP hydrolysis reaction. The reaction was incubated at 22 °C for an additional 60 min. Inorganic phosphate release was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 360 nm using a spectrophotometer.

## FRET-FLIM Analysis.
FRET-FLIM analysis was used to observe the in vivo interaction between ROPGAP and PIN2 as described previously (29). Six-day-old seedlings of the single ProPIN2:PIN2:GFP transgenic line or the double transgenic line containing both ProPIN2:PIN2:GFP and ProROPGAP3:RFP:ROPGAP3 (or Pro35s:RFP:ROPGAP1) were observed for the FRET-FLIM analysis. Fluorescence confocal imaging and fluorescence lifetime imaging experiments were performed using an inverted-type scanning confocal microscope (SP8 FALCON, Leica Microsystems) equipped with a 40× (air) objective lens. For excitation, picosecond pulsed laser lines (488 and 594 nm) from a white light laser (WLL) system were utilized. Two hybrid photon detectors (HyD1 & HyD3) were employed to collect emissions in the ranges of 495 to 580 nm and 600 to 770 nm, respectively, from the root samples. Fluorescence confocal and lifetime images, consisting of 512 × 512 pixels, were simultaneously recorded using a galvo-stage and time-correlated single-photon counting technique. The pinhole was set to 1.0 during the imaging process. All data manipulations were carried out using the Leica suited software (LAS X Ver.3.5.2). Average lifetime values were calculated using the amplitude-weighted method, with the Region of Interest (ROI) defined as a rectangle area encompassing the apical PM, cytoplasm, or entire epidermal cells within the root meristem zone (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B).

## Sequence Alignments and Phylogenetic Analysis.
Amino acid sequences were aligned using CLUSTAL W (DNASTAR) with the default settings (gap penalty, 10.0; gap length penalty, 0.2; delay divergent seqs, 30%; DNA transition weight, 0.5). Phylogenetic trees were generated by the Maximum likelihood method with the Poisson model using the MEGA version 10 software package after CLUSTAL W alignment of the whole amino acid sequences. The number of bootstrap replicates was set to 1,000.

## Confocal Microscopy of Fluorescent Proteins.
General observation of fluorescence reporters using a confocal microscope (LSM700, Carl Zeiss) was conducted as described previously (66). To observe the PIN2 clustering pattern, the Airyscan mode of a confocal microscope (LSM980, Carl Zeiss) was used. To observe the cytological effects of BFA, seedlings were pretreated with CHX (100 μM) for 30 min and then treated with BFA (concentrations as indicated in corresponding figures) and CHX (100 μM) for variable time periods according to the experiment. NAA treatment was conducted as described previously (9). Quantification of fluorescence signals was performed using ImageJ software.

## FRAP Analysis.
FRAP analysis was conducted as previously described with modifications (28). Four-day-old ProPIN2:PIN2:GFP seedlings were mounted on a slide glass using 0.5× MS medium and 0.1% agarose and then imaged using an LSM700 confocal microscope. For photobleaching, 4 μm-wide ROIs were selected at the apical PM side of root epidermal cells and bleached using 100 iterations of 488 nm set at 100% transmission. Fluorescence recovery was recorded for 10 min after bleaching with a 5-min interval between frames. FRAP data were normalized using the following equation: In = [(It – Imin)/(Imax – Imin)] × 100, where In is the normalized intensity, It is the intensity at any time t, Imin is the minimum postphotobleaching intensity, and Imax is the mean prephotobleaching intensity (25). Fluorescence signal intensity was analyzed with ImageJ software.

## RNA Isolation and RT-qPCR Analysis.
Total RNA was extracted from the 4-d-old seedlings using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was carried out following the previously described method (67). RT-qPCR analyses were conducted using the SensiFAST SYBR No ROX kit (Bioline) and a CFX connect Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad). Gene-specific signals were normalized to the ACTIN2 transcript level. For each sample, three independent RNA extractions were performed, and three technical replicate RT-qPCRs were conducted for each RNA sample. The primers used for quantitation are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1.

## Observation of Biological Parameters.
Emerged lateral roots (LRs) were observed in 8-d-old seedlings. LR density was estimated by dividing the number of emerged LRs by the primary root length in centimeters. Root gravitropism was observed in light-grown seedlings. Root images were captured at specified time intervals after 135° gravity stimulation, and the bending angle was recorded. Hypocotyl gravitropism was observed in dark-grown seedlings. Hypocotyl images were taken at specified time intervals after 90° gravity stimulation with different samples, and the bending angle was recorded. Phyllotactic analysis was conducted by measuring the phyllotactic angles of vegetative leaves from 24-d-old plants. The angle formed by two consecutive leaf axes was estimated using ImageJ software.

## Statistical Analysis and Web Tools.
Statistically significant differences are represented by different letters (one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s unequal N HSD post hoc test, P < 0.05) or by asterisks (Student’s t test, as indicated in figures). The Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (R2) was utilized to determine whether the correlation coefficient significantly differs from zero. AlphaFold v2.3.1 algorithm (31) was used to predict 3-dimensional structure of ROPGAP3.

## Figure Fig. 1.: ROPGAP3 directly interacts with the PIN-HL. (A) In vitro pulldown assays demonstrating the interaction between ROPGAP3 and PIN-HLs. Immunoblot analyses were conducted for GST:PIN-HL and His:ROPGAP3 with antibodies against GST and His, respectively, after pulldown of His:ROPGAP3 with PIN-HL. (B) Schematic structures of ROPGAP3 and its PI-deleted form (ΔPI). CRIB, Cdc42/Rac-interactive binding domain; GAP, Rho GAPs catalytic domain; PI, PIN-interacting motif. Numerics indicate amino acid numbers. (C) Immunoblot analysis of His:PIN2-HL after its pulldown with GST:ROPGAP3 and GST:ΔPI-ROPGAP3. PIN2-HL levels after pulldown (the bar graph) were normalized with the input level. Data represent means ± SE from 12 independent assays (Significant differences are designated by * [Student t test]). (D) GTPase activities of ROP6 with ROPGAP3. Similar amounts of the proteins (in GST-fused forms) used for the assays were depicted by immunoblots, and the GTPase activity was normalized by the protein level. Molecular sizes of the recombinant proteins are 25 (GST), 42 (HIS:PIN2-HL), 80 (GST:ROPGAP3), 77 (GST:ΔPI-ROPGAP3), and 47 (GST:ROP6) kDa (A, C, and D). Data represent means ± SD from three independent assays (ns, not significant). (E–G) FRET-FLIM analyses. GFP lifetimes of single PIN2:GFP transformant and of cotransformants (PIN2:GFP and RFP:ROPGAP) in root epidermal cells were quantitatively compared. Data represent mean ± SE (n = 11 to 17 ROIs of whole meristematic root epidermis). The confocal root epidermal images were from the cotransformants. Bar = 10 µm.

## Figure Fig. 2.: Subcellular localization of ROPGAPs. (A) Subcellular localization of PIN2:GFP and RFP:ROPGAP proteins around the apical PM of root epidermal cells of the cotransgenic plant with PIN2:GFP and RFP:ROPGAP. The relative fluorescence intensity along the arrow was obtained by assigning “1” to the maximum fluorescence intensity value in the observation of each cell. (B) The ratio of RFP:ROPGAP fluorescence intensity along the arrow in the image with (+) or without (−) auxin (NAA, 5 μM). Bar = 10 µm (A and B). (C) The relationship between RFP:ROPGAP3 intensity and PIN2:GFP intensity in the PM (described in SI Appendix, Fig. S11). R2 denotes Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Data represent means ± SE (n = 10 cells from 3 to 5 roots [A], 35 to 50 cells from 7 to 12 roots [B], and 850 PM regions from 34 cells [C]).

## Figure Fig. 3.: ROPGAP3 modulates PIN2 polarity and clustering. (A–C) ROPGAP3 reinforces PIN2 polarity. (A) PIN2 (PIN2:GFP) localization patterns in the root epidermis of WT, ropgap mutants, and ROPGAPox lines. Bar = 10 µm. “A” and “L” denote apical and lateral PIN2 signal, respectively. (B and C) The relative ratio of A/L PIN2 polarity seen in A (n = 25 to 272 cells from 5 to 77 seedlings). The short horizontal bar in violin plots indicates a mean. Significant differences are denoted by different letters (ANOVA test, P < 0.05). (D–G) ROPGAP3 enhances PIN2 clustering. (D and E) Profiles of PIN2 intensity along the apical PM of epidermal cells of WT and ROPGAP3ox. (F and G) PIN2 clustering parameter (SD) obtained as described in SI Appendix, Fig. S8. n = 30 to 45 cells from 3 to 9 seedlings (F) and 30 to 32 cells from 3 to 4 seedlings (G). Mean values are denoted in each violin plot, and significant differences are indicated by different letters (ANOVA test, P < 0.05) or by * (Student t test; ns, not significant). (H–J) ROP6 does not affect PIN2 clustering. (H) Subcellular localization of PIN2 in the root epidermis. Bar = 10 µm. (I) The relative ratio of A/L PIN2 polarity seen in A (n = 20 to 27 cells from 4 to 5 seedlings). (J) PIN2 clustering parameter (n = 27 to 45 cells from 3 to 9 seedlings). Significant differences are denoted by different letters (ANOVA test, P < 0.05).

## Figure Fig. 4.: ROPGAP3 modulates lateral diffusion and endocytosis of PIN2. (A–H) ROPGAP3 inhibits PIN2 lateral diffusion. (A–F) Kinetics of FRAP of PIN2 in ROPGAPox lines, ropgap mutants, rop6 mutants, and ROP6ox line. Seedlings were pretreated with cycloheximide (CHX; 100 μM, 10 min), and then cotreated with CHX (100 μM) and BFA (25 μM) for 5 min before photobleaching. Data represent mean ± SE (n = 12 to 55 cells from 2 to 9 seedlings; Significant differences are denoted by * [Student’s t test; ns, not significant]). PIN2:GFP images were taken 10 min after photobleaching. Arrows indicate the photobleached regions under recovery. (I and J) ROPGAP3 can inhibit PIN2 endocytosis. (I and J) Internal BFA-body formation (endocytosis) of PIN2 in ropgap mutants (I) and ROPGAPox lines (J). Seedlings were pretreated with CHX (100 μM) for 30 min and then cotreated with CHX (100 μM), and BFA (25 μM) for 90 min. The signal intensity ratio of internalized vs. PM-localized PIN2:GFP was estimated after the treatments. n = 45 to 55 cells from 11 to 13 seedlings. Significant differences are denoted by different letters (ANOVA test, P < 0.05). Representative confocal images at the bottom show the endocytosed PIN2:GFP-including vesicles. Bar = 10 µm.

## Figure Fig. 5.: The role of ROPGAP3 in PIN2 lytic trafficking. (A) Root gravitropic kinetics of wild type (WT) and ROPGAPox lines in the PIN2:GFP background. (n = 18 to 78 roots). (B) Confocal images showing PIN2:GFP signals in the root of the lines in A at 0 or 4 h after gravity stimulation. (Scale bar, 20 μm.) Arrowheads indicate internalized PIN2:GFP vesicles, and the arrow indicates the direction of gravity (g). (C and D) Ratios of PIN2:GFP signal level of upper (U)/lower (L) root epidermis in the PM (C) or cytoplasm (D) 0 or 4 h after gravi-stimulation as shown in B. Data represent mean ± SE (n = 19 to 48 cells from 7 to 16 seedlings [C and D]; Significant differences are denoted by different letters [ANOVA test, P < 0.05]). (E) Vacuolar-trafficked PIN2 in root epidermal cells of WT and ropgap mutants. PIN2:GFP signals were observed 12 h after dark treatment. Bar = 10 µm. (F) The signal intensity ratio of internalized vs. PM PIN2:GFP as shown in E (n = 35 to 130 cells from 7 to 26 seedlings). Different letters indicate significant differences (ANOVA test, P < 0.05). (G) The canonical ROP signaling model in PIN2 endocytosis. ROPGAP3 enhances PIN2 endocytosis by inhibiting ROP in auxin-mediated ROP signaling. (H) The direct interaction model of ROPGAP3 with PIN2. ROPGAP3 interacts with, enhances clustering, and inhibits lateral diffusion and endocytosis of PIN2, resulting in the reinforcement of PIN2 polarity in the PM. The ROPGAP3-mediated PIN2 clustering in endosomes may facilitate the lytic trafficking of PIN2.
