# Genome-wide identification of castor bean OSCA genes and analysis of expression patterns under drought and salt stress

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundOSCA (hyperosmolality-gated calcium-permeable channel) is an important class of mechanosensitive calcium channels in plants. OSCAs, which was located on the plasma membrane, function as osmosensors that enable plants to perceive hyperosmotic environments, such as drought and salt stress, and initiate downstream calcium ions signaling. However, a systematic genome-wide identification and functional characterization of the OSCA gene family in castor bean (Ricinus communis L.) under abiotic stresses remain unelucidated.ResultsIn this study, we conducted a comprehensive genome-wide identification and analysis of the OSCA gene family in castor bean using bioinformatics approaches based on its whole-genome sequence. A total of 10 OSCA genes (RcOSCAs) were identified, which were unevenly distributed on five chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis classified the RcOSCA proteins into four distinct subfamilies and clarified their evolutionary relationships with homologs from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Triticum aestivum, Zea mays, and Glycine max. All proteins encoded by RcOSCA genes contain three conserved domains including RSN1_TM, PHM7_cyt, and RSN1_7TM. RT-qPCR analysis displayed that the transcript levels of several RcOSCA genes were significantly upregulated in roots, true leaves, or cotyledons under drought or salt stress. Notably, RcOSCA6 was rapidly and strongly induced in true leaves within 6 h of salt stress, highlighting its potential key role in castor bean's adaptation to abiotic stress.ConclusionsThis study constitutes the first genome-wide identification of the OSCA gene family in castor bean and provides preliminary insights into its evolutionary features and, more importantly, the species- and tissue-specific expression dynamics under salt and drought stresses. Future functional studies are warranted to fully elucidate the roles of these RcOSCAs in stress adaptation. These results contribute to understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying drought and salt tolerance in this resilient oil crop and offer valuable candidate genes for future functional characterization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08420-1.Keywords: Ricinus communis, OSCA gene family, Abiotic stress, Gene expression

## Introduction
The OSCA gene family encoding a class of hyperosmolality-gated calcium channel proteins which widely present in plants. These channels play a key role in the initial response to hyperosmotic stress by mediating Ca2⁺ influx, which triggers downstream signaling pathways that regulate stress-related gene expression and enhance plant stress tolerance [1]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, an unbiased forward genetic screen based on calcium imaging identified mutants with impaired elevation of cytosolic Ca2⁺ upon osmotic treatment. The corresponding mutant was designated reduced hyperosmolality-induced [Ca2⁺]i increase 1 (osca1) [2]. Since the identification of AtOSCA1 as the first plasma-membrane channel induced by osmotic treatment in A. thaliana, subsequent studies have revealed that the OSCA gene family is widespread in the plant kingdom and comprises numerous members. Phylogenetic analyses indicate that this family underwent significant expansion during plant evolution from aquatic to terrestrial environments, which reflects the adaptive evolution of OSCAs to complex land habitats [3].
Drought and salt stress represent two major abiotic challenges throughout the plant life cycle, affecting species distribution, limiting crop productivity, and threatening global food security [4, 5]. The osmotic stress induced by drought and salinity typically disrupts plant water balance and ultimately damages cellular membrane systems, thereby impairing normal physiological activities [6]. To cope with such stress, plants have evolved a sophisticated signal-perception and transduction system that regulates the expression of genes involved in stress-responsive, leading to adaptive physiological and morphological changes that enhance stress resistance [7]. When plants perceive external osmotic changes, Ca2⁺ serving as key secondary messengers participates in regulating growth and development [8]. Importantly, signals induced by different stimuli can activate distinct Ca2⁺ channels, generating Ca2⁺ signatures with specific spatiotemporal patterns, a phenomenon that underscores a high degree of stimulus–response specificity [9].
From model plants such as A. thaliana to various crops including Zea mays and Oryza sativa, the OSCA gene family has been shown to play a crucial role in responses to drought and salt stress [2, 10, 11]. The OSCA gene family is characterized by a conserved RSN1_7TM domain, which consists of seven transmembrane regions and functions as a calcium channel in response to osmotic stress [12, 13]. In A. thaliana, the OSCA family consists of 15 members. AtOSCA1 located on the plasma membrane, acts as a core component of the plant osmosensor which can directly perceive extracellular hyperosmotic stimuli, mediate Ca2⁺ influx, and thereby initiate downstream osmotic-stress signaling pathways [2]. In Z. mays, most ZmOSCA genes are significantly upregulated under ABA, PEG, and NaCl treatments [10]. Overexpression of ZmOSCA2.4 markedly enhances drought tolerance in transgenic A. thaliana and increases the expression of drought-responsive genes (e.g., MYB44, DREB2A, NCED3) [10]; In O. sativa, the transcript levels of eight OsOSCA genes could were upregulated under osmotic stress [12]. These findings in major crops highlight the conserved role of OSCAs in osmotic stress adaptation. However, whether the OSCA family in the stress-tolerant, non-model oilseed crop castor bean exhibits analogous characteristics remains an open question. In summary, members of the OSCA gene family are closely associated with osmotic stress responses and thus influence plant growth, development. Therefore, the evolutionary and functional analysis of OSCAs provides a valuable theoretical foundation for breeding osmotic stress-tolerant crops through genetic improvement.
Castor bean is an oilseed crop belonging to the Euphorbiaceae family [11, 14]. In China, it is traditionally cultivated as a specialty oil crop and is widely grown in eastern Inner Mongolia [15]. Moreover, castor bean exhibits strong tolerance to drought and salt and plays a significant ecological role in improving soil structure and enhancing soil fertility [16].
Although castor bean exhibits notable tolerance to drought and salinity, the molecular basis of this trait remains unclear. The OSCA family, encoding crucial hyperosmolality-gated calcium channels, has been extensively studied in model plants like A. thaliana [2] and in crops such as Z. mays [10] and O. sativa [12]. These findings in major crops highlight the conserved role of OSCAs in osmotic stress adaptation. However, whether the OSCA family in the stress-tolerant, non-model oilseed crop castor bean exhibits analogous characteristics remains an open question. Consequently, for castor bean, a genome-wide identification of the OSCA family is still lacking, and the expression patterns as well as functional roles of its members under abiotic stress remain to be elucidated. Therefore, mining and identifying abiotic stress related genes in castor bean will not only facilitate the improvement of its own stress tolerance but also provide theoretical insights and genetic resources for stress-resistance breeding in other crops.
In this study, we aimed to answer the following questions: What is the composition, evolution, and expression landscape of the OSCA gene family in castor bean? How do specific members respond transcriptionally to drought and salt stress to potentially regulate early adaptive responses? This work provides important theoretical insights into the molecular mechanisms of drought and salt tolerance in castor bean and lays a foundation for future studies aimed at its genetic improvement.

## Materials and methods

## Identification of OSCA gene family members in castor bean
The genome and annotation files of castor bean were obtained from the Oil Plant Database (http://oilplants.iflora.cn). The A. thaliana OSCA protein sequences (AtOSCAs) were retrieved from the TAIR database (https://www.Arabidopsis thaliana.org/). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile for the OSCA domain (PF01501) was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/). Candidate OSCA sequences were identified utilizing the HMM Search [17] and BLASTp [18] functions within TBtools v2.309 [19] to search the entire castor bean proteome with an E-value of ≤ 1 × 10⁻5, followed by the combination and deduplication of the resulting candidates; conserved domains in the candidate sequences were further verified using the NCBI CDD tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/) and the EBI InterPro database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) [20, 21]. Hits with E-values > 1 × 10⁻5 or covering < 80% of the reference domain length were considered partial or low-confidence and thus excluded from the final gene list. Finally, only sequences harboring the complete, characteristic OSCA domain (PF01501) were retained as definitive RcOSCA members; multiple sequence alignment was carried out with Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/) [22].

## Physicochemical properties, chromosomal localization, and collinearity analysis of RcOSCAs
The number of amino acids, isoelectric point (pI), and molecular weight of each RcOSCA protein were calculated using the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) [23]. Subcellular localization was predicted with WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) [24]. Chromosomal positions and collinearity analysis of the RcOSCA genes were extracted from the castor bean genome annotation file and visualized using TBtools v2.309 [19].

## Phylogenetic analysis of RcOSCAs
To examine the evolutionary relationships among OSCAs, protein sequences from R. communis, A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, Triticum aestivum, and Glycine max were aligned using MEGA 11. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with the Maximum Likelihood method and 1000 bootstrap replicates [25]. The best-fit amino acid substitution model (JTT + G + I) was selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion. And subsequently visualized and annotated using the Evolview online platform (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/#/).

## Analysis of conserved motifs, domains, and gene structure of RcOSCAs
Conserved protein motifs were identified using the MEME online tool with the following parameters: a maximum of 20 motifs, an optimum motif width between 6 and 50 amino acids, and an E-value threshold < 1 × 10⁻10. Conserved domains were analyzed using the NCBI Batch CD-Search tool [26]. Gene structures (exon–intron organization) were then analyzed and plotted using TBtools v2.309. Conserved motifs were identified using the MEME suite (v5.5.0).

## Analysis of cis-acting elements in RcOSCAs promoters
To identify cis-acting regulatory elements, genomic sequences spanning 2 kb upstream of the start codon of each RcOSCA gene were extracted. These promoter regions were systematically scanned using the PlantCARE database [27].

## Plant material and stress treatments
To investigate the roles of RcOSCAs in plant development and abiotic stress responses, we analyzed publicly available RNA-Seq data from Brown [28], which includes expression profiles across 8 tissues: seed, leaf, root, stem, inflorescence, pollen, ovule, and capsule. Due to the unavailability of raw replicate data, statistical testing was not performed. The heatmap visualizes clear expression trends (high/medium/low) across tissues.
The castor bean self-cross lines were obtained from the Key Laboratory of Castor bean Breeding in Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, Inner Mongolia MinZu University. Strains “2129” was used in this study. Castor bean seeds were germinated and seedlings were grown in a soil:vermiculite (3:1) mixture under controlled conditions: a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, light intensity of 150 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1, constant temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 60–70%. Four-leaved castor bean seedlings were selected for salt stress and drought stress using 10% PEG-6000 and 200 mM NaCl solutions, and equal amounts of distilled water were used as controls. These concentrations were adopted from prior studies [29, 30] and were selected to effectively induce osmotic stress while maintaining seedling viability under our experimental conditions. Samples were taken at 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h after the stress treatments, respectively. Roots, true leaves and cotyledons treated at different times were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in −80 °C refrigerator. Total RNA of castor was extracted by Trizol method and detected by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

## RT-qPCR analysis and statistical methods
The relative expression level of RcOSCA in roots, true leaves and cotyledons at different treatment times was detected by RT-qPCR. Primer specificity was confirmed by single-peak melting curves. Amplification efficiencies for all primer pairs, determined by standard curve analysis, ranged between 90 and 110%, and the correlation coefficients (R2) were > 0.99. The experimental design included three biological replicates. The RcSKIP gene, which has been validated to exhibit stable expression under various abiotic stresses in castor bean [29, 30], was used as the internal reference and analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method [31]. In this study, statistical analysis of the RT-qPCR data was performed using t-tests for pairwise group comparisons. While this pairwise approach does not constitute a full multiple comparison adjustment across all genes, tissues, and time points, it is suitable for initial screening of expression changes under defined conditions. We applied relatively stringent significance thresholds (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). The results are therefore interpreted cautiously as preliminary transcriptional evidence pointing to responsive genes, rather than definitive functional validation, thus guiding targets for future in-depth investigation.

## Results

## Genome-wide identification, chromosomal localization and collinearity analysis of the RcOSCAs
Through a combined BLASTp and Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-based screening, we identified a total of 10 OSCA genes in the castor bean genome. These genes were designated RcOSCA1 to RcOSCA10 based on their order of appearance along the chromosomes. Chromosomal localization analysis showed that the RcOSCA genes are unevenly distributed across five chromosomes (chr1, chr2, chr5, chr7, and chr9) (Fig. 1). Chr 5 harbors the largest number of RcOSCA members, containing five genes (RcOSCA4, RcOSCA5, RcOSCA6, RcOSCA7, and RcOSCA8). Chr 1 carries two genes (RcOSCA1 and RcOSCA2), while chr 2, chr 7, and chr 9 each contain a single RcOSCA gene (RcOSCA3, RcOSCA9, and RcOSCA10). Collinearity analysis revealed a segmental duplication event between Chr1 and Chr5, involving the genes RcOSCA1 and RcOSCA8. Moreover, the Ka/Ks ratio of the duplicated RcOSCA gene pairs was calculated to evaluate the molecular evolution. The Ka/Ks ratio was less than 1 (Table 1).

## Physicochemical Properties of the RcOSCAs
Physicochemical property analysis indicated that the coding sequences of the 10 RcOSCA genes encode proteins ranging in length from 715 (RcOSCA9) to 1031 (RcOSCA2) amino acids, with an average length of 793.1 aa (Table 2). Their predicted molecular weights varied between 80,645.53 Da (RcOSCA9) and 117,025.91 Da (RcOSCA2), averaging 94,398.93 Da. The predicted isoelectric points (pI) spanned from 6.32 (RcOSCA7) to 9.44 (RcOSCA5), with a mean pI of 8.66. Notably, only RcOSCA7 exhibited a pI below 7, and the remaining nine members showed pI values above 7. The instability index ranged from 38.77 (RcOSCA6) to 47.78 (RcOSCA1), averaging 43.67. Based on these values, RcOSCA6 was predicted to be a stable protein, while the other nine members were classified as unstable. Aliphatic index values fell between 98.47 (RcOSCA2) and 110.19 (RcOSCA1), with an average of 104.16, indicating a potential for relatively high thermostability. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) ranged from 0.042 (RcOSCA5) to 0.362 (RcOSCA1), with a mean of 0.22. Subcellular localization predictions further confirmed that all 10 RcOSCA proteins are plasma membrane-localized, aligning with their proposed function as mechanosensitive Ca2⁺-permeable channels in osmosensing and stress signaling.

## Structural features of the RcOSCAs
Most RcOSCA genes (RcOSCA4, RcOSCA10, RcOSCA5, RcOSCA9, RcOSCA3, RcOSCA1, RcOSCA8, and RcOSCA6) share a largely conserved motif pattern, generally comprising Motifs 8, 5, 3, 1, 4, 2, 6, 9, and 10, albeit with slight variations in their order and presence (Fig. 2A). The motif composition of RcOSCA7 proteins appeared relatively simpler. The common motif arrangement among most RcOSCA proteins indicated that these isoforms retain typical channel functions, while the presence of isoform-specific motifs might suggest potential fine-tuning in regulation or functional specialization.

Conserved domains are represented by boxes of different colors. C Gene structure of RcOSCAs. Three conserved domains, RSN1_TM, PHM7_cyt, and RSN1_7TM, in all RcOSCA family members were identified (Fig. 2B). The RSN1_7TM domain occupies the majority of the RcOSCA protein sequence. The arrangement and relative lengths of these domains vary among members, contributing to functional diversity within the family. A notable exception was RcOSCA2, which encodes a significantly larger protein due to an extended N-terminal region outside the conserved OSCA domains.
Intron–exon structure analysis revealed considerable diversity in the gene architectures of the 10 RcOSCA genes, with variation in both the number and length of exons and introns (Fig. 2C). Gene lengths ranged from approximately 2 kb (RcOSCA9) to 10 kb (RcOSCA4). The number of exons per gene varied from 1 to 25, and intron lengths spanned from several hundred base pairs to several kilobases. Most RcOSCA genes (RcOSCA4, RcOSCA10, RcOSCA2, RcOSCA5, RcOSCA3, RcOSCA1, RcOSCA6, and RcOSCA7) contained multiple exons, which were typically flanked by 5’- and 3’-untranslated regions (UTRs). In contrast, RcOSCA8 was found to consist of a single coding exon.

## Classification and phylogenetic analysis of RcOSCAs
A phylogenetic tree was constructed using OSCA protein sequences from R. communis, A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, T. aestivum, and G. max. The sequences clustered into four distinct clades (Clade I–IV) (Fig. 3). Clade I–IV comprised genes from all six species, among these clade, Clade I was the largest group which include four RcOSCA proteins; Clade II harbored four RcOSCA members. Only one gene positioned within Clade III (RcOSCA6) and Clade IV (RcOSCA7).

## Analysis of cis-acting elements in the RcOSCAs
Analysis of the 2 kb promoter regions upstream of the RcOSCA genes identified numerous cis-acting elements associated with hormone signaling, abiotic stress responses, and developmental regulation (Fig. 4). Most RcOSCA genes (RcOSCA4, RcOSCA10, RcOSCA2, RcOSCA1, RcOSCA8, RcOSCA6, and RcOSCA7) exhibited extensive enrichment of such elements, suggesting that their transcription is likely regulated by multiple signaling pathways and environmental cues.

Several abiotic stress-related elements, including MBS, ARE, LTR, and TC-rich repeats, were detected in multiple promoters. ## Expression analysis of RcOSCAs under drought stress
To investigate the expression patterns of RcOSCA genes under drought stress, four-leaf-stage seedlings were treated and sampled at different time points (Fig. 7). Gene expression was analyzed in roots, true leaves, and cotyledons. In roots (Fig. 7A), most of this genes were up-regulated under drought treatment compared with control condition. Among them, RcOSCA1, RcOSCA3, RcOSCA5, and RcOSCA7 were mainly expressed at 24 h; the expression of RcOSCA2, RcOSCA9, and RcOSCA10 were peaked at 12 h; RcOSCA6 and RcOSCA8 were persistently induced by 24 h. In true leaves (Fig. 7B), RcOSCA1 and RcOSCA3 were significantly upregulated at 24 h. RcOSCA5, RcOSCA6, and RcOSCA7 displayed a biphasic pattern: induced at 6 h, reduced at 12 h, and then upregulated at 24 h. In contrast, RcOSCA2 and RcOSCA9 were downregulated under drought treatment for 6–24 h. In cotyledons (Fig. 7C), RcOSCA1 and RcOSCA8 were significantly induced at 6 h, with little change thereafter. RcOSCA3 and RcOSCA10 showed marked upregulation at 24 h.

## Expression analysis of RcOSCAs under salt stress
To investigate the expression patterns of RcOSCA genes under salt stress, four-leaf-stage seedlings were treated with NaCl at different time points (Fig. 8). Gene expression was analyzed in roots, true leaves, and cotyledons.In roots (Fig. 8A), RcOSCA1 was significantly induced at 6 h, while RcOSCA3, RcOSCA5, RcOSCA7, and RcOSCA10 showed marked upregulation by 24 h. In contrast, RcOSCA4 and RcOSCA6 were downregulated under salt treatment. In true leaves (Fig. 8B), most RcOSCA genes exhibited significant induction at 6 h, followed by a rapid decline. Particularly striking was the response of RcOSCA6, which showed an exceptionally strong induction (up to approximately 58-fold) at 6 h post-treatment, the most dramatic change observed among all RcOSCAs under salt stress. In cotyledons (Fig. 8C), RcOSCA3 and RcOSCA8 displayed early upregulation, whereas RcOSCA10 was significantly induced at 12 h. Under salt stress, RcOSCA1 was strongly induced in roots; RcOSCA1, RcOSCA3, RcOSCA4, RcOSCA5, RcOSCA6, RcOSCA7, RcOSCA8, and RcOSCA10 were markedly induced in true leaves; and RcOSCA10 showed strong induction in cotyledons.

## Discussion
OSCA proteins function as osmosensors in plants, playing crucial roles in perceiving exogenous and endogenous osmotic changes and regulating growth, development, and reproduction [2, 32]. Castor bean is an economically valuable industrial oilseed crop with notable adaptability to various environmental stresses, including drought and salinity. This study reports the first identification of 10 OSCA genes in the castor bean genome. In most plant species, OSCA gene numbers typically range from 11 to 21 [33]. The fewer number of OSCA genes in castor bean may reflect the functional diversification within RcOSCA family. Beyond evolutionary characterization, a key finding was the distinct, stress-specific expression pattern of RcOSCAs, with RcOSCA6 showing a potent, transient early induction specifically under salt stress.
The two main causes of gene family amplification in plants are segmentation and tandem duplication [34]. To investigate the evolutionary mechanisms underlying the expansion of the RcOSCA family, we performed intra-genomic collinearity analysis. The result revealed a clear syntenic relationship between RcOSCA1 (on Chr 1) and RcOSCA8 (on Chr 5) (Fig. 1), suggesting that these two genes likely originated from a segmental duplication event. Ka/Ks ratios for the duplicated RcOSCA gene pairs were less than 1, suggesting that the duplicated RcOSCAs might have experienced purifying selective pressure (Table 1). Although RcOSCA4 to RcOSCA8 are located on the same chromosome, their physical separation exceeds 2 Mb and their coding sequences share only about 48.39% identity on average. These characteristics are inconsistent with a typical recent tandem duplication event, which commonly generates closely spaced, highly similar gene copies [35].
With the exception of RcOSCA7 (pI < 7), all other RcOSCA members display pI values above 7, indicating that most of these channels carry a net positive surface charge at physiological pH. This charge property may thus be relevant to their function as cation-permeable channels and to their interactions with membrane lipids or signaling molecules. All members displayed positive GRAVY values, consistent with their hydrophobic character and supporting their predicted role as membrane-localized ion channels [36]. The subcellular localization of OSCA proteins has been widely reported [2, 37]. In this study, physicochemical analysis indicated that all ten RcOSCA members are localized to the plasma membrane, consistent with the subcellular localization observed for GmOSCA2.3, GmOSCA3.2 [38] and OsOSCA4 [10]. This supports the proposed role of RcOSCA proteins as mechanosensitive calcium-permeable channels involved in osmosensing and stress signaling [2].
Phylogenetic analysis provides fundamental insights into evolutionary relationships [39]. Here, OSCA proteins from R. communis, A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, T. aestivum, and G. max were classified into four clades (I–IV), consistent with previous reports in Z. mays [12], Cucumis sativus [40], Medicago sativa [41] and Solanum lycopersicum [42]. The RcOSCA genes are distributed across all clades: five in Clade I, three in Clade II, and one in Clades III and IV. The expansion of Clade I most likely resulted from gene duplication. Gene duplication represents a major driver of family expansion and functional innovation, supplying the genetic material for subfunctionalization or neofunctionalization [34] and potentially enhancing the plant’s capacity to sense complex environmental cues such as drought and salt stress [3]. In contrast, Clades III and IV exhibit high sequence conservation, indicative of strong purifying selection. Supporting this, the Ka/Ks ratio for the duplicated pair RcOSCA1/RcOSCA8 was significantly below 1, providing direct evidence of purifying selection acting on these genes. In summary, the phylogenetic pattern of the OSCA family in castor bean likely reflects an evolutionary history influenced by both lineage-specific expansion and widespread purifying selection.
Gene domains are critical determinants of protein function and regulatory activity. The RSN1_7TM domain is a hallmark of the OSCA family, representing the core transmembrane channel-forming region [11]. Our results confirm that all RcOSCA proteins contain the RSN1_7TM domain, consistent with findings in Z. mays [12], O. sativa [10], and Medicago sativa [41]. The unusually large size of RcOSCA2 represents another potential mechanism for functional diversification within the OSCA family—similar to how differential amphipathic helix, beam-like domain, and fenestration properties contribute to stimulus-specific gating in OSCA1.2 vs. OSCA3.1 [43]. This extension could provide novel protein–protein interaction interfaces or distinct regulatory properties not present in other family members, a hypothesis that awaits experimental validation.
Cis-Acting elements in promoter regions play pivotal roles in transcriptional regulation. The presence of diverse cis-elements suggests that a gene may participate in multiple stress-response pathways [44]. Our promoter analysis revealed that RcOSCA genes contain elements associated with hormone response, light response, metabolic regulation, and stress responses (e.g., low temperature, drought, and defense) (Fig. 4), indicating their likely involvement in abiotic/biotic stress adaptation and hormone signaling. Most RcOSCA genes (RcOSCA4, RcOSCA10, RcOSCA2, RcOSCA1, RcOSCA8, RcOSCA6, and RcOSCA7) showed pronounced enrichment in cis-acting elements, implying that their transcription may be finely tuned by multiple signaling pathways and environmental cues. Notably, RcOSCA promoters harbor abundant elements such as ABRE, MBS, and LTR. MBS motifs, for instance, can be recognized and bound by MYB transcription factors to modulate gene expression [45]. These cis-elements are known to enhance plant tolerance to drought and low temperature by regulating the expression of stress-related genes. Consistent with our predictions, previous study in Z. mays indicated that the specific cis-elements in OSCA gene promoters participate in hormone or abiotic stress responses [12].
Drought and salt stress represent two of the most prevalent abiotic stresses affecting plants. To enhance stress tolerance and adaptive capacity, plants modulate physiological responses by inducing the expression of stress-related genes [46, 47]. The OSCA gene family has been widely implicated in regulating plant responses to osmotic stress [2, 10]. For instance, among the OSCA genes identified in Vigna radiata [48], most (e.g., VrOSCA1.4, VrOSCA2.2) were significantly upregulated under ABA, PEG, and NaCl treatments, particularly during early stress stages. In Z. mays, most of ZmOSCAs showed pronounced upregulation under ABA, PEG, and NaCl treatments [12]. Notably, the expression of ZmOSCA2.3 and ZmOSCA2.4 increased over 200-fold and correlated positively with proline accumulation, highlighting their role in osmotic adjustment [12]. Functional validation demonstrated that overexpression of ZmOSCA2.4 markedly enhanced drought tolerance in transgenic A. thaliana, concurrently upregulating drought-responsive genes (e.g., MYB44, DREB2A, NCED3) while suppressing senescence-related genes. Heterologous expression of GmOSCA1.1–GmOSCA1.5 from G. max in the A. thaliana osca1 mutant fully restored the impaired hyperosmolality-induced calcium signaling, confirming their function as osmosensors [36]. Furthermore, GmOSCA1.1–GmOSCA1.5, together with GmOSCA3.1 and GmOSCA3.2, were significantly induced under osmotic stress, and the overexpression of these genes improved drought tolerance in transgenic lines [36]. In the present study, most RcOSCA genes responded positively to drought and salt stress (Figs. 7 and 8). Expression profiling of the RcOSCA family revealed both common and stress-specific transcriptional responses. Several members, including RcOSCA2, RcOSCA3, and RcOSCA8, were induced under both drought and salt stress across multiple tissues, indicating their conserved role in osmotic sensing and signaling. In contrast, RcOSCA4 was specifically repressed under salt stress, highlighting a response unique to ionic/osmotic imbalance. This divergence in expression patterns underscores the complexity of osmotic stress signaling and suggests that members of the RcOSCA family may have undergone functional specialization, enabling fine-tuned adaptation to distinct environmental challenges. Under salt stress, the majority of RcOSCA genes in true leaves exhibited pronounced early induction at 6 h, consistent with observations in V. radiata [48]. In contrast to drought stress, salt treatment triggered a sharp, transient upregulation of RcOSCA6, with expression peaking at approximately 58-fold before declining rapidly. This pronounced early induction under salt, but not drought, prompts the hypothesis that RcOSCA6 could be involved in ion-specific signaling or homeostasis, in addition to its putative role in general osmotic sensing. Supporting this stress specific functional divergence, RcOSCA4 and RcOSCA6 were downregulated by salt stress but remained unchanged or were induced under drought, highlighting distinct regulatory programs activated by each stressor. Future functional studies are required to test this hypothesis.
In summary, this study provides the first genome-wide identification and bioinformatic characterization of the OSCA gene family in castor bean and preliminarily reveals its expression dynamics under salt and drought stresses. The distinct expression profiles of RcOSCA genes, particularly the marked early induction in true leaves under salt stress, suggest their critical functions in stress perception and adaptation.
These findings provide valuable candidate genes for uncovering the molecular basis of salt and drought tolerance in castor bean and lay a foundation for subsequent molecular investigations. In particular, genes displaying stress-specific induction patterns, such as RcOSCA6, warrant further functional validation to evaluate their potential for improving stress tolerance through genetic engineering or breeding.

## Conclusions
In this study, we identified 10 OSCA genes in the castor bean genome. A series of comprehensive analyses were performed, covering chromosomal localization, physicochemical properties, subcellular localization, gene structure, phylogenetic relationships, cis-acting elements, and tissue-specific expression pattern. The promoter regions of RcOSCA genes contain various elements associated with growth and development, hormone response, light response, and stress response, including MBS and TC-rich repeats. Under salt and drought stress, RcOSCA genes displayed distinct expression patterns, underscoring their potential roles in regulating plant responses to abiotic stress. This study provide a foundation for understanding the molecular evolution of the OSCA gene family in castor bean and offer valuable candidate genes for further investigation into abiotic stress tolerance in R. communis.
