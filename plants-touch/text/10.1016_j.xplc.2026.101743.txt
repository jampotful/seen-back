The LPA1-ITA1-BRXL4 module regulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice.

# The LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4 module regulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice

## Abstract
AbstractTiller angle is a critical agronomic trait that influences rice plant architecture and yield potential. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying its regulation remain incompletely understood. Here, we report that ITA1 (LOC_Os01g51260), which encodes an MYB family transcription factor, positively regulates shoot gravitropism and restricts tiller angle in rice. The ita1 mutant, which is characterized by a 740-bp deletion in the ITA1 promoter, displays an increased tiller angle due to elevated ITA1 expression. Functional assays demonstrate that ITA1 directly activates BRXL4, a gene that modulates auxin transport by influencing the subcellular localization of LAZY1, a key regulator of shoot gravitropism. Yeast one-hybrid assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation–qPCR, electrophoretic mobility shift assays, and luciferase assays reveal that the transcriptional repressor LPA1, a member of the IDD family, directly binds to the 740-bp region deleted in the ita1 mutant and represses ITA1 expression. Genetic evidence shows that the lpa1 mutant phenocopies ita1 and that the increased tiller angle phenotype is partially suppressed in the ita1-c1 lpa1 double mutant. Together, our findings define a previously unknown LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4 regulatory cascade that controls shoot gravitropism and tiller angle by modulating auxin distribution. This study provides new insights into plant architectural regulation and identifies potential genetic targets for optimizing rice planting density and yield.Key words: rice, ITA1, tiller angle, shoot gravitropism, auxin

## Introduction
Rice (Oryza sativa) sustains more than half of the global population and has a pivotal role in ensuring food security worldwide. Improving rice yield and adaptability has long been a central objective of crop breeding programs. Among the diverse traits that define rice plant architecture, tiller angle is particularly important because it directly affects planting density, canopy structure, light interception, and ultimately grain yield (Wang and Li, 2008; Wang et al., 2022). Tiller angle, defined as the angle between a tiller and the vertical axis, determines the spatial arrangement of shoots and affects the efficiency of both photosynthesis and nutrient distribution (Li et al., 2021). Accordingly, an optimal tiller angle is a key feature of ideal plant architecture and enables high-density cultivation without compromising individual plant performance.
Accumulating evidence indicates that rice tiller angle is tightly regulated by the plant’s response to gravity, known as shoot gravitropism (Li et al., 2007, 2019; Wu et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Cai et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2025). This complex biological process enables plants to orient their shoots upward and roots downward, thereby ensuring proper growth orientation and optimal resource use. In rice, tillers arise from axillary meristems, and their orientation largely depends on the plant’s ability to perceive and respond to gravitational signals (Wang and Li, 2011). A central mechanism underlying shoot gravitropism is the asymmetric redistribution of auxin, a key plant hormone that promotes differential cell elongation in response to gravity (Wang et al., 2022). Genetic studies have identified numerous genes that contribute to this process by regulating gravity perception, signal transduction, auxin transport, and downstream growth responses (Li et al., 2007, 2019, 2024; Wu et al., 2013, 2018; Hu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2025).
The domestication and subsequent diversification of rice have shaped its tiller angle through the selection of architecture-related genes (Wu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019). During the early domestication of Asian and African rice, genes such as PROG1, PROG7, and RPAD were selected to promote more erect growth habits that facilitated intensive cultivation (Jin et al., 2008; Tan et al., 2008; Hu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2018). Further post-domestication selection within the two main subspecies, japonica and indica, resulted in distinct tiller angle phenotypes (Yu et al., 2007; Dong et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021). Loci such as TAC1, TAC3, TAC4, TIG1, and D2 have been associated with this divergence, revealing a complex genetic basis for tiller angle regulation. Despite these advances, the transcriptional and signaling networks that fine-tune this trait remain incompletely understood.
At the physiological level, shoot gravitropism in rice proceeds through four well-defined steps: gravity sensing, signal transduction, polar auxin transport, and organ curvature (Morita and Tasaka, 2004; Strohm et al., 2012). The starch–statolith hypothesis is a widely accepted model for gravity sensing; it proposes that starch-filled amyloplasts (statoliths) sediment within specialized cells in response to reorientation, thereby triggering downstream signaling events (Okamura et al., 2013; Morita et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2021). Disruption of starch biosynthesis or statolith sedimentation impairs gravity perception and often results in increased tiller angles. Several genes involved in this pathway, such as LAZY2 (LA2) (Huang et al., 2021), LAZY3 (LA3) (Cai et al., 2023), phosphoglucomutase (PGM) (Slewinski and Braun, 2010; Song et al., 2021), ADP–glucose pyrophosphorylase 1 (AGPL1), AGPL3 (Okamura et al., 2013), and loose plant architecture 1 (LPA1) (Wu et al., 2013), have been shown to affect shoot gravitropism and tiller angle through their roles in starch metabolism or statolith function. More recently, the identification of LA4, which regulates tiller angle independently of the canonical PGM–LA2–LA3 module, has revealed the existence of alternative, potentially redundant, gravity-sensing pathways (Wang et al., 2024).
The LAZY1 (LA1) gene is a central node in the shoot gravitropism signaling cascade (Li et al., 2007). It functions downstream of statolith sedimentation by modulating auxin transport and asymmetric auxin distribution. Its activity depends on its intracellular distribution; it is localized to both the plasma membrane and the nucleus. Recent findings have shown that the Brevis Radix-like protein BRXL4 interacts with LA1 and inhibits its nuclear localization. RNA interference–mediated knockdown of BRXL1, BRXL4, and BRXL5 enhances LA1 nuclear accumulation, leading to a stronger gravitropic response and narrower tiller angles, whereas BRXL4 overexpression produces the opposite effects (Li et al., 2019). These observations highlight the importance of the BRXL family in regulating LA1 function and, consequently, in shoot orientation.
Another key regulator of rice tiller angle is LPA1 (Wu et al., 2013), a transcription factor orthologous to Arabidopsis SGR5 (Yamauchi et al., 1997; Tanimoto et al., 2008). LPA1 promotes shoot gravitropism by facilitating statolith sedimentation in gravity-sensing tissues such as the coleoptile. Overexpression of LPA1 enhances PIN1a-mediated auxin transport and reduces tiller angle, supporting its role as an upstream regulator of the gravity–auxin–gravitropism pathway (Wu et al., 2013). However, despite the identification of these core regulators, the transcriptional hierarchy linking gravity perception to LA1 function remains largely uncharacterized.
In the present study, we report the identification and functional characterization of a novel regulatory module, LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4, that modulates tiller angle in rice. We isolated a rice mutant, ita1 (increased tiller angle 1), which displays a significantly larger tiller angle due to a 740-bp deletion in the promoter region of the MYB26 gene, which we designated ITA1. This deletion leads to elevated ITA1 expression, which in turn upregulates BRXL4, thereby influencing the subcellular localization of LA1 and altering auxin distribution. Notably, we demonstrate that LPA1 functions as a transcriptional repressor of ITA1 by directly binding to the 740-bp region in its promoter. In the ita1 mutant, the deletion abolishes LPA1 binding, resulting in derepression of ITA1 and subsequent activation of the ITA1–BRXL4 signaling axis. These findings establish a previously unrecognized link between gravity sensing and auxin-mediated tiller angle regulation through transcriptional control. The LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4 module provides new insights into the hierarchical control of shoot orientation in rice and highlights potential targets for molecular breeding. Moreover, our findings extend the understanding of transcription factor–mediated fine-tuning of plant architecture and offer novel strategies for engineering rice cultivars with desirable growth habits and improved agronomic performance.

## Results

## The ita1 mutant displays defective shoot gravitropism and an increased tiller angle
To investigate the molecular mechanisms that regulate rice tiller angle, we screened for novel genes that influence this trait. An increased tiller angle mutant, ita1, was isolated from the japonica cultivar Wuyunjing 7 (W7) following heavy ion beam mutagenesis. Compared with the wild type (WT), ita1 displayed a prostrate growth habit and a significantly increased tiller angle (Figures 1A–1C). Rice tiller angle is largely determined by the plant’s ability to respond to gravitational cues (Wang and Li, 2008). To evaluate whether the increased tiller angle of ita1 was caused by defective shoot gravitropism, we examined gravitropic responses in seedlings. Compared with WT seedlings, ita1 seedlings displayed significantly reduced shoot curvature upon gravistimulation (Figures 1D and 1E). These findings suggest that ITA1 acts as a positive regulator of shoot gravitropism in rice and that the impaired gravitropic response observed in the ita1 mutant underlies its increased tiller angle.

## ITA1 may regulate the asymmetric distribution of auxin in response to gravistimulation
The establishment of an asymmetric distribution of auxin between the upper and lower sections of stems via lateral auxin transport (LAT) is a critical step in gravitropic responses (Li et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2018). To investigate whether ITA1 is involved in auxin redistribution, we evaluated LAT in both ita1 mutants and WT plants. 3H-IAA was applied to the apical ends of coleoptile segments, which were then incubated in a horizontal position for 2.5 h. Following incubation, radioactivity levels were measured on the upper and lower sides of the coleoptiles to assess asymmetric auxin distribution (Figure 1F). In ita1 plants, significantly less 3H-IAA accumulated on the lower side of the coleoptile than on the upper side, in contrast to the distribution observed in WT plants (Figure 1G). To further confirm the effects of ita1 on LAT, we measured the expression of the auxin-responsive marker gene OsIAA20 in the shoot bases of WT and ita1 seedlings following gravistimulation. We divided the shoot bases into upper and lower sides along the direction of gravistimulation and found that OsIAA20 was asymmetrically expressed in the shoot bases of both WT and ita1, with higher expression on the lower side (Figure 1H). However, OsIAA20 expression on the lower side was significantly reduced in ita1 compared with WT (Figure 1H), implying that asymmetric auxin distribution upon gravistimulation was impaired in ita1. WOX6 and WOX11 are also asymmetrically expressed in response to asymmetric auxin distribution following gravistimulation (Hu et al., 2020). We therefore measured the expression of WOX6 (Figure 1I) and WOX11 (Figure 1J) on the upper and lower sides of the shoot bases and found that their preferential expression on the lower side was also significantly reduced in ita1 seedlings. These results indicate that ITA1 positively regulates the asymmetric distribution of auxin in response to gravistimulation and functions upstream of WOX6 and WOX11.

## Map-based cloning of ITA1
To investigate the molecular basis of the increased tiller angle phenotype observed in the ita1 mutant, we carried out a series of genetic and molecular analyses. First, we crossed the ita1 mutant with its corresponding WT; all F1 individuals exhibited the WT phenotype. In the F2 population, 426 individuals displayed the WT phenotype whereas 112 showed the ita1-like phenotype characterized by increased tiller angles. This segregation pattern fits a Mendelian 3:1 ratio (χ2 = 0.382 < χ20.05 = 3.84; p > 0.05), indicating that the increased tiller angle phenotype in the ita1 mutant is controlled by a single recessive nuclear gene. To map the ita1 locus, we crossed the ita1 mutant with 93-11, an indica variety, to generate a mapping population. Using a map-based cloning approach, we initially mapped the ita1 locus between the molecular markers ASR30 and ASR33 on chromosome 1. Fine mapping further narrowed the candidate region to an approximately 25-kb interval flanked by the insertion/deletion (indel) markers FM4 and FM5 (Figure 2A). Within this region, three predicted open reading frames were identified (Figure 2A). Sequence analysis revealed a 740-bp deletion in the promoter region of LOC_Os01g51260 (Figures 2B and 2C), which was subsequently confirmed by PCR amplification spanning the deleted region (Figure 2D).

Because promoter mutations typically affect gene expression, we next examined whether the 740-bp deletion influences LOC_Os01g51260 transcription. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR) analysis showed that ITA1 expression was significantly elevated in the ita1 mutant compared with the WT, suggesting that the deletion leads to ITA1 upregulation. To determine whether increased ITA1 expression is responsible for the increased tiller angle phenotype, we generated transgenic lines overexpressing ITA1 in the WT background. These overexpression lines exhibited significantly higher ITA1 transcript levels (Figure 2G) and displayed noticeably larger tiller angles than the WT (Figures 2F and 2H). To further confirm that LOC_Os01g51260 corresponds to ITA1, the gene responsible for the mutant phenotype, we used CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing to knock out ITA1 in the ita1 mutant background. We obtained two independent knockout lines (Supplemental Figure 1), both of which showed a substantial reduction in tiller angle compared with the ita1 mutant (Figures 2I and 2J), partially restoring the phenotype toward WT levels.
Overall, these results demonstrate that LOC_Os01g51260 is the causal gene underlying the ita1 mutant phenotype and that the 740-bp promoter deletion leads to increased ITA1 expression, resulting in increased tiller angle in rice.

## ITA1 encodes an MYB family transcriptional activator
To investigate the spatial and temporal expression pattern of ITA1, we performed RT–qPCR to examine its transcript levels across various tissues. The results showed that ITA1 is predominantly expressed in panicles at the heading stage and exhibits high expression in the shoot base during the seedling stage (Figure 3A). Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that the shoot base is critical for shoot gravitropism and tiller angle determination (Wu et al., 2013). Therefore, the higher expression of ITA1 in the shoot base is consistent with its potential function in regulating tiller angle. Gene annotation revealed that ITA1 encodes an MYB family transcription factor, MYB26. A defining characteristic of transcription factors is their nuclear localization. To investigate the subcellular localization of ITA1, we constructed an ITA1 fusion protein with a C-terminal green fluorescent protein (GFP) driven by the CaMV 35S promoter (p35S::ITA1-GFP). Transient expression of this construct, followed by confocal laser scanning microscopy, showed that the ITA1–GFP fusion protein was predominantly localized to the nucleus (Figure 3B and Supplemental Figure 2), consistent with its function as a transcription factor. To determine whether ITA1 has intrinsic transcriptional activation activity, we performed a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay. The coding sequence of ITA1 was fused to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain in the pGBKT7 vector, and the construct was transformed into yeast. Transformants were able to grow on both SD/−Trp/−Leu medium and the stringent selective medium SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade, which indicates that the ITA1 protein is a transcriptional activator in yeast (Figure 3C). To further delineate the activation domain within the ITA1 protein, we generated a series of truncated constructs covering its N- and C-terminal regions (Figure 3C, left), which were also cloned into pGBKT7 and tested in yeast. The results showed that the C-terminal region of ITA1 is responsible for transcriptional activation (Figure 3C, right). We validated this result using a dual-luciferase reporter (DLR) assay in rice protoplasts. Full-length ITA1 and a series of truncation variants were fused to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain, and the resulting constructs were co-transformed with a GAL4-responsive LUC reporter. Compared with the negative control (GAL4-BD alone), constructs ITA1-M2 (amino acids 117–307), ITA1-M4 (185–307), and ITA1-M5 (1–280) showed strong activation of the LUC reporter. In contrast, ITA1-M1 (1–116), ITA1-M3 (1–184), ITA1-M6 (281–307), and the deletion mutant ITA1-M7 (lacking amino acids 185–280) failed to activate LUC expression (Figure 3D). Together, these findings demonstrate that the C-terminal region of ITA1, specifically amino acids 185–280, confers strong transcriptional activation, consistent with its role as an MYB family transcription factor.

## ITA1 modulates tiller angle through direct transcriptional regulation of BRXL4 expression
To explore the molecular mechanism by which ITA1 regulates tiller angle, we first analyzed the expression of previously characterized tiller angle-associated genes. RT–qPCR analysis revealed that, among the candidate genes examined, only BRXL4 showed significantly increased expression in the ita1 mutant compared with the WT, whereas the transcript levels of the other tested genes remained largely unchanged (Supplemental Figure 3). To further assess the relationship between ITA1 and BRXL4, we quantified BRXL4 expression in ITA1 overexpression lines as well as in CRISPR-edited ITA1 knockout (ita1-c1) plants. BRXL4 expression was significantly elevated in both ita1 and ITA1-overexpressing plants, whereas it was markedly reduced in ita1-c1 plants (Figure 4A). These findings suggest that ITA1 functions as a positive regulator of BRXL4 expression, providing a potential mechanistic link between ITA1 activity and tiller angle modulation in rice.

To determine whether ITA1 directly regulates BRXL4 expression, we conducted a yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay to test the interaction between ITA1 and the BRXL4 promoter (Figure 4B). The Y1H results showed that ITA1 activated the expression of the LacZ reporter gene driven by the BRXL4 promoter, whereas the negative control carrying the empty pB42AD vector did not (Figure 4C). These findings suggest that ITA1 directly binds to the BRXL4 promoter in yeast.
MYB family transcription factors typically regulate gene expression by binding to specific cis-elements, such as MYB-binding elements (MBEs), which are characterized by AC-rich motifs. To investigate whether ITA1 binds to the BRXL4 promoter through an MBE, we performed a sequence analysis and identified a putative MBE within the BRXL4 promoter region (Supplemental Figure 4). To test this interaction in rice, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays using WT and pActin1::ITA1-FLAG overexpression lines. The BRXL4 promoter fragment containing the MBE site (designated P7) was significantly enriched in immunoprecipitated chromatin from ITA1-FLAG plants, indicating in vivo binding of ITA1 to this region (Figure 4D). For in vitro validation, we performed an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) using a recombinant ITA1–glutathione S-transferase (GST) fusion protein. The GST-ITA1 fusion protein specifically bound to the P7 probe containing the MBE sequence, as evidenced by a clear mobility shift, whereas the negative control (GST alone) did not produce a shift (Figure 4E). Moreover, competition assays with increasing concentrations of unlabeled probe progressively reduced the binding signal, confirming the specificity of the ITA1–MBE interaction (Figure 4E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ITA1 functions as a transcriptional activator by directly binding to the MBE site in the BRXL4 promoter, as demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo, thereby establishing a direct regulatory link between ITA1 and BRXL4 expression.
To further validate ITA1 as a positive regulator of BRXL4 expression, we performed a DLR assay in rice protoplasts. A reporter construct was generated by fusing a 2-kb fragment of the BRXL4 promoter upstream of the LUC gene (Figure 4F). This reporter was co-transfected with an effector construct expressing ITA1 under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. Co-expression with ITA1 led to an approximately 8-fold increase in LUC activity compared with the negative control, which indicates strong transcriptional activation of the BRXL4 promoter (Figure 4F). These findings provide additional evidence that ITA1 acts as a transcriptional activator by directly promoting BRXL4 expression in rice cells.
To genetically confirm this regulatory relationship, we knocked down BRXL4 expression in ita1 plants, and two independent BRXL4-RNAi lines with significantly reduced BRXL4 expression were selected for phenotypic analysis (Figure 4G). The suppression of BRXL4 expression in the ita1 background restored the increased tiller angle phenotype to near WT levels (Figures 4H and 4I). These findings provide further evidence that BRXL4 is a downstream target of ITA1.

## Identification of LPA1 as an upstream regulator of ITA1 in rice
To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which the 740-bp deletion in the ITA1 promoter leads to an increased tiller angle, we performed a Y1H screen using the deleted promoter fragment as bait. A complementary DNA (cDNA) library from the aerial tissues of rice seedlings, fused to the yeast GAL4 activation domain (AD), was used as the prey. This screen identified LPA1, a transcription factor belonging to the indeterminate domain (IDD) family (Supplemental Table 1). Notably, previous studies have reported that loss-of-function mutations in LPA1 result in an increased tiller angle phenotype (Wu et al., 2013), which suggests that LPA1 may directly regulate ITA1 expression. Sequence analysis of the 740-bp promoter fragment revealed the presence of two putative IDD-binding elements (Supplemental Figure 5). To test whether LPA1 binds this region, we performed a Y1H assay using the promoter fragment fused to a LacZ reporter. The results showed that LPA1 specifically bound this promoter fragment and activated reporter expression, confirming the interaction in yeast. To test this interaction in vivo, we conducted ChIP assays using WT rice plants and pActin1::LPA1–FLAG overexpression lines. ChIP–qPCR revealed that the ITA1 promoter region containing the predicted IDD-binding element (IBE) was significantly enriched in immunoprecipitates from LPA1–FLAG plants, which indicates that LPA1 binds this region in planta (Figure 5C). We further confirmed this direct interaction in vitro using EMSA. A biotin-labeled DNA probe containing the IBE showed a distinct mobility shift when incubated with recombinant GST-LPA1 protein. Addition of excess unlabeled competitor probe abolished the shift, confirming binding specificity (Figure 5D). Expression analysis demonstrated that ITA1 transcript levels were significantly increased in lpa1 knockout lines and markedly reduced in LPA1 overexpression lines (Figure 5E). Consistent with this, a DLR assay in rice protoplasts showed that LPA1 strongly repressed luciferase activity driven by the full-length ITA1 promoter. In contrast, LPA1 failed to repress promoter activity when the 740-bp binding region was deleted (Figure 5F), further supporting its role as a transcriptional repressor of ITA1 through this cis-regulatory element. To genetically validate the regulatory relationship between LPA1 and ITA1, we generated an lpa1-c1 ita1-c1 double mutant by crossing the lpa1-c1 mutant, which displays an increased tiller angle, with ita1-c1. The double mutant exhibited a significantly smaller tiller angle than the lpa1-c1 single mutant (Figures 5G and 5H), which suggests that ITA1 functions downstream of LPA1 in the regulatory pathway that controls tiller angle. Moreover, expression analysis of BRXL4, a known downstream target of ITA1, showed significantly higher expression in lpa1 mutants and reduced expression in LPA1 overexpression lines (Figure 5I), which indicates that LPA1 negatively regulates the ITA1–BRXL4 module.

For further genetic validation that ITA1 functions downstream of LPA1 through a cis-regulatory mechanism, we generated promoter-edited mutants of ITA1 using CRISPR–Cas9. Two guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the upstream promoter region were designed, and among the edited lines we identified a mutant (designated ita1-pro-c) carrying a 70-bp deletion in the ITA1 promoter (Figures 6A and 6B). Notably, this deleted fragment encompassed the predicted IBE recognized by LPA1. Phenotypic analysis revealed that the ita1-pro-c mutant exhibited a significantly larger tiller angle than WT plants (Figures 6C and 6E). Consistent with this phenotype, RT–qPCR analysis showed that ITA1 transcript levels were markedly increased in ita1-pro-c plants relative to WT (Figure 6D). Moreover, the expression of BRXL4, a direct downstream target of ITA1, was also significantly upregulated in the promoter-edited line (Figure 6F), indicating the activation of the ITA1–BRXL4 regulatory module. Given the established role of ITA1 in shoot gravitropism, we also examined the gravitropic responses of ita1-pro-c seedlings. Upon gravistimulation, ita1-pro-c plants displayed reduced shoot curvature compared with WT, indicating an attenuated gravitropic response (Figures 6G and 6H). These phenotypes closely resembled those observed in ITA1 overexpression lines and were consistent with elevated ITA1 activity.
Promoter editing of ITA1 confirms that deletion of the IBE derepresses ITA1 expression and impairs shoot gravitropism.

## Discussion
Plant architecture is a fundamental determinant of crop yield and environmental adaptability. In rice (Oryza sativa), tiller angle is a key architectural trait that directly influences planting density, canopy structure, and light interception efficiency, thereby affecting grain productivity (Wang et al., 2018, 2022). Although numerous genes involved in tiller angle regulation have been identified, how perception of gravity is transcriptionally coupled to downstream growth responses remains incompletely understood. In this study, we identify a previously uncharacterized transcriptional regulatory module, LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4, that integrates gravity sensing with auxin-mediated shoot gravitropism and ultimately controls tiller angle determination in rice.
Our results establish ITA1, which encodes an MYB family transcription factor homologous to MYB26, as a positive regulator of rice tiller angle through its role in shoot gravitropism. Altered ITA1 expression leads to pronounced changes in tiller angle (Figure 2), underscoring the importance of precise transcriptional control of this gene for normal shoot architecture. Functional analyses further demonstrate that ITA1 is required for proper gravitropic responses, as disruption of ITA1 activity results in reduced shoot bending and impaired asymmetric auxin redistribution following gravistimulation (Figure 1). These findings position ITA1 as a key regulatory node linking gravity-induced signaling to auxin-dependent differential growth. Shoot gravitropism depends on the establishment of asymmetric auxin gradients, which drive differential cell elongation on opposing sides of the shoot (Morita and Tasaka, 2004). Altered ITA1 activity compromises auxin asymmetry during gravistimulation and is accompanied by changes in the expression of auxin-responsive genes (Figures 1G–1J). Rather than acting directly on the auxin transport machinery, ITA1 primarily exerts its effects through transcriptional regulation of downstream components in the gravitropic signaling pathway. Consistent with this, we identified BRXL4 as a direct transcriptional target of ITA1. BRXL4 has been shown to interact with LAZY1 and regulate its subcellular localization, thereby attenuating shoot gravitropism and influencing auxin redistribution (Li et al., 2019). We demonstrated that ITA1 directly binds to an MYB-binding element within the BRXL4 promoter to activate transcription, and that genetic suppression of BRXL4 partially rescues the increased tiller angle phenotype caused by elevated ITA1 activity (Figure 4). Together, these results define a transcriptional cascade in which ITA1 regulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle determination by activating BRXL4 and modulating the LAZY1-dependent auxin redistribution pathway.
Upstream of ITA1, we identified LPA1, an IDD family transcription factor previously implicated in gravity perception, as a direct transcriptional repressor of ITA1. LPA1 binds to the IBE within the ITA1 promoter and suppresses its transcription, thereby regulating ITA1 expression levels (Figure 5). Loss of LPA1 function leads to derepression of ITA1, whereas increased LPA1 activity strongly represses ITA1 expression, establishing a clear genetic and transcriptional hierarchy between these two regulators.
Previous studies have reported that LPA1 primarily regulates shoot gravitropism by influencing gravity perception through the sedimentation of gravity-sensing amyloplasts in rice coleoptiles, consistent with the starch–statolith hypothesis (Wu et al., 2013). In that study, LAZY1 expression was not altered in lpa1 mutants. However, complementary studies of IDD family transcription factors suggest that additional transcriptional regulatory mechanisms may also contribute to auxin-mediated gravitropic responses. Functional homologs of LPA1 in Arabidopsis, including IDD14, IDD15/SGR5, and IDD16, have been shown to directly regulate auxin biosynthesis and polar auxin transport, thereby modulating auxin gradients and gravitropic responses (Cui et al., 2013). These findings indicate that IDD transcription factors coordinate auxin-mediated growth responses independently of direct changes in LAZY1 transcription.
Our study provides a mechanistic framework that reconciles these observations. We show that LPA1 does not regulate LAZY1 expression but instead modulates auxin-mediated gravitropic responses through the LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4 regulatory cascade. In this pathway, LPA1-mediated transcriptional repression controls ITA1 abundance, ITA1 activates BRXL4, and BRXL4 modulates the subcellular localization of LAZY1, ultimately influencing auxin redistribution and asymmetric growth during gravistimulation (Figure 7). Therefore, although LAZY1 transcript levels remain unchanged in lpa1 mutants, auxin-mediated gravitropic responses are altered through changes in LAZY1 localization and downstream auxin flow.
Proposed model of the LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4 module that regulates rice tiller angle.
In WT rice, LPA1 binds to and represses the ITA1 promoter. ITA1 directly binds to the BRXL4 promoter and activates its expression. BRXL4 regulates the subcellular localization of LA1, which is essential for establishing asymmetric auxin distribution between the upper and lower sides of the shoot base. This leads to asymmetric expression of WOX6 and WOX11 and ultimately maintains normal shoot gravitropism and tiller angle. In the ita1 mutant, a 740-bp deletion in the ITA1 promoter disrupts LPA1 binding, leading to upregulated ITA1 expression. Elevated ITA1 levels result in activation of BRXL4, leading to BRXL4 accumulation. This alters LA1 localization and disrupts auxin asymmetry, resulting in abnormal WOX6/WOX11 expression and an increased tiller angle.
Collectively, these findings support a layered regulatory model in which LPA1 controls rice shoot gravitropism through two coordinated mechanisms: one acting at the level of gravity perception via amyloplast sedimentation (the starch–statolith hypothesis), and the other acting through transcriptional control of auxin redistribution via the LPA1–ITA1–BRXL4 pathway (the Cholodny–Went hypothesis). This integrated transcriptional network provides new mechanistic insights into how gravity perception is translated into directional growth and tiller angle determination in rice and identifies potential molecular targets for optimizing plant architecture in breeding programs.

## Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
The ita1 (increased tiller angle 1) mutant was derived from the japonica cultivar Wuyunjing7 (WYJ7) via heavy ion beam mutagenesis. To generate a mapping population, the ita1 mutant was crossed with the indica cultivar 93-11, and the resulting F2 population was used for genetic analysis. To generate the lpa1-c1 ita1-c1 double mutant, the lpa1-c1 and ita1-c1 mutants were crossed, and F2 progeny were genotyped by PCR to identify homozygous double-mutant plants. All rice plants were cultivated under natural field conditions at the Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Hefei, China) during the summer season, and in Sanya (Hainan Province, China) during the winter season.

## Shoot gravitropism assay
WT and ita1 seeds were surface sterilized using 0.15% (w/v) sodium hypochlorite and germinated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (pH 5.8) at 28°C for 48 h. Uniformly germinated seeds were then transferred to square plates containing half-strength MS medium and grown vertically at 28°C. After 4 days, seedlings were subjected to a gravitropic stimulus by rotating the plates by 90°. Shoot curvature angles were recorded 48 h after reorientation using ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/).

## LAT assay
The polar auxin transport assay was performed with slight modifications based on a previously published method (Li et al., 2007). Five-day-old etiolated rice seedlings were used. A one-centimeter segment was excised from the basal portion of each coleoptile and pre-incubated in half-strength liquid MS medium (pH 5.8) for 2 h with gentle shaking to remove endogenous indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). After equilibration, the coleoptile segments were positioned horizontally in 0.6-ml tubes with their apical ends inserted into 10 μl agar blocks containing 0.5 μM [3H]-IAA. After 2.5 h of incubation in the dark, the 5-mm segments from the basal (non-apical) ends were collected and longitudinally divided into upper and lower halves. Each half was immersed in 1 ml scintillation solution (0.27 mM 2,5-diphenyloxazole in 60% [v/v] xylene and 40% [v/v] ethanol) for 18 h. Radioactivity in each sample was measured using a liquid scintillation counter (1450 MicroBeta TriLux, PerkinElmer).

## Quantification of gene expression
Total RNA was extracted from the indicated rice tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA contamination was removed by DNase I treatment, and cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (GeneStar) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT–qPCR) was performed using gene-specific primers (listed in Supplemental Table 2) and an RT–qPCR kit (GeneStar) on a LightCycler96 instrument (Roche). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and three independent biological replicates were included. The rice OsActin1 gene (LOC_Os03g50885) was used as the internal control to normalize gene expression levels. Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method.

## Map-based cloning
The ita1 locus was mapped and cloned using individuals from the F2 mapping population described above. Simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers distributed across the rice genome were used to genotype 42 mutant individuals, which enabled rough localization of the ita1 locus. Fine mapping was subsequently performed using a larger population of 1326 mutant plants. Additional SSR markers and custom-designed indel markers within the initially mapped region were used to further narrow the candidate interval. Candidate genes within the fine-mapped region were identified by PCR amplification and sequencing analysis. Sequences were compared between WT and ita1 mutant plants to identify the causal mutation. All primers used for map-based cloning are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

## Vector construction and rice transformation
Full-length ITA1 and LPA1 coding sequences were amplified from WYJ7 and inserted into the pActin1::FLAG-nos vector to generate pActin1::ITA1-FLAG and pActin1::LPA1-FLAG constructs for overexpression analysis. A 250-bp fragment of the OsBRXL4 coding sequence was amplified and cloned into the pUCC vector as previously described (Wang et al., 2015) and subsequently used to generate the pActin1::BRXL4-RNAi construct for gene silencing analysis. For targeted mutagenesis, CRISPR–Cas9-based genome editing constructs were designed using gRNAs specific to ITA1 and LPA1. For targeted editing of the ITA1 promoter, two gRNAs were designed to target sequences flanking the IBE within the ITA1 promoter region. Assembly of gRNA expression cassettes and binary vectors followed established protocols (Ma et al., 2015). All constructs were introduced into rice via Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain AGL1)–mediated transformation. Transgenic plants generated by CRISPR–Cas9-mediated genome editing, including ita1 and lpa1 knockout mutants as well as ITA1 promoter-edited lines, were screened by PCR and Sanger sequencing to identify mutations at the target sites. For the ITA1 promoter-edited lines, a mutant carrying a small deletion encompassing the IBE motif was selected for further analyses. Primer sequences used for vector construction and plant genotyping are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

## Subcellular localization
To investigate the subcellular localization of ITA1, the full-length ITA1 cDNA was fused to the GFP coding sequence under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S (CaMV 35S) promoter to generate the p35S::ITA1-GFP construct. CEF1, a gene previously reported to localize to the nucleus (Ye et al., 2015), was cloned into the p35S::mCherry-nos vector as a nuclear marker. These constructs were transiently expressed in both rice protoplasts and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via polyethylene glycol (PEG)–mediated transformation and Agrobacterium infiltration, respectively. Fluorescence signals were observed 24 h after transformation using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope. Primer sequences used for construct generation are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

## Transcriptional activation assay in yeast
Full-length ITA1 cDNA and various domain-specific fragments were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate fusion constructs with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain. These constructs were transformed into the yeast strain AH109 using the lithium acetate method. Transformed yeast cells were first cultured on SD/−Trp/−Leu (double-dropout) medium to select for transformants and then transferred to SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade (quadruple-dropout) medium to assess transcriptional activation. Growth on the selective quadruple-dropout medium indicated transcriptional activation by the inserted ITA1 fragments. Primer sequences used for vector construction are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

## Y1H assays
The full-length coding sequences of ITA1 and LPA1 were cloned into the pB42AD vector to generate fusion constructs with the GAL4 activation domain. Promoter fragments of approximately 2.5 kb upstream of BRXL4 and the 740-bp promoter region of ITA1 (corresponding to the deleted region in the ita1 mutant) were amplified from WYJ7 genomic DNA and inserted into the pLacZi vector upstream of the lacZ reporter gene. The pB42AD effector and pLacZi reporter constructs were co-transformed into the yeast strain EGY48. Positive colonies were selected on SD/−Trp/−Ura medium and then cultured in SD/−Ura liquid medium for 48 h at 30°C.
For Y1H library screening, the 740-bp ITA1 promoter fragment was inserted into the pHIS2 vector and transformed into the yeast strain Y187. A seedling shoot cDNA library was constructed using the pGADT7-Rec2 vector (Clontech). Screening was performed using the Matchmaker One-Hybrid Library Construction and Screening Kit (K1617-1, Clontech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (User Manual PT3529-1, Clontech). The bait yeast strains were mated with yeast carrying the cDNA library and plated on SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His selection medium supplemented with 20 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) to suppress background expression. Positive clones were selected and subjected to either plasmid extraction or colony PCR using primers AD-F and AD-R. PCR amplification was performed under the following conditions: 95°C for 5 min; 36 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 56°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 2 min; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min followed by a hold at 12°C. Primer sequences used for vector construction and screening are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

## ChIP–qPCR assays
ChIP was performed using 14-day-old seedlings of transgenic rice lines expressing pActin1::ITA1-FLAG or pActin1::LPA1-FLAG. Approximately 2 g of fresh seedling tissue was harvested and cross-linked in 1% (v/v) formaldehyde under vacuum for 10 min to preserve protein–DNA interactions. Cross-linking was quenched by adding 0.125 M glycine, followed by rinsing with cold sterile water. Nuclei were isolated and lysed, and chromatin was sheared to an average size of ∼500 bp using sonication. The resulting chromatin solution was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C using anti-FLAG antibodies (Sigma, F1804) with gentle rotation. As a control, a mock immunoprecipitation without an antibody was performed in parallel. The immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and used as a template for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Primers targeting putative binding sites in the ITA1 and BRXL4 promoters were designed for qPCR analysis and are listed in Supplemental Table 2. Enrichment was calculated relative to input DNA and normalized to a negative control genomic region.

## EMSA assays
The full-length coding sequences of ITA1 and LPA1 were cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 expression vector to generate GST fusion constructs. These plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells (Sangon Biotech, B528414-0100). Protein expression was induced at 16°C with 0.15 mM IPTG for 12 h at 150 rpm. The resulting GST-ITA1 and GST-LPA1 fusion proteins were purified using glutathione–Sepharose 4B beads according to the manufacturer’s instructions and previously established protocols. Double-stranded DNA probes containing the target MBE or IBE sequences were synthesized and labeled at the 3′ end with biotin. For binding reactions, purified recombinant proteins were incubated with the labeled probes in the reaction buffer provided with the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20148). DNA–protein complexes were resolved on native polyacrylamide gels and transferred to nylon membranes for detection via chemiluminescence according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Unlabeled competitor probes were included in some reactions to assess binding specificity. The DNA probe sequences and primers used for probe preparation are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

## Transactivation assays in rice protoplasts
Transactivation assays were performed in rice protoplasts as previously described (Ye et al., 2018), with minor modifications. For effector constructs, the full-length coding sequences of ITA1 and LPA1 were amplified and fused to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain (GAL4BD). The empty GAL4BD vector and the GAL4BD-VP16 fusion construct were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. For reporter constructs, potential self-activation was assessed using a firefly luciferase (LUC) gene driven by a minimal 35S promoter (TATA box only) with five tandem GAL4-binding elements in a pUC19-based vector. To analyze transactivation of downstream targets, approximately 2.5-kb promoter fragments upstream of candidate gene start codons were amplified and inserted upstream of the LUC reporter gene to generate promoter–reporter constructs. A pTRL plasmid containing the Renilla luciferase (REN) gene driven by the CaMV 35S promoter was co-transformed as an internal normalization control. Reporter, effector, and internal control plasmids were co-transformed into rice protoplasts using PEG-mediated transformation. After incubation in the dark for 16 h at 25°C, dual-luciferase activity was measured using a commercial assay kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative firefly LUC activity was calculated by normalizing to Renilla REN activity. Primer sequences used for vector construction are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

## Statistical analyses
All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 20.0). Significance between two groups was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and all figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.
