## Introduction
Plants are constantly exposed to SVs from different sources such as rain, wind, animals, neighboring plants, and human activities. These acoustic stimuli trigger short-term molecular reprogramming, long-term changes in plant architecture, and developmental and adaptive responses [1], [2], [3]. Owing to a lack of knowledge about the plant sensory system, the opinion on SV signaling in plants has long been regarded as a contentious fringe theory. However, recent findings on plant SV interactions have opened new frontiers in plant acoustic biology that further provide evidence of plants’ ability to emit and respond to SVs [1], [2]. For instance, Khait et al. [1], recent work provides a novel and reliable perspective, demonstrating how tomato and tobacco plants emit sound during drought and wound stress. Also, this study provided valuable information on plants’ ability to emit distinct sounds against different stressors [1]. Likewise, SVs can improve plant fitness and growth under unfavorable environmental circumstances [4], [5]. These studies further support the notion that SV perception in plants can have beneficial roles in plants in terms of ecological and adaptive ways. At present, phytoacoustics is one the most exciting and growing fields in plant sensory biology with many things awaiting discovery. Furthermore, it is not surprising if plants can sense touch, wind, rain, wounding, or other mechano stimuli why can’t they sense SVs?
Over the last two decades, significant advances have been made in understanding how plants respond to other mechanostimuli like touch, rain, and wind, thereby broadening our fundamental understanding of the evolved signaling pathways. For instance, leaves of touch-sensitive plants, such as Dionaea muscipula and Mimosa pudica, fold, bend, or close to capture their prey, or fold and bend in response to mechanostimuli [6]. Interestingly, some plants respond to touch stimuli in seconds, whereas others undergo morphogenetic changes over days or weeks. Similarly, mechanostimuli also alter plant architecture, with plant stems becoming shorter and stiffer when exposed to wind or touch indicating that plants may change their phenotypic traits in response to their mechanical environment [7]. With the technical revolution, various sensors, signaling molecules, and transcription factors have been identified in plants in response to different mechnostimuli. For instance, Moerkercke et al. [8] presented a high-resolution panorama of the transcriptional, proteomic, and hormonal changes after water spraying in Arabidopsis, paving the way for exploring new horizons of rain mechanosimulation in plants. Previous studies have also shown the role of ion channels, such as mid1-complementing activity 1 (MCA1), during touch treatment in Arabidopsis [9]. Recently, Arabidopsis Piezo-type mechanosensitive (MS) ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1), an ortholog of mammalian PIEZO, was shown to sense mechanical stress in roots [10]. On the other hand the MS channel of small conductance (MscS)-like 10 (MSL10) was recently identified as a molecular component for the perception of oscillations triggered by the wind in Arabidopsis [11]. These discoveries on plants’ response to mechanical stimulation both locally and systematically have broadened our understanding of plant mechanosensing. Also, how they translate mechanical information into biochemical signaling events that elicit biological responses via mechanotransduction opened new paradigms in plant sensory biology. Interestingly, from a plant ecological and adaptive perspective mechanosensing allows plants to detect a wide range of mechanostimuli (e.g., touch, gravity, wind, rain, SVs, and turgor pressure) that aids in sensing, trapping, devouring nutritious animals, and tracking water [12]. In general, the signaling of mechanostimuli and its effects on different cellular components are better addressed in animals than in plants. For instance, animal cells perceive mechanical inputs via different MS players, such as G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), stretch-activated ion channels, integrins, and growth factor receptors, thereby activating diverse mechanotransduction pathways [13], [14]. In contrast, there is very little information available on mechanoreceptors in plants. Most pioneering work in plant mechanobiology has primarily focused on MS channels. However, there could be other key sensors or receptors, similar to those in animals that require further investigation.
Nature is full of sound. Thus, it is not surprising that plants can sense and emit SVs and use them for their ecological and adaptive benefits. However, one fundamental question in phytoacoustics is where, when, and how SVs regulate plant cellular machinery. Secondly, how biotic and abiotic cues trigger plants to emit SVs remains open to the scientific body. To answer these questions, researchers must focus on deciphering the core SV perception and signal transduction mechanisms rather than its application for understanding the regulatory mechanism of SVs in plant cellular machinery. Plant behavior should be investigated to understand whether plants can hear or emit SVs for communication. Also, identifying bonafide SV receptors and their transducing signaling molecules that execute or coordinate complex signaling cascades in plants will be a game changer in overcoming these roadblocks in phytoacoustics. Unfortunately, the relationship between mechanical and biochemical information during plant–SV interactions at the cellular level is largely enigmatic despite the availability of new high-throughput tools. However, some insights into plants’ response to SVs at the molecular, metabolic, and proteomic levels are slowly emerging due to multi-omics. For instance, SV treatment in plants causes massive reprogramming at the transcriptional level, phytohormonal production, calcium ion burst, and ROS production, all of which are key factors in signaling pathways [2], [15], [16]. Many of these findings are constantly reshaping our understanding and the framework of phytoacoustics. The current evidence is intriguing, and it appeals to more scientific research to answer the following questions in phytoacoustics: (i) How do plants sense SVs in the environment? (ii) How do plants decode or translate SV signals into cellular signaling? (iii) Do SVs have adaptive significance in plants? (iv) Which plants can communicate acoustically? (v) What effects do aboveground SV stimuli have on belowground plant–plant communication and with other organisms? (vi) Do plants use SVs for wireless signal transfers to communicate with each other or with organisms? (vii) Most importantly, how can we translate this increased understanding of plant–SV interactions from the lab to the field? (viii) Is it possible to identify SV-responsive genes that can be used in modern breeding or modified via genome editing to develop acoustic-based future smart crops? To answer these questions there is a need for comprehensive research on phytoacoustics in both model and non-model plants using different high-throughput tools to determine the mechanism of SV perception and transduction. Nevertheless, several excellent research and reviews have been published in phytoacoustics; however, most of them focused on its application, with only a few on perception and transduction, leaving significant knowledge gaps. Here, based on the available data on plant mechanobiology, we provide a multiscale viewpoint on phytoacoustics. We also integrated our perspectives and hypotheses and constructed a model that could be useful for future investigations of SV perception and transduction. We primarily focused on how cell wall sensors, cell membrane channels, various organelles, and other signaling components might be involved in SV perception and transduction and how they might elicit downstream signaling. We also highlight the importance of multi-omics, biophysical, computational, live imaging tools, in addition to epigenetics, and gene editing for underpinning the intricacy of plant–SV signaling. Finally, we conclude how SVs improve plant growth and allow them to acclimate to stressful circumstances.

## Phytoacoustics: How plants respond to acoustic ecosystem
Plant life is still full of unknown secrets. One such secret is how they sense and respond to SVs from various sources. With recent advances in phytoacoustics, we might be able to discover new secrets of plant life, such as how plants communicate with other organisms as receivers or senders, which can result in a synergistic or antagonistic outcome. Notably, Chamovitz [17] proposed: “Music is not ecologically relevant for plants, but there are SVs that they could benefit from hearing.” Phytoacoustic research began in the 1950s, with numerous controversial reports claiming that musical sounds affect plants [18]. Despite being contentious, these claims have drawn increasing scientific interest in phytoacoustics worldwide [2], [5], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]. As sessile organisms, plants are frequently exposed to SVs of various frequencies from various abiotic and biotic sources (Fig. 1). For example, various SVs from bee buzzing, bird chirping, neighboring plants, insects, animals, and humans. In addition, SVs are produced physically through flowing water, blowing winds, and so on. These acoustic signals may have morphological, adaptive, and ecological impacts on plants. In other words, plant-SV interactions are both mutualistic as well as antagonistic in nature. For example, in ‘buzz pollination,’ pollen from anthers is released only in response to a precise frequency of SV (buzz) produced by a particular pollinator. Similarly, plants become defensive when exposed to SVs produced by caterpillar eating [24]. Numerous reports have highlighted the beneficial role of SV stimuli in plants, such as an increase in the rate of seed germination [25], plant growth [26], ripening delay [27], increase in cell energy balance [28], change in endogenous hormone levels, cell cycle [29], and enzyme activities [30]. Several distinct responses were observed in plants following SV treatment. For example, SV priming increases disease resistance against the fungal pathogen Botrytis cinerea [4]. Similarly, SV priming in Arabidopsis has been shown to improve drought resistance [31]. Previous studies have shown that different types of SVs increase the in vitro growth and development of several plants [32]. Further, we elaborated on the detailed role of SV priming in plants in the next section. For a better viewpoint we have summarised the changes that occur in plants after SV treatment in (Fig. 2). These studies strengthen the notion that plants must have evolved sophisticated sensing systems that allow them to emit, detect, analyze, interact, and convert mechanical information from their surroundings into biological responses. Recently, Khait et al. [1] reported that stress plants emit SVs that act as potential wireless signals or stimuli for their environment which has changed our understanding of the acoustic nature of plants that were traditionally considered a contentious theory. This study further opens a new paradigm in plant acoustic biology and builds a new bridge of communication between plants and their environment that can have huge ecological significance. These findings also indicate the existence of molecular systems in plants for SV perception and signal transduction. Therefore, there is a need for the integration of molecular and ecological studies to underpin how plants emit and sense acoustic signals [33]. At the same time, there is a need to understand the potential risks of SVs in plants as well. For instance, different plant species may differ in the frequency, timing, and duration of sound, which may have a substantial effect on their molecular and physiological characteristics of plants. Thus, it is imperative that future studies should also investigate the potential negative effects of SVs in addition to their beneficial roles in both crop and model plant systems. ## Can plants communicate through SVs with other organisms?
Sound communication is well-studied in animals but it has received less attention in other organisms, especially in plants [34]. Plants live in communities of one or more species that interact with each other using the different physical, mechanical, and biochemical modes of communication also called plant behavior. There are numerous studies that have shown that plants use different volatile organic compounds (VOCs) to communicate with plants, pollinators, microbes, animals, and insects which has significant adaptive and ecological implications [34], [35]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that plants may identify close relatives through root communication, suggesting the possibility of kin selection in plants [36]. A previous study has also shown that cell elongations and vertical orientation in plants occur after being touched by neighbouring plants [21]. These studies further support the notion that plants can communicate with each other or other organisms using different modes of communication. One such communication in plant behavior may also involve SVs [1], [37] Recently, there was a major breakthrough in the plant acoustic field where it was found that plants emit SVs after facing external pressures and they might use them as wireless communication with neighboring plants [1]. Interestingly, expanding the range, this study also acquired acoustic recordings from tomato mosaic virus (TMV) infected crops such as corn, wheat, grapevine, and tomato plants. This study opens new perspectives in plant SV communication and plant sensory biology. Another study has reported that plants emit SVs between 10 and 240 Hz (audible acoustic emissions) and 20 and 300 kHz (ultrasonic acoustic emissions) [19]. Previous studies have shown that plants emit SVs during wounding and drought stress, which may positively impact neighboring plants [38]. Gagliano et al. [39] reported that plant roots appear to move toward a water source in response to vibrations if their presence is detected. On the other hand, there is mounting evidence that plant-animal interactions also involve acoustic communication. For example, in bat–plant interactions, plants have evolved morphological structures, such as flowers and leaves, that produce audible echoes to attract echolocating bats that aid in plant pollination, benefiting both organisms [40]. Similarly, plants also respond to animal-origin SVs, for instance, pre-exposure to Pieris rapae chewing sound in Arabidopsis triggers increased anthocyanin and aliphatic glucosinolate accumulation which decreases P. rapae growth by 15–20 %. These studies provide evidence of the existence of acoustic communication within plants and their surrounding biota [24]. Another study found that pre-exposure of tobacco plants to Phtorimaea operculella SVs causes biochemical changes in the plants, such as an increase in nicotine levels, which have insecticidal effects [41]. Nonetheless, it will be fascinating to investigate in the future how plants react to SVs from various animal species and how important they are from an ecological standpoint. As this will offer new perspectives in plant sensory biology and also the importance of SV communication. Our advances in understanding the complex feedback between plant-phytoacoustic communications come from plants in controlled experiments. Further research using various crop species under different stress conditions is required to understand SV communication between plants in open fields, especially in the presence of other modes of communication, such as VOCs, and the impact of overall plant adaptive responses due to their crosstalk. An interesting avenue is to see how plants communicate with belowground and aboveground organisms using SVs (e.g., plants, microbes, animals, insects) and how this communication is different from chemical communication (Fig. 1). Plants and microbes coexist, which can be considered a plant secondary genome. It is unclear whether plants could use SVs to shape their microbiome and distinguish between pathogens and beneficial microbes. Future studies are required to underpin how SVs can affect root or leaf exudates which are the primary drivers of shaping plant microbiomes. The most important aspect of SV communication will be how it triggers plant microbiome functional traits which can have a significant ecological impact on plant growth and resilience. For deeper understanding, future studies are required to study how SVs could modify plant microbiome which can have huge ecological and adaptive roles in plants in terms of growth and stress resilience. There remain many questions on how plants use SV for their mode of wireless communications with others. For example, which organs or tissue acts as SVs perceivers? Secondly, whether plants use similar or different SV frequencies for their wireless communication with other plants or animals. Also, whether plants can act as receivers, emitters or both during these communications warrants future investigation. Will plant-emitted SV signals act as systemic signals or will they be localised in plants need further investigation. Finally, timing, nature of stress, age, cultivar dependent, and other environmental factors warrant further attention for a broader understanding of plant SV communication.

## Perception of SV signals in plants
Plant acoustics have been overlooked in the past because they lack the obvious sound-producing or hearing organs. However, plants are now considered smart organisms because they can memorize past events, communicate wirelessly, and respond to various mechanostimuli, making them somewhat similar to humans and animals. Whether plants have special organs that allow them to receive aboveground and belowground SV signals is unknown. Additionally, how distinct mechanostimuli can be sensed and differentiated by plant organs or cells when they are exposed singly or in combination is unclear. It is not possible to answer these questions at this time owing to limited information, but they may have organs or cells that can detect mechanical vibrations and translate them into biochemical information.” For instance, the trichrome, leaf, stem, flower, and root may operate as aboveground and belowground SV perceiving organs and thus should be investigated to uncover the hidden world of phytoacoustic biology [3], [42].
In the past, few studies have been conducted on trichrome as mechanoreceptors and their role as acoustic antennae or SV receptors. The revelation by Appel and Cocroft [24] that Arabidopsis leaves respond profoundly to the noises made by P. rapae caterpillars feeding on them raises issues about which cells are involved in the receptor system(s), how they work, and how these responses are initiated. Later, Liu et al. [43] provided evidence for the participation of trichomes in caterpillar-transmitted vibrations; however, it is still unknown how they respond to diverse sources of SVs with varying frequencies. Furthermore, trichomes were recently identified as an important mechanoresponsive organ in Arabidopsis by directly sensing the external mechanical forces caused by raindrops to predict waterborne infections [44]. Previous studies have shown that trichomes sense touch responses. Interestingly, brushing or bending trichomes causes Ca2+ oscillations, stiffness, and a decrease in wall pH in skirt cells, all of which are hallmarks of plant mechanotransduction signaling [45]. These findings highlight the importance of trichomes as key mechanoreceptors or switches in plants, which may also be important in perceiving SVs in plants from different sources and frequencies, and thus warrant further investigation. Gene knock-out experiments on the involvement of trichomes as acoustic antennae should be conducted to provide a more concrete basis for their participation in SV signal perception and transduction. Some plant species respond swiftly (within seconds) to external stimuli. For example, the sensitive leguminous plant, M.pudica, quickly folds its leaves in response to mechanical stimulation. Although rapid plant movement and long-distance signaling have been the subject of many studies, the mechanisms driving rapid plant movement and mechanostimuli-triggered signal transduction are still unknown [46]. Hence, the role of trichomes, leaves, and flowers, require further attention, as aboveground SV perceiving organs in plants.
Roots are important participants in belowground SV perception because they can convert vibrational impulses into biochemical signals. Previous studies have revealed that mechanically stimulating Arabidopsis roots generate a brief and local increase in intracellular Ca2+, which decodes mechanical signals into biochemical responses, thereby modulating different traits [47]. In light of the above findings, there is a possibility of the existence of aboveground and belowground SV perceiving organs in plants, however, due to simple ignorance of plants being receptive to SVs, there remain knowledge gaps and await discoveries. It will be interesting to explore whether plants use the same or different organs for perceiving different SVs originating from different sources and frequencies.

## Role of plant cell wall sensors in SV sensing
Owing to the physical characteristics of acoustic signals, it is anticipated that SVs will mechanically affect plant cell walls, plasma membranes, and other intracellular organelles. Additionally, it may have a similar or different mode of signal perception and transduction from other mechanical stimuli like touch, rain, wind, and gravity. Here, we first focused on the cell wall compartment an important podium for sensing mechanical cues, and how it modulates or regulates downstream signaling cascades. Plant cell walls are dynamic external structures that provide shape and protection to cells and sense environmental abrasions. Plant cells must constantly sense and control changes in cell wall mechanics during growth or in response to environmental perturbations. The integrity of the plasma membrane is jeopardized when structural support of the wall is lost, which may result in cell bursting and death [48], [49]. Plant cell walls contain both elastic (reversible) and plastic (irreversible) characteristics (viscoelastoplastic), allowing them to be both flexible and robust enough to expand with growth while remaining intact [50]. Recently, this model has been refined. Xyloglucans, which are hemicelluloses, can produce hybrid molecules between cellulose microfibrils (CMFs) and hemicelluloses, resulting in the development of ‘mechanical hotspots’ where the mechanics and extensibility of the cell wall are controlled [51]. Interestingly, CMFs are typically deposited in highly structured arrangements in plants, and their direction imparts a mechanical bias in the cell wall in a given direction, thereby controlling directional cell expansion [52]. In general, plant cell wall biomechanics (mechano-signals or mechano-strain) is the balance between turgor pressure and cell wall stiffness and has become a promising area of research. Turgor pressure in the cell creates in-plane mechanical tension by pushing the plasma membrane against the cell wall. Although variations in turgor and cell-wall stiffness appear to be coordinated, the mechanism that induces these changes is unknown. A comprehensive and dynamic model of plant cell walls and their remodeling properties during stress is absent. Plants, like yeast, have a specific cell wall integrity (CWI) maintenance mechanism, which monitors cell wall functional integrity and ensures that it stays that way through adaptive responses to cell wall damage (CWD) during growth, maturation, and interactions with the environment.
In plants, both endogenous (cell division, movement, and morphogenesis) and exogenous (mechanostimuli, biotic, and abiotic) signals cause significant changes in the cell wall, which must be quickly sensed and in order to survive. Interestingly, these functional changes and responses to CWD are controlled by the plant CWI maintenance system. Based on these findings, the plant CWI maintenance system appears to be an important component of cell wall plasticity [53]. This process appears to involve receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and ion channels, which constantly monitor the condition of the cell wall and respond to CWD by initiating adaptive changes in cellular and cell wall metabolism [54]. As CWD can occur in several ways, CWD detection requires both ligand-mediated processes and mechanoperception [55]. Although various potential genes have been linked to CWD perception, experimental evidence confirming their involvement remains elusive [54]. Two potential genes, FERONIA (FER) and THESEUS1 (THE1), were identified in Arabidopsis, which are homologs of plasma membrane-localized RLK1-like proteins first described in Catharanthus roseus, and modulate intricate upstream and downstream signaling cascades [49]. In addition, leucine-rich repeat (LRR), wall-associated kinase 1 (WAK1), WAK2, RLK male discoverer 1 (MDIS1)-interacting receptor-like kinase 2 (MIK2), as well as the putatively stretch-activated MS Ca2+ channel, (MID1)–complementing activity 1 (MCA1), and mating pheromone induced death 1 (MID1), have also been linked to CWI maintenance in Arabidopsis [9], [56]. Interestingly, MCA1 plays a crucial role in CWI maintenance in Saccharomyces cerevisiae [9], [57]. A distinguishing trait of RLKs (FER, THE1, WAK1, and WAK2) is the presence of an extracellular domain that may bind cell wall-derived epitopes or ligands [48], [54] and a cytoplasmic domain (kinase) to transmit those signals. For example, RLKs detect damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) created by pathogens and trigger a cascade of intracellular signaling events, including ROS burst, Ca2+ spiking, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades [58]. In Arabidopsis, CWD stimulates ROS production in the apoplast via respiratory burst oxidase homolog D (RBOHD). Receptor-like kinases play various roles in biotic and abiotic stress signaling pathways; however, their role in plant mechanostimulation is limited. RLKs have received wide attention and are assumed to be vital in mechanosignal perception and transduction in plant biology.
So, do plant cell walls sense SV signals? In fact, there are no models to directly answer this question. Because this field is still in its infancy, and yet is not as advanced and recognized as other fields. However, previous studies have shown that SVs trigger transcriptional changes in many genes, such as those encoding cell-wall-modifying enzymes such as xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase, expansins, and exordium, which are most likely to be involved in the CWD/CWI signaling mechanism during SV treatment [2]. At present, how SVs mechanical forces are translated into biochemical cues in different cellular compartments, are yet to be identified. We have illustrated how SVs could be possibly sensed by cell wall sensors and trigger apoptotic signaling in plants (Fig. 3). In this model, we speculated that SVs may cause turgor pressure which puts the cell wall under tension that in turn leads to cell wall modifications. After SV treatment, plant cell walls may undergo constant synthesis and remodeling, which is aided by multifaceted proteins such as hydroxyproline-rich cell wall glycoproteins (HRGPs), hydrolytic enzymes (xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase, pectin methylesterases, expansins, polygalacturonases, and peroxidases), and rapid alkalization factors (RALFs) in order to maintain CWI and trigger downstream signaling cascades (Fig. 3). We assume that during SV-induced cell wall reprogramming, some oligosaccharides (OGs) are released and can be sensed by different cell wall receptor like kinase such as WAKs, FER, THE1, LRR embedded in the membrane, which triggers an array of signaling cascades such as cytosolic calcium burst, ROS wave formation, pH modification (apoplastic alkalization) and hormonal signaling activation via phosphorylation, all of which leads the activation of a large number of genes responsible for different plant traits (Fig. 3). Secondly, after SV-mediated cell wall reprogramming, various peptides such as RALFs may be released and activated which can be sensed by cell wall receptor like kinases such as FER, or THE1, which in turn triggers downstream signaling by phosphorylating calcium channels or RBOHs. Generally, RALFs are cell wall-localized peptides that can be trapped by non-covalent bonds with cell wall components [59]. SVs or other mechanostimuli might reduce the affinity of these cell wall components to peptides, leading to peptide release and subsequent perception by membrane receptors such as FER and THE1. A previous study has reported that FER maintains CWI during touch treatment by participating in several pathways, including calcium signaling and the expression of touch-responsive genes. Additionally, fer knock-out mutants completely block the calcium burst and lower the expression of touch marker genes in touch-treated Arabidopsis, opening up new paths to further investigate FER in plant–SV interactions [60]. A recent study on plant-touch interactions highlighted the role of FER as an important regulator of touch-responsive genes in Arabidopsis which further highlights its key role in plant mechanostimulation [61]. FER’s closest homologs, ANXUR1, and ANXUR2, could be also important players in maintaining CWI during SV plant treatment. On the other hand, THE1 is another important cell wall sensor activated only when cell wall properties are compromised, such as reduced cellulose biosynthesis. Additionally, THE1 can modify MS channels such as MCA1 to promote lignification and hormone accumulation [55], which makes it a promising player in SV perception and transduction. Similarly, WAKs are also important cell wall sensors that bind pectin or its derivatives, thereby playing a role in cell wall sensing [56]. More than 600 RLKs have been encoded in the Arabidopsis genome [62]. Despite this, only one RLK (FER, which belongs to the C. roseus RLK1-like subfamily) is involved in plant mechanical signal transduction. Hence, future research should identify cell wall components that can bind to FER, ANXUR1, ANXUR2, THE1, WAK1, and WAK2 during SV treatment. In plants, SVs may also stimulate ROS production in the apoplast through RBOH activation, which may be regulated by RLKs, such as FER or THE1, through Ca2+-dependent and Ca2+-independent mechanisms. Biophysical and molecular studies are required to prove the above hypothesis and determine how SV-induced cell wall tension or modifications may affect the distribution of other signaling molecules, such as OGs and peptides, and trigger downstream signaling cascades. The mechanism by which the nucleus and other organelles detect SV-triggered cell wall mechanotransduction is a hot topic in phytoacoustic biology. Currently, there is no data on cell wall perception of SVs and transduction mechanisms in plants. The precise mechanism by which RLKs regulate mechanical signal transduction remains unclear. ## Beyond cell wall checkpoints, the systemic dynamics of SV signaling in plants
The next layer of plant-SV signaling within the cell is the plasma membrane and cytosol, which consists of different mechano-sensors, signaling molecules, and sites for numerous molecular and biochemical processes. The plasma membrane is a crucial podium for plant cells to perceive mechanical input [63]. Monitoring the lateral tension in the plasma membrane allows a plant cell to detect an array of intrinsic cell wall loosening [64], cell expansion [65], and extrinsic factors, such as osmotic cell swelling [66] and mechanical cues like vibrations [2], [11]. The plasma membrane primarily perceives and signals these tensions through the action of specialized mechanosensitive (MS) ion channels which function as gated conduits for ion passage (Ca2+, K+, and Cl-) in and out of cells or between cellular compartments. MS or stretch-activated ion channels are found in all domains of life and are expressed in various cell types and developmental stages in plant cells, where they are found in plasma and organellar membranes [67]. Readers are referred to additional in-depth reviews of MS channel structures, functions, and regulations across species [75], [134]. Calcium waves are the hallmarks of plant mechanostimultion-triggered signal transduction pathways which are gated through different MS channels. Likewise, there are many reports on SV-triggered Ca2+ bursts in plants. For example, in roots application of a stretch-activated Ca2+ channel blocker, Gd3+ diminishes the SV-induced cytosolic Ca2+ increase which further highlights the role of MS channels in plant acoustic signaling [3]. Gene expression studies have shown that SV modulated the expression of various MS channels which further provides evidence of their involvement in SV signal perception. To date, no SV MS-specific channel has been identified therefore, there is a need to use different chemical inhibitors and other genetic tools to screen and identify the bonafide MS channels as logical SV sensors. However, numerous studies have reported the role of a few MS channels as mechanosensors in response to touch, oscillations, and abiotic stress-induced mechanical stress which makes them logical sensors for SV perception. For example, MCA1 enhances lignification when the cell wall is pharmacologically softened with cellulose synthase inhibitors [55], [135], which could contribute to cell wall strengthening. Similarly, MCA1 and MSL10 promoted the expression of touch-induced (TCH) genes, indicating their role in mechanostimulation. Recently, Arabidopsis PIEZO1, an ortholog of mammalian PIEZO, has been shown to sense mechanical stress in the roots [10]. These findings also highlight the conserved functions of PIEZOs from plants to mammals. Recently, MSL10 was identified as a molecular component for the perception of oscillations triggered by the wind in Arabidopsis hence could be an important sensor for SV [136]. Similarly, ion channel families FLYC and OSCA have been discovered to function as potential mechanosensors in rapid touch sensation in carnivorous plants [137]. Therefore, we speculated that these channels (MCA1/MSL10, PIEZO1, OSCA, and FLYC) could be involved in SV perception in plants. However, many more channels that may be significant in plant SV perception are likely to be discovered. For example, other channel members of MCA, MSL, OSCAs, PIEZOs, TPKs, GLR, and CNGC, need further attention during plant–SV interactions to identify potential SV perception channels (Fig. 4). It is well documented that MS channels interact with NADPH oxidase, RLKs, H+-ATPase, hormone pathways, and other signaling cascades and form a signaling hub that determines the outcome of mechano signal transduction in plants. Therefore, it will be interesting to further investigate how MS channels are activated during SV treatment and how they interact with other upstream and downstream key signaling pathways which will provide novel insights into decoding the molecular basis of SV signal transduction (Fig. 4). Together with a variety of ion channels, H+-ATPase is also regarded as an important component of the SV signaling pathway, in terms of its ability to regulate various cellular and molecular events during plant growth and stress conditions [138]. Furthermore, activated H+-ATPase is thought to trigger cell wall rearrangement by decreasing the pH of the apoplast. It will be interesting to determine how SV controls H+-ATPase activity and how it affects SV signal transduction. Here, we have shown a model highlighting how plants may detect SV signals through either directly activating MS channels or by RLK-mediated signaling, which could trigger hallmarks of physiological responses, such as Ca2+ spiking, ROS production, extracellular ATP release, hormonal crosstalk, and other key signaling cascades (Fig. 4). ## SV sensing by intracellular organelles and interconnecting signaling cascades in plants
Additional SV mechanosensory components beyond the plant cell wall and plasma membrane podium await discovery. Generally, the cell wall or plasma membrane is assumed to be the site of initial SV sensing. These acoustic signals will be translated into biochemical signals and further transferred to other subcellular sites, including the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, vacuoles, plastids, and nuclei, which can modulate an array of interconnected signaling cascades or ion channel trafficking (Fig. 4). They could also play a critical role in plant-SV signal transduction by sorting and guiding key proteins and signals for different cellular destinations. Therefore, sensors present in organelles should be identified in the future to gain new insights into SV organelle signal transduction. For instance, vacuolar channels, such as TPK1 (tonoplast-localized), PIEZO (tonoplast-localized), plastid-based sensors (MSL or their homologs), mitochondrial MSL channels and their regulation could be important players in SV intracellular signal transduction (Fig. 4). Similarly, ER and its signatures could also play a key role in plant SV mechano-stimulation because they receive and decode communication from the plant cell wall and plasma membrane via actin and microtubules. For example, synaptotagmin 1 (SYT1), an ER membrane protein abundant at ER–plasma membrane contact sites, aids in the maintenance of membrane integrity in response to salt stress and mechanical strain [139]. Hence, a similar type of SV-responsive proteins needs to be identified which can translate the mechanical signals from the cell wall or plasma membrane to ER during plant SV interactions. Mitochondria and possibly peroxisomes, such as chloroplasts, can provide retrograde signals that are critical for stress responses in plants. Thus, communication between organelles and the nucleus, so-called retrograde signaling, during plant–SV stimulation could be an essential aspect of studying how organelles release various signals that orchestrate substantial changes in nuclear gene expression during SV stimulation. Secondly, how this information is translated to the nucleus from the cell exterior, such as through transporters, carrier proteins, transcription factors, and proteins, needs to be further identified. Also how the nucleus undergoes SV-mediated cell wall, plasma membrane, and other cellular modifications, as well as how it translates or undergoes transcriptional, translational, or epigenetic changes, is needed to answer the SV signal transduction conundrum. Furthermore, we have illustrated how these organelles are crucial for phytoacoustic signal transduction (Fig. 4).

## Role of different signaling molecules in plant SV signal transduction
Significant research has been conducted to understand the roles of different signaling signatures, such as Ca2+, ROS, hormones, nitric oxide (NO), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), as essential modulators or regulators of plant developmental and stress responses. However, their roles in plant mechano-signal transduction are at a nascent stage. There are few reports which highlight the role of these universal signaling messengers during plant SV interaction. For instance, Ca2+ transients have been observed in cells treated with SVs, which differ depending on the stimuli [140]. SV treatment causes a considerable redistribution of Ca2+ in Chrysanthemum callus cells [141]. Similarly, following SV treatment, vacuolar Ca2+ began to accumulate in the vacuolar membrane, accompanied by an increase in the cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration. Ca2+ concentrations also increased in the nucleus, golgi complexes, and chloroplasts. Pharmacological experiments utilizing Ca2+ blockers confirmed the function of Ca2+ in SV-regulated growth response in Chrysanthemum callus formation, showing that the Ca2+-deficient medium delayed the SV-mediated acceleration of Chrysanthemum callus production [142]. This suggests that SVs trigger Ca2+ signatures by transporting cell wall-associated Ca2+ to the cytoplasm via Ca2+ channel activity, resulting in the elicitation of a signaling cascade. Gene expression studies have reported that SV treatment induces the expression of various calcium sensors like calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs), calmodulins (CaMs)/calmodulin-like proteins (CMLs), calcineurin B-like protein (CBL)–CBL interacting protein kinase (CIPK) modules; Ca2+/CaM-dependent protein kinases (CCaMKs)] [2]. However, despite the availability of high-throughput techniques, locating proper Ca2+ sensors, channels, and their associated downstream signaling cascades in plants following SV treatment remains a challenge. Also, the detailed roles of individual Ca2+ exporters and importers remain largely unknown. Therefore, deciphering the role of SV-triggered Ca2+ sensors warrants future investigation to fill the missing gaps of SV signal transduction.
ROS has long been recognized as a universal secondary signaling messenger that plays vital roles in many cellular processes, including stomatal opening, development, programmed cell death (PCD), gravitropism, salt stress, drought, and hormonal sensing [143], [144]. ROS can also regulate Ca2+ movement from the apoplast and vacuole via plasma membrane Ca2+ channels and two-pore channel 1 (TPC1), respectively [145]. Furthermore, ROS and Ca2+ have a reciprocal interaction, a phenomenon known as Ca2+-induced ROS production (CIRP) and ROS-induced Ca2+ release (RICR). Plants produce reactive ROS by two different mechanisms: apoplastic ROS, which is produced by cell wall peroxidases, and amine oxidases, or NADPH oxidases (RBOHs), which are found in the plasma membrane. Intracellular ROS, which is mostly produced by chloroplasts and peroxisomes/glyoxysomes, initiates another ROS signaling in plants [146]. Like other stressors, SV also triggers ROS production and enhances the activity of ROS scavenging enzymes in plants which further provides evidence that ROS is an important player of SV signal transduction [147]. However, it is still unknown how SV activates ROS-generating players such as RBOHs warrants future investigation. In general, the activity of RBOHs in plants under stress is regulated by both cell wall sensors like HPCA1 (H2O2-induced Ca2+ increases 1) and calcium channels. Therefore, we assume that SV may activate RBOHs through cell wall modification and mechano-calcium channel activation-dependent manner that leads to ROS formation in plants. However, gene knockout studies are required to identify the role of different RBOHs (RBOHA–RBOHJ) and MS channels like MCA, MSL, PIEZO, and OSCA in SV-triggered ROS formation. In addition, the role of cell wall integrity sensors like FER, THE1, WAKs, LRR, and other CRLKs need to be further studied in relation to ROS formation during SV treatment. The role of ROS sensors and transporters are also important themes for future SV research in plants. It's interesting to note that, similar to Ca2+ and electrical signals, ROS can produce waves that serve as systemic signals in plants [148]. Consequently, it will be fascinating to investigate the function of ROS wave in SV-triggered systemic signaling in plants, since this will offer fresh perspectives on SV signal transduction. It is currently unclear how two chemical messengers (ROS and Ca2+) interact with each other or with distinct signaling pathways during plant–SV interactions that lead to a range of functions in plants, such as activation of Ca2+ ion channels, K+ efflux, hormonal sensing, and intracellular signaling cascades (activation of MAPK) (Fig. 4). In addition, it is unclear how their crosstalk can affect nuclear protein translocation or activity, resulting in the control of specific gene expression. Furthermore, a variety of factors that impact the biological outcomes of Ca2+ and ROS need to be considered when deciphering their involvement in plant-SV interactions. These aspects include the site of synthesis, signal amplitude and duration, and interaction with other signaling molecules. Also, how SV-triggered ROS can activate the MAPK signal cascade a molecular switch of the intracellular signal transduction pathways needs further attention.
Apart from ROS, reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and H2S are essential gaseous signaling molecules in plants that regulate several developmental and adaptative traits. In plants, NO and H2S regulate an array of developmental and physiological processes such as seed germination, root development, cell differentiation, stomatal movement, transition to flowering, and senescence in addition to adaptive responses. In plants, NO and H2S trigger post-translational modifications like S-nitrosylation and tyrosine nitration leading to conformational modifications of target proteins [149], [150]. Furthermore, Ca2+ and ROS signaling are frequently intertwined with NO, a reactive radical that functions as a signaling molecule in animal and plant cells [151]. For example, NO can influence the activity of Ca2+ channels directly or indirectly or can quickly react with superoxide radicals (O2•–) to generate peroxynitrite (ONOO–), a powerful oxidant capable of oxidizing a wide range of biological macromolecules directly or indirectly [152]. The spatiotemporal synthesis of NO, as well as its interaction with other macromolecules during plant–SV interactions, is an essential determinant of its specificity as a signaling molecule, just as they are for ROS and Ca2+. How SV triggers NO and H2S production that can regulate a myriad of biochemical and physiological processes in plants warrants future investigation. Also, decoding Ca2+, ROS, NO, and H2S signaling cascades during plant-SV signaling will provide a mechanistic understanding of SV signal transduction. Further, we provide a brief overview of the potential roles of these signatures in plant–SV signal transduction, emphasizing how they mediate communication between different organelles and compartments, translate SV mechanical signals into biochemical information, and highlight knowledge gaps (Fig. 4). Recently Tyagi et al [153] have reported that SV triggers the expression of plant neurotransmitters (NTs) thus opens a new paradigm of SV signaling transduction. Interestingly, plant NTs have emerged as potential signaling molecules in a variety of plant systems where they play an important role in plant developmental biology and stress adaptability. Therefore, it will be interesting to unravel the role of NTs in SV signal perception and transduction.
Phytohormones play an important role in plant growth and development and fine-tune multiple responses to environmental cues. Likewise, the hormone status of plants affects mechano signal transduction. In Arabidopsis, for example, mechano-stimulation, such as thigmomorphogenesis, is caused by a decrease in bioactive gibberellin (GA) levels and activation of the GA-inactivating enzyme gibberellin 2-oxidase 7 (GA2OX7) [154]. Similarly, JA biosynthesis or signaling genes [allene oxide synthase (AOS), jasmonate resistance (JAR), and coronatine insensitive 1 (COI1)] are important in touch-mediated growth changes in Arabidopsis [155]. Recently, the JA-activated transcription factor MYC2/MYC3/MYC4 was found to be vital for touch mechanostimulation in Arabidopsis [8]. Like other mechano stimuli, SV also triggers the activation of different hormonal pathways in plants associated with both growth and adaptive responses. For instance, several studies have demonstrated that SV improves growth features in plants by activating GA, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and cytokinin (zeatin riboside, ZR) hormonal cascades all of which are important for controlling growth responses [16]. In Chrysanthemum callus, AUX- and ABA-level were increased and decreased, respectively, under SV treatment [29]. Besides, production of AUX, JA, SA, and GA were also observed under SV treatment in Arabidopsis [2]. SV treatment changes the concentration of ZA (increased) and AUX (decreased), resulting in longer root growth compared to the non SV treated Arabidopsis plants [156]. Previous studies have also reported that SV-triggered auxin production also stimulates the activity of H+-ATPase in the plasma membrane which further highlights its key role in SV cellular signaling [29], [157], [158]. In response to other stimuli like light, temperature, and gravity, auxins regulate many essential elements of plant growth and development, including petiole and stem elongation and root architecture [159]. Therefore, in addition to its role in plant growth and development, auxin signatures may be crucial in the investigation of root-to-root acoustic interactions in plants. Ethylene (ET) signaling regulates the SV-dependent rate of Arabidopsis seed germination. Also, SVs delay the ripening of tomato fruits by blocking ET production and signaling genes [27]. It is interesting to note that SVs dynamically regulate defense hormones like SA and JA and provide disease or pest resistance in plants, which further supports the notion that SVs play an important role in activating plant immunity against diverse pathogens [4], [160]. It will be interesting to further study the role of SA, JA, and ET biosynthesis genes, as well as their receptors such as non-expressers of pr 1 (NPR1), jasmonate-zim-domain protein (JAZ), constitutive triple response (CTR), and ethylene insensitive 2 (EIN2) in response to SVs, which might appear as crucial targets for understanding the process of SV-triggered hormonal regulation and their crosstalk during plant SV interaction. Previous studies have shown that ABA increases cellular ROS levels in Arabidopsis, which activates ABA-regulated hyperpolarization-activated Ca2+ channels in the plasma membrane, thereby influencing stomata closure [161]. It has been claimed that SVs enhance plant drought resistance, although the exact mechanism is unknown. We speculate that during drought stress, SVs may cause ABA-dependent/independent ROS/Ca2+ production, or vice versa, which may subsequently activate calcium channels and cause stomatal closure. However, further studies are required to identify the key genes implicated in SV-induced drought resilience. Overall, the above studies highlight that SVs significantly change the hormonal dynamics in plants however, future studies are required to identify positive and negative regulators of SV-mediated hormonal regulation in plants. This will pave the way for understanding their role in SV signal transduction and SV driven growth and adaptive responses in plants. In particular, how SV-induced Ca2+ and ROS regulate hormonal signatures warrants future investigation.
Several questions on SV signal transduction in plants remain unanswered: 1) How do SVs trigger intracellular and extracellular Ca2+ bursts, and what SV-specific Ca2+ channels and sensors further decode SV information into a biochemical one? 2) How and where are ROS produced after SV treatment, and how is it regulated by cell wall or plasma membrane-based receptors such as CrRLKs, FER, and THE1? 3) The interaction between ROS, Ca2+, and other molecular signatures, such as NO, H2S, SA, JA, AUX, ET, ABA, and NTs during plant SV interaction warrants further investigation 4) How do SVs regulate or activate hormonal signaling pathways to control cellular machinery responses to environmental and developmental events? Although great discoveries of ROS, Ca2+, and hormone signal transduction in plants in response to biotic and abiotic stresses have been made, their role in phytoacoustics remains largely unknown. ## Decoding the complexity of acoustic signaling in plants using high throughput molecular and live cell imaging tools
Timeline breakthroughs in phytoacoustics have supported the notion that plants can emit and respond to SVs just like other species. However, there is limited information on how they emit or detect SVs at the molecular level. For instance, the role of MS calcium channels, RBOHs, receptors, hormonal regulators, and gene networks is still largely unknown. Furthermore, following SV treatment, plants may generate a large number of structurally and functionally diverse signaling molecules, much as they do in response to other stresses. However, most of them are yet to be discovered. In this regard integration of genetic (omics), biophysical, and computational tools are required for deciphering the molecular mechanism of acoustic signaling and also identifying molecular players that are evolved. Interestingly, multi-omics has greatly expedited the discovery of genes, proteins, and metabolites involved in different plant signaling cascades which has paved the way for decoding the molecular complexity of plant responses to environmental stressors. Additionally, the integration of functional genomics with other omics provides the relationships between plant genotypes and phenotypes under different physiological and environmental conditions. Over the last 10 years, many studies have reported how SVs cause transcriptional, proteomic, and metabolomic reprogramming in various plants [2], [162]. For example, based on microarray data SVs induced a number of genes related to mechanostimulus responsive genes (TCH4, TCH2, DREB, CML38, MYB77, WRKY, ANAC) in Arabidopsis. Recently, the transcriptomic analysis showed that SVs induce an array of genes in duckweed related to Ca2+, glutamate, and ROS signaling as well as metabolic processes such as cell wall biosynthesis, glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and pentose phosphate pathways which further provide evidence that plants respond to SVs by undergoing molecular and metabolic reprogramming that may influence their growth and adaptive responses [15]. Transcriptome profiling in Arabidopsis plants after SV treatment induces a number of genes related to phenylpropanoid catabolism (LAC11, PRR1, and CTL2) and cell wall biogenesis IRX6 and NAC007 respectively. Although many SV-induced genes or transcriptional factors have been identified in Arabidopsis they lack functional validation. Also, the core signaling SV receptors that regulate SV-induced genes remain largely unknown. In this regard, gene knock-out studies are required to determine the role of SV regulatory genes or transcription factors in SV perception and transduction. Owing to its ease of use and accuracy, the CRISPR/Cas9 system is a flexible genome editing technique that can be used to target different genes involved in SV perception and transduction in plants with the aim of identification of major signaling players and their regulatory pathways. Altogether these studies call for a deeper understanding of the links between cell wall, cell membrane, and intracellular signaling events for finding the hidden molecular players in SV signal perception and transduction. Recent studies have found that SV treatment causes metabolic changes such as increased accumulation of key enzymes and secondary metabolites, such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and catalase, and biosynthesis of alkaloids, terpenes, phenols, and oxylipin-derivative VOCs [2], [160], [163], which may improve plant resistance or act as signals in plant–plant communication. Generally, plants produce diverse secondary metabolites in response to environmental stress. Hence it is important to use in-depth metabolic profiling tools LC/GC–MS, GC/EI-TOF-MS, HPLC, and NMR in plant acoustics to identify core metabolites that are responsive to SV signaling in plants. Future Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) can be used to learn more about the role of key metabolites in SV perception, transduction, and plant-plant communication. Also, how SV changes the total ionome in plants is another important aspect of plant acoustics. Ions play an important role in plant signaling perception and transduction and are considered important indicators of plant physiological and adaptative responses. There have been no reports on ionomic profiling after SV treatment in plants. In this regard, the use of high throughput elemental profiling tools like inductively coupled plasma-mass/optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-MS/OES), neutron activation analysis (NAA), and X-ray crystallography can be used to examine the effect of SV on plant ionome [164], [165]. Studying the impact of SVs on plant phenotype also requires precise phenotyping tools. There has been a significant advancement in plant phenotyping through automated high-throughput imaging technologies [166]. Therefore, phenomics applications could also play an important role in assessing crop phenotypic parameters under SV treatment.
With the advent of whole plant imaging platform, the role of Ca2+ and ROS as a subcellular, cellular, and systemic signal have been well documented, especially during biotic and abiotic stressors but their role in SV signaling transduction is yet to be discovered. In the past, it was difficult to track the Ca2+ in a living cell by using conventual chemical methods like murexide [167]. However, the discovery of photoproteins, such as Aequorin and green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a special jellyfish, Aequorea victoria, has greatly contributed to the detection of Ca2+ at intracellular locations. The photoprotein used as luminescent techniques, such as aequorin, halistaurin, phialidin, obelin, and others, are widely used for measuring the cytosolic Ca2+ in cells [168], [169]. A significant factor in the discovery of single molecules within cells was the advancement of microscopy. Using a standard microscope, for instance, it was not possible to observe the real-time assessment of molecular changes in cellular levels and precise localization [170]. In this regard, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) have been utilized for tracking the movement of Ca2+ molecule in the cellular levels, at different organelles, by using a probe, Cameleon [170], [171], [172]. Moreover, Ca2+(GCaMP6s, GEM-GECO) [173], [174]-, ROS (FROG/B, HyPer7-GFP) [175], [176]-, and tissue-specific pH (pHusion) [43]-sensor protein have also been used for observing its specific molecule. Despite their success, these photoproteins have limitations as they gradually lose their function by photo-bleaching, slow analysis, and limited cell layer [173], [177]. To overcome these problems, selective plane illumination microscopy (SPIM) which serves higher quality imaging than CLSM has been developed for observing nuclear- and cytosolic-targeted Yellow Cameleon (YC3.6) in transgenic plants [178]. However, it takes a lot of time and effort to introduce a transgenic reporter gene into a plant and generate specialized plants. Recently, Fichman and Mittler [179], used ROS (H2DCFDA), Ca2+ (Fluo-4-AM), and membrane potential (DiBAC4(3) detection probes for monitoring live cell imaging at the whole plant level under high light- and wounding-stress which generated incredible findings on calcium and ROS wave formation. Recently, Brillouin spectroscopy, which observes and access the viscoelastic properties of condensed matter and living cell, is also considered an emerging tool to probe biomechanical properties of plant cells and tissues in 3D, label-free, and contact-free [180]. Its combination with the confocal microscopy technique will become a powerful tool for plant biomechanics, including phyto-acoustic fields [181]. Therefore, there is a need to apply the above live cell imaging tools in plant acoustics in order to decipher the role of ROS and calcium signatures in SV signal transduction. Studies on Ca2+ and ROS influx and efflux mechanisms during plant-SV face some experimental limitations, thus making our understanding of the exact functional mechanisms difficult. In this regard, designing the SV-proof chamber with live imaging tools and other phenotypic setups can address the above limitations. We also recommend developing fluorescent biosensors that can quantitatively detect physical parameters such as membrane tension or turgor pressure, cell wall remodeling events, and hormones in real-time during SV treatment that could be game changers in phytoacoustic biology. Also, in silico-based modeling, along with biophysical examinations of different channel proteins, could identify special structures that could aid in stretching the channels during SV treatments. Epigenetic regulation of animal and plant genomes has received considerable attention. Previous studies have revealed that the chromatin-modifying gene, set domain group 8 (SDG8), is known to modulate the expression of various genes involved in mechanostimulation, such as touch, in Arabidopsis [182], [183]. The rapid activation of numerous pathways in response to mechanical stimulation may be ensured by SDG8-mediated enrichment of H3K4 trimethylation surrounding many TCH gene loci. Previous studies have identified RNA polymerase II-associated factor 1 (PAF1) as the primary element for root and stem thigmomorphogenesis after repeated mechanical disturbances [182], [183]. These studies have highlighted the importance of epigenetic changes during plant mechanostimulation. We anticipate that epigenetic regulation is at the heart of a wide range of mechanical activities in plants during SV treatment. Based on previous reports, SVs are known to shape plant immunity or resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses and also increase an array of growth traits in plants, implying that SVs may be engaged in epigenetic modification. How SVs promote DNA methylation, histone conformational changes, modification of transcription changes, transcriptional repression of SV-responsive genes, and other relevant genes in plants remains largely unknown. A molecular-level study is challenging owing to the paucity of molecular data. Therefore, molecular-level studies are required to unravel how SV-induced epigenetic signals may benefit plants in terms of stress resilience and growth benefits in sustainable agriculture. We are starting to discover the putative functions of DNA methylation and histone modification during plant mechanostimulation. In this regard, high-throughput bisulfite or methylation-sensitive amplification polymorphism (MSAP) sequencing in plants following SV treatment will aid in detecting differentially methylated regions at the organ or single-cell level. Furthermore, characterizing the genome-wide landscape of different epigenetic marks in response to SV would help researchers better understand the acoustic regulation of plant genetic signaling mechanisms and their connections to other stress signaling pathways. Identifying epigenetic signatures, such as transcription factors, long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), small RNA, differentially expressed genes, DNA methylation, histone modification, and RNAi processes during plant–SV interaction will provide novel insights into plant-SV perception and transduction. Identification of SV-dependent epigenetic markers may play a key role in answering some of the remaining questions in phytoacoustics.
Altogether, future studies are required for deeper understanding of the links between the cell wall, cell membrane, and intracellular signaling events for understanding SV signal perception and transduction. Furthermore, we have emphasized the importance of various high-throughput tools (multi-omics, biophysical, computational, bioimaging, and microsensors) and the use of inhibitors (agonists and antagonists) to identify sensors, channels, and intracellular events of SV perception and transduction. ## SV priming, a new tool for improving growth and stress tolerance in sustainable agriculture
Various approaches have been used to improve multiple stress tolerance. Few of them are time-consuming (e.g., conventional breeding), whereas others are currently unacceptable in many nations worldwide (e.g., plant genetic modification). As an alternative, crop plants can be primed to handle future biotic and abiotic stressors efficiently [184]. Recent research suggests that plants can be primed by various methods, such as bio-, chemical-, nanotechnology-based, and mechano-priming, to tolerate different stresses effectively. Although these tools are widely used, some of them have limitations. For example, plant ecosystems and microbiota are profoundly altered by the uncontrolled deposition of chemical compounds during chemical priming to boost plant protection, which has an impact on soil fertility and crop production. Beneficial microbes can be used instead of chemicals to prime plants, which can solve some of the concerns with chemical priming, and their high-efficiency formulations are dependent on several conditions [185]. In this context, mechano-priming (touch or SVs) is an eco-friendly approach to boosting stress resilience in sustainable agriculture. Several studies conducted over the last decade have shown that regular mechanical stimulation can significantly improve plant stress resilience and fitness through mechanical priming. For example, gentle rubbing has been found to increase immunity against B. cinerea in Arabidopsis [155]. Similarly, intermittent touching of Arabidopsis leaves activates the JA pathway, which in turn leads to systemic resistance to aphids, as well as B. cinerea [186]. Previous studies have shown that wind-treated bean plants were more resistant to Colletotrichum lindemuthianum than control plants and had higher lignin content and higher activity of cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase and peroxidase enzymes than control plants [187]. Interestingly, touching maize leaves produces volatile signals that activate chemical defense reactions in neighboring plants [188]. This study further revealed the identification of chemicals like sesquiterpenes (E)-nerolidol and (E)-β-caryophyllene that best distinguished the volatile blend of touched plants from that of untouched plants. Previous studies have reported that these chemicals can dramatically lower aphid activity and settlement on plants [188], [189]. Similarly, SVs also enhance plant resilience and improve growth traits through SV priming [4], [16]. For example, in Arabidopsis, exposure to 1000 Hertz (Hz) airborne vibrations at 80 decibels (dB) boosts the SA-mediated defense response against B. cinerea and slows disease progression [4]. This study showed that SV increases SA levels and its signature genes like Pathogenesis related genes while there was no effect on JA. This study further provides the evidence that SVs may activate systemic acquired resistance in Arabidopsis thereby can be used for defence priming against pathogens. Pre-treatment of Arabidopsis plants with SVs generated by caterpillar feeding triggers chemical defense response by increasing the glucosinolate and anthocyanin levels when compared to untreated plants [24]. On the other hand, SV treatment also increases stress tolerance to abiotic stressors. For example, compared to plants that had not been subjected to SV treatment, SV-treated Arabidopsis had a greater survival rate under drought stress and considerable upregulation of abiotic stress-responsive genes [31]. Likewise, SV improves drought tolerance in rice plants by increasing their relative water content, stomatal conductance and reduced ROS content when compared to non-SV treated plants [190]. Further, SV-treated Arabidopsis plants have a higher survival rate during drought stress and significant upregulation of stress tolerance [31]. These studies further support the notion that SV fine tunes plant defence response against different biotic and abiotic stressors. SV priming has been reported to improve root growth in paddy rice and Actinidia chinensis by increasing their hormonal contents [191], [192]. Previous studies have shown that different types of SVs increase the in vitro growth and development of several plants. For example, in Chrysanthemum SV (1400 Hz) exhibited a decrease in ABA and an accumulation of IAA that increases callus growth [29]. In plants, SV stimulation enhanced plant plasma-membrane H+, amylase activity, antioxidant enzyme activity, soluble sugar, and protein content all of which lead to increased plant growth [5], [16]. SV priming also increases the seed germination rate in different crops [16]. In addition, SVs are known to increase Agrobacterium-mediated transformation efficiency in many crops, including Triticum aestivum, Glycine max, Zea mays, and Vigna unguiculata [32]. ## Balancing trade-offs between SVs and other stimuli in plants
Plants may be exposed to various mechanostimuli such as sound, touch, wind, wounding, and rain, as well as biotic and abiotic stresses, either individually or in combination, and must respond appropriately or prioritize one over the other to maintain growth defense trade-offs. Plants are thought to engage in growth–defense trade-offs due to resource constraints, which necessitate prioritization of either growth or defense, depending on external and internal factors. The nature and extent to which plants use inducible mechanisms to cope with a combination of physical, mechanical, and biological stresses are unknown [218]. It will be interesting to study how SVs combined with the above stimuli and unveil a genetically controlled mechanism by which plants balance trade-offs prioritized among them. Previous research has revealed that SV improves or promotes plant growth features [16]. In contrast, the touch response causes stunting and a rise in secondary growth, demonstrating how both mechanisms regulate development differently. However, at the transcriptional level, both SVs and touch treatments showed upregulation of some of the same genes [TCH2, TCH3, TCH4 CML38, MYB77, dehydration response element-binding protein 26 (DREB26), HS1 PRO-1 2 (HSPRO2), and related to ABI3/VP1 (RAV1)] in Arabidopsis, implying the possibility of crosstalk between them [2], [219]. A plausible explanation for the similarity in gene induction between SV and touch mechanostimuli in Arabidopsis is that they could be detected by the same apoplast cell wall sensors or by MS channels that trigger calcium-dependent gene induction through cell wall modification. Interestingly, in nature, plants can very often experience both touch and SV simultaneously from different sources such as rain, wind, animals, and insects which can have a significant impact on plant growth and development. Therefore, plants must have evolved a sophisticated mechanism to respond to the dual mechanical cues (touch and SV) in order to finetune their growth and adaptive responses. However, future studies are required to underpin the molecular mechanism of how SV and touch are sensed by plants and how they trigger transcriptional and translation reprogramming related to growth and adaptive responses. Also, finding common master regulators of touch and SV signaling can provide novel insights into genetically tailored plants that optimize trade-offs to maximize crop yields under fluctuating environmental conditions. The behavior of SVs in the presence of other mechanostimuli, such as water spray, rain, and wounding, should be further studied. For example, during SV treatment, if any other mechanostimuli, such as rain, occur concurrently, the outcome and the effectiveness of the SV-modulated response will need to be explored further. To date, most research on SVs has been conducted on individual stresses in the lab, which have improved growth traits. However, in the future, it will be necessary to design experiments on plant–SV interactions that more accurately reflect natural environmental fluctuations, exposure to multiple stresses, and field studies to identify network interactions and test putative molecular mechanisms. It is crucial to understand how SV signaling regulates plant stress tolerance to biotic and abiotic stressors, individually and when combined, and how they balance the growth and adaptation trade-offs. SVs enhance stress resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses and improve growth traits, implying that SVs prioritize or balance the trade-offs between growth and adaptation. In this review, we have illustrated the possibilities of SV-mediated growth-defense trade-offs during multiple stressors with several specific knowledge gaps that require additional research (Fig. 7). Future research should also focus on where and how molecular regulators of growth and stress resilience interact following SV treatment because this will provide genetically tailored plants that optimize this balance and enhance agricultural yield under variable environmental conditions. ## Future molecular perspectives of plant SV signal perception and transduction
Over the last ten years, there has been significant development in the field of phytoacoustics which has generated useful scientific data and the entrance of a new era in plant sensory biology. It is now well known that plants react outside the acoustic world and also emit SVs which can have ecological significance. Accumulating evidence indicates that plants undergo early and essential biochemical events such as calcium burst, ROS formation, and hormonal reprograming after SV treatments which are the key players of signal transduction. However, the molecular and ecological basis of plant acoustics is still unknown. Numerous questions need to be addressed in the future to decipher the molecular mechanism of SV perception and signal transduction in plants. For example, (i) How do plants sense SVs in the environment? (ii) Which MS channels or RLKs function as SV sensors (iii) How do plants decode or translate SV signals into cellular signaling? (iv) How do SVs trigger intracellular and extracellular Ca2+ bursts, and what SV-specific Ca2+ channels and sensors further decode SV information into a biochemical one? (v) How and where are ROS produced after SV treatment, and how is it regulated by cell wall or plasma membrane-based receptors such as CrRLKs, FER and THE1 (vi) The interaction between ROS, Ca2+, and other molecular signatures, such as NO, H2S, SA, JA, and ABA, is the most exciting. (vii) How do SVs regulate or activate hormonal signaling pathways to control cellular machinery responses to environmental and developmental events? (viii) How do SVs have adaptive significance in plants? (ix) Which plants can communicate acoustically? (x) What effects do aboveground SV stimuli have on belowground plant–plant communication and vice versa? (xi) Do plants use SVs for wireless signal transfers to communicate with each other or with organisms? (xii) How do plants undergo biochemical reprogramming to emit SVs after facing environmental pressures? (xiii) Do plants communicate through SVs if so how it differs from other modes of communication? Addressing the above questions will aid in both understanding the molecular complexity of plant SV signal transduction and perception as well as in identifying the critical players involved in the conversion of mechanical SV signals into biochemical responses.

## Conclusion
From an ecological standpoint, the perception and processing of SVs are extremely beneficial; thus, it is unfair to exclude plants from this fascinating field of research. However, there has been little investigation into ‘how’ plants perceive mechano-vibrational cues and transfer them to biochemical signaling. Most phytoacoustic research has been based on its application, which has been instrumental in highlighting the importance of acoustics in agriculture. There is a long-lasting question in phytoacoustics, how plants can hear or emit SVs and, if they could, what intelligence they would employ. To elucidate, future research should target some important themes such as the role of trichomes, cell wall sensors, plasma membrane channels, intracellular organelle sensors, signaling molecules (Ca2+, ROS, NO, hormones, and their crosstalk), as well as interconnected signaling pathways between organelles and the nucleus to underpin the molecular mechanism of SV signal perception and transduction. In addition, the mechanism by which SVs cause epigenetic changes remains unknown. Therefore, the putative epigenetic mechanisms underlying the regulation of complex gene networks should be understood by examining DNA methylation patterns and histone changes in specific genomic areas after SV treatment. Integrating multi-omics with high-throughput biophysical and computational tools will provide a mechanistic understanding of SV signal perception and transduction. In addition, understanding complicated plant behavior using plant acoustics would necessitate the collaboration of plant scientists with other allied disciplines and backgrounds (physics and engineering) to better understand the plant SV interaction. Future research should also focus on decoding the hidden world of plants’ behavioral systems, with a particular focus on how they receive, share, store, and process SV information from their environment and translate it into responsive behavior (both adaptive and developmental), which will open new platforms for modern breeding and genetic engineering to develop smart crops.
In this review, we presented a model that defines how plants could involve different signaling players in SV perception and transduction at different cellular compartments which will provide a roadmap for future phytacoustic research. Further research on the molecular and biophysical mechanisms of this paradigm is required to support these presumptions. However, there are many challenges in plant acoustics, such as designing the plant SV experimental setup, optimizing SV frequency and duration, real-time data coverage, and access and availability of tools. In this regard, there is a need to integrate acoustic ecology, auditory mechanics, live cell imaging, and plant physiology-based tools to address the above challenges. This will advance our knowledge of plant SV signaling and spur the development of novel concepts and perspectives on plant communication.
Plants communicate to each other or with other organisms using different chemical signals which have huge impact on their ecological and adaptive responses. However, a recent study has reported that plants emit SVs under stress which could benefit other plants by fine-tuning their adaptive responses as well as making them alert for future stress [1]. This opens a new direction in plant sensory biology and highlights that plants may involve SVs as their wireless mode of communication. It is interesting to note that whether plants emit similar SVs in terms of frequency and cultivar specific against different stressors or whether they may vary warrants future investigation. Future studies should focus on how plants use SVs or their induced VOCs for communication and their ecological effects. Further, how plants will use acoustic communication with above and below-ground organisms warrants future investigation. In particular, how SVs can affect or modulate plant microbiome is also a key theme for future study since this will provide novel avenues for plant growth promotion and stress adaptability. In plants, SVs or other mechanical stimuli like touch can cause biochemical changes, such as the activation of ROS, Ca2+, and hormone signaling cascades. These changes can alter root exudates, which are the main drivers of shaping the plant microbiome. Previously, a cell wall mechanosensor FER was found that it modulates root microbiome by regulating ROS [220]. So, we anticipate that SV may influence plant microbiome by modulating root exudates through cell wall receptor-like kinases or Ca2+ channels. It is important to note that how SVs will affect plants during multiple environmental and mechanical pressures warrants further investigation.

## Figure Fig. 1: An illustration of plant behavior showing how plants listen or communicate with each other without having complex sensory organs, such as eyes or ears. Numerous natural and artificial/synthetic sounds in the environment are perceived by the plants as mechanical signals and converted them into chemical signals, which might act as wireless communication between plants and with other neighbouring organisms. This wireless communication within the plants can modulate the plant response either by systemic or long-distance signaling. SVs may also change the plant exudates that can shape specific microbiota in plants. In addition, during any external stimuli, plant (from the phyllosphere region) or microbes (from the rhizospheric region) also emits some volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which can act as a source of wired plant-plant communication in addition to SVs.

## Figure Fig. 2: Illustration showing plants’ intelligence to sense different sources of SVs (natural and artificial sources) that have various ecological, behavioral, and adaptive impacts on them. These interactions can be mutualistic and antagonistic which depends on the source of SVs and type of plant system.

## Figure Fig. 3: A hypothetical model showing how SVs can be sensed by the plant cell wall sensors. We have shown two parts (A) Cell under resting stage and (B) SV triggered cell wall modification. Under normal conditions, there will be no effect on the cell wall structure or other chemical signaling, as shown in the figure. After SV treatment, mechanical vibrations hit the wall, causing an array of changes so-called cell wall remodeling such as wall loosening, stiffness, turgor control, releasing oligosaccharides (OGs), activation of peptides like RALFs and other molecules which will bind different cell wall receptors like RLKs (FER, THE1, WAKs, LRR, etc.) that triggers a hub of upstream and downstream cytoplasmic signaling cascades (receptor activation) as shown in the figure. Some of the important events are apoplast alkalization, calcium burst, ROS production, and hormonal regulation driven by phosphorylation, all of which are connected with cell wall remodeling and SV signal transduction, and also can modulate various molecular, biochemical, and physiological adaptive responses in plants. Further, we have also shown how RLKs can regulate calcium and H+-ATPase activity and its regulation. Other events which are interlinked with the cell wall and cytoplasmic signaling are shown in Fig. 4.

## Figure Fig. 4: An updated and expanded hypothetical model depicting molecular and biochemical events during plant SV interaction. The possible initial players of SV perception are cell wall sensors, which can trigger cell wall remodeling or modifications and further activate plasma membrane receptors and intracellular organelle checkpoints. The plasma membrane is a ‘hot spot’ for the activation of SV signal transduction cascades as it contains diverse, versatile sensors including mechanosensitive calcium channels like MCA, MSL, OSCA, CNGC, GLR, and PIEZO; RLK sensors (FER, THE1, ANXUR1/ANXUR2, WAKs, and LRR); RBOHs, and ATPase all of which are crucial for sensing mechanical cues and translating them into biochemical responses. For example, after SV treatment, these MS channels may be activated directly or through RLKs and triggers cytosolic Ca2+ burst. These Ca2+ waves will be decoded by different Ca2+ sensors such as CDPKs; CaMs/ CMLs; CCaMKs, CBL, and CIPK modules and translates them into physiological, biochemical, and molecular traits in different cellular components (ER, nucleus, chloroplast, Golgi bodies, mitochondria, and vacuoles) with diverse functions, as shown in the figure. At the same time, Ca2+ influx is controlled by the action of multifaceted Ca2+ transporters [Ca2+ATPases, P1-ATPases (HMA1), the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter complex (MCUC), and Ca2+-exchangers (CAXs)] that are involved in the extrusion of Ca2+ ions to restore basal Ca2+ levels to prevent any unwanted damage to the cellular machinery. Secondly, ROS formation will be another SV-triggered response in cells that can have a significant impact on various pathways such as calcium and K+ influx or efflux, calcium transport in apoplast and vacuole, and activate MAPK signal transduction pathways. We have also shown how ROS and Ca2+ have a reciprocal interaction after SV exposure in which each individual can be a cause and an effect, a phenomenon known as Ca2+-induced ROS production (CIRP) and ROS-induced Ca2+ release (RICR). How SV activates different enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants is also shown in the figure. SV-triggered hormonal reprogramming (SA, JA, ABA, and auxin), and their crosstalk can operate different signaling cascades during SV treatment. Also, the role of NO, H2S, sugar, and other solutes during SV signaling is depicted in the figure. Several kinds of genes/transcriptional factors (TCH, XTH, GA2OX, WRKY, NAC, MYB, MYC, and HSPS) and proteins are revealed to be differentially expressed/activated when the plant cell is exposed by SVs. There are numerous information gaps in plant SV signal transduction, including cell wall-mediated SV signal transduction, epigenetic changes, anterograde and retrograde signaling, and other physiological processes involved in SV signal perception and transmission, as shown in the figure.

## Figure Fig. 5: Decoding the molecular complexity of SV perception and transduction using multi-omics, joint computational and biophysical modeling, electrophysiology, and machine learning tools. (A-C) Identification of SV sensor genes /transcriptions factors and other signaling genes from SV multi-omics data that can be further validated using gene knock-out studies to find the target genes for SV perception and signal transduction. Identification of SV mechanosensitive channels using inhibitors (antagonistic/agonistic) can provide novel insights into SV signaling. Using high-throughput tools to identify the key players involved in SV-mediated intracellular and organelle signaling (retrograde), as well as epigenetic modifications, could further unravel hidden secrets in plant acoustics.
