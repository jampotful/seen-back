The acoustic channel: characteristics and constraints
Summary
In recent years, the idea has flourished that plants emit and perceive
sound and could even be capable of exchanging information
through the acoustic channel. While research into plant bioacoustics
is still in its infancy, with potentially fascinating discoveries awaiting
ahead, here we show that the current knowledge is not conclusive.
While plants do emit sounds under biotic and abiotic stresses such as
drought, these sounds are high-pitched, of low intensity, and
propagate only to a short distance. Most studies suggesting plant
sensitivity to airborne sound actually concern the perception of
substrate vibrations from the soil or plant part. In short, while lowfrequency, high-intensity sounds emitted by a loudspeaker close to
the plant seem to have tangible effects on various plant processes
such as growth – a finding with possible applications in agriculture –
it is unlikely that plants can perceive the sounds they produce, at
least over long distances. So far, there is no evidence of plants
communicating with each other via the acoustic channel.

Introduction
Animals everywhere, from temperate forests to lush tropical jungles
and from freshwater rivers to oceanic coral reefs, use sound to
communicate. These exchanges of information are essential for
finding a mate, defending a territory, warning of the presence of a
predator, indicating a food source, collaborating to hunt, and
recognizing and interacting with individuals in the group. Decades
of research in bioacoustics, the scientific discipline that studies the
sounds of the living world, has enabled us to understand how
animals produce these sound signals, what information is coded
and transmitted, and how the mechanisms of sound production
and perception have evolved over time. Although many questions
await scientific investigation, our knowledge of animal sounds is
considerable (Mathevon, 2023). But are animals the only ones to
communicate through sounds? Recently, bioacoustics research has
entered the plant kingdom, and it has been suggested that plants
could also be using sounds to communicate (Hussain et al., 2023;
Waqas et al., 2023). After a thorough review of the current literature
(see Appendix A1 for a complete list of references), we assert here
that we have no evidence for this. Given the specific constraints of
airborne sound production, propagation and perception, the

Sound is a wave composed of extremely small variations in the
pressure of a medium (air or water), propagating at an average speed
of 340 m s 1 in air and 1500 m s 1 in water. A sound is defined
by two essential dimensions: intensity and frequency (the third
dimension, phase, is not essential here). Sound pressure level (SPL),
expressed in decibels (dB), corresponds to the amplitude (intensity)
of sound waves. For reference, a whisper is emitted at c. 30 dB SPL;
we talk at c. 50 dB SPL; a jet in flight emits a sound of c. 140 dB
SPL. Sound frequency, expressed in Hertz (Hz), corresponds to the
rhythm of pressure variations and translates into its high-pitched or
low-pitched character. Humans can hear sounds within a frequency
range of 20–20 000 Hz. Sounds with frequencies below 20 Hz
(infra-sounds) and above 20 000 Hz (ultra-sounds) are too lowpitched and high-pitched, respectively, to be heard by humans.
The use of sound as a vector for information is subject to several
constraints. The first two constraints relate to the production and
reception of sound waves. The reason why so few animal groups use
acoustic communication is primarily because producing and hearing
sounds is not easy. Producing informative sounds in a controlled
manner generally requires specialized, often anatomically complex
structures (such as tymbals in cicada, larynx in mammals, and syrinx
in birds). The reception of sound waves also requires specialized
structures, which essentially are derivatives of mechanoreceptors. The
third constraint is linked to the degradation of sound during
transmission. When propagating through the environment, sound
waves decrease in intensity, are modified by reverberation and other
phenomena, and get mixed with ambient noise. The volume of
information available to the receiving organism therefore decreases
with distance from the transmitter. This is particularly pronounced
for high-pitched ultra-sounds, which propagate only short distances.

Sound emission by plants
It has long been known that plants can generate sounds caused by
cavitation, which occurs especially under water-deficient conditions (Milburn & Johnson, 1966; De Roo et al., 2016). Cavitation
occurs when the tension generated in the xylem sap by
transpirational pull leads to the aspiration of air into the conduits
through pit membranes, forming air bubbles. The expansion of
these bubbles disrupts water transport, resulting in vessel wall
vibrations (Sandford & Grace, 1985; Borghetti et al., 1989; H€oltt€a
et al., 2005). This results in the emission of broadband, mainly high
pitched, acoustic signals whose frequencies vary among plants
(Cochard et al., 2008; Savi et al., 2015). For instance, tomato and

away from the plant. Besides cavitation, there have been reports of
other kinds of acoustic emissions. For instance, the young roots
of corn (Zea mays) emit a spike-like acoustic signal in the frequency
range of a few hundred Hz (Gagliano et al., 2012).

Sound perception by plants
Most of the studies suggesting that plants can perceive airborne
sounds actually focused on the perception of vibrations propagating
in a solid substrate. Plants can indeed detect the vibrations induced by
herbivores chewing on their shoots or fruits and can initiate defense
responses, such as by producing toxins or insect repellents (Appel &
Cocroft, 2014; Pinto et al., 2019; Kollasch et al., 2020). The buzzing
of bees was also reported to induce vibrations in flower petals and
trigger the production of nectar (De Luca & Vallejo-Marin, 2013;
Veits et al., 2019; Pyke et al., 2020; Raguso et al., 2020). There have
however been some studies testing the perception of aerial sound
waves by plants, but the results are not conclusive and shed serious
doubts on the ability of plants to perceive the sounds emitted by other
plants. First, most of the studies fail to describe the characteristics of
the sound stimuli used (in terms of frequency spectrum, intensity,
and temporal dynamics) and their experimental playback protocols.
Second, experiments were usually conducted using artificial loud
sounds that plants cannot naturally produce (e.g. 100 dB SPL at
1 m). Third, in many cases, it is difficult to determine whether plants
are responding directly to airborne sounds or to the substrate
vibrations induced by the sounds (e.g. vibrations of the shelf on which
the plants are placed).
Collins & Foreman’s (2001) study is one of the few studies that
found evidence of the effect of sound on plants using wellcontrolled stimuli. The authors showed that bean and Impatiens
plants are sensitive to sounds emitted from a source close to the
plant, and reported plant growth when the wavelength of sound
waves coincided with the width of plant leaves. However, the
authors used sounds emitted at 91–94 dB SPL, an intensity far
from what plants can produce.
Some trichomes may perceive sounds emitted by not-too-distant
sources, and probably respond to only high-intensity (loud)
sounds. Computational modeling suggests that Arabidopsis
thaliana trichomes could vibrate in response to sound (Liu
et al., 2017; Yin et al., 2021). Another theoretical modeling study
suggested that tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) plants could sense
acoustic emissions from bats, birds, and insect herbivores through
trichomes (Peng et al., 2022). However, if plants may indeed
perceive sounds using trichomes, this ability of plants is most likely
limited to sounds emitted at a short distance and those with a high
intensity (loud sounds).
Plants might also perceive sounds through mechanosensitive ion
channels, which are widely distributed across a variety of plant taxa,
or through vibration-triggered alterations in the extracellular
matrix (Anderson et al., 2001; Hematy & H€ofte, 2008; Shih
et al., 2014). However, these potential mechanisms of sound

Conclusions
As summarized in Fig. 1:
 Plants can emit low-intensity, high-pitched sounds. These include
sounds produced by cavitation, but there may be others. The active
space of these sounds (the maximum distance at which the plant
sounds can be perceived by any living organism) is very limited.
 Little is known about the information contained in the sounds
emitted by plants, except that most known sounds indicate water
stress. It can be a good topic to investigate whether plant emits
specific sounds by infection of microbes like bacteria, fungi, and
phytoplasma and where the sounds come from.
 Plants are sensitive to substrate vibrations (vibrations of leaves
generated by the chewing of herbivores and the spattering of rain
drops; vibrations of the soil induced by various elements, such as the
underground passage of running water, etc.). This perception could
(probably) be mediated by sensory structures such as trichomes.
 Loud sounds experimentally emitted through loudspeakers may
have wide-ranging effects (e.g. on plant growth). It is possible that
human activity-generated noise pollution (> 100 dB SPL) can
impact plants, although this needs to be tested.
 Plants seem to be sensitive to certain airborne sounds, including
those of high intensity (c. 100 dB SPL) and those emitted close to
the plant. There have been extremely few studies seeking to identify
the ‘hearing curve’ of plants. The sensitivity of plants to sounds has
started to be examined under both glasshouse and field conditions,
suggesting potential positive effects on plant growth. However, the
synthetic sounds used are of significantly higher intensity than
actual plant emitting sounds such as cavitation sounds suggesting
lack of biological relevance. The application of sound in agriculture
remains unexploited and its utility remains questionable.
 It is unlikely that plants perceive ‘natural’ sounds emitted by
other plants, such as cavitation sounds, especially over long
distances. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that plants
might perceive these sounds at an extremely close range. Certain
sophisticated evaluation on the plant responses to natural sounds
(20–150 kHz) needs to be conducted to understand biologically
relevant plant acoustic communications.
 Most studies carried out to date to test sound perception by plants
lack rigor in controlling the sound stimuli. In many cases, there is
high probability that substrate vibrations, instead of airborne sounds,
are transmitted. Additionally, many studies ignore the intensity of
signals emitted and fail to provide technical data for the loudspeakers
used. Furthermore, the playback conditions are poorly controlled.
Researchers investigating these questions must work closely with
acousticians who are experts in the properties and techniques of
sound signal recording and playback.
 No sound receptors have been identified in plants. Mechanoreceptive trichomes are thought to be good candidates, but they
will likely not be very sensitive (and therefore will be restricted to
perception at short distance or of loud sounds).

Fig. 1 Emission and reception of airborne sounds
and substrate vibrations by plants: current
knowledge. (a) Under stressful conditions, such as
desiccation, herbivory, physical damage, or
pathogen infection, plants emit low-intensity,
high-pitched airborne sounds (frequencies ranging
from 20 to 150 kHz). These ultrasonic sounds
might be perceived by neighboring plants or other
organisms such as phytophagous insects, if
positioned very close to the emitting plant.
However, no experimental proof has yet been
provided. Based on current knowledge, plants do
not perceive the sounds they emit, and this
perception becomes practically impossible at
greater distances. (b) Vibrations of the substrate
(plant parts, soil, etc.) generated by pollinators,
herbivores, and other phenomena, such as water
diffusing into the soil, may have various
physiological consequences in plants, notably
concerning processes involved in reproduction,
immune system activation, and stress response.
Loud, low-frequency airborne sounds emitted
from a loudspeaker appear to have similar effects,
possibly because they induce vibrations of plant
parts. Specific mechanisms and receptors that
perceive substrate vibrations or airborne sounds in
plants have not been identified. Currently, only
mechanoreceptive trichomes are hypothesized to
be involved in sound perception.

 There is no evidence that plants use sounds to exchange
information with other plants. Today, plant communication via
acoustics remains an unconfirmed hypothesis. If it exists, acoustic
plant communication likely occurs at an extremely short range.
