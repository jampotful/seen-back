# Arabidopsis kinesins KCH3 and KCH7 promote root gravitropism through actin bundling in the stele

## Abstract
SignificanceGravitropism is essential for root orientation and nutrient acquisition in plants. While gravity sensing via amyloplast sedimentation in columella cells is well studied, the role of the root stele in mediating the directional growth remains elusive. Here, we identify the plant-specific kinesin proteins KCH3 and KCH7 as key regulators of root gravitropism in Arabidopsis. These dual actin/microtubule-binding motors bundle actin filaments in the root stele, ensuring proper auxin transport and redistribution. Our findings thereby uncover a function of the actin cytoskeleton in inner root tissues and establish a motor-mediated mechanism that coordinates cytoskeletal dynamics with hormone transport to drive adaptive plant growth.
Gravitropism enables plants to sense and respond to Earth’s gravity, adjusting their growth direction and morphology to maintain normal growth and development. Among the various forms of gravitropism, root gravitropism is crucial for anchoring plants and facilitating efficient water and nutrient absorption (1–5). This process consists of three sequential stages: gravity sensing/perception, signal transduction, and differential growth (6, 7).
The starch-statolith hypothesis proposes that the sedimentation of amyloplasts in columella cells initiates gravity sensing, and the underlying molecular mechanism has been elucidated by our work and others (8–13). Subsequent directional growth is driven by asymmetric auxin distribution, in accordance with the Cholodny–Went theory (14). In Arabidopsis, auxin synthesized in young aerial tissues is transported basipetally through the stele toward the root tip, where it combines with locally produced auxin to establish an auxin maximum in the quiescent center (15–17). In this process, transporters PIN1, PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7 in the stele play a decisive role in directing auxin flow from the aboveground “source” to the root tip “sink” (18). Auxin then flows basally along the lateral root cap, epidermis and cortical cells to the transition and elongation zones in an “inverted fountain” pattern, critical for root elongation, gravitropic bending, and lateral root initiation (19, 20). Moreover, auxin regulates the expression of key starch granule synthesis genes SS4, PGM, and ADG1, further integrating gravity sensing and signal transduction (21).
The actin cytoskeleton has been implicated in multiple steps of the gravitropic response, particularly in amyloplast sedimentation and auxin transport (9, 22, 23). Numerous studies have investigated the functions of actin using microscopic imaging or cytoskeletal-disrupting agents. However, there remains debate about whether actin promotes or inhibits gravitropism due to differences in research methodologies, experimental materials, and organ types (9, 22–27). In columella cells, amyloplasts are surrounded by a randomly oriented actin filament network, and disruption of actin organization by actin inhibitors or genetic mutations affects the sedimentation of amyloplasts (25, 28, 29). Several actin regulators, including SGR9, RMD, and the ARP2/3 complex, modulate amyloplast movement during gravity responses (3, 30–32). However, existing studies focused on the role of actin in regulating amyloplast sedimentation, whereas its functions in other gravitropic processes remain to be further explored.
Kinesins are a class of microtubule-dependent motor proteins that play important roles in vesicle transport and cytoskeletal organization (33–38). The plant-specific KCH (kinesin with calponin homology) subfamily within kinesin-14 is unique to bind not only microtubules but also actin filaments, suggesting a specific biological role for its interaction with actin in cellular processes (39–41). KCH proteins play roles in nuclear migration, cell elongation, and organ development (38, 42, 43). In the moss Physcomitrium patens (P. patens), the KCH protein GTRC (Gravitropism Group C) transports unknown cargoes toward the minus ends of microtubules, leading to the accumulation of actin filaments in the upper region of the apical cell upon gravistimulation, thereby inducing negative gravitropism (44). However, the functions of KCH proteins in higher plants, specifically in gravitropic regulation, remain largely unexplored.
Here, we report that the kch3 kch7 (kch37) double mutant exhibits a significantly defective root gravitropism. This defect can be rescued by stele-specific expression of either KCH3 or KCH7. We show that KCH3 and KCH7, hereafter collectively referred to as KCH3/7, modulate the morphology and dynamic turnover of actin filaments in the root stele through CH domain-mediated actin-bundling activity. Furthermore, the loss of KCH3/7 function disrupts auxin transport in the stele, reduces auxin accumulation in the columella cells of root cap, and inhibits auxin redistribution in response to gravistimulation. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized role of the cytoskeleton in the root stele in facilitating polar auxin transport and regulating root gravitropism, thereby providing insights into the underlying mechanisms of plant gravitropism.

## Results

## KCH3 and KCH7 Positively Regulate Root Gravitropic Responses.
The cytoskeleton plays an important role in gravity sensing, signal transduction, and cell growth in plants, but the underlying mechanisms remain obscure. Our previous study showed that the KCH homolog GTRC (PpKCHb) in P. patens determines the direction of gravitropism in protonemata (44). To investigate whether KCH homologous proteins have conserved functions during evolution, a systematic study was conducted on the gravitropic function of KCHs in plants. Phylogenetic analysis of KCH revealed the presence of seven KCHs in Arabidopsis thaliana (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). First, we obtained T-DNA insertion mutants of six Arabidopsis KCH genes and generated CRISPR-Cas9 gene-edited mutants for KCH5. A semiquantitative analysis of the T-DNA insertion mutants revealed that all KCHs T-DNA insertion mutants were knockout mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B–D). Furthermore, the characterization of the CRISPR-Cas9 mutants of KCH5 exhibited premature translational termination due to large fragment deletions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Next, we performed gravitropic experiments on seven single mutants of the KCH family and found that kch3 and kch7 single mutants showed root gravitropic response defects, whereas none of the other mutants had significant gravitropic response defects (Fig. 1 A–C and E). To investigate whether there is functional redundancy in the KCH family in root gravitropism, we obtained different combinations of double and higher-order mutants by hybridization for the gravitropic experiment. The results demonstrated that the gravitropic phenotype was significantly exacerbated in the kch37-1 double mutant, whereas higher-order mutants showed no significant further enhancement (Fig. 1 D and F). Furthermore, we used CRISPR-Cas9 technology to generate two mutants simultaneously editing KCH3 and KCH7, named kch37-2 and kch37-3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B), which showed similar gravitropic defect phenotypes as the kch37-1 (Fig. 1 G and I). These findings suggest that KCH3 and KCH7, members of the Arabidopsis KCH family, participate in the root gravitropic response and exhibit functional redundancy in this process.

To determine whether the delayed gravitropic response resulted from general root growth defects, we compared the root lengths of kch mutants and wild-type plants under normal growth conditions. These measurements detected no significant differences in root length between the kch3, kch7, kch37-1 and the wild type plants (Fig. 1 H and J), further demonstrating that KCH3/7 plays a role in gravitropism rather than affecting the rate of root elongation. In addition, we observed the gravitropic phenotypes of the wild type and the kch37-1 mutant at different times of gravitropic stimulation and found that the absence of KCH3/7 function delays the gravitropic response, but that the kch37-1 mutant eventually reaches a similar level of gravitropism as the wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Taken together, these results suggest that KCH3 and KCH7 positively regulate root gravitropism in Arabidopsis.

## Stele-Expressed KCH3 and KCH7 Are Required for Root Gravitropism.
To analyze the expression pattern of KCH3 and KCH7, promoter-driven GUS assays were performed. KCH3 is expressed in inflorescences, leaves, and roots, whereas KCH7 is expressed in inflorescences and roots (Fig. 2A). Since kch37 mutant showed obvious root gravitropism defects, we further generated GFP (Green Fluorescent Protein) fusion lines in this mutant to check the expression pattern of KCH3 and KCH7 in roots. pKCH3-KCH3genome-GFP (pK3-K3g-GFP) and pKCH7-KCH7genome-GFP (pK7-K7g-GFP) were introduced into the kch37-1 background, respectively, and homozygous complementation transgenic lines were obtained (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). In roots, KCH3 is predominantly expressed in the endodermis and stele, whereas KCH7 is mainly confined to the stele (Fig. 2B). These complementation lines could rescue the kch37-1 mutant phenotype to the single mutant level (Fig. 2 C and D), indicating these GFP-fused KCH proteins are functional.

When eGFP-K3g or eGFP-K7g was specifically expressed in the epidermis, cortex, or endodermis of roots, it failed to rescue the root gravitropic defect in kch37-1 mutants. In contrast, when eGFP-K3g or eGFP-K7g was expressed specifically in the stele, the root gravitropism of kch37-1 was restored to single mutant levels (Fig. 3 B, C, E, and F). These results indicate that the function of KCH3/7 in the stele is essential for normal root gravitropism in Arabidopsis.

## Both the CH and Motor Domains Are Essential for KCH3’s Function in Root Gravitropism.
To investigate the intracellular function of KCH3/7, we first transiently expressed 35S-eGFP-KCH3 (1-764) and 35S-eGFP-KCH7 (1-762), hereafter referred to as eGFP-KCH3 and eGFP-KCH7, in tobacco epidermal cells and also introduced the actin marker chromobody-mScarlet and the microtubule marker mCherry-TUB6. In tobacco leaf epidermal cells, eGFP-KCH3 and eGFP-KCH7 displayed obvious filamentous localization and partially colocalized with actin filaments and microtubules (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–H). In addition, we exogenously applied actin- or microtubule-depolymerizing drugs and observed changes in KCH localization. Consistent with previous reports on KCH homologs (48), depolymerization of F-actin by Latrunculin B (LatB) caused the elongated filamentous structures of eGFP-KCH3 to shorten and become more dispersed. In contrast, microtubule depolymerization by oryzalin disrupted the localization pattern and resulted in an aggregated localization (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 I and J). These results suggest that KCH3 and KCH7 may localize on the actin filaments and microtubules in vivo.
Our previous study showed that in P. patens, the N-terminal, C-terminal, and motor domains of the kinesin GTRC are essential for gravitropism of protonemata, but the CH domain is not (44). To investigate the domains responsible for the function of Arabidopsis KCHs in gravitropism, we constructed various truncated and point mutated versions of KCH3 with GFP fused to their C-terminus (Fig. 4A). These constructs were transformed into the kch37-1 mutant, and KCH3 expression was verified by semiquantitative analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Then, their complementation of kch37-1 and the localization of KCH3 variants were examined (Fig. 4 B–D). Furthermore, the domain truncation and point mutation constructs of KCH3 were transiently expressed in tobacco epidermal cells (Fig. 4 E–M). The deletion of the CH domain led to reduced accumulation of KCH3 in the root stele and disrupted its actin-associated localization, thereby preventing its complementation of the kch37-1 mutant’s gravitropic defects (Fig. 4 B–J). The ATP binding site point mutation (ATP-DT) maintained both the tissue and sub-cellular localization pattern of KCH3, but was unable to complement the defect of root gravitropism (Fig. 4 B–D and K–M). Previous studies demonstrated that the point mutation in the ATP-binding motif generates rigor kinesin, which binds to microtubules but cannot dissociate from or move along them (49). This may explain why KCH3 (ATP-DT) fails to complement the kch37-1 mutant.

These findings suggest that both the CH and motor domains are essential for KCH3 function. This contrasts with the role of KCHb/GTRC in P. patens, where the CH domain is dispensable for its function in gravitropism (44). These data indicate that the KCH proteins in both P. patens and Arabidopsis regulate gravitropism, but the underlying mechanisms may differ.

## KCH3 and KCH7 Bundle Actin Filaments through Their CH Domains.
The CH domain is considered to be a typical actin-binding module (50, 51), and KCH proteins have been demonstrated to function in actin bundling in species such as tobacco and cotton. To further investigate the function of the Arabidopsis KCH3/7 proteins, we expressed the recombinant proteins His-KCH3 (1-764) and His-KCH7 (1-762) in Escherichia coli cells. Both high-speed (Fig. 5 A–D) and low-speed (Fig. 5 E–H) cosedimentation experiments showed that KCH3 and KCH7 could bind and bundle F-actin. Further studies revealed that the CH domain of KCH3 and KCH7 bind and bundle F-actin (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–H). In addition, we analyzed the interactions of KCH3 and KCH7 with F-actin by fluorescence microscopy, and the results showed that KCH3 (1-764) and KCH7 (1-762), as well as KCH3-CH and KCH7-CH proteins, could promote the bundling of F-actin in vitro (Fig. 5 I and J and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 I and J). These results indicate that KCH3/7 binds and bundles F-actin in vitro via its CH domain.

## KCH3 and KCH7 Promote Actin Bundling and Dynamic Turnover in the Root Stele.
Given that the CH domain is a key functional domain in the KCH3/7-regulated root gravitropic response process (Fig. 4) and that KCH3/7-CH functions in actin binding and bundling in vitro (Fig. 5 A–J), we hypothesized that the actin plays a key role in the KCH3/7-regulated root gravitropic response process in Arabidopsis. To further observe the regulation of KCH3/7 proteins on actin morphology and kinetic properties in vivo, we expressed the actin marker pSHR-eGFP-ABD2-eGFP (ABD2-eGFP) in the stele of Col-0 and kch37-1 roots. The experimental results demonstrated that actin filaments within the stele of the elongation zone of kch37-1 mutant roots exhibited reduced bundling and increased density in comparison with the wild type (Fig. 5 K–M). This finding is consistent with the biochemical function of KCH3/7 in bundling F-actin in vitro. Concurrently, analysis of dynamic correlation coefficients revealed a slower dynamic turnover of actin in the elongation zone stele of kch37-1 mutant roots compared to wild-type (Fig. 5 N and O). These results suggest that KCH3/7 proteins in the root stele promote actin bundling and dynamic turnover, thereby regulating root gravitropism.

## KCH3 and KCH7 Facilitate Auxin Accumulation at the Root Tip, Thereby Promoting Proper Auxin Redistribution during Gravistimulation.
To test whether the delayed gravitropic response in kch37-1 mutants resulted from disrupted asymmetric auxin distribution across the root tip, we measured auxin levels using DR5rev::GFP reporter lines (20). Compared to the wild type, the kch37-1 mutant exhibited reduced asymmetry of DR5rev::GFP signal between the lower and upper sides of the root tip after 3 h of gravistimulation (Fig. 6 A and B). Similarly, the DII-VENUS signal asymmetry between the two sides of the root tip was significantly reduced in kch37-2 compared to wild type (52) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). Furthermore, to assess the dynamics of auxin asymmetry formation, we analyzed the DR5rev::GFP signal in the root tips of Col-0 and kch37-1 at 0, 1, 3, and 6 h following gravistimulation. We found that mutation of KCH3/7 significantly disrupted auxin asymmetric distribution at multiple time points, with the most pronounced difference observed at 3 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D). Additionally, we examined the localization of the auxin transporter PIN2-GFP in the epidermal cells on the upper and lower sides of the meristem and elongation zones during root gravitropic bending. The results indicated that after gravistimulation, the asymmetrical distribution of PIN2 was reduced in kch37-1 compared with the wild type (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–D). These results indicate that the redistributions of auxin and PIN2 are inhibited in the kch37 mutant under gravistimulation, consistent with a defect of gravitropic phenotype.

To determine whether the delayed gravitropic response of the kch37 mutant is related to auxin levels in the root tip, we analyzed auxin levels in root tip using DR5rev::GFP. Since the fourth columella layer (S4) undergoes programmed cell death and lacks PIN expression, it is excluded from quantitative analyses (53, 54). The results demonstrated that auxin levels in the kch37-1 mutant were notably reduced in the quiescent center (QC) and the S1 to S3 layers of columella cells (Fig. 6 C–E). Auxin accumulation at the root tip primarily depends on stele-mediated downward transport from the shoot, which is precisely regulated by auxin transporters PINs (18). Therefore, we further examined auxin levels in stele cells as well as the abundance and polarity of stele PIN1, PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7. Compared with the Col-0, the DR5rev::GFP signal in the stele of kch37-1 was significantly reduced (Fig. 6 F and G), whereas DII-VENUS fluorescence in the stele of kch37-2 was increased (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). These results indicate a substantial decrease of auxin in the kch37 root stele. Meanwhile, in the root stele of kch37-1, PIN1-GFP signal intensity increased, PIN4-GFP and PIN7-GFP signals decreased, and PIN3-GFP signal showed no obvious change (Fig. 6 H–M and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D), suggesting that loss of KCH3 and KCH7 function disrupts the normal accumulation of PIN proteins in the stele. In addition, qRT-PCR analysis showed no significant differences in the transcript levels of PIN1, PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7 in kch37-1 roots compared to Col-0, indicating that the loss of KCH3/7 function does not affect the transcription of these PIN genes in the stele (SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). Furthermore, using a confocal microscope with a vertical stage, we examined the polarity of PIN1, PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7 in root stele cells. The results showed no significant difference in the polarity of PIN1-, PIN3-, and PIN7-GFP between Col-0 and kch37-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 F–K). In contrast, the apical-basal polarity of PIN4-GFP in root stele cells was reduced in kch37-1 (Fig. 6 N and O), which may hinder the acropetal transport of auxin in roots.
Previous studies have reported that auxin regulates the expression of key genes involved in starch granule synthesis, thereby affecting starch formation (21). To examine starch accumulation in columella cells, we stained roots with modified propidium iodide. No significant difference in starch content was detected between the root columella cells of Col-0 and kch37-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B), which is probably due to that the reduction of auxin level in the mutant was insufficient to affect starch accumulation. Consistently, amyloplast sedimentation in columella cells showed no significant difference between wild type and mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 C–E). In addition, PIN3-GFP and PIN7-GFP showed comparable expression levels in columella cells of Col-0 and kch37-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 F–I).
These results suggest that loss of KCH3 and KCH7 function disturbs the abundance and polarity of multiple PIN proteins in the stele. This disruption impedes acropetal auxin transport in root tip, lowers auxin levels in columella cells, perturbs asymmetric auxin redistribution upon gravistimulation, and ultimately slows root gravitropic bending.

## Discussion
Gravitropism is an important adaptation of plants to terrestrial environments, occurring in three spatially and temporally distinct processes: gravity sensing/perception, signal transduction, and differential growth response. The cytoskeleton plays essential roles in gravitropism, with actin long recognized for its involvement in amyloplast sedimentation, thereby contributing to gravity sensing. In this study, we found that KCH3/7 regulates actin bundling in the root stele, which in turn affects the accumulation of auxin in the root apex and the asymmetric distribution of auxin during the root gravitropic response (Fig. 7). The function of KCH3/7 requires not only its motor domain but also its CH domain, which is in contrast to P. patens, where the CH domain is not essential for gravitropism (Fig. 4 B–J) (44). This highlights the functional evolutionary differences across species. These results expand our understanding of KCH function and underscore the role of the cytoskeleton in the regulation of auxin distribution and gravitropic signaling.
A proposed model for the role of Arabidopsis KCH3/7 in root gravitropism. During vertical growth, KCH3/7 regulates actin bundling and its dynamic turnover in root stele cells. This process modulates the abundance and polar localization of auxin transporters (e.g. PIN1, PIN4, and PIN7), thereby facilitating auxin accumulation in the root cap. In the kch37 mutant, reduced actin bundling and slower actin turnover in the root stele disrupt the abundance and polarity of several PIN proteins, leading to decreased auxin levels in the root cap. Consequently, when plants are reoriented relative to the gravity vector, the kch37 mutant exhibits a weakened asymmetric auxin distribution between the two sides of the root tip compared to the wild type. This results in root gravitropism defect in the kch37 mutant. Arrows labeled “g” indicate the direction of gravity.
This study reveals the specific expression of the Arabidopsis KCH family member KCH3/7 in the root stele and its key role in the gravitropic response. In contrast to the previously reported function of KINESIN G (KinG) in regulating SHR intercellular transport (41, 55), KCH3/7 regulates actin bundling (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6) and influences both the morphological maintenance and dynamic assembly of actin in the stele (Fig. 5). KCH3/7 loss-of-function mutants exhibit a delayed gravitropic response (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), clearly demonstrating its role in the regulation of plant gravitropism.
Transport of auxin from the source to the sink primarily relies on the stele-localized transporters PIN1, PIN3, PIN4, and PIN7, and auxin accumulation at the root tip is essential for auxin distribution and root patterning (18–20). AUX1 is another auxin transporter, and its mutation abolishes root gravitropic curvature. Expression of AUX1 in the root stele and columella cells failed to rescue aux1 gravitropic defect. In contrast, expression in the lateral root cap and epidermal cells rescued the gravitropism of aux1 mutants, even when AUX1 function in the stele was missing (56). Therefore, AUX1 activity in the stele is dispensable for the delivery of auxin from the upper root region to the root cap. Consequently, we focused on analyzing the abundance and polarity of stele-localized PIN proteins, but not AUX1. In our study, auxin levels were markedly reduced in both the root cap and the stele of the kch37 mutant (Fig. 6 C–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B), suggesting that KCH3/7 contributes to the auxin homeostasis. Previous studies have shown that actin filaments control the intracellular dynamics and polar localization of PIN proteins, thereby maintaining their polarity at the plasma membrane and ultimately influencing auxin polar transport and tropic responses (57–60). For example, treatment with the actin depolymerizing agent Cytochalasin D disrupts PIN1 polarity, demonstrating that actin is essential for PIN endocytic recycling (57). Moreover, the stability of the actin cytoskeleton affects PIN sorting and polarity maintenance; for instance, the actin-binding protein VILLIN modulates actin dynamics to influence PIN polarity (61). Our results further show that KCH3/7 can bind and bundle actin filaments, promoting dynamic actin turnover in the stele (Fig. 5). Loss of KCH3/7 function disturbs the abundance and polarity of several PIN proteins in the stele (Fig. 6 H–O). Taken together, this study identifies KCH3/7 as a regulatory component of the auxin “source-sink” transport model, via influencing PIN abundance and polarity through its role in actin bundling. However, how KCH3/7-mediated stele actin architecture and dynamics controls the abundance and polarity of PIN proteins remains a subject for future research.
The functional roles of the KCH family exhibit significant divergence across different plant species. In P. patens, GTRC (PpKCHb) regulates protonema gravitropism via binding microtubules, but not actin filaments. This function depends on the integrity of its N-terminal region and motor domain, whereas the CH domain is dispensable. In the protonemata of P. patens, GTRC moves on the microtubule and indirectly mediates the polar distribution of actin, regulating apical cells to grow upward under gravity stimulation (44). In contrast, Arabidopsis KCH3/7 not only colocalizes with microtubules but also actin filaments (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Specifically, we demonstrated that the CH domain regulates the localization of KCH3 and also directly influences the gravitropic response of Arabidopsis root (Fig. 4). KCH3/7 requires the intact CH domain to bundle F-actin for proper regulation of root gravitropism (Fig. 5). These differences may be related to species-specific growth patterns and tissue complexity, hence the KCH family proteins likely underwent functional diversification to adapt to distinct developmental processes.
Using interspecies genetic complementation assays, we found that PpKCHb from P. patens was able to rescue the gravitropic defects of the Arabidopsis kch37-1 mutant, whereas the Marchantia polymorpha (M. polymorpha) homolog, which we hereafter refer to as MpKCHL (KCH-LIKE), failed to do so (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–E). Although MpKCHL represents the closest homolog of Arabidopsis KCH proteins in M. polymorpha, it lacks a recognizable CH domain, suggesting functional divergence across species. To further explore the relationship between CH domain features and functional conservation, we investigated additional KCH family proteins in Arabidopsis beyond KCH3/7. We found that KCH4 was able to complement the kch37-1 gravitropic defects, whereas KCH6 and KCHL1 could not (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A–G), indicating that functional divergence also exists among Arabidopsis KCH paralogs.
To investigate the molecular basis underlying these differences, we compared the amino acid sequences of KCH and KCH-Like proteins across species. The motor domains of these proteins are highly conserved, whereas the regions corresponding to the CH domain exhibit substantial variability (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 A and B). Notably, PpKCHb, AtKCH3, AtKCH4, and AtKCH7-the proteins that successfully complemented the kch37-1 mutant-share a higher degree of sequence similarity within their CH domains. In contrast, AtKCH6, AtKCHL1, and MpKCHL, which failed to complement, are more divergent in this region. Comparative analysis revealed that the CH domains of PpKCHb, AtKCH3, AtKCH4, and AtKCH7 share highly conserved sequence features, which are absent or poorly conserved in AtKCH6, AtKCHL1, and MpKCHL. Together, these findings suggest that the sequence composition of the CH domain is crucial for the gravitropic regulatory function of KCH proteins.
With the adaptation of plants to terrestrial environments, the KCH protein family has gradually expanded and developed functional differentiation during the evolutionary process. Their regulation of gravitropism has evolved from single-gene control to synergistic multigene action. The functional study of KCH proteins across different species, particularly their role in how terrestrial plants adapt to gravity, remains limited and requires further investigation.
