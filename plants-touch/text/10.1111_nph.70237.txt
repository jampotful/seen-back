HSFA2D-LAZY6-LAZY1 module regulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice.
Liang Y, Yuan Y, Cai Y, Ma M, Lin X, Sun X, Zhao H, Zhai J, Han N, Zhou J, Wang W, Wang Y, Zhang N. (2025). The New phytologist. . doi:10.1111/nph.70237. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# HSFA2D–LAZY6–LAZY1 module regulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice

## Abstract
Summary Tiller angle is a critical architectural trait in rice (Oryza sativa), affecting planting density, light interception, and grain yield. While HEAT STRESS TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 2D (HSFA2D) has been shown to regulate tiller angle via LAZY1 (LA1)‐mediated auxin distribution, the precise mechanisms governing this pathway remain elusive.We identified and characterized LA6, which encodes a heat shock protein 70‐interacting protein, through mutant screening and the MutMap strategy. Biochemical, molecular, and genetic studies were used to elucidate the functional relationship of LA6 with HSFA2D and LA1 in mediating shoot gravitropism and tiller angle.The la6 mutant exhibited impaired shoot gravitropism and increased tiller angle due to reduced lateral auxin transport and disrupted asymmetric auxin distribution. The LA6 gene was highly expressed in shoot bases, and the LA6 protein was localized to the nucleus, cytoplasm, and plasma membrane. Intriguingly, LA6 physically interacted with HSFA2D and LA1. Genetic analyses showed that LA6 acts downstream of HSFA2D and upstream of LA1 within the same regulatory pathway.Our findings reveal that the HSFA2D–LA6–LA1 module orchestrates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle in rice, which not only advances our understanding of plant architecture regulation but also provides potential targets for yield improvement.

## Introduction
Rice (Oryza sativa L.), a staple crop for over half of the global population, exhibits yield potential closely tied to plant architecture. Among key traits, tiller angle, the inclination between the main culm and side tillers (Xu et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2022), is especially critical, as it influences planting density, light interception, and, ultimately, grain productivity (Tan et al., 2008; Wang & Li, 2008; Ikeda et al., 2013). Optimizing tiller angle is thus essential for meeting the rising global food demand.
The process of rice domestication has profoundly shaped plant architecture, particularly tiller angle. Selective breeding favored more erect growth habits over the prostrate form typical of wild rice (Sarla & Swamy, 2005; Wang et al., 2014). This transition involved several domestication‐related genes, such as PROSTRATE GROWTH 1 (PROG1), PROG7, and RICE PLANT ARCHITECTURE DOMESTICATION (RPAD) (Jin et al., 2008; Tan et al., 2008; Hu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2018; Tokuyama et al., 2023). Post‐domestication selection further refined tiller angle in the two major subspecies of Asian cultivated rice, japonica and indica. For instance, alleles like tiller angle control 1 (tac1), tac3, and dwarf 2 (d2) alleles were selected during the transition from domesticated Asian rice to japonica rice, while the tiller inclined growth 1 (tig1) and tac4 alleles were chosen during the transition from domesticated Asian rice to indica cultivated rice (Dong et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021; Xu & Sun, 2021; Wang et al., 2022).
Gravitropism is a fundamental process allowing plants to orient their shoots upward and roots downward. This response proceeds through four sequential steps: gravity sensing, signal transduction, auxin redistribution, and organ curvature (Tasaka et al., 1999; Strohm et al., 2012; Kolesnikov et al., 2016; Su et al., 2017). Previous studies have shown that shoot gravitropism largely determines rice tiller angle (Abe & Suge, 1993; Firn et al., 2000; Li et al., 2007, 2019, 2024; Sang et al., 2014; Harmoko et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2023). The starch‐statolith hypothesis posits that starch‐filled amyloplasts act as statoliths to perceive gravity (Wang et al., 2022), and their sedimentation in response to orientation changes initiates gravitropic signaling (Sack, 1991; Singh et al., 2017). Mutations in genes required for amyloplast formation and sedimentation, such as LAZY2 (LA2), LA3, PHOSPHOGLUCOMUTASE (PGM), ADP‐GLUCOSE PYROPHOSPHORYLASE 1 (AGPL1), AGPL3, LOOSE PLANT ARCHITECTURE 1 (LPA1), and LARGE TILLER ANGLE 1 (LATA1), can lead to reduced shoot gravitropism, resulting in larger tiller angles (Okamura et al., 2013, 2014; Huang et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2024). Recent work further identified LA4 as a regulator of shoot gravitropism and tiller angle acting upstream of LA1 through a mechanism independent of the PGM‐LA2‐LA3 module mediated gravity‐sensing pathway (Wang et al., 2024), expanding our understanding of the gravitropic network.
LA1 is a central regulator of shoot gravitropism and tiller angle, functioning through auxin transport–mediated redistribution (Jones & Adair, 1938; Li et al., 2007; Yoshihara & Iino, 2007; Zhu et al., 2021). Strigolactones (SLs) independently modulate this process by suppressing local auxin biosynthesis, thereby attenuating gravitropism and influencing tiller angle (Sang et al., 2014). The function of LA1 is dependent on its cellular localization, where it is localized to both the plasma membrane and the nucleus (Li et al., 2007, 2019; Zhu et al., 2021). BREVIS RADIX LIKE 4 (BRXL4) interacts with LA1 at the plasma membrane, reducing its nuclear localization. Downregulating BRXL1, BRXL4, and BRXL5 via RNAi increases LA1 nuclear localization, enhances shoot gravitropism, and reduces tiller angle, while BRXL4 overexpression produces opposite effects (Li et al., 2019). Recent studies showed that LA1 and PROG1 play antagonistic roles in regulating rice tiller angle, and they could inhibit each other's expression as repressors by directly binding to the promoter, respectively (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Despite these advances, the molecular mechanisms underlying LA1 function remain incompletely defined.
Further, the dynamic transcriptome assay explores a core regulatory pathway of rice tiller angle mediated by the LA1‐dependent asymmetric distribution of auxin. In this pathway, HEAT STRESS TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR 2D (HSFA2D) acts upstream of LA1 to mediate asymmetric auxin distribution and, in turn, induces the asymmetric expression of two additional redundant auxin‐responsive transcription factor genes WUSCHEL RELATED HOMEOBOX6 (WOX6) and WOX11 (Zhang et al., 2018). Subsequently, the identification of new components increases our knowledge about this core pathway. For instance, two redundant transcription factors OsHOMEOBOX GENE 1 (OsHOX1) and OsHOX28 bind the CAAT(C/G)ATTG element in the HSFA2D promoter, repressing its expression to modulate tiller angle (Hu et al., 2020). Another regulatory layer involves microRNAs (miRNAs). OsmiR167a represses AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 12/17/25 (ARF12/17/25) to regulate the asymmetric redistribution of auxin, consequently regulating rice shoot gravitropism and tiller angle (Li et al., 2020). Notably, the expression of OsARF12/17/25 also decreases in hsfa2d and la1 (Li et al., 2020). These results imply that HSFA2D and LA1 might regulate auxin asymmetric distribution via interconnected yet distinct pathways. While our understanding of HSFA2D and LA1 is growing, the precise mechanism of their function remains unclear.
In this study, we identified a novel tiller angle mutant, lazy6 (la6), characterized by reduced shoot gravitropism and increased tiller angle. LA6 encodes a putative HSP70‐interacting protein localized to the nucleus, cytoplasm, and plasma membrane. LA6 physically interacts with both HSFA2D and LA1 and participates in the regulation of auxin asymmetry and downstream expression of WOX6 and WOX11. Genetic analyses revealed that LA6 functions downstream of HSFA2D and upstream of LA1, positioning it as a key component of the gravitropic signaling pathway. These findings establish the HSFA2D–LA6–LA1 module as a critical regulator of gravitropism and tiller angle in rice, providing a new framework for understanding the molecular control of rice plant architecture.

## Materials and Methods

## Plant materials and growth conditions
The la6 mutant and its complementation line pLA6C were derived from the Oryza sativa (L.) ssp. japonica NG3 background. CRISPR‐induced la6 mutants (CR‐la6‐1, CR‐la6‐2, CR‐la6‐3), hsfa2d, and la1 mutants, along with the transgenic lines Ubi:LA1/la6, Ubi:LA6, and Ubi:LA6/hsfa2d, were generated in the japonica cultivar Zhonghua11 (ZH11) background. The hsfa2d and la1 mutants were previously described (Zhang et al., 2018). Double mutants hsfa2d CR‐la6‐3 and la1 CR‐la6‐1 were created by crossing hsfa2d or la1 with the corresponding CR‐la6 lines. For field experiments, plants were cultivated under natural conditions at experimental farms in Beijing and Lingshui (Hainan Province, China). For seedling analyses, materials were grown in a glasshouse at 28°C under a 16 h : 8 h, light : dark photoperiod.

## Cloning of LA6
LA6 was cloned using the MutMap approach (Abe et al., 2012). An F2 population was generated by crossing the la6 mutant with NG3 and selfing the F1 progeny. Genomic DNA from at least 50 F2 individuals with extreme phenotypes (compact or loose plant architecture) was pooled to form two DNA bulks. Whole‐genome sequencing was performed on the bulks, as well as on the mutant and wild‐type parents, to identify the causative mutation.

## Plasmid construction and genetic transformation
To construct pLA6C for complementation, a 4567‐bp genomic fragment – including 1770 bp upstream, the 2277‐bp coding region, and 520 bp downstream – was amplified and cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector. This construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and transformed into la6 mutant calli as previously described (Huang et al., 2021).
For overexpression, the full‐length coding sequences (CDSs) of LA6 and LA1 were cloned into the pTCK303 vector to generate Ubi:LA6 and Ubi:LA1, respectively. Ubi:LA6 was transformed into ZH11 and hsfa2d mutant calli, and Ubi:LA1 into CR‐la6‐1 mutant calli using Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation.
For CRISPR/Cas9 knockout, two target sequences for LA6 were cloned into the VK005‐01 vector (Viewsolid Biotech, China). The resulting construct was introduced into ZH11 calli via Agrobacterium. All primers used are listed in Supporting Information Table S1.

## Reverse transcription‐quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and treated with DNase I (Ambion, USA). First‐strand cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript III First‐Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen). Quantitative PCR was performed using SsoFast™ EvaGreen® Supermix (Bio‐Rad) on a CFX96 Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad). Ubiquitin was used as an internal control. Primers are listed in Table S1.

## Subcellular localization of LA6
To analyze subcellular localization, the LA6 CDS was cloned into SCC‐GFP and pCK303‐GFP to generate 35S:GFP‐LA6 and Ubi:LA6‐GFP constructs, respectively. 35S:GFP‐LA6 was transiently expressed in rice protoplasts, while Ubi:LA6‐GFP was agroinfiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. The nucleus and plasma membrane were stained with Hoechst and FM4‐64, respectively. Fluorescent signals were observed using a FluoView 1000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus) with excitation at 488 nm for GFP, 405 nm for Hoechst, and 561 nm for FM4‐64. Primers used for fusion construct generation are listed in Table S1.

## Yeast two‐hybrid assay
The full‐length CDS of LA6 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector (Clontech, USA) containing the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain (BD), generating LA6‐BD. cDNA libraries and the CDSs of HSFA2D and LA1 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector (Clontech) containing the GAL4 activation domain (AD). Various combinations of constructs and empty vectors were co‐transformed into the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain Y2HGold (Clontech). Transformants were selected on SD/–Trp–Leu and SD/–Trp–Leu–His–Ade media. Primers are listed in Table S1.

## Firefly luciferase complementation imaging assay
Luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assays were performed in N. benthamiana leaves as described previously (Huang et al., 2021). Full‐length CDSs of LA6, HSFA2D, and LA1 were cloned into nLUC or cLUC vectors to generate LA6‐LUC N , LUC C ‐HSFA2D, and LUC C ‐LA1. These constructs, along with their respective empty vectors, were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Bioluminescence signals were detected 2–3 d post‐infiltration using the NightShade LB 985 In Vivo Plant Imaging System. Primers used are listed in Table S1.

## Shoot gravitropism assay
Shoot gravitropism was assessed as previously described (Huang et al., 2021). The angle of shoot curvature was measured every 12 h for 3 d following gravistimulation.

## Lateral auxin transport assay
Lateral auxin transport (LAT) was analyzed with slight modifications to the previously established protocol (Li et al., 2007). Briefly, 1‐cm segments of 4‐d‐old etiolated coleoptiles were excised and pre‐incubated in ½‐strength Murashige and Skoog liquid medium (pH 5.8) for 2 h to deplete endogenous indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA). Segments were then subjected to a 2.5‐h transport assay in the dark. The basal 0.5‐cm portion of each segment was bisected into upper and lower halves. After 24 h of incubation in 600 μl of scintillation fluid, radioactivity was measured using a 1450 MicroBeta TriLux liquid scintillation counter (PerkinElmer).

## Dual‐luciferase transcriptional activity assay
To assess transcriptional regulation, the promoter region of LA6 was amplified and cloned into the pGreenII0800‐LUC vector to serve as a reporter. The full‐length CDS of HSFA2D was inserted into the pSCC‐GFP vector to generate the 35S:HSFA2D‐GFP effector construct. The 35S:GFP‐LA6 construct was also used as an additional effector. Dual‐luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), with LUC activity normalized against REN activity. Primer sequences used for vector construction are listed in Table S1.

## Cell‐free protein degradation assays
The full‐length LA1 CDS was cloned into the pET28a vector, and the resulting His‐tagged LA1 recombinant protein was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) and purified according to the manufacturer's protocol. Total protein extracts were prepared from 2‐wk‐old seedlings of ZH11, CR‐la6‐1, and Ubi:LA6 #1 using a degradation buffer composed of 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, 4 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, and 10 mM ATP. The recombinant His‐LA1 protein was incubated with these protein extracts at 28°C for specified durations. At each time point, aliquots were collected, mixed with SDS‐PAGE loading buffer to terminate the reaction, and subjected to SDS‐PAGE followed by western blotting. Immunodetection was performed using anti‐His (1 : 10 000; Abmart, M20001M) and anti‐ACTIN (1 : 10 000; Abmart, M20009L) antibodies. Primers used for cloning are provided in Table S1.

## Results

## Phenotypic characterization of the rice tiller angle mutant la6
To elucidate the molecular regulation of tiller angle in rice, we screened a collection of mutants exhibiting altered tiller angles and identified la6, a naturally occurring mutant derived from the japonica cultivar Ninggeng3 (NG3). Compared to the wild type, la6 displayed a prostrate growth habit with significantly increased tiller angles (Fig. 1a–c). Since shoot gravitropism is known to influence tiller angle (Wang et al., 2022), we examined the shoot gravitropic response of la6 seedlings. The la6 seedlings exhibited a weaker shoot gravitropic response than the wild‐type plants, which was further confirmed by kinetic studies (Fig. 1d,e). These results suggest that LA6 modulates tiller angle by regulating shoot gravitropism in rice.

Despite the increased tiller angle, la6 mutants showed reduced leaf angle and plant height compared to the wild type (Figs 1a, S1, S2a). Tiller number and panicle branching were comparable between la6 and wild‐type plants (Fig. S2b–d). Although la6 exhibited a significantly higher 1000‐grain weight due to altered grain shape (Fig. S2e,h–j), the reduced setting rate resulted in a reduction of grain yield per plant (Fig. S2f,g). These findings indicate that LA6 plays pleiotropic roles, impacting tiller angle, plant architecture, and grain yield components.

## Cloning and functional characterization of LA6
To understand the genetic basis of the enlarged tiller angle in la6, we employed the MutMap strategy (Abe et al., 2012), which combines whole‐genome resequencing of pooled F2 individuals from a backcross between la6 and NG3. Phenotypic segregation in the F2 population followed a 3 : 1 ratio (wild type : mutant), consistent with a single recessive mutation. Whole‐genome resequencing of DNA pools from individuals with wild type and mutant phenotypes revealed that the la6 prostrate trait co‐segregated with a mutation in LOC_Os05g34820, which contained a 1‐bp deletion in its first exon, causing a frameshift and premature stop codon (Figs 2a, S3). Sequencing confirmed that this deletion was specific to la6 (Figs 2a, S3), and LA6 transcript levels were significantly reduced in the mutant (Fig. 2b). These data strongly suggest that LOC_Os05g34820 corresponds to LA6.

To confirm that the 1‐bp deletion in LOC_Os05g34820 was responsible for the la6 phenotype, we performed a complementation test. A 4,567‐bp genomic fragment containing the 1,770‐bp upstream region, the entire LA6 gene including 2 exons and 1 intron, and a 520‐bp downstream region (plasmid pLA6C) was introduced into la6 plants (Fig. 2c). Transgenic lines harboring pLA6C exhibited tiller angles indistinguishable from wild‐type plants (Fig. 2d), validating LOC_Os05g34820 as LA6 and confirming that the deletion caused the mutant phenotype. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9‐generated knockout lines (CR‐la6) recapitulated the increased tiller angle and impaired shoot gravitropism (Figs 2e,f, S4), further demonstrating LA6's function in tiller angle regulation.
The CDS of LA6 consists of 1806 nucleotides encoding a protein of 601 amino acids, which was annotated as a putative HEAT SHOCK PROTEIN 70 (HSP70)‐interacting protein in the Rice Genome Annotation Project. Amino acid sequence comparisons revealed homologs across a broad range of plant lineages, including representative species from monocots, dicots, bryophytes, and ferns (Fig. S5), indicating that there is evolutionary conservation of LA6 proteins. However, the functional roles of LA6 and its homologs remain largely uncharacterized, with the exception of SHADE AVOIDANCE 4 (SAV4), the Arabidopsis homolog, which has been implicated in shade‐induced hypocotyl elongation and ethylene‐mediated root hair development (Ge et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2022).
To explore the functional role of LA6, we analyzed its expression profile in various wild‐type rice tissues using RT‐qPCR. LA6 was most highly expressed in shoots, shoot bases, tiller buds, and young panicles; moderate expression was detected in roots, internodes, and leaf sheaths, while expression was lower in mature leaves and lamina joints (Fig. 3a). This expression pattern aligns with the pleiotropic phenotypes observed in la6. To determine the subcellular localization of LA6, we transiently expressed a green fluorescent protein (GFP)‐tagged LA6 fusion protein in rice protoplasts. The GFP‐LA6 signal localized to the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus, whereas the GFP control was distributed throughout the cell (Fig. 3b). Similar subcellular localization was observed in N. benthamiana mesophyll cells (Fig. S6), indicating that LA6 may function in multiple cellular compartments.

## LA6 regulates rice tiller angle by mediating asymmetric auxin distribution
To elucidate how LA6 regulates rice tiller angle, we first assessed its expression in response to gravitational stimuli. RT‐qPCR analysis showed that LA6 transcript levels remained unchanged following gravistimulation, suggesting that LA6 could mediate shoot gravitropism but was not directly regulated by gravity itself (Fig. S7). Given the central role of auxin in gravitropic responses, we next investigated the positioning of LA6 within the auxin signaling cascade. Notably, LA6 expression was not induced by the auxin analog naphthalene acetic acid, indicating that LA6 likely functions upstream of auxin signaling (Fig. 4a).

Shoot gravitropism relies heavily on auxin redistribution toward the lower side of the responding stem (Wang et al., 2022). To determine whether LA6 influences auxin distribution, we examined the expression of IAA20, an auxin‐responsive marker gene, in the shoot base following gravistimulation. Compared to wild‐type plants, la6 mutants exhibited significantly reduced IAA20 expression (Fig. 4b). We further examined IAA20 expression levels in the upper and lower sides of la6 and wild‐type seedling shoot bases upon gravistimulation. The results showed that the lower part of the la6 seedling shoot bases had a reduced expression level of IAA20 compared with that of wild type (Fig. 4c). We therefore tested whether LA6 influenced the auxin asymmetric distribution by monitoring 3H‐IAA (indole‐3‐acetic acid) movement in etiolated coleoptiles of la6 and the wild type. Compared with the wild type, the la6 lower side coleoptiles had much less accumulation of 3H, indicating that LAT was attenuated in la6 (Fig. 4d).
Auxin‐mediated asymmetric expression of WOX6 and WOX11 has been shown to regulate tiller angle in rice (Zhang et al., 2018). Consistent with the reduced auxin responsiveness in la6, both WOX6 and WOX11 exhibited lower expression in the lower portions of la6 shoot bases than wild type (Fig. 4e,f). Collectively, these results suggest that LA6 modulates rice tiller angle by facilitating auxin redistribution and the subsequent asymmetric expression of WOX6 and WOX11.

## LA6 and HSDA2D form a feedback loop to regulate rice tiller angle
As LA6 is annotated as a putative HSP70‐interacting protein (Rice Genome Annotation Project), we hypothesized a functional interaction with the heat shock transcription factor HSFA2D, which also regulates tiller angle through modulation of auxin asymmetry (Zhang et al., 2018). Yeast two‐hybrid and firefly LCI assays confirmed a physical interaction between LA6 and HSFA2D (Fig. 5a,b). To dissect their regulatory relationship, we analyzed reciprocal gene expression in hsfa2d and la6 mutants. While LA6 expression was significantly reduced in the hsfa2d mutant, HSFA2D levels remained unchanged in la6, suggesting that LA6 functions downstream of HSFA2D (Fig. S8a,b). Genetic evidence further supported this relationship. The hsfa2d CR‐la6‐3 double mutant exhibited a tiller angle comparable to la6, but significantly larger than hsfa2d alone (Fig. 5c,d). Moreover, overexpression of LA6 restored the wild‐type tiller angle in hsfa2d mutants (Fig. 5e,f), reinforcing that LA6 acts downstream of HSFA2D.

We hypothesized a feedback regulatory mechanism in which HSFA2D activates LA6 transcription, while the LA6 protein attenuates this activation through direct interaction with HSFA2D, thereby maintaining balanced LA6 protein levels. Supporting this model, transient expression assays demonstrated that HSFA2D could transcriptionally activate the pLA6 promoter, while co‐expression with LA6 significantly suppressed this activation (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, transgenic plants overexpressing LA6 showed enlarged tiller angles compared to wild‐type ZH11 (Figs 5h,i, S8c). Together, these results support a feedback loop model in which HSFA2D and LA6 cooperatively regulate the tiller angle through fine‐tuning of LA6 expression.

## LA6 functions upstream of LA1 in the regulation of rice tiller angle
To identify additional LA6‐interacting proteins, we performed a yeast two‐hybrid screen, which revealed LA1 as a candidate interactor. Their interaction was validated by both yeast two‐hybrid point‐to‐point assays (Fig. 6a) and LCI assays in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 6b), indicating a direct physical association. We next examined whether there is transcriptional regulation between LA6 and LA1. LA6 transcript levels remained unchanged in the la1 mutant, and conversely, LA1 expression was not affected in la6 or Ubi:LA6 plants (Fig. S8d–f), suggesting no reciprocal transcriptional regulation. To assess genetic interactions, we generated the la1 CR‐la6‐1 double mutant, which exhibited a tiller angle similar to la1 single mutants (Fig. 6c,d). In addition, overexpression of LA1 rescued the tiller angle phenotype of CR‐la6‐1 (Fig. 6e,f). These results indicate that LA1 functions downstream of LA6 in the regulatory pathway controlling rice tiller angle.

## Discussion
The tiller angle is a critical determinant of rice plant architecture, directly influencing light interception efficiency and, ultimately, grain yield. A deeper understanding of the genetic and molecular mechanisms governing tiller angle is essential for optimizing plant architecture and improving rice productivity. In this study, we identified LA6 as a novel regulator of tiller angle that interacts with LA1 and HSFA2D to modulate asymmetric auxin distribution. Our findings establish LA6 as an integral component of the genetic network that regulates tiller angle via shoot gravitropism and elucidate its functional relationship with HSFA2D and LA1 (Fig. 7).

LA6 acts in the same pathway as HSFA2D and LA1 to regulate tiller angle, likely by mediating asymmetric auxin distribution. Purple arrows indicate the lateral auxin transport, and the width of the arrows represents the intenity of lateral auxin transport. Black arrows and blunt ended arrows indicate the positive and negative regulation, respectively.

## LA6 exhibits pleiotropic roles in rice development
We isolated a naturally occurring rice mutant, la6, which displays a markedly increased tiller angle (Fig. 1a–c). Further phenotypic characterization revealed that la6 also exhibits reduced leaf angle, plant height, seed‐setting rate, grain length, and grain yield per plant, but increased grain width and 1000‐grain weight stemming from altered grain shape (Figs S1, S2). These diverse phenotypes suggest that LA6 plays a multifaceted role in rice development.
Several lines of evidence support the pleiotropic nature of LA6 function. First, RT‐qPCR analysis showed that LA6 is broadly expressed across various tissues, with particularly high expression in the shoot base where shoot bending occurs; the internode, which is related to plant height; and the young panicle, which has a connection with yield‐related traits (Fig. 3a). Second, subcellular localization assays revealed that LA6 localizes to the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus (Figs 3b, S6), implying that it may participate in diverse signaling and regulatory processes within different cellular compartments. Third, protein domain analysis identified two putative ARM/TPR‐like domains in LA6 (Fig. S9), which are known mediators of protein–protein interactions (Ge et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2022). Consistent with this, yeast two‐hybrid screening identified multiple LA6‐interacting proteins, which likely function across different developmental pathways. Further characterization of these interactors will help unravel the molecular basis of LA6's broad developmental roles.
Interestingly, SAV4, the Arabidopsis homolog of LA6, localized to the plasma membrane and nucleus, is involved in shade avoidance and ethylene‐induced root hair elongation (Ge et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2022), reinforcing the functional versatility of this protein family. Although there is no direct evidence linking SAV4 to branch angle regulation, its high sequence similarity to LA6 (Fig. S9) suggests a potential role in this process. Given the differences in developmental architecture between monocots and dicots, future phenotypic analysis of sav4 mutants will be required to determine whether this function is evolutionarily conserved.

## LA6 modulates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle via asymmetric auxin distribution
Shoot gravitropism largely contributes to rice tiller angle (Gao et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022). In this study, we found that the la6 mutant exhibited a markedly attenuated gravitropic response, implicating LA6 as a key regulator of shoot gravitropism and, consequently, tiller angle (Fig. 1d,e). Notably, LA6 expression was unresponsive to either gravitropic stimulation or exogenous auxin treatment, suggesting that LA6 functions upstream of auxin signaling. Supporting this, the expression of the auxin‐responsive gene IAA20 and LAT were both diminished in the shoot base of la6 seedlings relative to the wild type (Fig. 4c,d). In parallel, the auxin‐dependent asymmetric expression of WOX6/11 was disrupted in the la6 mutant (Fig. 4e,f), further reinforcing the role of LA6 in establishing auxin gradients.
These results identify LA6 as a novel component in the shoot gravitropism‐mediated genetic network that governs rice tiller angle. To understand how LA6 contributes to asymmetric auxin distribution, we examined its subcellular localization. GFP‐tagged LA6 localized to the plasma membrane in rice protoplasts and in N. benthamiana leaves (Figs 3b, S6), indicating a possible role in mediating directional auxin transport. Interestingly, SAV4, the Arabidopsis homolog of LA6, is polarly localized to the plasma membrane and is known to facilitate proper auxin gradient formation (Ge et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2022). This raises the possibility that LA6 contributes to polar auxin distribution through its plasma membrane localization. In future studies, we aim to generate transgenic rice lines expressing LA6‐GFP to directly test this hypothesis in planta.

## The HSFA2D–LA6–LA1 module coordinates shoot gravitropism and tiller angle formation
Building on our previous transcriptomic analysis that uncovered a core regulatory module of tiller angle formation through shoot gravitropism (Zhang et al., 2018), subsequent studies have expanded the genetic framework governing this trait (Wang et al., 2022, 2024; Cai et al., 2023). However, the molecular connections between key components in this network have remained poorly defined. For example, while HSFA2D and WOX6/11 act upstream and downstream of LA1, respectively, in mediating auxin redistribution (Zhang et al., 2018), the intermediate factors bridging HSFA2D and LA1 were previously unknown. In this study, we demonstrated that LA6 physically interacts with HSFA2D and LA1, thereby linking these two regulatory nodes. Genetic analysis further revealed that LA6 functions downstream of HSFA2D but upstream of LA1 to regulate tiller angle (Figs 5, 6). This positioning establishes LA6 as a critical intermediary within the gravitropism‐mediated regulatory network.
To explore the biological significance of the HSFA2D–LA6 interaction, we examined LA6 expression in the hsfa2d mutant, which showed reduced LA6 transcript levels. Coupled with physical interaction data, this suggests that HSFA2D directly activates LA6 transcription (Fig. S8a). Intriguingly, LA6 was also able to repress HSFA2D‐driven LA6 transcription in a transient expression assay (Fig. 5g), suggesting the existence of a negative feedback loop. This was further supported by phenotypic evidence: LA6‐overexpressing lines exhibited significantly larger tiller angles than wild type (Figs 5h,i, S8c). Together, these results indicate that the HSFA2D–LA6 module forms a feedback circuit that fine‐tunes LA6 expression and, by extension, tiller angle development. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanism through which the HSFA2D–LA6 interaction inhibits HSFA2D's transcriptional activity remains to be clarified.
Unlike the well‐characterized HSFA2D–LA6 relationship, the functional consequences of the LA6–LA1 interaction are largely unknown. Cell‐free degradation assays showed no difference in LA1 protein stability among wild type, CR‐la6, and Ubi:LA6 lines (Fig. S10), indicating that LA6 does not regulate LA1 through protein stability. Given that OsBRXL4 modulates LA1 nuclear localization (Li et al., 2019), we hypothesized that LA6 might influence the subcellular localization of LA1. However, co‐expression experiments in rice protoplasts revealed no change in the localization patterns of either LA6 or LA1 upon co‐transfection (Fig. S11).
Despite this, the known polar plasma membrane localization of Arabidopsis SAV4 suggests that LA6 may exhibit a similar polarity. If so, it could serve to spatially direct LA1 localization at the plasma membrane, contributing to asymmetric auxin distribution. Intriguingly, recent studies in Arabidopsis demonstrated that LA/LZY proteins accumulate on the lower side of columella cell membranes in response to gravity and translocate from amyloplasts to establish new polarity (Chen et al., 2023; Nishimura et al., 2023). Whether LA6 and LA1 display a similar dynamic polar distribution remains an important question for future investigation.
While LA6 interacts with both HSFA2D and LA1, these interactions appear to serve distinct functions. The HSFA2D–LA6 interaction suppresses HSFA2D's transcriptional activation of LA6 (Fig. 5g), while the LA6–LA1 interaction may be involved in spatially regulating LA1's localization. These divergent roles suggest that LA6 forms separate functional modules with HSFA2D and LA1 rather than a single complex. This notion is further supported by the distinct tiller angle phenotypes observed in each of the single mutants.
