# Mycobacterium chimaera choroidal granuloma: A case report and review of the literature

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeTo report a case of a patient that developed a choroidal granulomatous lesion secondary to disseminated Mycobacterium chimaera (MC) infection that was successfully treated with systemic and ocular steroids.ObservationsA 60-year-old male with a history of multiple cardiothoracic surgeries complicated by disseminated MC was referred to retina clinic. On initial examination, bilateral choroidal lesions were noted consistent with MC choroiditis. These lesions were treated with systemic antimicrobials. Five months later, the patient was found to have a new choroidal lesion in his right eye concerning for a choroidal granuloma. Optical coherence tomography showed an elevated choroidal lesion with subretinal fluid. The granulomatous choroidal lesion regressed after starting systemic and local steroids. There was no recurrence of the granulomatous choroidal lesion at the most recent follow up visit.Conclusions and ImportanceThis is an extremely rare report of a patient who received successful treatment for a MC related choroidal granulomatous lesion with accompanying multimodal imaging. The report suggests that similar lesions can be treated with systemic and ocular steroid therapy in combination with systemic antimicrobials.Keywords: Mycobacterium chimaera, Choroidal granuloma, Chorioditis

## Introduction
Mycobacterium chimaera (MC) infection is devastating and life threatening. Even with aggressive treatment, there are reported mortality rates of 46–63 % with disseminated MC infection.1 This rare infection has been associated with the contaminated heater cooler units used in cardiothoracic surgery.2 Disseminated infections have a predilection for the eyes, with choroiditis being a frequent ocular manifestation.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Other reported ocular manifestations include uveitis, optic disc swelling, and neovascularization.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8
Choroidal granulomas are an extremely rare manifestation of disseminated MC infection.5,8 Our case describes a patient that developed a choroidal granulomatous lesion secondary to disseminated MC infection that was successfully treated with systemic and ocular steroids. This case highlights successful treatment of a MC related choroidal granulomatous lesion and suggests that similar lesions can be treated with systemic and ocular steroid therapy in combination with systemic antimicrobials.

## Case report
A 60-year-old male was referred to our retina clinic for MC choroiditis. His past medical history was relevant for ascending aortic aneurysm repair complicated by a pulmonary artery graft abscess secondary to disseminated MC. Initial brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not show any central nervous system involvement (CNS). Ophthalmology was consulted to rule out ocular involvement. On initial ophthalmic examination, bilateral choroidal lesions were noted, consistent with MC choroiditis. These lesions were monitored and treated with systemic antimicrobials, including azithromycin, rifampin, ethambutol, clofazimine and IV amikacin, in coordination with infectious disease (ID).4
However, two months later a repeat brain MRI was obtained due to the patient reporting a new complaint of generalized headaches and it demonstrated progression of the MC infection to the CNS with numerous intraparenchymal nodules noted. Subsequent positron emission tomography (PET) of the pulmonary outflow tract demonstrated a recurrent abscess between the aortic root graft and pulmonary outflow tract. Similar findings had been seen previously and debrided at the time of his diagnosis of M. chimaera infection two months prior. Lumbar puncture was performed and CSF cultures were negative. Blood cultures had been positive at the time of diagnosis two months earlier but were negative on repeat at this time. The patient had developed an acute kidney injury (AKI) after 10 weeks on IV amikacin, so he was switched from systemic amikacin to linezolid by ID to continue additional antibiotic coverage and in an effort to improve antimicrobial drug penetration into the CNS.
At four months from initial presentation, the patient developed a new inferotemporal elevated choroidal lesion in his right eye consistent with a choroidal granuloma. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed an elevated choroidal lesion with subretinal fluid (Fig. 1A and B). Fluorescein angiography revealed leakage and staining of the choroidal lesion (Fig. 2). After discussion with ID and the patient about the role of amikacin in M. chimaera management9 as a first-line agent, the patient received a right eye intravitreal injection of amikacin in an attempt to increase drug concentration locally and slow down progression. He continued a combination antibiotic regimen of rifampin, azithromycin, ethambutol, linezolid and clofazimine during this time.

Laboratory workup for other causes of infectious choroidal granulomas including toxoplasmosis, CMV, TB, syphilis, HIV, EBV, aspergillus, histoplasma, cryptococcus, Coccidioides, Blastomyces, toxocara, cysticercosis, and bartonella was negative. The patient had no significant change after these injections. After further discussion with ID and the patient, it was decided to pursue topical prednisolone four times daily in the right eye and later oral dexamethasone with a prolonged taper. The patient was treated with steroids due to the possibility of granuloma formation from immune overactivation in response to infection. Regression of the choroidal granulomas was noted two weeks later. Six months later, the patient received a sub-Tenon's Kenalog (STK) injection in the right eye and discontinued prednisolone eye drops due to suspected enlargement in the choroid lesion and new brain lesions on MRI. The choroidal granuloma completely regressed one month following STK.
At five months post-regression, the patient had a visual acuity of 20/25 and normal intraocular pressure. No recurrence of the granulomatous choroidal lesion was seen at this time. (Fig. 1C and D). The patient's most recent MRI showed improvement in some of the CNS lesions with slight enlargement of others. The patient is currently being considered for compassionate use bacteriophage treatment per ID due to the persistence of fluid/phlegmon between the aorta and pulmonary artery, as he was deemed not to be a surgical candidate for repeat debridement.

## Discussion
Mycobacterium chimaera is a slow growing, non-tuberculous mycobacterium characterized by acid-fast, non-motile, and non-spore-forming coccobacilli.10 MC is classified as a member of the mycobacterial avium complex.10 Infection with MC has been associated with use of contaminated cardiopulmonary bypass machines during cardiothoracic surgeries.3,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Clinical symptoms of infection can take months to years to develop after exposure, with an average of 1.5 years of symptom onset after exposure.11
Patients can experience either a focal respiratory infection or disseminated infection. Disseminated infection is a concern in vulnerable populations such as those with compromised respiratory status or those who are immunosuppressed.3 The most common clinical symptoms include fever, malaise, unintentional weight loss, cough, and dyspnea.2 Diagnosis of MC infection remains challenging due to nonspecific symptoms, intermittent bacteremia, and normal echocardiography.2 Culture-based methods of detection remain the gold-standard for diagnosis.2
Recommended treatment for disseminated MC infection includes an antibiotic regimen with amikacin, a macrolide such as azithromycin or clarithromycin, rifamycin, ethambutol, and another oral agent such as moxifloxacin or clofazimine.2,4 Additionally, The American Heart Association recommends early surgery in patients with prosthetic valve endocarditis caused by highly resistant microorganisms, which includes MC.1 Our patient was not able to undergo surgical debridement or removal of prosthetic material due to high mortality risk.
Ocular involvement by MC following cardiothoracic surgery is well documented in the literature. In one study, clinical ocular findings, histopathologic analysis, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) from seven patients (fourteen eyes) with systemic MC after cardiac valvular surgery found all seven patients had choroidal lesions, with six out of the seven patients (12 eyes) having bilateral, multifocal choroiditis.7 Another study followed nine patients diagnosed with systemic MC infection and found five patients to have progressive ocular disease.8 Evaluation of progression of the morphologic characteristics of choroiditis in these patients with disseminated MC was done with enhanced depth imaging OCT (EDI-OCT), fluorescein angiography, and OCT angiography (OCTA).8 Active lesions correlated with hyporeflective choroidal areas on EDI-OCT and appeared as full-thickness well-defined round lesions leading to focal choroidal thickening.8 Conversely, inactive lesions on EDI-OCT had poorly defined borders and the internal reflectivity similar to the choroid.8 On fluorescein angiography (FA), two patients with progressive disease demonstrated mild optic disk leakage. Active lesions demonstrated hypofluoresence with staining in later frames, while inactive lesions demonstrated hyperfluorescence in early and late frames. Categorizing lesions as active or inactive via EDI-OCT can help guide treatment management and analyze disease progression. In this case, the new choroidal lesion the patient developed demonstrated leakage over the choroidal lesion and optic nerve with staining in later frames, consistent with active progressive disease. Thus, the decision was made to treat locally with intravitreal antimicrobials.
Large choroidal granulomas have not been commonly reported in literature with disseminated MC infection.5,8 In one case, a post-mortem histopathology of both eyes found prominent patchy lympho-histiocytic and granulomatous choroiditis with few giant cells and no necrosis.5 Although the RNA analysis of ocular tissue did not reveal MC in ocular choroidal tissue, disseminated MC infection was documented.5 OCT scans demonstrated visible hyporeflective, relatively homogenous round lesions in the choroid, with elevation of the overlying retinal layers, further confirming the presence of granulomas.5 The patient became anxious with the granuloma lesions expanding. Steroid use for Tuberculosis granulomas affecting the CNS and heart is well established.17 After discussion with the ID team on the potential role of steroids in treating the choroidal granuloma lesion while also receiving combination systemic antimicrobial agents, it was determined we would trial topical steroids drops. This patient showed improvement in his choroidal granuloma while on topical steroids, and later STK. The patient also received systemic steroids for systemic granulomas. It is critical to stress that steroid use in such M. chimera infection needs approval and close supervision of ID colleagues.
In one case of a living patient with a choroidal granuloma secondary to systemic MC, EDI-OCT, OCTA, and fluorescein angiography showed full-thickness choroidal granuloma with the presence of active choroidal neovascularization.8 The choroidal neovascularization was treated with intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy.8 After four weeks of treatment with anti-VEGF, OCTA showed reduced neovascularization.8 The patient was also placed on systemic antimicrobial treatment.8 The researchers did not report a decrease in size of the choroidal granuloma despite aggressive systemic antimicrobial treatment or intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy.8
In conclusion, choroidal granuloma formation secondary to disseminated MC infection is an extremely rare manifestation. We report a case of successful MC associated choroidal granuloma treatment with systemic, topical, and sub-Tenon's steroids in addition to systemic antimicrobials. Collaboration between ophthalmologic and ID specialists is crucial in treating such patients.
