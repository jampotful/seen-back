# Long-lived photoinduced polar states in metal halide perovskites

## Abstract
Ferroic polarization in hybrid perovskites is crucial for enhancing photovoltaic performance and developing potential electronic applications. Controlling ferroic polarization with an optical field enables probing of ferroic polarization without the unwanted interface ionic effects caused by electronic contacts. This study employs ultrafast near-infrared photoexcitation to control dynamic structural transitions in soft single crystalline hybrid Cu (II) halide perovskites, achieving a long-lived polar state (beyond 104 s) at room temperature. We probe reversible long-lived polar domains under near-infrared photoexcitation using in-situ second harmonic generation microscopy. Theoretical calculation informs the polar lattice microstrain likely induced by anisotropic structure deformation in octahedral copper halide under near-infrared photoexcitation. The reversible slow structure deformation is further confirmed by in-situ photo-induced X-ray diffraction measurement. This work provides a material platform for understanding, controlling, and probing polarization under photoexcitation. Our methodology enables the identification of previously undiscovered polar phases in ferroelectric halide perovskites.

## Introduction
Hybrid perovskites have garnered significant attention for their remarkable photovoltaic performance. Ferroelectricity in hybrid perovskites is particularly important as it is proposed to enhance photovoltaic efficiency by reducing charge carrier recombination1,2. Additionally, as traditional perovskite oxides are among the most crucial ferroelectric materials, the ferroelectric hybrid perovskites show promise for various applications such as spintronics, memory devices, communication, and quantum computing3–7. However, understanding ferroelectric polarization in traditional lead-based 3D metal halide perovskites (i.e., MAPbI3) remains challenging and controversial8,9. Crystal structure distortion is crucial for generating ferroelectric polarization in hybrid perovskites, thus developing hybrid perovskites with a large dynamic distortion is essential to explore the ferroelectricity10–13. Additionally, the probe of ferroelectric polarization is challenging in hybrid perovskites; the common probe method usually employs a high electric field which requires electrode contact and may be interfered by surface and interface ionic effects9,10,14. Instead, the optical control can avoid the unwanted ionic effect near the contact, and thus the crystal structural polarization can be controlled and enhanced under light. For example, a ferroelectric phase in paraelectric and centrosymmetric strontium titanate (SrTiO3) was accessed using a mid-infrared photoexcitation15. Although a second harmonic signal was initially absent at equilibrium, through the cumulative exposure to mid-infrared radiation, the photoinduced broken inversion symmetry phase became accessible.
However, photo-induced polarization is challenging to investigate in hybrid perovskites because of their ultrafast structural dynamics and limited structural polarization state at room temperature11,16,17. There is a gap in the precise optical control of a polar phase in organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites and their corresponding functional responses. The 2D hybrid materials contain soft and dynamic ionic bonds, resulting in enhanced deformability under external stimuli, providing the opportunity to enable the investigation. Such hybrid materials with general formula (L)2MX4 form sheets of inorganic metal halide layers arranged between layers of self-assembled organic cations. The tilt and distortion of the inorganic coordination geometry can be precisely controlled by the choice of organic cation, providing a versatile method to control the inversion symmetry18–20. The low-dimensional copper (Cu) halide perovskites have shown improper molecular ferroelectricity and ferromagnetism due to order–disorder phase transition mechanisms21–23. Additionally, Cu (II) halide perovskites exhibit Jahn–Teller (JT) distortion24,25, offering the possibility of unveiling distinctive functionalities when subjected to external stimuli.
Here, we extend this capability in the discovery of a long-lived polar phase in hybrid copper halide (HCH) perovskite solids. By employing molecular engineering in hybrid structures, we explore three representative coordination geometries of copper chloride building blocks: octahedral, square planar, and tetrahedral. Based on the in-situ time-dependent second harmonic generation (SHG) measurements, we observe that within the perovskite structure, Cu (II) halide building blocks with octahedral coordination exhibit distinct evidence of photoinduced symmetry breaking, while those with square planar and tetrahedral coordination maintain original symmetry. The reduced crystal symmetry in octahedral HCH leads to significant changes in the SHG response and generates microscale polar domains over time under near-infrared (NIR) photoexcitation. The power- and time-dependent SHG enhancement follows the stretched exponential function, which indicates the polar domains are induced by defect states and the lifetime of the polar states is as long as 5.7 × 104 s at room temperature. Via the first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations, the lattice deformation mechanism under photoexcitation is found to result from the octahedral packing mode, in which the distorted corner-sharing copper chloride units pack with alternating short-long directions. This photoinduced structural deformation induces localized in-plane micro-strain, leading directly to the emergence of a photoinduced polar state in copper halide perovskites. By conducting quantitative structural analysis through in situ photoinduced synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements, we observe that direct NIR photoexcitation induces a fast (seconds scale) structural expansion and a slow (hours scale) photoinduced anisotropic octahedra distortion, which agrees with our prediction from DFT. In summary, the soft ionic HCH crystal provides a valuable platform to monitor the structure evolution under light, and our distinctive methodology enables the identification of previously undisclosed polar phases in halide perovskites. This study is foundational in transitioning the typical photoresponsivity of hybrid halide perovskites towards a functional photoinduced structural polarity desired in future responsive memory devices and nonlinear photonic applications.

## Results and discussion

## Crystal structures of hybrid copper (II) halide crystals
HCHs can crystallize into a range of coordination environments such as square planar, tetrahedral, or octahedral structures through the incorporation of various organic ligands25–32. This is commonly due to the d9 Cu (II) compound, characterized by unequally occupied degenerate orbitals, exhibiting a significant JT distortion, resulting in structurally diverse configurations25,33,34. Here, we synthesized a series of HCH single crystals by varying the organic cations (Fig. 1a), each demonstrating one of three distinctive coordination geometries of copper halides: square planar, tetrahedral, and octahedral (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1). The crystal structures and lattice parameters were determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1, 2). We noted that HCHs with octahedral geometries tended to crystallize into two-dimensional structures, whereas the other two geometries formed one- or zero-dimensional structures. Among the three copper halide coordination geometries, the tetrahedral and octahedral structures are JT active, while the square planar is JT inactive and relatively rigid compared to the two others34,35. We evaluated the softness of the crystal structure by using the Baur distortion factor D24,36. ## Probing reversible long-lived photoinduced polar states
We then studied whether external optical excitation could induce alterations in the structure, consequently leading to changes in structure polarity. With a 1060 nm NIR excitation of 2.63 mJ cm−2, the optically induced electric field was calculated as 4.45 × 108 V\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cdot$$\end{document}⋅M−1 (Supplementary Note 2), which is comparable to the common electric field generated from applied bias (~108–109 V\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cdot$$\end{document}⋅M−1) in ferroelectric materials. SHG provides a sensitive and versatile means for rapid screening of materials that lack crystal inversion symmetry38,39. Consequently, we utilized SHG measurements as a probing tool to monitor symmetry changes. In contrast to Pb (II) halide perovskites, where strong two-photon photoluminescence can often interfere with symmetry-breaking-induced SHG signals40,41, the photoluminescence in d 9 Cu (II) halide perovskites is nearly negligible due to the forbidden transitions42. We examined the impact of direct excitation of d 9 Cu (II) transitions using the NIR laser as the d 9–d 9 transition energies in HCHs are located within the NIR range, which can be identical to the excitation wavelengths used for SHG measurements. To systematically investigate the influence of direct d 9 band excitation, we focused on three crystals in each of three types of HCH systems with octahedral, tetrahedral, and square planar geometries (Supplementary Fig. 3). For crystals 1–6 with square planar and tetrahedral Cu (II) chloride geometries, no changes in SHG intensity were observed over time under NIR excitation, consistently across all crystals measured (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 3). For octahedral-based HCHs, all three crystals possess a centrosymmetric crystal structure and exhibit negligible SHG signals initially as shown in Fig. 2c. The SHG signal was confirmed by the wavelength-dependent SHG peak and quadratic laser power-dependent SHG intensity (Supplementary Fig. 4). Intriguingly, all three octahedral crystals exhibited a continuous increase in SHG intensity at hours’ time scale. The PEA2CuCl4 crystal reaches saturation at approximately two hours (7.2 × 103 s) on average under continuous NIR pulse laser exposure of 2.63 mJ cm−2. The time to increase the SHG intensity to saturation is highly dependent on the laser fluence. (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, when the NIR illumination was discontinued, the SHG intensity from octahedral HCH crystal 7 (PEA2CuCl4) exhibited a gradual decrease with a relaxation time of ~5.7 × 104 s (Fig. 2e). And the light-induced SHG exhibited reversibility, which can be recovered after one cycle’s NIR illumination on and off (Supplementary Fig. 6). The changes in SHG intensity, whether increasing or decreasing, persisted upon toggling the NIR laser on or off. In addition, the PEA2CuCl4 crystal exhibited larger SHG intensities under NIR light wavelengths around 1.1 eV (1127 nm) with the same excitation time (Supplementary Fig. 7), which agrees well with the DFT calculated d 9–d 9 transition energy level (Fig. 1i). Then, to further study the role of d 9–d 9 transition, we conducted SHG measurements under continuous UV light excitation for comparison. Interestingly, no SHG signal was observed after UV light illumination (Supplementary Fig. 8), indicating that the direct excitation of d 9–d 9 transitions is central to the underlying mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 2). ## Long-lived photoinduced polar domain imaging
To understand whether the changes in SHG over time are caused by the formation of polar domains, we employed scanning SHG microscopy measurement on HCHs (Fig. 3a). This technique with polarization-resolved measurements has been widely utilized as a robust and direct method for probing ferroelectric polar domains, particularly in oxide perovskite ferroelectrics43,44. Initially, we measured an octahedral-coordinated PEA2CuCl4 single crystal (Supplementary Fig. 9), which exhibited a negligible SHG intensity across the entire mapping region. After NIR illumination, we observed an inhomogeneous increase in SHG intensity over the illumination area (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 10). The SHG intensity enhancement is highly dependent on the incident NIR laser fluence (Fig. 3c), as we observed in our routine SHG measurements shown in Fig. 2d. To further study the nature of polar domains, the SHG polarization plot was measured after continuous photoexcitation of 60 min (Fig. 3e). Subsequently, we scanned SHG images under varied polarized light excitations. SHG images were obtained from excitation polarization angles of 0°, 135°, 90°, and 45°, and the extraction between 135°/45° and 90°/0° generated the linear dichroism images (Fig. 3f, g). These images revealed the presence of in-plane micron-sized polarization domains across the region exposed to NIR excitation. The progressive development of in-plane linear dichroism domains over time provides additional confirmation of the polar domain in HCH crystals. In comparison, we measured the SHG intensity map of square planar coordinated crystal 1 ((4,4-DFPD)2CuCl4) which has a non-centrosymmetric structure and exhibits intense SHG intensity at room temperature. However, the square planar (4,4-DFPD)2CuCl4 crystal does not show any evidence of domains after NIR illumination even if the crystal shows intrinsically strong SHG (Supplementary Fig. 11). Therefore, the progressively formed in-plane polar domain further verifies the long-lived photoinduced polar phase in the octahedral HCH single crystal.

## Polar domain formation mechanism from simulation
To better understand the mechanism behind the long-lived polar phase we turn to DFT to simulate the effects of NIR excitation on the lattice. In octahedral HCH crystals, JT distortion results in the further splitting of the eg orbitals. The conduction band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) of crystal 7 (PEA2CuCl4) are derived from dx2−y2 and dz2, respectively (Supplementary Note 1). The dx2−y2 has two components with one along the out-of-plane a-direction and one along the in-plane bc direction (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 12). In the case of the octahedral compound under photoexcitation, the electron wave function is derived from the Cu dx2−y2 and Cl p orbitals. The four lobes of dx2−y2 have direct interaction with four Cl p orbitals along the Cu–Cl bonds in the equatorial bc plane. Stretching these four Cu–Cl bonds reduces the overlap of the wavefunctions, thus reducing the electronic repulsion between Cu dx2−y2 and Cl p and lowering the corresponding energy levels (Cu dx2−y2), which further stabilizes the excited electrons. Similarly, contraction of the Cu–Cl bonds along the axial direction would increase the interaction between Cu dz2 and Cl p and raise the corresponding energy levels (Cu dz2), which further stabilizes the excited holes (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 12b)50,51. Therefore, one would expect the elongation of Cu–Cl bonds in the dx2−y2 and contraction of the Cu–Cl bonds along the dz2 under photoexcitation. Based on the above theory, we conclude that the out-of-plane a-direction will be elongated due to the Cu–Cl bond expansion in the dx2−y2. For the in-plane lattice, the Cu–Cl bonds are from both long dz2 and short dx2−y2 orbitals that are perpendicular to each other. As a result, the long Cu–Cl bond at dz2 direction will be contracted while the short Cu–Cl bond at dx2−y2 direction will be elongated upon photoexcitation (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, we investigated the effects of micro-strain. For the in-plane octahedral packing pattern, the corner-sharing octahedral Cu-Cl units pack with alternating short-long directions as shown in Fig. 4e. The deformation of individual copper halide units under photoexcitation will simultaneously distort the lattice. Within the NIR laser spot size, there is an unavoidable intensity distribution, e.g., Gaussian (Supplementary Fig. 13)52, causing local sample regions to be exposed to different intensities and therefore undergo different degrees of deformations. Consequently, micro-strain will be formed within the exposed area as shown in Fig. 4f. The polarity will be significantly affected by the micro-strain, which explains the formation of photoinduced defects and, consequently, polar domains. The strain relaxation process is known to last for a long time period15, consistent with our observed prolonged recovery time of the SHG signal. In comparison, for square planar and tetrahedral copper halides (compounds 1 and 4, respectively), due to the orbital configurations of electrons and holes, the bond change of the excited state is negligible due to the cancellations of electron and hole (Fig. 4a and b, Supplementary Note 1). More importantly, in the square planar crystal 1 and tetrahedral crystal 4, each Cu–Cl building block is isolated. As a result, photoinduced lattice deformation becomes impossible. In summary, the NIR photoinduced anisotropic octahedral distortion in the soft copper halides triggers the formation of micro-strain, which subsequently results in the NIR-induced long-lived polar phase in octahedral HCHs.

## Structural evolution under the photoexcitation
To explore anisotropic octahedral distortion under photoexcitation, we performed in situ NIR photoinduced XRD using an 800 nm pulsed laser on a crystal 7 ((PEA)2CuCl4) single crystal with octahedral geometry (Fig. 5a). We focused on photoinduced changes in d-spacing at different lattice planes and crystal axes over time. After NIR light illumination, we observed a rapid 5.06% increase in the d-spacing from (0 0 4) plane, and this change reversibly relaxed back to the original spacing with 5.40% contraction, which occurred abruptly after NIR light exposure ceased (Fig. 5b). Similarly, the (2 2 18) plane showed reversible rapid expansion in both out-of-plane and in-plane directions, in which the lattice d-spacing changed ~2.09% in the out-of-plane direction and 1.45% in the in-plane direction. (Fig. 5c and d). Intriguingly, besides instant lattice changes right after applying NIR illumination, prolonged NIR exposure led to slow continuous structural changes, including slow out-of-plane expansion and in-plane contraction (Fig. 5b–d). This two-step lattice change of (PEA)2CuCl4 crystal under NIR illumination involves a large instant expansion followed by a gradual elongation, leading to the anisotropic octahedral lattice distortion as predicted by DFT calculation (Fig. 4c). In addition, the Bragg peak broadening under the NIR light excitation provides direct evidence of the formation of micro-strain (Supplementary Fig. 14). The NIR photoinduced two-step anisotropic octahedra lattice distortion also exhibits laser fluence dependence. As shown in Fig. 5c and d, at low laser fluence (0.9 mJ cm−2), the lattice d-spacing change is negligible, while at higher power density (2.4 mJ cm−2), both in-plane contraction and out-of-plane expansion become more pronounced. The enhanced anisotropic structure deformation under higher laser fluence will lead to a stronger larger SHG intensity, which is consistent with the laser fluence-dependent SHG intensity change (Figs. 2d and 3c), which further proves that the polar phase is induced by the structure deformation under NIR exposure. In contrast, employing visible photoexcitation with a 400 nm pulsed laser which is off the d 9–d 9 transition excitation range, the crystal only showed a rapid lattice expansion of ~0.58%, with no sustained structural changes over time (Supplementary Fig. 15). These phenomena suggest that the immediate lattice expansion within the second timescale could be related to the photothermal-induced overall lattice expansion53, while direct photoexcitation from the d 9–d 9 Cu (II) transition results in a gradual octahedral elongation on a longer timescale. Furthermore, temperature-dependent powder XRD of crystal 7 revealed lattice expansion at elevated temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 16), suggesting that the immediate lattice expansion is induced by laser heat. Additionally, SHG measurements at higher temperatures indicated that heating itself is unlikely to generate the polar phase but, instead, a more centrosymmetric structure (Supplementary Fig. 17)54,55. Ultimately, the in situ NIR photoinduced X-ray diffraction visualized the photoinduced metastable anisotropic octahedral distortion under NIR light and successfully investigated the evolution of slow octahedral elongation over time. The investigations of structure and SHG evolution at the same time scale allow the detailed exploration of the relationship between the structure change and polar states. In this study, we conducted a systematic exploration of the structure softness and crystal symmetry across copper halide perovskites with square planar, tetrahedral, and octahedral geometries, and highlighted the large distortion factor in the centrosymmetric octahedral HCH crystals. SHG probe measurements confirmed the loss of centrosymmetry under NIR light, and SHG imaging and linear dichroism measurements demonstrated the successful access of NIR light to the long-lived photoinduced polar phase in soft octahedral HCH crystals, which is attributed to the photoinduced defect states. Through DFT calculation, we revealed that NIR light excitation induces significant micro-strain in the soft octahedral HCH crystals, which support the formation of photoinduced stretched exponentially distributed defects and polar domains. The in situ NIR photoinduced X-ray diffraction visualized the anisotropic octahedral distortion evolution under NIR light, successfully investigated the crucial slow octahedral elongation, and further verified the important role of d 9–d 9 transition to the formation of the long-lived polar phase. This work not only sheds light on the discovery of photoinduced polar phases in the family of copper halide perovskites, a promising material platform for ferroelectrics and ferromagnetism. In addition, this work contributes to a design principle of optical control of inversion symmetry breaking which is important to a wide range of materials physics from optoelectronics to magnetism and superconductivity.

## Methods
Crystal 1 (4,4-DFPD)2CuCl4: Adding 4,4-difluoropiperidinium hydrochloride (315.18 mg, 2 mmol) and Cu (II) chloride dihydrate (170.4 mg, 1 mmol) to a 20 mL vial, then dissolve the chemicals by adding 1 ml methanol and 1.5 mL hydrochloric acid under 90 °C with an oil bath, then cooling down the fully dissolved solution to room temperature. Then the solution mixture was set to slow evaporation until the green crystals formed.
Crystal 2 (AMPM)2CuCl4: 2-Amino-4-methylpyrimidine and copper(II) chloride dihydrate were dissolved in 1 M HCl aqueous solution in a molar ratio of 2:1. Crystals were obtained by slow evaporation at room temperature over several days31.
Crystal 3 (N-MPEA)2CuCl4: N-Methyl-2-phenylethylamine and copper(II) chloride dihydrate were dissolved in acetone in a molar ratio of 2:1. Crystals were obtained by slow evaporation at room temperature over several days27.
Crystal 4 (R-NEA)2CuCl4: Add R-1-(1naphthyl)ethylamine (6.2 mmol, 1 mL) in methanol (3 mL) The 0.5 mL hydrochloric acid was added dropwise under stirring. The fully dissolved solution was kept on a hot plate at 90 °C and then pumped under vacuum overnight to evaporate the solvent. Then white R-NEACl salt precipitates were collected. The R-NEACl salt (411.38 mg, 2 mmol) and copper (II) chloride dihydrate (170.4 mg, 1 mmol) were added to 3 mL of methanol under 75 °C in an oil bath. The solution was set to slow cooling down at 4 °C h–1 to 25 °C, the orange crystal was obtained after the cooling process.
Crystal 5 (S-TFPD)2CuCl4: Adding (S)-2-Trifluoromethylpiperidine hydrochloride (315.18 mg, 2 mmol) and Cu (II) chloride dihydrate (170.4 mg, 1 mmol) to a 20 mL vial, then dissolve the chemicals by adding 1 mL methanol and 1.5 mL hydrochloric acid under 90 °C with an oil bath, then cooling down the fully dissolved solution to room temperature. Then the solution mixture was set to slow evaporation until the yellow crystals formed.
Crystal 6 (R-MBA)2CuCl4:): Stoichiometric amounts of copper (II) chloride (170.4 mg, 1 mmol) and R-methylbenzylamine (254 μL, 2 mmol) were first dissolved in a mixture of 3 mL HCl in a sealed vial at 80 °C in an oil bath for about 1 hour. Slow cooling down 2 C h−1, Then the solution was slowly evaporated at 40 °C30.
Crystal 7 PEA2CuCl4: Phenethylammonium chloride (473.9 mg) and copper (II) chloride dihydrate (256.5 mg) were dissolved in 9 mL of methanol at 75 °C in an oil bath. The solution was then slowly cooled to 25 °C at a rate of 4 °C per hour to get the yellow crystal flake29.
Crystal 8 (MA)2CuCl4: Stoichiometric amounts of copper (II) chloride and methylammonium chloride were dissolved in methanol at 75 °C in an oil bath. The solution was then slowly cooled to 25 °C at a rate of 4 °C per hour to get the yellow crystal flake32.
Crystal 9 (DPA)2CuCl4: Adding 1,3-Diaminopropane dihydrochloride (294.10 mg, 2 mmol) and Cu (II) chloride dihydrate (170.4 mg, 1 mmol) to a 20 mL vial, then dissolve the chemicals by adding 1 ml methanol and 1.5 mL hydrochloric acid under 90 °C with an oil bath, then cooling down the fully dissolved solution to room temperature. Then the solution mixture was set to slow evaporation until the green crystals formed.

## Single crystal XRD
The crystals were precisely positioned on the goniometer of a Rigaku Oxford Diffraction Synergy-S diffractometer equipped with a HyPix6000HE detector and operating with Mo Kα radiation. The data collection routine, unit cell refinement, and data processing were carried out with the program CrysAlisPro. The structure was solved using SHELXT56 and refined using SHELXL via Olex257.

## Time and power-dependent SHG measurement
The laser source for NIR excitation was generated from the Astrella-F-1K one-box femtosecond amplifier with an optical parametric amplifier system from Coherent Company. The laser is ~100 fs pulses with a 78 MHz repetition rate, laser power is between 4.5 and 10 mW, and the laser spot size is about 0.0038 cm2. The SHG signal was monitored by the HRS-300 spectrometer with a PyLoN camera system from Princeton Instruments used to detect the transmitted laser intensity. The SHG intensity is measured from the single crystal sample, the single crystal thickness and roughness will slightly affect signal collection.

## Excitation wavelength-dependent SHG measurement
For the second harmonic generation (SHG) observations presented in Figs S5 and S9, a Coherent Chameleon 80 MHz Ti:Sapphire ultrafast laser system was used as the excitation source between 1000 and 1040 nm. The laser power was swept between 2 and 18 mW. Several long-pass filters (720, 800, 900 LP) were employed on the incident excitation path to ensure a clean excitation source. The angle of incidence for laser excitation was kept at 30° to the sample normal and resulting SHG emission was collected via a Horiba IHR-550 spectrometer, set to its 150-grating density and 0.25 mm slit size with an appropriate short pass filter to eliminate the laser source. For the detection of SHG emission, a LN2 CCD camera was employed at 120 s of exposure time. All SHG emissions were collected at room temperature.

## SHG scanning microscopy measurement
SHG measurements were carried out in a normal-incidence reflection geometry. A Ti:Sapphire oscillator was used for light excitation with ~100 fs pulses, a center wavelength of 900 nm, a 78 MHz repetition rate, and an average power of <1 mW. To control the polarization of the incoming light, we used a Glan–Thompson polarizer and subsequently sent the light through a half-wave plate. The polarized light was then sent through a short-pass dichroic mirror and focused on the sample using a ×60 objective lens (numerical aperture NA = 0.7). The back-reflected SHG signal was sent through a short-pass filter and detected using a spectrometer (SpectraPro 500i, Princeton Instruments) with a charge-coupled device camera (Peltier-cooled CCD, ProEM+:1600 eXcelon3, Princeton Instruments). A linear polarizer on the back-end optics was used to select emitted light polarization for detection. Diffraction-limited confocal scanning microscopy was used to create SHG intensity maps. A commercial Thorlabs polarimeter (PAX1000IR1) was used at the sample location to confirm the incoming light polarization and power incident on the sample, as well as the light polarization entering the detector.

## Photoinduced X-ray diffraction
In situ, time-resolved XRD experiments were measured by synchrotron radiation at Advanced Photon Source with beamline 7-ID-C, Argonne National Laboratory. The 800 nm pump laser pulses were generated from the Ti: Sapphire regeneratively amplified laser with 1 kHz repetition rate, at normal incidence to the crystal surface. The laser size is about 517 µm at the horizontal direction and 639 µm at the vertical direction. The X-ray probe beam wavelength was 1.06 Å. The laser pump and X-ray probe overlapped onto the crystal surface.

## NIR light-assisted cross-polarized optical microscopy
The microscope was the Horiba Xplora plus system confocal Raman microscope. The NIR laser was from the harmonic generator (Ultrafast Systems LLC, third harmonic) equipped with a Pharos laser (Light Conversion, 1 kHz, 1025 nm, 290 fs).

## DFT calculation
Density functional theory calculations were performed using the VASP code58,59 with projector augmented-wave potentials60. A kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV and Γ-centered k meshes of 2 × 2 × 1 (octahedral), 1 × 4 × 4 (square planar), 2 × 4 × 2 (tetrahedral) were employed. The exchange-correlation interactions were treated with the generalized gradient approximation of the PBE parametrization61. Grimme’s D3 correction was also included to deal with the van der Waals interactions62. Geometry relaxations were performed with a force tolerance of 0.01 eV Å−1. Both ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic configurations for each structure were relaxed, and it was found that the former are more stable.
As shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, with simple PBE functional, the d 9−d 9 transitions of the minority spin can be observed. However, the band gap is usually underestimated by PBE functional due to the self-interaction error (SIE).
To compare the electronic structures of different levels of theory, we adopted DFT + U63 with onsite U of 7.28 eV for Cu 3d orbitals, hybrid functional with the HSE0664 parametrization, and many-body perturbation theory within GW approximation65. Single-shot GW calculations with a response function cutoff of 333 eV, 2520 bands, and 12 frequency points in the cubic-scaling scheme were performed. From the DOS calculations, we observed that either DFT + U or HSE06 pushed the frontier Cu d orbitals deep into the valence bands, breaking the d 9–d 9 transitions. Instead, the GW calculations not only correct the band gap but also preserve the d 9–d 9 transitions, thus providing accurate electronic structures of copper halide compounds.
