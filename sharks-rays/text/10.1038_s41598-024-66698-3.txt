Real time eye dose reduction in fluoroscopy with auditory and visual feedback dosimeter through swine model experiments.

# Real time eye dose reduction in fluoroscopy with auditory and visual feedback dosimeter through swine model experiments

## Abstract
This paper proposes measurement and reduction of eye dose in real time for the physician and the assistant performing fluoroscopy guided arterial puncture. Eye dose rates were measured for 30 fluoroscopy-guided punctures of bilateral femoral arteries in pigs. Fifteen fluoroscopy-guided punctures were performed using real time radiation dosimeter without auditory and visual feedback and other fifteen punctures were done using real time radiation dosimeter with visual and auditory feedback worn on forehead by an interventional cardiologist having experience of more than 10 years. The mean radiation dose rate for eyes of physician during arterial puncturing with real time radiation dosimeter with auditory feedback was 0.07 mSv/h (n = 15) whereas it was 0.18 mSv/h (n = 15) without visual and auditory feedback. The percentage of reduction with the device was 61% for eyes. In case of assistant the reduction was 33% for eyes (n = 15). The real time visual and auditory feedback dosimeter has reduced the eye dose rate of the physician and assistant and also helped him staying away from the X-ray source. Real time radiation dosimeters can be an effective tool to measure and reduce the dose to the eyes. The radiation eye dose rate for physician and assistant was significantly reduced by using real time radiation dosimeter with visual and auditory feedback. The real time radiation dosimeter not only helps in measuring but also help in minimizing the radiation dose rate for the physician and assistant in real time.

## Introduction
Radiation can have undesirable effects on the eye, including a gradual loss of vision. Since the human eye lens is the most radiosensitive and delicate organ, fluoroscopy-guided interventional methods for measuring the eye dose necessitate extra safety precautions1–3. Fluoroscopy guided interventions are used for the precise targeting of blood vessel. The major advantage lies in their potential to improve the efficacy of procedures by reducing the time to place a needle into a lesion which in turn resulted in shorter hospital stays, small scars and faster patient recovery. Fluoroscopy is used in several interventional procedures because it produces clearer images than ultrasonography and takes less time overall to insert a needle4–8.
However, techniques like fluoroscopy and X-rays share the issue of radiation exposure, which has a negative impact on the patient's, staff's, and doctor's health. Because of their career-long vocation, medical professionals and employees are more exposed to radiation than patients. Ionizing radiation exposure to patients and intervention staff is a significant drawback of using fluoroscopy. In these procedures physicians are quite near to the source of scattered radiation. The major concern with the use of fluoroscopy guided interventions is the high radiation exposure. Both the physician and medical staff are at greater risk of radiation exposure and as a result dose limiting measures are required9,10.
Fluoroscopy guided operations carry a higher risk of ionizing radiation exposure than other radiological modalities. Time-consuming procedures, close proximity of the operator to the source, and radiation scatter from patients are a few factors that could enhance the operator's occupational radiation eye dosage11,12. In the study by Wilson-Stewart et al., a significant concern is raised regarding the eye dose radiation exposure faced by cardiologists and interventional radiology staff during complex transcatheter procedures, such as transaortic valve implantations (TAVI) and endovascular aneurysm repairs (EVAR). These procedures, often necessitating the use of high-dose digital subtraction angiography (DSA), are identified as leading contributors to elevated levels of eye dose radiation for both patients and healthcare professionals. Particularly, the research underscores that TAVI and EVAR are associated with the highest eye dose radiation, highlighting a crucial need for cardiologists in interventional radiology to critically evaluate the use of DSA techniques to mitigate radiation exposure risks to the eyes13.
One of the body's most radiosensitive tissues is the lens of the eye. Cataracts, or lens opacity, which can be brought on by radiation exposure can impair vision and even cause blindness. Nuclear and non-nuclear regulatory authorities from all over the globe have set comparable dosage limits for the eye's lens for occupational exposure under planned exposure conditions in order to prevent radiation-induced cataracts. Radiation-induced damage to the eye’s lens can cause opacification or clouding years after exposure. The effect is heavily depending on the radiation exposure and how the lens was exposed. An annual effective dose limit of 20 mSv is set for occupational exposure, with no single year exceeding the 50 mSv limit, on average over a 5-year period14,15. Ikezawa et al. highlighted the significant risk of radiation exposure to medical staff's eyes during Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) procedures, pointing out the potential for cataract formation and the importance of lead glasses for eye protection16.
Typically, assessing eye lens dose involves the utilization of a thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD) affixed to the head or collar. While these TLDs serve as effective tools for measuring eye dose, they do come with a drawback: the inability to provide real-time measurements. This limitation underscores the significance of real-time dosimeters, which have the potential to offer immediate and dynamic feedback. By relying on the insights furnished by real-time dosimeters, both medical practitioners and staff members can proactively distance themselves from radiation sources, enhancing safety protocols and minimizing exposure risks17–25.
Masaki et al. evaluated the annual eye-lens radiation doses among nuclear medicine (NM) department staff, revealing that the doses were below the International Commission on Radiological Protection's 20 mSv threshold. However, the study highlighted the necessity for protective measures, especially for PET nurses, where a significant correlation was found between procedure frequency, radiopharmaceutical quantities, and lens doses. Differences between lens and personal dosimeter readings suggest inaccuracies in current dosimetry methods for assessing eye-lens exposure in specific roles26.
In the environment of the operating room (OR) ensuring the safety of physicians, from radiation exposure has become a priority. Various strategies are employed to effectively reduce risks. The use of gear including lead aprons, vests, sleeves and auxiliary supplements plays a crucial role in significantly minimizing radiation exposure27–29. Equally important is the positioning of staff within the OR taking into account factors such as distance from the radiation source and alignment with radiation protection shields30–32. By integrating layers of defense like radiation protection walls we further enhance our ability to safeguard against radiation doses. It is worth noting that there is a connection between proficiency and radiation exposure as more experienced surgeons tend to utilize fluoroscopy less frequently in terms of time spent image count and dose administered. Prior to surgery implementing radiation safety measures such as briefings and time outs significantly contribute to reducing radiation doses. In today’s era of real time technology advancements adopting radiation dosimetry systems that provide feedback on dosage levels proves to be a tool, for monitoring and controlling radiation exposure levels closely33–36. Active and passive robotic systems have been designed to minimize radiation exposure to physicians. With active systems, physicians use a master–slave technique, directing the robot from a distance. Conversely, with passive systems, physicians operate the robot from a nearby safe location, using the passive robot as an intermediary during procedures37–39.
Since both underestimating and overestimation of the lens dose can happen, adequate monitoring is crucial for the effective management of the eye dose. Therefore, it is usually advised to wear dosimeters during fluoroscopy either on the chest or abdomen inside the protective apron and the other usually on the neck outside the protective apron40–42. In both the cases the estimation would not be accurate because the placement is away from the eyes. In various studies eye dosimeters based on TLD technique have been also used. However, these dosimeters have two main drawbacks: one they are passive and the second one is the location because they cannot be directly placed just above the eyes. They are either placed on the sideways inside the glasses or outside the glasses and in both cases the readings would be different43,44.

## Methods
The study received approval from the Animal Ethical Committee at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, under the approval code BA1708-230/075-01. Informed consent was obtained from all human participants. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations by an interventional cardiologist and one assistant. All experimental protocols were approved by Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. All procedures conducted were in alignment with the guidelines provided by the Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals45 and in compliance with ARRIVE guidelines.

## Animal preparation
Fifteen male crossbred pigs (weight range 17–35 kg) were administered aspirin (300 mg) and clopidogrel (300 mg) 1 day prior to the experiment. Pretreatment included intramuscular injection of atropine sulfate (0.05 mg/kg), followed by anesthesia induction using Zoletil (5 mg/kg) and Xylazine (4.4 mg/kg). The pigs were intubated, ventilated with room air, and maintained under isoflurane anesthesia on the experimental day. Under ultrasound guidance, the right carotid artery was punctured, and a 6-Fr. sheath was inserted. Prior to femoral artery angiography, a dose of 5000 U heparin was administered intravenously. Using the Seldinger technique, both femoral arteries were punctured and cannulated with a diagnostic catheter (Judkins right 4).

## Measurement of radiation dose
The radiation assessment was measured using the Ray 3000 X-ray (Kedian, Jining, China) radiation measuring dosimeter employs a compensated Geiger–Müller (GM) counting tube as its detection method, designed to accurately measure radiation doses across a broad spectrum by mitigating the tube's inherent energy-dependent response. It is equipped with audible alarm systems and visual display as shown in Fig. 1. This dosimeter has a radiation intensity indicator, offering a measuring range that spans from 0.01 to 99.99 millisieverts per hour (mSv/h). The dosimeter was calibrated by exposing them to known radiation sources under controlled conditions to align their readings with established standards. This process, conducted by certified calibration laboratory, involves comparing the dosimeter's measurements against reference values and adjusting the device's response accordingly.
It also has a distinct set of indicators for varying radiation levels. The “N” symbolizes normal conditions, indicating a dose rate below 0.5 µSv/h. The “L” designation represents low radiation, encompassing the range between 0.5 and less than 50 µSv/h. Meanwhile, “H” signifies high radiation levels, spanning from 50 to less than 500 µSv/h. In instances of extremely elevated radiation, denoted by “!”, the dosimeter signals dangerous levels between 500 and less than 1000 µSv/h. The dose rates for a total of 30 fluoroscopy-guided bilateral femoral artery punctures in 15 pigs, both with and without auditory feedback were measured. As an X-ray imaging instrument, the Philips BV Pulsera mobile fluoroscopy system (Philips Medical Systems, Bothell, USA) was employed. A dosimeter that was fastened to the doctor's forehead was used to measure the radiation dose rates. The distance of the dosimeter to the eye lenses is 1.5 cm. All dosage rates in this investigation were expressed in mSv/h.

## Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with special attention given to their adherence to a normal or nearly normal distribution, as confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (p = 0.918). Consequently, the assessment of mean differences between the two groups was conducted through the Student t-test. A two-tailed probability value of < 0.05 was deemed as indicative of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were carried out using R software (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

## Results
The experiment aimed to assess the impact of real-time radiation dosimeters with auditory feedback on radiation exposure during arterial puncturing procedures for physicians and their assistants. The results showed significant reductions in radiation dose rates when the dosimeters with visual and auditory feedback were utilized. The mean radiation dose rate for eyes in case of physician decreased from 0.18 to 0.07 mSv/h, resulting in a notable 61% reduction. Similarly, assistant standing next to physician also experienced a reduction from 0.09 to 0.06 mSv/h, corresponding to a 33% decrease as shown in Table. 1. With auditory feedback, the maximum distance maintained by surgeons from the radiation source was observed to be 200 cm, while the minimum distance was reduced to 100 cm. Without auditory feedback, the maximum distance decreased to 150 cm, and the minimum distance approached closer, at 50 cm. ## Discussion
Recent insights from the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) have shed light on the vulnerability of ocular tissues to radiation exposure. The ICRP's findings suggest that adverse tissue reactions, notably cataracts, might manifest at radiation doses lower than previously studied. This revelation underscores the significance of accurate and real-time measurements of radiation eye doses, particularly in medical procedures like fluoroscopy-guided arterial punctures. By considering these updated guidelines, proactive measures to minimize eye dose rates through tools like real-time dosimeters with auditory feedback gain new importance, contributing to the protection and well-being of physicians engaged in interventional practices46.
This timely assessment of radiation exposure, facilitated by the utilization of real-time radiation dosimeters, empowers the operating room (OR) staff with the knowledge needed to proactively manage and mitigate their own radiation exposure levels. Armed with instant feedback on radiation dose rates, the physician and staff can readily adapt their behaviors and positions during the intervention to optimize their safety. This real-time awareness enables them to make informed decisions on distancing themselves from radiation sources and adopting protective measures, ensuring a safer environment for both the medical team and the patients undergoing procedures47.
The significance of adequate training for physicians in reducing radiation exposure during medical procedures is explored. The outcomes revealed that the initial training itself did not lead to substantial reductions in radiation doses. This observation suggests that the pivotal factor for radiation dose reduction could be the continuous visual and auditory cues provided by the current dosimeter, rather than solely relying on training or a heightened awareness of wearing a real-time dosimeter. This highlights the role of real-time monitoring tools in maintaining a consistent awareness of radiation exposure levels and fostering effective dose management practices among medical professionals.
The incorporation of personal dosimeters offers physicians and their assistants a tangible advantage in radiation safety. By promptly triggering an alarm when radiation levels reach critical thresholds, these devices empower medical personnel to respond swiftly by stepping away from the radiation source. While an initial sense of discomfort was noted due to the repetitive alarm beeping, an interesting evolution in behavior was observed with prolonged usage. Over time, physicians adapted to this auditory cue, forming a habit of promptly relocating themselves away from the source whenever the dosimeter's alarm was activated. This shift in behavior underlines the dosimeter's role not only in providing immediate alerts but also in fostering lasting radiation-aware practices among medical professionals. The reduction in radiation exposure is just one of the advantages gained from the use of auditory feedback dosimeters during fluoroscopy-guided punctures. An additional prominent observation was that the presence of the dosimeter's auditory feedback altered the behavior of physicians. In instances where the dosimeter was absent, physicians forget to maintain a distance. However, with the introduction of the auditory feedback dosimeter, physicians became more acutely aware of their radiation exposure levels, leading to heightened consciousness and potentially safer work practices. This increased awareness could be attributed to the real-time information provided by the dosimeter, which actively informed physicians about their radiation exposure throughout the procedure. This behavioral shift further underscores the positive impact of auditory feedback dosimeters in fostering a safety-conscious environment and contributing to the overall improvement of procedural practices. Our study observed that physicians, when alerted by the dosimeter, often immediately halt radiation by foot pedal. This reflexive cessation of radiation not only minimizes unnecessary exposure to the patient but also significantly reduces the radiation dose to the medical staff, aligning with the universally recognized ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle.
Conventional eye dosimeters have traditionally been placed on either the left or right side of the eye, making the dose readings susceptible to variations based on the positioning of both the radiation source and the physician. In contrast, our dosimeter, situated above the eyelids supported by nasal bone, offers a distinct advantage by consistently reflecting accurate readings when the physician is facing the radiation source. This positioning ensures that the dosimeter captures the true extent of radiation exposure, mitigating the influence of source position. This aligns well with the International Atomic Energy Agency's (IAEA) recommendation, advocating for dosimeters to be positioned as closely as possible to the eyes to provide a true representation of the actual radiation dose experienced.
Acknowledging the small sample size as a limitation, this pilot study was intentionally designed to assess the efficacy of a radiation dosimeter device in reducing eye lens dose during interventional radiology, providing foundational data to inform future, larger-scale research and refine safety protocols.

## Conclusion
In this study, we have measured and reduced eye dose rates of both the physician and the assistant. The use of real time dosimeter with visual and auditory feedback has resulted in the decrease of eye mean dose rates by 61% for physician and 33% for assistant respectively. Through the immediate feedback provided by triggering an alarm as radiation levels reach critical points, the physician and their team are empowered to proactively adjust their actions and positions during the procedure, prioritizing their safety. This awareness enables them to make informed choices about maintaining a safe distance from radiation sources and applying appropriate protective measures. Ultimately, this approach establishes a secure environment that benefits both the physician and the medical staff involved.
