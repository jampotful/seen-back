# Origin of fast charging in solid-state batteries revealed by Cryo-transmission X-ray microscopy

## Abstract
SignificanceThe high-impedance non-Faraday electric field restricts rapid ion transfer at the interface, resulting in solid-state batteries (SSBs) exhibiting limited C rates (<1 C). Most research on the non-Faradaic electric field of SSBs faces challenges of relaxation-induced ion distribution self-equilibration and perspective limitations. Here, we developed an operando Cryo-transmission X-ray microscopy (Cryo-TXM) technique that facilitates the segmented dissection of the non-Faradaic electric field generation and elimination processes, elucidating that the irreversible local electric field is the main cause of high polarization during late charging. This approach advances the capture and resolution of transient opacity signals inside SSBs and provides theoretical guidance for the design of fast-charging SSBs.Keywords: solid-state batteries, fast-charge, cryo-transmission X-ray microscopy, intermittent reverse potential
The widespread application of electrified equipment in transportation, industrial production, and other fields has heightened concerns about charging speed, aiming to reduce downtime and improve operational efficiency (1–4). High charging power of liquid lithium-ion batteries can lead to an increase in device temperature, posing a risk of thermal runaway (5–7). Intrinsic thermally stable sulfide solid electrolytes (SSE) demonstrate ion conductivity at room temperature comparable to that of liquid electrolytes (8–11). Therefore, SSBs composed of SSE possess inherent potential for safe, high-power operation (12–14). However, the high-impedance non-Faradaic electric field generated by the lithium depletion layer restricts rapid ion transport at the interface, resulting in SSBs typically exhibiting limited C-rates (<1 C) (15, 16). Elucidating the impact of non-Faradaic electric fields on ion transport in active particles (AMs) is crucial for enhancing SSB fast-charging performance (17, 18).
Most studies on fast charging of SSBs are limited to the interface under complete discharge, often disregarding the dynamics changes of the non-Faraday electric field during charging (19–21). Theoretical studies propose that at high charging current densities, the lithium depletion layer near the interface in SSEs may thicken, resulting in higher interface impedances during the later stages of charging (22). However, theoretical simulations fall short in replicating the large-scale ion transport behavior during the actual charging process (23, 24). Although electron microscopy-based studies filled the gap in theoretical studies by directly observing the Li+ distribution (25–30), the relaxation of the non-Faraday electric field during the test leads to Li+ rearrangement, which significantly deviates from the actual Li+ distribution during charging. In addition, such studies are limited to the microscale, lacking a comprehensive understanding of the particle level. Therefore, the relationship between the non-Faradaic electric field and fast-charging degradation in practical SSBs remains unclear.
The synchrotron-based X-ray imaging technique is a vital tool for tracking lithium-ion transport at the single particle scale due to its high spatial and energy resolution (31–36). However, AMs experience significant relaxation within a single test period following fast charging (37, 38). Capturing the generation and elimination processes of non-Faradaic electric field on a single particle via TXM is constrained by exposure time and X-ray energy range. As a result, only the average information during the relaxation period can be collected, leading to a loss of transient signals (39). Shortening the exposure time reduces the degree of relaxation but results in a significant drop in imaging quality. The key to solving this problem lies in slowing down the dissipation of non-Faradaic electric fields to prolong the relaxation time.
Here, we developed a nondestructive in situ Cryo-transmission X-ray microscopy (Cryo-TXM) characterization technique. This technology exploits the suppression of ion diffusion by cryogenic freezing, improving the resolution limit of microscopic imaging and allowing for the fragmented dissection of the complete pathway of non-Faraday electric field dissipation. Results indicate that during fast charging, non-Faradaic Li+ migration at the interface between AMs and SSE enhances the local electric fields, significantly increasing the resistance for Li+ to cross the interface and deteriorating the surface lattice of the AMs. By intermittently applying a reverse electric field during fast charging to promptly eliminate the non-Faradaic electric field at the cathode interface, the fast-charging capacity and cycle stability of SSBs are significantly improved. This work provides a unique characterization approach for understanding the rapid ion transport behavior in SSBs and proposes universal charging protocol design principles to enhance fast-charging performance.

## Conversion of Motion to Static by Cryo-TXM
As shown in Fig. 1A, at the initial open circuit state, due to the chemical potential difference between the AM and the SSE, lithium ions spontaneously migrate from the SSE side to the AM side, thereby forming a high-impedance non-Faraday electric field at the interface (40). It should be noted that due to the existence of potential differences, the non-Faraday electric field will persist throughout the charging and discharging process. Additionally, the potential difference between the by-products formed by the solid electrolyte and the cathode material and the cathode material itself is still significant, so the non-Faraday electric field will not disappear due to interfacial side reactions (41–44). During charging, the accumulation of Li+ is impeded by the non-Faradaic electric field and the continuous depletion of lithium on the SSE side, rapidly increasing the interfacial transfer resistance. In the later stages of charging, the accumulated Li+ on the AM side begins to transfer back to the SSE to compensate for the previously formed lithium vacancies. Conversely, during fast charging, the early cutoff voltage (Fig. 1B) is triggered by the continuously increasing polarization caused by the non-Faradaic field. Compared to a termination voltage of 4.0 V, SSBs with a stronger non-Faradaic electric field at a 4.3 V termination voltage exhibit a higher reversible capacity, yet the capacity retention rate rapidly declines (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The continuous high current input during fast-charging forces rapid changes in the non-Faradaic electric field, complicating the analysis of ion diffusion mechanisms associated with it. The self-balancing process of the non-Faradaic electric field in the open-circuit state can map the ion diffusion path, which is particularly evident in the relaxation stage after charging (45, 46). X-ray diffraction shows that with the increase in relaxation time after rapid charging to 4.3 V, the diffraction peak of NCM811 near 19° gradually shifts to lower 2θ values (phase transition from H3 to H2) (Fig. 1D) (47). This confirms the reinsertion of lithium ions from the non-Faradaic electric field into NCM811, while indicating that utilizing the relaxation process to analyze ion diffusion paths is feasible.

Inspired by the delayed photography technique, we have developed a Cryo-TXM technique to track the fast-charging relaxation process in SSBs, as illustrated in Fig. 1 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2. During a single data collection session of traditional synchrotron-based TXM, active particles undergo significant self-balancing. According to the Arrhenius formula (48), Cryo-TXM utilizes a cryogenic medium to remove environmental energy, significantly amplifying the impact of the temperature exponent on the intrinsic diffusion coefficient. This method reduces ion vibrational frequencies without altering the diffusion pathway, thus significantly decelerating ion diffusion rates. By segmenting and analyzing equilibrated results from traditional TXM without affecting the duration of single tests or image data quality, this method enables effective tracking of lithium-ion diffusion behavior and pathways during the relaxation stage.
The diffusion of lithium ions within a single particle was assessed quantitatively by imaging multiple in situ model batteries, which comprised NCM811 cathode, graphite anode, and Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl) SSE, utilizing a nanoimaging line station equipped with an advanced light source operating between 8.3 keV and 8.45 keV (49). The results indicated that the frozen cathode particles exhibited a significantly more pronounced concentration gradient distribution and a change pattern when compared to the unfrozen particles following 30 min of simultaneous relaxation (Fig. 1 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig S3). In addition, the time required for the frozen cells to reach an equivalent relaxation voltage was noticeably extended (to 3.5 V, the duration increased by approximately 1.5 h) (Fig. 1I), which corroborated the efficacy of our proposed method for investigating ion diffusion dynamics within single electrode particles.

## The Principles of Cryo-TXM
Fig. 2A depicts the basic principle of Cryo-TXM, where the application of a freezing effect to the cell significantly restricts the energy transfer within the environment. Consequently, the lithium ions within the system lack the requisite energy to overcome the diffusion energy barriers, resulting in a substantial reduction in their diffusion rate under identical charging conditions. To further quantitatively investigate the imaging principle and basic effect of Cryo-TXM, we selected the layered oxide NCM811 as the subject, Ni-Co-Mn oxides as layered bodies (NCMO), constructed material models at various delithiation states, and determined the lithium-ion diffusion behavior within the material as a function of temperature using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) (50).

The diffusion trajectories of lithium ions within Li(1-x)Ni0.8Co0.1 Mn0.1O2 at different temperatures were calculated for a 10 ps duration, and the migration probability density of Li+ was represented by green isocontours. Taking x = 0.6 as an example, the diffusion probability density cloud of lithium ions was found to be more localized at 200 K compared to 500 K (Fig. 2B). Further analysis of the van Hove correlation function for collective lithium-ion migration (Fig. 2C) revealed that at 200 K, the range of lithium-ion diffusion was confined within 0 to 0.5 Å, whereas at 500 K, it extended to 0 to 6 Å. The mean square displacement (MSD) of cells at different temperatures after charging (x = 0.6) was extracted (Fig. 2D), further confirming that cryogenic conditions significantly restricted the internal ion diffusion within active particles. Additionally, similar analyses were conducted for other delithiation states (SI Appendix, Figs. S4–S9). Based on the MSD fitting results, the diffusion coefficient and diffusion barrier of lithium ions in NCM811 under different charging states were calculated (Fig. 2E) (51). The results show that the temperature-induced diffusion difference decreases as the state of delithiation decreases, indicating that the influence of temperature on diffusion strengthened gradually as the charging proceeded, and the differences in ion diffusion became pronounced after cryogenic treatment. This validates the effectiveness of the proposed method in studying the relaxation process of batteries after fast charging.
Furthermore, we elucidated the method impact via in situ impedance measurements (Fig. 2F). By comparing the impedance of frozen and unfrozen batteries postcharging over time, it was observed that the impedance of the cells postfreezing escalated significantly, reaching tenfold compared to room temperature conditions. Additionally, the elevated electrochemical impedance persisted over time postfreezing, suggesting persistently high resistance to lithium-ion transport within the battery. Concurrently, distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis (52), depicted in Fig. 2G, reveals a marked increase in the peak intensities corresponding to diffusion impedance following freezing (x = 0.01 to 0.1), corroborating the reduced diffusion rate and simplifying the investigation of diffusion behavior.

## Origin of Fast-Charging Limitation Revealed by Cryo-TXM
As previously mentioned, the ion transport pathways during the later stages of fast charging determine the electrochemical performance of SSBs, and segmented analysis of the relaxation process can accurately capture these pathways. Therefore, in situ imaging analysis was conducted on NCM811 cathodes of SSBs fast charged to different cutoff voltages. Fig. 3A on the right illustrates the experimental setup where two batteries were rapidly charged to 4.0 V and 4.3 V, respectively, and underwent rapid Cryo-TXM characterization while the relaxation voltages were monitored in situ. Fig. 3A on the left displays our findings, showing that the periphery of NCM811 particles at a cutoff voltage of 4.3 V exhibited a higher state of delithiation compared to those at 4.0 V. This indicates that after charging to 4.3 V, the AMs internally experienced a higher Li+ concentration gradient. As relaxation progresses, the Li+ concentration gradients within the particles gradually diminish. Once the relaxation voltage stabilizes, the differences in Li+ concentration within the NCM811 particles will be eliminated. Notably, in NCM811 particles charged rapidly at higher cutoff voltages, the self-balancing of concentration polarization is delayed (4.0 V, 120 min; 4.3 V, 300 min). We hypothesize that in the later stages of charging, the internal lithium-ion transport pathways within the particles are obstructed, leading to a sustained intensification of the high-impedance non-Faradaic electric field. In addition, we simultaneously completed ex situ Cryo-TXM with multiple-layer electrodes and obtained near-unanimous results, further demonstrating the generalizability of the experimental results with single-layer granular electrodes (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

During charging, the diffusion of lithium ions is restricted to specific surface regions due to the dual effects of high-impedance non-Faraday electric fields and limited interface contact between AMs and SSEs. Rapid charging exacerbates this localized delithiation, leading to structural collapse and lattice distortions in these areas (Fig. 3B). These distortions hinder the already restricted lithium-ion pathways, ultimately leading to an increased polarization voltage and significantly reduced reversible capacity of the battery during fast charging. To validate our claim, the surface layers of NCM811 particles after rapid charging to different cutoff voltages were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 3C, at a cutoff voltage of 4.0 V, the NCM811 surface exhibits a layered structure (R-3 m space group) with a regular (003) crystal face, consistent with the initial uncycled particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). In contrast, at a cutoff voltage of 4.3 V, noticeable lattice stripe distortions appear on the particle surface, directly causing the lower reversible capacity during SSB fast charging.
We further hypothesize that lattice distortions slow the self-equilibration of lithium concentration gradients, resulting in particles internally sustaining higher Li+ gradients over an extended period. This prolonged thermodynamic instability leads to greater unbalanced stresses within the particles, increasing the likelihood of fractures and ultimately shortening the lifespan of the battery fast-charging cycles (53, 54). This viewpoint was validated by COMSOL finite element simulations, which modeled polycrystalline NCM811 particles using an uneven Voronoi tessellation (55). As illustrated in Fig. 3D, the simulation showed stress distribution in NCM811 particles within the SSB, revealing that stress increases along the lithium concentration gradient and peaks at the boundaries. This finding is consistent with previous analyses, with quantitative stress results shown in Fig. 3E. Concurrently, stress gradually decreases during the relaxation phase, suggesting that this process can reduce the uneven distribution of stress within the particles, providing insights for subsequent improvements (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Further parametric scanning of stress distribution at different charging rates was conducted (Fig. 3F), showing that particle surface stress is minimally sensitive to charging rates at lower voltage thresholds (<4.0 V), maintaining a comparable level across different rates. Upon reaching higher voltage ranges, the impact of charging rate on stress significantly increases, with the stress in particles at a 5 C charging rate at 4.0 V significantly exceeding that at a 0.1 C rate. Additionally, this difference in stress expands with increasing charging voltages. This observation aligns with our assertion that strong non-Faradaic electric fields in high-voltage areas cause surface lattice distortions. The slowing of self-equilibration hinders the release of stress in active particles, ultimately leading to a reduction in the lifespan of rapid charging cycles in SSBs.

## Reverse Potential Eliminating Non-Faraday Electric Field
Studies have shown that rapid elimination of concentration polarization within the particles is crucial for enhancing the battery’s fast-charging capabilities. This challenge can be addressed by increasing the contact area between the particles and the electrolyte, thereby creating numerous ionic transport pathways, as illustrated by the core-shell diffusion mode in liquid batteries (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Regrettably, lattice distortions in the high-voltage region of SSB during fast charging, along with the continuous current input, result in a lack of ion transport pathways, hindering fast lithium-ion diffusion. Although incorporating relaxation steps into the charging protocol can mitigate the aforementioned issues, extended relaxation periods adversely affect battery charging time. Here, by intermittently applying reverse potentials in the high-voltage range, we aim to rapidly eliminate the high-impedance non-Faradaic electric fields, thus alleviating the obstructions caused by lattice distortions to the transport channels and enhancing the rapid charging performance of SSBs.
As shown in Fig. 4A, we applied a reverse potential every 0.05 V interval for 10 s within the high-voltage range of 4 V to 4.3 V. Compared to the continuous constant current charging process (Fig. 4B), the optimized charging protocol for the SSB resulted in higher initial discharge voltages and greater discharge capacities. The capacity in the first cycle increased from 101.7 mAh g−1 to 142.6 mAh g−1, adequately demonstrating that polarization during rapid charging was significantly alleviated. It should be noted that both batteries were activated with one cycle of 0.1 C charge/discharge (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). We measured the relaxation voltage curves of batteries under two charging protocols. After a long period of relaxation, the battery without added reverse potential showed a greater relaxation voltage drop, which further demonstrates that our method can effectively eliminate high polarization voltage (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). For experimental consistency, the battery in Fig. 4A was similarly activated with the addition of reverse potential. In addition, the capacity retention rate increases by 27% after 250 cycles (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Cryo-TXM characterization and quantitative results further indicate that the concentration polarization within the NCM811 particles has been significantly reduced after optimizing the charging protocol. Additionally, the absorption spectra of nickel reveal diminished valence variations in the radial direction. Five points along the radial direction on each of the two particles were selected and the corresponding Ni K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) was extracted (Fig. 4C). The results based on the features in the direction of the particle arrows clearly indicate that the concentration gradient within the electrode particles has been eliminated. Analysis of the quantitative spectra shows a significant reduction in the high valence state of elemental Ni. This suggests that the desulfurization of the particle surface layer is weakened due to the counteracting potential dynamics counteracting the non-Faraday electric field, thus potentially reducing the associated lattice distortion and fracture problems (Fig. 4D). The comparative data of liquid batteries under two charging strategies further indicate that the proposed non-Faradaic battery represents a unique mechanism limiting the fast-charging performance of SSBs (SI Appendix, Fig. S17).

Postcycling analysis of the two disassembled batteries, as visualized through micro-CT and nano-CT characterization (Fig. 4 E and F), reveals that NCM811 particles treated with the intermittent reverse potential maintain higher structural integrity and exhibit a more consistently ellipsoidal shape (The blue and pink ellipsoidal particles are NCM positive particles, and the dark pink and green fillers are mixtures of LPSCl electrolyte and VGCF). Scanning electron microscopy results further reveal that electrode particles not subjected to the reverse potential suffer from increased fragmentation and pulverization (SI Appendix, Fig. S18), underscoring the efficacy of our method. Indeed, it is promising that merely by modifying the charging protocol, without altering the system materials, one can enhance the fast-charging performance of SSBs. Meanwhile, by testing the GITT curves of the two batteries after cycling, we calculated that the lithium-ion diffusion coefficient of the battery regulated by the reverse potential is significantly higher than that of the unregulated battery (SI Appendix, Fig. S19), which indicates that the elimination of the non-Faraday electric field effectively reduces the lithium-ion diffusion obstruction due to lattice distortion during cycling.
To confirm the universality of our proposed method in the field of sulfide-based SSBs, we conducted additional validation on an SSB with Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) as the anode and NCM811 as the cathode. By applying intermittent reverse potential in the later stage of charging, the C-rate performance of the SSB is significantly improved. SSB maintains a reversible capacity of 51 mAh g−1 under 10 C fast-charging conditions (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Furthermore, this charging approach significantly improved the charge-discharge capacity of drone batteries operating in cold environments (Fig. 4H and SI Appendix, Fig. S21), demonstrating the practicality of the method. This is significant for guiding low-temperature fast-charging strategies for drones in cold climate conditions.

## Conclusions
Our results demonstrate that the developed synchrotron radiation-based Cryo-TXM method achieves short-time dynamic capture of ion diffusion inside electrode particles by controlling the rate of the ion diffusion, and it accurately delineates the transport path of lithium ions during the relaxation phase of SSB after fast charging. In this paper, we examine the single-particle relaxation behaviors resulting from fast charging at different cutoff voltages, and our findings indicate that lattice distortion, induced by high charging cutoff voltages and fast-charging rates, impairs Li-ion transport among electrode particles and electrolyte in SSB, and delays the resolution of the particle concentration gradient while intensifying the accumulation of stress within particles in high-pressure regions. Based on these observations, we contend that dynamic stress relief for fast charging of SSB can be facilitated by incorporating an intermittent reverse potential at high-voltage regions. We achieve a significant improvement in the reversible capacity of SSBs for fast charging (1 C to 10 C) and validate it under multiple SSB regimes. These results underscore the critical role of the ion diffusion self-balancing within electrode particles for the fast charging of SSB and offer methodological and theoretical guidance to enhance the fast-charging performance of SSBs.

## Methods

## Material Preparation.
The active material (NCM811) was provided by Rong Bai. Artificial graphite (S360-L2-H), Ketjen Black(KB) and Li6PS5Cl (SCD-2) SSE came from Ke Jing. Vapor-grown carbon fiber (VGCF, >98%, D × L 100 nm × 20 to 200 μm) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. LTO, Polyvinylidene difluoride(PVDF) and Xylene (AR,99%) came from Aladdin. Lithium-ion battery electrolyte (LiPF6+EC/DMC(1:1)) came from DoDoChem. The electrodes used in the liquid batteries (cathode NCM811 and anode graphite) were obtained from Ke Jing.

## Operando Cryo-TXM Device.
In the cryogenic device, a button cell featuring through holes is positioned on a PTFE charge/discharge mold, with wires connected to both sides using metal spacers. The mold is secured within the metal casing depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, with the cell sealed on both sides using Krafton tape. Subsequently, the assembly was pressurized and secured using insulation-treated steel plates and bolts to ensure the stable operation of the sulfide solid-state button cell. A 3 mm radius hole was created in the cryostat to allow precise X-ray penetration. The metal device is double-insulated, with liquid nitrogen entering through a bottom opening and exiting at the top, while a 1 mm circular hole on the side serves to equalize air pressure within the chamber. Additionally, insulating cotton and a phase change material composed of ethyl acetate were integrated within the housing's sandwich structure to minimize heat transfer. Tests conducted with a thermistor temperature sensor demonstrated that the device's internal temperature could be stably maintained between −75 °C and −85 °C for extended periods (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). Electrical insulation and sealing were achieved using insulating tapes and ethylene vinyl acetate copolymers.

## Electrochemical Measurements.
For electrochemical performance testing, the cathode powder was mixed and milled by the active material (NCM811), VGCF, and Li6PS5Cl in the ratio of 70:5:25 for 1 h, and the anode powder was mixed and milled by the synthetic graphite, VGCF, and Li6PS5Cl in the ratio of 50:47:3 for 1 h. The NP ratio was selected to be 1.5. LTO composite anode was made by mixing LTO, LPSCl, and VGCF in 40:50:10 for 1 h. The cathode active material loading of all laboratory cells was 5 mg cm−2. The solid-state battery was tested at 25 °C for graphite anodes and 60 °C for LTO anodes. The NCM811 electrode ratio used for Cryo-TXM characterization was adjusted to 59:34:5 to enable single-particle scale analysis in Cryo-TXM, in addition for the fabrication of in situ button cell pole pieces, an additional 2% of NBR-based hydrocarbon polymer binder was mixed in dehydrated xylene. The pole piece was coated on a carbon-coated aluminum foil at a thickness of 50 μm with an additional 50 μm thick layer of Li6PS5Cl SSE. The anode was subjected to the same operation. The electrochemical cycling test (25 °C) voltage range was chosen as 2.6 to 4.3 V (vs. Li+/Li). The liquid battery cathode powder was mixed and milled by the active material (NCM811), PVDF, and KB in the ratio of 80:10:10(coated on the surface of aluminum foil, 0.64 mg cm−2), and the anode was Li metal. In preparing the slurry for the pouch cell anode layer, 50 wt% of synthetic graphite powder, 47.5 wt% of LPSCl, and an additional 2.5 wt% of Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) were mixed with anhydrous p-xylene. The LPSCl layer was then cast onto the anode. Upon completion, the films were transferred to a vacuum oven for drying, and the multilayers were cut into specific sizes of 50 * 25 mm2 for pouch cell assembly. The cathode consisted of 59% NMC811, 34% LPSCl, 5% VGCF, and 2% additional binder, with an active cathode loading of 15 mg cm−2. The anode/LPSCl and cathode films were stacked and pressed at 350 MPa. The electrodes of the liquid battery were prepared with N:P = 1.1, with a cathode active material loading of 12.1 mg cm−2 and an anode active material loading of 11.0 mg cm−2. The electrodes were cut into circular discs with areas of 1.5 cm2 and 1.0 cm2, respectively, and 50 μL of LiPF6 + EC/DMC (1:1) electrolyte was added before assembling them into a liquid button battery. Tests were performed in a pressurized environment with PTFE plates. The impedance test frequency range was chosen as 0.1 to 100,000 Hz.

## Characterization Based on Synchrotron Source.
Two-dimensional TXM-XANES imaging of the above modeled coin cell was performed during post-fast-charging relaxation. In order to study the evolution of the chemical state, we acquired complete XANES image series for each charging stage during the decay process. Each XANES image series was measured by scanning the Ni absorption k-edge over the range 8,300 to 8,450 eV in steps of 2 eV, with 1 TXM image per energy step. Each image was acquired with an exposure time of 200 ms. Camera pixels (2 × 2) were split into one output image pixel. The 2D TXM-XANES data were fitted using the spectra of standard samples to obtain the final relaxation profile.
Nano-CT imaging was collected at the beamline BL18B, Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) to analyze the 3D morphology of regions of interest within the battery by reconstructing the particles which after cycling. The nanotomography dataset imaging dataset was collected by 8 keV X-rays using 1,440 projections over a 360° angular range, and the resolution of this TXM has been quantified to a 3D resolution of less than 50 nm. The data were reconstructed in 3D using the TXM-Wizard software (20180319 SSRF China). The 3D visualization and data analysis were performed using the commercial software package Avizo (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (56).
The synchrotron tomography images were collected at the beamline BL13HB, SSRF at 15 keV, for ∼900 projections over an angular range of 180° with a field of view of 1,000 × 1,000 μm (with a 2 k × 2 k CCD camera binning 2 × 2 camera pixels into one output pixel). The raw data obtained are tomographically aligned and then reconstructed. The 3D visualization and data analysis were also performed using the commercial software package Avizo (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (57).

## COMSOL Finite Element Simulation.
The stress distribution of NCM811 particles during charging was investigated using the finite element method, where the two-dimensional geometry was modeled by the composition of primary ions represented by randomly generated arbitrary polygons, and the particles were embedded in two-dimensional circles using the Voronoi method. The electrochemical reactions were set to occur on the surface of the particles and the local current density was set to a constant value. The inhomogeneous particle-SSE contact in SSBs was simulated by setting 40% of the active surface. Li diffusion kinetics followed Fick's law of diffusion. The finite element software COMSOL 6.0 was utilized for model building and solving. The meshing process of the simulation was carried out according to the following parameters. The mesh was a free triangular mesh consisting of 4,788 triangle cells, 1,175 edge cells, and 305 vertex cells. In this study, the minimum mesh mass is 0.273 and the average mesh mass is 0.784.

## Concentration Gradient Normalization Algorithm.
In order to extract and normalize the gray values of an image, we have developed a concentration gradient extraction function. The detailed steps of the function are as follows: First, the function reads the input image file and loads the image data by calling the imread function. The input color image (i.e., contains three color channels) is converted to a grayscale image using the rgb2gray function. Grayscale images make subsequent processing easier and more consistent by eliminating color information and retaining only luminance information. Next, for normalization, the image data are converted to a double-precision type (double). This conversion ensures accuracy and correctness when performing mathematical operations. Specifically, each pixel value of the grayscale image is converted to a double precision floating point number. Finally, the function normalizes the grayscale values of the image, and since the original grayscale image had a range of pixel values between 0 and 255, after dividing by 255.0, the range of pixel values is scaled down to between 0 and 1.

## AIMD Simulation.
We performed AIMD simulations to study the ion diffusion of various charge states of NCM811 at different temperatures. The AIMD simulation was carried out using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) and the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. A nonspin mode was used for sampling the k-space at the Gamma point to reduce computational cost, with a minimal 1 × 1 × 1 k-point grid centered at Γ and a time step of 2 fs. Starting from the initial NCM811 model, a certain number of lithium atoms were randomly removed to construct various charge state models of NCM811. In each temperature simulation, the model generated from the above method was slowly heated to the corresponding temperature (0 to 200 K, 300 K, 400 K, 500 K; 1 ps) to stabilize the model.
