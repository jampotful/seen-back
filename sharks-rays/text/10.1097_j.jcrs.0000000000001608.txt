Depth of focus as a function of spherical aberration using adaptive optics in pseudophakic subjects.
Tabernero J, Otero C, Kidd J, Zahiño L, Noia A, Güell JL, Artal P, Pardhan S. (2025). Journal of cataract and refractive surgery. . doi:10.1097/j.jcrs.0000000000001608. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Depth of focus as a function of spherical aberration using adaptive optics in pseudophakic participants

## Abstract
DoF generally increased when negative spherical aberration was added. However, variability was high. Clinical adaptive optics simulators were an effective tool for testing individual tolerance to spherical aberration.
Some levels of optical blur at the retina are not perceived by the human visual system. This amount of unperceived blur is known as the depth of focus (DoF). A simple geometric paraxial model establishes that DoF (expressed in diopters) is directly proportional to the diameter of the just-detectable blur circle at the retinal plane and inversely proportional to the pupil diameter.1 Studies in a normal participant population have shown that DoF varies roughly from 0.1 to 0.5 diopter (D).2–5 Therefore, in the event of nonactive accommodation, it is quite plausible that the defocus imposed by an object placed up to 2 m (0.5 D) away from the eye might go unnoticed compared with an object placed at infinity.6,7
After standard cataract surgery, the eye loses any remaining natural accommodative power, and accurate vision is restricted to objects only on a single plane (plus/minus DoF). Recently, IOLs designed to partially increase the range of the depth of field, such as extended depth-of-focus (EDOF) and enhanced monofocal IOLs, have been introduced.8 Using this technology, the just-detectable blur circle can be achieved over an extended range of distances compared with the aberration-free model. For instance, if the focal region extends beyond the intrinsic DoF of the eye, vision at closer ranges might be possible.
The benefits of an extended focal region have their limitations. The visual cost is a reduced optical quality (due to more aberrations) in the extended focal “region,” which needs to be tolerated by participants.9 Some previous research has shown that the relationship between aberrations and vision quality is not straightforward, suggesting that individual tolerance to aberrations is highly variable.10–13 Therefore, a proper prior selection of participants is required to optimize patient satisfaction.
To facilitate this, adaptive optics visual simulators emerge as an advanced optical technology to establish individual tolerance to different aberrations.14–17 Studies in a laboratory environment have estimated the amount of just noticeable spherical aberration that is perceived by the human eye.18 Through-focus curves can also be simulated with high accuracy, which could potentially bring significant advantages to the clinic because it allows the patient to experience the best IOL before surgery to maximize the visual performance postcataract surgery.19
Most of the published studies are confined to a research laboratory environment. Ideally, adaptive optics visual simulators should be able to operate in a clinical environment with real participants to characterize the interparticipant variability of DoF and the crossed interaction between DoF and the added negative spherical aberration. Recently, a commercially available adaptive optics visual simulator (VAO, Voptica SL) has been introduced for clinical use. Some of the capabilities of VAO have been validated in a clinical environment, which also includes measuring refractive errors.20–23 More advanced options, like measuring through-focus curves with and without spherical aberration, have only been tested in a pilot study in young participants (17 healthy participants with a mean age of 30.2 ± 5.7 years).19 Our aim was to simulate the effects of different EDOF IOLs (by adding different amounts of negative spherical aberration to the eye) in real participants who previously underwent cataract surgery and monofocal IOL implantation. In addition, using computational techniques, we will model our results to better understand the effects of adding negative spherical aberration with IOLs.

## METHODS

## Participants
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Instituto Microcirugia Ocular (Barcelona, Spain). It followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants gave informed written consent. The inclusion criteria were healthy pseudophakic participants without any other comorbidities (dry eye, age-related macular degeneration, glaucoma, and high myopia) implanted only with a posterior chamber monofocal intraocular lens. Patients who had cataract surgery in the past 5 weeks were excluded, as were patients who had YAG laser capsulotomy or laser refractive surgery (laser in situ keratomileusis [LASIK] and photorefractive keratectomy). Regarding refractive error, the only inclusion criterion was to be within the operative range of the VAO in sphere and cylinder (sphere = ±9 D and cylinder = ±9 D).

## Instrumentation
VAO is an adaptive optics visual simulator designed for clinical practice. The principles of visual simulators have been explained elsewhere.14,15 In brief, VAO consists of a Hartmann-Shack wavefront sensor that relays information to a liquid crystal spatial light modulator that can change the shape of the light wavefronts. A visual test is projected into the participant's eye through the liquid crystal so that it can be visualized. Aberrations are introduced by the liquid crystal device. The operator can therefore change any particular aberrations during the visualization of the test. In addition, defocus can be introduced to optically simulate different distances of the visual test to the eye. In this study, it has been used to (1) get objective and subjective refractions, (2) measure the spherical aberration of the eye, (3) modify the total value of the spherical aberration of the eye, and (4) measure visual acuity at different defocus planes.

## Experimental Protocol
All measurements were obtained under pharmacologically dilated conditions (1 drop tropicamide 1%) and in the monocular testing condition. After pupil dilation, an objective refraction was obtained using the Hartmann-Shack wavefront sensor embedded within VAO. Three Hartmann-Shack measurements were taken, and the average was taken to obtain the objective refractive error. The spherical aberration of the eye (Z12eye) was also obtained. A subjective refraction with VAO followed according to previously established procedures.22,23 Then, visual acuity at 3 different defocus planes was measured: 0 D (best focal plane), −1.5 D, and −3 D. VAO was used to modify the total spherical aberration of each participant so that the visual acuities at the 3 different defocus planes were taken under 5 values of induced spherical aberration (Z12induced). Those values were personalized for each participant according to the following conditions:(1) Z12eye+Z12induced=0 (μm)(2) Z12eye+Z12induced=−0.07 (μm)(3) Z12eye+Z12induced=−0.15 (μm)(4) Z12eye+Z12induced=−0.23 (μm)(5) Z12eye+Z12induced=−0.30 (μm)
All spherical aberration values corresponded to a pupil diameter of 4.5 mm (which was also used to perform all measurements). The 5 spherical aberration conditions were applied in a random manner to the measurement of visual acuity.

## Optical Modelling and Simulation
Personalized optical models were used to perform optical simulations of DoF with different values of spherical aberration.24,25 Computer ray tracing was performed with the Zemax OpticStudio 18 optical design package. The optical models consisted of 2 surfaces, a real corneal surface and a paraxial surface of 21D as an IOL. The distance between the 2 surfaces was 4.5 mm. A pupil stop aperture was placed 3.5 mm away from the cornea. The refractive index was 1.3375 for the anterior chamber (cornea to pupil) and 1.336 for the posterior chamber (pupil to retina). The geometry of the cornea was imported from corneal topography measurements (Atlas 9001, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG) into Zemax, point by point, using a grid sag surface to reproduce corneal elevation data. These data were taken from a database of corneal surfaces obtained from a previous article.26 In total, we used data from 29 healthy participants (1 eye per participant) to generate 29 eye models. The average age (data used for optical modeling) was 54 years old (SD: 7 years). To simulate eyes with different spherical aberration, we incorporated into each model a Zernike phase surface with values of the spherical aberration coefficient, optimized to obtain a total spherical aberration target value identical to the conditions (1) to (5). The area under the averaged sagittal and tangential modulation transfer functions (MTFa) was used as an optical quality metric. A frequency cutoff of 50 cycles per mm was used, as well as a 3 mm pupil diameter, following published modelling procedures.27,28 The MTFa was computed for every spherical aberration condition, and as a function of the object distance from 2000 mm to 250 mm in steps of 125 mm. The MTFa values were then converted to simulated visual acuity values using the following equation27:VA(logMAR)=5.17MTFa−0.25
The outcome of this calculation consisted of 5 different simulated through-focus curves (logMAR vs distance) for each participant, each corresponding to a different value of spherical aberration. From each curve, we also estimated the minimum object distance that provides a visual acuity of 0.15 logMAR. This distance, converted into diopters, was used to calculate the change in DoF as a function of spherical aberration.

Each graph in Figure 2 was analyzed using a repeated measures analysis of variance test that examined the effect of defocus on visual acuity for each spherical aberration condition. For all spherical aberration conditions, there was a statistically significant interaction between defocus and visual acuity. Post hoc comparisons with a Bonferroni correction indicated that for each value of spherical aberration, visual acuity was always significantly different (P < .001) when compared across the 2 levels of consecutive defocus (ie, 0 D vs −1.5 D and −1.5 D vs −3 D) except in 1 case. When spherical aberration was −0.15 μm, visual acuity did not significantly differ (P = .492) between the 0 D (far) and −1.5 D (intermediate) defocus condition. In other words, under these conditions of spherical aberration, visual acuity did not significantly change from far to the intermediate viewing conditions, showing an effective extension of the subjective DoF. ## Optical Modelling and Simulation
Figure 3 shows the results of the DoF optical simulations. Each graph represents calculations from eye models with induced values of spherical aberration identical to those used in the real measurements. At far distances (2 m and beyond), simulated visual acuity progressively decreased as negative spherical aberration increased. By contrast, at near and intermediate distances (marked with 2 green dots at 333 mm and 666 mm on the figures), the simulated visual acuity increased as negative spherical aberration increased. The behaviour of the simulated visual acuity followed a similar pattern to the real visual acuity as a function of spherical aberration. If the minimum distance that provides a visual acuity of 0.15 logMAR is calculated and expressed in diopters, for each of the plots in Figure 3, an estimation of the change in DoF (in diopters) as a function of increasing spherical aberration can be obtained (Figure 4). From the condition of 0 to −0.07 μm of spherical aberration, the effects on DoF were negligible. Starting from −0.07 to −0.3 μm of spherical aberration, the increase in DoF followed a linear trend, with a rate of change of 0.4 D of DoF per −0.1 μm of spherical aberration.

## DISCUSSION
Presbyopia-correcting IOLs have become very popular in cataract surgery. According to recent literature, in the year 2020, there were around 100 different presbyopia-correcting IOLs (including EDOF) on the ophthalmic market.7 However, it is not clear whether all potential participants are equally suited for the predicted benefits from these IOLs. Neither does a systematic path exist for the selection of patients for the different types of IOLs on the market. In this study, we provide evidence that it is possible to recreate the visual conditions of different EDOF IOLs, with different amounts of spherical aberration, in real pseudophakic participants in the clinic. We have characterized the levels of DoF that were achieved with different amounts of spherical aberration for each particular participant. On average, the results showed a classic pattern in which more negative spherical aberration generally led to lower visual acuity at far distance, while, at the same time, the peak of the highest visual acuity was shifted to intermediate or near distances. It then becomes a question to determine what the tolerable far visual acuity is. Figure 2 showed that on average, when ocular spherical aberration increases more than −0.15 μm (all spherical aberration data in this section refer to a pupil diameter of 4.5 mm), the far distance visual acuity is worse than 0.15 logMAR units, which is hardly clinically acceptable. To be able to achieve a far visual acuity better than this value, the most effective EDOF IOL (on average) to extend the DoF would correspond to values of spherical aberration that range between −0.07 μm and −0.15 μm.
However, examining the individual data, we observed that 7 of 26 participants (27%) were able to sustain far distance visual acuity below 0.1 logMAR units even with an addition of −0.15 μm of spherical aberration. These participants might realize a greater benefit from an EDOF IOL that induces greater amounts of spherical aberration than −0.15 μm. Conversely 6 of 26 participants (23%) had visual acuity above 0.2 logMAR units when spherical aberration was −0.15 μm. These participants might do best with a low spherical aberration EDOF IOL or a standard monofocal lens.
To maximize the outcome of cataract surgery with EDOFs, our results suggest 2 lines of action. First, presurgery measurements of DoF using adaptive optics should simulate different amounts of spherical aberration to predetermine the tolerance to an EDOF lens inducing a particular amount of spherical aberration. Second, if a set of EDOF lenses with different values of added spherical aberration were available, cataract surgery could be better customized, to address the far and near visual demands of each patient.29
Optical modeling of the DoF as a function of spherical aberration showed results that were qualitatively very similar to the visual simulations, that is, when spherical aberration increased, the simulated visual acuity decreased at far distances and increased at near distances. However, when comparing real visual acuity with simulated visual acuity, significant differences were observed in the absolute values. In general, simulated logMAR values were slightly lower than the measured logMAR values. This discrepancy can be partly explained by the differences in pupil size. Computer simulations required a much smaller pupil diameter (3 mm) to convert the calculated MTFa values into visual acuity, compared with the diameter used in the real measurements (4.5 mm). The large, dilated pupils used in the real measurements were intentionally chosen to maximize and demonstrate the effects of spherical aberration on visual acuity. Conversely, the optical simulations showed that with a more realistic smaller pupil diameter, the effects of spherical aberration on visual acuity are fully scaled to what occurs with larger pupil diameters. In addition, these results reflect the difficulties that have always existed to predict visual acuity from optical measurements.10–13,27,28 They also reinforce the idea of conducting visual presurgery testing with adaptive optics visual simulators.
The influence of spherical aberration on the DoF has previously been studied using adaptive optics instrumentation in laboratory environments, for instance, to optimize monovision procedures with the addition of spherical aberration, or to compare with other procedures such as using a small pupil approach to extend DoF.30,31 In addition, the combination of primary and secondary spherical aberration might be useful to improve future approaches.32 On the other hand, real clinical measurements and procedures are still limited. Examples of clinical data with adaptive optics visual simulators include the optimization of aspheric profiles of hyperopic LASIK to increase DOF, and also, the optimization for the IOL profile to extend DoF with light adjustable IOLs.33,34 Recently, visual simulations of DoF with 2 different values of negative spherical aberration have been performed. Hervella et al. measured 17 relatively young participants (mean age 30 years) and observed a pattern similar to ours.19
In this study, we investigated additional spherical aberration conditions. More importantly, the participants were pseudophakic individuals at an advanced age (63 years), demonstrating that visual simulations are feasible at such ages, provided the ocular optics are still clear. How clear the lens needs to be to perform such simulations is an area of ongoing investigation and may depend on the type and progression of lens opacity. More research is needed to fully understand this limitation.
We tested visual acuity at 3 distances, which was a compromise to avoid patient fatigue and obtain sufficient through-focus visual acuity information. In addition, measurements were performed with a monocular visual simulator because a binocular clinical adaptive optics visual simulator does not yet exist, although several laboratory devices have been proposed and a number of studies have been performed, which may enable the future translation of the binocular technology to the clinic.15,35–40
In conclusion, we have demonstrated for the first time that measurements of DoF with different levels of spherical aberration are possible in a clinical environment, with older participants who are at the age for cataract surgery. The procedure closely recreated the visual conditions of different EDOF lenses.
