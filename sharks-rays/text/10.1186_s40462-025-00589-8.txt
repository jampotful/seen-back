Inferring leader-follower dynamics in three shark species using acoustic telemetry data.

# Inferring leader-follower dynamics in three shark species using acoustic telemetry data

## Abstract
BackgroundUnderstanding collective behaviours and interactions in sharks is still in its infancy. Although recent studies have revealed some social structures in several shark species, little is known about complex interactions and social processes such as leader-follower dynamics. Recognising the dynamics in shark populations can help to further understand population structure and the influence of specific individuals.MethodsWe developed a methodological approach to detect and analyse leader-follower behavioural patterns using acoustic telemetry data. By utilising lag-time distributions from acoustic telemetry detections for pairs of individuals we infer directed relationships based on temporal patterns. We applied this method to existing datasets from grey reef sharks (Carcharhinus amblyrhynchos), blacktip reef sharks (Carcharhinus melanopterus), and tiger sharks (Galeocerdo cuvier).ResultsWe found evidence of leader-follower behaviour in both reef-associated species, with half of the tagged grey reef sharks forming leader-follower networks at eight locations. Size was a significant influence on female grey reef sharks leading-following behaviour. We found similar behaviours at three locations for blacktip reef sharks, with one-third of the tagged individuals forming separate and non-overlapping networks. Size was a significant influence on male blacktip reef sharks leading-following behaviour. No species showed a significant effect of sex alone on leader-follower behaviours. Aggregating networks did not show an overall hierarchy for either species but showed that grey reef shark coordinated in smaller networks than expected with strong influences from more dominant individuals. We found no leader-follower networks for tiger sharks.ConclusionsOur methodology reveals leader-follower behaviours in blacktip reef sharks and grey reef sharks and corroborates findings from the literature which have previously either been described using visual observation or using a different analytical approach. We demonstrate how existing acoustic telemetry datasets are a valuable source which can be used to detect social interactions associated with leader-follower behaviours in sharks, especially when visual observations are not feasible. Our approach provides new insights into understanding the social dynamics in sharks and offers a way to be applied to many more species already acoustically tagged.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-025-00589-8.

## Background
Moving is an integral component of animal behaviour [1]. The ability to move facilitates interactions between individuals and their environment [1] and allows animals to adapt their behaviours to changing environmental factors [2, 3], to increase their chances to successfully mate and forage [3, 4], to evade competition [5] and to avoid predation [6]. All these behaviours can take place at different temporal and spatial scales [7], ranging over distances of a few metres [8, 9] to thousands of kilometres [10–12]. When animals cover these distances together, their movements often give rise to coordinated and collective group dynamics [13, 14]. Examples include flocking [15], schooling [16] or grouping in multiple species, with relationships among conspecifics being formed through cooperation, competition, mating and foraging [1, 17–20].
Collective behaviours reflect emerging patterns that result from the transfer of information from individuals to the group level. The process of information transfer in groups varies across species and time scales, and can be achieved by chemical-, acoustic-, movement- or vision-based mechanisms [14, 21–24]. Information transfer at the group- or population-level can lead to the creation of dynamic, complex and hierarchical networks among conspecifics [17, 25, 26]. These networks enable its members to collectively process information and adapt their behaviour accordingly [17]. It has been shown that sharing knowledge and information with conspecifics can increase the cognitive abilities of the whole group, allowing the group to make more complex decisions that can exceed the capabilities of single individuals [27–29].
The processes underpinning grouping animals can be captured through social network theory, which has led to an increased understanding of population structures and then transformed into ecological meaning [17, 25, 26]. Examining social networks is often based on the ‘gambit of the group’ approach, which infers associations between individuals based on their spatial proximity assuming that individuals are engaging in meaningful interactions [30, 31]. The social network analysis and the ‘gambit of the group’ approach have shown to provide a practical framework for quantifying social relationships in animals when interactions are difficult to describe or quantify [32, 33]. This framework allows to capture the overall dynamics in a group and helps to identify individuals who can hold significant influence (temporarily or permanently) within a group, i.e. ‘keystone individuals’ [31, 34]. Keystone individuals can share knowledge with naïve individuals [21, 35], lead conspecifics to a new location [36, 37], take over the decision-making for the whole group [35, 38] or even alter the hierarchical regime when present or absent and thereby influence the group dynamics and fitness [39, 40].
To examine behavioural and social patterns in the marine environment, where visual observations are challenging, specifically for non-air breathing animals, the utilisation of telemetry devices has played a significant role over the last decades [41]. Technological advancements and miniaturisation of tags allow for the collection of large movement datasets for a wide variety of species. The most common approach to collect these data for fish is through the use of acoustic telemetry, which relies on tags that are either surgically implanted or attached externally onto the animals [41, 42]. These tags send out an acoustic signal with a unique ID, which can be collected by either fixed or mobile underwater receivers (detection range is typically < 1000 m) to record their presence [41–43].
Due to their important role for ecosystems, telemetry studies on sharks have more than doubled over the last ten years alone [44–46]. Telemetry research has elevated our understanding of shark movement and their behaviours [47, 48]. Especially aggregation events have prompted social network studies on sharks to investigate behaviours and population dynamics [47, 49]. Social behaviours detected for aggregating individuals seem to not be limited to known grouping shark species, but solitary species seem to follow similar patterns for social behaviours at aggregation sites [50]. Through quantifying movements and examining interactions between conspecifics, studies have shown that sharks are not only capable of social learning through observing conspecifics but also exhibit social preferences, with body size and sex potentially influencing behaviour, social structure and hierarchies [32, 47, 49, 51–54]. Hierarchical structuring within animal societies critically shapes patterns of movement, resource access, and information transfer, with profound implications for both individual fitness and population dynamics [17, 18, 55]. The position an individual occupies within the network is seen as a proxy for its dominance or influence which predicts its role in initiating collective movements and in disseminating information through the group [17, 18].
In elasmobranchs, dominance hierarchies have been documented to govern spatial displacement and agonistic interactions, as larger individuals typically displace smaller conspecifics from high-value patches, thereby influencing group movement trajectories and habitat use [47]. Lemon sharks for example maintain the same positions within their social networks over time, suggesting that these sharks adopt stable social roles, which appear to serve important functions such as keeping the group together and enabling the flow of information through social learning [56, 57]. This shows that hierarchies in shark societies do not only regulate competitive interactions but also orchestrate collective movement and behaviour, with significant consequences for ecological processes and conservation strategies. Even though hierarchies have been examined for many shark species over more than five decades, specific leadership behaviour, where certain individuals lead others, is only described for three: blacktip reef sharks (Carcharhinus melanopterus), grey reef sharks (Carcharhinus amblyrhynchos) and lemon sharks (Negaprion brevirostris) (see review: 47). The studies on blacktip reef sharks and lemon sharks relied on work-intensive visual observations either from land or within the water [53, 58]. Acoustic telemetry, with its higher frequency of possible detections over long periods, has demonstrated its potential in a study by Jacoby et al. (2016) on a grey reef shark (Carcharhinus amblyrhynchos) population using a Gaussian mixture modelling approach, showing that some individuals exhibit leadership patterns with sex being a significant driver [51].
Here we develop a novel approach focusing on the detection of leader-follower behaviours in pairs of conspecifics for three shark species. The methodology described follows a different statistical approach compared to Jacoby et al.’s (2016) study by focusing on lag-time distributions of detections at a receiver with up to four times as much data used in the present study [51]. The tested species include reef-associated and relatively social blacktip reef sharks [33] and grey reef sharks [32, 59], as well as solitary species that occasionally form small-scale aggregations, such as tiger sharks (Galeocerdo cuvier) [60, 61]. Our research aims to contribute to the understanding of social structures that might influence the collective behavioural patterns of shark species by providing a new methodological approach and validating it with real world data. By utilising both reef-associated species that have been part of similar research efforts, though different methodologies, before, as well as tiger sharks that have not been tested before, it allows us to directly compare our results with the literature.

## Methods

## Acoustic telemetry datasets
We sourced acoustic telemetry data for the three different shark species from the Integrated Marine Observing System Animal Tracking Facility (IMOS ATF) database [62] (data downloaded in June 2023). The datasets were collected by three projects (see acknowledgements) between 2011 and 2017 [63–65]. All sharks were tagged with V16 acoustic tags (pseudorandom repeat rate: 50–100 s; estimated battery life of 832days; Vemco/Innovasea Systems Inc., USA) and the resulting tracking datasets were collected at 48 unique positions with VR2W receivers (Vemco/Innovasea Systems Inc., USA); at a reported range of a minimum of 250–300 m [66, 67]). The VR2W receivers were deployed at the Heron Island Reef, Sykes Reef and One Tree Island Reef (23.45° S, 152° E) on the southern tip of the Great Barrier Reef off the east coast of Australia (Fig. 1).

The datasets downloaded from the IMOS ATF database were quality-controlled using the “remora” R package (v.0.7.1; R v.4.3.0) which has a specific functionality to evaluate the likelihood of an acoustic detection being valid or not. All data points flagged as “invalid” or “likely invalid” by the ‘remora’ package ‘runQC’ function were removed before any analysis as recommended by Hoenner et al. (2018) [68, 69]. A total of 2,732,260 datapoints for ten tiger sharks, 42 grey reef sharks and 34 blacktip reef sharks remained after the quality-control (Table 1), which are likely to reflect only a small fraction of the respective populations in the area [70, 71], and were subsequently used for analysis (Table 1). Each datapoint contained the timestamp of detection, corresponding receiver ID, receiver deployment latitude and longitude, acoustic tag ID, species, sex and length (one tiger shark individual had no length recorded). Length measurements were either recorded as “length”, “total length” or “fork length” in millimetres. “Length” was treated as “total length” and to standardise values within and across datasets, “fork length” measurements were converted into “total length” using published equations for tiger sharks [72], blacktip reef sharks [73] and grey reef sharks [74].

## Inference of leader-follower interactions
The approach we used to examine leader-follower behaviours is based on a refined version of the unpublished work by Fernández-Gracia et al. (preprint, 2024; 78), who developed an approach to analyse the distribution of lag-times in acoustic telemetry data to infer leader-follower behaviours. We utilised this idea and further developed this methodology to infer potential leader-follower behaviours and to create networks that show individuals as nodes and their behaviour as directed connections (edges) between them. For this, we examined interactions between two individuals (A&B) at the same acoustic receiver, whereas interactions were defined as the chronological detection of individual A followed by the detection of individual B (and vice versa). To ensure the analysis was based on an appropriate sample size of interactions we performed a network stability analysis on the most comprehensive dataset (grey reef sharks, Table 1) to determine the minimum number of interactions required for each pair to be eligible for the analysis. Both the number of generated individuals (nodes) and their connections (edges) showed relative stabilisation beyond 266 interactions (See supplementary materials and Sup-Fig. 1). This threshold ensured the analysis is based on an adequate number of interactions and our findings reflect non-random patterns. Our analysis for the present study utilises the time lag between the interactions of individual A and B (Fig. 2B). A time lag is calculated as the time difference between the last detection of one individual and the first detection of the other individual with no maximum time lag set (Not setting a maximum time threshold was chosen to avoid setting an arbitrary limit for the shark’s sensory capabilities to detect conspecifics). The calculation is performed in both directions creating two separate lag time distributions for each pair of individuals, i.e., we calculate the time lag between detections from individual A to B (A: B) and from individual B to A (B: A) (Fig. 2C). We calculated the direction and weight of the relationship between both individuals as the signed Kolmogorov-Smirnoff distance (AKS) (not using the p-value), which is defined as the maximum distance between the cumulative distribution from B: A to the distribution from A: B, with its direction signalling which distribution lies above the other [75, 76] (Fig. 2C). The AKS is always calculated starting from individual B, to establish a consistent directionality for that value, which can range from − 1 to 1 [75, 76]. An AKS value > 0 implies that individual B is following individual A while a value < 0 implies the opposite (i.e. A following B).
We created a null model to validate the findings by randomising the timestamps in the detection data for each individual separately. The randomisation was done by measuring the time in between consecutive detections and reshuffling them to create a new randomised dataset (A* & B*), while the first timestamp was kept (Fig. 2D). The lag time distribution of A*:B* and B*:A* was then used to calculate a new AKS*. We repeated the randomisation 10,000 times following Farine (2017) [77] (Fig. 2E). Subsequently, the AKS value obtained from the real sample was compared to the distribution of AKS* values retrieved from the null model to test whether it deviates significantly from the null distribution (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2F).
Compared to Fernández-Gracia et al. (preprint, 2024; [78]) our approach offers a more refined calculation of the null model to avoid false-positive leader-follower detections. For the case a reshuffling sample did not reach the required minimum threshold of interactions, we assigned it an AKS* value of 1, ensuring all iterations contribute to the null distributions based on the same set of conditions. Our conservative approach shifts the distribution of the null model towards 1, which might increase the chance of missing some true relationships (false negatives), but it also substantially reduces the risk of incorrectly identifying random patterns as significant (false positives). We chose this conservative approach to ensure high confidence in any significant behaviours identified.
If the AKS from the real sample deviates significantly from the null model distribution, a leader-follower behaviour is inferred with the directionality of that interaction based on the positive or negative AKS value calculated for the real sample (Fig. 2 [G]). After checking for leader-follower behaviours across all pairs observed at a given receiver, a directed network of all significant behaviours found is generated. In the visual representation, individuals are plotted as nodes with grey squares for males and white diamonds for females. Their interactions are shown as directed edges drawn as an arrow between the nodes, where the individual leading the pair is positioned at the arrowhead.

For each species separately, we aggregated all resulting networks into a combined network by extracting all node and edge information from the respective networks corresponding to single receivers and combining them in a single network. This way we preserve the individuals present and the directionality of the edges which helps to facilitate hierarchical analysis of individuals within the aggregated network. If the same pair is detected at multiple receivers with the same leader-follower relationship the AKS values are summed. However, if opposite directions are observed (for example, A → B at one receiver and B → A at another), the corresponding edge in the network will be depicted with a double arrow. To assess how close the combined network is to a perfect hierarchical structure, we computed the smallest set of edges that needed to be removed to obtain a directed acyclic network. Directed acyclic networks are network structures where edges between nodes have a direction and do not form any cycles, meaning one cannot start at one node and follow the directed edges to eventually return to the same node. To evaluate the resulting hierarchical structure, we examined the combined network’s proximity to a directed acyclic network and the ratio of the largest out-component to the largest connected component in comparison to 10,000 random null model networks. The largest out-component is the largest set of nodes that can be reached from a single node when following the direction of the edges. The largest connected component is the largest cluster of nodes that are connected through edges. For the directed acyclic graph analysis each random network was generated by swapping edges between nodes, while keeping the out- and indegrees of the nodes, i.e. the number of edges starting and ending at each node, the same. We then compared the minimum number of edges needed to be removed in the aggregated network of the real data against the distribution of removed edges of the null model. Specifically, we calculated the p-value as the probability that the number of removed edges in the ensemble of the null model is equally separated or farther away from the peak of the distribution than the value calculated on the original network.
To assess the dominance of a single individual within the largest network component, we calculated the ratio between the largest out-component (+ 1) and the size of the largest connected component. By adding 1 to the largest out-component and dividing this by the largest component size, we create an index ranging from 0 to 1. This index quantifies the extent to which the largest component is dominated by a single individual. We created two types of random directed networks (n = 10,000 each) for comparison: one matching the node and edge count of the combined networks, and another preserving the exact in-degree and out-degree distribution of the combined network.
To quantify individual leader or follower trends, we utilised the AKS value to create a leadership index. For each statistically significant leader-follower pair, the corresponding AKS value was assigned as positive for the leader and negative for the follower. All these AKS values were then summed for each individual across all receivers where they were part of a significant leader-follower pair. An overall positive leadership index value indicates the specific individual was typically “leading”, while a negative value implies that the individual was mostly “following”. To test if size or sex affected the likelihood of an individual behaving as a “leader” or “follower” respectively we used linear regression and a parametric t-test.
All analyses were implemented in Python 3.10.11, with all code available on GitHub (see Availability of data and materials section).

## Results
Our analysis resulted in leader-follower networks for blacktip and grey reef sharks but not for tiger sharks. Twenty-one grey reef shark individuals (11 Female (85–169 cm TL); 10 Male (74–162 cm TL) appeared in at least one of the networks generated at eight locations throughout the receiver array (Fig. 3 & Sup. Figure 5). Eleven blacktip reef shark individuals (8 Female (128–151 cm TL); 3 Male (135–139 cm TL)) exhibited leader-follower behaviours at three locations (Fig. 3 & Sup. Figure 3). All networks differed in the number of nodes and edges detected. The number of edges varied from one (when only two individuals displayed leader-follower behaviour) to five (grey) or seven (blacktip) at a single receiver. The grey reef shark networks had an average density (ratio of actual connections to potential connections) of 0.3 (range 0.11–0.5) compared to an average density of 0.35 (range: 0.23–0.5) in blacktip reef sharks (Sup. Figure 3&5). The average number of nodes in the networks is four for grey reef sharks (range: 2–7) and 3.66 for blacktip reef sharks (range: 2–6).

All receiver-specific networks created for both species were directed acyclic graphs and thus already perfect hierarchical structures. When aggregating all blacktip reef shark networks, they continued to appear as separate clusters, which were found at the single receivers. For the grey reef sharks, however, half of the single receiver networks were connected to each other (Fig. 4). The aggregated grey reef shark network is not a directed acyclic graph due to three individuals forming a circular following (Fig. 4; dashed area). One edge had to be removed to transform the combined network into an acyclic graph and this value fell within the range of edges to be removed generated by the randomised null model (range: 0–2, p = 0.22) (Fig. 5). Since the number of edges to be removed fell within the range of the null model, the observed organisation among the individuals in the combined network is statistically as near from a DAG as randomisations of the network, so that we cannot conclude that the aggregated network has a markedly hierarchical structure other than what is expected by chance.

## Discussion
We developed and applied a novel approach to examine the potential for leader-follower dynamics in three shark species based on acoustic tracking data collected from single receivers. We discovered evidence for leader-follower behaviours in grey reef sharks and blacktip reef sharks but not in tiger sharks. Overall, we found leader-follower dynamics to be of varying densities, which suggests either a balance between social cohesion in the group and individual independence or generally weak distinct patterns of leader-follower behaviour. A flexible cohesion between group members may allow individuals to benefit from the group while remaining independent enough to respond to environmental changes, threats or their individual preferences.
Our overall findings for grey reef sharks exhibiting leader-follower behaviours align with Jacoby et al.’s (2016) research, which used a substantially different methodological approach [51]. However, in contrast with the results from Jacoby et al. [51] we found larger female grey reef sharks to significantly lead more often, whereas sex alone, did not show any significant influence on leader or follower behaviour. This further highlights that sample size, data availability and overall individual variation can play a huge role when trying to infer specific behaviours from acoustic telemetry data. When examining collective behaviours such as hierarchies in the networks, half of the separated networks could be aggregated. The hierarchical analysis of the combined network did not differ from the null model, indicating that either no hierarchy is present or that the sample size of tagged sharks is too small compared to the population size to infer any hierarchies. Another explanation could be that the population is more organised in clusters with only a few individuals moving between groups. This could explain why we detected acyclic localised networks at the single receivers but the combined network to be less connected with a cyclic section. The analysis to assess the dominance of individuals within the network revealed that the grey reef sharks coordinate in smaller groups than expected with a strong dominance of a few individuals. This is the case for both approaches with random in- and outdegrees as well as the preserved in- and outdegrees from the combined network. The ratio of out-component size to connected component size indicates an effective information transfer through the network and suggest that information from certain nodes can reach a large proportion of the connected network [17, 79]. The combined network with its smaller connected components may be more vulnerable to fragmentation if key nodes are removed. With grey reef sharks known to form stable social networks that can last multiple years and using social information while foraging, it would be worth investigating if hierarchies and larger clusters can be found in populations with a higher percentage of individuals tagged [32].
As previously described, blacktip reef sharks are known to form stable groups too, as well as displaying following and social behaviours [33, 58]. The three networks we found remained as separate clusters and did not merge into a larger connected network when aggregated, potentially indicating a less connected overall population with smaller sub communities as shown by Mourier et al. (2012) in a pacific atoll [33]. Almost 75% of follower behaviours were from females despite making up only 59% of the tagged individuals, which is corroborating previous studies on blacktip reef sharks which suggested a female preference in engaging in leader-follower behaviours [58]. However, we could not find an overall influence of sex alone on leading or following behaviours. For male sharks there was positive relationship between size and leadership but due to the low sample size of three individuals, this should be interpreted with caution. This result corroborates the findings of Porcher (2023) and Mourier et al. (2012) who described size as an influential factor on population structure [33, 58]. Both Mourier (2012) and Porcher (2023) relied on visual observations and photo identification of individuals [33, 58], while the present study discovered leader and follower behaviours for the first time in blacktip reef sharks using acoustic telemetry data.
The number of tagged individuals was similar for grey and blacktip reef sharks with only the number of detections differing, indicating that the number of leader-follower networks obtained with our method is likely more dependent on the number of detections than on the number of individuals tagged. The lack of detections could be one explanation why tiger sharks were not found to form leader-follower networks. Even though eight out of ten individuals were detected over a longer period in the region/array, the number of detections was only a small fraction compared to the number of detections of the other two species. Finding no networks for tiger sharks, which are often regarded as solitary [60, 61], may also be attributed to the fact that they might be migrating longer distances, only sporadically having the opportunity to exhibit social behaviours. Perhaps, the non-existence of leader-follower networks for the tiger sharks detected within the same time span could indicate an active avoidance behaviour of dominant individuals, as documented for other shark species [47]. Overall, avoidance behaviour or leading/following other non-tagged sharks in all three species may have contributed to our findings of leader-follower networks only in 50% or less of the tagged individuals in this study. The potential for avoidance behaviour and niche separation as shown for grey reef sharks and blacktip reef sharks should be taken into account when future studies are trying to examine similar behaviours in other sharks [47, 80]. Additionally, in contrast to teleost species, the role of olfactory cues which could influence the presence of other individuals or species is unknown for sharks [81, 82]. Future studies could also incorporate environmental data, presence of prey or time of day that could potentially influence the behaviour of these species [83].
There are certain limitations and assumptions when applying a theoretical methodology to acoustic telemetry data. For example, the hydrophones on the acoustic receivers are omnidirectional, meaning that the detected animal could be in any direction within the range of a few hundred meters, therefore leading to positions of low precision [52]. Uncertain positioning can lead to a potential bias in systems with a high range and should be considered when deriving social interactions from acoustic telemetry data [47]. A high number of tagged individuals in the vicinity of a receiver could also lead to code collisions, effectively cancelling each other out and resulting in loss of transmissions [84]. Using a variety of receiver types in future studies, potentially on the animals themselves, to study social networks could more accurately infer interactions. Furthermore, fine-scale acoustic array designs (commonly referred to as VPS designs) could show specific leader-follower tracks [52]. The range of each receiver in the present dataset was described as 250–300 m in 2014 & 2015 [66, 67], with unknown fluctuations throughout the seasons and years which can significantly alter the range [85]. These detection limits could mean that two individuals detected at the same receiver could be ~ 600 m apart or even further. Our main assumption, and underlying principle of the method presented, is that if two conspecifics consistently show up at the same receiver in a non-random fashion our confidence to observe a real association is high. However, inferring leader and follower behaviour always comes with the caveat that sharks can be submissive and could actively avoid conflicts with more dominant individuals [47].
Being able to infer leader-follower behaviours and showing a combined sex- and size-specific influence on leading behaviours, further adds complexity to the presumed social structure of grey and blacktip reef sharks [32, 33, 47]. The methodological approach presented, demonstrates that existing acoustic telemetry datasets have the potential to reveal intricate social behaviours beyond their original purpose of tracking movement patterns and habitat usage.

## Conclusions
Our study demonstrates that acoustic telemetry data can be used to reveal complex social behaviours like leaders and followers in sharks beyond the more traditional applications such as habitat usage and migration patterns. The novel methodological approach detected leader-follower dynamics in two reef-associated sharks, providing new insights into their social organisation. Grey reef sharks formed distinct leader-follower networks across eight locations throughout the array, while blacktip reef sharks formed networks in three locations. In these leader-follower behaviours, we found a positive relationship between sex and size on leading for female grey reef sharks and male blacktip reef sharks. Sex or size alone were not found to be a predictor of leading or following. Aggregating networks did not result in a hierarchy for any of the two species. The aggregated network for grey reef sharks however revealed that a few individuals assert strong dominance on the network and thereby have the potential to significantly influence the information transfer. This can result in serious implications for the network if these individuals are removed. The complete absence of leader-follower networks in tiger sharks could either reflect their typically solitary and highly mobile lifestyle or the limited detection data available for analysis. The method’s ability to detect leader-follower behaviours in existing datasets opens new opportunities for the analysis of acoustic telemetry data, potentially revealing previously undetected social patterns in other shark species. Our findings contribute to the growing understanding of shark social behaviour and population dynamics that can have important implications for both ecological research and conservation strategies. Future research should examine the drivers of such behaviours and incorporate the influence of environmental factors.
