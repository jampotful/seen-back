Widespread and Convergent Evolution of Cone Monochromacy in Galeomorph Sharks

# Widespread and Convergent Evolution of Cone Monochromacy in Galeomorph Sharks

## Abstract
AbstractColor vision is widespread in marine vertebrates but is notably lacking in whales, dolphins, seals, and apparently also sharks. All sharks studied to date possess only a single spectral class of cone and are thus potentially totally color blind. The reason why sharks lack color vision is unclear, but as the visual pigments of only a handful of this large and ecologically diverse taxon have been studied, more data are required to address this question. Here, we assembled the retinal transcriptomes of 9 species from 7 families and 3 orders within the superorder Galeomorphii to screen for visual opsin and phototransduction genes. We reveal that cone monochromacy is widespread in galeomorph sharks, but the type of cone opsin expressed varies, with lamniform and orectolobiform sharks expressing a long-wavelength-sensitive (LWS) opsin, and carcharhiniform and heterodontiform sharks expressing a rhodopsin-like 2 (RH2) opsin. Cone monochromacy has evolved from a dichromatic ancestral state at least 4 times, implying strong selection pressure to prioritize achromatic over chromatic vision. While all species express the GRK1A and GRK7 isoforms of G protein-coupled receptor kinase, only sharks with the LWS cone opsin express the GRK1B isoform, which suggests that nonspectral functions of photoreception may have influenced, or result from, the opsin complement in the shark retina. Finally, we show that the shark rod (RH1) opsin gene shows evidence of positive selection at sites known to influence pigment kinetics (i.e. metarhodopsin II stability) and that the rate of retinal release likely differs substantially between species in ways that reflect their physiology and ecology.

## Introduction
Sharks possess an impressive array of sensory systems to gather information from the world around them. These senses, which include olfaction, gustation, vision, hearing, electroreception, and the mechanosensory lateral line, provide information over different temporal and spatial scales and are used in species-specific ways to guide behavior (Gardiner et al. 2014). The information gathering capacity of each sense is constrained to some degree by the physical properties of the aquatic habitat. This is particularly evident in the case of vision, where the amount and spectral distribution of light available is dependent on absorption and scattering by the water and any dissolved or suspended matter. As a result, the quantity and quality of light change dramatically based on depth and location, as well as daily or seasonal changes in turbidity and other biotic and abiotic processes (Lythgoe 1979).
As in other fishes, the eyes of sharks show numerous specializations for vision in different habitats and for different lifestyles. For example, spatial resolving power (the ability to discriminate fine detail in the retinal image) varies from a relatively poor 3 cycles deg−1 in benthic, nocturnally active species, such as the Port Jackson shark Heterodontus portusjacksoni (Peel et al. 2020), to more than 10 cycles deg−1 in large pelagic species, such as the bigeye thresher shark Alopias superciliosus (Lisney and Collin 2008). These lower and upper estimates of visual acuity reflect those measured in diverse teleost fishes and equate to the ability to discriminate objects ranging in width from 2.9 mm (3 cycles deg−1) to 0.9 mm (10 cycles deg−1) at a distance of 1 m, compared with a standard human observer (30 cycles deg−1) who can discriminate objects 0.3 mm wide at the same distance. Many sharks also possess a duplex retina containing both rod and cone photoreceptors (Schieber et al. 2012), which likely enables vision over a broad range of light intensities, and high contrast sensitivity that allows them to detect differences in stimulus brightness of <2% (Ryan et al. 2017). However, compared with many other fishes—especially teleosts—the ability of sharks to see color is greatly diminished, with all species studied to date being cone monochromats that possess only a single spectral type of cone (Hart et al. 2011, 2020; Hara et al. 2018; Fasick et al. 2019).
Color vision arises from the comparison of spectrally distinct photoreceptor types, typically cones, by opponent retinal circuits. The spectral sensitivity of a photoreceptor is largely determined by the type of visual pigment expressed in the outer segment. A visual pigment molecule comprises an opsin protein covalently bound to a light-sensitive chromophore. Differences in the amino acid composition of the opsin, especially in the vicinity of the chromophore binding pocket, alter the wavelengths of light that are absorbed most strongly by the chromophore. In vertebrates, there are 5 main classes of visual opsin protein, which arose from a series of gene duplication events prior to the divergence of jawed and jawless vertebrates: SWS1, SWS2, RH1, rhodopsin-like 2 (RH2) and long-wavelength-sensitive (LWS) (Lamb 2013). Typically, RH1 opsins are expressed in rods, with the other opsins expressed in cones. The wavelengths of maximum absorbance (λmax) of the visual pigments generated by these different opsin types typically fall into the following ranges when conjugated with the vitamin A1-derived chromophore 11-cis retinal: SWS1 ∼360 to 440 nm; SWS2 ∼400 to 480 nm; RH1 ∼450 to 520 nm; RH2 ∼440 to 530 nm; and LWS ∼495 to 575 nm (Yokoyama 2000; Musilova et al. 2019).
Previous studies reveal that cartilaginous fishes (Chondrichthyes) possess a reduced set of opsins, having apparently lost the SWS1 and SWS2 opsin types prior to the divergence of the Holocephali (chimeras) and the Elasmobranchii (sharks, skates, and rays) (Hart 2020). The elephant shark Callorhinchus milii, a holocephalan, expresses RH1, RH2, and 2 isoforms of the LWS opsin in its retina (Davies et al. 2009). Similarly, many rays (Batoidea) express the RH1, RH2, and LWS opsins in rods and 2 spectrally distinct classes of cone, respectively, and are also known to have color vision (Hart et al. 2004, 2020; Van-Eyk et al. 2011; Schluessel et al. 2021). On the other hand, while sharks universally express the RH1 rod opsin, each species found to possess cones expresses either the RH2 or the LWS opsin, but seemingly not both cone opsins simultaneously (Hart et al. 2011). Consequently, it appears that sharks lack the potential for cone-based color vision and are potentially completely color blind (see also Schluessel et al. 2014). However, the opsin complement has been characterized in fewer than 3% of the more than 500 extant shark species, and there are substantial taxonomic sampling gaps that prevent a more comprehensive view of their visual opsin diversity and visual ecology.
The visual systems of lamniform sharks are particularly poorly studied compared with other shark species but are of interest given their phylogenetic position and ecological diversity. Extant members of the Lamniformes include the iconic mackerel sharks (Lamnidae), to which belong the CITES-listed great white shark Carcharodon carcharias, and the thresher sharks (Alopiidae), all of which are large, fast swimming, pelagic predators. The order also contains the coastal benthopelagic sand tiger sharks (Odontaspididae) and 2 filter-feeding species, namely the basking shark Cetorhinus maximus (Cetorhinidae) and the rare megamouth shark Megachasma pelagios (Megachasmidae), as well as other deep-water species. Extinct members of the order include the “megatoothed” sharks of which the most notable is the megalodon Otodus megalodon, perhaps the largest shark that has ever lived.
Here, we screened for the presence of visual opsins in de novo assembled retinal transcriptomes of 3 lamniform species: the great white shark (Lamnidae), shortfin mako shark Isurus oxyrinchus (Lamnidae), and the common thresher shark Alopias vulpinus (Alopiidae). For comparison, we also screened for opsins in 6 other ecologically diverse galeomorph shark species from 5 different families. These were the heterodontiform Port Jackson shark H. portusjacksoni (Heterodontidae), and 5 carcharhiniform species: the dusky shark Carcharhinus obscurus (Carcharhinidae), blue shark Prionace glauca (Carcharhinidae), tiger shark Galeocerdo cuvier (Galeocerdonidae), gummy shark Mustelus antarcticus (Triakidae), and Australian swellshark Cephaloscyllium laticeps (Scyliorhinidae). We also screened for associated components of the phototransduction cascade that may affect nonspectral photoreceptor functions such as response kinetics and absolute sensitivity. This study contributes to a growing body of work on the visual capabilities of sharks and provides new sensory data for species that are of commercial significance, are threatened by human activities such as overfishing, and/or are of concern for the safety of people undertaking marine recreational activities.

## Visual Opsin Classes
In all 9 shark species, a complete (355aa) coding sequence was obtained for an RH1 opsin, which is typically expressed in rod photoreceptors. A complete RH2 (355aa) cone opsin coding sequence was found in the retinas of the tiger, blue, gummy, and dusky sharks. Only a partial RH2 sequence (264aa) was found in the Port Jackson shark, presumably resulting from very low transcript levels in this rod-dominated species and/or incomplete sequence assembly. A complete LWS (365 to 367aa) cone opsin coding sequence was found in the retina of shortfin mako, great white, and common thresher sharks. These new opsin sequences have been deposited in GenBank with accession numbers OQ466326 to OQ466342. No cone opsin sequence was obtained from the retina of the Australian swellshark, and we found no evidence for the presence of SWS1 or SWS2 opsins in any shark species, consistent with findings from other studies (Hart et al. 2020). ## RH1 Rod Pigment
The spectral tuning of vertebrate RH1 pigments is known to be influenced by the identity of the amino acid residues at 27 sites in the opsin protein (Musilova et al. 2019). Using conventional numbering based on bovine rod RH1 opsin sequence (GenBank accession number NP 001014890.1) (Palczewski et al. 2000), these sites are: 83, 90, 96, 102, 113, 118, 122, 124, 132, 164, 183, 194, 195, 207, 208, 211, 214, 253, 261, 265, 269, 289, 292, 295, 299, 300, and 317. The shark RH1 pigments identified in the present study differ from each other, and from selected shark RH1 pigments for which the λmax is known (see Table 2), at 7 of these sites: 96, 124, 183, 194, 292, 299, and 300.
By comparing the new RH1 sequences with that of the blacktip reef shark (Carcharhinus melanopterus), which has a rod pigment λmax at 505 nm, we can make some predictions for species where the RH1 pigment λmax is unknown. Substitutions at site 96 only shift the λmax if site 102 is also altered (Yokoyama et al. 2008a), but all of the sharks examined retain Y102, so the effect of valine or isoleucine at site 96 is ignored here. An A124S substitution in bovine RH1 opsin results in a −2 nm shift in λmax but A124G is not expected to have an effect (Hunt et al. 2001; Castiglione and Chang 2018). Based on comparisons within mammalian RH1 pigments, a L183M substitution would be expected to shift the λmax by +2 nm (Zhao et al. 2009). Substitutions at site 194 only shift the λmax substantially when serine rather than alanine is present at site 292 (Yokoyama et al. 2008a), so this is likely only relevant for the Australian swellshark, although the specific effect of P194L is unclear. An A292S substitution, as seen in the Australian swellshark, results in a −10 nm shift in λmax (Janz and Farrens 2001), and an S299A substitution, observed in all sharks except the swellshark, causes a −2 nm shift (Dungan and Chang 2017). The effect of an L300 V is unknown although leucine and valine are functionally similar (Grantham score 32), suggesting that this substitution is unlikely to substantially affect the λmax (Musilova et al. 2019). Taken together, we predict that the rod pigments of the blue, gummy, shortfin mako, great white, and common thresher sharks have λmax values at ∼503 to 505 nm, and that the Australian swellshark has a rod λmax shifted toward shorter wavelengths at ∼495 nm. By comparison, the dusky, tiger, and Port Jackson sharks have measured λmax values at 502, 499, and 496 nm, respectively.

## RH2 Cone Pigment
Sites 83, 97, 122, 207, 292, and 295 (bovine RH1 opsin numbering) are thought to be particularly important for spectral tuning in vertebrate RH2 cone pigments (Davies et al. 2007; Takenaka and Yokoyama 2007; Yokoyama and Jia 2020), and other sites may have smaller effects (Chinen et al. 2005). From previous work, we know that the blacktip reef shark RH2 opsin has DTEMAA at these sites and a λmax at 528 nm (Hart et al. 2020). The dusky shark RH2 pigment has an identical complement of amino acids at these sites, and so we predicted it will have a similar λmax to the blacktip reef shark. The tiger and gummy shark differ from the other sharks by having asparagine rather than aspartic acid at site 83. A D83N substitution is known to cause a shift of up to −9 nm in RH1 pigments (Takahashi and Ebrey 2003) but only −1 nm in RH2 pigments (Yokoyama and Jia 2020). Both species also differ from the blacktip reef shark in that they have A295S; although not always associated with spectral shifts in RH2 pigments, this substitution causes a small shift of up to −5 nm in RH1 pigments (Lin et al. 1998). Thus, the predicted λmax of the tiger and gummy shark RH2 visual pigment is likely to be close to that of the blacktip reef shark but possibly slightly blue-shifted (∼522 nm).
Compared with the blacktip reef shark, the blue shark, and the Port Jackson shark have leucine rather than methionine at site 207. An M207L substitution in engineered ancestral agnathan, tetrapod, and teleost RH2 opsins causes a shift of up to −10 nm (Yokoyama and Jia 2020). The blue shark also differs from the other species by having serine rather than threonine at site 97, although the effect on spectral tuning is unclear. In the chameleon RH2 pigment a T97A substitution causes a −8 nm shift (Takenaka and Yokoyama 2007), but a T97S substitution may be more conservative in its effect on protein function. Overall, the blue shark and Port Jackson shark RH2 pigments are predicted to have a λmax at ∼518 nm (although position 83 remains to be resolved for the Port Jackson shark).

## LWS Cone Pigment
Amino acids at 5 key sites in the opsin explain most of the spectral variation in LWS pigments, i.e. sites 164, 181, 261, 269, and 292 (bovine RH1 pigment numbering, which corresponds to residues 180, 197, 277, 285, and 308 in human M/LWS opsin) (Yokoyama et al. 2008b). Based on measurements of native pigments and site directed mutagenesis studies, the substitutions S164A, H181Y, Y261F, T269A, and A292S result in spectral shifts of −5, −24, −10, −17, and −33 nm, respectively (Yokoyama et al. 2008b). Moreover, 2- and 3-way combinatorial interactions at these sites (e.g. + 3 nm for S164A/A292S) yield further minor shifts in λmax of between −4 and +13 nm (for details see Table 3 in Yokoyama et al. 2008b). LWS pigments in a range of vertebrates with the amino acid complement SHYTA at these sites have λmax values at ∼560 nm (Yokoyama and Radlwimmer 2001). By comparison, the common thresher, great white, and shortfin mako sharks instead have the unusual combination of AHFAS. Based on the foregoing, the LWS pigment of all 3 species would be predicted to have a λmax at ∼501 nm. Likewise, based on known elasmobranch LWS pigments, i.e. those in the brownbanded bamboo shark Chiloscyllium punctatum (SHFAA; λmax 532 nm; Hart et al. 2011; Hart et al. 2020) and the blue-spotted maskray Neotrygon kuhlii (SHFTA; λmax 552 nm; Theiss et al. 2007; Hart et al. 2020), a similar prediction of 500 to 503 nm is obtained.

## Shark RH1 Opsins Have Experienced Episodic Positive Selection at Several Sites
The branch-site unrestricted statistical test for episodic diversification (BUSTED) analyses revealed the presence of gene-wide episodic positive selection in shark RH1 opsin genes, both in the shark-only gene set (P = 0.009) and the expanded cartilaginous fish gene set (P = 0.021). While BUSTED does not provide statistical evidence for positive selection at individual sites, it calculates evidence ratios that indicate which sites could have evolved under positive selection. Using an evidence ratio threshold of 10, the sites identified as potentially under positive selection in the shark RH1 gene set were 89, 93, 107, 124, 194, 213, 217, 277, and 297; and in the cartilaginous fish RH1 gene set were 89, 107, 124, 213, and 277. The mixed effects model of evolution (MEME) analysis subsequently identified several sites under episodic positive selection (threshold P < 0.05) in both the shark-only RH1 gene set (n = 10 sites: 10, 89, 93, 119, 124, 217, 277, 297, 299, and 337) and the expanded cartilaginous fish RH1 gene set (n = 9 sites: 89, 119, 124, 209, 270, 277, 297, 299, and 337). Further details of the BUSTED and MEME analyses are provided in supplementary File S2, Supplementary Material online.
Of the sites identified as being under positive selection in the shark-only RH1 opsin set, only 3 sites (119, 124, 299) are known to affect RH1 pigment spectral tuning, although some of the other sites are close to known spectral tuning sites (supplementary table S6, Supplementary Material online). This may be because there was limited variation in the measured or predicted rod λmax across the 22 RH1 opsin sequences used in the analysis, from 482 nm in the blackmouth catshark Galeus melastomus to 506 nm in the common blacktip shark Carcharhinus limbatus. However, it also suggests that some sites have been positively selected for their effect on nonspectral pigment functions, such as protein stability.

## Predicted Impacts of Amino Acid Sequence on RH1 Pigment Retinal Release Rates
The stability of the light-activated form of rhodopsin (metarhodopsin II) determines the kinetics of light activation and regeneration of the visual pigment and can be inferred experimentally by measuring the rate of release of all-trans-retinal from the opsin following light activation of the visual pigment (van Hazel et al. 2016). Interestingly, in addition to sites 119, 124, and 299, 2 more of the sites identified as being under the influence of positive selection (217 and 297) are known to affect retinal release rates in RH1 pigments (Dungan and Chang 2017; Morrow et al. 2017; Castiglione and Chang 2018; Xia et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2023) (supplementary table S6, Supplementary Material online). In vitro measurements of retinal release rate have now been made of a diverse range of reconstituted wild-type and mutated RH1 (rhodopsin) pigments and this has enabled the identification of key amino acid residues that may influence in vivo metarhodopsin II stability (Morrow and Chang 2015; Guo et al. 2023). These sites are summarized in supplementary table S7, Supplementary Material online.
We compared the amino acid sequences of 16 shark RH1 pigments and our reconstructed ancestral shark RH1 pigment (supplementary table S8, Supplementary Material online) to a reconstructed ancestral gnathostome RH1 pigment having a measured retinal release rate half-life t1/2 of 15.3 min (Liu et al. 2019). The shifts in t1/2 associated with different substitutions are summarized in Fig. 2 and supplementary File S3, Supplementary Material online. The 17 shark sequences differed from the ancestral gnathostome sequence at 27 sites known to be associated with shifts in t1/2, although in many cases, the effect of the substitutions seen at these sites has not been characterized. However, at least 16 of these sites showed variability in one or more shark pigments that have known effects on t1/2. For example, in the great white and shortfin mako sharks, an A124S substitution is predicted to increase t1/2 by 10.2 min (Castiglione and Chang 2018), giving an absolute t1/2 of 25.5 mins and representing a substantial decrease in retinal release rate (an increase in metarhodopsin II stability) when compared with the ancestral gnathostome RH1 pigment. Conversely, in the blue shark, I189 V and I217T are together predicted to decrease t1/2 by 8.6 mins (Morrow and Chang 2015; Guo et al. 2023), giving an absolute t1/2 of 6.7 min and an increase in retinal release rate. In some cases, the calculated aggregate change in retinal release rate (delta t1/2) meant that the absolute t1/2 of the pigment (calculated relative to the ancestral gnathostome RH1 pigment) would be <0 min, indicating that substitutions are not necessarily additive and that complex interactions between sites likely influence the overall t1/2 of the pigment (Castiglione and Chang 2018).

## Proteins Involved in the Shut-off Steps of Phototransduction
Different isoforms of G protein-coupled receptor kinase (GRK) occur in rod and cone photoreceptors. In each case, they work in concert with their respective arrestin (Arr) proteins to prevent photoactivated visual pigment molecules from further activating transducin and, therefore, contribute to shutting off the phototransduction process (Lamb et al. 2018). In all 9 shark species, sequences were obtained for both the GRK1A and GRK7 isoforms. However, only in the common thresher, great white, and shortfin mako sharks, which were also found to express an LWS rather than an RH2 cone opsin, did we find the GRK1B isoform. These sequences have been deposited in GenBank with accession numbers XEL39811 to XEL39831. Combined with the findings of previous studies (Lamb et al. 2018; Hart et al. 2020), this implies that in sharks, and possibly other elasmobranchs, the GRK1B isoform is associated exclusively with LWS-expressing cones. By contrast, RH2-expressing cones likely use the GRK1A and/or GRK7 isoforms. The inferred phylogenetic tree is shown in supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online.
All 9 species of shark were found to express 2 isoforms (Arr-S1 and Arr-S2) of the rod-associated arrestin Arr-S previously identified in cartilaginous fishes (Lamb et al. 2018). The expression levels of RH1 opsin and Arr-S1 (Table 1 and supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online) appeared to show a medium positive correlation (linear mixed model: estimate, b = 0.553; SE = 0.270; t[11.99] = 2.049; P = 0.063) although the relationship was not significant. There was no apparent correlation between RH1 and Arr-S2 (b = 0.147; SE = 0.305; t[9.72] = 0.481; P = 0.641). Although neither of these relationships was significant, it adds support to the suggestion that only Arr-S1 is expressed in the rods (Lamb et al. 2018). The cone-associated isoform of arrestin Arr-C was found in all species except the Australian swellshark, which also lacked a cone opsin; taken together, this suggests that the Australian swellshark has very few or likely no cones in the retina and may be a rod monochromat. The Arr sequences have been deposited in GenBank with accession numbers XEL39832 to XEL39857. The inferred phylogenetic tree is shown in supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online.

## Discussion
Sequencing of the visual opsins present in the 9 species of shark studied here, together with data from previous studies, reveals a complex distribution of cone opsin-type across 4 orders and 10 families of sharks. All species of shark that possess cone photoreceptors appear to be cone monochromats, but the type of cone opsin expressed in the retina differs: lamniform and orectolobiform species possess an LWS opsin, whereas carcharhiniform and heterodontiform species possess an RH2 opsin. It is possible that additional opsin genes could be present in some of these species but not expressed in the retina at certain times or expressed only at undetectable levels. However, inspection of the genomes of the whale shark Rhincodon typus and cloudy catshark Scyliorhinus torazame indicates that opsins have indeed been lost, with RH1 and LWS retained in the whale shark and only RH1 in the cloudy catshark (Hara et al. 2018). Further genomic data will be required to answer this question definitively, but the current results are certainly suggestive of widespread cone monochromacy in sharks.
Most studies of shark phylogeny, based on molecular sequence data (Heinicke et al. 2009; Li et al. 2012; Sorenson et al. 2014; Amaral et al. 2018) and morphological characters (Compagno 1977), indicate that lamniform and carcharhiniforms sharks are most closely related to each other, with the orectolobiforms forming a sister group to this clade and the heterodontiforms sharing a more distant ancestor, as depicted in Fig. 3. Such a relationship would imply that a reversion to cone monochromacy from a dichromatic ancestor has occurred at least 4 times within the galeomorph shark lineage. Other molecular-based phylogenies have determined either (i) that lamniform and orectolobiform sharks are most closely related, with the carcharhiniforms as the closest sister taxon (Mallatt and Winchell 2007), or (ii) that carcharhiniform and orectolobiform sharks are most closely related to each other, with lamniforms and then heterodontiforms as increasingly distant sister taxa (Vélez-Zuazo and Agnarsson 2011). If these alternative phylogenetic relationships are true, then reversion to cone monochromacy has still occurred on at least 3 or 4 occasions, respectively.

The presence of only a single spectral cone type in the shark retina is strongly suggestive of a lack of color vision, as appears to be the case in the very few behavioral tests of color discrimination that have been conducted in sharks (Schluessel et al. 2014). Moreover, based on the identity of amino acid residues at key spectral tuning sites, we predict that the LWS cone pigment in the retina of lamniform sharks has a spectral sensitivity (range of λmax 492 to 501 nm) that overlaps almost completely with that of their rod visual pigment (range of λmax 503 to 505 nm). Consequently, even if the retinal circuitry to compare rod and cone photoreceptor responses were present in these species, there would be no possibility of chromatic discrimination under mesopic illumination conditions when both receptor types are functional.
Potential reasons for the loss of all but one cone type from the retina in some aquatic vertebrate taxa have been discussed elsewhere, both for sharks (Hart et al. 2020) and marine mammals, which have also reverted to cone monochromacy on multiple occasions (Meredith et al. 2013). Given the very different evolutionary trajectories of these taxa, perhaps the most widely applicable explanation for the convergent evolution of cone monochromacy is the potentially deleterious impact of color vision circuitry on the ability to discriminate small differences in visual stimuli, i.e. threshold contrast sensitivity. Inhibitory interactions in color opponent circuits potentially decrease signal-to-noise ratio in the retinal output (Van Hateren 1993; Kelber and Roth 2006). Thus, animals operating in low visual contrast environments such as the ocean (and especially in turbid waters or at depth where light is scarce) may benefit from the use of a single spectral type of photoreceptor under both photopic (cone-based) and scotopic (rod-based) conditions. This appears to be borne out in both sharks and pinnipeds, which can detect brightness differences as small as 2%, a level of performance exceeding that seen in teleost fishes with multiple spectral cone types (Hanke et al. 2011; Ryan et al. 2017). However, it remains to be explained why cone monochromacy appears to be the norm in sharks, whereas multiple cone opsins are present in many bony fishes (Schweikert et al. 2018), rays (Hart et al. 2020), and at least 1 species of chimaera (Davies et al. 2009) that inhabit similar environments. Perhaps the frequent need to change depth rapidly during foraging—a feature common to many predatory sharks, cetaceans and pinnipeds—drove the evolution of a visual system specialized for achromatic rather than chromatic vision.
If indeed there were one or more selection pressures operating on sharks to abandon color vision in favor of improved brightness discrimination, the question then becomes why a particular cone opsin, either RH2 or LWS, was retained. It may have been that the loss of a particular opsin was simply the result of a random mutation, depending on lineage that rendered it nonfunctional, and the resultant phenotype was more successful. Subsequent spectral shifts in λmax may have further improved visual performance. Alternatively, a particular opsin was selected for because it was better suited to the visual environment and/or visual tasks. This could be in terms of the spectral tuning of the visual pigment as it pertains to photon capture, or other properties of the visual pigment (e.g. thermal stability) and associated components of the phototransduction process that affect the sensitivity or temporal response properties of photoreceptors.

## Spectral Tuning of Shark Visual Pigments Reflects Visual Ecology
Except for some orectolobiform sharks, i.e. the wobbegongs, which possess a LWS pigment with a λmax at ∼553 to 561 nm, sharks with LWS opsins have a measured or predicted cone pigment whose λmax falls in the medium-wavelength region of the spectrum (∼492 to 532 nm). This spectral range overlaps substantially with the range of λmax values seen in sharks with RH2 opsins (∼518 to 534 nm), and resembles the situation in teleost fishes, where those species lacking RH2 opsins (but typically also retaining an SWS2 pigment) have tended to shift the LWS cone opsin λmax toward shorter wavelengths (Escobar-Camacho et al. 2020; Musilova and Cortesi 2023). While the narrow range of λmax values of the extant shark RH2 and LWS pigments suggests some convergence in spectral tuning after the loss of the second ancestral cone opsin, it is also likely that differences in cone pigment λmax are adaptive for the visual ecology of each species. In ray-finned fishes, the number of cone opsins present in the retina and the spectral tuning of the cone pigment(s) reflect the amount and spectral distribution of light, which is heavily influenced by habitat type, depth, and diel activity pattern (Loew and Lythgoe 1978; Schweikert et al. 2018; Cortesi et al. 2020). Similarly, we predict that cone pigment λmax in sharks will reflect differences in habitat light as well as visually driven behaviors.
The LWS pigments of lamniform sharks are especially interesting given that they are expected to have λmax values that are considerably blue-shifted (492 to 501 nm) compared with other shark RH2 and LWS cone pigments (518 to 561 nm). Great white sharks frequently attack pinniped prey at the surface of the water using a rapid ascent from below (Martin et al. 2005). Based on an estimated threshold distance (∼3 m) at which a downward-looking seal swimming at the surface can detect a counter-shaded shark swimming below against the upwelling light, and the speed at which the shark can accelerate to top speed (∼5 to 10 m s−1), it has been predicted that the shark should begin its approach from a depth of at least 7 m under typical conditions (Martin and Hammerschlag 2012). Indeed, tagging data and crittercam footage of successful predation events indicate that great white sharks often begin their predatory attack at the limit of underwater visibility (7 to 15 m) (Semmens et al. 2019).
Under these conditions, the shark must detect a dark silhouette with minimal contrast against the lighter background, a visual task that is best achieved using a visual pigment with a λmax that is matched to the spectral distribution of the downwelling light (McFarland and Munz 1975). Great white sharks inhabit both greenish coastal waters and bluer open oceanic waters (Rigby et al. 2019) that will differ markedly in the spectrum of light available for vision (Jerlov 1976). The wavelength of peak transmission of light in these water types occurs between ∼460 and 480 nm for the clearest oceanic waters to between ∼500 and 550 in more coastal waters, depending on the concentration of chlorophyll and dissolved organic matter (Baker and Smith 1982). The λmax of the great white shark LWS pigment (∼501 nm) would therefore be a suitable compromise for such a visual task under a range of optical water conditions.
Shortfin mako sharks are closely related to great white sharks and occupy a similar range of ocean water types; although they spend more time offshore (Compagno 2001). They also tend to stay close to the surface at night, dive deeper during the day, and make rapid ascents to capture prey (Sepulveda et al. 2004). On this basis, it is perhaps unsurprising that the predicted λmax of the shortfin mako shark LWS pigment (∼501 nm) is identical to that of the great white shark.
The common thresher shark, on the other hand, is predicted to have a more blue-shifted LWS pigment λmax (∼492 nm) compared with the great white and shortfin mako sharks. Common thresher sharks more frequently occur in coastal waters over the continental shelf rather than the open ocean (Compagno 2001) and their distribution is more similar to that of the great white shark than the shortfin mako shark (Compagno 2001). Common thresher sharks are primarily diurnal predators (Cartamil et al. 2010) and are known to use their elongated whip-like tail to corral and stun fish in midwater (Aalbers et al. 2010), a behavioral task that presumably relies heavily on vision. In this situation, sharks likely detect and target fish against the underwater horizontal (sidewelling) radiance. At a distance, such a visual task would be aided by a visual pigment with λmax matched to the wavelength of maximum background radiance, but at closer range, and particularly for bright objects such as reflective fishes, visual contrast would be improved by using a visual pigment with λmax offset to shorter or longer wavelengths (Lythgoe 1968). This difference in feeding ecology may to some extent explain the blue-shifted LWS λmax pigment in the common thresher shark.
The λmax values of the RH2 pigments in the dusky, blue, tiger, gummy, and Port Jackson sharks (518 to 528 nm), and the LWS pigment in the bamboo shark (532 nm), are spectrally similar to the typical RH2- and LWS-based cone pigments observed in marine ray-finned fishes, especially those expressed in twin or double cones (∼515 to 535 nm) (Schweikert et al. 2018). Blue sharks and (to a lesser extent) tiger sharks are highly migratory pelagic species that frequently range offshore into clear blue oceanic waters (Nakano and Stevens 2008; Holmes et al. 2014) and see summary in Table 3. This may explain their relatively blue-shifted RH2 pigments (λmax values 518 and 522 nm, respectively) compared with the more coastal/pelagic shelf-associated dusky shark (λmax 528 nm) and the inshore-dwelling bamboo shark (λmax 532 nm) (Last and Stevens 2009), which likely encounter more turbid and/or greenish water. This shift in cone pigment λmax toward shorter wavelengths with increasing offshore activity is likely adaptive in providing maximum visual sensitivity by tuning pigment spectral sensitivity to match the most abundant wavelengths present in the different water types (Lythgoe et al. 1994).
The gummy shark (λmax 522 nm) and Port Jackson shark (λmax 518 nm) are shallow-dwelling coastal species (Last and Stevens 2009) and so their cone pigments might be expected to be more similar to those of the dusky or bamboo sharks. However, their relatively blue-shifted λmax values may reflect the fact that both species are predominantly active at night (Barnett et al. 2010; Kadar et al. 2019). Visual pigments can be activated by thermal energy alone, a process known as thermal isomerization, which contributes to the so-called “dark noise’ of photoreceptors and raises the threshold for visual detection (Ala-Laurila et al. 2004). The probability of thermal isomerization is low but physiologically relevant, and increases with λmax. It follows that nocturnal species, or those needing vision in dim-light, should have visual pigments with λmax at shorter wavelengths, and this is thought to be why most terrestrial vertebrates have rod pigments with a λmax close to 500 nm, despite the fact that more light is often available at longer wavelengths (500 to 560 nm) (Ala-Laurila et al. 2004; Osorio and Vorobyev 2005). The same trend is evident in cone pigments with, for example, nocturnal geckos having blue-shifted λmax values compared with diurnal species (Ellingson et al. 1995), and this is perhaps reflected in the RH2 pigments of the dusky and Port Jackson sharks. Similarly, the predicted spectral sensitivities of the shark RH1 pigments conforms to a general trend in fishes where strongly nocturnal and/or deep-diving species have their rod λmax values shifted toward shorter wavelengths, with the Australian swellshark and Port Jackson shark having predicted and measured λmax values at ∼495 and 496 nm, respectively, and the more diurnal and/or shallow-dwelling sharks having λmax values at ∼503 to 505 nm (Hart et al. 2020). This highlights the fact that visual pigment spectral tuning is likely the result of selection pressures acting on both spectral and nonspectral aspects of pigment function (Castiglione et al. 2017).

## Photopigment Kinetics and Phototransduction Proteins
Over a wide range of light intensities, the sensitivity of the visual system to temporal changes in image contrast is limited by the response kinetics of the rod and cone photoreceptors, which are largely determined by the physicochemical properties of their visual pigments and related components of the phototransduction cascade (Umino et al. 2019).

## Retinal Release Rates are Predicted to Vary Widely Across Shark RH1 Rod Pigments
In addition to spectral tuning of the visual pigment, some opsin amino acid substitutions are thought to influence photoreceptor kinetics by altering the rate of either formation or decay of metarhodopsin II, which is the active (G protein-binding) photointermediate of the visual pigment (Sugawara et al. 2009; van Hazel et al. 2016). For example, a D83N substitution in some cichlid rhodopsins, which causes only a modest (2 to 4 nm) blue-shift in rod λmax, more than doubles the rate of formation of metarhodopsin II immediately following light activation, potentially increasing the amplitude of the rising phase of the rod photoresponse and thus dim-light visual sensitivity (Sugawara et al. 2009). Although not observed in the shark species characterized in this study, N83 is observed in some deep-living sharks, such as the blackmouth catshark G. melastomus (Bozzano et al. 2001), which would benefit from adaptations that increase sensitivity in such dimly lit habitats.
Substitutions that alter the stability of metarhodopsin II may also be important for the process of dark adaptation, i.e. recovery from photobleaching. Following its deactivation by Arr, metarhodopsin II decays and releases all-trans-retinal, a necessary step in regenerating the visual pigment (Heck et al. 2003). Lower metarhodopsin II stability would mean a more rapid decay and faster rate of retinal release, and thus faster pigment regeneration and recovery of photosensitivity (van Hazel et al. 2016; Dungan and Chang 2017). This would be adaptive for animals that experience rapidly changing light levels, such as deep-diving aquatic species (Dungan and Chang 2017; Xia et al. 2021; Gai et al. 2023). Indeed, while many of the sharks examined in this study (e.g. blue shark and tiger shark) spend much of their time in shallow water <50 m deep, they also frequently dive to over 1000 m (Andrzejaczek et al. 2022). Conversely, cross-species comparisons indicate that slow rates of retinal release (i.e. high metarhodopsin II stability) are correlated with nocturnality and the need to see well in dim-light (Gutierrez et al. 2018).
While empirical measurements are lacking, based on the known effects of amino acid substitutions on retinal release rates in the rhodopsins of other species, we speculate that there is substantial interspecific variability in metarhodopsin II stability in shark RH1 pigments (Fig. 2) and that this likely reflects their life history and visual ecology (Liu et al. 2019). For example, our analysis suggests that Moller's lanternshark (Etmopterus molleri), a mesopelagic species that employs bioluminescence for camouflage or communication (Claes et al. 2014), has a very slow rate of retinal release compared with epipelagic species that would be adaptive for enhancing absolute sensitivity in very dim habitats. By contrast, shallow-dwelling and predominantly diurnal species such as the blacktip reef shark are predicted to have rod pigments with a considerably faster rate of retinal release, which would help to ensure high temporal contrast sensitivity suitable for detecting rapid motion.
The interspecific variability in retinal release half-life estimated for the lamnid sharks RH1 pigments is intriguing, with the fastest rate of retinal release (i.e. shortest t1/2) predicted in the common thresher shark, and far slower retinal release rates predicted for the great white shark and shortfin mako shark, even though all 3 species hunt fast-moving prey in well-lit surface waters. One key difference between these species is in their hunting mode, with the common thresher shark frequently using its whip-like tail to strike at and stun fast-moving fishes (Aalbers et al. 2010), a behavior that may require a more rapid processing of visual information (higher temporal resolution) compared with the pursuit and ambush behaviors of great white and shortfin mako sharks (Semmens et al. 2019; Waller et al. 2023).
An alternative explanation may lie in differences between these species in their thermoregulatory physiology. Some fishes, including billfishes, tunas, and lamniform sharks, possess vascular counter-current heat exchangers (retia mirabilia) that transfer heat generated by contraction of skeletal red muscle to the arterial blood supply feeding the muscles and viscera, allowing them to maintain a body temperature that is higher than the surrounding water, a process known as regional endothermy (Block and Carey 1985; Dickson and Graham 2004). Lamnid sharks, including the shortfin mako shark and great white shark, also possess orbital retia that heat the blood feeding the eyes and brain, keeping them up to 5 °C warmer than the surrounding water (Block and Carey 1985). Neural activity in the retina and brain is strongly influenced by temperature: in swordfish (Xiphias gladius) and tuna (Thunnus spp.), which use different mechanisms to heat the eyes and brain (Carey 1982; Dickson and Graham 2004), the additional heat provided to the retina is thought to dramatically improve temporal resolution and, consequently, their ability to detect rapid motion (Fritsches et al. 2005). However, the common thresher shark lacks orbital retia, and it is unclear if any warming is provided to the eyes (Block and Carey 1985; Alexander 1998). Accelerated decay of light-activated rhodopsin (i.e. shorter retinal release half-life) is observed in the RH1 pigments of fishes that are adapted to cold water (Castiglione et al. 2023) whereas endotherms such as mammals and birds typically have relatively long retinal release half-lives (van Hazel et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2023). This pattern may explain the apparently very short retinal release half-life predicted for the common thresher shark, without any ocular heating, and the relatively long half-life predicted for the shortfin mako and great white sharks.
The predicted retinal release rates for some species, such as the very rapid rate estimated for the Port Jackson shark, are not so easily explained. In part, this may be because epistatic interactions between sites mean that substitutions are not necessarily additive in their effects and the effect on t1/2 of substitutions at many of the identified sites in shark RH1 opsins are unknown. Our predictions of retinal release rate are necessarily tentative, and further work will be required to accurately characterize the variation in shark RH1 pigments, but this is likely to be informative given that many of the opsin sites identified as being under positive selection are potentially related to photopigment kinetics.

## Cone Opsin-type-specific GRK Proteins Indicate Further Differentiation of Shark Cone Photoreceptor Function
Temporal contrast sensitivity is also dependent on the rate at which metarhodopsin II is deactivated and thus prevented from further activating the phototransduction cascade (via transducin). Deactivation is a 2-stage process, with metarhodopsin II first phosphorylated by a GRK and then quenched by Arr (Renninger et al. 2011). While the binding of Arr is the dominant process in the deactivation of metarhodopsin II (Lamb 2022), it is known that different GRK isoforms can have very different levels of enzymatic activity that may influence photoreceptor sensitivity and response kinetics (Wada et al. 2006).
Here, we found that while all shark species expressed GRK1A and GRK7, only those species with an LWS cone opsin expressed GRK1B. This supports earlier findings of GRK1B expression in the retina of the brownbanded bamboo shark C. punctatum, which has an LWS opsin, and the blue-spotted maskray N. kuhlii, which expresses both RH2 and LWS cone opsins, but not the gray reef shark Carcharhinus amblyrhynchos, which possesses only an RH2 cone opsin (Lamb et al. 2018). In the Australian swellshark, we detected neither a cone opsin nor the cone isoform of arrestin Arr-C, which suggests that this species either lacks cones or they are present in extremely low numbers. Nevertheless, expression levels of GRK1A and GRK7 were comparable to other species (supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online), which implies that both isoforms are co-expressed in rods. We cannot yet determine whether the RH2 and LWS cones also contain GRK1A and/or GRK7, although cones express GRK7 in most jawed vertebrates (Lamb et al. 2018). However, the fact that different spectral types of cones in elasmobranchs possess different complements of GRK isoforms may indicate functional differences in their photoresponses that could have contributed to selection for one or other cone opsin-type in the different shark lineages. This is clearly a potentially valuable line of enquiry for understanding how selection operates on aspects of phototransduction beyond just the visual opsins.

## Materials and Methods

## Sample Collection and Processing
Retinal tissue was collected from 9 species of shark comprising 3 orders and 7 families within the Galeomorphii (specimen numbers and morphometric details are provided in supplementary table S9, Supplementary Material online). Samples were taken from 2 different individuals of each species, except for the Australian swellshark (n = 1) and the Port Jackson shark (n = 3). Sharks were donated from other projects, from recreational and commercial fishers, or collected under Animal Research Authorities approved by the ethics committees of Macquarie University (ARA2017/039), the University of Tasmania (A0015366), and the University of Newcastle (A-2016-621); as well as AFMA Scientific Permit No. 1003182, NSW DPI Scientific Collection Permit s37, Permit No. P01/0059(A)-3.0, and PIRSA Ministerial Exemption ME9902972.
The retina, often with a small amount of vitreous and choroid attached, was removed from each eye as soon as possible after death and preserved in RNAlater (Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were stored at room temperature for a few hours and then transferred to −30 °C for long-term storage. Retinas were thawed on ice and homogenized in TRIzol Reagent (Ambion). Total RNA was extracted and purified using a PureLink RNA Mini Kit with a PureLink DNase treatment step following the kit instructions (Invitrogen). Sample integrity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA samples were submitted to the Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics (UNSW Sydney, Australia) for further quality control, cDNA library preparation, and next-generation sequencing (Illumina NextSeq 500 or NovaSeq 6000). Further details regarding the next-generation sequencing process are provided in supplementary table S9, Supplementary Material online.

## Read Processing and de novo Transcriptome Assembly
Sequencing produced between ∼34 and ∼57 M paired-end read pairs per sample, which were used to generate de novo retinal transcriptomes for each species. Processing of raw reads and transcriptome assembly was done using the Australian National Computational Infrastructure supercomputer Gadi, which runs Rocky Linux v8.6.
Demultiplexed raw read files provided by the Ramaciotti Centre were concatenated into R1 (antisense) and R2 (sense) read sets, and read quality was assessed using FastQC (Andrews 2019). Reads were corrected for sequencing errors using Rcorrector v1.0.5 (Song and Florea 2015) and then processed to remove unfixable reads with the python script FilterUncorrectablePEfastq (Freedman 2016). Corrected reads were then processed to remove adapters and low-quality bases using Trim Galore! (Kreuger 2019) with default settings, except length = 36, quality = 5.
An over-assembly (i.e. multiassembler and multikmer) and reduction approach was used to generate a single transcriptome for each species as this method tends to yield the largest number and most accurate set of unique contigs in a de novo assembled transcriptome (MacManes 2018). The cleaned and trimmed paired-end reads output by Trim Galore! were used as input for 3 different de novo assembly tools. SPADES v3.15.5 (Bushmanova et al. 2019) was used with default parameters, including auto-kmer selection. Trans-ABySS v2.0.1 (Robertson et al. 2010) was used with default parameters (including k = 32), except length = 250. Trinity v2.12.0 (Grabherr et al. 2011) was run using a modified version of the scripts written for a staged workflow on multiple nodes of the Gadi supercomputer (Chew et al. 2021), with default parameters and without in silico normalization of reads. The 3 assemblies produced were combined and reduced using the trformat and tr2aacds scripts of the EvidentialGene (Gilbert 2019) workflow using default settings.
Additionally, for the great white shark, we used Trinity in genome-guided assembly mode using the NCBI sCarCar2.pri C. carcharias annotation release (GenBank assembly accession GCF_017639515.1) as a reference. The genome fasta (.fna) file was indexed, and cleaned reads aligned to the index using HiSat2 (Kim et al. 2019). A coordinate-sorted.bam file was generated using Samtools v1.12 (Li et al. 2009) and used as input to Trinity. We set the maximum intron size to be 500,000 bp given that intron lengths over 100,000 bp have been reported in sharks (Hara et al. 2018). This genome-guided assembly was subsequently combined with the other de novo assemblies for the great white shark as described above.
Final assemblies were checked for completeness using the BUSCO (v5.5.0) software package (Simão et al. 2015) hosted on the Galaxy online workbench (usegalaxy.org) (Community 2022). Protein sequences were queried against the vertebrata_odb10 database (Creation date: 2021 February 19, number of genomes: 67, number of BUSCOs: 3354).

## Transcript Quantification, Annotation, and Phylogenetic Analysis
Transcript abundance (transcripts per million, TPM) was assessed using RSEM v1.3.3 (Li and Dewey 2011). In each case, the cleaned reads from each individual sample were aligned against a reference generated from the de novo assembly (constructed from combined samples for each species) using Bowtie2 v2.2.5 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). Correlations between the expression levels of the different opsins (fixed effect) and selected putative phototransduction genes (response variable) were assessed with linear mixed models in R v4.4.2 (R Core Team 2024) using the packages lme4 v1.1.35.5 (Bates et al. 2015) and lmerTest v3.1.3 (Kuznetsova et al. 2017). In each case, TPM values for each gene were standardized using the scale() function, and samples were grouped by species (random effect), giving 2 replicates for most species, 3 for the Port Jackson shark, and 1 for the Australian swellshark.
To identify putative visual opsin and related phototransduction genes, each de novo transcriptome was screened (blastx) against a custom database of visual and nonvisual opsins and phototransduction genes (supplementary File S4, Supplementary Material online) using the DIAMOND sequence aligner v2.0.4 (Buchfink et al. 2015) with default parameters, except max-target-seqs = 1.
To generate a phylogenetic tree of the newly identified shark opsins, these were aligned with a selection of other vertebrate visual opsins from each of the 5 opsin classes, together with a set of VAL opsin outgroup sequences. Protein sequences (n = 85) with stop codons removed were aligned using the online version (v7) of MAFFT (Katoh et al. 2017) with default parameters, with the L-INS-i iterative refinement strategy selected automatically (supplementary File S1, Supplementary Material online). Next, a maximum likelihood tree was constructed using IQ-TREE web server (multicore version 1.6.12) using default parameters (Nguyen et al. 2014; Trifinopoulos et al. 2016). The model used for tree reconstruction (LG + I + G4) was selected automatically based on the lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC) score (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017). Branch support was calculated in IQ-TREE (1,000 replicates) using the ultrafast bootstrap approximation (Hoang et al. 2017). The maximum likelihood tree was formatted for display using iTOL (version 6) (Letunic and Bork 2024). An identical methodology was used to confirm the identity and phylogenetic relationships of the putative GRK and Arr sequences obtained from the screening step, with different outgroup sequences (supplementary files S5 and S6, Supplementary Material online).

## Prediction of Visual Pigment λmax Values
To facilitate predictions of visual pigment spectral sensitivity based on the identity of amino acids at known spectral tuning sites, separate multiple sequence alignments for each opsin class were obtained using the blastp algorithm (Altschul et al. 1997; Sayers et al. 2024). In each case, the new shark visual opsin protein sequences were aligned against a reference elasmobranch opsin sequence for which the visual pigment λmax value is known (supplementary figs. S3 to S5, Supplementary Material online). The literature was then searched for known amino acid substitutions that affect visual pigment spectral tuning, either singly or in combination, and spectral shifts were calculated accordingly (Musilova et al. 2019).

## Selection Analysis
We tested for evidence of episodic positive/diversifying selection within a set of 22 shark RH1 opsin genes (supplementary File S7, Supplementary Material online) and a second set of 30 cartilaginous fish RH1 opsins that included all the shark RH1 opsins plus additional sequences from rays and the elephant shark (supplementary File S8, Supplementary Material online). In each case, the RH1 nucleotide sequences with stop codons removed were first aligned with MAFFT (version 7) (Katoh et al. 2017). All analyses were conducted using HyPhy (Hypothesis Testing using Phylogenies; version 2.5) (Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2019) running on the Datamonkey Adaptive Evolution Server (Weaver et al. 2018).
First, we used the BUSTED (version 4.5) to test for gene-wide selection assuming site-to-site synonymous rate variation (Murrell et al. 2015; Wisotsky et al. 2020). For the shark RH1 sequence set, the entire phylogeny was selected as the foreground partition; for the cartilaginous RH1 sequence set, the shark lineages were selected as the foreground partition, with the ray and elephant shark sequences acting as the background partition. Subsequently, we used the MEME (version 4.0) to identify those sites evolving under positive selection on a proportion of branches (Murrell et al. 2012).

## Reconstruction of an Ancestral Shark RH1 Opsin Sequence
Complete RH1 opsin DNA coding sequences were obtained for 19 shark species (including the new sequences for 9 species obtained in this study) and the 8 outgroup species also used in the selection analysis (the elephant shark Callorhinchus milii and 7 ray species). Nucleotide sequences with stop codons removed were aligned using MAFFT (version 7) with the alignment algorithm (L-INS-i) selected automatically (Katoh et al. 2017) and are available as supplementary File S9, Supplementary Material online. A maximum likelihood tree (supplementary File S10, Supplementary Material online) was constructed using IQ-TREE web server (multicore version 1.6.12) using default parameters (Nguyen et al. 2014; Trifinopoulos et al. 2016). The model used for tree reconstruction (K3P + I + G4) was selected automatically based on the lowest BIC score (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017). Ancestral sequence reconstruction was performed with FastML (Ashkenazy et al. 2012) using a codon-based evolutionary model (M5) of selection (Nielsen and Yang 1998) and a gamma distribution to model among-site rate variation.

## Prediction of RH1 Pigment Retinal Release Rates
To explore potential interspecific variability in shark rod photoreceptor kinetics, we searched the literature to identify amino acid substitutions that are associated with shifts in the retinal release rate of light-activated RH1 pigments, following the approach used for teleost fishes by Fogg et al. (2023). These sites are summarized in supplementary table S7, Supplementary Material online. In the absence of any empirical measurements of retinal release rates in cartilaginous fish visual pigments, the effects of different amino acid substitutions on shark RH1 pigment kinetics at these sites are necessarily tentative. However, previous studies on wild-type and mutant RH1 pigments suggest that substitutions at some key sites have similar effects on retinal release rate in widely divergent taxa (e.g. Castiglione et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2023). Thus, we compared the amino acid sequences of 16 shark RH1 opsins, selected based on the species’ different ecological characteristics, and our reconstructed ancestral shark RH1 sequence (see above), against a reconstructed ancestral gnathostome RH1 pigment having a measured λmax at 500 nm and a retinal release rate half-life t1/2 of 15.3 min (Liu et al. 2019). We assumed that the effects of amino acid substitutions on t1/2 are additive and that where the reverse mutation is unknown it has an equal but opposite effect to the forward mutation. In many instances, this appears to be a reasonable assumption; for example, the single mutations of L88F and P194L on their own result in increases in t1/2 of 0.6 and 6.1 min, respectively, whereas the double mutant shows an increase in t1/2 of 7.1 min (Xia et al. 2021). Similarly, a H195T mutation causes a 6.9 min decrease in t1/2 that is comparable to the 4.9 min increase in t1/2 caused by the reverse T195H mutation (Gai et al. 2023). However, in separate studies, an F88L mutation caused a decrease in t1/2 by 10.7 min (Guo et al. 2023) whereas the reverse L88F mutation caused an increase in t1/2 by only 0.6 min (Xia et al. 2021), indicating the need for caution in making and interpreting these predictions.

## Figure Fig. 3.: Chronogram of extant cartilaginous fishes showing the phylogenetic distribution of different opsin classes. Galeomorph shark lineages where a reversion to cone monochromacy has occurred from a presumed dichromatic ancestor are marked with the type of opsin lost. Hol stands for superorder Holocephalimorpha, here represented by the elephant shark (Chimaeriformes), which has 2 LWS cone opsins in addition to an RH2 cone opsin (Davies et al. 2009). Most batoids (rays) express both RH2 and LWS cone opsins (e.g. Hart et al. 2020), except for skates (Rajiformes) which have a rod-only retina (O’Brien et al. 1997). The color of the cone opsin triangle is indicative of the spectral location of the pigment λmax as measured directly or predicted from the opsin sequence. In the case of the Orectolobiformes, the bicolor cone triangle indicates the substantial range of LWS cone λmax observed in members of this order, from the red-shifted LWS opsin expressed in wobbegong sharks (553 to 561 nm) to the blue-shifted LWS opsin expressed in the whale shark (λmax ∼492 to 504 nm; based on opsin amino acid sequence [Fasick et al. 2019]). Tree topology and divergence times (millions of years ago) were taken from (Heinicke et al. 2009). The collapsed Rhinopristiformes clade reflects the recent inclusion in this order of the Rhiniformes, Rhinobatiformes, Pristiformes, and Rhynchobatiformes (Aschliman et al. 2012).
