# First systematic experimental 2D mapping of linearly polarized γ-ray polarimetric distributions in relativistic Compton scattering

## Abstract
ABSTRACTThe interaction of photons with relativistic electrons constitutes a fundamental electromagnetic process whose polarization-transfer mechanics remain incompletely characterized. We report the first systematic measurement of the spatial polarization distribution for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays generated via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} slant inverse Compton scattering (ICS) between linearly polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $0.117 \,\mathrm{eV}$\end{document} photons and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $3.5 \,\mathrm{GeV}$\end{document} electrons, performing full two-dimensional mapping of the intensity, angle of polarization (AOP) and degree of polarization (DOP). The measurements reveal an asymmetric beam profile along the laser polarization direction that resembles observations from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} backward ICS. The central beam region exhibits DOP near 1.0, with the AOP rigidly aligned at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document}, while peripheral regions display complex, non-uniform polarization distributions. These findings confirm quantum electrodynamics predictions of near-complete polarization transfer along the beam axis in slant geometries, thereby establishing slant scattering as a viable alternative to head-on configurations for generating high-DOP \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays.

## INTRODUCTION
The interaction of photons with electrons is one of the most fundamental electromagnetic processes and has been systematically studied over a long history since the 1920s [1,2]. Within the theoretical framework of quantum electrodynamics (QED), scattered photons naturally inherit the polarization information of the incident photons. If the incident electrons are relativistic—in other words, endowed a high momentum—then scattered photons propagate predominantly along the direction of the electron momentum [3–7].
The properties of photons scattered from electrons can be well described by QED [6]. Especially in the non-relativistic case, by employing the Lorentz-invariant Stokes parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\xi _i$\end{document} [8], the spatial distributions of the degree of polarization (DOP) and angle of polarization (AOP) can be obtained [9,10]. For instance, in interactions between completely linearly polarized incident photons (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\xi _2=0$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\xi _1^2 + \xi _3^2=1$\end{document}) and non-relativistic electrons, QED predicts that the scattered photons remain linearly polarized, with the spatial intensity distribution exhibiting azimuthal asymmetry [4,6]. Furthermore, non-relativistic Compton scattering of polarized photons by electrons has been experimentally validated with high precision and successfully applied in Compton camera technology [11] and astrophysics [12–14].
In relativistic regimes, however, QED-based descriptions of inverse Compton-scattered photon properties necessitate more sophisticated treatments, and approximations remain nearly unavoidable during theoretical derivations and calculations [3,15,16]. Recently, multiple research groups have simulated polarization distributions of photons scattered from collisions between polarized photon and relativistic electron beam using Monte Carlo methods within QED or classical electrodynamics frameworks, yielding consistent results [8,15–21].
Over the past two decades, experimental investigations into the polarization properties of inverse Compton scattering have progressed through several key milestones. Initial studies focused on the spatial intensity of polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays in the standard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 \,{}^{\circ }$\end{document} backscattering geometry [16,22]. A subsequent study achieved a significant milestone at HI\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}S by quantifying the DOP of linear polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays in this geometry, although the measurements were confined to the center of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray beam [23]. Tsinghua University also experimentally demonstrated the average DOP in the central region of Thomson-scattered X-rays [18]. More recently, research has expanded to non-standard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $90 \,{}^{\circ }$\end{document} geometries at UVSOR-III and has employed advanced laser modes [24,25]. In particular, a systematic measurement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays with different polarization states was performed at different regions of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray spot [26]. Nevertheless, experimental data on the spatial distributions of the DOP and AOP of photons scattered from relativistic electrons remain neither systematic nor sufficient.
The scarcity of experimental data on the polarization properties of photons scattered from relativistic electrons hinders both theoretical and experimental research. In the absence of systematic data on the spatial distributions of the DOP and AOP of scattered photons, the above-mentioned theoretical models of photon–relativistic electron interactions cannot be systematical verified. In specialized experimental scenarios involving the inverse Compton scattering (ICS) source, such data are even more indispensable [27–34], as they directly determine the accuracy of polarization-dependent measurements [35,36]. Beyond fundamental science, these precious polarization parameters are also crucial for advancing frontier applications such as nuclear security interrogation [37,38], polarized positron sources [39] and astrophysical observation [14].

## Intensity azimuthal distribution
Figure 2a shows the measured spatial distributions of the incident \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays, acquired using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray image system. This system relies on scintillator fluorescence captured by a CCD camera, offering direct 2D \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray imaging capability that is essential for spatially polarization analysis. In contrast, previous studies [22,24–26] have employed pixelated detectors, such as MiniPIX and CdTe image sensors, which provide only beam-profile information. Although the optical response of scintillators and system scattering effects can complicate absolute intensity calibration [49–51], the imaging system delivers indispensable spatial information for beam alignment and AOP mapping.
For accurate intensity measurements, we complemented the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray imaging system with a BGO detector, which provides a well-understood and calibrated response to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays, enabling direct and reliable quantification of the intensity—a capability that has been extensively validated in prior experiments [49–51]. This hybrid approach ensures comprehensive spatial mapping and quantification, advancing beyond methods that rely solely on pixelated detectors. The comparative analysis in Fig. 2b quantifies this difference along the beam-spot axes. To our knowledge, this represents the first systematic measurement of linearly polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray intensity distributions using scanned BGO detection in combination with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray imaging system.
These results clearly show that the intensity distribution of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays is not continuously rotationally symmetric but is instead ‘pinched’ along the AOP of the laser beam. This behaviour is consistent with prior investigations at HI\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}S [23] and NewSUBARU [22] under \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} backward ICS, as well as at UVSOR-III under \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $90 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} slant ICS [24].

## Asymmetry and DOP distribution
The average DOP of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray beam within the 1-mm-diameter collimator was obtained at all scanning points using Equation (8) and is shown in Fig. 5a. The uncertainty in the DOP arises mainly from statistical errors (less than 5.6% for the R0 data), efficiency-correction errors (less than 3.2%) and geometry-correction errors (less than 0.3%; see Section S5.2).
(a) DOP distribution, where the blue peanut-shaped contour indicates the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray spot and the pink circle represents \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1/\gamma _{L}$\end{document}. (b) Comparison between the experimental results and calculations based on QED [52].
Near the beam center, the measured DOP is close to 1.0 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1.03\pm 0.13$\end{document} at R0) whereas significant fluctuations are observed in the peripheral regions. Figure 5b compares experimental and theoretical DOP values along four scanning directions, showing good agreement.
Near-complete polarization transfer from the laser photons to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays is observed at the beam center for an incident angle of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document}. This result demonstrates that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} slant scattering provides an alternative pathway to generating high-DOP \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays beyond the traditional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} backward ICS.
Chi [20] extended polarization-property calculations from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} backward ICS to arbitrary slant geometries using a dipole-radiation model, predicting geometry-independent polarization transfer while noting degraded DOP and bandwidth with increasing collection angle. Similarly, QED-based simulations reported in [19] confirm this conclusion. Our measured peripheral DOP exhibits analogous spatial variations. Moreover, in previous work, under the assumption that the incident laser was 100% polarized, the DOP of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays scattered at an angle of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1/\gamma _{L}$\end{document} was predicted to be close to 100%. However, under practical detection conditions, which involve averaging the polarization of photons over a solid angle, we found that the DOP decreases. Starting from the central point of the entire \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray spot, the AOP line can be extended and intersects the circle corresponding to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1/\gamma _{L}$\end{document} scattering angle at two points. Near these two intersection points, the AOP of the photons exhibits rapid variations, analogous to the distribution of electric field lines around a point charge. Consequently, although the polarization degree of each individual photon is theoretically 1 according to polarization-transfer arguments, the average DOP within a 1 mm collimation aperture near these two regions is expected to decrease, as reflected by the two valleys in the QED-calculated curve shown in the upper-right panel of Fig. 5b. Unfortunately, the current experimental uncertainties prevent definitive quantitative validation of the theoretical DOP distributions in the peripheral regions, thereby limiting a complete characterization of polarization-transfer efficiency in slant scattering geometries. In high-energy ICS sources, the contribution of peripheral \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays becomes non-negligible owing to collimator limitations and could introduce systematic errors in polarization-sensitive measurements. This highlights the need for higher-precision polarization measurements in slant ICS experiments.

## CONCLUSION
Addressing a critical knowledge gap in the polarization properties of photons scattered from relativistic electrons, we have conducted the first systematic measurement of the spatial polarization distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays generated via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} slant inverse Compton scattering between linearly polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $0.117 \,\mathrm{eV}$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\lambda$\end{document} = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $10.64 \,\mathrm{{\mu }m}$\end{document}) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$\end{document} laser photons and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $3.5 \,\mathrm{GeV}$\end{document} relativistic electrons. Leveraging the fundamental principle that linearly polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays induce asymmetric angular distributions in secondary scattered photons, we systematically measured intensity profiles, the AOP, asymmetry parameters and the DOP. The resulting linearly polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray beam exhibits an asymmetric intensity profile, consistent with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} backward ICS observations at HI\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}S [23] and NewSUBARU [22], with the beam spot ‘pinched’ along the laser AOP direction. At the beam center, the AOP strictly aligns with the short-axis direction at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} (consistent with HI\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}S observations [23]), and the DOP measures almost 1.0. Near the beam axis, the AOP remains uniformly distributed around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document}, whereas the peripheral regions exhibit tangential alignment relative to concentric circles and complex DOP variations.
These results demonstrate equivalent polarization-transfer efficiency near the beam axis for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $45 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} slant ICS and traditional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $180 {}^{\circ }$\end{document} backward ICS, thereby establishing slant scattering as a viable alternative for generating high-DOP \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document} rays and significantly expanding the parameter space for polarized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\gamma$\end{document}-ray sources. The uncertainties in the present experiment limit precise quantification of the DOP in the peripheral regions, precluding a full characterization of polarization-transfer efficiency in slant geometries. Future investigations are planned at SLEGS to achieve enhanced measurements of the polarization distribution.
Furthermore, this work lays the foundation for a distinct and complementary approach to beam modulation. By employing a precision-controlled collimator for spatial filtering, key beam properties—including energy, monochromaticity, intensity and polarization—can be selectively tuned in a simple, cost-effective and geometry-independent manner, without modulating the electron parameters, laser parameters or interaction geometry. This ‘post-generation’ spatial-filtering method could significantly simplify the design and operation of future ICS sources for applications requiring tailored beam profiles.
