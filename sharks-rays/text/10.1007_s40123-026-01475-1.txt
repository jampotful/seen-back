# Initial Visual and Refractive Outcomes of Ray-Tracing-Guided LASIK (Wavelight Plus) Versus KLEx (SMILE Pro)

## Abstract
AbstractIntroductionThis study evaluates visual and refractive outcomes of our initial experience using ray-tracing-guided Wavelight Plus (WLP) LASIK and SMILE Pro KLEx carried out by one surgeon at a single center.MethodsA retrospective, non-randomized study was conducted at one refractive surgery center. Patients undergoing WLP or SMILE Pro with at least 1 month of postoperative follow-up were included. Outcomes were assessed at 1 and 3 months and included uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), safety and efficacy indices, and vector analysis of astigmatism. Additionally, a comparison with wavefront-optimized (WFO) LASIK, an established procedure with a well-refined nomogram at the study center, was provided as an internal benchmark.ResultsA total of 160 eyes from 93 patients were analyzed, including 50 eyes treated with WLP, 55 with SMILE Pro, and 55 with WFO. WLP demonstrated significantly better UDVA than SMILE Pro at both 1 month (p < 0.001) and 3 months (p = 0.033) and was more likely to achieve a UDVA of 20/20. Residual astigmatism was significantly lower in the WLP group than in both the WFO and SMILE Pro groups at 1 and 3 months (all p < 0.001). Vector analysis showed a correction index of 1.13 for SMILE Pro, 1.11 for WFO, and 1.08 for WLP at 3 months.ConclusionsIn our initial experience with the WLP and SMILE Pro platforms, WLP achieved better UDVA and more precise astigmatic correction compared with SMILE Pro. Additionally, WLP, without the use of a nomogram, had comparable safety and efficacy outcomes to WFO. Based on our initial experience at one center, astigmatic correction appears to be a limiting factor for KLEx platforms in achieving outcomes similar to modern LASIK platforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40123-026-01475-1.Keywords: Wavelight Plus, SMILE Pro, LASIK, KLEx, Wavefront optimized, Ray Tracing Guided, Visumax 800, Nine standard graphs, Vector analysis

## Key Summary Points

## Introduction
Impressive advances have been made in the field of refractive surgery in the last few years. Improvements in excimer laser technology have led to the development of increasingly customizable and precise treatments, allowing correction of patient-specific higher-order aberrations (HOAs). Historically, wavefront-optimized (WFO) LASIK, a treatment designed to minimize the induction of aberrations, has been the most commonly utilized LASIK modality [1]. This platform, however, primarily relies on measurements of the cornea and does not model refractive interactions of the entire eye. To address this limitation, ray-tracing-guided LASIK was developed, creating a custom eye model that simulates the path of light rays through major refractive structures of the eye until an ideal wavefront is achieved [2]. In March 2025, Wavelight Plus (WLP) LASIK was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), emerging as a novel approach utilizing ray-tracing to customize LASIK [3, 4]. Using an artificial intelligence algorithm, WLP tracks directional changes of light passing through the eye to calculate both HOAs and lower-order aberrations. The software combines wavefront aberrations, corneal topography, anterior lens position, and axial length to create a unique patient-specific 3D model and calculate a customized laser ablation profile [5]. Unlike prior versions of LASIK, WLP does not require subjective manifest refraction or multiple biometry modalities, instead using the comprehensive InnovEyes Sightmap device (Alcon, Erlangen, Germany) for all ocular biometrics. Early studies have suggested that ray-tracing-guided LASIK may exceed visual and refractive outcomes of previous LASIK generations [6, 7].
Concurrently, continued innovation in small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) surgery led to the introduction of SMILE Pro utilizing the VisuMax 800 (Carl Zeiss, Göschwitzer Str., Jena, Germany) femtosecond laser platform in January 2024 [8]. SMILE Pro has a faster laser and improved centration algorithm, allowing lenticule creation to be completed in < 10 s per eye rather than the 25–30 s with the previous VisuMax 500 platform (Carl Zeiss, Göschwitzer Str., Jena, Germany) [9]. The introduction of WLP LASIK and SMILE Pro represents the latest evolution of both procedures, and direct comparisons between these technologies are currently lacking. As refractive surgery continues to advance toward increasingly personalized treatments, comparative studies are needed to better characterize the relative performance of these new platforms.
The purpose of this study is to compare the 1- and 3-month postoperative visual and refractive outcomes of the initial experience with ray-tracing-guided WLP LASIK and SMILE Pro carried out by a single surgeon at one refractive surgery center. To provide an internal benchmark, outcomes were additionally compared with those of WFO LASIK, a procedure that is well established with a refined nomogram at the center.

## Methods
A non-randomized retrospective study was conducted at one refractive surgery center in Draper, Utah. Patients who were treated with SMILE Pro, WLP LASIK, or WFO LASIK with at least 1 month of postoperative follow-up were evaluated for inclusion. Included patients were all > 18 years of age and were treated for myopia with or without astigmatism. Patients were excluded if they had previously undergone refractive surgery or had ocular disease, hyperopia, or clinically significant dry eyes. To limit confounding bias based on preoperative characteristics, patients were selected based on age, sex, preoperative sphere, preoperative cylinder, and preoperative manifest refraction spherical equivalent (MRSE) and matched among the three platforms for inclusion in the study. Each variable was standardized by calculating z-scores, which were then combined into a composite z-score for each patient. Patients with the most similar composite z-scores were manually matched across the three groups to achieve comparable baseline demographic and refractive characteristics. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of the New York Institutional Review Board (#A20-12–547-823). All patients provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.
Retrospective chart review was performed to collect data. All patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination preoperatively, which included slit-lamp examination and dilated fundoscopy. Demographic data, preoperative uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), and manifest refraction values were obtained. Keratometry and pachymetry measurements were collected from Pentacam HR (Oculus, Arlington, WA, USA). Postoperative data, including UDVA, CDVA, and MRSE, were obtained from the 1- and 3-month follow-up appointments.
Nine standard graphs of visual and refractive outcomes were created. Additionally, safety and efficacy indices were calculated for each procedure at 1 and 3 months postoperatively. Vector analysis was performed using AstigMATIC software developed by Gauvin et al. [10]. The intended vector change in astigmatism and actual astigmatic change caused by surgery were represented by target-induced astigmatism (TIA) and surgically induced astigmatism (SIA), respectively. The difference vector, representing the difference between TIA and SIA, and correction index (CI), the ratio of SIA to TIA, were also evaluated with the software. The ideal value of CI is 1, with CI > 1 reflecting an overcorrection and CI < 1 representing an undercorrection.

## Surgical Procedure
All patients underwent surgery by a single surgeon (MM) and were targeted for emmetropia.

## Wavefront-Optimized LASIK
The Wavelight FS 200 femtosecond laser (Alcon, Erlangen, Germany) was used for flap creation. A targeted flap size of 9.0 mm and thickness of 100 μm were programmed with a 130° side-cut angle. The Allegretto EX500 (Alcon, Erlangen, Germany) excimer laser was used to perform the wavefront-optimized corneal ablation. The most updated nomogram, Datagraph-med version 5.80 (Datagraph, Chapel Hill, NC, USA), for the wavefront-optimized system was used with an optical zone of 6.50-mm to 9.00-mm transition zone.

## Wavelight Plus LASIK
For the WLP group, the Wavelight FS 200 femtosecond laser was used to create a flap in a similar fashion as WFO. The InnovEyes Sightmap device was used to create a customized eye model, and the generated ablation profile that best agreed with the patient's manifest refraction (sphere, cylinder, and axis) was selected. The cylinder was reduced by 10% according to the manufacturer's recommendation (Alcon). No nomograms were used to adjust the ablation profile. The Allegretto EX500 excimer laser was used to ablate the corneal stromal bed based on the customized ablation profile with an optical zone of 6.50-mm to 9.0-mm transition zone.

## SMILE Pro
An established nomogram for Visumax 500 developed on Datagraph-med version 5.80 was used. For patients with simple myopia or myopic astigmatism with a cylinder of ≤ 0.5 D, we used the nomogram to select the appropriate attempted spherical equivalent correction. For patients with compounded myopic astigmatism with a cylinder of ≥ 0.75 D, the refined nomogram was used to select an appropriate sphere and cylinder. The VisuMax 800 femtosecond laser was used with pulse durations ranging from 220 to 580 fs. The following laser parameters were used: 3.93-mm superior corneal incision, 90° side-cut angle, 60° incision angle, laser-bed energy of 145 nJ, and spot separations of 4 µm (lenticule), 2 µm (side cut), 4 µm (cap), and 2 µm (incision side cut). The cap thickness was 120 µm, the cap diameter was 7.5 mm, and the lenticule thickness was 6.5 mm with a 0.5-mm blend zone.
Patients undergoing all three procedures received topical moxifloxacin 0.5% in the surgical eye(s) following the procedure. Patients continued moxifloxacin eye drops four times a day (QID) and prednisolone acetate 1% four times a day (QID) in the surgical eye(s) for 1 week. For SMILE Pro, the patients continued prednisolone acetate 1% twice a day for a week, followed by once a day for 2 weeks. Preservative-free artificial tears were recommended as needed.

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analysis was conducted in Python (V3.14.3) using the matplotlib and statsmodels libraries. For patients with bilateral treatment, both eyes were included in the analysis to achieve an adequate sample size. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of data. Generalized estimating equations (GEEs) were used to account for inter-eye correlation and within-subject bias. An identity link function with a Gaussian distribution and an exchangeable working correlation structure was applied. Pairwise post hoc contrasts with Bonferroni adjustment were performed to compare between platforms. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GEE with Bonferroni correction was used to evaluate differences in postoperative manifest refraction, visual acuity, and safety and efficacy indices. Additionally, it was used to compare proportions of patients achieving 20/20 and 20/16 UDVA, gain of lines of vision in postoperative UDVA, and postoperative refractive accuracy. As this study reflects our preliminary experience with the WLP and SMILE Pro devices, findings should be interpreted as explorative rather than definitive evidence.

## Results

## Patient Demographics
A total of 93 patients (160 eyes) were included, composed of 28 patients (50 eyes) in the WLP group, 33 patients (55 eyes) in the WFO group, and 32 patients (55 eyes) in the SMILE Pro group with 1-month postoperative data available. Three-month data were available for 40 eyes in the WLP cohort, 55 eyes in the WFO cohort, and 46 eyes in the SMILE Pro cohort. Sixty-seven patients underwent bilateral surgery. Mean age ranged from 31.3 to 34.2 years (Table 1). Mean preoperative spherical error ranged from − 3.42 to − 3.97 D, while mean preoperative cylinder ranged from − 0.47 to − 0.75 D. Age (p = 0.13), sphere (p = 0.22), cylinder (p = 0.14), and MRSE (p = 0.09) were not significantly different between groups preoperatively.

## Efficacy
At 3 months, all three groups achieved a UDVA of ≥ 20/40 (Fig. 1); 98% of eyes in the WLP and WFO groups achieved a UDVA of 20/20 compared with 74% in the SMILE Pro cohort. Compared with SMILE Pro, both WLP (1 month: OR = 0.14, [95% CI: 0.03 to 0.64], p = 0.01; 3 months: OR = 0.07, [95% CI: 0.008 to 0.57], p = 0.01) and WFO (1 month = OR: 0.12, [95% CI: 0.03 to 0.55], p = 0.007; 3 months: OR = 0.05, [95% CI: 0.006 to 0.4], p = 0.005) were significantly more likely to achieve UDVA of ≥ 20/20 at both 1 and 3 months. Forty-eight percent of eyes in the WLP group achieved a UDVA of ≥ 20/16 at 3 months compared with 42% in the WFO-optimized group and 37% in the SMILE Pro group. Compared with SMILE Pro, WLP (OR: 0.25, [95% CI: 0.09 to 0.71], p = 0.009) and WFO (OR = 0.12, [95% CI: 0.03 to 0.55], p = 0.007) were significantly more likely to achieve UDVA of ≥ 20/16 at 1 month, but no difference was noted at 3 months (p = 0.55, p = 0.76, respectively). WLP was significantly more likely to achieve a 3-month UDVA ≥ baseline CDVA than SMILE Pro (97.5% vs. 76.1%, p = 0.02) (Fig. 2). Of the eyes in the SMILE Pro cohort, 2.2% had a postoperative UDVA three lines worse than the preoperative CDVA.

Both WLP (p < 0.001) and WFO (p = 0.002) demonstrated significantly better UDVA than SMILE Pro at 1 month (Table 2). WLP also showed significantly better UDVA than SMILE Pro at 3 months (p = 0.033). Cohen’s d of 1-month UDVA between WLP and SMILE Pro was 0.75 with a power of 0.97. At 3 months, Cohen’s d for UDVA was 0.60 with a power of 0.78. Efficacy indices at 1 month were 1.04 ± 0.19, 1.06 ± 0.19, and 0.93 ± 0.21 for WLP, WFO, and SMILE Pro, respectively (Table 2). At 3 months, they were 1.01 ± 0.17, 1.07 ± 0.19, and 0.97 ± 0.21 for WLP, WFO, and SMILE Pro, respectively. Efficacy of WLP (p = 0.04) and WFO (p = 0.007) were significantly higher than that of SMILE Pro at 1 month; however, there was no significant difference between any groups at 3 months (all p > 0.05).

## Safety
No eyes in the cohort lost > 2 lines of CDVA postoperatively (Fig. 3). In the SMILE Pro cohort, 2.2% of eyes demonstrated a loss of 1 line of CDVA at 3 months, all due to dry eyes. WLP was significantly more likely to achieve a gain of ≥ 1 line in postoperative UDVA than preoperative CDVA compared with SMILE Pro at 1 month (OR = 0.34, [95% CI: 0.12 to 0.96], p = 0.04), although this finding was not seen at 3 months (p = 0.68). When adjusted for preoperative CDVA, WLP demonstrated significantly higher postoperative CDVA than WFO and SMILE Pro at 1 month, although no significant differences were noted between groups at 3 months. Safety indices at 1 month were 0.97 ± 0.18, 1.01 ± 0.13, and 0.97 ± 0.14 for WLP, WFO, and SMILE Pro, respectively (Table 2). At 3 months, they were 0.98 ± 0.16, 1.05 ± 0.16, and 0.97 ± 0.14 for WLP, WFO, and SMILE Pro, respectively. Safety indices were not significantly different between modalities at 1 month or 3 months (all p > 0.05).

## Accuracy and Stability
When comparing attempted and achieved SEQ, all eyes were within 1.0 D of the target at both 1 and 3 months (Fig. 4). Analysis of postoperative refractive accuracy showed that the WLP cohort had 100% of eyes within 0.5 D of the intended target, while SMILE Pro and WFO had 96.4% and 97.8% within 0.5 D, respectively (Fig. 5). WLP was significantly more likely than SMILE Pro to achieve a SEQ within ± 0.13 D of the intended target (OR = 0.37, [95% CI: 0.14 to 0.97], p = 0.04). No eyes in the WLP or SMILE Pro groups demonstrated a change in cylinder > 0.5 D between 1 and 3 months compared with 3.6% in the WFO cohort (Fig. 6). When adjusting for the preoperative sphere, patients who underwent WFO were significantly more myopic than patients who underwent SMILE Pro at 1 month (p = 0.048), although no difference was noted between the two at 3 months (p = 0.938) (Table 2).

## Astigmatism Analysis
One hundred percent of eyes showed < 0.5 D of astigmatism at 3 months in the WLP cohort compared with 98% in the WFO group and 87% in the SMILE Pro group (Fig. 7). The WLP cohort had significantly less postoperative cylinder than patients who underwent WFO and SMILE Pro at both 1 and 3 months (all p < 0.001) (Table 2). Cohen’s d for 3-month cylinder between WLP and SMILE Pro was 0.84 with a power of 0.97. TIA and SIA were highly correlated for all three platforms with R2 of 0.829 at 3 months for WLP, 0.896 for WFO, and 0.809 for SMILE Pro (Fig. 8); 71.4% of eyes in the WLP group had an angle of error of < 5° at 3 months compared with 57.7% and 56.7% in the WFO and SMILE Pro groups, respectively, although this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons) (Fig. 9).

## Vector Analysis of Astigmatism
Vector analysis demonstrated mild overcorrection across all platforms (CI > 1.0). At 1 month, mean TIA/SIA/CIs were 0.75 D/0.84 D/1.11 for SMILE Pro, 0.75 D/0.76 D/1.12 for WFO, and 0.51 D/0.54 D/1.08 for WLP. These values remained largely unchanged at 3 months, with TIA/SIA/CI of 0.72 D/0.83 D/1.13 for SMILE Pro, 0.75 D/0.75 D/1.11 for WFO, and 0.51 D/0.54 D/1.08 for WLP (Fig. 10).

## Discussion
Our study represents one of the earliest direct comparisons of visual and refractive outcomes of WLP and SMILE Pro. Better visual outcomes were found in the WLP cohort, which demonstrated significantly better overall UDVA than SMILE Pro at both 1 (p < 0.001) and 3 (p = 0.03) months and was significantly more likely to achieve a UDVA of 20/20 (98% vs. 74%, p = 0.01) (Table 2, Fig. 1). Our WLP cohort exceeded the findings of Schumacher et al., who reported that 87% of eyes treated with ray-tracing-guided LASIK achieved 20/20 UDVA at 3 months in their cohort [11]. However, their cohort used three different devices and manual calculations, whereas we used the integrated InnovEyes Sightmap system, likely contributing to differences in our outcomes. More recent investigations using the Sightmap system have shown findings comparable to our study, with 98.1–100% of eyes achieving 20/20 UDVA at 3 months [12–14]. In contrast, the proportion of eyes achieving 20/20 UDVA in the literature published on SMILE Pro varies with an estimated range of 57–99%, with a higher preoperative sphere typically corresponding to lower achievement of 20/20 vision [9, 15–17]. The preoperative sphere of our SMILE Pro cohort was more myopic than in studies reporting ≥ 80% of their cohort achieving 20/20 UDVA, which may explain the suboptimal outcomes observed in our study. Additionally, our WLP group achieved more eyes with 20/20 vision than SMILE Pro despite matched preoperative spheres and cylinders, suggesting that WLP may provide better visual outcomes over a wider range of myopia. Furthermore, residual astigmatism was significantly higher in the SMILE Pro cohort, which may also have contributed to the suboptimal visual outcomes in this group.
Regarding efficacy and safety, favorable outcomes were demonstrated over all three platforms. Although efficacy of WLP (1.01 ± 0.17) and WFO (1.07 ± 0.19) was significantly better than for SMILE Pro (0.97 ± 0.21) at 1 month postoperatively (p = 0.01, p = 0.002), no significant difference was noted at the 3-month postoperative period (Table 2). Previous studies have reported that visual recovery following KLEx surgery may be slower than after LASIK procedures. One proposed explanation is that the stromal disruption that occurs as a result of lenticule creation and extraction may affect short-term visual recovery, potentially explaining our initial differences in efficacy indices [18–20]. All three platforms demonstrated safety indices of approximately 1 (Table 2). The safety and efficacy outcomes observed in our cohort met or exceeded those reported in the respective FDA clinical trials for each platform, reinforcing our findings [4, 8, 21].
Correction of astigmatism may be one difference between modalities. All eyes in the WLP cohort achieved < 0.50 D of residual astigmatism by 3 months (Fig. 7). WLP showed the closest CI to 1 and the highest proportion of eyes with a < 5° angle of error (Figs. 9, 10). Previous studies have similarly noted favorable astigmatism correction of ray-tracing-guided LASIK in patients with both low and high preoperative cylinders, with reported values between 97–99% achieving a refractive astigmatism of < 0.5 [12, 22]. In contrast, previous studies evaluating SMILE Pro for astigmatism correction report a wider range of 83–95% for eyes with residual astigmatism < 0.5 D, similar to our finding of 87% [9, 16, 17] (Fig. 7). These findings indicate that WLP may provide more precise astigmatic correction than SMILE Pro.
Several factors may explain the improved astigmatism outcomes seen with WLP. Ray-tracing-guided technology does not require the use of manifest refraction or nomograms to set the ablation laser. In contrast, SMILE Pro treatments use a nomogram that programs the laser to create a lenticule based on spherocylindrical manifest refraction. In our cohort, a nomogram optimized for the Visumax 500 platform was used, as a SMILE Pro nomogram was not available. Although prior literature suggests that the same nomogram can be applied to both the Visumax 500 and 800, it is possible that the faster cut of the laser and centration techniques in SMILE Pro may require a unique updated nomogram to produce optimal outcomes [9, 23]. Therefore, the differences in astigmatic outcomes in our study may also reflect the need to continue refinement of use of the SMILE Pro system rather than a limitation of the technology itself. However, regardless of nomogram use, KLEx surgeries have historically demonstrated a tendency toward undercorrection of astigmatism [24]. Although SMILE Pro has shown better astigmatism correction than Visumax 500 due to incorporation of the OcuAlign and CentraLign features designed to improve centration, these findings persist [25]. KLEx platforms cannot correct preoperative cylinders of < 0.5 D, instead requiring use of an adjusted spherical equivalent to select a treatment profile, limiting their astigmatism correction ability. WLP, on the other hand, allows for targeted correction of both symmetrical and irregular astigmatism with the ability to make a more nuanced treatment profile, rather than being restricted by the 0.25-D increments of the SMILE Pro software [22]. This enhanced customization likely contributed to WLP’s improved astigmatic outcomes. Notably, despite the absence of a formal nomogram, WLP treatment planning also involved selecting the most optimal ablation profile based on patient manifest refraction and reducing the cylinder by 10% based on the manufacturer’s recommendation, indicating that even the WLP platform is not entirely free from operator adjustments. Therefore, these findings showcase the better short-term outcomes of WLP over SMILE Pro with current software implementation. Additional fine-tuning or the use of nomograms with either system may result in improved future visual and refractive outcomes.
Comparing our initial experience with WLP to the well-refined nomogram and technique used with WFO LASIK at our center, WFO had higher efficacy and safety indices, although these were not statistically significant. In contrast, WLP exceeded WFO visual acuity outcomes and demonstrated significantly lower postoperative cylinder (Table 2). This indicates that further experience with the WLP system may eventually show consistently better outcomes over WFO LASIK at our site, despite the use of a refined and well-established WFO nomogram, perhaps becoming a preferred technique for surgeons when attempting to correct a higher degree of preoperative astigmatism.
Limitations of our study include its non-randomized retrospective design, which introduces the possibility of selection bias. The results in this study reflect our preliminary patients with the WLP and SMILE Pro devices; therefore, comparisons should be considered as explorative rather than definitive and be restricted to this early, single-surgeon, single-center, non-randomized cohort. Patients were matched by age, sex, sphere, cylinder, and MRSE to limit baseline differences; however, unmeasured characteristics, including corneal thickness, keratometry, axial length, and pupil diameter, may still have influenced outcomes. These additional characteristics were not included in the matching process to ensure a sufficient sample size for analysis. Nevertheless, the single-surgeon, single-center nature of the study limits the presence of confounding bias because of technique variations. Inclusion of both eyes from patients introduces the potential for inter-eye correlation bias, although this was addressed with GEE and allowed us to analyze a larger sample size. The relatively short follow-up period limited our assessment of long-term refractive and visual outcomes. In addition, fewer follow-up data were available at 3 months, resulting in 40 eyes in the WLP cohort and 46 eyes in the SMILE Pro cohort, which may have impacted findings because of attrition bias. With our sample size of 160 eyes, a power of 0.8, and an alpha level of 0.05, the smallest difference we can reliably detect is a Cohen’s d effect size of 0.55–0.61. This is well powered to assess the large differences in cylinder and UDVA but may be underpowered for smaller differences in safety and efficacy indices. Additionally, we randomly selected one eye from each bilateral patient and ran a sensitivity analysis, which found that UDVA and cylinder were still statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that our sample size is sufficient to evaluate the claims in this study (Supplemental Table 1). Finally, as this study evaluated our initial experience with these new platforms, HOAs, contrast sensitivity, and patient reported subjective outcomes were not evaluated. Further studies evaluating these visual quality metrics will be important for assessing the true clinical value of WLP.

## Conclusion
Our initial experience with the WLP and SMILE Pro platforms showed that WLP achieved better UDVA and lower residual astigmatism than SMILE Pro. Additionally, without the use of any nomogram, WLP showed safety and efficacy outcomes comparable to those of the well-established WFO with a refined nomogram at our center. Based on our initial findings at one center, astigmatic correction may be a limiting factor of KLEx platforms. Improving the accuracy of astigmatism correction of KLEx platforms may ultimately lead to visual and refractive outcomes comparable to those of modern LASIK platforms.

Why carry out this study?
WaveLight Plus (WLP) LASIK and SMILE Pro represent the latest advances in refractive surgery but direct comparative data between these newly introduced platforms are limited.
This study was conducted to compare the visual and refractive outcomes of the initial experience with WLP LASIK and SMILE Pro, with wavefront-optimized (WFO) LASIK as an internal benchmark, in a matched cohort treated by a single surgeon.
What was learned from this study?
Initial experience at a single center with WLP and SMILE Pro showed that WLP achieved significantly better uncorrected distance visual acuity and lower residual astigmatism than SMILE Pro at 1 and 3 months postoperatively.
Visual and refractive outcomes following WLP were comparable to those achieved with a well-established WFO LASIK nomogram, despite WLP not requiring use of a formal nomogram.
Astigmatic correction may be one limiting factor of KLEx in achieving similar outcomes to modern LASIK platforms.
