# Three new species of Argathona (Isopoda: Corallanidae) from Indonesian and Malaysian Borneo, with the first records from elasmobranch hosts

## Abstract
AbstractAlthough Borneo lies within the highly biodiverse Coral Triangle, its reported isopod diversity remains poorly documented and underexplored. This study documents four species of the corallanid isopod genus Argathona collected from elasmobranch hosts in Indonesian and Malaysian Borneo, namely A. lineata, A. magnocula sp. nov., A. lebara sp. nov., and A. terputa sp. nov. Specimens were identified based on detailed morphological characters. Argathona magnocula sp. nov. is characterised by a posteriorly elongate frontal lamina that is three times longer than its posterior width; eyes separated by ∼34 % of head width; a pleotelson with a sub-truncate posterior margin and dorsal fields of hyaline setae; and uropodal rami barely extending beyond the pleotelson. Argathona lebara sp. nov. is distinguished by its short, broad frontal lamina (shorter than wide), small eyes, deeply concave maxillula mesial lobe, and exceptionally long antennae reaching pereonite 7. Argathona terputa sp. nov. features a broadly truncate pleotelson, short antennula (extending only to the eye's posterior), and uropodal rami that do not extend past the pleotelson apex. It can be separated from A. lineata by its broader pleotelson margin, shorter frontal lamina, reduced setation, and the presence of two simple setae on the maxillula mesial lobe. This study describes three new species, reveals novel host associations with four elasmobranch species, and transfers Alcirona grandis into the genus.Keywords: Coral triangle, Temporary parasite, Elasmobranch host, Micropredator, Taxonomy

## Introduction
The Central Indo-Pacific (Spalding et al., 2007) is home to more marine species than any other region (Poore and Bruce, 2012). Within this realm lies the Coral Triangle, recognised as a global centre of extreme marine biodiversity (Veron et al., 2009 and subsequent authors). Despite this extraordinary richness, marine isopods remain comparatively poorly studied across much of the region. Most records are concentrated in Singapore and Indonesia, where several recent studies have advanced knowledge of free-living marine isopod diversity (Cai and Teo, 2012; Bruce and Wong, 2015; Bruce et al., 2022; Sidabalok, 2013; Sidabalok and Bruce, 2015, 2016, 2018a, 2018b, 2018c; Sidabalok et al., 2020; Wong and Sidabalok, 2021). In contrast, other areas within the Coral Triangle, such as Borneo and Papua New Guinea, remain under-documented, with information relying largely on historic works (e.g., Richardson, 1910; Nierstrasz, 1931) and scattered later records (Bruce, 1993, 1995). Consequently, substantial gaps persist in our understanding of marine isopod diversity across the Coral Triangle.
The family Corallanidae Hansen, 1890 currently includes six genera and 82 known species worldwide. While most members of this family are free-living, some genera exhibit parasitic behaviour, acting as temporary ectoparasites or micropredators of fish and other invertebrates (Bruce et al., 2002). Species of the genus Argathona Stebbing, 1905 are predominantly found in the tropical waters of the Indo-Pacific region, although a few species have also been discovered in the cooler waters of South Australia and Japan (Bruce et al., 2022). This genus currently comprises 16 accepted species, with the majority (14) recorded from the Central Indo-Pacific, reinforcing the region as a centre of high diversity for marine organisms.
Species of Argathona are temporary parasites that detach readily and rapidly from their hosts, and these isopods are more frequently collected from sediment rather than directly from their hosts. Consequently, knowledge on their host associations remains limited, although most Argathona species are known to feed primarily on fish (Bruce et al., 2022), while others appear to be associates of Porifera (e.g., Argathona rostrata Bruce, 1982). This study identified three new species in this genus and provides novel records on hosts.

## Methods and materials
Specimens were collected in 2002, 2003, and 2006 from Borneo (encompassing both Indonesian and Malaysian waters) in the Central Indo-Pacific MEOW realm (defined by Spalding et al., 2007), as part of a larger comprehensive investigation into the parasite diversity of elasmobranch species (see the Global Cestode Database; Caira et al., 2022). The isopod samples were removed from their elasmobranch hosts collected from four sites (Fig. 1 and Table 1), preserved in 70 % ethanol and donated to the North-West University (NWU) for further studies. Ethical clearance for studying donated material was received from the NWU AnimCare research ethics committee (NWU-01922-24-A5).

## Results
A total of 140 elasmobranch specimens representing 32 species were examined for corallanid isopods. Of these, only four species were found to host isopods identified as belonging to the genus Argathona. Four distinct Argathona species were identified based on morphological characteristics, three of which were identified as new to science.
Hosts infested with Argathona (Table 1) included six individuals of Chiloscyllium punctatum (brownbanded bambooshark), four of which were mature females and two males (one immature and one mature). Additionally, one mature male and one mature female Chiloscyllium hasseltii (Hasselt's bambooshark) were parasitised. Single infestations were also recorded in a mature male Glaucostegus typus (giant shovelnose ray) and a female Himantura tutul (fine-spotted leopard whipray). Some of the hosts exhibited co-infestation, with a single host infested with two different Argathona species. Remarks
Genera within the family Corallanidae are distinguished primarily by the morphology of their mouthparts (Delaney, 1989). Species of Argathona are characterised by the maxilliped palp (without an endite) with 4 or 5 broad articles, with articles 2 and 3, 3 and 4, or 4 and 5 being partly or wholly fused; a single-lobed quadrate or sub-quadrate maxilla; and a maxillula with one prominent falcate robust seta at the apex, along with smaller falcate setae at the base. The mandible incisor is relatively larger and broader than in other genera and may be tridentate in some species. Other characters include a narrow and flat frontal lamina that is not posteriorly narrowed, the first three pairs of legs with simple setae and lacking spines on the dactyli, and dorsal body surfaces that are often moderately to heavily setose, particularly on the pleotelson. Some species also have a long antennal flagellum and antennula with unexpanded basal articles.
Species of Argathona can be distinguished from each other by the unique combination of characters, including frontal lamina shape, rostral shape, eye size, pleotelson shape, as well as details of all appendages. See Delaney (1989) and Bruce et al. (2022) for more detailed information on the genus.
Host records indicate a strong preference for Serranidae. However, it should be noted that serranids are highly valued as food fish and are therefore heavily targeted by both commercial and recreational fishers, which may bias the available host records.

Remarks
Argathona lineata can be identified by the combination of a number of characters. The body is three times as long as its greatest width; approximately 41 % of the head width separates the eyes; the frontal lamina is narrow (posteriorly elongated) and 2.8 times longer than the posterior width; and the posterior margins of pleonites 3–5 feature transverse rows of small nodules. The posterolateral margin of pleonite 4 is sub-truncate and forms a ventral point. The antenna extends to the posterior of pereonite 4 and has a flagellum with 37 articles. The uropodal rami are rounded and barely extend beyond the apex of the pleotelson, and there are additional hyaline setae on their dorsal surfaces. The pleotelson also has a sub-truncate posterior margin, with its dorsal surface displaying two dense sub-median fields of acute hyaline setae. The maxillula mesial lobe is distally truncate with one acute robust seta, and the maxilliped palp only contains simple setae. The maxilla lateral lobe contains four simple setae and is 1.5 times as long as wide.

Remarks
Argathona magnocula sp. nov. can be identified by the unique combination of the following characters. The body is three times as long as its greatest width, approximately 34 % of the head width separates the eyes, it has a pentagonal frontal lamina that is 3.2 times longer than the posterior width, and the posterior margins of pleonites 3–5 feature transverse rows of small nodules. The antenna extends to the posterior margin of pereonite 4 and has a flagellum with about 32 articles. The uropodal rami are rounded and barely extend beyond the apex of the pleotelson, and there are additional hyaline setae on their dorsal surfaces. The pleotelson also has a sub-truncate posterior margin, with its dorsal surface displaying two dense sub-median fields of acute hyaline setae. The maxillula mesial lobe is distally truncate with three simple setae and the maxilla has only two simple setae.
The most similar species to A. magnocula sp. nov. is A. lineata; however, A. magnocula sp. nov. differs distinctly by having a much smaller head separation between the eyes (34 % vs 41 % separation in A. lineata), the antennal flagellum of A. magnocula sp. nov. has 32 articles (37 in A. lineata), the pleotelson is sub-truncate in shape (narrowly truncate in A. lineata), the whole body has more setae with coxae setation starting from the second coxae to the seventh (coxae 4–7 in A. lineata), the ischium of pereopod 1 contains no molariforms setae and the merus only contains 5 (1 molariform seta on the ischium and 7 molariform setae on the merus in A. lineata).
Arathona terputa sp. nov. (see below) is also similar to A. magnocula sp. nov. but has a broader and more truncate pleotelson posterior margin, as well as a shorter antenna flagellum that extends to the middle of pereonite 4 with only 25 articles (see Table 2 for other differences).

Remarks
Argathona lebara sp. nov. can be identified by the relatively short body length-to-width ratio and small eyes in comparison to other species. Furthermore, the frontal lamina is distinctively short, being shorter than wide. The antennula flagellum extends to the middle of pereonite 2, while the antenna is exceptionally long, reaching pereonite 7. In addition, the maxillula mesial lobe is deeply convex and the maxilla lateral lobe has a produced distomesial angle.
Argathona trichota Bruce, Wong & Merrin, 2022 is the most similar species, with the same uniquely shaped and short frontal lamina, a character not commonly present in other species. Furthermore, both have relatively small eyes and the maxillae with a produced distomesial angle on the lateral lobe. However, Argathona lebara sp. nov. differs in having a slightly shorter body, a frontal lamina with convex anterolateral margins (straight anterolateral margins in A. trichota), longer antennae that extend to pereonite 2 and pereonite 7 respectively (extending to the anterior of pereonite 1 and mid-pereonite 4 respectively in A. trichota); a much less setose body; and a more deeply convex maxillula mesial lobe. Furthermore, there are numerous differences in the setation of the pereopods between the two species (see Table 2); most notably pereopod 1 merus in A. lebara has 5 molariform RS, whereas A. trichota has 9 molariform RS, and pereopod 7 in A. lebara has approximately half the number of robust setae on all margins than does A. trichota.

## Argathona grandis(Nunomura, 2006) comb. nov
Alcirona grandis Nunomura, 2006: 13–16.
Remarks
Argathona and Alcirona are generally similar in appearance, particularly as both genera can show head dorsal setation. Argathona grandis (Nunomura, 2006), comb. nov., originally described from Hokkaido, northern Japan as Alcirona grandis, clearly exhibits the diagnostic mouthpart features of Argathona as defined by Delaney (1989) and reiterated by Bruce et al. (2022). These include a maxillula with only one large falcate robust seta on the lateral lobe, accompanied by smaller accessory robust setae at its base, as well as a quadrate maxilla with a wide, truncate or subtruncate apex. Accordingly, the species is here transferred to Argathona. A. grandis may prove to be a junior synonym of an already described species within Argathona, such as Argathona rhinoceros. This potential synonymy has yet to be formally resolved.

## Discussion
The distribution of Argathona is predominantly concentrated in the tropical waters of the Indo-Pacific region, with exceptions such as Argathona japonica Shiino, 1961, which extends into the subtropical and temperate waters surrounding Japan (see Shiino, 1961) and Argathona confine Hale, 1925, which occurs in the subtropical waters of Australia (see Hale, 1925). The newly described species therefore aligns well with this range, further emphasising the genus's concentrated distribution within the Indo-Pacific.
Species of Argathona are reported for the first time from Borneo, with three new species described and a new host record documented for A. lineata. While the presence of this genus in the region is not unexpected, given that congeners are already known from nearby areas such as the Philippines, Singapore, Java, and Sulawesi, its occurrence further reinforces the status of this region as a global marine biodiversity hotspot. The discovery of multiple Argathona species in Borneo also supports the hypothesis that the Coral Triangle and surrounding South China Sea may represent a centre of diversification for the genus.
The new species described from Borneo, together with the new host record of A. lineata, adds to the growing evidence of high species richness and diversity of Argathona in the Coral Triangle region. Current host records indicate that species of Argathona has been found on various groupers (species of Epinephelus, Plectropomus and Cromileptes), snappers (Lutjanus), angelfish (Pomacanthus), grunts (Diagramma), wrasse (Pseudolabrus), pufferfish (Tetraodon), and large moray eels (Muraena) (Chellandurai et al., 2016; Anil et al., 2018; Delaney, 1989). The novel host associations reported here further expand the known diversity of Corallanidae by documenting, for the first time, parasitism on shark hosts, as well as providing the first specific record of a species from a ray (previous reports noted ray hosts without details). This finding not only broadens the ecological range of Argathona but also provides important insights into potential host-parasite relationships within the region.
The co-occurrence of multiple parasite species on a single host has been noted, and while fish-parasitic isopods are typically found as a single species on an individual host, recent findings have demonstrated that this may differ in some cases. Instances of simultaneous co-parasitism have been documented within the family Cymothoidae, such as Anilocra capensis Leach, 1818 and Ceratothoa africanae Hadfield, Bruce & Smit 2014, co-infesting Pachymetopon blochii Valenciennes, 1830, as well as Anilocra grandmaae Aneesh, Hadfield, Smit & Kumar, 2021 and Agarna malayi Tiwari, 1952 parasitising Tenualosa toli Valenciennes, 1847 (see Welicky and Smit, 2018; Aneesh et al., 2022). A similar case of co-parasitism has also been recorded within the genus Argathona, with Epinephelus coioides being infested with both A. macronema and A. rhinoceros (see Purivirojkul and Songsuk, 2020). The cases of simultaneous infection in the current study are thus not unexpected and are anticipated due to the temporary association of these isopods with their respective hosts.
Lastly, it is noted that taxonomic assignment within this genus remains challenging, with a high likelihood of misidentifications or the presence of cryptic species. Bruce et al. (2022, pp. 507–508) noted that the widely reported A. macronema likely represents at least six distinct species and is a complex taxonomic tangle requiring resolution through type-based redescription of all the taxa involved. Without more detailed and targeted studies, misidentifications within this genus are likely to persist. By refining the diagnostic characters and drawing attention to key morphological features, the present study aims to provide a clearer framework for future species identification and reduce the risk of misidentifications.
