# Sensor-driven digital motion correction of robotically-aligned optical coherence tomography retinal volumes

## Abstract
AbstractOptical coherence tomography (OCT) has revolutionized diagnostics in retinal ophthalmology. Traditional OCT requires minimal relative motion between the subject and scanner, which is difficult to achieve with handheld devices and/or non-stabilized subjects. We recently introduced robotically-aligned OCT (RAOCT) as an alternative that promises to alleviate these minimal-movement requirements by tracking the subject and compensating for their motion with dynamic hardware components in real-time. However, hardware and image processing delays lead to residual motion artifacts even after automatic alignment and motion compensation. Here, we introduce a novel sensor-driven digital motion correction approach that overcomes these shortcomings. Our method leverages synchronized sensing of both the subject’s eye and the scanner hardware to continuously estimate the imaging system state during acquisition. The A-scans are then remapped using a ray-tracing model of the system at the precise moment of acquisition. We demonstrate that, in addition to motion compensation from RAOCT, our method further reduces residual artifacts by 88.3 %, 80.4 %, and 62.6 % across axial, lateral, and rotational motions, respectively. We also show our correction in human retinal OCT images where residual errors from acquisition were reduced down to 12.4 µm, 0.11°, and 0.39° for axial, lateral, and rotational motion, respectively.

## Introduction
Optical coherence tomography (OCT) revolutionized ophthalmology diagnostics by enabling high-quality non-invasive imaging [1–4]. However, OCT systems assume both the patient and the optical hardware behave as instructed: the subject will remain static during acquisition and the hardware will follow the drive signal with predictable latency and high precision. Subject stability is often ensured using techniques such as bite bars or chin and forehead rests. However, these measures prove inadequate when imaging non-stabilized subjects or when utilizing handheld probes [5–12], where operator reaction time and physiological tremors introduce challenges leading to image degradation and motion artifacts in the OCT data. Imaging non-stabilized subjects becomes necessary in scenarios where traditional stabilization methods, such as bite bars or chin and forehead rests, are impractical. This includes subjects who may be unconscious or undergoing surgery and those who need urgent evaluations in non-specialist settings.
We introduced robotically-aligned OCT (RAOCT) to relax patient stability requirements by utilizing active tracking and real-time hardware-based motion compensation through a combination of a robotic arm and optomechanical hardware [13–15]. RAOCT offers not just the maneuverability and workspace of handheld imaging but enhances it by incorporating automated alignment that can respond to motion more promptly and accurately than human operators. Moreover, RAOCT and similar robotic OCT systems have also been used to increase the effective field-of-view of OCT imaging systems through automatic scanner motion and image montaging [16–22]. While RAOCT has demonstrated reduced motion artifacts in unstabilized freestanding subjects, it remains subject to motion correction latency arising from image processing delays, I/O lag times, and mechanical hardware dynamic limits. This latency manifests as small-scale motion artifacts as well as discontinuities during rapid eye motion in saccades. In this manuscript, we introduce sensor-driven digital motion correction (DMC), a technique to correct motion artifacts by measuring the real-time pose of the eye as well as that of dynamic hardware components in the scanner to remap acquired A-Scans to their corresponding physical location instead of relying on assumption-driven scan patterns for formatting.
Prior to sensor-driven DMC, residual motion artifacts have been corrected in post-processing using registration approaches that optimize predefined image quality metrics such as cross-correlation [23]. However, these methods require a high-quality initial guess for optimization and typically do not register individual A-Scans within a B-scan. Moreover, such registration frequently destroys important image features, such as retinal curvature and, for active tracking scanners, ignores high-resolution telemetry regarding eye position during acquisition. This telemetry, providing spatial information about the imaging state at the time of acquisition, directly relates to the acquired signal and offers valuable insights into its interpretation. While addressing subject motion is crucial, achieving accurate correction of residual motion also depends on the hardware’s ability to closely track its input signal with high precision and low latency. Significant efforts have been recently made to guarantee that hardware components closely adhere to their drive signals. For instance, Tang et al. meticulously fine-tuned the parameters of the closed-loop galvanometer controller to optimize their response characteristics [24]. Draelos et al. generated drive signals that conformed to the dynamic limits-such as velocity and acceleration–of the galvanometers, avoiding requests for unattainably large, discontinuous jumps [25].
Despite efforts in conforming to hardware dynamic limits, current RAOCT methods involve changing the hardware drive signals on-the-fly to compensate for eye motion. Unfortunately, these changes to drive signals compromise any prior efforts to generate waveforms that adhere to hardware dynamic limitations. Furthermore, any allocation of resources to ensure on-the-fly changes align with hardware limitations could, in turn, lead to an increase in motion correction latency.
These limitations currently necessitate the use of image registration techniques to generate images of acceptable quality. While image registration is undeniably useful in OCT, advanced techniques often rely on a priori assumptions about data acquisition. For instance, many post-processing algorithms [26–28] assume raster scan patterns to identify the most likely direction of motion artifacts for subsequent scan pattern and reconstruction algorithm adjustments. Other robotic OCT systems utilize a pre-trained deep-learning model to register the OCT image space to physical space [29]. Some acknowledge that ocular eye motions, such as blinks or saccades, are mostly present as large, discontinuous motions that can be identified either in real-time [30] or in post-processing [27,31]. Others rely on assumptions about the physiological structure of the imaged blood vessels in the eye for feature recognition and registration [32–34]. While these registration algorithms are based on reasonable assumptions, they cannot replace direct measurements of motion artifact magnitude and their explicit correction. Direct knowledge of motion during an imaging session enables us to rely on analytical motion correction pipelines that are more widely applicable and less susceptible to broken assumptions.
This manuscript’s proposed sensor-driven DMC (hereafter referred to as DMC) achieves motion-reduction results limited only by sensor and ray-tracing accuracy, without the need for registration based on image metrics. Our approach exploits synchronized sensing of both the scanner’s optomechanical hardware and the subject’s eye to reassign individual A-Scans to their correct spatial location within the volume, as predicted by a ray-tracing approximation of the system at their time of acquisition. From the spatial location of the A-Scans, we can then generate a conventional A-Scan raster display. Our method is a significant extension of pixel-remapping algorithms, such as [35], taken to 3D data with integration of sensor signals from five tracking dynamic mirrors in the scanner as well as 5D eye tracking telemetry. DMC not only mitigates real-time motion correction latency but also overcomes imperfections in optical hardware positional accuracy.
We designed DMC to address motion artifacts traditionally corrected through registration, intending to complement registration’s role, if not replace it, by providing stable high-quality data before any reliance on optimization. With DMC, we aimed to eliminate significant motion artifacts that pose challenges for registration algorithms, as these algorithms rely on accurate initial guesses to optimize effectively. Although we designed DMC in the context of an RAOCT system, its applicability extends beyond these systems—any retinal imaging platform with tracking capabilities can leverage DMC. For example, handheld probes with integrated iris cameras, such as those in [36], or pupil-tracking-based robotic OCT probes without scanner-integrated dynamic tracking hardware, like the system presented in [37], could leverage their integrated eye-tracking data to apply DMC and correct for detected leftover residual motion.
For the remainder of this manuscript, we first describe the RAOCT system and hardware used for data collection, which closely resembles the setup presented in our previous work [14]. Then, in the Methods section, we introduce the novel synchronization and post-processing algorithms provided by DMC, which operate independently of the RAOCT’s hardware-based motion correction, yet integrate seamlessly when that hardware compensation is available. Finally, we present results from human subject imaging, demonstrating the effectiveness of our method in mitigating motion artifacts.

## System

## OCT engine
We operated our OCT scanner using a custom-built swept-source OCT engine (Fig. 1). The engine featured a swept-source laser (Axsun Technologies; Billerica, MA) emitting light at 1060(50) nm with an A-scan rate of 200 kHz. We detected the interferograms with a balanced photoreceiver (Thorlabs; Newton, NJ) in combination with a high-speed 12-bit digitizer (AlazarTech; Quebec, Canada). We performed real-time OCT processing and rendering through a graphics processing unit (GPU) and custom software developed using the Vortex open-source OCT software library [38]. The scanning mechanism consisted of Saturn 1B galvanometers (Pangolin Laser Systems; Orlando, FL) which steered a 2.7 mm beam launched from a collimator. A 2 in. fold mirror was used to direct the scan onto a retinal-conjugate fast steering mirror (FSM), which facilitated translating the beam at the pupil plane without changing its angle of incidence into the eye [39]. A short-pass dichroic mirror with a 930 nm cutoff (Semrock; Rochester, NY) folded the scanner into that of a pupil camera. Incorporated within the engine was a reference arm retro-reflector mounted onto a voice coil motor (VCM), enabling rapid path length adjustments. We designed the system for diffraction-limited OCT performance at 1060 nm of 8.5 µm full-width-half-max (FWHM) over a 20° field-of-view. We acquired volumes consisting of 500 B-scans per volume, 500 A-scans per B-scan, and 450 depth samples per A-scan.

## Automatic alignment
Our tracking system utilized 5 cameras for feedback control: two RealSense D415 3D cameras (Intel; Santa Clara, CA) for subject face tracking and three BlackFly 04S2M-CS monochrome cameras (FLIR; Richmond, BC, Canada) with 850 nm bandpass filters mounted to the scanner for pupil tracking. Our robot control software processed tracking data from both sets of cameras to automatically guide the scanner to the target eye with a UR5e robotic arm (Universal Robots; Odense, Denmark) without need for operator intervention. Upon coarse mechanical alignment of the robot, active tracking hardware further guided the OCT beam into the center of the ocular pupil by rotating and translating the beam at the pupil plane. Additionally, active tracking path length matched the sample and reference arms according to the measured distance to the eye (Fig. 1).
Active tracking is best described in [14], but we summarize it here for convenience. Three pupil and gaze tracking cameras identified eye motion in five dimensions (three translational and two rotational). See Fig. 2 for an example of pupil tracking camera images and their measured location and orientation. Leveraging this tracking data, subject motion along each dimension is individually counteracted in hardware by aiming the optical beam such that it enters the eye at the same point and orientation as it would had the subject not moved in the first place. Gaze changes, θ , are compensated by offsetting the corresponding galvanometer waveform by α=Mθ where M=f2f1 is the magnification of the scanner, f1 is the relay lens focal length, and f2 is the objective’s focal length. To address lateral pupil offsets ( d ), the FSM is tilted vertically and horizontally to translate the beam at the pupil plane and ensure incidence at the center of the ocular pupil. This mirror is conjugate to the retina and operates at an angle β≈d2f2 in each direction. Axial pupil offset z is compensated by axially displacing a retro-reflector in the reference arm δ=z to equalize the path lengths of both sample and reference arms.

## Eye and hardware sensing
Our DMC sensors, in charge of measuring eye and hardware states throughout the imaging session, consisted of the scanner-mounted pupil cameras and the controllers for the galvanometers, FSM, and VCM. The frame trigger for the pupil cameras was wired to an NI DAQmx card and driven by the laser sweep source. However, we note that DMC sensors could be synchronized using other I/O hardware components such as those based on FPGAs. The images captured by the pupil cameras were transmitted to a computer via USB interfaces. Furthermore, each hardware controller was equipped with analog position readout interfaces, which were directly connected to the NI DAQmx card. This configuration allowed the hardware states to be digitized by our OCT processing system in synchronization with the OCT trigger. ## Methods

## Sensor-driven digital motion correction
In traditional OCT, the conversion from multiple one-dimensional A-Scans into a 3D volume involves stacking A-Scans in a pre-configured pattern, commonly a raster scan, matching the commanded scanner positions. Unfortunately, this relies on assumptions that become problematic in non-ideal environments, where hardware configurations may deviate from expectations and subject eye movements are more likely to occur. Active tracking systems, such as RAOCT, minimize eye motion artifacts during acquisition by feeding forward eye tracking signals to optical or mechanical alignment tools, but still leave residual error. Here we took a novel volume reconstruction approach to address this residual error. Instead of stacking A-Scans to a predefined location, we leveraged eye and hardware sensor signals acquired during the imaging session to localize individual A-Scans to their correct spatial location within the volume (see Fig. 4). These can then be turned into a conventional A-Scan raster display as described in Sec 3.6. We note that because DMC tracks A-scans based on measured hardware configurations rather than predefined scan patterns such as straight B-scans, it can be applied to arbitrary scanning trajectories beyond the raster scan pattern we utilized here. For example, circular [40,41] and Lissajous [42] scan patterns, which have been utilized for motion correction, can be seamlessly integrated with DMC to leverage the benefits of both approaches.

In this approach, we synchronize sensor signals with the acquired A-Scans and post-process them to reduce noise and eliminate lag. Given the DMC sensor readings for each A-Scan, we ray trace the A-Scan to estimate its location in physical space relative to the retina. We then structure the A-Scans into a point cloud and interpolate the point cloud to generate a structured and renderable volume. The remaining subsections will delve into the details of each step of our approach.

## Sensor synchronization
The subject motion and active tracking hardware positions cannot be predicted in advance. Thus, we monitor their statuses with the aforementioned DMC sensors in synchronization with the OCT data acquisition. Galvanometer angles, FSM angles, and the linear VCM stage position were digitized with the NI DAQmx card by acquiring a new reading every 16th laser trigger, resulting in 12.5 kHz readout signals. The subject’s eye state was measured using the pupil tracking cameras (Fig. 2). Linear triangulation, along with pupil center and corneal reflection analysis [43], enabled us to measure the eye’s location and rotation. We synchronized the pupil camera images with the OCT data by forwarding every 1,000th laser sweep trigger to the cameras’ frame trigger, resulting in an effective drive frequency of 200 Hz. This allowed us to identify, for each pupil camera frame, the corresponding A-scan in the OCT data. See Fig. 5 for a schematic of our system with the synchronized DMC sensors.

## Sensor signal processing
Sensor signals, both from eye tracking and hardware readouts, exhibited noise and lag relative to the actual eye and hardware motion. The noise within eye tracking signals resulted from noise generated during image acquisition and processing. Meanwhile, hardware readout noise can be attributed to encoder imperfections and inherent analog-to-digital conversion noise. The lag encountered in pupil tracking signals originates from the exposure time allocated for each frame. Despite sharing a trigger with a well-known A-Scan, the necessity for exposure time meant the pupil tracking signal was time shifted relative to the OCT signal. Lag in hardware readouts arose from I/O delays stemming from their controllers.
We low-pass filtered all sensor signals with cubic Savitzky-Golay filters to reduce their noise. Then, we time shifted them by pre-calibrated amounts to compensate for their lags. Finally, since we acquired A-Scans at a higher rate (200 kHz) than our sensor signals, we upsampled sensor signals with linear interpolation to provide both eye and hardware position values for every A-scan. This way, every A-Scan had a total of 10 corresponding DMC sensor signal values: two galvanometer angles, two FSM angles, two gaze angles, two pupil lateral offsets, a pupil axial offset, and a VCM displacement.

## Ray Tracing Approximation
Commercial ray tracing software provides the ability to model dynamic perturbations optically, including eye and hardware motions. In principle, this allows inputting the detected DMC sensor signals into the model to trace the beam’s path and retrieve the spatial configuration of each A-Scan, as in [44,45]. However, due to the unique dynamic perturbations affecting each A-Scan within an imaging session, ray tracing would need to be conducted for every A-Scan in every volume. With our scan pattern consisting of 500 A-Scans per B-scan and 500 B-scans per volume, this would require 25,000 ray tracing evaluations per volume, making standard ray tracing software impractically time-consuming.
To address this, we proposed an alternative approach: a linear model calibrated to approximate the transfer function from our ray tracing software, OpticStudio. Our model operated with a 21×6 matrix to fit DMC sensor signals to desired spatial outputs, reducing the computational load while maintaining accuracy. The 21 inputs comprised the original 10 DMC sensor signals and an additional 11 supplementary terms chosen to better approximate effects that exhibit high non-linearity with respect to the original signals. One notable adjustment involved representing some sensor signals in polar coordinates instead of the original Cartesian ones, a modification aimed at more accurately modeling circularly symmetric effects. See Section S1 for more details on the additional terms. The resulting output parameters comprised 6 values that uniquely defined the estimated physical position of the A-Scans: including x , y , and z coordinates intersecting with the retina, along with pitch, yaw, and optical path length.
To generate the training data required for computing the values of this matrix, we performed ray tracing simulations with OpticStudio. The input parameters were galvanometer angles, FSM angles, gaze angles, and 3D pupil offsets. Here, we define a "perturbation" as a unique set of these inputs. The corresponding outputs were the 3D coordinates, 2D angle, and optical path length of the OCT beam’s intersection at the retina. For simulating the VCM effect, we adjusted the optical path length output by an offset equivalent to the VCM’s displacement. We utilized the Goncharov and Dainty eye model [46] which incorporates a GRIN lens in modeling the lens’ refractive profile. This model eye, widely used in OCT eye modeling, was designed to be accurate within 40°, which is larger than the field of view of our scans (20° measured outside the eye) and any expected angular rotation from the eye.
We conducted ray tracing simulations for 10,000 distinct perturbations, where the input parameters for each perturbation were randomly selected from a uniform distribution covering their permissible range. The permissible range for the galvanometers matched the entire size of the volume scan, while for other dynamic components, it was set at 10 % of their complete range of motion. The narrower range was chosen due to the relatively small changes during imaging sessions, facilitated by the robotic arm’s ability to minimize relative motion. See Fig. 6 for examples of the perturbations to our optical system.

To fit the matrix to our generated data, we employed a linear regression approach, optimizing the coefficients to minimize the error between the predicted outputs and the actual values obtained from the training data. This final linear model transformed DMC sensor inputs into 6 spatial configuration outputs, significantly reducing computation time and enabling efficient processing of high-density volumetric scans.
We reported the ray tracing approximation error along the axial, lateral, and rotational dimensions, presenting these results alongside eye tracking error data re-adapted from [14]. These two errors represent the primary sources of inaccuracies in DMC and correspond to the quantified performance along each dimension described in Sec 3.7. While the original report of eye tracking error in [14] assumed motion in the pupil plane, we have adapted the units here to reflect motion in the retinal plane, ensuring consistency with the context of DMC evaluation. Details on the unit standardization for both ray tracing and eye tracking errors are provided in Section S2.

## Point cloud generation
After performing ray tracing for each A-Scan within a volume, we created a point cloud using the acquired data. This point cloud was constructed with the initial pixel of an A-Scan aligned to its 3D location determined through ray tracing. Subsequent pixels were evenly distributed along the direction specified by the angle of incidence. Because B-scans at the retina follow a fan-beam pattern rather than the uniform rectangular grid seen in raw data, accurate 3D reconstruction requires dewarping through ray tracing at the A-scan level, even in the absence of motion. Although motion between consecutive A-scans is minimal, small errors accumulate across a B-scan when the eye or scanner moves rapidly, making individual A-scan remapping essential for preserving spatial accuracy.
To efficiently render and store the point cloud data for visualization, we employed a custom CUDA-based 3D Approximate Nearest Neighbor algorithm aiming to rasterize the data. This algorithm, essentially a resampling of the point cloud, utilized a depth-first search mechanism within a k-d tree data structure of the A-Scans. This approach enabled the algorithm to efficiently identify the nearest point cloud intensity value for each voxel on the rasterized volume.

## Motion reduction quantification lookup table
Mapping A-Scans to their estimated physical locations in space is valuable; however, this representation is not directly comparable to traditionally formatted volumes acquired without motion. These standard volumes remain formatted based on galvanometer angle-driven A-Scan stacking rather than the physical coordinates at the retina. To facilitate meaningful quantification and comparison, we opt for a rendering approach where we map the data from their estimated physical location back to the rectangular grid format of traditional raster volumes. To perform this transformation, we computed the mapping from physical space to rectangular grid by creating a coordinate lookup table where the coordinate of each entry corresponded directly to a voxel in the rectangular grid. That way, to compute the intensity of a voxel in the rectangular grid, we lookup the coordinate of its corresponding point and evaluate the intensity of the point cloud at that coordinate using the Approximate Nearest Neighbor algorithm. This lookup table effectively identified where the samples would have been acquired had there been no subject motion during the imaging session.
To compute this lookup table, we ray traced the location for each A-Scan, as previously described. However, this time we introduced the following modifications to the sensor signals before performing ray tracing. First, we replaced the galvanometer readout signal with the input galvanometer drive waveform minus the mean of the readout signal. Second, we set the eye’s gaze angles to their median values and kept them static throughout a volume acquisition. These galvanometer and gaze values were chosen as an approximation of the desired angle of incidence into the eye for the specific region of interest at the retina. Finally, all the remaining sensor signals were explicitly set to zero. This effectively simulated what the sensor signals would be in an ideal scenario, free from error, motion, and noise. The coordinates for the voxels of the ray traced A-scans were then the entries of the lookup table.
To illustrate the functionality of this lookup table, consider a scenario in which a traditional OCT volume is acquired from a stationary subject. Although this data is stored in a rectangular, rasterized format, it is essential to recognize that the scanning process occurs in a fan beam pattern at the retina instead. Our lookup table captures this fan beam’s shape, effectively mapping the physical coordinates of the A-Scans to the traditional rasterized volume format. The coordinates within this lookup table were the locations where we resampled our data to convert it back into rasterized format.

## Performance quantification
We assessed the accuracy of our correction methods for axial, lateral, and rotational dimensions using a model eye mounted on motorized stages. For each dimension, we conducted a series of experiments aimed at quantifying the effectiveness of our correction techniques. Notably, we rotated the eye for what we refer to as the “lateral” dimension and translated it horizontally for the “rotational” dimension. We inform the reader that this terminology arises from the fact that rotating the eye causes a horizontal translation in the image, while a horizontal translation of the eye results in a rotation in the image (see Section S2 for a more detailed explanation of this terminology).
Employing DMC, we analyzed data acquired with active tracking both enabled and disabled. We also subjected the model eye to movements of varying magnitudes: we covered small and large ranges of motion to demonstrate DMC over different types of motion as well as underscore the significance of active tracking and its synergy with DMC. We then presented the results as qualitative demonstrations of the improved image quality from DMC and report the results of quantification on the following cases: small motion with active tracking on, small motion with active tracking off, and large motion with active tracking on. We did not report quantitative results for large motion with active tracking off because there was too much loss of data to compute the relative error left in the images.

## Axial error correction
To measure axial error correction, we conducted a series of experiments using a linear stage aligned axially. In the first experiment, we collected repeated A-Scans while actively tracking the eye during ±0.5 mm back-and-forth motions at 5 mm/s. We then replicated the experiment with active tracking disabled, relying solely on pupil tracking camera telemetry for DMC. Following that, we repeated the experiment with active tracking enabled, this time increasing the motion range to ±2 mm. Lastly, we conducted the same experiment with an extended motion range but with active tracking turned off once again. In each case, we applied sensor-driven DMC to rectify any residual motion artifacts. To evaluate the remaining error, we calculated the standard deviation of voxel translations required to register each A-scan with a reference A-scan obtained when the stage was stationary. This translation was computed as the translation that maximized cross correlation between each A-scan to the reference.

## Lateral error correction
For lateral error correction, we conducted a series of experiments analogous to the axial ones. First, we performed a 500 × 500 raster scan on the model eye while a rotary stage rotated ±1.5° along the horizontal direction at a speed of 20 °/s. Next, we replicated this experiment with active tracking turned off, relying solely on gaze tracking telemetry for DMC. Afterward, we repeated both experiments with the range of motion increased to ±5°. We applied sensor-driven DMC and quantified the remaining error by calculating the standard deviation of translations needed to align a B-scan with its corresponding ground truth B-scan, obtained when the stage was stationary. This translation was computed as the translation that maximized cross correlation between each B-scan to the reference.

## Rotational error correction
To evaluate rotational error correction, we utilized the linear translation stage, placed horizontally instead of axially this time. We translated the eye in ±0.5 mm back-and-forth motions at 7 mm/s while imaging a repeated B-scan in the horizontal direction. Subsequently, we repeated this experiment with active tracking turned off, relying solely on pupil camera telemetry for DMC. Furthermore, we replicated both experiments with the range of motion increased to ±5 mm. We then applied sensor-driven DMC and quantified the remaining error by calculating the standard deviation of rotations required to align each B-scan with its corresponding ground truth B-scan obtained when the stage was stationary. This rotation was computed as the rotation component of the rigid body transform that maximized cross-correlation between each B-scan and the reference.

## Human imaging
We conducted imaging of consented human subjects under a protocol approved by the Duke University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB). During imaging, subjects positioned themselves in front of the RAOCT system, which then proceeded to align itself with the subject’s eye. An operator supervised the alignment process and, upon alignment, focused a tunable lens to correct for defocus error; the tunable lens power and eye defocus were then assumed not to change throughout the imaging session. Furthermore, the operator initiated the storage of OCT volumes once the robotic alignment was completed.
To assess the precision of DMC error correction in human subjects, we acquired 50 repeated B-scans and applied our sensor-driven DMC technique to them. Quantifying the remaining error involved selecting the first B-scan as a reference and employing rigid body registration for subsequent B-scans. Similar to the quantification in Sec 3.7, we evaluated axial, lateral, and rotational residual errors for each B-scan as the necessary vertical translation, horizontal translation, and angular rotation, respectively, needed for registration. This procedure was evaluated on the raw data as well as the DMC corrected data.
In addition to collecting repeated B-scans for quantitative evaluation, we extended the application of DMC to full 3D raster volumes acquired from our freely standing subjects. After collecting 3D volumes, we applied sensor-driven DMC on them to demonstrate the recovery of high-quality data in a three-dimensional context.

## Compatibility with registration
To underscore DMC’s compatibility with traditional registration pipelines and its role in furnishing a high-quality initial estimate for optimization, we stacked, registered, and averaged repeated B-scans acquired in Sec 3.8 before and after applying DMC to compare the quality of accepted registration techniques with and without DMC. Stack registration was performed by registering sequential B-scans using the standard techniques of rigid body registration presented in [23]. For this qualitative comparison, we selected a set of B-scans with particularly large visible residual motion artifacts to highlight the correction capabilities of DMC.

## Discussion
We have demonstrated substantial motion error reduction capabilities with DMC. Comparison of the residual errors from both human (Table 5) and eye model experiments (Table 2 to 4) theoretical limits of eye tracking and ray tracing errors (Table 1) suggests that these two factors are the main sources of residual error in our system. The ray tracing axial error is close in magnitude to the eye tracking error — smaller than the accuracy but larger than the precision. The lateral error is significantly smaller than that from gaze tracking, while the rotational error is notably larger. This suggests that enhancements to the ray tracing model are most likely to yield significant improvements in the rotational dimension, while improvements to gaze orientation prediction in eye tracking would best translate to higher-quality lateral results. For instance, if a user was willing to wait for results using an exact ray tracing, they could expect to see approximately a 0.3° improvement in the rotational dimension but should not expect a significant improvement in the axial or lateral dimensions, since performance is limited by eye tracking quality.
Figure 7 also highlights how the system performs differently depending on the frequency and magnitude of the subjected motion on hardware and subject. Across all dimensions, DMC performance for active tracking on is worse in the Small Motion condition than in the Large Motion condition. This is attributed to the higher frequency of Small Motion, which, despite its smaller magnitude, leads to more jerky movements and sharper turns that are harder for the system to correct. This demonstrates that the system is more tolerant to large-scale motion than to high-frequency, jerky movements.
Notably in Fig. 7, Large Motion experiments with active tracking off resulted in data clipping. This issue is not fully corrected by DMC alone, but when combined with active tracking, the clipping is effectively mitigated. This underscores the complementary nature of the two methods and highlights the importance of active tracking in scenarios involving substantial motion. An additional advantage of DMC is that errors in active tracking—such as regional instability at the retina due to galvanometer or FSM compensation fluctuations—do not manifest as artifacts from assuming stability. Instead, A-scans are remapped to their estimated acquired locations, preserving structural integrity despite hardware tracking inaccuracies.
A comparison of the Small Motion results with active tracking on (Table 2) versus active tracking off (Table 3) shows that image quality, residual error, and percentage error reduction are consistently better without active tracking. This is unsurprising, as the static nature of the imaging engine in the active tracking off state eliminates any error sources from tracking hardware components, leaving only eye tracking errors as the main source of error. This observation demonstrates that DMC can be applied to non-RAOCT systems, such as more economical traditional OCT devices, provided motion remains small. In these systems, an accurate eye tracking system would suffice to achieve high-quality results without the need for dynamic tracking hardware components. However, in RAOCT scenarios with large subject motion, the results from Large Motion with active tracking off clearly show the necessity of active tracking to prevent motion-induced data loss.
A further observation concerning the “Error After DMC” values for both Small and Large Motions with active tracking on (Table 2, Table 4), reveals that while the residual rotational error was approximately the same for both small and large motions, the corresponding panels of Fig. 7 show a sharper retina in the Large Motion case. This can be attributed to unintended consequences of translating the eye horizontally. While horizontal eye movement was performed to induce rotational error at the retina, it also causes the laser beam to illuminate the cornea at an offset. This in turn leads to changes in optical path length which themselves manifest as error unrelated to the induced rotation.
The results on human subject data further demonstrated DMC’s ability to correct motion artifacts, even in freestanding, non-stabilized subjects. As shown in Fig. 8, DMC successfully recovers retinal features otherwise lost due to subject instability. Figure 10 illustrates that DMC provides sufficiently fine-grained motion correction to reduce motion blur in averaged frames. Finally, Fig. 9 demonstrated DMC’s motion correction flexibility addressing even large and unpredictable motion artifacts—cases so severe that traditionally result in data being discarded rather than corrected.
While DMC delivers high-quality data that often obviates the need for further optimization, there may be scenarios where additional precision is required. In such cases, registration pipelines can seamlessly integrate with DMC, using its output as a refined starting point, as demonstrated in Fig. 11. By doing so, they avoid the pitfalls of relying on raw data, which is often laden with artifacts, thus enabling more accurate and reliable imaging results. Preceeding registration with DMC increases image quality by reducing the likelihood of failed registration on images with substantial motion artifacts.
We note that DMC takes less than 2 minutes to process the sensor signals, generate the 3D point cloud via ray tracing, and interpolate it into a motion-corrected 500×500×450 (B-scans, A-scans/B-scans, depth samples) volume. While this means DMC is not yet real-time, it still offers the ability to obtain results without significant delays post-acquisition. Importantly, DMC does not introduce any performance impact during acquisition, as the only additional overhead is recording sensor signals, which are minimal in size compared to the OCT volume. The primary constraint on reducing DMC processing time is the interpolation of the point cloud to a rasterized format, with k-d tree construction representing the most time-intensive component. Parallelizing this construction step, which is currently performed in a single-threaded environment, would likely lead to a substantial improvement in DMC’s overall speed. Moreover, GPU-based parallelization of A-scan ray tracing—which consists of a single matrix multiplication in our approximation—could significantly accelerate point cloud generation. For live-view rendering of the point cloud, sampling only a subset of the imaged space (e.g. the central B-scan), rather than sampling every voxel, could enable real-time visualization.
DMC could be further expanded by introducing additional sensors and tracking components. For instance, the tunable lens in our system (Fig. 1) could be dynamically adjusted and tracked as well, enabling focus-aware reconstruction such as that presented in [47]. Additionally, our ray tracing approach could be expanded to account for differently shaped eyes to better model subjects with significant refractive differences from the eye model utilized to construct the matrix. While DMC requires storing additional signals compared to traditional OCT, these sensor signals are small relative to the OCT data, contributing only a 4.4 % increase in total storage. As such, the inclusion of additional sensor values is unlikely to pose a significant storage burden.
DMC performance could be further enhanced by improving the accuracy of galvanometer sensor signals. Errors in the tuned sensor signal delay—required for synchronizing with A-scan inputs—can degrade image quality. Additionally, noise in the galvanometer sensor necessitated a large smoothing kernel; reducing ADC noise—such as through custom hardware optimized for low-noise acquisition rather than off-the-shelf I/O cards—could improve performance. These enhancements, particularly in precise galvanometer tuning and hardware-based noise reduction, would contribute to higher-quality DMC results, especially in the lateral dimension, where galvanometer signal errors become artifacts across B-scans.

## Conclusion
We corrected for residual motion in RAOCT by leveraging synchronized hardware and pupil tracking to estimate the state of the imaging system and used it to remap every acquired A-scan to its corresponding location in the volume. This technique yielded a substantial reduction in axial, lateral, and rotational motion artifacts in RAOCT, from both mechanically moved model eyes as well as free standing human subjects. DMC represents a significant advancement in OCT imaging, particularly in dynamic scenarios, by providing motion reduction capabilities that are inherently integrated into the imaging process and by freeing data formatting from its conventional tie to pre-configured scan patterns.
