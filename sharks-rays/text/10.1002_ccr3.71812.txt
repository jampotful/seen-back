# Beyond Treatment: The Strategy of Nonintervention in Choroidal Hemangioma

## Abstract
ABSTRACTThe circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH) is a benign congenital eye tumor whose etiology is not clear. A 41‐year‐old female patient came with a 2‐year history of slowly progressive deteriorating vision in her right eye. In the left eye, the best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 6/6, and counting fingers at 1 m in the right eye. The wide‐field fundus showed a yellow macular lesion with indistinct limits, which is a sign of serous retinal detachment. B‐scan ultrasonography revealed a clear‐cut choroidal lesion, high internal reflectivity, and visible intrinsic vascularity on color Doppler. OCT has shown a massive choroidal mass with damaged RPE and outer retinal layers, which has been obscured by serous retinal detachment. The chest x‐ray revealed bronchiectatic opacities, the CT scan revealed no lung lesions, and LFTs and RFTs were normal. The fluorescein angiography revealed a choroidal hemangioma with early vascularity and late hyperfluorescence. Nonetheless, the retinal team chose the option of observation instead of TTT, radiotherapy, or verteporfin photodynamic therapy due to the closeness of the lesion to the macula, low visual acuity, and lack of verteporfin PDT in Pakistan. To sum up, the present case demonstrates the significance of multimodal imaging in the proper diagnosis of choroidal hemangiomas and the necessity to pay close attention to patient‐specific factors when making treatment decisions, especially in limited resource settings.
Key Clinical MessageMultimodal imaging is essential for the timely diagnosis of circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH) to avoid irreversible visual impairments. Though photodynamic therapy (PDT) is the preferred treatment to preserve vision loss, close observation remains a justifiable approach in resource‐limited settings.

## Key Clinical Message
Multimodal imaging is essential for the timely diagnosis of circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH) to avoid irreversible visual impairments. Though photodynamic therapy (PDT) is the preferred treatment to preserve vision loss, close observation remains a justifiable approach in resource‐limited settings.

## Introduction
Circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH) is a benign and congenital tumor of the eye that has an unclear pathogenesis. It is normally classified into specific, circumscribed, and diffuse. The circumscribed variant is often solitary and is likely to be independent of the systemic ailments. The diffuse type, on the other hand, is associated with Sturge–Weber syndrome, which is a rare neurocutaneous condition that has port‐wine stains and choroidal lesions [1]. The majority of patients exhibiting CCH start showing symptoms and seeking medical attention in adulthood, which is usually between the fourth and sixth decades of their lives [2]. It is usually sporadic, and there is a slight male predisposition [3]. The exact incidence of CCH is not known, with only the cases that are symptomatic or accidentally diagnosed being reported. Nevertheless, it is estimated that every fifteen cases of choroidal melanoma have one case of CCH [4].
CCH is usually a red‐orange lesion in the posterior pole. The signs of CCH are mild, and it is not uncommon to find a choroidal hemangioma on the examination of an ophthalmologist. Notable complications are serous retinal detachment and macular oedema that may result in the loss of vision [5]. The symptoms of visual field include accumulation of subfoveal fluid and hyperopic shift. The other causes of vision loss are the elevation or distortion of the macula, cystoid macular edema, changes in the pigment epithelium (RPE), and photoreceptor degeneration [6]. Its differential diagnoses are ocular tumors like amelanotic choroidal melanoma, choroidal metastasis, and choroidal osteoma, and nonneoplastic ones such as central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR). Non‐specific results of the disease, including macular oedema and serous retinal detachment, may pose a challenge in diagnosis, and thus, proper application of ocular tests is essential [2]. Indeed, in an investigative study of 200 CCH cases by Shields et al. [7], it was found that 38 of them were misdiagnosed with choroidal melanoma or choroidal metastasis.
Although there are no specific treatment guidelines to follow, CCH specialists were able to provide some guidelines. These are routine monitoring of asymptomatic patients with isolated lesions and treatment of people with impaired eyesight or complications [8]. The modalities of interventional treatment include verteporfin photodynamic therapy, transpupillary thermotherapy, and radiotherapy [5]. It is necessary to stress that the primary goal of the treatment of choroidal hemangioma is not to decrease the volume of the tumor but simply to avoid the development of blindness and the appearance of complications that can lead to sight loss.
The present case reveals two significant issues with the CCH management. The first is the importance of multimodal imaging in the proper differentiation of CCH and other choroidal masses. Secondly, it illuminates the logic of refusing active treatment, even though there are complications and impaired vision.

## Case History
The patient in question is a 41‐year‐old female who presented to the outpatient clinic and has a history of gradually deteriorating vision in her right eye for the last two years. She did not experience any visual impairments before that time, and her medical history showed that there were no instances of inherited tumors or eye diseases in the family and no comorbidities were identified.
On examination, she had her best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of counting fingers at 1 m in the right eye and 6/6 in the left eye. Anterior chamber depth was found to be normal, and intraocular pressure was 10–21 mmHg. The examination showed equal and round pupils responsive to light without relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD). Examination with a slit lamp showed no defects of the anterior chamber, with the patient being identified as phakic. The conjunctiva and the cornea were clear. Additional examination at dilated fundus showed an indistinct yellow‐orange lesion at the macular area, which spreads toward the disc with indications of serous detachment of the retina below the lesion. On the basis of these clinical data, a different diagnosis of melanoma and choroidal hemangioma was made. Further tests were done to ascertain the diagnosis.

## Investigations
Fundal optical examination of the patient revealed the existence of a yellow lesion in the macular zone having poorly demarcated boundaries, as a sign of exudative retinal detachment (Figure 1). The B‐scan of the right eye showed a distinctive hyper‐echoic lesion in the chorioid at the temporal position that has internal vascularity observed in color Doppler and detached retina. These results of B‐scan were more in favor of a diagnosis of choroidal melanoma than of choroidal hemangioma.
Optos imaging revealed an orange‐yellow lesion overlying the macular and signs of serous retinal detachment.
The Brain and orbit Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) showed a clear crescent‐shaped, hyperintense lesion and a central dome on the temporal side of the globe. The lesion was about 0.9 × 0.4 × 1.0 cm (TR × AP × CC) in size, and it had a hyperintense signal on T1/FLAIR, hypointensity on T2, and lacked any diffusion restriction. It reached all the way to the optic disc without any sign of invasion into the disc/optic nerve. Scleral integrity was preserved, and no breach was observed; no retinal detachment was seen. The findings of the B‐scans were corroborated by the MRI impression, which indicated a right‐sided choroid‐based lesion, which is indicative of choroidal melanoma. A definitive diagnosis was suggested to use a contrast‐enhanced MRI. The optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the right eye showed that the patient had a huge choroidal mass, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and outer retinal layers disrupted, several cystic subretinal‐hyporeflective material, and foveal contour loss. Nonetheless, the quality of the OCT image was not that good because the signal was obscured by the serous retinal detachment.
Since the diagnosis was inconclusive, additional imaging tests and lab tests were done to explore choroidal metastasis. Chest x‐ray revealed bilateral bronchiectatic opacities in the middle zone, and chest CT scan revealed no signs of lung lesions. No abnormalities were observed in the laboratory tests, such as liver function tests (LFTs) and renal function tests (RFTs). The results of these studies resulted in the major differentials of choroidal hemangioma and amelanotic melanoma. This was followed by subsequent fluorescein angiography, which revealed the early phase of vascularity and the late phase of hyperfluorescence, which proved the existence of choroidal hemangioma as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3, respectively.
Early‐phase fluorescein angiography showing vascularity.
Late‐phase fluorescein angiography shows hyper‐fluorescence in the area of the lesion.
Indocyanine green angiography (ICG) and high‐resolution optical coherence tomography (OCT) were not performed at the initial visit because ultrasonography findings were highly suspicious for choroidal melanoma, prompting urgent MRI to exclude extraocular extension. Furthermore, OCT and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) were not available the same day, leading to MRI being prioritized. Once melanoma became less likely, OCT and FFA were completed to further characterize the lesion. Although additional modalities such as wide‐field OCT angiography could have enhanced assessment of intrinsic vascular patterns and helped differentiate CCH from amelanotic melanoma or metastatic lesions, the combination of ultrasonography, MRI, and subsequent fundus imaging provided sufficient diagnostic confidence to confirm CCH and guide management.

## Outcome and Follow‐Up
Treatment modalities that may be used with regard to the patient are TTT, radiotherapy, and photodynamic therapy. TTT can be used to treat certain intraocular tumors with the use of infrared radiation and may be effective in some cases of choroidal hemangiomas that are not associated with the fovea. TTT, however, is not very applicable in choroidal hemangiomas because of its specificity and the risk of damage to normal tissue. It mostly affects melanocytes and, thus, is ineffective with vascular lesions such as hemangiomas. In addition, it has doubtful efficacy in the selective destruction of abnormal blood vessels because hemangiomas are highly vascularized.
One of the methods of treating ocular tumors is radiotherapy (RT), in addition to surgery, laser therapy, cryotherapy, and chemotherapy. External beam RT (EBRT) as well as brachytherapy are employed. Radiotherapy is, however, not the most suitable treatment for choroidal hemangioma because of its lower effectiveness and possible complications, including destruction of normal ocular tissues such as the retina and optic nerve, as well as the possibility of impaired vision. Other therapies, including photodynamic therapy, laser photocoagulation, or observation alone, are favored since they are not as invasive and would not cause as many complications. This results in radiotherapy being a non‐first‐line choice in the treatment of choroidal hemangiomas because of the risk–benefit ratio.
Photodynamic therapy is a relatively noninvasive method that involves the use of a photosensitizing agent and light to selectively destroy diseased, abnormal cells or tissue. It can be used in the treatment of choroidal hemangioma, and it may be the treatment of choice, particularly for those that are situated at the fovea. Because of its safety and repeatability, photodynamic therapy can be performed to cure recurring incidences of the tumor. Verteporfin, used in photodynamic therapy, would be an appropriate choice in this case because it is less invasive and the probability of complications is lower. But in Pakistan, verteporfin is not available, and so this cannot work. Since the lesion is located in the macula, interventional treatment may deteriorate the visual acuity. The vision of the patient is now at the counting‐fingers level, and this is why it is important to maintain the vision and reduce risks. As the vision of the patient did not deteriorate in one year, it is not likely to deteriorate without interventions. Thus, the patient was decided to be observed once every six months. Strategies to be used in monitoring will refer to visual acuity examination, fundus, and imaging studies by conducting regular follow‐up with the patient. Even though choroidal hemangioma (CCH) is typically unilateral, the other eye will also be checked every visit so as to make sure that the vision in the unaffected eye is being maintained.
The case has highlighted the importance of considering all the characteristics of the patient's condition, including clinical presentation and availability of the treatment, in the decision‐making process. The processes that may have stimulated vascular proliferation in choroidal hemangiomas are poorly understood, and further development of knowledge on their pathophysiology and molecular pathways will result in novel medical interventions against particular pathways. This could provide the substitution of interventional treatments that are potentially sight‐threatening.

## Discussion
The CCH are benign but may enlarge because of the congestion and the venous vasodilation with the histological classifications of capillary, cavernous, or mixed [1, 3, 5, 9, 10]. This expansion may result in the leakage of fluid and cause complications such as macular edema and serous retinal detachment, which cause symptoms such as blurred vision and visual distortion [11]. CCH are uncommon and are usually identified when symptomatic or as a routine checkup [12]. According to Shields et al., this has been misdiagnosed, with 38% of 200 CCH cases initially diagnosed as either choroidal melanoma or metastasis. Since the development of retinal and choroidal imaging, including OCT, has led to a better level of diagnostic accuracy [7].
As in the case of choroidal haemangioma, the primary goal of treatment is to prevent vision loss in both the affected eye and the unaffected eye. Once visual stability is established, considerations can be made to reduce tumor size, which in turn would resolve any complications. Different treatment options are available to treat the patients according to the nature of the case [11]. The current case presents a clinical dilemma when deciding to manage a case of CCH. Active intervention is typically reserved for complications that can lead to vision loss. These include serous retinal detachment or macular edema. Monitoring is sufficient for uncomplicated, asymptomatic cases, contrasting with aggressive neoplasms, where treatment is more common [2]. The main concern was the risk of worsening visual loss associated with interventional treatment, as previously discussed.
Blasi et al. [13] found that 76% of patients experienced a two‐line improvement in visual acuity, along with complete resolution of subretinal fluid and no observed complications. Interventional treatments can result in chorioetinal scarring, particularly concerning unfavorable visual outcomes, especially when the hemangioma affects the foveal region, as in this patient. Therefore, monitoring the patient was the only option in this case, which remains a viable choice even for advanced stages of the disease, considering that further progression is unlikely [5]. A retrospective review by Shields et al. [14, 15] demonstrated that tumor progression does not necessarily occur in patients who were under observation, suggesting that simple monitoring may be an acceptable strategy in cases where Photodynamic therapy is contraindicated or unavailable, as in our case.
Patients presenting with CCH involving the macula or with markedly reduced baseline vision, such as perception of light (PL) or ≤ 20/200, generally have a poor visual prognosis. Chronic macular detachment and irreversible photoreceptor damage limit the likelihood of meaningful visual improvement, and long‐term studies show that many patients with macular involvement continue to have vision worse than 20/200 even after resolution of subretinal fluid [7]. When vision has already declined to the PL level, structural damage is typically permanent, making functional recovery unlikely regardless of intervention. In such circumstances, observation is a reasonable management strategy, particularly when the lesion is stable and the expected benefit of treatment is minimal. Because CCH often demonstrates slow growth and may remain clinically stable for years, conservative monitoring is appropriate when anatomical recovery is improbable or when the eye is unlikely to gain functional vision despite treatment [16].
