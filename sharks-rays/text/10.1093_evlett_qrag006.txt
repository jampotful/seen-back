Visual pigments of basal lineages of bony fishes support independent ecological shifts from a shallow marine to a freshwater niche.
Cui Y, Li N, Chen H, Xing L, Xie Y, Xie Y, Ma M, Zhao J, Nan W, Lei L, Wang K, Rossiter SJ, Liu Y. (2026). Evolution letters. . doi:10.1093/evlett/qrag006. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Visual pigments of basal lineages of bony fishes support independent ecological shifts from a shallow marine to a freshwater niche

## Abstract
AbstractBony fishes (Osteichthyes) occupy a diverse range of aquatic habitats, yet the ecological transitions underlying their early evolution remain debated. Extant “living fossil” lineages—such as lungfishes and basal ray-finned fishes—are primarily restricted to benthic freshwater habitats, raising questions about the ancestral ecology of bony fishes. To investigate this, we reconstructed and expressed visual pigments from both extant and inferred ancestral taxa in vitro, enabling characterization of their spectral sensitivities. The results reveal that the ancestral visual phenotype is most consistent with adaptation to shallow-water light conditions. Furthermore, parallel shifts in the spectral tuning of visual pigments across both lobe-finned and ray-finned fish lineages were observed, with consistent patterns of shorter wavelength tuning in middle/long-wavelength-sensitive pigments, paired with longer wavelength shifts in others. The shifts of spectral tuning support an ecological transition from marine to freshwater habitats. Additionally, changes in rhodopsin retinal release rates and signatures of positive selection on opsin genes further point to independent visual adaptations to freshwater environments in both lineages. These findings suggest that early bony fish evolution involved ecological expansion from shallow marine habitats into deeper or more turbid freshwater environments, as reflected in parallel adaptations of visual systems to benthic photic conditions.Keywords: Osteichthyes, opsin, spectral tuning, retinal release rate, visual adaptation

## Introduction
Visual pigments, comprising opsins bound to a light-sensitive chromophore, mediate vision across diverse light environments and often underpin ecological adaptation in vertebrates (Davies et al., 2012; Hauser & Chang, 2017). Early opsin duplications in vertebrates gave rise to five major kinds of photopigments, each associated with distinct spectral sensitivities (Collin et al., 2003). Rhodopsin (RH1) mediates dim-light vision and is expressed in rod cells, while four cone visual pigments mediate bright-light vision: rhodopsin-like (RH2), middle/long-wavelength-sensitive (M/LWS), and two short-wavelength-sensitive opsins (SWS1 and SWS2). The spectral tuning of these visual pigments is determined by residues at multiple critical sites that influence interactions with the bound chromophore group (Davies et al., 2012; Yokoyama, 2008).
In most vertebrates, including terrestrial taxa and the majority of fishes, visual pigments are attached to an 11-cis-retinal chromophore (vitamin A1). In contrast, some lineages of fishes, amphibians, and reptiles instead use an 11-cis-3,4-dehydroretinal (vitamin A2) (Liebman & Entine, 1968; Martin et al., 2015; Provencio et al., 1992; Toyama et al., 2008). The occurrence of A2 confers increased sensitivity to longer wavelengths and is thought to be an adaptation to aquatic habitats characterized by red-shifted light environments, including turbid freshwater rivers and lakes as well as estuaries and swamps (Bridges, 1966; Toyama et al., 2008). Indeed, while marine waters are enriched with blue light as depth increases, in freshwater systems the shorter wavelengths are absorbed by higher levels of suspended particulates (Terai et al., 2017; Van Nynatten et al., 2021).
Bony fishes retain the five types of visual pigments, though the diversity of opsin genes is especially expanded in teleost fishes (Musilova et al., 2021). While visual pigments have been well studied in teleosts, phenotypic data remain limited for basal osteichthyan lineages (Cortesi et al., 2020; Fujiyabu et al., 2019; Hart et al., 2008). Following the divergence of the bony fish ancestor, two major evolutionary lineages emerged: lobe-finned fishes (Sarcopterygii), represented by freshwater-dwelling lungfishes and deep-sea coelacanths, and ray-finned fishes (Actinopterygii), including both teleosts and non-teleost groups such as gars, bowfins, sturgeons, and bichirs (Bowmaker, 2008; Hart et al., 2008; Hughes et al., 2018; Lagman et al., 2013). The ancestors of lungfishes and non-teleost ray-finned fishes survived the Late Devonian extinction (Sallan & Coates, 2010) and are now largely restricted to benthic freshwater habitats (FishBase, www.fishbase.org). While the bony fish ancestor is often considered marine (Allulee & Holland, 2005; Darby, 1982; Davies et al., 2007; Sallan et al., 2018), another hypothesis proposes it may have inhabited freshwater (Janvier, 2007; Moyle & Cech, 2004). Moreover, basal lineages differ in cone oil droplets, which can further shape spectral sensitivity via light filtering (Toomey & Corbo, 2017).
The RH1 pigment of the bony fish ancestor has been experimentally reconstructed with a λmax of 501 nm based on data from 196 representative vertebrate species (Liu et al., 2019). In contrast, the spectral properties of the four cone visual pigments (SWS1, SWS2, RH2, and M/LWS) have only been inferred or reconstructed using much smaller datasets—typically ranging from 7 to 37 species—and have revealed contradictory results (Yokoyama & Jia, 2020; Yokoyama & Radlwimmer, 2001; Yokoyama & Tada, 2003; Yokoyama et al., 2016). In particular, the reported ancestral RH2 of bony fish (488 nm) was close to a deep-sea type (Yokoyama & Jia, 2020; Yokoyama et al., 1999), whereas the RH1 phenotype (501 nm) was hypothesized to reflect a surface-dwelling niche (Yokoyama et al., 2008). These contrasting inferences mean that the light environment and thus habitat of early osteichthyans remain unresolved.
To investigate the early origin and freshwater adaptations of these basal lineages, we resurrected four of the five visual pigments (SWS1, SWS2, RH2, and M/LWS) in the ancestor of bony fish using large gene datasets, and conducted in vitro assays from pigments of ancestors and extant basal fishes. For RH1, we used our recently published ancestral phenotype based on the same experimental protocol (Liu et al., 2019). By comparing phenotypes of pigments to the ancestral forms, we aimed to gain insights into the ecological niche of the earliest bony fish and to trace visual adaptations across their evolutionary diversification.

## Methods

## Opsin sequence collection and ancestral sequence reconstructions
To determine the evolutionary history of visual pigment spectral tuning in bony fishes, we obtained a dataset of cone opsin genes comprising 141 SWS1, 186 SWS2, 125 RH2, and 154 M/LWS sequences from representative vertebrate families obtained from GenBank (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) (Supplementary Data S1–S4). Ancestral reconstructions of each opsin were performed using Codeml from PAML 4 under the best-fit amino acid substitution models: JTT+Γ for SWS1, LG+Γ for SWS2, LG+ I+Γ for RH2, and LG+Γ for M/LWS (Yang, 2007). Phylogenetic tree topologies were obtained from TimeTree 5 (www.timetree.org) (Kumar et al., 2022). For duplicated genes within each of the four cone opsins, Bayesian phylogenetic trees were inferred using MrBayes 3 (Ronquist et al., 2012) (Supplementary Data S5–S8).
For extant basal bony fishes, we focused on two clades—lungfishes and non-teleost ray-finned fishes (Bi et al., 2021; Braasch et al., 2016; Hughes et al., 2018; Meyer et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021)—both of which retain all five opsins (Bailes et al., 2007; Braasch et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2017). In contrast, only three opsin genes (RH1, RH2, and SWS2) are present in the deep-sea-adapted sarcopterygian coelacanth (Lagman et al., 2013). Our dataset included three of the six extant lungfish species covering all three families: the Australian lungfish (Neoceratodus forsteri, family Neoceratodontidae), the South American lungfish (Lepidosiren paradoxa, family Lepidosirenidae), and West African lungfish (Protopterus annectens, family Protopteridae) (Marshall, 1986). For non-teleost ray-finned fishes, opsin sequences from gars (Lepisosteus oculatus and Atractosteus spatula), bowfin (Amia calva), sturgeons (Acipenser ruthenus and Polyodon spathula), and bichirs (Polypterus senegalus and Erpetoichthys calabaricus) that are close relatives to teleost fishes were obtained (Supplementary Data S9).

## Phenotypic in vitro assays for visual pigments
To express opsin proteins for ancestral cone visual pigments and each of the basal bony fishes, we ligated coding sequences into a pcDNA 3.1 expression vector (Invitrogen). The Kozak sequence (5′ CCACC 3′) was added at the 5′ of the open reading frame, and a tag for purification (5′ ACA GAG ACC AGC CAA GTG GCG CCT GCC 3′) at the 3′ end. Plasmids containing target opsin genes were transfected (48 hf) in HEK293T cells using LipoFiter transfection reagent (Hanbio Biotechnology). Visual pigments were then regenerated by coupling expressed opsins at 4 °C with the chromophore 11-cis-retinal (vitamin A1) (Boston University). After solubilization using n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (Macklin), visual pigments were purified using monoclonal antibody Rho1D4 (The University of British Columbia) and then eluted using a buffer consisting of 140 mM NaCl, 50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (pH = 6.6), 3 mM MgCl2, 40 μM rhodopsin epitope (GenScript), 0.1% maltoside, and also 20% (w/v) glycerol, as previously described (Chi et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2021; Yokoyama et al., 2008).
For each visual pigment, we obtained the λmax (maximum absorption wavelength) by taking the peak value from the absorption spectrum generated using a U-3900 (Hitachi) spectrophotometer under dark conditions (dark spectrum). For pigments with a low dark peak, we also measured and calculated either dark-minus-light or dark-minus-acid difference spectrum. For SWS2, RH2, or M/LWS, this was done using light bleaching (dark-minus-light spectrum), and for SWS1 using acid (H2SO4) denaturation (dark-minus-acid spectrum) according to published methods (Chi et al., 2024, 2025). We also measured the retinal release rate of RH1 (30-s interval) after 1 min of light bleaching, using a Cary Eclipse (Agilent) fluorescence spectrophotometer at 20 °C. The excitation and emission wavelengths were 295 nm (2.5 nm slit) and 330 nm (10 nm slit). After fitting release rates to y = y0 + a(1–e−bx), the t1/2 (half-life of retinal release rate) values were calculated following ln2/b (Xia et al., 2021). For each RH1 pigment, three experimental replicates were carried out, and t1/2 values of retinal release rates were compared using a two-tailed t-test. Raw data from the phenotypic assays are provided in Supplementary Data S10.

## Selection tests for lobe-finned fish and ray-finned fish opsin genes
To assess whether sequence variation observed in lobe-finned fish and ray-finned fish opsin genes potentially arose through molecular adaptation, we tested for positive selection on the ancestral branches of lungfishes and ray-finned fishes for five opsin genes. For RH1, a total of 205 sequences, including those from basal fishes and other vertebrates (Liu et al., 2019), were aligned using MEGA 11 (Tamura et al., 2021) (Supplementary Data S11). For cone visual pigments, we used the same sequences that were used for ancestral reconstruction. Then, we used the branch-site model (test 2) in Codeml (Yang, 2007), in which the alternative model assumes a class of sites under positive selection ω (dN/dS) > 1, while the null model assumes neutral evolution (Zhang et al., 2005). For this analysis, we assessed significance using a likelihood ratio test (Zhang et al., 2005), a topology of RH1 by combining published phylogenies of lungfishes (Tokita et al., 2005), non-teleost ray-finned fishes (Hughes et al., 2018), and also other vertebrates (Liu et al., 2019) and species tree topologies of cone visual pigments were obtained from the TimeTree 5 database (www.timetree.org) (Kumar et al., 2022) (Supplementary Data S12). To ensure the robustness of these findings, we also applied the aBSREL method in HyPhy to test for episodic positive selection on the same basal fish branches (Smith et al., 2015).

## Results and discussion
For the functional characterization of extinct SWS1, SWS2, RH2, and M/LWS pigments in fishes, we first undertook ancestral reconstructions based on large sequence datasets. Using these sequences, we then expressed the visual pigments in vitro, and performed spectral tuning measurements. In addition, we carried out in vitro expression and phenotypic analysis of visual pigments from several extant species: SWS2 from the deep-sea coelacanth, and all five visual pigments (SWS1, SWS2, RH1, RH2, and M/LWS) from two lungfish species (lobe-finned fishes) as well as from five non-teleost ray-finned fishes from four orders (Polypteriformes, Acipenseriformes, Amiiformes, and Lepisosteiformes). For each of these taxa we assessed the spectral tuning of the five visual pigments containing the A1 chromophore, focusing on their adaptations to the specific light conditions in aquatic environments (Carleton & Yourick, 2020; Musilova et al., 2021; Yokoyama et al., 2008). Additionally, we investigated the retinal release rate of RH1, which provides insight into adaptations for dim-light vision (Bickelmann et al., 2015; Gutierrez et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019).
For the resurrected visual pigments of the ancestral bony fish, the λmax values of the four cone opsins range from 357 to 563 nm (357 nm for SWS1, 441 nm for SWS2, 507 nm for RH2, and 563 nm for M/LWS, respectively) (Figure 1). These newly measured phenotypes are largely consistent with previously published values (Yokoyama & Jia, 2020; Yokoyama & Radlwimmer, 2001; Yokoyama et al., 2016). Notably, this study presents the first experimental expression of the ancestral SWS2 pigment from early Osteichthyes, which supports the earlier predicted λmax value (Yokoyama & Tada, 2003). Interestingly, our updated RH2 ancestral phenotype (507 nm) supports a shallow-water origin hypothesis (Jablonski et al., 1983; Sallan et al., 2018). This value aligns more closely with those of extant basal fishes and ancestral surface-type RH1 (∼500 nm) (Yokoyama et al., 2008), rather than earlier RH2 estimates (488 nm) (Yokoyama & Jia, 2020), which reflect deep-sea adaptations similar to those seen in the coelacanth (Yokoyama et al., 1999).

To explore freshwater adaptations in extant basal lineages, we also measured the λmax values of five visual pigments in lungfishes and non-teleost ray-finned fishes. In SWS1 (UV-sensitive), some basal fishes retain spectral tuning similar to the ancestral state. However, the spotted gar shows a >5-nm shift toward shorter wavelengths (blue-shift), while the paddlefish, Australian lungfish, and West African lungfish display >5-nm shifts toward longer wavelengths (red-shift) (Figure 2A and B; Figure S1). Although UV vision is generally considered ecologically important in fishes (Cortesi et al., 2020), the adaptive relevance of the shorter wavelength shift observed in the duplicated SWS1 pigments of the spotted gar remains unclear.

For the blue-sensitive SWS2 pigment, bowfin, sturgeons, and bichirs retain a λmax near 441 nm—consistent with the ancestral state—suggesting conservation of spectral sensitivity (Figure 2A and B; Figure S1). In contrast, the Australian lungfish SWS2 shows a red-shifted λmax of 466 nm, representing a >10-nm shift toward longer wavelengths. This likely reflects adaptation to freshwater habitats rich in algae (Marshall et al., 2011). Meanwhile, the spotted gar SWS2 shows a blue-shifted λmax of 431 nm, diverging by over 10 nm from other basal fishes and the ancestral state. No spectral sensitivity could be measured in vitro for the coelacanth SWS2 pigment, suggesting a possible loss of light sensitivity (Figure 2A and B; Figure S1). Indeed, mutations at sites 177 and 190, found only in coelacanth SWS2 (Figure 2C), are known to impair the chromophore binding in bovine rhodopsin (Janz et al., 2003). It is thus possible that these substitutions in the coelacanth have led to functional pseudogenization despite the gene sequence appearing intact, as was recently suggested for whale cone pigments (Chi et al., 2024).
RH2 pigments (green-sensitive), with λmax values ranging from 505 to 518 nm in extant species, exhibit multiple independent red shifts relative to the ancestral value (507 nm) (Figure 2A and B; Figure S1). Notably, sturgeons exhibit independently evolved duplicated RH2 genes, as previously reported (Musilova & Cortesi, 2023), while displaying similar λmax values. Additionally, while the dim-light-sensitive rhodopsin is generally conserved across basal fishes, we identified longer wavelength-sensitive shifts in the pigments of sturgeon and paddlefish, with λmax values of 508 and 510 nm, respectively (Figure 2A and B; Figure S1). Most adult sturgeons inhabit dim and turbid environments on the bottoms of freshwater habitats (Bowmaker, 2008), suggesting an adaptive response to such conditions. In contrast, the coelacanth, which is adapted to deep-sea environments, has been reported to exhibit both blue-shifted RH1 and RH2, reflecting the short-wavelength-rich conditions of the deep sea (Yokoyama et al., 1999).
For the M/LWS pigments (red- or green-sensitive), all species except the spotted gar (λmax = 560 nm, a 5-nm shift) exhibit substantial shifts toward shorter wavelengths. The Australian lungfish, bowfin, sturgeon, and bichir show λmax values around 550 nm, representing shifts of more than 10 nm relative to the ancestral state (Figure 2A and B; Figure S1). In general, compared to marine fishes, freshwater species exhibit red-shifted visual pigments, often associated with the use of vitamin A2-based chromophore, which promotes sensitivity to longer wavelengths (Bhattacharya, 2018; Corbo, 2021; Lythgoe & Brighton, 1972; Toyama et al., 2008; Van Nynatten et al., 2021). Additionally, similar spectral tuning patterns—such as RH1 red shift and M/LWS blue shift—have been reported in Lake Victoria cichlids adapted to deeper layers enriched in long-wavelength light, using both A1- and A2-derived visual pigments (Terai et al., 2017). Consistent with this, we find that in comparison to the ancestral bony fish, lungfishes and non-teleost ray-finned fishes display red-shifted λmax values in SWS1, SWS2, RH1, and RH2 pigments, whereas M/LWS pigments show a notable blue shift (Figure 2B; Figure S1). These changes likely reflect parallel adaptations to benthic freshwater environments from an ancestral shallow-marine origin. This conclusion is further supported by the detection of positive selection across all five opsin genes, indicating these visual changes were ecologically adaptive (Figure 3; Tables S1–S6). We propose that this suite of visual adaptations was fine-tuned for life in freshwater habitats, which may have served as refuges during widespread anoxic events such as the Late Devonian extinction (Clack, 2007). Alongside the evolution of air-breathing via lungs (Li et al., 2025), these visual adaptations may have been critical in enabling these lineages to survive the mass extinctions.

To further investigate freshwater adaptation, we measured the retinal release rates in Australian lungfish and the spotted gar. Our results show that the half-lives (t1/2) of rhodopsin retinal releases are significantly faster in both lungfish (t1/2 = 9.81 ± 1.76 min, p = 0.004) and gar (t1/2 = 8.56 ± 0.68 min, p = 0.001) compared to our published data for the ancestral bony fish (Liu et al., 2019) (Figure 4; Figure S1), indicating that the accelerated kinetics evolved independently in each species. Slower retinal release (longer half-life) is associated with enhanced dim-light sensitivity and nocturnal activity (Gutierrez et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019), whereas a fast retinal release rate confers keen response for changing light conditions, such as those that might be experienced by deep-diving species (Dungan & Chang, 2022; Xia et al., 2021). These rapid kinetics in lungfish and gar may thus reflect independent adaptations to dynamic freshwater lighting, consistent with findings in freshwater croakers (Van Nynatten et al., 2021). For lungfishes, the fast retinal release rate might also be compensated by their large rod cells that possibly improve sensitivity (Appudurai et al., 2016; Bailes et al., 2006; Collin et al., 2009).

## Conclusions and caveats
Our findings demonstrate that both spectral tuning and rhodopsin retinal release kinetics have undergone shifts in several fish species of the so-called “living fossils.” The M/LWS pigment, which typically absorbs the longest wavelength sensitivity among the five visual pigments, shows consistent blue shifts in both lungfishes and non-teleost ray-finned fishes. In contrast, the remaining visual pigments—SWS1, SWS2, RH1 and RH2—generally display red-shifted sensitivities in these groups. These patterns suggest adaptive responses to benthic freshwater environments, where light conditions differ markedly from those in open marine waters. Additionally, shifts in RH1 retinal release kinetics provide further evidence that lungfishes and non-teleost ray-finned fishes independently adapted to dynamic freshwater light environments following divergence from their common marine ancestor. Our newly reconstructed ancestral RH2 phenotype (λmax = 507 nm) supports a shallow-water origin for early bony fishes. Taken together, the spectral and kinetic shifts observed in basal bony fish lineages relative to their reconstructed ancestor indicate evolutionary transitions from surface marine to benthic freshwater habitats.
Finally, it is important to emphasize that our results and interpretations assume that in vitro λmax values of recombinant pigments (reconstituted with the A1 chromophore) provide a reliable proxy for the intrinsic spectral tuning of opsins in vivo. While this assumption underpins most comparative studies of opsin function, experiments in fishes to assess the color sensitivity of individual pigments are challenging due to the presence of many pigments. However, mounting evidence supporting the correspondence between in vitro and in vivo results comes from gene-editing experiments in fish and mice (Homma et al., 2017; Jacobs et al., 2007; Kanazawa et al., 2020). For these reasons, we interpret our λmax estimates as robust for comparing opsin-based tuning across lineages under standardized conditions, although recognize that additional physiological and optical effects may modify spectral sensitivity in vivo, particularly in lungfishes where colored oil droplets are present (Toomey & Corbo, 2017).
