# Synergistic Band-Gap Engineering and Visible-Light Activation in Fe, La Doped ZnO Nanoparticles

## Abstract
AbstractFe and La-co-doped ZnO nanoparticles were prepared by using the sol–gel method. We investigated the samples’ structural, morphological, vibrational, and optical properties using X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. Rietveld refinement of the XRD pattern confirmed the p63mc space group and the hexagonal wurtzite structure of the samples, with no formation of secondary phases. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis revealed that, in Fe- and La-doped ZnO, zinc exists as Zn2+, iron as Fe3+, and lanthanum as La3+. These substances have substantial surface oxygen defects, which account for the visible emission bands. FTIR spectra confirm the presence of a hexagonal wurtzite structure and of the chemical bonds and functional groups in the sample. UV–vis DRS spectroscopy revealed a decrease in the band gap due to Fe doping. Fe and La doping reduce the band gap of ZnO, causing a redshift that enhances interaction with visible light. This makes Fe, La-doped ZnO a potential photocatalyst. The PL spectra exhibit UV emission from excitonic recombination and visible emissions, including violet light from zinc vacancies and green light from zinc–oxygen divacancy defect transitions. The correlated color temperature values are 6425–6503 K, which closely match ideal cool white light. This makes it ideal for outdoor white LED applications due to its enhanced visual acuity and brightness.

## Introduction
Semiconductor nanoparticles have attracted significant interest due to their distinct physical and chemical properties compared to bulk materials. As their size approaches the exciton Bohr radius, quantum confinement effects alter their optical, electrical, structural, and other characteristics. , Among these nanoparticles, zinc oxide (ZnO) is the most widely studied due to its broad industrial applications. In recent decades, ZnO, a key metal oxide semiconductor, has garnered significant research interest due to its exceptional properties, which enable a wide range of applications. ZnO is an n-type semiconductor with a direct band gap of 3.37 eV and an exciton binding energy exceeding 60 meV. ZnO exhibits diverse morphological forms, including nanotubes, nanorods, core–shell structures, nanowires, helices, nanobelts, and nanoplatelets. The surface-to-volume ratio of these structures varies significantly, which influences their electrical conductivity, magnetic properties, and gas sensing performance. −
The physical properties of ZnO nanomaterials can be tailored by modifying the synthesis technique and processing conditions or by incorporating dopants into the host matrix. A common strategy employed by researchers to fine-tune the characteristics of ZnO is doping it with transition metals (TMs), such as Ni, Co, Cr, Fe, Cu, V, or Mn. Furthermore, studies indicate that Fe-doping significantly improves the physical properties of ZnO-based nanostructures. − Selvanayaki et al. observed an increase in the band gap when the Fe doping concentration in ZnO was increased. Mohammadigharehbagh et al. reported a decrease in the band gap due to Fe doping. Valentin et al. observed a decrease in the optical bandgap of ZnO nanoparticles with Fe doping. Gupta et al. reported the same type of result. Currently, ZnO nanoparticles doped with rare earth (RE) ions are of significant interest due to their ability to enhance the optical properties of semiconductor nanoparticles. The importance of RE metals lies in their ability to capture electrons, which prevents the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs. RE elements are extensively utilized as activators to achieve strong fluorescence and narrow emission bandwidths in various host materials, such as ZnO and TiO2. − In RE-doped ZnO nanoparticles, fluorescence primarily arises from electronic transitions between the 4f-4f or 4f-5d energy levels, resulting in sharp and intense emission spectra. These properties make RE-doped ZnO nanoparticles highly promising for use in optoelectronic devices such as light-emitting diodes and laser systems. In contrast, the fluorescence of TM-doped ZnO nanoparticles originates from defect-related energy levels, including oxygen vacancies, zinc interstitials, and zinc antisites. The emission spectra associated with transitions from these defect levels to the ground state are generally broader and less intense than those of RE-doped ZnO. Green fluorescence, which is attributed to these defect-mediated transitions, has been widely reported in TM-doped ZnO nanoparticles.
A codopant, or second dopant, is often incorporated into the host lattice to achieve the desired properties and significantly improve the physical characteristics of the host material. Co-doping ZnO with multiple elements is commonly employed to synergistically enhance its properties, offering improved control over its characteristics. Various codoping strategies have been documented that demonstrate significant impacts on ZnO’s structural, defect-related, optical, and electrical properties. Several codoping techniques have been investigated to improve material properties, including Fe–Co, La–Ce, La–Cu, Mg–La, Ce–Fe, Fe–Co, La–Dy, Co–Cu, Co–Ni, La–Ce, La–Cu, and Fe–B. Recent studies highlight codoping as a promising yet underexplored strategy for enhancing material properties. La-doped ZnO nanocrystals have shown remarkable photocatalytic activity and gas sensitivity, , yet they remain understudied. This study examines the microstructure, vibrational properties, morphology, and optical properties of ZnO nanoparticles prepared via the sol–gel method, with a focus on the influence of La doping concentration. Notably, the codoping of Fe and La in ZnO has received minimal attention. A thorough review of scientific databases reveals few studies on the effects of Fe–La codoping on ZnO nanoparticle properties. The integration of Fe and La as codopants is highly promising because it significantly tailors bandgap properties. These attributes of Fe, La codoped ZnO make it a promising material for advanced functional applications.
In this study, we report on the synthesis and physical characterization of ultrafine, homogeneous Fe, La codoped ZnO nanoparticles prepared via the sol–gel method. Nanoparticles have been synthesized using various methods, including the sol–gel method, coprecipitation, hydrothermal, etc. The sol–gel approach stands out among these methods due to its significant advantages, including low cost, ease of handling, minimal requirement for sophisticated laboratory equipment, and the ability to produce large quantities of nanoparticles. These attributes make the sol–gel method a highly preferred technique for nanoparticle synthesis and explain its prominence in research and applications. The finer particle size of the nanoparticles synthesized via the cost-effective, well-controlled sol–gel method enhances their morphology. Doping influences their optical and structural properties. Incorporating Fe and La ions into nonmagnetic ZnO nanoparticles significantly improves their structural, morphological, vibrational, and optical properties at room temperature. Here, we analyzed Fe–La codoped ZnO nanoparticles, which were not extensively studied before. The La concentration was taken as 2% depending on the solubility limits of La3+ in the ZnO lattice, and to maintain the phase purity. Because La3+ and Zn2+ have different ionic radii, it is expected that higher La3+ concentrations promote secondary phase development and lattice distortion; therefore, La3+ was fixed at 2%. The single-phase wurtzite structure does not change, while controlled defect engineering and band gap variation are made possible by the simultaneous introduction of Fe3+ and La3+ ions. Specifically, there is a lack of systematic attention to the controlled variation of oxygen-related defects and their direct impact on visible photoluminescence and optical band gap narrowing. This study thus provides a thorough structure-defect-optical correlation in a Fe–La codoped ZnO system that had not been previously documented. Through detailed analysis, this work establishes clear correlations among the properties of pure ZnO (1ZO), Zn0.97Fe0.03O (3FO), Zn0.98La0.02O (2LO), Zn0.95Fe0.03La0.02O (3F2LO), Zn0.93Fe0.05La0.02O (5F2LO), and Zn0.91Fe0.07La0.02O (7F2LO) nanoparticles using techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–vis) diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), and Photoluminescence (PL) Spectroscopy. This highlights their potential for advanced functional applications.

## Experimental Details

## Experimental Procedure
In the current study, zinc–iron–lanthanum oxide (Zn1–x–y Fe x La y O) nanoparticles with nominal compositions of Fe (x = 3%, 5%, and 7%) and La (y = 2%) were synthesized using the sol–gel process. High-purity (>99%) precursors were used, including ferric nitrate nonahydrate [Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O], lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate [La­(NO3)3·6H2O], and zinc oxide (purity 99.5%). An appropriate amount of the ferric nitrate nonahydrate and the lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol. The solution was stirred for 1.5 h. The resulting solutions were evaporated in a hot air furnace and then dried in a microwave oven for three to 4 h. The dried precursors were collected and ground with ZnO host powder in an agate mortar. Other samples were prepared by repeating this process. The collected nanoparticles were annealed at 600 °C in an air environment for 2 h and then cooled in a furnace. The resulting particles were then ground into a powder and are now ready for characterization.

## Characterization Techniques
We analyzed the crystalline structure of Fe and La-doped ZnO nanoparticles using XRD on a Rigaku Miniflex 600G diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 20°–90°. The scan used a step size of 0.01°, a rate of 10°/min, and a voltage of 40 kV. The diffraction patterns revealed the phase composition, crystallite size, and structural parameters of the samples. The surface morphology and grain distribution of the nanoparticles were analyzed using FESEM. EDS mapping confirmed the elemental composition and homogeneity. XPS spectra of the samples were recorded using a double-crystal silicon monochromator, a SPECS PHOIBOS 225 HV hemispherical electron energy analyzer, and an X-ray source at 4360 eV. XPS experiments were conducted on the Photoelectron Spectroscopy beamline (BL-14) at the Indus-2 synchrotron radiation facility, Raja Ramanna Centre for Advanced Technology, Indore, India, in an ultrahigh vacuum environment. The FTIR spectroscopy was used to analyze the vibrational properties and bonding interactions of the Fe, La-doped ZnO nanoparticles. Spectra were recorded in the 400–4500 cm–1 range using a Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer to identify functional groups and confirm dopant incorporation. We studied the optical properties of the Fe, La-doped ZnO nanoparticles using UV–vis DRS on a PerkinElmer Lambda 365 spectrophotometer to determine the bandgap energy. PL measurements, conducted at NIT Rourkela using WITec Alpha300R Model, having a laser wavelength: 355 nm, recorded emission spectra from 350 to 650 nm at room temperature. These measurements revealed defect-related emissions and electronic transitions in the Fe, La-doped ZnO system.

## Results and Discussions

## Structural Analysis
Figure a shows the XRD patterns of pure ZnO and Fe, La-doped ZnO samples. The intense, well-defined XRD peaks indicate that the as-synthesized samples are highly crystalline and pure. The XRD spectra of the Fe, La-doped ZnO nanoparticles reveal their polycrystalline nature; each sample exhibits two to three prominent peaks. These peaks are indexed as (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), and (103). All of the observed diffraction peaks correspond exclusively to the hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure of ZnO as confirmed by their alignment with the standard reference pattern. The strongest diffraction intensity occurs at the (101) plane, observed between 36° and 37° for all samples, indicating a preferred crystallographic orientation. There is no evidence of contaminants or secondary phases, such as FeO, Fe2O3, Fe3O4, La2O3, and ZnFe2O4, confirming a high level of phase purity within the detection limits of the XRD technique. These results suggest that the Fe and La dopants are successfully incorporated into the ZnO lattice and that adding Fe and La ions does not affect the structure of ZnO nanoparticles. The XRD data indicate that the incorporation of TM and RE metal ions does not alter the crystal.
(a) XRD patterns, (b) magnified view, (c–h) Rietveld refinement for pure and Fe, La codoped ZnO nanoparticles.
Figure b shows a magnified view of the primary diffraction peaks ((100), (002), and (101)) to illustrate the subtle changes in peak position that occur with varying doping levels. Slight peak shifts were observed in the XRD patterns of both single and double-doped ZnO materials. The diffraction peaks of ZnO nanoparticles doped with either Fe or La, or both, show clear shifts. At low Fe doping levels, Fe atoms substitute for Zn2+ sites in the ZnO wurtzite lattice, including minimal strain and preserving structural integrity. Strain is negligible at low doping levels, but becomes significant at higher levels. In Fe-doped ZnO, Fe ions can exist as Fe2+ (0.78 Å) or Fe3+ (0.68 Å), which differ from Zn2+ (0.74 Å). Substitution with Fe2+ induces compressive strain due to its larger radius, shifting XRD peaks toward lower angles. In contrast, substitution with Fe3+ causes tensile strain, shifting XRD peaks toward higher angles, as explained by Salaken et al. According to XPS analysis by Wang et al. Fe ions in Fe-doped ZnO thin films predominantly exist as Fe2+. However, Kim and Park reported the coexistence of both Fe2+ and Fe3+ in similarly prepared thin films. Goktas et al. revealed that doping Fe into ZnO confirms the existence of the Fe3+ state in Fe-doped ZnO thin films. Furthermore, XPS studies on Fe-doped ZnO nanorods by Baek et al. revealed that Fe is incorporated into the ZnO lattice in a state resembling Fe3+. Thus, the Fe valence in ZnO is complex and significantly influences lattice strain. As the Fe-doping concentration increases, the peaks shift to higher angles, accompanied by an increase in the lattice parameters of the Fe-doped ZnO nanoparticles. Based on these analyses, it can be inferred that most of the Fe ions in ZnO nanoparticles are present in the Fe3+ state. The diffraction peak shifts to higher angles as the Fe concentration increases, which is consistent with prior reports on Fe-doped ZnO thin films. ,, This indicates an increase in the lattice parameters as the level of Fe-doping rises, confirming the successful substitution of Zn2+ ions by Fe3+ in the ZnO lattice. While the observed expansion may seem counterintuitive given that the ionic radius of Fe3+ (0.68 Å) is smaller than that of Zn2+ (0.74 Å) in tetrahedral coordination, it can be attributed to factors such as charge compensation mechanisms, lattice distortion due to valence mismatch, or the incorporation of Fe in interstitial sites alongside substitutional doping. Several factors influence the variation of the lattice parameters, including uniform stress, the incorporation of impurities with differing ionic radii into the lattice, and deviations in stoichiometry. Consequently, the observed change in the parameter in this study arises from the interplay of these competing effects, which may cause the value to either increase or decrease depending on which effect is dominant.
The integration of Fe ions into the ZnO lattice was evidenced by the linear modification of the lattice constants a and c with increasing Fe concentration. The crystallite size was determined using Debye–Scherrer’s formula: D = Kλ/β cosθ. Here, θ is the Bragg angle, β is the full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the (101) peak, k is Scherrer’s constant, and λ is the incident X-ray wavelength. The crystallite sizes decrease from pure ZnO to codoped ZnO. According to XRD data, all of the produced samples are polycrystalline with a prominent (101) peak. The peak intensity decreases as compared to pure ZnO, indicating that substitution of the dopants does not disrupt the wurtzite structure. However, increasing the dopant content reduces the (101) peak intensity and crystallinity. This is accompanied by peak broadening due to higher lattice strain. The average crystallite sizes of the samples range from 38.22 to 36.18 nm, and the FWHM values increase from 0.188° to 0.205° as the concentrations of Fe and La in the ZnO crystals increase. The ionic radii of Fe3+ and La3+ differ from those of Zn2+, causing increased lattice deformation and strain. Thus, higher concentrations of Fe3+ and La3+ reduce the nucleation and subsequent growth rate of the ZnO lattice. The d-spacing is around 2.47 Å. In La-doped ZnO, a shift to higher angles suggests compressive strain. This is likely due to partial substitution or defect-induced lattice contraction. Despite La3+ larger ionic radius (∼1.03 Å), this strain is consistent with observations in RE-doped systems. The induced strain decreases the sample’s crystallinity. Crystalline size decreases with increasing Fe doping content. This trend is also observed in the SEM result, which reveals a corresponding reduction in grain size. This behavior may arise from dopant ions disrupting the ZnO crystal lattice, thereby hindering crystal growth. These findings are consistent with reports by Wang et al. and Chen et al. In codoped variants, the interaction between Fe and La ions creates competing strains that vary with concentrations. The lattice distortion rate was calculated for all pure and doped samples to determine the degree of lattice distortion by Fe and La codoping quantitatively. The calculated values of lattice distortion are found to be 0 for 3FO, 0.0616 for 2LO, 0 for 3F2LO, 0.0308 for 5F2LO, and 0.0308 for 7F2LO, clearly demonstrating dopant-induced lattice distortion. Which indicate that doping of La3+ develops significant lattice distortion because of a higher ionic radius, but negligible distortion is created by doping of the Fe3+ ion into ZnO. In 3F2LO, the lattice distortion is negligible, indicating strain compensation between the La3+ and Fe3+ ions. The strain-balancing effect lowers the lattice distortion rate value and increases the crystallinity. But the increase of Fe doping concentration may disturb the balance, which leads to the development of the lattice distortion rate value, microstrain, and peak broadening. The strain and lattice distortion rate value indicate the changes in the local crystal field and defect environment in the host matrix, favoring the development of defect-related states near the band boundaries. It is believed that these structural distortions will alter the electrical structure by improving charge carrier separation and reducing the optical band gap. At 3% Fe-2% La codoping, the interaction between the two ions reduces strain energy and improves crystallinity by partially compensating for lattice distortions. However, as more Fe is added, the excess Fe causes lattice instability and increases the defect density, which lowers crystallinity. The following equations were used to calculate the (101) peak’s dislocation density (δ) and lattice strain (ε): Dislocation density is δ=1D2 , and lattice strain is ε=βcosθ4 . The broadening of the (101) diffraction peak is also indicative of increased strain and lattice deformation caused by the substitution of dopants with different ionic radii than Zn2+. As the codoping concentration increases, the dislocation density rises from 0.507 × 10–3 to 0.600 × 10–3 nm–2, and the lattice strain rises from 0.781 × 10–3 to 0.849 × 10–3. The enhanced microstrain is responsible for the decrease in the sample’s crystalline size.
Rietveld refinement of XRD data is an effective method for analyzing the influence of dopant ions on the phase composition and lattice parameters of samples, assuming the P63mc space group for the hexagonal structure. This method obtains precise lattice parameters by fitting the different parameters to the data points using the pseudo-Voigt function. As shown in Figure c–h, FullProf software was used to perform Rietveld refinements on pure and doped samples. The Rietveld method is a least-squares refinement technique and is still the most popular method for diffractogram analysis when the crystal structure is understood. This work used the Rietveld method to improve the structure. EDS uses X-ray emissions from electron-sample interactions. Incident electrons excite atoms, triggering transitions that produce characteristic X-rays. These X-rays are detected and analyzed to identify elemental compositions. This process confirms structural and chemical modifications in the doped ZnO nanostructures. EDS spectra (not shown) was employed to subjectively confirm the existence and spatial homogeneity of Zn, O, Fe, and La in the samples, rather than for accurate quantitative assessment of dopant levels. To confirm the dopant incorporation and uniform distribution, the elemental compositions derived from EDS are therefore summarized in Table and shown in terms of atomic percentages. Minor differences in nominal and EDS-derived values are to be expected due to local sampling effects, matrix effects, and nanoscale compositional inhomogeneities. FESEM images Figure a–f reveal that all samples display distinct nanorod-like structures with hexagonal, faceted surfaces, which is typical of the wurtzite ZnO crystal structure. Inset histograms show a uniform particle size distribution for both pure and doped ZnO samples with an average particle size between 151.37 and 142.45 nm. As the concentration of the dopant increases, the average particle size decreases. This is consistent with the XRD analysis and similar results reported in Kanwal et al. There is a slight broadening in the doped samples due to the dopant ions influencing the crystal growth kinetics, surface energy, and local strain fields during nucleation. In the case of pure ZnO, the presence of Zn and O signals is indicative of stoichiometric ZnO, characterized by high homogeneity and crystallinity. The incorporation of Fe into the material may result in the introduction of Fe3+ ions. These ions have the potential to induce a charge imbalance within the material. This imbalance can be compensated for by the presence of oxygen vacancies or interstitial defects. These defects act as shallow donor sites, thereby affecting the carrier concentration, optical transitions, and magnetic ordering within the material. The uniform Fe distribution, as depicted by the mapping and substantiated by XRD, signifies substitutional doping devoid of Fe-related secondary phases. La-doped ZnO exhibits successful La3+ incorporation, confirmed by the La signal, with a larger La3+ ionic radius (1.03 Å) compared to Zn2+ (0.74 Å). This larger ionic radius of La3+ results in induced lattice strain and altered defect structures by generating oxygen vacancies or Zn interstitials (Zn i ), which are necessary to maintain charge neutrality. The homogeneous La3+ distribution, as demonstrated by the mapping results, signifies the presence of well-dispersed La3+ ions, devoid of any secondary phases. In the 3FO, the presence of Fe-KA signals indicates a uniform substitution of Fe3+ at the Zn2+ sites, suggesting successful doping without the formation of clusters. In 2LO, the uniform La-LA distribution serves to verify La3+ integration, resulting in slight lattice distortion and defects. In Fe–La codoped ZnO, EDS mapping reveals a uniform distribution of Zn, O, Fe, and La, thereby confirming the efficiency of the codoping process and the presence of compositional homogeneity, irrespective of the elevated dopant levels employed. The presence of Fe3+ has been shown to enhance magnetic moments and oxygen vacancies, while La3+ has been observed to stabilize the lattice and reduce defect clustering. This synergistic effect results in the modification of structural and electronic properties. The results of the FESEM and EDS analyses support the hypothesis of effective lattice incorporation, thereby enabling tailored optical properties in ZnO nanoparticles.

## X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy
We investigated the chemical composition and elemental oxidation states of the Fe- and La-doped ZnO sample using XPS. The survey spectrum (Figure a) confirmed the presence of Zn, Fe, La, and O, with no impurities observed. Figure b shows the high-resolution XPS spectrum of Zn 2p with Zn 2p 3/2 and Zn 2p 1/2 peaks, indicating that most Zn ions are in the +2-oxidation state. The O 1s XPS spectrum was deconvoluted into two Gaussian components: a low-binding-energy peak I, which is attributed to O2– ions in the hexagonal wurtzite ZnO lattice surrounding Zn ions; and a high-binding-energy peak II (Figure c), which is associated with oxygen-deficient regions (oxygen vacancies) and chemisorbed/dissociated oxygen species on the surface. The high concentration of oxygen vacancies observed in the XPS study corresponds to the defect states that contribute to the visible PL emissions, as discussed in the PL section. These collectively contribute to the broad visible emission, in addition to the near-band-edge UV excitonic recombination. The defect-rich nature revealed by XPS supports the PL emission profile. As shown in Figure d, the Fe 2p peak for the 3FO and 3F2LO samples is split into Fe 2p 3/2 and Fe 2p 1/2 components due to spin–orbit coupling. The Fe 2p XPS spectrum (Figure d) exhibits peaks at 710.83 eV (Fe 2p 3/2) and 724.23 eV (Fe 2p 1/2), with a spin–orbit splitting of 13.4 eV. These binding energies differ from those of metallic Fe and Fe2+ in FeO, indicating the presence of Fe3+. This assignment is supported by the absence of the Fe2O3 phase in XRD analyses, which confirms that iron exists in the +3-oxidation state. The observed Fe 2p binding energy peaks at 710.70 eV (Fe 2p 3/2) and 724.30 eV (Fe 2p 1/2) closely match the reported values for Fe3+ in the X-ray photoelectron Spectroscopy Handbook, confirming the presence of iron in the +3-oxidation state. The observed Fe 2p 3/2 and Fe 2p 1/2 peaks at 711.13 and 724.66 eV are indicative of Fe3+ oxidation state and are in good agreement with those reported by Rambu et al. for Fe-doped ZnO films deposited using spin coating. Similar results were reported by Abdolahzadeh Ziabari et al. who observed peaks at 710 and 724.8 eV for Fe 2p 3/2 and Fe 2p 3/2, respectively, indicating that Fe occurs in the +3 state. The La 3d core-level spectrum (Figure e) exhibits peaks at binding energies of 835.30 and 854.30 eV. These energies are characteristic of La3+ ions in the 3FO and 3F2LO states. They are also in good agreement with the energies observed by Hao et al. in La-doped ZnO.

## Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy
FTIR spectroscopy is a pivotal technique for analyzing chemical bonds, functional groups, and lattice vibrations in materials such as ZnO. FTIR is a technique that has been utilized to confirm the incorporation of dopants into a material. It is also capable of detecting defects such as oxygen vacancies and revealing shifts in vibrational modes due to lattice changes. The findings from the FTIR analysis provided further evidence to support the hypothesis that the wurtzite structure is indeed formed in the Fe, La codoped ZnO. The Fe-doped ZnO samples exhibited comparable spectra, as depicted in Figure a. The FTIR spectra of Fe and La codoped ZnO exhibited multiple absorption peaks within the 4000–400 cm–1 range. A broad peak was observed between 3735 and 3023 cm–1, indicative of O–H stretching from intramolecular hydrogen bonds. The peak observed at 2364 cm–1 in the FTIR spectra is likely attributable to ambient CO2 adsorption by metallic cations present in the apparatus during analysis. In the case of La-doped ZnO nanoparticles, the presence of strong FTIR bands at 1500–1300 cm–1 is indicative of Zn–La bond vibrations, thereby confirming the efficiency of the La ion doping process. A band at ∼872 cm–1 corresponds to Fe–O or La–O stretching vibrations, thereby confirming the integration of the dopant into the ZnO lattice. In the case of Fe, La-doped ZnO, the Zn–O stretching vibration, which typically occurs within the range of 400–700 cm–1 in pure ZnO, may undergo a shift. Additionally, there is an increase in peak broadening, which is attributable to the incorporation of Fe and La dopants. These shifts in FTIR spectra are consistent with the extant literature on doped ZnO. − The slight shifts in peak intensity and position with doping suggest that substituting Fe3+ and La3+ ions for Zn2+ sites modifies local bonding environments and induces lattice strain.

## UV–Visible Spectroscopy
The optical properties of Fe, La codoped ZnO nanoparticles with varying Fe doping concentrations were investigated using UV–vis reflectance spectra, as illustrated in Figure b. The analysis of the spectra indicates a substantial shift in reflectance between 370 and 390 nm, which corresponds to the optical band edge of ZnO. At this point, the material exhibits a marked increase in light absorption, attributable to the bandgap energy of the material. The 1ZO demonstrates the highest reflectance in the visible spectrum (400–800 nm), suggesting a smoother surface and fewer defect states that minimize light absorption and scattering. In contrast, Fe and La doping have been observed to significantly reduce reflectance, particularly in samples like 5F2LO and 7F2LO with higher Fe–La codoping concentrations. The incorporation of Fe3+ (3d 5) and La3+ (4f 0) ions into Zn2+ lattice sites of ZnO has been demonstrated to enhance light absorption in the doped samples, resulting in reduced reflectance. The doping of Fe into ZnO results in the introduction of intermediate energy levels within the bandgap, thereby enhancing visible light absorption and facilitating electronic transitions. Furthermore, La3+ doping, due to its comparatively larger ionic radius compared to Zn2+, has been shown to induce lattice deformation and promote oxygen vacancy formation. These alterations have been shown to enhance light scattering and diminish reflectance. The combined effect of Fe and La codoping on the band gap is attributable to the creation of defect levels that collectively reduce the material’s optical performance.
The optical band gap (E g ) can be determined by plotting (F(R) hν)2 against photon energy (hν) in a Tauc plot, assuming direct allowed transitions. As shown in Figure c, this is done by plotting (F(R) hν)2 against hν. The Kubelka–Munk function converts diffuse reflectance spectroscopy data into absorption-like spectra, enabling reliable band gap estimation for powdered semiconductors. The band gap energy is obtained by extrapolating the linear portion of the curve to the x-axis at zero absorption. The optical band gap energy was determined using Equation (F(R)hν)1/n = A(αν – Eg ), where h is Planck’s constant, ν is the photon frequency, hν represents the photon energy, A is a constant independent of energy, E g is the optical energy band gap, and n = 1/2 denotes a semiconductor with a direct band gap, and n = 2 denotes a semiconductor with an indirect band gap.
The band gap of ZnO is approximately 3.24 eV, which is consistent with typical values for undoped ZnO. ZnO is characterized by its wide band gap and high crystallinity, which enable significant UV absorption. The incorporation of Fe and La dopants slightly reduces the band gap to 3.21–3.23 eV across compositions. The lowest values are observed in the 3F2LO and 5F2LO samples, indicating a redshift that enhances visible-light interaction. The inset of Figure c shows the nonlinear variation of E g with doping levels, suggesting that the concentration of Fe and La influences band gap tuning. Optimal codoping balances defect introduction with minimal lattice disruption. The primary reason for the reduction in the band gap is the sp-d exchange interaction, in which delocalized s and p electrons in the conduction and valence bands of ZnO couple with the localized 3d electrons of the Fe ions. This splits the energy levels and reduces the band gap. Fe3+ ions introduce impurity states near the conduction band, enhancing charge carrier transitions at lower energies. Substituting Zn2+ with La3+ creates lattice strain and oxygen vacancies, which narrows the band gap via defect states. At low Fe doping levels, Fe incorporation may operate as a donor, possibly resulting in a Burstein–Moss band-filling effect and an apparent widening of the optical band gap. As the concentration of Fe–La codoping increases, lattice distortion, defect-induced disorder, and the production of localized states along the band edges become more prominent, resulting in band-tail (Urbach) states and an overall narrowing of the optical band gap visible in the UV–vis spectra. Pure ZnO has a high band gap energy, likely due to its high crystallinity, as confirmed by XRD and FE-SEM images. The inset of Figure c shows that the doping with a higher concentration of Fe reduces the band gap.

## Photoluminescence Spectroscopy
Pure and Fe, La-doped ZnO nanoparticles were examined using PL spectroscopy at room temperature. These measurements provide insight into surface oxygen vacancies and defects, as well as the separation and recombination of photoinduced charge carriers. This enables the study of the optical properties and electronic structure of semiconductor materials. The PL spectra of pure and Fe, La-doped ZnO typically consist of multiple components. The first component, for example, addresses emissions resulting from the recombination of energy states near the bandgap, which are associated with bound and free excitons. In the visible range, the second component represents the defect-related band. Key native defects include oxygen vacancies (Vo), zinc vacancies (VZn), oxygen interstitials (Oi), and oxygen antisites (OZn). Fe and La doping can influence the intensity and characteristics of these defect-related emissions. Figure shows the deconvoluted PL spectra of pure and doped samples at different concentrations of doping. Figure a–f shows the deconvolution of the near-band-edge (NBE) emission peaks across different dopant concentrations. Table provides the associated area percentages. Peak I occurs at 384 nm (3.23 eV) for 1ZO; at 383 nm (3.24 eV) for 3FO, 2LO, and 3F2LO; and at 384 nm (3.23 eV) for 5F2LO and 7F2LO. Peak II appears at 396 nm (3.13 eV) for 1ZO; 391 nm (3.17 eV) for 3FO; 394 nm (3.15 eV) for 2LO; 390 nm (3.18 eV) for 3F2LO; 391 nm (3.17 eV) for 5F2LO; and 389 nm (3.19 eV) for 7F2LO. Peak III, the green emission peak, occurs at 522 nm (2.38 eV) for 1ZO, 526 nm (2.36 eV) for 3FO, 524 nm (2.37 eV) for 2LO, 525 nm (2.36 eV) for 3F2LO, 524 nm (2.37 eV) for 5F2LO, and 524 nm (2.37 eV) for 7F2LO. The PL spectra of all the samples (Figure a–f) exhibit emission bands in both the UV and visible regions. The UV emission peak corresponds to near-band-edge (NBE) emission, which arises from the radiative recombination of free excitons. The UV peak position of the ZnO nanostructures lies between 383 and 384 nm. The absence of shifting in the NBE emission, while band gap narrowing is observed in UV–vis spectra, implies that doping of Fe largely introduces defect-related band-tail states rather than appreciably modifying the inherent band-edge recombination energy. This slight variation is due to the different coupling strengths between excitons and longitudinal optical (LO) phonons, which are governed by the degree of crystal imperfections near the nanostructure surface. Visible emissions observed in the PL spectra originate from native point defects, such as vacancies, interstitials, and antisites in the ZnO lattice. There is a strong correlation between the defect states found by XPS analysis and the visible PL emissions. A large concentration of oxygen vacancies, which create localized electronic states within the band gap, is indicated by the prominent high-binding-energy component in the O 1s spectrum. These defect levels enhance radiative recombination processes, resulting in the observed emissions. The green emission is mostly due to recombination involving Zn–O divacancy-related states, whereas the violet emission is caused by transitions connected with zinc vacancies. Deconvoluted spectra reveal violet emission centered around 388–396 nm (3.13–3.20 eV). These emissions correspond to electrons transferring from the conduction band minimum to the energy level associated with VZn.

## Peak Area Percentage Distribution of Pure and Doped Samples Obtained from Deconvoluted Spectra
The electronic band structure and defect states in ZnO-based materials are modified by dopants such as Fe and La. Excitonic recombination and defect-related emissions, particularly the green emission band, dominate the NBE emissions observed in PL spectra. Slight energy shifts in the NBE peaks, labeled Peak I and Peak II, which arise from band-to-band transitions or excitonic recombination, are caused by dopant-induced lattice strain, bandgap narrowing, or the formation of shallow defect levels. Specifically, Peak I (3.23–3.24 eV) and Peak II (3.13–3.20 eV) exhibit energy shifts with varying concentrations of Fe and La, reflecting alterations in the electronic structure. These shifts arise from the incorporation of Fe3+ and La3+ ions, which modify the local crystal field and introduce shallow donor or acceptor levels. Additionally, all samples exhibit green emissions within the range of 522–525 nm (∼2.36–2.38 eV). In ZnO, this emission is often tied to deep-level emissions (DLE). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations explain that these emissions are attributed to transitions involving defect complexes, such as zinc–oxygen divacancies (VZnO), whose energy levels lie below the conduction band edge, as reported in Vidya et al. Singly ionized zinc interstitials or oxygen vacancies are often associated with this green emission. , Therefore, the green emission observed in ZnO, Fe-doped ZnO, La-doped ZnO, and Fe–La codoped ZnO nanostructures can be ascribed to the transition of electrons from the conduction band edge to the defect levels associated with VZnO. This results from midgap states created by these defects, which enable radiative recombination. Slight variations in the green emission wavelength (522–526 nm) with doping suggest changes in the defect environment, possibly due to La3+ causing lattice distortions or Fe3+ substituting Zn2+ sites and altering the formation energy of Vo or VZn. Deconvoluting the PL peaks shows that the dopants adjust the balance between excitonic and defect-related emissions. The PL spectra of ZnO samples doped with varying concentrations exhibit three distinct emission peaks, the relative area percentages of which are detailed in Table . Peak I, which is attributed to NBE emission, arises from excitonic recombination and varies in intensity from 22.106% to 38.801%, reflecting differences in recombination efficiency. Peak II, associated with donor–acceptor pair transitions or shallow defect states, increases from 9.025% to 22.868%, suggesting that higher doping levels intensify the contribution of shallow defect states. Peak III corresponds to green emission from deep-level defects and dominates all samples. Its area percentages range from 48.768% to 65.350%, which signifies significant contributions from defect states modulated by Fe and La doping.
To investigate the color properties of Fe and La-doped ZnO nanoparticles, we first evaluated their visible color under white light to establish the desired color profile. Then, the photoluminescent emission from these materials was analyzed using CIE 1931 chromaticity coordinates (x, y, z), which are defined by the International Commission on Illumination (CIE). These coordinates represent normalized fractions and quantitatively link the material’s emission to human color perception through the three distinct receptors in the eye. , The CIE coordinates provide a standardized method for characterizing perceived colors. The correlated color temperature (CCT), measured in Kelvin (K), describes the color appearance of a light source. It indicates whether the source appears warm (e.g., yellowish, ∼2700 K, like an incandescent bulb), neutral, or cool (e.g., bluish, ∼5000 K, like daylight). , The color rendering index (CRI), on a 0–100 scale, measures a light source’s ability to accurately reproduce colors compared to a reference source with the same CCT. A CRI of 100, as seen in natural daylight, indicates perfect color rendering. The R9 value specifically assesses the rendering of deep red hues, and the Duv value indicates the distance from the blackbody locus on the CIE chromaticity diagram. A positive Duv value reveals chromatic deviations, such as a greenish tint.
The spectral power distribution (SPD) of photoluminescent from Fe and La-doped ZnO nanoparticles, shown in the insets of Figure a–f, reveals broadband emission with strong visible components. The colorimetric parameters include a CCT range of 6425–6503 K, a CRI of 79–85, and a Duv range of 0.0198–0.0232. These values indicate cool white light with good color fidelity, though the chromaticity coordinates fall beyond the blackbody locus, giving the light a slight greenish tint. The Duv value of 0.0198 and CCT of approximately 6425 K exceed the white light limits set by ANSI C78.377. The R9 values, ranging from −31 to −40, indicate difficulty in accurately rendering saturated red hues.
Figure shows the CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram for pure and doped ZnO. The chromaticity coordinates are (0.31, 0.35), (0.31, 0.37), (0.31, 0.36), (0.31, 0.37), (0.31, 0.36), and (0.31, 0.36). These coordinates indicate consistent white light emission across all samples. The u′ and v′ coordinates provide a perceptually uniform color representation and are listed in Table as follows: (0.19, 0.48), (0.18, 0.49), (0.18, 0.48), (0.18, 0.49), (0.18, 0.49), and (0.18, 0.48). These coordinates align closely with the “ideal white” region (near 0.33, 0.33) for bright, cool white light emitters, which typically have a CRI above 80 and a CCT around 6500 K, resembling cool daylight. , The highly luminescent nanomaterials show strong potential for future LED production due to their near-perfect white light chromaticity, daylight CCT, high CRI values in both dispersion and solid forms, and long-term chemical, physical, and photostability. The CCT significantly influences mood, visual comfort, and color perception. Higher CCT values enhance visual acuity and perceived brightness compared to lower values. Despite suboptimal R9 values ranging from −31 to −40 and a slight greenish tint (Duv ranging from 0.0198 to 0.0232), the Fe, La-doped ZnO phosphors exhibit characteristics suitable for white light-emitting diodes (WLEDs) in outdoor lighting applications, where cool white light is preferred. For general illumination, WLEDs require a broadband spectrum to achieve a CRI above 80, which these phosphors satisfy. Consequently, Fe, La-doped ZnO nanoparticles are well-suited for use as a white light-emitting layer in electroluminescent devices, such as white LEDs, due to their favorable colorimetric properties.

## Conclusions
Fe, La-doped ZnO was prepared employing a low-cost sol–gel method. The Rietveld refinement of XRD analysis confirms the successful incorporation of Fe and La into pure ZnO, which has a hexagonal wurtzite structure without any secondary phase. FESEM images reveal the presence of distinct nanorod-like structures with hexagonal facets, indicative of wurtzite ZnO. EDS mapping further confirms the uniform distribution of Zn, O, Fe, and La, thereby verifying the successful incorporation of Fe3+ and La3+ dopants into ZnO. XPS analysis confirms the successful substitution of Fe3+ and La3+ into the ZnO lattice without impurities. This process is accompanied by abundant oxygen vacancies, which account for the defect-mediated broad visible emissions. The use of FTIR spectroscopy has been instrumental in confirming dopant incorporation, as well as identifying lattice vibrations and structural alterations. This technique has been found to result in the observation of shifted Zn–O stretching, expanded peaks, and separate Zn–La and Fe–O/La-O bands. These findings demonstrate the occurrence of lattice strain and the formation of wurtzite structures. The codoping of Fe and La in ZnO has been shown to result in the formation of defects and lattice distortions, which, in turn, lead to an increase in light scattering and performance reduction in reflectance. This phenomenon, when considered collectively, has a detrimental effect on the material’s optical performance. The emission peak may be stabilized by codoping transition metals and RE elements. Fe, La-doped ZnO phosphors, with CCT ranging from 6425 to 6503 K, and CRI from 79 to 85, exhibit characteristics that align with the ideal cool white light spectrum. This alignment has the potential to enhance visual acuity and brightness, making it suitable for outdoor white LED applications.
