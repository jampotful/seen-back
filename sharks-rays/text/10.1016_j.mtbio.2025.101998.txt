Zwitterionic chitosan-modified amorphous ZnCuAl-LDH eye drops for efficient treatment of bacterial keratitis.

# Zwitterionic chitosan-modified amorphous ZnCuAl-LDH eye drops for efficient treatment of bacterial keratitis

## Abstract
AbstractBacterial keratitis caused by microbial invasion represents a significant cause of visual impairment characterized by corneal perforation, epithelial defect, intense pain, and conjunctival congestion. The widespread misuse of antibiotic eye drops in clinical treatment has accelerated the global rise of antibiotic-resistant infections, dimming the prospects for antibiotic therapy. In response, inorganic nanomaterial-based antibacterial eye drops have been developed for bacterial keratitis leveraging their structural controllability, composition diversity and ease of functionalization, However, they typically exert antibacterial effects with the assistance of laser, ultrasound, or X-ray irradiation that may cause irritation or damage to the human eye, or involve intricate structural modifications for antibacterial activation. Herein, we propose zwitterionic chitosan-modified defective amorphous ZnCuAl-layered double hydroxide nanosheets (ZC@a-LDH) as an antibacterial agent for bacterial keratitis treatment. ZC@a-LDH nanosheets demonstrate significantly enhanced antimicrobial efficacy compared with their defect-free counterparts, effectively suppressing bacterial proliferation at a minimum inhibitory concentration of 12 μg mL−1 without relying on external stimuli. The enhanced antimicrobial performance may be attributed to the combined action of electrostatic interactions, antibacterial ions (Zn2+/Cu2+) and enhanced charge transport facilitated by structural defects. Notably, ZC@a-LDH antibacterial eye drops demonstrate effective intervention in bacterial keratitis in mice, outperforming the conventional tobramycin in therapeutic efficacy.Keywords: Bacterial keratitis, Layered double hydroxides, Defect engineering, Antibacterial ions

## Introduction
Bacterial keratitis, a common corneal inflammation caused by bacterial infection, is an important cause of visual impairment worldwide, leading to corneal perforation and permanent vision loss [[1], [2], [3]]. The typical clinical manifestations of bacterial keratitis are epithelial defect, corneal infiltration, severe pain, and conjunctival congestion [[4], [5], [6]]. Currently, the primary approach for managing bacterial keratitis in clinical settings involves the application of topical antibiotics [7]. Unfortunately, antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections have rapidly emerged worldwide due to the overuse of antibiotics, dimming the prospects for antibiotic treatment [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Therefore, developing novel treatment approaches to address antibiotic-resistant pathogens has become a critical priority. Recent years have witnessed significant research efforts focused on advancements in creating antibacterial eye drops utilizing active inorganic nanomaterials (e.g., Cu2-xSe, Ag NPs, Ag/Cu-NPs, Ag2Te NPs, CuS/MnS, MoS2 and WS2 quantum dots) [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]], owing to their structural controllability, composition diversity and ease of functionalization. For example, Xiang et al. fabricated Cu2-xSe@BSA@NTRP nanoparticles by integrating nitroreductase-responsive probes into Cu2-xSe@BSA for antibacterial therapy of bacterial keratitis, achieving efficient bacterial eradication via the combined action of copper ions and low-temperature photothermal therapy [12]. Li et al. prepared WS2-y quantum dots with sulfur vacancies using ion irradiation technique for the treatment of bacterial keratitis [17]. WS2-y quantum dots effectively alleviated bacterial infections in the rats’ eyes due to the sulfur vacancy-mediated charge transport between microbial membrane and WS2–y. However, these reported inorganic nanomaterials typically exert antibacterial effects with the assistance of laser, ultrasound, or X-ray irradiation that may cause irritation or damage to the human eye, or require complex techniques to regulate their structure to activate antibacterial activity. Therefore, it still remains a daunting challenge to develop a new type of inorganic nanomaterial-based antibacterial eye drop with the simple preparation, high biocompatibility, and efficient antibacterial effect without external irradiation.
Layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanosheets, featured with superior biocompatibility, pH-responsive biodegradability, tunable chemical composition and structure, and ease of preparation, have been demonstrated to be promising inorganic nanomaterials for a variety of biomedical applications, including drug delivery, imaging diagnosis, cancer therapy, biosensing, tissue engineer, and anti-bacteria [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. In terms of anti-bacteria, LDH nanosheets can not only serve as carriers for delivering antibacterial agents [[28], [29], [30]], but also release metal ions with antibacterial activity (e.g., Zn2+, Cu2+, Co2+, Mn2+, Ni2+, etc.) on theri own for inhibiting bacteria growth and reproduction [[31], [32], [33]]. Moreover, positively charged LDH nanosheets can bind to negatively charged bacterial membrane through electrostatic interactions, promoting the adsorption of bacteria and further enhancing the antibacterial effects [34]. However, due to the lack of high dispersion stability required for the treatment of ocular diseases, currently LDH nanosheets are mainly used for antibacterial purposes in the field of tissue engineering, such as bone infection and wound repair. The application of LDH nanosheets in the treatment of ocular diseases, especially bacterial keratitis, has not yet been reported. In particular, how defects or crystallinity will affect the antibacterial performance of LDH nanosheets still remain unexplored. Therefore, the development of highly active LDH-based antibacterial eye drops with high dispersion stability is a huge challenge.
Herein, we report an acid etching strategy for the preparation of defective amorphous ZnCuAl-LDH nanosheets (a-LDH) as an antibacterial agent for the treatment of bacterial keratitis (Fig. 1). Specifically, ZnCuAl-LDH nanosheets (LDH) are prepared by co-precipitation method and then etched by acidic buffer solution to obtain a-LDH nanosheets. To enhance the dispersion stability and biocompatibility, a-LDH nanosheets are further modified with QCS-PBA-PEG by forming boron ester bonds with phenylboric acid functional groups, resulting in zwitterionic chitosan-modified a-LDH antibacterial eye drops (ZC@a-LDH). Importantly, ZC@a-LDH nanosheets possess superior antimicrobial activity compared with defect-free ZC@LDH nanosheets (pristine LDH modified with zwitterionic chitosan), effectively inhibiting bacterial growth with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 12 μg mL−1, without any additional exogenous stimuli. The enhancement of antimicrobial performance is likely due to the synergistic effects of multiple factors: electrostatic interactions between positively charged ZC@a-LDH and negatively charged bacterial surfaces promoting bacterial adsorption, blocking nutrient transport channels and inducing membrane rupture; antibacterial active ions (Zn2+/Cu2+) triggering protein/enzyme denaturation or nucleic acid damage; abundant defect-induced charge transport facilitating membrane disruption. More importantly, ZC@a-LDH antibacterial eye drops effectively intervene in bacterial keratitis in mice, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy compared to tobramycin (TOB, a commercial antibacterial eye drop). Our work represents a promising paradigm of LDH nanosheets for bacterial keratitis treatment, further expanding the biomedical applications of LDH-based nanomaterials.

## Experimental section

## Materials
Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, >99.0 %), copper nitrate trihydrate (Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, >99.0 %), aluminum nitrate hexahydrate (Al(NO3)3·9H2O, >99.0 %), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, >98.0 %), and polyethylene glycol-2000 (PEG-2000) were obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China).
S. aureus was obtained from the microbiology lab of the Henan Eye Institute. Bacteria were cultivated on trypticase soy agar (TSA) liquid culture medium and trypticase soy broth (TSB) plate at 37 °C. TOB and TSA were bought from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).
HCE-2 human corneal epithelial cells and CCL-20.2 conjunctival epithelial cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/nutrient mixture F12 (DMEM/F12, Solarbio, Beijing, China) containing streptomycin (100 μg/mL), penicillin (100 U/mL) and 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator.

## Characterizations
The morphology of LDH and a-LDH nanosheets was characterized by high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The crystal structure of LDH and a-LDH nanosheets was determined using a Shimadzu XRD-6000 diffractometer (SHIMADZU, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. Sample thickness was measured using a Bruker MultiMode 8 atomic force microscope (AFM) operating in tapping mode. The chemical composition of LDH and a-LDH nanosheets was analyzed using an Escalab 250Xi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo Scientific, USA) with Al Kα X-ray source. Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrum was performed on a EMX1598 spectrometer (Bruker, USA). The sizes and zeta potentials of the samples were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Malvern Zetasizer UV instrument (U.K.).

## Preparation of LDH nanosheets
Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (1 mmol), Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (1 mmol) and Al(NO3)3·9H2O (0.5 mmol) were dissolved in deionized water (10 mL) and ultrasonically mixed as solution A. NaOH (8 mmol) was dissolved in deionized water (10 mL) as solution B. Then the solution B was slowly dropped into the solution An until the pH reaches 9.5. The mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for 20 min in N2 atmosphere. Finally, the obtained LDH nanosheets were rinsed three times with deionized water.

## Preparation of a-LDH nanosheets
LDH nanosheets (30 mg) were dispersed into PBS (30 mL, pH 4.0) solution and stirred continuously at room temperature for 4 h to collect a-LDH nanosheets.

## Synthesis of QCS-PBA
QCS-PBA was synthesized via a two-step procedure. First, chitosan was reacted with three-fold molar excess of glycidyltrimethylammonium chloride at 60 °C for 24 h to yield quaternized chitosan (QCS), which was purified by dialysis (MWCO 3500 Da) for three days. Subsequently, QCS was conjugated with 4-carboxyphenylboronic acid (PBA) through EDC/NHS-mediated coupling (molar ratio 1:0.6:0.6:0.6 for QCS/PBA/NHS/EDC) under ambient conditions overnight, followed by another three-day dialysis (MWCO 3500 Da) to obtain the final QCS-PBA conjugate.

## Preparation of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH nanosheets
The PEG-2000 (10 mg) and QCS-PBA (2 mg) were added to the LDH or a-LDH suspension (10 mg, 10 mL) under stirring at room temperature for 12 h. The obtained ZC@LDH or ZC@a-LDH was centrifuged for 5 min and washed thoroughly with deionized water for three times.

## Biocompatibility evaluation
The cytotoxicity of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH was evaluated on HCE-2 and CCL-20.2 cells using a Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK-8) assay. HCE-2 and CCL-20.2 cells were plated in 96-well plates at densities of 0.8 × 104 and 1 × 104 cells per well, respectively, and cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. Following this, the cells were exposed to different concentrations of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH (0, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100 μg mL−1) diluted with DMEM/F12 medium for another 24 h. Then the medium was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10 % CCK-8 solution in each well for 4 h of incubation at 37 °C. Following this, the optical density (OD values) at 600 nm was measured using an Enzyme Labeler (PerkinElmer EnVision, England).

## Determination of MIC
The MIC of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH was measured to evaluate their antibacterial ability on bacterial growth. 100 μL of bacterial solution was seeded onto a 96‐well plate with an initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1. Then 100 μL of different concentrations of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH solution was added to the sterilized 96-well plate with the final concentrations of 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 μg mL−1, respectively. The OD600 values were measured and marked as OD0h using an Enzyme Labeler (PerkinElmer EnVision, England). After 12 h of incubation at 37 °C, the OD600 values were measured again and marked as OD12h. The formula for S. aureus viability (%) is (OD12 h-OD0 h/OD12 h-OD0 h) × 100 %. MIC is defined as the minimum concentration at which no discernible bacteria growth can be seen.

## Determination of growth kinetics
Briefly, an equal amount of 100 μL of bacterial solution prepared in TSA medium was added to each well of a 96-well plate. Then, 100 μL ZC@LDH or ZC@a-LDH was added to achieve a final concentration of 48 μg mL−1. The plate was incubated with shaking at 37 °C for 72 h. The bacterial growth curves were taken by recording the OD600 of the test strains every 12 h.

## Determination of growth inhibition curves
The plate counting method was conducted to evaluate the antibacterial activity of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH. The prepared S. aureus bacterial solution was treated with 48 μg mL−1 ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH for 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h at 37 °C, respectively. After that, 100 μL of the bacterial solution (104 CFU/mL) was pipetted and spread evenly onto a blood agar plate for 12 h incubation at 37 °C. Growth inhibition curves were obtained based on counting CFUs at different time intervals.

## Live/dead bacteria staining assay
Live/dead bacteria staining assay was carried out to visualize the antibacterial effect using SYTO9 and PI. SYTO9 is a green fluorescent nucleic acid stain capable of labeling both live and dead bacteria, while PI is a red fluorescent dye that specifically targets bacteria with compromised cell membranes and walls. Together, these dyes are commonly used in viability assays to differentiate between live (SYTO9-positive) and dead (PI-positive) bacterial cells. In brief, 107 CFU/mL of bacterial solution was treated with ZC@LDH or ZC@a-LDH (48 μg mL−1 (4 × MIC)) for 8 h. The bacteria cells were then centrifuged and washed three times with PBS. Following this, bacteria cells were stained with SYTO 9 and PI in the dark for 15 min to allow for proper labeling of live and dead cells, respectively. After removing the excess dye by centrifugation, a 10 μL aliquot of the stained bacterial suspension was placed onto a glass slide and covered with a coverslip. The morphology of the stained bacteria was then examined using a fluorescence microscopy (Nikon ECLIPSE 80i, Japan).

## In vivo antibacterial assay
Animal experiments were conducted on adult C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks, 18–22 g). The mice were kept under a 12 h light/dark cycle at room temperature with ad libitum access to water and food. The mice were anesthetized with 4 % chloral hydrate, and 0.4 % oxybuprocaine hydrochloride was used for topical anesthetization around ocular tissues. Then, the central part of the right eye of mice was marked with a ring drill (d = 2 mm), followed by scraping the marked location using a 45° blade and removing the corneal epithelium down to the superficial stroma layer under an anatomic microscope (Topcon OMS90, Japan). The left eye served as an untreated control. A bacterial keratitis model was established by applying 5 μL of S. aureus suspension (108 CFU/mL) to the corneal wound. At 12 h post-infection, mice were randomized into three groups and administered 5 μL topical eye drops (0.9 % sterile saline (control), TOB (100 μg mL−1, positive control), ZC@a-LDH (100 μg mL−1)) twice daily (8 a.m./PM) for 7 days. Corneal pathological observations were observed via a slit lamp microscope and scored on days 1, 3, 5, and 7. After day 7, the mice were euthanized via anesthetic overdose. For histological analysis, eyeballs were fixed in FAS eyeball fixative, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned sagittally (5 μm thickness). To assess bacterial load, corneal tissues were homogenized in sterile saline and plated on TSB agar. Ultimately, sagittal sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathology, while immunofluorescence staining of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) was performed to evaluate inflammatory responses.

## Safety evaluation
After 7 days of twice-daily topical administration of ZC@a-LDH (100 μg mL−1), eye tissues including conjunctiva, cornea, lens, iris, and retina, as well as organs such as heart, liver, lungs, spleen, and kidneys, were collected to prepare H&E-stained specimens for further evaluation of biological safety in mice.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc.), and presented as the means ± standard deviation (S.D.), with significance defined as P < 0.05.

## Results and discussion

## Synthesis and characterization of LDH
LDH nanosheets were synthesized using a hydrothermal method. As identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. 2a, red line), the characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 10.15° and 20.36° are attributed to the (003) and (006) planes of LDH crystal, indicating the formation of crystal structure. The thickness of LDH nanosheets determined by AFM is around 6–9 nm (Fig. 2b and c). HRTEM image reveals that the obtained LDH nanosheets exhibit 2D nanosheet morphology with a size of 70–120 nm (Fig. 2d). The lattice fringes with a 0.383 nm spacing were observed, corresponding to the (006) plane of LDH. Notably, the hexagonal-shaped bright diffraction spots are presented in the selected region electron diffraction (SAED) pattern of a typical LDH nanosheet (Fig. S1), indicating its single-crystalline characteristic. Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping images reveal that Zn, Cu and Al elements are homogeneously distributed throughout the LDH nanosheet (Fig. 2e). The element content of LDH was quantitatively detected by inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) with results summarized in Table S1.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the chemical composition of LDH nanosheets. The deconvolution of XPS spectrum in the Zn 2p region shows two peaks at 1022.0 eV and 1045.1 eV attributable to Zn2+ 2p3/2 and Zn2+ 2p1/2 (Fig. 2f), respectively. In the XPS Cu 2p spectrum, two peaks at 955.1 and 935.0 eV are attributed to Cu2+ 2p1/2 and 2p3/2 (Fig. 2g). For the XPS Al 2p spectrum, due to the overlap of peaks in the Al 2p and Cu 3p regions, there is a main peak at 74.2 eV assignable to Al3+ accompanied by two peaks at 77.0 and 79.4 eV corresponding to Cu2+ cations (Fig. 2h). The XPS O 1s spectrum indicates that the oxygen species is composed of lattice oxygen (OL: 532.1 eV) and adsorbed oxygen (OS: 534.0 eV) (Fig. 2i).

## Synthesis and characterization of a-LDH
Subsequently, LDH nanosheets were etched in an acidic environment (PBS 4.0) to obtain a-LDH nanosheets. XRD, HRTEM, XPS, and ESR spectroscopy were carried out to explore the morphology changes of LDH nanosheets after acid etching. As revealed by XRD pattern, no diffraction peaks were presented in a-LDH sample (Fig. 2a, blue line), suggesting its amorphous structure. According to HRTEM image in Fig. 3a, a-LDH maintains the nanosheet-like morphology of LDH with a size of 60–110 nm. The contents of Zn, Cu and Al elements in a-LDH significantly decrease in comparison with LDH (Table S1), indicating the etching away of partial metal ions. In the XPS analysis (Fig. 3b–e), the Zn 2p and Al 2p spectra of a-LDH nanosheets present that the peak positions are similar to those of LDH nanosheets, while deconvoluted peaks at about 955.3 eV (2p1/2) and 935.5 eV (2p3/2) in the Cu 2p region and 533.1 eV in the O 1s region suggest the existence of Cu+ and oxygen vacancies (OVs), demonstrating that acid etching can induce the valence change of Cu element and the generation of OVs. Moreover, the existence of defects was confirmed by ESR spectroscopy, as a strong signal at G = 2.2 was observed in a-LDH nanosheets instead of LDH nanosheets (Fig. 3f). The zeta potentials of LDH and a-LDH nanosheets are 44.8 ± 1.3 mV and −15.1 ± 1.8 mV (Fig. 3g), respectively, and their hydrodynamic sizes measured by DLS technique are about 107.8 ± 8.9 nm and 91.3 ± 9.1 nm, respectively (Fig. 3h), indicating that acid etching leads to a decreased zeta potential and hydrodynamic size.

In addition, to improve the dispersion stability and biocompatibility, LDH and a-LDH nanosheets were modified with QCS-PBA-PEG (mQCS-PBA: mPEG = 1: 0.2) to form ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH. PEG could effectively inhibit the aggregation of LDH nanosheets through steric hindrance conferred by its hydrophilic chains, while QCS-PBA further reinforce the dispersion stability of LDH nanosheets by forming boron ester bonds between the phenylboronic acid groups in PBA and the hydroxyl groups on the LDH surface. The successful modification was verified using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra, where the characteristic bands of PEG, QCS-PBA, and LDH were observed at 2877 cm−1 (C-O-C tensile vibration of PEG), 1641 cm−1 (C-O stretching vibration in acetyl group and C=N and C-O stretching vibration in amide II band of QCS-PBA) and 1384 cm−1 (N-O stretching vibration in NO3− of LDH) in ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH samples (Fig. 3i). Zeta potentials provide further evidence for the successful modification of LDH and a-LDH nanosheets (Fig. 3g), as their zeta-potential values become 46.1 ± 2.2 mV (ZC@LDH) and 40.8 ± 1.5 mV (ZC@a-LDH) after modification with QCS-PBA-PEG (10.8 ± 1.0 mV). According to DLS analysis, the size of ZC@LDH (122.4 ± 3.7 nm) and ZC@a-LDH (111.3 ± 3.4 nm) increased slightly after QCS-PBA-PEG modification (Fig. 3h), and both of them remained stable during 7 days (Fig. S2). The photos of the materials in aqueous solution at different time points further proved that QCS-PBA-PEG modification enhanced the dispersion stability of LDH and a-LDH (Fig. S3).

## In vitro antibacterial activity
Based on the aforementioned results, the antibacterial properties of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH were also determined using S. aureus. As shown in Fig. 4a, with the increase of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH concentration, the growth of bacteria was inhibited to varying degrees. The MICs of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH against S. aureus were about 16 μg mL−1 and 12 μg mL−1, respectively, and the bacterial growth was inhibited at this concentration, which indicated that ZC@a-LDH possessed superior antimicrobial activity and could effectively inhibit bacterial growth at low concentration.

Subsequently, the effects of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH on the growth of S. aureus were assessed by incubation with 4 × MIC of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH. In Fig. 4b, it was found that the absorbance of the bacterial solution remained essentially unchanged in the first 6 h. This stage was the delayed period of bacterial growth, during which bacteria were adapting to the new culture environment, reproducing at a slow rate, and enhancing their metabolic activities in preparation for subsequent division and proliferation. In the following 8–24 h, the untreated bacteria transitioned into the exponential (logarithmic) growth phase, and the absorbance OD values showed a linear increase, indicating a significant increase in bacteria number. However, the growth rate of ZC@LDH- and ZC@a-LDH-treated bacteria was significantly slowed down, indicating the inhibition of bacterial growth. Notably, ZC@a-LDH had a more significant bacteriostatic effect than ZC@LDH, which could be attributed to defect-mediated charge transport between microbial membrane and ZC@a-LDH facilitating membrane disruption. Growth inhibition experiments were also conducted and the results indicated that the bacterial cells showed a significant decrease following 4 h of 4 × MIC ZC@a-LDH treatment, and the live bacterial cells were almost negligible after 8 h treatment. In contrast, the growth of bacteria treated with 4 × MIC ZC@LDH still could not be completely inhibited, revealing that the antibacterial activity of ZC@a-LDH was stronger than that of ZC@LDH (Fig. 4c).
To visualize the antibacterial effect, bacteria were further stained with SYTO 9 and PI after incubation with saline (control), ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH for 8 h [35]. As presented in Fig. 4d and e, only green fluorescence was observed in the control group, suggesting the intact membranes of the bacterial cells. Weak red fluorescence was observed in the ZC@LDH group, while strong red fluorescence was found in the ZC@a-LDH group, demonstrating that most of the cell membranes of S. aureus were severely damaged after treatment with ZC@a-LDH, which was further evidence of its excellent antibacterial effect. Moreover, the morphological changes of S. aureus after different treatments were observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM) [36]. In Fig. S4, compared with the control group, bacterial surfaces in the ZC@LDH group exhibited slight collapse, whereas those treated with ZC@a-LDH displayed the most severe structural damage, confirming its superior bactericidal ability.
In addition, the cytotoxicity of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH against HCE-2 and CCL-20.2 cells was assessed by treatment cells with varying concentrations of ZC@LDH and ZC@a-LDH, respectively. In Fig. 4f and g, both of them exhibited favourable biocompatibility, as the cell viability remained >80 % at a concentration of 100 μg mL−1.

## In vivo antibacterial activity
Based on the positive antimicrobial properties and good biocompatibility observed in vitro, we established a bacterial keratitis model for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of ZC@a-LDH in vivo. On days 1, 3, 5 and 7 after modelling, the eyes of mice in the different treatment groups were examined by slit lamp microscopy. As shown in Fig. 5a–c, corneal epithelial defects, edema and corneal opacity were found in all three groups of mice on day 1, and infections were homogeneous among all groups. On day 3, corneal edema and cloudiness were more pronounced in the control and TOB (positive control) groups, while the ZC@a-LDH group exhibited only slight cloudiness. By day 5, the control group still had corneal defects, and the TOB group showed reduced defect size, but both groups remained opaque. Whereas the ZC@a-LDH group had a small amount of punctate staining. On day 7, the corneas of the control group remained opaque, those of the TOB group were less opaque, while the corneas of the ZC@a-LDH group were clearly transparent with complete epithelial repair. Additionally, mice in the ZC@a-LDH group exhibited the lowest clinical scores compared to the control and TOB groups (Fig. 5d). The above results indicated that ZC@a-LDH effectively inhibited bacterial infection in the mouse cornea throughout the treatment period, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy compared to TOB. It can be concluded that ZC@a-LDH antibacterial eye drops can effectively intervene in bacterial keratitis, possessing potential therapeutic advantages. Specifically, the superior efficacy of ZC@a-LDH allow for a reduction in the required dosage and administration frequency compared to TOB. This is of great clinical importance as it can improve patients’ compliance, which is a critical factor in the successful management of ocular infections. Patients are more likely to adhere to a treatment regimen that requires less frequent instillation of eye drops, thereby achieving better therapeutic outcomes and reducing the progression of bacterial keratitis.

Subsequently, H&E staining assay was performed on corneal tissue [37]. The results revealed a substantial infiltration of inflammatory cells and a significant increase in corneal thickness in the control group, a moderate number of inflammatory cells in the TOB group, whereas no inflammatory cells in the ZC@a-LDH group with corneal structure and thickness resembling that of normal corneal tissue (Fig. 5e and f). Additionally, to characterize the anti-infective properties of ZC@a-LDH in bacterial keratitis, the expression of anti-inflammatory markers, including IL-1β and TNF‐α, in corneal tissues was examined using immunofluorescence. In Fig. 5g–j, the expression levels of IL-1β and TNF-α were significantly increased in the control and TOB groups, while the expression levels in the ZC@a-LDH group were markedly decreased to normal levels, demonstrating that ZC@a-LDH possessed excellent antibacterial effect and could effectively protect the cornea from further bacterial infection and severe inflammatory reactions. Furthermore, ocular tissues (conjunctiva, cornea, lens, iris, retina) and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) of mice were collected on the 7th day after treatment with ZC@a-LDH for in vivo safety evaluation. H&E staining revealed that no histopathological abnormalities were observed in all tissues, indicating the excellent in vivo biocompatibility of ZC@a-LDH (Fig. S5a and b). Further blood biochemical analyses demonstrated that the levels of all indexes (including liver/kidney function markers and blood cell counts) exhibited no significant variations in the control and ZC@a-LDH groups, confirming its satisfactory biocompatibility (Fig. S6).

## Conclusion
In summary, we successfully fabricated ZC@a-LDH antibacterial eye drops with high dispersion stability through acid etching strategy combined with zwitterionic chitosan modification. Importantly, the antimicrobial activity of ZC@a-LDH nanosheets outperformed that of ZC@LDH nanosheets, effectively inhibiting bacterial growth with a MIC of 12 μg mL−1, attributing to the synergistic effect of electrostatic interactions, antibacterial active Zn2+/Cu2+ ions and defect-induced charge transport. More importantly, ZC@a-LDH antibacterial eye drops with excellent biocompatibility effectively intervened in bacterial keratitis in mice. Compared to commercial TOB antibacterial eye drop, ZC@a-LDH demonstrated superior therapeutic effect, indicating its great clinical application potential. This work offers a promising paradigm for the treatment of bacterial keratitis with LDH nanosheets, inspiring us to develop more LDH-based nanomaterials for other ocular diseases therapy.
