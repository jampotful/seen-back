# Biometry study of foveal isoplanatic patch variation for adaptive optics retinal imaging

## Abstract
The change in ocular wavefront aberrations with visual angle determines the isoplanatic patch, defined as the largest field of view over which diffraction-limited retinal imaging can be achieved. Here, we study how the isoplanatic patch at the foveal center varies across 32 schematic eyes, each individualized with optical biometry estimates of corneal and crystalline lens surface topography, assuming a homogeneous refractive index for the crystalline lens. The foveal isoplanatic patches were calculated using real ray tracing through 2, 4, 6 and 8 mm pupil diameters for wavelengths of 400-1200 nm, simulating five adaptive optics (AO) strategies. Three of these strategies, used in flood illumination, point-scanning, and line-scanning ophthalmoscopes, apply the same wavefront correction across the entire field of view, resulting in almost identical isoplanatic patches. Two time-division multiplexing (TDM) strategies are proposed to increase the isoplanatic patch of AO scanning ophthalmoscopes through field-varying wavefront correction. Results revealed substantial variation in isoplanatic patch size across eyes (40-500%), indicating that the field of view in AO ophthalmoscopes should be adjusted for each eye. The median isoplanatic patch size decreases with increasing pupil diameter, coarsely following a power law. No statistically significant correlations were found between isoplanatic patch size and axial length. The foveal isoplanatic patch increases linearly with wavelength, primarily due to its wavelength-dependent definition (wavefront root-mean-squared, RMS <λ/14), rather than aberration chromatism. Additionally, ray tracing reveals that in strongly ametropic eyes, induced aberrations can result in wavefront RMS errors as large as λ/3 for an 8-mm pupil, with implications for wavefront sensing, open-loop ophthalmic AO, spectacle prescription and refractive surgery.

## Introduction
The human eye is one of the most studied optical systems, yet our understanding of the change of wavefront aberrations with visual angle across eyes is still lacking. This limits the improvement of ophthalmoscope optical performance, which depends on the wavefront aberrations of both the ophthalmoscope’s optics and the eye itself. Current ophthalmoscopes are designed using single eye models that represent an average eye [1–4], failing to capture the variation of aberrations across the population [5–7]. This leads to an inconsistency in image resolution that is well known in clinical settings, but not discussed in the optical design literature. As a step towards addressing this problem in the context of adaptive optics ophthalmoscopy, we generated a set of schematic eyes that were individualized using optical ocular biometry from human subjects, and used them to study foveal isoplanatism using various combinations of wavefront sensing and correction strategies.

## Adaptive optics ophthalmoscopy
Traditional ophthalmoscopes, defined here as those with focus adjustment as the only form of wavefront correction, capture retinal images through pupils typically smaller than 2 mm in diameter and fields of view ranging from 15 to 120° of visual angle [8–13]. Larger pupils are desirable to improve transverse image resolution and light collection efficiency, which can be critical for the safe implementation of spectroscopic and fluorescence imaging modalities [14–18]. Larger pupils, however, result in larger wavefront aberrations, both those that are constant across the retina and those that vary with retinal location. Most adaptive optics (AO) ophthalmoscopes to date seek to mitigate the former, by applying a field-independent wavefront correction over small fields of view over which the aberrations do not vary substantially (see Table 1). Despite having been demonstrated in various modalities, including flood illumination [19], line-scanning [20], and point-scanning [21], and applied to studies of numerous conditions, including age-related macular degeneration [22,23], diabetic retinopathy [22,23], glaucoma [24], multiple sclerosis [25], and inherited retinal degenerations [22,23], the small fields of view of current AO ophthalmoscopes prevent the adoption of this imaging technology in clinical settings.

## Ocular isoplanatism
The ocular isoplanatic patch, which varies across the retina, can be defined as the field of view over which wavefront aberrations remain “relatively constant,” according to a metric of choice [19,26–31]. Here we adopt wavefront RMS as such metric, in relation to Maréchal’s criterion [32], that is, the isoplanatic patch is the largest solid visual angle over which the residual wavefront root-mean-square (RMS) error after AO wavefront correction is less than one fourteenth of the imaging wavelength. In this way, in flood-illumination ophthalmoscope imaging through a circular aperture stop that is uniformly illuminated, the point spread function at any point within the field of view would be considered diffraction-limited.
It is important to recognize that because the refractive surfaces of the eye change over time, ocular isoplanatism is under physiological (i.e., normal) conditions an instantaneous, not a permanent, construct. Some aberration changes can take place in tens of milliseconds, such as those induced by tear film topography dynamics [33–36] and involuntary fixational eye movement, while others can take minutes, such as those in axial length [37,38] and corneal topography [39–41]. Therefore, the accuracy and precision of isoplanatic patch estimation is fundamentally limited by these physiological optical changes.
Previous studies, summarized in Table 1, have provided initial insights into ocular isoplanatism, but they have been limited by small sample sizes, narrow refractive error ranges, and coarse retinal sampling, sometimes as large at the isoplanatic angle itself. This study aims to partially address these limitations seeking to better estimate the variation of isoplanatism across the population, as well as with wavelength and pupil, to better inform the design and operation of AO ophthalmoscopes.

## Methods
Optical biometry was used to measure the topography of the four major ocular refractive surfaces to generate a set of individualized schematic eyes, the aberrations of which were evaluated using real ray tracing. Optical ocular biometry was chosen because it is widely used in ophthalmology clinics for estimating the prescription of intraocular lenses to be implanted during cataract surgery, as well as in AO ophthalmoscopy and vision science settings to calculate retinal magnification factors derived from individualized schematic eyes [42]. Therefore, it is a technique that would allow others to reproduce the methodology presented next.

## Human subjects
A cohort of 22 subjects 17 to 60 years of age (average 39, standard deviation 12) with natural crystalline lenses were recruited following the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and a protocol approved by the institutional review board of Stanford University. Written informed consent was obtained after the explanation of possible adverse effects of using one drop of 2.5% phenylephrine and one drop of 1% tropicamide to induce mydriasis and cycloplegia. Ocular biometry data were captured in 32 eyes (some subjects only wanted to dilate one eye) using a hyper-parallel optical coherence tomographer (HP-OCT) by Cylite (Notting Hill, Victoria, Australia) [43] without the use of spectacles, contact lenses or optometric lenses. A summary of the resulting data is provided in Table 4 (Appendix A).

## Individualized schematic eyes
Individualized optical models consisting of five surfaces were created for each human eye for which biometry data was captured, in order to estimate wavefront aberrations around the preferred retinal locus of fixation, hereon, the foveal center. In these models, the first four surfaces represent, in order, the anterior and posterior surfaces of the cornea, and the anterior and posterior surfaces of the crystalline lens. The topography of these surfaces, their separation (including their distance to the retina), their decentering and tilt were derived from the optical biometry using real ray tracing, as previously described [42]. In brief, the biometer illuminates the eye with 1008 parallel beamlets of 840 nm light, providing an axial resolution of ∼9.4 µm and a transverse resolution of 33 µm [44]. The spectrum of each beamlet after interference with a reference beam is used to calculate a 3-dimensional (3D) image of the anterior segment of the eye together with an ocular axial length. The biometer analysis software automatically segments the aforementioned ocular surfaces and fits them using Noll standard Zernike polynomials [45], using the first 45, 36, 11 and 11 terms to describe the anterior and posterior surfaces of the cornea, and the anterior and posterior surfaces of the crystalline lens, respectively. The number of Zernike terms is set by the Cylite software, and it is based on the size of the fitted area, such that comparable fitting errors are achieved for all four surfaces. Fan distortion, as noted in Ref. [46], is corrected in the segmented surface data. Points over these four surfaces were then expressed as sets of Cartesian coordinates (x0,y0,zOPL) to calculate the corresponding optical path length (OPL) differences between each surface and for each beamlet. Real ray tracing was then performed along each beamlet, assuming homogeneous ocular refractive indices, to calculate the refractive index-corrected ocular surfaces (i.e., accounting for Snell’s law). The center thicknesses between surfaces were calculated by dividing the OPL by the group refractive index of the ocular media at 840 nm [47]. The four ocular surfaces after refraction correction were then least-squares fit with 45 lowest-order Noll’s standard Zernike polynomials and imported into OpticStudio (Ansys Zemax, Kirkland, WA, USA).
The retinal surface of each schematic eye was modeled as an individualized non-rotationally symmetrical ellipsoid with radii of curvature (Rx,Ry) in units of millimeters, and conic constants (Qx,Qy) along the x-z (horizontal plane, z being the optical axis) and y-z (vertical plane) planes [3]:

The aperture stop of each schematic eye was placed at the vertex of the crystalline lens anterior surface, with ray tracing for estimating residual wavefront aberrations after wavefront correction performed from the retina towards the cornea.
The refractive indices for the ocular media and their chromatic variation in the 380-900 nm range were specified using the averaged dispersion data from both Le Grand [48] and Navarro eye [49], as proposed in Ref. [50], with values between 900 and 1200 nm extrapolated. Dispersion was applied to each ocular medium separately. The resulting data over the complete wavelength range were then fit to Schott’s dispersion formula using OpticStudio’s glass fitting tool, since the Cauchy dispersion equation in Ref. [50] is not supported by this software. The use of a different formula did not affect the fidelity of the dispersion modeling, as both mathematical models have a very low fitting error to the experimental data.
The assumption of homogeneous ocular media is reasonable for the cornea, the aqueous humor, and the vitreous humor [51,52], but it is less so for the crystalline lens, which can be better described through a gradient refractive index (GRIN) distribution [2,53–57]. Although GRIN crystalline lens models have been developed [2,4,56], and in vitro individualization using optical ocular biometry has been demonstrated [58], in vivo individualization has not yet been reported. This is due to two reasons, first, the lack of approaches for in vivo non-invasive measurement of the refractive index variation within the crystalline lens, and second, the lack of population-wide GRIN distributions (a limitation acknowledged by Siedlecki et al. [58]). Also, although it is possible to model the crystalline lens as a GRIN optical element, it cannot be guaranteed that such modeling would produce solutions that are unique or even consistent with the crystalline lens anatomy. The latter is illustrated by the wide-field schematic eye by Polans et al. [4], in which the tuning of a GRIN crystalline lens model to match average defocus and astigmatism values across the retina resulted in a refractive index distribution with higher refractive index values near its surfaces and iso-indicial curves that are inconsistent with known lens structure, as noted by Akram et al. [59]. Therefore, in the absence of adequate data for individualized modeling of the crystalline lens as a GRIN optical element, this study can be thought of as an initial exploration of isoplanatism using an approach that must be refined once the refractive index variation within the crystalline lens can be measured non-invasively.

## Adaptive optics strategies
Adaptive optics wavefront sensing and correction were simulated using Matlab (Natick, Massachusetts, USA) in conjunction with OpticStudio, through its Interactive Extension feature. Five adaptive optics strategies, depicted in Fig. 1, were simulated, with three representing current paradigms in which the same wavefront correction is applied across the entire field of view. The first approach, which is representative of flood illumination AO ophthalmoscopes (AO-FIO) [19], the wavefront sensing is performed at the center of the field of view. The second strategy emulates AO line-scanning ophthalmoscopes (AO-LSOs) [20], with the wavefront sensing performed as the wavefront aberration average along a line segment centered on the field of view. The third strategy represents point-scanning AO ophthalmoscopes, such as AO scanning light ophthalmoscopes (AO-SLOs) [21] and some AO optical coherence tomographers [60], the wavefront sensing performed by averaging the aberrations across a rectangular field of view.
Depiction of wavefront sensing, and correction strategies used to estimate isoplanatic patches in the human eye. The three leftmost columns represent most flood illumination, line-scanning, and point scanning adaptive optics ophthalmoscopes, respectively, with a single wavefront correction across the field of view. The two rightmost columns illustrate the proposed time-division multiplexed adaptive optics, in which the scanning of the wavefront sensing beacon is used to sequentially sample and correct aberrations across the field of view. The squares represent regions of the field of view, with their fill color indicating where each wavefront measurement was performed, and each corresponding wavefront correction is applied.
Two additional, and to the best of our knowledge novel, AO strategies that are suitable for line- and point-scanning ophthalmoscopes were also simulated, seeking larger isoplanatic patches after applying wavefront corrections that vary across the field of view. In these strategies, the wavefront sensing is scanned in synchrony with the imaging beam to perform multiple measurements across the field of view. Borrowing language from electrical engineering, we term this approach time-division multiplexed AO (TDM-AO), which can be thought of as creating multiple AO control systems that time-share hardware by synchronizing the scanning, the wavefront sensing, and the wavefront correction, as depicted in Fig. 1. For the purpose of illustrating TDM-AO, the field of view was divided into seven equal regions, of which only the central five are used for wavefront sensing and all seven for wavefront correction, but the approach can be implemented with an arbitrary number of regions, which is only limited by the wavefront sensing and correction rates. Here it is important to note that in point-scanning AO ophthalmoscopes, the fast scanning of the imaging raster is outside the bandwidth of current ophthalmic wavefront correction technology, such as, voice-coil or electrostatic deformable mirrors (DMs). Therefore, it is not possible to correct wavefront variations along this scanning axis. Hence, our TDM AO-SLO simulation assumes multiple wavefront aberration measurements along the slower scanning axis of the imaging raster.
Wavefront correction for all AO strategies in OpticStudio was simulated by placing a Zernike Standard Phase surface with 45 Zernike coefficients at the aperture stop. This modeling was validated against placing the simulated wavefront correction on a pupil conjugate created using a 4-f afocal relay consisting of lenses free of aberrations (termed paraxial lenses in OpticStudio). Multiple AO wavefront correction iterations were applied to simulate the operation of closed-loop AO systems, to account for potential induced aberrations, that is, the amplitude of the Zernike polynomials coefficients was updated after each successive iteration. The number of iterations for all AO strategies was determined as that needed to reach a residual wavefront RMS (excluding piston, tip, and tilt, which do not induce image blur) < λ/140 at the central field point in AO-FIO.

## Isoplanatic patch size calculation
The maximum fields of view selected for this study were ±0.4, ± 1.0, ± 3.0, and ±10.0° for 8, 6, 4, and 2 mm aperture stop (i.e., the physical pupil of the eye) diameters, respectively, at 800 nm. The field of view is originally sampled over a square grid of 7 × 7, with ray aiming feature in OpticStudio enabled to ensure that the traced rays fill the stop surface for a given stop size. After calculating the residual wavefront RMS using OpticStudio’s Zernike Standard Coefficients analysis with 256 × 256 ray sampling, and 45 Zernike polynomials, the resulting values were exported to Matlab for interpolation onto a 200 × 200 square grid across the field of view using cubic splines to more precise estimate the isoplanatic patch dimensions. This process was repeated across wavelengths ranging from 400 to 1200 nm, in increments of 100 nm, with fields of view that increased linearly with wavelength.
For AO-FIO, AO-LSO, and AO-SLO, the isoplanatic patch was calculated as the largest circle containing the central field point (i.e., fixation) and containing only diffraction-limited field points. For the TDM-AO approaches, the isoplanatic patches are more appropriately described by a rectangle, but for ease of comparison with the traditional AO strategies, we report the equivalent circle diameter, calculated as 2A/π , where A is the area of the largest inscribed rectangle containing the central field point. All these values are provided in Table 4 (Appendix A).

## Results

## Isoplanatic patch variation with adaptive optics strategy
Let us start by examining the isoplanatic patches for 800 nm light in two eyes, one with the average axial length of this study (∼25 mm; 270 OD), and one with the strongest refraction error (-14.25D; 304 OD). For each of the simulated pupil diameters, the leftmost column in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 displays the total wavefront RMS without wavefront correction, while the remaining columns, one per AO strategy, present the residual wavefront RMS maps after iterative wavefront correction. The extreme values on the color bars in the left column show that the wavefront aberrations increase with pupil diameter, while their variation across the field (0.1-0.2 waves) is comparable for all pupil diameters. The residual wavefront RMS maps reveal that the three traditional AO strategies yield nearly identical isoplanatic patch dimensions for 4-8 mm pupils and, comparable for 2 mm pupils. The proposed TDM-AO approaches, by design, produce isoplanatic patches that are larger along the wavefront sensing scanning axis, and similar to those of traditional AO strategies in the orthogonal axis.

RMS wavefront error maps for 800 nm light in units of waves for various pupil diameters and AO strategies for the eye with largest prescription error in our study (-14.25 D, subject 304 OD). The isoplanatic patch is shown as the largest black circle/rectangle within which diffraction limited imaging can be achieved.
The isoplanatic patch dimensions across the entire set of eyes, summarized in Fig. 4, are consistent with those previously reported (see Table 1). As with the previous two eyes, when considering all the eyes in the study, the conventional AO strategies result in comparable isoplanatic patch sizes with average differences being 0.3% for 4-8 mm pupil diameters, and 21% for 2 mm pupils. Interestingly, in the simulated scanned conventional AO strategies using 2 mm pupils, some eyes have no isoplanatic patch. The data also indicate that for the chosen TDM-AO parameters, the diameter of the circular equivalent isoplanatic patch diameter increases by 75% on average (50-103% range), which corresponds to 206% average increase in area (125-312%).
Estimated foveal isoplanatic patch diameters (circle or equivalent circle) at 800 nm in a set of eyes after applying various AO correction strategies. Each shape represents the distribution (histogram) of the values, with the number indicating the median, and the central vertical black lines denoting 25th and 75th percentiles.
The relative variation in isoplanatic patch size across eyes for 6 mm pupils and 800 nm is approximately 30% larger than that reported by Tarrant & Roorda for 780 nm in a study with 5 eyes [26]. As we shall see later, this difference is too large to be attributed solely to the wavelength difference (780 vs. 800 nm), and more likely due to the different subject cohort. For a 4 mm pupil diameter, the relative estimated average variation (maximum/minimum) is 4.4, more than double the 1.6 reported by Tarrant & Roorda [26] and 1.7 by Dubinin et al. [28] For 2 and 8 mm pupils, we find an average relative variation of 4.3 and 2.5, respectively. The substantial variation of isoplanatic patch size across eyes suggests that the size of the field of view in AO ophthalmoscopes should be adjusted for each eye.
Importantly, we find that six eyes (359 OS, 359 OD, 300 OS, 300 OD, 304 OD, 302 OS) yielded zero size isoplanatic patch after the first AO iteration for the AO-FIO case at pupil diameters of 8 mm. These eyes, except for 359 OS and OD for which we do not have prescription data, are myopes with refractive error greater than 9 D. This is not a result of sampling error, as we get the same outcome when increasing pupil sampling 16 times (from 256 × 256 to 4096 × 4096 rays). Non-zero patches, however, are achieved after the second iteration, indicating that the residual wavefront aberrations from the initial iteration are due to the interaction of aberrations from one refractive surface with those of the others, a phenomenon referred to as “induced aberration” [61]. Such significant level of induced aberration in high myopes indicates that wavefront sensing measurements are not adequate for validating individualized schematic eyes the models, or at least, not without some form of wavefront correction that mitigates the induced aberrations. Also, the presence of such strong induced aberrations indicates that open-loop ophthalmic AO, irrespective of application, would not be able to deliver diffraction limited performance in all subjects (particularly in strong myopes or hyperopes).

## Isoplanatic patch variation with axial length
Despite being a substantial and growing fraction of the population, high myopes (>5D) have often been excluded from ocular isoplanatism and AO retinal imaging studies. The set of eyes studied here includes 4 myopic eyes with spectacle prescription stronger than -8 D and axial lengths greater than 27 mm, in contrast with the average eye length, which is considered 24 mm, although this varies with study population [62]. Despite their low number, these strongly myopic eyes provide an opportunity to explore the relationship between the isoplanatic patch and axial length [3].
The scatter in the data plotted in Fig. 5, indicates that the isoplanatic patch cannot be well described by a function of axial length alone, and in particular with a linear function, as shown by the low correlation values (-0.27 ∼ 0.48) and high p-values (mostly > 0.1) when testing the hypothesis that there is no relationship between axial length and the isoplanatic patch diameter (null hypothesis) using Matlab’s function corrcoef (see Table 2). Also, the low values of the linear model slopes, suggest that imaging highly myopic eyes should not be any more challenging (i.e., smaller isoplanatic patch) than those with smaller refractive errors, which is in consistent with our experience.

## Isoplanatic patch variation with pupil diameter
The relationship between the median isoplanatic patch diameter for traditional AO strategies and equivalent diameter for TDM-AO strategies was fitted to a power law function of the form aϕb+c with ϕ being the pupil diameter in units of mm by minimizing the relative fitting error, as opposed to the absolute error. The results, plotted in Fig. 6, show that the power law is a coarse but reasonable model with few parameters.

## Isoplanatic patch variation with wavelength
The variation of isoplanatic patch size with wavelengths in the 400-1200 nm range was evaluated using published ocular media dispersion data [50]. This range contains all wavelengths with >10% total transmission through the ocular media [63]. Since the three conventional AO methods exhibit similar patch sizes, the isoplanatic patch variation with wavelength was only evaluated for a simulated AO-FIO. The results, summarized in Table 3 and plotted in Fig. 7(a), reveal a linear relationship (r ≥ 0.99, p ≤ 10−11) between isoplanatic patch diameter and wavelength across all pupil sizes, in agreement with the findings by Han et al. [31].
Median isoplanatic patch diameter vs. wavelength for 6 mm pupil diameter in eye 270 OD, when simulating AO flood-illumination ophthalmoscope correction using a: (a) wavelength-dependent criterion (wavefront RMS < λ/14), and (b) a wavelength-independent criterion (wavefront RMS < 0.8 µm/14). The vertical bars extend from the 25th to the 75th percentile, and the dashed curve shows a linear (least-squares) fit.

## Linear equations describing isoplanatic patch diameter as a function of wavelength (λ; in µm) for AO-FIO using Maréchal's wavelength dependent criterion.
Interestingly, the linear relationship between isoplanatic patch diameter and wavelength does not stem from the chromatic dispersion of the ocular media, but rather from the selection of an imaging performance metric that changes linearly with wavelength (Maréchal's criterion) [32]. This can be tested by comparing the isoplanatic patch against a wavelength-independent metric (e.g., RMS in units of microns), as depicted in Fig. 7(b). This plot shows that isoplanatic patch change with wavelength is reduced by 92%, with the remaining 8% being consistent with third and higher order aberrations in the eye that do not substantially changing with wavelength, as previously reported [64–66].

## Summary
Individualized schematic eyes with four refractive surfaces and homogeneous media were derived from optical ocular biometry from 32 eyes with focus errors in the -14.25 to +1.25 D range. Ray tracing through these schematic eyes for different pupil sizes, wavelengths, and adaptive optics strategies, shows substantial variation of isoplanatic patch size across eyes. This variation indicates that AO ophthalmoscopes should be designed and operated with adjustable field of views to take advantage of the larger isoplanatic patches, and to use smaller fields of view when needed to retain a desired level of image resolution and/or signal-to-noise ratio.
Importantly, the ray tracing indicates that in eyes with large focus error, wavefront sensing could be affected by induced aberrations with wavefront RMS as large as λ/3 for an 8-mm pupil. Although these aberrations would not degrade the performance of iterative closed-loop AO wavefront sensing and correction, they could degrade the performance of wavefront sensors used for refraction (i.e., spectacle prescription) or any open-loop ophthalmic AO application, unless defocus, and perhaps other dominant aberration, are pre-corrected.
The ray tracing through the schematic eyes also revealed that the three evaluated AO strategies that apply the same wavefront correction across the entire field of view, provide equivalent isoplanatic patches to within 0.3% for 4 mm pupils and larger. Two new AO strategies, suitable for AO scanning ophthalmoscopes, were proposed to increase the isoplanatic patch by delivering field-varying wavefront correction. Assuming the same retinal image sampling density, this increase in field of view would come at the expense of a proportional reduction in ophthalmoscope frame rate, and thus, a reduction of AO wavefront correction bandwidth.
The simulations show no statistically significant correlation between axial length and isoplanatic patch, which suggests that other than focus correction, no special considerations should be necessary to image the retina of eyes with large refractive errors in closed-loop AO ophthalmoscopes. It was also found that the change in median isoplanatic patch diameter with pupil diameter can be approximated by a power law with almost identical coefficients for the three conventional AO strategies and both TDM-AO strategies. The majority of the linear increase in median isoplanatic patch diameter with wavelength (92%) is attributable to the wavelength dependence of our isoplanatic patch definition, with only a small fraction (8%) due to chromatic variation of monochromatic aberrations of the eye, once piston, tip, tilt, and defocus are excluded. This variation of isoplanatic patch with wavelength may explain why visible light AO fluorescence retinal imaging has not achieved the expected transverse resolution in fields of view selected for infrared imaging.
It might be tempting to argue that the study approach could have been validated against wavefront sensing measurements, but, as we mentioned earlier, this is not straightforward due to the dynamic nature of the ocular wavefront aberrations. In fact, this is the very reason why since the earliest days of AO retinal imaging, AO ophthalmoscopes are constantly updating the wavefront correction, rather than applying a static correction, as aberrations changes over just a few seconds result in noticeable image degradation [67]. Thus, any wavefront sensing validating measurements would have to be performed simultaneously with the biometry data capture and, moreover, wavefront sensing would have to be performed at all retinal locations of interest simultaneously, which is currently outside our capabilities. Irrespective of the limitation of the simple modeling of the crystalline lens, the results from this study, which are consistent with prior human ocular isoplanatism studies, provide new insight into various aspects of ocular isoplanatism and its implications for AO ophthalmoscopy. Future modeling studies of ocular isoplanatism should incorporate GRIN crystalline lenses, provided these models can be individualized with physically and anatomically plausible geometries. It is likely that such models will lead to a reduction in spherical aberration [56,68–70]. However, since this spherical aberration occurs close to the eye’s aperture stop, it is expected to be nearly field-independent and, therefore, it should not significantly affect isoplanatism in AO ophthalmoscopy.
