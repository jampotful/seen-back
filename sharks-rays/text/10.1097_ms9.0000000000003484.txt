# The beneficial effects of bevacizumab on the progression of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy: a case-control study

## Abstract
AbstractBackground:This study was designed to explore the effect of intravitreal bevacizumab injection on the progression of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) patients, offering a ray of hope in treating this condition.Materials and methods:The current investigation was a case-control study in which the demographic records of patients diagnosed with NAION. The total included 30 patients, divided into two main groups: the treatment group, which received intravitreal bevacizumab administration (n = 15), and the control group (n = 15), which was treated without bevacizumab. Bevacizumab (1.25 mg) was injected into the eye through a transscleral approach using a 25-gauge needle. Tetracaine, lidocaine, and gatifloxacin drops were applied for anesthesia and post-treatment. Visual acuity was measured using letter scores at a distance of 2 meters. Visual fields and optic nerve imaging were used to assess retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness.Results:Eye involvement was evenly distributed between the right and left eyes. A two-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in RNFL thickness between injection groups (F(2, 28) = 3.985, P = 0.03), with a significant decrease over time (P < 0.0001). Visual acuity also differed significantly (F(2, 28) = 103.0, P = 0.001). Bevacizumab injection led to marked visual acuity improvement at one and three months (P = 0.022, P = 0.0001, P = 0.001). The non-injection group showed improvement only at three months (P = 0.0001, P = 0.0004).Conclusion:The data obtained from this study suggest that intravitreal bevacizumab administration may offer promising effects on the progression of NAION patients, providing a reassuring and confident outlook for the future of NAION treatment.Keywords: angiogenesis, bevacizumab, ischemic, optic neuropathy, non-arteritic anterior ischemic, optic neuropathy

## Introduction
Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) is a significant cause of visual impairment or blindness in the elderly population (>55 years old)[1], and its annual incidence is estimated to be between 2.9 and 10 per 100 000 individuals[2]. The NAION hallmark is painless visual loss, typically occurring in one eye over days. Likewise, the visual disturbance is often noticed upon awakening, likely due to nocturnal blood pressure drop and underlying systemic abnormalities such as high blood pressure and diabetes[1]. It has been established that eye pain or headaches are experienced in some patients, which complicates the differentiation of NAION from optic neuritis. In NAION, the condition may remain stable, showing minimal or no changes in visual acuity after the initial loss, or it may progress, characterized by intermittent, gradual decreases or a consistent decline in vision for days to weeks before reaching a stable state[3]. Various therapeutic strategies for NAION patients have been investigated, including systemic corticosteroids, anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, phenytoin, and levodopa. To date, none of these treatments have proven to be effective[4]. In this respect, corticosteroids, vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) (e.g., bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept), erythropoietin, caspase-2 inhibitor (QPI-1007), and fasudil are commonly intraocular prescribed for acute NAION[5]. VEGF is an essential intraocular cytokine that affects vascular permeability and plays a significant role in forming abnormal blood vessels[6]. Conversely, inhibiting VEGF and angiogenesis could be a fundamental treatment strategy for various ophthalmology[7].

Bevacizumab is defined as a monoclonal antibody with the potential of binding to the VEGF-A family that blocks the surface of endothelial cells via its receptors such as VEGFR-1 (fit-1) and VEGFR-2 (KDRflk-1)[8]. Bevacizumab has been approved to treat cervical, colorectal, glioblastoma (recurrent and progressive), and lung cancers[9]. Therefore, the administration of bevacizumab could prevent the angiogenesis in cancer cells[10]. Bevacizumab is widely administrated for the treatment of intraocular diseases. However, some side effects, such as subconjunctival hemorrhage, cataracts subconjunctival hemorrhage, cataract progression, acute vision loss, retinal artery occlusion, and retinal pigment epithelium tear, have been reported[11]. In contrast, the visual acuity and fields were compared before and after intravitreal bevacizumab injection. The results noted that bevacizumab did not have a significant impact on the clinical side effects in the NAION patients[12].
The present study aimed to investigate the changes in retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness and visual acuity for NAION patients treated with intravitreal bevacizumab compared to the control group.

## Methods

## Study design and subjects
The current investigation was a case-control study. The demographic records of patients diagnosed with NAION and undergoing treatment in 2021–2022 were extracted. The patients were divided into two groups: the first group consisted of patients who received intravitreal bevacizumab administration (treatment group, n = 15), while the second group was treated without bevacizumab (control group, n = 15). The present study was reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[13].

## Ethics consideration
The research protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Azad University of Medical Sciences. Throughout the study, stringent measures were taken to safeguard all patient-related information, and data extraction was performed in a manner that ensured anonymity. In instances where it was deemed necessary, patients were contacted, and their informed consent was duly obtained. Important safety considerations for intravitreal injection were also taken into account, including the use of proper injection technique with attention to the patient’s lens status and ocular axis, thorough disinfection of the injection site, use of a speculum to prevent eyelid contact with the injection area, and avoiding any contact with the needle or syringe after the medication was prepared[14].

## Data collection
According to the previous research[15], bevacizumab (1.25 mg) was intravitreal administered (transscleral approach). After that, the affected eye was treated with sterile tetracaine and lidocaine 1%. A 25-gauge needle was inserted through the marked point (pars plana) into the vitreous cavity for the administration of bevacizumab. The needle was removed after the injection, and a drop of topical gatifloxacin was applied to the eye. Patients were advised to continue using the gatifloxacin drops for five days. Visual acuity (VA) data were recorded using ETDRS letter scores measured at a distance of 2 meters. For each visit, the best available VA measurement was included in the analysis, typically based on habitual correction instead of formal refraction, in line with standard clinical procedures. Visual fields and optic nerve computed tomography (OCT) were also applied to determine the RNFL.

## Statistical analysis
Quantitative and qualitative data were presented in the form of numbers (percentages) and means with standard deviations, respectively. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For quantitative data comparison, the analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 27) and Prism GraphPad (V 10.4.1) with a significance level of P <0.05.

## The beneficial effects of bevacizumab on the progression of NAION
In the current study, a significant difference in RNFL thickness was observed between the two injection groups using a two-way ANOVA test (F(2, 28) = 3.985, P = 0.03). Additionally, Post hoc Tukey analysis revealed a significant difference in baseline thickness between the two groups (P = 0.05). Furthermore, the results indicated a significant decreasing trend in RNFL thickness over time in both groups. Specifically, RNFL thickness showed a significant reduction at one month compared to baseline, and at three months compared to both baseline and the first month post-injection (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1 and Table 2).

Furthermore, the two-way ANOVA analysis revealed a significant difference in visual acuity between the two injection groups (F(2, 28) = 103.0, P = 0.001). However, post hoc Tukey test showed that intravitreal injection of bevacizumab resulted in a statistically significant improvement in visual acuity at both one and three months after treatment (P = 0.022, P = 0.0001, and P = 0.001). In the non-intravitreal injection group, no significant difference was observed between baseline and one month post-treatment. Nonetheless, significant improvements were detected between baseline and three months, as well as between the first and third months following treatment (P = 0.0001 and P = 0.0004) (Fig. 2 and Table 3).

## Discussion
As previously mentioned, NAION is the second most common optic neuropathy after glaucoma. It results from infarction of the short posterior ciliary arteries, which supply the anterior portion of the optic nerve head. This leads to axonal edema and a compartment syndrome in an already crowded optic disc, ultimately causing vision loss. In the United States, approximately six new cases are reported annually. The present study was conducted to investigate the effect of intravitreal bevacizumab injection on the clinical course of patients with NAION. In the current section, the discussion will be based on the obtained results.
In the present study, intravitreal bevacizumab injections led to a reduction in RNFL thickness in both groups, with a more pronounced decrease observed in the intravitreal group. Additionally, both types of injection resulted in an improvement in visual acuity. In this regard, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a potent angiogenic factor that influences vascular permeability. Some physicians have used intravitreal anti-VEGF agents in patients with acute NAION. It is believed that anti-VEGF agents may exert two potentially beneficial effects on the course of NAION: one is the possible reduction of subretinal fluid during resolution, and the other is the reduction of optic disc edema through decreased capillary permeability[16]. Administration of anti-VEGF agents leads to decreased capillary permeability, thereby accelerating the resolution of optic disc edema. With reduced pressure on the capillaries at the optic nerve head and improved blood flow, the function of ischemic axons – which are still viable but non-functional may potentially be restored. Also, since acute disc edema may contribute to further axonal damage in NAION through a vicious cycle of ischemia, ultimately leading to edema and compartment syndrome, many medical and surgical treatment strategies aim to shorten the duration of disc edema to prevent further axonal ischemia and reduce neuronal loss[17,18]. However, to date, no accepted treatment for NAION has been established.
On the other hand, some studies have reported conflicting outcomes regarding the efficacy of anti-VEGF therapies in patients with recent-onset NAION. Bennet et al presented their findings from a patient with acute NAION who received an intravitreal bevacizumab injection. They observed rapid resolution of optic disc edema and improved visual outcome within nine days post-injection[17]. Pece et al described three patients treated with a single intravitreal ranibizumab injection within 1 to 2 days of NAION onset. Early resolution of optic disc swelling was noted in all patients within one week; however, visual acuity or visual field improvement did not accompany the anatomical improvement[19]. Saatci et al conducted a retrospective study involving 17 eyes of 16 patients with NAION, treated within 15 days (range: 2 to 15 days) with a single intravitreal ranibizumab injection. One year post-injection, visual improvement was observed in 14 eyes, no change in one eye, and a decrease in visual acuity in two eyes. Visual fields improved in nine eyes and remained unchanged in eight. The mean RNFL thickness significantly decreased in all patients during follow-up[20]. Bajin et al reported four patients with acute NAION who received a single intravitreal ranibizumab injection and experienced visual loss for 15 days or less. All patients showed visual improvement during a three-month follow-up. The researchers noted a significant reduction in mean RNFL thickness in all eyes, as assessed by spectral-domain optical coherence tomography. No injection-related complications were reported during the follow-up period[21].In a non-randomized controlled clinical trial on patients with NAION, Rootman et al evaluated the effects of a 1.25 mg intravitreal bevacizumab injection in 25 patients (17 treated eyes and 8 control eyes). The data revealed no significant differences between the treatment and control groups in terms of changes in visual field, visual acuity, or optic nerve OCT thickness[15]. Furthermore, two patients in the treatment group experienced a second NAION episode in the same eye during the follow-up period.
Other intravitreal agents have also been reported in the treatment of NAION. A single case report on aflibercept, another anti-VEGF agent, demonstrated promising visual outcomes in a patient with unilateral NAION, showing an improvement in visual acuity from 1/10 to 7/10, a reduction in average RNFL thickness from 182.4 µm to 159.7 µm, and significant visual field improvement post-injection[22]. Similarly, Entezari et al found that intravitreal bevacizumab administration could reduce RNFL thickness[23]. In a retrospective observational case series, Horsley et al showed that repeated intravitreal injections (more than 10) of anti-VEGF agents – including pegaptanib, bevacizumab, and ranibizumab – did not have a significant effect on RNFL thickness in patients with neovascular AMD. However, they compared baseline thickness with measurements taken at the final follow-up, which was no less than 12 months, thus potentially missing early transient changes[24]. It is also worth noting that each intravitreal injection may transiently increase intraocular pressure (IOP), which could theoretically harm the optic nerve. However, this concern may not apply to the current study, as our cases received only two injections, and any brief post-injection IOP elevation was likely insufficient to cause RNFL damage. In a case report, Chen et al observed acute vision loss and enlargement of the foveal avascular zone following an intravitreal injection for diabetic macular edema. They hypothesized that bevacizumab may have compromised an already tenuous vascular perfusion status, leading to worsening macular ischemia and resulting in visual acuity loss[25]. However, in our study, no acute loss of vision was observed following the injection.

## Limitations
The findings may not be generalizable to NAION patients across Iran or other regions with differing healthcare facilities and socio-economic conditions, as the study was conducted in a single center. While the current study’s single-hospital setting provides valuable insights, it also introduces several potential biases and limitations related to geographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare access disparities. Additionally, one significant limitation of the study is its small sample size, which may affect the statistical results. Future research should include a more diverse sample from multiple healthcare facilities across different regions to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, multicenter studies can help mitigate the bias in the results.

## Implications for clinical practice
Bevacizumab may offer a viable therapeutic option for NAION, especially in cases where there are limited effective treatments available. Clinicians should consider its use in patients at risk of severe visual loss or where other treatments fail. Given the progressive nature of NAION, early diagnosis and timely administration of bevacizumab could help slow disease progression and improve visual outcomes. Incorporating bevacizumab into the treatment plan early could be crucial for preserving vision. Clinicians should carefully select patients who may benefit most from bevacizumab, particularly those with more severe cases or those showing early signs of ischemic damage to the optic nerve. Personalized treatment decisions could improve patient outcomes. Regularly monitoring patients receiving bevacizumab for both visual function and ocular health is critical. Clinicians should be aware of the potential side effects, particularly increases in intraocular pressure, and ensure appropriate follow-up. Collaboration between ophthalmologists, neuro-ophthalmologists, and vascular specialists may be beneficial in managing NAION patients who are candidates for bevacizumab treatment, as it involves both vascular and ocular components. Due to the limited number of studies on bevacizumab in NAION, clinicians need to discuss with patients the experimental nature of the treatment, potential risks, and expected outcomes to make an informed decision. If bevacizumab is consistently beneficial, clinical guidelines could be updated to include it as part of the standard treatment options for NAION, particularly in specific patient populations.

## Recommendations for future research
It is suggested that the effectiveness of bevacizumab be assessed in a larger study population compared to other treatment methods, such as anti-angiogenic drugs. Conduct randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with long-term follow-up to evaluate the sustained effects of bevacizumab on visual recovery and disease progression in patients with NAION. To determine its benefits, compare the efficacy of bevacizumab with other treatment options, such as corticosteroids, neuroprotective agents, and other anti-VEGF drugs. Investigate the optimal dose and frequency of bevacizumab administration to maximize its therapeutic benefits while minimizing side effects. Explore the underlying molecular mechanisms by which bevacizumab affects ischemic optic neuropathy, particularly its impact on retinal ganglion cells and the optic nerve. Identify biomarkers or clinical features that predict which patients with NAION will benefit most from bevacizumab, focusing on early versus late intervention. Further assess the safety of bevacizumab in treating NAION, particularly concerning ocular and systemic side effects, to ensure the therapy’s acceptable risk-benefit ratio.

## Conclusions
In sum, the results indicate that the group receiving intravitreal bevacizumab injections showed a more significant reduction in RNFL thickness compared to the non-injection group. Moreover, both groups demonstrated significant within-group changes over time, with a notable decrease in RNFL levels. Additionally, while there was a statistically significant improvement in visual acuity over time within each group, no significant difference was observed between the two groups in terms of visual acuity outcomes.
