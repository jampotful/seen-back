Distinct Tear Element Signatures in Primary Acquired Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction Identified by Particle-Induced X-Ray Emission.
Rabinovich Y, Darawshe M, Girshevitz O, Sela T, Zahavi A, Goldenberg-Cohen N, Vardizer Y. (2026). Investigative ophthalmology & visual science. . doi:10.1167/iovs.67.6.54. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Distinct Tear Element Signatures in Primary Acquired Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction Identified by Particle-Induced X-Ray Emission

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the etiology of primary acquired nasolacrimal duct obstruction (PANDO) by characterizing the elemental composition of tear and blood samples from affected patients.MethodsTear samples were prospectively collected from each eye of adults with PANDO and matched controls using Schirmer strips. Fingertip blood was obtained using the same absorbent material. Samples were analyzed with particle-induced x-ray emission (PIXE) to quantify multi-element profiles. Statistical analysis included paired testing for intraindividual comparisons in unilateral PANDO, independent-group comparisons between PANDO and controls, and multivariate methods to assess overall group separation.ResultsParticipants included 15 patients with PANDO and 15 controls (14 females each), with mean ages of 57.29 ± 15.35 and 53.27 ± 14.89 years, respectively. Twelve elements were detected in tears and 11 in blood. Compared with controls, the PANDO group had significantly (P < 0.05) higher tear levels of sodium (Na) and chlorine (Cl) in both affected and unaffected eyes, and higher phosphorus (P) and sulfur (S) levels in unaffected eyes. There were no significant differences between affected and unaffected eyes in unilateral PANDO. In blood, only copper (Cu) differed significantly between groups. Multivariate analyses demonstrated clear group separation in tears but not blood profiles.ConclusionsPatients with PANDO have a distinct tear elemental signature characterized by high Na levels. Our findings suggest that bilateral biochemical alterations may precede clinically detectable obstruction and that PANDO is a product of local ocular processes rather than systemic influences. Tear elemental analysis is a promising noninvasive approach for detecting early or subclinical changes in PANDO and improving understanding of its pathophysiology.Keywords: primary acquired nasolacrimal duct obstruction, tear biomarkers, particle-induced x-ray emission (PIXE), trace elements, tears
Trace elements are essential for numerous physiological processes, and disturbances in their concentrations can influence cellular function and contribute to a wide range of systemic and ocular disorders.1 As such, they have the potential to serve as biomarkers of nutritional status, environmental exposures, and disease activity, and their analysis in human biofluids and appendages, such as tears, blood, urine, hair, and nails, is being increasingly recognized as a promising noninvasive diagnostic tool in clinical practice.2
Tears are a particularly valuable biofluid for such investigations. They support ocular surface homeostasis by providing lubrication, metabolic substrates, and immune mediators, and alterations in their composition have been associated with several ocular conditions.3 For example, patients with type II diabetes mellitus demonstrate significantly elevated tear concentrations of zinc (Zn), chromium, cobalt, manganese, barium, and lead compared with healthy individuals.4 However, the small sample volumes typically obtained from tear collection, often around 20 µL, pose analytical challenges, particularly for multi-element profiling.
Particle-induced x-ray emission (PIXE) is a highly sensitive analytical technique capable of simultaneously quantifying multiple elements in body fluids with minimal sample preparation and without damaging the specimen. It offers detection limits in the microgram-per-gram range and spatial resolution down to a few micrometers, making it well-suited for analyzing the small tear volumes obtained in clinical studies. Previous PIXE investigations have demonstrated that tear elemental profiles can vary significantly among populations, including rural versus urban groups.5
The lacrimal system continuously produces tears, which drain through the nasolacrimal duct into the nasal cavity. Primary acquired nasolacrimal duct obstruction (PANDO) disrupts this process, leading to epiphora, recurrent infection, and ocular discomfort. Unlike secondary obstruction, which can arise from trauma, infection, neoplasms, or mechanical obstruction such as dacryolithiasis, PANDO is characterized by chronic, idiopathic inflammation and fibrosis of the nasolacrimal duct. Its etiology is thought to be multifactorial, involving anatomical, hormonal, microbial, and environmental contributors.6 Epidemiologic studies indicate that PANDO is more common in women and typically presents in the seventh decade of life.7,8 Significant associations have been reported with chronic conjunctivitis, vernal keratoconjunctivitis, and blepharitis, as well as, to a lesser degree, with systemic atopic disease.9 Gastroesophageal reflux disease has also been identified as a risk factor, particularly among women over the age of 60.7 In contrast, male sex and smoking have been associated with dacryolith formation.10
PANDO is increasingly viewed not only as a mechanical outflow disorder but also as a chronic inflammatory-fibrotic disease.6 Elemental and ionic imbalances may contribute to this process by disrupting key tear film functions. Disruption of the ionic microenvironment may promote epithelial stress, cytokine and matrix metalloproteinase activation, and extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby contributing to progressive nasolacrimal duct stenosis.11–13 Although previous studies have reported alterations in tear pH, calcium concentration, protein fractions, and cytokines, all profiles in PANDO, comprehensive multi-element profiling remains limited.12–14 The aim of this study was to characterize and compare the elemental profiles of tears and blood in patients with PANDO and matched controls using PIXE.

## Methods
This prospective observational comparative study was conducted at a tertiary medical center between 2021 and 2022. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, and written informed consent was secured from all participants. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The cohort consisted of adults with unilateral or bilateral PANDO and an equal number of healthy sex- and age-matched control subjects without clinical symptoms or signs of nasolacrimal duct obstruction. The diagnosis was confirmed by clinical examination and lacrimal irrigation. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy; inability to provide informed consent; recent ocular inflammation or infection (which was defined based on medical history and slit-lamp examination and included conditions such as dacryocystitis, blepharitis, conjunctivitis, keratitis, or other overt inflammatory findings that could independently affect tear composition, including clinically significant ocular surface disease such as dry eye); ocular surgery within the previous 3 months; and use of recent or ongoing ocular lubricants for dry eye. This study was designed as an exploratory pilot study to assess the feasibility of PIXE-based tear element profiling in PANDO. Therefore, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. Instead, we enrolled all eligible participants who met the inclusion criteria during the study period. The final sample was comprised of 15 patients with PANDO and 15 matched controls and was intended to provide preliminary data on feasibility, variability, and potential biological signal rather than definitive statistical power for all comparisons.
For all participants, tear samples were collected separately from each eye using Schirmer strips. A fingertip blood sample was obtained using the the same absorbent strip material. All samples were anonymized, coded, and transported for PIXE. For each element, concentrations measured as zero were treated as missing values to account for possible detection limits or technical artifacts.
Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using JASP 0.19.3 and MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Elemental concentrations recorded as zero were treated as missing values to account for possible detection limits or technical artifacts. For intra-individual comparisons in unilateral PANDO, only elements with at least three valid paired measurements from affected and unaffected eyes were analyzed. The normality of paired differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the distribution, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied. Inter-group comparisons of tear and blood samples were conducted using independent two-sample t-tests with Welch correction for unequal variances. Both eyes from healthy control subjects were included in the control group; in the affected-eye group, both affected eyes of bilateral PANDO patients (n = 2) were included. To evaluate whether the inclusion of both eyes from bilateral PANDO cases influenced the findings, an additional analysis was performed comparing the affected eyes from bilateral cases with those from unilateral PANDO cases. Paired comparison between the right and left eyes of healthy controls was also performed to assess inter-eye differences. In patients with unilateral PANDO, affected and unaffected eyes were analyzed separately.
A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied to all statistical analyses. For multivariate analysis, elements with more than 50% missing values were excluded. The remaining missing values were removed before processing. Data were normalized to the total element signal within each sample and log-transformed. Auto-scaling was performed by mean-centering each variable and dividing by its standard deviation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for unsupervised exploration of data structure, and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was used for supervised group separation. Hierarchical clustering with heatmap visualization was applied to evaluate elemental profile patterns across individuals. In addition, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed to assess overall differences in elemental composition between groups.

## Results
A total of 60 tear samples and 30 blood samples were collected from 30 participants: 15 with PANDO and 15 healthy controls. Patient demographics and elemental characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The groups were demographically comparable, with a predominant female majority (14/15 each, 93.33%). Mean age was 57.29 ± 15.35 years (range, 29–83) in the PANDO group and 53.27 ± 14.89 years (range, 26–78) in the control group. Among the patients with PANDO, 13 had unilateral obstruction, and two had bilateral obstruction.

Twelve different elements were identified in tear samples, and 11 in blood samples, with magnesium (Mg) detected only in tears. Measurements below the detection limit of the assay were twice more common in tears than in blood, particularly for trace elements such as aluminum (Al), Mg, and Zn. In patients with unilateral PANDO (n = 13), intra-individual comparisons of tear elemental measurements were made between the affected and unaffected sides. Paired statistical tests were performed for each element with sufficient data (≥3 matched pairs); the remainder—namely, Mg, Al, copper (Cu), and silicon (Si)—were excluded from the analysis. The results are shown in Figure 1. No statistically significant differences were found between the affected and unaffected sides for any element (Table 2). In the sensitivity analysis, the four affected eyes from the two bilateral PANDO cases did not differ significantly from the affected eyes of unilateral PANDO cases for all measured tear elements (all P > 0.05), and the overall multivariate profile was likewise not significantly different (PERMANOVA P = 0.75). Similarly, paired analysis in healthy controls showed no significant differences between right and left eyes. Accordingly, both eyes from bilateral PANDO cases and both eyes from healthy controls were retained in the between-group tear analyses.

Inter-group comparisons of tear samples from affected eyes in the PANDO group and control eyes (Table 3; Fig. 1) revealed statistically significant differences in sodium (Na; P = 0.003) and chloride (Cl; P = 0.031). There were no statistically significant differences in phosphorus (P), sulfur (S), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), and Zn. Similarly, comparison of tear samples from unaffected eyes in the PANDO group versus control eyes yielded significant differences in Na (P = 0.001) and Cl (P = 0.033). There was also a significant difference in P (P = 0.021) and S (P = 0.041). On blood analysis comparing the PANDO and control groups, a statistically significant difference was found only for Cu (P = 0.034). Si could not be statistically analyzed due to insufficient data points.

Multivariate analysis revealed distinct elemental composition profiles in tear samples among the examined groups (Figs. 2a–c). On PERMANOVA, there were statistically significant differences between the control eyes and each PANDO subgroup: eyes affected with PANDO (P = 0.0285) and contralateral (unaffected) eyes of patients with unilateral PANDO (P = 0.027). However, no significant difference was observed between the two eyes of patients with unilateral PANDO (P = 0.512). In the variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis, Na had the highest score, highlighting its key role in distinguishing PANDO groups from controls. The PCA score plot supported this finding, demonstrating considerable overlap among groups. Similarly, the heatmap showed mixed clustering patterns. By contrast, elemental analysis of blood samples (Fig. 3) did not yield significant differences between groups (P = 0.841), suggesting that the systemic elemental profiles in blood lacked the power to discriminate between PANDO and control groups, unlike tear sample composition.

## Discussion
In this study, we explored differences in the trace element composition of tears and blood between patients diagnosed with PANDO and healthy controls. We utilized PIXE, which can simultaneously detect multiple trace elements with limited interference from proteins or contaminants. Furthermore, its non-destructive nature and ability to accommodate small sample volumes are particularly beneficial for analysis of tears collected with Schirmer strips.15
A consistent signal emerged in tears, particularly for Na, whereas blood showed minimal separation between groups. Aside from elevated Cu levels, no significant differences in elemental composition were observed in blood samples between patients and controls. Multivariate analyses, including hierarchical clustering, showed much less distinct group separation for blood samples than for tear samples. Among tear samples, Na levels were consistently higher in those from both affected and unaffected PANDO eyes than in control eyes, with no meaningful intra-individual differences. Univariate analysis also showed a parallel increase in Cl and elevations in P and S in samples from unaffected eyes. However, these additional changes did not persist in multivariate models.
We found a bilaterally symmetric pattern, despite clinically unilateral disease, which mirrors previous studies of magnetic resonance imaging findings in unilateral PANDO, in which both lacrimal glands showed increased water fraction compared with controls, with no significant differences between the obstructed and non-obstructed sides.16 This concept is further supported by epidemiologic data from one study that found that the mean age at diagnosis of PANDO was approximately 7 years earlier in patients with unilateral than with bilateral disease.17 Moreover, in the same study, objective evidence of obstruction was identified in 90 out of 124 patients who presented with unilateral symptomatic PANDO.17
To approximate the risk of fellow-eye involvement in PANDO, which has never been directly quantified in a population-based study, we used published epidemiologic data. We used a crude odds-ratio framework, treating bilateral PANDO as the outcome and pre-existing unilateral PANDO as the exposure. In one study, the incidence of PANDO in Olmsted County, Minnesota, from 1976 to 2000 was 30.47 per 100,000 population.18 In another study, fewer than 10% of patients who underwent dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) for unilateral obstruction required DCR in the contralateral eye during 5.58 years of follow-up.19 In addition, the reported ratio of bilateral to unilateral PANDO is approximately 1:5.5.20 Taken together, these figures suggest that, when PANDO is present in one eye, the risk of obstruction in the fellow eye is dramatically increased compared with the background population, likely exceeding a 10- to even 100-fold, with potential risks reaching several hundred-fold. That said, the true magnitude of this association and the causative relationship between risk factors and the disease should be clarified through prospective studies and meta-analytic investigations.
The pathophysiological basis for elevated Na levels in tears of patients with PANDO may involve several factors. Animal models suggest that obstruction of the tear drainage system may disrupt the lacrimal gland secretory function, leading to altered tear composition.21 Inflammation is both a key contributor to the pathophysiology of PANDO6,9,11 and a consequence of the obstruction itself.12 Inflammatory processes can increase vascular permeability, leading to the leakage of plasma components such as albumin into the tear film.22 Furthermore, Na can induce inflammation independently through hyperosmotic mechanisms23 and by possible immune modulation.24 It is noteworthy that elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, are commonly detected in the tears of patients with various forms of lacrimal duct obstruction.11–13,25 Na and Cl are key ions involved in maintaining the osmotic gradient, and their concentrations often change in parallel due to coupled transport mechanisms. Therefore, their simultaneous elevation is not unexpected.26 In experimental and clinical dry eye models, hyperosmolar ionic stress activates mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-/nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-related inflammatory pathways, increases cytokine and matrix metalloproteinase activity, and contributes to epithelial barrier disruption.26–28 Similar mechanisms may be relevant in PANDO, where chronic inflammation, cytokine imbalance, and mucosal remodeling are already implicated in disease progression. Therefore, the bilateral elevation of Na observed in our cohort may reflect a disturbed lacrimal functional-unit environment that could amplify epithelial inflammation and contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling and progressive nasolacrimal duct stenosis. Large case–control studies have demonstrated that dry eye is associated with an increased risk of PANDO (odds ratio = 1.43). Still, they did not address the possible effect of dry eye treatment on the incidence or natural history of PANDO.29
Contrary to our findings, previous studies of tear composition in patients with PANDO and dacryoliths have reported significantly elevated Ca and K concentrations, rather than Na and Cl.14,30 This discrepancy may be explained by the different methods used, as measurements made with ion-selective electrode systems can lead to an overestimation of ionized Ca concentrations in the presence of increased protein content in the sample.31 Notably, an analysis of dacryolith composition revealed a heterogeneous mixture of organic materials, with approximately one-fifth consisting of various amino acids.32 In the same study, nearly all samples contained Si and Mg. At the same time, Ca and P were relatively more abundant than Na and Cl.32 In contrast, PIXE enables simultaneous, multi-element analysis with minimal sample preparation and without chemical manipulation, thereby reducing matrix-related bias and capturing total elemental content across a broader spectrum. Accordingly, in our analysis, there were no significant differences in Mg and Ca between the groups. Moreover, elevated P and S levels were unique to patients’ unaffected eyes compared with controls. Thus, this increase may reflect a shift in protein and cytokine content, a finding previously documented in the literature.12–14,22 Although the reason for the increased P levels remains unclear, phosphorus-containing compounds such as dinucleoside polyphosphates are known to support ocular surface health by stimulating tear secretion, promoting mucin release, enhancing epithelial healing, and upregulating antimicrobial proteins, potentially indicating a compensatory or protective response.33 Thus, this may reflect changes in tear protein, mucin, or cytokine composition rather than isolated inorganic ion abnormalities.
Considering the evidence, it is plausible that an underlying, elusive pathophysiological process in PANDO initiates a self-perpetuating negative feedback loop. Specifically, a primary reduction in lacrimal gland output leads to insufficient aqueous tear production, which in turn increases the osmolarity of the tear film, stressing the ocular surface epithelium and activating inflammatory cascades (Fig. 4). The sustained inflammation may not only worsen ocular surface damage but also contribute to downstream obstruction of the nasolacrimal duct system. Early intervention with artificial tears may help restore tear film homeostasis, interrupt the self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation, and alleviate symptoms. Long-term, randomized controlled trials are necessary to establish the efficacy and durability of such treatments in modifying disease progression.
Proposed self-perpetuating cycle in PANDO. An initial decrease in lacrimal gland secretion may lower the aqueous tear component and alter tear ionic composition, including increased Na and Cl, thereby increasing tear osmolarity. These changes may promote ocular surface epithelial stress and inflammatory signaling. Chronic inflammation may propagate to the lacrimal drainage apparatus and facilitate nasolacrimal duct obstruction, establishing a self-amplifying loop that may be mitigated by tear supplementation.
There are several limitations to this study that should be considered. First, the relatively small sample sizes and limited number of valid paired measurements might have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle intra-individual differences. Second, the study population was predominantly female, which may affect gender representation and restrict the broader applicability of the findings, even though sex is an accepted risk factor for PANDO.7,9 A further limitation is that systemic comorbidities and use of systemic medications were not systematically incorporated into the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Because such factors may influence elemental composition, residual systemic confounding cannot be excluded, even though blood elemental profiles showed far less separation between groups than tear samples. Finally, many of the elements measured in this study either were undetectable or exhibited sparse and widely variable concentrations. Consequently, they were excluded from the statistical analysis, and their potential roles warrant further investigation. To date, there is a significant knowledge gap concerning the composition of tears in both healthy and diseased states.

## Conclusions
This study demonstrates that patients with PANDO exhibit distinct tear elemental profiles, notably elevated Na levels in both eyes, regardless of symptomatic status, suggesting bilateral involvement. These findings suggest that tear composition is altered bilaterally in patients with PANDO, including in clinically unaffected eyes, raising the possibility that biochemical changes may occur before overt obstruction becomes apparent. Although this points to a potential role for tear elemental analysis in studying early disease processes, its utility for detecting subclinical disease or identifying at-risk individuals remains unproven and requires validation in prospective longitudinal studies. Importantly, this raises the possibility that early, simple interventions such as regular use of artificial tears could help restore tear film homeostasis, potentially interrupting progression of the disease before it becomes symptomatic.
