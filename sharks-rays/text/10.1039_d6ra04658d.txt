Enhancing broadband electromagnetic wave absorption performance of NZFO/BSTO multiferroic materials in the X and K<sub>u</sub> bands.

# Enhancing broadband electromagnetic wave absorption performance of NZFO/BSTO multiferroic materials in the X and Ku bands

## Abstract
AbstractMultiferroic composites with the composition xNi0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4/(1 − x)(Ba0.8Sr0.2TiO3) (denoted as NZFO/BSTO) (where x = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 1.0) were fabricated using a solid-state reaction method combined with spark plasma sintering. The obtained materials exhibited an average particle size of approximately 500 nm. X-ray diffraction analysis confirms the co-existence of the ferromagnetic Ni0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4 (NZFO) phase and the ferroelectric Ba0.8Sr0.2TiO3 (BSTO) phase. As the NZFO content increases from x = 0.1 to 0.4, the saturation magnetization (Ms) rises from 13.6 to 56.9 emu g−1, while the remanent magnetization (Mr) increases from 0.71 to 1.88 emu g−1. Simultaneously, the P–E hysteresis loops become more pronounced, with a significant increase in both the maximum polarization (Pm) and remanent polarization (Pr). The coercive electric field (Ec) also increases markedly from 1.94 kV cm−1 to 3.56 kV cm−1 under an applied electric field of 12.5 kV cm−1. The composite with the ferromagnetic phase fraction x = 0.4 exhibits excellent broadband electromagnetic wave absorption performance. At thicknesses of 2.0 mm and 2.5 mm, the effective absorption bandwidth exceeds 5.4 GHz and 5.7 GHz, respectively. Notably, minimum reflection loss (RLmin) values of −48.3 dB and −43.9 dB are achieved at 15.7 GHz and 12.6 GHz, respectively. These results demonstrate the strong potential of the material for practical applications in electromagnetic wave absorption in the X-band and Ku-band frequency ranges, particularly in radar stealth and military technologies.

## Introduction
Multiferroic materials, characterized by the coexistence of ferroelectric and ferromagnetic orders, have attracted considerable attention in materials science due to their multifunctional properties.1–5 Their importance arises from the magnetoelectric (ME) coupling effect, which enables mutual control between electric polarization and magnetic fields via strain-mediated interactions.6–11 In the microwave frequency range of 2–18 GHz, covering radar bands from S to Ku, multiferroic materials exhibit significant potential for electromagnetic (EM) wave absorption. This performance originates from the synergistic contribution of dielectric and magnetic loss mechanisms, enabling the development of lightweight, thin, and tunable absorbers for stealth technology and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding applications.12–14 Barium titanate (BaTiO3) is a typical ferroelectric material with a perovskite crystal structure, known for its high dielectric constant and strong ferroelectric polarization, which contribute to effective dielectric loss. However, pure BaTiO3 presents several limitations, including a relatively high Curie temperature (TC ≈ 130 °C) and restricted dielectric response at room temperature.3,15,16 In microwave absorption applications, BaTiO3 generally exhibits moderate dielectric loss, with reflection loss (RL) values typically in the range of −10 to −15 dB in the X-band (8–12 GHz) at optimal thicknesses.16,17 The absorption mechanism is mainly attributed to dielectric relaxation and interfacial polarization effects.17–19 To address these limitations, partial substitution of Ba2+ by Sr2+ to form Ba0.8Sr0.2TiO3 (BSTO) has been widely adopted as an effective approach.20,21 This modification reduces the Curie temperature to approximately 80–100 °C, bringing the ferroelectric phase transition closer to room temperature, while enhancing the dielectric constant and remanent polarization.20,21 Consequently, more symmetric P–E hysteresis loops and improved dielectric loss behavior are achieved, making BSTO a suitable matrix for tunable microwave absorption in the 2–18 GHz frequency range.6,7,20
To further improve electromagnetic wave absorption performance and broaden the effective bandwidth, the incorporation of a ferromagnetic phase into the composite system has been demonstrated as a promising strategy.22,23 This approach enhances impedance matching and absorption efficiency through the combined effects of dielectric loss, magnetic loss, and interfacial polarization at the phase boundaries between the ferroelectric and ferromagnetic components.24,25 Among ferromagnetic materials with high magnetic permeability, Nickel Ferrite (NiFe2O4, NFO) is a typical inverse spinel ferrite with a cubic structure. In this structure, Fe3+ ions are distributed over both tetrahedral (A) and octahedral (B) sites, while Ni2+ ions preferentially occupy the B sites.26–30 This cation distribution leads to the establishment of ferromagnetic ordering through strong A–B superexchange interactions.31–33 However, the microwave absorption performance of pure NiFe2O4 remains relatively moderate, with reported reflection loss (RL) values typically ranging from −5 to −10 dB in the Ku-band (12–18 GHz) at thicknesses exceeding 3 mm. This limitation is mainly attributed to relatively low magnetic permeability and the presence of eddy current losses, despite contributions from magnetic resonance mechanisms.34,35
Recent studies have demonstrated that improving the dielectric properties of ferrites is an effective strategy to enhance intrinsic impedance matching. In this context, Zn2+ substitution for Ni2+ in NFO has been widely adopted to tailor both magnetic and dielectric properties.36–38 Among these materials, NZFO has attracted significant attention due to its optimized electromagnetic characteristics.39,40 Upon Zn2+ substitution, the crystal structure evolves from an inverse spinel toward a mixed spinel configuration. Non-magnetic Zn2+ ions preferentially occupy the tetrahedral A sites, forcing a larger fraction of Fe3+ ions to migrate to the octahedral B sites.39–42 This cation redistribution enhances A–B superexchange, resulting in a substantial increase in saturation magnetization, typically reaching 70–80 emu g−1.40,41 In terms of microwave absorption performance, nano-sized NZFO particles exhibit significantly improved properties compared to pure NFO, with reflection loss (RL) values of −20 to −30 dB reported in the C-band (4–8 GHz) at thicknesses of approximately 2–4 mm, and effective absorption bandwidths up to 5–6 GHz (RL < −10 dB). This enhancement is attributed to the combined effects of dual magnetic resonance and improved impedance matching.41,42 Building upon the intrinsic properties of the aforementioned materials, integration of the ferromagnetic NZFO phase with the ferroelectric BSTO phase to form multiferroic composites with the general formula xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO represents a promising strategy for achieving broadband and high-performance electromagnetic wave absorption. In such composites, the ferroelectric BSTO phase provides high dielectric loss (DL) and flexible tunability (FT), while the ferromagnetic NZFO phase contributes significant magnetic loss (ML). More importantly, the effective coupling between these two phases lead to enhanced magnetoelectric (ME) interactions mediated by interfacial strain. This synergistic effect improves overall energy dissipation, promotes impedance matching, and significantly enhances electromagnetic wave absorption performance.21–25 Recent advances in composite fabrication techniques have demonstrated that spark plasma sintering (SPS) is a highly effective method for producing dense bulk materials with refined microstructures. SPS enables rapid densification while preserving grain sizes in the nano or submicron range, which is crucial for maintaining the intrinsic magnetic and dielectric properties of multiferroic materials.43–48 In contrast, conventional high-temperature sintering often leads to excessive grain growth and degradation of functional properties.26 SPS-fabricated materials typically exhibit highly uniform microstructures and high relative densities (approximately 97–98%),8,9 which significantly reduce porosity.43–47 The reduction of pores, which act as low-dielectric regions, contributes to an increase in the effective dielectric constant.8 Furthermore, high density also improves electromagnetic impedance matching, which is a key factor governing microwave absorption performance.8,10 Another advantage of SPS is the significantly reduced processing temperature (by approximately 200–525 °C) and extremely short holding times (typically 0–10 minutes) compared to conventional ceramic processing methods.26–34,43–48 This conditions not only improve energy efficiency but also suppress undesirable side reactions and thermal decomposition of metastable multiferroic phases. Moreover, SPS-derived materials (such as manganese ferrites) exhibit enhanced DL and ML due to improved dipolar polarization, interfacial polarization, and microstructural anisotropy,26–34 all of which contribute to stronger reflection loss (RL) and broader effective absorption bandwidths.48–53 The rapid sintering process also limits the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+, thereby reducing excessive electrical conductivity and preserving dielectric stability and breakdown strength.26 In addition, during reactive SPS processing, the simultaneous application of uniaxial pressure and pulsed electric current can induce magnetic anisotropy, enhancing magnetostrictive properties and magnetoelectric coupling coefficients. This further improves the multifunctional performance of multiferroic composites for advanced electromagnetic applications.26–34,43–46 Based on the fundamental characteristics of ferroelectric, ferromagnetic materials, as well as advances in composite fabrication techniques, multiferroic composites with the composition xNi0.5Zn0.5Fe2O4/(1 − x)Ba0.8Sr0.2TiO3 (denoted as NZFO/BSTO) (where x = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 1.0) were successfully synthesized in this study using a solid-state reaction method combined with SPS. While BSTO offers high dielectric loss together with good tunability near room temperature, NZFO contributes notable magnetic loss. Nevertheless, achieving pronounced synergistic effects in such multiferroic composites remains challenging, primarily due to the significant disparity in the optimal sintering temperatures of the ferroelectric and magnetic phases. This incompatibility frequently leads to interfacial defects or undesired secondary phases during conventional processing. In the present study, Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS) was employed as an effective approach to overcome this limitation.
The integration of ferroelectric and ferromagnetic phases is expected to enhance both dielectric and magnetic properties through synergistic effects, thereby improving electromagnetic wave absorption performance. In particular, the composites are designed to achieve broadened absorption bandwidth and enhanced absorption efficiency in the 2–18 GHz frequency range. These characteristics demonstrate the strong potential of the developed materials for practical applications in high-efficiency electromagnetic wave absorption, especially within the X-band and Ku-band frequency ranges.

## Experiment
The multiferroic xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO composites, with x = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4, were synthesized using a combination of the conventional solid-state reaction method and SPS. The starting materials included high-purity powders 99.9% of BaCO3, SrCO3, TiO2, NiO, ZnO and Fe2O3. Initially, the ferroelectric BSTO and ferrimagnetic NZFO phases were prepared separately. The raw precursor powders for each phase were ground and mixed in an agate mortar for 2 hours in air. Following homogenization, the mixtures were pressed into pellets under a pressure of 7000 kg cm−2. These pellets were then calcined in air for 5 hours at 1250 °C for the BSTO samples and 1000 °C for the NZFO samples, both using a heating rate of 5 °C min−1, followed by furnace cooling to room temperature.
In the subsequent step, the pre-synthesized BSTO and NZFO were finely reground and blended into xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO composite powders. The constituent phases were weighed according to their respective molar ratios for x = 0.1–0.4, ensuring a total mass of 5 g for each composition. These mixtures were further homogenized in an agate mortar for 1 hour. The final stage involved SPS of the composite powders using a Sinterland Labbox 350 system. Approximately 2 g of each powder mixture was loaded into a 10 mm diameter graphite die and sintered at 700 °C for 30 minutes under a uniaxial pressure of 40 MPa and a vacuum environment, with a heating rate of 100 °C min−1. The resulting products were uniform solid pellets with a diameter of 12 mm and a thickness of 1 mm.
The structural and functional characteristics of the synthesized materials were then rigorously evaluated: (i) the crystal structure of all samples was analyzed via X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Thermo Scientific Equinox 5000 diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation λ = 1.54056 Å; (ii) Raman spectra were recorded at room temperature using a LabSpec 6 HORIBA (Japan) spectrometer; (iii) surface morphology and grain size were determined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) using a Hitachi S4800 system; (iv) elemental composition and chemical distribution were analyzed through Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) using an Oxford Instruments Aztec system; (v) analysis of the chemical binding energies of elements using the Thermo Scientific™ Nexsa G2™ X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) System; (vi) typical magnetic and ferroelectric properties were assessed via M(H) and P(E) hysteresis loops, measured at room temperature using a VersaLab Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (Quantum Design) and a Precision LC II Model 609 system. For the P(E) measurements, an external voltage of 1.25 kV was applied, corresponding to an electric field of 12.5 kV cm−1; (vii) to investigate microwave absorption properties, S-parameters were measured using a Vector Network Analyzer (VNA, PNA-X, Keysight). For this measurement, the final composite powder was mixed with paraffin in a 40 : 60 volume ratio and pressed into toroidal specimens (inner diameter: 3.04 mm, outer diameter 7 mm). The complex permittivity and permeability were extracted using the Nicolson–Ross–Weir (NRW) method, and the microwave absorption performance was subsequently calculated based on transmission line theory.

## Result and discussion
The room-temperature X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of all samples are presented in Fig. 1a. The results show that the sample with x = 0 exhibits a single-phase BSTO with a tetragonal crystal structure, as confirmed by the characteristic diffraction peaks indexed to the (hkl) planes of (100), (110), (111), (002), (210), (211), and (220). For the composite samples (x ≠ 0), additional diffraction peaks corresponding to the cubic NZFO phase (marked with asterisks *) are observed alongside those of BSTO indicating the coexistence of both phases. Notably, no significant peak shift or formation of impurity phases is detected. These results confirm that BSTO and NZFO coexist as distinct phases within the composites, and that the SPS process does not induce the formation of new secondary phases. Furthermore, the particle size remained consistent, regardless of the sample composition or the content of NZFO. To verify the presence and distribution of elements in the BSTO sample, specifically Ba, Sr, Ti, and O, as well as Ba, Sr, Ti, Ni, Zn, Fe, and O in the composite samples, we conducted elemental mapping analysis, as shown in Fig. 2a and b. The results indicate that all components are evenly distributed throughout the material. This finding confirms that the multiferroic composites have been fabricated, with the ferromagnetic and ferroelectric components thoroughly mixed and uniformly dispersed within the samples. Since the composites are primarily based on the BaTiO3 (BTO)-type perovskite structure, their Raman spectra exhibit characteristic vibrational modes similar to those of pure BTO. The band assignments were performed based on previously reported data for BTO. The characteristic modes corresponding to [E(LO), A1(LO), E(TO)], [E(TO), A1(TO)], and [E(LO), A1(LO)] are observed at approximately 436.32, 523.32, and 721.11 cm−1, respectively, for all samples, confirming the presence of the tetragonal phase. With increasing NZFO content, the intensities of the Raman peaks at 523.32 and 721.11 cm−1 decrease noticeably and exhibit peak broadening. In addition, the Raman bands at 171.61, 523.32, and 721.11 cm−1 shift slightly toward lower wavenumbers (163.52, 509.9, and 693.8 cm−1, respectively). This red shift can be attributed to lattice distortions and local structural fluctuations within the perovskite framework. Although the ferroelectric BSTO and ferrimagnetic NZFO phases coexist without any chemical reaction,20,39 interfacial electromagnetic interactions between the two phases can induce subtle structural perturbations. The Raman spectra provide important evidence of the critical role of spark plasma sintering (SPS) in the fabrication of NZFO/BSTO multiferroic composites. The use of SPS at a relatively low temperature (700 °C) effectively preserved the characteristic crystal structures of both phases, as evidenced by the typical vibrational modes of BSTO at approximately 436, 523, and 721 cm−1. Simultaneously, with increasing NZFO content, the main peaks of the BSTO phase showed a significant decrease in intensity, substantial broadening, and a clear red shift (523 → 509 cm−1 and 721 → 693 cm−1). The observed red shift and broadening of the characteristic Raman modes (523.32 → 509.9 cm−1 and 721.11 → 693.8 cm−1) with increasing NZFO content indicate significant lattice distortions and local structural fluctuations within the perovskite BSTO framework. These perturbations, with a magnitude of Δω ≈ 13–27 cm−1, reflect changes in Ti–O bond lengths and TiO6 octahedral tilting induced by interfacial strain and lattice mismatch at the phase boundaries. Notably, these distortions are clearly revealed by Raman spectroscopy due to its high sensitivity to short-range structural coherence and phonon vibrations, whereas the XRD patterns (Fig. 1a) remain relatively unchanged. This is because XRD probes the long-range average crystalline structure, which may preserve its macroscopic symmetry despite localized interfacial stresses. This differential sensitivity between the two techniques provides robust evidence of strong interfacial interactions and strain-mediated coupling in the NZFO/BSTO composites, which are critical for optimizing the dual-loss mechanism for electromagnetic wave attenuation. These changes indicate that SPS produced composites with high-quality phase boundaries, enabling strong interactions between the two phases in the form of lattice distortion and strain. By precisely controlling the sintering process, SPS not only suppressed the formation of secondary phases but also maintained a fine grain size (∼500 nm), thereby increasing the density of interfaces and promoting strain-mediated coupling. As a result, SPS plays a decisive role in generating strong electromagnetic interactions at the phase boundaries, leading to the optimization of the Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization and the dual-loss mechanism, which significantly contribute to the superior electromagnetic wave absorption performance of the BSNZF4 composite. This behavior serves as an important indication of interfacial coupling effects in the multiferroic composites. The morphological characteristics and grain sizes distribution of the samples were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Representative SEM images of four typical samples, namely BSTO, NZFO, BSNZF1, and BSNZF4, are shown in Fig. 2a–d. Overall, the samples exhibit a relatively non-uniform grain distribution, with grain sizes ranging from several tens to several hundreds of nanometers. A notable feature is that under the high-pressure conditions of SPS, the grain boundaries appear densely compacted, which is particularly evident in the BSTO sample. In the composite samples, this feature is less distinct due to the adhesion of finer particles onto the surfaces of larger grains. In addition, the grains are randomly oriented, likely due to various internal interaction forces during the sintering process. Quantitative analysis performed using the integrated software of the Hitachi S4800 FE-SEM system indicates that the average grain size (D) is approximately 500 nm, with a relatively broad size distribution. Due to the inherently low electrical conductivity of the ceramic materials, grain boundaries appear less distinct, leading to partial agglomeration into clusters. Notably, the grain size remains relatively stable and does not exhibit significant variation with changes in composition or NZFO content. Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis, as presented in Fig. 2e–h, confirmed the elemental composition of the samples. The BSTO sample contains Ba, Sr, Ti, and O, while the NZFO sample consists of Ni, Zn, Fe, and O. For the BSNZF1 and BSNZF4 composites, the simultaneous presence of elements from both contituent phases in near-stoichiometric proportions confirm the successful formation of the composite structure. Furthermore, no evidence of secondary or impurity phases is detected, indicating high compositional purity of the synthesized materials. The elemental compositions determined by EDX (Fig. 2e–h) and mapping (Fig. 3) are close to the nominal stoichiometry, with minor deviations including slight oxygen deficiencies and local Fe enrichment in the NZFO phase. These features are commonly observed in multiferroic composites prepared via solid-state reaction and SPS. The oxygen vacancies (VO), formed under vacuum-assisted sintering, play a constructive role by acting as polarization centers that facilitate Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization at the phase boundaries. This enhancement of interfacial effects is critical for optimizing electromagnetic energy attenuation. Simultaneously, the slight Fe enrichment, likely due to minor Zn volatilization at high temperatures, promotes the migration of Fe3+ ions to octahedral sites, thereby strengthening the A–B superexchange interactions and enhancing the saturation magnetization (Ms). The absence of any secondary phases in the XRD patterns (Fig. 1a) further confirms that these non-stoichiometric features are intrinsic to the pure NZFO/BSTO phases and contribute positively to the material's functional synergy. Fig. 3 presents the EDX elemental mapping images of representative samples: (a) BSTO, (b) NZFO, (c) BSNZF1, and (d) BSNZF4. The results clearly demonstrate a homogeneous distribution of constituent elements throughout all samples, indicating good compositional uniformity and effective phase integration within the composites. The magnetic properties of the samples at room temperature are illustrated by the M(H) hysteresis loops show in Fig. 4. The single-phase Ba0.8Sr0.2TiO3 (BSTO) sample exhibits paramagnetic behavior with negligible magnetization. In contrast, all composite samples display well-defined ferromagnetic characteristics, confirming the contribution of the (NZFO) phase to the overall magnetic response of the materials. As the NZFO content increases from x = 0.1 to 0.4, the magnetization properties of the NZFO/BSTO composites are significantly enhanced. Specifically, the Ms increases from 13.6 to 56.9 emu g−1, while the remanent magnetization (Mr) rises from 0.71 to 1.88 emu g−1. This improvement is consistent with previous studies on spinel ferrite systems, where the magnetic response is primarily governed by the ferrite phase.21,24 In contrast, the coercive field (Hc) exhibits a decreasing trend, declining from 65 Oe to 48.2 Oe with increasing NZFO content. Although the relationship between ferrite concentration and coercivity can be complex, the observed reduction in Hc can be attributed to two main factors. First, the increased proportion of the ferromagnetic phase facilitates easier domain wall motion, thereby reducing resistance to magnetization reversal.9 Second, as the NZFO content increases (and the BSTO phase correspondingly decreases), the number of non-magnetic ferroelectric grains acting as pinning centers is reduced. This weakens domain wall pinning effects and leads to lower coercivity values.6,18,21,24 To investigate the influence of the ferrimagnetic NZFO phase on the optical band gap of NZFO/BSTO multiferroic composites, UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy was employed. The corresponding spectra are presented in Fig. 5a, showing that the absorption intensity gradually increases and the absorption edge shifts toward longer wavelengths (redshift) with increasing NZFO content. The optical band gap (Eg) values were estimated using the Tauc plot method, as illustrated in Fig. 5b. The results reveal a significant reduction in Eg from 3.20 eV to 1.85 eV as x increases from 0 to 0.4. This band gap narrowing can be attributed to several factors related to the electronic structure of the composite system. In the NZFO/BSTO composites, Ni2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+ ions coexist and contribute to the modification of the electronic band structure. The partially filled d-orbitals of Ni2+ and Fe3+ are split into t2g and eg states, while Zn2+ ions, with a closed d10 configuration, preferentially occupy tetrahedral (A) sites in the spinel lattice. This preferential site occupancy induces cation redistribution, driving more Fe3+ ions to octahedral (B) sites. As a result, the superexchange interactions are altered, leading to modifications in the electronic band structure and a reduction in the band gap.36–38 Furthermore, the valence band (VB) maximum of NZFO is higher than that of BSTO, while the conduction band (CB) minimum is lower, resulting in the formation of an effective reduced band gap in the composite system. In addition, the intrinsic internal electric field in the ferroelectric BSTO phase, combined with the introduction of transition-metal d-states from the NZFO phase near the Fermi level, facilitates electronic transitions. This effectively lowers the excitation energy and enhances phonon-assisted absorption processes. Consequently, the absorption edge of the NZFO/BSTO composites shifts toward longer wavelengths compared to pure BSTO. This behavior may arise from electronic transitions from the valence band to intermediate energy levels associated with Ni2+ and Fe3+ ions, as well as from these states to the conduction band. The redistribution of cations, where Zn2+ substitution forces Fe3+ ions from tetrahedral A-sites to octahedral B-sites, significantly modifies the electronic environment of the NZFO phase. In octahedral coordination, the metal 3d orbitals split into t2g and eg levels. The intensified B–B superexchange interactions strengthen the p–d hybridization between the Fe/Ni 3d states and the O 2p orbitals, leading to a substantial broadening of the d-bands. This increased covalency, combined with the presence of oxygen vacancies that introduce mid-gap states, results in a dramatic reduction of the effective band gap (Eg = 3.20 → 1.85 eV). The upward shift of the valence band edge and the proliferation of intermediate states facilitate phonon-assisted electronic transitions, which are instrumental in enhancing the dual-loss mechanism. These modifications at the t2g and eg levels provide the physical basis for the superior electromagnetic energy attenuation observed in the BSNZF4 sample, as it enables a more responsive dielectric polarization under high-frequency stimuli.32,33,36

At x = 0.4, the band gap reaches its minimum value, indicating that the dielectric properties of the material can be more readily tuned under external optical or electromagnetic stimuli.11,49 The room-temperature ferroelectric P–E hysteresis loops of the xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO composites, measured under a maximum electric field of 12.5 kV cm−1 at 50 Hz, are presented in Fig. 6a–d. These results are clearly illustrated in the inset of Fig. 4 and 6 and summarized in Table 1. These research results are entirely consistent with prior studies on BaTiO3-based multiferroic composite material systems.6,10,11,15,21 In this multiferroic system, which combines the perovskite ferroelectric phase (BSTO) with the spinel ferrit phase (NZFO), increasing the NZFO content from x = 0 to 0.4 leads to pronounced modifications in ferroelectric behavior. Specifically, the P–E loops become progressively broader with increasing NZFO content, accompanied by a notable increase in both the maximum polarization (Pm) and remanent polarization (Pr), although full saturation is not reached at 12.5 kV cm−1. Meanwhile, the Ec increases significantly from 1.94 kV cm−1 to 3.56 kV cm−1, indicating an enhanced ferroelectric response and highlighting the crucial role of the NZFO phase in modulating the BSTO matrix. In conventional multiferroic composites, the incorporation of a ferrite phase often deteriorates intrinsic ferroelectric properties due to increased leakage current, dilution of the ferroelectric phase, and mechanical clamping effects that hinder domain switching. The observed increase in energy loss density (Wloss) with NZFO content (Fig. 6e) is consistent with enhanced leakage behavior. However, the simultaneous increase in Pm and Pr at moderate composition (x = 0.4) suggests that constructive interfacial effects outweigh these detrimental factors. Mechanisms such as strain-mediated coupling, where the magnetostrictive NZFO phase promotes domain switching in BSTO, and Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization due to charge accumulation at phase boundaries, are likely responsible for the enhanced polarization. The increase in Ec further reflects stronger domain wall pinning and increased resistance to polarization reversal. These ferroelectric characteristics have direct implications for electromagnetic (EM) wave absorption. The highest Ec value at x = 0.4 identifies the BSNZF4 sample as the most promising absorber. A high coercive field indicates strong domain wall pinning by defects and interfacial regions. Under an alternating EM field, the forced oscillation and reversal of pinned domain walls result in significant internal friction, facilitating efficient conversion of EM energy into heat.6 To further validate the ferroelectric behavior, the P–E hysteresis loops were carefully analyzed with respect to potential leakage current contributions. As shown in Fig. 6, the loops exhibit relatively symmetric shapes with clear coercive fields, suggesting that the measured polarization is primarily of ferroelectric origin. However, with increasing NZFO content, both the maximum polarization (Pm) and remanent polarization (Pr) increase, accompanied by a noticeable rise in the coercive field (Ec) from 1.94 to 3.56 kV cm−1 and higher energy loss density (Wloss). This trend indicates a moderate contribution from leakage current and conduction effects, which is commonly observed in ferrite–ferroelectric composites due to the lower resistivity of the NZFO phase and the presence of oxygen vacancies at the phase boundaries (as confirmed by XPS analysis). Despite this, the well-defined hysteresis behavior and the significant enhancement of Pr at x = 0.4 demonstrate that intrinsic ferroelectric polarization remains dominant. The observed increase in Ec further reflects domain wall pinning by defects and interfacial strain rather than purely conductive losses.

## The values of the characteristic parameters of NZFO/BSTO multiferroics, including Ps, Pr, Ec, Eg, Ms, Mr, and Hc
Moreover, synergistic coupling between the two phases further enhances absorption. The electric component of the EM wave induces deformation in the ferroelectric phase, which is transferred to the magnetic phase via interfacial coupling. This process strengthens magnetoelastic interactions and contributes to magnetic loss. The increase in Pr with NZFO content indirectly supports this mechanism, as higher remanent polarization correlates with stronger internal stress and enhanced coupling effects.3,4,6 Consequently, the elevated Ec helps sustain a strained state that intensifies magnetic loss, forming a “dual-loss mechanism” that significantly improves the overall absorption performance.5 The influence of the ferroelectric phase (BSTO) and the ferrimagnetic phase (NZFO) on the properties of the material was investigated in detail using XPS spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 7. The XPS spectra provide atomic-level evidence of changes in the chemical and electronic environment when NZFO is doped into BSTO. These changes form the foundation for the multiferroic coupling (ferroelectric–ferrimagnetic interaction) and the superior electromagnetic wave absorption performance observed in the BSNZF samples, particularly BSNZF4 (x = 0.4). Such modifications go beyond mere mechanical mixing and reflect strong electronic interactions at the phase interfaces, consistent with studies on ferrite–perovskite composites. The shift in Binding Energy (BE) of the Ba 3d5/2 peak from 782.18 eV (pure BSTO) to approximately 779.4 eV in BSNZF1 and BSNZF4 indicates an increase in electron density around Ba2+ ions, resulting from charge transfer from Fe3+, Ni2+, and Zn2+ ions in the NZFO phase. Simultaneously, the O 1s spectra demonstrate a significant increase in the proportion of oxygen vacancies (VO), evidenced by the stronger peak at ∼530–531 eV. These oxygen vacancies act as polarization centers and promote Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization at the perovskite–spinel phase boundaries.54 As a result, the P–E hysteresis loops become broader and more pronounced: Pm increases from 0.050 to 0.121 µC cm−2, Pr from 0.011 to 0.038 µC cm−2, and Ec from 1.94 to 3.56 kV cm−1 (at x = 0.4) under an applied field of 12.5 kV cm−1. The increase in Ec reflects stronger domain wall pinning by defects (VO) and interfacial strain, which is consistent with the findings of Qin et al. on the NZFO/BSTO system, where interfacial strain-mediated coupling enhances the ferroelectric response.55 The observed enhancement in Pm and Pr with increasing NZFO content indicates that the constructive interfacial effects particularly Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization and strain-mediated magnetoelectric coupling predominate over the adverse influence of increased conductivity. As evidenced by XPS analysis (Fig. 7), the elevated concentration of oxygen vacancies at the perovskite–spinel interfaces generates mid-gap states that effectively trap charge carriers, thereby promoting interfacial polarization. Concurrently, the increase in Ec signifies stronger domain wall pinning induced by these defects and interfacial strain, a behavior also reported in rare-earth-doped BST/ferrite composites. This retained ferroelectric response is strategically exploited to optimize the dual-loss mechanism, wherein the synergistic interaction between the piezoelectric and magnetostrictive phases enhances energy dissipation, contributing to superior electromagnetic wave attenuation performance. The utilization of low-temperature SPS (700 °C) plays a decisive role in suppressing the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+. In spinel ferrites, this reductive process often triggered by high-temperature oxygen loss releases free electron carriers (2Fe3+ + O2− → 2Fe2+ + VO + 1/2O2) and creates hopping sites that significantly increase n-type conductivity via the Verwey mechanism. By minimizing Fe2+ concentration, the BSNZF composites maintain high electrical resistivity, which is essential for preventing excessive dielectric loss and preserving high breakdown strength. This is experimentally validated by the well-defined symmetry of the P–E loops and the predominant Fe3+ signal in XPS Fe 2p spectra, indicating that the functional multiferroicity is not compromised by leakage-induced artifacts.

The stability of Fe3+ and its interaction with the BSTO lattice through oxygen vacancies further enhances magnetoelectric (ME) coupling. Consequently, the saturation magnetization (Ms) increases significantly from 13.6 emu g−1 (x = 0.1) to 56.9 emu g−1 (x = 0.4), and the Mr rises from 0.71 to 1.88 emu g−1, while the Hc decreases slightly due to easier domain wall motion. These results are in good agreement with reports on NZFO/BTO composites, where the ferrite phase dominates the magnetic properties and facilitates strain transfer from the magnetostrictive NZFO phase to the piezoelectric BSTO phase.56 The XPS spectra also provide important microscopic evidence explaining the narrowing of the band gap (Eg) from 3.20 eV in pure BSTO to 1.85 eV in BSNZF4 (x = 0.4), as determined from UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) and Tauc plots. This band gap narrowing originates from charge redistribution and the increased oxygen vacancies observed in the XPS analysis, serving as a key driver for enhanced multiferroic coupling and superior broadband electromagnetic wave absorption performance.
In addition, the observed band gap narrowing in the xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO system plays a crucial role in enhancing electromagnetic interactions, particularly in the microwave region.10 A reduced band gap increases charge carrier density, thereby enhancing electrical conductivity and dielectric loss via Maxwell–Wagner polarization. Simultaneously, the introduction of transition-metal d-states near the Fermi level facilitates spin-related magnetic resonance and strengthens magnetoelectric coupling. This also improves strain transfer efficiency across interfaces, ultimately enhancing the cross-coupling between electric and magnetic responses the defining feature of multiferroic materials.11 The shift of the Ba 3d5/2 binding energy toward lower values (∼2.7–3 eV), along with the significant increase in the proportion of oxygen vacancies (O 1s peak at ∼530–531 eV), indicates the formation of mid-gap states due to defects. The pronounced shift of the Ba 3d5/2 peak from 782.18 eV (pure BSTO sample) to approximately 779.4 eV in BSNZF1 and BSNZF4 demonstrates an increase in electron density around Ba2+ ions. This serves as direct evidence of charge transfer from the NZFO phase (Fe3+ and Ni2+ ions) to the BSTO phase across the phase boundary. This change in the chemical environment indicates strong magnetoelectric interaction, which reduces the ionicity of the Ba–O bond. Oxygen vacancies act as intermediate energy levels, narrowing the band gap (Eg) and increasing charge carrier density, thereby enhancing Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization. The O 1s spectra reveal a marked increase in the ratio of oxygen vacancies (VO) in the doped samples (the peak at ∼530–531 eV shows significantly higher intensity compared to pure BSTO). Oxygen vacancies play a crucial bridging role, creating mid-gap states and promoting Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization at the boundaries between the two phases with different conductivity and dielectric constants. As a result, Pm increases from 0.050 to 0.121 µC cm−2, Pr from 0.011 to 0.038 µC cm−2, and Ec from 1.94 to 3.56 kV cm−1.57 The Fe 2p spectra confirm the stability of Fe3+, which supports superexchange interactions within NZFO. The band gap narrowing due to d-orbitals from Ni2+/Fe3+ ions and oxygen vacancies enhances electron transition capability, thereby supporting magnetostriction and strain-mediated ME coupling. The Fe 2p3/2 peak at ∼710.5–711.8 eV, along with the characteristic satellite peaks, confirms that iron exists predominantly in the Fe3+ state. The stability of Fe3+ and the change in its ligand environment (influenced by the BSTO lattice) indicate the presence of superexchange interactions through oxygen bridges between the two phases. Oxygen vacancies alter the Fe–O–Fe/Ti bond angle, enhancing strain transfer from the magnetostrictive NZFO phase to the piezoelectric BSTO phase. Consequently, Ms increases to 56.9 emu g−1 and Mr increases to 1.88 emu g−1 at x = 0.4.58 Based on the combined analyses of magnetic M(H), ferroelectric P(E), and optical (Eg) properties, the variation of NZFO content from x = 0.1 to 0.4 does not induce anomalous behavior but instead results in nearly monotonic trends. Therefore, compositions x = 0, 0.1, and 0.4 were selected for further investigation. The electromagnetic (EM) wave absorption performance of the materials can be quantitatively evaluated based on the complex permittivity (ε = ε′ − jε″) and complex permeability (µ = µ′ − jµ″), as shown in Fig. 8. The real parts (ε′, µ′) represent the energy storage capability, while the imaginary parts (ε″, µ″) correspond to dielectric and magnetic losses, respectively. For all samples, ε′ exhibits a decreasing trend with increasing frequency, which can be attributed to dielectric dispersion behavior. The ε″ values show multiple relaxation peaks, indicating the presence of various polarization mechanisms, including dipolar polarization, interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarization, and defect-induced relaxation processes. Notably, the BSNZF4 sample (x = 0.4) demonstrates enhanced ε″ values, suggesting stronger dielectric loss due to increased charge carrier density and interfacial polarization arising from the coexistence of BSTO and NZFO phases. Similarly, the µ′ and µ″ spectra reveal the magnetic response of the composites. The µ″ curves exhibit distinct resonance peaks in the GHz range, which can be attributed to natural resonance and exchange resonance mechanisms. The presence of NZFO significantly enhances magnetic loss, particularly in BSNZF1 and BSNZF4, due to domain wall resonance and spin rotational losses. The excellent EM absorption performance of the BSNZF composites arises from a synergistic “dual-loss mechanism”, which includes: (i) dielectric loss (dipolar polarization, interfacial polarization, conduction loss); (ii) magnetic loss (natural resonance, exchange resonance); and (iii) interfacial magnetoelectric coupling between BSTO and NZFO phases. In particular, the enhanced interfacial polarization and strain-mediated coupling in the BSNZF4 sample significantly improve impedance matching and attenuation capability, leading to strong and broadband microwave absorption. As shown in Fig. 8a, the real part of the complex permittivity (ε′) for all samples exhibits pronounced dispersion, decreasing monotonically with increasing frequency. This behavior is characteristic of dielectric relaxation in the 2–18 GHz range and can be attributed to interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarization, dipolar reorientation, and the inability of ionic polarization to follow high-frequency alternating fields.6,24 The pure ferroelectric BSTO sample shows the highest ε′ values, decreasing from approximately 13.5 at 2 GHz to 5.5 at 18 GHz. The BSNZF4 composite follows a similar trend, decreasing from 12.0 to 4.5, indicating the dominant contribution of the ferroelectric phase. BSNZF1 exhibits intermediate values (10.5 to 5.5), while pure NZFO shows the lowest ε′, with a more gradual decrease at higher frequencies. This trend confirms that increasing ferrite content effectively dilutes the high dielectric constant of the ferroelectric phase, in agreement with effective medium theories such as the Maxwell–Garnett model for multiphase systems.7–9Fig. 9b presents the imaginary part of permittivity (ε″), which reflects dielectric loss. All samples exhibit a decreasing trend in ε″ with increasing frequency, indicating reduced energy dissipation at higher GHz frequencies. This behavior is particularly evident in the 2–10 GHz range and can be attributed to the suppression of space charge polarization and defect-related relaxation processes. Notably, BSNZF4 exhibits the highest ε″ values, decreasing from 4.2 at 2 GHz to 2.2 at 10 GHz. BSNZF1 shows lower values with a slower decay (2.8–2.2), while NZFO displays a relatively fluctuating behavior. These results indicate that dielectric loss in BSNZF4 is significantly enhanced due to strong interfacial polarization and increased charge carrier density, making it highly favorable for electromagnetic wave attenuation. The frequency dependence of the real part of magnetic permeability (µ′) is shown in Fig. 8c. As expected, the non-magnetic BSTO sample exhibits a nearly constant µ′ ≈ 1.0 across the entire frequency range. In contrast, pure NZFO demonstrates higher µ′ values (∼1.5 at 2 GHz), with a slight decrease in the 10–12 GHz region, likely associated with ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) or domain wall resonance. With increasing NZFO content, the µ′ values of the composites increase significantly, indicating that the ferrimagnetic phase effectively introduces magnetic permeability into the system. Additionally, strain-mediated magnetoelectric coupling further modifies the magnetic response of the composites. Fig. 8d illustrates the imaginary part of permeability (µ″), corresponding to magnetic loss. The BSTO sample shows negligible µ″ values, as expected for a non-magnetic material. However, within the 2–10 GHz range, BSNZF4 consistently exhibits the highest µ″ values, exceeding not only BSNZF1 but also pure NZFO. This observation highlights the strong enhancement of magnetic loss due to interfacial coupling and optimized magnetic phase distribution within the composite. It is observed that the real permittivity (ε′) at low frequencies decreases with increasing NZFO content, despite the narrowing of the optical band gap. This apparent deviation from the general inverse relation between Eg and ε′ (common in single-phase materials) is attributed to the dominance of the phase dilution effect in the multiferroic composite. The high ε′ ferroelectric BSTO phase is progressively substituted by the lower ε′ NZFO ferrite phase, a trend consistent with observations in similar substituted titanate–ferrite ceramics. Furthermore, the band gap narrowing increases the concentration of mobile charge carriers, which, while enhancing the imaginary permittivity (ε′′) via Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization and conduction loss, can simultaneously dissipate stored polarization charges, thus lowering the measured ε′. From a functional standpoint, this moderate reduction in ε′ is highly beneficial for impedance matching, as it prevents the excessive permittivity typically found in pure ferroelectrics that leads to undesirable surface reflection. The resulting synergy between optimized ε′ and enhanced ε′′ is a key factor for the superior broadband absorption performance of the BSNZF4 composition.3,9,10 Overall, the results in Fig. 8a–d reveal that the BSNZF4 sample exhibits simultaneously enhanced ε″ and µ″ values over the entire frequency range. Notably, the similar variation trends of ε″ and µ″ suggest a highly synchronized dielectric–magnetic response, which is critical for achieving optimal impedance matching and strong electromagnetic wave absorption. To further elucidate the roles of dielectric and magnetic losses, the dielectric loss tangent (tan δε = ε″/ε′) and magnetic loss tangent (tan δµ = µ″/µ′) were evaluated, as shown in Fig. 9. These parameters provide deeper insight into the attenuation mechanisms governing the EM absorption performance of the materials. Fig. 9 illustrates the frequency dependence of the dielectric loss tangent (tan δε), defined as the ratio of the imaginary part (ε″) to the real part (ε′) of the complex permittivity, which reflects the capability of a material to dissipate electromagnetic energy into heat. For the pure BSTO sample, the dielectric loss exhibits a slight increase with frequency, with noticeable peaks around 10 GHz and 16 GHz. This behavior is consistent with previous studies on BaTiO3-based materials, where such peaks are attributed to intrinsic dielectric resonance phenomena associated with dipolar and lattice polarization mechanisms. With the introduction of the ferrimagnetic NZFO phase, the BSNZF1 composite shows a moderate enhancement in dielectric loss, particularly in the low-frequency range (2–8 GHz) and in the 12.5–15.5 GHz region. More prominently, the BSNZF4 sample demonstrates significantly higher dielectric loss values—nearly twice those of pure BSTO in the 2–8 GHz and 13–18 GHz ranges. This enhancement can be attributed to the increased contribution of the ferrimagnetic phase, which possesses lower electrical resistivity, thereby promoting leakage current loss. Moreover, this behavior highlights the critical role of interfacial polarization in the composite system. As the ferrite content increases, the interfaces between the high-resistivity ferroelectric BSTO phase and the relatively conductive NZFO phase facilitate charge accumulation, leading to pronounced Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization. This interfacial effect gives rise to relaxation resonance processes, significantly enhancing dielectric loss. Consequently, the BSNZF4 composition emerges as a highly promising candidate for exploiting dielectric loss in both the 2–8 GHz and 13–18 GHz frequency bands. Simultaneously, the magnetic loss behavior was analyzed through the magnetic loss tangent. As expected, the non-magnetic BSTO sample exhibits negligible magnetic loss. In the 2–7.5 GHz range, both BSNZF1 and BSNZF4 composites display higher magnetic loss values than the pure NZFO sample, with BSNZF4 showing nearly double the magnitude. Around 10 GHz, both dielectric and magnetic losses reach their minimum values across all samples, with minimal variation between compositions. At higher frequencies (13–18 GHz), the magnetic loss of BSNZF4 again dominates, mirroring the trend observed in dielectric loss. This indicates a synergistic enhancement of both dielectric and magnetic dissipation mechanisms, which is highly favorable for broadband electromagnetic wave absorption.

Typically, a single semicircle in a Cole–Cole plot corresponds to a single Debye polarization relaxation process. In Fig. 10, the Cole–Cole plots of NZFO, BSTO, BSNZF1, and BSNZF4 are presented, where the real part of the complex permittivity (ε′) is plotted along the abscissa and the imaginary part (ε″) along the ordinate. The appearance of semicircular arcs in these plots confirms the existence of Debye-type relaxation processes within the materials.3,7 In general, a greater number of semicircles or semicircle-like segments indicates stronger polarization capability and enhanced dielectric loss.54 As observed, all samples exhibit one or more semicircular features, demonstrating that polarization relaxation mechanisms are active across all compositions during electromagnetic wave interaction. When incident microwaves interact with structural defects, interfaces, or heterogeneities, localized polarization centers are generated, giving rise to Debye relaxation and corresponding Cole–Cole features.42,46 However, due to the composite nature of the materials and the specific synthesis route, ideal and well-defined semicircles are not observed. Instead, the plots display multiple distorted arcs and inflection points. The presence of numerous inflection points indicates the coexistence of multiple relaxation processes with different relaxation times, commonly referred to as multi-Debye relaxation behavior. A closer examination reveals that the Cole–Cole curves of BSNZF1 and especially BSNZF4 exhibit significantly more complex profiles, characterized by pronounced distortion, twisting, and fluctuation over the entire range. This behavior suggests a higher density of polarization centers and more intense interfacial interactions compared to the single-phase materials.2,13 Such distorted and intertwined Cole–Cole characteristics are indicative of strong structural heterogeneity, multiple competing dielectric loss mechanisms (including dipolar polarization, interfacial polarization, and conduction loss), and enhanced electromagnetic coupling at phase boundaries. The Cole–Cole plots (ε′ vs. ε′′) in Fig. 10 exhibit significant deviations from ideal Debye semicircles, characterized by distorted, multiple arcs and inclined linear segments. According to Debye relaxation theory, each arc corresponds to a specific relaxation process. The observation of overlapping arcs indicates a multi-Debye relaxation behavior stemming from the coexistence of dipolar reorientation within the BSTO phase and Maxwell Wagner interfacial polarization at the ferrite perovskite boundaries. Furthermore, the inclined linear segments serve as a distinct physical indicator of conduction loss, where charge carrier hopping facilitated by oxygen vacancies and the relatively conductive NZFO grains contributes to energy dissipation. The significantly more complex and intertwined profiles of the BSNZF4 composite reflect the intensified interfacial coupling and structural heterogeneity enabled by SPS processing. This complex multi-relaxation behavior confirms that the superior broadband microwave absorption in BSNZF4 is not a result of a single mechanism, but a synergistic dual-loss process (dielectric + magnetic) that maximizes the attenuation constant (α) across the X and Ku bands. These features are typical of multiferroic composites designed for microwave absorption applications and are closely associated with superior dielectric loss performance. Therefore, the Cole–Cole analysis further confirms that the NZFO/BSTO composite system particularly the BSNZF4 composition exhibits strong polarization relaxation behavior and complex loss mechanisms, highlighting its great potential for efficient electromagnetic wave absorption in the 2–18 GHz frequency range.5,14
To evaluate the electromagnetic (EM) wave absorption and shielding performance, multiferroic xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO composites with x = 0, 0.1, 0.4, and 1.0 (denoted as BSTO, BSNZF1, BSNZF4, and NZFO, respectively) were incorporated as fillers into a paraffin matrix with a fixed loading ratio of 40 wt% to fabricate BSTO–paraffin, BSNZF1–paraffin, BSNZF4–paraffin, and NZFO–paraffin composites.

When the reflection loss (RL) value is below −10 dB, more than 90% of the incident electromagnetic (EM) wave energy is absorbed, which is generally considered effective absorption. The maximum continuous frequency range satisfying RL < −10 dB is defined as the effective absorption bandwidth (EAB). For the NZFO material (Fig. 11b, f and i): NZFO exhibits outstanding broadband absorption behavior, primarily governed by magnetic loss mechanisms, including domain wall resonance, natural ferromagnetic resonance (NFMR), and eddy current loss. The maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax) reaches up to 11.1 GHz, indicating strong potential for broadband applications spanning the X- and Ku-bands. At relatively thin thicknesses (2.0–2.5 mm), NZFO achieves excellent absorption performance, with minimum reflection loss (RLmin) values of −48.2 dB (14.5 GHz) and −39.9 dB (13.9 GHz), and EAB values of approximately 6.8–7.0 GHz. For the BSTO material (Fig. 11a, e and j): BSTO exhibits microwave absorption behavior dominated by dielectric loss mechanisms, including dipolar polarization and interfacial polarization. The material shows exceptionally deep RLmin values, indicating highly efficient absorption at specific frequencies. At a thickness of 2.0 mm, BSTO achieves the deepest RLmin among all samples (−57.9 dB at 16.7 GHz), corresponding to near-total absorption (>99.999%) in the Ku-band. For the BSNZF1 material (Fig. 11c, g and k): the BSNZF1 composite demonstrates a balanced absorption performance due to the combined contributions of dielectric and magnetic losses. At a thickness of 2.0 mm, it achieves an RLmin of −50.3 dB at 16.7 GHz, with an EAB exceeding 4.5 GHz (13.5–18 GHz). Within the thickness range of 2.0–3.5 mm, BSNZF1 maintains stable absorption performance, with RLmin values ranging from −30.3 to −34.5 dB across 8.8–18 GHz and EAB values of 4–6 GHz. Compared to pure BSTO, the incorporation of NZFO significantly broadens the absorption bandwidth due to the added magnetic loss contribution. However, the absolute RLmin values are reduced compared to the single-phase BSTO and NZFO materials, indicating a trade-off between absorption depth and bandwidth. As a result, these materials exhibit excellent electromagnetic attenuation capability, making them highly promising candidates for radar stealth and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding applications.

In contrast, the present NZFO/BSTO system, fabricated via solid-state reaction combined with SPS, offers competitive or superior performance in the X and Ku bands at relatively low thicknesses (2.0–2.5 mm), benefiting from strong interfacial interactions and an optimized dual-loss mechanism. This positions the BSNZF4 composite as a highly promising candidate for practical applications in radar stealth and EMI shielding. As the testing frequency increases and the sample thickness decreases, the reflection loss (RL) values of all samples exhibit a gradual reduction. For all samples, the complete profile of the maximum RL bandwidth cannot be fully captured within the measurement range; however, the observed trends indicate that the absorption bandwidth remains extensive, highlighting strong potential for high-frequency applications. ## The parameters (RLmin, resonant frequency f and the EAB corresponding to the RL value at −10 dB) related to the microwave absorption of NZFO/BSTO composites with different thickness and comparison with some similar composites
The attenuation constant α quantitatively describes the intrinsic ability of a material to dissipate electromagnetic energy during propagation. A higher α value indicates stronger attenuation capability, which is essential for achieving excellent microwave absorption performance. From a physical perspective, α is governed by both dielectric loss (related to polarization relaxation and electrical conduction) and magnetic loss (associated with natural ferromagnetic resonance, domain wall resonance, and eddy current effects). In multiferroic composites such as NZFO/BSTO, the coexistence of ferroelectric and ferrimagnetic phases promotes simultaneous dielectric and magnetic dissipation mechanisms. Moreover, interfacial polarization (Maxwell–Wagner effect) at the phase boundaries significantly enhances α by introducing additional relaxation processes and charge accumulation. However, a high attenuation constant alone is not sufficient to guarantee optimal absorption performance. Impedance matching between the material and free space must also be considered to minimize reflection at the surface.
This condition is typically evaluated through the normalized input impedance Zin/Z0, where ideal absorption occurs when Zin ≈ Z0. In addition, the quarter-wavelength (λ/4) cancellation model explains the dependence of absorption peak position on sample thickness, where destructive interference minimizes reflected waves at specific frequencies. Therefore, optimal microwave absorption performance is achieved through a synergistic balance between high attenuation constant, proper impedance matching, and thickness-dependent interference effects.
To identify an optimal thickness for practical applications, a thickness of 2.5 mm was selected as the representative condition for detailed investigation. In general, a higher attenuation constant (α) indicates a stronger capability to dissipate electromagnetic waves. As shown in Fig. 13a, the attenuation constant of the BSNZF4–paraffin composite is significantly higher than that of the other samples within the frequency ranges of 2–7.5 GHz and 12.7–18 GHz. This result confirms that the incorporation of the NZFO ferrimagnetic phase at a composition of x = 0.4 effectively enhances the electromagnetic wave absorption capability of the BSNZF4 composite, particularly in the 12–15 GHz range, in excellent agreement with the previously discussed results. To further evaluate the absorption mechanism, the impedance matching performance was quantitatively analyzed. Specifically, the impedance matching ratio (Z) as a function of frequency in the 2–18 GHz range was calculated for the NZFO, BSTO, BSNZF1, and BSNZF4 samples at a thickness of 2.5 mm. According to eqn (3),54 when Z approaches unity (Z ≈ 1), the reflection coefficient tends toward zero. This implies that incident electromagnetic waves from free space can enter the material without reflection at the interface, achieving ideal impedance matching. Therefore, the combination of a high attenuation constant and optimal impedance matching in the BSNZF4 composite is the key factor responsible for its superior microwave absorption performance. The electromagnetic wave absorption performance of a material is governed not only by its intrinsic electromagnetic parameters but also critically by the thickness of the absorbing layer. For the NZFO–paraffin and BSTO–paraffin composites with a thickness of 2.5 mm, the effective absorption bandwidth corresponding to RL ≤ −10 dB exceeds 10.5 GHz, demonstrating excellent broadband absorption capability. As established in previous studies, a reflection loss (RL) value below −10 dB corresponds to effective electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption, meaning more than 90% of the incident EMW is attenuated. In the present work, the NZFO/BSTO multiferroic composites exhibit significantly lower RL values (below −15 dB), corresponding to over 96% EMW absorption, over a broad frequency bandwidth.52 These results demonstrate the excellent microwave absorption performance of the developed composites. These results are consistent with previously reported studies on ferrite- and BaTiO3-based microwave absorbers.6,26,60–63 As the operating frequency increases and the sample thickness decreases, the RL values of all samples show a gradual reduction. Although the complete RL bandwidth cannot be fully captured within the measured frequency window, the overall trend clearly indicates that the absorption bandwidth remains significantly wide, especially at higher frequencies, confirming strong potential for practical high-frequency applications.

As shown in the experimental results, the BSNZF4 composite (x = 0.4) exhibits the highest attenuation constant α across a wide frequency range, particularly in the X and Ku bands. The superior absorption in the BSNZF4 sample originates from a tailored dual-loss mechanism. The enhanced Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization, facilitated by the sub-micron interfaces and verified by oxygen vacancy increase in XPS, complements the magnetic loss from NZFO. Furthermore, the fine microstructure from SPS processing enables excellent impedance matching (Z ≈1), allowing electromagnetic waves to enter the material with minimal reflection. This superior α value originates from the enhanced dual-loss mechanism: strong dielectric loss due to interfacial polarization and oxygen vacancies (confirmed by XPS), combined with significant magnetic loss from natural resonance and domain wall motion provided by the NZFO phase.Complementing the high attenuation capability, the impedance matching ratio (Zin/Z0) plays an equally important role in determining the overall absorption performance. An ideal impedance matching ratio close to 1 allows maximum incident electromagnetic wave to enter the absorber rather than being reflected at the surface. As illustrated in Fig. 13b, all samples show favorable impedance matching (in the range of 0.8–1.2) within the 12.5–18 GHz frequency region. Notably, in the 12.5–17 GHz range, the impedance matching curves of BSTO, NZFO, BSNZF1, and BSNZF4 nearly overlap and stabilize around ∼0.8–1.0. This indicates that the composites fabricated by solid-state reaction combined with SPS possess excellent and relatively similar impedance matching characteristics in the target frequency band, regardless of phase ratio.
The excellent agreement between high attenuation constant (α) and good impedance matching explains the outstanding reflection loss performance observed in Fig. 11. In particular, the BSNZF4 sample achieves both deep absorption (RLmin = −48.3 dB at 15.7 GHz) and broad effective absorption bandwidth (EAB = 5.7 GHz at 2.5 mm thickness). These results demonstrate that the NZFO/BSTO multiferroic composites function as highly efficient microwave absorbers through the synergistic interplay between dielectric loss (dominated by interfacial polarization and oxygen vacancies at phase boundaries) and magnetic loss (from the ferrimagnetic NZFO phase), together with optimized impedance matching enabled by the fine microstructure from SPS processing. This combination of strong internal dissipation (high α) and good wave-entry condition (good impedance matching) is consistent with recent studies on advanced perovskite–spinel multiferroic composite systems for high-performance electromagnetic wave absorption.64,65
The interaction between the magnetostrictive NZFO and piezoelectric BSTO phases is mediated by interfacial strain, a hallmark of the magnetoelectric (ME) effect in 0–3 composites. Although the direct voltage coefficient was not the primary focus, the Raman red-shifts and XPS binding energy variations provide compelling evidence of local structural perturbations and charge transfer across phase boundaries. This interfacial stress effectively modifies the dielectric environment of the BSTO matrix in response to high-frequency magnetic excitations of the NZFO phase. Such coupling is critical for the observed broadband synergy, where the energy dissipation is not merely the sum of individual components but a collective response of the multiferroic interfaces. This mechanism ensures ideal impedance matching and maximizes the attenuation constant (α), positioning BSNZF4 as a superior candidate for radar stealth applications. Fig. 14 schematically illustrates the electromagnetic wave absorption mechanism of the BSNZF4 composite over the 2–18 GHz frequency range. The superior absorption performance of this system originates from the cooperative interaction between intrinsic loss mechanisms and microstructural features. Dielectric loss is primarily governed by the BSTO phase, where electric dipoles undergo continuous polarization, reorientation, and relaxation under an alternating electromagnetic field, leading to the conversion of electromagnetic energy into heat. Concurrently, the NZFO phase contributes dominant magnetic loss through mechanisms such as domain wall resonance and natural ferromagnetic resonance, effectively dissipating the magnetic component of the incident wave. At the interfaces between the ferroelectric BSTO and ferrimagnetic NZFO phases, pronounced interfacial polarization (Maxwell–Wagner effect) occurs due to charge accumulation at grain boundaries, forming large effective dipoles that significantly enhance energy dissipation, especially at high frequencies. Moreover, the heterogeneous and randomly distributed microstructure induces multiple scattering and reflection of electromagnetic waves within the material. This effect prolongs the propagation path and increases the probability of repeated interactions with both dielectric and magnetic domains, thereby maximizing attenuation before the waves can be reflected or transmitted. A comprehensive analysis of the structural, microstructural, magnetic, ferroelectric, optical, and electromagnetic properties demonstrates that the xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO multiferroic composite system exhibits a strong synergistic coupling between its constituent phases. The integration of the ferroelectric BSTO phase and ferrimagnetic NZFO phase not only preserves their intrinsic properties but also generates enhanced interfacial effects, including Maxwell–Wagner polarization and strain-mediated magnetoelectric coupling. These interactions play a decisive role in optimizing impedance matching, increasing attenuation constants, and promoting simultaneous dielectric and magnetic losses across a broad frequency range.

The XPS evidences (BE shift of Ba, increased Vo, and stable Fe3+) demonstrate that the interaction between the two phases is not merely physical contact but a strong electromagnetic interaction across the phase boundary: charge transfer, oxygen vacancies, interfacial polarization and strain-mediated coupling, and dual-loss mechanism (dielectric and magnetic) the BSNZF4 sample (x = 0.4) emerges as the most promising candidate, exhibiting an optimal balance between dielectric and magnetic contributions, superior reflection loss, and a wide effective absorption bandwidth within the 2–18 GHz range. The improved performance is attributed to enhanced interfacial polarization, multiple relaxation processes, and efficient energy dissipation mechanisms arising from the heterogeneous microstructure. Overall, this study highlights the effectiveness of combining compositional design with advanced fabrication techniques such as Spark Plasma Sintering to engineer high-performance multiferroic composites. The obtained results not only provide deeper insight into the fundamental mechanisms governing electromagnetic wave absorption but also open up significant opportunities for practical applications in radar stealth technology, electromagnetic interference shielding, and next-generation microwave devices.

## Conclusions
The multiferroic xNZFO/(1 − x)BSTO composites (x = 0–1.0) were successfully fabricated by solid-state reaction combined with spark plasma sintering. Incorporation of the ferrimagnetic NZFO phase significantly enhanced both ferromagnetic (Ms and Mr) and ferroelectric (Pr) properties, with optimal multiferroic coupling observed at x = 0.4. All composites exhibited strong microwave absorption in the 6–18 GHz range, achieving maximum effective absorption bandwidths (EAB) of 8.9–11.5 GHz. Notably, the BSNZF4 composite (x = 0.4) delivered superior broadband absorption, with RLmin values of −48.3 dB (15.7 GHz) and −43.9 dB (12.6 GHz), and EAB of 5.4 GHz and 5.7 GHz at thicknesses of 2.0 mm and 2.5 mm, respectively. XPS analysis revealed a binding energy shift of Ba, increased oxygen vacancy concentration, and stable Fe3+ state, providing atomic-level evidence of strong interfacial electromagnetic interactions including charge transfer, oxygen vacancies, interfacial polarization, and strain-mediated coupling. These effects enable an efficient dual-loss mechanism (dielectric magnetic), optimized impedance matching, and enhanced wave attenuation. The NZFO/BSTO system thus demonstrates excellent potential as a high-performance electromagnetic wave absorber for X- and Ku-band trend to applications in stealth technology and EMI shielding.
