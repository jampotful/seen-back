# Prediction of Monocular and Binocular Visual Acuity for the Pseudophakic Patients Using Simulated Modulation Transfer Function

## Abstract
PurposeTo develop a correlation of simulated modulation transfer function (MTF) to clinical monocular and binocular visual acuity (VA) to predict the VA defocus curves of intraocular lenses (IOLs) in the early preclinical development.MethodsRay tracing software was used to generate MTF area (MTFa) data at defocus positions from −3.0 D to +2.0 D. which were then correlated with clinical monocular and binocular VA data to predict preclinical metrics. Simulated MTF data was generated (with mean photopic pupil size of 3 mm with photopic spectral weighting) in a modified pseudophakic Navarro eye model, with corneal spherical aberration (SA) both at the matched and the average human corneal SA. MTFa was calculated across two spatial frequency ranges, and linear and polynomial functions were fit to the pairs of MTFa−1 and VA data for different groups of the lens models. MTFa and VA data of five single-piece acrylic IOL models were analyzed, including aspheric monofocal, non-diffractive extended depth of focus (EDOF), and diffractive multifocal designs.ResultsSimulated MTFa (with mean photopic pupil size of 3 mm with photopic spectral weighting) is broadly well correlated with clinical VA (R2 ≥ 0.9). Simulated VA defocus curves followed the clinical VA curves within the clinical standard deviation. Division of IOL models by type generally increased correlation strength compared to fitting MTFa-VA data for all lens models at once. Corneal spherical aberration showed minimal impact on MTFa-VA correlation. A wider spatial frequency integration interval for the MTFa calculation yielded higher R2, except in the case of grouping of diffractive-only IOLs.ConclusionMonocular and binocular fittings showed similar correlation coefficients for the prediction of VA. VA calculated from simulated MTFa can be an accurate predictor of the clinical performance of an IOL design, helping to identify promising IOL designs early in the development process, improving optical quality and reducing the risk of suboptimal lenses advancing to manufacturing and clinical trials.

## Introduction
Advances in intraocular lens (IOL) design continue to improve postoperative image quality, aiming to optimize outcomes for cataract patients and increase overall satisfaction. To support these innovations, objective optical-quality metrics such as the point spread function, optical transfer function (OTF), and modulation transfer function (MTF) have become essential tools for characterizing IOL performance in preclinical settings.1–5 These metrics allow researchers to quantify image quality and simulate expected visual outcomes before clinical trials, streamlining the development of novel IOL designs.
Among these, the area under the MTF curve (MTFa) has gained traction as a robust predictor of visual acuity (VA). Early work by Lang et al3,4 laid the foundation for interpreting MTF in the context of multifocal IOLs, while Alarcon et al2 formalized the use of MTFa for predicting through-focus VA in pseudophakic patients. Their study demonstrated strong correlations between binocular VA and bench-measured MTFa across spatial frequencies up to 50 lp/mm, incorporating average corneal SA. Vega et al6 and Felipe et al7 expanded this approach to monocular VA and broader frequency ranges, respectively, reinforcing the clinical relevance of MTFa. More recently, Armengol et al8 and Yan et al9 have shown equivalence between different optical quality metrics and their predictive power for multifocal IOLs, further validating the use of MTFa in preclinical modeling. These findings have influenced regulatory standards, with ANSI Z80.35-201810 and ISO 11979–2 (2024)11 now requiring manufacturers to report expected monocular VA as part of IOL optical characterization. Very recently, Loicq et al12 demonstrated correlation between MTF at 50 lp/mm, MTFa, and clinical VA, emphasizing the importance of accurate preclinical modeling.
In parallel with bench-based studies, ray-tracing simulations have emerged as a powerful tool for evaluating IOL performance in model eyes. Fernandez et al13 demonstrated the utility of this approach by using ray-tracing software to simulate a diffractive multifocal IOL under varying pupil sizes, correlating MTFa with both VA and contrast sensitivity. While their study focused on a single lens design and included contrast sensitivity as an endpoint, it highlighted the potential of simulation-based methods to complement bench testing and expand the scope of preclinical evaluation. Central to these simulations is the use of schematic eyes, such as those described by Navarro et al,14 and Grievenkamp et al,15 which provide a structured framework for modeling optical performance under controlled conditions. These schematic models enable exploration of physiological factors like pupil size, aberrations, and retinal image quality, but further refinement is needed to account for variability in corneal shape, pupil dynamics,16–18 and higher-order aberrations.19,20
However, some modeling parameters remain underexplored. The spatial frequency integration interval can influence MTFa calculations and, by extension, expected VA predictions.2 Additionally, the impact of corneal SA in model eyes has not been fully characterized, even though it plays a critical role in shaping retinal image quality.21–23 These gaps in modeling present opportunities to improve the accuracy of preclinical VA prediction.
In this study, we investigate the influence of spatial frequency integration interval and corneal SA on preclinical VA prediction using ray-tracing simulations of monofocal and simultaneous vision IOLs (including extended depth of focus and multifocal designs) in a model eye. We develop a method of estimating VA and defocus curves in the design phase of IOL development, effectively providing a view of clinical VA before lenses of a particular design are even manufactured, leveraging insights from the works of Alarcon et al,2 Del Aguila-Carrasco,24 and Fernandez.7 By correlating simulated MTFa with clinical VA data, we assess the sensitivity of VA prediction to these modeling parameters and evaluate the robustness of linear and polynomial fitting approaches. Using simulated optical image quality metrics to estimate VA can support the design of new IOLs, provide standardized methods for regulatory authorities, and offer insights into expected clinical benefits for patients and clinicians. Preclinical VA prediction should be a primary tool in identifying promising designs earlier in the development process, improving the optical quality and performance of IOLs, and reducing the risk of inadequate designs moving into clinical evaluation.

## Methods
This study employed a simulation-based approach to evaluate IOL optical performance and predict VA. Simulations were conducted using ray tracing software to generate MTF data at different defocus positions. The resulting objective optical measures were correlated with clinical VA data to assess the predictive value of preclinical metrics. Multiple fitting techniques were explored to establish robust relationships between simulated optical quality and patient visual outcomes.

## Intraocular Lens Models
The analysis included several single-piece acrylic IOL models: an aspheric monofocal IOL (SN60WF), a non-diffractive extended depth of focus (EDOF) IOL (DFT015), two apodized bifocal diffractive IOLs (SV25T0 and SN6AD1), and a full visual range (FVR) trifocal IOL (TFNT00). The monofocal SN60WF and EDOF DFT015 represent non-diffractive technologies, while SV25T0, SN6AD1, and TFNT00 are diffractive multifocal designs. The SV25T0 model has an add power of +2.5 D, and the SN6AD1 has an add power of +3.0 D. The TFNT00 model has intermediate and near add powers of +2.17 D and +3.25 D, respectively.

## Model Eye Setups
Simulations were performed with Ansys Zemax OpticStudio 2023 R1.02, using a modified pseudophakic Navarro eye model, where the crystalline lens is replaced by an IOL, and the spherical aberration of the cornea is adjusted through the anterior corneal conic constant.14 The MTF was generated by inducing defocus via object distance adjustments for each IOL. Spectral weighting was applied over the visible range (450–670 nm) according to the photopic luminosity function for the human eye.16 IOLs were modeled with a 3-mm aperture at the iris, simulating average photopic pupil conditions observed in cataract patients.16,17
Pupil size is a critical factor in optical simulation, affecting image quality metrics. Statistical analysis was conducted across pupil diameters from 2.1 mm to 4.2 mm in 0.3 mm increments, centered around the mean photopic pupil size of 3 mm. No significant difference was obtained between using a single pupil or multiple pupils. The MTFa values were weighted by pupil size distribution. The effect of statistical distribution of pupil sizes was averaged out and produced similar results, supporting the use of a representative 3-mm pupil diameter for all reported simulations. This approach ensures relevance to typical clinical conditions and agrees with the published standards for the photopic testing.
SA settings were based on the manufacturer specifications and published literature. The analyses were conducted for both the matched SA and the average SA human cornea (+0.28 µm). The matched SA cornea eliminates residual SA by applying the corresponding opposite SA used by the designed IOL. For the matched SA cornea analysis, the SA of SN6AD1 and TFNT00 is −0.1 µm, while the SA of SN60WF, DFT015, and SV25T0 is −0.2 µm at 6 mm entrance pupil diameter.

## Spatial Frequency Integration Range
The area under the MTF was calculated over two spatial frequency ranges: 0–50 lp/mm and 0–100 lp/mm. These ranges were chosen based on published optical bench studies correlating MTFa with clinical VA outcomes.3,6,7 Lower frequencies (up to 50 lp/mm) are commonly employed in clinical and regulatory contexts due to their relevance for typical visual tasks; higher frequencies (up to 100 lp/mm) may provide additional insight into lens performance at finer detail levels. For a model eye with a mid-power IOL, a spatial frequency of 100 lp/mm approximately corresponds to an angular spatial frequency of 30 cycles per degree (cpd) or the 20/20 Snellen equivalent, and 50 lp/mm approximately corresponds to 15 cpd or the 20/40 Snellen equivalent. The study compared both ranges to assess their impact on correlation strength and predictive accuracy.

## Clinical Defocus Curve Data
Clinical defocus curves were obtained from four clinical studies performed by Alcon Research LLC (Fort Worth, TX, USA; Table 1). Defocus curve testing was conducted under photopic conditions (>85 cd/m2), using a 100% contrast Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study chart positioned at 4 m. Patients viewed the chart with best distance correction and were presented with monocular or binocular defocus, depending on the study protocol. For SN60WF and DFT015 IOL models, defocus curve testing was performed with defocus between +1.50 and −2.50 D in 0.50 D steps. For SV25T0, SN6AD1, and TFNT00 IOL models, defocus curve testing was performed with defocus between +2.00 and −5.00 D in 0.50 D steps. ## Fitting Methods
Correlations between simulated MTFa and clinical VA were evaluated using linear, polynomial (second and third degree), and logarithmic functions. Fitting equations were applied to plots of clinical logMAR VA against inverse MTFa (1/MTFa) values. Higher-order polynomials (up to fifth degree) and logarithmic fits were initially explored but discontinued for binocular data due to poor suitability and omitted here. Monocular and binocular data were analyzed separately, and fitting coefficients were determined for each lens type: non-diffractive and diffractive. Additionally, fitting coefficients were determined for a combination set containing all lens types in the study. ## Effect of Spatial Frequency Integration Range
Monocular and binocular R2 fitting options and the values are described in Table 4 and plotted in Figure 4. A total of 12 fittings were considered (Table 3), and the spatial frequency integration interval varied. For monocular VA fitting, a wider frequency interval of 0 to 100 lp/mm resulted in higher average R2 values for the four lenses (SN60WF, DFT015, SV25T0, and SN6AD1) compared with the 0 to 50 lp/mm frequency interval for both matched cornea and a 0.28 -µm SA cornea (Table 4 and Figure 4A). The non-diffractive IOL fitting with SN60WF and DFT015 had similar R2 values at different spatial frequency intervals. Diffractive IOLs fitting with SV25T0 and SN6AD had higher R2 values for the 0- to 50 -lp/mm vs 0 to 100 lp/mm frequency interval (Table 4 and Figure 4A). ## Effect of Spherical Aberration
SA did not significantly affect the R2 values for VA fittings for the 4 IOLs (Table 4). At the 0 to 50 lp/mm frequency interval and monocular vision, the average R2 for all four lenses was similar for the matched and 0.28-µm SA corneas (Figure 4A). At the 0 to 100 lp/mm frequency interval and monocular vision, the average R2 for all four lenses was likewise similar between the matched cornea cases compared with a 0.28-µm corneal SA case. At the 0 to 50 lp/mm frequency interval and monocular vision, R2 for non-diffractive IOLs was slightly lower for the matched cornea vs the 0.28-µm SA average cornea (Figure 4A). Similar results for diffractive IOLs were observed at the 0 to 100 lp/mm frequency interval. At the 0 to 50 lp/mm frequency interval and monocular vision, R2 for diffractive IOLs was similar for the matched cornea compared to 0.28-µm SA cornea. However, at the 0 to 100 lp/mm frequency interval, R2 for diffractive IOLs was higher for the matched cornea compared with the 0.28-µm SA cornea (Figure 4A). Similar results were observed with the binocular vision (Figure 4B).

## Effect of Grouping Lens Models
The lenses were analyzed in three groups: all lenses together, nondiffractive lenses, and diffractive lenses. It is apparent from the R2 values in Table 4 that fitting all lenses together provided a suitable fit with R2 values ≥0.9. In most cases, splitting up the lenses into non-diffractive and diffractive groups resulted in an increased R2 value (Table 4).

## Discussion
This study advances the methodology for preclinical prediction of VA in pseudophakic patients using simulated MTFa from ray tracing analysis. By evaluating the effects of spatial frequency integration interval and corneal SA, this study addresses modeling parameters that have remained underexplored in the published literature. These findings demonstrate robust correlations between simulated MTFa and clinical VA, supporting the use of both linear and polynomial fittings for accurate preclinical estimation of patient outcomes.
The principal outcome of this study is the quantification of the impact of spatial frequency integration range on the strength of the correlation for two important parameters, such as MTFa and VA. The results reveal that a wider frequency interval (0–100 lp/mm) yields higher average R2 values for all lens types in monocular vision, compared to the more commonly used 0–50 lp/mm frequency interval. This is consistent with the work of Felipe et al,7 that emphasized the clinical relevance of broader frequency ranges in optical bench studies. Non-diffractive IOLs exhibited similar R2 values across both intervals, whereas diffractive IOLs showed a slight reduction in correlation strength at the higher frequency range. These findings suggest that the choice of spatial frequency interval should be tailored to the specific lens design under evaluation. In this study, VA prediction is based on equal weighting of the MTF across spatial frequencies. Further investigation of contrast sensitivity function–based frequency weighting is needed to better understand the relationship between contrast sensitivity and VA.
Corneal SA showed minimal impact on the correlation coefficients between simulated MTFa and clinical VA. Both matched cornea and average human cornea conditions produced similar R2 values, irrespective of lens type or frequency interval. This result aligns with the hypothesis that the 3 mm photopic pupil size employed in simulations mitigates the influence of SA, as larger apertures are more susceptible to aberration-induced image degradation.21–23 The insensitivity of the model to corneal SA under these conditions suggests that preclinical VA prediction may be robust to physiological variability in corneal shape, at least within the context of typical photopic pupil size.
The grouping of lens models for curve fitting further elucidated the nuances of MTFa-VA correlation. Analyzing all lenses together provided suitable fits (R2 ≥ 0.9), but division into non-diffractive and diffractive groups generally increased the correlation coefficients. This separation is supported by prior studies, such as those by Lang and Portney3 and Armengol et al,8 which highlighted the distinct optical behaviors of multifocal and monofocal IOLs. The present findings reinforce the importance of lens-specific modeling in preclinical evaluation and suggest that tailored fitting approaches may yield more precise predictions of clinical performance.
The comparison of monocular and binocular VA fittings shows differences in correlation coefficients, with binocular VA exceeding monocular VA, consistent with the phenomenon of binocular summation.25,26
Unlike previous studies that focused exclusively on either monocular or binocular data,3,6 the current analysis evaluated both, providing a more comprehensive assessment of predictive accuracy across clinical endpoints. The derived fitting coefficients for binocular VA closely matched those reported in ANSI Z80.35-2018,10 with simulated defocus curves differing by less than two letters from the regulatory standard, underscoring the validity of the simulation-based approach.
The use of optical simulations in lieu of bench measurements offers several advantages, including the flexibility to employ key parameters, such as lighting conditions, pupil size, and corneal aberration. However, this approach is contingent upon the model eye and the access to detailed IOL design specifications, which may be proprietary. The methodology described here could be extended to other vision science applications, such as contact lens evaluation or refractive surgery planning. Future studies should consider validating simulation results with optical bench measurements and exploring the effects of polychromatic vs monochromatic light for the correlation of MTFa to VA. Loicq et al12 recently linked MTF at 50 lp/mm to polychromatic MTFa, which is well correlated with the clinical VA, but emphasized the importance of frequency integrated metrics in predicting visual performance. Future work could expand upon the study of Loicq et al,12 along with the work in this article, to explore the correlation of monochromatic MTF or MTFa to clinical VA as an accurate predictor of visual performance in the early stages of pre-clinical development.
In the context of previous work, this study builds upon the foundational efforts of Alarcon et al2 and Vega et al,6 who established the utility of MTFa as a predictor of clinical VA. By integrating ray tracing simulations with clinical defocus curve data, the present analysis refines the predictive framework and addresses critical modeling variables that influence preclinical assessment. The findings support the adoption of simulated MTF metrics as standardized tools for IOL design and regulatory evaluation, in accordance with evolving international standards.10,11
These results underscore the value of preclinical VA prediction in identifying promising IOL designs at the early stage of the development processes, thereby improving optical quality, and reducing the risk of suboptimal lenses advancing to clinical trials. This also reduces the reliance on physical prototypes and accelerates the innovation cycle. The demonstrated robustness of the MTFa–VA correlation across the modeling parameters enhances confidence in simulation-based methodologies and provides a foundation for future innovation in the development of intraocular lenses.

## Conclusion
This study evaluated the correlations between preclinical simulated MTF metrics and clinical VA, including the effects of spatial frequency integration interval and SA. Using linear fits, MTFa was well correlated in predicting VA for pseudophakic patients for both monocular and binocular VA. For most cases, the frequency interval 0–100 lp/mm resulted in higher correlation coefficient values. The SA of the cornea did not show a strong impact on the correlations for VA fittings. Monocular and binocular fittings showed similar correlation coefficients for the prediction of VA. Resulting equations generated from this study will be used for IOL design, evaluating preclinical optical performance of novel IOLs, and predicting the visual performance for cataract patients.
