# Intra-orbital Foreign Body

## Abstract
AbstractBackground:This case report describes a young man with an intra-orbital foreign body (FB) in both eyes.Case Report:Intra-orbital FBs are rare, with few cases reported in the literature. This is because the orbit protects the globe from external forces, and the orbit is a closed cavity with one opening. This report details the case of a 28-year-old man who presented to the emergency unit with an 11-hour history of an intra-orbital FB. The patient was lying on a bench when a stationary motorcycle fell on him. He noticed that he was unable to open both eyes and was rushed to a peripheral hospital, then subsequently referred to this tertiary center. On examination, he was ill-looking and anxious but not febrile or pale. Systemic examination did not reveal any abnormality. Unaided visual acuity in the right eye (OD) was nil perception of light, while left eye visual acuity (OS) was counting fingers. The right eyelid was ptotic due to edema. He underwent basic laboratory investigations that were all normal. A plain X-ray revealed a radio-opaque FB penetrating the left orbit, fracturing the medial walls into the right orbit, with the round end positioned behind the right globe. The FB was identified as a broken part of a brake/clutch lever from the motorcycle. A computerized tomography scan showed a metallic object in both orbits, accompanied by pneumocephalus, and suggested transection of the right optic nerve. The patient was prepared for FB removal and repair of the lid penetration under general anesthesia, following review by the ENT surgeon and neurosurgeon. The brake lever was successfully removed, and the patient was administered antibiotics. Post-operatively, visual acuity in the right eye remained at nil perception of light, while that in the left eye improved to 6/9 upon discharge.Conclusion:There is no established protocol for managing intra-orbital FBs, as removal depends on factors such as the size, composition, and potential compression damage to structures around the globe, which could further compromise vision or function. Small inert objects may be left in place if removal would cause damage. However, in this case, removal was deemed necessary for several reasons.Keywords: Foreign body, intra-orbital, removal

## Introduction
The orbit provides protection for the globes, with the lateral wall being the strongest and the medial walls being the weakest. There are few reports on intra-orbital foreign bodies (FBs). They refer to objects that lie within the orbit but outside the globe, which usually occur after high-velocity trauma such as gunshot wounds.[1] These injuries are common among males aged 11 to 30 years.[2] The size, composition, location of the object, and the duration of the injury will determine the extent of the structural and functional damage.[3] These objects can be metallic or non-metallic.[4] Non-metallic objects include glass, plastic, and organic material such as vegetation.[4] Organic materials are associated with a high incidence of infection, while metallic objects such as iron, copper, and lead may cause complications such as siderosis, chalcosis, and systemic toxicity.[5]
Diagnostic imaging is vital in the management of intra-orbital FBs because it aids it determining the composition, size, and location of the object in relation to the globe and important structures.[6] Computed tomography scan (CT scan) shows objects as small as 0.06 mm3. It is relatively safe and quick to perform, unlike magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which is contraindicated in metallic objects because the magnetic field can dislodge them.[7] The prognosis of vision is worst with posteriorly located objects due to optic neuropathy.[8] Thorough investigation and examination are essential in managing intra-orbital FBs, with tetanus prophylaxis and broad-spectrum antibiotic instituted upon presentation. Furthermore, surgical management depends on the size and risk of infection, as surgical removal of the FB is controversial. Management is based on the preservation of the globe, extraocular muscles, and optic nerve. In this case report, we present a metallic brake lever lodged in both orbits.

## Case Report
A 28-year-old male patient presented to the ophthalmic emergency unit with a brake lever retained in the left orbit. According to the history provided, the patient was taking a nap beside a motorcycle when it fell on him, causing the brake lever to break and penetrate the left orbit. The patient was rushed to a peripheral eye center, where no attempt made to remove the object. The young man noticed that he could not see or open either of his eyes, with no associated loss of consciousness, convulsions, or change in behavior. The patient was hydrated with a liter of normal saline due to blood loss, tetanus vaccine given, and subsequently referred to this center after a plain X-ray of the skull was ordered.
The examination in the emergency room revealed a young conscious man who was oriented in time, person, and place. He appeared neither pale nor dehydrated and had stable vital signs. He was wearing a vest soaked with blood. On ophthalmological examination, severe eyelid edema with complete ptosis and mild proptosis were observed in the right eye. Meanwhile, in the left eye, a metallic handle was seen to have penetrated the orbit just beneath the junction of the medial 1/3 and lateral 2/3 of the superior orbital rim, displacing the left globe downwards. The lower lids obscured 2/3rd of the globe [Figure 1]. The object pushed the left upper lid into the orbit, with the object directed nasally. Both globes were frozen, showing restricted gaze in all directions. The anterior segment findings were normal, except for an unreactive mid-dilated pupil in the right eye.
Patient at presentation
The visible part of the metal handle penetrating the left orbit was 4 cm in length. Visual acuity in the right eye was nil perception of light, while in the left eye (on retraction of the lower lids), it was counting fingers at 1 m. Fundoscopy in the right eye revealed a mildly hyperemic disk with distinct margins with cup-to-disc ratio of 0.3, along with tortuous vessels and a flat retina. In contrast, the left eye exhibited a pink disk with distinct margins, a cup-to-disk ratio of 0.3, normal caliber and distribution of vessels, a flat retina, and a good macula reflex. Plain film radiography [Figure 2] and a CT scan were performed.
Plain skull X-ray showing an opaque FB in the orbits
The axial CT scan showed a radio-opaque linear object penetrating the left orbit and fracturing the ethmoidal bones, with the rounded tip positioned behind the right globe, pushing it forwards. Additionally, a fragment of ethmoidal bone on the right was observed to transect the right optic nerve. Areas of radiolucency around the frontal lobes were also evident, suggestive of pneumocephalus [Figure 3]. Both the ENT surgeons and neurosurgeons were consulted to review the patient. He received tetanus toxoid, analgesics, and prophylactic antibiotics. Blood samples were collected to prepare for FB removal under general anesthesia. The FB was extracted using controlled traction, taking care not to injure the globe. Upon removal of the brake lever, there was a gush of air and minimal cerebrospinal fluid leakage.
CT scan done pre-operatively showing a metallic FB and pneumocephalus
The globe was examined and found to be intact, with the left globe returning to its normal position intraoperatively. A laceration was observed on the left upper lid, which was sutured. The length of the brake handle was measured to be 11 cm [Figure 4].
Brave lever removed from the orbit measuring 11 cm in length
On the first day postoperatively, the patient had no perception of light in the right eye and 6/60 (Snellen’s chart) in the left eye, with restricted adduction in the right eye and elevation in the left eye. Over 2 weeks, there was gradual resolution of lid edema. A post-operative CT scan showed resolution of the pneumocephalus [Figure 5]. The patient was discharged after 2 weeks with complete resolution of the lid edema and visual acuity of no perception of light in the right eye and 6/9 on the left eye [Figure 6]. He was scheduled for a two-week follow-up appointment in both ophthalmology and neurosurgery clinics.
Post-operative CT scan with normal findings
Patient at 2 weeks post-FB removal
A follow-up appointment was conducted over the phone 4 weeks after discharge, as the patient resides in a distant village. He reported that he is doing well and that his vision was preserved in the left eye.

## Discussion
FBs in the orbit can cause vision loss due to injury to the globe or optic nerve. Visual prognosis depends on the extent of damage sustained at impact and, to a lesser extent, on the delay in surgical intervention, except in cases of compressive injury. There is no ideal management of retained intra-orbital FBs, and it remains a controversial subject. The decision regarding removal depends on the risk of infection, size, location, and composition of the object. It is important to note that damage can occur to structures during the withdrawal of foreign objects from the orbit.[1,2]
Metallic objects are well tolerated, especially if there is no indication for removal, and therefore can be managed conservatively. MRI is contraindicated in cases of metallic FBs because it can dislodge them, potentially causing more damage. FBs should be removed if they are impeding ocular movements or causing compression on a nerve or vessel. Additionally, objects made of copper should be considered for removal. The role of imaging is crucial in managing intra-orbital FBs, as it guides in precisely locating the object, thereby reducing damage to nearby structures.[1,5,6]
In this patient, the indication for the removal of the FB was the risk of infection, the size of the metallic lever, compression on the right optic nerve, and most importantly, the only seeing eye was depressed downwards by the object, resulting in a frozen globe.[9] The FB was removed by simple traction, which relieved the pneumoencephalus and helped in preserving vision in the left eye without requiring sophisticated surgery. The frozen globe was likely due to mechanical restriction upon presentation, but the mild restriction in adduction of the right eye and elevation of the left Eye suggests extraocular injury.
In conclusion, cases of metallic lever in the orbit are rare, and this patient represents one of the few cases. This case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in prognosticating orbital traumas, emphasizing the significance of imaging in diagnosis and management. The patient benefited from the straightforward surgical removal of the metallic object.
