# Class-attention pooling and token sparsity based vision transformers for chest X-ray interpretation

## Abstract
Chest radiography is the most widely used imaging technique for the diagnosing of lung diseases, but interpreting X-rays can be very difficult due to the subtle abnormalities and variations in image quality taken during X-rays. While deep learning models, especially the Convolution Neural Networks (CNNs), have shown strong performance when it comes to detecting pneumonia and other conditions, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have recently surpassed CNNs on several chest X-ray benchmarks by dividing images into small patches and learning global relationships. However, standard ViTs can sometimes focus on irrelevant regions, making their decisions less interpretable. To address this, we propose an enhanced ViT model tailored for chest X-ray analysis that prioritizes both accuracy and explainability. Our model introduces class-attention pooling technique, where each disease-specific class token learns to highlight relevant regions of the image, improving disease-wise focus. Token sparsity and random token dropping further help the model attend to only the most informative patches, enhancing robustness against noise. A convolutional stem is added before patch creation to extract fine local features like edges and textures, ensuring early capture of lung-specific patterns. Additionally, each X-ray undergoes preprocessing using Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), which enhances local contrast and makes subtle lesions more visible. The model is trained with mixed-precision computation, a warm-up cosine learning rate schedule, and the AdamW optimizer, allowing stable and efficient training on large datasets. It is then evaluated on Tuberculosis Chest X-Rays and Pulmonary Chest X-Rays datasets which are publicly available, the proposed framework achieved 99.19% Training accuracy, a Validation Accuracy of 97.78%, an F1-score of 0.94, and an AUC of 0.99, outperforming the baseline ViT. It is pointed out that the above scores are obtained based on the image-level split, owing to the limitations of the dataset, and the performance may be over-estimated compared to the validation on the patient level. The Grad-CAM heatmaps further confirm the fact that the model focuses on clinically relevant areas such as opacities or nodules, reinforcing interpretability and trust. Overall, this improved ViT framework offers both high diagnostic accuracy and also clear visual explanations, implying its possible usage in acting as an AI assistant for radiologists in efficiently detecting lung diseases.

## Introduction
With several decades of concerted control efforts and the presence of effective therapies, Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the most refractory and lethal infectious diseases worldwide. While the disease is generally confined to the lungs, it may also affect the brain, spine, lymph nodes, and kidneys. Its persistent burden across low and middle-income countries is influenced by crowding, delayed treatment, and unequal access to diagnostic services. These facts render early, precise, and easily deployable screening a pressing public health priority. Traditionally, laboratory diagnosis has depended on sputum smear microscopy, culture, and polymerase chain reaction-based tests. Though useful, these are laborious, infrastructure-driven, or insensitive in important subgroups, limiting large-scale screening. Chest X-ray screening bridges this limitation since it is cheap, non-invasive, and widely accessible. However, image interpretation is tedious and inconsistent, particularly under high volume. This has stimulated interest in computer-aided, learning-based systems capable of providing standardized assistance to clinicians.
Initial deep-learning models using convolutional neural networks set good baselines for detecting tuberculosis by learning hierarchical features from pixels directly and, in other instances, combining classification with visual rationales using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping1. More recent work provided stronger attention and architectural enhancements that enhanced sensitivity towards disease areas and stabilized training on small medical datasets2–4. More recently, Vision Transformers have been an attractive alternative since self-attention reasons across the whole image, capturing long-range dependencies and bilateral patterns that traditional convolutions can overlook5. Transformer-forward and hybrid designs have posted competitive or better performance compared to traditional convolutional neural networks on tuberculosis benchmarks while still being feasible to deploy if well-engineered6. Alongside this is clinical adoption, which relies on informative explanations with strong generalization. Comparing interpretability research has highlighted how explanation quality can vary by method and dataset, which further illustrates the value of pipelines that output repeatable, clinically interpretable heatmaps as opposed to headline metrics in isolation7. Ensembles and cross-architecture comparisons further emphasize the potential of transformer representations while identifying the value of thoughtful training recipes and equitable comparisons8,9. Along the way, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping continues to be a popular model for bridging prediction and human inspection, as long as it is done so in a manner that is balanced and true to the acquired evidence10.
This work proposes a Position Encoding Generator–augmented Vision Transformer with an improved explanation pipeline for self-supervised tuberculosis detection from chest radiographs. The Position Encoding Generator enforces spatial locality following patch embedding such that the network retains fine structure as it reasons globally. A light convolutional stem extracts low-level texture and edge information; class-attention pooling subsequently pools evidence from all patches into a compact, informative image representation. To encourage interpretability and stability, the training approach integrates a token-level prediction head with sparsity induction, stochastic drop of tokens, drop-path regularization, label-smoothing cross-entropy, warmup with cosine learning-rate decay, adaptive optimizer with decoupled weight decay, mixed-precision training, and exponential moving average of model weights. During pre-processing, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization equalizes contrast over heterogeneous studies. In order to ensure transparency, the explanation phase outputs equal numbers of tuberculosis-positive and normal samples with token-aligned, class-balanced heatmaps so the visual evidence is interpretable and reviewable for the radiologists.

## Literature review
The computer-aided detection of tuberculosis from chest X-ray images has, over the past decade, undergone significant improvement from initial feature-engineering methods to transformer-based models that can learn end-to-end. This section discusses relevant literature under four methodological categories: encoder-based architectures, convolutional neural network (CNN) methods, transformer-based models, and hybrid or explainable-AI models with incorporated interpretability components like Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM).

## Encoder-based and feature-extraction approaches
Early studies in tuberculosis image analysis relied on handcrafted features combined with classical machine-learning classifiers. Ignatius et al.11 used histogram-matched feature encoders with a support-vector-machine classifier on the Shenzhen dataset, achieving 88% accuracy but showing poor generalization to unseen hospitals. Urooj et al.12 introduced a stochastic-learning artificial-neural-network encoder that extracted multi-scale lung features. Although sensitivity improved to 91%, the system required extensive parameter tuning and was computationally expensive. In a similar vein, Rajakumar et al.13 employed Mayfly-algorithm optimization in choosing subsets of deep features from various encoders to achieve 94% accuracy on the Montgomery dataset but showed instability when there were artifacts in images or poor lighting. These papers laid the groundwork for automatic learning of representations but exposed the limitations of shallow or handcrafted encoders to learn intricate pulmonary patterns.

## Convolutional neural network (CNN) based methods
The invention of deep convolutional architecture revolutionized computer-aided tuberculosis screening. Sharma et al.14 developed deep learning models for tuberculosis detection and infected-region visualization in chest X-ray images using convolutional neural networks and explainability tools, achieving high diagnostic accuracy and robust lesion localization. Xu and Yuan4 applied coordinate-attention modules to upgrade a VGG-16 network to achieve an area-under-curve of 97.7% on multi-institutional datasets. Huy and Lin3 proposed an enhanced DenseNet-based CNN, termed CBAMWDNet, integrating channel and spatial attention mechanisms to refine lung-texture representations and improve tuberculosis detection accuracy to approximately 98.8% on benchmark chest X-ray datasets. Devasia et al.15 used a multi-label CNN to classify tuberculosis manifestations into lung zones, enhancing lesion-level interpretability at the cost of dense annotations that made it non-scalable. Although CNNs resulted in exceptional accuracy, their adoption of local receptive fields limited their ability to learn long-range dependencies, prompting the discovery of transformer-based architectures.

## Transformer-based frameworks
Transformers introduced global self-attention, enabling models to capture long-range spatial relationships across both lungs. Duong et al.5 integrated a Vision Transformer (ViT) module with EfficientNet, increasing tuberculosis-classification accuracy to 97% on the TBX11K dataset while reducing training time. El-Ghany et al.6 proposed a hybrid ViT with principal-component-analysis regularization to stabilize learning on small datasets, achieving an AUC of 0.98. Kotei and Thirunavukarasu9 combined DeiT and ResNet16 into a hybrid transformer-convolutional model that reached 99% accuracy and 0.995 AUC, demonstrating the efficiency of global attention for chest-radiograph interpretation. Vanitha et al.16 further incorporated Grad-CAM into a transformer backbone, revealing that transformer attention maps aligned more closely with radiological regions of interest, strengthening clinician confidence. Despite their promise, conventional transformers occasionally lost fine-grained spatial context because of fixed positional encodings.

## Hybrid and explainable AI approaches
To combine interpretability with diagnostic accuracy, several researchers explored hybrid frameworks integrating explainable-AI modules. Ennab and Mcheick7 compared pixel-level interpretability methods with Grad-CAM on X-ray diagnosis using VGG-19, confirming that Grad-CAM produced clearer lesion localization and better clinical usability. Rajaraman et al.8 ensembled CNN and transformer features for lateral chest-radiograph analysis, improving sensitivity for obscure lesions to 97.2%. Kotei and Thirunavukarasu9 and Vanitha et al.16 also confirmed that incorporating Grad-CAM into transformer models enhanced doctor trust with clear decision maps. Overall, these studies emphasized the importance of equilibrium interpretability in both positive and normal cases because classic Grad-CAM tended to highlight disease-positive areas only.

## Position-encoding and advanced regularization enhancements
Previous developments have been directed at improving spatial awareness and stability training in transformer architectures. The Position Encoding Generator (PEG) that has been proposed in this current work extends previous fixed-encoding approaches by dynamically inserting positional information at various attention layers17. Additional engineering decisions, such as class-attention pooling, token-sparsity loss, stochastic token drop, and drop-path regularization, all combine to make the model robust enough to learn stable global local features10. Label smoothing, mixed-precision training, and exponential moving-average weight updates have also proven to enhance model generalizability and reproducibility18. All these advancements combine to narrow the gap between clinical interpretability and diagnostic performance, making up the impetus for the PEG-Enhanced Vision Transformer with Balanced Grad-CAM discussed in this work.

## Proposed methodology

## Problem definition
Tuberculosis remains one of the deadliest infectious diseases worldwide. While radiological screening methods have significantly improved, manual interpretation of CXRs is inherently subjective and time-consuming and generally prone to inter-observer variability in results. The key aim of this research is to develop an automated, interpretable, computationally efficient framework for TB detection from CXR images through a refined architecture of the Vision Transformer, thus ensuring high reliability and transparency in diagnosis. The proposed model incorporates a Position Encoding Generator (PEG)19, Class-Attention pooling20, and Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)10 to provide high diagnostic performance together with visual interpretability.
The objectives are threefold:
(i)To enhance accuracy in TB detection with a hybrid approach incorporating ViT.(ii)To enhance positional and contextual representation learning by dynamic encoding.(iii)To provide explainable visual outputs highlighting the regions responsible for the classification decisions21.
To enhance accuracy in TB detection with a hybrid approach incorporating ViT.
To enhance positional and contextual representation learning by dynamic encoding.
To provide explainable visual outputs highlighting the regions responsible for the classification decisions21.

## Proposed algorithm
Input: Pre-processed CXR images (TB and Normal), model hyperparameters (epochs, learning rate).
Output: Predicted class labels (TB or Normal) and corresponding Grad-CAM heatmaps.
Step 1: Each CXR image is firstly resized to a uniform dimension and normalizes it using ImageNet mean and standard deviation values. Alternatively, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is applied on CXR images to amplify local contrast and accentuate subtle lung abnormalities.
Step 2: The pre-processed images are passed through the Conv-Stem-Lite module, consisting of depth-wise and point-wise convolutions followed by an SiLU activation to capture the low-level spatial feature representations.
Step 3: These feature maps are then divided into non-overlapping patches, each of which is flattened and linearly projected into a token embedding to be further processed in the transformer architecture.
Step 4: It uses the PEG, which computes adaptive positional encodings through depth-wise convolution and then combines them with the patch embeddings to retain important spatial contextual information.
Step 5: The encoded token sequence is passed through several Transformer encoder layers, which include Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA), Layer Normalization (LN), and a Feed-Forward Network (MLP) in order to obtain high-level features.
Step 6: Introduce learnable class token and perform class-attention pooling to aggregate the global contextual information from all patch tokens.
Step 7: The aggregated class token is fed to the linear classification head, which further outputs the class logits for the TB or Normal prediction.
Step 8: During training, the total loss is computed as a combination of cross-entropy loss and token sparsity loss. Model parameters are updated using the AdamW optimizer with cosine learning rate scheduling.
Step 9: In the process of inference, Grad-CAM heatmaps are generated by backpropagation of the TB class logit to the final convolutional feature map, which highlights informative regions of the lungs.
Step 10: The model provides the predicted class label, that is, TB or Normal, along with its Grad-CAM heatmap for clinical interpretation and diagnostic support.

## Dataset description
In this work, we make use of two publicly available tuberculosis chest X-ray datasets: the Tuberculosis (TB) Chest X-ray Dataset24, and the Pulmonary Chest X-ray Abnormalities Dataset25. These provide chest radiographs in standard medical imaging formats (PNG, JPG, JPEG, BMP, TIFF) that could be used for binary classification of TB-positive and normal cases. A harmonization pipeline was implemented for this dataset to maintain data integrity. This allowed automatic categorization of images into TB-positive and normal classes in a path-based fashion with keyword matching. Keywords such as ‘tuberculosis’, ‘positive’, and ‘infected’ were used for TB images and ‘normal’, ‘negative’, and ‘healthy’ for normal images. A deduplication procedure was executed based on filename and file size to remove redundant entries, making sure that each chest radiograph occurred only once in the dataset. The pre-processed dataset was organized into the Image-Folder hierarchy based on an image-level 85:15 train–validation split, stratified by class to maintain balance across classes, The split in the dataset was implemented with a fixed random seed = 42 for reproducibility, since patient identifiers were not available. Consequently, the split was done at the image level, not the patient level. Sample images for the Tuberculosis Chest X-ray dataset are presented in Fig. 2. Each image in this dataset was annotated with binary ground truth (0 = Normal, 1 = TB) to enable supervised learning. The validation dataset had a dual purpose: quantitative performance evaluation through traditional performance metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUROC, and qualitative assessment by way of Class Activation Map (CAM) visualization to support clinical interpretability of model predictions.

## Handling radiograph variability
In order to handle the variations in exposure, illumination, and contrast common in chest radiographs, all images are first resized to a standard resolution and then normalized before feeding into the model. During training, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) with probabilistic application, p = 0.7, clip limit = 2.0, and tile grid size = 8 × 8 enhances local contrast and simulates variable imaging conditions across datasets. Additionally, token sparsity regularization, random token dropping, mixed-precision training, and EMA weight stabilization all contribute to improving generalization by preventing overfitting to dataset-specific acquisition artifacts, which in turn promotes robustness under diverse clinical image conditions.

## Comparative insights
Because of this token-based representation, the Vision Transformer has better contextual learning than conventionally designed CNN-based architectures. Although a high accuracy on the training set, as well as on the validation set, is a strong indicator of a robust performance, these experiments should not be considered a demonstration of the superiority of the architecture, but rather a proof of the efficiency of the developed framework on the used datasets. Otherwise, it would be difficult to distinguish between the two factors: the architecture design and the dataset properties. While CNNs rely on a localized receptive field, ViT captures both fine-grained and holistic dependencies along the entire image, enabling the effective detection of subtle or low-contrast abnormalities. This global attention mechanism promotes enhanced recall and sensitivity by modelling long-range feature dependencies across the radiograph2728.
Recent studies have demonstrated that transformer-based models outperform traditional convolutional approaches such as DenseNet and ResNet in disease detection tasks, thus achieving salient improvements in F1-score and accuracy. For example, Singh et al. showed that ViT models yielded higher recall and precision values than CNNs in pneumonia detection performance29. Similarly, Regmi et al. obtained a high F1-score of 0.95 and ROC-AUC of 0.97 for the ViT-based classifier on chest X-ray and gastrointestinal image datasets, reflecting a consistent 6–8% improvement over CNN baselines30. Moreover, comparisons corroborated that ViTs capture better contextual representation and are more robust against inter-class variability in medical image analysis31. Therefore, all these studies confirm the better performance of the ViT model in global feature reasoning and learning cross-region dependencies.

## Discussion and interpretation
The obtained results confirm that the Vision Transformer can be used as a reliable CAD module for radiography screening from a diagnostic point of view. High precision and recall values significantly reduce both Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors. The transformer successfully captures feature-level diversity, improving its internal representation of pathological regions, as evidenced by the progressive learning pattern where recall increases from 0.36 to 0.89 over epochs. Moreover, even in the face of high inter-class variability, the model is able to distinguish well between normal and diseased samples, which is reflected by the near-unity AUC of 0.9945 validating near-ideal discriminative capability. This will overall set up an accurate, scalable solution for automatic chest X-ray disease classification with high generalizability using the proposed Vision Transformer-based framework, with significantly higher interpretability and statistical reliability compared to conventional approaches with convolution architecture. These GRAD-CAM visualization results point to areas that correspond to findings from radiological interpretations. It must be noted that despite these visualization results appearing to represent anatomy realistically, these results have not been clinically validated by a team of radiologists.

## Conclusion
In this work, we propose an effective token-sparse ViT framework with strong training techniques and class-attention pooling for accurate and explanatory chest X-ray disease classification. Token sparsity leads the model to learn how to suppress unimportant background signals and only focus on the most discriminative patches, which yields sharper attention maps for more focused learning. Class-attention pooling enhances the image-level representations by incorporating the crucial regional information based on a certain class token, which increases visibility and leads to a better performance. Furthermore, we enhance the generalization and robustness of our framework when dealing with different data distributions via incorporating several other techniques, including label smoothing, drop-path regularization, and stochastic token drop. This brings in explainable visualizations by incorporating balanced token-level Grad-CAM analysis, an essential ingredient for clinical deployment, into the decision-making process. These collectively contribute to improved diagnostic accuracy and foster confidence among healthcare providers in the adoption of AI-powered radiology tools. This architecture can be further extended with multimodal medical imaging and multi-disease classification, where modalities such as CT and MRI will be put together for better diagnostics. Further refinement of adaptive attention mechanisms and token sparsity strength can yield dynamic inference with reduced computation cost without accuracy loss. Furthermore, it would also enable training across decentralized hospital data without the loss of data confidentiality with the integration of federated or privacy-preserving learning paradigms. In all, the proposed approach constitutes a novel direction towards effective and explainable yet reliable Vision Transformers in medical imaging, connecting dependable clinical decision support to complex AI architectures.

## Future scope and limitations

## Limitations and generalisation scope
The scope of the current assessment is restricted to binary tuberculosis classification, owing to the binary nature of the annotations in publicly available datasets employed in this work. In effect, the performance of the model has not been tested on more uncommon disease types, which are typically more varied, similar to multi-label chest X-ray collections, where a patient might have multiple simultaneously co-existing pathologies. The performance of the current architecture on such a problem setting is still to be ascertained.

## Clinical applicability and multi-label diagnosis
The current study focuses only on single-label tuberculosis classification, reflecting the labelling structure of the datasets that were used. Correspondingly, the current model has not been tested for the simultaneous detection of multiple thoracic pathologies-a process that is usually needed during routine diagnostics. While the existing architecture easily supports an extension to a multi-label classification task, such empirical testing remains to be done. Future studies will thus be directed at the adaptation and validation of the framework within large-scale, multi-label chest X-ray data sets in order to establish its clinical scalability.

## Management of prediction errors in clinical settings
This proposed system generates probability-confidence scores for its output predictions via the softmax layer, which defines how confident the system is with regard to a particular prediction. Although models that specifically define uncertainty are not used in this work, probability-confidence scores can still be used by radiologists to screen patients via a certain threshold level. This system is meant to serve as a tool that complements a radiologist’s assessment, such that all predictions are still checked by radiologists. The potential false positives can thus be readily confirmed or ruled out, with doubtful or low-confidence predictions possibly warranting closer evaluation to avoid cases of missed diagnoses. The addition of interpretation aids such as Grad-CAM explains the model’s results with a focus on anatomy-related regions of the input image.

## Extension to 3D imaging modalities
The current framework has been designed and tested only on 2D chest radiograph analysis. While volumetric modalities like CT are not explored within this work, the basic architectural elements comprising class-attention pooling and token sparsity mechanisms are intrinsically general and could thus be extended to 3D Vision Transformer models working on volumetric patches. This would nevertheless entail the development of dedicated 3D patch embedding methods and much greater computational resources, and as such remains a direction of future investigation.

## Deployment considerations
Although the current research is centered on developing and validating a model, it is not a complete clinical implementation, but the devised architecture is also compatible with a standard clinical imaging inference loop on commonly available GPU machines. The complete clinical implementation includes integrating the trained model into a PACS environment for institutes, with the predicted probabilities and Grad-CAM analysis displayed side by side with the original images for radiologic interpretation. Threshold calibration would be needed to align sensitivity specificity trade-offs with local screening protocols. Such comprehensive prospective testing addressing clinical latency, workflow integration, and usability will be essential for routine clinical deployment, and continues to be a focus for future work.

## Data splitting considerations
For this evaluation, train/validation split definitions were differentiated at the image level because a patient-level identity did not exist consistently across all used databases. Although this represents a typical practice in publicly available CXR databases, a potential problem of subject-level information leakage may occur when more than one image from the same patient is included. Future assessments would emphasize databases which assist patient-level identity annotations that facilitate splitting on a subject level.

## External generalization considerations
The present study solely depends on internal training-validation splits on publicly available data. No independent external test unit was utilized, and hence cross-institutional generalization remains to be conclusively validated. The performances reported here are good, although they can partly be indicative of dataset-specific qualities. The follow-up efforts are thus bound for the testing of the framework on externally available chest X-ray units from independent healthcare institutions to establish real life robustness and clinical transferability.

## Interpretability validation considerations
The Grad-CAM visualization results obtained in this work are meant to serve as a qualitative tool for interpreting what contributes to predictions made by models. This research, though, has not formally been clinically verified to have anatomic correspondence with pathologic findings by expert radiologists. Future work would include studies with resident radiologists to evaluate the accuracy of attention localization and potential use in enhancing diagnostic confidence on a quantitative scale.
