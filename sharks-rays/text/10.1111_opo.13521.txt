Optical characterisation and vision quality assessment of two myopia control contact lenses.

# Optical characterisation and vision quality assessment of two myopia control contact lenses

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeThis investigation examined the image and vision quality of two commercially available daily disposable myopia control soft contact lenses.MethodsWavefront errors were measured with an SHS Ophthalmic aberrometer for two myopia control soft contact lenses: a coaxially designed dual‐focus lens (omafilcon A, CooperVision MiSight® 1 day, MS1d) and a design employing multiple add powers that included non‐coaxial optics in annular add zones (senofilcon A, Johnson & Johnson Vision ACUVUE® Abiliti™ 1‐Day, AB). Geometric optics ray tracing generated point‐spread functions and wave optics were used to compare modulation transfer functions (MTFs) and simulated letter images. Twenty‐six myopic children completed a randomised, non‐dispensing, contralateral double‐masked clinical trial. After 1 h of wear, right and left eye visual acuity (VA), subjective vision quality and lens preference (Likert) were assessed while viewing monocularly.ResultsThe lens containing non‐coaxial optics employed a small central zone with approximately +10.00 D of added power and two annular rings with a power gradient typical of non‐coaxial optics. The coaxial design contained a centre zone with a distance correction and two annular zones with a fixed add power of approximately +2.00 D. MTFs and simulated images were better with small pupils, which was most noticeable with the coaxial design. Distance VA was ‐0.02 ± 0.04 with MS1d and 0.09 ± 0.08 with AB, p < 0.01. The majority of participants (77%) reported a preference for one lens; 54% preferred the MS1d and 23% preferred the AB lens.ConclusionsMyopia control contact lenses employing coaxial or a mixture of coaxial and non‐coaxial optics both reduced retinal image contrast but successfully imaged high spatial frequencies and provided high quality of vision. Image and vision quality were slightly superior in the lens employing coaxial optics alone.Keywords: coaxial, contact lens, myopia, non‐coaxial, optics

## Key points
Ex‐vivo optical aberrometry and on‐eye visual performance were examined for myopia control contact lenses using coaxial or a mix of coaxial and non‐coaxial add powers.
The design with non‐coaxial add power introduced a greater drop in image contrast and slightly inferior visual performance.
The use of non‐coaxial optics did not reduce the optical and visual consequences of adding myopia control optics to contact lenses.

## INTRODUCTION
Although clinically manifesting as a refractive problem, myopia is a pathology of accelerated eye growth during childhood and adolescence and a significant risk factor for retinal pathology later in life. 1 , 2 Studies in animal models have shown that accelerated eye growth can be slowed with dual‐focus optics, which include added plus power 3 that lowers image contrast. 4 Dual‐focus optics have been included in both contact 5 and spectacle lenses 6 , 7 for the treatment of myopia. This strategy has proven effective at slowing axial elongation and myopia progression in children and teenagers. 5 , 8 , 9
A fundamental challenge of these myopia control interventions is balancing the competing demands of slowing axial elongation while maintaining good visual quality. Different lens designs have assigned more or less area within a lens for myopia control 10 , 11 and include more or less add power in these myopia control zones. 10 , 12 , 13 Although various levels of defocus and area of myopia control have been utilised, most studies of these lenses have reported visual acuity (VA) better than 0.00 logMAR, 5 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 indicating that it is possible to include myopia control optics while achieving high‐quality VA.
Two myopia control soft contact lenses, 10 , 14 each employing radially varying added plus power but with different myopia control zone geometries, areas and powers, are regionally available in North America, Europe, and Asia. One lens includes added plus power into two annular regions, both sharing a common (coaxial) axis with the distance correction 15 , 16 while reserving the central zone of the lens for distance correction. The add annuli of the second lens include non‐coaxial optics with some added coaxial plus power in the lens centre. 10 , 17 , 18 , 19 Non‐coaxial optics can be used to reduce the resultant blur levels from added plus power with the intent of minimising the impact of added plus power on vision. 6 , 7 , 17 , 20 The purpose of the current investigation was to examine the optical properties, imaging characteristics, and participant‐reported experience associated with these differing optical designs.

## METHODS

## Lens measurement
Optical characteristics of two myopia control soft contact lenses, Johnson & Johnson Vision ACUVUE® Abiliti™ 1‐Day (AB, jjvision.com) and MiSight® 1 day (MS1d, coopervision.com), with labelled powers of −3.00 D were measured using a single‐pass Shack–Hartmann wavefront sensor (SHS Ophthalmic Optocraft GmbH, optocraft.de/en). Contact lenses were placed in a wet cell and carefully centred on the instrument measurement axis. The aberrometer (λ = 540 nm) sampled the lens every 39 μm over an 8‐mm diameter circular area.

## Lens power and image quality calculations
Wavefront errors were exported to custom MATLAB (mathworks.com) software (Indiana Wavefront Analyzer). 6 Horizontal and vertical tilts (prism) and piston of the wavefronts were subtracted. After refractive index correction from saline to air, 21 the optical power was calculated at each point in the pupil as local wavefront slope/r (radial distance) or as local curvature (Laplace transform). 22 Spherical wavefronts corresponding to the labelled power were subtracted from the measured wavefront errors as if the distance optic of the lens was perfectly focused, simulating an aberration free −3.00 D myope wearing a –3.00 D lens. Lens optics with the distance optic focused were evaluated for pupil diameters of 3, 4, 5 and 6 mm. Ray tracing diagrams were calculated from vectors normal to the wavefront at each sampled point. Modal reconstruction was performed to reduce noise in the experimental data and improve ray diagram presentation (Figure 1). This was performed by zone using Zernike polynomials to avoid fitting issues at sharp zone transitions. Image plane point‐spread functions (PSFs) were calculated first by ray tracing and assessing the ray density at the image plane along the optical axis by means of a histogram. 23 PSFs were also created by performing a Fourier transform of the pupil function to generate a wave optics PSF. ## Clinical assessments
A clinical cohort of 26 myopic participants (17 males) aged 8–15 years (mean [standard deviation] = 11.6 [2.1]) with no history of contact lens wear or myopia control intervention completed a randomised, non‐dispensing, contralateral double‐masked clinical trial. Mean [standard deviation] spherical equivalent subjective refractive error for the right and left eyes was −2.18 [0.99] and −2.02 [1.01] D, respectively. The lens design assigned to each eye was randomised and allowed to settle for 10 min before conducting an over‐refraction. If over‐refractions revealed a residual error of 0.25 or greater, a new lens was fitted utilising the sum of the nominal lens power and the measured over‐refraction. Lens preference (Likert), subjective vision (0–100 scale, 100 = best) and distance VA were assessed monocularly with the right eye followed by the left eye after 1 h of lens wear. Finally, participants indicated whether their reason for the lens preference was because of either better comfort or better vision. All clinical procedures were conducted at the University of Waterloo, were approved by the Research Ethics Committee and performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Categorical Likert responses for lens preference and the dichotomous reason for lens preference were recorded. Differences between lenses for subjective ratings, VA and lens preference were evaluated using the Wilcoxon matched pairs, whereas the Chi‐squared test of independence was used to indicate the significance level of the dichotomous lens preference reason in which responses for no preference were evenly split between lenses. Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (v4.3.0; R Core Team 2023—r‐project.org).

## RESULTS

## Lens power and image quality calculations
Individual uniformly distributed rays (N = 205,890) derived from wavefront slopes across an 8‐mm diameter pupil (4096 rays/mm2) were plotted as they approached the retina of the AB (top) and MS1d (bottom) designs in Figure 1. Rays focused at the retinal plane indicate lens powers ideal for correcting the model spherical refractive error (−3.00 D). Blue rays correspond to rays passing through the distance correction zones of the contact lens. The lens with non‐coaxial add power in two annuli (AB [top], inner add annulus rays plotted in orange) also includes high add coaxial power in a small central zone (green). Rays passing through the inner narrow ~0.65 mm annuli of add power (orange) produced local foci at approximately 6.00 D anterior to the retinal plane that were laterally displaced from the optical axis by approximately 0.1 mm. Rays passing through the central coaxial zone (green) crossed the optical axis 10.00 D anterior to the retinal plane. The non‐coaxially focused rays crossed the optical axis between the distance focus and 1.80 D anterior to this focus. The coaxial design (MS1d, bottom) includes a large central zone containing the distance correction and rays from the two annular add zones (orange) that cross the axis when focused approximately 2.00 D in front of the retinal plane. Rays from the distance correction zones may not focus exactly on the nominal focal plane (retinal plane) due to negative spherical aberration of the measured negative lenses, resulting in outer zone distance corrections focusing slightly behind the retina (blue rays in the top and bottom schematics). The negative spherical aberration of these lenses may be partially cancelled by ocular positive spherical aberration when on the eye.

Geometrical PSFs at the distance focal plane (Figure 3) for the AB (top row) and MS1d (bottom row) lens designs are computed for 3, 4, 5 and 6 mm pupil diameters. As pupil size increases, certain lens zones contribute proportionally more or less of the total light, reducing the intensity of certain components of the PSF. Low‐intensity regions of the PSFs created by defocused optics were enhanced by treating them as high dynamic range (HDR) images to which a tone map was applied in MATLAB. With a 3‐mm pupil, the MS1d design PSF has a small central core created by the focused distance optic, whereas the PSF for the design with non‐coaxial optics (AB) also includes a blur disc (35 arcmin diameter) produced by the approximately +10.00 D add included in the centre of the lens. With the 4, 5 and 6 mm pupil diameters, an annular halo is observed in the PSF of the MS1d design created by the inner coaxial add ring of the lens, whereas an additional blur disc can be seen in the PSF of the non‐coaxial lens created by the non‐coaxial add optics in the annular zone. The halo and second blur circle outer diameters are 27 and 24 arcmin for the MS1d and AB lens designs, respectively. As the pupil diameters increase to 5 and 6 mm, the added focused light in the PSF of the non‐coaxial design lowers relative intensities of the 35 and 24 arc minute blur discs in these normalised PSFs.

The image quality generated by both lens designs was quantified using wave optics MTFs with 3, 4, 5 and 6 mm pupils (Figure 4). In each case, the distance optic has been focused. As pupil size increases, the MTF values decline. The higher image contrasts generated by the MS1d design were largest with the 3 mm pupil and present at all spatial frequencies (SFs). With 5 and 6 mm pupils, the greater contrast achieved with the coaxial MS1d design was retained at higher SFs (>5 cycles per degree [c/d]), whereas the non‐coaxial AB design generated higher contrasts over a narrow range of low SFs (1–5 c/d). The area under the MTF ratio (AB/MS1d) is 0.65, 0.59, 0.85 and 0.72, respectively, for 3, 4, 5 and 6 mm pupils. At the 6/6 scale (30 cycles per degree), the MTF ratios of AB/MS1d were 0.53, 0.46, 0.75 and 0.28 for respective pupil diameters of 3, 4, 5 and 6 mm.

Images with the contact lens centred (panel a) and decentred (panel b) by the average on‐eye decentration of soft contact lenses (−0.23 mm temporal and −0.08 mm inferior) 25 were computed for all four pupil diameters. ## Clinical assessments
Monocular photopic high‐contrast distance logMAR VA (left) and patient‐reported distance vision ratings (right) (0–100 rating, 100 = best) for both the AB (green) and MS1d (purple) lens designs after 1 h of lens wear are shown in Figure 6. On average, vision quality was high and VA was close to 6/6 with both lenses. A matched pairs test revealed that, on average, myopic subjects rated their vision significantly better while wearing the MS1d (mean standard error [SE] = 88 [14]) than the AB (mean [SE] = 79 [18]) lenses (p < 0.01). Similarly, distance logMAR VA was slightly, yet significantly better with the MS1d lens (mean [SE] = 0.02 [0.04]) compared to the AB lens (mean [SE] = 0.09 [0.08]) (p < 0.01).

## DISCUSSION
This study evaluated the optical and visual performances of myopia control soft contact lenses that utilise concentric lens geometries featuring distance correction zones interleaved with plus powered add zones. These designs included differences in zone geometries and add powers (Figure 2). Specifically, the “non‐coaxial” design (AB) incorporated a central region with add power and non‐coaxial add power in annular rings, while the “coaxial” lens design (MS1d) incorporated a central distance vision correcting zone and alternating add power rings. These geometric and power variations created complex PSFs at the distance focal plane that included well‐focused light combined with blur circles and annuli for both lenses (Figure 3). However, despite the presence of significant blurred light in the PSFs, both designs were able to image +0.30 logMAR characters over a wide range of pupil sizes with and without lens decentration (Figure 5). Both lenses produced contrast demodulation that can be seen in the MTFs (Figure 4) and in the reduced contrast of the simulated +0.30 logMAR images (Figure 5). Most eyes fitted with these lenses had VAs within one line of 0.00 logMAR and experienced high vision quality and comfort (Figures 6 and 7).
Imaging small letters and achieving excellent VA in the presence of high levels of defocus (e.g., +2.00, +4.00 or +10.00 D) seems surprising. However, although increasing levels of defocus reduce the MTF, 26 contrast sensitivity 27 and VA 28 in monofocal systems, this is not the case in a bifocal system. 29 De Gracia et al. showed that when a focused image and a defocused image are present simultaneously in a bifocal system, increasing defocus in the second image can lead to increased image contrast and VA. 29 As the defocus is increased, the contrast reduction can then be visible when viewing larger objects, but the amount of contrast reduction is determined primarily by the proportion of defocused light. 4 Using the lens geometries shown in Figure 2, the proportion of the pupil areas covered by myopia control optics was approximately 25%, 58%, 37% and 26% for AB and 0%, 28%, 45% and 32% for MS for 3, 4, 5 and 6 mm pupil diameters, respectively. These results emphasise that including up to and even exceeding 50% defocused light will lower image contrast while retaining good VA. 30
However, overall the differences in the optical designs of the two lenses tested produced small differences in image quality (higher MTF values [Figure 4] and slightly sharper images [Figure 5] for the coaxial design). The small differences in VA (Figure 6) and vision quality (Figure 7) between these lenses are consistent with previous reports (e.g., monocular VA of −0.03 with the coaxial design 31 compared with +0.02 with the non‐coaxial design 5 ). Both lenses have demonstrated myopia control efficacy by slowing axial elongation in the eyes of myopic children. 5 , 10 Because of uncertainties in the mechanism of action for these myopia control interventions, 32 it is difficult to attribute differences in efficacy to any specific aspect of the myopia control optics. The features common to both designs include light focused in front of the retina and reduced image contrast. When lenses have similar efficacy and optical quality, clinical treatment choices may be based on individual patient preferences or characteristics rather than myopia control or vision, such as patient‐perceived comfort.
