# Microstructure and transcriptomics reveal rapid light-induced visual plasticity in the non-obligate cave dweller Oreolalax rhodostigmatus (Megophryidae, Anura)

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundPhenotypic plasticity is a specific area of interest that refers to the ability of an organism to alter its phenotype in response to environmental changes. Adaptive visual plasticity is a fascinating phenomenon observed in various animal species. Cave-dwelling organisms often exhibit visual degeneration, but non-obligate species like Oreolalax rhodostigmatus may retain adaptive plasticity. Currently, no laboratory simulations of light-induced conditions have been reported. This study investigated the morphological, histological, and molecular responses of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles to prolonged light exposure, hypothesizing structural retinal remodeling and upregulation of phototransduction genes reflecting reversible plasticity.ResultsHistological analysis revealed significant thickening of retinal layers (particularly inner retinal and ganglion cell layers) and structural refinement of photoreceptor outer segments in light-exposed (3- and 10-week) groups compared to dark-adapted controls (0-week). Immunohistochemistry showed restoration of organized rod and cone morphology after light exposure. Transcriptomic profiling identified 2,937-4,847 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups. Key phototransduction genes (GNAT1, GRK7, CRYBA2, BEST2, MYO3B) were significantly upregulated in light-exposed tadpoles, while the cave-adaptation associated photoreceptor gene s26 was downregulated. Ultrastructural analysis indicated increased mitochondrial density and improved photoreceptor structure following light treatment.ConclusionProlonged light exposure induces significant structural, ultrastructural, and molecular adaptations in the visual organs of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles, including retinal layer thickening, photoreceptor regeneration, and upregulation of phototransduction pathways. The downregulation of s26 and absence of mutations common in obligate cavefish suggest retained latent pathways enabling reversible plasticity, rather than irreversible degeneration. This adaptive plasticity allows non-obligate cave dwellers to balance energy conservation in darkness with functional vision restoration upon light exposure, representing a distinct evolutionary strategy compared to obligate cave species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12379-y.Keywords: Oreolalax rhodostigmatus, Visual organs, Plasticity, Adaptive evolution, Genetic basis, Retinal degeneration

## Introduction
Phenotypic plasticity is a fundamental biological concept that describes an organism’s capacity to modify its phenotype in response to environmental fluctuations. This adaptability permits species to adjust their phenotypic traits in response to environmental changes without necessitating alterations to their genetic code [1]. Such adaptability is essential for survival and reproduction in dynamic environments. The mechanism underlying phenotypic plasticity facilitates organisms’ adaptation to diverse environmental conditions, thereby playing a critical role in their survival and evolutionary success [2]. A notable area of investigation within the broader context of phenotypic plasticity is visual phenotypic plasticity. Adaptive visual plasticity represents a compelling phenomenon observed across various animal species [3]. This form of plasticity enables organisms to modify their visual systems in response to environmental changes, thereby ensuring optimal visual performance under diverse conditions. Such adaptability is particularly pronounced as visual systems are intricately calibrated to their specific ecological niches [4] and light environments [5]. For example, research has demonstrated that fish lenses possess a significant degree of optical plasticity [6], which is essential for sustaining visual acuity across varying light conditions. The crystalline lens in fish serves as the primary refractive element, and its complex optical properties are subject to meticulous regulation [7]. This regulatory process encompasses both circadian reversible adjustments and irreversible developmental tuning, enabling fish to optimize the transmission of visual information to the retina in a range of environmental contexts.
Visual plasticity is essential for sustaining optimal visual function across varying lighting conditions and developmental stages. Research on the Drosophila larval visual system illustrates how fluctuations in sensory input can result in substantial structural plasticity within dendritic arbors, as well as alterations in physiological output. This study identifies the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) pathway as a pivotal element in the regulation of these experience-dependent modifications, thereby highlighting the complex interplay between sensory input and neuronal architecture [8]. Furthermore, in the developing brain, visual stimulation can elicit coordinated changes in neuronal responses and dendritogenesis, as evidenced by rapid time-lapse two-photon calcium imaging. The transcription factor MEF2A/2D is instrumental in modulating neuronal responses to stimuli that promote plasticity, orchestrating both structural and functional adaptations. This underscores the critical role of transcription factors in modulating plasticity thresholds during the formation of neural circuits [9].
Research on reef fish has demonstrated that their retinas exhibit rapid and reversible changes in response to varying light conditions, characterized by significant alterations in opsin gene expression and retinal cell densities [10]. This phenotypic plasticity enables fish to adapt to transient fluctuations in their light environment, such as those caused by algal blooms and light pollution, thereby illustrating the retina’s capacity for swift molecular and cellular adaptations in response to environmental stimuli [10]. Furthermore, investigations into dark exposure in adult animals reveal that visual deprivation can elicit critical period-like plasticity within the visual cortex, involving modifications in the expression of synaptic molecules and the structural organization of interneurons. These findings suggest that inhibitory circuits and plasticity-related molecules are integral to the visual system’s adaptation processes during adulthood [11]. Such plastic responses persist into adulthood in specific populations, highlighting the significance of phenotypic plasticity in the rapid evolutionary adaptations of the visual system in response to novel environmental challenges [12]. Collectively, these studies elucidate the intricate interplay between light-induced morphological and molecular alterations and visual plasticity, thereby emphasizing the adaptability of the visual system across various species and developmental stages.
Phenotypic plasticity plays a crucial role in the adaptation and survival of non-obligate cave-dwelling species. These organisms, unlike obligate cave dwellers, do not spend their entire life cycle in subterranean environments and thus must adapt to varying conditions both inside and outside caves. The ability to exhibit phenotypic plasticity allows these species to rapidly adjust their physiological and morphological traits in response to environmental changes, such as variations in light, temperature, and humidity [13]. A notable example of phenotypic plasticity in non-obligate cave dwellers is observed in the red-spotted toothed toads, Oreolalax rhodostigmatus. This species is endemic to China and is primarily found in the karst mountainous regions of southwestern China [14]. Most tadpole populations of this species typically inhabit dark caves for several years. Meanwhile, some populations are also known to reside in illuminated streams [14]. Furthermore, juvenile and adult frogs are often observed foraging outside of cave environments [15]. Research into the transcriptomes of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles has revealed that genes involved in melanogenesis, such as TYR, TYRP1, and DCT, as well as their transcriptional factor MITF, show increased transcription in response to light. This suggests that morphological color change plays a dominant role in their optical plasticity. Additionally, the transcription of genes related to growth factor, MAPK, and PI3K-Akt pathways also increases with light exposure, indicating that light induces significant growth signals that may facilitate rapid skin darkening [16].
Despite significant advances in our understanding of visual systems, the mechanisms by which vision adapts to variations in light environments remain inadequately explored. Notably, while the majority of tadpoles thrive in the dark conditions of cave habitats, they also demonstrate the capacity to survive in environments with typical light exposure. Our observations reveal that when tadpoles from dark caves are transferred to a well-lit laboratory setting for a specified duration, a marked alteration in the interocular distance occurs. This phenomenon may be indicative of the rapid adaptation of their visual organs to fluctuating light conditions. However, to date, there has been a lack of dedicated research aimed at elucidating the underlying mechanisms of this intriguing adaptation. Consequently, the O. rhodostigmatus serves as an exemplary model for investigating the rapid adaptation of visual organs to both dark and illuminated environments.We hypothesized that prolonged exposure to light would induce structural remodeling of retinal layers and upregulate phototransduction genes, indicative of adaptive plasticity in non-obligate cave dwellers. The species O. rhodostigmatus requires approximately five years to complete its metamorphosis, during which it remains in stage 25 for an extended period characterized by minimal changes in ocular structures [17]. To test our hypothesis, we investigated the effects of light on the visual organs of tadpoles at stage 25 through histological, morphological, and comparative transcriptomic analyses. By analyzing variations in retinal structure and morphology, as well as the expression of vision-related genes under different durations of light exposure, this study explores how the visual organs of tadpoles undergo rapid adaptive evolution from dark to normal light conditions. This research not only establishes a foundation for understanding the mechanisms underlying visual plasticity in O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles within cave ecosystems but also serves as a reference for future investigations into the adaptations of cave-dwelling amphibians and their conservation strategies.

## Meterials and methods

## Sample collection
In October 2022, a total of 45 O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles were collected and examined from a cave and its associated underground river, totally devoid of light, located in Gaopo Township, Guiyang City, Guizhou Province (26.232566°N, 106.826192°E, at an elevation of 1205 m above sea level). Following collection, the tadpoles were transported to the laboratory and housed in culture tanks measuring 140 cm × 160 cm × 80 cm. These tanks were equipped with a water recycling and filtration system, and the light intensity was maintained at 500 lx, as measured by an LI-250 A light meter (LI-COR). A 12:12 h light-dark cycle was implemented (light from 8:00 am to 8:00 pm and dark from 8:00 pm to 8:00 am). The light sources, consisting of LED tubes (Philips Master TL-D 90 DeLuxe), emitted a wavelength range of 400–700 nm, simulating the natural daylight spectrum. The culture water was aerated, and its temperature was maintained between 10 °C and 18 °C. Water replacement occurred every three days, with half of the water volume being renewed. The tadpoles were fed minced meat, lettuce, algae, and rotifers twice daily, with any remaining minced meat removed one hour after each feeding. All zoological experiments were conducted with the approval of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou University.
The species O. rhodostigmatus exhibits an extended growth and development period, with approximately two years required for fertilized eggs to undergo complete metamorphosis. Anuran embryos and larvae progress through 46 distinct developmental stages; notably, O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles remain in stage 25 for an extended duration of about one year [17], while development at other stages occurs relatively rapidly. To mitigate the influence of developmental stage on visual perception [18], we selected tadpoles at stage 25 as the subjects for our experiments. A total of 33 tadpoles were allocated into three groups: EEG-1 (0-week control, n = 11), EEG-2 (3-week light treatment, n = 11), and EEG-3 (10-week light treatment, n = 11). For each group, the eyeballs of five tadpoles were pooled into a single sample and preserved in liquid nitrogen for RNA sequencing. The eyeballs of three tadpoles were fixed in a tissue tube containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde for ultrastructural analysis. The remaining three tadpoles were placed in a tissue tube filled with Bonn’s solution for structural observation. Additionally, 12 tadpoles were randomly assigned to four groups for immunohistochemical fluorescence staining at 0, 2, 4, and 6 weeks. All tadpoles were maintained at Gosner stage 25 throughout the experiment, confirmed via morphological criteria [19]. No metamorphic changes were observed during the 10-week treatment.

## Morphological, histological and immunohistochemical analyses
Prior to sampling, a series of morphological measurements were conducted on the tadpoles, which included body length, weight, tail length, body height, interocular distance, eye diameter, head length posterior to the eyes, orbital diameter, overall head length, and head width. These measurements were obtained using an electronic Vernier caliper (Mitutoyo 500-196-30, Japan). Statistical analyses, including regression analysis of interocular distance and eye diameter, were performed using GraphPad Prism Software (San Diego, California, USA). Multiple comparisons of interocular distance, eye diameter, and other variables were conducted using one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s post-hoc test applied across the samples.
For each experimental group, tadpoles were euthanized using MS-222 (100 mg/L, Sigma Chemical Company, St. Louis, MO) at 8:00 am, after which their eyes were excised and fixed in Bouin’s fluid for subsequent histological analysis. Following a 24-hour fixation period, serial paraffin sections were prepared, and the slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) in accordance with established protocols [20]. Sample preparation for transmission electron microscopy was conducted following standardized procedures, utilizing an ultrathin slicing mechanism to obtain sections approximately 60–90 nm in thickness. The resulting images were captured using a JEM-1400FLASH transmission electron microscope manufactured by Nippon Electronics. Additionally, cryosectioning and immunostaining of fixed fish eyes (three individuals per group) were performed as previously described [21]. Monoclonal antibodies targeting Zpr-1 (ab134970: 1:200; Abcam, Shanghai, China) and 4D2 (ab98887: 1:200; Abcam, Shanghai, China) were employed to label red-green cones and rod photoreceptors, respectively. Immunoreactivity was assessed utilizing an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (A11017; dilution 1:200; Life Technologies, Eugene, Oregon), with the exception of the staining procedure for 4D2. Prior to examination, slides were treated with the nuclear stain ToPro3 (T3605; dilution 1:1,000; Life Technologies, Eugene, Oregon) and subsequently analyzed under a NIKON 80i microscope. Fluorescent images were processed using Adobe Photoshop 7.0. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). The thickness of retinal layers was evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test.

## Transcriptomic sequencing

## RNA Extraction, cDNA library construction, and transcriptome sequencing
Five individuals of each group were chosen and anesthetized with MS-222 (100 mg/L, Sigma Chemical Company, St Louis, MO) at 8:00 am, and eyeballs tissues immediately collected. The tissues were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and placed in a −80°freezer prior to processing for total RNA isolation. For each group, eyeball tissues from 5 individuals were mixed. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies Corp., Carlsbad, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (TaKara). Then RNA quality was determined using 2100 Bioanalyser (Agilent) and quantified using the ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies). High-quality RNA sample (OD260/280 = 1.8 ~ 2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RIN ≥ 6.5,28 S:18 S ≥ 1.0) is used to construct sequencing library [22] with the Illumina Truseq™ RNA sample prep Kit for Illumina, which was then sequenced using Illumina HiSeq platform. Short sequence reads of paired end (PE) 150 bp were generated. All of the data were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database (Accession No.: PRJNA1111820, https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/object/PRJNA1162713?reviewer=51l3t5lki193bdnu30rdej3uh6).

## Do novo transcriptome assembly and transcriptome annotation
The raw paired-end reads were subjected to trimming and quality control using Trimmomatic [23], employing the parameters SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 and MINLEN:75 (version 0.36; available at http://www.usadellab.org/cms/uploads/supplementary/Trimmomatic). Subsequently, the total clean reads from all libraries were assembled de novo into a reference transcriptome utilizing Trinity [24]. The resulting unigenes were annotated by querying the NR, NT, and Swiss-Prot databases with an E-value threshold of 1.0E-5, and the KOG database with an E-value threshold of 1.0E-3. GO annotations, categorized by molecular function, cellular component, and biological process ontologies, were obtained using Blast2GO [25] software. Pathway assignments were determined based on the KEGG database through BLASTX analysis, applying an E-value threshold of 1.0E-5.

## Differential expression analysis
To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the three distinct tadpole groups, the expression levels of each transcript were quantified using the fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) method [26]. The RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization (RSEM) algorithm was employed to assess gene and isoform abundances (http://deweylab.biostat.wisc.edu/rsem/). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the EdgeR package [27] within the R statistical environment (Empirical analysis of Digital Gene Expression in R, http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/2.12/bioc/html/edgeR.html). The false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied to adjust the P values, with the criteria for identifying differentially expressed genes established as |log2(FC)| ≥ 1 and FDR ≤ 0.05.

## Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes
Functional-enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), were conducted to identify significantly enriched GO terms and metabolic pathways among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A Bonferroni correction was applied, with a threshold P-value ≤ 0.05, to determine the functional clusters that were significantly enriched. GO functional enrichment analysis was performed using Goatools, while KEGG pathway analysis was carried out using KOBAS8.

## Quantitative real-time PCR validation of RNA-Seq data
Given that the eyes of Tadpoles are very small, to facilitate RNA extraction, tissues from 5 individual specimens were pooled to form a single sample. RNA was extracted from three groups using an RNeasy® plus universal mini kit (QIAGEN, Leipzig, German). Complementary DNA was synthesized using a PrimeScriptTM reverse transcription (RT) reagent kit with gDNA eraser (TaKaRa, Peking, China). Real-time PCR was conducted using SYBR® Premix Ex TaqTM II (TaKaRa, Peking, China). the β-actin was used an internal control. Primers were designed based on the relevant genes from our transcriptome, with synthesis being performed by Invitrogen. Realtime PCR was conducted using SYBR® Premix Ex TaqTM II (TaKaRa, Peking, China) and an ABI StepOne™ real-time PCR system thermal cycling block.

## Results

## Morphological, microstructure and ultramicrostructure differences traits of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles after light treatment
The morphological characteristics of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles were quantitatively assessed (Table 1). Statistical analysis of the measured parameters indicated significant differences in weight, eye spacing, and the ratio of eye spacing to body length between tadpoles subjected to a 10-week light treatment and those that were untreated (P < 0.05), while other comparisons yielded non-significant results. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of eye sections from three distinct light-treated O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles demonstrated that individuals exposed to varying light treatment cycles maintained structurally intact eyes, with no significant differences observed in eye diameter (Fig. 1a, b, c; Table 1). In comparison to their untreated counterparts, light-treated tadpoles exhibited an increase in the average thickness of retinal cell layers, with the most pronounced effect observed at EEG-2. Notably, the thickness of the inner retinal layer (IRL) and the ganglion cell layer (GCL) displayed a consistent upward trend correlating with the duration of the phototreatment cycle (Figs. 1d, e and f and 2). Light-treated tadpoles demonstrated statistically significant differences in various measurements of retinal thickness, including ganglion cell layer thickness, inner retinal layer thickness, inner nuclear layer thickness, in comparison to untreated tadpoles. Additionally, the duration of light treatment correlated positively with the structural integrity of the cell layers, as evidenced by the more complete outer structures of cone and rod cells (Fig. 3). Notably, the number of EEG-2 cells exhibited significant differences when compared to the other two treatment groups. Within each group, the cell count in the inner nuclear layer surpassed that of the outer nuclear layer, while the outer nuclear layer contained a greater number of cells than the lymph node cells. The cell nuclear ratios for the respective groups were as follows: EEG-1: 2.43:4.11:1, EEG-2: 2.11:4.56:1, EEG-3: 2.43:3.85:1.

In light-treated O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles, the morphology and structure of pigment epithelial layer cells in the retina appeared relatively normal. Some autophagy was observed in the cytoplasm. The structure of the outer end of the mitochondrial optic cells in the optic cell layer (the membrane disk) was complete and clear (Fig. 1g). The optic cells were densely packed in the optic cone optic rod layer, with a higher number of optic cone cells. The inner end of the optic rod layer showed larger ellipsoids, containing a large number of mitochondria, mostly in the form of ovals and long strips, with a distinct outer membrane (Fig. 1h). In the outer nuclear layer, some cells exhibited a slight loss of ribosomes. The nuclei of the optic rod cells were neatly arranged, with an obvious outer reticulum. No apparent abnormalities were observed in the cells of the inner nuclear layer (Fig. 1i). In untreated O. rhodostigmatus, a few autophagosomes were observed in the pigment epithelium of the retina, and the pigment cells were tightly arranged (Fig. 1j). A small number of optic cells were present in the optic rod layer of the optic cones, with a majority being optic rod cells, and smaller ellipsoids (Fig. 1k). The outer nuclear layer was not clearly demarcated from the inner nuclear layer, and the arrangement of the cells was irregular. Additionally, the outer retinal layer was thinner compared to the light-treated group (Fig. 1l).

## Comparison of immunohistochemistry in tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus under different light exposure periods
Utilizing the 4D2 antibody for immunofluorescence labeling of tadpole rod cells revealed its specificity for the outer segment of retinal rod cells. In cave-dwelling tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus, the rod cell bodies exhibit shortening and malformation, accompanied by tightly packed cell nuclei. Following a four-week exposure to light treatment, the morphology of the tadpole rod cells becomes more defined, characterized by tightly arranged outer segments and a more organized yet loosely arranged configuration of cell nuclei. Structural differences are observed in rod cells due to variations in light exposure duration, with the outer segments of rod cells in O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles exhibiting marked degeneration. In comparison to rod cells subjected to four weeks of light exposure, those observed after this treatment show structural degradation, with shortened cell bodies and a loss of distinguishable rod-like morphology (Fig. 3a, c, e, g). Additionally, the cone cells in these tadpoles are disorganized, predominantly exhibiting pancake-like or rod-like shapes, with tightly packed nuclei and malformed cell bodies, resulting in a substantial loss of their fundamental morphology and structure (Fig. 3b). After light exposure treatment, the cone cells in the retina of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles are neatly arranged, with cell bodies exhibiting a conical structure and cells arranged closely (Fig. 3d, f, h). These findings highlight critical morphological disparities in the visual cells of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles subjected to differing periods of light exposure.

## Transcriptomic analysis of different light processing cycles of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles

## RNA-sequencing, assembly, and functional annotation
A total of 112,269,800 clean reads, covering 16,578,670,177 bases were produced from the transcriptomes of the O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles’ eyeball. In total, 127,035 unigenes were obtained, with an average length of 891 bp and an N50 of 1082 bp. We used gene coverage to assess how well the assembled sequences represented the actual transcriptome population. The transcriptome gene coverage was judged by comparing it with the sequence information available for O. rhodostigmatus. All 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes in the NCBI database were present in full length in our assembled transcripts. Transcriptome quality was assessed by comparing the mitochondrial protein-coding genes found in the assembled sequences to the mitochondrion sequence in GenBank (ON646613). A total of 18,679 nucleotide identities were observed out of the 18,854 bp (99.1%) total nucleotide length of the contig to the coding mitochondrial sequences in the BLAST matches, suggesting very good transcriptome sequence quality.
The BLASTX analysis of unigenes against the NCBI non-redundant database (Nr) indicated that 30,881 unigenes exhibited sequence homology with various species. Additionally, a total of 191,130, 22,423, 15,607, and 21,113 unigenes were annotated within the Gene Ontology (GO), eggNOG, KEGG, and Swiss databases, respectively. To enhance the understanding of the functional roles of the identified unigenes, a GO analysis was conducted. Based on the GO terms, 191,130 unigenes were categorized into level 2 terms across three primary functional categories: “biological process,” “cellular component,” and “molecular function,” with a predominant focus on biological processes. The KEGG pathway database was utilized to elucidate the biological pathways associated with these unigenes, resulting in the annotation of 15,607 unigenes across 276 established KEGG pathways. The five most represented pathways, ranked by the number of associated unigenes, included Metabolic pathways (2,383 unigenes), Biosynthesis (744 unigenes), Ribosome (508 unigenes), PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (488 unigenes), and Biosynthesis of antibiotics (449 unigenes).

In this study, the intra-group correlations were observed to be significantly higher than the inter-group correlations, thereby reinforcing the validity of our transcriptome data. The RNA sequencing results revealed substantial alterations in gene expression among tadpole groups subjected to varying conditions (Fig. 4a). Specifically, the EEG-2 group exhibited an up-regulation of 1,273 genes and a down-regulation of 1,663 genes when compared to the EEG-1 group. Additionally, a total of 4,671 genes were identified as differentially expressed between the EEG-3 and EEG-1 groups, with 2,397 genes up-regulated and 2,274 genes down-regulated. Furthermore, between the EEG-3 and EEG-2 groups, 4,847 differentially expressed genes were detected, comprising 2,525 genes that were up-regulated and 2,322 genes that were down-regulated. ## Target DEGs selection and expression
Through DEG functional analysis and pathway annotation, many DEGs associated with cave fitness in the visual organ of the O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles were screened among the DEGs of the three comparison groups. Such as photoreceptor protein G protein subunit alpha transducin 1, (GNAT1)ཤG protein-coupled receptor kinases 7 (GRK7), myosin IIIB (MYO3B), bestrophin 2 (BEST2), calmodulin 2 (CALM2), serine protease 56 (RPSS56), Crystallin Beta A2, (CRYBA2), Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 1 Group D Member 1 (NR1D1), regulators of Gprotein signalin (RGS9), Regulating Synaptic Membrane Exocytosis 2 (RIMS2), Crystallin Beta A4 (CRYBA4),Calcium Binding Protein 5 (CABP5), Prominin 1 (PROM1), Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor Interacting Protein Like 1 (AIPL1), lens fiber major intrinsic protein(MIP), photoreceptor protein s26 is associated with lens development, while there are several other differentially expressed genes that are related to the development of retinal photoreceptor cells and the eye. When comparing EEG-1 to EEG-2, it was found that the gene expression of BEST2 and MYO3B was significantly increased in EEG-2. In EEG-3, which had a longer light treatment period, the gene s26 was significantly down-regulated, while the expression of GRK7, CRYBA2, GNAT1, RGS9, CRYBA4, BEST2, RIMS2, MYO3B, NR1D1, and MIP was significantly up-regulated.

## Discussion

## The roles of the pigment and photoreceptor layers in the protection of vision
The habitat was observed to transition from dark to low light during the developmental process [15, 17]. Notably, tadpoles inhabiting dark environments do not undergo complete degeneration into a state of blindness; rather, the structural integrity of their visual organs remains relatively preserved under these conditions. In the dark-adapted retina, pigment granules are primarily localized in the cytoplasm surrounding the nucleus and infrequently migrate into the cone-rod layer [28]. In contrast, tadpoles exposed to light treatment demonstrated a migration of melanin granules into the cone-rod layer. Following light exposure, the pigment epithelium of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles displayed protrusions containing pigment granules that extended inward toward the outer segments of the photoreceptor cells, resulting in the formation of a tightly organized complex. This intricate structure is crucial for facilitating the transport of nutrients and metabolic waste, thereby supporting normal retinal metabolism, as corroborated by prior research [29]. Prolonged and uncontrolled exposure of the retina to intense light can result in retinal degeneration, with the pigment epithelium functioning as a protective barrier against such damage [30]. In the tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus, the thickening and inward extension of the pigment epithelium likely serve to protect photoreceptor cells from light-induced damage. The photoreceptor layer, comprised of the outer segments of photoreceptor cells, serves as a direct indicator of the characteristics and photosensitivity of the visual system [31]. The density and distribution of photoreceptor cells, specifically rod and cone cells, along with the complexity of the neural network, are critical determinants of visual function [32]. Furthermore, environmental variations during an organism’s development can induce significant changes in the photoreceptor cells of the retina [33].Following exposure to light, the photoreceptor layer in the tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus demonstrated a significant increase in thickness, characterized by elongated outer segments of photoreceptor cells and a more preserved morphology, thereby enhancing their sensory capabilities. Furthermore, after phototreatment, the arrangement of optic cells within the tadpole retina exhibited a more orderly organization and a more distinct morphology. A comparative analysis utilizing transmission electron microscopy at equivalent magnification revealed a greater density of rod cells in the optic cell layer, which may be attributed to the nocturnal behavior of tadpoles, as rod cells are critical for scotopic vision [34]. It is widely recognized that when the cell count in the inner nuclear layer exceeds that of the outer nuclear layer, and the cell density in the outer nuclear layer surpasses that of the ganglion cell layer, multiple photoreceptors integrate sensory information from a larger number of contact cells into a single ganglion cell. This configuration leads to an increased receptive field for each ganglion cell, thereby enhancing visual sensitivity; however, it may also result in a reduction in visual acuity. In the case of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles, the cell density in the inner nuclear layer exceeds that of the outer nuclear layer, with both layers exhibiting a higher cell count than the ganglion cell layer. This observation correlates with the fact that O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles inhabit environments characterized by extended periods of low light availability. The morphological structure and spatial distribution of photoreceptor cells within the retina are critical determinants of the organism’s photoreceptive capabilities, which subsequently adapt to the species’ ecological niche and behavioral patterns.

## Mitochondria and energy allotment
Transmission electron microscopy has revealed that the optic cells of cave tadpoles are predominantly composed of rod cells, which contain smaller ellipsoidal structures. These ellipsoids are characterized by a reduced number of irregularly shaped mitochondria, consistent with the observed microstructural features. Mitochondria within optic cells are essential organelles that support cellular functions and energy storage. The health of these mitochondria is critical for the optimal functioning of photoreceptor cells and the maintenance of normal vision [35]. Mitochondria act as cellular powerhouses, generating adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation, thus providing the energy required for various activities of optic cells, including light signal transduction and neural signaling [36]. Additionally, mitochondria are integral to the regulation of intracellular calcium ion levels, which are vital for light signal transduction and neurotransmitter release in optic cells [37]. The degeneration of mitochondria in the burrowing tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus may lead to a compromised energy supply and impaired regulation of calcium ions within the optic cells. This mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with a reduced metabolic rate and suboptimal differentiation of optic cells, which correlates with the observed decline in visual acuity among the tadpoles.
Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis identified several pathways pertinent to metabolism and growth, including glycerophospholipid metabolism, the synthesis, secretion, and action of growth hormone, purine metabolism, carbohydrate digestion and absorption, and pyrimidine metabolism. The enrichment of these pathways is correlated with the alterations in cellular functions and metabolism observed in O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles. Additionally, light exposure has been shown to influence glycerophospholipid synthesis and catabolism [38], thereby regulating cell membrane function and impacting cell signaling pathways [39]. Moreover, light may influence the synthesis of growth hormone, purine metabolism, and carbohydrate production, thereby contributing to the provision of additional energy for cellular activities. Cave-dwelling organisms typically inhabit environments characterized by extremely low light levels, where food resources are often limited. As a result, cave-dwelling fish have evolved to exhibit reduced metabolic rates to minimize energy expenditure [40], enhance food utilization efficiency, and adapt their energy storage strategies [41] to survive in resource-scarce environments. This adaptation suggests that certain organisms can endure extended periods of food deprivation. Additionally, the temperature within cave environments tends to be more stable and lower than that of surface environments. This cooler setting contributes to a reduction in basal metabolic rate, with metabolic capacity being influenced by environmental conditions [42].The conservation of vision and the reduction of stress-related energy expenditure in resource-limited cave environments [43], coupled with the absence of light in the habitats of untreated O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles, necessitate energy conservation strategies to ensure survival under extreme conditions. This is accomplished by selectively reducing the energy expenditure of less efficient organs, such as those involved in vision. This phenomenon is consistent with histological findings that reveal a low density of mitochondria in the optic cells of untreated O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles, which correlates with diminished energy consumption. Following exposure to light treatment, there was a notable increase in the number of mitochondria within the optic cells, leading to enhanced energy availability and increased energy consumption by these cells. Additionally, metabolic and energy-related pathways were significantly enriched to facilitate cellular development.

## Critical genes involved in phototransduction and their contribution to visual plasticity in O. rhodostigmatus
Our transcriptomic analysis, which examined the tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus subjected to various light treatment cycles, demonstrated a reduction in the expression of the s26 gene, alongside an increase in the expression of the GRK7 and GNAT1 genes. These genes are integral to the phototransduction process. Phototransduction involves a complex array of molecules and proteins, including retinal in rod cells and cone pigments in cone cells, which serve as photoreceptor molecules that capture photons and initiate a cascade of biochemical reactions. These reactions lead to alterations in cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) levels, resulting in changes to cell membrane potential and signaling to the brain via a network of neurons [44]. The protein encoded by the GNAT1 gene represents the α subunit of G proteins in photoreceptor cells, specifically the optic rods (Gαt), which are essential for visual signaling within the retina and facilitate rapid light adaptation [45].
GRK1 is categorized as a retinoid kinase-like kinase that operates as a ligand-activated G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR). Specifically, GRK1 is involved in the regulation of retinal photoreceptors, whereas GRK7, a member of the GRK1 subfamily [46], is primarily localized to cone photoreceptor cells and functions to inhibit the phosphorylation of GPCRs. Retina-specific kinases are essential for the termination of the light response and for facilitating adaptation to varying light conditions through the phosphorylation of cone opsins, including rhodopsin (RHO). Investigations into the GRK gene in zebrafish have revealed that GRK1b and GRK7a are crucial for visual function in larval cone cells [47]. The knockdown of GRK7 significantly delayed the recovery of the light response in zebrafish larval optic cone cells, highlighting the critical role of GRK7 in the normal functioning of these cells [48]. S-modulin, a calcium-binding protein specific to photoreceptors, shares homology with recoverin, which is vital for light adaptation through its modulation of uveosin phosphorylation in rod photoreceptor proteins [46]. Similarly, the protein s26, identified in the frog retina, inhibits retinal phosphorylation in the presence of elevated Ca2+ concentrations following light exposure, thereby prolonging the viability of retinal cells [49]. In unlight-treated tadpoles of O. rhodostigmatus, which reside in dark environments for extended durations, the expression of the photoreceptor protein s26 enhances the photosensitivity of optic cells. Upon exposure to light, O. rhodostigmatus gradually acclimatized to the bright environment, leading to a decrease in light sensitivity and a corresponding reduction in s26 expression, which in turn inhibits retinal phosphorylation. The results of this study demonstrate that the GRK7 and GNAT1 genes exhibit significantly elevated expression levels in light-treated tadpoles compared to their non-light-treated counterparts. Both GRK7 and GNAT1 are involved in the phototransduction pathway, indicating that O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles are adapting to variations in their light environment. This adaptation is associated with enhanced phototransduction capabilities, allowing photoreceptor cells to effectively receive and integrate visual signals, thereby progressively restoring vision-related functions. Furthermore, investigations of cave-dwelling fish have shown that the downregulation of transcription factors associated with GNAT and GRK genes correlates with visual degradation [21, 50]. Additionally, short-term light and dark treatment experiments in amphibians have demonstrated differential expression of GRK7, GNAT1, and other related genes [51].

## Evolutionary implications of visual plasticity
Our study presents robust evidence that extended exposure to light induces both structural and molecular adaptations in the visual organs of O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles, thereby supporting our hypothesis that non-obligate cave dwellers exhibit adaptive plasticity in response to variations in environmental light conditions. Histological analyses demonstrated significant increases in the thickness of retinal layers, particularly within the inner retinal layer (IRL) and the ganglion cell layer (GCL), alongside structural refinement of photoreceptor outer segments following prolonged light exposure (Fig. 1). These morphological alterations were accompanied by the upregulation of transcriptomic profiles related to phototransduction, including genes such as GNAT1, GRK7, and CRYBA2 (Fig. 6), which play essential roles in the generation of electrical signals within the retina [44, 52].The protein encoded by the GNAT1 gene encodes the α subunit of G proteins in photoreceptor cells, specifically the optic rods (Gαt), which play a critical role in visual signaling within the retina and facilitate rapid light adaptation. GRK7, a member of the GRK1 subfamily [46], is primarily localized in cone photoreceptor cells and functions to inhibit the phosphorylation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) [45, 47]. Additionally, the observed upregulation of the BEST2 and MYO3B genes, which are associated with ion transport and the maintenance of photoreceptors, further emphasizes the rapid remodeling of retinal infrastructure aimed at optimizing light sensitivity [53]– [54].
The downregulation of s26, a gene associated with cone photoreceptors and linked to cave adaptation [49], indicates a potential trade-off between energy conservation in dark environments and photoreceptor activation in light [55]. This observation is consistent with the “purifying selection” hypothesis, which posits that relaxed selection pressures in dark habitats allow for reduced gene expression. However, non-obligate cave species, such as O. rhodostigmatus, maintain latent pathways that enable reactivation [51]. Additionally, the structural integrity of mitochondrial membranes in light-exposed tadpoles (Fig. 1g–i) further corroborates the metabolic reconfiguration necessary to fulfill the energetic demands of phototransduction [56]. Collectively, these findings substantiate the notion that light exposure elicits a coordinated response across morphological, ultrastructural, and molecular levels, underscoring the species’ unique capacity for reversible visual plasticity.

## The adaptive plasticity in cave-dwelling tadpoles: bridging gaps in evolutionary theory
The evolutionary trajectory of cave adaptation in amphibians encompasses not only morphological degeneration but also dynamic phenotypic plasticity, which enables facultative species such as O. rhodostigmatus to thrive in variable light environments. Our findings are consistent with emerging frameworks in evolutionary biology that highlight plasticity as a crucial mechanism for rapid adaptation [56]. Specifically, the structural remodeling of retinal layers and the regeneration of photoreceptors in light-treated O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles (Figs. 1 and 3) exemplify the adaptive plasticity observed in cave-dwelling and nocturnal species [57]. These species exhibit reduced eye structures and enhanced capabilities in alternative sensory modalities, such as olfaction [58] and mechanoreception [59]. This phenomenon reflects the trade-offs associated with decreased investment in vision, which are compensated by increased investment in other sensory systems to gather environmental information in the absence of visual cues. It is noteworthy that multiple physiological mechanisms and/or variations in opsin expression [60] are likely involved in the modulation of visual sensitivity in response to environmental variations in lighting conditions, particularly in fish [61]. Furthermore, our transcriptomic analysis indicates that O. rhodostigmatus upregulates phototransduction genes (GNAT1, GRK7) in response to light exposure (Fig. 6), suggesting an inherent capacity to reactivate ancestral visual pathways that are suppressed in dark environments. This plasticity-driven adaptation challenges the conventional perspective of cave evolution as a unidirectional process toward degeneration, instead emphasizing the role of reversible trait modulation as a survival strategy in transient habitats [62].

## The visual adaptations different with cavefish and other cave-dwelling organisms
The visual adaptations observed in O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles exhibit both parallels and distinctions relative to obligate cavefish. Like Astyanax mexicanus, non-obligate cave-dwelling O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles display retinal thinning and photoreceptor disorganization in darkness (Fig. 1j–l), reminiscent of the apoptotic lens degeneration described in cavefish [62]– [63]. However, unlike cavefish, which exhibit irreversible eye loss due to mutations in shh and pax6 [64], O. rhodostigmatus retains functional plasticity, enabling rapid retinal restructuring upon light exposure. This distinction may reflect differences in evolutionary trajectories: obligate cavefish undergo genetic assimilation of troglomorphic traits, whereas facultative species like O. rhodostigmatus maintain phenotypic flexibility to exploit both cave and surface niches [65].
The upregulation of CRYBA2 and GNAT1 in light-treated tadpoles mirrors gene expression patterns in surface-dwelling fish, suggesting conserved pathways for photoreceptor maintenance [66]. Conversely, the absence of positive selection in pitx3 and lim2, genes under strong purifying selection in cavefish [67], implies that O. rhodostigmatus avoids permanent degenerative mutations, preserving ancestral traits. Furthermore, the restoration of rod and cone morphology after light exposure (Fig. 3) contrasts sharply with the arrested development seen in Proteus anguinus, where photoreceptor degeneration persists even under light [68]. These comparisons underscore a continuum of cave adaptation strategies, with facultative species occupying an intermediate niche between surface and obligate cave forms.
The rapid visual plasticity observed in O. rhodostigmatus tadpoles offers critical insights into the evolutionary mechanisms enabling survival in fluctuating environments. Unlike obligate cave species, which trade visual acuity for energy efficiency [69], O. rhodostigmatus leverages phenotypic plasticity to balance metabolic costs with functional vision. This “bet-hedging” strategy may confer a selective advantage in karst ecosystems, where seasonal flooding or habitat shifts expose tadpoles to variable light conditions [14]. The upregulation of NR1D1 and RGS9, genes regulating circadian rhythms and G-protein signaling, suggests an integration of light sensing with broader physiological networks, facilitating rapid environmental acclimatization [14].
Such plasticity may also buffer against extinction risks in vulnerable species. As O. rhodostigmatus faces habitat fragmentation, retaining adaptive visual pathways could enhance resilience to anthropogenic disturbances. Moreover, the reactivation of developmental pathways (e.g., CRYBA4, MIP) after prolonged darkness challenges the notion of irreversible degeneration, proposing instead a latent capacity for trait reversal under ancestral conditions [62]. This aligns with the “standing genetic variation” model, where cryptic alleles retained in populations enable rapid adaptation without de novo mutations [70]– [71].
Nevertheless, this study is subject to certain limitations. The untreated tadpoles were euthanized shortly after collection, whereas the light-treated tadpoles were maintained for up to 10 weeks in a laboratory setting. The potential impact of increased nutrient intake on the observed differences in visual organs between the control and treatment groups cannot be definitively ruled out. Conducting a control experiment in which tadpoles are maintained in a laboratory environment without light for durations comparable to those of the light-treated tadpoles would provide more robust evidence. Initially, we designed a completely dark group to account for the influence of a stable environment and consistent feeding. Unfortunately, due to unforeseen circumstances, all tadpoles in this group perished after three weeks. We intend to pursue further research on this topic in the future [72].

## Conclusion
Collectively, our study demonstrates that Oreolalax rhodostigmatus tadpoles exhibit rapid, reversible visual plasticity in response to light exposure, characterized by:
Structural remodeling of retinal layers (e.g., thickening of inner retinal/ganglion cell layers) and regeneration of photoreceptors;
Transcriptomic reprogramming involving upregulation of phototransduction genes (e.g., GNAT1, GRK7, CRYBA2) and downregulation of cave-adaptive genes (s26);
Metabolic reconfiguration via mitochondrial biogenesis to support energy demands.
Evolutionarily, this plasticity represents a distinct adaptation strategy: Unlike obligate cavefish (e.g., Astyanax mexicanus) that undergo irreversible eye degeneration via mutations, O. rhodostigmatus retains latent developmental pathways enabling rapid trait reversal.
We propose a “bet-hedging” model where non-obligate cave dweller balance energy conservation in darkness with functional vision restoration in light, leveraging standing genetic variation rather than de novo mutations. These findings redefine cave adaptation paradigms, emphasizing phenotypic plasticity as a key survival mechanism in fluctuating karst ecosystems. The reactivable visual pathways may inform conservation strategies for threatened amphibians facing habitat fragmentation. This work seems to provide support for negative selection against vision in cave-dwellers, given the clear metabolic expense revealed by both increased mitochondrial activity/presence and gene upregulation.
