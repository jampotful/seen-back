Passive acoustic monitoring reveals seasonal patterns in European green toad calling activity but fails to accurately reflect population abundance.
Kaczmarski M, Kaczmarek JM, Radzińska A, Budka M. (2025). Scientific reports. . doi:10.1038/s41598-025-11706-3. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Passive acoustic monitoring reveals seasonal patterns in European green toad calling activity but fails to accurately reflect population abundance

## Abstract
AbstractAmphibians are among the most endangered groups of vertebrates worldwide; therefore, effective population monitoring is crucial for understanding the factors driving these unfavourable trends. In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of fully automated passive acoustic monitoring in estimating the distribution and population size of the European green toad (Bufotes viridis)—an anuran species in which males vocalize intensively during the breeding season. We conducted visual counts with human observers, recorded the soundscape using autonomous sound recorders, applied automatic call detection, and calculated soundscape complexity using acoustic indices adjusted to the frequency range of European green toad vocalizations. Our algorithm generated a calling intensity index, which detected 36% of calls with 96% precision. The calling intensity index showed a significant correlation with the number of calls detected manually (r = 0.727). Additionally, we found moderate but significant correlations between acoustic indices (Acoustic Complexity Index and Bioacoustic Index) and both the manually and automatically detected call counts. The calling intensity index and acoustic indices did not significantly correlate with the number of males counted by human observer, but they reflected the phenological changes in species’ reproductive activity. Our study demonstrates that passive acoustic monitoring enables the assessment of presence and distribution of the European green toad, as well as daily and seasonal patterns in calling activity. However, due to the specific biology and ecology of the species, calling intensity alone is insufficient for accurately estimating male abundance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-11706-3.Keywords: Acoustic indices, Amphibian abundance, Anura, Toad calls, Soundscape, Urban environmentSubject terms: Biodiversity, Herpetology

## Introduction
Amphibians are among the most globally endangered groups of vertebrates, with over 40% of species classified as declining or threatened1. Effective monitoring of their populations is therefore essential for understanding the factors driving these unfavourable trends. However, obtaining reliable population estimates remains challenging and is influenced by multiple factors2. Most anuran amphibians are highly vocal animals, producing a wide variety of calls in different contexts, such as advertisement, courtship, aggressive, release, and distress calls3. Advertisement calls—typically produced by males during the breeding season—serve to attract mates and provide a valuable opportunity for auditory monitoring4. In traditional monitoring, human observers visit potential breeding sites one or several times during the breeding season to confirm species presence and estimate abundance based on visual observations and vocalization intensity4,5. However, this method is time-consuming and difficult to implement across multiple locations simultaneously6. Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) has therefore been proposed as an effective alternative to traditional, human-based surveys7. This technique involves two key components: collecting soundscape recordings in the field and analysing the data in the laboratory. Since the detection range of autonomous sound recorders is generally comparable to that of the human ear8. PAM is expected to yield statistically similar estimates of the richness of vocalizing species as human-based surveys9. Moreover, PAM offers the advantage of enabling continuous data collection at multiple sites simultaneously. These recordings can be used not only to detect species presence but also to provide detailed insights into species’ biology, ecology, phenology, and behaviour10–13. Despite the fact that many anuran species vocalize intensively during the breeding season, the application of PAM in this group remains relatively uncommon and warrants further research14.
The key question is whether calling intensity—measured as the number of calls produced per unit of time—corresponds to population size, especially given that in most species, only males vocalize. Assessing this relationship is challenging, and as a result, the available data have yielded mixed results. Some authors have attempted to estimate the relative abundance of calling individuals by ranking the density of call clusters using an ‘amphibian calling index’ ranging from 0 to 3: 0 – no frogs calling; 1 – isolated calls with no temporal overlap; 2 – overlapping calls with intermittent breaks; and 3 – full chorus vocalizations4. In several species, this approach has yielded reliable results. For example, in the Green frog (Lithobates clamitans), a relationship between calling characteristics and estimated population size has been observed. Both the mean population size estimated via mark-recapture and the average number of calls recorded during five one-minute intervals were higher in ponds with elevated amphibian calling index values15. Additionally, the number of calls per minute increased with population size, although this relationship weakened at higher densities16. In contrast, studies on Woodhouse’s toad (Anaxyrus woodhousii) and Boreal chorus frog (Pseudacris maculata) found no clear relationship between abundance and calling intensity17. These contrasting findings suggest that the relationship between calling intensity and the population size of vocalizing amphibians requires further investigation. Clarifying this link is essential not only for evaluating the effectiveness of passive acoustic monitoring in assessing species richness and community composition but also for its potential to estimate population abundance14. The simplest—but most time-consuming—method for analysing soundscape data involves manually scanning spectrograms and listening to recordings. This approach enables the identification and counting of species-specific vocalizations within the recordings18. Alternatively, advanced machine learning algorithms can be employed for automatic signal detection and classification. Such algorithms have been developed for many animal taxa (primarily birds), though their effectiveness varies among species19 and even within the same species20. Another approach involves the use of acoustic indices, which quantify the complexity of the soundscape21. These indices are mathematical representations of the distribution of acoustic energy over time and frequency within a single recording22. They are often used as proxies for species richness, diversity, and abundance, based on the assumption that more diverse and abundant communities generate more complex soundscapes. Although acoustic indices typically describe the acoustic complexity of the entire animal community, it is possible to adjust their calculation parameters to focus on the frequency range relevant to target species23.
In this study, we examined whether passive acoustic monitoring of the synanthropic yet declining European green toad (Bufotes viridis), can yield results comparable to traditional field surveys in estimating the number of calling males and its fluctuations throughout the breeding season. As a pioneer species originating from steppe habitats, European green toad possesses adaptations that enable it to thrive in increasingly urbanized areas across Central, Southern, and Eastern Europe24–27. Its high ecological plasticity—including tolerance to high water conductivity during larval development, rapid growth, high thermal tolerance, and the ability to utilize anthropogenic shelters—allows this species to persist in heavily transformed urban environments and isolated patches of green space within cities28–31. Moreover, the species can attempt reproduction over a span of nearly five months, making it an opportunistic breeder with considerable variation in its annual reproductive cycle26,32. Breeding typically begins with rising air temperatures and the arrival of the first males at breeding sites, where, under favourable thermal conditions, they initiate vocal activity to attract females32,33. In this species, females do not produce advertisement calls and remain in the breeding ponds for much shorter periods than males. Male vocalizations are most intense during twilight and consist of monotonous trills, composed of a series of pulses with a fundamental frequency of approximately 1200–1400 Hz and a pulse rate of about 20 pulses per second, with call durations of around 4 s27,34,35. Females are known to prefer longer and lower-pitched calls36. Previous acoustic studies on this species have primarily focused on detection effectiveness37, breeding phenology33, and the taxonomy of the genus Bufotes, the Palearctic green toads24,38. To our knowledge, no previous studies have attempted to estimate the relationship between vocal activity and population size in European green toad. While vocal activity may provide an estimate of the number of calling males, females cannot be detected using PAM. However, as the sex ratio in this species is typically close to 1:1, the number of adult females should not differ substantially from that of adult males29.
In this study, we tested whether fully automatic passive acoustic monitoring provides a reliable estimation of the number of calling males in European green toad, as well as whether it is able to detect seasonal changes in their abundance and vocal activity. To achieve this, we recorded soundscapes using autonomous sound recorders at three locations, monitored the number of individuals through human observation (in two of the locations), applied automatic signal detection and classification methods to identify and count male calls, and calculated acoustic indices as proxies for calling intensity. We hypothesized that calling intensity would correlate with the number of males present at the breeding sites. This study demonstrates the practical application of a fully automated passive acoustic monitoring approach for monitoring European green toad populations.

## Methods

## Study site
The study sites were located within the urban area of Poznań, a city in western Poland with a human population of over 500,000. The focal species exhibits a patchy distribution throughout the city, with a few relatively large populations thriving in city parks and smaller and/or ephemeral populations occurring in urban wastelands, construction sites, and similar habitats—a pattern also observed in other Central European cities such as Vienna and Košice27,33. Overall, the number of European green toad breeding sites in Poznań has decreased substantially since the 1990s39. Data were collected at three locations. Site 1 and Site 2 are both located within Cytadela Park—a large urban park established on the grounds of a former Prussian fortress. Site 1 – Rosarium [52°25’26.02"N, 16°55’56.32"E] is a shallow artificial pond with a concrete bottom (approx. 5000 m²), built in the 1970 s, which hosts a large European green toad population that has been extensively monitored over the past decade29–39. Site 2 – Water Garden [52°25’15.48"N, 16°56’22.81"E] is a smaller artificial pond (approx. 400 m²) with a concrete bottom and a fountain, reconstructed in 2019 and subsequently colonized by European green toad. The two sites are located over 600 m apart and calls from one site are inaudible at the other. Site 3 – Hippodrome [52°25’26"N, 16°55’56"E] is a shallow artificial pond (approx. 900 m²) with a geomembrane bottom covered with gravel, created in early 2022 for the purpose of translocating European green toad population from a breeding site scheduled for demolition. The population was translocated in 2022, with mass breeding events occurring in both 2022 and 2023, the latter being the year when the acoustic data were collected.
Soundscape recordings aimed at evaluating the effectiveness of automated acoustic methods and analysing patterns of vocal activity were conducted at all three sites. However, field surveys to assess the relationship between calling intensity and the number of observed males were carried out only at Sites 1 and 2.

## Field survey
Breeding activity of European green toad was monitored in 2023 (April 14th – July 2nd) at Site 1 and Site 2. Field surveys were conducted by a single observer. Observations began approximately one hour after dusk (around 21:30 CEST from April to mid-May, 22:00 CEST in late May, and 22:30 CEST in June and July). Each site was surveyed 21 times, with each visit lasting approximately 30 min. During each survey, the observer searched for individuals in the breeding pond using a 120-lumen flashlight, recording the number of males, females, and amplexed pairs. Additionally, vocal activity, presence of egg strings, and tadpoles were noted.

## Soundscape recording
At all three locations, we recorded soundscapes using three autonomous sound recorders (Song Meter Mini, Wildlife Acoustics) between April 12 and July 10, 2023. This period should cover the entire breeding season of the European green toad. Each recorder (one per location) was mounted on a tree approximately 5 m above ground level and positioned 10–15 m from the edge of the water body. Recordings were made daily, beginning two hours before sunset and continuing until two hours after sunrise. We recorded 60-minute WAV files at a sampling rate of 22.05 kHz/16-bit, with an 18 dB gain applied. The microphone sensitivity was tested before the start of recording using VOLTCRAFT SLC-100 sound level calibrator.

## Manual spectrogram scanning and listening to recordings
In the first step, we selected one-minute sound samples (one minute every 20 min), considering only the days when a field observer surveyed European green toads on site. We analysed the first minute of each 60-minute recording using manual spectrogram scanning and listening to recording (AS) in Raven Pro 1.6.5 software (window: Hann, FFT: 512 points, 50% overlap). Each vocalization of the European green toad was annotated, and we measured its peak frequency, along with the 5th (L5) and 95th (U95) percentiles of the energy distribution (i.e., the frequencies below which 5% and 95% of the acoustic signal’s energy are concentrated, respectively). These manual detections were then used to evaluate the effectiveness of automatic call detection and the performance of acoustic indices.

## Automatic detection of European green toad calls
The remaining one-minute sound samples (from the 20th and 40th minute of each hourly recording, totalling 954 samples) were used to develop an algorithm for the automatic detection and classification of European green toad calls using Cluster Analysis in Kaleidoscope Pro 5.6.8 software. We calculated the average spectral parameters of the calls: L5 (1229 ± 114 Hz), peak frequency (1341 ± 110 Hz), and U95 (1409 ± 106 Hz) (Fig. 1). To constrain the recognizer to the frequency range characteristic of the studied species, we set the minimum and maximum frequency limits to 1000 Hz and 1700 Hz, respectively. Additionally, we specified the following parameters: a minimum call duration of 1 s, a maximum duration of 4 s, a maximum inter-syllable gap of 0.1 s, a maximum cluster distance of 1, and an FFT window size of 128. Using these settings, we scanned the recordings to detect and cluster vocalizations. In the next step, each detection was manually reviewed and classified as either a European green toad call or another type of sound. In this way, we removed false positive detections to obtain a more reliable recognizer. Subsequently, we re-scanned the recordings using the manually edited clusters to build the final recognizer.
Spectrogram of a male European green toad call. The call consists of a series of pulses repeated multiple times. In this study, the frequency range for analysis was limited to 1.0–1.7 kHz (grey band), which is characteristic of the target species’ vocalizations. Spectrogram generated using tuner and seewave packages in R.
The final recognizer was then applied to the one-minute sound samples in which all calls had been manually identified. The recognizer was trained on one dataset (20th and 40th minute of each hourly recording) and tested on a separate dataset (1th minute of each hourly recording) to objectively assess its performance on unseen data and to prevent overfitting. To evaluate its performance, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between the number of calls detected automatically and manually in each one-minute sample, along with recall (TP/[TP + FN]) and precision (TP/[TP + FP]). The abbreviations are defined as follows: TP – true positives; FN – false negatives; FP – false positives. Recall represents the proportion of correctly detected vocalizations relative to all vocalizations identified manually. Precision indicates the proportion of correctly detected vocalizations relative to all detections made by the algorithm. A high recall value suggests that the algorithm detected most vocalizations, while a high precision value indicates a low rate of false detections.

## Acoustic indices calculations
We calculated two commonly used acoustic indices: the Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) and the Bioacoustic Index (BI). The ACI quantifies the absolute difference in amplitude between two adjacent time samples within a frequency bin, relative to the total amplitude40. Higher ACI values are generally indicative of a more complex soundscape, which is typically associated with greater species diversity and abundance41. The BI measures the area under the log-amplitude spectrum curve, with the minimum decibel level set to zero. It was originally developed to estimate avian abundance and community composition in Hawaiian ecosystems42. More recent studies have shown a positive correlation between BI and both species richness and abundance in other regions41,43. The selection of these two indices from among the dozens used in ecoacoustics was motivated by the desire to provide a comprehensive description of the acoustic diversity of the soundscape, capturing both the complexity and temporal dynamics of sounds (ACI) as well as their overall intensity (BI).
In our study, we restricted the frequency range for both indices to match that of European green toad calls (1000–1700 Hz) and used a 256-point FFT window. For the ACI, we additionally set the j parameter to 5 and applied an amplitude threshold of −50 dB (Fig. 1).

## Seasonal and daily pattern of calling intensity
We calculated the BI and ACI for one-minute sound samples and averaged the values for each full hour, starting two hours before sunrise. Recordings from the end of each session that were shorter than one hour were excluded from the analysis. Simultaneously, we used the number of calls automatically detected for each hour of recording. Additionally, since the sound recorders logged temperature data every minute (built-in temperature logger inside the recorder), we were able to calculate hourly average temperatures. This analysis included soundscape data from all three locations.

## Statistics
To examine the relationship between the number of males detected by a human observer at the two study locations and the outputs of an automatic acoustic approach, we conducted two sets of generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) in glmmTMB package44. In the first set, we considered the number of calls detected automatically, along with the average ACI and BI calculated during the second hour after sunset. In the second set, we used the total number of calls and the average ACI and BI values for the entire night. In each model, the dependent variable was the number of males detected by the observer. In this way, we focused both on the specific time when the field surveys were conducted by the observer (approximately the second hour after sunset) and on the entire night during which the field surveys took place. Predictors included the number of calls, ACI, BI, time within the season (in days), and temperature (either the daily minimum or the average during the second hour after sunset). Location ID was specified as a random effect. All models were fitted using a negative binomial distribution with a log link function. The negative binomial distribution was used because the response variable (number of males detected) is count data that exhibited overdispersion. Variance inflation factors in all models were below 5.0.
To analyse daily and seasonal patterns of vocal activity in relation to temperature, we conducted three separate GLMMs. The dependent variables were the average number of calls detected automatically, the BI, and the ACI—each calculated as an hourly average. Predictors included time within the season (as a continuous variable), time relative to sunset (a continuous variable ranging from two hours before sunset to the last full hour of recording, up to two hours after sunrise), and the average hourly temperature. Recording point ID was included as a random effect. We used a negative binomial distribution for the number of calls and a gamma distribution with a log link function for ACI and BI. All statistical analyses were performed in R 4.3.345, and model diagnostics were conducted using the DHARMa package46.

## Results

## Effectiveness of acoustic automatic methods
In 499 one-minute sound samples, we manually detected 1,000 calls of the European green toad. The automatic detection method identified 376 calls in the same recordings, of which 360 were true positives and 16 were false positives. The Pearson correlation between the number of manually detected calls per one-minute recording and the number of automatic detections was strong and significant (r = 0.727, p < 0.001). The recognizer’s recall was 0.36, meaning the algorithm detected approximately 36% of all actual European green toad calls. Its precision was 0.96, indicating that when the algorithm identified a call, it was correct about 96% of the time.
Both the Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) and the Bioacoustic Index (BI) showed a moderate, statistically significant, and positive correlation with the number of calls detected manually through spectrogram scanning and listening to recordings (ACI: r = 0.425, p < 0.001; BI: r = 0.449, p < 0.001), as well as with the number of calls detected automatically (ACI: r = 0.384, p < 0.001; BI: r = 0.519, p < 0.001).

## Number of observed males and acoustic characteristics
During 22 field surveys conducted by an observer at each of the two sites, we recorded between 2 and 30 males per survey (mean: 14.8) at one site, and between 6 and 137 males per survey (mean = 85.8) at the other. On the same days, the automatic detection method recorded between 11 and 1,024 calls per day (mean = 340.4) at the first site, and between 7 and 2,896 calls per day (mean = 720.5) at the second.
We found no significant relationship between the number of European green toad males detected by observers in the field and the number of calls detected automatically, nor with the values of the Bioacoustic Index (BI) or Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI), regardless of whether we analyzed data from the second hour after sunset or from the entire night. Additionally, none of the models indicated a significant seasonal trend in male numbers. The only significant relationship observed was a positive association between the number of males and temperature (Table 1).

## Daily and seasonal pattern of calling intensity
Although we did not observe significant seasonal changes in the number of males detected by the observer, the analysis of acoustic parameters revealed clear seasonal and daily patterns influenced by temperature (Table 2; Fig. 2). The number of detected calls declined as the season progressed, peaked between two and three hours after sunset, and was constrained by both low and high temperatures. The Bioacoustic Index (BI), which reflects the distribution of energy within the 1.0–1.7 kHz frequency band, also decreased over the season, reached its highest values approximately two to four hours after sunset, and showed a positive correlation with temperature. Similarly, the Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) declined as the season progressed, peaked during the evening and morning hours, and—like call counts—was negatively affected by both low and high temperatures.

## Discussion
Our study demonstrated that passive acoustic monitoring, when combined with automatic call detection and recognition, is an effective tool for estimating the occurrence of the European green toad. On every day when at least one male was observed during field surveys at either of the two monitored water bodies, the automatic detection system also identified at least a few vocalizations. Given the exceptionally prolonged vocal activity of the European green toad during the breeding season, PAM offers a rapid and highly efficient method for monitoring its presence and, consequently, its distribution. This is particularly valuable in urban environments. While our study sites were publicly accessible, the species often occurs in industrial or construction areas37, where access may be restricted—especially for repeated visits and, crucially, after dusk, when vocal activity peaks and detection is most effective. The high effectiveness of PAM for determining the distribution of terrestrial vertebrates—especially those that are elusive or vocalize at unusual times—has been confirmed for amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals14,47. However, the real challenge lies beyond detecting presence: estimating population size based on soundscape recordings remains difficult4,48–50.
In traditional observer-based calling surveys—such as those conducted under the North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (1997–2015)—anuran abundance is commonly estimated using a simplified amphibian calling index. This index categorizes vocal activity into four levels: no vocalizations, single calls, overlapping calls with pauses, and continuous/full chorus vocalizations4. However, the value of such generalized indices is often limited, especially considering interspecific variation in calling behavior and call rate15. In our study, we employed two complementary approaches to relate calling intensity to the abundance of European green toads: (1) automatic detection of individual calls and (2) measurement of soundscape complexity. For the first approach, we developed a recognizer that detected 36% of all calls with high accuracy—only 4% of detections were false positives. This enabled us to derive a calling intensity index that, similar to previous studies48,49,51,52, can be used to monitor daily and seasonal patterns in vocal activity.
For the second approach, we hypothesized that more abundant toad populations would produce a more complex soundscape, which we measured using two acoustic indices: the Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) and the Bioacoustic Index (BI). In both approaches, we restricted the analyzed frequency range to that characteristic of the European green toad, with the aim of improving the performance of the recognizer and strengthening the relationship between soundscape complexity and male abundance23. Although both the number of automatically detected calls and the acoustic indices showed moderate correlations with manually counted calls in one-minute sound samples, we found no significant relationship between these acoustic metrics and the number of males recorded by human observers in the field. While calling intensity could theoretically serve as a proxy for the number of calling males, in practice the relationship is often complex and nonlinear15. Even when the number of calling males is reliably assessed, it may not directly reflect population size due to factors such as variation in chorus tenure among individual males53.
We demonstrated that call counts supported by automatic detection, along with acoustic indices, can serve as reliable indicators of calling intensity in the European green toad. However, neither the number of calls nor the values of acoustic indices were correlated with the number of males present at the breeding sites. Similar discrepancies have been reported for several North American Lithobates species15,48,50. This decoupling between acoustic parameters and observed male counts cannot be attributed to imperfect detectability, as the simple structure of the breeding habitats (artificial, shallow ponds at sites 1 and 2) allowed us to reliably count all present individuals. Instead, it may reflect the diversity of male reproductive strategies. For instance, in the Natterjack toad (Epidalea calamita), a portion of males adopt alternative, non-calling strategies—such as acting as silent satellites54, or actively searching for females without vocalizing55. Furthermore, larger males are more likely to vocalize and appear earlier in the breeding season, while smaller males often remain silent56. As a result, only a subset of males in our study system may have contributed to the observed calling activity, and increased male density may not lead to proportional changes in acoustic parameters. Therefore, the reliable estimation of calling male abundance from acoustic data may be feasible only in species where alternative reproductive strategies are absent, as is the case for South African moss frogs49.
Passive acoustic monitoring can be highly effective in studying anuran phenology, particularly for cryptic species49,51. Notably, anuran breeding phenology is expected to be strongly influenced by climate change57–59. Urbanization, through the formation of urban heat islands, may also contribute to shifts in phenological patterns60. The European green toad, our study species, is especially likely to exhibit such changes due to its frequent association with anthropogenically modified habitats and its ability to respond rapidly to weather and hydroperiod fluctuations—for example, through repeated breeding bouts within a single season28. Extremely early breeding onset has already been documented in one of the study populations61, and long-term shifts in breeding timing have been observed more broadly in the species62. The effectiveness of the automated call detection methods used in our study is comparable to that reported in other research on anuran phenology51. However, PAM in our study began after the breeding season had already started and ended before the cessation of calling activity. Despite this limited timeframe, we observed the expected seasonal decline in vocal activity, with both the number of detected calls and the values of acoustic indices decreasing as the season progressed. Implementing PAM over an extended and multi-year period could yield reliable, easily obtainable data on the breeding phenology of the species in urban environments. Our data on diel patterns of acoustic activity indicate that most vocalizations occur within two to four hours after sunset. Consequently, future PAM schemes could focus on this specific time window to maximize efficiency while minimizing the energy consumption of recording devices.
In conclusion, for the European green toad, passive acoustic monitoring is a valuable tool for assessing presence and breeding phenology, though not for estimating abundance. More broadly, continued acoustic studies of amphibian phenology using PAM can enhance our understanding of the mechanisms driving anuran adaptation to anthropogenically modified habitats and a changing climate.
