Cathelicidin-Ka, the first frog-derived TLR2 and TLR4 agonist, induces macrophage activation and promotes inflammation.

# Cathelicidin-Ka, the first frog-derived TLR2 and TLR4 agonist, induces macrophage activation and promotes inflammation

## Abstract
AbstractTLR-targeted immunotherapy represents a promising strategy for combating infectious diseases by initiating or enhancing protective antimicrobial immunity. Here, we identified the first frog-derived TLR2 and TLR4 agonist, Cathelicidin-Ka (Cath-Ka), from the skin of Kaloula pulchra. The presence of Cath-Ka significantly enhanced proliferation, cytokine production, polarization, chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and intracellular bacterial killing of macrophages and peritoneal cells by targeting TLR2 and TLR4, rather than other pattern recognition receptors, and subsequently activated the downstream MyD88-MAPKs pathway. Cath-Ka also promoted macrophage polarization towards the M1 rather than M2 phenotype, and its intraperitoneal injection significantly promoted the chemotaxis of pro-inflammatory monocytes/macrophages into the peritoneal cavity. Finally, the mutant of Cath-Ka with amination at C-terminus had stronger effects on macrophage function modulation than the original peptide. These findings suggest that Cath-Ka and its amidated mutant are promising candidates for the treatment of TLR2 and TLR4-related diseases, including infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-06068-y.Keywords: Host defense peptides, Immunostimulation, Macrophages, TLR2 and TLR4, Kaloula pulchra

## Introduction
Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are among the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) which have become a target in immunomodulator development due to their crucial roles in the innate immune response as the detectors of invading pathogens [1]. Upon infection, microbes are recognized by TLRs expressed on immune cells, such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, which subsequently initiate the immune response [2]. The activation of most TLRs leads to the recruitment of a common adaptor, MyD88, and triggers NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase cascades (MAPKs) signaling, subsequently instigating the activation of a multitude of genes crucial for mounting the inflammatory response. These genes encode for proteins including cytokines/chemokines, antimicrobial molecules, as well as major histocompatibility (MHC) and costimulatory molecules which are important for adaptive immune activation [1, 2]. TLR agonists have exhibited considerable promise as potent antimicrobial agents and immunomodulators. Some TLR agonists such as Monophosphoryl Lipid A have been proposed as vaccine adjuvants while imiquimod is used in the treatment of warts, both are currently approved by the FDA [2–4]. Macrophage-activating lipopeptide-2, which targets the TLR2/TLR6 heterodimer, and its analogs are also well-studied immunomodulators [2]. Many TLR agonists, however, can induce excessive inflammatory responses that many harm the host [2]. Thus, the development of novel TLR agonists with mild inflammatory activatity is needed.
Host defense peptides (HDPs) abundant in nature can be considered as alternatives to conventional antibiotics due to their unique suppressive mechanism against microbes, particularly bacteria. In addition to the direct-bactericidal activity, HDPs have also been investigated as potential anti-infective agents via modulating the host immune response [5]. Members of the Cathelicidin family have already been characterized as having a highly varied structure and bioactivity, making the specific family one of the most abundant and best-characterized HDPs in mammals and amphibians. Most amphibian-derived Cathelicidins demonstrate LPS-neutralizing, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities [6]. For instance, Cathelicidin-PY shows bactericidal and anti-inflammatory activities by disrupting bacterial membranes and blocking TLR4-mediated inflammatory response, respectively [7]. Despite the promising application in clinics, the immunomodulatory mechanism of HDPs, particularly those isolated from non-mammal species, is largely undefined. Kaloula pulchra, also known as the banded bullfrog, belongs to the Microhylidae family which is commonly found in Southeast Asia and Southern China. When encountering danger, these frogs secrete a sticky and white poisonous substance from their skin that contains a variety of bioactive compounds [8]. Ignoring the widespread distribution and long application as a traditional Chinese medicinal material of this species, few reports exist concerning the bioactive components present within their skin secretions [9–12]. Here, we identified a novel Cathelicidin, named as Cathelicidin-Ka (Cath-Ka), from the skin of K. pulchra (where ‘Ka’ originates from the genus name). Cath-Ka exhibits immunostimulatory properties and functions as a macrophage-dependent immunomodulator by selectively targeting TLR2 and TLR4.

## Materials and methods

## Animals and ethical statement
Adult K. pulchra (40–60 g, n = 6) were collected from Baiyun District, Guangzhou City, Guangdong Province, China. The frogs were fed a commercial diet twice per day and were allowed to acclimatize to laboratory conditions for 2 weeks. Male Balb/c mice (six-week-old, weighing 20 ± 2.0 g) were bought from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). The animals were kept in a SPF facility and were free to water and food during the experiments. All procedures involving the mice and frogs were authorized by Southern Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. SMUL202404026).

## Identification and bioinformatics analysis of Cath-Ka
Frog skins and their secretions were collected as described previously [13]. The frogs were euthanized humanely and their dorsal skins were cleaned with distilled water. Thereafter, the skins were sheared and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen until cDNA cloning was performed for transcriptome sequencing. The Cath-Ka gene sequence was derived from the skin transcriptome of K. pulchra. The translated amino acid sequence was aligned by Protein BLAST in the NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Theoretical isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (Mw) were calculated using the ProtParam service (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The interaction mode of Cath-Ka and TLR4 was computationally predicted with AlphaFold2 server (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk).
For the identification of whether Cath-Ka can be secreted by K. pulchra, the skin surface of K. pulchra was first washed with water before the frogs were placed in a sealed container. Copious skin secretions were induced by applying anhydrous ether to the dorsal region of the frogs, followed by rinsing with 0.1 M PBS. The collected solution was immediately centrifuged to remove debris. Supernatant was lyophilized, then dissolved in distilled water containing 0.1% formic acid and 5% acetonitrile. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an Easy-nLC II HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) coupled to an Orbitrap QE-HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The acquired data were processed with PEAKS software for database searching and identification of Cath-Ka.

Escherichia coli (E. coli) ATCC 25,922 and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) ATCC 25,923 were purchased from the Guangdong Institute of Microbiology and cultured in LB medium.

## Circular Dichroism (CD) analysis
CD spectroscopy of 50 µM Cath-Ka in different solutions was conducted at 25 °C using a Jasco-810 spectropolarimeter (Jasco, Tokyo, Japan) as described previously by our group [14]. In brief, for secondary structure investigation, peptide (50 µM) was dissolved in different concentrations of SDS solutions (0, 30, 60, and 90 mM) at 25 °C, respectively. For stability examination, Cath-Ka (50 µM) dissolved in 60 mM SDS solution was incubated in various temperatures (25, 37, 50, 70, and 90 °C) for 1 h. The wavelengths ranging from 185 to 260 nm were recorded and the data were converted to the mean residue ellipticity (θ) of three consecutive scans per sample in deg·cm2·dmol− 1.

## In vivo peritoneal cavity cell activation
Cath-Ka was dissolved in 100 µL sterile saline and intraperitoneally injected in male Balb/c mice at a final concentration of 5 mg/kg. Control mice similarly received an identical volume of sterile, endotoxin-free saline. At 2 and 4 h post-injection, peripheral blood was collected to measure cell chemotaxis and finally the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Subsequently, 1 mL of saline was injected into the peritoneal cavity of dead mice to collect lavage fluid. Following centrifugation, the number, phagocytosis and bacterial killing ability, signaling pathway activation, as well as chemotaxis of the peritoneal cells and the concentration of inflammatory cytokines in the peritoneal supernatant were measured.

## Trans-well cell migration assays
Trans-well cell migration assays were performed to study the effects of Cath-Ka on RAW264.7 cell migration as described previously by our group with some modifications [15]. Briefly, the cells (5 × 104 cells/100 µL) were re-suspended in DMEM medium containing 2% FBS. They were subsequently added to the 8.0 μm pore-size trans-well filters (Corning Inc, NY, USA). 500 µL of DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and Cath-Ka (2.5–20 µM) was applied to the lower chamber. After 24 h, the chambers were removed and washed, and the non-invading cells were gently erased. After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, the migratory cells were dyed with 0.1% crystal violet for another 10 min before being photographed under a bright field microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) with a magnification of 200 × LPS (100 ng/mL) was used as the positive control for cell migration.

## Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) detection
The intracellular ROS level was measured using DCFH-DA as described previously by our group [16]. In brief, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were plated in 24-well plates and co-cultured with Cath-Ka (1.25–20 µM) or LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Afterwards, the cells were harvested and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 20 min at 37 °C before the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured using a FACSCanto flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

## Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis
RT-qPCR was performed to determine the mRNA expression levels of iNOS and cytokines as described previously by our group [14]. In brief, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were treated with Cath-Ka (10 µM) for 2 h or Cath-Ka (1.25–20 µM) for 4 h at 37 °C and their total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, China) and RT-qPCR was accomplished with SYBR Green PCR kit (Takara, Dalian, China) with the isolated RNA serving as the template. The corresponding primers are listed in Table S1. The mRNA levels of the groups treated by Cath-Ka were determined as fold differences compared to the control group which was set arbitrary as “1”.

## ELISA measurement
The production of NO, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and CXCL2 in RAW264.7 cells was measured by Griess reaction and ELISA assays as described previously by our group [14]. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were treated with Cath-Ka (1.25–20 µM) or LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h in the 96-well plates. Then, the cell supernatants were collected for the detection of NO and all the above-mentioned factors using the NO assay kit (Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China) and ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In the experiment designed for the determination of potential endotoxin contamination in Cath-Ka preparations, Cath-Ka peptide (2.5–10 µM) or LPS (100 ng/mL) was incubated with polymyxin B (PMB, 10 µg/mL) for 30 min before the mixtures were added into RAW264.7 cells and the levels of TNF-α in the supernatant was measured.

## Cell surface markers analysis
Expression of MHC-II, CD206, and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80 and CD86) in RAW264.7 cells was examined by flow cytometry. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) cultured in 24-well plates were treated with Cath-Ka (1.25–20 µM) or LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h before being stained with MHC-II (PE, 1: 100), CD80 (FITC, 1: 100), CD86 (APC, 1: 100) and CD206 (APC, 1: 100) for 30 min at 4 °C avoiding light. Their MFI was finally measured using a FACSCanto flow cytometer.

## Phagocytosis and intracellular bacterial killing assays
The phagocytosis activity of RAW264.7 cells was first measured by the uptake of FITC-Dextran and neutral red. In short, after being treated with Cath-Ka (1.5–20 µM) or LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h, the cells were co-cultured with FITC-Dextran (1 mg/mL) for 1 h before their MFI was measured by flow cytometry, or were cultured with neutral red solution (1 mg/mL) for 4 h before being lysed with lysis buffer (glacial acetic acid: ethanol = 1: 1). After overnight lysis at 4 °C, the optical density (OD) of neutral red was finally measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite M1000 Pro, Tecan Company, Männedorf, Switzerland).
E. coli phagocytosis and intracellular bacterial killing effects were evaluated as a further test as previously described (Pang et al., 2019). In short, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells/500 µL) in poly-lysine-coated 24-well plates were treated with Cath-Ka (2.5–20 µM) for 24 h. Thereafter, the cells were washed and incubated with E. coli at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in an antibiotic-free medium. After 1 h of infection, external bacteria were removed and the cells were washed for three times with PBS, and then PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 was used to lyse the cells and to release phagocytosed bacteria. The bacterial numbers were counted by plating 5 µL of cell lysates on LB agar plates and cultivating overnight at 37 °C. For intracellular bacteria killing determination, after incubating with E. coli for 1 h, the external bacteria were removed and Gentamicin (100 µg/mL) was added. After 2 h, the cells were lysed and the bacterial numbers were counted as above. As a further confirmation of the bacteria phagocytosis and intracellular killing effects, after RAW264.7 cells were treated with Cath-Ka (10 µM) for 24 h as described above, phagocytic bacteria by macrophages were finally observed by transmission electron microscope (TEM) as previously reported [17].

## Inhibition experiments
To identify the effects of Cath-Ka on the activation of different TLRs and signaling pathways related to TLR2/TLR4, RAW264.7 cells were pre-treated with the various inhibitors including C29, CU-CPT 4a, TAK-242, TH1020, Hydroxychloroquine, Enpatoran, SB203580, U0126, SP600125 for 30 min at 37 °C, respectively. The concentration of PPR inhibitors are listed in Table S2. Then, the cells were cultured with fresh medium containing Cath-Ka (10 µM) plus inhibitors for the corresponding time before measuring in RAW264.7 cells the expression of signaling proteins and inflammatory cytokines as well as their E. coli phagocytosis ability and cell migration.

## Cell binding measurement
FITC-labeled Cath-Ka binding on RAW264.7 cells was detected by flow cytometry as described previously by our group [14]. In short, RAW264.7 cells were incubated with FITC-labeled Cath-Ka (1.25–20 µM) for 30 min at 37 °C. Then, the cells were washed with PBS and the MFI was examined using flow cytometry. For the competitive binding experiment, RAW264.7 cells were pre-incubated with TLR2 and TLR4 blocking antibodies (1: 200, 1: 1000, and 1: 5000 dilutions) or lipoteichoic acid/lipopolysaccharide (LTA/LPS, 1, 5, and 25 µg/mL) for 1 h before being incubated with Cath-Ka (20 µM) for 30 min and analyzed using a FACSCanto flow cytometer.

## SPRi measurement
SPRi assay was carried out to investigate the interactions between Cath-Ka and TLR2, TLR4, MD2, LPS, as well as LTA using a PlexArray HT A100 system (Plexera LLC, Bothell, Washington) as described in our previous study [14]. Briefly, recombinant mouse TLR2, TLR4, and MD2 (1 mg/mL), as well as LPS/LTA (1 mg/mL), were immobilized on an activated bare gold SPRi chip. After the SPRi chip was blocked using 1 M ethanolamine aqueous solution (pH 8.4), Cath-Ka (0.25–4 µM) was injected at a flow of 2 µL/s. BIA evolution software was used to analyze the data and calculate the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) value.

## Determination of TLR2/4-MyD88-MAPKs pathways activation
The effect of Cath-Ka on the activation of TLR2/4-MyD88-MAPKs pathways was measured with western blot (WB) as described previously by our group [14]. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were incubated with Cath-Ka (10 µM) for different times (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h) or with different concentrations of Cath-Ka (2.5–20 µM) for 0.5 h (for MAPKs) and 4 h (for TLR2, TLR4, and MyD88) at 37 °C. Then, the total cell proteins were extracted using the protein extraction kit (KeyGEN, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and WB detection was finished with the corresponding antibodies against TLR2/TLR4, MyD88, p38, JNK, ERK and GAPDH.

## Binding of Cath-Ka to TLR2/4
The binding of Cath-Ka to TLR2/4 was identified by ELISA, biotin-based pull-down, and cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). For ELISA assays, RAW264.7 cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were incubated with Cath-Ka (10 µM) for 1 h in 96-well plates before being incubated with LTA (10 µg/mL) or LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. The supernatant was then collected to measure the level of TNF-α by ELISA. TLR2/TLR4 binding of Cath-Ka was further evaluated by pull-down. In brief, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 107 cells) at logarithmic growth period were lysed with Pierce IP lysis buffer from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) and incubated with biotin-tagged Cath-Ka-bound Streptavidin resin overnight at 4°C according to the manufacturer’s instructions (EZ-Link Desthiobiotinylation and Pull-Down Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific Company). Thereafter, the prey proteins were eluted in a biotin elution buffer and used for WB analysis. Cath-Ka-1 which has been shown to lack the ability to activate macrophages was used as the negative control. For cellular thermal shift assay, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 107 cells) at logarithmic growth period were treated with Cath-Ka (10 µM) for 1 h and heated at various temperatures (37–60.5 °C) for 3 min, followed by cooling at 4°C. After 3 cycles of freeze-thawing with liquid nitrogen, the cell lysates were collected and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatants were finally analyzed using WB.

## Statistical analysis
Student’s unpaired two-tailed t-test and one-way ANOVA were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Values are given as mean ± SD. Representation of the P-value is *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

## Results

## The sequence and structure of Cath-Ka
The cDNA and protein sequences of Cath-Ka are shown in Fig. 1A. The complete cDNA sequence is 549 bp long and encodes a precursor protein of 182 amino acid residues including the signal peptide, the conserved N-terminal cathelin region, as well as the mature peptide which consists of 32 amino acids (AGFLGSGHKHPVEAHASCIACIIEELGKVSRG) after the typical enzymatic cleavage site RR. The pI and Mw of Cath-Ka without a disulfide bond were predicted to be 7.04 and 3274.76 Da, respectively. However, the Mw of synthetic Cath-Ka (99.45% purity) was 3272.68, while that of DTT-treated Cath-Ka-1 was 3274.69 Da (Fig. S1A-C), implying a possible disulfide bond formation between cysteines 18 and 21. To identify whether the peptide can be genuinely secreted in K. pulchra skin, its skin secretions were analyzed by LC-MS/MS and the results showed that the amino acid sequence of Cath-Ka is contained in the detected sample (Fig. S1D, E).

CD spectroscopy data revealed that Cath-Ka dissolved in water exhibited a negative peak at 198 nm, indicative of a random coil structure (Fig. 1B). When dissolved in SDS, Cath-Ka displayed a charcteristic α-helical CD signature, with a pronounced positive peak at 192 nm and two smaller negative peaks at 208 nm and 222 nm. Cath-Ka also showed some slight spectral variations in different SDS (30–90 mM) (Fig. 1B). Although minor changes were observed in the CD spectra of Cath-Ka subjected at varying temperatures, its α-helical conformation was maintained (Fig. 1C).

## Cath-Ka induces peritoneal cell activation in mice
The immune-activating activity of Cath-Ka, including the migration of phagocytes, was investigated by means of intraperitoneal injection of the peptide into mice. After 2 and 4 h of exposure to Cath-Ka, both the migratory cell number and the production of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α as well as CXCL2 in mice peritoneal fluid were significantly increased time-dependently (Fig. 2A, B). Cathelicidins are generally known to exhibt direct antimicrobial activity against various pathogens, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [6]. Unexpectedly, Cath-Ka at a concentration of 50 µM concentration didn’t show any antimicrobial activity against E. coli and S. Aureus (Fig. S2). The number of bacteria phagocytosed and killed by peritoneal cells, however, was increased in a time-dependent manner, indicating that Cath-Ka to enhances the phagocytic and bactericidal function of phagocytes (Fig. 2C, D). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that intraperitoneal injection of Cath-Ka did not affect the proportion of leukocytes (CD45hi), but significantly increased the recruitment of myeloid cells (CD45hiCD11b+) including inflammatory monocytes (CD45hiCD11b+Ly6ChiF4/80low), macrophages (CD45hiCD11b+Ly6ChiF4/80hi), and neutrophils (CD45hiCD11b+Ly6Clow Ly6G+) in the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 2E, G). Similarly, Cath-Ka did not change the overall leukocytes proportion in peripheral blood, while it elevated the percentages of inflammatory monocytes and neutrophils (Fig. 2F, H). The proportions of myeloid cells and neutrophils in peripheral blood rose markedly at 2 h and returned to baseline by 4 h after Cath-Ka injection (Fig. 2F, H). The chemotactic kinetic assay revealed that the Cath-Ka-induced recruitment of myeloid cells, monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils in the peritoneal cavity could persisted for approximately 12, 24, 24, and 24 h, respectively (Fig. S3). The proportion of pro-inflammatory macrophages peaked at 12 h, followed by a gradual decline. Collectively, these findings indicated that intraperitoneal administration of Cath-Ka strongly activates phagocytes and promotes the influx of myeloid cells into the peritoneal cavity and peripheral blood of mice.

## Cell chemotaxis promoted by Cath-Ka mainly related to its effects on macrophages
Considering the increase in peritoneal pro-inflammatory monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils following intraperitoneal injection of Cath-Ka, we next investigated whether Cath-Ka directly promotes macrophage and neutrophil chemotaxis in vitro. The trans-well experiments showed that Cath-Ka (10 µM) significantly induced the migration of murine bone marrow neutrophils and RAW264.7 cells in vitro, with the effect on macrophage migration being notably stronger than on neutrophils (Fig. 3A, B). We next examined the effect of Cath-Ka on neutrophil chemotaxis in the presence of macrophages. The results showed that the number of migrated neutrophils induced by Cath-Ka was almost three times higher in the group containing macrophages when compared to the group without macrophages, indicating that the powerful in vivo chemotactic effect of Cath-Ka on neutrophils may be partly dependent on macrophages (Fig. 3C). To further investigate whether Cath-Ka-induced phagocyte chemotaxis is related to the induction of macrophage immune responses, we examined the effect of Cath-Ka on the mRNA levels of macrophage chemokines in vitro. 10 µM Cath-Ka treatment of macrophages significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of the detected chemokines after 2 h when compared to the untreated control group, ranging from 1.30 to 130.00-fold upregulation, except for the CCL1 (Fig. 3D). The dynamic time point detection showed that the CXCL2 mRNA level reached the highest value of upregulation at 4 h after treatment and then gradually decreased (Fig. 3E). Further studies showed that Cath-Ka treatment for 4 h significantly upregulated CCL4, CXCL2, and CXCL9 mRNA levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3F). In agreement, ELISA results showed that Cath-Ka caused a remarkable increase in the secretion of CXCL2 in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3G). and that Cath-Ka was stronger than LPS in enhancing the expression of CXCL2. The above results indicated that in addition to directly promoting macrophage chemotaxis, Cath-Ka can also induce macrophages to produce chemotactic attractants that are critical for phagocyte recruitment.

## Cath-Ka promotes macrophage activation and M1 polarization
Next, we examined the effect of Cath-Ka on other indicators of RAW264.7 cell activation. Under a light microscope, Cath-Ka- and LPS-treated RAW264.7 cells exhibited an activated phenotype including increased cell size, and a transition from spherical to elongated and attached morphology, while normal, untreated cells showed plump and round morphology (Fig. 4A). Additionally, increased cell number was observed by microscopy in the Cath-Ka-treated groups but not in the LPS-treated group, which was further confirmed by cell viability assay (Fig. 4B), suggests a potential pro-proliferative effect. In agreement with its effects on chemokine expression, 2 h of Cath-Ka treatment significantly upregulated mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory mediators like iNOS, IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, but not the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 4C). Further studies showed that the mRNA and protein levels of the above pro-inflammatory mediators were gradually increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4D, E). Notably, Cath-Ka was weaker than LPS in enhancing the expression of the four pro-inflammatory cytokines. To detect whether endotoxin contamination in the Cath-Ka sample solution was responsible for the activation of RAW264.7 cells, TNF-α production was evaluated in the cells treated with Cath-Ka alone or with PMB. The ELISA result displayed that TNF-α production induced by LPS rather than Cath-Ka was significantly inhibited by PMB, suggesting that the activation of RAW264.7 cells was uniquely aroused by Cath-Ka presence (Fig. 4F). Moreover, the MFI of ROS in RAW264.7 cells was concentration-dependently increased after Cath-Ka and LPS treatment (Fig. 4G). ## Cath-Ka enhances the phagocytosis and intracellular bacterial killing activity of RAW264.7 cells
Considering that the phagocytosis and intracellular bacterial killing functions of macrophages are closely associated with the production of ROS, NO, and cytokines [18], the effects of Cath-Ka on phagocytotic and intracellular bacterial killing activity were evaluated in RAW264.7 cells. Compared with the control group, Cath-Ka (2.5–20 µM) and LPS increased the FITC-Dextran MFI and neutral red absorbance in a concentration dependent manner in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 5A, B), indicating enhanced endocytic capacity of macrophages. The E. coli phagocytosis assay showed that Cath-Ka administration augmented the number of bacteria engulfed into RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 5C). The intracellular bacterial killing assay demonstrated that the number of live bacteria in Cath-Ka treated cells was remarkably lesser compared to the control group (Fig. 5D), indicating that Cath-Ka can promote the intracellular bacterial killing activity of RAW264.7 cells. TEM provides morphological validation of the internalization process. The intracellular E. coli within macrophages was further examined by TEM. At 1 h post-infection, E. coli was present in vacuoles within macrophages (Fig. 5E, red arrows). Relatively more bacteria were accumulated in the Cath-Ka-treated cells compared to the control cells. Particularly, several E. coli was visible in one vesicle after Cath-Ka treatment. At 3 h post-infection, the signs of intense killing activity were observed in the Cath-Ka-treated group (Fig. 5F). Not only more bacteria were observed within RAW264.7 cells after treatment with Cath-Ka, but complete E. coli degradation was also observed (orange arrows), which was not observed in the untreated cells at the same experimental timepoint. These results collectively suggested that Cath-Ka can promote bacterial phagocytosis and decrease their intracellular survival in macrophages.

## Cath-Ka directly binds to both TLR2 and TLR4
The ability of Cath-Ka to bind to RAW264.7 cells was first observed using flow cytometry. The MFI of the FITC-labeled Cath-Ka-treated cells was dose-dependently increased compared with the control cells (Fig. 6A), which confirmed the binding of Cath-Ka to RAW264.7 cells. To determine whether Cath-Ka targets innate immune PRRs on macrophages, we investigated the effect of already known corresponding antagonists on Cath-Ka-mediated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The ELISA results demonstrated that the TLR2 inhibitor C29 and the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 but not antagonists for NLRP3, NOD1/2, RIG-I, cGAS-STING, or other TLR inhibitors suppressed Cath-Ka-induced TNF-α or IL-1β production (Fig. 6B and S4). Next, the binding of Cath-Ka to TLR2 and TLR4 was validated. The ELISA results showed that LTA/LPS stimulated TNF-α production more than Cath-Ka. However, the LTA/LPS-induced elevation of TNF-α was reduced after pretreatment with Cath-Ka, although Cath-Ka alone also stimulated TNF-α production (Fig. 6C). SPRi was further conducted, and the results indicated that Cath-Ka directly bound to TLR2 and TLR4. The respective binding affinities were strong, with KD values calculated as approximately 108 and 192 nM, respectively (Fig. 6D). However, Cath-Ka was unable to bind to MD2, which suggested that unlike LPS, Cath-Ka directly bound to TLR4 rather than MD2 (Figure S5A). Additionally, the peptide failed to bind to LPS and LTA. Consistently, the results of Native PAGE also showed that two new complex bands were formed when Cath-Ka was incubated with TLR2 or TLR4, demonstrating the direct binding of Cath-Ka to TLR2 or TLR4 protein. Moreover, the addition of MD2, LPS, and LTA did not alter the observed Cath-Ka band (Fig. S5B). Pull-down assays showed that Cath-Ka successfully pulled down TLR2, which was not the case for the negative control of Cath-Ka-1 (Fig. 6E). In agreement, CETSA showed that Cath-Ka significantly protected TLR2 and TLR4 from temperature-dependent denaturation, suggesting that Cath-Ka directly interacts with TLR2 and TLR4 (Fig. 6F).

## Cath-Ka activates TLR2/4-MyD88-MAPKs signaling pathways in RAW264.7 and peritoneal cells
Since Cath-Ka can interact directly with TLR2 and TLR4, we then investigated the effect of Cath-Ka on TLR2, TLR4 and the downstream MyD88-MAPKs signaling pathways. WB results displayed that the levels of TLR2, TLR4, and MyD88 began to increase at 1 h and reached the highest values at 4 h after Cath-Ka treatment (Fig. 7A). Meanwhile, strong phosphorylation of ERK, p38, and JNK was observed in Cath-Ka-treated cells at 0.5–1 h, 0.5–1 h, and 0.5–6 h after stimulation, respectively. Maximal phosphorylation of these proteins occurs at 0.5 h of Cath-Ka stimulation (Fig. 7A). Further, the activation of MAPKs at 0.5 h and the expressions of TLR2, TLR4, and MyD88 at 4 h in RAW264.7 cells were promoted by the presence of Cath-Ka in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7B). The expression of the NF-κB p65 subunit in the cytosolic fraction was not altered by Cath-Ka (Fig. S6), which may suggest marginal nuclear translocation of p65. This is consistent with the weak induction of the NF-κB-dependent cytokine TNF-α by Cath-Ka (Fig. 4F). Additional experiments showed that the enhanced protein phosphorylation as well as the augmented signaling gene expression induced by Cath-Ka were significantly reduced by C29 or TAK-242 (Fig. 7C, D). In an effort to further establish the contributions of TLR2, TLR4, and MAPKs to the observed activation of RAW264.7 cells in the presence of Cath-Ka, the cells were treated with specific inhibitors against each pathway. Pre-treatment with C29, TAK-242, U0126 (an ERK inhibitor), SB203580 (a p38 inhibitor), and SP600125 (a JNK inhibitor) all significantly repressed Cath-Ka-stimulated cell proliferation, as well as NO, IL-6, and TNF-α production (Fig. S7A). Consistently, the addition of the above inhibitors suppressed the Cath-Ka-induced bacterial phagocytosis and intracellular bacterial killing of macrophages (Fig. S7B-C). Therefore, these data suggested that Cath-Ka may induce multiple events associated with activated macrophages via activation of the TLR2/4-MyD88-MAPKs signaling axis. In addition, in vivo experiments showed that intraperitoneal injection of Cath-Ka also significantly up-regulated the levels of TLR2, TLR4 and MyD88 as well as phosphorylated ERK, p38 and JNK in peritoneal cells (Fig. 7E), indicating the importance of the TLR2/4-MyD88-MAPKs signaling pathway in activating peritoneal cells in the presence of Cath-Ka in vivo.

## The residues at N-terminus, disulfide bond and net charge of Cath-Ka greatly affects its functions
To define the structure and function relation of Cath-Ka, molecular docking and mutation analysis were carried out. As shown by Fig. 8A, Cath-Ka form an intramolecular disulfide bond which is in agreement with its MS results, and 3 hydrogen bonds with Arg234, His506, Gln482 in TLR4 which coupled with its Ala1 and Ser30. Of note, bioinformatics simulation study with LIGPLOT also supported the hydrogen bond formation between Ala1 and Arg234. Moreover, the four residues in N terminus of Cath-Ka also interacted with TLR4 through 14 hydrophobic interactions with Glu313, Glu264, Ser291, Phe265, Arg266, Ser289, Arg234, Arg332 and His311 (Fig. 8B). A series of Cath-Ka analogues were synthesized according to molecular docking results (Fig. 8C). These peptides exhibited CD spectra in SDS solution similar to that of the original peptide with some slight variations, and their contents of α-helix and random-coli altered (Fig. 8C, D). To explore the biological effects of its residues at N-terminal and disulfide bridge in middle on macrophage functions, the effects of Cath-Ka and its reducing and mutated peptides were compared for macrophage morphology, proliferation and TNF-α production. In comparison with the control group, four mutants, Cath-Ka-1 to -4, lost or decreased their abilities to change the morphology and promote the proliferation and TNF-α production. However, Cath-Ka-5 with amidation at C-terminus had stronger effects on macrophage function modulation than the original peptide (Fig. 8E-G).

## Discussion
Beneficial innate immune stimulation has been proven to be a useful therapeutic strategy in many contexts, including the prevention and treatment of infections [19, 20], particularly, bacterial elimination by phagocytes is nonspecific and effective across a broad spectrum of pathogens, making it difficult for bacteria to develop resistance to peptides that exert anti-infective properties by modulating the innate immune response. Although the frog immunostimulatory peptides such as Frenatin 2D, Ot-WHP, PopuCATH, Plasticin-L1, and Frenatin 2.1 S have been reported to enhance the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by mouse peritoneal macrophages, no one has defined clearly their targets on immune cells [21–25]. In this work, a novel Cathelicidin, Cath-Ka, is identified from the skin of K. pulchra. Cath-Ka is found to lack direct antimicrobial activity, but has the ability to enhance macrophage bactericidal function by binding TLR2 and TLR4 rather than other PRRs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a non-bactericidal Cathelicidin and of a frog skin-derived peptide that can specifically act on TLR2 and TLR4 to stimulate and modulate immunity. Moreover, different with Ot-WHP and PopuCATH which did not act as chemoattractants for neutrophils and macrophages [22, 25], Cath-Ka is capable of directly promoting both neutrophil and macrophage migration, and exhibits an even stronger effect in promoting neutrophil recruitment in the presence of macrophages, suggesting that Cath-Ka not only acts as a chemoattractant but also enhances the production of chemokines facilitating immune cell migration. Cath-Ka’s dual role in acting as a chemoattractant and stimulating chemokine production highlights its unique ability to orchestrate immune cell recruitment and activation.
M1 macrophages generally express phenotypic markers like CD80, CD86, and MHC-II on their surface and exert the antimicrobial activity by stimulating the overproduction of ROS and NO, as well as by inducing the activation of inflammasomes and the secretion of cytokines and chemokines [18, 26]. Consistent with its promoting effects on bacterial scavenging and the production of above factors by macrophages, Cath-Ka upregulates the expression of the M1 rather than M2 markers. Furthermore, engagement of TLR2 and TLR4 by some agonists such as rLsa21, SslE, and LL-37 reportedly augments the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and ROS, and bacterial phagocytosis as well as killing activity via differentially inducing the MyD88 and MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathways in macrophage [1, 2, 18, 27–31]. Consistent with its direct binding to TLR2 and TLR4, Cath-Ka promotes the proliferation, differentiation, migration, cytokines/chemokine production, and ROS accumulation in macrophages, thereby enhancing their endocytic capacity and overall bacterial clearance. Cath-Ka can’t activate the NF-κB pathway. Furtherly, Cath-Ka increases the expression of TLR2 and TLR4 in RAW264.7 cells, a phenomenon also observed with SslE in the same cell line and with LL-37 in mast cells [29, 32, 33]. Some cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α have been reported to increase TLR2 and TLR4 expression in macrophages and mast cells [34–36]. Likewise, it is possible that those cytokines induced by Cath-Ka cause the expression enhancement of TLR2 and TLR4 in RAW264.7 cells. Overall, Cath-Ka enhances the innate immune response by interacting directly with TLR2 and TLR4, promoting M1 macrophage polarization, and activating the MyD88-MAPKs pathway. This suggests that Cath-Ka could be a promising therapeutic agent for treating TLR2 and TLR4-related infectious diseases such as sepsis.
LPS is an important pathogenic factor for the patients infected by E. coli due to its strong inflammatory induction effect in vivo. However, the early inflammation is helpful to clear pathogenic microorganisms, which limits the usage of host defense peptides with LPS binding activity in anti-infection treatment in vivo. According to our molecular docking analysis, the binding region of Cath-Ka is close to the binding sites of MD2 and LPS on TLR4 [37, 38]. Therefore, it can be imaged that the competitive binding of Cath-Ka to TLR4, a phenomenon occurring between TLR4 and SPA4 as well as DiC14-amidine [39–41], prevents the formation of the TLR4/MD2-LPS complex due to steric hindrance effect and partially inhibit the strong effect of LPS from E. coli on the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, consequently protecting infected host. Notably, the SPA4 peptide derived from SP-A conforms to a “U shaped” structure when binding the activated TLR4 [39, 41]. Cath-Ka has also similar conformation in spite of its great difference in secondary structural components with SPA4. In addition, the hydrophilicity and the increasing cationic property of SPA4 have been though helpful for its binding to the activated TLR4 on the extracellular surface of immune cells [39–41]. Similarly, the residues of Cath-Ka form 14 hydrophobic interactions with those in TLR4, and those hydrophobic residues in N-terminus of Cath-Ka has been demonstrated to greatly affect its activity. Finally, the mutant with increasing charge value due to amidation modification, Cath-Ka-5, has also stronger activity than the original peptide. Thus, it seems that U shaped structure, increasing hydrophilicity and positive charge help the agonists bind TLR4.
Intraperitoneal injection of Cath-Ka markedly enhanced neutrophils recruitment to the mouse peritoneal cavity and peripheral blood. Neutrophils are not typically, resident in body cavities but are produced and maintained in the bone marrow and blood [42]. Cath-Ka increased CXCL2 production by RAW264.7 and peritoneal cavity cells, a key chemokine involved in neutrophil attraction [43]. Treatment with Cath-Ka also elevated the number of infiltrating neutrophils, particularly in the presence of macrophages, consistent with reports that TLR2 and TLR4 activation in macrophages induce the release of neutrophil survival factors and chemoattractants [44, 45]. These observations suggest that tissue-resident macrophages activated by Cath-Ka secrete neutrophil chemoattractants and pro-inflammatory mediators including CXCL2 and TNF-α, leading to rapid neutrophil influx and prolonged survival at the site of infection [46, 47].
Neutrophils also express TLR2 and TLR4 proteins on their surfaces, enabling direct regulation of their activation and survival [48, 49], and Cath-Ka can directly promote the neutrophil migration in vitro, indicating that it may act on neutrophils independently of macrophages The enhanceed expression of co-stimulatory molecules and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by Cath-Ka–stimulated macrophages may further facilitate interaction with and subsequent activation of T cells [18, 50]. Additional studies are required to fully elucidate the immunomodulatory capability of Cath-Ka as a potential immunotherapy agent to other immune cells including neutrophils and T cells. Given its selective activation of TLR2/4 signaling, Cath-Ka represents a promising therapeutic candidate for conditions involving insufficient immune activation, such as certain intracellular infections or immunosuppressed states. Unlike broad-spectrum antimicrobials, its targeted mechanism may help restore immune balance without triggering excessive inflammation.
In summary, our study identifies and characterizes Cath-Ka from the skin of K. pulchra. This novel Cathelicidin is able to induce macrophage activation and promotes inflammation. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a non-bactericidal, frog skin-derived Cathelicidin that can acts through macrophage TLR2 and TLR4, but not on other PRRs, to enhance pathogen clearance and modulate immune response. Cath-Ka may therefore function as a potent immunity activator and a promising candidate for immunomodulatory therapy. These findings also deepen our understanding of K. pulchra’s survival mechanisms and expand the known repertoire of natural Cathelicidins.
