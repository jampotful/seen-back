Abundance in secret: a review of the genus <i>Leptobrachium</i> (Anura, Megophryidae) in India, with descriptions of two new species.
Sarmah ANDA, Garg S, Tajo T, Upadhyaya K R, Hanken J, Biju SD. (2026). PeerJ. . doi:10.7717/peerj.20397. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Abundance in secret: a review of the genus Leptobrachium (Anura, Megophryidae) in India, with descriptions of two new species

## Abstract
AbstractOf the 39 currently recognized species in the Slender Armed Frog genus Leptobrachium, four are reported from India, Leptobrachium aryatium, L. bompu, L. smithi, and L. sylheticum. Based on new collections, we review these reported species by integrating molecular, morphological, and behavioral data. Our comparative analyses of external and internal morphology as well as molecular data and acoustic properties reveal two new species-level lineages from the Lower Dibang Valley and Shi Yomi districts of Arunachal Pradesh, India, which we describe here as new species. Phylogenetically, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. are members of the L. bompu species group, but they are distinct from L. bompu sensu stricto by DNA sequence divergence in the 16S mitochondrial rRNA gene of 3.6–4.2% and 4.5–4.7%, respectively. A population previously identified as L. cf. bompu from Medog County, China, is also assignable to Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. Each new species is distinguished from congeners by a combination of diagnostic morphological characters, including in the case of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., a unique advertisement call comprising two distinct call types; unicolored silvery white iris; moderately large webbing between toes; and ventral coloration. Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. on the other hand, is distinguishable by its head length equal to width; nostril equidistant from the snout tip and the eye; internarial distance equal to the distances from nostril to eye and from nostril to snout tip; distance from the tip of inner metatarsal tubercle to the tip of first toe shorter than the length of inner metatarsal tubercle itself; and frontoparietals separated at the midline by a moderate fontanelle. Analyses of multiple new populations representing members of the L. smithi group reveal that L. smithi is absent from the Indian fauna; all previous records attributed to this species from India instead represent L. aryatium or L. sylheticum. These discoveries highlight the prevalence of taxonomic misidentifications in the absence of detailed systematic studies as well as the underestimation of diversity in the genus Leptobrachium, not only from India but across its known range. Our results underscore the need for dedicated surveys that document amphibian diversity in underexplored regions of Northeast India.Keywords: Amphibia, Arunachal Pradesh, Bioacoustics, Cranial osteology, Integrative taxonomy, Leptobrachium bompu group, Leptobrachium smithi group, Mitochondrial phylogeny, Northeast India, Tadpoles

## Introduction
With 39 recognized species, Leptobrachium Tschudi, 1838 is the second most speciose genus within the anuran subfamily Leptobrachiinae of the family Megophryidae (AmphibiaWeb, 2025; Frost, 2025). These frogs are found across Asia, from the Sunda and Philippine Islands in the east, north towards Indochina and southern China, and finally to northeastern India and Bangladesh in the west (Stuart et al., 2020; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Frost, 2025). Despite a recent surge in taxonomic studies, with 17 of the currently known species described only in the last decade, the diversity of Leptobrachium still remains underestimated (Matsui et al., 2010; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Stuart et al., 2020; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Purkayastha et al., 2025; Sarmah et al., 2025). Molecular studies have revealed that several Leptobrachium species comprise multiple cryptic lineages that may represent new species, although few have been formally described (Brown et al., 2009; Matsui et al., 2010; Hamidy et al., 2012; Yang, Wang & Chan, 2016; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Stuart et al., 2020; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021). These findings suggest the likely existence of additional undescribed taxa, either among the known yet understudied populations of the genus or in unexplored regions within its range (Stuart et al., 2011; Hamidy et al., 2012; Hamidy & Matsui, 2014; Yang, Wang & Chan, 2016; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Stuart et al., 2020; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Sarmah et al., 2025). Northeast India, with four currently recognized species, lies along the westernmost distribution limit of the genus. Leptobrachium smithi Matsui, Nabhitabhata & Panha, 1999, the earliest reported of the four, has been recorded from the states of Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Tripura (Sengupta, Choudhury & Das, 2001; Dutta et al., 2013; Purkayastha, Khan & Roychoudhury, 2020), while Leptobrachium sylheticum Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021 has been genetically confirmed from Tripura (Mahony et al., 2017; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021). Another closely related species, Leptobrachium aryatium Purkayastha et al., 2025, was recently described from Assam and also reported from the northern Khasi Hills region of Meghalaya (Purkayastha et al., 2025). Finally, the phylogenetically distinct Leptobrachium bompu Sondhi & Ohler, 2011 is to date the only species of the genus described and reported from Himalayan regions in the state of Arunachal Pradesh (Sondhi & Ohler, 2011; Sarmah et al., 2025).
Despite its description nearly 15 years ago, studies of Leptobrachium bompu and records of its occurrence within India are scarce (Saikia, Sinha & Kharkongor, 2017; Ohler et al., 2018). Only recently, Sarmah et al. (2025) confirmed the phylogenetic relationships of L. bompu based on genetic samples from topotypic specimens, reported additional populations and assessed intraspecific variation, and provided initial accounts of bioacoustics and tadpole morphology from Indian populations. The study also revealed that populations from Medog County, China, previously assigned to this species do not represent the nominal L. bompu. More precise delineation of species boundaries in the L. bompu complex thus becomes pertinent, especially given the possibility of additional but unrecognized cryptic diversity in underexplored regions within its geographic range, as is well documented for various anuran taxa (Fouquet et al., 2007; Funk, Caminer & Ron, 2012; Biju et al., 2014a; Garg & Biju, 2016, Garg & Biju, 2017; Mahony et al., 2018).
The vividly colored eyes of Leptobrachium frogs have long represented an important diagnostic characteristic for the genus, with the iris being either bicolored (a portion is red, orange, white, blue, or green) or uniformly colored (Dubois & Ohler, 1998; Ohler, Teynié & David, 2004; Brown et al., 2009; Matsui et al., 2010; Hamidy & Matsui, 2010; Stuart et al., 2011; Stuart et al., 2012; Stuart et al., 2020; Wogan, 2012; Yang, Wang & Chan, 2016; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021). Numerous species have been diagnosed as having a “red-eye” (L. pullum, L. mouhoti, L. hendricksoni, L. smithi, and more recently L. rakhinense, L. lunatum, and L. sylheticum), but species boundaries in this “red-eyed” group are confusing (Stuart et al., 2020). Various studies have since delimited these “red-eyed” species in an effort to resolve taxonomic ambiguities and define species boundaries (Matsui, Nabhitabhata & Panha, 1999; Stuart, Sok & Neang, 2006; Matsui et al., 2010; Stuart et al., 2011; Stuart et al., 2020; Wogan, 2012; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021).
The first report of a “red-eyed” Leptobrachium population from India was made by Pillai & Chanda (1979), who identified the specimens as Leptobrachium hasseltii. However, following the description of L. smithi from Thailand (Matsui, Nabhitabhata & Panha, 1999) and the subsequent geographic restriction of L. hasseltii to Indonesia, all earlier Indian records of L. hasseltii were reassigned to L. smithi (Sengupta, Choudhury & Das, 2001; Pawar & Birand, 2001; Choudhury et al., 2002). In 2012, Leptobrachium rakhinense was described from Myanmar, and based on photographic comparisons, personal communications, and geographic proximity, the northeast Indian populations then attributed to L. smithi were proposed to represent L. rakhinense (Wogan, 2012). However, Dutta et al. (2013) rejected this proposal and reaffirmed the identity of these populations as L. smithi based on morphological and acoustic comparisons. Consequently, L. smithi continued to be included in national as well as state-level inventories for Northeast India (Lalremsanga, Sailo & Hooroo, 2007; Das et al., 2009; Mathew & Sen, 2010; Decemson et al., 2021). Subsequently, Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021) described L. sylheticum from Bangladesh and, based on molecular data, assigned a population previously reported from the Indian state of Tripura to this new species (Mahony et al., 2017). Due to a lack of comprehensive genetic data, the authors refrained from reassigning other “red-eyed” Leptobrachium populations from Northeast India. Most recently, Purkayastha et al. (2025) described L. aryatium, a species closely related to L. sylheticum, and confirmed only the identity of populations from Garbhanga Reserve Forest and nearby areas in Assam and adjacent areas in the northern Khasi Hills of Meghalaya as belonging to this new species. The taxonomic status of the remaining known “red-eyed” Leptobrachium populations across Northeast India remains unresolved due to the absence of genetic data and other complementary data types. The coexistence of L. sylheticum and L. aryatium, two morphologically similar and closely related species, combined with the uncertain presence of L. smithi in India, continues to further obscure the species boundaries and distribution limits of the genus in the region.
During field surveys across northeastern India, we collected samples of “red-eyed” Leptobrachium populations from multiple states, along with populations resembling Leptobrachium bompu from Arunachal Pradesh. To resolve the taxonomic identity of these populations, clarify the species boundaries among Indian Leptobrachium, and assess their phylogenetic relationships, we conducted a comprehensive investigation built upon more than 15 years of extensive fieldwork. This investigation provided an opportunity to revise the taxonomy of all known Indian members of the genus by using an integrative approach that includes adult and tadpole morphology, bioacoustics, cranial osteology, and molecular phylogenetics. Below, we present the results of these analyses and discuss their implications for the taxonomy and distribution of Leptobrachium in India.

## Materials and Methods

## Field surveys and sampling
During herpetological explorations in northeastern India over the past 15 years, we sampled multiple populations representing members of the “red-eyed” Leptobrachium group from the states of Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, and Tripura. We also sampled populations of Leptobrachium that resembled L. bompu at Tiwarigaon (28°13′28.2″N 95°49′55.2″E, 1,393 m asl) in the Lower Dibang Valley district, and at Mechuka (28°35′48.6″N 94°07′20.9″E, 1,950 m asl) in the Shi Yomi district of Arunachal Pradesh. Field surveys and sampling were carried out as described in previous publications (e.g., Garg & Biju, 2017; Garg & Biju, 2019). Sampling was done through opportunistic searches from April to September, mostly at night but also in the daytime. Adult frogs were located by males calling at night; tadpoles were collected from small stagnant pools and still parts of nearby streams using scoop nets. Live specimens were photographed in the field or in captivity. Tissue samples were excised from the thigh muscle of adult specimens or tail muscle of tadpoles euthanized in aqueous tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222), preserved in absolute ethanol and later stored at −20 °C for genetic studies. Euthanized adult specimens were fixed in 4% formalin, rinsed in water and preserved in 70% ethanol. Tadpoles were preserved in 4% neutral-buffered formalin (NBF). Type specimens are deposited in the Zoological Survey of India (ZSI), Kolkata, and referred specimens are available at the Systematics Lab, University of Delhi (SDBDU), India. Calls were recorded in the wild. Geographical coordinates and elevation data were recorded using a Garmin Etrex 10x GPS with the WGS84 datum system. Locality coordinates for vouchers outside India, when used, were either taken from published sources or estimated using Google Earth if exact coordinates were not provided in the respective literature (Table S1). Maps were prepared using QGIS version 2.6.1 (http://www.qgis.org).
Fieldwork, including collection of animals in the field, was conducted with permissions and following guidelines from the responsible authorities in the State Forest Departments of Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya and Tripura, India (Study permits: No. CWL/GEN/355/2021/3182, No.FWLG/Permission/Study/Pt/4, No.F.8(163)/For-WL-2001/38-40, No.34/2/TRG/2006/Forests/451, No. FWC. G/16 and No. FWC/Research/10/1010-1018). Research received ethical approval from Department of Environmental Studies, University of Delhi (DES/1020 dated 9 February 2015), India.

## Molecular study
Methodologies were largely similar to those described in our previous work (Sarmah et al., 2025). Genomic DNA was extracted from newly sampled specimens by using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Two mitochondrial gene fragments, ∼540 base-pairs (bp) of 16S rRNA for all new tissue samples and ∼750 bp of 12S rRNA + tRNA-VAL for representative samples of species in the focal region, including L. bompu, were PCR-amplified and cycle-sequenced using published primer sets and protocol (Simon et al., 1994; Richards & Moore, 1996). DNA sequences were checked and assembled in ChromasPro v1.34 (Technelysium Pty Ltd., Australia) and deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank under accession numbers PX050546–PX050559 and PX050561–PX050565 (Table S2). The data is additionally deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) at European Molecular Biology Laboratory’s European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI) under accession numbers OZ365166–OZ365184 for project accession PRJEB102631. Additional sampling was carried out by retrieving homologous DNA sequences from previous studies available in the NCBI GenBank. These included all sequences available for members of the Leptobrachium bompu and L. smithi groups, and sequences representing exemplars of all other currently recognized Leptobrachium species (AmphibiaWeb, 2025; Frost, 2025) and of five outgroup taxa comprising four other Megophryidae genera, and a member of Pelodytidae. Following recent phylogenetic studies (Liang et al., 2017; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Stuart et al., 2020; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Sarmah et al., 2025), a concatenated dataset from a total of 120 samples (Table S2) comprising regions of the 12S rRNA, tRNA-Val, and 16S rRNA genes was assembled, aligned using the ClustalW tool (Thompson, Higgins & Gibson, 1994), and manually optimized in MEGA 7.0 (Kumar, Stecher & Tamura, 2016).
Phylogenetic analyses were performed on the resulting alignment of 2,495 bp using Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) methods. The best-fit model of DNA evolution was determined through a greedy search in PartitionFinder 2 (Lanfear et al., 2017) using the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc). Bayesian analyses were performed in MrBayes 3.1.2 (Ronquist & Huelsenbeck, 2003) using the best determined GTR+I+G model. Four parallel runs of four Metropolis coupled Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMCMC) chains were executed for 20 million generations with trees sampled after every 1,000 generations. Convergence of the runs was confirmed by average standard deviation of the split frequencies of less than 0.01 and potential scale reduction factors of ∼1.0. Stationarity of log likelihood trends and effective sample sizes (ESS) for all parameters were observed in the software itself. Bayesian posterior probabilities (BPP) were summarized after discarding the first 25% trees as burn-in (Huelsenbeck et al., 2001); clades with BPP ≥ 95% were considered strongly supported (Leaché & Reeder, 2002). Maximum Likelihood (ML) analysis was performed using the IQ-TREE (v. 1.6.12) software (Nguyen et al., 2015) with 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFB) replicates (Hoang et al., 2018) executed with the GTR+F+I+G4 model. Nodes with UFB support values ≥ 95% were considered strongly supported (Minh, Nguyen & Haeseler, 2013). Intra- and interspecific uncorrected pairwise distances in the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene were computed in MEGA 7.0 (Kumar, Stecher & Tamura, 2016) using a trimmed dataset of 534 bp for 82 sequences representing members of the Leptobrachium bompu and L. smithi groups.

## Morphological study
Adult morphology. Newly sampled specimens were examined, measured, and compared with closely related species through topotypic material, original descriptions and other relevant literature. Sex and sexual maturity were determined externally by the presence of vocal sacs in males, or by examination of gonads through a small lateral or ventral incision. Only sexually mature individuals were used for morphometric studies. Methodologies, measurements and associated terminology follow Biju et al. (2020) and Garg et al. (2022). The following measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mm using digital slide-calipers with the aid of a Nikon SMZ 1500 stereomicroscope: SVL (snout-vent length); HW (head width, at the angle of the jaws); HL (head length, from the rear of the mandible to the tip of the snout); MN (distance from the rear of the mandible to the nostril); MFE (distance from the rear of the mandible to the anterior orbital border); MBE (distance from the rear of the mandible to the posterior orbital border); SL (snout length, from the tip of the snout to the anterior orbital border); EL (eye length, horizontal distance between bony orbital borders); IUE (inter upper eyelid width, shortest distance between the upper eyelids); UEW (maximum upper eyelid width); IN (internarial distance; IFE (internal front of the eyes, shortest distance between the anterior orbital borders); IBE (internal back of the eyes, shortest distance between the posterior orbital borders); NS (distance from the nostril to the tip of the snout); EN (distance from the front of the eye to the nostril); TYD (greatest tympanum diameter); TYE (distance from the tympanum to the back of the eye); FAL (forearm length, from flexed elbow to base of outer palmar tubercle); HAL (hand length, from base of outer palmar tubercle to tip of third finger); FLI–IV (finger lengths I–IV); TL (thigh length, from vent to knee); ShL (shank length, from knee to heel); FOL (foot length, from base of inner metatarsal tubercle to tip of fourth toe); TFOL (total foot length, from heel to tip of fourth toe); FD (maximum width of finger disc); FW (finger width, measured at the base of the disc); TD (maximum width of toe disc); TW (toe width, measured at the base of the disc); and TLI–V (toe lengths TLI–V). Digits are numbered by Roman numerals I–V. Webbing formulae follow Savage & Heyer (1967) as modified by Myers & Duellman (1982). The amount of webbing relative to subarticular tubercles is described by numbering the tubercles 1–3, starting from the base of the digits (Biju et al., 2014b). All measurements were taken from the right side, except when a character on that side was damaged and the left side was used instead.
Morphometric analysis. We conducted both univariate and multivariate analyses of 28 characters to assess the degree of morphological differentiation among species. Measurements were obtained from adult males of the Leptobrachium bompu group and Indian members of the Leptobrachium smithi group. All characters except SVL were size-adjusted (Thorpe, 1975) to reduce the impact of allometry. The size-adjusted measurements were then subjected to separate principal component analyses (PCA) for each group. For the L. bompu group, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) had eigenvalues greater than 1.0 and were retained, while for the L. smithi group, the first five components (PC1–PC5) met this criterion and were retained. The raw (unadjusted) values of all measured characters were also subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) separately for each group. When ANOVA results were statistically significant, Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc tests were used for pairwise species comparisons. The six most significant characters contributing to variation within each group were visualized using box plots. All analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2021) and visualized using the ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) and ggfortify (Horikoshi & Tang, 2018) packages.
Cranial osteology. We prepared high-resolution micro-computed tomography (µCT) scans for the skulls of four Leptobrachium species. Specimens were scanned using a SkyScan 1273 scanner (Bruker MicroCT, Belgium) with voltage and current set to 70 kV and 300 μA, respectively, exposure times ranging between 300 and 400 ms, and voxel resolutions between 38 and 40 µm. The raw X-ray data were reconstructed using NRecon version 1.6.6.0 (Bruker MicroCT). The resulting image stacks were processed and visualized in VG StudioMax version 2025.1 (Volume Graphics, Germany) using various rendering and segmentation tools. The exported 3D mesh files are available online on MorphoSource. Osteological descriptions follow the terminology of Trueb (1973), Trueb (1993). Descriptions are based on a single specimen for each species and do not account for intraspecific variation. We also note the limitations of micro-CT scanning in rendering poorly ossified or cartilaginous structures, which may preclude the resolution of apparent continuity, or lack of it, between adjacent bones. CT scans from additional individuals or cleared-and-stained specimens may be needed to further assess such features.
Tadpole morphology. The newly sampled tadpoles were examined under a Nikon SMZ 1500 stereomicroscope and staged following Gosner (1960). Twelve tadpoles ranging from Gosner stages 28 to 39 were utilized for morphometric studies. A detailed morphological description was made based on a Gosner stage-37 tadpole. Measurements were taken to the nearest 0.1 mm using digital slide-calipers. The following 18 characters were measured: total length (TL); body length (BL); body height (BH); maximum body width (BW); snout to upper tail fin (SU); snout to spiracle (SS); tail length (TAL); maximum tail height (MTH); upper fin height (UFH); lower fin height (LFH); tail muscle height (TMH); tail muscle width (TMW); snout to nares (SN); nares to eye (NE); maximum eye diameter (ED); interorbital distance (IOD); internarial distance (IND); and oral disc width (ODW). Methodologies, larval measurements and terminology are as described in Gopika et al. (2023) following Altig & McDiarmid (1999) and Altig (2007). Mouthparts were illustrated with the aid of photographs taken using a digital Q-imaging camera fitted to the stereomicroscope. Oral morphology and margins of papillae were visualized using blue ink.

## Bioacoustics
Vocalizations from males of two newly sampled populations were recorded at Tiwarigaon and Mawlynnong villages. The calls were recorded at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz with 16-bit resolution on a Zoom H6 portable audio recorder connected to a Sennheiser K6 unidirectional microphone held 10–30 cm from the calling animal. Input levels were adjusted prior to each recording to avoid clipping of the amplitude envelopes and to maintain a uniform signal-to-noise ratio throughout the call. Air temperature at the calling site was measured using a thermometer at the time of recording.
Acoustic properties of the calls were analyzed using Raven Pro (v.1.6.4) software (K. Lisa Yang Center for Conservation Bioacoustics at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, 2024), following Charif, Waack & Strickman (2010). Thirteen acoustic properties (12 temporal: number of calls in a call group, call group duration, call duration, call rise time, call fall time, number of pulses, pulse rate, pulse duration, pulse rise time, 50% pulse rise time, pulse fall time, 50% pulse fall time; and one spectral: overall dominant frequency) were measured following the definitions, terminology, and methods described in Bee, Suyesh & Biju (2013). Oscillograms showing the amplitude-vs.-time waveform were prepared for visual representation of the call segments at different time frames (30 s, 10 s, 1s, and 0.1 s). The overall dominant frequency was obtained using Raven’s spectrogram function (1,024-point fast Fourier transform, Hann window, 50% overlap, 43.1-Hz resolution), and spectrograms were prepared to match the 1s time frame oscillograms. Representative cuts of the call recordings analysed are deposited in the Zenodo repository under DOI 10.5281/zenodo.16626216.

## Results

## Phylogenetic relationships and genetic divergence
Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) analyses yield trees with identical topology (Fig. 1). Moreover, except for the placement of the Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) bompu group as discussed below, major clades and relationships recovered are also broadly congruent with recent mitochondrial phylogenies of Leptobrachium, particularly those that include representatives of nearly all recognized species (e.g., Matsui et al., 2010; Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Sarmah et al., 2025).

## Phylogenetic relationships of the genus Leptobrachium and geographical distribution of species in India.
(A) Maximum Likelihood phylogram for the genus Leptobrachium based on 2,495 bp of mitochondrial DNA. The phylogenetic position and relationships of newly sampled populations from India include two new species in the L. bompu species group and multiple new localities for members of the L. smithi species group. Branch values indicate Bayesian Posterior Probabilities (BPP < 0.50) and Ultrafast Bootstrap Support (UFB < 50%), respectively. Voucher details for taxon labels at the terminal nodes are referenced in Table S2. Colored bars next to species names are referenced on the maps in Figs. 1B and 2. Asterisks denote paraphyly for the clades representing the subgenus Leptobrachium. Clade names (V–I to V–II and L–I to L–IV) are referenced in Results. Key morphological traits in the two focal species groups are illustrated to the right of the colored bars (see Results and Discussion). (B) Geographical distributions of five Leptobrachium species herein recognized to occur in India.
The genus Leptobrachium sensu lato is recovered as a monophyletic group (BPP 1.0, UFB 92) within the subfamily Leptobrachiinae, with two major previously identified subgenus-level groups, Leptobrachium sensu stricto (BPP 1.0, UFB 100) and Vibrissaphora (BPP 1.0, UFB 97), recovered as well-supported, monophyletic clades. In addition, we recover a third major clade representing the recently recognized Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) bompu group (Sarmah et al., 2025) as sister to the clade containing Leptobrachium sensu stricto + Vibrissaphora. This branching scheme renders Leptobrachium sensu stricto, as presently recognized, paraphyletic. The latter clade, referred to as L-IV (sensu Pawangkhanant et al. (2018), Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021), and Sarmah et al. (2025); subclade II sensu Matsui et al. (2010)), was previously shown to be nested within Leptobrachium sensu stricto (Sarmah et al., 2025), although previous analyses did not include some of the new populations sampled in the present study.
The Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) bompu group contained the previously reported topotype of L. bompu from India and a phylogenetically sister (BPP 1.0, UFB 96) yet divergent population (distances of 3.6–4.2% in 16S) from Medog, Tibet, China, identified as “L. cf. bompu” (Sarmah et al., 2025). Our new sample from Mechuka town in Arunachal Pradesh, India, nests with “L. cf. bompu” from China, and the two populations show a shallow genetic distance of 0.2% in 16S; we regard them as conspecific. Additionally, our new sample from Tiwarigaon village in Arunachal Pradesh is recovered as a distinct lineage that is basal to all other known populations, viz., L. bompu + “L. cf. bompu,” with high support (BPP 1.0, UFB 100). This new mtDNA lineage exhibits considerable DNA sequence divergence 4.5–4.7% from L. bompu and 4.9–5.2% from “L. cf. bompu”. Pairwise sequence comparisons between all included samples are provided in Table S3.
The three other previously identified subclades within Leptobrachium sensu stricto (L-I, L-II, and L-III) and their respective species and species-group-level clusters are otherwise largely in agreement with previous studies, although some specific relationships differ slightly. Subclade L-I (sensu Pawangkhanant et al. (2018), Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021), and Sarmah et al. (2025); subclade II sensu Matsui et al. (2010)), which corresponds to the Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) smithi group (BPP 1.0, UFB 100), shows a sister group relationship with subclade L-II (sensu Pawangkhanant et al. (2018), Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021), and Sarmah et al. (2025)); subclade II sensu Matsui et al. (2010)), which in turn corresponds to the Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) montanum group (BPP 1.0, UFB 100). These two subclades together (BPP 0.97, UFB 95) are well-supported (BPP 1.0, UFB 100) as sister to the Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) hasseltii group (BPP 1.0, UFB 98) represented by subclade L-III (sensu Pawangkhanant et al. (2018), Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021), and Sarmah et al. (2025); subclade II sensu Matsui et al. (2010)).
All remaining new Indian samples are recovered within the Leptobrachium (Leptobrachium) smithi group, but surprisingly none nests with L. smithi, a species long presumed to occur in India, or with L. rakhinense and L. tenasserimense described from adjacent Myanmar and Thailand, respectively. Instead, populations from the states of Manipur, Mizoram, and Tripura, and from the southern East Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, nest with typical L. sylheticum from Bangladesh and with a previously reported population from Tripura. Furthermore, the populations sampled from two localities in Assam and three from the East and West Garo Hills of Meghalaya nest with the typical populations of L. aryatium. Intraspecific DNA sequence divergence between various populations of L. aryatium and of L. sylheticum are on average only 0.1% and 0.4%, respectively. All the species-level clusters and relationships within the group are recovered with high support (BPP 1.0, UFB > 99), except for the sister group relationship between L. smithi and L. rakhinense (BPP 0.57, UFB 79).
The basal subgenus Vibrissaphora is further resolved into two well-supported subclades: (1) the Leptobrachium (Vibrissaphora) boringii group (BPP 1.0, UFB 100) (i.e., subclade IV sensu Matsui et al. (2010); subclade V-I sensu Pawangkhanant et al., 2018, Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021), and Sarmah et al. (2025)); and (2) the Leptobrachium (Vibrissaphora) pullus group (BPP 1.0, UFB 100) (i.e.. subclade V sensu Matsui et al. (2010); subclade V–II sensu Pawangkhanant et al. (2018), Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021), and Sarmah et al. (2025)).

## Taxonomic grouping of species
Owing to the complex taxonomic history of species currently recognized in the genus Leptobrachium sensu lato and the ongoing lack of consensus regarding taxonomic classification (e.g., Liu, 1945; Dubois & Ohler, 1998; Rao & Wilkinson, 2008; Zheng, Li & Fu, 2008; Matsui et al., 2010), comprehensive reassessments of species groupings within the genus are lacking. For the purpose of clarity and to better enable a working taxonomy, we suggest the following tentative division of all currently recognized species based on their broadly consistent recovery as species-group-level clades across several recent phylogenetic studies (e.g., Pawangkhanant et al., 2018; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Sarmah et al., 2025) and the present study: (1) L. bompu group (L. bompu, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov.); (2) L. boringii group (L. ailaonicum, L. boringii, L. chapaense, L. huashen, L. leishanense, L. liui, L. masatakasatoi, L. promustache, and L. tengchongense); (3) L. hasseltii group (L. hasseltii, L. hendricksoni, L. ingeri, L. kanowitense, and L. nigrops); (4) L. montanum group (L. abbotti, L. gunungense, L. kantonishikawai, L. lumadorum, L. mangyanorum, L. montanum, L. tagbanorum, and L. waysepuntiense); (5) L. pullus group (L. banae, L. buchardi, L. guangxiense, L. hainanense, L. leucops, L. lunatum, L. mouhoti, L. ngoclinhense, L. pullus, L. xanthops, and L. xanthospilum); (6) L. smithi group (L. aryatium, L. rakhinense, L. smithi, L. sylheticum, and L. tenasserimense). Three of these groups—the L. smithi group, L. montanum group, and L. hasseltii group—form a well-supported, monophyletic clade that corresponds to the subgenus Leptobrachium, also referred to as the “Sundaland + Thai” clade (Matsui et al., 2010). Two additional groups—the L. pullus group and L. boringii group—comprise species predominantly assigned to the subgenus Vibrissaphora, which aligns with the “China + Indochina” clade (Matsui et al., 2010). Furthermore, we identify a sixth, distinct group, the L. bompu group or the “Eastern Himalaya” clade, composed of species endemic to the eastern Himalayan region of India and China. Two of the six recognized groups, L. bompu and L. smithi, are distributed in India (Fig. 2).

Eye coloration, a character long regarded as taxonomically important, exhibits a distinct pattern (unicolored vs. bicolored) across these major biogeographic clades. The “Sundaland + Thai” clade is predominantly characterized by unicolored eyes, with 13 of 18 species exhibiting this trait. In contrast, the “China + Indochina” clade is dominated by species with bicolored eyes, present in 16 of the 20 species. Notably, all species within the “Eastern Himalaya” clade consistently display unicolored eyes. Among the various species groups, the L. bompu and L. montanum groups are entirely composed of species with unicolored eyes, as is the L. hasseltii group, with the exception of L. hendricksoni, which also exhibits bicolored eyes (Matsui, Nabhitabhata & Panha, 1999). All members of the L. smithi group display bicolored eyes, as do members of the L. boringii group, except for L. chapaense, which likely shows a unicolored state too (Fei, Ye & Jiang, 2010). Members of the L. pullus group, however, show a mixed composition, with both unicolored and bicolored-eyed species. Detailed species-wise variation in eye color pattern and iris coloration is provided in Table S4.

## Morphometric differentiation in the focal species groups
Statistical analyses of morphometric data from adult specimens document morphological differentiation among recognized and new candidate species in the two focal species groups of our study. For the Leptobrachium bompu group, the first two principal components (PCs) together account for 91.62% of the total variance (Table S5). The PCA scatterplot reveals three distinct and non-overlapping clusters that correspond to L. bompu and the two newly sampled populations from Mechuka and Tiwarigaon (Fig. 3). On the other hand, for the Indian members of the L. smithi group, PC1 and PC2 explain 65.15% of the total variation. The remaining significant components, PC3 to PC5, collectively account for 28.76% of the variation (Table S5). Although the PCA scatterplot shows slight overlap between the 95% confidence ellipses of L. sylheticum and L. aryatium, the two species still form distinct clusters (Fig. 3).

To assess species-level morphological divergence, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on the unadjusted raw morphometric data for both the studied groups. Multiple characters differ significantly among species within each group (p < 0.05). Subsequent Tukey HSD post hoc tests identify specific pairwise differences: while some characters consistently distinguish all three L. bompu group species, others differentiate only specific species pairs, with different characters separating different taxon combinations. In the L. smithi group, despite only two species analysed, several characters show statistically significant divergence. Six characters showing the highest interspecific variance and strongest pairwise discriminatory capacity were selected from each group and visualized using box-and-whisker plots (Fig. 3). For the L. bompu group, the most informative characters are SVL, EL, FOL, HW, IN, and TFOL. For the L. smithi group, the top diagnostic traits are ShL, MN, FAL, IBE, HL, and length of finger I (FIL). The combined results of the ANOVA and Tukey HSD tests provide robust statistical support for species differentiation based on morphometric data and complement the clustering patterns observed in the PCA. More importantly, these morphometric findings strongly corroborate the results of our multigene phylogenetic analysis in delineating the Mechuka and Tiwarigaon populations from Leptobrachium bompu, as well as distinguishing the various populations of L. sylheticum and L. aryatium.

## Systematic accounts and descriptions of new species
Etymology. The etymology of the name Leptobrachium was not reported by Tschudi (1838). It is likely a combination of two words—‘leptos’ Greek for thin or delicate, referring to the thin limbs of the members of this genus, and the Latin ‘brachium,’ referring to the upper arm. Hence, the proposed common name, Slender Armed Frogs.
Diagnosis. Small to large frogs, SVL 30–102 mm; body stocky with slender forelimbs and shorter hind limbs; maxillary and premaxillary teeth present, vomerine teeth absent (based on present study); skin of dorsum shagreened to granular or with a network of dermal ridges; axillary glands mostly present, rarely absent; and fingertips without prominent discs.
Distribution. The genus Leptobrachium is widely distributed across Asia, from northeastern India to southern China and mainland Southeast Asia, into the Sundaic and Philippine Islands (Frost, 2025).

## Indian members of the genus Leptobrachium
The previously known diversity of Leptobrachium in India includes four species in two species groups, all reported only from the northeastern region. Within the L. smithi species group, L. aryatium, L. smithi, and L. sylheticum have been documented from most northeastern states, excluding Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland. In contrast, L. bompu is the only recognized member of the L. bompu species group from India, with a distribution restricted to Arunachal Pradesh (Fig. 2). Here we describe two new species assigned to the L. bompu group and assign populations previously misidentified as L. smithi from various parts of northeastern India to two other species of the L. smithi group, L. aryatium and L. sylheticum. By integrating multiple lines of evidence, we provide detailed systematic accounts and descriptions for all five species currently known to occur in India, as well as revised group-level diagnosis based on new data from adult and larval external morphology, osteology, molecular phylogenetics, bioacoustics, and natural history.

## Leptobrachium bompu group
This group is distinguished from other Leptobrachium species groups by the following suite of morphological characters: (1) habitus stocky; (2) medium-sized adults (male SVL 46–57 mm); (3) tympanum indistinct; (4) snout nearly rounded in dorsal view; (5) prominent, circular inner and outer palmar tubercles; (6) finger and toe tips swollen without discs; (7) foot webbing small to medium, reaching just above, but not beyond, the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV; (8) dorsal skin with irregular glandular dermal reticulum; (9) dorsum and lateral side of abdomen (including flank) without prominent markings; (10) iris unicolored, greyish-white with silver tinge or grey-blue; (11) sclera black; (12) elongated femoral glands on posterior surface of each thigh; (13) axillary glands posterior to arm insertion; (14) maxillary and premaxillary teeth present, vomerine teeth absent; (15) the paired frontoparietals extend anterolaterally beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid and are only slightly broader posteriorly; (16) the maxillary process of the nasal is relatively long and extends posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view; (17) the medial ramus of the pterygoid is relatively long, more than half the length of anterior ramus. In phylogenetic terms, the Leptobrachium bompu group is the most inclusive clade that contains Leptobrachium bompu, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., but none of the other currently recognized species within Leptobrachium. This clade is an Eastern Himalayan radiation found north of the Brahmaputra River in Northeast India and southern China (Figs. 1 and 2).

Cranial osteology. This description is based on SDBDU 2022.5250, an adult male, SVL 53.3 mm. The skull is wider than long (Figs. 5A–5C). It measures 16.9 mm from the tip of the snout (anterior tip of the premaxillary bone) to the posterior border of the occipital condyles (exoccipital bones); maximum width across the quadratojugals is 20.5 mm. The skull lacks prominent dermal sculpturing dorsally and ventrally. The dorsally exposed sphenethmoid has slight sculpturing and a shallow depression posteromedially, where it is fused with the anterior borders of the paired frontoparietals. Ventrally, the bone forms a broad articulation with the anterior third of the cultriform process of the parasphenoid. Frontoparietals are well developed. They articulate and may fuse at a few sites along the midline but otherwise remain separated by a narrow fontanelle. They broaden slightly posterolaterally, where they unite with the prootics and exoccipitals. Nasals are well developed but separated medially by a wide gap. They barely articulate with the anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid. A sharply pointed maxillary process extends ventrolaterally, nearly parallel to but separate from the underlying neopalatine, but does not contact the maxilla. It is relatively long and extends posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view. The parasphenoid is T-shaped. The median cultriform process narrows to a pointed tip anteriorly; posterior alary processes are nearly as wide medially as laterally, where they articulate with the medial process of the pterygoid. The palate comprises a pair of long and thin neopalatines that extend from the sphenethmoid to the maxillae and form the posterior margins of the internal choanae. Vomers are small; they are separated from each other and from other bones. Anterior, prechoanal and postchoanal processes are modestly developed; the dentigerous process is lacking and there are no teeth.

The maxillary arcade is complete. Premaxillae are robust but separated medially by a narrow gap. The ascending (alary) process tapers slightly dorsolaterally and has two rudimentary “cusps” at its tip; it approaches but remains separate from the septomaxilla. The pars palatina bears two well-defined processes: a long and slender medial process ascends nearly parallel to the ascending process; and a broad, horizontally deployed lateral process. Each premaxilla bears 10 to 12 teeth. Septomaxillae are small but well developed, possessing anterior, posterior, lateral and medial rami that roughly spiral dorsoposteriorly. The elongate maxilla bears 40–50 teeth. The pars dentalis overlaps the anterior (maxillary) process of the quadratojugal caudally and the anterior ramus of the pterygoid medially, while the thin and sharply pointed preorbital process broadly articulates with the neopalatine dorsomedially. The broad pars facialis remains narrowly separated from the premaxilla. Quadratojugals are L-shaped. The dorsal process broadly articulates with the ventral ramus of the squamosal but then joins with the lateral (maxillary) process to form a bulbous process dorsal to the jaw joint. Squamosals are triradiate: a posterior (otic) ramus extends medially to articulate with the crista parotica of the neurocranium; an anterior (zygomatic) ramus extends anteriorly towards the parietal fenestra with a pointed tip; and an elongate ventral ramus descends to the jaw joint. Pterygoids are moderately broad and triradiate: the anterior ramus barely articulates with the pars dentalis of the maxilla on its lingual surface; the posterior ramus articulates with the concave bulbous process of the quadratojugal at the jaw joint; and the medial ramus overlaps the alary process of the parasphenoid on its anterior face. Well-developed columellae extend laterally from the otic complex, parallel to the medial ramus of the squamosal. Small ossifications within remnants of the palatoquadrate cartilage are present within the jaw joint. The exoccipital bone is incomplete dorsally, insofar as the roof of the cartilaginous otic capsule, which ossifies fully in many adult anurans, remains cartilaginous in this specimen and is invisible in its µCT scan (Fig. 5A).
The mandible (lower jaw) is edentate. The elongate angulosplenial is slightly broader posteriorly, its labial side sculpted inwards longitudinally while the lingual side is smooth and concave. It is separated from the mentomeckelian anteromedially by a narrow gap. The dentary is moderately long and slender. It is aligned parallel to but separate from the angulosplenial and articulates with the mentomeckelian anteriorly. Mentomeckelian bones are small and cylindrical and separated medially.
Cranial comparison. The skull of Leptobrachium bompu differs from that of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. in having the frontoparietals fused at a few sites along the midline but otherwise mostly separated by a narrow fontanelle (vs. separated by a moderate-sized fontanelle along their entire length) and the nasals barely articulated with the anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid (vs. articulated with thinly ossified anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid along most of their anterior length). It differs from the skulls of L. aryatium and L. sylheticum by the frontoparietals extending anterolaterally beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid and being only slightly broader posteriorly (vs. frontoparietals relatively narrower anteriorly and wider posteriorly and not extending anteriorly beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid), medial ramus of pterygoid relatively long, more than half the length of the anterior ramus (vs. relatively short, less than half the length of anterior ramus), and the maxillary process of the nasal relatively long and extending posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view (vs. relatively short and not extending posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view). It further differs from L. aryatium by the frontoparietals being fused at a few sites along the midline but otherwise separated by a narrow fontanelle (vs. fused medially along approximately half their length, sites of fusion alternating with narrow gaps that are remnants of the frontoparietal fontanelle), and from L. sylheticum by relatively small vomers that remain separate from other bones (vs. moderate-sized and articulating with the anterolateral edge of the sphenethmoid on its ventral side).
Tadpole description. A Gosner stage 36 tadpole of Leptobrachium bompu has an elliptical, dorsoventrally depressed body with a rounded snout, lateral nares positioned closer to the snout than the eyes, and dorsolaterally placed eyes. The spiracle opens sinistrally at hind limb level, while the vent tube is dextral. The muscular tail (∼2/3 of total length) tapers to a rounded tip, with the dorsal fin taller than the ventral. The large ventral oral disc bears a single row of conical papillae, with a labial keratodont row formula of 5(2–5)/5(1–4), and keratinised U-shaped upper and V-shaped lower jaw sheaths. A detailed description of the larva was published by Sarmah et al. (2025). For comparative purposes, key characters are tabulated in Table S7 and external morphology is depicted in Figs. 6A–6E.

Etymology. The species is named after the place Mechuka, a small town where the type series was collected. The specific epithet is treated as an invariable noun in apposition to the generic name.
Diagnosis. Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. differs from other congeners by the following combination of characters: (1) medium adult size (male SVL 54.0–58.0 mm); (2) head nearly as long as wide; (3) nostril equally distant from snout and eye; (4) dorsal skin with a network of ridges; (5) distinct axillary glands; (6) iris uniformly silver white, or with light blue tinge; (7) dorsum light or dark blackish-brown with irregular dark spots; (8) throat light or dark brown; belly pinkish-brown with minute light grey marbling; (9) hind limbs light or dark pinkish-brown with ash-colored marbling; (10) foot webbing moderate, reaching the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV; (11) dermal fringe well developed on all toes; (12) inner metatarsal tubercle large, well developed; (13) distance from the tip of inner metatarsal tubercle to the tip of toe I shorter than the length of inner metatarsal tubercle.
Phylogenetic relationship. Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. is a member of the L. bompu group and shows a well-supported sister-group relationship with L. bompu (Fig. 1). Within this group, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. shows a DNA sequence divergence (mitochondrial 16S rRNA) of 3.6–4.2% from L. bompu and 4.9–5.2% from Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. (Table S3).

Morphological comparison. Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. is easily distinguished from all known congeners except L. bompu, L. nigrops, Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., and L. waysepuntiense by its entirely silvery white iris (vs. bicolored or unicolored (usually black) iris with variably colored scleral arc). When further compared to congeners with a unicolored iris, the new species differs from the Malay Peninsula (including Singapore) endemic L. nigrops by its larger body size, male SVL 54–58 mm (vs. smaller, 33.7–42.7 mm), rounded finger tips (vs. moderately pointed), dorsum (including head) uniformly brown with reddish tinge and irregular faint black spots (vs. shades of grey with prominent light to dark greyish-brown linear markings), and iris silver greyish-white with light blue tinge (vs. black); and from the Indonesian endemic L. waysepuntiense by its silvery white iris with light blue tinge, without reticulations (vs. light blue iris with fine black reticulations), dorsal skin including head shagreened with prominent irregular glandular dermal reticulations (vs. relatively smooth, with minute granules scattered posteriorly), tympanum not visible externally (vs. tympanum distinct), and foot webbing moderate, reaching up to the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV (vs. poorly developed). Within the L. bompu group, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. differs from L. bompu and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. by its head length nearly equal to its width, HL/HW ratio 0.99 (vs. wider than long, HL/HW ratio 0.93–0.97 and HL/HW ratio 0.88–0.92, respectively); nostril placed at equal distance from both snout and eye, EN 4.3 ± 0.2 mm, NS 4.3 ± 0.3 mm, N = 5 (vs. closer to eye, EN 3.5 ± 0.2 mm, NS 4.3 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5, in L. bompu; closer to snout, EN 4.5 ± 0.2 mm, NS 3.6 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5, in Leptobrachium somani sp. nov.); internarial distance nearly equal to the distances from nostril to eye and nostril to snout, IN 4.4 ± 0.2 mm vs. EN 4.5 ± 0.2 mm, NS 4.3 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5 (vs. wider, IN 4.7 ± 0.2 vs. EN 3.5 ± 0.2 mm, NS 4.3 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5; IN 5.2 ± 0.1 mm vs. EN 4.5 ± 0.2 mm, NS 3.6 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5, respectively); distance from the tip of inner metatarsal tubercle to the tip of first toe shorter than the length of inner metatarsal tubercle, IMT 4.1 ± 0.1 mm, IMT–TLI 3.5 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5 (vs. longer, IMT 2.6 ± 0.3 mm, IMT–TLI 3.5 ± 0.1 mm; IMT 3.3 ± 0.1 mm, IMT–TLI 4.6 ± 0.2 mm, respectively); and foot webbing moderate, reaching up to the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV (vs. small, reaching halfway between the first and second subarticular tubercles on either side of toe IV in L. bompu; and just above the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV in Leptobrachium somani sp. nov.). Differs from L. bompu by its internarial distance shorter than upper eyelid width, IN 4.4 ± 0.2 mm vs. IUW 6.0 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5 (vs. equal, IN 4.7 ± 0.2 mm vs. IUW 4.7 ± 0.2 mm, N = 5); throat light grey with pinkish tinge and prominent light brown reticulations (vs. light greyish-brown without prominent reticulations); belly prominently pinkish-brown with minute greyish spots (vs. light greyish-brown without prominent reticulations). Differs from Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. by its shank nearly equal to foot, ShL 23.0 ± 0.2 mm vs. FOL 23.6 ± 0.4 mm, N = 5 (vs. shorter, ShL 22.4 ± 0.6 mm vs. FOL 24.0 ± 0.3 mm, N = 5).
Secondary sexual characters. Male: single gular pouch.
Tadpole. The following description is based on a Gosner stage 37 tadpole (SDBDU 2023.6550). Body oval-shaped in dorsal view, with maximum width just after the gill region; oval-shaped in lateral view, with maximum height at mid-abdomen; dorsoventrally depressed (BH 78.7% of BW); snout roughly round; nares closer to the snout than the eyes (SN 78.6% of NE), positioned laterally and oriented more anterolaterally, narial aperture round, encircled by several minute projections; eyes medium-sized (ED 8.8% of BL), positioned dorsolaterally but oriented more laterally; spiracle opens sinistrally and located laterally on the anterior part of the abdomen (SS 52.6% of BL); spiracular tube short, oriented dorsoposteriorly, attached to the body wall except at the tip, which remains free; vent tube dextral, at the base of ventral tail fin; tail large (TAL 66.3% of TL), heavily muscularised (TMH 66.9% of MTH and TMW 46.8% of BW), proximal half of tail muscle straight, gradually tapering into a blunt tail tip; the dorsal (or upper) fin originates slightly after the body-tail junction, and the ventral (or lower) fin near the ventral terminus of the body, upper fin higher than the lower fin (UFH 33.0% of MTH and LFH 29.5% of MTH), both fins gradually increase in height, reaching maximum height at mid-region of the tail, and gradually taper into the tail tip (Fig. 6; Table S9).
The large oral disc (ODW 45.4% of BW) is positioned antroventrally and oriented more ventrally, with no lateral emarginations. It is completely bordered by a single row of moderately large, conical papillae, with a tiny gap of one papilla width on the upper labium. The lower labium has a ventral extension medially; few submarginal papillae are present on the lateral sides of the lower labium. Labial keratodont row formula (LKRF) 5(2–5)/5(1–4), the upper labium has five uniserial rows of keratodont of which rows 2–5 have a medial gap, and five uniserial rows of keratodont on the lower labium of which rows 1–4 have a medial gap; no additional submarginal keratodont rows observed. Jaw sheaths are heavily keratinized with serrated, inverted U-shaped upper jaw sheath, and V-shaped lower jaw sheath (Fig. 6).

Color in life. The body has an overall dark brown color with yellowish-brown patches scattered in the dorsal and lateral surfaces, except the belly area, and anterior part of tail muscle; lateral and ventral surfaces of the body have patches of golden iridocytes; lateral line system clearly visible on snout, around the eyes, abdomen, dorsal and lateral sides of the body and tail; tail fins mostly translucent; abdominal area silvery white and translucent, gills and gut coil partially visible; eyes black with golden iridocytes scattered on the outer side of the sclera, denser towards the pupil forming a ring around the pupil; oral disc large and creamy white with some pigmentation on the papillae tips (Fig. 6).
Tadpole comparison. As Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. belongs to the L. bompu group, we compared it with the tadpoles of L. bompu described by Sarmah et al. (2025). The tadpoles of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. differ from those of L. bompu by smaller size, TL 61.4–64.6 mm, Gosner stages 35–37 (vs. larger, TL 72.1–85.0 mm, Gosner stages 35–37); and predominantly dark brown dorsal coloration, interspersed with contrasting patches of yellowish-brown on the dorsal and lateral surfaces of the body and anterior part of tail muscle (vs. uniform coloration ranging from yellowish-brown to light brown without prominent contrasting color patches). The labial keratodont row formula was the same for both species, 5(2–5)/5(1–4), and there was no variation in the LKRF within all the studied Gosner stages of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. (Fig. 6). Further comparison with published tadpoles is provided in Table S7.

Distribution and natural history. Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. is currently known from its type locality, Mechuka, in the Shi Yomi district of Arunachal Pradesh state, India at elevations of ∼1,900 m asl (Figs. 1 and 2; Table S1). Additionally, a population from Medog (29°13′28.2″N 95° 11′08.5″E, 1,600 m asl), China, reported as “L. bompu” by Liang et al. (2017) and referred to as “L. cf. bompu” by Sarmah et al. (2025), is conspecific with this species based on available mitochondrial 16S sequences (Fig. 1; Tables S2 and S3). At Mechuka, the vegetation is comprised of temperate evergreen forests and grasslands. Males were found calling from the banks of a small stream that flowed through a grassland. Surveys were conducted in late April in 2023 and 2025. However, due to unexpected late rainfall in this region in both years, only sporadic calling activities were observed during either survey. Most individuals were collected through opportunistic searches along the stream. The males started calling around 19:30 hr and continued till about 23:30 hr, although the calling period could vary during the peak breeding season. The immediate habitat consisted of open grassland with no tree cover. Tadpoles of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. were collected from small stagnant water pools adjacent to the same stream in the month of September.

Etymology. The species is named after the late E. Somanath (Ettazhi Somanath), a notable Indian journalist from Kerala, in appreciation of his dedicated reporting of environmental issues and his ardent love for nature. He frequently accompanied SDB on field expeditions in search of frogs. The specific epithet somani is treated as an invariable noun in apposition to the generic name.
Diagnosis. Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. differs from known congeners by the following combination of characters: (1) medium adult size (male SVL 55.5–56.8 mm); (2) head wider than long; (3) nostril closer to tip of snout than to anterior corner of eye; (4) iris uniformly silver white; (5) dorsal skin with a network of ridges; (6) large axillary glands; (7) dorsum light or dark brown with ash-colored irregular dark patches; (8) inner metatarsal tubercle large, well developed; (9) distance from the tip of inner metatarsal tubercle to the tip of first toe longer than the length of inner metatarsal tubercle; (10) dermal fringe on all toes well developed; (11) webbing moderate, reaching above the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV; (12) throat light or dark brown with pinkish tinge; (13) belly creamy white with prominent pinkish-brown marbling on lateral sides; (14) complete hind limbs light or dark brown with ash-colored marbling; (15) the paired frontoparietals extend anterolaterally beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid and are only slightly broader posteriorly; (16) frontoparietals approach one another at the midline but remain separated by a moderate-sized fontanelle along their entire length; (17) paired nasals articulate with thinly ossified anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid along most of their anterior length; (18) the maxillary process of the nasal is relatively long and extends posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view; (19) the medial ramus of the pterygoid is relatively long, more than half the length of the anterior ramus; (20) vomers are relatively small and do not articulate medially with the anterolateral edges of the sphenethmoid or with other bones; (21) two types of male advertisement calls, with pulsatile temporal structure and dominant frequency between 1.12 and 1.38 kHz.
Phylogenetic relationship. Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. is a member of the L. bompu species group and shows a well-supported sister-group relationship with the clade containing L. bompu and Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. (Fig. 1). Within the bompu group, Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. shows a DNA sequence divergence (mitochondrial 16S rRNA) of 4.5–4.7% from L. bompu and 4.9–5.2% from Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. (Table S3).

Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. is easily distinguished from all known congeners except L. bompu, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., L. nigrops, and L. waysepuntiense by its entirely silvery white iris (vs. bicolored or unicolored [usually black] iris with variably colored scleral arc). When further compared to congeners with a unicolored iris, the new species differs from the Malay Peninsula (including Singapore) endemic L. nigrops by its larger body size, male SVL 56–58 mm (vs. 33.7–42.7 mm), rounded finger tips (vs. moderately pointed), dorsum uniformly greyish brown with irregular light-grey markings (vs. shades of grey with prominent light to dark greyish-brown linear markings), canthal region, sides of snout, tympanic and supraorbital areas dark blackish brown that can be interpreted as a dark blackish-brown streak from tip of snout to tympanum (vs. absent), and iris silvery white without reticulations (vs. black); and from the Indonesian endemic L. waysepuntiense by its silvery white iris without reticulations (vs. light blue iris with fine black reticulations), dorsal skin including head shagreened with prominent irregular glandular dermal reticulations (vs. relatively smooth, with minute granules scattered posteriorly), tympanum not visible externally (vs. tympanum distinct), and foot webbing moderate, extending above the second subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV (vs. poorly developed). Within the L. bompu group, Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. differs from L. bompu by its larger adult male body size, SVL 56–57 mm, N = 5 (vs. SVL 42–53 mm), nostril closer to tip of snout than to anterior corner of eye, NS 3.6 ± 0.2 mm vs EN 4.5 ± 0.2 mm (vs. closer to eye NS 4.3 ± 0.2 mm vs EN 3.5 ± 0.2 mm), shank length shorter than foot length, ShL 22.4 ± 0.6 mm vs FOL 24.0 ± 0.3 mm (vs. nearly equal ShL 18.7 ± 0.3 mm vs FOL 18.9 ± 0.3 mm), ventral surfaces of throat and chest dark blackish brown with white spots, belly dark brownish grey with white spots in center, limbs (including fingers and toes) blackish brown with irregular white spots with blueish tinge, and greyish-white bands on shank (vs. light greyish brown without prominent reticulations, especially on hind limbs that lack markings (Fig. 8). For more differences from Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., see ‘Morphological comparison’ for that species.
Secondary sexual characters. Male: single gular pouch.
Cranial osteology. This description is based on ZSI A16334 (field number SDB 2023.6409), an adult male, SVL 56.3 mm. The skull is wider than long (Figs. 5D–5F). It measures 17.5 mm from the tip of the snout (anterior tip of the premaxillary bone) to the posterior border of the occipital condyles (exoccipital bones); maximum width across the quadratojugals is 22.3 mm. The skull lacks prominent dermal sculpturing dorsally and ventrally. The dorsally exposed sphenethmoid has slight sculpturing and a shallow depression posteromedially, where it is fused with the anterior borders of the paired frontoparietals. Ventrally, the bone forms a broad articulation with the anterior third of the cultriform process of the parasphenoid. Frontoparietals are moderately well developed. They approach one another at the midline but remain separated by a moderate-sized fontanelle. They broaden slightly posterolaterally, where they unite with the prootics and exoccipitals. Nasals are well developed but separated medially by a wide gap. They broadly articulate with the anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid. A sharply pointed maxillary process extends ventrolaterally, nearly parallel to but separate from the underlying neopalatine, but does not contact the maxilla. It is relatively long and extends posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view. The parasphenoid is T-shaped. The median cultriform process narrows to a blunt tip anteriorly; posterior alary processes are nearly as wide medially as laterally, where they articulate with the medial process of the pterygoid. The palate comprises a pair of long and thin neopalatines that extend from the sphenethmoid to the maxillae and form the posterior margins of the internal choanae. Vomers are small; they are separated from each other and from other bones. Anterior, prechoanal and postchoanal processes are modestly developed; a rudimentary dentigerous process is present on the right side, but there are no teeth on either side.
The maxillary arcade is complete. Premaxillae are robust but separated medially by a narrow gap. The ascending (alary) process tapers slightly dorsolaterally and is bifurcated with two rudimentary “cusps” at its tip; it approaches but remains separate from the septomaxilla. The pars palatina bears two well-defined processes: a long and slender medial process extends nearly parallel to the ascending process; and a narrow, horizontally deployed lateral process. Each premaxilla bears 10 to 12 teeth of variable length. Septomaxillae are small but well developed, possessing anterior, posterior, lateral and medial rami that roughly spiral dorsoposteriorly. The elongate maxilla bears 40–50 teeth. The pars dentalis overlaps the anterior (maxillary) process of the quadratojugal caudally and the anterior ramus of the pterygoid medially, while the thin and sharply pointed preorbital process broadly articulates with the neopalatine dorsomedially. Quadratojugals are L-shaped. The dorsal process broadly articulates with the ventral ramus of the squamosal but then joins with the lateral (maxillary) process to form a bulbous process dorsal to the jaw joint. Squamosals are triradiate: a posterior (otic) ramus extends medially to articulate with the crista parotica of the neurocranium; an anterior (zygomatic) ramus extends anteriorly towards the parietal fenestra with a pointed tip; and an elongate ventral ramus that descends to the jaw joint. Pterygoids are moderately broad and triradiate: the anterior ramus barely articulates with the pars dentalis of the maxilla on its lingual surface; the posterior ramus articulates with the concave bulbous process of the quadratojugal at the jaw joint; and the medial ramus overlaps the alary process of the parasphenoid on its anterior face. Well-developed columellae extend laterally from the otic complex, parallel to the medial ramus of the squamosal. The exoccipital bone is incomplete dorsally, insofar as the roof of the cartilaginous otic capsule, which ossifies fully in many adult anurans, remains cartilaginous in this specimen and is invisible in its µCT scan (Fig. 5D).
The mandible (lower jaw) is edentate. The elongate angulosplenial is slightly broader posteriorly, its labial side sculpted inwards longitudinally while the lingual side is smooth and concave. It is separated from the mentomeckelian anteromedially by a moderately narrow gap. The dentary is moderately long and slender. It is aligned parallel to and closely approaches the angulosplenial and is fused with the mentomeckelian anteriorly. Mentomeckelian bones are small and cylindrical and separated medially by a narrow gap.
Cranial comparison. The skull of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. differs from that of L. bompu in having the frontoparietals separated by a moderate-sized fontanelle along their entire length (vs. fused at a few sites along the midline but otherwise separated by a narrow fontanelle) and the nasals articulated with thinly ossified anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid along most of their anterior length (vs. barely articulated with the anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid). It differs from the skulls of L. aryatium and L. sylheticum by the frontoparietals being separated by a moderate-sized fontanelle along their entire length (vs. fused medially along approximately half their length, sites of fusion alternating with narrow gaps that are remnants of the frontoparietal fontanelle in L. aryatium; and fused at a few sites along the midline but otherwise separated by a narrow fontanelle in L. sylheticum), frontoparietals extending anterolaterally beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid and being only slightly broader posteriorly (vs. frontoparietals relatively narrower anteriorly and wider posteriorly and not extending anteriorly beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid), medial ramus of pterygoid relatively long, more than half the length of the anterior ramus (vs. relatively short, less than half the length of anterior ramus), and the maxillary process of the nasal relatively long and extending posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view (vs. relatively short and not extending posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view). It further differs from L. aryatium by nasals articulated with thinly ossified anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid along most of their anterior length (vs. barely articulated posteromedially with the anterior margin of the sphenethmoid); and from L. sylheticum by relatively small vomers that remain separate from other bones (vs. moderate-sized and articulate with the anterolateral edge of the sphenethmoid on its ventral side).
Vocalization. Male calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. were recorded at Tiwarigaon between 20:00 and 21:00 h on 25 April 2023. The frogs were calling from rocks and leaf litter beside a small stream. Dry bulb and wet bulb temperatures were 15.5 °C and 14.5 °C, respectively. Two types of pulsatile calls (type 1 and type 2) were mostly delivered in groups of 2–9 calls each. Mean duration of call groups was 2,776.1 ± 1,774.0 ms (range, 224.9–5,133.6 ms). When both call types were present in the same group, type 2 calls were always produced first.
Type 1 calls (N = 34) had a mean call duration of 214.6 ms, a rise time of 95.7, and a fall time of 118.9 ms. A typical call had 22–35 pulses delivered at a pulse rate of 122.3 ± 18.8 pulses/s. The spectrum showed a single broad peak with a mean dominant frequency of 1.33 ± 0.05 kHz (range, 1.12–1.38 kHz). The maximum amplitude pulse had a duration of 9 ms, a pulse rise time of 1.4 ms, and a fall time of 7.6 ms. The 50% pulse rise time was 0.9 ms; the 50% pulse fall time was 6.5 ms. Type 1 calls showed amplitude modulation without any frequency modulation. Pulses started with low amplitude, which peaked in the middle and gradually dipped towards the last few pulses.
Type 2 calls (N = 7) were longer than type 1 calls, with a call duration of 645.1 ms. The amplitude envelope had a call rise time of 479.9 ms and a fall time of 165.2 ms. A typical type 2 call had 32–64 pulses delivered at a rate of 70.7 pulses/s. The spectrum showed a single broad peak with a mean dominant frequency of 1.35 ± 0.02 kHz (range, 1.34–1.38 kHz). The maximum amplitude pulse had a duration of 13.7 ms with a pulse rise time of 1.2 ms and a pulse fall time of 12.5 ms. The 50% pulse rise time was 0.7 ms; the 50% pulse fall time was 11.6 ms. Type 2 calls also showed amplitude modulation without any frequency modulation (Figs. 7A–7G; Table S10).
Call comparison. Currently there are 39 recognized species in the genus Leptobrachium, of which calls are described for 17. Given the variation in methodologies and acoustic parameters reported across studies, only those call properties that are directly comparable to the terminology and definitions employed in the present study are included in our analysis. Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. is exceptional in having two distinct call types (vs. a single call type in the known congeners). Across the genus, the overall dominant frequency of calls is low, ranging from 0.9 to 1.46 kHz with little variation. The temporal structure of calls within the group is mostly pulsatile in nature.
Type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. can be differentiated from those of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., L. liui, L. masatakasatoi, L. mouhoti, and L. smithi by their higher dominant frequency, 1.33 ± 0.05 kHz (vs. 1.26 ± 0.05, 0.92, 1.10 ± 0.010, 0.9, and 1.30 kHz, respectively), while they differ from calls of L. sylheticum, L. bompu, and L. tengchongense in their lower dominant frequency, 1.33 ± 0.05 kHz (vs. 1.41 ± 0.03, 1.53 ± 0.1, and 1.46 kHz, respectively). The dominant frequency range of L. leucops overlaps that of type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. but the calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. have a slightly lower dominant frequency (1.12–1.38 kHz vs. 1.0–1.6 kHz), while the calls of L. lunatum (1.3 kHz) and L. pullus (1.0–1.3 kHz) have a similar dominant frequency as type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. (1.33 ± 0.05 kHz). Type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. can be differentiated from calls of L. boringii, L. liui, L. mouhoti, and L. tengchongense by their faster pulse rate, 122.3 ± 18.8 pulses/s (vs. 87, 92.4, 99, and 95.8 pulses/s, respectively), while they differ from the calls of L. sylheticum and L. lunatum in their slower pulse rate, 122.3 ± 18.8 (vs. 165.2 ± 18.3 and 131 pulses/s, respectively). Type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. have a slightly faster pulse rate than the calls of L. bompu (122.3 ± 18.8 pulses/s vs. 120 ± 21.8 pulses/s). Type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. have similar pulses per call as calls of L. sylheticum, L. liui, L. mouhoti, and L. pullus (22–35 pulses/call vs. 19–32, 11–26, 12–26, and 27–40, respectively), but they differ in pulse rate. The calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. have more pulses per call than those of L. leucops and L. tengchongense (7–15 pulses/call and 12–21 pulses/call vs. 22–35 pulses/call). Type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. have longer call duration than the calls of L. sylheticum, L. bompu, L. lunatum, L. masatakasatoi, and L. mouhoti (214.6 ms vs. 136.8, 182.4, 110, 199, and 190 ms, respectively), while they have shorter call duration than the calls of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., L. hasseltii, L. kantonishikawai, L. liui, L. lumadorum, and L. tagbanorum (214.6 ms vs. 3,169.6, 1,800–2,700, 2,400–3,500, 100–1,700, 1,700–3,500, and 1,500–2,00 ms).
Type 2 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. can be differentiated from the calls of L. sylheticum, L. bompu, L. boringii, L. liui, L. lunatum, L. mouhoti, L. sylheticum, and L. tengchongense by their slower pulse rate, 70.7 ± 11.1 pulses/s (vs. 165.2 ± 18.3, 120 ± 21.8, 87, 92.4, 131, 99, 124.78, and 95.8 pulses/s, respectively). Type 2 and type 1 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. have a similar dominant frequency, hence the frequency comparison of type 2 calls with calls of other species is the same as for type 1 calls. The calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. can be differentiated from calls of L. sylheticum, L. bompu, L. leucops, L. liui, L. mouhoti, L. pullus, and L. tengchongense by their higher number of pulses per call, 49 (vs. 22, 16, 7–15, 11–26, 19, 30, and 12–21, respectively). They also have longer call duration in comparison to L. sylheticum, L. bompu, L. lunatum, L. masatakasatoi, and L. mouhoti (645.1 ms vs. 136.8, 182.4, 110, 199, and 190 ms, respectively). They have shorter call duration than the calls of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., L. hasseltii, L. kantonishikawai, L. liui, L. lumadorum, L. mangyanorum, and L. tagbanorum (645.1 ms vs. 3,169.6, 1,800–2,700, 2,400–3,500, 100–1,700, 1,700–3,500, 3,400–3,700, and 1,500–2,500 ms, respectively (Table S8).
Distribution and natural history. Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. is currently known only from its type locality, Tiwarigaon, which is situated close to Mehao WLS in the Lower Dibang Valley district of Arunachal Pradesh state, India at elevations of ∼1,400 m asl (Figs. 1 and 2; Table S1). The vegetation here is of tropical evergreen, sub-tropical, and temperate types. Individual males were calling from under leaf litter on the forest floor close by a low-water stream or from rock crevices at the stream edge. Surveys were conducted in late April in 2023 and 2025. However, due to unexpected late rainfall for this region in both years, only sporadic calling activities were observed during either survey. Males started calling around 17:30 hr and continued until about 22:30 hr.

## Leptobrachium smithi group
The Leptobrachium smithi group can be distinguished from other Leptobrachium species groups by the following suite of morphological characters: (1) habitus stocky; (2) medium-sized adults (male SVL 36–61 mm); (3) tympanum weakly or well developed; (4) finger and toe tips swollen without disc; (5) dorsal skin light or dark brown, or light to dark grey with distinct darker markings (blotches or spots) edged with or without brown; (6) dark canthal stripe; (7) dense dark and contrasting reticulation or large dark blotch from groin continuing to anterior part of arm insertion through flank except, L. tenasserimense which has reticulation on flank and groin; (8) dorsal skin shagreened to granular; (9) iris bicolored, black ventrally and orange, red, or turquoise dorsally; (10) sclera bright or light blue, or black; (11) maxillary and premaxillary teeth present, vomerine teeth absent; (12) paired frontoparietals relatively narrow anteriorly and wide posteriorly, lateral edges do not extend anteriorly beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid; (13) the maxillary process of the nasal is relatively short and does not extend posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view; (14) the medial ramus of the pterygoid is relatively short, less than half the length of anterior ramus. In phylogenetic terms, the Leptobrachium smithi group is the most inclusive clade that contains L. aryatium, L. smithi, L. sylheticum, L. rakhinense, and L. tenasserimense, but no other currently recognized species of Leptobrachium. This clade is a “Thai-Myanmar” (sensu Matsui et al., 2010) radiation distributed south of the Brahmaputra River in northeast India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, and Peninsular Malaysia (Figs. 1 and 2).

Diagnosis. Leptobrachium aryatium is distinguished from known congeners by the following combination of characters: (1) medium adult size (male SVL 49.0–55.4 mm); (2) head narrower than wide; (3) tympanum distinct, light or dark greyish brown; (4) webbing small, reaches the first subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV; (5) dorsal skin sparsely to prominently granular; (6) large axillary glands; (7) canthal stripe; (8) iris bicolored, black ventrally but orangish-red in dorsal third, with bright-blue sclera; (9) dorsum light to dark brown with brown blotches; (10) lateral surfaces of abdomen including flanks ash grey with light to dark blackish brown spots; (11) ventral skin light to dark brown with light pinkish brown reticulation and greyish white spots; (12) paired frontoparietals relatively narrow anteriorly and wide posteriorly, lateral edges do not extend anteriorly beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid; (13) frontoparietals fused medially along approximately half their length, sites of fusion alternate with narrow gaps that are remnants of the frontoparietal fontanelle; (14) paired nasals barely articulate posteromedially with the anterior margin of the sphenethmoid; (15) the maxillary process of the nasal is relatively short and does not extend posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view; (16) the medial ramus of the pterygoid is relatively short, less than half the length of the anterior ramus; (17) vomers are relatively small and do not articulate medially with the anterolateral edges of the sphenethmoid or with other bones.
Phylogenetic relationship. Leptobrachium aryatium is a member of the L. smithi species group and shows a well-supported sister-group relationship with L. sylheticum (Fig. 1). Within the L. smithi group, L. aryatium shows a DNA sequence divergence (mitochondrial 16S rRNA) of 1.8–2.5% from L. sylheticum, 2.8–4.9% from L. smithi and 3.6–3.9% from L. rakhinense (Table S3).

Distribution and natural history. Leptobrachium aryatium was previously reported from the Garbhanga Reserve Forest and adjoining areas in the Kamrup and Kamrup Metro districts of Assam state, as well as the Ri-Bhoi district of Meghalaya state, India. During the present study we recorded additional populations from Goalpara district (26°10′37.2″N 90°33′22.7″E, 53 m asl) of Assam and both the East (Daribokgre: 25°29′34.4″N 90°19′27.5″E, 1,117 m asl) and West Garo (Asanang: 25°35′23.6″N 90° 15′44.3″E, 486 m asl) hills districts of Meghalaya. The vegetation across these areas ranges from tropical moist evergreen, to semi-evergreen, to moist deciduous types. Altitudinally, the species was found from approximately 50 m to over 1100 m asl. During monsoon season, breeding individuals were encountered under leaf litter and low vegetation near slow-moving streams and rivulets.

Diagnosis. Leptobrachium sylheticum is distinguished from known congeners by the following combination of characters: (1) medium adult size (male SVL 50.2–60.9 mm); (2) head nearly as long as wide; (3) tympanum light or dark greyish brown; (4) webbing small, reaches the first subarticular tubercle on either side of toe IV (Fig. 13); (5) dorsal skin shagreened to sparsely granular; (6) large axillary glands; (7) raised axillary lands, positioned at level of axilla; (8) canthal stripe; (9) iris bicolored, black ventrally but fire-red in dorsal third, with bright-blue sclera; (10) dorsum light to dark brown with brown blotches; (11) groin ash-white light-brown markings; (12) venter light to dark brown with or without blue tinge and with light pinkish-brown reticulation and greyish-white spots; (13) paired frontoparietals relatively narrow anteriorly and wide posteriorly, lateral edges do not extend anteriorly beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid; (14) frontoparietals closely approach one another at the midline but mostly remain separated by a narrow fontanelle; (15) paired nasals articulate posteromedially with the anterior margin of the sphenethmoid for about half their length; (16) the maxillary process of the nasal is relatively short and does not extend posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view; (17) the medial ramus of the pterygoid is relatively short, less than half the length of the anterior ramus; (18) vomers are moderate-sized and articulate medially with the anterolateral edges of the sphenethmoid.
Phylogenetic relationship. Leptobrachium sylheticum is a member of the L. smithi species group and forms a well-supported clade with L. aryatium. This clade in turn shows a well-supported sister-group relationship with the clade containing L. smithi and L. rakhinense (Fig. 1). Within the L. smithi group, L. sylheticum shows a DNA sequence divergence (mitochondrial 16S rRNA) of 1.8–2.5% from L. aryatium, 2.8–5.2% from L. smithi and 4.4–4.9% from L. rakhinense (Table S3).
Description of holotype. A detailed general description and comparison was published by Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021).
Variation. Measurements of five adult males reported in the present study are reported in Table S6. Additionally, external morphology and color variation in life and preservative are depicted in Figs. 13–15.
Cranial osteology. This description is based on SDBDU 2009.490, an adult male, SVL 55.5 mm. The skull is wider than long (Figs. 5J–5L). It measures 19.3 mm from the tip of the snout (anterior tip of the premaxillary bone) to the posterior border of the occipital condyles (exoccipital bones); maximum width across the quadratojugals is 22.9 mm. The skull lacks prominent dermal sculpturing dorsally and ventrally. The dorsally exposed sphenethmoid has slight sculpturing and a shallow depression posteromedially, where it articulates with and may be partly fused to the anterior borders of the paired frontoparietals. Ventrally, the bone is broadly fused to the anterior third of the cultriform process of the parasphenoid. Frontoparietals are moderately well developed. They closely approach one another at the midline but remain separated by a narrow fontanelle. They broaden posterolaterally, where they unite with the prootics and exoccipitals. Nasals are well developed, nearly rectangular in shape, and separated medially by a wide gap. Each articulates solidly with the anterior margin of the sphenethmoid posteromedially for about half its length. The bluntly pointed maxillary process extends ventrolaterally but is relatively short; it does not extend posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view. The parasphenoid is T-shaped. The median cultriform process narrows anteriorly; posterior alary processes are narrower medially than laterally, where they articulate with the medial process of the pterygoid. The palate comprises a pair of long and thin neopalatines that extend from the sphenethmoid to the maxillae and form the posterior margins of the internal choanae. Vomers are moderate-sized and separated from one another medially, but each articulates with the anterolateral edge of the sphenethmoid on its ventral side. Anterior, prechoanal and postchoanal processes are discrete, but there is no dentigerous process and there are no teeth.
The maxillary arcade is complete. Premaxillae are robust but separated medially by a narrow gap. Each ascending (alary) process tapers dorsolaterally and has two rudimentary “cusps” at its tip. One cusp on the left side is especially well developed; it approaches but remains separate from the adjacent septomaxilla. The pars palatina bears two well-defined processes: a relatively short and slender medial process extends nearly parallel to the alary process; and a broad and horizontally deployed lateral process. Each premaxilla bears 10–12 teeth. Septomaxillae are small but well developed, possessing anterior, posterior, lateral and medial rami that roughly spiral dorsoposteriorly. The elongate maxilla bears 40–50 teeth. The pars dentalis slightly overlaps the anterior (maxillary) process of the quadratojugal caudally and extends alongside but remains separated from the anterior ramus of the pterygoid medially, while the thin and sharply pointed preorbital process broadly articulates with the neopalatines dorsomedially. The broad pars facialis is separated from the premaxilla by a narrow gap. Quadratojugals are L-shaped. The dorsal process broadly articulates with the ventral ramus of the squamosal but then joins with the lateral process to form a bulbous process dorsal to the jaw joint. Squamosals are triradiate: a posterior (otic) ramus extends medially to articulate with the crista parotica of the neurocranium; an anterior (zygomatic) ramus extends rostrally towards the parietal fenestra with a pointed tip; and an elongate ventral ramus descends to the jaw joint. Pterygoids are moderately broad and triradiate: the anterior ramus overlaps the pars dentalis of the maxilla on its lingual surface; the posterior ramus articulates with the concave bulbous process of the quadratojugal at the jaw joint; and the medial ramus slightly overlaps the alary process of the parasphenoid on its anterior face. Well-developed columellae extend laterally from the otic complex, parallel to the medial ramus of the squamosal. The exoccipital bone is incomplete dorsally, insofar as the roof of the cartilaginous otic capsule, which ossifies fully in many adult anurans, remains cartilaginous in this specimen and is invisible in its µCT scan (Fig. 5J).
The mandible (lower jaw) is edentate. The elongate angulosplenial is slightly broader posteriorly, its labial side sculpted inwards longitudinally while the lingual side is smooth and concave. It is separated from the mentomeckelian anteromedially by a moderately wide gap. The dentary is long and slender. It is aligned parallel to but separate from the angulosplenial and is fused to the mentomeckelian anteriorly. Mentomeckelian bones are small and cylindrical and separated medially by a narrow gap.
Cranial comparison. The skull of Leptobrachium sylheticum differs from that of L. aryatium by the frontoparietals closely approaching one another at the midline but remaining separated by a narrow fontanelle (vs. fused medially along approximately half their length, sites of fusion alternating with narrow gaps that are remnants of the frontoparietal fontanelle), nasals articulated with the anterior margin of the sphenethmoid for about half its length (vs. barely articulated with the anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid), and vomers moderate-sized and articulated with the anterolateral edge of the sphenethmoid on its ventral side (vs. relatively small vomers that remain separate from other bones). It differs from the skulls of L. bompu and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. by the frontoparietals being relatively narrow anteriorly and wide posteriorly and not extending anteriorly beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid (vs. extending anterolaterally beyond the caudal edge of the sphenethmoid and being only slightly broader posteriorly), frontoparietals closely approaching one another at the midline but remaining separated by a narrow fontanelle (vs. fused at a few sites along the midline but otherwise separated by a narrow fontanelle in L. bompu, and separated by a moderate-sized fontanelle along their entire length in Leptobrachium somani sp. nov.), maxillary process of the nasal relatively short and not extending posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view (vs. relatively long and extending posterior to the neopalatine in dorsal view), medial ramus of pterygoid relatively short, less than half the length of anterior ramus (vs. relatively long, more than half the length of the anterior ramus), and vomers moderate-sized and articulated with the anterolateral edge of the sphenethmoid on its ventral side (vs. relatively small vomers that remain separate from other bones). It further differs from L. bompu by nasals articulated with the anterior margin of the sphenethmoid posteromedially for about half its length (vs. slightly articulated posteromedially with the anterolateral margins of the sphenethmoid).
Vocalization. Calls of male Leptobrachium sylheticum were recorded between 19:30 and 20:00 hr on 29 June 2023 at Mawlynnong village (25°12′43.6″N 91°53′59.3″E, 586 m asl), East Khasi Hills district, Meghalaya. The frogs were calling from under wet leaf litter slightly away from the stream. The dry bulb temperature was 24 °C, the wet bulb temperature was 23.5 °C. All males produced a single type of pulsatile call delivered in groups. Each group contained 2–6 calls that lasted 739.5 ms in duration. A typical advertisement call had a call duration of 136.8 ms, with a rise time of 74.7 ms and a fall time of 62 ms. Individual calls had 19–32 pulses delivered at a rate of 165.2 ± 18.3 pulses/s. The spectrum showed a single broad peak with a mean dominant frequency of 1.41 ± 0.03 kHz (range, 1.34–1.46 kHz). The maximum amplitude pulse had a pulse duration of 7.4 ms, with a pulse rise time of 74.7 ms and a pulse fall time of 5.9 ms. The 50% pulse rise time was 0.9 ms, the 50% pulse fall time was 4.9 ms. The calls showed amplitude modulation without any frequency modulation (Table S10).
Call comparison. Calls of Leptobrachium sylheticum can be differentiated from the type 1 and type 2 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. and the calls of L. aryatium, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., L. liui, L. lunatum, L. masataksatoi, L. mouhoti, L. pullus, and L. smithi by their higher dominant frequency, 1.41 ± 0.03 kHz (vs. 1.33 ± 0.05, 1.35 ± 0.02, 1.031, 1.26 ± 0.05, 0.92, 1.3, 1.10 ± 0.098, 0.9, 1.0–1.3, and 1.296 kHz, respectively), while they differ from calls of L. bompu and L. tengchongense in their lower dominant frequency, 1.41 ± 0.03 kHz (vs. 1.53 ± 0.1 and 1.46 kHz, respectively). Although the dominant frequency of calls of L. sylheticum falls within the range of the dominant frequency of L. leucops (1.41 ± 0.03 kHz vs. 1.0–1.6 kHz), the former calls differ from the latter in having more pulses per call (19–32 pulses/call vs. 7–15 pulses/call). The calls of L. sylheticum can also be differentiated from type 1 and type 2 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. and from calls of L. bompu, L. boringii, L. liui, L. lunatum, L. mouhoti, and L. tengchongense by their faster pulse rate, 165.2 ± 18.31 pulses/s (vs. 122.3 ± 18.8, 70.7 ± 11.1, 120 ± 21.8, 87, 92.4, 131, 99, and 95.8 pulses/s, respectively), which is the fastest pulse rate reported in the genus. The calls of L. sylheticum can also be differentiated from type 1 and type 2 calls of Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., and from calls of L. bompu, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., L. hasseltii, L. kantonishikawai, L. lumadorum, L. mangyanorum, L. mouhoti, and L. tagbanorum by their shorter call duration, 136.8 ± 11 ms (vs. 214.6 ± 45.4, 645.1 ± 182.8, 182.4 ± 159.6, 3169.6 ± 1,526.5, 1,800–2,700, 2,400–3,500, 1,700–3,500, 3,400–3,700, 199 ± 19, 190, 1,570, and 1,500–2,500 ms, respectively), while they differ from the calls of L. lunatum by their longer call duration, 136.8 ± 11 ms (vs. 110 ms). The call duration of L. sylheticum (119.3–165.9 ms) falls within the range of L. liui (100–1,700 ms).
We compared vocalizations of L. sylheticum from our study with those presented in the original species description by Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021). Due to differences in terminology used by the two studies, only dominant frequency and pulse rate can be compared directly (Table S8). The mean dominant frequency observed by us (1.41 kHz), while slightly lower than that reported by Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021) (1.6 kHz), nevertheless falls within the range reported in their study, 1.34–1.46 kHz (vs. 1.4–1.9 kHz). Similarly, while the mean pulse rate in our study (165.2 pulses/s) is higher than the mean reported by Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov (2021) (124.8 pulses/s), it lies within the broader range reported in their work, 150.6–197.2 pulses/s (vs. 64.4–208.3 pulses/s).
Distribution and natural history. In Bangladesh, Leptobrachium sylheticum is known from the lowland mixed evergreen forests of Lawachara National Park and adjoining areas, as well as the hilly regions of the Sylhet Division. The species is also recorded from the tropical wet evergreen forests of the Chittagong Hill Tracts. In India, L. sylheticum has been genetically confirmed from Mawlynnong (25°12′43.6″N 91°53′59.3″E, 586 m asl) in the southern Khasi Hills region of Meghalaya and nearby areas adjoining Bangladesh, as well as from the states of Manipur (24°55′23.2″N 93°23′33.0″E, 200 m asl), Mizoram (23°41′19.3″N 92°27′09.7″E, 290 m asl), and Tripura (23°27′02.5″N 91°48′51.5″E, 200 m asl). Populations previously reported as L. smithi from adjacent areas of southern Assam, including the Barak Valley and the Karbi Anglong Hills, are also most likely assignable to L. sylheticum, considering their geographical proximity and range continuity. The species is also likely to occur in the Indian state of Nagaland. The vegetation across these Indian localities ranges from evergreen and semi-evergreen to mixed deciduous forest types. Altitudinally, L. sylheticum is a lowland species, found from approximately 50 m to 600 m above sea level. Calling males were typically observed on stream banks, hidden under leaf litter. Calling activity during the breeding season was recorded between 19:30 and 23:00 hrs.

## Discussion
Our phylogenetic analyses identify six major species groups within Leptobrachium sensu lato, broadly corresponding to the “Sundaland + Thai” clade (subgenus Leptobrachium) and the “China + Indochina” clade (subgenus Vibrissaphora) sensu Matsui et al. (2010), along with a distinct “Eastern Himalaya” clade represented by the L. bompu group. However, the phylogenetic position of the L. bompu group requires further investigation, as Sarmah et al. (2025) recover this group within Leptobrachium sensu stricto, albeit as a distinct clade, while our current study and Liang et al. (2017) place it as basal or as a sister group to all other species groups within Leptobrachium sensu lato (i.e., subgenus Leptobrachium sensu stricto + subgenus Vibrissaphora). Apart from these studies, no other investigations include members of the L. bompu group; future studies incorporating additional genetic markers and other lines of evidence are necessary to conclusively resolve its phylogenetic placement. Interestingly, eye color, an important taxonomic trait, shows a clear biogeographic pattern across Leptobrachium clades. Unicolored eyes dominate the “Sundaland + Thai” and “Eastern Himalaya” clades, whereas bicolored eyes are prevalent in the “China + Indochina” clade. A more comprehensive compilation of eye color and pattern data across the Leptobrachium phylogeny could yield additional phylogenetic and evolutionary insights into the genus, as demonstrated in other anuran groups (e.g., Amat, Wollenberg & Vences, 2013; Glaw et al., 2018; Cervino et al., 2021; Garg et al., 2021; Seshadri, Gangothri & Thaker, 2024).
The Leptobrachium bompu group represents a previously understudied phylogenetic lineage within Leptobrachium, highlighting the evolutionary and biogeographic distinctiveness of the Eastern Himalayas and the Himalayan fauna as a whole. This finding adds to the accumulating evidence that highlights the Himalaya as a global hotspot of biodiversity and endemism across multiple faunal and floral groups (Myers et al., 2000; Brooks et al., 2006; Manish & Pandit, 2018; Khatiwada et al., 2019; Wambulwa et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021; Shrestha et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023; Chettri & Tamang, 2024; Hofmann, Jablonski & Schmidt, 2024; Tajo et al., 2025). Our study also suggests that the Brahmaputra River acts as a biogeographical barrier for Leptobrachium within Northeast India, with members of the L. smithi group restricted to the south and those of the L. bompu group limited to the north. Similar distributional patterns, though scarce, have been observed in other amphibians (Pawar et al., 2006; Kamei et al., 2013) as well as in other animal groups such as primates (Choudhury, 2006; Lwin, Sukumal & Savini, 2021), reptiles (Pawar et al., 2006; Sayyed et al., 2025), and birds (Pawar et al., 2006). Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that riverine barriers can influence geographic distribution and diversification patterns in amphibians (Funk et al., 2007; Fouquet et al., 2012; Gehring et al., 2012; Dias-Terceiro et al., 2015). Further comprehensive investigations across other amphibian taxa in this region could help test the hypothesis of riverine vicariance driven by the Brahmaputra.
Populations from the Indian states of Manipur, Mizoram, and Tripura previously presumed or identified as Leptobrachium smithi (Decemson et al., 2021; Dutta et al., 2013; Purkayastha, Khan & Roychoudhury, 2020) are, in fact, referable to L. sylheticum. In contrast, populations sampled from the Brahmaputra Valley region in Assam belong to L. aryatium (Purkayastha et al., 2025; current study). The state of Meghalaya shows a more complex distribution pattern, as both L. aryatium and L. sylheticum are found here. Specifically, populations from the southern Khasi Hills, adjacent to the Bangladesh border, are assignable to L. sylheticum, while those from the northern Khasi Hills and Garo Hills correspond to L. aryatium (current study). This distribution pattern additionally suggests that L. sylheticum may also occur in the Barak Valley region and adjoining areas such as the Karbi Anglong Hills in Assam and parts of Nagaland. Further surveys and molecular data are needed to confirm this. Current distribution data indicate that these two species are allopatric and do not occur in sympatry. These findings, along with previous studies (Wogan, 2012; Mahony et al., 2017; Al-Razi, Maria & Poyarkov, 2021; Purkayastha et al., 2025), support the conclusion that all Indian populations previously assigned to L. smithi instead represent either L. aryatium or L. sylheticum. In the absence of definitive molecular evidence to the contrary, L. smithi should no longer be considered part of the Indian amphibian fauna and the species should be excluded from current national and state-level inventories.
The type localities of the newly described species Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. are separated by approximately 170 km in a southeastern direction. They lie 120–250 km northeast and 200–350 km east of the known localities of L. bompu. Although closely related, all three species are distinguishable from one another and from their closest congeners by a suite of characters including genetics, adult and larval morphology, cranial osteology, and bioacoustics. These aspects not only offer insights into their biology but also provide crucial evidence in revealing their cryptic nature and enabling their delineation, underscoring the importance of integrating multiple lines of evidence in species delimitation (Garg & Biju, 2016; Garg & Biju, 2017; Garg & Biju, 2019). Cranial osteology has been shown to have great systematic value for many amphibian groups, but especially for plethodontid salamanders where it provides robust taxonomic characters useful for differentiating closely related species that otherwise may be indistinguishable morphologically (e.g., Parra-Olea et al., 2016; Parra-Olea et al., 2020). Such data, however, remain underutilized in taxonomic studies of many arurans. The adult skulls of L. bompu, Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., L. aryatium, and L. sylheticum differ in several features of cranial osteology (Fig. 5). These include the degree to which the paired frontoparietal bones articulate or even fuse in the dorsal midline (e.g., separate in Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., but articulate in L. aryatium), the relative development of the nasal bone (broad in L. bompu and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., but thinner in L. sylheticum and especially in L. aryatium), and the variable shape of the vomer. These observations are based on only a single skull per species, and without additional knowledge of the level of intraspecific variation in such features, including possible sexual dimorphism, it is not possible to say whether the above and other differences effectively diagnose individual species. Compiling such knowledge is a worthy goal of future research that might pay substantial dividends by further resolving the species diversity of Leptobrachium, as well as other anurans of Southeast Asia. Similarly, anuran bioacoustics is shown to be able to delineate species boundaries and has often been employed in recent anuran taxonomic studies (Rowley et al., 2015; Köhler et al., 2017; Wijayathilaka et al., 2018; Garg et al., 2019; Garg et al., 2021; Stuart et al., 2020). The vocalizations of the compared Leptobrachium species (Table S8) show substantial variation in temporal properties, notably in pulse rate, number of pulses per call, and call duration. Overall dominant frequencies, however, fall within a similar range across all the studied species (0.8–1.9 kHz). Furthermore, Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. is exceptional in possessing two distinct call types. Further detailed investigations into these characters, along with additional aspects such as postcranial osteology, reproductive behavior, ecology, and natural history, would enhance our understanding of their biology, inter- and intraspecific variation, and broader evolutionary relationships.
Despite recent advances in the taxonomy of Leptobrachium, it is likely that species-level diversity of the genus remains underestimated, especially in underexplored regions such as Northeast India. Additional surveys are needed that characterize, for example, populations from neighboring and intervening regions of the genetically verified localities of L. bompu, Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov., and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov., as well as L. aryatium and L. sylheticum. Genetic investigation of such populations would provide better understanding of inter- and intraspecific variation and introgression, if any, among these closely related species. Additionally, such surveys are essential for accurately delineating the geographic distribution of these species and assessing their formal conservation status. The description of Leptobrachium mechuka sp. nov. and Leptobrachium somani sp. nov. raises the number of species of Leptobrachium formally described from India to four, and the total number of species in India to five. This further enhances Northeast India’s standing as a center of amphibian diversity by adding to its already high level of endemism, which now includes 58 frog species (AmphibiaWeb, 2025; Frost, 2025). Numerous studies have emphasized the underestimation of amphibian diversity in Northeast India and the need for dedicated surveys in this region (Biju et al., 2016; Mahony et al., 2018; Das et al., 2019; Saikia et al., 2024). Our findings underscore this need for targeted surveys that assess the diversity and distribution of not only Leptobrachium but amphibians as a whole, particularly in remote locations within Arunachal Pradesh and the rest of Northeast India.

## Conclusion
This study reveals two species-level lineages of the genus Leptobrachium from Arunachal Pradesh, India, both closely related to L. bompu, and describes them as new to science, integrating evidence from genetics, adult and larval external morphology, cranial osteology, and bioacoustics. It also resolves long-standing taxonomic misidentifications and confirms that L. smithi is absent from India. These findings add to our understanding of Leptobrachium in India and highlight both the underestimated diversity of anurans in Northeast India and the need for integrative taxonomic approaches and focused surveys in its underexplored regions.
