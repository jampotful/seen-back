# Evolutionary constraints on anuran melanin for 45 million years

## Abstract
SummaryFossil evidence of melanin is a remarkable palaeobiological resource, but the evolution of melanin through deep time is poorly resolved, partly because few studies have integrated data from fossils and their extant relatives. Here, we use data on melanin-rich organelles – melanosomes – in extant and fossil anurans to show that the evolution of melanin in the skin during the last 45 million years is decoupled from that in the eyes and internal tissues. Skin melanosomes differ in geometry (i.e., length, width, and aspect ratio) between extant and fossil species. In contrast, melanosomes from the eyes and internal tissues show strongly conserved geometries in all fossil and extant species. These tissue-level trends in melanosome evolution are probably controlled by physiological constraints on melanosome functions such as photoprotection, visual signaling, thermoregulation, and metal homeostasis, that have been in place at least since the Eocene. Skin melanosomes are not subject to such constraints on geometry, which may reflect evolutionary tradeoffs linked to the diversification of integumentary coloration and/or habitat.

## Introduction
Melanin is a light-absorbing polymeric pigment1,2,3 that is widespread in living organisms and serves critical physiological roles in photoprotection, visual signaling, thermoregulation, metal regulation, and as an antioxidant.4 In vertebrates, melanin is packaged as membrane-bound organelles – melanosomes – that usually occur as a continuum of geometries from spheres to elongate rods, but also as flattened discs.5 Most melanosomes are solid, but some are hollow or have a spongy texture.6 Melanosomes typically occur in connective tissues, the eyes, integument (e.g., feathers, hair, and skin), and internal organs (e.g., heart, kidneys, liver, lungs, and spleen).1,2,3,4,7 Melanosomes comprise primarily melanin, occurring as two forms: eumelanin, derived from the amino acid tyrosine, and phaeomelanin, from tyrosine and cystine.2,8,9 Both forms of melanin function in visible coloration and also function in metal homeostasis and immunity.2,4,10,11,12
Fossil evidence of melanosomes can inform on the original colors,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 physiology,7,22 internal anatomy,23,24 and phylogenetic position25 of fossils and on physical and chemical taphonomic processes.8,26,27,28,29,30,31,32,33 There is increasing interest in the application of fossil data to understand the evolution of melanosomes and melanin in various fossil groups. Previous studies have examined integumentary melanosomes in dinosaurs and their relatives,22 in pterosaurs,13 in marine reptiles34 and in fish.17 Given the diverse biological functions of melanin,2,4,35 especially of non-integumentary melanins,7,36 comparative analysis of melanosomes in extant and fossil representatives of related taxa has the potential to shed light on the evolution of vertebrate physiology and ecology.22,23 Here, we present the first attempt to characterize shifts in melanosome geometry in multiple tissues within a single vertebrate group. We use anurans as a case study for the following reasons. First, anurans are important components of extant terrestrial habitats and are abundant in many lacustrine Konservat-Lagerstätten,37,38,39,40,41,42,43,44 often preserving melanosome-rich soft tissues.38,39,45 Second, to our knowledge, anurans are the only vertebrate group for which a dataset is available7,24 on the geometry of integumentary and non-integumentary melanosomes, that includes both extant and fossil species. These published data confirm that melanosomes in extant and fossil anurans show tissue-specific geometries.7,36 Third, the anuran Bauplan has been conserved since the Late Triassic46,47,48 and is largely decoupled from habitat47,49: extant anurans that occupy different habitats (including woodlands, swamps, meadows, tundra, and deserts) retain a similar Bauplan49 and show similar adaptations, e.g., most anurans are adapted to nocturnal vision.50,51,52 It is, however, unclear whether evolutionary constraints in modern anuran taxa apply to anuran melanosomes in different tissues. We hypothesize that the conservation of anuran anatomy suggests the conservation of melanin function in anurans. To test this, the tissue origins of fossil melanosomes must be known. Consequently, our study has three aims. First, to discriminate fossil melanosomes from different tissues in fossil anurans from various localities using data on melanosome geometry and chemistry. Second, to deduce the tissues of origin of preserved melanosomes. The third aim is to assess the evidence for melanosome evolution in anurans over geological timescales. Melanosomes from extant and fossil anurans vary in length (mean ± SD; extant: 693 ± 244 nm; fossil: 651 ± 321 nm; Figures 1, 2, 4, S9, S11, S13, S15, and S17) and aspect ratio (mean ± SD; extant: 0.672 ± 0.17; fossil: 0.820 ± 0.18; Figures 1, 2, 4, S10, S12, S14, S16, and S17). Melanosomes from the extant species studied show tissue-specific geometry (Figures 2 and S9–S16); melanosomes from a given tissue, however, vary somewhat in geometry among species (Figures 2 and S9–S16). Melanosomes from fossil specimens show different geometries among body regions, i.e., abdomen, eyespots, skin, and torso (Figures 3 and S9–S16). The morphospace of the total dataset largely corresponds to the morphospace of melanosomes from the eyespots of fossil specimens and eyes of extant specimens, indicating that the eye melanosomes are responsible for most of the observed melanosome diversity (Figures 3A, 3B, and S3). The morphospace for melanosomes from a particular body region/tissue is independent of sample size (Pearson’s r(14) = 0.117, p = 0.666 if morphospace were defined by 95% confidence ellipses).

## Eyes and eyespots
Melanosomes from the eyes of extant anurans and from the eyespots of Libros and Geiseltal specimens show populations of spheroids, ovoids and rods (Figures 1A–1C, 1I, 2A, and 2B); each dataset (i.e., one dataset per fossil biota and the dataset comprising all extant anurans studied) occupies a large region of morphospace (Figures 3B and S3). Populations of rods are usually mixed with ovoids and spheroids (Figure 2B), but rods can also form spatially separated clusters and layers (Figures 1A–1C, 1I, and 2A). Melanosomes from the eyespots of the Bechlejovice anurans show only ovoid geometries (Figure 1G) that define a small morphospace (Figure 3B). Accordingly, boxplots of melanosome length (Figure S9) and aspect ratio (Figure S10) show that, except for Bechlejovice, eye melanosomes from each dataset show similar variation in aspect ratio and length. Melanosomes from the four datasets are similar in length (differences are not significant; Welch test, p = 0.210; Table S1), and melanosomes from the Libros specimens and extant anurans are similar in aspect ratio (differences are not significant; Dunn’s post hoc test, p = 0.646; Table S2). Melanosomes from the Geiseltal and Bechlejovice specimens, however, differ significantly in aspect ratio from extant anuran melanosomes (Table S2).

## Skin
SEM images of melanosomes from the skin of extant and fossil anurans show intermingled spheroids and ovoids (Figures 1F, 1H, 1L, 2H, and 2I). Each of the four datasets occupies a similar region of morphospace (Figure 3C). Melanosomes from the Bechlejovice, Libros, and extant specimens are similar in length (Figure S11; differences are not significant; Table S2), but melanosomes from Geiseltal are significantly smaller than all others (Figure S11; Table S2). Melanosomes from all fossil skin samples show similar aspect ratios; those aspect ratios are higher than in extant anurans (Figure S12; differences are significant; Table S2).

## Abdomen (fossil specimens)/internal tissues (extant specimens)
SEM images show spheroids in the Geiseltal specimens predominantly (Figure 1E) and ovoids in the Libros specimen (Figure 1K) and in extant anurans (Figures 2C–2G).
Melanosomes from the abdomen of fossils from Libros and Geiseltal show little overlap in morphospace (Figures 3E and 3F) and differ significantly in geometry (Table S2). The morphospace for the Geiseltal abdominal melanosomes shows little overlap with that of connective tissue (Figures S6C and S8C) or the spleen (Figures S6D and S8D) in extant anurans and is much smaller than that for the lungs (Figures S6E and S8E) and liver (Figures 3E and S4C). Instead, the morphospace of the Geiseltal abdominal melanosomes most closely resembles the position, size, and spatial extent of the morphospace of the gonads of extant anurans (Figure 3F). Indeed, the aspect ratio of the fossil melanosomes strongly resembles that of the gonads of Bombina (Figure S14; Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p = 0.7829). Except for these data on Bombina, the melanosomes from all internal organs of extant anurans differ in geometry from those from the abdomen of Geiseltal specimens (Figures S13 and S14).
Melanosomes from the abdomen of the Libros specimen form two layers that differ in melanosome geometry. These layers, therefore, represent two populations and thus likely derive from different tissues. Melanosomes from abdomen population 1 (La1) occupy a similar morphospace to that of melanosomes from the liver (Figure 3E), gonads (Figure 3F), and lungs (Figures S4C and S8E) but show little overlap in morphospace with that of connective tissue (Figures S6C and S8C) and the spleen (Figures S6D and S8D). The morphospace for the Libros abdominal melanosomes is greater than that for the gonads (Figure 3F) and lungs (Figures S6E and S8E). Statistical analysis of the geometry data reveals that the Libros melanosomes of population 1 do not differ significantly in geometry from those from the connective tissue (Figures S6C and S8C) or from the lungs (Figures S6E and S8E) of extant anurans (Table S2). The morphospace of the liver overlaps with that of La1 (Figure 3E). The aspect ratio of population 1 and that of extant liver melanosomes are similar.
Melanosomes from abdomen population 2 (La2) occupy a similar morphospace to the liver and gonads of extant anurans. The former differs significantly in size from all melanosome populations in extant specimens and has similar aspect ratios to those from connective tissue (Figures S6C and S8C), the liver (Figure 3F), lungs (Figures S6E and S8E), and the spleen (Table S2).

## Torso (fossil specimens)/internal tissues (extant specimens)
SEM images show that melanosomes are spheroids and ovoids in the Geiseltal specimens (Figure 1D) and ovoids in the Libros specimen (Figure 1J). Melanosomes from the torso of specimens from Libros and Geiseltal overlap considerably in morphospace (Figure 3D) but differ significantly in size (Table S2), suggesting different tissue origins for each specimen.
The morphospace for melanosomes from the torso of the Libros specimen shows extensive overlap with that for several tissues in extant anurans, i.e., the heart (Figures S6A and S8A), lungs (Figures 3D and S4B), liver (Figures S5B and S7D), connective tissue (Figures S5C and S7E), and the spleen (Figures S5D and S7F). Melanosomes from the liver and spleen of extant specimens differ significantly in length from the fossil melanosomes (Table S2). Statistical analysis of the geometry data reveals no significant differences between melanosomes from the lungs of extant specimens and the melanosomes from the torso of the Libros specimen (Table S2).
The morphospace for torso melanosomes from Geiseltal specimens is much larger than that for connective tissue (Figures S5C and S7E), the spleen (Figures S5D and S7F), and the gonads (Figures S6B and S8B) in extant anurans. The morphospace for the Geiseltal torso melanosomes is, however, smaller than that for the heart (Figures S6A and S8A) and liver (Figures S5E and S7D). Notably, the morphospace of the Geiseltal torso melanosomes closely resembles that of melanosomes from the lungs of extant anurans (Figures 3D and S4B); both melanosome length and aspect ratio, however, differ significantly between these two datasets (Figures S15 and S16; Table S2).

## Principal component analysis
PCA of the geometry data for the total dataset successfully resolves major patterns in melanosome geometry; the first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) combined explain 80% of the variation in the raw dataset (Figure 4, Methods S1). The PCA plot reveals that the data for different tissues and body regions are spatially separated in the morphospace (Figure 4). The differences between groups primarily reflect variation in PC1, which in turn primarily reflects aspect ratio skew (Figure 4). Data for all fossil eyespots and eyes of extant specimens form a distinct group in the far right of the plot; the data for skin melanosomes from all fossil and extant specimens form a tight cluster in the lower left of the plot. Between these two groups lie the data for the lungs, liver, and gonads of extant specimens and for the torso and abdomen of Geiseltal and Libros specimens. For all these samples (liver, lungs, gonads for extant specimens; torso and abdomen for fossil specimens), the position of the relevant datapoints along the PC1 axis is most critical, as this PC is responsible for most of the variation in the dataset (Figures 4, S18, and S19). Libros abdomen populations 1 and 2 (La1 and La2) plot close to the extant lungs, skin, gonads, and liver along the PC1 axis (Figure 4). ## Synchrotron rapid scanning-X-ray fluorescence analysis constrains interpretations of fossil soft tissues
Chemical data have not been reported previously for specimens from Geiseltal. We therefore used SRS-XRF to test our interpretation of a single tissue source for the preserved melanosome films from the torso and abdomen of Geiseltal specimen GMH CeIII 4967-1932 (Figures 5A–5I, S1F, and S21A–S21C; Methods S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the SRS-XRF element maps (Figures 5D–5H and S20) yields various components that, in conjunction with the element maps (Figure 6), facilitate the identification of 13 regions of interest (ROIs) selected for further analysis (Figures S19–S25 and S26A). To aid the visualization of chemical variation among ROI, the data were log10-transformed and combined to form six groups (Figures 7 and S26B): Groups I–IV collectively represent all soft tissue samples and are characteristically enriched in Ca and Ni (and, to a lesser extent, Cu, Ti, Co, V, Mn and Cr) relative to Groups V and VI, which collectively represent sedimentary matrix and embedding resin. Groups I and II (from the torso and abdomen, respectively) are enriched in Zn, with slightly more Ni and/or Ca in Group II. ## Discussion
This study shows that the melanosomes from various body regions of fossil anurans from the Miocene, Oligocene, and Eocene are similar in geometry to melanosomes in specific body tissues in extant anurans.

## Interpretation of fossil melanosome source based on geometry
The tissue of origin of the fossil melanosomes is tightly constrained for those in the eyespots, based on the anatomical location of the samples. The PCA results confirm similar geometries for fossil and extant eye melanosomes. The differences in aspect ratios for eye melanosomes from Geiseltal and Bechlejovice and from extant anuran melanosomes (Table S2) reflect, at least in part, the large spheroidal melanosomes in the Geiseltal dataset and the absence of rod-shaped melanosomes in the Bechlejovice dataset.
The anatomical location of samples also assists in the identification of fossil skin melanosomes; the latter are positioned external to the mineralized fossil dermis. In the PCA plot, the data for all skin samples form a cluster on the left half of the plot, clearly separated from most other data (Figure 4).
The geometry of melanosomes from the abdomen of fossils from Libros and Geiseltal differs significantly (Table S2), suggesting that the melanosomes originate from different tissues. The abdominal melanosomes from all internal organs of extant anurans differ in geometry from those from the abdomen of Geiseltal specimens (Figures S13 and S14), except for the aspect ratios of melanosomes from the Geiseltal abdomen and melanosomes of the gonads of Bombina. The gonads are thus the most plausible source for the abdominal melanosomes in the Geiseltal anurans. The PCA plot is not inconsistent with this interpretation: the Geiseltal abdominal melanosomes plot separately from the gonads, but are closer to these than all other internal tissues.
Melanosomes from population 1 of the Libros specimen (La1) have several potential origins: the liver, gonads, and/or lungs. The melanosomes of La1 are not skin melanosomes, as reported by Rossi et al.7 Our reinterpretation is supported by statistical analyses: the geometry of melanosomes from La1 (in specimen NHML-R4982) differs from that of unequivocal skin melanosomes (in specimen MNCN 63798; aspect ratio: two-sample t test, p = 0.001; length: two-sample t test, p = 1.189∗10−4). Further, melanosomes from La1 are significantly larger than melanosomes from the skin of extant anurans (two-sample t test, p = 3.231∗10−8; Table S1). The spleen can be excluded as a likely source because the morphospace for the Libros abdominal melanosomes is greater than that for the spleen (Figures S4 and S8). Given the smaller sample size for the Libros melanosomes than for the extant tissue, it is thus unlikely that the fossil melanosomes derive entirely from the spleen. The same limitation applies to melanosomes of the gonads and lungs; their morphospace is smaller than that of the fossil melanosomes. Further, the morphospace and aspect ratio for La1 more closely resemble these attributes of melanosomes from the liver rather than the lungs (Figure 3E; Table S2). Finally, melanosomes are much more abundant in the liver of anurans relative to other internal tissues.7,36 Despite these arguments in favor of a liver source for La1, the position of the data in the PCA plot is not consistent with a single source (Figure 4). The La1 data are therefore inconclusive.
The geometry of fossil melanosomes from abdomen population 2 of Libros specimens (La2) indicates liver and gonads as potential sources. The PCA plot suggests that melanosomes from La2 derive mainly from the gonads and lungs. The La2 melanosomes are unlikely to derive from connective tissue (due to low abundance of melanosomes in vivo), from the lungs and gonads (little overlap in morphospace), or from the spleen (due to the small size of the tissue in vivo). The lack of a clear match for the La2 melanosomes could indicate that the sample represents a mixture of melanosomes from several sources. This mixing would require extensive decay (i.e., liquefaction of internal tissues) before preservation. The fossil anuran specimen, however, is well articulated with a clear body outline, indicating that any mixing was limited. It is therefore considered more likely that La2 melanosomes derive at least in part from an alternative internal source (e.g., oocytes63,64).
Our data indicate that the lungs are the most likely source for the torso melanosomes of the Libros specimen. In the PCA plot, Libros torso melanosomes plot closest to the lungs (Figure 4); this is consistent with both the morphospace plots (Figure 3) and statistical analyses (Table S2). Melanosomes from the liver and spleen of extant specimens can be excluded as a likely source for the fossil torso melanosomes, as melanosomes from these tissues each differ significantly in length from the fossil melanosomes (Table S2). Of the remaining tissues, the lungs are the most plausible source for the fossil melanosomes.
Geiseltal torso melanosomes are difficult to assign confidently to a particular tissue based on geometry data. The melanosomes probably represent several tissues. The much larger morphospace for torso melanosomes from Geiseltal specimens than for connective tissue (Figures S5C and S7E), the spleen (Figures S5D and S7F), and the gonads (Figures S6B and S8B) in extant anurans suggests that these three tissues are unlikely to represent the source of the fossil melanosomes. The morphospace for the Geiseltal torso melanosomes could potentially represent a subset of that for the heart (Figures S6A and S8A) and liver (Figures S5E and S7D), as their collective morphospace is larger than that for the fossil torso melanosomes. The heart is an unlikely source because it contains few melanosomes in extant anurans. The liver, however, contains abundant melanosomes in vivo7,36 and is thus a more plausible source. Melanosomes from the lungs are, in terms of morphospace, superficially similar to the Geiseltal torso melanosomes (Figures 3D and S4B), but statistical analysis of data on length and aspect ratio indicates significant differences between these and the Geiseltal torso melanosomes. Instead, the PCA plot suggests that the Geiseltal torso melanosomes derive from the liver and especially the gonads and/or lungs. This is supported by data from three sampling points (torso sample 1, torso sample 2, and abdomen sample) from the Geiseltal specimen GMH CeIII 4967-1932 (Figure 4). Torso sample 1 (St1) plots close to the abdomen sample and not far from the gonads, whereas torso sample 2 (St2) plots very close to the gonads and lungs (Figure 4). The uncertainty in assigning the Geiseltal torso melanosomes to one tissue can be explained by decay processes. Liquefaction of internal organs in extensively decayed specimens, and/or physical disturbance of carcasses by bottom currents, may have produced melanosome layers that contain a mixture of melanosomes from several sources. In contrast to the Libros anurans, certain Geiseltal specimens (Figure S1) show patchy skin, disarticulated skeletal elements, and incomplete skeletons that indicate prolonged decay before deposition.54,65,66 This does not appear to apply to specimen GMH CeIII 4967-1932. This specimen, although incomplete, lacks indicators for prolonged decay as it is articulated with a well-defined and continuous body outline. Similar to the Libros specimen discussed above, some melanosomes may derive from an internal source that was not studied here.
The uncertainty in assigning certain fossil melanosome populations to tissues based on melanosome geometry highlights the need for a more complete dataset on melanosome geometry in different tissues.

## Interpretation of fossil melanosome source based on chemistry
Preserved melanosomes from the torso and abdomen of the fossil specimens cannot be confidently attributed to specific internal tissues using anatomical location alone. Basic geometry data (length and aspect ratio) eliminate certain potential candidate tissues but does not identify a single tissue of origin. Consideration of melanosome morphospace, the relative size of tissues in vivo, and the relative abundance of melanosomes in different tissues in vivo eliminate additional candidate tissues. Multivariate analysis of a broad suite of melanosome geometric parameters provides additional constraints on interpretations. By placing the results in the context of the metal chemistry of the fossil melanosomes, it may be possible to successfully determine whether the fossil melanosomes represent one or more tissues (Figures 5B–5H and 6). Chemical mapping using SRS-XRF can reveal cryptic tissue-specific variation in the chemistry of fossil melanosome films, even if the preserved chemistry has been impacted by diagenesis.24 Previous SRS-XRF analyses of the adult anuran specimen from Libros revealed a relatively homogeneous metal chemistry for the internal melanosomes.7 The PCA results reported here indicate the lungs as the source of the torso melanosomes and several tissues as the sources for melanosomes from abdominal population 1 and abdominal population 2 from Libros. This shows that variation in the geometry of fossil melanosomes is not always accompanied by variation in melanosome chemistry, as in extant anurans.7 The homogenous chemistry of the internal melanosomes in the Libros anurans may be an original biological feature (i.e., whereby melanosome geometry and chemistry are independent in certain taxa) or may reflect overprinting of original chemical variation during diagenesis.30 Some fossil melanosomes in this study can be ascribed to a single tissue origin, respectively, the lungs, liver, or gonads. Exceptions include the melanosomes from sample St2 from the torso of one Geiseltal specimen (GMH CeIII 4967-1932).
The metal chemistry of GMH CeIII 4967-1932 can shed light on the tissue assignment for various fossil tissues (Methods S2). In the LDA plot (Figure 7), the chemistry data form six clusters (groups). Variation in Ca among groups most likely reflects differences in the relative thickness and/or continuity of the E-K layer (which is replicated in calcium phosphate) in these sample regions; Ni shows similar spatial variation to Ca and is thus probably associated primarily with the mineralized E-K layer. Variation in Cu and Zn primarily reflects variation in the composition of the melanosome films.
These data indicate that Groups I (torso) and II (abdomen) likely represent melanosomes from a common source (Figure 7). This is not fully supported by the PCA of the melanosome geometry parameters (Figure 4), which indicates that melanosomes from the torso (including sample St2) and the abdomen derive primarily from the gonads, but also derive from other tissues (especially sample St1). There is no evidence from the SRS-XRF data for additional melanosome sources in the torso (i.e., liver and/or lungs; Figures 5B–5H and 6). This can be explained as the melanosomes from the gonads and other tissues (in torso and abdomen samples) do not differ in chemistry in this fossil taxon; as with the Libros anuran (see above), this could be explained as a real biological feature or as a diagenetic artifact. Given the limited sample size, it is not possible to determine whether the disparity between the chemical and geometric data is an original biological signal or an artifact of fossilization.30,67 Given the consistent preservation of tissue-specific chemical differences between melanosomes from the eyes, skin, and internal organs (the latter considered collectively), diagenetic overprinting seems unlikely. Decoupling of geometry from chemistry in the internal melanosomes of fossil anurans also seems unlikely, as it has implications for the physiological functions of those melanosomes in metal homeostasis.4 The discrepancy between the chemical and geometric data may be most plausibly interpreted as a function of the small size of the SEM sample used for analysis of melanosome geometry. Testing this is beyond the scope of this study; more extensive sampling of fossil melanosome films could benefit future studies, but may not always be feasible.
Groups III and IV likely represent melanosomes from a different source to Groups I and II (Figure 7); the location of these samples (Figure 5A) and the lack of evidence for the redistribution of internal melanosomes into the limbs (as would be evidenced by elevated Zn concentrations in the melanosomes from the hindlimbs; Figure 7), suggests that Groups III and IV represent skin melanosomes. The chemical differences between Groups III and IV may reflect a greater contribution of preserved E-K layer to the signal for Group III and/or a greater contribution of host matrix to the signal for Group IV.

## Conservation of anatomy suggests the conservation of function
The fossil anurans from Geiseltal and Bechlejovice (Pelobatidae and Palaeobatrachidae) and Libros (Ranidae) closely resemble extant anurans in skeletal anatomy and skin structure.39,54,59,68 The habitats and lifestyles of the fossil anurans from each locality and the extant specimens are broadly comparable. It is therefore reasonable to infer broad similarities in organ function among the anurans in our dataset; it is unlikely that minor differences in habitat (e.g., lake physiography/water depth/water chemistry) and anuran lifestyle (e.g., predominantly aquatic or terrestrial) would impact organ function. The functional roles of melanin in the anuran eye, skin, and internal organs (e.g., lungs, liver, and gonads) were therefore probably established by the Late Triassic. Notwithstanding this, the skin of extant anurans shows marked interspecific variation in terms of coloration, presence/absence of a lateral line system, and adaptations to particular habitats.49

## Conservation of melanosome geometry
Our data show that the geometry of melanosomes in the eyespots of fossil anurans from the Miocene and Eocene is similar to that of ocular melanosomes in extant anurans (Figures 3B, S7, S9, and S10). Data for the Libros frogs is fully consistent with that for extant anurans; excepting some large spheroidal melanosomes, the Geiseltal eye melanosomes are also consistent with extant examples. Differences between the Bechlejovice eye melanosomes and all others can be explained by the sampling of only one eye tissue (and thus only one melanosome population) in the fossil. Collectively, these data indicate that eye melanosome geometry has been conserved in anurans since the Middle Eocene, i.e., for ca. 45 million years. This, in turn, strongly suggests that the functions of ocular melanins in anurans have been conserved over this period. The internal melanosomes of the studied fossil anurans can be assigned to the liver, lungs, and gonads using a combination of data on melanosome geometry and chemistry; thus, the preserved attributes of melanosomes from internal tissues are also consistent with evolutionary conservatism since the Middle Eocene.
Conservation of anatomy implies the conservation of function. The functional implications of melanosome geometry in vertebrates are not fully understood; the former is hypothesized to relate to melanosome monomer chemistry and, in turn, metal chemistry, UV absorption capacity, oxidative properties, and antioxidant properties,4 but this hypothesis has not been tested. More superficial links between melanosome geometry and tissue function have been characterized for feathers and hair,21 whereby melanosome geometry is linked to visible color. The controls on melanosome geometry in other tissues have not been investigated, but potential functions can be discussed in the context of existing literature. In the vertebrate eye, melanosomes are considered to function primarily in photoprotection and in the regulation of oxidative tissue damage25,69,70; it is reasonable to assume such functions apply to anuran eye melanosomes. The visual system is highly conserved in anurans: they typically see in color,71 with high spatial resolution and broad spectral sensitivity, including low ambient illumination.72,73,74 Like most anurans,50,51,52 all of the extant anurans studied are adapted to nocturnal vision. It is therefore not unreasonable to consider that strong conservatism in the anuran visual system may be reflected in conserved melanosome geometries in the group. Interspecific variation in eye melanosome geometry may relate to plastic characters of the eye74,75,76,77,78 such as iris color, pupil shape, ecology, and/or activity period.
Similarly, there are no definitive data on the specific controls on the geometry of melanosomes in internal organs, although these melanosomes are linked to functional roles in metal homeostasis and antioxidant functions.4 Irrespective of the precise physiological functions of melanosomes in the eyes and internal tissues of anurans, the similar geometry of these melanosomes in the fossil and extant anurans strongly implies that the most important functional constraints have not changed since the Middle Eocene.
The geometry of the skin melanosomes in all fossil anurans studied differs from that in the extant anurans in our dataset, indicating that skin melanosomes are not subject to the same functional constraints as melanosomes from other tissues. Potential links between melanosome geometry and skin color have not been investigated for extant anurans, but previous work on reptiles has shown that melanosome geometry is independent of skin color in the group.22 A similar relationship may also apply to amphibians, but our dataset is not sufficiently large to allow definitive assessment of the nature of any links between the geometry of skin melanosomes in anurans to skin color.
Irrespective of the above interspecific differences in skin melanosome geometry, our dataset reveals some notable shared features. Skin melanosomes are smaller than most or all non-integumentary melanosomes in a given taxon and are similar in different body regions (dorsal/ventral). These features suggest the presence of large-scale functional controls that are yet unknown.
Notably, our data on melanosome geometry and chemistry provide strong support for the preservation of gonad melanosomes in the torso and abdomen of the anuran specimen GMH CeIII-4967-1932 from Geiseltal. The extent of these melanosomes in the torso could be explained by the mobilization of melanosomes following the liquefaction of the gonads during decay. The gonads are, however, small organs, with relatively little melanin (compared to e.g., the liver7,36). Alternatively, the fossil melanosomes may derive from internal oocytes,63,64,79 which in gravid female anurans can occupy a large proportion of the volume of the torso. The geometry of melanosomes from oocytes, however, has not been investigated, so it is unknown at what stage during oocyte and/or tadpole development the melanosomes from the gonads and oocytes can be discriminated. If oocytes are indeed the source of the fossil melanosomes, the specimen would be a gravid female. This is consistent with other evidence54,59 that the Geiseltal anurans visited the depositional environment to breed. These data therefore represent tentative evidence for the sex of fossil anurans, with potential applications to other vertebrates.
Ultimately, we show that the combination of multivariate analysis of a comprehensive suite of melanosome geometry attributes, plus chemical data using SRS-XRF, is a much more powerful tool to constrain the origins of fossil melanosomes than basic geometric attributes alone – at least for certain datasets. The major benefit of using SRS-XRF is to chemically discriminate layers of overlapping fossilized soft tissues without the need for destructive sampling. By using this approach, our study provides first-time evidence for tissue-specific trends in the evolution of melanin in anurans over the last ca. 45 million years, whereby the evolution of melanosomes in the skin is decoupled from that in the eyes and internal tissues. We report that specific melanosome geometries in the eyes and internal body tissues (lungs, liver, and gonads) have been evolutionarily conserved since the Middle Eocene, but skin melanosomes in fossil and extant anurans differ in geometry. These patterns most likely reflect disparate physiological constraints on melanosome function in these tissues since the Middle Eocene. These constraints do not affect integumentary melanosomes. The liberation of integumentary melanosomes from these constraints may reflect evolutionary tradeoffs linked to the diversification of integumentary coloration, activity period, the presence/absence of a lateral line system, and/or habitat. This study advances our understanding of evolutionary processes, functional drivers, and selection pressures on melanosome geometry in vertebrates. Broader application of this approach has the potential to reveal novel aspects of the evolution of anatomy, physiology, and behavior in other vertebrate groups.

## Limitations of the study
The limitations of this study relate mainly to sample size, i.e., the number of fossil and extant taxa and the number of fossil samples used. Only six specimens of the Geiseltal anurans were available for the destructive sampling of soft tissues for SEM analysis; one specimen was available for the SRS-XRF analysis. In particular, the destructive sampling of the soft tissues was limited to a maximum of five sample locations per specimen. Small sample sizes may limit the ability to detect variation in melanosome geometry and chemistry within individual specimens and between specimens. Such limitations on the sampling of fossils from Konservat-Lagerstätten are typical for this fossil type; in the case of the Geiseltal anurans, the collection has National Heritage status, which places additional constraints on sampling and international loans of specimens.
Only four extant taxa were used in our dataset. This dataset is not sufficiently large to confidently assess potential links between melanosome geometries and specific ecological adaptations, for example, links between integumentary coloration and/or habitat and skin melanosomes. This should be a target for future investigation. Sampling of additional extant taxa would have exceeded the financial and temporal limitations of this study.
Finally, although the impact of diagenesis on melanosomes is well characterized,30,32,33,67,80,81,82 we cannot completely exclude the possibility that unknown taphonomic factors may impact preserved melanosome geometries.
Future studies should investigate the melanin geometry and chemistry of different skin colors in extant anurans and test for differences in melanosome geometry in anurans from different habitats.

## Method details

## Fossil material
In this study, we examined fossil anuran specimens from three stagnation-type Konservat-Lagerstätten (Table 1): Geiseltal (Eocene, Germany), Bechlejovice (Oligocene, Czech Republic) and Libros (Miocene, Spain). All studied specimens represent adults.
The Eocene Geiseltal biota includes amphibians, reptiles, mammals, birds and insects. Circa 50,000 specimens from the biota are hosted in the Geiseltal collection of the Natural Sciences Collections (ZNS) of Martin Luther University Halle-Wittenberg, Halle (Saale), Germany. Many specimens of fossil vertebrates show evidence for preserved soft tissues, such as (part or all of) the body outline. The preservation of Geiseltal fossil latex (monkeyhair laticifers) indicates diagenetic temperatures of max. 100°C.86 Previous experiments revealed little to no impact on melanin chemistry at maturation temperatures of 100°C,32 and thus changes in geometry at these temperatures should be minimal. Anurans are abundant, represented by at least 180 specimens, with reports of hundreds of specimens lost during excavations.53,87 The anurans include representatives of Pelobatidae, Palaeobatrachidae44,88,89,90,91 and probably Discoglossidae.44,92 Specimens vary in their taphonomy but are usually well-articulated skeletons.54 Many Geiseltal anurans preserve soft tissues,59,93,94,95 i.e., fossil skin, melanosomes and aragonite crystals of the endolymphatic sac.59 Here, we examine the fossil melanosomes from various body regions (i.e., eyespot, skin, torso = the upper region of the body of the anuran specimens, which is posterior to the cervical vertebrae and anterior to the ilia, abdomen and limbs) of six Geiseltal anuran specimens. Four of the studied specimens were previously assigned to the Pelobatidae,44,88,89,91 which are predominantly terrestrial; two specimens are of undetermined affinity (Figure S1). Five specimens are conserved in nitrocellulose glue and one specimen is preserved in situ in sedimentary matrix (i.e., brown coal59,96); the slab is conserved in water. All slabs retain small (< 10 mm2) patches of dark organic-rich sedimentary matrix; no distinctive sedimentary textures or fabrics are evident.
The Oligocene Bechlejovice biota includes anurans, caudates, reptiles and fish.55,56,97,98,99 The Lagerstätte was formed in a large, permanent lake surrounded by temporarily active volcanoes.56 Adult anurans are abundant (> 1,000 specimens) and are often near-complete and well-articulated54,55; many specimens show patches of dark brown (1–3 cm2) carbonaceous soft tissues in the cranium and torso. Here, we examine the soft tissues from the eyespot and torso in one specimen (Palaeobatrachus luedecki (Anura: Palaeobatrachidae), NHML-OR35814, Natural History Museum London) from Bohemia. The specimen is conserved in laminated mudstone (Figure S1). The state of preservation of the specimen and microscopic study results of the sediment are consistent with specimens from Bechlejovice (M. E. McNamara pers. obs.). There are no other known Konservat-Lagerstätten from Bohemia (Czech Republic) that preserve articulated anurans of this taxon with soft tissues and thus the specimen is most plausibly interpreted as from the Bechlejovice locality.
The Miocene Libros Konservat-Lagerstätte includes amphibians, birds, snakes, and insects16,39,58,80,100 with adult anurans being the most abundant faunal element (n > 73).39 Several vertebrate groups preserve soft tissues.16,23,36,39,58,68,100 The maximum burial temperature for Libros is estimated at < 40°C.80 Anurans are usually (near-)complete and well-articulated and often preserve skin and melanosomes; many specimens also preserve stomach contents39,68 and aragonite crystals of the endolymphatic sac.39 Here, we study soft tissues from two frog specimens (Pelophylax pueyoi (Anura: Ranidae), NHML-R4982 and MNCN 63798). Both NHML-R4982 (Natural History Museum London, UK) and MNCN 63798 (Museo Nacional de Ciencias Naturales, Madrid, Spain) are hosted in laminated mudstone. Both specimens preserve soft tissues of the body outline as a combination of dark carbonaceous material rich in melanosomes7,39,68 and a pale material that represents the preserved Eberth-Katschenko layer of the skin.39 The data presented here represent an excerpt of the dataset in Rossi et al.7 and unpublished data provided by VR.

## Extant material
Tissue samples from eleven adult specimens of four extant anuran species were analyzed (Table 1): Bombina variegata (yellow bellied toad; Anura: Bombinatoridae; n = 3), Hymenochirus boettgeri (Congo dwarf clawed frog; Anura: Pipidae; n = 2), Rhaebo guttatus (smooth-sided toad; Anura: Bufonidae; n = 3) and Xenopus laevis (African clawed frog; Anura: Pipidae; n = 3). Tissue samples were dissected using clean dissection needles, scalpels and forceps. Most dissections were performed under a Leica EZ4W stereo microscope. Melanosomes were extracted from tissues using the enzymatic digestion protocol in Rossi et al.7 for Hymenochirus boettgeri and Xenopus laevis and using a modified version of that protocol for Bombina variegata and Rhaebo guttatus. In brief, over the course of ten days, the tissue samples were washed with acetone and deionized water and treated with daily cycles of a solution of phosphate buffer, Proteinase-K (days 1, 2, 4–10), Papain (day 3) and dithiothreitol (DTT) and incubated at 37.5°C overnight. The modified version of the protocol includes two additional days of sample preparation in a fume hood as follows. At the end of day 10, samples were immersed in HCl overnight; on day 11, samples were washed using biomolecular grade water, ethanol, acetone and heptane, then immersed in heptane for 24 hours. On day 12, samples were washed using ethanol and acetone and then centrifuged for 20 minutes at 15,000 rpm. Finally, the samples were pelletized in acetone and air-dried in a fume hood.

## Identifying the tissue origins of fossil melanosomes
Our dataset spans ca. 45 million years and thus provides an excellent opportunity to investigate evolutionary trends in melanosome geometry. This requires, however, that the tissue of origin is known for melanosomes preserved in different body regions of fossils. While melanosomes in the eyespots of fossils can reasonably be interpreted as derived primarily from the eye (i.e., iris, choroid and/or retinal pigmented epithelium), fossil melanosomes from the body outline have many potential origins.7,36 The fossil anurans used in this study all preserve (at least locally) the E-K layer of the mid-dermis in calcium phosphate.24,39,59 Fossil melanosomes located external to this layer derive from the skin (i.e., the upper dermis and/or epidermis).23,39,59 Melanosomes preserved medial to the mineralized skin most plausibly derive from one, or more, of the internal organs. In extant anurans, melanosomes show tissue-specific variation in geometry and chemistry7; these attributes may therefore constrain interpretations of the tissue of origin. Prior to using preserved melanosome attributes for evolutionary studies, however, the impact of diagenesis on these attributes must be considered. The elemental and organic chemistry of fossil melanosomes are subject to strong diagenetic alteration and thus vary among biotas.16,24,29,30,31,32,67 As a result, it is not clear whether the preserved melanosomal metallome is a reliable indicator for the tissue of origin. Changes in melanosome geometry during fossilization are controlled primarily by temperature80; maximum burial temperatures are inferred to be similar for all three of the biotas studied here (see STAR Methods) and thus geometry is the key melanosome attribute examined in this study.
Melanosome geometries and / or chemistries from anurans from Geiseltal, Bechlejovice and Libros have been reported in previous studies. Spheroidal and ovoid melanosomes have been identified from integumentary and non-integumentary fossil soft tissues in the Geiseltal specimens (e.g., in specimen GMH Celll 4967-1932).59 Melanosomes were previously identified from the skin and eyespot of a specimen from Bechlejovice.24 Previous reports of melanosomes preserved in the fossil anuran specimen NHML-R4982 from Libros include data on melanosome geometry and chemistry,7 summarized as follows. Melanosomes from the eyespot are spheroids, ovoids and rods; in contrast, melanosomes from both the torso and abdomen include spheroids and ovoids. In the abdomen, these melanosomes are separated spatially into two populations (interpreted as skin and liver melanosomes, respectively). In specimen NHML-R4982, Cu and Zn are enriched in the torso and pelvis; Zn is enriched in the eyespot and the body outline (interpreted as skin melanosomes7). Comparable data have not been reported for fossil anurans from Geiseltal and Bechlejovice.

## Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
Six specimens from the Geiseltal biota were sampled (Table 1). Small (1–2 mm2) fragments of fossil soft tissues were dissected from several body regions including the eyespot, torso, abdomen and limbs using scalpel and forceps. Soft tissue samples were mounted on aluminum stubs with carbon tape for subsequent analysis. Selected soft tissue samples were sputter coated with Au. Samples were analyzed using a JEOL JSM IT100 VP-SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5–20 kV and a working distance of 6–10 mm for SEM images. High resolution images were obtained using a high resolution aperture with the microscope in high resolution mode and with probe currents of 14–37 A.U. Brightness and contrast were adjusted for selected SEM images in Figures 1 and 2 using Adobe Photoshop CC 2019.

## Quantification and statistical analysis

## Measurements
Melanosomes were measured from SEM images using ImageJ v. 1.54g.85 Melanosomes selected for analysis lack evidence for degradation and are consistent in tone and thickness along their length (and are thus orthogonal to the line of sight). Length and width were measured from at least 50 melanosomes per sample; the total dataset comprises 6713 melanosomes (including fossil and extant melanosomes).

## Analysis of melanosome geometry data
Fossil samples from the abdomen are located posterior to the joint between the sacral vertebra and last free vertebra; samples anterior to this joint are from the thorax. Melanosome geometry was assessed for each tissue region (Table 1) as follows. Data were first calculated for eight melanosome parameters: mean length, mean aspect ratio, kurtosis (for length and, separately, aspect ratio), skew (for length and, separately, aspect ratio) and interquartile range (for length and, separately, aspect ratio). Melanosome geometry forms a continuum between spheres and rods, but in very general terms, an aspect ratio close to 1 indicates spheroidal melanosomes, an aspect ratio between 0.4 and 0.9 indicates ovoid melanosomes and an aspect ratio < 0.4 indicates rods. These data were analyzed using Principal Components Analysis in PAST (Paleontological Statistics) v. 4.16c83 (Figure 4). The PCA was performed using a standardized dataset due to the dominance of certain PC variables when using the raw data. Data were standardized using the following data transformation: (x – μ) / σ (where μ denotes the mean, x denotes the value to be transformed, σ denotes the standard deviation).
Data from each body region (in fossils) and tissue (in extant anurans) were tested for normality and homoscedasticity prior to further analysis. Differences in melanosome geometry among the samples were explored using various significance tests, i.e., the independent t-test, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, ANOVA, Welch test and Kruskal-Wallis test. The data were then investigated further using the Tukey or Dunn post-hoc test. The significance and post-hoc test used for a particular analysis was dependent on sample size, homogeneity of variance in the data and normality of the data.
The area of melanosome morphospace was calculated from scatterplots of geometry data in Adobe Illustrator using the GetShapeArea script. The relationship between the number of data points for each sample and the area of the corresponding PCA morphospace was assessed using Pearson’s correlation test in Microsoft Excel.

## Synchrotron rapid scanning-X-ray fluorescence analysis (SRS-XRF)
Specimen GMH CeIII-4967-1932 was mounted directly on the sample stage. SRS-XRF data were collected at beam line 10-2 at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource (USA). The incident X-ray energy was set to 11 keV and 16.5 keV, respectively, using a Si (111) double crystal monochromator with the storage ring containing 500 mA in top-off mode at 3.0 GeV. Specimens were mounted at 45° to the incident X-ray beam and spatially rastered at 50 ms / pixel dwell time. The entire fluorescence spectrum was collected at each data point and the intensity of fluorescence lines for pre-selected elements (K, Ca, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, AsPb, Cd, Sb, and Hg) was monitored using a silicon drift Vortex detector. X-ray energies were determined using a Quantum Detectors Xpress3 pulse processing system. Element concentrations (μg/cm2) were calibrated using NIST traceable thin film elemental standards. Data were processed using Sam’s MicroAnalysis Toolkit (SMAK) software version 3.0.9.84

## Analysis of SRS-XRF data
Initial data processing included edge removal and normalization to the time channel. Preliminary data analysis in SMAK used a built-in image analysis function to analyze chemical variation in maps for Ca, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu and Zn using principal components analysis (PCA), with the output comprising a map for each component. Thirteen regions of interest (ROI) were selected for further analysis using these component maps (Figures 5 and S20) and the original SRS-XRF element maps (Figure 6). SRS-XRF spectra were produced using the PyMCA algorithm101 in SMAK.84 Peaks were usually fitted using the following parameters: spectrometer zero, spectrometer gain, detector width and a fixed PileUp factor of 1.0∗10-8 ± 1∗10-8. Selected spectra were fitted using an adjusted pileup factor of 3.0∗10-6–4.5∗10-6 ± 1∗10-8 in order to remove pile up peaks for Ca (Figures S21–S25).
All subsequent analyses were performed in PAST (Palaeontological Statistics) v. 4.16c.83 PCA was used to assess chemical variation among the ROI analyzed. Differences in elemental concentrations among ROI were visualized using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). Due to the high abundance of Ca relative to other elements, the data were log10-transformed (with zero values replaced by 10-11 prior to data transformation). One datapoint belonging to ROI 6 was identified as an outlier and omitted. Based on the LDA results, the 13 ROI were organized into six groups for further analysis (Figures 7, S21, and S26). The data for these groups are visualized using 95% confidence ellipses in order to show the chemospace that will contain the mean in 95% of cases where the data distribution is subsampled. Chemical differences among the ROI were assessed for statistical significance using the Welch test or the Kruskal-Wallis test (depending on the characteristics of the data, i.e., normality and homoscedasticity) and Dunn’s post-hoc test.
The relationship between the number of data points for each ROI and the area of the corresponding LDA chemospace was assessed using Pearson’s correlation test in Microsoft Excel.
