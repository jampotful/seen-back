# Versatile Xenopus tropicalis model with targeted integration of human BRAFV600E

## Abstract
SignificanceGene knock-in animal models have been hindered by low efficiency. We addressed these limitations by leveraging the high fecundity of Xenopus tropicalis and its observable pigment phenotypes to construct a versatile BRAFV600E -targeted knock-in model. This model uniquely integrates BRAFV600E at the mitf locus, generating both a retinal regeneration model and a tumor model. The BRAFV600E mutation induced RPE cell transdifferentiation, leading to the creation of eyeless frogs, which provide a valuable platform for retinal regeneration research. Additionally, these eyeless frogs exhibit abnormal pigment cell proliferation, offering insights into nevi formation and melanoma biology. Our work highlights the BRAFV600E -targeted knock-in X. tropicalis provides a strategy for gene knock-in animal models and a versatile tool for retinal regeneration and tumor research.Keywords: X. tropicalis, BRAFV600E knock-in, retinal regeneration, melanocytic and xanthocytic nevi, xanthophoroma
Given the inherent challenges of studying disease mechanisms and treatment strategies directly in humans, and the inability to fully recapitulate human disease progression in a single model, alternative disease models are continually being developed (1–3). Advances in gene-editing technologies have enabled the generation of genetically engineered animal models that more closely model human diseases by replicating gene mutations at both molecular and cellular levels (4–7). However, reports on animal models developed using large-fragment, multigene knock-in techniques via CRISPR/Cas9 remain scarce (3–5). These models offer distinct advantages over traditional genetically engineered models, such as the use of endogenous promoters to drive gene expression and the ability to achieve cell-specific expression (5). The scarcity of large-fragment, multigene knock-in animal models is primarily due to the low efficiency of exogenous gene integration and the difficulty in obtaining F1 progeny with correct gene integration from mosaic G0 founders (5). Additionally, although zebrafish, as a model organism, produce more eggs than mammals, the number remains constrained to a few hundred per spawning. This limited embryo availability in both mammals and zebrafish compounds the challenges of developing large-fragment, multigene knock-in disease models.
Xenopus tropicalis is a diploid amphibian model organism with a genome that exhibits 79% homology to the human genome (8). Its organ development processes are conserved compared to those of humans. Furthermore, X. tropicalis offers valuable attributes, including high fecundity—producing up to 8,000 eggs per spawning—and efficient gene-editing capabilities. As a result, X. tropicalis plays a pivotal role in life sciences research, particularly in studies related to cell cycle regulation, mitosis, meiosis, oogenesis, embryonic development, disease modeling, and drug screening (8–17). In light of these characteristics, we aimed to leverage its high fecundity to establish a large-fragment, multigene knock-in disease model in X. tropicalis. We plan to establish a X. tropicalis melanoma model due to the ease of observing its pathological phenotypes. Human melanoma is characterized by high metastatic potential, recurrence, and elevated mortality rates, highlighting the need for new animal models to investigate the mechanisms underlying melanoma development and therapeutic strategies(18, 19). While we have developed tp53−/− and cdkn2b−/−/tp53−/− melanoma models in X. tropicalis, there remains a gap for a model driven by the common driver mutation BRAFV600E (18–26).
Hence, in this study, we targeted the mitf locus of X. tropicalis for exogenous gene knock-in, integrating a large DNA fragment containing the BRAFV600E, EGFP, and CreERT2 genes. In humans, the MITF locus transcribes multiple mRNAs with distinct 5′ ends and identical 3′ ends, and each mRNA translates into MITF proteins that have distinct tissue expression patterns. Among these, one MITF isoform is specifically expressed in melanocytes and serves as a master regulator of their development (27). In both mice and zebrafish, promoters that specifically drive MITF expression in melanocytes are frequently employed to develop transgenic melanoma models (18–20). Although research on the mitf locus in X. tropicalis is limited, it is highly conserved among vertebrates (23, 27). BRAFV600E is a prevalent mutant form of BRAF in humans and is associated with various tumor types, leading to persistent activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, which regulates tumor initiation, progression, and drug resistance (24, 28, 29). Over 80% of human melanomas are driven by BRAFV600E, and most melanoma models in mice and zebrafish are also based on this mutation (18–20, 24). In this research, EGFP is employed to indicate BRAFV600E expression, while CreERT2 is introduced for future lineage tracing of melanoma development using the CreERT2-LoxP system (6).
Collectively, we have implemented a method for constructing animal models of human diseases, establishing a versatile and heritable disease model in X. tropicalis through the targeted integration of the human BRAFV600E mutation at the mitf locus. Following this targeted knock-in, we simultaneously generated both a melanoma model and a retinal regeneration no-eye frog model in X. tropicalis. While our previous study established the BRAFV600E knock-in X. tropicalis model to validate mitf expression in xanthophores and granular glands (23), the current work reveals several biological and methodological findings. First, we present a vertebrate BRAFV600E knock-in model of retinal regeneration, in which BRAF/MAPK activation induces RPE-to-retina transdifferentiation, and demonstrate BRAF/MAPK-driven fate conversion between melanophores and xanthophores. Second, we observe differential immune infiltration, with nevi exhibiting CD3+ T cell enrichment and xanthophoromas displaying immune exclusion. Third, we establish a cost-effective, phenotype-based knock-in strategy leveraging Xenopus fecundity. We also characterize the mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf- line, revealing that C-terminal peptide fusion impairs Mitf function, informing future knock-in design. These findings underscore the complexity of endogenous gene transcriptional regulation, suggesting that simple transgenic expression driven by exogenous promoters cannot fully recapitulate the in vivo expression patterns of endogenous genes. Furthermore, the findings of this work offer valuable insights and considerations for retinal regeneration, pigment cell biology, cell fate determination, tumor biology, and vertebrate disease model development.

## Results

## Efficient Generation of a BRAFV600E Knock-In X. tropicalis Line.
To rapidly generate a BRAFV600E knock-in X. tropicalis line, we employed a two-pronged approach. In theory, G0 embryos carrying the BRAFV600E mutation at the mitf locus should exhibit abnormal melanocyte development, characterized by visible pigmentation defects. This phenotype enabled efficient screening and facilitated the rapid identification of G0 individuals for further breeding. Second, leveraging the high fecundity of X. tropicalis, we can increase the number of F1 BRAFV600E knock-in individuals, facilitating the establishment of a stable and heritable knock-in line (Fig. 1A). We targeted the final intron of the mitf locus as the integration site (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Sanger sequencing confirmed an 80% efficiency of Cas9-mediated cleavage at this site, making it suitable for CRISPR/Cas9-directed exogenous DNA integration (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

Previous studies have shown that overexpressing BRAFV600E mRNA in Xenopus laevis zygotes leads to abnormal embryonic development (30). In this study, we overexpressed the BRAFV600E mutation or a truncated version of BRAF containing only the kinase domain in X. tropicalis zygotes, driven by mitf or slug promoters. Developmental abnormalities were observed in regions where either BRAFV600E or the truncated BRAF was expressed (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C–E). These results suggest that constitutively active BRAF mutants also disrupt embryonic development in X. tropicalis.
Using the above knock-in strategy, we generated a BRAFV600E knock-in vector (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Initially, we expected to observe melanocyte-specific EGFP expression in G0 generation BRAFV600E knock-in tadpoles (before stage 48). However, despite extensive examination, no EGFP signal was detected in melanocytes of G0 tadpoles (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 F-G). By stage 51, we identified eight G0 generation BRAFV600E knock-in tadpoles exhibiting abnormal melanocyte and xanthophore development (Fig. 1 B and C). In each round of BRAFV600E plasmid microinjection, approximately 40% of the embryos showed EGFP-positive signals in the pancreas (This result is due to EGFP expression in the pancreas driven by the elastase promoter carried on the injected plasmid), confirming successful plasmid integration into the X. tropicalis genome (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and G). In previous studies, we have confirmed that this phenomenon is due to the expression of mitf in xanthophores and melanocytes in X. tropicalis (23).
Subsequently, we raised the eight G0 BRAFV600E knock-in tadpoles to sexual maturity and mated them with adult wild-type (WT) X. tropicalis to produce the F1 generation (Fig. 1 D–F). During the early developmental stages of the F1 generation, tadpoles with abnormal eye pigmentation were readily observed (Fig. 1F). By stage 48, these individuals exhibited not only abnormal eye pigmentation but also structural abnormalities in the eyes (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Additionally, F1 tadpoles with eye defects showed significant melanocyte developmental abnormalities throughout their bodies (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Compared to stage-matched WT tadpoles, the F1 generation displayed a reduced number of melanocytes and diminished or absent dendritic structures. Consequently, at stages 40, 42, and 48, dorsal melanocyte coverage in the F1 tadpoles was notably reduced, especially on the abdomen and back (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). EGFP expression analysis revealed that tadpoles with abnormal pigmentation and eye development exhibited EGFP expression in the pancreas, indicating successful plasmid integration (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Furthermore, the EGFP signals appeared discretely distributed, resembling melanocyte patterns, with some exhibiting dendritic shapes, suggesting the fluorescence originated from EGFP-BRAFV600E expression in melanocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Based on these findings, we concluded that the F1 tadpoles exhibiting abnormal pigmentation and eye development are the desired heterozygous BRAFV600E knock-in X. tropicalis.
A statistical analysis of G0 transmission efficiency revealed a successful BRAFV600E knock-in rate of 75% (SI Appendix, Table S1). Starting from embryonic microinjection of the knock-in plasmid, it took about 6 mo to generate a substantial number of F1 BRAFV600E knock-in X. tropicalis. We also applied this strategy to establish a mitf–/– rescue line (23), which similarly required 6 mo. Statistical data (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Table S2) indicate that microinjecting ~300 to 1,000 oocytes, followed by screening for pigmentation defects 20 to 30 d postinjection (stage 51–56), typically yields 2 to 10 or more G0 tadpoles with abnormal pigmentation. Subsequent breeding establishes stable, heritable X. tropicalis lines with targeted gene knock-ins, a process requiring about 6 mo (requiring careful rearing to sexual maturity). Owing to the large number of eggs and the ease of microinjection, this approach efficiently generates ample target G0 tadpoles and F1 X. tropicalis. This suggests that phenotype-based selection, followed by breeding, is an effective strategy for generating F1 BRAFV600E knock-in X. tropicalis. For further studies, we selected the F1 pair with the highest transmission efficiency (SI Appendix, Table S1).
To molecularly confirm that the F1 tadpoles obtained through phenotypic screening resulted from targeted integration of BRAFV600E at the mitf locus, we designed specific primers to detect upstream integration and downstream integration sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The results confirmed that F1 tadpoles with abnormal pigmentation and eye development were indeed X. tropicalis with BRAFV600E targeted integration at the mitf locus. Genotyping revealed a 6 bp deletion at the upstream integration site and a 21 bp deletion at the downstream site (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–D). The PCR identification results, together with digital PCR copy number data, confirmed that a single copy of BRAFV600E was integrated at the expected site within the mitf locus (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3G). For simplicity, we refer to this X. tropicalis line with a single-copy BRAFV600E integration as “mitf KI line 1”, also denoted as mitf-BRAFV600E± or BRAFV600E±.
To further confirm that the pigmentary abnormalities observed in mitf-BRAFV600E± X. tropicalis were caused by BRAFV600E knock-in, we performed BRAFV600E knockout in these frogs. In G0 mosaics, some pigment cells exhibited restored normal development (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). In previous studies, we found that overexpression of etv1 mRNA in X. laevis led to eye development defects resembling those in mitf-BRAFV600E± embryos (31). Other studies have also shown that activation of the FGF/MAPK/ETV1 pathway can induce eye malformations similar to those reported here (32). Consistently, overexpression of etv1 mRNA in WT X. tropicalis embryos resulted in comparable ocular abnormalities (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Together, these results further support that the abnormal phenotypes in mitf-BRAFV600E± frogs were driven by BRAFV600E knock-in.
These findings demonstrate that by first using abnormal phenotypes as screening markers to identify BRAFV600E knock-in G0 mosaic X. tropicalis individuals, followed by breeding with WT X. tropicalis, a stable mitf locus-targeted BRAFV600E knock-in line can be efficiently obtained. Offspring from mitf-BRAFV600E± females crossed with WT males exhibited Mendelian inheritance of the BRAFV600E allele and associated phenotypes, enabling stable generation of the mitf-BRAFV600E± model (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The genotype resulting from BRAFV600E knock-in, along with the associated pigment cell and ocular developmental phenotypes, followed Mendelian inheritance patterns, indicating an almost 100% penetrance. From 2020 to 2025, we repeatedly confirmed the genotype of mitf KI line 1 X. tropicalis using the aforementioned PCR strategy, and consistently observed that both pigment cell and eye abnormalities adhered to Mendelian ratios with near-complete penetrance. However, the fertility of mitf-BRAFV600E± males and the development of mitf-BRAFV600E+/+ individuals require further investigation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

## The mitf Allele with BRAFV600E Knock-In Exhibits Impaired Protein Function.
To assess the impact of BRAFV600E knock-in at the mitf locus on Mitf function in X. tropicalis, we crossed mitf-BRAFV600E± animals with mitf−/− mutants(23, 33) to generate mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf- compound heterozygotes (Fig. 2A). This model enables direct phenotypic assessment of Mitf function. If the knock-in disrupts Mitf, compound heterozygotes should exhibit pigment cell defects. Offspring showed a ~1:1 ratio of normal to abnormal pigmentation, consistent with Mendelian inheritance (Fig. 2 A and B). Genotyping confirmed that pigment-deficient individuals were mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf-, lacking melanophores but retaining xanthophores (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Their eye defects mirrored those in mitf-BRAFV600E± animals, likely due to preserved retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) development in mitf−/− frogs (23, 33). Notably, xanthophores persisted in mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf- mutants, and iridophore development may also be affected (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that either distinct Mitf isoforms differentially regulate melanophore and xanthophore development, or the Mitf-MAPK signaling network operates through more complex mechanisms in different pigment cell types, warranting further investigation. Importantly, the mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf- mutants represent a model for studying pigment cell development in X. tropicalis and, together with WT, mitf−/−, and mitf-BRAFV600E± animals, constitutes a robust system for dissecting pigment cell development.

Additionally, western blot analysis detected Mitf protein in WT and mitf-BRAFV600E± skin, but not in mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf- or mitf−/− mutants (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A), indicating impaired translation or rapid degradation. Although AlphaFold shows intact Mitf structure/DNA-binding post-P2A fusion (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B), experimental results reveal functional disruption (Fig. 2 A–D), highlighting the limitations of structural predictions in assessing protein functionality.
In summary, BRAFV600E knock-in at the mitf locus disrupts Mitf function, likely via impaired protein expression or stability. These findings underscore the importance of experimentally validating fusion strategies when generating knock-in models, rather than relying solely on structural predictions.

## Retinal Regeneration Model: BRAFV600E-Driven Transdifferentiation of RPE Cells Results in Symmetrical Retina Formation.
The abnormal eye development in mitf KI line 1 X. tropicalis ultimately leads to no-eye, providing a retinal regeneration model through transdifferentiation of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. To investigate the developmental trajectory of these eye abnormalities, we continuously monitored eye development in heterozygous F2 mitf KI line 1 frogs and backtracked the onset of these defects (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

Up until stages 33–34, no visible differences were observed between WT and mitf KI line 1 embryos (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). By stages 35–36, mitf KI line 1 embryos began showing subtle, uneven eye pigmentation, though not distinct enough to differentiate from WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). By stages 37–38, phenotypic differences became clear, with mitf KI line 1 embryos showing uneven melanocyte pigmentation, while WT embryos had uniform pigmentation (Fig. 3A). At stage 40, WT embryos had fully pigmented eyes, while mitf KI line 1 embryos exhibited incomplete pigmentation with more evident unpigmented regions (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). As development progressed, eye abnormalities in mitf KI line 1 tadpoles worsened, with stage 49 embryos showing abnormal pigmentation and structural defects (Fig. 3B). By stage 57, their eyes had regressed almost entirely, with skin covering the original eye region (Fig. 3B). After metamorphosis, both juvenile and adult mitf KI line 1 frogs were completely eyeless, with skin covering the eye area, leading to their designation as “eyeless frogs” (Fig. 3B).
Histopathological analysis at stage 49 in mitf KI line 1 tadpoles revealed that regions formerly occupied by the RPE layer developed disorganized structures resembling a nascent retina, with scattered RPE cells present throughout (Fig. 3C). At this stage, the primordial retina exhibited all retinal layers, the lens was fully formed, and melanocytes were visible in the choroid (Fig. 3C). By stage 57, these eyes had formed two structurally symmetric retinas: the original primordial retina and a regenerative retina occupying the former RPE region (Fig. 3 C–E). The axis of symmetry was aligned with the residual RPE cells (Fig. 3 C–E). Although the lens remained intact, both the RPE layer and choroidal melanocytes were absent, with remaining RPE cells distributed along the retinal midline (Fig. 3C). In addition, extensive melanocyte proliferation in the ciliary body disrupted the ciliary marginal zone (Fig. 3C). Eventually, the lack of an RPE layer to support ocular development and function led to complete eye degeneration, resulting in skin overgrowth and the formation of an eyeless phenotype (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that retinal regeneration occurs during development, whereby the RPE regenerates a new retina while the primordial retina develops normally, yielding two symmetric retinas within a single eye. At the molecular level, abnormal eye development in mitf KI line 1 frogs should be attributed to BRAFV600E-driven MAPK pathway hyperactivation in RPE cells, promoting their transdifferentiation into retinal cells (34–42). This sustained MAPK activation may also represent a mechanism for in vivo retinal regeneration (36, 37, 41, 42).
Overall, the eyeless frog offers a model for retinal development and regeneration (42). It enables investigation into the cellular and molecular mechanisms by which the continuous activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, driven by BRAFV600E, induces the transdifferentiation of RPE cells into retinal tissue during early RPE development. This model can provide crucial insights into the mechanisms of symmetric retinal pattern formation and contribute to advances in retinal regenerative medicine. Furthermore, the eyeless frog can address key questions related to retina development, such as the extent of RPE cell dependency during early retinal development.

## Pigment Cell Abnormal Development Model: BRAFV600E Induces Transdifferentiation of Xanthophores and Melanocytes.
In addition to pronounced ocular defects, eyeless frogs exhibit abnormal development of melanocytes and xanthophores throughout the body. In WT X. tropicalis tadpoles, dendritic melanocytes appear on both dorsal and ventral surfaces by stages 37–38. In contrast, melanocytes are absent from both sides in eyeless tadpoles at this stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). By stage 40, WT tadpoles develop abundant melanocytes dorsally and ventrally, whereas eyeless tadpoles show only sparse melanocyte presence (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Before stage 50, melanocyte numbers in eyeless tadpoles remain low, followed by extensive, abnormal proliferation (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–F). To quantitatively assess these changes, we compared melanocyte morphology and abundance between stage 45 eyeless and WT tadpoles (Fig. 4 A–C). Overall, eyeless tadpoles exhibit significantly fewer melanocytes, particularly in the abdominal and tail regions (Fig. 4C).

Xanthophores in eyeless frog tadpoles first appear at stage 49, when spontaneous green fluorescence is undetectable in WT tadpoles (Fig. 4D; SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Between stages 50 and 53, both xanthophores and melanocytes proliferate extensively in eyeless tadpoles, nearly covering the entire body, particularly the tail region (Fig. 4 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). At later stages (56–58), melanocytes outnumber xanthophores, resulting in a darker overall appearance (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). Following metamorphosis, juvenile and adult eyeless frogs exhibit dorsal skin entirely pigmented by melanocytes, with an abnormally high number of melanocytes on the ventral side, while surface xanthophores are scarcely visible (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E and F).
Histopathological analysis of the tails of eyeless frog tadpoles, as described in SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B, revealed significant proliferation of melanocytes and xanthophores in the US and BS regions compared to WT tadpoles at the same developmental stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). Similarly, in the dorsal skin of 2.5-y-old eyeless frogs, a large number of melanocytes proliferated beneath the basal membrane of the dermis, with substantial proliferation of xanthophores underneath the melanocyte layer (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D–F). This abnormal proliferation filled the stratum spinosum, increasing skin thickness (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). It also disrupted both the stratum spinosum and dermal glands, reducing mucus secretion (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). A large number of melanosomes were present in epidermal cells, and both melanocytes and xanthophores proliferated in the epidermis—an observation absent in WT X. tropicalis (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D and F). The proliferation of melanocytes above xanthophores, along with extensive melanosomes in epidermal cells, contributed to the darker skin pigmentation in eyeless frogs. This tissue structure differed markedly from that of WT dorsal skin. Notably, some proliferating xanthophores contained abundant melanosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S11E). Given that amphibian pigment cells may derive from a common progenitor (23, 43), these findings suggest that sustained activation of the BRAFV600E-driven MAPK pathway may induce xanthophore-to-melanocyte transdifferentiation, similar to BRAFV600E-induced RPE cell transdifferentiation. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that compared to WT X. tropicalis, mitf-BRAFV600E± frogs exhibited significant proliferation of both melanocytes and xanthophores (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A–C). Moreover, no significant difference was observed between the numbers of transdifferentiated and nontransdifferentiated xanthophores (SI Appendix, Fig. S12D).
To further confirm melanocyte and xanthophore proliferation in eyeless frog skin, we used TEM to examine the dorsal skin (SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and S14). TEM revealed a distinct layer of proliferating melanocytes beneath the basal membrane, followed by a layer of proliferating xanthophores interspersed with scattered melanocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A). In contrast, WT skin typically displays a layer of xanthophores directly below the basal membrane, followed by melanocytes (SI Appendix, Figs. S13A and S14A). Melanocytes were also present in the epidermis of eyeless frogs, and melanosomes were detected in basal cells of the stratum basale (SI Appendix, Figs. S13 B and C and S14 B and C), confirming the presence of epidermal melanosomes and melanocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D and F). Additionally, proliferating xanthophores contained not only typical pterinosomes but also melanosomes and mixed pigment granules comprising both, referred to as “mix-somes” (SI Appendix, Fig. S13D). These findings corroborate the presence of numerous melanosomes in xanthophores in eyeless frogs (SI Appendix, Fig. S11E).
In highly proliferative xanthophores, we observed three distinct states of mix-somes: mix-some 1, representing the initial emergence of melanin; mix-some 2, containing substantial melanin with shallow pigmentation; and mix-some 3, characterized by fully formed melanosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S13E). These stages reflect the process of mix-some formation and indicate that melanogenesis occurs in xanthophores under sustained BRAFV600E-driven MAPK pathway activation. This suggests that continuously activated xanthophores may undergo transdifferentiation, acquiring melanocyte-like features. In X. laevis, iridophores can be induced to transform into melanophores in vitro, and both melanophores and xanthophores are thought to arise from a common progenitor (43). Thus, persistent MAPK pathway activation may drive xanthophores to dedifferentiate into a progenitor-like state and subsequently redifferentiate into an aberrant xanthophore with melanocyte characteristics (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 D and E). If so, melanocytes with sustained MAPK activation may similarly acquire xanthophore-like features. Supporting this, we observed mix-somes in proliferating melanocytes in the skin of eyeless frogs (SI Appendix, Fig. S13F) and mix-cells containing both pterinosomes and melanosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S14D). Together, these findings support the potential for bidirectional transdifferentiation between xanthophores and melanocytes under persistent BRAFV600E-driven MAPK pathway activation.
To confirm that BRAFV600E integration at the mitf locus causes the observed phenotype, we performed immunofluorescence and western blot analyses to address two key questions: 1) whether BRAFV600E was properly expressed, and 2) whether the MAPK pathway was activated. Western blot results showed significantly elevated BRAFV600E expression and MAPK activation in all BRAFV600E± groups compared to WT skin (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Furthermore, a marked increase in EGFP-positive cells in the dorsal skin of BRAFV600E± and BRAFV600E± BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± xanthophoroma samples, relative to WT, confirmed that BRAFV600E expression drives MAPK activation and cellular expansion (Fig. 5 C and D). cdkn2b knockout data are detailed below. Immunofluorescence analysis of the tails of eyeless tadpoles revealed colocalization of Tyr and Mitf proteins, consistent with melanocyte dendritic morphology (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A). Additionally, colocalization of EGFP with Tyr and Mitf with BRAFV600E confirmed successful expression of the EGFP-BRAFV600E fusion protein under the endogenous mitf promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 B and C). MAPK activation was observed exclusively in EGFP-positive cells, but not in EGFP-negative cells or WT melanocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 D–F). Thus, the above data demonstrate that BRAFV600E activates MAPK signaling in this model.

In summary, in mitf-BRAFV600E X. tropicalis, the endogenous mitf promoter drives BRAFV600E expression in RPE cells, melanocytes, and xanthophores, leading to sustained MAPK pathway activation and abnormal development of the eyes and pigment cells. However, it is noteworthy that in vertebrates, the Mitf locus gives rise to multiple transcripts that include the final exon, resulting in broader BRAFV600E expression beyond just RPE cells, melanocytes, and xanthophores. Accordingly, we examined BRAFV600E expression and histopathology in major organs of mitf-BRAFV600E frogs. While BRAFV600E expression was detected in some organs, no significant pathological changes were observed, except for pigment cell abnormalities; all major organs remained functional, supporting survival (SI Appendix, Figs. S17 and S18). Thus, mitf-BRAFV600E frogs provide an opportunity to further explore the physiological roles and regulatory mechanisms of the MAPK-Mitf signaling network. This targeted expression model serves as a valuable platform for studying retinal regeneration, ocular development, pigment cell biology, and broader cellular processes.

## Xanthophoromas X. tropicalis Model: cdkn2b Knockout Promotes BRAFV600E-Induced Xanthophoromas Formation.
In the course of raising two groups of eyeless frogs—one approximately 4 y old (n = 7) and the other around 3 y old (n = 32)—we observed that their skin remained persistently dark, with no spontaneous development of melanomas or xanthophoromas. Based on the histopathological features of melanocytic nevi in tp53−/− X. tropicalis, the skin of eyeless frogs exhibits a condition where melanocytic and xanthocytic nevi coexist (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S11) (22). This suggests that BRAFV600E expression, driven by the endogenous mitf promoter in melanocytes and xanthophores, is insufficient to induce melanoma or xanthophoroma formation. In fact, in human BRAFV600E mutations typically do not lead to spontaneous melanoma formation but rather maintain a long-term state of nevi (24). Thus, eyeless frogs may serve as a model for studying melanocytic nevus formation, benefiting research on BRAFV600E-induced nevus pathogenesis and potential therapeutic approaches.
Inactivating mutations in human CDKN2A promote melanoma progression (44, 45). Similarly, Cdkn2a knockout in mice accelerates the formation of BRAFV600E-driven melanomas (45–47). Therefore, we hypothesized that knockout of cdkn2b, the X. tropicalis ortholog of human CDKN2A, would also promote the formation of melanomas and xanthophoromas in eyeless frogs. To test this, we crossed heterozygous BRAFV600E-targeted mitf knock-in X. tropicalis (referred to as BRAFV600E±) with cdkn2b−/− frogs, generating four genotypes: BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b+/+, BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b±, BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b−/−, and BRAFV600E−/−/cdkn2b+/+ (WT). WT tadpoles and adult frogs developed normally, with no spontaneous formation of nevi or melanomas (SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). In contrast, BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b+/+, BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b±, and BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b−/− tadpoles exhibited proliferative melanocytes and xanthophores (SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). Notably, BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b−/− tadpoles developed visible xanthophoromas by 1.5 mo (stage 58), although no significant signs of melanocyte tumorigenesis were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). All BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b−/− tadpoles died around 2 mo of age, shortly after completing metamorphosis, due to widespread xanthophoroma development (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).
During the course of our study, no spontaneous formation of melanomas, xanthophoromas, or other notable tumors was observed in eyeless frogs (SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). However, approximately 10% of BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± tadpoles developed visible xanthophoromas on their skin within 2 mo, prior to completing metamorphosis. Nearly all of these tadpoles died shortly after metamorphosis. While most BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± tadpoles did not develop obvious spontaneous xanthophoromas or melanomas during the tadpole stage, they exhibited evidently increased proliferation of melanocytes and xanthophores (SI Appendix, Fig. S19A). By 4 mo of age, 7% (2/30) of BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± frogs showed spontaneous xanthophoroma formation. By 8 mo, the penetrance of spontaneous xanthophoromas had reached 100% (30/30) (Fig. 6 A–D). Despite continuous observation over approximately 2 y of BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± frogs, no spontaneous melanomas were detected. The underlying reason for this remains unclear. H&E staining confirmed extensive proliferation of xanthophores, along with significant melanocyte proliferation within the xanthophoromas (Fig. 6 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S20). TEM further corroborated the presence of numerous proliferating xanthophores and revealed pigment granules, known as mix-somes, within these cells (Fig. 6D). These findings suggest the occurrence of transdifferentiation in BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b+/+ xanthophoroma cells.

Allogeneic transplantation experiments demonstrated that xanthophoromas from BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b−/− tadpoles possess metastatic potential, spreading throughout the host and forming distant lesions (SI Appendix, Fig. S19 B–D) (23). In BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± frogs, both tumor and peritumoral tissues exhibited significant MAPK pathway activation (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Furthermore, CD3 immunofluorescence staining revealed that xanthophoromas in these frogs were immune-cold, with few CD3+ T cells infiltrating the tumor core (Fig. 6 E–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). In contrast, the peritumoral regions—identified histologically as xanthophore and melanocyte nevi—were immune-hot, exhibiting abundant CD3+ cells (Fig. 6 E–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). These findings suggest that the failure of melanocytic or xanthophoric nevi to progress into malignancy may be associated with local CD3+ immune infiltration. It is possible that CD3+ cells suppress melanoma progression in melanocytic nevi but are less effective in preventing malignant transformation of xanthophores, explaining the selective spontaneous development of xanthophoromas—but not melanomas—in BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± frogs. This regulatory mechanism warrants further investigation.

## Discussion
In this study, we screened for the phenotype of abnormal pigment cell proliferation induced by BRAFV600E and utilized the high fecundity of X. tropicalis to develop a versatile BRAFV600E knock-in model. As a result, we generated a stable, heritable line of X. tropicalis with BRAFV600E integrated into the mitf gene locus, designated as the eyeless frog. This line serves as a valuable model for studying retinal regeneration, pigmented cell nevi, and xanthophoromas, providing a robust platform for research in retinal regenerative medicine and tumor biology.
In X. tropicalis with a BRAFV600E knock-in, the mitf allele harboring the integration fails to express Mitf protein. Thus, in mitf-BRAFV600E± animals, only the wild-type mitf allele is active in melanocytes, xanthophores, and RPE cells. MITF is essential for the development of these cells and can be regulated by MAPK signaling, which modulates its nuclear translocation, stability, and transcriptional activity(23, 27, 48). Therefore, mitf-BRAFV600E± X. tropicalis provides a valuable model to study MAPK-Mitf interactions in pigment cell development. Furthermore, the mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf- genotype, derived from mitf−/− and mitf-BRAFV600E± lines, lacks melanocytes, retains xanthophores, and may exhibit potential iridophore abnormalities. The resulting pigment phenotypes offer insights into how Mitf-MAPK interactions regulate pigment cell fate. Given the importance of the MITF locus and MAPK pathway in human melanoma, this model system (mitf−/−, mitf-BRAFV600E±, mitf-BRAFV600E+/mitf-) offers unique insights into vertebrate pigment cell regulation and disease mechanisms.
In eyeless frogs, the endogenous mitf promoter was used to drive BRAFV600E expression, leading to phenotypes in RPE cells, melanocytes, and xanthophores—all of which express mitf and were affected by BRAFV600E expression. Given the conservation of the Mitf protein across vertebrates, every Mitf isoform in X. tropicalis likely drives BRAFV600E expression (27). Consequently, cells expressing Mitf, such as mast cells, osteoclasts, immune-related cells, RPE cells, xanthophores, and melanocytes, are likely to activate the MAPK signaling pathway in response to BRAFV600E expression, producing corresponding cellular phenotypes (23, 24, 27, 49, 50). However, in the eyeless frogs, we have only observed abnormal development in RPE cells, melanocytes, and xanthophores. This may be because other cell types expressing BRAFV600E have not yet been studied, or because BRAFV600E expression in these cells does not produce noticeable phenotypes. Given the complexity of the mitf locus in vertebrates, further research is needed to elucidate the expression of mitf isoforms and BRAFV600E in eyeless frogs (27, 51).
In mice, altered BRAFV600E expression levels can drive the malignant transformation of melanocytes into melanoma (24). Therefore, the absence of melanoma in mitf-BRAFV600E± X. tropicalis, despite the presence of nevi, may be due to insufficient BRAFV600E expression. Notably, a hallmark of human melanoma is the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes within the stroma and overlying epidermis (52). BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± frogs exhibit distinct features of immune-cold xanthophoromas and immune-hot nevi. This model thus offers a platform to investigate CD3+ T cell infiltration in pigment cell tumors and its implications for immunotherapy. Whether the exclusive development of xanthophoromas, rather than melanomas, in BRAFV600E±/cdkn2b± frogs is due to CD3+ T cells remains unclear, but future exploration of CD3+ T cell subtype heterogeneity may help clarify this mechanism.
In summary, eyeless frogs serve as a valuable vertebrate model for studying the effects of BRAFV600E/MAPK pathway activation on the development of RPE and pigment cells. Elucidating and visualizing the molecular mechanisms and cellular processes through which BRAFV600E/MAPK signaling alters cell fate will significantly advance research in retinal regenerative medicine and tumor biology. Conveniently, eyeless frogs exhibit a prominent phenotype in which RPE cells undergo transdifferentiation into the retina, as well as a notable phenotype where xanthophores and melanocytes transdifferentiate during nevus and tumor formation, providing a platform to address these issues.
