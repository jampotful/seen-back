RPE Abnormality Is a Potential Primary Cause for Retinal Degeneration in Mucopolysaccharidosis Type VI Patients and a Rat Model.

# RPE Abnormality Is a Potential Primary Cause for Retinal Degeneration in Mucopolysaccharidosis Type VI Patients and a Rat Model

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeMucopolysaccharidosis type VI (MPS VI) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by pathogenic variants in ARSB, leading to deficiency of N-acetylgalactosamine 4-sulfatase and accumulation of glycosaminoglycans. Although enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) alleviates systemic symptoms, its efficacy for ocular complications is limited. Because ocular manifestations may require distinct therapeutic approaches, a precise understanding of the underlying retinal pathology is essential. This study aimed to characterize ocular and retinal involvement in MPS VI through clinical and experimental analyses.MethodsComprehensive ophthalmic examinations were performed in siblings with MPS VI, and histological and electrophysiological assessments were conducted in an MPS VI rat model. Retinal morphology, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) integrity, and electroretinographic responses were evaluated.ResultsIn patients, no apparent photoreceptor degeneration was detected, although subtle functional impairment could not be excluded. Consistently, MPS VI rats exhibited preserved photoreceptor structures but reduced electroretinogram amplitudes. Although RPE abnormalities were not evident in patients, rats showed pronounced RPE alterations, suggesting RPE involvement as a potential origin of retinal dysfunction.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that retinal dysfunction in MPS VI may primarily arise from RPE pathology rather than photoreceptor loss. Detailed retinal evaluations in aging patients are warranted, and therapeutic approaches targeting the RPE, such as localized ERT or RPE cell transplantation may provide future benefits.Keywords: mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS), mucopolysaccharidosis type VI (MPS VI), retinal dystrophy, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)
Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a group of lysosomal storage disorders caused by genetic defects that result in the absence or reduced activity of enzymes responsible for the degradation of glycosaminoglycans.1–4 Consequently, undegraded mucopolysaccharides accumulate progressively within the body. Nine clinical subtypes of MPS have been described (types I–IX, with types V and VIII omitted), and the disease is currently not curable. Depending on the specific substrate accumulated, some subtypes predominantly affect the eyes, brain, bones, and joints, whereas others have more widespread systemic involvement, including the heart, spleen, and liver. Ultimately, nearly all organs and tissues are impaired, leading to progressive debilitation. Life expectancy is estimated at only 10 to 15 years.
In recent years, systemic enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has extended patient survival, and the number of adult patients with MPS is steadily increasing.3 Alongside classical and treatable ocular manifestations, such as corneal clouding and glaucoma, retinal dystrophy is now being observed more frequently.4 Unfortunately, systemic ERT has a controversial effect on ocular complications, possibly due to insufficient drug targeting. With the expected rise in the adult MPS population, management of ocular manifestations has become increasingly important for maintaining their quality of life (QOL). In particular, elucidating the mechanisms of retinal dystrophy and establishing therapeutic strategies for this currently untreatable complication are urgent priorities.
In this study, we first conducted a detailed ophthalmic evaluation of siblings with mucopolysaccharidosis type VI (MPS VI), an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by pathogenic variants in Arylsulfatase B (ARSB) which codes a lysosomal enzyme N-acetylgalactosamine 4-sulfatase, to deepen our understanding of ocular manifestations in patients. In parallel, comprehensive analyses of an MPS VI rat model were performed. By integrating clinical and experimental findings, we aim to elucidate the pathophysiology of retinal dystrophy in MPS and to explore potential therapeutic strategies.

## Methods

## Patients
Siblings with MPS VI, a 23-year-old brother who began ERT at 5.5 years old and a 17-year-old sister who started at 6 weeks old, underwent ophthalmic examination. Retinal images were obtained using spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Ultra-widefield color fundus photograph and autofluorescence (AF) images were obtained using Optomap (Optos Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA, and Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Electroretinogram (ERG) was recorded using RETeval (Mayo, Aichi, Japan, and LKC Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) or LE-4000 (Tomey Co., Aichi, Japan). Anterior segment of the eye was imaged using Specular microscope EM-4000 (Tomey, Japan) and anterior segment OCT (CASIA2, Tomey, Japan). This study used anonymized patient data from medical records, with patient confidentiality maintained in accordance with general hospital guidelines, and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Animals
MPS VI rats (WIAR.MPR-Arsbabd/Ncchd or Arbs−/−) were derived from embryos provided by the National BioResource Project (NBRP, Japan).5 Genotyping was performed in two steps. First, genomic DNA was amplified using the primers ArsbF (5′-GCGAAGAACCTGCAAAAGAG-3′) and ArsbR (5′-TGCACACACACACACACACA-3′). The resulting amplicon was then sequenced.

## Fundus Photography and SD-OCT
To monitor retinal changes, fundus photography and SD-OCT (ENVICE R2210, Leica, Germany) were performed in anesthetized animals using an intraperitoneal injection of a mixed anesthetic consisting of medetomidine hydrochloride (0.75 mg/kg body weight; Domitor, Zenoaq, Japan), midazolam (4 mg/kg body weight; Dormicum; Maruishi Pharmaceutical, Japan), and butorphanol tartrate (5 mg/kg body weight; Vetorphal; Meiji Animal Health, Japan).

## Corneal Evaluation
Corneal clarity was evaluated by slit-ramp examination.

## Electroretinogram
Rats were dark-adapted overnight before the recording sessions. These rats were anesthetized immediately prior to data acquisition as described above. The pupils were dilated using MydrinP (Santen Pharmaceutical, Japan). Corneal ERGs were acquired with contact lens electrodes (Mayo, Japan) while placing the reference electrode in the oral cavity and the ground electrode on the tail. Signals were sampled at 1250 hertz (Hz) using the PuREC system (Mayo, Japan). Full-field flashes were generated with a Hemisphere Ganzfeld dome (Mayo, Japan) and controlled by an LED stimulator (Mayo, Japan).
Dark adapted ERGs were recorded with flashes at 0.001, 0.01, 3, and 10 cd·s/m2. After 10 minutes of light adaptation to a 31.6 cd/m2 background, photopic responses were elicited with a 3 cd·s/m2 flash. Raw traces underwent noise reduction with the PuREC built-in algorithm, were low-pass filtered at 70 Hz, exported to Python 3, and subjected to subsequent amplitude and implicit time measurements.

## Retinal Histology
Retinal histological analyses were conducted using paraffin sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and epon-embedded eyes with toluidine blue staining. Ultrastructural analyses were performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hanaichi Ultrastructure Research Institute, Aichi, Japan). Rats were maintained at the RIKEN Animal Research Facility, and all experiments were conducted following the approved institutional animal research protocol.

## Results

## Clinical Observation
A 23-year-old brother (case 1) and a 17-year-old sister (case 2) had received ERT since the age of 5.5 years and 6 weeks, respectively. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.22 logMAR in the right eye and 0.10 logMAR in the left eye for case 1, and −0.18 logMAR in the right eye and 0.10 logMAR in the left eye for case 2. Mild corneal clouding was observed bilaterally in both cases and specular microscopy suggested corneal endothelial alternations (Fig. 1A). Goldmann perimetry revealed normal visual fields with V4e, but I4e demonstrated constriction in both cases, indicating reduced dark-adapted sensitivity (Fig. 1B). Fundus color photography and AF imaging revealed no obvious abnormalities in both cases, although image quality was limited by corneal clouding (Fig. 1C). SD-OCT showed preservation of photoreceptors but thinning of the inner retina, including the ganglion cell layer (GCL) and inner plexiform layer (IPL) with more obvious thinning in case 1 (Fig. 1D). ERG was performed using the RETeval system with skin electrodes for case 1, as contact lens electrodes were not applied due to ocular discomfort. ERG waveforms were preserved, although amplitudes appeared reduced (Fig. 1E). ERG was performed using the IL4000 system with contact lens electrodes for case 2, showing no rod or cone dysfunction.

Collectively, the siblings exhibited mild corneal clouding. Apparent retinal abnormalities were not detected, but subtle dysfunction could not be excluded, especially in the older brother with reduced ERG amplitudes.

## Animals
Arsb −/− rats exhibited relatively normal growth. Overgrowth of the front teeth was observed in a few animals. Compared with littermate controls, Arsb−/− rats were generally calm in temperament. No apparent abnormalities were observed in behavior, except for the lack of reproductive activity and reduced survival. Because of their absence of mating behavior, the colony was maintained either by embryo transfer or by mating between heterozygous Arsb−/+ rats.

## Historical Changes of the Retina in Arsb-Null Rats
Retinal structure was first evaluated using SD-OCT. Thickness of outer nuclear layer (ONL) and that of the inner part of the retina including the GCL, IPL, and inner nuclear layer (INL) were examined at 2, 3, 4, and 6 months of age. As shown in Figure 2A, although age-related changes were observed, no genotype-dependent differences were detected, indicating an absence of photoreceptor degeneration and inner retinal damages in Arsb-null animals by 6 months of age. Retinal histology demonstrated the presence of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) abnormalities with vacuole formation in Arsb−/− rats, whereas such vacuoles were not observed in Arsb+/+ rats at 10 months of age (Fig. 2B). Alcian blue (pH 1.0) staining, which specifically detects sulfated glycosaminoglycans, demonstrated clear accumulation within the RPE of Arsb−/− rats, consistent with the known buildup of sulfated glycosaminoglycans in MPS VI. No staining was detected in Arsb+/+ rats (Fig. 2C). Ultrastructural analyses revealed enlarged septated vacuoles bounded by a single membrane, suggesting abnormal accumulation of mucopolysaccharides within lysosomes of RPE cells (Fig. 3A). Enlarged vacuoles were also observed in ganglion cells of Arsb−/− rats (Fig. 3B). No obvious ultrastructural differences were observed in the IPL between Arsb−/− rats and controls (Fig. 3C). In addition, mitochondria in the photoreceptor inner segments appeared shortened in Arsb−/− rats compared with those in controls, suggesting possible mitochondrial stress (Fig. 3D).

## Functional Evaluation Using ERG
Retinal functions were evaluated by ERG (see the Table). Arsb−/− rats (n = 2 animals, 4 eyes) consistently showed smaller dark-adapted amplitudes than Arsb+/+ rats (n = 3 animals, 6 eyes) across stimulus intensities (Figs. 4A, 4B). The reduction was most pronounced for the a-wave at higher flash strengths, indicating a potential impairment of photoreceptor function in the Arsb-deficient retina.

Under photopic conditions, Arsb−/− rats (n = 2 animals, 4 eyes) likewise exhibited smaller responses than controls, with a clear decrease in b-wave amplitudes compared with Arsb+/+ rats (n = 3 animals, 6 eyes; Figs. 4C, 4D). Because the b-wave reflects ON bipolar cell activity, these data suggest that Arsb-deficiency also affects ON bipolar cell responses.
Collectively, Arsb−/− exhibited reduced ERG responses under both scotopic and photopic conditions, consistent with impaired photoreceptor and ON bipolar cell function. One limitation of this study is that, due to the systemic manifestations and limited survival of this rat model, the number of animals available for ERG analysis was restricted, precluding balanced sex representation. In addition, repeated ERG examinations were technically challenging. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as preliminary trends rather than statistically confirmed differences, and further studies with larger cohorts are required to draw definitive conclusions.

## Corneal Changes
Corneal clouding was observed in only 1 of 10 eyes (1 of 5 rats) at 6 months of age. Because corneal involvement caused by a genetic defect is usually bilateral, the unilateral finding in this case may have been incidental. Consistent with this observation, Alcian blue staining showed increased signals in the corneal stroma and endothelium of Arsb−/− rats, whereas baseline staining was detected in Arsb+/+ rats (Fig. 5). These findings suggest that, although sulfated glycosaminoglycan accumulation may occur in the cornea, the overall impact of Arsb-deficiency on the cornea appears to be limited in this model.
Detection of sulfated glycosaminoglycans in Arsb−/− rats. Alcian blue (pH 1.0) staining of sulfated glycosaminoglycans in Arsb−/− and Arsb+/+ cornea at 6 to 8 months of ages. Bar indicates 30 µm.
Collectively, Arsb−/− rats demonstrated RPE abnormalities without evidence of photoreceptor degeneration by 10 months of age. Despite preserved photoreceptor structure, reduced rod and cone function was observed. These findings suggest that in human MPS, retinal dysfunction may originate primarily from RPE abnormalities, which could subsequently lead to secondary photoreceptor degeneration.

## Discussion
Patients with MPS including MPS VI increasingly survive into adulthood owing to systemic ERT, yet progressive ocular complications such as retinal dystrophy threatens their quality of vision (QOV). Motivated by these clinical observations, we conducted comprehensive ophthalmic examinations in patients with MPS VI and performed parallel analyses in an MPS VI rat model. By integrating clinical and experimental data, we aimed to advance understanding of retinal pathology in MPS VI and to explore the potential of existing systemic therapies and approaches for preventing or treating retinal degeneration.
First, regarding the apparent discrepancy in ocular findings between patients and the rat model, corneal opacity is a well-recognized clinical feature in patients with MPS VI, whereas no obvious corneal opacity was observed in the Arsb−/− rat model in this study. However, histological analysis revealed increased accumulation of sulfated glycosaminoglycans in the rat cornea. These findings suggest that, despite the absence of overt clinical opacity, structural alterations may already be present in the rat cornea, potentially representing early pathological changes associated with glycosaminoglycan accumulation. In contrast, our rat analyses revealed clear abnormalities in the RPE. It is possible that similar microscopic alterations in the RPE may also occur in patients but remain undetectable with current clinical imaging modalities.
Previous reports have suggested ocular involvement in animal models of MPS VI. In 1986, histological studies of a naturally occurring MPS VI cat model indicated abnormalities in the RPE, such as vacuolated inclusions and cellular hypertrophy.6 More recently, an MPS VI mouse model was studied in 2022, with analyses focusing on corneal opacity. Although ERG abnormalities were recorded, detailed retinal analyses were not conducted.7 Because mucopolysaccharide accumulation occurs in virtually all cell types, to deepen our understanding of retinal dystrophy complication, we specifically targeted both photoreceptors and RPE cells in our analyses. Our findings suggest that RPE abnormalities precede photoreceptor degeneration as reported in MPS VII mice.8 Similar observation was made in MPS VI cat model, and RPE-specific gene therapy was tested for the rescue.9,10
Although ERT has improved life expectancy in patients with MPS, the impact of retinal degeneration on QOL and QOV is emerging as an important clinical concern. Our interactions with severely affected patients with MPS VI who have remained in good systemic health following early initiation of ERT, but who face the risk of ocular complications and visual decline, underscored the urgent need to clarify disease mechanisms and establish effective treatments for MPS-associated retinal disease.
In contrast to other lysosomal storage disorders, such as GM2 gangliosidosis (Tay–Sachs disease), where neuronal cell death has been relatively well studied,11–16 research into the retinal mechanisms of MPS remains sparse. To date, only one study has analyzed the mechanism of accumulated MPS to the retina in an MPS III mouse model, reporting that heparan sulfate accumulation induces autophagy defects in photoreceptors, thereby driving retinal degeneration.17 In addition, ultrastructural analysis in the present study revealed shortened mitochondria in photoreceptor inner segments of Arsb−/− rats, suggesting mitochondrial stress. Lysosomal dysfunction in MPS has been reported to disrupt autophagy pathways in patients with MPS VI as well as in animal models of MPS III.17–19 Such impairment of the lysosome–autophagy axis may lead to accumulation of damaged organelles and secondary metabolic stress. Therefore, the mitochondrial alterations observed in photoreceptors may reflect downstream consequences of lysosomal dysfunction associated with ARSB deficiency. Beyond this, very little is known regarding retinal pathophysiology in MPS.
The early dysregulation observed in the RPE of Arsb−/− rats may reflect the unique metabolic and functional demands. The RPE plays a critical role in maintaining photoreceptor homeostasis through the daily phagocytosis and degradation of photoreceptor outer segments, a process that relies heavily on efficient lysosomal function. Because of this high lysosomal burden, RPE cells may be particularly vulnerable to defects in lysosomal enzymes. Although MPS share a common underlying mechanism involving lysosomal enzyme deficiency and glycosaminoglycan accumulation, different MPS subtypes often exhibit distinct clinical and tissue phenotypes. This variability likely reflects differences in the specific enzyme involved, the types of glycosaminoglycans that accumulate, and the metabolic requirements of different cell types.
Meanwhile, the development of systemic therapies for MPS has advanced considerably, with ERT approved for MPS I, II, IV, VI, and VII, and clinical trials ongoing for MPS III.20 Gene therapy approaches are also under development for nearly all subtypes except MPS IX. However, systemic treatments have limited efficacy in ocular disease due to the blood–retina barrier, underscoring the need for eye-specific treatment strategies. To our knowledge, no studies have systematically investigated the use of these systemic therapies for ocular manifestations of MPS.
Several groups worldwide, including ours, have been developing regenerative approaches using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived RPE cells to address RPE pathology, and clinical trials and researches on stem cell-based RPE transplantation for RPE dysfunction have been currently underway.21–25 The present findings, which implicate RPE abnormalities as a primary driver of retinal degeneration in MPS VI, support the rationale for considering regenerative approaches, such as RPE transplantation as a potential therapeutic option for these patients along with RPE-specific gene therapies. Such strategies could expand the therapeutic repertoire and ultimately provide meaningful benefits to individuals affected by MPS-related retinal disease.
