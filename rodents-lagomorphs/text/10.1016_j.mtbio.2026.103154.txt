Injectable alginate hydrogels improve the effects of subretinal hiPSC-derived RPE cell therapy on retinal degeneration in rats.
Tian Z, Guo M, Kang X, Jiang C, Liu X, Yang M, Gao M, Wang T, Higuchi A. (2026). Materials today. Bio. . doi:10.1016/j.mtbio.2026.103154. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Injectable alginate hydrogels improve the effects of subretinal hiPSC-derived RPE cell therapy on retinal degeneration in rats

## Abstract
Subretinal delivery of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells is an important treatment for retinal degeneration patients, and the cell suspension method is the most common approach. Injectable hydrogel-mediated cell engraftment is expected to potentially improve cell transplantation outcomes. Here, alginate hydrogels were used to deliver human-induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-differentiated RPE cells or an RPE cell line (ARPE-19 cells) into the subretinal sites of Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rats with retinal degeneration. RPE cell-grafted sites were monitored operating a retinal imaging instrument, and the visual functions of RCS rats were assessed via optomotor response (OMR) and electroretinogram (ERG), and retinal structure was analyzed histologically. The OMR results revealed similar spatial vision recovery among the cell transplantation groups injected with and without hydrogels. However, the ERG results revealed superior light perception in RCS rats treated with hydrogel-mediated RPE cell transplantation, particularly compared to hiPSC-derived RPE cell transplantation. This is because OMR evaluates movement of RCS rat eyes, which is difficult to evaluate visual acuity precisely. Optical coherence tomography at four and eight weeks post-transplantation confirmed long-term RPE cell retention and a thickened outer nuclear layer at injection sites after RPE cell engraftment with injectable hydrogels but not without injectable hydrogels; this was supported by the histological results showing retinal outer nuclear layer preservation for 8 weeks. These findings indicate that compared with RPE cell suspensions, alginate hydrogels enhance RPE cell transplantation outcomes and that the structural and functional rescue effects of hiPSC-derived RPE cells with injectable hydrogels are valuable. Hydrogel-mediated delivery represents a promising strategy for improving subretinal cell transplantation efficacy.

## Introduction
Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a genetically heterogeneous disease that causes blindness and is known as progressive photoreceptor degeneration and dysfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Currently employed therapies—including gene therapy, pharmacological intervention, and retinal prostheses—are limited by mutation specificity [5], short-term efficacy [6], or low resolution and device dependency, highlighting the urgent need for strategies that restore RPE function and retinal homeostasis [7]. Subretinal delivery of functional RPE cells can theoretically restore photoreceptor support and slow degeneration [[8], [9], [10]]. Human induced pluripotent stem cell-differentiated RPE (hiPSC-RPE) cells represent an ideal cell source that expressing markers such as Bestrophin-1 and RPE65 [11,12]. However, conventional suspension transplantation of RPE cells faces major challenges: mechanical shear reduces cell survival [13], imprecise localization of RPE cells diminishes the functional integration of RPE cells [13,14], and immune-mediated rejection further limits the efficacy of RPE cell transplantation [[14], [15], [16]].
To address these limitations, thermosensitive hydrogels composed of hyaluronic acid (HA) and methylcellulose (MC) have been developed [17]. These hydrogels support the survival and proliferation of retinal stem cells (RSCs) or retinal progenitor cells (RPCs) but not RPE cells in vitro, and upon subretinal injection in mice, the hydrogels promote uniform cell distribution, thereby facilitating host–donor integration [17]. Liu et al. applied a similar approach by delivering RPCs via thermosensitive cross-linked HA hydrogels, achieving a uniform cell distribution without evidence of damage to the host retina [18]. In addition, some researchers have proposed oxidized glutathione as a crosslinker for HA-based hydrogels to improve gelation kinetics [19]. This method enabled the crosslinking of thiolated HA within 5 min, supported adipose-derived stem cell proliferation, and demonstrated good biocompatibility in preliminary in vivo experiments but not in a study of RPE cells transplanted with injectable hydrogels [19]. Hertz et al. further developed a hydrogel library based on PEG and poly(L-lysine) [20]. The synthesized hydrogels exhibited diverse chemical and mechanical properties, supporting the survival, migration, and neurite extension of retinal ganglion and amacrine cells in vitro but not for the study of RPE cells with injectable hydrogels. Collectively, these findings highlight the considerable progress made in the hydrogel-mediated delivery of cells to degenerating retinas. While hydrogels could enhance the differentiation, survival, and integration of transplanted cells [21,22], additional in vivo studies of engrafted cells, especially RPE cells, are necessary to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of the transplantation of stem cell-derived cells.
The mucoadhesive properties of alginate extended its residence time on the ocular surface and enhanced drug bioavailability. Alginate polymeric chains swell and interact noncovalently with mucins [23,24]. Moreover, alginate exhibits pH-responsive gelation behavior, making it a suitable carrier for controlled drug release. In the presence of divalent cations, most commonly calcium, alginate forms hydrogels with excellent biocompatibility, nontoxicity, and water-retentive capacity, making it an ideal candidate for ocular sustained-release systems [24,25]. While hydrogels have been broadly explored for retinal cell delivery, this study provides a critical technical advancement by developing a precisely optimized injectable CaCO3-glucono-δ-lactone (GDL)-alginate system that offers spatiotemporal control over gelation kinetics, ensuring a sufficient injection window followed by rapid in situ stabilization specifically tailored for hiPSC-differentiated RPE cells. We employed a retinal degeneration animal model established with Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rats, in which Mertk mutation causes defective RPE cell phagocytosis, faithfully recapitulating human RP pathology [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. An alginate-based hydrogel is suitable for minimally invasive subretinal injection, and rapid in situ gelation was optimized (Fig. 1) [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. In vitro, the effects of the hydrogels on ARPE-19 cell viability, proliferation, and functional maturation were also assessed in this study. In vivo, 3-week-old RCS rats were assigned to five groups of RPE cell transplantation: the (i) hiPSC-derived RPE (hiPSC-RPE) cell transplantation with alginate hydrogels, (ii) ARPE-19 cell transplantation with alginate hydrogels, (iii) hiPSC-derived RPE (hiPSC-RPE) cell transplantation in cell suspension, (iv) ARPE-19 cell transplantation in cell suspension, and (v) untreated control groups. ## Materials and methods

## Cell lines
The human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) line HPS0077 was obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Research Center (RIKEN BRC, Tsukuba, Japan). hiPSCs were proliferated on Matrigel-coated culture plates (356230; Corning, USA) in mTeSR1 media (85850; STEMCELL Technologies, Canada). The medium was changed daily, and the cells were passaged every 5–7 days using Accutase (A6964; Innovative Cell Technologies, USA) following the manufacturer's guidance. The human retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19 was purchased from iCell Bioscience, Inc. (Shanghai, China). ARPE-19 cells were proliferated in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (11320033, DMEM/F-12, Gibco, USA) with supplementation of 10% fetal bovine serum (A5256701, FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, USA). Subculture was proceeded at 75–85% confluence utilizing 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (25200056; Gibco, USA). All the cell cultivation was proceeded at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The other chemicals, proteins or biomaterials utilized in the present study are tabulated in Supplementary Table S1.

## In vitro and in vivo experimental design
We employed a retinal degeneration animal model established with Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rats, in which Mertk mutation causes defective RPE cell phagocytosis, faithfully recapitulating human RP pathology [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. An alginate-based hydrogel is suitable for minimally invasive subretinal injection, and rapid in situ gelation was optimized (Fig. 1) [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. In vitro, the effects of the hydrogels on hiPSC-derived RPE (hiPSC-RPE) and ARPE-19 cell viability, proliferation, and functional maturation were assessed in this study. In vivo, 3-week-old RCS rats were assigned to five groups of RPE cell transplantation: the (i) hiPSC-RPE cell transplantation with alginate hydrogels, (ii) ARPE-19 cell transplantation with alginate hydrogels, (iii) hiPSC-RPE cell transplantation in cell suspension, (iv) ARPE-19 cell transplantation in cell suspension, and (v) untreated control groups. Cell-hydrogel composites or cell suspensions (2 μL, 5 × 104 cells/μL) were injected into RCS rats subretinally, with fundus imaging confirming graft placement. We evaluated multimodal studies, including (a) electroretinography (ERG) for photoreceptor and bipolar cell responses, (b) optomotor response (OMR) for visual acuity, (c) optical coherence tomography (OCT) for retinal layer integrity, and (d) histological studies of retinal sections with immunofluorescence to assess the survival and integration of grafted RPE cells.

## Induction into RPE cells from hiPSCs
hiPSC (HPS0077) differentiation into RPE cells was performed using a modified NIC84 protocol [35,36]. HPS0077 cells were seeded at 20,000 cells/cm2 on Matrigel-coated surface and cultured in mTeSR1 medium until the cells reached confluence (∼5 days). Differentiation of hiPSCs was initiated on day 0 by hiPSC culture in differentiation medium (DM; 85% DMEM/F-12, 15% KnockOut™ Serum Replacement, 2 mM GlutaMAX™, 0.1 mM Non-Essential Amino Acids (NEAA), and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic). On day 2, the DM was included with 10 mM nicotinamide (NIC) as well as 10 nM chetomin (CTM). The cells were subsequently cultivated in DM containing 10 mM NIC and 30 nM CTM from days 3 to 14, after which the medium was replaced daily. From days 14 to 28, the cells were maintained in DM including only 10 mM NIC, and the medium was changed daily. On day 28, the cells were passaged using Accutase, centrifuged, and reseeded at 2.5–3.0 × 105 cells/cm2 in a 1:1 mixture of DM and RPE maturation medium (RPEMN; 70% DMEM, 30% F-12, 2% B27, and 1% L-glutamine). Cells were subsequently cultivated in RPEMN with daily medium changes until day 56, when the cells were passaged again, and the cells (RPE cells) were maintained in RPEMN until day 84.

## Preparation of alginate hydrogels
Sodium alginate was dissolved in deionized water to obtain a stock solution (1% w/v). Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) and glucono-δ-lactone (GDL) were used as the calcium ion sources to trigger the gelation of alginate. The molar ratio of CaCO3 to GDL was maintained at 0.5 to ensure gradual Ca2+ release under near-neutral pH conditions [37]. The molar ratio of Ca2+ to alginate carboxyl groups was determined to be 0.18 at the initial stage (1 × concentration). Afterward, the crosslinking density was further adjusted to 0.75 × , 0.5 × , or 0.25 × the molar ratio of Ca2+ to the alginate carboxyl groups. During the hydrogel formation process, sodium alginate solution was slowly inserted into the CaCO3 solution and vortexed for 1 min. The newly prepared GDL solution was then added to the alginate-CaCO3 solution, and the mixed solution was vortexed for another 1 min to initiate gelation. The gelation time was determined by tilting small vials every 15 s until the mixture no longer flowed.

## Microstructural characterization
For microstructural analysis, hydrogel samples were frozen at −80 °C, freeze-dried, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, sputter-coated with gold, and took images operating scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The pore size distribution of the hydrogels was studied operating ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/).

## Rheological characterization
The rheological properties of 1 × alginate hydrogels were evaluated utilizing a Discovery Hybrid Rheometer (DHR, TA Instruments, USA) fitted with 20 mm-diameter parallel plates at 37 °C. To maintain hydration, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was supplemented around the hydrogel samples. Oscillatory frequency sweep tests were proceeded over an angular frequency range of 0.1–150 rad/s under a fixed strain of 1%. Strain sweep tests were studied at an angular frequency of 10 rad/s with strains ranging from 0.05% to 500%. Time sweep tests were carried out in controlled-strain mode (0.5 mm gap, 10 rad/s frequency, 1% strain) to induce hydrogel disruption and assess network stability. Steady shear tests were implemented over a shear rate range of 0.001–1000 s−1 to characterize the shear-thinning behavior of the hydrogels.

## Physicochemical characterization
To assess the environmental stability of the hydrogels, extract solutions were obtained according to ISO 10993 extraction principles. Briefly, hydrogels (0.2 g) were immersed in complete cultivation media (2 mL) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, extract solutions were collected, and pH values were measured using a calibrated digital pH meter. All measurements were performed in triplicate.
The swelling behavior of the 1 × alginate hydrogels was evaluated using a gravimetric method. Freeze-dried hydrogels were weighed to know the initial dry weight (Wd) and immersed in complete cultivation media at 37 °C. At designated time points, hydrogels were taken out, gently blotted to eliminate excess surface liquid, and weighed immediately to obtain the swollen weight (Ws). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The swelling ratio (SR) was derived from the equation of SR(%)=(Ws−Wd)/Wd×100.
The in vitro degradation behavior of the hydrogels was studied by monitoring mass loss over time. Hydrogels were immersed in complete culture medium at 37 °C under static conditions. At predetermined time points, samples were collected, rinsed with deionized water to taken off residual salts, freeze-dried, and weighed to obtain the remaining dry weight (Wt), where W0 represents the initial dry weight prior to incubation. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The percentage of remaining mass was evaluated from the equation of MassRemaining(%)=Wt/W0×100.

## In vitro biocompatibility evaluation
The cell toxicity of the hydrogel extracts was evaluated by incubating the alginate hydrogels in culture medium (0.1 g/mL) for 24 h at 37 °C, followed by sterilization of the extracted solution through a 0.22 μm filter. hiPSC-derived RPE (hiPSC-RPE) and ARPE-19 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Then, the medium was exchanged with 100 μL of hydrogel extract (experimental group) or fresh medium (control group), with triplicate wells per condition. Cell viability was assessed on days 1, 3, and 5 using calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) staining (live/dead cell viability assay). After the cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were immersed with calcein-AM and PI staining solution at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired operating an inverted fluorescence microscope, and three random fields per well were recorded. Then, the cells were washed once with PBS, and PBS was added for fluorescence measurements using a microplate reader (calcein-AM: Ex/Em = 485/520 nm; PI: Ex/Em = 535/620 nm). The cell viability (%) was calculated as follows:Cellviability(%)=(CellNo.expressingCalcein−AM÷(CellNo.expressingCalcein−AM+CellNo.expressingPI))×100.
To evaluate the biocompatibility of the hydrogels, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, A311-02-AA, Vazyme, China) assay was proceeded to determine the viability of ARPE-19 cells in a 3D culture environment. Briefly, ARPE-19 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and resuspended in complete media (DMEM/F12 including 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin). The cell density was controlled to 1 × 105 cells/mL. The alginate hydrogel precursor solution was gently mixed with the cell suspension at a 1:1 vol ratio. The mixture was then transferred into multi-well culture plates and proceeded to incubate at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 30 min to allow for complete gelation. Following gelation, fresh complete media were added to each well, and the cells were cultivated for 7 days with medium exchange one time during this period. After 7 days of 3D encapsulation, CCK-8 solution (10% of the total volume) was added to each well and located in the dark at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was obtained measuring a microplate reader (NEO2SM, BTNEO2SM, BioTek Instruments, USA). The relative cell viability was obtained calculating the following formulation:CellViability(%)=(ODexpeᵣᵢmental–ODblank)/(ODcontᵣol–ODblank)×100%

## Immunofluorescence staining assay
Cells were seeded on glass-bottom plate surface and cultured for 2–3 days. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for half hour, proceeded permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 for quarter hour, and blocked with Blocking buffer (P0102; Beyotime) for 30 min at 20-25 °C. Primary antibodies were inserted into PBS including 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, followed by secondary antibodies for 2 h. The nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5–10 min. After the cells were washed with PBS, the cells were imaged using a confocal laser microscope (LSM900 with Airyscan, Zeiss, Germany). The antibodies used in this study and their dilution rates are tabulated in Supplementary Table S2.

## Flow cytometry assay
The cells were harvested, washed with PBS containing 2% FBS, fixed with 1% PFA for 20 min, and permeabilized with 90% methanol at 4 °C for quarter hour. The cells were proceeded incubation with primary antibodies against Nanog, SOX2, SSEA-4, Oct4, MITF, RPE, PAX6, ZO-1 or isotype antibody controls for 1 h, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 35 min, after which the cells were washed in PBS including 2% FBS after each step. The cell samples were filtered and studied operating a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Life Sciences, OR, USA). The antibodies used in the flow cytometry analysis and their dilution rates are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

## CellTracker labeling
CellTracker Green dye was solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to make a 10 mM stock solution. The CellTracker Green dye solution was diluted in serum-free media to the working concentration (0.5–25 μM) and equilibrated at 37 °C. The RPE cells were obtained by centrifugation, resuspended in CellTracker Green dye working solution, and incubated for 35 min. The labeled cells were washed and resuspended in 1% sodium alginate or PBS for subsequent use.

## Subretinal injection of RPE cells into RCS rats
Male and female RCS rat littermates at postnatal day 21 (P21) were randomly assigned to experimental groups (approximately 1:1 of male:female ratio). The number of rats (n) used on each experiment is 4 rats (OMR, OCT, immunofluorescence staining assay and histological assay at 4 weeks), 5 rats (histological assay at 8 weeks) or 6 rats (ERG assay). Animals were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg), and the pupils were proceeded dilation with 0.5% tropicamide. The RCS rats were positioned laterally with the eye receiving the injection facing upward, and the cornea was protected with ophthalmic ointment. A 33G needle was used to puncture the sclera 1–2 mm posterior to the limbus. A blunt 33G microinjection needle was inserted into the subretinal space at a 30°–45° angle to avoid penetration through blood vessels. Two microliters of cell suspension with and without alginate hydrogels was injected at a constant rate. To ensure a precise final volume of 2 μL per injection, the cell pellet volume was accounted for during the formulation process. Cells were resuspended in sodium alginate solution or PBS at a specific volume to make sure that the final alginate solution with CaCO3 and GDL becomes 2 μL with the targeted cell density and cross-linking ratio. The needle was held in place for 10–15 s and then slowly withdrawn. Successful injections were confirmed by fundus images showing localized retinal blebs without leakage. Postoperatively, the cornea was covered with ointment, and the RCS rats were kept on a 37 °C heating pad while their recovery was monitored. Subsequently, the optomotor response (OMR) was evaluated on all RCS rats at 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks post-transplantation. Then, rats were separated into 4 weeks or 8 weeks post-transplantation groups. The fundus imaging, optical coherence tomography (OCT), electroretinography (ERG), and H&E staining were performed on each RCS rats at 4 or 8 weeks for 4 or 8 weeks post-transplantation groups, respectively.

## Fundus imaging acquisition
Postoperative fundus images were acquired operating an IISCIENCE (EYEMERA, Yangsan, Korea) instrument of a retinal imaging microscope. RCS rats were placed prone on an adjustable platform, and the imaging lens was positioned to center the injection site. Bright-field and green fluorescence images were captured to confirm the location of the transplanted RPE cells.

## Optomotor response (OMR) evaluation
Visual functional improvement in RCS rats after subretinal engraftment of RPE cells with and without alginate hydrogels was assessed 1–4 weeks after cell transplantation using a virtual optomotor response (qOMR) system (PhenoSys, Berlin, Germany). RCS rats were put on a central elevated platform within a four-screen enclosure. The movement of black-and-white gratings was presented with 12 spatial frequencies (0.05–0.5 cycles/degree). Head and body movements were tracked by a camera, and OMR scores were calculated as the ratio of consistent responses to inconsistent responses. Scores above 1.0 indicated that the rats could perceive the stimulus, whereas scores below 1.0 indicated the inability of the rats to perceive the stimulus. Visual acuity thresholds were determined by fitting response curves, and the differences between groups were studied by two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA).

## Slit-lamp examination
Corneal morphology of RCS rats was assessed at 4 weeks post-transplantation using a slit-lamp microscope (SLM-8E; Chongqing, China). Parameters such as corneal transparency, edema, neovascularization, and epithelial integrity were recorded and compared among the data measured for eyes transplanted with RPE cells with and without alginate hydrogels and control (untreated) eyes.

## Optical coherence tomography (OCT) evaluation
RCS rats at 4 and 8 week post-transplantation of RPE cells with and without alginate hydrogels were anesthetized (1% pentobarbital sodium, 40 mg/kg), and furthermore the pupils were proceeded dilation with 0.5% tropicamide. RCS rats were positioned prone on the OCT platform (Heidelberg Engineering, Franklin, USA) with corneal protection using ophthalmic ointment. High-resolution radial scans centered on the optic disc were acquired to observe the outer nuclear layer (ONL), photoreceptor inner/outer segments (IS/OS), and RPE morphology at the injection site.

## Electroretinography (ERG) evaluation
RCS rats were dark-adapted for more than 6 h before ERG measurement was performed with an electrophysiology system (RETI-Port21, Roland, Germany). Under anesthesia and pupil dilation, as described in the previous section, corneal gold-ring electrodes and subcutaneous needle electrodes were positioned to record retinal responses on RCS rat eyes, which were transplanted with RPE cells with and without alginate hydrogels. Scotopic responses were elicited at 0.01, 3.0, or 10.0 cd s/m2 for rod-dominated and mixed responses. Signals were amplified with baseline calibration prior to recording. Raw ERG traces were imported into MATLAB (2024a; MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) as 512 × 1 arrays. Data were smoothed using the “loess” method with a span of 0.1. Smoothed traces were plotted with a y-axis range of −100 to 100 μV, divided into four 50 μV intervals. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the mean trace was overlaid for visualization.

## Preparation of paraffin-embedded retinal sections and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining
Eyes from RCS rats at four and eight weeks after the engraftment of RPE cells with and without alginate hydrogels were fixed in Davidson's fixative overnight at 4 °C. The cornea, iris, and lens were taken off, and subsequently, the tissue samples were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and made sections into 5 μm-thick sections. The tissue section samples were proceeded staining with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and digitization performing a 3D slide scanner (Pannoramic SCAN II, 3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) at 20 × /40 × magnification. Images were visualized and analyzed using CaseViewer software (v2.4, 3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). ONL cell counts were measured at 200 μm intervals from the optic nerve head for 14 positions, and the values were averaged.

## Immunofluorescence evaluation of retinal cryosections
Eyes from RCS rats taken at four weeks and eight weeks post-transplantation were proceeded fixation in 4% PFA, performed cryoprotection in 30% sucrose, embedded in frozen section medium (Epredia™ Neg-50™), and finally frozen at −79-81 °C. Sections (20 μm) were proceeded permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100, blocking, incubation with primary (anti-human nuclear antigen (HNA) and anti-RPE65) and secondary antibodies, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted with antifade medium. Confocal images of the samples were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 900 (20 × objective and Z-stack scanning (1 μm steps, 17–20 μm)) confocal laser microscope.

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism 10.6.1.892 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) was utilized for data study and mapping. The data are displayed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-way ANOVA with post hoc tests was performed for group comparisons. p < 0.05 was considered to allow statistical significance.

## Results

## Characterization of hiPSCs and hiPSC-RPE cells in vitro
hiPSCs can induce into cells differentiated from all three germ layer lineages [38]. We evaluated the expression of pluripotency markers in hiPSCs (HPS0077) using immunofluorescence and flow cytometry, and the results are displayed in Fig. S1A and S1B, respectively. hiPSCs strongly positively expressed OCT4, SOX2, SSEA-4, and NANOG according to the immunostaining images (Fig. S1A). Flow cytometry analysis further revealed that the percentage of cells positive for each of these pluripotent markers exceeded 90% (Fig. S1B), confirming that the hiPSCs used in this study maintained a stable pluripotent state and were suitable for subsequent differentiation into RPE cells.
Differentiation of the hiPSCs into RPE cells was performed according to the previously published NIC84 protocol using the hiPSCs (HPS0077 line) in this study (Fig. S1C) [35]. By day 84, differentiated cells exhibited typical RPE morphology, including polygonal cobblestone-like organization (Fig. S1D), visible pigmentation within the culture dish (Fig. S1E(i)), and dark cellular pellets following centrifugation (Fig. S1E(ii)). Confocal microscopy of the hiPSC-RPE cross-section (Fig. S1F(i)) distinctly resolved the apical and basal surfaces of the monolayer (Fig. S1F(ii)). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the differentiated cells expressed the RPE-specific markers RPE65, MITF, PAX6, and ZO-1 (Fig. S2A). Flow cytometry analysis of hiPSC-RPE cells also confirmed robust expression of RPE-specific markers, including RPE65, MITF, PAX6, and ZO-1 (Fig. S2B).

## Characterization of alginate hydrogels
Alginate hydrogels with varying crosslinking densities were successfully fabricated by modulating the concentrations of calcium ions and GDL, and their gelation behavior, morphology, and injectability were systematically characterized in this study; the results are shown in Fig. 2A–G. Inverted vial tests revealed that the 1 × hydrogels formed a uniform and transparent gel with a smooth surface, whereas the 0.25 × hydrogels failed to maintain structural integrity (Fig. 2A–C). Injection assessment revealed that the 1 × hydrogels exhibited higher resistance during extrusion because of their dense cross-linked network. However, the 1 × hydrogels could still be delivered into the tissue through a syringe under moderate pressure (Fig. 2D). After gelation experiments of alginate solution using 30G needle (diameter of needle is 0.31 mm), we found that alginate hydrogels can be injectable using smaller size diameter (33G, diameter of needle is 0.20 mm) of needle, which is more suitable to transplant RPE cells with and without alginate hydrogels into RCS rats subretinally. Therefore, we performed transplantation of hiPSC-derived RPE cells and the ARPE-19 cells into RCS rats with and without alginate hydrogels using 33G needle.
Gelation kinetics further confirmed the Ca concentration-dependent effect on the structural integrity and gelation time of the hydrogels: the gelation time of the 1 × hydrogels (Ca2+/carboxyl molar ratio = 0.18) was 216.7 ± 9.8 s, whereas that of the 0.25 × hydrogels (Ca2+/carboxyl molar ratio = 0.045) was prolonged to 1287 ± 92 s (Fig. 2E).
The microstructural features of the freeze-dried 1 × alginate hydrogels, which were prepared with a Ca2+/carboxyl molar ratio of 0.18, were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The freeze-dried 1 × alginate hydrogel displayed a typical interconnected three-dimensional porous network with an irregular polygonal pore morphology (Fig. 2F). Quantitative image analysis of the pore size using ImageJ revealed that the average pore diameter was 215 ± 81.1 μm (Fig. 2F), suggesting a relatively uniform pore size distribution, which contributes to excellent nutrient and metabolite exchange.
Rheological characterization using a rheometer confirmed that the 1 × alginate hydrogels exhibit robust solid-like viscoelastic behavior, accompanied by excellent shear-thinning and self-healing capacities as displayed in Fig. 2G. The oscillatory frequency sweep tests revealed that the storage modulus (G′) remained consistently higher than the loss modulus (G″) across the entire tested frequency range, evidencing the formation of a stable elastic network (Fig. 2G (i)). Strain sweep tests demonstrated that the hydrogels maintained linear viscoelastic behavior at strains below 10% (Fig. 2G (ii)). Moreover, when the hydrogel network was disrupted under a 500% strain amplitude, it achieved self-healing with an efficiency showing over 90% within 60 s, as reflected by the rapid recovery of the modulus (Fig. 2G (iii)). Steady shear tests further verified that the hydrogels displayed significant shear-thinning characteristics (Fig. 2G (iv)), confirming its ideal injectability for cell delivery.
Furthermore, pH of the extract from 1 × alginate hydrogels remained within a narrow physiological range (6.7–6.8) throughout the observation period (Fig. S3A). The absence of detectable acidification or alkalization indicates that the ionically crosslinked alginate network does not significantly alter local medium homeostasis. The hydrogels also exhibited time-dependent water swelling behavior in the culture medium (Fig. S3B). Water uptake increased during the initial phase and reached equilibrium at approximately 18 h, with a maximum swelling ratio of less than 220%. Finally, the 1 × alginate hydrogels showed progressive degradation over time, with a mass loss of over 40% observed after 14 days of incubation (Fig. S3C). This gradual degradation profile indicates that the hydrogels can provide temporal mechanical support for the grafted cells while allowing for progressive degradation of the hydrogels. However, the grafted cells can adhere on the retinal tissue site where the cells are transplanted with alginate hydrogels before the most of alginate hydrogels are going to degrade.
Mechanistically, gelation was initiated by the reaction between CaCO3 with GDL and alginate; the hydrolysis of GDL released gluconic acid, lowering the pH, thereby promoting the gradual release of Ca2+ ions from CaCO3 and subsequent ionic crosslinking with the carboxyl groups in the alginate chains to form a stable three-dimensional network of alginate [37]. Collectively, the 1 × hydrogels achieved an optimal balance between gelation time, structural uniformity, and injectability, making the hydrogels the most suitable formulation for further transplantation applications in this study.

## Assessment of the visual behavior of RCS rats using qOMR
To evaluate the effects of subretinal transplantation of RPE cells on the visual function of RCS rats, we employed a quantitative optomotor response (qOMR) system equipped with four synchronized display screens on the walls of a behavioral chamber (Fig. S7). The fitted response curves at 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. 4C a(i)–(iv)) demonstrated the optomotor response outcomes in RCS rats that received either hydrogel-mediated RPE cells or an RPE cell suspension. The differences between the RPE cell-transplanted group and the control group were slightly significant in some cases at weeks 2 and 3 (Fig. 4C b(ii)–(iv)) when they were compared among age-matched rats.

## Discussion
Hydrogels, as multifunctional biomaterials, have been widely applied in the treatment of ocular diseases, primarily as drug carriers, cell delivery vehicles, and vitreous substitutes [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43]]. Our data demonstrate that the hydrogel-mediated transplantation of hiPSC-derived RPE cells and ARPE-19 cells provides structural and functional benefits in a retinal degeneration RCS rat model. Unlike GelMA/chitosan microspheres [44], our hydrogels lack chemical crosslinkers and degrade into natural saccharide fragments, offering superior biocompatibility. However, the 4-week degradation profile of alginate hydrogels may still require adjustment to align with retinal repair kinetics.
The outcomes of visual function differed across cell sources in this study. At 8 weeks post-transplantation, the b-wave amplitude in the hiPSC-RPE cell group with and without alginate hydrogels was 42% greater than that in the ARPE-19 cell group with and without alginate hydrogels (p < 0.01). hiPSC-RPE cells expressed ZO-1 and RPE65 and formed tight junctions, whereas ARPE-19 cells displayed weaker barrier (no tight junction) and phagocytic properties. Hydrogel-mediated hiPSC-RPE cell delivery also resulted in better preservation of ONL cell counts (4.0 ONL cell layers) than did the suspension transplantation of hiPSC-RPE cells (3.4 ONL cell layers) at 8 weeks post-transplantation (p < 0.001).
The benefits of usage of injectable alginate hydrogels for the engraftment of RPE cells are likely derived from the reduced dispersion (migration) of transplanted hiPSC-RPE cells, maintenance of local trophic factor gradients (e.g., PEDF/VEGF balance), and protection from microglial infiltration. Similar to the findings of Mitrousi et al. [45], in which they reported that cotransplantation of RPE cells and photoreceptors within hyaluronic acid-based hydrogels restored vision function in degenerative mouse models, they reported that single-cell transplantation was insufficient to maintain vision function in mice [45].
This study provides a direct comparison between hiPSC-RPE cells and ARPE-19 cells delivered via alginate hydrogel carriers and cell suspensions. hiPSC-RPE cell transplantation demonstrated promising efficacy but was limited by high production costs (>$5000/dose) and long preparation times (>12 weeks). In contrast, ARPE-19 cells are easier to standardize and may be suitable for broader applications in animal disease models. These findings support a “tiered treatment strategy”, as suggested by Kashani et al. [14], where hiPSC-RPE cells are reserved for early-stage precision therapy and ARPE-19 cells for advanced disease and require generalized support. Moreover, hydrogel carriers may offer opportunities for multifunctionalization, such as the incorporation of anti-inflammatory agents (e.g., dexamethasone) or neurotrophic factors (e.g., BDNF), which could prolong therapeutic benefits.
Sodium alginate is widely recognized for its excellent cytocompatibility [46], which is corroborated by the robust viability of ARPE19 cells observed during our 7-day 3D encapsulation (Fig. S3F–G). Despite this supportive microenvironment, pure alginate inherently lacks cell-adhesive motifs (e.g., RGD sequences) [47], and our in vitro assessment was limited to a relatively short duration. A limitation of this study, therefore, is the absence of long-term in vivo tracking to evaluate how the degradation rate of hydrogels aligns with cell integration and whether it provokes any delayed foreign body response in the subretinal space [17]. Nevertheless, several additional limitations of this study remain. First, rodent models cannot fully recapitulate the chronic progression of human RP, underscoring the need for validation in human retinal organoids and evaluation using larger animal models like rabbits, pigs or non-human primate. Additionally, while the rapid in situ setting of the hydrogel suggests a potential to prevent surgical cell reflux, we acknowledge that the small size of the RCS rat eye is not ideal for clinically quantifying this effect. Thus, future studies employing larger animal models, such as rabbits, are necessary to validate this hypothesis and further evaluate long-term safety.
We used two different cell types of RPE, hiPSC-RPE cells and ARPE-19 cells in this study. ARPE-19 cells are the cells of immortalized human RPE cell line, which cannot be used in clinical transplantation and might have some different characteristics of primary human RPE cells. The reason we also used ARPE-19 cells in this study is to evaluate difference and similarity of the data obtained using hiPSC-RPE cells and immortalized human RPE cells (RPE-19 cells).
Another limitation of this study is that we have no idea how the transplanted RPE cells (hiPSC-RPE cells and ARPE-19 cells) with alginate hydrogels contribute to the maintenance of visual function of RCS rats such as the enhancement of ERG b-wave amplitude compared to non-treated groups or RPE cell suspension groups. It should be valuable to investigate the mechanism how the transplanted RPE cells contribute to the maintenance of visual function of RCS rats from the measurements using retinal microenvironment cytokine array and/or single-cell sequencing assay.
Furthermore, combining this hydrogel-mediated delivery system with universal hiPSC-RPE cells [48] could eliminate the need for immunosuppressants, enhancing therapeutic efficacy and improving patient quality of life (QOL), thereby broadening the clinical potential of RPE transplantation.

## Conclusion
We successfully developed injectable alginate hydrogels optimized for the subretinal engraftment of hiPSC-RPE cells. OCT and histological analyses confirmed 8-weeks survival of hiPSC-RPE cells with alginate hydrogels and the greater preservation of ONL cell counts in the hiPSC-RPE cell engraftment group with alginate hydrogels than in the cell suspension group for up to 8 weeks. Functional assessment by ERG demonstrated that compared with cell suspension delivery, hydrogel-mediated RPE transplantation resulted in relatively better visual restoration than the suspension transplantation of RPE cells without alginate hydrogels. Moreover, compared with the transplantation of ARPE-19 cells, the transplantation of hiPSC-derived RPE cells resulted in more sustained visual functional recovery in RCS rats. This strategy represents a promising therapeutic avenue for treating retinal degenerative diseases and warrants continued development toward clinical translation.
