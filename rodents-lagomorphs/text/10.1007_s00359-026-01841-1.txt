Abstract
Left/right and front/back sound-localization thresholds, as well as the ability to use binaural time and intensity cues, are
presented for one lagomorph (domestic rabbits) and five species of rodents (fox squirrel, black-tailed prairie dog, groundhog, eastern chipmunk, and spiny mouse). These localization abilities are compared to those of other mammals and all
three are shown to vary widely. Many species do not localize as accurately as the available physical locus cues would seem
to permit. Instead, localization acuity is negatively correlated with the width of the field of best vision. This coordination
of hearing and vision allows auditory information on the location of sound sources to direct the reflexive orientation of
vision and attention for further evaluation. It is possible that the coordination of hearing with vision drives the evolution
of sound localization among mammals. Species differences in front/back discrimination are less well studied but also vary
widely, and may be influenced by complex interactions between pinna size and complexity. Finally the use of interaural
phase differences varies in unexpected ways raising questions about the anatomical and neural mechanisms that underlie
binaural analysis in the nervous system.
Keywords Noise localization · Vision · Binaural cues · Pinnae · Evolution

Introduction
In nature, most brief sounds (e.g., 100 ms) are made by animals as they step on twigs and leaves or utter quick grunts or
chirps. Localizing the sources of those brief sounds enables
an animal to locate other animals even when they are hidden
from view. Despite its seeming utility, there is wide variation in the sound-localization acuity of mammals. That is,
not all mammals localize accurately. At one extreme is the
near inability of three subterranean rodents to localize brief
sound at all (for details, see Heffner and Heffner 1994). In
contrast, the passive left/right localization acuity of humans,
elephants, and domestic pigs is the best known so far—
about 1–4° (Mills 1958; Heffner and Heffner 1982, 1989,

We once believed that mammals maximized localization
acuity, and that their acuity was determined by the available
physical cues and their phyletic proximity to humans (i.e.,
primates, Masterton et al. 1969). But as the sample of mammals increased in number and breadth, in the last decades
of the twentieth century it became clear that good localization acuity could also be found in non-primates (Ravizza
and Masterton 1972; Heffner and Heffner 1982), and that
large interaural distances generating large interaural difference cues were neither necessary nor sufficient to result
in good acuity. Some very small species such as Jamaican
fruit bats have good passive localization acuity (e.g., Heffner et al. 2001b), and some species with large heads, such
as cattle, have surprisingly poor localization acuity (e.g.,
Heffner et al. 1992a). The gradual emergence of such great
variation, even within phyletic groups such as artiodactyls
and rodents, suggested that at least some aspects of hearing are not conservatively inherited features of mammalian
lineages, but rather are more influenced by ecological niche
and the integration with other senses (particularly vision) to

promote successful adaptation. Further, the limited correspondence between the magnitude of physical cues to locus
(interaural time and intensity differences) and localization
acuity implies that mammals are not under selective pressure to localize as accurately as the locus cues physically
available to them should permit. This is illustrated by mammals with relatively large interaural distances nevertheless
having poorer acuity than species with much smaller interaural distances as discussed below.
As the comparative data accumulated, we were forced to
consider that the ability to localize sound might be only as
accurate as needed for successful adaptation to a particular
niche and that it might differ within phyletic groups. One
feature seemed to be important: the width of the field of
best vision. Species seem to localize well enough to direct
their best vision to the source of a sound (Heffner and Heffner 1992b). Thus the evolution of sound-localization acuity
may not be driven simply by the physical size of locus cues;
it is even conceivable that different species employ different
physical cues and possibly different neural mechanisms to
achieve the same degree of localization acuity (cf., Grothe
and Park 2000; Pätz et al. 2022). Admittedly, the possibility
that a particular localization acuity might be based on different mechanisms in different species makes understanding
physiological mechanisms more difficult, but at the same
time reveals the opportunism of nature.
The purpose of this report is to describe minimum audible angles for both left/right and front/back discriminations
and the ability to localize pure tones for five species of wild
rodents and one species of rabbit. (Front/back localization
was not available for chipmunks and tone localization was
not available for rabbits.) We also report the horizontal
width of their field of best vision and its relation to auditory
localization among mammals more broadly.

a minimum of 10 cycles onset for pure tones. Sound levels

Behavioral methods and threshold determination
Thirsty animals (23-h water restricted) were allowed to
drink from a water spout that dispensed a trickle of water
as long as an animal was making mouth contact. Drinking kept the animals’ heads oriented toward 0° (midline).
The water rate was set to provide all the animals wished to
drink in about 30–60 min. The animals were weighed daily
and given supplements if needed to maintain healthy body
weights. Once trained, animals maintained body weights at
least 80% of free-food/water weights, usually above wild
weights for the species.

Every 2 s a sound was emitted from a speaker to the right
of 0°. At random intervals, from 3.5 to about 35 s, in 22% of
trials, the sound would emanate from a speaker to the left of
0°. Those left sounds warned of impending shock from the
water spout within 2 s. The animals learned to maintain contact as long as a sound came from the right of midline and to
stop drinking when a sound came from the left. Ceasing to
drink broke contact with the spout and thereby avoided the
shock. If the animal did not break contact, either because of
inattention or inability to detect the change in sound location,
it received a mild shock accompanied by a flash of light at
the end of the 2-s trial. The shock level, in the range of 0–1
milliamp (Coulbourn Precision Animal Shocker), was set to
to zero. It was then gradually raised to the lowest level that
each individual subject would consistently avoid. To allow
for decision time, the response was recorded during the final
200 ms of the 2 s trial. If the animal was not in contact with
the spout during the final 200 ms, the response was recorded
as a Hit, if the animal remained in contact, it was recorded
as a Miss. The 2-s trials were followed by a 1.5-s intertrial
interval with the result that a sound was presented as often
as once every 3.5 s as long as an animal maintained contact
with the spout. For trials in which the sound remained to the
right of midline, contact during the final 200 ms of each trial
was considered a Correct Rejection, absence of contact was
considered a False Alarm. (For details of sound presentation
see, for example, Heffner, Koay, and Heffner 2020.)
This behavioral procedure had several advantages. The
animals’ heads remained fixed and oriented toward the midline. Because warning sounds alerted the animals to avoid
the shock at the end of a trial, very few shocks were actually
received once an animal learned to listen to sound locus.
This usually required about one to three weeks, but varied
between species. The small number of shocks meant that
testing was not stressful and animals readily entered the
test cage and drank from the spout. It also meant that water
restriction could remain minimal since severe thirst was not
needed to overcome a fear of the spout. Because the water
restriction was fairly mild with animals drinking to satiation

Minimum audible angle at 0° azimuth. To determine
azimuthal minimum audible angle (MAA), blocks of trials at a fixed angle were presented for 5–8 warning trials
with the number depending on how long a species would
work—smaller species required less water and ran fewer
trials than larger species. Once trained, corrected detection
scores were computed for every block of trials: Hit rate −
Hit rate * False alarm rate that corrected the raw hit rate to
compensate for the associated false alarm rate. The score
ranged from 0 (no detection) to 1.0 (perfect detection with
no false alarms). Testing was continued with progressively
smaller angles until the animal could no longer detect the
change in locus above a level expected by chance, defined
as the hit rate no longer differing reliably from the false
alarm rate (binomial distribution). The test angle was then
increased for a block of trials until performance improved,
then smaller angles were again tested. Testing continued in
this way until performance no longer improved with practice and every angle was tested on at least three days. Final
average for each individual was plotted as asymptotic performance, the mean of the best three blocks of trials at each
angle. Some species required extensive practice. In those
cases in which many test sessions were involved and performance was variable, asymptote was computed as the best
half of scores, since often a very high score could not be
repeated and could not be considered to represent performance at a particular angle. Importantly, there was always
an angle at which performance consistently fell to chance
indicating that the speakers could not be discriminated on
the basis of some attribute other than locus. Threshold was
interpolated to be the angle at which performance equaled
0.50 (50% corrected detection).
Minimum audible angle at 90° azimuth. All species
except chipmunks were tested for discriminations centered
around 90° to the right—front/back discrimination on the
cone of confusion. The head remained oriented to 0° but
speaker pairs were placed symmetrically in front of and
behind 90° right (for illustration, see Heffner, Koay, and
Heffner 2014). Sounds behind the interaural line (located
at 100° to 170°) were Safe, and sounds in front of the interaural line (10° to 80°) served as Warning signals. This was
a difficult discrimination and testing was often carried out

13

with both brief noise and with longer durations as noted in
the results for each species. Easily discriminated stimuli as
well as difficult stimuli were included as blocks of trials in
each session. Asymptotic performance and thresholds were
calculated as described for testing at 0° azimuth.
Pure-tone localization. All of the rodents were also
tested for their ability to localize pure tones at a fixed angle
of 60° separation, 30° to the left and right of midline. This
angle allows comparisons with other mammals and has the
advantage that it is moderately difficult for most species so
that a performance measure can reveal which frequencies
sustain relatively good performance and which are barely
localizable or even, in some cases, not localizable at all. By
comparing the localizability of different frequencies with an
estimate of whether those frequencies could produce interaural phase or intensity differences (based on a spherical
head of the same diameter as each species), we can estimate
the ability of a species to use the interaural phase difference
(an ongoing time cue) and interaural intensity difference,
two important binaural cues to sound locus.
Thresholds were not determined, instead an estimate of
the best sustainable performance was sought for each frequency. This was defined as the mean of all but the lowest
two blocks of trials for each frequency with the requirement
that the scores had been obtained in different test sessions
and were obtained after overall performance had stabilized.
If none of the scores for a frequency were above chance, the
mean of all the blocks of trials was used. Because some frequencies for some species were not localizable at 60° separation, more than one frequency was tested in every session
so that blocks of trials at localizable frequencies were interspersed with blocks of trials at frequencies that were not
localizable to maintain motivation.
Field of best vision. By 1990 it was becoming clear that
simple magnitude of available locus cues did not explain
the great variation in mammalian sound-localization acuity.
Rather it seemed that the width of the field of a species’
best vision exerted selective pressure on the locus information provided by hearing (Heffner and Heffner 1992b).
This relationship was originally based on 23 species. In
order to update the relationship between the field of best
vision and localization acuity, we were able to make and
analyze retinal wholemounts of chipmunks, prairie dogs,
groundhogs, and spiny mice. Retinal analyses of domestic
rabbits are well known (e.g., Oyster et al. 1987). Retinae
were prepared in the standard way by careful dissection of
the retina from the eye, slitting the edges to flatten the retina
but avoiding damage to the horizon of the eye, then gently brushing away vitreous humor. The retina is then defatted, rehydrated, and stained with a standard Nissl stain for
viewing under 100 × oil immersion (for details see Heffner
and Heffner 1992b). Detailed counts of Nissl-stained retinal

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

ganglion cells within a rectangle 0.0019 mm2 were made
every 200 µ in order to plot ganglion-cell density maps of
each retina. The maximum density was determined based
on the rectangle with the highest number of ganglion cells.
By observing the distribution of densities on a large scale
map of a retina, the contours of the areas encompassing at
least 75%, 50%, and 25% of the maximum density could be
defined and plotted. Finally, the horizontal width, in degrees
of visual angle of the region that contained at least 75% of
maximum density was defined as the “width of the field of
best vision” for cross-species comparisons.

The remarkable variation in sound-localization acuity
does not seem to depend on the simple magnitude of physical interaural locus cues. The correlation between functional interaural distance and sound-localization threshold,

13

although statistically reliable, is weak (r = -0.377, p < 0.0001,
n = 43). Only 14% of the variance is accounted for; some
very small species are fairly good localizers, and even the
very large interaural distances of large hoofed mammals
do not guarantee good acuity (Fig. 10). Even this limited
correlation is dependent on a third factor, as shown below.
It appears that not all species are under selective pressure
to localize as accurately as the physically available cues
should permit. Even the precision of active echolocation is
not reflected in the passive localization of the species examined so far (Renaud and Popper 1975; Heffner and Heffner
1992b; Simmons et al. 2014). Rather, the passive localization thresholds of both echolocating and non-echolocating
bats lie in the midrange for mammals as a whole suggesting
that many factors play a role in the passive localization acuity of mammals (Fig. 10).
The lack of explanatory value of the magnitude of simple
physical cues led us more than 30 years ago to seek other factors that might account for the variation in sound localization
(Heffner and Heffner 1992b). Those possibilities included
several lifestyle factors (predator/prey, nocturnal/diurnal)
and visual factors (visual acuity). Only one of the factors
explored, the width of the field of best vision, appeared to
have strong explanatory value based on the thirteen species
then available (r = 0.911, p < 0.0001). It was proposed then

that the basis for this relationship lay in the rapid and accurate orientation of vision toward sound sources. It seemed
that hearing had to pinpoint the location of a sound source
accurately enough for the eyes to reflexively orient their best

For example, humans, with their best vision concentrated
in a narrow fovea, use similarly acute sound localization
to enable rapid foveal orienting. In contrast, species (usually prey animals) like horses, gerbils, and cottontail rabbits
have their best vision concentrated in a broad visual streak
that encompasses nearly the entire horizon and have poor
sound-localization acuity—it appears that their ears do not
need to be very accurate to enable them to capture the sound
source within such a broad field of good vision. (For further

discussion of the importance of such rapid visual orienting
to sound, see Heffner and Heffner 1992b.)
This integration of hearing with vision continues to gain
interest (e.g., Spence et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2024). If hearing
detects and locates unseen sound sources in order to guide
the visual orienting reflex, then the auditory locus information would need to be accurate enough to guide an animal’s
best vision to the sound source. Hence the strong correlation between sound-localization acuity and the width of the
visual field with the best visual acuity.
With the addition of vision and localization data for five
of the species reported here, we can now calculate the correlation between the width of the field of best vision and
sound localization acuity among a larger and more representative sample of mammals. Note that the exclusively subterranean species that do not localize brief sounds and whose
vision is vestigial to absent are not included in this analysis; they are consistent with the analysis but are reviewed
elsewhere (Heffner and Heffner 1994). The correlation, now
among 35 species, between sound-localization acuity and
width of the field of best vision remains high as well as reliable (r = 0.700, p < 0.0001). However, a further analysis is
warranted that takes into account the interrelation between
functional interaural distance and width of the field of best
vision (r = -0.371, p = 0.024). A multiple regression analysis of the relation of each factor to localization acuity after
removing the contribution of the other factor reveals that
only the visual factor is robust (Fig. 11). Specifically, the
width of the field of best vision remains strongly correlated
with sound-localization acuity (r = 0.703, p < 0.0001) after
removing the contribution of the magnitude of physical
locus cues (represented by functional interaural distance),
but the reverse is not true. The correlation between the magnitude of the physical locus cues and sound-localization acuity falls to chance when the contribution of the visual factor
is removed: r = -0.328, p = 0.067. It seems that any ability
of the magnitude of the physical cues to predict localization acuity is due primarily to its correlation with vision.
We believe this correlation derives from a major function of
hearing—to direct the eyes to the source of a sound. Thus
animals with narrow fields of best vision require more accurate sound-localization acuity to direct their gaze to sound
sources than do animals with broad fields of best vision. The
coordination between hearing and vision permits animals
to detect, locate, and scrutinize other animals—the natural
sources of brief sounds. That function appears to be primary.

the importance of high frequencies for discriminating front/
back sound sources. Without the high-frequency component
of the noise signal they were not able to distinguish front
from back sources above a level expected by chance. However, they differed in their reliance on high frequencies for
left/right localization. The spiny mice, like several bats and
rodents that could not use binaural time cues, required highfrequency spectral cues for both front/back and for left/right
localization (e.g., Koay et al. 1998; Heffner et al. 2007). The
prairie dogs, because they were able to use binaural time
cues, performed the left/right discrimination even without
frequencies higher than 500 Hz. However, when forced to
make front/back discriminations where binaural cues were
not useful, they could not discriminate even long-duration
sounds above chance when high frequencies were removed
from the signal.

of some species is owing to their limited high-frequency
hearing. But for neither prairie dogs nor groundhogs (153°
and 90° thresholds respectively) does that seem to be the

60 dB SPL), not very different from chinchillas that hear
only up to 33 kHz but that have a much smaller front/back
threshold (36°). Even humans with even more limited highfrequency hearing have front/back thresholds around 10°.

on pinna cues may be owing to small size of the pinnae
rather than to limited high-frequency hearing. However,
even this does not account for the poor acuity of alpacas.

Use of phase
Fifty years of comparative hearing data have led us to suspect that not only does localization acuity vary in unexpected ways, but also that the cues used to underpin acuity
probably vary as well. We once assumed that mammals
would use all the cues available to them to maximize sound
localization. However, early in the history of behavioral
testing, species were discovered that did not use both of the
binaural locus cues, yet acuity did not seem to be compromised (Masterton et al. 1975; Heffner and Heffner 1989).
This suggested that the two binaural locus cues might each
be powerful and under independent selective pressure. That
in turn implied that comparing different species could help
us understand the evolution of sound localization as well as
its physiological mechanisms.
The ability of an animal to use the binaural locus cues
can be demonstrated by its ability to localize pure tones.
Pure tones of wavelengths long-enough to bend around the
head without attenuation must be localized by comparing
the time of arrival of the phase of each cycle of the tone
at the two ears. High-frequency tones that are amplitudemodulated at a slow rate can be localized by comparing the
phase of the envelope of the sound at the two ears (e.g., Heffner et al. 2010a 2014). When the frequency of an unmodulated tone increases and its wavelength becomes short
enough so that more than one cycle arrives at the opposite
ear with the same phase angle, the nervous system cannot
match the arrival of a particular wave at each ear thus making the phase cue physically ambiguous. This ‘frequency of
ambiguity’ depends on the angle of the sound source relative to an animal’s midline and the distance a sound must
travel to get from one ear to the other. These higher frequencies can be localized by comparing the intensity of the
tone at the two ears, provided that the wavelength of the
tone is short enough to be deflected by the head and pinnae—an approximate estimate being wavelengths shorter

hear below 500 Hz, also fall into two groups. Most cannot use the interaural phase-difference cue, but three species
of bats can, even at surprisingly high frequencies. It should
be noted, however, these conclusions do not rule out use
of time cues by bats when interpreting the return echoes of
frequency-modulated sweeps.

mammals as a whole, larger species tend not to use phase at
frequencies higher than about 3 kHz. Several large species
including humans relinquish the use of phase at even lower
frequencies even though the cue remains at least physically
unambiguous at higher frequencies. Phase locking is a likely
limiting factor since, at least in chinchillas, it becomes less
synchronous beginning around 1 kHz and their ability to
localize pure tones falls sharply at that point (Woolf et al.
1981). Others have reported similar sharp drops in phase
locking in this frequency range (Grothe et al. 2010; Simmons and Simmons 2011; Bremen and Joris 2013). Yet the
fox squirrel reported here seems to be able to use the phasedifference cue at frequencies higher than 5 kHz suggesting
that the mammalian nervous system, in some species, may
be able to synchronously phase lock at higher frequencies
than commonly documented. This unusual finding warrants
replication and extension to similar species.
Such comparative behavioral data raise the question of
why large species do not take advantage of this potential.
Perhaps at least some have large heads and pinnae making
the intensity-difference cue, with its accompanying spectral
differences, more powerful at relatively low frequencies
thereby relieving the nervous system of the need to phase
lock at those frequencies.
For species whose low-frequency hearing does not
extend below about 500 Hz, there is no correlation with
low-frequency hearing at all: Most cannot use the phase
cue regardless of how low they can hear (gray shading).
Yet three distantly related bats occupying distinct ecological niches have the remarkable ability to use the phase cue
at high frequencies (Heffner et al. 1999, 2001b, 2015). The
neural mechanisms that enable these bats, one of which
is not a typical echo locator, to use the phase cue are not
known. Their use of phase at frequencies much higher than
larger mammals suggests that neural mechanisms, such as
phase locking, may be exceptional, or that other mechanisms not yet known enable them to perceive binaural phase
differences at high frequencies.
Finally, it is worth noting that there is not a clear correspondence between overall acuity (minimum audible angle
for brief noise) and the ability to use, or not use, the phase
cue as shown in Table 4. Why do some small species (such
as the common vampire bat with it interaural distance of
61 µs) use the phase cue when it would seem so unlikely
with such a small interaural distance? It has even been suggested that very short interaural distances—50 μs or less—
would not permit use the phase cue since responses to such
small delays have not been recorded with any consistency
(Grothe et al. 2010), and so far no species of that size has
been shown to use the phase cue.

Conclusion
Comparative approaches like the one presented here allow
us to appreciate the wide variation of abilities in mammals
and to seek similarly wide variation in the neural mechanisms underlying them. They also stimulate a search for
selective pressures that may have influenced the development of such different auditory abilities, and perhaps different mechanisms and structures coopted in evolution to
serve the same abilities even in distantly related species (c.f.
McCullagh et al. unpublished preprint). As more comparative data accumulate we may discover that the mechanisms
underlying localization are more varied and more subject to
independent selective pressures than we once thought. As
we progress, we should not lose sight that selective pressures act directly on behavior and only indirectly on the
mechanisms that make that behavior possible.
