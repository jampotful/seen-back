# Kynurenine-Derived UV Filters and Other Cytoprotective Metabolites in the Eye Lenses of Rodents From the Squirrel Family (Sciuridae)

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeMetabolites play a crucial role in the protection of the eye lens from damage caused by UV radiation, with molecular UV filters absorbing UV light, antioxidants preventing oxidative stress, and osmolytes regulating cellular pressure. This study is aimed at identifying cytoprotective metabolites in rodent eye lenses including full set of UV filters in squirrel lenses.MethodsA quantitative metabolomic analysis of the lenses from seven rodent species of the Sciuridae (squirrel) and Cricetidae (hamster) families was performed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). A total of 68 metabolites were measured, and cytoprotective metabolites of rodent lenses were revealed.ResultsAnimal lifestyle was found to be a major factor determining the set of cytoprotective metabolites in the lens. Squirrels are diurnal animals, and their lenses contain high levels of kynurenine derivatives acting as UV filters, myo-inositol as an osmolyte, and glutathione and ascorbic acid as antioxidants. This makes the squirrel lenses similar to human lenses and suggests that squirrels can be considered as good models for studying human ophthalmic diseases. We detected and identified 16 UVA-absorbing compounds in squirrel lenses, with N-acetyl-3-hydroxykynurenine being the most abundant UV filter. In contrast, hamster lenses have no UV filters at all, and their major osmolytes and antioxidants are taurine and glutathione, respectively.ConclusionsOur results shed light on the cytoprotective mechanisms of mammalian eye tissues and generate interest in the squirrel eye as a model for studying human ocular diseases.Keywords: lens, rodents, UV filters, antioxidants, osmolytes
The lens is an important part of the eye apparatus of many animal species that provides clear vision. The nucleus and inner cortex of the lens are composed of fiber cells. To avoid incident light scattering, these cells are devoid of nuclei and other organelles, and contain only large amounts of structural proteins, crystallins, water with dissolved metabolites, and almost no enzymes. Crystallins in the lens ensure its transparency and high refractive index, but they do not turn over throughout the lifespan of an individual. Conventional cellular activity within lens fiber cells is minimal, and the protection of the lens structures is almost exclusively provided by metabolites. Cytoprotective metabolites can be roughly divided into three groups. The most important group includes antioxidants. Oxidative stress is the main cause of lens damage, leading to the development of cataracts,1,2 and antioxidants play a key role in protecting crystallins from oxidation. Different species contain different sets of lenticular antioxidants. In particular, the major antioxidants in the fish lens are ovothiol and glutathione,3–5 whereas in the lenses of birds and mammals (including humans) they are glutathione, ergothioneine, and ascorbate.6–14 The second group of cytoprotective metabolites includes osmolytes. Fiber cells in the lens are tightly packed, and osmotic stress can disrupt this packing, causing increased intercellular space and impeding the diffusion of metabolites across the lens. Osmolytes also vary between species. In the fish lens, the osmotic protection is provided by myo-inositol, N-acetyl-histidine, N-acetyl-aspartate, threonine–phosphoethanolamine, and serine–phosphoethanolamine4,15–17; the main osmolytes in the bird lens are myo-inositol and taurine13; in the rat lens, they are taurine and hypotaurine,10 and in the primate lens, it is myo-inositol.11,12,14,18
The third group of cytoprotective metabolites in the lens is composed of molecular ultraviolet (UV) filters. These compounds absorb light in the UVA range (315–400 nm) and convert the absorbed energy into heat without undergoing chemical transformations.19,20 The eyes of some animals are exposed to high doses of solar radiation, including the UV light. The lens of the eye plays a critical role in protecting the retina from UV radiation, which can cause significant photochemical damage to retinal cells. Unlike the skin, which contains melanin and can regenerate after UV exposure, the retina is highly sensitive and lacks the regenerative capacity. Furthermore, whereas other parts of the body are covered with feathers, scales, hair, or fur that block UV and visible light by shading and scattering it, the protection of the retina from UVA radiation almost completely relies on the lens. Lenticular UV filters protect both the retina and the lens itself from harmful UV radiation, and improve visual acuity by reducing chromatic aberration. UV filters are only present in the lenses of diurnal animals with very acute vision or animals whose eyes are often exposed to strong solar radiation. In particular, mycosporine-like amino acids,21,22 kynurenine,23 and 3-hydroxykynurenine,24 which absorb in the UV range have been found in the lenses of some tropical fish. Reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) acts as a UV filter in the lenses of birds of prey and waterfowl.25 In mammals, UV filters are currently found and identified only in primates and squirrels. In both types of species, these UV filters are derivatives of kynurenine (KN). The composition of UV filters in the human,12,20,26,27 and macaque14 lenses, as well as the chemical and photochemical properties of these compounds, have been well studied.28–31 The most abundant UV filter in the primate lens is 3-hydroxykynurenine O-β-D-glucoside (3OHKG), followed by KN, 4-(2-amino-3-hydroxyphenyl)-4-oxobutanoic acid O-β-D-glucoside (AHBG), and several other KN derivatives.12,19,20,26,27 These compounds feature a high absorption in the UVA range with a maximum near 360 nm, and very low quantum yields of photodecomposition and generation of photoexcited triplet states and radicals: the main channel of the photoexcited singlet state decay is the rapid internal conversion to the ground state.30
So far, UV filters in the lenses of squirrels have been reported in only two species: the Thirteen-lined ground squirrel (Spermophilus tridecemlineatus)32,33 and the Gray squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis).34,35 These UV filters include N-acetyl-3-hydroxykynurenine (NAc3OHKN), which is the main UV filter in squirrel lenses, along with N-acetyl-kynurenine (NAcKN), 3-hydroxykynurenine (3OHKN), and 2-acetamido-3-(9-hydroxy-11-oxo-2,3,5,10,11,11a-hexahydro-1H-benzo[e]pyrrolo[1,2-a][1,4]diazepin-5-yl)propanoic acid (Pro-NAc3OHKN), the condensation product of proline with NAc3OHKN (Fig. 1).
Chemical structures of the main UV filters identified in the lenses of rodents from the family Sciuridae.
In the present study, we investigated for the first time the metabolomic composition of the eye lenses of five rodent (Rodentia) species from the family Sciuridae (squirrels), and, for comparison, two species from the family Cricetidae, namely Golden hamster (Mesocricetus auratus) and muskrat (Ondatra zibethicus). The study focuses on the identification and quantification of UV filters in the lenses of these species. Additionally, we analyzed the composition of other cytoprotective compounds (antioxidants and osmolytes) and other metabolites in the lenses of squirrels and hamsters.

## Methods

## Chemicals
Methanol and chloroform (HPLC grade) from J. T. Baker (Radnor, USA), 99.9% D2O from Astrachim (St. Petersburg, Russia), acetonitrile “0” grade from Cryochrom (St. Petersburg, Russia), formic acid (HPLC grade) and ammonium formate from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), sodium 4,4-dimethyl-4-silapentane-1-sulfonate (DSS) from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (Tewkesbury, MA, USA), kynurenine and 3-hydroxykynurenine from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) were used as received. H2O was deionized using an Ultra Clear UV plus TM water system (SG Water, Hamburg, Germany) to the quality of 18.2 MΩ × cm.

## Species Description and Sample Collection
The study was conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and the European Union Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, and with the ethical approval from the International Tomography Center SB RAS (Protocol No. ECITC-2017-02).
Samples from two marmot species, red squirrel, and muskrat studied in this research were obtained during the hunting season with licenses from the regional Ministries of Ecology and Natural Resources (Novosibirsk Region, Omsk Region, Russia) as a part of the annual collection of biological material under the program for studying infectious diseases of wild animals, with approval from the Biomedical Ethics Committee of the Federal Research Center of Fundamental and Translational Medicine (Protocol No. 2021-10). We also used samples from two Siberian chipmunks and one Long-tailed ground squirrel sourced from Novosibirsk Region, Russia, provided by the Center for the Rehabilitation of Wild Animals (CRWA, Novosibirsk, Russia) after the humane euthanasia of mortally wounded individuals. Additionally, female Golden hamsters housed in the SPF animal facility (SPFAF) of the Institute of Cytology and Genetics SB RAS (Novosibirsk, Russia) were purchased for this study. Lenses were extracted from the bodies 10 to 60 minutes postmortem, placed in plastic vials and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples were transferred to the laboratory and kept at −70°C until the analysis.

## Sample Preparation
The procedure of sample preparation is described in detail in our previous publications.4 For lens samples obtained from the Bobak marmot, Forest-steppe marmot, and red squirrel, one lens per sample was used. For samples from the Long-tailed ground squirrel, Siberian chipmunk, Golden hamster, and muskrat two lenses from the same individual were pooled together. Briefly, frozen tissues were homogenized in cold methanol (−20°C) using a TissueRuptor II rotary homogenizer (Qiagen, The Netherlands). Metabolites were extracted from the homogenized lenses by adding cold water and chloroform (H2O:MeOH:CHCl3 at a ratio of 1:2:2) to the homogenate, followed by shaking at +4°C, incubation at −20°C, centrifugation at 16,100×g (+4°C), and collection of the upper (MeOH-H2O) layer. The collected metabolomic fraction was divided into two parts for NMR (2/3) and LC–OD/MS (1/3) analyses and vacuum dried.

## Optical Measurements of Whole Metabolomic Extracts
We obtained stationary UV-Vis spectra of whole metabolomic extracts using an Agilent 8453 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, La Jolla, CA, USA). Measurements were carried out for ⅔ of the total lens extract, dissolved in 600 µL of deuterated phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.2) and placed in a quartz optical cuvette with a 2 mm optical path.

## NMR Measurements
The extracts for the NMR measurements were re-dissolved in 600 µL of 50 mM deuterated phosphate buffer (pH of 7.2) containing 20 µM DSS as an internal standard. The 1H NMR measurements were carried out at the Center of Collective Use “Mass spectrometric investigations” SB RAS using an AVANCE III HD 700 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker BioSpin, Ettlingen, Germany) equipped with a 16.44 Tesla Ascend cryomagnet.
For the metabolomic profiling of extracts from rodent lenses, we obtained 1D NMR spectra as described in Reference 4. Briefly, the spectra were obtained with water signal presaturation, a 70 degrees detection pulse, 64 accumulations, and a repetition time of 10 s between scans. We performed phase and baseline corrections as well as signal integration using MestReNova version 12 software (Mestrelab Research S.L., A Coruna, Spain). Unless stated otherwise, metabolite identification was based on their NMR data (chemical shift and multiplicity) available in the HMDB (https://hmdb.ca)36 and AMDB (https://amdb.online)37 databases and in our previous reports on lens metabolomic composition.4,5,12,14 The concentrations of the metabolites in the lenses (in nmol/g) were calculated by integrating the NMR signals relative to the internal standard DSS, followed by normalization to the tissue wet weight.
To identify UV filters found in rodent lenses, we performed LC-OD/MS with a 1:10 flow splitter as described below. Chromatographically separated fractions were collected into separate vials, dried, and re-dissolved in NMR buffer. For the fractions of interest that contained UV filters, we obtained their 1D and 2D NMR spectra. The 1D spectra were obtained using the same parameters as for metabolomic profiling. To identify the UV filters, present in the solution, we conducted two-dimensional NMR TOCSY (Total Correlation Spectroscopy) experiments. The TOCSY spectra were recorded using a standard Bruker “dipsiphpr” pulse sequence with low-power presaturation of the residual water signal during the relaxation delay D1. Data acquisition was performed with 2048 points in the F2 dimension (direct dimension) and 256 points in the F1 dimension (indirect dimension). The acquisition time for each FID was 0.12 s, and the relaxation delay (D1) was set to 1.5 s. Spin mixing was achieved using the DIPSI-2 sequence with a pulse duration (p9) of 30 µs, corresponding to a radiofrequency field strength (gB1) of 8.3 kHz. Mixing times of 25 ms and 60 ms were utilized to optimize the detection of spin-spin correlations. The number of scans was set to 128, resulting in a total acquisition time of 15 hours for one sample. A long mixing time (60 ms) was used to detect cross-peaks of the entire spin system, providing a comprehensive view of the spin-spin interactions in the molecules of interest. A short mixing time of 25 ms was used to obtain cross-peaks from directly interacting spins only, allowing signals to be more accurately assigned to the corresponding metabolites.

## Liquid Chromatography With Optical and Mass Spectrometric Detection
Combined liquid chromatography with optical and mass spectrometric detection (LC-OD/MS) measurements were performed using an UltiMate 3000RS chromatograph (Dionex, Germering, Germany) equipped with a flow cell diode array UV-Vis detector (DAD) with the spectra registration within 190 to 800 nm range and subsequently connected to an ESI-Q-TOF high-resolution hybrid mass spectrometer maXis 4G (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany), yielding combined LC-OD and LC-MS data. For the LC-OD/MS measurements, the extracts were re-dissolved in 200 µL of 10 mM ammonium formate buffer. LC separation was carried out using reversed-phase chromatography on an Intensity Solo 2 C18 column (2 × 100 mm, 2 µm, 90 Å, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) with a pre-column. Solvent A consisted of 0.1% formic acid solution in 10 mM ammonium formate buffer, and solvent B consisted of 0.1% formic acid solution in acetonitrile. The following gradient (solvent B) was used to study the composition of the lens metabolomic extracts: 0% (0–5 minutes), 0 to 60% (5–25 minutes), 60 to 95% (25–31 minutes), 95% (31–36 minutes), 95 to 0% (36–38 minutes), and 0% (38–45 minutes). The flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, the injection volume was 7 µL, and the autosampler and column temperatures were 4°C and 40°C, respectively. Mass spectra were recorded in a positive mode within the 50 to 1000 m/z mass range with 3 Hz scan rate. Each LC-MS chromatogram contained a calibration segment at the beginning, where sodium formate clusters used as a calibrant were supplied by the syringe pump into a 1 µL loop connected to an electrospray ionization (ESI) source via the divert valve. The typical MS resolution was approximately. 50,000 and accuracy <1.5 parts per million (ppm).

## LC-MS/MS Analysis and LC Fraction Collection
For three species, the Long-tailed ground squirrel, Forest-steppe marmot, and Bobak marmot, we additionally performed liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) fragmentation experiments. A sample from each species was subjected to LC-MS/MS with three different collision induced dissociation (CID) energies of 10 eV, 20 eV, and 40 eV, executed in AutoMS/MS mode with a precursor ion list. To obtain MS/MS spectra for 3OHKN, we directly injected dissolved chemical standard to the ESI source via automatic syringe and applied manual fragmentation with the same CID energies. In LC-MS/MS measurements, not all ions of interest were picked automatically for the fragmentation, thus for several UV filters MS/MS data are either absent at certain CID energies or absent at all.
Additional LC-OD/MS/MS experiments were performed to gather, accumulate, and identify UV filters contained in selected LC fractions with fixed retention time (RT) windows and used for 1D and 2D NMR analysis, as described in detail earlier.3 We increased the injection volume to 20 µL and modified the gradient to reduce the LC time and improve the separation of only the peaks of interest with noticeable UVA absorbance: 0% (0–3 minutes), 0 to 5% (3–6 minutes), 5% (6–10 minutes), 5 to 20% (10–15 minutes), 20 to 95% (15–16 minutes), 95% (16–19 minutes), 95 to 0% (19–20 minutes), 0% (20–25 minutes) for solvent B. After passing through a flow optical cell, a home-made flow splitter (1:10) directed the smaller flow to the MS system, whereas the majority of the flow was collected into separate vials during 7 to 8 LC runs according to their RT windows. Combined this way fractions were vacuum dried for further NMR analysis. Thus, for each LC fraction, its content was examined using optical, MS, MS/MS, and 1D and 2D NMR data.

## MS Data Analysis
LC-OD/MS/MS data were analyzed in DataAnalysis software version 5.3 (Bruker Daltonik, Bremen, Germany). Molecular formulae were reconstructed from the coincidence of exact masses and relative abundances for all isotopes in isotopic distributions using SmartFormula tool in DataAnalysis. Optical spectra of compounds were retrieved using Find Compounds tool in DataAnalysis. MS spectra were retrieved by averaging several points at the top of LC peak. MS/MS spectra were retrieved by averaging all MS2 spectra belonging to one parent MS ion in the LC peak.
Where no MS/MS spectra are available for a UV filter, we tried to retrieve its fragments formed during ionization by in-source decay (ISD). For this, we reconstructed manually the extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) for all co-eluting ions in MS spectrum at specified RT. A coincidence of LC peak shapes and RTs of maxima for EICs indicated that the corresponding ion is an ISD fragment for an ion of interest.
Structures of UV filters were confirmed or reconstructed manually by the analysis of differences in exact masses between MS peaks, and corresponding fragmentations of structures yielded in ChemDraw software version 16 (CambridgeSoft, Cambridge, MA, USA).
Thus, for each UV filter, we obtained a full or partial combination of optical, MS, MS/MS, and NMR data.

## Chemometric Data Analysis
Principal component analysis (PCA) of quantitative metabolomics data was performed using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 web-platform (www.metaboanalyst.ca).38 PCA was carried out using auto-scaled data.

The comparison of concentrations of individual compounds in lenses of different species shows a significant difference for some metabolites. In particular, in the group “Organic acids” the most pronounced interspecific differences are found for 3-hydroxybutyrate and GABA (gamma-aminobutyrate), in the group “Alcohols, amines, sulfones, and carbohydrates” for scyllo-inositol, in the group “Nitrogenous bases, nucleotides, and nucleosides” for inosine and creatinine (see Supplementary Table S1).
The obtained data on cytoprotective metabolites – osmolytes, antioxidants, and UV filters – clearly demonstrate the difference in the metabolomic composition of the lenses of animals from Cricetidae and Sciuridae families (see Table 2). In all rodents of the Sciuridae family, the main lenticular osmolyte is myo-inositol at a concentration of 15 to 25 µmol/g. In the lens of hamster and muskrat (family Cricetidae), the level of this compound is almost an order of magnitude lower, but the concentration of taurine increases to 9 µmol/g in the muskrat lens and to 17 µmol/g in the hamster lens, making taurine the main lenticular osmolyte for these species. The levels of ascorbate and dehydroascorbate in the hamster and muskrat lenses, in contrast to the lenses of animals from the Sciuridae family, were below the level of NMR detection. The most significant difference was found for lenticular UV filters: a rich set of UV filters was detected in the lenses of rodents from the Sciuridae family (especially in lenses of the Forest-steppe marmot, Ground squirrel, and Bobak marmot), whereas no UV filters was found in the hamster and muskrat lenses. To identify and quantify UV filters in the rodent lens, we performed a combined LC-OD, LC-MS, LC-MS/MS, and NMR study of lenticular chromophores.

## Identification and Quantification of UV Filters Present in Rodent Lenses
We measured UV-Visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra of whole metabolomic lens extracts (Fig. 4). The spectra in Figure 4 are given in units of optical absorption of the metabolomic fraction of the lens tissue (for optical path of 1 cm), calculated using the lens mass, the solution volume, and the cuvette optical path. For the hamster and muskrat lenses, we observed only minor absorption in the UVA region (see Fig. 4, violet and blue lines), whereas the spectra obtained for the squirrel lenses demonstrate a strong absorption band with a maximum near 360 nm, characteristic of kynurenine derivatives.

The next step was to identify compounds that had not been previously identified in the literature. To identify the most abundant compounds, we performed an LC-MS fractionation experiment. The compound eluting at 3.02 minutes has the same m/z and formula as Pro-NAc3OHKN (RT = 5.55 minutes). Both fractions with m/z 346.1397 at RTs of 3.02 and 5.55 min were subjected to 1D and 2D NMR and additionally to LC-OD/MS/MS. The compound eluted at 3.02 minutes has the same UV-Vis and MS/MS spectra as Pro-NAc3OHKN (RT = 5.55 minutes), but the NMR spectra differ. Pro-NAc3OHKN has 2 chiral centers, so we attributed the compound eluted at 3.02 minutes to the diastereomer of Pro-NAc3OHKN. Supplementary Figure S3 demonstrate the NMR spectra of both diastereomers (Pro-NAc3OHKN-1 and Pro-NAc3OHKN-2), and the assignment of NMR signals is given in Table 4 and Figure 1. It should be noted that the signal of #2 proton of both diastereomers is hidden under strong signal of residual protonated water, and it is invisible in 1D NMR spectra. However, we detected a weak cross peak of #2 at 4.80 ppm coupled with #3 protons (3.64 ppm and 3.89 ppm) in 2D TOCSY spectrum of Pro-NAc3OHKN-2, obtained using short mixing time of 25 ms (data not shown). For Pro-NAc3OHKN-1, we were unable to detect the signal from #2 proton due to the residual water signal.
The NMR spectrum of the Pro-NAc3OHKN-1 fraction also contained signals corresponding to the Pro-NAc3OHKN-2 isomer, with an intensity of approximately 20% relative to the main compound. On the other side, the signals corresponding to Pro-NAc3OHKN-1 isomer (approximately 70% relative to the main compound) were observed in the spectrum of the Pro-NAc3OHKN-2 fraction (Supplementary Fig. S3). This indicates slow racemization of diastereomers in D2O solution. Taking into account that additional signals in the spectrum of the Pro-NAc3OHKN-2 fraction correspond to the Pro-NAc3OHKN-1 isomer deuterated at the #3 position (#3 signals are absent, and #2 appears as a singlet at 4.38 ppm), one can conclude that racemization occurs through the formation of a resonance structure with a double bond between the #3 and #4 carbon atoms.
The compound eluted at 10.07 minutes has m/z 378.1293, and the reconstructed molecular formula is C17H20N3O7. Fragmentation pattern (see Fig. 6; Supplementary Scheme S4) as well as 1D and 2D NMR spectra (see Supplementary Fig. S3) unambiguously indicate that this compound is 2-acetamido-3-(3-(2-carboxyethyl)-9-hydroxy-2-oxo-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzo[e][1,4]diazepin-5-yl)propanoic acid (product of glutamate and NAc3OHKN condensation, Glu-NAc3OHKN, see Fig. 1).

The structure of this compound is rather similar to the structure of Pro-NAc3OHKN with the difference that Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN are the products of the addition of proline and glutamate to NAc3OHKN, respectively. Inspection of LC-MS data shows that a compound with the same m/z 378.1293 elutes almost simultaneously with NAc3OHKN at RT = 9.84 minutes; for that reason, this compound is not visible in the LC-OD chromatogram at 360 nm shown in Figure 5. By optimizing LC parameters, we managed to separate this compound as an individual peak, to collect the fraction containing this compound, and to obtain its NMR and MS/MS spectra. As expected, this metabolite was found to be a diastereomer of Glu-NAc3OHKN. The NMR and MS data on both isomers Glu-NAc3OHKN-1 and Glu-NAc3OHKN-2 are given in Figure 6; Supplementary Figure S3; Tables 3, 4, and Supplementary Scheme S4.
The quantities of other chromophores detected in the lens extract were not sufficient for NMR measurements, so characterization of these metabolites was performed by LC-OD/MS and LC-MS/MS methods only (see Supplementary Table 3). We obtained MS/MS spectra for UVF279, UVF281, UVF293, and UVF360. For compounds UVF223, UVF265, and UVF297, we were unable to obtain clean MS/MS spectra, mostly due to their co-elution with more abundant chromophores; however, we isolated ISD fragments for these compounds. According to established molecular formulas and UV spectra, all these UVF### compounds are derivatives of kynurenine. Without NMR data, it is difficult to determine the exact chemical structures of these compounds. However, MS/MS fragmentation patterns help to make assumptions about possible structures (see Supplementary Fig. S2). UVF223 (m/z 223.0712) lacks two hydrogen atoms (Δm/z 2.0157) as compared to 3OHKN (m/z 225.0871), and elutes after 3OHKN. Thus, it is probably a cyclized or double bonded derivative of 3OHKN. Supplementary Figure S12 shows the EICs for established ISD fragments, mass spectrum at RT = 2.88, and the annotated fragments of UVF223.
Similarly, UVF265 (m/z 265.0818) lacks 2 hydrogen atoms as compared to NAc3OHKN (m/z 267.0974); it also elutes after NAc3OHKN. Supplementary Figure S13 shows EICs for found fragments of UVF265, mass spectrum at RT = 9.94, and the annotated fragments of UVF265. Most likely, UVF265 is a cyclized or double bonded derivative of NAc3OHKN.
The analysis of MS/MS spectrum of UVF281 (m/z 281.1131; Supplementary Fig. S7) indicates that this compound is the O-methylated product of NAc3OHKN. Cyclized or double bonded derivative of this compound, UVF279 (m/z 279.0976, Supplementary Fig. S14), lacks 2 hydrogen atoms and elutes after UVF281.
MS/MS spectra for UVF360-1 and UVF360-2 (m/z 360.1555, Supplementary Fig. S10) points to the O-methylated derivatives of diastereomers Pro-NAc3OHKN-1 and Pro-NAc3OHKN-2, respectively. No cyclized derivatives of these compounds (Δm/z 2.0157, m/z 358.1397) have been detected.
LC-MS ISD fragments of UVF297 are shown in Supplementary Figure S15, whereas no clean MS/MS spectra were available. Consequently, identification of UVF297 structure is quite unreliable, so we provide only its chemical formula and UV-Vis spectrum. UVF293 is likely a derivative of UVF297, lacking four hydrogen atoms (Δm/z 4.0313) and eluting after UVF297. MS/MS spectra and possible structure of UVF293 are shown in Supplementary Figure S8. Cyclized or double bonded derivative of UVF297 lacking 2 hydrogen atoms (Δm/z 2.0157, m/z 295.0925, C13H14N2O6) was found in LC-OD/MS data only in trace amounts (data not shown).
UV spectra of unknown chromophores (see Table 3; Supplementary Fig. S1) confirm proposed above structures: the O-methylation practically does not change the observed electronic absorption spectra, whereas the cyclization (or double bond formation) shifts the absorption maxima of the long-wave band from 360–370 nm to 320–330 nm, which indicates the involvement of the chromophoric center in the chemical modification.
The suggested assignment of signals helps to estimate the concentrations of UV filters, not detected by the NMR method. We assumed that compounds with similar structures and similar UV-Vis spectra have the same absorption coefficients ε360 at 360 nm. In other words, we assumed the same absorption coefficients for the following groups: (a) NAcKN and KN; (b) UVF281, UVF297, and NAc3OHKN; and (c) UVF360 and Pro-NAc3OHKN. Concentrations of KN in the extracts were determined from a calibration curve constructed by injecting known amounts of KN chemical standard into the LC-OD/MS setup. Concentrations of NAcKN, UVF281, and UVF360 were calculated from the ratio of the integrals of the chromatographic peaks of these compounds to their counterparts (KN, NAc3OHKN, and Pro-NAc3OHKN), the concentrations of which were measured by NMR. The obtained values are given in Table 1. The concentrations of UVF223, UVF265, UVF279, and UVF293 were not calculated due to the strong difference between their absorption spectra and those of known UV filters.

## Discussion
The results obtained in this study indicate that genetic separation between the Sciuridae and Cricetidae families results in significant differences in the metabolomic composition of the eye lens. On the PCA plot (see Fig. 3), the conspecific samples are grouped together, whereas phylogenetically distant species are distant on the plot. Earlier, we have already demonstrated the influence of the genetic factor on the metabolomic composition of lenses, and have shown the similarity of metabolomic and phylogenetic dendrograms for 14 species of class Aves (birds).13 Cytoprotective metabolites play an important role in the separation of the Cricetidae and Sciuridae families: the sets of osmolytes, antioxidants, and UV filters in lenses of animals of the families Cricetidae and Sciuridae differ significantly (see Table 2). One can suggest that this difference at least partly originates from the circadian behavior: the squirrels are diurnal animals, whereas the rodents from the Cricetidae family (hamsters and muskrats) are nocturnal. Therefore, during evolution, they did not have a need to develop mechanisms to protect the eye from solar UV radiation. This explains the very low content of UV filters that absorb UV radiation, and ascorbate, which efficiently quenches the reactive triplet states of chromophores formed under radiation,39 in the Golden hamster and muskrat eye lens. Even the difference in main lenticular osmolytes – myo-inositol in squirrels and taurine in rodents of the Cricetidae family – can be attributed to the factor of UV protection: it has been reported40 that myo-inositol can act as a chaperone preventing light-induced crystallin aggregation. Regarding cytoprotective compounds, the metabolomic composition of the hamster and muskrat lenses is somewhat similar to the rat lens: the rats (the family Muridae) are also nocturnal animals. Correspondingly, their lenses also have very low levels of UV filters and ascorbate, and the main osmolytes in the rat lens are taurine and hypotaurine.10
Glutathione (GSH), a common antioxidant of animal tissues, was found in the lenses of all studied species in concentrations up to 2600 nmol/g. Two oxidized forms of glutathione, disulfide GSSG and sulfonate GSO3, were also found. The ratio GSH/GSSG is an important characteristic of the lens redox status. It was shown that lowering GSH/GSSG correlates with aging and cataract development in the human lens.41 In the current work, rodent lenses are characterized by high variability in GSH/GSSG ratio ranging from 0.8 (M. bobak) to 8.3 (M. kastschenkoi). Because we do not have any estimations concerning the age of animals or primary characteristics of their health, it can be suggested that high GSSG levels may correspond to some biological processes, such as aging or lens pathogenesis, similar to those observed in the human lens. Previously, there have been no reports on GSO3 being present in human or animal lenses, but we have found it in high concentrations in rodent lenses. The mechanism of GSO3 formation as well as its effect on GSH metabolism inside the lens needs further research.
Interestingly, the composition of cytoprotective metabolites in the squirrel lens to some extend is similar to that in the human lens,12 including high abundance of kynurenine-based UV filters, antioxidants ascorbate and GSH, and osmolyte myo-inositol. Therefore, squirrels seem to be excellent model animals for studying human ophthalmic diseases such as cataracts. Although the chemical composition of the primate lens14 is closer to the human lens than the squirrel lens, squirrels are animals that are significantly more common, have fewer ethical constraints, and therefore they are more accessible for experimental studies.
The focus of this study was on the identification and quantification of UV filters in the squirrel lens. In previously published papers, it was reported that lenses of some squirrel species are yellow pigmented42 and contain molecular UV filters including NAc3OHKN, 3OHKN, NAcKN32,35 and ProNAc3OHKN.33 In the present study, we observed a total of 16 peaks in the LC-OD profiles of squirrel lens extracts monitored at 330 and 360 nm, corresponding to compounds absorbing in the UVA region. Using combined LC-OD, LC-MS, LC-MS/MS, and 1D and 2D NMR methods, we reliably identified and quantified 8 chromophores, including KN, 3OHKN, NAcKN, and NAc3OHKN, 2 diastereomers of Glu-NAc3OHKN, and 2 diastereomers of Pro-NAc3OHKN. Glu-NAc3OHKN, a UV filter in the animal lens, is reported for the first time. For 7 compounds, UVF223, UVF360-1, UVF360-2, UVF265, UVF281, UVF279, and UVF293, we suggested tentative chemical structures. It should be noted that most of the detected UV filters have absorption maxima at 360 to 370 nm; this indicates that all these compounds have similar KN-like structures of chromophore centers. However, for compounds, UVF223, UVF293, UVF265, and UVF279, the absorption maxima are shifted to 320–330 nm, indicating modifications of chromophore centers caused by double bond formation or cyclization.
NAc3OHKN is the most abundant UV filter in the squirrel lens followed by Pro-NAc3OHKN, 3OHKN, and Glu-NAc3OHKN. The concentrations of other chromophores are too low to make a substantial contribution to the lens absorption in the UVA region. Most likely, they are either the starting compounds for the intracellular synthesis of the main UV filters or the products of their degradation. Taking the typical diameter of a squirrel lens to be 3 mm, and taking typical concentrations and absorption coefficients of UV filters, we can estimate that the optical density of the lens at 360 nm OD360 is approximately 3 to 4, that is, the lens transmits to the retina only 0.01% to 0.1% of the incident UVA light. These estimations are supported by the data from UV-Vis spectra of whole metabolomic lens extracts (see Fig. 4) recorded in a 2 mm optical cuvette: an optical density of about 0.2 was registered for a solution diluted approximately 10 times compared to the intact lens. It is interesting to compare the optical density of the squirrel lens with the human lens. The transmission of the human lens at 360 nm is approximately 0.01%,43 which again indicates the similarity of human and squirrel lenses from this point of view.
The mechanism of formation and biological role of Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN are unclear. Lyons et al.33 incubated NAc3OHKN with proline, and did not detect the formation of Pro-NAc3OHKN; therefore, specific enzymes are possibly needed to form this chromophore. Our data are in line with this hypothesis: significant amounts of Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN were found in the lenses of the Forest-steppe marmot, Ground squirrel, and Bobak marmot, but not in the lenses of the red squirrel and Siberian chipmunk, although the concentrations of NAc3OHKN, proline and glutamate in the lenses of all these animals are similar (see Supplementary Table S1). This observation indicates the enzymatic formation of Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN, and, accordingly, the specific biological role of these unusual metabolites in maintaining lens homeostasis. An enzyme responsible for the addition of Pro to NAc3OHKN33 can probably use Glu as a substrate to produce Glu-NAc3OHKN. On the other hand, it is known that glutamic acid and proline interconvert in vivo. Pro can be produced from Glu via consecutive reactions involving enzymes Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase and pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase.44 Glu, in turn, can be formed by oxidation (breakdown) of Pro by proline oxidase/dehydrogenase44 and L-glutamate γ-semialdehyde dehydrogenase actions.45 Thus, it remains unclear whether Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN were formed independently, or whether one of them is a precursor to the other. Both UV filters were detected within the same species in comparable amounts (see Table 2), but the concentrations of Pro-NAc3OHKN were always somewhat higher.
The biological advantages of Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN compared to parent NAc3OHKN are currently unknown. The most abundant UV filter in the human and primate lens, 3OHKG, is the product of addition of glucose to 3OHKN. This probably makes 3OHKG thermally or photochemically more stable than 3OHKN, and thus less reactive toward lens tissues.20 To elucidate the formation and biological function of Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN, a separate study of their chemical and photochemical properties is required, which we plan to conduct in the near future.
In addition to UV filters, we found surprisingly high concentrations of the well-known neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyrate (GABA) in the lenses of both species from Marmota genus (nΣ = 5; see Supplementary Table S1). Typical GABA concentration in human blood is approximately 0.01 to 0.5 µmol/g,36 in the human brain – approximately 1 to 2 µmol/g,46,47 whereas in Marmota lenses these concentrations are approximately 4 to 5 µmol/g, which is 5 to 50 times higher than concentrations in the lenses of other species, and even several times higher than in the human brain. Obviously, GABA is either required in the Marmota lenses for a yet unknown biological purpose, or it is the product of some biochemical pathway, but we have no feasible explanation for this phenomenon yet. The establishment of the role of GABA in the eye lens requires a separate study.

## Limitations
In the present work, due to ethical constraints and difficulties in collecting samples, several biological species, including Bobak marmot, red squirrel, Siberian chipmunk, and Long-tailed ground squirrel, are represented by only 1 to 2 individual animals. Such small sample groups do not allow to conduct any statistical test for comparative analysis between species.
Sample collection was mostly performed by hunters rather than professional veterinarians or zoologists; that is why it was impossible to assess the age and health status of collected individuals. All collected lenses were frozen at the collection spot and remained frozen until analysis to capture the metabolome in its native state. Therefore, we were unable to detect the lens opacity if it was present.

## Conclusions
The results of this study demonstrate that lenses from the eyes of Golden hamsters and muskrats (family Cricetidae) do not contain UV filters, whereas the lenses from squirrels (family Sciuridae) contain a rich set of UV filters. Apparently, this difference should be attributed to the lifestyle of animals: hamsters are nocturnal, and, therefore, do not require specialized eye protection from solar UV radiation. In contrast, most animals of the family Sciuridae are diurnal, and, through evolution, they have developed a complex system for protection of their eye tissues from UV-light. In the eye lens, this system includes high concentrations of UV filters, antioxidants, and osmolytes, which absorb UV light (NAc3OHKN and its derivatives), quench highly reactive triplet states (ascorbate), and prevent light-induced crystallin aggregation (myo-inositol).
We detected 16 UVA-absorbing compounds in squirrel lenses, with NAc3OHKN being the most abundant UV filter. The lenses of the Bobak marmot, Forest-steppe marmot, and Long-tailed ground squirrel also contain rather high levels of Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN, whereas the lenses of the red squirrel and Siberian chipmunk showed only very low concentrations of these metabolites. It is highly likely that Pro-NAc3OHKN and Glu-NAc3OHKN are synthesized enzymatically within the lens, although their specific biological roles and potential advantages over unmodified NAc3OHKN remain unknown.
The composition of cytoprotective compounds and the UV filtering capacity in the lenses of rodents from the family Sciuridae is notably similar to that of the human lens. This makes squirrels valuable models for studying human ophthalmic diseases, second only to primates, which are much less accessible than squirrels.
