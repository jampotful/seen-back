Neuroprotective Agents for Photoreceptor Rescue Following Experimental Retinal Detachment in Rodents: A Systematic Review

# Neuroprotective Agents for Photoreceptor Rescue Following Experimental Retinal Detachment in Rodents: A Systematic Review

## Abstract
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to systematically evaluate neuroprotective interventions for photoreceptor preservation in rodent models of retinal detachment (RD).MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar identified studies evaluating neuroprotective agents in rodent RD models. The primary outcome was a reduction in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)-positive cells quantified on retinal tissue sections (histology-based); secondary outcomes included preservation of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) and electroretinography (ERG). Risk of bias was assessed using the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) tool.ResultsThirty-six studies (2007–2025) met inclusion criteria: 19 rat, 12 mouse, and 5 combined-species. Interventions included small molecules and recombinant proteins (n = 30), monoclonal antibodies (n = 3), peptides (n = 2), and extracellular vesicle therapy (n = 1). Eight (22.2%) evaluated US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved agents for non-retinal indications. Anti-inflammatory interventions achieved the highest median TUNEL reduction (84.5%, interquartile range [IQR] = 62.3%–88.1%), followed by anti-inflammatory/antioxidant approaches (70.8%, IQR = 54.3%–76.0%), metabolic modulators (59.6%, IQR = 42.9%–64.8%), and anti-apoptotic agents (59.0%, IQR = 56.6%–69.3%). Between-group differences were not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.32). Quantitative ERG data were available in only six studies (16.7%). The risk of bias was largely unclear across selection and performance domains, with assessor blinding most reported (63.9% low risk). Ten studies additionally evaluated genetically modified backgrounds as complementary mechanistic strata (n = 11 experiments).ConclusionsInflammation-targeted therapies showed the most consistent numerical photoreceptor rescue across rodent RD studies. With eight studies evaluating FDA-approved agents, the field has clear repurposing opportunities; closing the translational gap will require rigorously designed, functionally oriented preclinical trials that reflect clinically realistic treatment delays.
Retinal detachment (RD) is a condition in which the neuroretina separates from the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). It is a relatively common condition, with an estimated occurrence of 100 to 150 per million people.1,2 It disrupts the symbiosis between the RPE and the photoreceptors, disrupting the transport of nutrients, the oxygen tension, the phagocytic activity of the RPE, and the visual cycle of chromophore regeneration.3–5 Modern surgical techniques, such as vitreoretinal surgery and scleral buckling, can achieve an anatomic reattachment rate of more than 90%.6,7 However, the visual recovery remains poor, with a median postoperative visual acuity of 0.30 logMAR despite anatomic reattachment.8
A meta-analysis has shown that visual recovery is inversely proportional to the duration of the detachment, with a significant improvement in visual acuity when surgery is performed within the first 3 days.9,10 However, surgery is often performed several days after the onset of symptoms, often after the peak of photoreceptor apoptosis on days 2 and 3.11 Secondary degeneration of the photoreceptors continues after reattachment, limiting visual recovery.12 Between diagnosis and surgery, a window of opportunity for neuroprotection opens.
Over the last 2 decades, preclinical studies have tested strategies to prevent photoreceptor death after experimental RD. The method of injection of sodium hyaluronate into the subretinal space has been found to be the best, as it produces a detachment with a well-defined pathology, similar to that of the human eye.13,14 Anti-apoptotics, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidants, and metabolic modifiers have been tested.15–20 In spite of this, no neuroprotective agent has been approved for use in the treatment of RD, indicating a lack of translation from the laboratory to the clinic. The number and diversity of the preclinical studies call for a synthesis of the available data. Until now, no systematic review has been carried out of all the neuroprotective agents tested in rodent models of RD, comparing their efficacy and assessing their methodological quality using a validated risk-of-bias tool. Narrative reviews have not been able to compare the different therapeutic approaches.3,21
Using the MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases, we conducted a systematic review of the neuroprotective agents tested in animal models of RD. We compared the efficacy of these neuroprotective agents using a standardized outcome measure. We assessed the methodological quality of the animal studies using the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) animal study risk of bias tool.

## Methods

## Protocol Registration and Eligibility
This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251177792) on December 17, 2025, prior to data extraction and analysis. Literature searches and screening were conducted prior to registration.
Studies were included if they: (1) used rodent models (rat or mouse) of experimental RD; (2) administered any pharmacological or biological agent intended to be neuroprotective; (3) included a concurrent control group (vehicle, placebo, or no treatment) subjected to the same RD model; (4) reported quantitative outcome measures for photoreceptor survival or death, including terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay quantified on retinal tissue sections (histology-based), outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness, or electroretinography (ERG); and (5) were peer-reviewed articles published in English.
Studies were excluded if they: (1) were in vitro, ex vivo, or computational studies without in vivo validation; (2) used non-rodent animal models; (3) were reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, or conference abstracts without full data; (4) lacked quantitative outcome measures for photoreceptor survival; (5) investigated only surgical techniques without pharmacological intervention; or (6) compared only active interventions without a vehicle or no-treatment control; or (7) reported photoreceptor apoptosis or cell-death outcomes only by flow cytometry of dissociated retinal cells (including flow-cytometric TUNEL or apoptosis-marker quantification such as cleaved caspase) without histology-based section quantification on retinal tissue, to ensure methodological consistency across the synthesis; or (8) used genetic deletion or knockout as the sole intervention without administration of a pharmacological or biological neuroprotective agent, to maintain the agent-intervention focus of this synthesis (transgenic comparisons were retained only when reported within otherwise eligible agent-intervention studies and were treated as complementary mechanistic strata rather than primary interventions).

## Literature Search and Selection
A systematic literature search was conducted across 4 electronic databases from inception through April 25, 2025: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, and Google Scholar (first 200 results). The search strategy used a three-domain approach combining Medical Subject Headings, Emtree terms, and free-text keywords (see the full Boolean syntax in the Supplementary Methods):•Domain 1 (Animal Model): rodent, rat, mouse, mice, and murine.•Domain 2 (Condition): “retinal detachment,” “detached retina,” “retinal separation.”•Domain 3 (Intervention/Outcome): neuroprotect*, “neuroprotection,” “neuroprotective,” “neuroprotectant,” “neuronal protection,” “photoreceptor survival,” “retinal neuroprotection,” “neuroprotective agents,” “neuroprotective therapy.”

Database-specific search strings were constructed using Boolean operators (AND, OR) and truncation symbols adapted for each database's syntax. No date restrictions were applied.
Records were managed and screened in Covidence systematic review software by two independent reviewers (authors K.G.B. and D.B.). Screening proceeded through title/abstract and full-text phases. Inter-rater agreement was substantial for abstract screening (κ = 0.803) and perfect for full-text screening (κ = 1.0). All conflicts were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers without requiring third-party adjudication. Of 46 full-text articles assessed, 36 met the inclusion criteria.

## Data Extraction and Risk of Bias
Two reviewers (authors K.G.B. and D.B.) independently extracted data using a pre-piloted, standardized extraction form. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus. Data were extracted directly from text, tables, and figures; when outcome data were presented only in graphical format, WebPlotDigitizer was used to extract numerical values.22
Extracted variables included: bibliographic details (first author, year, and journal); study design (randomization, blinding, and sample size per group); animal model characteristics (species, strain, sex, age, method of RD induction, and duration of detachment); intervention details (agent name, dose, route, timing, and frequency); and outcome measures (TUNEL-positive cell counts, ONL thickness, and ERG amplitudes). Pharmacological details, including US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval status and mechanism of action, were also recorded.
Risk of bias was assessed using the SYRCLE tool for animal studies, comprising 10 domains: selection bias (sequence generation, baseline characteristics, and allocation concealment), performance bias (random housing, investigator blinding, and random outcome assessment), detection bias (assessor blinding), attrition bias, reporting bias, and other biases.23 Each domain was scored as 0 (low risk), 1 (unclear risk), or 2 (high risk). Results were summarized descriptively by domain rather than by aggregate numerical scoring, consistent with SYRCLE methodology recommendations.

## Data Analysis
The primary outcome was the percentage reduction in TUNEL, calculated as [(TUNEL_Control − TUNEL_Treated)/TUNEL_Control] × 100; complete calculations for all interventions are provided in Supplementary Tables S1a to S1c. TUNEL outcomes were harmonized by computing percent reduction relative to control; day 3 post-detachment was preferentially selected a priori for temporal consistency, corresponding to the peak of photoreceptor apoptosis reported across experimental RD models. When day 3 data were unavailable (n = 4 pharmacological and n = 4 transgenic), outcomes were extracted at the nearest reported timepoint and annotated accordingly in the figures and supplementary tables. Secondary outcomes included ONL thickness (mean difference in micrometers or percentage preservation) and ERG amplitudes (a-wave and b-wave, mean difference in microvolts).
Interventions were classified by primary mechanism of action: anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory/ antioxidant (dual mechanism), metabolic modulation, or other. Interventions were categorized by primary mechanism as stated in the original study; overlap with secondary pathways is acknowledged. Metabolic Modulator interventions target cellular metabolism, including energy metabolism (glycolysis and pyruvate kinase activation), iron homeostasis/ferroptosis inhibition, and hypoxia-related pathways (hypoxia-inducible factor [HIF] stabilization). Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12 (scipy, pandas, and matplotlib). Non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis) were used for between-group comparisons given the non-normal distribution of effect sizes. Correlations were assessed using Spearman's rank coefficient.
Planned subgroup analyses examined: species (rat versus mouse), transgenic status (wild type versus genetically modified), route of administration (subretinal, intravitreal, intraperitoneal, and oral), treatment timing relative to detachment, and risk of bias level.

## Results

## Study Selection
The systematic search yielded 449 records across 4 databases. After removing 141 duplicates, 308 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 262 were excluded based on predetermined criteria. A full-text assessment of 46 articles identified 36 studies that met all eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). Ten full-text articles were excluded, with the most common reason being lack of full-text availability (n = 9), followed by use of non-rodent models (n = 1). Inter-rater agreement was substantial for abstract screening (κ = 0.803) and perfect for full-text screening (κ = 1.0).

## Study and Intervention Characteristics
The 36 included studies17,18,24–57 (2007–2025) were authored by 32 unique first authors and 23 unique senior authors (see the Table). Species distribution comprised rat (n = 19, 52.8%), mouse (n = 12, 33.3%), and both (n = 5, 13.9%); 10 studies (27.8%) used genetically modified models, evaluated as complementary mechanistic strata within otherwise eligible agent-intervention studies (see Supplementary Table S1c). RD was induced predominantly via subretinal sodium hyaluronate injection (one study used carboxymethylcellulose), with day 3 post-detachment as the predominant assessment timepoint (88.9%). Interventions included small molecules and recombinant proteins (n = 30), monoclonal antibodies (n = 3), peptides (n = 2), and extracellular vesicle therapy (n = 1), administered via intraperitoneal (36.1%), subretinal (30.6%), intravitreal (13.9%), or oral (13.9%) routes (Fig. 2). Eight studies (22.2%) evaluated agents with FDA approval for non-retinal indications, representing potential repurposing candidates.

## Therapeutic Efficacy by Mechanism of Action
Twenty-seven intervention arms from 36 studies provided quantifiable TUNEL data for the primary synthesis (Fig. 3, see Supplementary Table S1a); 10 additional intervention arms were excluded due to use of alternative endpoints (n = 5), non-quantifiable TUNEL data (n = 3), or function as mechanistic probes demonstrating worsened outcomes (n = 2; see Supplementary Table S1b). Of note, some studies contributed both included and excluded intervention arms. Anti-inflammatory interventions (n = 6) achieved the highest median TUNEL reduction at 84.5% (interquartile range [IQR] = 62.3%–88.1%), followed by combined anti-inflammatory/antioxidant approaches (n = 7, 70.8%, IQR = 54.3%–76.0%), metabolic modulators (n = 4, 59.6%, IQR = 42.9%–64.8%), and anti-apoptotic agents (n = 8, 59.0%, IQR = 56.6%–69.3%). Two interventions using other mechanisms achieved a median of 50.2% (values 32.7% and 67.6%). Despite the 25.5 percentage-point difference between anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic categories, the between-group comparison did not reach statistical significance (Kruskal-Wallis H = 4.65, P = 0.32). Given heterogeneity and small category sizes, these numerical medians should be interpreted as exploratory signals rather than definitive rankings. Notably, necrostatin-1 was evaluated in two studies targeting the necroptosis pathway via RIPK1 inhibition,29,35 however, Dong et al. used propidium iodide (PI)-based membrane integrity endpoints rather than TUNEL (see Supplementary Table S1b). An additional study reported suppression of necroptosis markers with rapamycin-based therapy via autophagy activation (see Supplementary Table S1b).46

## Secondary Outcome Measures
ONL thickness or cell count data were reported in 35 studies (97.2%); interventions achieving >70% TUNEL reduction generally preserved ONL thickness, with anti-inflammatory approaches showing sustained protection in long-term studies (>1 month). Quantitative ERG data were available in only 6 studies (16.7%), precluding robust assessment of functional outcomes; among these, interventions achieving >60% TUNEL reduction generally preserved a-wave and b-wave amplitudes.

## Subgroup Analyses
Planned subgroup analyses examined species, transgenic status, route of administration, and treatment timing as potential sources of heterogeneity; only the species comparison revealed a statistically significant difference. Among the 27 intervention arms, arms from mouse studies (n = 11) showed significantly higher median TUNEL reduction than arms from rat studies (n = 12, 77.1% vs. 61.5%, P = 0.039); 4 arms from studies using both species were excluded. Analysis of 11 individual genetically modified model experiments (10 studies) revealed heterogeneous responses: monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)−/− (+82.6%) and LPR (+75.0%) models demonstrated the highest TUNEL reductions, whereas interleukin-6 (IL-6)−/− (−113.4%) and heat shock protein 70 (HSP70)−/− (−205.6%) exhibited increased photoreceptor death, confirming complex pathway interactions where eliminating individual mediators of inflammation or stress response may disrupt compensatory protective mechanisms (Fig. 4). Among administration routes, subretinal delivery achieved the highest numerical efficacy (68.5%) versus oral the lowest (51.1%), but differences were not significant (P = 0.861). Most studies (n = 32, 88.9%) initiated treatment concurrent with detachment induction; 3 studies examining therapeutic windows reported reduced efficacy when treatment was delayed beyond 24 hours.

## Risk of Bias Assessment
Risk of bias was assessed using the SYRCLE tool across 10 domains for all 36 studies, and results were summarized descriptively by domain (Supplementary Table S2). Assessor blinding (Q7) was the most frequently reported safeguard (63.9% low risk). However, randomization was documented in only 5.6% of studies, and neither allocation concealment nor investigator blinding was reported in any. This pattern of poor reporting across the selection and performance bias domains limits confidence in internal validity, consistent with the broader preclinical literature.58,59

## Publication Trends
Publication frequency increased over time, with 39% of studies published since 2019. Early studies (2007–2012) focused on single-target anti-apoptotic interventions, whereas recent investigations increasingly explored novel mechanisms, including ferroptosis inhibition, metabolic reprogramming, and extracellular vesicle therapeutics.

## Discussion
This systematic review of 36 preclinical studies (2007–2025) yielded 3 main results: (1) anti-inflammatory treatments showed the highest numerical median TUNEL reduction (84.5%), although the between-group comparison was not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.32) and should be interpreted as an exploratory signal; (2) 8 studies (22.2%) evaluated agents with FDA approval for non-retinal indications, indicating opportunities for drug repurposing; and (3) despite 36 preclinical studies over 18 years, none has been approved for clinical use, indicating a persistent translational gap.
The high degree of efficacy of the various treatments, such as neutralization of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; 92.1%), inhibition of MCP-1 (88.9%), inhibition of macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) (85.7%), and depletion of microglia with PLX3397 (83.3% reduction in acute TUNEL, but no improvement in long-term photoreceptor survival)56 suggests that the upstream immune modulation may interfere with several death-effector pathways simultaneously. This interpretation is strengthened by the finding that not only microglia, but also peripheral monocytes and neutrophils, actively promote photoreceptor death,60 and that the RPE itself mounts an innate immune defense and recruits immune cells to the subretinal space.61 The numerically higher efficacy of anti-inflammatory agents (median TUNEL reduction 84.5% vs. 59.0% for anti-apoptotics) is in line with the accumulating evidence that the innate immune response, microglial activation, and monocyte infiltration, is a central driver of photoreceptor death in retinal degenerative conditions, including RD.3,62 The TREM-2-deficient mice showed accelerated photoreceptor death due to impaired microglial phagocytic clearance and compensatory infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils.63 Moreover, inhibition of CSF-1R with PLX5622 worsened the acute outcome in the Okunuki et al. study, indicating that microglia are neuroprotective in the immediate post-RD period.64 However, the immune modulation must be precisely calibrated rather than broadly suppressive. TNF-α activates caspase-8-dependent apoptosis32 and regulates photoreceptor autophagy,65 blurring the distinction between the two mechanistic categories.
Pan-caspase inhibition paradoxically increases RIP kinase-mediated necroptosis, a compensatory switch demonstrated by Trichonas et al. and Dong et al.29,35 The relative ineffectiveness of anti-apoptotics reinforces the need for upstream intervention. Moreover, photoreceptor death after RD involves at least four overlapping pathways: apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis,29,55,66–68 with the FSP1-CoQ10-NADH and GSH-GPx4 axes identified as protective mechanisms against ferroptosis in the RPE.69,70 This apoptosis-necroptosis crosstalk extends beyond RD: RIP3-mediated microglial necroptosis drives neuroinflammation in diabetic retinopathy,71 and the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL axis contributes to ganglion cell death in glaucoma.72 Qin et al. provided direct experimental evidence that simultaneous inhibition of apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis improved ganglion cell survival beyond the effects of single-pathway inhibition.73 Deferiprone, which we have included in this review as an example of iron-mediated ferroptosis, exemplifies this expanding mechanistic landscape.57 These observations help to explain the greater efficacy of anti-inflammatory therapies that modulate the inflammatory milieu rather than inhibiting individual death effectors. The paradoxical photoreceptor death in IL-6−/− and HSP70−/− knockout mice further illustrates this complexity: the elimination of individual mediators disrupts compensatory protective networks whose net effect is beneficial.
Eight studies (22.2%) evaluated 10 drugs (9 distinct interventions, including one combination of nelfinavir and ritonavir) already approved by the FDA for nonretinal indications (2 studies each tested 2 approved drugs), including minocycline,27,74,75 edaravone, erythropoietin, selumetinib, pexidartinib, deferiprone, nelfinavir, and ritonavir (used in combination), doxazosin, and guanabenz. However, the translation of these preclinical data into clinical practice is hampered by the specificity of these drugs. These examples illustrate that FDA approval alone is insufficient to predict ophthalmic translational feasibility; each agent must be evaluated for its pharmacological profile, ocular safety, and suitability for the surgical workflow that RD requires. ONL1204 (xelafaslatide), a first-in-class small peptide Fas-receptor antagonist with orphan drug designation for macula-off RD, is the only drug from our review to have reached a clinical trial (NCT05730218); the contrast-sensitivity primary endpoint was not met at week 24,76 although a subgroup analysis of pseudophakic patients showed a 1 to 2 line improvement in visual acuity; a phase Ib study demonstrated favorable safety in geographic atrophy.77 Doxazosin is associated with intraoperative floppy iris syndrome, complicating the cataract surgery often required after RD surgery78; and deferiprone carries a 1% to 2% risk of agranulocytosis, requiring weekly monitoring.57,79 The fact that only a single agent has reached a clinical trial after nearly 2 decades of preclinical research is itself a measure of the translational gap.
Schmidt-Pogoda et al. found that 69% of preclinical stroke studies reported positive results, whereas only 6% of phase III clinical trials did.80 This discrepancy is not unique to RD, but reflects a systemic failure in neuroprotection research that requires structural explanation.81 In ophthalmology, two phase III trials of memantine for neuroprotection in glaucoma (2298 patients, 48-month follow-up) failed to show efficacy,82 resulting in the pharmaceutical industry's reluctance to engage with neuroprotection.83 Tsilidis et al. confirmed this pattern in 4445 animal studies of 6 different neurodegenerative diseases, finding a nearly twofold excess of statistically significant results.84
Nevertheless, several of the compounds in our review are advancing through the clinical trial process in adjacent retinal conditions, providing translational context. Oral ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), the parent compound of tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA; 50.5% TUNEL reduction in our review), has been shown to cross the blood-retina barrier in patients with retinitis pigmentosa (phase 1; NCT02841306), and a recent systematic review has confirmed TUDCA's neuroprotective effects in a wide range of retinal disease models.85 Kamuvudine-8, related to kamuvudine-9 evaluated in our review,54 has been found to be well tolerated in a phase 1 trial for the treatment of geographic atrophy (NCT06164587).86 These developments, together with systematic evidence of neuroprotective efficacy in other retinal degenerative conditions,87,88 suggest that the mechanistic pathways identified in RD neuroprotection research may have broader translational relevance across retinal degenerative conditions.83 The emerging paradigm of multi-drug combinations, as demonstrated by Leinonen et al. with tamsulosin, metoprolol, and bromocriptine for mutation-agnostic photoreceptor protection,89 may prove to be more effective than single-agent approaches, given the convergent death pathways identified here.

## Strengths and Limitations
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review of neuroprotection in RD models, using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool.
The limitations of this review are: (i) the exclusive use of rodent models, which limits anatomic translation because of the absence of the macula, but rodent models are the standard platform for mechanistic neuroprotection research; (ii) the reliance on TUNEL, which primarily detects apoptosis, but necroptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis can also produce TUNEL-positive signal, which may under-represent the protection afforded by non-apoptotic agents; (iii) limited ERG data, which prevents robust assessment of function; (iv) the mechanistic classification is inherently reductive; for example, Fas signaling participates in inflammatory cell recruitment beyond apoptosis initiation, and TNF-α engages caspase-8-dependent apoptosis beyond its inflammatory role; (v) the Domain 3 search terms were neuroprotection-specific, which may miss mechanistic RD studies not framed as neuroprotection; the four candidate publications named by the reviewer were nevertheless excluded by independent eligibility criteria (intervention type or outcome modality).

## Conclusions
This systematic review of 36 preclinical studies (2007–2025) demonstrates that anti-inflammatory interventions achieve the highest numerical photoreceptor rescue in rodent RD models. Bridging the persistent translational gap will require preclinical trials that incorporate functional outcome measures, clinically realistic treatment delays, and standardized reporting practices to advance neuroprotective strategies toward clinical application.

## Figure Figure 1.: PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection. A systematic search across four databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar) yielded 449 records. After duplicate removal (n = 141) and screening, 36 studies met the inclusion criteria. Inter-rater agreement was substantial for abstract screening (κ = 0.803) and perfect for full-text screening (κ = 1.0).

Donut chart showing the distribution of administration routes across 36 studies: intraperitoneal (36.1%, n = 13), subretinal (30.6%, n = 11), intravitreal (13.9%, n = 5), oral (13.9%, n = 5), retro-orbital injection (2.8%, n = 1), and combined subretinal/intraperitoneal (2.8%, n = 1).

## Figure Figure 3.: TUNEL reduction by mechanism of action. Box plots showing median TUNEL reduction (%) with IQR for each mechanism category. Diamonds indicate mean values; individual data points represent single interventions. Anti-inflammatory interventions (n = 6) achieved the highest median efficacy (84.5%, IQR = 62.3%–88.1%), followed by anti-inflammatory/antioxidant (n = 7, 70.8%), anti-apoptotic agents (n = 8, 59.0%), metabolic modulators (n = 4, 59.6%), and other mechanisms (n = 2, 50.2%). Differences between categories were not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.32). Sample sizes shown in parentheses on x-axis labels. Data reflect day 3 post-detachment unless indicated. †Tyr-Val-Ala-Asp (YVAD, a caspase-1 inhibitor) (day 1), BNN27 (day 1), recombinant nerve growth factor (day 4), and human transferrin (day 7). Necrostatin-1 + Z-VAD-FMK (Trichonas et al., targeting RIPK1 and pan-caspase inhibition) is annotated within its primary mechanism category.

## Figure Figure 4.: TUNEL reduction in genetically modified models. (A) Box plots comparing TUNEL reduction between anti-inflammatory (n = 5), anti-apoptotic (n = 5), and metabolic modulator (n = 1) mechanism categories in genetically modified models. Median TUNEL reduction was 51.4% (anti-inflammatory), 54.2% (anti-apoptotic), and 56.3% (metabolic modulator); a Mann-Whitney U comparison between the two largest groups (anti-inflammatory versus anti-apoptotic) yielded P = 1.000, with small group sizes limiting power to detect between-category differences. (B) Individual genetically modified models ranked by TUNEL reduction. Positive values indicate photoreceptor protection; negative values indicate increased cell death compared with wild-type controls. Color coding: teal = anti-inflammatory mechanism, orange = anti-apoptotic mechanism, and pink = metabolic modulator. Notable findings include the highest protection in MCP-1−/− mice (+82.6%) and paradoxical increases in photoreceptor death in IL-6−/− and HSP70−/− knockout models, demonstrating that eliminating individual mediators of inflammation or stress response can disrupt endogenous protective mechanisms. Data reflect Day 3 post-detachment unless indicated. †Assessed at a different timepoint: hTF (Day 7), Rip3−/− (2015) (Day 1), MIFKO (Day 14), and Nlrp3−/− (Day 1).
