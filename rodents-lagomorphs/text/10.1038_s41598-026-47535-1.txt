# In vivo and ex vivo evaluation of the responses of rat Müller glia after injury

## Abstract
AbstractRetinal ex vivo systems have been widely used to investigate diverse physiological and pathological processes in the retina. Although retinal ex vivo systems are generally considered to approximate in vivo environment of the retina, careful evaluation of how ex vivo conditions affect cellular function is necessary for accurate interpretation of such data. Here we analyzed the responses of retinal Müller glia to photoreceptor injury in vivo and ex vivo to assess the effects of ex vivo culture on the phenotypes of Müller glia in pathological contexts. A rat model of photoreceptor injury was generated by intraperitoneal injection of methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) and retinal ex vivo cultures were prepared 1 or 2 days after MMS treatment. A substantial number of Müller glia die by apoptosis after injury-induced proliferation in vivo, but not ex vivo. While the pattern of Müller glia proliferation was similar between the models, the extent of phagocytic activity, migration, DNA damage response, and neurogenic factor expression varied across the models. These findings reveal previously unrecognized effects of ex vivo culture on Müller glia in disease contexts, highlighting the need for caution when assessing injury-induced phenotypes of Müller glia using retinal ex vivo systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47535-1.Subject terms: Diseases, Neuroscience

## Introduction
In the diseased retina, Müller glia show a variety of reactive responses, collectively called gliosis, which include hypertrophy, upregulation of intermediate filaments, proliferation, migration, phagocytosis, as well as dedifferentiation to progenitor-like phenotype1,2. In lower vertebrates like zebrafish, neurogenic responses are dominant, generating highly proliferative neuronal progenitors which completely regenerate lost retinal neurons3,4. In contrast, mammalian Müller glia have only limited proliferative and neurogenic potentials and maintain glial identity after injury5. The presence of intrinsic regulatory mechanisms restricting neurogenic competence of mammalian Müller glia has been reported6,7, and recent evidence also suggests involvement of microglia/macrophages as a barrier to retinal regeneration in mammals8–10.
Most of the recent studies on the neurogenic potential of mammalian Müller glia have been conducted in mice. However, Müller glia in adult mice, especially the commonly used B6 strain, virtually lack proliferative and neurogenic potentials in response to injury in vivo11,12, and overexpression of mitogenic or neurogenic factors are required to stimulate the regenerative response of mouse Müller glia13–16. In contrast to mouse Müller glia, rat Müller glia are able to proliferate and express proneural transcription factor Ngn2 after injury in vivo17–19, indicating that rat Müller glia may have higher neurogenic potential than mouse Müller glia. However, cell cycle reentry of rat Müller glia is followed by DNA damage response, which likely induces cell cycle exit and cell death of Müller glia17. Recent evidence also suggests that cell cycle progression of Müller glia is associated with upregulation of gliogenic transcription factor Nfia, which may in turn promote gliosis and quiescence of Müller glia18. Thus, while the proliferation of Müller glia drives retinal regeneration in zebrafish4,20, whether a similar process occurs in mammals remains unclear.
Retinal ex vivo cultures, which allow pharmacological or genetic manipulation while maintaining in vivo-like tissue architecture of the retina, have been widely used to investigate diverse physiological and pathological processes in the retina. By using pharmacological inhibitors in this system, we previously showed that the proliferative and phagocytic activities of rat Müller glia are regulated by common molecular mechanisms involving phosphatidylserine recognition and Rac1 activation21. We also reported that expression of neurogenic factors, such as Ngn2 and Ascl1, and gliosis marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), as well as the migratory behavior of Müller glia are all activated in a cell-cycle dependent manner19. These findings suggest that the apparently unrelated processes of Müller glia induced by injury are mechanistically linked to the cell cycle. Although retinal ex vivo systems have been generally considered to approximate in vivo environment of the retina22–24, the conditions inherent to ex vivo preparation, such as separation from the retinal pigment epithelium or transection of the optic nerve, may interfere with the viability and/or function of Müller glia. Furthermore, when ex vivo cultures are conducted after inducing retinal injury, the timing of tissue harvesting may affect the responses of Müller glia. A thorough evaluation of the similarities and differences in the responses of Müller glia between ex vivo and in vivo systems as well as between different ex vivo models is crucial for the effective application of ex vivo systems for Müller glia research.
In the present study, we comparatively analyzed the responses of rat Müller glia to chemically induced photoreceptor injury across the in vivo and two ex vivo models. We found that a substantial number of Müller glia die by apoptosis after injury-induced proliferation in vivo, but not ex vivo. While the pattern of Müller glia proliferation was similar between the in vivo and ex vivo models, the extent of phagocytic activity, migration, DNA damage response, and neurogenic factor expression varied across the models. These data illustrate previously unrecognized effects of ex vivo culture on injury-induced responses of Müller glia, underscoring the need for careful interpretation of the phenotypes of Müller glia under pathological conditions when using retinal ex vivo systems.

## Results

## Apoptosis and phagocytic response in the rat retinas after MMS-induced injury in vivo and ex vivo
We started our analysis by TUNEL assay to evaluate apoptotic cell death of photoreceptors and their phagocytic elimination in the rat retinas after intraperitoneal injection of methyl methanesulfonate (MMS)25,26 in the in vivo and ex vivo models. Most photoreceptors in the outer nuclear layer (ONL) became TUNEL+ by 2 days post-injection (dpi) and these TUNEL+ cells were almost completely cleared by 3 dpi (Fig. 1A), consistent with the previous evidence that dead photoreceptors are quickly phagocytosed by Müller glia in the rat retinas after methyl nitrosourea (MNU) treatment21. However, we unexpectedly observed many TUNEL+ cells reappear in the ONL at 4 dpi and these TUNEL+ cells were mostly eliminated by 7 dpi (Fig. 1A). The late occurrence of TUNEL+ cells in the ONL may indicate the death of photoreceptors which survived the early toxic effects of MMS. Alternatively, these cells may be Müller glia or microglia/macrophages, which migrate to the ONL and phagocytose cell debris. To identify the TUNEL+ cell types at 4 dpi, we conducted TUNEL in combination with immunofluorescence for cell type markers. While no colabeling was observed between TUNEL and photoreceptor marker Crx (Fig. 1B), some TUNEL+ nuclei were colabeled with Müller glia marker Sox9 (Fig. 1C). Cell counting revealed that approximately half of TUNEL+ cells (49.4 ± 4.8%, mean ± SEM, n = 3) were Sox9+. TUNEL also colabeled with Iba1, the microglia/macrophage marker, suggesting apoptosis of this cell type (Fig. 1D). However, because Iba1 is a cytoplasmic marker, it is also likely that TUNEL+ nuclei of other cell types were ingested by Iba1 + microglia/macrophages. We next conducted TUNEL assay in the ex vivo retinas. The retinas were explanted at 1 dpi (1 dpi ex) or 2 dpi (2 dpi ex) and each cultured for 1 to 3 days ex vivo (dev) until analysis (Fig. 1E). When 1 dpi ex retinas were analyzed by TUNEL, most photoreceptors in the ONL were intensely labeled, indicating that ex vivo cultures did not interfere with MMS-induced apoptosis of photoreceptors (Fig. 1F). However, in contrast to the in vivo situation, TUNEL+ photoreceptors persisted in the ONL at 2 dev (corresponding to 3 dpi) and 3 dev (corresponding to 4 dpi), indicating impaired phagocytosis of photoreceptor debris (Fig. 1F). Photoreceptor debris were eventually lost by 6 dev in the 1 dpi ex retinas (Supplementary Fig. S1). In contrast, the 2 dpi ex retinas showed almost complete elimination of TUNEL+ photoreceptors by 1 dev (corresponding to 3 dpi), consistent with the in vivo retinas. We also performed TUNEL assay combined with Sox9 immunofluorescence to confirm the presence of apoptotic Müller glia ex vivo. However, we failed to find TUNEL+ Müller glia in both 1 dpi ex and 2 dpi ex retinas at the timing of 4 dpi (Fig. 1G). Taken together, we observed TUNEL+ Müller glia at 4 dpi in vivo but not ex vivo. Furthermore, ex vivo cultures starting at 1 dpi impaired phagocytic elimination of dead photoreceptors.

## Cell cycle reentry and migration of Müller glia after MMS-induced injury in vivo and ex vivo
We next analyzed cell cycle reentry and migration of Müller glia after MMS-induced injury in vivo and ex vivo. We conducted triple immunofluorescence for Sox9, phospho-pRb (ppRb, pan-cell cycle marker), and phospho-histone H3 (pH3, M phase marker). In the in vivo retinas, ppRb+ proliferating Müller glia were absent in the control and 1 dpi retinas, but increased drastically by 2 dpi, when 97.4 ± 0.13% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) of Müller glia were ppRb+ (Fig. 2A and B). ppRb+ Müller glia then decreased to 19.5 ± 2.1% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) by 3 dpi and disappeared by 4 dpi (Fig. 2A and B). pH3+ Müller glia were found only at 3 dpi (Fig. 2A and B). The proportion of Sox9+ Müller glia located in the outer plexiform layer (OPL) or ONL, representing injury-induced glial migration, significantly increased to approximately 67.3 ± 0.5% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) by 3 dpi and remained the equivalent level at 4 dpi (Fig. 2A and C). Müller glia in the ex vivo retinas showed expression patterns of cell cycle markers similar to those in vivo. In the 1 dpi ex retinas, most Müller glia were ppRb + at 1 dev (corresponding to 2 dpi) (Fig. 2D). The proportion of ppRb+ Müller glia decreased to approximately 29.8 ± 4.8% (mean ± SEM, n = 6) by 2 dev (corresponding to 3 dpi) followed by further decrease by 3 dev (corresponding to 4 dpi) (Fig. 2D and E). In the 2 dpi ex retinas, the proportion of ppRb+ Müller glia was approximately 30.0% ± 4.3% (mean ± SEM, n = 6) at 1 dev (corresponding to 3 dpi) and virtually no ppRb+ Müller glia were observed at 2 dev and 3 dev (corresponding to 4 dpi and 5 dpi, respectively) (Fig. 2D and E). The occurrence of pH3+ Müller glia at 2 dev in the 1 dpi ex retinas and at 1 dev in the 2 dpi ex retinas (both corresponding to 3 dpi) was consistent with the timing in vivo (Fig. 2D and F). When ectopic Müller glia were counted, approximately 30–40% of Müller glia were located in the OPL/ONL at the timing of 3 dpi and 4 dpi in both 1 dpi ex and 2 dpi ex retinas, indicating that significantly lower proportion of Müller glia migrated to the site of injury in the ex vivo retinas compared to in vivo (p < 0.01, Fig. 2D and G).We also examined the cell cycle reentry of Müller glia ex vivo by continuous exposure to EdU. Vast majority of Müller glia incorporated EdU by 3 dpi in both 1 dpi ex (90.0 ± 3.6%, mean ± SEM, n = 6) and 2 dpi ex retinas (97.1 ± 0.7% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) (Fig. 2H and I). There were no statistically significant differences in the proportions of ppRb+, pH3+, or EdU+ Müller glia as well as displaced Müller glia when the 1 dpi ex and 2 dpi ex retinas were compared at the timing of 3 dpi and 4 dpi (Fig. 2E-G and I). We also examined glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in Müller glia in the ex vivo retinas to confirm injury-induced reactivity of Müller glia. GFAP+ Müller glia processes extending across the thickness of the retina were clearly visualized at 3 dpi in both 1 dpi ex and 2 dpi ex retinas (Supplementary Fig. S2). Taken together, while cell cycle activity of Müller glia was similar across the in vivo and two ex vivo models, migration of Müller glia toward the ONL was inhibited ex vivo.

## DNA damage response in Müller glia after MMS-induced injury in vivo and ex vivo
We previously reported that rat Müller glia exhibit the DNA damage response such as phosphorylation of histone H2AX (γH2AX) and the activation of the p53 pathway in association with cell cycle reentry after MNU-induced injury17. We thus analyzed γH2AX immunoreactivity in Müller glia after MMS-induced injury in vivo and ex vivo. γH2AX immunoreactivity occurred in many Müller glia by 2 dpi and peaked in intensity by 3 dpi in vivo (Fig. 3A). γH2AX+ Müller glia substantially decreased in number by 4 dpi, but some Müller glia were still intensely positive for γH2AX (Fig. 3A). Because we observed TUNEL+ Müller glia at 4 dpi (Fig. 1A and C), we examined the possibility that the same subset of Müller glia are positive for both γH2AX and TUNEL. We thus conducted TUNEL assay together with immunofluorescence for Sox9 and γH2AX in the 4 dpi retinas. 31.0 ± 1.8% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) of Müller glia were labeled for γH2AX or TUNEL; however, only 3.8 ± 0.9% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) of Müller glia were found positive for both γH2AX and TUNEL (Fig. 3B and C). In the 1 dpi ex retinas, most Müller glia were negative or only weakly labeled for γH2AX while dead photoreceptors remaining in the ONL were intensely γH2AX+ (Fig. 3D). In contrast, in the 2 dpi ex retinas, many Müller glia intensely positive for γH2AX were observed at 1 and 2 dev (3 and 4 dpi, respectively) displaying γH2AX immunoreactivity similar to the in vivo retinas (Fig. 3D).

## Ngn2 expression in Müller glia after MMS-induced injury in vivo and ex vivo
Our previous results that rat Müller glia express proneural transcription factor Ngn2 after MNU-induced injury19 prompted us to examine Ngn2 expression in Müller glia after MMS treatment in vivo and ex vivo. Double immunofluorescence for Sox9 and Ngn2 in the in vivo model revealed that the proportions of Müller glia expressing Ngn2 reached 11.0 ± 1.6% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) by 3 dpi, followed by a significant decrease thereafter (Fig. 4A and B). In the 1 dpi ex model, 11.6 ± 1.2% (mean ± SEM, n = 6) of Müller glia expressed Ngn2 at 2 dev (3 dpi) yielding values equivalent to the in vivo conditions (Fig. 4C and D, p > 0.05). In the 2 dpi ex retinas, the proportions of Ngn2+ Müller glia reached 33.2 ± 2.3% (mean ± SEM, n = 6) by 1 dev (3 dpi), which were significantly higher than those in the in vivo and 1 dpi ex retinas (both p < 0.01), but decreased to 18.2 ± 3.0% (mean ± SEM, n = 6) by 2 dev (4 dpi) (Fig. 4C and D).

## Effects of ex vivo cultures on cell viability and the phenotypes of Müller glia
Because ex vivo cultures are thought to influence retinal cell viability and Müller glia reactivity, we examined the cell death and the phenotypes of Müller glia in the ex vivo-cultured retinas without prior treatment with MMS. A small number of TUNEL+ cells were detected in the INL and ONL as early as 1 dev and TUNEL+ cells drastically increased in number in the ONL and the GCL by 3 dev (Supplementary Fig. S3). TUNEL+ Müller glia were rarely observed (Supplementary Fig. S3). In accordance with prominent neuronal cell death at 3 dev, reactive changes of Müller glia including GFAP expression, cell cycle reentry and migration were observed only at 3 dev (Supplementary Fig. S3). Ngn2 expression was not detectable during this period of observation.

## Discussion
Rat Müller glia are unique from mouse Müller glia in their capacity to proliferate after photoreceptor injury in vivo17. Virtually all Müller glia reenter the cell cycle by 2 days after chemically-induced photoreceptor injury and traverse a single cell cycle before exiting the proliferative state17,26. In the present study, we conducted retinal explant cultures at two different timings after injury, 1 dpi, prior to cell cycle reentry of Müller glia, and 2 dpi, when most Müller glia are in the cell cycle, expecting that the timing of explant culture may have impact on the proliferative response of Müller glia. Contrary to our expectation, the proliferative response of Müller glia in the two ex vivo models was very similar to that in vivo, indicating that the present ex vivo systems effectively recapitulated the proliferative behavior of Müller glia in vivo regardless of the timing of explanting after injury.
Upon cell cycle reentry, rat Müller glia exhibit robust phagocytic ability and play a dominant role in the elimination of photoreceptor debris prior to microglia/macrophage activation21. Surprisingly, the present study revealed that the timing of explant culture significantly influences the phagocytic activity of Müller glia. When the retinas were explant-cultured at 2 dpi, photoreceptor debris were mostly eliminated in a similar manner to the in vivo conditions. However, photoreceptor clearance was notably impaired when ex vivo cultures were started at 1 dpi. The phagocytic ability of Müller glia in the 1 dpi ex retinas may not be blocked completely, because photoreceptor debris were eventually eliminated by 6 dev. These findings suggest that the exposure of Müller glia to the in vivo environment for the initial two days after injury may be critical for the activation of their phagocytic ability. We previously reported that both proliferative and phagocytic activities of rat Müller glia require phosphatidylserine recognition and Rac1 activation21, suggesting that these apparently distinct responses of Müller glia to injury are mechanistically correlated. However, that the majority of Müller glia reenter the cell cycle in the two ex vivo models indicates that whether phagocytosis occurs or not may have minimal effects on the proliferative response of Müller glia. A recent report has also suggested involvement of the engulfment receptor MERTK and its activators galectin-3 and protein S in phagocytic clearance of apoptotic photoreceptors by Müller glia27. It is thus likely that the molecular mechanisms regulating phosphatidylserine recognition by engulfment receptors may be impaired by ex vivo conditions, for example, by the lack of interactions with the retinal pigment epithelium. Further comparative analyses of the two ex vivo models may be useful to define the complex mechanisms regulating the phagocytic ability of Müller glia.
Along with active proliferation and phagocytosis, nuclear migration to the site of injury has been recognized as a characteristic behavior of rat Müller glia after injury17,19. While approximately 70% of Müller glia migrate to the OPL/ONL in vivo, only 30–40% did so in the two ex vivo models, indicating that explant cultures may inhibit glial migration. We previously reported that S phase entry is required for the migration of Müller glia after photoreceptor injury19. Given that the majority of Müller glia enter S phase in the explants, S phase entry may be required but not sufficient to trigger Müller glia migration. Because phagocytic clearance of photoreceptor debris precedes Müller glia migration in vivo17,21, we first assumed that the phagocytic activity of Müller glia may affect their migration. However, the extent of glial migration was equivalent between the two ex vivo models, suggesting that the migration of Müller glia may not be affected by their phagocytic activity.
The present study also revealed that a significant proportion of Müller glia die by apoptosis at the timing of 4 dpi in vivo. In vivo environment may be critical for this event, because apoptotic Müller glia were not observed in the ex vivo retinas. Although the mechanism underlying the cell death of Müller glia in vivo remains unclear, the involvement of cells derived from circulating blood, e.g., blood-borne macrophages, may be likely and warrants further investigation. We previously reported that rat Müller glia exhibit the DNA damage response during cell cycle progression after injury17. Because DNA damage, unless properly repaired, induces cell death programs such as apoptosis28, we hypothesized that DNA damage induced by replication stress may cause apoptosis of Müller glia after cell cycle exit. Based on this hypothesis, we conducted colabeling of γH2AX, the DNA damage marker, and TUNEL, expecting that intense γH2AX labeling is observed in TUNEL+ apoptotic Müller glia. However, γH2AX expression peaked at 3 dpi followed by a notable decrease by 4 dpi while Müller glia apoptosis was observed only at 4 dpi. Thus, the timing of DNA damage does not coincide with the timing of apoptosis. Furthermore, when examined at 4 dpi, γH2AX and TUNEL rarely colocalized in the same cells, indicating that DNA damage and apoptosis occur in different Müller glia populations. These data argue for the presence of distinct mechanisms regulating DNA damage and apoptosis in Müller glia although the possibility that DNA damage triggers apoptosis after a delay rather than immediately cannot be excluded. The possibility that MMS directly stresses Müller glia may also need to be considered. Interestingly, the two ex vivo models showed distinct patterns of γH2AX expression; In the 2 dpi ex retinas, high levels of γH2AX occurred in Müller glia in a pattern similar to the in vivo retinas while, in the 1 dpi ex model, γH2AX was only weakly expressed in Müller glia. Considering that the majority of Müller glia reenter the cell cycle in both models, these data indicate that cell cycle activity may not be sufficient to induce DNA damage in Müller glia. Because phagocytic clearance of photoreceptor debris is evident in the 2 dpi ex but not 1 dpi ex retinas, it is tempting to speculate that the aberrant phagocytic activity after photoreceptor damage may be causally related to DNA damage in Müller glia.
Ngn2 is a proneural transcription factor essential for neuronal fate specification29. In the retina, Ngn2 is expressed in retinal progenitors and drives neuronal differentiation while inhibiting glial cell fate30,31. We previously reported that rat Müller glia express Ngn2 after MNU-induced photoreceptor damage19, and the present study also revealed Ngn2 expression in rat Müller glia in the MMS model, confirming that rat Müller glia dedifferentiate into the progenitor-like state after injury. However, the occurrence of Ngn2 in Müller glia was transient, peaking at 3 dpi followed by a drastic decrease by 4 dpi. Considering the presence of apoptotic Müller glia at 4 dpi, Ngn2+ Müller glia may be eliminated by apoptosis. However, Ngn2 expression also declines by 4 dpi in the ex vivo models despite the absence of Müller glia cell death, which does not support this possibility. An alternative possibility is that Müller glia may simply return to normal differentiated state by declining neurogenic protein expression. Induction of gliogenic or gliosis-related proteins has been associated with cell cycle activity of Müller glia18,19 and thus maintaining glial fate by upregulating gliogenic factors while downregulating neurogenic factors like Ngn2 after cell cycle activity may be one of the mechanisms that hamper neuronal regeneration by Müller glia in mammals. It is of interest that the timing of retinal explant culture affected Ngn2 expression in Müller glia; significantly higher proportions of Müller glia expressed Ngn2 in the 2 dpi ex retinas (approximately 30%) compared to approximately 10% in vivo or in the 1 dpi ex retinas. It has been reported that growth factor stimulation induces Ngn2 expression in mouse Müller glia32, and we also reported that Ngn2 expression in rat Müller glia requires S phase entry19. However, it is difficult to attribute the differences in Ngn2 expression to variations in the cell cycle given the almost equivalent cell cycle activity between the in vivo and two ex vivo models. Nevertheless, the pronounced Ngn2 expression in the 2 dpi ex retinas implies that the ex vivo environment may be more suitable to promote dedifferentiation of Müller glia than the in vivo setting.
Ex vivo cultures are thought to affect viability of retinal cells, especially ganglion cells and photoreceptors. Thus, the phenotypic changes of Müller glia observed in the present ex vivo analyses may reflect the combined effects of MMS- and ex vivo culture-induced injury. However, when normal retinas were explant-cultured, significant neuronal cell death in the ONL and GCL accompanied by reactive changes of Müller glia (GFAP expression, cell cycle reentry and migration) were not observed until 3 dev while these glial changes were evident within 2 days of ex vivo cultures when treated with MMS. Thus, it is reasonable to conclude that the phenotypic changes of Müller glia observed in the present ex vivo models are largely elicited by MMS-induced photoreceptor injury rather than neuronal cell death induced by ex vivo cultures. Nevertheless, it is likely that explant-induced injury of inner retinal neurons such as ganglion cells may perturb unidirectional migration of Müller glia to the ONL, and further research is needed to explore this possibility.
In conclusion, the present study revealed both similarities and differences in the responses of rat Müller glia after injury across in vivo and two ex vivo systems. Although the ex vivo system is a highly valuable approach, the effects of different experimental conditions should be carefully considered when investigating the phenotypic changes of Müller glia after injury. Further comparative analyses using different ex vivo systems are warranted to uncover novel aspects of the mechanisms regulating the function of mammalian Müller glia.

## Methods

## Animals
Male Wistar rats (Charles River Laboratories Japan, Yokohama) aged 5 weeks were maintained under a 12:12 h light-dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Euthanasia was performed using CO2 gas inhalation. Rats (approximately 200 g of body weight) were transferred to a chamber without anesthesia and exposed to CO2 gas using a gradual filling method at a replacement rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute according to the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) 2020 Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. After respiratory arrest, CO2 flow was maintained for at least one minute. After death was determined by cessation of heartbeat and breathing, the animals were enucleated and the retinas harvested for histological analysis and ex vivo culture. All animal experiments in this study were designed in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines for laboratory animals and approved by the institutional review committee (Tokyo Women’s Medical University, Approval Number A2025-056). The study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

## Induction of retinal degeneration
To induce photoreceptor degeneration in rats, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS, nacalai tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was administered intraperitoneally at the dose of 75 mg/kg body weight as described previously26.

## Retinal ex vivo culture
Retinas were harvested from rat eyes at 1 or 2 days after MMS treatment and retinal ex vivo cultures were conducted as described previously21. Briefly, retinas were spread over membranes placed in 6-well plates and cultured in 1 ml of retinal culture medium at 37 °C under humidified conditions with 5% CO₂ in air.

## EdU incorporation assay
To visualize proliferating cells in the ex vivo retinas, 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assay was performed using Click-iT™ Plus EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Imaging Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. EdU was added to the retinal culture medium at the final concentration of 80 µM during the entire culture period.

## Immunofluorescence
Retinas were fixed by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and 10 µm-thick vertical sections were obtained on a cryostat as reported previously17. Immunofluorescence staining was performed as reported previously17 using antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S1. To visualize the cell nuclei, 4’, 6-diamidino-2phenyl-indole (DAPI; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used. Immunofluorescence images were obtained using confocal laser scanning microscopes (LSM710 and LSM900; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

## TUNEL assay
To visualize apoptotic cells, TUNEL assay was performed using the in situ cell death detection kit, TMR red (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Cell counting
Fluorescently labeled cells were counted on confocal images obtained from the central region of the vertically sliced retinas. At least five fields were analyzed per animal, and 3 animals in the in vivo experiments and 5 or 6 animals in the ex vivo analyses were used per experimental condition.

## Statistical analysis
One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc comparison and Student’s t-test were conducted using SPSS software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

## Figure Fig. 1: Apoptotic cell death in the rat retinas after MMS-induced photoreceptor injury in vivo and ex vivo. (A) TUNEL staining in vivo. The outer nuclear layer (ONL) becomes intensely TUNEL + by 2 days post-injection (dpi). Arrows indicate TUNEL+ nuclei in the ONL at 4 dpi. DAPI nuclear staining shown in grayscale. INL, inner nuclear layer; GCL, ganglion cell layer. (B) TUNEL staining in combination with Crx immunofluorescence (photoreceptor marker) at 4 dpi showing no colocalization. (C) TUNEL staining in combination with Sox9 immunofluorescence (Müller glia marker) at 4 dpi. Arrows indicate colocalization. (D) TUNEL staining in combination with Iba1 immunofluorescence (microglia/macrophage marker) at 4 dpi. Arrows indicate colocalization. (E) Experimental design showing the time schedule of explant culture. Retinas were explanted at 1 or 2 days after MMS injection (1 dpi ex or 2 dpi ex) and cultured for 1 to 3 days ex vivo (dev) until fixation. (F) TUNEL staining ex vivo. TUNEL+ photoreceptors remain in the ONL in the 1 dpi ex retinas but are largely eliminated in the 2 dpi ex retinas. (G) TUNEL staining in combination with Sox9 immunofluorescence in the ex vivo retinas showing no colocalization. Arrows indicate Sox9+/TUNEL- Müller glia nuclei. Scale bars in A to D, F and G = 20 μm.

## Figure Fig. 2: Cell cycle reentry and migration of Müller glia in the rat retinas after MMS-induced photoreceptor injury in vivo and ex vivo. (A) Triple immunofluorescence for Sox9, ppRb (pan-cell cycle marker), and pH3 (M phase marker) in vivo. Arrows indicate pH3+/ppRb+ Müller glia. dpi, days post-injection. ONL, outer nuclear layer, INL, inner nuclear layer. (B) Proportions of ppRb + or pH3+ Müller glia in vivo. (C) Proportions of Müller glia displaced in the outer plexiform layer (OPL) or ONL in vivo. (D) Triple immunofluorescence for Sox9, ppRb, and pH3 ex vivo. Retinas were explanted at 1 or 2 days after MMS injection (1 dpi ex or 2 dpi ex) and cultured for 1 to 3 days ex vivo (dev). (E) Proportions of ppRb+ Müller glia ex vivo. (F) Proportions of pH3+ Müller glia ex vivo. (G) Proportions of Müller glia displaced in the OPL or ONL ex vivo. (H) EdU incorporation assay in combination with Sox9 immunofluorescence ex vivo. (I) Proportions of EdU+ Müller glia ex vivo. Scale bars in A, D, and H = 20 μm. Each bar of the graphs represents the mean ± SEM. N = 3 for in vivo experiments and n = 5 or 6 for ex vivo experiments. **P < 0.01.

## Figure Fig. 3: DNA damage response of Müller glia in the rat retinas after MMS-induced photoreceptor injury in vivo and ex vivo. (A) Double immunofluorescence for Sox9 and γH2AX (DNA damage marker) in vivo. Arrows indicate γH2AX+ Müller glia. dpi, days post-injection. ONL, outer nuclear layer; INL, inner nuclear layer. (B) Double immunofluorescence for Sox9 and γH2AX in combination with TUNEL at 4 dpi in vivo. Green arrows, Sox9+/γH2AX-/TUNEL- cells; yellow arrows, Sox9+/γH2AX-/TUNEL+ cells; cyan arrows, Sox9+/γH2AX+/TUNEL- cells; pink arrows, Sox9+/γH2AX+/TUNEL+ cells; red arrows, Sox9-/γH2AX-/TUNEL+ cells. (C) Proportions of Müller glia classified by γH2AX and TUNEL labeling at 4 dpi in vivo. Each bar represents the mean ± SEM (n = 3). (D) Double immunofluorescence for Sox9 and γH2AX ex vivo. Arrows indicate γH2AX+ Müller glia while asterisks denote γH2AX-negative Müller glia. Retinas were explanted at 1 or 2 days after MMS injection (1 dpi ex or 2 dpi ex) and cultured for 1 to 3 days ex vivo (dev). Scale bars in A, B, and D = 20 μm.

## Figure Fig. 4: Expression of proneural factor Ngn2 in Müller glia in the rat retinas after MMS-induced photoreceptor injury in vivo and ex vivo. (A) Double immunofluorescence for Sox9 and Ngn2 in vivo. Arrows indicate Ngn2+ Müller glia. dpi, days post-injection. ONL, outer nuclear layer; INL, inner nuclear layer; GCL, ganglion cell layer. (B) Proportions of Ngn2+ Müller glia in vivo. Cont, control. (C) Double immunofluorescence for Sox9 and Ngn2 ex vivo. Retinas were explanted at 1 or 2 days after MMS injection (1 dpi ex or 2 dpi ex) and cultured for 1 to 3 days ex vivo (dev). Arrows indicate Ngn2+ Müller glia. (D) Proportions of Ngn2+ Müller glia ex vivo. Scale bars in A and C = 20 μm. Each bar of the graphs represents the mean ± SEM. N = 3 for in vivo experiments and n = 5 or 6 for ex vivo experiments. **P < 0.01.
