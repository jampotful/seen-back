Abca4 Knockdown in the Cone-Rich Rodent Psammomys Obesus Leads to Stargardt's Disease-Like Progressive Retinal Degeneration

Investigative Ophthalmology & Visual Science. https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.67.5.47. doi:10.1167/iovs.67.5.47. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Abca4 Knockdown in the Cone-Rich Rodent Psammomys Obesus Leads to Stargardt's Disease-Like Progressive Retinal Degeneration

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeMutations in the gene ABCA4 coding for photoreceptor-specific ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 4, are responsible for Stargardts disease type 1 (STGD1), the most common form of inherited macular degeneration. We recently showed that injection of viral vectors expressing CRISPR/Cas9 tools directed against Abca4 into young Sand Rat (Psammomys obesus) eyes led to extensive structural and functional retinal degeneration resembling STGD1. Here we provide further evidence that this is highly likely due to specific knockdown of Abca4 and not off-target errors.MethodsWe performed subretinal injections of Adeno-Associated Virus-CRISPR/Cas9-Abca4 constructs into postnatal (∼P15) Psammomys obesus. Eyes were examined by noninvasive exploration (ocular coherence tomography, fundus and electroretinography) at 15–60 days after injection. Additionally, subgroups were euthanized over the same time period, and ocular tissue was used for immunochemical analyses.ResultsRNAscope analysis of injected eyes showed knockdown of Abca4, rhodopsin and cone transducin mRNA in transduced regions; neighbouring tissue that was not transduced showed robust expression of all three. Injection of control AAV, expressing CAS9 alone, induced only mild glial activation. Statistically significant decreases in visual responses to light flashes were only seen in eyes injected with the fully active CRISPR/Cas9-Abca4 probes.ConclusionsTaken together, these data rule out off-target effects as responsible for the observed degeneration, and indicate that Psammomys obesus faithfully recapitulates many of the features seen in human STGD1, thus positioning it as an important research opportunity to further explore genotype-phenotype relationships and test putative therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Stargardt's disease, retinal degeneration, animal model, photoreceptors, electrophysiology
Stargardt disease type 1 (STGD1) is the most common inherited maculopathy, with carrier frequency of up to 1:50 in the general population.1 The causal gene has long been identified as ABCA4, encoding the photoreceptor (PR)-specific flippase ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 4.2 More than 1200 mutations have been identified, the majority of which are compound heterozygous, resulting in retinal degeneration of variable phenotype but in which early foveal damage and severe loss of central vision are commonly observed.3 Most ABCA4 mutations lead to a central-peripheral gradient of retinal damage, almost always with macular involvement.4,5 The most severe forms are linked to null mutations, resulting in cone-rod dystrophy that appears early in life and evolves rapidly toward severe visual handicap.6 It has been shown that ABCA4 is expressed by both rods and cones in the human retina.7 A pathogenic mechanism has been suggested in which ABCA4 malfunction impairs the visual cycle, with slowed clearance of all-trans retinal (atRal) leading to formation of toxic bisretinoids within PR outer segments (OS). Daily phagocytic removal of PROS membrane debris by the adjacent retinal pigmented epithelium (RPE) leads to accumulation and modification of these bisretinoids into toxic products—especially A2E, a component of lipofuscin, a hallmark feature of STGD1 in humans and mouse models.1,3,4,8–11 Lipofuscin toxicity eventually reaches a threshold level at which RPE cells start to die, and since the RPE is critical for the continued maintenance and survival of PR, these latter cells also die in their turn. However, this hypothetical mechanism does not account for the particular phenotype seen in many STGD1 patients, in which there is pan-retinal loss of cone function while rod function remains relatively normal,5,6 and doesn't seem to be reflected in the knockout mouse model.11 Furthermore, in severe forms of human STGD1 it has been reported that retinal abnormalities appear prior to visible changes in the RPE.12
Abca4 −/− mice display accumulations of the bisretinoid lipofuscin9–11 as seen in the human disease. Pigmented Abca4−/− mice show no structural or functional defects except for small areas of reduced autofluorescence in 18 month old mice;11 albino Abca4−/− mice exhibit substantial PR loss at eight or 11 months of age according to the strain, and electroretinography in both scotopic and photopic condition shows functional impairment already at three months.13–15 One drawback of modelling maculopathies in mice is the low cone numbers in nocturnal rodents: <1% in rats16 and <3% in mice.17 We reasoned that animals containing high numbers of cones, more closely resembling the cone-rich composition of human macula, would possibly constitute superior models for such diseases. In keeping with their temporal niche, diurnally active rodents possess high numbers of cone PR, ∼30% to 40% for those murid species studied.18,19 We showed in a recent publication that knockout of Abca4 transcription in such a diurnal rodent led to widespread progressive PR degeneration, with prominent structural and functional decline in cones, more closely echoing the STGD1 phenotype.20 Here we show that nonspecific retinal damage or off-target effects are highly unlikely to be compounding factors in the observed degeneration, and demonstrate clear knockdown of photoreceptor-specific mRNA exclusively in transduced PR.

## Material and Methods

## Animals
Adult couples of Fat Sand Rats Psammomys obesus were originally imported from the laboratory of Dr. N. Kronfeld-Schor, Tel Aviv, Israel, and have since been maintained as a viable breeding colony in the Chronobiotron CNRS UMR3415, Strasbourg. Animals are kept on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, in environmentally enriched cages and fed a low-calorie diet (Altromin International, Germany), which does not induce diabetes,21 and water as desired. Their sanitary status was assayed regularly, and the animals were free of known pathogens and parasites. We used both male and female animals in the present project, which was authorized by the local ethics committee (CREMEAS) and by APAFIS no. 36472-2022103117061181 v8 from the Ministry of Education, Science and Research, France. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, with strict compliance to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Subsequent to noninvasive analyses, except where indicated animals were euthanized using excess CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, as per institutional protocols. Eyes were rapidly enucleated and processed according to the protocol in question. Breeding couples of clean Psammomys obesus are available on demand.

## Guide RNA Design
RNA guides for the CRISPR/Cas9 strategy were designed and optimization was performed in collaboration with personnel at the IGBMC/PHENOMIN-ICS. The Psammomys obesus Abca4 gene sequence was aligned against Rattus norvegicus (identity 94%) and Mus musculus (identity 92%) Abca4 gene sequences. Exon 5 was chosen to obtain a frame shift and gene deletion. The CRISPOR website (http://crispor.tefor.net/crispor.py) was used to search for the guide RNAs, according to the webmasters instructions. Two guide RNAs were selected, a 5′ guide (termed gR58) and a 3′ guide (gR80) (Figs. 1A, 1B). Both should cut close to the splice acceptor or donor sites of exon 5 to increase cut efficiency. The guides were synthesized and tested in vitro (on the PCR fragment containing the target sequence and in the presence of the CAS9 protein, data not shown). ## Plasmid Construction and AAV Production
Details of plasmid construction and virus production are provided in our previous publication and according to published protocols.20,23 The complete viral construction maps are reprinted in Figures 1A and 1B. For the sake of simplicity, the viruses expressing guide RNAs were termed “AAV2/8 gRNA/GFP”, and “AAV2/8 Cas9” for the one expressing the Sp CAS9. In figures, infected eyes were respectively designated CAS9 or CAS9 + gRNA.

## Subretinal Injection
As described previously by us,20 young postnatal (PN14-17) Psammomys obesus were lightly anaesthetized by inhalation of gaseous anaesthetic (Isoflurane 2%) administered constantly through a small animal mask connected to a pump. The eyes were anesthetized by local anaesthetic (Tetracaine) before surgery. The eyeball was exposed by pulling down the skin, and a small incision was made through the ora serrata on the temporal side using the tip of a beveled 33-gauge needle. Visualization of the incision was improved by placing a coverslip over the eye, acting as a small magnifying lens. A blunt-ended injection needle (Hamilton syringe, 33 gauge) was inserted into the eyeball through the incision until slight resistance was felt, and slowly 1.8 µL of AAV per eye were injected into the subretinal space. In terms of viral genome copies per injection, AAV2/8 gRNA/GFP concentration was 1.4 × 1010, and AAV2/8 Cas9 was 1.4 × 1010. Analgesic cream was applied to the eye, and animals were allowed to recover and returned to their mother. They were inspected daily for any possible ocular irritation or health problems.

## Electroretinography (ERG)
Scotopic and photopic single flash, and photopic flicker full-field Ganzfeld ERGs (Roland Consults srl, Brandenburg, Germany), were performed as described previously at eight weeks after injection.20

## Noninvasive Ocular Imaging
Psammomys obesus were anaesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (IsoFlo; Zoetis, Malakoff, France) administered constantly at 4% through a small animal mask in a 50/50 mix of air and O2 at 0.8 L/min. A single drop of tropicamide 0.5% (Mydriaticum; Théa Pharma, Clermont-Ferrand, France) was used for pupil dilation, and the ocular surface was lubricated with either artificial tears (Artelac; Bausch & Lomb, Montpellier, France) for optical coherence tomography (OCT), or with ophthalmic gel (Ocry-Gel; TVM, Lempdes, France) for fundus imaging. Retinas were first imaged with an Envisu R2200 SD-OCT (Bioptigen-Leica, Durham, NC, USA), then with a Micron III fundus/fluorescence camera (Phoenix Research Laboratories, Pleasanton, CA, USA) during the same period of anaesthesia. Short sequences of fundus images were aligned, averaged and sharpened in Fiji24,25 using a macro developed by Mark Krebs (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA).

## Western Blot Analysis
Western blots were performed on 15 µg total retinal protein as previously reported.21 Electrophoresis was performed using SDS-PAGE gradient gels 4%–12% (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), transferred to nitrocellulose using a trans-blot turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad) and incubated using polyclonal antibody anti-GFAP (23935-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA) and monoclonal anti-beta-actin (A5441; Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA), 1:500 final concentration. Anti-rabbit and anti-mouse IgG conjugated with HRP, diluted 1:1000, were used as secondary antibodies. Western blots were performed by chemiluminescence using the Super Signal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate system (Pierce, Appleton, WI, USA). Immunoreactive bands were detected using the Amersham Imagen 680 acquisition system (GE-Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) and were quantified using ImageJ software.

## RNAscope Hybridization
The protocol provided by Advanced Cell Diagnostics (UM 323100 Multiplex Fluorescent v2_RevB protocole RNAscope; Newark, CA, USA) was followed to the letter. Selected animals at 15 days after injection (total age ∼1 month) were euthanized by ethics board–approved procedures (inhalation of CO2), and eyes were rapidly enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Frozen sections were prepared from eyes, ensuring that sections included both GFP-positive (transduced tissue, AAV-gRNA/GFP+ AAV-CAS9) and GFP− (non-transduced tissue) to enable signal comparison on single sections. Customized probes made against Psammomys obesus genomic sequences of Abca4, Cas9, cone transducin (Gnat2) and rhodopsin were prepared by Advanced Cell Diagnostics (Hayward, CA, USA). GFP expression was detected as endogenous fluorescence from the AAV vector. Different combinations of these probes were hybridized on the sections and examined by confocal microscopy, and visualized using Opal 570 (red: Abca4) and Opal 620 (far red: CAS9, Gnat2 and rhodopsin) fluorophores. Data acquisition was performed using confocal laser scanning Leica SP5 with the LAS-AF program (Leica Application Suite Advanced Fluorescence; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. For each region of interest, fluorescence intensity and area values were determined, and the intensity/area ratio was calculated. This ratio was then compared between transduced and non-transduced regions.

## Statistics
Statistical analyses were performed using SigmaPlot software or PRISM. ERG data were analysed by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc tests in case of significant interaction between group and light intensity. A t-test (normal data) or non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test (one-way ANOVA on ranks: non-normal data) was applied to assess differences in RNAscope signal staining between groups.

## Results

## Off-Target Effects Are Not Responsible for the Observed Retinal Changes
Guide RNAs were designed against the rat genome available in the data base, and subsequently verified against the Psammomys obesus genomic sequence (Fig. 1). The two gRNA probes were screened for potential off-target hybridization on the CRISPOR website (http://crispor.tefor.net/crispor.py). The specificity scores for the 5′ guide are 58 against the rat and 63 against Psammomys (even higher when using the improved CRISPOR engine, score of 75); the 3′ guide gave a specificity score of 80 for the rat, and 75 and 83 for Psammomys using the MIT and CFA engines, respectively.

## Retinal Degeneration Induced by Viral Co-Injection Is Not Due to Nonspecific Effect of CAS9
Cargo capacity of AAV being limited, it was necessary to divide the CRISPR/Cas9 constructs into two separate viruses which were co-injected in order to achieve knockdown of Abca4 expression. One virus contained the Abca4 gRNAs and a GFP tag, whereas the second contained the Cas9 sequence (Figs. 1A, 1B). The targeted sequence of Abca4 exon 5 and the injection protocol are depicted in Figures 1C and 1D, respectively.
As there was a slight possibility that CAS9 overexpression alone could be harmful to the retina and provoke tissue damage independently of Abca4 inhibition, we examined whether AAV/CAS9 alone could lead to any upregulation of GFAP expression in the retina, and also whether we could detect any structural or functional effects. Sub-retinal injection of AAV/CAS9 construct alone did not lead to any structural changes, as observed at four and eight weeks after injection by fundus examination (Figs. 2A, 2B). Fundus images showed a uniform featureless aspect except for the presence of the optic nerve papilla and a faint circumscribed spot corresponding to the injection site. Notably this small spot did not evolve between four and eight weeks after injection (Figs. 2A, 2B). On the other hand, co-injection into the twin eye of both AAV revealed pronounced retinal loss by four weeks, becoming especially marked by eight weeks after injection (Figs. 2C, 2D). Fundus images of co-injected eyes at four weeks post-injection showed widespread expression of GFP, indicating successful viral infection. At the center of this fluorescence, depigmented patches indicated tissue loss. By eight weeks after injection, the area of tissue loss had greatly expanded, covering the central and mid-peripheral retina (Figs. 2C, 2D). In keeping with this, GFP fluorescence was now limited to the edges of the lesion area. AAV/CAS9 alone did not contain any GFP marker and we could not monitor expression by fluorescence.

In agreement with these fundus observations, OCT analysis of the two conditions, AAV/CAS9 alone and AAV-gRNA+ AAV/CAS9 combined, showed very different profiles. Eyes injected with AAV/CAS9 alone showed regular laminated retinal architecture, except for the small areas damaged by the injection needle, at both 4 and 8 weeks after treatment (Figs. 2E, 2F, 2I, 2J). However, twin eyes injected with both viruses showed extensive loss of the outer nuclear layer (ONL), whereas the inner retina, composed of inner nuclear and retinal ganglion layers (INL and GCL, respectively) remained intact (Figs. 2G, 2H, 2K, 2L). OCT cross-sections made outside the areas of visible tissue disruption exhibited normal lamination, whereas profiles within the affected areas showed almost total loss of the ONL. As with the fundus explorations, greater damage was again seen at eight compared to four weeks, with more extensive loss of ONL (Figs. 2K, 2L).
Upon Western Blot analysis we found moderate GFAP expression in AAV/CAS9-infected retinas, increasing at four and eight weeks after injection. However, the level of expression at eight weeks was much lower in AAV/CAS9 compared to AAV/CAS9+ gRNA (Fig. 2M), confirming that infection with the AAV/CAS9 alone does not induce marked tissue response in the retina.

## Functional Loss of Light Sensitivity Is Only Seen After Co-Injection of Both AAV
To see whether AAV/CAS9 alone could affect visual function, in spite of the lack of structural changes, we undertook both fully dark-adapted scotopic and light-adapted photopic ERG recordings, and flicker ERG recordings. At very low intensity light flashes under scotopic conditions, there were no significant differences between AAV/CAS9 alone or both viruses, although there was a tendency for higher b wave amplitudes in AAV/CAS9-only injected eyes (Figs. 3A, 3B). However, at moderate and higher light flash intensities, eyes injected with AAV/CAS9 alone showed significantly greater amplitude responses compared to twin eyes injected with AAV/CAS9+ gRNA (Figs. 3A–C). This was true for both a and b waves. Furthermore, scotopic ERG amplitudes in AAV/CAS9-injected eyes were not significantly different from non-injected control animals (data not shown). Similar findings were seen when photopic ERGs were recorded, again for both a and b waves (Figs. 4A–C). Finally, photopic flicker ERG showed significantly reduced amplitudes in dual virus injections compared to AAV/CAS9 alone (Fig. 5). There were no differences in flicker ERG latency values (neither for scotopic and photopic simple flash ERGs, data not shown).

## Co-Injection of AAV Vectors Leads to Knockdown of Abca4 Expression in Transduced Photoreceptors
To demonstrate that virally-mediated knockdown of Abca4 expression was actually occurring, we conducted RNAscope labeling of Psammomys obesus retinas at two weeks after injection. This early time point was chosen to visualize any reduced expression prior to actual cell loss, which had already started to occur by four weeks and was widespread by eight weeks after injection. Also, at this earlier time point viral spread was less extensive (as determined by GFP expression) and allowed us to compare transduced (GFP+) and non-transduced (GFP−) areas within the same eye. We first used this approach to confirm co-infection by the two AAVs within identified photoreceptors. As seen in Figures 6A and 6A′, DAPI staining showed the regular stratified organization of the ONL containing rod and cone cell bodies, and the INL (GCL is out of frame). Cone nuclei, recognizable from their normal nuclear architecture, form a sublayer lying at the scleral border of the ONL. Outside of the injection zone, GFP staining could not be seen in any cells (Fig. 6B), and neither was CAS9 (Fig. 6C). Within transduced cells near the injection site, GFP expression was intense within the ONL but below detection limits in the INL (Fig. 6B′), demonstrating the specific infection of rod and cone photoreceptors by this serotype. RPE cells also appeared non-transduced (data not shown). Although GFP expression appeared more intense in rod cell bodies compared to those of cones, there were also significant levels of GFP in cones (asterisks, Fig. 6E′ inset). Cas9 expression was seen in both rods and cones but not in the INL in transduced areas, showing the presence of active protein (Fig. 6C′). Finally, in non-transduced areas RNAscope probes designed against Psammomys obesus Abca4 showed robust signal, especially prominent within the IS and also scattered throughout both rod and cone cell bodies (Fig. 6D). Within transduced GFP-positive areas, Abca4 signals were greatly reduced, indicating that knockdown occurs exclusively when both viruses are co-expressed (Fig. 6D′). ## Co-Injection of AAV Vectors Leads to Knockdown of Rod- and Cone-Specific Gene Expression in Transduced Photoreceptors
Parallel sections labeled with DAPI, GFP, Abca4 and either rhodopsin or cone transducin (Gnat2) probes, showed that signals of all these genes were robust in non-transduced areas (Fig. 8): Abca4 and rhodopsin showed intense staining at the level of the IS with more moderate signal over the ONL (Figs. 8C, 8D), and as depicted in merged images mapped to the same subcellular regions (Fig. 8E). The rhodopsin label overlying the cone cell body sublayer is presumably due to the cytoplasmic extensions of rods coursing through this layer. Gnat2 signals were spatially distinct, with Gnat2 message present within the IS and over the cone cell bodies (Fig. 8J). Gnat2 message was completely absent from the rod cell body sublayer. In transduced areas, there were markedly reduced levels of Abca4, rhodopsin and Gnat2 expression, though there was still residual signal present from the surviving photoreceptors (Figs. 8A′–J′). Hybridization fluorescence intensity for these three genes was quantified over two regions, the OS/IS and the ONL (Fig. 9). We observed significant reduction of Abca4 signal (32-fold in median values) over the OS/IS in transduced retinas; rhodopsin and Gnat2 signals were also significantly decreased in transduced eyes (threefold and 90-fold in median values respectively) (Fig. 9).

## Discussion
The data presented here provide strong evidence that CRISPR/Cas9 mediated knockdown of Abca4 by intraocular injection generates a robust animal model resembling ABCA4 retinopathy. Addressing possible off-target effects, if such non-specific effects occur they must be limited to genes expressed in the outer retina/RPE because of the highly localized nature of viral delivery. For the 5′ gRNA there is one potential off-target for a sequence with two mismatches (two differences at the level of 20 bp of genome recognition) and no off-target expected at less than three mismatches in exons, meaning the risk of off-target is very low and the risk of disrupting expression of another gene is even lower. For the 3′ gRNA there is a possibility of off-target effects with 2 mismatches and a PAM which is not an NGG but an NGA (therefore even less probability of having a double-strand break) in an exon of the Slc12a6 gene. This gene codes for potassium chloride co-transporter 3; we could find no documented expression of KCC3 in the retina, although it has a widespread distribution in both the central and peripheral nervous system.26 Closely related KCC2 is expressed in the inner retina but not in the ONL.27 These considerations suggest that off-target effects can be effectively ruled out as interfering with interpretation.
The data argue strongly that the observed retinal degeneration is indeed due to knockdown of Abca4 within transduced cells; significant structural and functional decline are only seen when the two viral vectors are co-injected, whereas injection of either the AAV-gRNA/Abca4-GFP20 or AAV/CAS9 (present study) alone did not lead to any detectable changes compared to naive uninjected eyes. In addition, the inflammatory cytokines IL-1 and TNFα were either below detection limits or only transiently raised in both AAV/CAS9- and AAV/CAS9+ gRNA-injected eyes (data not shown), suggesting inflammatory responses do not underlie the observed cell loss. On the other hand, there was upregulation of the reactive glial marker GFAP in AAV/CAS9+ gRNA–injected eyes compared to AAV/CAS9-injected eyes, indicating tissue damage was occurring only in the presence of both viruses.
As OCT analysis showed no detectable cell loss prior to one month after injection, we performed RNAscope staining at 15 days after injection to examine the actual effects of CRISPR/Cas9 on expression levels of key genes. At this time point, AAV had not spread so far from the injection site, allowing us to compare transduced (GFP-positive) and non-transduced (GFP-negative) PR in the same section. Although the GFP signal appeared weaker in cone compared to rod cells, enlarged images confirmed presence in both types. Possibly the difference in staining intensity is related to cytoplasmic volume, since this is much more restricted in rods compared to cones.28 In keeping with the injection strategy, GFP-positive regions were also strongly stained for Cas9 expression. We observed very robust Abca4 expression in non-transduced cells, particularly strong in IS, with scattered message lying over both rod and cone cell bodies within the ONL. On the contrary, in neighbouring transduced areas, Abca4 expression was very significantly reduced within the IS. At the same time point, expression levels of the rod-specific gene rhodopsin, and the cone-specific gene Gnat2 (cone transducin) were also quantified in the two areas. Rhodopsin signal was very intense within the IS, and although visibly decreased in transduced compared to non-transduced areas, moderate expression persisted; Gnat2 expression was localized to cone cell bodies and IS, and expression levels of both genes were statistically significantly decreased over the cell bodies. The fact that Abca4 expression was very impacted whereas moderate rhodopsin and Gnat2 expression was seen at two weeks post-injection suggests that these genes are not immediately shut down after Abca4 inhibition but decline more slowly.
Intriguingly, we also observed Abca4 expression within the upper INL, of approximately the same intensity as the ONL. This was not due to non-specific labeling, since other retinal areas (lower INL, IPL and GCL) were completely devoid of staining. Even if AAV8 was previously reported to infect the inner retina,29 expression at this level was not affected by viral transduction, presumably because the AAV serotype employed does not infect retinal cell types other than PR, as indicated by expression of GFP and CAS9 that appears strictly restricted to the ONL. This localization may correspond to bipolar cells or Müller glia, both of which are closely associated with PR function and metabolism. ON and OFF bipolar pathways convey the PR signal through the retina to the ganglion cells,30 and can be classified according to circuit-specific31 or phenotype (e.g., recoverin-expressing bipolar cells).32 Müller cells constitute a parallel pathway for the cone visual cycle, and express retinoid-related proteins such as CRALBP, CRBP and CRABP.33 It should be noted that proteomics analyses of Müller glia isolated from human macula regions have not detected expression of ABCA4 protein.34 One tentative explanation could be Abca4 mRNA transported in extracellular vesicles liberated by PR and taken up by INL cells.35 Although ABCA4 has been localized to RPE in addition to PR,36 to our knowledge this is the first observation of Abca4 mRNA in the INL.
Abca4 knockout mouse models have existed for many years,9–11,13 and more recently knock-in mutant Abca4 transgenic mice have been generated,37,38 and exhibit some cardinal features of the disease. Notably, lipofuscin accumulates in the RPE and PR loss occurs specifically in the albino Abca4−/− but not the pigmented strain, highlighting the contribution of light-mediated damage to the phenotype,14,15,39,40 but the degenerative phenotype and functional losses seen in patients are not fully reproduced. In the present study, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated inhibition of Abca4 transcription in Psammomys obesus led to rapid and profound degenerative changes in the retina, and especially affected cone function and survival already after one month. There are several differences between the current model and the existing mouse strains: (1) We performed virally-mediated transduction at late postnatal stages (∼P15) in contrast to germline deletion techniques in the mouse. This age was chosen because initial immunohistochemical studies showed no detectable expression prior to eye opening,20 since active vision only commences once retinal circuitry has sufficiently developed.41 Performing injections at such delayed times also avoids potential developmental side-effects and facilitates the small animal surgery. (2) A major difference between mouse and Psammomys models is the percentage of cones, <3% in mice17 compared to ∼33% in Psammomys.19 Given that the principal clinical feature of STGD1 is macular atrophy, we speculate that cone abundance/density constitutes a critical factor. There is clinical evidence that foveal loss precedes lipofuscin accumulation in early-onset severe STGD1.12,42 Diurnal rodents with a “macula-like” retina may provide an advantage for better understanding the detailed etiology of macular diseases.43 We recently demonstrated44 that cone membranes exhibit several compositional differences compared to rods: cones contain significantly more Abca4 mRNA and ABCA4 protein; they are highly impoverished in very long chain fatty acids in general, and the neuroprotective poly-unsaturated fatty acid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:n:6) in particular;45 they contain far less PE, leading to a PC:PE ratio of 86:14 (compared to the more typical ratio of 58:42 in rods); and show ∼7.5 fold less A2E and fivefold greater amounts of atRal-dimer. We hypothesize that these compositional and functional differences render cones more vulnerable to cellular stress such as oxidative damage, and that disease mutations in STGD1 would lead to preferential cone death as seen in the early macular defects.
Notwithstanding, rod loss is also seen in the more severe forms of human STGD1.5,6,42 The approach used in the present study, namely complete Abca4 knockdown, replicates these more extreme forms of the disease, and leads to rapid tissue loss. This accelerated time course makes it challenging to test possible therapies. To this end, and to model more common forms of the disease, we are currently using similar approaches to introduce identified human mutations and examine the effects on rod and cone loss in Psammomys, and to screen putative treatments to slow down PR loss.
