# Dynamic transcriptomic remodeling in grafted human neural progenitor cells uncovers mechanisms for vision preservation in a rat model of retinitis pigmentosa

## Abstract
Human neural progenitor cells (hNPCs) show promise in slowing retinal degeneration and are currently being tested in clinical trials for retinitis pigmentosa (RP). However, the long-term fate of grafted hNPCs and their interaction with host retinal cells remain unclear. Here, we used single-cell transcriptomics to investigate temporal gene expression changes in grafted hNPCs and host retinal cells following subretinal injection into a rodent model of RP, revealing dynamic transcriptomic changes in the degenerative retinal environment. Grafted hNPCs primarily differentiate into an astroglial phenotype and mature over time, contributing to photoreceptor protection through trophic factor secretion, metabolic modulation, suppression of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation, alongside extracellular matrix remodeling. CellChat analysis revealed a decline in intercellular signaling and communication strength over time, correlating with weakened hNPC-host interactions. These findings suggest that enhancing trophic factor support, particularly MANF, and improving the host retinal environment are critical for sustaining long-term vision preservation.

## Introduction
Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited retinal diseases characterized by progressive vision loss due to mutations in photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). More than 80 genes with over 3000 mutations have been linked to RP1. Gene therapy targeting specific mutations, such as RPE65, has been approved for treating RP patients2,3. Given the high genetic diversity of RP, pursuing a mutation-independent therapeutic strategy to slow disease progression and preserve vision is a logical and promising approach.
Stem cell-based therapies offer promising avenues for treating retinal degenerative diseases through two main strategies: cell replacement and preservation of host cells. Replacement therapies, such as RPE cell transplants for age-related macular degeneration (AMD), have shown mixed outcomes in animal models and clinical trials4. Similarly, photoreceptor transplants at the advanced stages of retinal degeneration have shown promising results in animal studies; however, establishing synaptic connections with host retinal cells remains challenging5,6. Therapies that target preservation of host retinal cells have employed various stem cells in both preclinical and clinical settings, including retinal progenitor cells (RPCs)7, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)8–10, and human neural progenitor cells (hNPCs)11–13. hNPCs have been tested in multiple preclinical models, including rd1 and USH2A mutant mice14,15, RGC-depleted glaucoma mice16, and spinal cord injury models17, where they consistently demonstrated neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects. Our preclinical studies demonstrate that hNPCs delivered to the subretinal space of an animal model survive long-term, migrate extensively, and protect vision by providing trophic support, regulating immune response, and enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms by upregulating Nrf212,18,19. These findings have led to the delivery of clinical-grade hNPCs (termed CNS10-NPC) to RP patients in an ongoing phase I/2a clinical trial (NCT04284293).
While stem cell-based therapies hold promise for retinal disease, long-term protection is compromised due to efficacy deterioration over time, as widely reported from both preclinical and clinical studies. Key questions remain about the behavior of grafted stem cells and the host retina in a progressively degenerative environment, including how grafted cells adapt to their environment, how preserved photoreceptors evolve over time, and how grafted stem cells interact with host retinal cells. Here, we investigated the changes in hNPCs and host retinal cells over time following subretinal injection into the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rat, a well-characterized model for retinal degeneration. Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), along with histological and functional assessments, we analyzed transcriptomic remodeling and intercellular interactions between grafted hNPCs and host retinal cells. Fourteen hNPC subpopulations with distinct transcriptional states were identified, indicating dynamic changes in grafted cells over time. Top-regulated genes, including mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF), myeloid-derived growth factor (MYDGF), midkine (MDK), and pleiotrophin (PTN), were identified in hNPCs. The host retina showed temporal changes in phototransduction, visual perception, glial responses, and metabolic processes. Grafted hNPCs protect vision through multiple modalities, including trophic factor release, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, metabolic regulation, and suppression of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Notably, the signaling interaction and strength between hNPCs and host retinal cells declined over time. These findings provide insight into the dynamic changes in grafted hNPCs and host retinal cells and highlight the importance of combining stem cell therapy with improving host retinal environment to sustain long-term vision preservation.

## Results

## Grafted hNPCs mature into astrocytic cells in the degenerative retina
To study the molecular insights of therapeutic effect and gradual changes of the grafted hNPCs over time in the degenerative retinal environment, hNPCs were delivered to the subretinal space of RCS rats at postnatal day (P) 21, followed by visual function evaluation at P60 and P90 (Supplementary Fig. 1a) using our previously published protocols19,20. After overnight dark adaptation, animals were prepared under dim red light. Scotopic ERGs were recorded using 5 ms white-light flashes at 25 cd·s/m² with 10-second interstimulus intervals to assess retinal function. Under these conditions, a-waves were not detectable at P60 and P90, due to rod bleaching and light adaptation. Our previous study demonstrated that rod function in RCS rats declines early21,22 however, rod-driven a-waves were detectable at P100 following hNPC transplantation when ERGs were recorded using rod-optimized conditions with longer dark-recovery intervals11. Despite this, ERG b-wave amplitudes remained significantly higher for hNPC-treated eyes compared to untreated controls at P60 and P90, indicating preservation of cone and inner retinal activity, consistent with our previous findings19,22. Notably, while visual acuity for hNPC-treated eyes remained significantly higher than untreated controls and stable over time, ERG b-wave amplitudes decreased from P60 to P90, reflecting progressive decline of retinal function (Supplementary Fig. 1b-c). Histological evaluation of retinal sections also revealed a reduction in the outer nuclear layer (ONL) of hNPC-treated retinas at P90 (6–8 layers) compared to P60 (8–10 layers) (Supplementary Fig. 1d-g). Immunofluorescence staining for recoverin, which labels both rods and cones, revealed a preserved ONL in the graft-associated areas, while untreated retinas showed only a single layer of photoreceptors (Supplementary Fig. 2a-b). Cone-arrestin staining further demonstrated well-organized cones with inner and outer segments (IS and OS), and pedicles in regions associated with the hNPC grafts. In contrast, age-matched untreated retinas displayed a degenerative cone morphology with diminished cone-arrestin signals (Supplementary Fig. 2c-d). hNPC treatment also maintained the structural integrity of photoreceptor IS and OS by preserving the level of interphotoreceptor matrix proteoglycans 2 (IMPG2), as evidenced by intense immunofluorescence staining in the hNPC-treated retina at P90, compared to the age-matched untreated control, where no positive staining was detected due to loss of both rod and cone segments (Supplementary Fig. 2e-f). IMPG2 is a key component of the interphotoreceptor matrix, which provides essential structural support to photoreceptors and facilitates nutrients and metabolic waste exchange between photoreceptors and RPE23. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining for RPE65 and collagen IV showed a well-organized layer of RPE cells in the hNPC-protected regions, whereas the untreated control showed a disorganized RPE layer with diminished RPE65 and collagen IV staining (Supplementary Fig. 2g-j). These findings suggest that hNPC treatment contributes to photoreceptor preservation, at least in part, by supporting RPE cell integrity. However, despite these protective effects, progressive photoreceptor loss was still evident over time in hNPC-treated retinas, indicating that while hNPCs slow disease progression, they do not completely halt the degenerative process. It is noted that sham surgery was not included in the current study, since the focus of this study was to investigate the interaction between grafted hNPCs and host retinal cells. Sham surgery would not address this goal, as it would not provide relevant mechanistic insight into donor cell behavior, survival, integration, or paracrine effects. However, sham-related transient effects have been demonstrated across multiple animal models24–26, including in our recent dose-ranging study, which shows that vision preservation is dose-dependent, with transient effects observed in vehicle-treated controls and limited efficacy at low cell doses12. In addition, removal of subretinal debris at the peak of accumulation in RCS rats has been shown to provide long-term visual protection27, underscoring the importance of distinguishing nonspecific surgical effects from cell-type specific therapeutic mechanisms.

Next, we identified the distinct cell-type clusters using known human and retinal cell-specific marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 3h). The 14 transcriptomically distinct hNPC subpopulations were classified as SOX9+/NES+/HOPX/SLC1A3+ astrocyte progenitor cells (APC; Clusters 0, n = 1316; 7, n = 531, and 10, n = 224), CD44+/AQP4+/GJA1+/FLT+ astrocyte precursors (APs; Clusters 4, n = 711; 6 n = 533; and 9, n = 337), GFAP+/S100B+/FABP7+ immature astrocytes (IASC; Cluster 11, n = 185), GFAP+/AQP4+/GJA1+ mature astrocytes (ASC; Cluster 1 n = 1042), PDGFRA+/OLIG1+/OLIG2+/SLC1A3+/S100B+ oligodendrocyte astrocyte progenitor cells (OAPCs; Cluster 2, n = 1042), PDGFRA+/OLIG1+/OLIG2+ oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC; Clusters 3, n = 755; and 5, n = 708), small subpopulation of SOX10+/MBP+/MYRF+ oligodendrocytes (OL; Cluster 13, n = 114), DCX+/SOX4+/SOX11+ immature neurons (IN1; Cluster 8, n = 505) and DDX17+/FTX+ immature neurons (IN2; Cluster: 12; n = 166) (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 3k-l).
To explore the impact of the host degenerative retinal environment on the grafted hNPCs over time, the cluster-specific gene expression profile of hNPCs both before grafting and at P60 and P90 after transplantation was compared (Fig. 1d–h, Supplementary Fig. 4a-f). We found that clusters 0, 2, 5, 7, and 10 of grafted hNPCs expressed progenitor marker genes (NES, SOX2) over time (Fig. 1d). We also observed that grafted hNPC clusters associated with astrocyte lineage (clusters: 0, 1, 7, 10, and 11) expressed mature astrocyte marker genes (AQP4, GJA1) compared to in vitro hNPCs with astrocyte lineage, suggesting a prevalence of mature astrocyte states of grafted hNPCs in vivo (Fig. 1g). Interestingly, we also noticed that cluster 2 of grafted hNPCs is enriched for both astrocyte- (S100B, GFAP) and oligodendrocyte-specific (PDGFRA, OLIG1, OLIG2) genes (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Further, we found that clusters 3 and 5 of grafted hNPCs are enriched for oligodendrocyte precursor markers (PDGERA, OLIG1, OLIG2) compared to clusters 3 and 5 of hNPCs in vitro, suggesting the acquisition of an early oligodendrocyte fate of grafted hNPCs in the host degenerative retinal environment (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 4b). Like previous studies28,29, both clusters 3 and 5 (oligodendrocyte precursor cell clusters: OPC1 and 2) also showed the enrichment of genes involved in neuron differentiation and development (HES6, SOX11, DCX; Supplementary Fig. 4d-e), indicating that they may have the capability to differentiate into neurons in vivo. Oligodendrocyte fate acquisition of grafted hNPCs is further supported by the presence of a small oligodendrocyte cluster enriched with mature oligodendrocyte genes (MBP, MOB), identified as cluster 13 only in grafted hNPCs, but absent in the in vitro hNPCs (Fig. 1f). Gene ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis of upregulated genes in cluster 13 also revealed the enrichment of biological processes related to oligodendrocyte differentiation, myelination, and galactolipid biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Clusters 8 and 12 were enriched with early-developing neuronal genes (Cluster 8: SOX4, SOX11, DCX and Cluster 12: SOX11, DDX17) in grafted hNPCs compared to in vitro hNPCs (Fig. 1c, h, Supplementary Fig. 4b). Clusters 4, 6, and 9, which are enriched with marker genes of different astrocyte maturation stages (CD44, HEPACAM, ALDOC, SLC16A3) were greatly reduced in grafted hNPCs (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 4a-b).
We further validated these transcriptomic findings at the protein level by performing immunofluorescence staining on hNPC-treated retinal sections (Fig. 1i). Grafted hNPCs formed a distinct cell layer in the subretinal space and were associated with photoreceptor protection, consistent with our previous studies12,19. These cells showed strong immunoreactivity for neural progenitor markers, Nestin and SOX2, and robust expression of astrocytic markers GFAP and AQP4. A small subset of OLIG2+ OPC-like cells was observed, while only a limited number of cells expressed neuronal markers MAP2 and DCX. Although scRNA-seq identified a small MBP+ mature oligodendrocyte cluster, MBP protein expression was not detected in tissue sections, likely reflecting the low abundance of these cells in vivo. Overall, these findings indicate that the majority of grafted hNPCs predominantly adopt an astrocytic fate and gradually mature over time within the host retinal environment.

## hNPCs secrete trophic factors for survival and retinal protection
We have previously shown that hNPCs provide retinal protection by secreting various neurotrophic factors, including insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2)11. Changes in the therapeutic profile over time may be partially due to reduced secretion of neurotrophic factors. Here, we found that in addition to FGF, hNPCs expressed mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF), myeloid-derived growth factor (MYDGF), midkine (MDK), and pleiotrophin (PTN) both in vitro and in vivo as well as the expression of their receptors (Fig. 2a–c). Notably, MANF, MYDGF, MDK, and PTN, as well as their receptors, were expressed at relatively high levels across the subpopulations of hNPCs (Supplementary Fig. 5). Interestingly, there was a significantly higher expression of MDK, PTN, and their receptors (PTPRZ1, CSPG5) in P60 compared to P90 grafted hNPCs (Fig. 2a). MDK and PTN have been shown to promote differentiation, migration, and survival of neural stem and progenitor cells30–32. These trophic factors provide neuroprotection, reduce oxidative stress, and have anti-apoptotic effects30,31,33. MDK and PTN may act on hNPCs via their receptors in an autocrine manner to promote their stability, survival, and migration in the degenerative retinal environment. Additionally, secreted MDK and PTN might act paracrinally on Müller glia through their receptors and protect photoreceptors34. We also found a significant increase in MANF, MYDGF, and their receptor KDELR1 in grafted hNPCs at P90 compared to P60 (Fig. 2b). MANF has significant neuro-regenerative potential and protects photoreceptors from degeneration35–37. MYDGF promotes NPC survival by maintaining their stemness38,39. Additionally, autocrine and paracrine effects of MANF and MYDGF can provide neuroprotection by inhibiting apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation, and enhancing metabolic activity36–38,40–45. We validated our findings using immunofluorescence of MANF, MYDGF, and their receptor KDELR in grafted hNPCs at P60 and P90. Double immunolabeling using human-specific nuclear marker HuNu and MANF demonstrated cytoplasmic and perinuclear puncta staining of MANF at both P60 and P90 (Fig. 2d, e, upper panel). ## Grafted hNPC adaptation in a degenerative retinal environment
Grafted hNPCs undergo extensive biological changes over time in response to the host degenerative retinal environment. To investigate the adaptation and functional shifts, we compared transcriptomic profiles of grafted hNPCs at P60 and P90 with in vitro hNPCs (Fig. 3a, b). At P60, grafted hNPCs displayed increased expression of genes involved in differentiation, neuron projection morphogenesis, synaptic signaling, and survival pathways, reflecting their integration within the host retina (Fig. 1i). By P90, fewer genes showed differential expression, indicating stabilization and maturation of hNPCs in the host environment (Fig. 3a–d). Common pathways were enriched across P60 and P90, including cellular processes supporting differentiation, migration, axonogenesis, endocytosis, and autophagy, as well as suppression of immune activation (e.g., TGF-β regulation). Temporal analyses revealed distinct transcriptomic changes in P60 and P90 hNPCs. At P60, grafted hNPCs upregulated genes linked to neuron development, adhesion, and axonogenesis while downregulated genes related to metabolic pathways, reflecting early adaptation to the host retina (Supplementary Fig. 6a-h). By P90, there was increased expression of genes associated with glial differentiation and immune responses, along with further suppression of metabolic pathways, suggesting a shift toward immune and stress-responsive phenotypes as degeneration progressed (Supplementary Fig. 6i-o). These changes reflect ongoing adaptation and maturation of hNPCs within a progressively degenerative environment.
To understand the impact of the degenerative retinal environment on the transcriptomic remodeling of grafted hNPCs over time, we conducted a comparative transcriptomic analysis between P60 and P90 hNPCs (Fig. 3f). At P90, hNPCs exhibited enhanced expression of genes related to phagocytosis, energy metabolism (e.g., mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, oxidative phosphorylation), and cellular stress and death (e.g., response to oxidative stress, apoptosis) (Fig. 3g), reflecting increased metabolic demands and stress responses. Notably, the upregulation of lysosomal and phagosome-related genes (e.g., CTSD, LAMP1, LAMP2) suggests active clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) from the subretinal space, which is critical for photoreceptor survival in the RCS rats (Fig. 3h)19. However, the downregulation of pathways such as FoxO signaling, TGF-β signaling, and neurotrophin signaling, coupled with increased oxidative stress and immune activation, indicates a probable gradual shift of hNPCs toward a stress-responsive phenotype as degeneration progresses (Fig. 3g–i, Supplementary Fig. 7a-l).

## Retinal transcriptomic remodeling following hNPC transplantation
To assess the impact of hNPC treatment on the host degenerative retinal cells, we performed scRNA-seq from untreated and hNPC-treated retinas at P60 and P90, alongside wild-type (WT) controls. A total of 118,478 high-quality cells, encompassing all major retinal cell types, were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 8a-g). These included rods (14,675), cones (4341), rod bipolar cells (RBCs: 40,882), cone bipolar cells (CBCs: 9650 cells), Müller glia (MULG: 14,614), retinal ganglion cells (RGCs: 9654), amacrine cells (ACs: 2577), as well as microglia (MGs: 15,084), macrophages (MO: 519), and endothelial cells (ECs: 6482) (Supplementary Fig. 8h-i). Retinal degeneration in RP is marked by progressive loss of rods and cones, with corresponding changes in Müller glia and microglia. Müller glia, known to support photoreceptor health through neurotrophic and metabolic functions, exhibited altered transcriptional profiles, reflecting their response to retinal stress46. Similarly, microglia showed signs of activation and infiltration, consistent with their role in degenerative processes47. Hence, we focused on rods, cones, Müller glia, and microglia for further downstream analysis.

## Grafted hNPCs modulate photoreceptor dysfunction and drive rescue
To understand the biological changes in photoreceptors during retinal degeneration, we analyzed transcriptional signatures in rods and cones at P60 and P90. In untreated retinas, 1,811 and 1,685 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in rods at P60 and P90, respectively, while cones exhibited 1,634 and 2,664 DEGs (Fig. 4a–c). Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed downregulation of photoreceptor signaling, phototransduction pathway, G protein transmits signals (Gnb1, Cnga1, Pde6b) and TOR signaling (Ddit4, Lars1) in rods at P60, alongside progressive declines in genes essential for visual perception, photoreceptor maintenance, and glucose metabolism by P90, compared to WT (Fig. 4d, f). Concurrently, untreated rods showed upregulation of genes associated with synapse organization, axonogenesis (Lrrtm4, Nrxn3, Gnb3), calcium channel activity (Calm1, Cacna2d1, Robo2), ATP synthesis, neurotransmitter secretion/transport and downregulation of genes related to glycolysis (Hk2, Ldha), indicating metabolic dysfunction and neuronal remodeling (Fig. 4e, g). In untreated retinas, cones displayed similar transcriptional trends, with reduced expression of genes related to phototransduction, visual perception, and ATP metabolism alongside increased expression of synaptic signaling and axon outgrowth genes (Fig. 4e, g). To elucidate the photoreceptor protective mechanism after hNPC treatment, we conducted transcriptomic analysis between hNPC-treated and untreated rods and cones at P60 and P90. In rods, 688 differentially expressed genes were identified at P60 and 2130 at P90 following hNPC treatment (Fig. 4a). Of these, 457 DEGs at P60 and 1,198 at P90 were shared between untreated/WT and hNPC-treated/untreated. Importantly, among these shared DEGs, only 382 at P60 and 985 at P90 in hNPC-treated rods showed expression patterns similar to WT rods, defining them as rod-specific rescue genes (Fig. 4b, c). Further analysis of time point-specific DEGs revealed upregulation of photoreceptor maintenance, photoreceptor cilium, retinal homeostasis, and glycolysis (Hk2, Ldha) and downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis coupled to electron transport in P60- and P90 hNPC-treated rods (Fig. 4d–g). Additionally, P90 hNPC-treated rods showed upregulation of genes involved in photoreceptor inner/outer segments, visual and sensory perception (Cnga1, Rho, Pde6b), highlighting enhanced photoreceptor integrity and function (Fig. 4d, f, h, j). These data strongly demonstrated that hNPCs protect rods and slow down the progression of retinal degeneration (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figs. 1d-g, 2a-b), consistent with our previous studies12,19.
Next, we evaluated the transcriptomic changes in cones at P60 and P90 following hNPC treatment. We identified 1,912 DEGs at P60 and 1,254 DEGs at P90 in hNPC-treated cones compared to untreated (Fig. 4a). Among these, 761 at P60 and 592 at P90 were shared between untreated/WT and hNPC-treated/untreated cones. Intriguingly, only 172 DEGs in P60 and 289 in P90 hNPC-treated cones displayed expression patterns similar to WT cones, annotated as cone-specific rescue genes (Fig. 4b-c). GO analysis of time point-specific DEGs revealed that at P60, hNPC-treated cones exhibited increased expression of genes associated with anterograde trans-synaptic signaling and GTPase-mediated signal transduction (Fig. 4d, j), while at P90 hNPC-treated cones demonstrated upregulation of genes involved in synapse organization and lipid metabolism (Fig. 4f, k). P60 and P90 untreated cones showed upregulated genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation; while genes related to ATP synthesis coupled to electron transport are increased in only P60, not in P90, compared to hNPC-treated cones (Fig. 4e, g). However, mitochondrial organization and transport genes were consistently downregulated across time points in treated rods and cones, indicating potential energy production deficits critical for sustained photoreceptor function (Fig. 4e, g, Supplementary Fig. 9a-b). In contrast, compared to hNPC treatment, untreated P60 cones and P90 rods showed upregulated genes associated with autophagic cell death, consistent with previous findings suggesting that dysregulated autophagy contributes to photoreceptor cell death in rodent models for retinal degeneration19,48. Moreover, P60 untreated cones also showed upregulation of genes associated with light detection and oxidoreductase activity compared to hNPC-treated cones (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 9c). Notably, untreated P90 cones retained some functionality, with higher expression of phototransduction and light response genes compared to hNPC-treated cones, suggesting residual light responsiveness despite degeneration (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 9d). These results align with recent studies indicating that cones retain partial functionality even as degeneration progresses49.
hNPC treatment also significantly increased the expression of Nxnl1, encoding the rod-derived cone viability factor (RDCVF)50–52, which reduces oxidative stress and promotes aerobic glycolysis to support cone survival53,54, underscoring the therapeutic potential of hNPCs (Fig. 4l–o, Supplementary Fig. 10). Interestingly, untreated cones at P60 also exhibited elevated Nxnl1 expression compared to hNPC-treated cones, potentially reflecting an intrinsic compensatory response to rod loss. However, downregulation of mitochondrial genes in hNPC-treated photoreceptors highlights a need to address energy deficits to enhance long-term photoreceptor survival55,56. These findings demonstrate that hNPC treatment preserves retinal integrity by promoting photoreceptor function and maintenance despite limitations in sustaining mitochondrial energy production at the late stage of degeneration.

By comparing untreated Müller glia with WT, we first investigated the disease-associated changes in these cells over time. We identified 1,710 differentially expressed genes at P60 and 1,685 at P90 (Fig. 5a). Untreated Müller glia displayed increased expression of genes associated with reactive gliosis (Gfap, Lcn2) at both early (P60) and late (P90) stages of the disease, compared to WT, concordant with other retinal degenerative diseases (Fig. 5d–g)57. Specifically, at P60, untreated Müller glia exhibited downregulation of early responsive genes (Egr1, Fos, Jun) and upregulation of genes involved in inflammation, defense response, and apoptosis (Ifi27, Cp, Cst3, Clu), indicating the activation of stress and immune responses at the early stage of retinal degeneration. Additionally, at P60, untreated Müller glia also showed increased expression of genes (Nlgn1, Ephb1, Nrxn3) related to neurotransmitter transport and synapse organization, suggesting their compensatory role in remodeling and stabilizing synapses to support retinal connections at the early stage of disease. However, downregulation of genes involved in visual perception, photoreceptor cell maintenance, cellular response to calcium ions, and retina homeostasis reflects impaired support for photoreceptor function at the early stage. As the disease progresses, untreated Müller glia exhibited further upregulation of genes associated with glial cell proliferation (Vim, Gfap, Clu), innate immune response (Apoe, C1s, Lcn2), and autophagy (Gabarap, Ctsd, Sqstm1), coupled with downregulation of genes related to oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, indicating metabolic dysfunction at the late stage of disease. Consistent with previous studies, these findings suggest a shift in the Müller glial reactive status, transitioning from an early-stage compensatory to a late-stage decompensatory state with retinal degeneration progression.
Differential gene expression analysis identified 1,801 DEGs at P60 and 1,707 DEGs at P90 in untreated microglia compared to WT (Fig. 5a). At both time points, untreated microglia exhibited upregulation of genes involved in antigen processing and presentation (B2m, Cd74) and integrin-mediated signaling (Tyrobp, Itgb1). At P90, additional upregulation of genes related to the adaptive immune response (B2m, C3), interleukin-10 production (Plcg2, Cd84), and autophagosome membrane docking (Vamp8, Calm1) was observed (Fig. 5a). However, genes associated with phototransduction, visual perception, photoreceptor cell maintenance, and retina homeostasis were downregulated (Fig. 5d, f). Together, these findings may indicate a dynamic transition of microglia to a reactive state to mitigate cellular stress by enhancing autophagy and adaptive immune response, along with disease progression58.
Comparative analysis between hNPC-treated and untreated Müller glia identified 478 DEGs at P60 and 461 DEGs at P90 (Fig. 5a). Among these, 265 DEGs at P60 and 299 at P90 were shared across untreated/WT and hNPC-treated/untreated groups, with 159 (P60) and 261 (P90) displaying expression patterns similar to WT. These were classified as Müller glia-specific rescue genes (Fig. 5b, c). GO analysis revealed that hNPC treatment at P60 upregulated genes associated with synapse organization (Nlgn1, Nrxn3), cell adhesion (Sema5a, Neo1), axon guidance (Ephb1, Epha5), and glutaminergic synapse (Nlgn1, Gria2), highlighting a neuroprotective role in maintaining synaptic stability and retinal integrity (Fig. 5d, h). By P90, upregulated genes were linked to cytokine production (Egr1, Csf1r), cell migration (Ccl4, Jun), as well as immune, inflammatory, and defense responses (C1qa, Nfkbia), indicating adaptation to the progressively degenerative environment (Fig. 5f, i). Interestingly, at P60, hNPC-treated Müller glia exhibited downregulation of genes related to phototransduction and light response, while at P90, genes related to cellular metal ion homeostasis and calcium ion transport (Mt2A, Smdt1, Calm1) were downregulated (Fig. 5e, g). However, key reactive gliosis genes (Gfap, Vim) were consistently downregulated in hNPC-treated Müller glia at both time points, indicating suppression of reactive gliosis following hNPC treatment (Fig. 5j), consistent with our previous studies19,20.
At P60, hNPC-treated microglia showed increased expression of genes related to oxidative phosphorylation, ATP synthesis, and NAD(P)H dehydrogenase (quinone) activity (Mt-nd3, Mt-atp8) (Fig. 5d), reflecting enhanced homeostatic functions such as surveillance and phagocytosis, which rely on oxidative metabolism59. By contrast, in inflammatory microglia, metabolic remodeling suppresses their oxidative phosphorylation60. However, by P90, hNPC-treated microglia showed upregulation of disease-associated microglial (DAM) genes61 related to inflammatory and immune responses (Icam1, Clec7a, Apoe, Il1b, Spp1) (Fig. 5f, k) along with genes involved in the homeostatic process (Igf1, Nfe2l), lipid metabolism (Apoe, Abca1, Trem2), and negative regulation of TNF production (Cd274, Tnfaip3), suggesting an adaptive response to stabilize retinal homeostasis, modulate inflammation, and mitigate damage. Conversely, hNPC-treated microglia at P60 showed downregulation of genes involved in peptide metabolism (Rpl30, Rps12), while at P90, genes related to ribosome biogenesis (Rplp1, Rps2), protein folding (Hsp90aa1, Hsph1), and complement cascades (C1qa, C3) were downregulated. These changes reflect metabolic and functional remodeling in microglia in response to a progressively degenerating retinal environment.
In sum, our findings suggest that hNPC treatment provides neuroprotection over time via dynamic transcriptomic remodeling of glial cells within the progressively degenerating retinal environment.

## Progressive loss of hNPC–retinal cell crosstalk impairs protection
To investigate how grafted hNPCs interact and communicate with host retinal cells over time, we analyzed cell–cell communication dynamics across the combined datasets. We first integrated single-cell datasets from grafted hNPCs with hNPC-treated host retinal cells by mapping high-confidence one-to-one orthologous genes using ENSEMBL multiple species comparison tool62. We then generated a joint UMAP using the Seurat v4 workflow and applied Harmony for batch correction63 (Fig. 7a). Next, we applied CellChat to identify and compare the signaling pathways mediating the interaction between hNPCs and retinal cells, based on the expression of ligand-receptor pairs. This analysis revealed a decreased number of signaling interactions (Fig. 7b) and intercellular communication strength (Fig. 7c) between hNPCs and host retinal cells at P90 compared to P60 (Fig. 7d-e). This analysis revealed downregulation of trophic factor, synaptic, and cell adhesion signaling pathways at P90 compared to P60 (Fig. 7h-i, Supplementary Figs. 13–16). MANF interacts with NPTN to mitigate inflammation and apoptosis by inhibiting NF-κB signaling43,65, and has demonstrated immunomodulatory properties that promote tissue repair and regeneration in the retina36. Additionally, MANF has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects in the brain following ischemic injury by increasing the number of phagocytic innate immune cells66. Interestingly, double immunostaining of IBA1 and MANF confirmed significantly higher MANF expression and increased MANF+ microglia in the hNPC-treated retina at P60 compared to P90 (Fig. 7k-m). Furthermore, overall increased MANF expression was detected in P60 hNPC-treated retina compared to P90 (Fig. 7k-l), reinforcing that MANF plays a critical role in photoreceptor protection and immune modulation, with its decline at P90 potentially contributing to reduced retinal protection. Further, while BMP can induce retinal regeneration and provide neuroprotection by upregulated FGF signaling67,68, the downregulation of BMP signaling at P90 (Supplemenaty Fig. 13g-h) underscores the progressive weakening of neuroprotective mechanisms over time.
Cell adhesion molecule (CAM) signaling, like NECTIN, NRXN, and NCAM1 was significantly upregulated at P60 (Supplementary Fig. 14a-f), contributing to synaptic stability and visual signal transmission. Immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis of NCAM1 further revealed a marked reduction in NCAM1 expression in grafted hNPCs and across multiple retinal cell layers in P90 hNPC-treated retina compared to P60 (Supplementary Fig. 14g-h). Notably, NECTIN signaling was detected across all retinal cell types except rods, hNPCs, and microglia. By P90, CAM signaling declined, potentially weakening synaptic connectivity between retinal neurons and disrupting visual signals transmission, ultimately compromising visual function69–71. Additionally, COLLAGEN and LAMININ signaling, essential for ECM remodeling, structural integrity, and cell-ECM interactions72–74 were significantly downregulated at P90 (Fig. 8a–d), indicating weakened ECM integrity and reduced structural support for retinal cells during disease progression. Protein level validation confirmed these findings, showing a significant decrease in Collagen IV (Col IV) and IMPG2 expression in P90 hNPC-treated retina compared to P60 (Fig. 8e–g). Furthermore, Tenascin C (TNC), an ECM glycoprotein that creates a specialized and supportive environment and promotes the migration of hNPCs75 was markedly reduced and exhibited a disorganized pattern in P90 hNPCs, whereas P60 hNPCs displayed a well-organized TNC+ ECM layer (Fig. 8h). Despite these declines, CADM signaling-based communications emerged at P90 between hNPCs and retinal neurons, including rods, cones, and CBCs, which were absent at P60 (Supplementary Fig. 15a-b). CADM signaling (CADM1 and CADM3) plays a key role in visual circuit formation by regulating axon guidance, neurite growth, and synapse formation69,76. Similarly, CDH signaling was reduced at P90 but showed newly established communications between rods and other retinal cells, including cones, RBCs, CBCs, and ACs, which may partially be compensating for declining synaptic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 15c-d). ## Discussion
Stem cell therapy for retinal degenerative diseases has been extensively investigated in preclinical and clinical settings, showing promise for vision preservation. However, efficacy deterioration over time has been reported consistently. The mechanisms underlying this decline as well as the status of grafted stem cells and their interaction with host retinal cells over time, remain poorly understood. Using scRNA-seq, we investigated temporal transcriptomic changes in grafted hNPCs and host retinal cells following subretinal injection into a rodent model of RP. Our findings revealed dynamic shifts in gene expression as degeneration progresses. Grafted hNPCs primarily differentiated into an astroglial phenotype and matured over time, contributing to vision protection through multiple mechanisms, including trophic factor secretion, metabolic regulation, suppression of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation, as well as ECM remodeling. Notably, MANF signaling emerged as a key mechanism underlying hNPC-mediated retinal protection, contributing to photoreceptor survival, immune modulation, and inflammation control. However, the gradual decline in therapeutic efficacy over time may reflect reduced trophic support from both donor cells and the host retina, coupled with increasing oxidative stress, inflammation, and weakened graft–host signaling interactions. Together, these findings highlight the central importance of the host retinal environment in supporting graft survival and function, underscoring the need to pair stem cell therapy with strategies that fortify the host retina to sustain long-term vision preservation.
We identified that hNPCs express key trophic factors such as MANF, MYDGF, MDK, and PTN, which play critical roles in photoreceptor survival and maintenance by mitigating oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. The stage-specific upregulation of these trophic factors indicates hNPCs dynamically modulate their trophic factor profile over time, adapting to the degenerative environment. Both MANF and MYDGF are known to exhibit autocrine and paracrine effects36,37,44,45. Increased expression of MANF, MYDGF, and their receptors in grafted hNPCs at P90 compared to P60 suggests their autocrine neuroprotection to hNPCs, supporting their self-protection, stability, and survival in the progressively degenerating retinal environment. Moreover, increased secretory MANF and MYDGF, alongside their autocrine protection to hNPCs, may also protect photoreceptors via KDELR through paracrine action77. At P60, increased MDK and PTN in grafted hNPCs likely promote their survival, migration, and differentiation. However, as retinal degeneration progresses and cellular stress increases, hNPCs exhibit a distinct shift in trophic factor expression, with a notable upregulation of MANF and MYDGF at P90. This shift may suggest an adaptive mechanism mediating the transition of hNPCs from developmental support (MDK, PTN) to stress-responsive neuroprotection (MANF, MYDGF) to counteract oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune activation, ultimately supporting photoreceptor survival. Altogether, these findings suggest that MDK and PTN promote grafted hNPC migration and survival through their autocrine and paracrine action, while MANF and MYDGF promote grafted hNPC survival and function via mitigating cellular oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune response as degeneration progresses. Given their critical roles in hNPC stability and retinal protection, these trophic factors could serve as therapeutic targets to enhance the efficacy of hNPC-based therapies for retinal degenerative diseases.
Grafted hNPCs also induced distinct transcriptomic changes across retinal cell types, preserving gene expression patterns associated with visual perception and photoreceptor maintenance, and mitigating degeneration. Notably, hNPCs significantly increased the expression of RdCVF: Nxnl1 in rods, a critical factor for cone survival and metabolic support53. In RP, rod degeneration depletes RdCVF, leading to cone loss78. In agreement with our previous findings19, hNPC treatment counteracted this degenerative cascade by restoring RdCVF expression, promoting cone viability and function.
Inflammation and immune activation are hallmarks of retinal degeneration, making immune regulation critical for stem cell therapy success79,80. Our findings indicate that grafted hNPC supports photoreceptor survival by modulating the behavior of retinal supporting cells, including Müller glia and microglia, to maintain the retinal microenvironment. At P60, hNPC treatment promoted microglial surveillance and phagocytosis, facilitating clearance of shed POS and mitigating oxidative stress, key processes for maintaining retinal homeostasis81. By P90, microglia transitioned to phagocytic phenotypes with increased lipid metabolism to mitigate further photoreceptor degeneration82,83. Similarly, Müller glia upregulated genes involved in phototransduction and light stimulus in response to retinal degeneration. Tomita et al. demonstrated a Müller glial compensatory increase in genes involved in phototransduction and light stimulus pathways to support photoreceptor function in the degenerating retina84. Consistent with this study, our findings indicate a reactive or compensatory response of Müller glia to photoreceptor loss and their function in the degenerate retina. However, in hNPC-treated Müller glia, downregulation of these genes suggests hNPC-mediated modulation of Müller glial reactivity and a transcriptional shift from compensatory photoreceptor-like responses toward a supportive, neuroprotective state. While these data indicate transcriptional changes, we cannot completely exclude the possibility of the presence of the phagocytosed photoreceptor transcripts in the Müller glia. Given their phagocytic activity, Müller glia can engulf the degenerating photoreceptors/POS, and their subsequent digestion may also lead to the appearance of photoreceptor transcripts in the transcriptomic profile. At early stage P60, hNPCs provide neuroprotection by stabilizing synaptic connections19,85, suppressing reactive gliosis19,20, and maintaining retinal homeostasis81,86. However, as the disease progresses, by P90, Müller glia exhibit increased immune and inflammatory responses, lipid metabolism, and cytokine production, consistent with a transition to a reactive state and a decline in hNPC-mediated protection. Together, these findings indicate that hNPC treatment dynamically modulates glial responses to preserve retinal structure at early stages, but its efficacy declines as Müller glia adopt a reactive and pro-inflammatory phenotype over time, which could, at least in part, contribute to the reduced therapeutic efficacy observed at late stages. hNPC treatment also stimulated Müller glia to upregulate neurotrophic signaling pathways, reinforcing their role in maintaining retinal homeostasis. Concurrently, hNPC treatment reduced pro-inflammatory gene expression in microglia, potentially due to MANF-driven immune modulation. Notably, weakened Müller-microglial interactions in untreated retinas were associated with increased photoreceptor apoptosis and impaired retinal homeostasis46. In contrast, hNPC treatment restored bi-directional Müller glia and microglia crosstalk, enabling a coordinated response to support retinal integrity and slow the progression of retinal degeneration.
Using CellChat, we identified trophic factor (MANF, FGF, BMP), synaptic (NRXN, NCAM), cell adhesion (CADM, CDH), and ECM-related (COLLAGEN, LAMININ) signaling pathways as key mechanisms of hNPC-mediated photoreceptor protection over time. At P60, these pathways were highly upregulated, ensuring trophic support, synaptic stability, and ECM integrity, essential for maintaining photoreceptor health. However, the downregulation of these pathways, including MANF signaling at P90, likely reflects a progressive decline in trophic factor support, synaptic stability, and ECM integrity, resulting in reduced photoreceptor protection. Interestingly, although MANF expression increased in P90 hNPCs, MANF signaling declined, suggesting that endogenous MANF is insufficient for sustained photoreceptor protection and immune modulation. These findings highlight the potential of genetically engineered hNPCs with enhanced MANF secretion as a promising therapeutic strategy for long-term photoreceptor survival and function. By augmenting MANF expression in hNPCs, this approach could counteract the progressive decline in trophic support and intercellular communication, ultimately improving the stability, survival, and therapeutic efficacy of hNPCs in the degenerative retinal environment.
Furthermore, these results also emphasize the need for a comprehensive therapeutic strategy that not only enhances MANF secretion but adapts to evolving hNPC-host retinal interactions. Combining hNPC therapy with interventions targeting synaptic support mechanisms in the early degeneration stage and immune modulation in the later stage may be critical for optimizing vision preservation.
Our study comprehensively explores the temporal gene expression dynamics in grafted hNPCs and their impact on host retinal cells within the degenerative retinal environment. Grafted hNPCs survived, migrated, and primarily differentiated into an astroglial phenotype, expressing multiple trophic factors that stimulated rods, cones, Müller glia, and microglia, promoting neuroprotection and retinal homeostasis. Overall, grafted hNPCs dynamically adapt to the degenerative retinal environment, exhibiting shifts in differentiation, metabolic activity, and immune response over time. Concurrently, hNPC transplantation modulates host retinal cell behavior, enhancing retinal protection and reducing inflammatory responses. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of hNPCs in mitigating retinal degeneration and preserving retinal function, highlighting them as a promising strategy for the treatment of retinal degenerative diseases.

## Limitations of the study
While this study provides valuable insights into the transcriptomic remodeling of grafted hNPCs and host retinal cells over time, additional early and later time points are needed to capture the temporal dynamics of these changes. A more comprehensive time-course analysis of host retinal degeneration will provide details of the molecular changes of photoreceptors and retinal supporting cells, which will assist in identifying the optimal therapeutic window for intervention. Another limitation is that a single subretinal injection of stem cells only affects about one-third of the retinal area, leaving the rest of the retina undergoing progressive retinal degeneration. Multiple injections covering most of the retinal area could reduce the negative impact of the degenerative retinal environment and promote donor cell survival and function. Finally, while our study identified multiple therapeutic targets and pathways, future research should explore the genetic manipulation of these targets and conduct functional studies to establish causality and assess their efficacy in enhancing hNPC-based therapy for retinal degeneration.

## Methods

## Ethical statement
All animal experimental procedures were approved by Cedars-Sinai Medical Center’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC 7611) and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

## Animals
The Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rats were maintained in a 12 h light/dark cycle at 20–25 °C and controlled humidity (30–70%) in a pathogen-free facility with ad libitum access to food and water. Both male and female rats were used for all experiments. The rats between postnatal day (P) 21-23 were randomly assigned to two groups: one group received a subretinal injection of human neural progenitors (hNPCs) (n = 65), while the other served as untreated control (n = 44). To minimize the immune response to human-derived cells in this xenograft model, cyclosporine A was administered via drinking water (210 mg/L), starting 1 day prior to transplantation and continuing until sacrifice. The experiment was repeated 3 times for P60 (untreated: n = 24; hNPC-treated: n = 30) and 2 times for P90 (untreated: n = 20; hNPC-treated: n = 35) time points.

## Cell preparation and transplantation
The generation and expansion of hNPCs were conducted by Svendsen et al. in 199887. Briefly, human neural progenitor cells were isolated from the developing human cortex and grown in culture as sphere-shaped clusters. The spheres were sectioned into quarters, and cell-cell contacts were maintained. This simple and novel culture method allows rapid expansion of large numbers of hNPCs. Research-grade hNPCs were used in this study under the Stem Cell Research Oversight Committee (Pro00025772) at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center. Cells were prepared according to our published protocol20. Briefly, hNPC vials were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath after removal from liquid nitrogen, followed by resuspension in prewarmed Stemline medium (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri) supplemented with rhEGF (100 ng/mL; Millipore, Billerica, MA) and rhLIF (100 ng/mL; Millipore). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in Balanced salt solution (BSS, Alcon). Cells were then labeled using PKH26 Red Fluorescent Cell Linker Kits (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and finally resuspended at a concentration of 30,000 cells/μL in BSS. Cells were kept on ice until transplantation. Cell injections were performed following a previously established protocol20. Briefly, 2 μL of the hNPC suspension (6 × 10^4 cells/eye) was injected into the subretinal space of both left and right eyes through a small scleral incision using a fine glass pipette (internal diameter 75–150 μm) attached via tubing to a 25-μL syringe (Hamilton). To reduce intraocular pressure and minimize cell efflux, the cornea was punctured prior to injection. Immediately after injection, the fundus was examined for retinal damage or signs of vascular distress. Animals showing such issues were excluded from further study.

## Efficacy evaluation

## Optokinetic response (OKR)
OKR testing provides a non-invasive method for assessing spatial visual acuity, measured in cycles per degree (c/d). Visual acuity was evaluated at P60 and P90, following our previously published protocol11,20. Animals were tested using a virtual reality–based OKR system consisting of an elevated circular platform surrounded by four computer monitors and a downward-facing overhead camera (OptoMotry HD, Cerebral Mechanics, Lethbridge, Canada). The monitors displayed rotating vertical grating patterns generated and controlled by OptoMotry HD software, with spatial frequencies ranging from 0.003 to 0.6 c/d. The movement of this grating induces an optokinetic response, where the rodents’ eyes and head track the movement involuntarily. Using the OKR OptoMotry system, measurements are limited to 0.6 c/d because reliability decreases at higher spatial frequencies. Due to lateralized visual processing and the rodent preference for temporal-to-nasal motion, each eye was assessed independently by presenting clockwise rotation to preferentially stimulate the left eye and counterclockwise rotation to stimulate the right eye. OKR testing was terminated when animals no longer exhibited tracking responses to the visual stimulus. The OKR results were observed and recorded by two independent, blinded investigators. Data were analyzed after incorporating our previous study datasets.

## Electroretinography (ERG)
Electroretinography (ERG) measures the overall electrical activity of the retina in response to light stimulation. ERG recordings were performed at P60 and P90 according to our previously established protocol19,20. In brief, following overnight (14–16 h) dark adaptation, animals were prepared under dim red light. Scotopic ERGs were recorded by stimulating the eyes with 5 ms white-light flashes at an intensity of 25 cd·s/m² with 10 s interstimulus intervals using a computer-controlled Espion system (Diagnosys LLC). A total of 20–30 sweeps were recorded for each animal, and the averaged responses were used to determine the response amplitude and assess rod-mediated retinal function. Data were analyzed after incorporating our previous study datasets.

## Isolation of grafted hNPCs and rat retinal cells
Rats were sacrificed at P60 and P90 time points after visual function tests. Eyes were enucleated, and retinas were dissected in AMES solution (equilibrated with 95% O2/5% CO2). Under a fluorescent dissecting microscope, red fluorescent region containing the injection site (dorsal-temporal part) was dissected from the retina. For untreated retinas, the dorsal-temporal part corresponding to the same location in the cell-treated eye was dissected. Retinas from 8-15 rats were pooled, digested with papain, and dissociated into single-cell suspensions through manual trituration in ovomucoid solution88. Single-cell suspensions from hNPC-treated and untreated retinas were labeled with DAPI. PKH26-labeled hNPCs, along with hNPC-treated and untreated retinal cells, were sorted using a FACS sorter (FACSAria III, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and collected into 1.7 mL RNase-free tubes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) containing 2% BSA in PBS. From hNPC-treated cell suspension, PKH26-labeled hNPCs and treated retinal cells were collected in separate tubes.

## Single-cell RNA sequencing and library preparation
The sorted grafted hNPCs, rat retinal cell suspensions, and in vitro hNPCs were immediately processed for single-cell RNA sequencing following the 10x Chromium v3 single-cell reagent kit protocol for library preparation. cDNA library quantification and quality were assessed with D1000 or D5000 HS kit for the Agilent TapeStation 4200. Libraries were sequenced using Illumina Novaseq 6000 S4 platform using 100 bp paired-end sequencing for a sequencing depth of 50,000 read pairs/cell.

## Histology and immunofluorescence staining
Eyes were enucleated from P60 and P90 time points, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded in OCT for cryostat sections. Retinal cryostat sections were placed on a series of 5 slides, with 4 sections per slide. The first slide of each series was used for cresyl violet staining to visualize retinal lamination and donor cell location. The remaining slides were then used for immunofluorescent staining following previously described protocols20,89. Briefly, retinal sections were rehydrated in 1× PBS and blocked for 1 h in 2.5% BSA solution containing 5% serum (respective to the antibodies). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight with following antibodies: rabbit anti-MANF (1:200; Sigma, HPA011175), rabbit anti-MYDGF (1:200; Proteintech, 11353-1-AP), rabbit anti-KDELR (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-101011), mouse anti-human nuclear marker (1:300; Millipore, MAB1281), goat anti-Iba1 (1:1000; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, 019-197441), goat anti-doublecortin (1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-8066), mouse anti-MAP2 (2a + 2b) (1:500; Sigma, M1406), mouse anti-RPE65 (1:500; Sigma, MAB5428), mouse anti-AQP4 (1:500; R&D Systems, MAB11659-SP), rabbit anti-collagen IV (1:200; Abcam, ab19808), mouse anti-tenascin C (1:500; Abcam, ab86182), rabbit anti-cone arrestin (1:1000; Millipore, AB15282), rabbit anti-GFAP (1:1000; Dako–Agilent, Z0334), rabbit anti-nestin (1:2000; Millipore, ABD69), rabbit anti-NCAM1 (1:200; Proteintech, 14255-1-AP), rabbit anti-IMPG2 (1:200; Abcam, ab272558), rabbit anti-oligodendrocyte-specific protein (1:100; Abcam, ab7474), rabbit anti-recoverin (1:2000; Millipore, AB5585), rabbit anti-SOX2 (D6D9) XP (1:400; Cell Signaling Technology, 3579), and rabbit anti-TXNL6 (1:200; Proteintech, 11203-1-AP). Following incubation with the primary antibodies, sections were washed three times for 5 min each in 1× PBS and incubated with anti-mouse or -rabbit or -goat secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor-488 or Alexa Fluor-568 for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with 49,69-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and mounted using Fluorescent Mount media (Sigma-Aldrich). Images were taken with a Leica or Nikon microscope.

## Image analysis
Differences in immunoreactivity of MANF, Col IV, IMPG2, and NCAM1 between untreated and hNPC-treated retina at P60 and P90 were assessed as fluorescent intensities (FI) with our previous protocol88. In brief, for FI measurements, MANF, Col IV, IMPG2, and NCAM1 positive region of interest (ROI) was selected using ImageJ freehand tool. Fluorescent intensities of ROIs were measured after normalizing to the background. Fluorescent intensity values were averaged (4 sections per retina and 8-9 retinas per group; each section 40μm apart) to determine signal density. The FI values were presented as arbitrary units. The processing and analysis were kept consistent between images. Statistical analysis was performed on average FI values (mean ± SEM). MANF+ microglia were counted manually (4 sections per retina and 10 retinas per group; each section 40 μm apart). Image analysis was performed blindly and separately by 2 observers.

## Preliminary processing scRNA-seq raw data of hNPCs and retinal cells
Cell Ranger software (version 4.0.0) (https://support.10xgenomics.com/single-cell-gene-expression/software/pipelines/4.0/what-is-cell-ranger) was used to perform preliminary processing of single-cell sequencing data, including aligning sequencing reads to the reference transcriptome (hNPCs: GRCh38; rat: Rnor6), estimating and filtering, and generating feature-barcode matrices. Wild-type rat sequencing data from Wang et al. were downloaded from the GEO database (GSE209872: GSM6403183 and GSM6403184).

## Removing low-quality cells and identification of cell types
Seurat (version 4.4.0) software was used to perform downstream quality control, integration, identification of cell types, and subsequent differential expression gene analysis on the gene-cell expression matrices obtained above90. For hNPC datasets, cells with less than 250 and greater than 20,000 UMIs, less than 200 genes, and mitochondrial gene percent greater than 20% were excluded as low-quality cells. For rat retinal cell datasets, cells with counts, genes, mitochondrial gene percentage, and ribosomal gene percentage outside of three standard deviations from the mean were excluded as low-quality cells.
For hNPCs, the ‘LogNormalize’ function was used for normalization and scaling of the expression matrix for each sample, and then the ‘SelectIntegrationFeatures’ and ‘FindIntegrationAnchors’ functions were used to select the features and anchors for downstream integration. These anchor points were then used to integrate all datasets of the hNPCs through the ‘IntegrateData’ function. Next, the ‘ScaleData’ and ‘RunPCA’ functions were used for scaling and calculating principal component analysis (PCA) dimensions in the hNPC integrated datasets. Unsupervised clustering was then performed using the ‘FindNeighbors’ and ‘FindClusters’ functions, grouping the datasets into distinct clusters. Dimensionality reduction was conducted using the ‘RunUMAP’ function. Clusters were annotated based on known retinal cell type markers and hNPC-specific gene markers. Subsequently, hNPC clusters were subset and reprocessed following the same steps described above. The ‘FindAllMarkers’ and ‘FindConservedMarkers’ functions were used to identify and annotate specific markers for the hNPC clusters.
Similarly, for retinal cells, the expression matrix for each sample was normalized and scaled using the ‘SCTransform’ function91. This was followed by feature and anchor selection with the ‘PrepSCTIntegration’ and ‘FindIntegrationAnchors’ functions. The identified anchors were then used to integrate all datasets from the same tissue using the ‘IntegrateData’ function. Subsequently, the ‘ScaleData’ and ‘RunPCA’ functions were employed to scale the data and perform principal component analysis (PCA) on the integrated datasets of hNPCs and rat retinal cells. Unsupervised clustering of the datasets into distinct clusters was performed using the ‘FindNeighbors’ and ‘FindClusters’ functions. Dimensionality reduction was carried out using the ‘RunUMAP’ function. To investigate the gene expression profile of each cluster, the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function (with avg_log2FC > 0.4) was used to identify highly expressed genes (marker genes) that are specific to each cluster and correspond to distinct cell types based on marker gene expression.

## Differential gene expression and functional analysis
To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between in vitro and in vivo hNPCs at P60 and P90, the ‘FindMarkers’ function with the MAST92 test in Seurat was applied. DEGs between wild-type, untreated, and hNPC-treated retinal cells, across different retinal cell types, were identified using the ‘FindMarkers’ function with the Wilcoxon test. For hNPCs, we used the EnrichR93 R package (version 3.2) and the ‘gseGO’ and ‘gseKEGG’ functions from the clusterProfiler94 R package (version 4.10.1) to identify enriched biological pathways and processes in our sets of DEGs, as well as in DEGs shared across different differential expression analyses. For rat retinal cells, we employed the ‘gseGO’ and ‘gseKEGG’ functions from the clusterProfiler R package to determine enriched biological pathways and processes in our DEG sets.

## Cell-cell ligand-receptor interaction analysis
We performed cell-cell ligand-receptor interaction in our scRNA-seq integrated rat retinal cell and hNPC datasets with CellChat95 (v 1.6.1), using cell type labels. The human CellChatDB ligand-receptor interaction database was used for this analysis. To facilitate downstream comparisons of the signaling networks in untreated versus hNPC-treated and P60 versus P90 hNPC-treated groups, we ran the CellChat workflow separately on each dataset from different time points. The CellChat object was created using the normalized gene expression matrix and the cell type annotations, removing any cell groups with fewer than 10 cells. We then ran the recommended CellChat workflow using the following functions: identifyOverExpressedGenes, identifyOverExpressedInteractions, projectData, computeCommunProb, filterCommunication, subsetCommunication, computeCommunProbPathway, aggregateNet, and netAnalysis_computeCentrality. We separately merged P60 and P90 untreated and hNPC-treated and P60 and P90 hNPC-treated CellChat objects into one object using the mergeCellChat function. We compared the signaling networks separately at P60 and P90 between untreated and hNPC-treated groups and then between P60 and P90 hNPC-treated groups both functionally and structurally using the computeNetSimilarityPairwise function. Further, we used the rankNet function to compute the relative information flow changes between the groups across all signaling pathways. We identified differentially expressed ligands and receptors as well as their signaling pathways using the identifyOverExpressedGenes function, visualizing results with the netAnalysis_signalingRole_heatmap.

## Statistics and reproducibility
No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Investigators were blinded to experimental group allocation during data collection and analysis, where applicable. Single-cell RNA sequencing data were analyzed with appropriate batch correction methods. Visual function tests were performed on multiple animals per group. Histological analyses were performed at least three times to ensure reproducibility. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Software’s PRISM (version 10), and the results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Results were analyzed by One-way ANOVA coupled with a Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for more than 2-group. For ERG and OKR, One-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test, coupled with Dunn’s Multiple comparison test were used. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No data were excluded from the analyses.

## Figure Fig. 7: CellChat analysis identified MANF signaling as a key mediator of hNPC-induced photoreceptor protection.a UMAP representation of integrated scRNA-seq data from in vivo hNPCs and rat retinal cells. b, c Bar plots showing the total number of cell-cell interactions (b) and their interaction strengths (c) in P60 and P90 hNPC-treated retinas. d, e Heatmap showing the differential number (d) and strength (e) of cell-cell communication interactions among host retinal cells and with grafted hNPCs in P60 and P90. f Joint dimensionality reduction and clustering of signaling pathways based on their functional similarity inferred from P60 and P90 data. Each dot represents a communication network of one signaling pathway. Dot size is proportional to communication probability and colors represent different signaling pathways groups. g Bar plots showing signaling pathways with significant differences between P60 and P90 hNPC-treated retina. All significant signaling pathways were ranked based on differences in overall information flow (sum of communication probability among all pairs of cell populations in the network) within the inferred networks between P60 and P90 hNPC-treated retinas. Green-colored pathways are enriched at P60, purple-colored pathways at P90, while black pathways are non-significant. h, i Network plots visualizing MANF signaling between different cell populations at P60 and P90. Circle sizes proportionate to the number of cells in each cell group, and edge width represents the communication probability. j Violin plots showing the expression of MANF (ligand) and their receptors (ERN1, NPTN, KDELR1, and KDELR2) in P60 and P90 hNPC-treated retinal cells. k, l Immunofluorescence images and quantitative analysis showing MANF expression (green) in Iba1+ microglia (red) in P60 and P90 hNPC-treated retinas compared to untreated controls (n = 8 per group, 3 repetitions). Scale bar: 50 μm and 10 μm. RPE, Retinal pigment epithelium; INL, Inner nuclear layer; ONL, Outer nuclear layer; RGC, Retinal ganglionic cell. m Bar graph showing increased counts of MANF+ microglia in P60 and P90 hNPC-treated retina compared to untreated (n = 10 per group, 3 repetitions). Notably, MANF+ microglia significantly decreased in P90 hNPC-treated retina compared to P60 hNPC-treated retina. Data are represented as mean ± SEM. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was used for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant. Source data are provided as a Source Data file.
