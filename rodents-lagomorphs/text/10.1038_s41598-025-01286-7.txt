Baicalin prevents experimental autoimmune uveitis by promoting macrophage polarization balance through inhibiting the HIF-1α signaling pathway.

# Baicalin prevents experimental autoimmune uveitis by promoting macrophage polarization balance through inhibiting the HIF-1α signaling pathway

## Abstract
Uveitis is a series of autoimmune eye diseases that can seriously damage people’s eyesight. This study aimed to explore the therapeutic potential of baicalin in treating uveitis, focusing on its modulation of HIF-1α expression and macrophage polarization. Using an experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) rat model, we found that baicalin can significantly reduce fundus inflammation in EAU rats. Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography revealed retinal vascular thickening in the EAU group, indicating severe inflammation, which baicalin effectively mitigated. Histopathological analysis confirmed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in the ciliary body and retina. Co-immunoprecipitation analyses showed that HIF-1αinteracted with macrophage-related factors, including iNOS and ARG1. Baicalin downregulated HIF-1α and iNOS while upregulating ARG1, balancing pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization. Flow cytometry demonstrated a reversal of M1/M2 macrophage ratios in the EAU group after baicalin treatment. Additionally, baicalin improved macrophage mitochondrial membrane potential and decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, shifting macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory state. These findings confirm that baicalin can effectively reduce inflammation and restore immune balance by orchestrating the HIF-1α pathway, establishing a promising therapeutic candidate for uveitis and highlighting the potential of natural bioactive compounds in treating and preventing inflammatory diseases through targeted immune modulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-01286-7.

## Introduction
Uveitis is an inflammatory condition of the uvea that can lead to vision loss if untreated. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of autoimmune uveitis (AU) and experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU). Inhibiting HIF-1α alleviates EAU symptoms and regulates Th17, Th1, and regulatory T cell ratios1. Melatonin, an endogenous hormone, has shown therapeutic potential in AU by modulating Th17 cells through the ROS/TXNIP/HIF-1α axis2. The melatonin receptor agonist ramelteon also reduces acute ocular inflammation by targeting the HIF-1α/VEGF/e-NOS pathway in endotoxin-induced uveitis (EIU)3,4. HIF-1α modulation has broader implications in ocular diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, and genome editing strategies targeting HIF-1α have been proposed to manage ocular angiogenesis5,6. Both HIF-1α and HIF-2α promote retinal neovascularization, underscoring their significance in ocular health7. These findings highlight HIF-1α as a potential therapeutic target for uveitis and other eye disorders.
Baicalin (BA) (Fig. 1), derived from Scutellaria baicalensis, activates the Nrf2/HO-1-mediated HIF-1α-BNIP3 pathway, which inhibits myocardial apoptosis8. Its derivative, baicalein, protects retinal ganglion cells from ischemia by downregulating HIF9. In inflammatory bowel disease, baicalin’s anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immune-regulating properties contribute to its therapeutic effects10. In ocular diseases, including uveitis, baicalin reduces inflammation by interfering with IL-1 signaling11. Since oxidative stress, inflammation, hypoxia, and vascular impairment contribute to glaucoma, baicalin’s effects on HIF-1α in chronic ocular hypertension models have been investigated12,13. Overall, baicalin and its derivatives offer therapeutic potential for uveitis and other eye diseases by modulating HIF pathways, though further research is needed to clarify their mechanisms and clinical applications.

Macrophage polarization is crucial in uveitis-related inflammation, with M1 macrophages promoting inflammation and M2 macrophages exerting anti-inflammatory effects14. This balance is regulated by HIF-1α and reactive oxygen species (ROS)15. Under hypoxia, HIF-1α influences macrophage polarization, particularly in tumor microenvironments16. In uveitis, HIF-1α may promote aberrant macrophage polarization via ROS interactions, exacerbating inflammation17. Other factors, such as glucocorticoids, can improve the M1/M2 balance in EAU through the P38MAPK-MEF2 C pathway18. Additionally, baicalin modulates macrophage polarization and autophagy, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for uveitis9.
The relationship between macrophage membrane potential, ROS levels, and HIF-1α is essential in macrophage function. HIF-1α regulates cellular responses to hypoxia and influences macrophage metabolism19. Its activation increases glycolysis and alters mitochondrial function, impacting membrane potential and macrophage responses20. Elevated ROS levels are linked to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, whereas lower ROS levels correspond to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This interplay is critical in physiological and pathological contexts, including uveitis21.
Baicalin’s potential as a uveitis treatment lies in its ability to modulate HIF pathways and reduce HIF-1α expression at the gene and protein levels. This reduction is linked to macrophage-related factors such as iNOS and ARG1. Using an EAU model, we assessed HIF-1α expression after baicalin treatment. Our findings suggest that baicalin regulates macrophage polarization, offering novel insights and potential therapeutic targets for uveitis, supporting the clinical application of traditional Chinese medicine.

## Materials and methods

## Animals and grouping
The present study has received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approved number: AWE-2022-017) and strictly abide by the guidance of the ARVO statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. We confirm that this study strictly adheres to ethical standards, including the ARRIVE guidelines and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All procedures were conducted in compliance with relevant regulations, ensuring rigorous ethical oversight in both reporting and experimentation.
Thirty-six healthy adult Lewis rats (6–8 weeks old, weighing 160–180 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) and were randomly assigned to six groups: the normal control (NC), EAU, EAU + shRNA-HIF-1α (OBio, Shanghai, China), EAU + shRNA-HIF-1α empty vector, EAU + 45 mg/kg baicalin, and EAU + 90 mg/kg baicalin groups. Rats in all groups except the NC group received subcutaneous injections with an emulsion mixture containing IRBP 1177–1191, Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37RA (tuberculin, TB), complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA), and sterile PBS in the hind paw pads, bilateral abdominal wall, and subepithelial areas of the trunk to induce uveitis, while those in the NC group only received CFA emulsified with TB in the same locations. On day 1 post-immunization, the rats in the EAU + baicalin groups received intraperitoneal injections of baicalin (MCE, USA) diluted in sterile saline at doses of 45 mg/kg and 90 mg/kg body weight for 12 consecutive days, respectively. The rats in the EAU + shRNA-HIF-1α group received an intravitreal injection of 4 µl of shRNA-HIF-1α lentivirus (5.0E8 TU/ml), while those in the EAU + shRNA-HIF-1α empty vector group received 4 µl of the empty vector lentivirus (5.0E8 TU/ml) intravitreally. At day 12 post-immunization, four rats per group were randomly selected and euthanized for subsequent analyses. Euthanasia was performed by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 120 mg/kg.

## Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SLO) and pathological examination
After immunization, the fundus of each rat was observed daily using a Genesis-D fundus camera to document signs of inflammation. On day 12 post-immunization, SLO (Wasatch Photonics, USA) was used to capture fundus images of the rats in each group. Subsequently, the eyes of the rats were enucleated after euthanasia (pentobarbital sodium 120 mg/kg, administered intraperitoneally), fixed in an ocular fixation solution in cell culture dishes (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) for 24 h, and then embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) for histological analysis. Pathological evaluation was conducted and graded based on established criteria22.

## Flow cytometry
The eyes from each group were collected on day 12 post-immunization and processed under sterile conditions. The tissues were ground, and monocytes were isolated using nylon wool filtration and a monocyte extraction kit. The isolated monocytes were placed in peripheral blood lymphocyte isolation tubes (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) and stained with direct immunofluorescence (APC-CD11b+, FITC-F4/80, PE-CD86+, PE-CD206+, Bioss, Beijing, China) and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Agilent, USA). To assess mitochondrial membrane potential and reactive oxygen species levels, cells harvested from the eyes on day 12 were treated with the appropriate detection kit following the manufacturer’s instructions for the respective kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and the measurements were conducted by flow cytometry. The flow cytometric gating strategy, including an isotype control, was provided in Supplemental Figs. 1–5.

## Q-PCR
Under sterile and enzyme-free conditions, the eyes of the rats from each group were collected, and the tissues were ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. An appropriate amount of the tissue powder was then processed for RNA extraction following the instructions provided by the SparkEasy RNA Quick Extraction Kit (Shandong Sparkjade Biotech., Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). Briefly, 30 mg of finely ground tissue was added to 600 µL of RLT Plus lysis buffer, vortexed thoroughly, and transferred the lysate to a DNA removal column. After centrifugation at 13,000 g for 60 s, the supernatants were mixed with an equal volume of 70% ethanol, loaded the mixture onto an RA adsorption column, and centrifuged for 30 s. Subsequently, the supernatants were washed with 700 µL of RW1 buffer and 500 µL of RW buffer twice, followed by centrifugation for 2 min to remove the residual wash buffer. Next, the column was supplemented with RNase-free H2O and incubated for 1 min at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 30 s to collect the solution. Finally, the extracted RNA in solutions was assessed using a K5600 spectrophotometer, ensuring OD260/OD280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.1.
The extracted RNA was used for cDNA synthesis with a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), followed by quantitative PCR (Q-PCR) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a Roche LightCycler® 480 system. Reactions were conducted in a 20 µL volume with three technical replicates for each gene, and β-actin was used as the internal control for all groups. The cycling conditions were set as follows: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 25 s, and 60 °C for 25 s.
The experimental design included the following controls: No-Template Control (NTC) and reactions incorporating all components—primers, master mix, and nuclease-free water— excluding the template. The cDNA was processed alongside the experimental samples. No amplification curves or Ct values were detected in the NTCs, thereby confirming the absence of exogenous contamination. A set of Reverse Transcription Control (RT-) was established by processing RNA samples from each experimental group without the addition of reverse transcriptase during cDNA synthesis (based on the same reaction conditions). All RT- controls showed no amplification for the target genes, confirming the successful DNase I treatment during RNA extraction and validating the specificity of mRNA detection. To further substantiate the specificity of the amplification products, a melting curve analysis was conducted, which resulted in a single peak. The primer sequences for each gene are detailed in Table 1. Following the completion of the quantitative PCR reactions, the levels of the relevant target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The results from the three groups were then compiled. Comparisons between groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA, with significance established at P < 0.05.

## Western blotting and Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
The eyes of the rats in each group were ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen, and RIPA lysis buffer along with PMSF was added to facilitate protein solubilization. The mixture was homogenized for 30 s using a mechanical homogenizer, followed by sonication on ice for 20 min. After this, the samples were centrifuged at 8000 r/min at 4 °C for 20 min to remove debris. Next, the supernatant was transferred to new cryopreservation tubes (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China), and protein concentrations for each group were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyontime, Shanghai, China). WB analysis was subsequently conducted with a 10% one-step PAGE colorful gel kit (Shandong Sparkjade Biotech., Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) to evaluate the expression levels of HIF-1α (No. 20960-1-AP, Rabbit, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), iNOS (No. 22226-1-AP, Rabbit, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and ARG1 (No. 16001-1-AP, Rabbit, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) proteins in each group.
The eyes of the rats in each group were ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen and subsequently lysed in 400 µL of lysis buffer containing detergents and protease inhibitors to preserve protein interactions. The clarified lysate was incubated with Protein A/G beads for 2 h to minimize non-specific binding. Next, the pre-cleared lysate was incubated with a primary antibody against HIF-1α. Protein A/G beads were then added to the antibody-protein mixture and incubated overnight. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer. After quantifying protein concentration using a BCA assay, Western Blot analysis was performed to detect protein expression levels of HIF-1α, iNOS, and ARG1.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and Immunofluorescence staining
The tissues of animals in each group were crushed in liquid nitrogen and then homogenized in 350 µL of RIPA lysis buffer. After sonication on ice, the samples were centrifuged at 8000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected and analyzed for protein quantification using a K5600 spectrophotometer (Beijing Kaiao Technology Development Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The concentrations of IL-1β, TGF-β, IL-10, and TNF-α were measured using the relevant ELISA kits (Jianglai Biotech., Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) on a multifunctional microplate reader (Envision, Perkin-Elmer, USA).
For immunofluorescence staining, frozen sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by blocking and staining (HIF-1α (No. 20960-1-AP, rabbit, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), iNOS (No. 22226-1-AP, rabbit, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and ARG1 (No. 16001-1-AP, rabbit, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Representative fields were captured using the Zeiss LSM780 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

## Molecular docking
The crystal structure of the target protein was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org), while ligand structures were retrieved in SDF format from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Since AutoDock Vina version 1.56 requires ligands in PDB or PDBQT formats for docking simulations, ligand structures were first converted from SDF to PDB format using Open Babel version 2.4.1. Protein preparation involved the removal of crystallographic water molecules and ions (unless functionally relevant), the addition of polar hydrogens, and the assignment of Kollman united-atom charges. The processed protein was then converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock Tools version 1.5.7. Ligand preparation included hydrogenation, assignment of torsional flexibility, and Gasteiger-Marsili partial charge calculations, followed by conversion to PDBQT format. A docking grid was defined based on the active site coordinates, ensuring adequate coverage of the binding pocket. During molecular docking, rotatable bonds in flexible ligands were carefully assigned, with non-essential torsions fixed to maintain structural integrity while allowing realistic conformational exploration. AutoDock Vina was then employed to predict binding affinities and poses. Post-docking, molecular interactions were analyzed and visualized using PyMOL version 2.5.7 to assess binding conformations, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions.

## Statistical analysis
Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 17.0 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Each experiment was performed in duplicate and repeated three times. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant difference comparisons of mean values were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by post hoc analysis with the LSD-t multiple comparison test to determine significance. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered a significant difference.

## Results

## Baicalin treatment prevents inflammatory infiltration in EAU rats
To assess the impact of baicalin treatment on fundus inflammation in EAU rats, different doses of baicalin solution were administered daily via intraperitoneal injection on day 1 post-immunization. SLO imaging revealed that, compared to the NC group, the EAU group showed significantly thickened retinal blood vessels 12 days after immunization, indicating a strong inflammatory response in the fundus. However, after baicalin treatment, fundus inflammation and vascular swelling were reduced. Histopathological examination demonstrated substantial inflammatory cell infiltration in the ciliary body and retina of the EAU group, which was reduced after baicalin treatment (Fig. 2). Regarding the potential side effects of baicalin treatment, the naïve rats received baicalin at 90 mg/kg for 12 consecutive days. No significant side effects, including edema in the retinal or iris tissues, were observed in these animals. Consequently, the above results indicate that baicalin treatment can prevent fundus inflammation in EAU rats.

## Baicalin suppresses the production of HIF-1α-mediated macrophage-related inflammatory factors
Baicalin has the potential to offer therapeutic advantages in managing uveitis and other ocular conditions through its modulation of HIF pathways, given that HIF-1α is closely linked to the expression of macrophage-related factors such as iNOS and ARG1. Surprisingly, baicalin was successfully docked with HIF-1α using molecular docking techniques, yielding a binding energy of −6.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 3A). Compared to the NC group, the EAU group showed increased expression of HIF-1α and iNOS, whereas ARG1 expression was reduced (P < 0.05). Moreover, PCR and WB results indicated that, compared to the EAU group, baicalin treatment significantly inhibited the gene and protein expression levels of HIF-1α, with the differences being statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B and D). The co-IP results confirmed that HIF-1α is associated with INOS and ARG1(Fig. 3C). Further, the IF assay showed that baicalin treatment significantly inhibited HIF-1α levels and revealed its localization in eye tissues (Fig. 3E). Thus, baicalin may provide therapeutic benefits for uveitis by modulating HIF pathways. It significantly reduces the expression levels of HIF-1α and associated macrophage-related factors like iNOS and ARG1 in EAU rats.

## Baicalin attenuates HIF-1α expression to improve M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance
Considering that HIF-1α may contribute to abnormal macrophage polarization in uveitis, it leads to an imbalance between pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. Consequently, following immunization, the rats were administered an intravitreal injection of 4 µl of shRNA-HIF-1α lentivirus to evaluate the intervention effect of HIF-1α in uveitis. The Western blot analysis showed that on day 12 post-immunization, compared to the EAU group rats, the EAU + shRNA-HIF-1α group exhibited downregulation of INOS expression, along with upregulation of ARG1 expression. A similar trend was observed in the baicalin treatment group, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A-C). IF further validated these results and showed the location of these factors (Fig. 4D). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that in the EAU model, the expression of M1 macrophages was upregulated while the expression of M2 macrophages was downregulated, indicating a pro-inflammatory trend. After baicalin treatment, M1 macrophage expression decreased while M2 macrophage levels increased, demonstrating an anti-inflammatory trend. Similarly, after the shRNA-HIF-1α intervention, the frequency of M1 macrophages decreased, and the frequency of M2 macrophages increased, also reflecting an anti-inflammatory shift trend (Fig. 5). In summary, baicalin treatment regulates HIF-1α expression to influence the polarization of M1 and M2 macrophages.

## Alterations in macrophage mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS levels after Baicalin treatments
The interplay between macrophage membrane potential, ROS levels, and HIF-1α is essential for understanding macrophage polarization and function. Analysis of the mitochondrial membrane potential levels in macrophages indicated that the EAU group exhibited a reduced mitochondrial membrane potential. After baicalin treatment, the mitochondrial membrane potential levels were significantly elevated compared to the level of the EAU group. Following shRNA-HIF-1α intervention, the mitochondrial membrane potential also increased, whereas no increase was observed in the empty vector group, with no statistically significant differences (Fig. 6A). Analysis of ROS levels in macrophages revealed that the EAU group had elevated ROS levels. After baicalin treatment, ROS levels decreased compared to the EAU group. Similarly, ROS levels reduced after shRNA-HIF-1α intervention, whereas no decrease was observed in the empty vector group, with no significant differences (Fig. 6B). Based on these findings, we conclude that baicalin regulates macrophage mitochondrial membrane potential and reactive oxygen species levels through inhibiting HIF-1α expression.

Studies show that M1 macrophages dominate in acute inflammation and pathogen defense, releasing TNF-α and IL-1β to amplify inflammation and recruit other immune cells; M2 macrophages are more involved in the resolution phase of inflammation, secreting IL-10 and TGF-β to downregulate inflammatory responses and promote tissue repair. In our study, we found that after treatment with baicalin, the expression of TNF-α and IL-1βdecreased, however, the expression of IL-10 and TGF-βincreased. It appeared the same results in the shRNA-HIF-1α intervention group (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that baicalin may influence the polarization balance of M1/M2 macrophages by inhibiting HIF-1α expression, thereby impacting the progression of uveitis in the EAU rats.

## Discussion
Uveitis is a complex inflammatory disorder characterized by the infiltration of immune cells into ocular tissues, leading to vision impairment or loss. The findings from this study highlight the therapeutic potential of baicalin, a flavonoid derived from the roots of “Scutellaria baicalensis”, in mitigating inflammatory responses in a rat model of EAU. This discussion will integrate our results with existing literature, focusing on the mechanisms of baicalin action, particularly its modulation of HIF-1α pathways, the balance between M1 and M2 macrophage polarization, and the implications for therapeutic strategies in uveitis. The results of our study indicate that baicalin treatment effectively reduces inflammatory cell infiltration in the EAU model. SLO imaging revealed significant retinal vascular thickening in the EAU group, suggesting pronounced inflammation. However, after baicalin treatment, the inflammation and vascular swelling were alleviated. Histopathological assessments corroborated these findings, revealing reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in ciliary body and retina of baicalin-treated EAU rats. This result is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that natural compounds, including flavonoids, can exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating immune responses23,24. The reduction in inflammatory markers suggests that baicalin may attenuate the pathogenesis of uveitis by acting on immune cells involved in the inflammatory cascade.
A central finding of our study is that baicalin treatment significantly suppresses the expression of HIF-1α, a key regulator of cellular responses to hypoxia and inflammation25. HIF-1α is known to orchestrate various metabolic and inflammatory pathways, including the expression of macrophage-related factors such as inducible iNOS and ARG126. The co-IP results confirmed the association of HIF-1α with these factors, highlighting its critical role in the inflammatory response in uveitis. In the EAU model, elevated levels of HIF-1α and iNOS were observed, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Baicalin’s ability to significantly reduce these expression levels suggests its potential as a therapeutic agent in controlling HIF-1α-mediated pathways. Previous studies have shown that HIF-1α contributes to macrophage activation and polarization, particularly toward the M1 phenotype, consisting of increased inflammatory cytokine production and tissue damage27,28. Thus, targeting HIF-1α with baicalin could represent a novel approach to modulating the inflammatory response in uveitis.
Macrophages play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of uveitis, and their polarization into M1 and M2 phenotypes is critical in determining the outcome of the inflammatory response29. TNF-α and IL-1β serve as key mediators of the inflammatory response, driving the differentiation of macrophages toward the M1 phenotype. In contrast, IL-10 and TGF-β play pivotal roles in skewing macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, facilitating healing and resolution of inflammation. The balance between these cytokines is crucial; an overabundance of M1-associated cytokines can lead to chronic inflammatory conditions, while excessive M2 signaling can contribute to tumor progression and fibrosis. In our study, the upregulation of M1 macrophages, which promote inflammation (TNF-α and IL-1β), and the downregulation of M2 macrophages (IL-10 and TGF-β), which have anti-inflammatory properties, were evident in the EAU group. However, following baicalin treatment, a shift in macrophage polarization was observed, with increased M2 macrophage levels and decreased M1 macrophage expression. This finding aligns with existing literature that emphasizes the role of HIF-1α in macrophage polarization30. HIF-1α activation is associated with the M1 phenotype, driving glycolytic metabolism and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. According to the results of PCR and WB, baicalin appears to promote an anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage phenotype by inhibiting HIF-1α, which is crucial for resolving inflammation and facilitating tissue repair. Additionally, shRNA-HIF-1α intervention corroborated the role of HIF-1α in regulating macrophage polarization, further supporting the idea that baicalin can modulate immune responses through HIF-1α pathways.
The interplay between macrophage mitochondrial function, ROS levels, and HIF-1α is crucial for understanding macrophage polarization and overall immune responses31. Our analysis revealed that the EAU group exhibited reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and elevated ROS levels, indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress linked to inflammation. Recent studies have shown that baicalin possesses strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties, positioning it as a promising therapeutic agent for preventing cell and tissue damage, particularly in retinal injuries caused by cerebral ischemia32. Baicalin can alleviate intracellular pyroptosis and prevent cell viability loss induced by amyloid-beta (Aβ) in human retinal pigment epithelial cells by modulating the miR-223/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway, suggesting its potential for treating age-related macular degeneration (AMD)33. Moreover, baicalin also inhibits immune cell infiltration and reduces the expression of key pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, iNOS, TLR2, TLR4, and NF-κB, all of which play significant roles in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke34.
In our study, baicalin treatment significantly improved mitochondrial membrane potential and reduced ROS levels, indicating baicalin not only modulates HIF-1α but also helps restore mitochondrial function. Macrophage mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to excessive ROS production, which exacerbates inflammation and tissue damage35. Elevated ROS levels are commonly associated with M1 macrophage polarization and pro-inflammatory responses, whereas reduced ROS levels are linked to M2 macrophages and anti-inflammatory effects31. By restoring mitochondrial function and reducing ROS levels, baicalin may shift macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory state, offering a potential therapeutic approach for managing uveitis and other inflammatory diseases (Fig. 7).

The findings of this study underscore the importance of HIF-1α modulation in macrophage polarization and inflammatory responses in uveitis. Further investigation is warranted to explore the detailed molecular mechanisms by which baicalin influences HIF-1α signaling and its downstream effects on macrophage function. Future studies could also assess the long-term effects of baicalin treatment on ocular inflammation and its potential in combination with other therapeutic agents. In a clinical context, baicalin and its derivatives could serve as promising candidates for developing novel therapeutic strategies for uveitis and other inflammatory ocular disorders. Understanding the pharmacokinetics and bioavailability of baicalin will be essential for optimizing its therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, clinical trials are needed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of baicalin in human subjects suffering from uveitis, potentially leading to new interventions that can improve patient outcomes.
Despite the promising findings of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the EAU model used in this study provides valuable insights into the inflammatory processes underlying uveitis, it may not fully replicate the complexity of human uveitis, which involves diverse etiologies and immune responses. Future studies should validate the therapeutic effects of baicalin in additional preclinical models and clinical settings to ensure its translational potential. While our findings demonstrate that baicalin modulates HIF-1α expression and macrophage polarization, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation need to be further elucidated. Additional research is essential to explore the baicalin’s direct targets within the HIF-1α signaling pathway and its broader impact on immune cell function. Finally, while our findings suggest that baicalin may be a promising therapeutic agent for uveitis, clinical studies are needed to confirm its effectiveness in human patients. Investigating its potential use in combination with existing anti-inflammatory treatments could further enhance its therapeutic benefits. By addressing these limitations in future research, we can better understand the therapeutic potential of baicalin and its applicability in treating uveitis and other inflammatory diseases.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that baicalin exerts preventive effects against inflammatory infiltration in a rat model of EAU. Through the modulation of HIF-1α, baicalin influences macrophage polarization, restores mitochondrial function, and reduces ROS levels, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for uveitis. These findings provide a foundation for further research into the mechanisms of action of baicalin and its clinical applications in managing ocular inflammatory diseases.
