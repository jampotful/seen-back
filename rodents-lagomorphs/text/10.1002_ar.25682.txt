# Anatolian ground squirrel ( Spermophilus xanthoprymnus ) retina: Comparative expression of synaptophysin, NeuN, calbindin‐D28k, parvalbumin, glial fibrillary acidic protein, and Iba‐1 during pre‐hibernation and hibernation

## Abstract
AbstractHibernation induces significant molecular and cellular adaptations in the retina to maintain function under reduced metabolic conditions. This study aimed to investigate the expression of neuronal, synaptic, and glial markers in the retina of Spermophilus xanthoprymnus during pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods using immunohistochemical staining. Synaptophysin expression, restricted to the inner plexiform layer (IPL) and outer plexiform layer (OPL) during pre‐hibernation, significantly increased in both layers during hibernation, with additional expression observed in the outer nuclear layer. NeuN immunoreactivity remained unchanged in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) but increased notably in the INL during hibernation. Calbindin‐D28k expression, prominent in the INL and plexiform layers during pre‐hibernation, decreased markedly in hibernation. In contrast, parvalbumin expression increased across all retinal layers, except the photoreceptor layer, during hibernation. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression, observed in the NFL and GCL, was significantly reduced during hibernation. Iba‐1 immunoreactivity, sparse in the IPL and OPL during pre‐hibernation, showed a pronounced increase in the IPL, OPL, and INL during hibernation periods. In conclusion, the expression of synaptophysin, NeuN, calbindin‐D28k, parvalbumin, GFAP, and Iba‐1 was investigated for the first time in the retina of the Anatolian ground squirrel during pre‐hibernation and hibernation. This study reveals region‐specific shifts in retinal marker expression during pre‐hibernation and hibernation, providing a basis for future research into visual system adaptations and retinal plasticity under metabolic suppression.Keywords: Anatolian ground squirrel, calbindin‐D28k, GFAP, hibernation, Iba‐1, NeuN, parvalbumin, retina, synaptophysin

## INTRODUCTION
The Anatolian ground squirrel (Spermophilus xanthoprymnus) is a rodent species native to Türkiye and nearby regions. Its primary habitat includes the central plains and elevated eastern areas of Anatolia. Additionally, populations of this species have been documented in regions adjacent to Armenia and northwestern Iran. Smaller, more isolated groups are also observed in southern Anatolia, particularly in areas such as the Teke Peninsula and the Çukurova Plain (Kart Gür & Gür, 2010). Ground squirrels are omnivorous animals that feed on a wide variety of food sources, including seeds, nuts, insects, and occasionally small vertebrates. Their advanced visual systems, which exhibit exceptional precision, motion detection, and color perception, make them valuable models for research in vision sciences (Li, 2020). During winter, ground squirrels enter a hibernation phase, a physiological state that enables survival under extreme environmental conditions through substantial metabolic alterations. These adaptations provide a unique opportunity to explore the relationship between metabolic processes and brain plasticity, as they have significant impacts on various physiological systems, particularly energy‐demanding tissues such as the retina (Merriman et al., 2016).
The structure of the retina is remarkably conserved across vertebrates, with its organization based on three nuclear layers containing five main types of neurons, interspersed with two synaptic layers. Photoreceptors, located in the outer nuclear layer (ONL), detect light and convert it into electrochemical signals. The inner nuclear layer (INL) houses three types of interneurons (bipolar, horizontal, and amacrine cells) while the ganglion cell layer (GCL) contains retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and displaced amacrine cells (Reichenbach & Bringmann, 2013). The formation of synaptic connections between neuronal dendrites and axons is essential for the normal functioning of retinal neural circuits. This interaction necessitates the extension of axons and dendrites toward their target neurons, directed by a sophisticated array of molecular signals (Mcfarlane & Lom, 2012). Synaptophysin, a synaptic vesicle membrane protein, plays a key role in vesicle formation, neurotransmitter release, and exocytosis. Additionally, it is believed to be significantly involved in synaptogenesis and synaptic plasticity during the development of neural tissues (Dan et al., 2008; Yuki et al., 2014). Synaptophysin is highly expressed in the retina. In both rats and humans, its immunoreactivity is predominantly observed in the synapse‐dense inner and outer plexiform layers (OPLs) of the retina (Nag & Wadhwa, 2001; Vanguilder et al., 2008). In synaptophysin‐deficient mice, rod photoreceptors exhibit a reduced number of synaptic vesicles, which are also larger in diameter (Spiwoks‐Becker et al., 2001). Besides synaptophysin, another essential protein implicated in neural circuitry balance, neurogenesis, and synaptogenesis is NeuN, now known as neuronal splicing regulator RBFOX3, which is well recognized as a marker of mature neurons (Duan et al., 2016). In the retina, NeuN is specifically expressed in cells of the GCL and a subset of cells in the INL. Notably, research on NeuN−/− mice demonstrated that both image‐forming and non‐image‐forming visual functions remain intact, suggesting that RBFOX3 is not critical for maintaining visual function (Lin et al., 2018). Furthermore, calcium‐binding proteins (CaBPs), ubiquitously expressed in retinal neurons, are integral to the regulation of neural pathways, synaptic interactions, and the characterization of specific retinal cell types. By modulating Ca2+ dynamics through their diverse affinities and kinetics, these proteins critically influence neuronal signaling and activity, underscoring their essential role in retinal neurobiology (Kovács‐Öller et al., 2019; Schwaller, 2010). This diverse family contains many proteins, including calbindin and parvalbumin (Chin & Means, 2000). In the primate retina, calbindin is localized in cones, neurons of the GCL (Chiquet et al., 2005; Pasteels et al., 1990), and specific cells within the INL, including H2 horizontal cells (Wässle et al., 2000), bipolar cells (Haverkamp et al., 2003), and various amacrine cell subtypes (Weltzien et al., 2015). In the mouse retina, calbindin has also been detected in approximately 18% of the ganglion cell population (Gu et al., 2016). Parvalbumin demonstrates a distinct expression pattern in vertebrate retinas, with notable variability in immunoreactivity across species (Sanna et al., 1993). In mammals, including rabbits, rats, and mice, it is primarily localized to the INL and GCL, marking amacrine cells and RGCs (Casini et al., 1995; Haverkamp & Wässle, 2000; Wäussle et al., 1993). In C57BL/6J mice, parvalbumin is abundantly present in the soma of RGCs and is prominently expressed in their axons within the optic nerve (Liu et al., 2023).
Retinal glial cells are divided into two main types: macroglial and microglial cells. Macroglial cells, including astrocytes and Müller cells, form extensive connections with neurons and capillaries. Microglial cells, on the other hand, are small, macrophage‐like cells (Bringmann et al., 2006). Müller cells, specialized radial glial cells comprising approximately 90% of retinal glia, extend across all retinal layers (Vecino et al., 2016). Unlike Müller cells, astrocytes are predominantly confined to the nerve fiber layer (NFL) and, to a lesser extent, the GCL (Fernández‐Sánchez et al., 2015). Macroglial cells in the mammalian retina play vital roles in maintaining normal retinal function and responding to pathological conditions. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a widely recognized marker of glial activity, is constitutively expressed in retinal astrocytes. In Müller cells, however, GFAP expression is significantly upregulated during reactive gliosis, a characteristic response to various retinal disorders, reflecting their adaptive role in retinal pathology (Dyer & Cepko, 2000; Newman & Reichenbach, 1996; Nork et al., 1986; Ramirez et al., 1994; Wu et al., 2003). In the healthy retina, microglial cells are primarily situated within the plexiform layers, where their highly ramified processes play a crucial role in immune surveillance and maintaining retinal homeostasis (Karlstetter et al., 2015). Iba‐1 is a widely recognized marker for identifying microglia and macrophages (Imai et al., 1996; Ito et al., 1998). However, studies have reported that Iba‐1 expression is upregulated in activated microglia, supporting its utility as a marker for microglial activation under certain conditions (Ibrahim et al., 2011; Maneu et al., 2014).
Hibernation serves as a natural model for studying synaptic plasticity, neuroprotection, and metabolic adaptation, as it represents a unique physiological state in which neural circuits undergo extensive yet reversible remodeling (Von Der Ohe et al., 2007). Previous studies in seasonal hibernators have demonstrated significant brain plasticity, including hippocampal dendritic retraction and a 50%–65% reduction in synapses, as indicated by changes in presynaptic and postsynaptic marker colocalization (Popov et al., 2007). However, while synaptic remodeling is well documented in the central nervous system, data on retinal synaptic changes, particularly those involving synaptophysin, remain limited. Similarly, NeuN, a key neuronal marker associated with various neurological disorders (Lal et al., 2013; Lucas et al., 2014) has been extensively studied in the brain, yet its expression and role in the retina remain largely unexplored. Given that hibernation induces a neurodegeneration‐like environment (de Veij Mestdagh et al., 2021), investigating both synaptophysin and NeuN expression in the retina could provide critical insights into the mechanisms of retinal plasticity and neuronal resilience under extreme metabolic conditions. Moreover, in neurodegenerative diseases, reactive gliosis is characterized by an increase in GFAP‐positive astrocytes (Pekny & Pekna, 2016), accompanied by heightened microglial activation marked by elevated Iba‐1 expression (Kenkhuis et al., 2022). However, our understanding of glial responses in the retina during hibernation remains largely unexplored, underscoring the need for further investigation into how astrocytes and microglia adapt to this extreme metabolic state. CaBPs, including calbindin and parvalbumin, not only influence and respond to Ca2+ transients but also regulate proteins involved in maintaining homeostasis and protecting cells under challenging environmental conditions (Gattoni & Bernocchi, 2019). While studies on the central nervous system, particularly in the context of hibernation, have reported specific alterations in CaBPs (Gattoni & Bernocchi, 2019), data on their role in the retina remain limited, highlighting a significant gap in current knowledge. Therefore, this study investigates the comparative expression of Synaptophysin, NeuN, Calbindin‐D28k, parvalbumin, GFAP, and Iba‐1 in the retina of the Anatolian ground squirrel (S. xanthoprymnus), aiming to elucidate how these markers respond to pre‐hibernation and hibernation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine these molecular markers in the retina of this species, providing novel insights into retinal plasticity, neuroprotection, and glial adaptation under extreme metabolic conditions.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Ethical compliance and experimental procedures for retinal tissue analysis
All animal experiments were performed in full compliance with international ethical standards for the care and use of laboratory animals, as outlined in the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. A total of 12 adult male Anatolian ground squirrels, weighing between 300 and 380 g, were included in the study. As the animals were wild‐captured in their natural habitat, their precise ages could not be determined. The squirrels were trapped using Tomahawk Traps (Model #202, Tomahawk, WI, USA) from steppe regions near Develi, Kayseri, Türkiye, in late August. Following their capture, the animals were transferred to the laboratory of the Department of Histology and Embryology at Erciyes University.
Upon arrival, all squirrels underwent a quarantine period lasting no less than 7 days. During this time, the animals were housed in individually ventilated cages within a climate‐controlled environment set at a constant temperature of 21°C, with an artificial light–dark cycle of 12 h each. This acclimation period was critical to ensure the animals were in good health and free from any stress or environmental adaptation issues before experimental procedures began. The squirrels were monitored closely for any signs of illness or distress, with a daily inspection of food and water intake, general behavior, and physical condition.
After the acclimatization period, the animals were divided into two distinct experimental groups based on their physiological states: the pre‐hibernation group (comprising six males) and the hibernation (late torpor) group (comprising six males). Animals in the pre‐hibernation group were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of xylazine and ketamine, as per the protocol detailed by Olson and McCabe (1986), at a dosage of 11.1 mg/ml xylazine and 88.9 mg/ml ketamine. Once anesthesia was confirmed through the absence of a pain reflex, the squirrels were euthanized via cardiac puncture. Immediately following euthanasia, both eyes were carefully enucleated to avoid damage to retinal tissues. Subsequently, retinal tissues were dissected with precision under a stereomicroscope to ensure the preservation of tissue integrity for downstream analyses. These retinal samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 18 h at room temperature. Following fixation, the tissues underwent sequential dehydration using graded ethanol concentrations, were cleared in methyl benzoate and benzol, and were finally embedded in paraffin wax to prepare them for immunohistochemical and histological evaluations.
In contrast, squirrels assigned to the hibernation group were subjected to an experimental protocol designed to mimic natural hibernation conditions. Initially, these animals were provided ad libitum access to a balanced diet comprising standard rodent feed, sunflower seeds, fresh fruits, and water, allowing them to accumulate sufficient lipid reserves to enter hibernation. Between September and December 2016, the laboratory conditions were gradually adjusted to replicate environmental cues signaling the onset of winter. This involved progressively lowering the ambient temperature to 6°C while simultaneously ceasing food availability and transitioning to complete darkness. To minimize disturbances, a low‐intensity red light (3–5 lx) was used during routine laboratory checks. After approximately 3 months, during which the animals remained in a torpid, hypothermic state characteristic of deep hibernation, the squirrels were anesthetized following the same protocol used for the pre‐hibernation group. In their deeply torpid state, the squirrels exhibited minimal metabolic activity and unresponsiveness to external stimuli, allowing for safe tissue collection. Both eyes were removed under sterile conditions, and the retinal tissues were processed identically to those in the pre‐hibernation group, ensuring consistency across the experimental timeline. This rigorous protocol ensured the high‐quality preservation of retinal tissues for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical analyses, enabling the precise examination of molecular markers.

## Immunohistochemistry
Immunohistochemistry was performed to assess the expression of synaptophysin, NeuN, calbindin‐D28k, parvalbumin, GFAP, and Iba‐1 in retinal tissues as previously described (Öztop, Özbek, Beyaz, et al., 2019). Detailed information regarding the primary antibodies used is provided in Table 1. Tissue sections were first deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by rinsing in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was conducted using a microwave‐assisted method, with the sections immersed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated in a microwave oven at medium power for 20 min to unmask the antigenic epitopes. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were rinsed thoroughly with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). To minimize nonspecific staining, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with hydrogen peroxide. Protein blocking was then performed using Ultra V Block (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking, the primary antibodies were applied to the sections, and the slides were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber to ensure optimal antigen–antibody binding. Excess primary antibodies were washed off with PBS. Biotinylated secondary antibodies (anti‐mouse and anti‐rabbit; Thermo Scientific) were applied to the sections for 30 min at room temperature. Following this step, HRP‐conjugated streptavidin (Thermo Scientific) was added to amplify the signal. The chromogen 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine was used for visualization, producing a brown precipitate that marked the antigen–antibody complexes. To enhance the clarity of cellular structures, Gill II hematoxylin was applied as a counterstain, providing a blue background contrast.
Specifications of antibodies used in immunohistochemical analysis.
To validate the specificity of the staining, positive and negative controls were included. Positive controls consisted of brain cortex tissue for calbindin‐D28k and parvalbumin, and hippocampal tissue for synaptophysin, NeuN, GFAP, and Iba‐1. For negative controls, PBS was used in place of the primary antibodies, and these sections underwent the same staining protocol under identical conditions in a humidified chamber.
After staining, the sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a permanent mounting medium. The slides were analyzed using an Olympus BX51 research microscope equipped with a DP72 digital camera for high‐resolution image capture. Each section was independently evaluated by at least two researchers to ensure reliability, and representative fields were photographed for further analysis.

## Image processing and quantitative analysis of immunohistochemical staining
To quantitatively analyze the immunohistochemical staining results, image analysis was performed on the stained retinal tissue sections as previously described (Öztop, Özbek, Liman, et al., 2019). Specific layers of the retina that exhibited immunopositive reactions were identified under an Olympus BX51 research microscope. High‐resolution images of these layers were captured using the DP72 digital camera integrated with the microscope. The captured images (n = 12 per group) were subsequently processed and analyzed using Adobe Photoshop 2025 (Adobe Inc., USA) to isolate immunoreactive retinal layers.
Retinal layer segmentation was initially performed using the AI‐assisted “Magnetic Lasso Tool” in Adobe Photoshop 2025 (Adobe Inc.) to delineate anatomical boundaries. In cases where automatic selection was insufficient—particularly in regions with low contrast or irregular staining—manual refinement was carried out using standard selection tools within Adobe Photoshop 2025 (Adobe Inc.) to ensure anatomical precision. The finalized immunoreactive regions were cropped and exported for quantitative analysis. Subsequent image processing was performed in ImageJ software (version 1.51, Java 1.8.0_112, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) for densitometric analysis of staining intensity (Jensen, 2013). RGB images were converted into optical density space using the color deconvolution plugin, with the Hematoxylin‐AEC vector selected to isolate the chromogenic signal. The AEC‐specific channel was extracted and converted to 8‐bit grayscale. Threshold adjustment was applied to segment immunoreactive regions, which were then binarized for quantitative evaluation. This image analysis pipeline allowed for standardized and reproducible assessment of immunohistochemical staining intensity, in accordance with previously established protocols (Chen et al., 2017; Varghese et al., 2014).

## Statistical analysis
The data of staining intensity values were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 for Windows (Version 8.0.2) software to determine differences in protein expression between the pre‐hibernation and hibernation groups. The Student's t test was applied to compare the mean staining intensities of the corresponding retinal layers across the two groups. All values are shown as the mean ± SD. The threshold for statistical significance was set at a p‐value less than 0.05. Any differences with p‐values below this threshold were considered statistically significant, indicating meaningful variations in protein expression between the pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods. This combination of precise image processing and robust statistical analysis ensured a comprehensive and reliable evaluation of the immunohistochemical results. By correlating the staining intensities with the physiological states of retinal layers, this approach provided deeper insights into the dynamic changes in protein expression during pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods.

## RESULTS

## Synaptophysin
In the hippocampal sections used as positive controls for synaptophysin, intense immunostaining was observed in the synaptic regions (Figure 1c). In the pre‐hibernation period, positive immunoreactivity was observed specifically in the inner plexiform layer (IPL) and OPL of the retina. No immunoreactive signals were observed in other retinal layers, including the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Figure 1a). In the hibernation period, a similar pattern of positive immunoreactivity was observed in the IPL and OPL (Figure 1b). Image analysis revealed that the staining intensity in both the IPL (Figure 1e) and OPL (Figure 1f) was significantly higher during the hibernation period compared to the pre‐hibernation period (p < 0.05). Additionally, in retinal sections from the hibernation period, positive immunoreactivity was detected in the ONL, a finding that was not observed in the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 1g).
Synaptophysin expression in the retina of the Anatolian ground squirrel. (a) Synaptophysin immunoreactivity in the retina during pre‐hibernation is localized to the IPL and OPL. (b) During hibernation, synaptophysin expression increases significantly in the IPL and OPL, with additional immunoreactivity observed in the ONL. (c) Positive control staining in the hippocampus confirms strong synaptophysin expression in synaptic regions (asterisk). (d) Negative control shows the absence of nonspecific staining in retinal sections. (e–g) Quantitative image analysis reveals a significant increase in synaptophysin staining intensity during hibernation compared to pre‐hibernation: (e) IPL, (f) OPL, and (g) ONL. *p < 0.05, ****p < 0.0001. Scale bars: 20 μm (a, b, d) and 100 μm (c).

## NeuN
Hippocampal tissue was used as the positive control for NeuN, and, as expected, positive immunoreactivity was detected in neurons (Figure 2c). During the pre‐hibernation period, strong immunostaining was observed in cells within the GCL, with the reaction predominantly localized to the nuclei. Notably, positive immunoreactivity was also detected in cells of the INL close to the IPL. However, in the central regions of the INL and in cells adjacent to the OPL, immunoreactivity was either absent or extremely rare (Figure 2a). In the hibernation period, positively stained cells continued to be present in the GCL (Figure 2b). Statistical analysis of the image data indicated no significant difference in staining intensity in the GCL between the pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods (Figure 2e). Moreover, during the hibernation period, positively stained cells were observed in the INL, specifically in cells located adjacent to both the OPL and IPL, a pattern not present in the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 2b). Image analysis further confirmed that the INL staining intensity was markedly higher during the hibernation period than in the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 2f).
NeuN expression in the retina of the Anatolian ground squirrel. (a) NeuN immunoreactivity in the retina during pre‐hibernation shows strong staining in the GCL and weak expression in the INL near the IPL. (b) During hibernation, NeuN expression remains stable in the GCL but increases significantly in the INL, particularly in cells adjacent to the IPL and OPL. (c) Positive control staining in the hippocampus confirms robust NeuN expression in neurons (N). (d) Negative control shows no nonspecific staining in retinal sections. Quantitative image analysis demonstrates no significant difference in NeuN staining intensity in the GCL (e) but a marked increase in the INL (f) during hibernation compared to pre‐hibernation. **p < 0.01. Scale bars: 20 μm (a–d).

## Calbindin‐D28k
Positive immunoreactivity was observed in the brain cortex, which served as the positive control (Figure 3c). During the pre‐hibernation period, weak immunoreactivity was identified in certain cells within the GCL. Additionally, slight immunostaining was detected in the IPL. In the INL, immunoreactivity was localized to cells situated near both the inner and OPLs. Compared to the IPL, the OPL exhibited more prominent staining, with a higher intensity of immunoreactivity observed (Figure 3a). In the hibernation period, the immunoreactivity in the GCL showed no significant change (Figure 3b), and image analysis confirmed that staining intensity remained similar to the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 3e). Similarly, the IPL showed very faint immunoreactivity during this period (Figure 3b), with a noticeable decrease in staining intensity relative to the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 3f). In the INL, the strong immunoreactivity previously observed in cells near the plexiform layers was absent (Figure 3b), and overall staining intensity in this layer was diminished (Figure 3g). Furthermore, the OPL displayed very weak immunoreactivity (Figure 3b), with a marked reduction in staining intensity compared to the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 3h).
Calbindin‐D28k expression in the retina of the Anatolian ground squirrel. (a) During the pre‐hibernation period, calbindin‐D28k immunoreactivity was weak in the GCL, slight in the IPL, and prominent in the OPL and in cells near the plexiform layers within the INL. (b) In the hibernation period, immunoreactivity persisted in the GCL without significant change, while staining intensity decreased in the IPL, INL, and OPL. Quantitative image analysis revealed no significant difference in staining intensity in the GCL (e), while a significant reduction was observed in the IPL (f), INL (g), and OPL (h) during hibernation. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001. Scale bars: 20 μm (a, b, d) and 100 μm (c).

## Parvalbumin
The expression of parvalbumin was evaluated using the cerebral cortex as a positive control, where neuronal staining confirmed the reliability of the immunohistochemical technique (Figure 4c). During the pre‐hibernation period, strong immunoreactivity was observed in the NFL. The GCL displayed heterogeneous staining, with some cells exhibiting positive immunoreactivity while others remained unstained. The IPL showed a diffuse and uniform staining pattern throughout the layer. In the INL, immunoreactivity was detected in a subset of cells, while others showed no staining. The OPL exhibited a diffuse and consistent staining pattern, similar to that of the IPL. In the ONL, immunoreactivity was localized exclusively to the cytoplasm, with no detectable staining in the nuclei. The photoreceptor layer (PRL) exhibited generalized positive immunoreactivity across the entire layer (Figure 4a). During the hibernation period, immunoreactivity was consistently maintained in all retinal layers that exhibited positive staining during the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 4b). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant increase in staining intensity in the NFL (Figure 4e), GCL (Figure 4f), IPL (Figure 4g), INL (Figure 4h), OPL (Figure 4i), and ONL (Figure 4j) during the hibernation period compared to the pre‐hibernation period. In contrast, the PRL exhibited consistent immunoreactivity intensity, with no statistically significant differences observed between the pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods (Figure 4k).

## Glial fibrillary acidic protein
GFAP immunostaining was performed using the cerebral cortex as a positive control, where a typical immunoreaction was observed in glial cells (Figure 5c). During the pre‐hibernation period, strong immunoreactivity was detected in the NFL. In the GCL, positive immunostaining was observed in some cells, while others remained unstained (Figure 5a). In contrast, immunoreactivity in the NFL was maintained during the hibernation period (Figure 5b); however, quantitative image analysis revealed a significant reduction in staining intensity compared to the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 5e). Similarly, the GCL exhibited reduced immunoreactivity during hibernation compared to the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 5f). Notably, no specific immunostaining was observed in retinal layers other than the NFL and GCL during either the pre‐hibernation or hibernation periods.

## Iba‐1
The validation of Iba‐1 immunostaining was performed using hippocampal sections, where expected positive immunoreactivity was observed in microglial cells (Figure 6c). During the pre‐hibernation period, weak immunoreactivity was detected in the IPL, with staining sparsely observed in the nuclei and extensions of some cells. In the INL, positive immunoreactivity was identified in the nuclei of certain cells. In the OPL, immunoreactivity was rarely observed, with only a few cells showing staining in their nuclei (Figure 6a). During the hibernation period, a pronounced increase in immunoreactivity was observed in the IPL (Figure 6b), with image analysis confirming a statistically significant enhancement compared to the pre‐hibernation period (Figure 6e). In the INL, immunoreactivity was detected not only in the nuclei of certain cells but also in the intercellular regions (Figure 6b), with image analysis revealing a significant increase during hibernation (Figure 6f). The OPL displayed a marked increase in immunoreactivity during hibernation (Figure 6b), with statistically significant intensification aligning with the patterns observed in other retinal layers (Figure 6g).

## DISCUSSION
This study represents the first comprehensive investigation of the comparative expression of synaptophysin, NeuN, calbindin‐D28k, parvalbumin, GFAP, and Iba‐1 in the retina of the S. xanthoprymnus during pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods. Our findings reveal significant seasonal variations in the expression of these markers, suggesting that hibernation induces profound molecular and cellular remodeling in the retina. These changes are likely associated with the physiological adaptations required for metabolic and environmental challenges during hibernation.
Synaptophysin, a fundamental component of the synaptic vesicle membrane, is indispensable for the modulation of endocytosis. Furthermore, it facilitates the intracellular trafficking of synaptobrevin II, a critical vesicular SNARE protein involved in the fusion of synaptic vesicles (Gordon et al., 2011; Kwon & Chapman, 2011). Synaptophysin levels have been reported to increase within the IPL, OPL, and ONL following intraocular pressure elevation, with this upregulation being particularly evident in bipolar cells in the innermost IPL. Despite the overall reduction in synapse numbers due to RGC loss, a compensatory mechanism involving increased synaptic vesicle protein expression and immature synapse formation between RGCs and bipolar cells has been observed in the inner retinal layers (Park et al., 2014). In another pathological condition, diabetes, the translation of synaptophysin mRNA is significantly elevated; however, this does not lead to an increase in the native, functional form of the protein. Rather, synaptophysin undergoes posttranslational modification in the form of mannose‐rich glycosylation (D'Cruz et al., 2012). Furthermore, as aging progresses, synaptophysin expression is elevated in the retinal plexiform layers, accompanied by a reduction in postsynaptic protein levels (Chang et al., 2020). In our study, synaptophysin expression was observed in the IPL and OPL during pre‐hibernation, consistent with the findings of (Linberg et al., 2002), who reported robust synaptophysin labeling in these layers in the California ground squirrels (Spermophilus beecheyi) retina during the active period. However, during hibernation, a marked increase in synaptophysin expression was detected in the OPL and IPL. This upregulation may be associated with synaptic remodeling or neurotransmitter exchange processes occurring during prolonged torpor. Moreover, we observed synaptophysin expression in the ONL during hibernation, consistent with studies on retinal synaptic changes under acute high intraocular pressure (Dan et al., 2008), indicating that hibernation may induce synaptic plasticity as an adaptive response.
In 1992, NeuN was identified as a soluble nuclear protein with the ability to bind DNA loosely in vitro (Mullen et al., 1992). Subsequently, Lind et al. (2005) discovered that NeuN is phosphorylated, and its immunoreactivity depends on its phosphorylation state (Lind et al., 2005). Later, it was revealed that NeuN is the gene product of RBFOX3, a member of the RNA‐binding RBFOX gene family (Kim et al., 2009). In the central nervous system, RBFOX3 is crucial for synaptic function and is required for synaptogenesis in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (Lin et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2015). However, its specific role in the retina has not yet been fully elucidated. A study on the retina demonstrated that RBFOX3 is developmentally regulated and specifically expressed in horizontal cells, amacrine cells, and ganglion cells. Notably, deletion of RBFOX3 resulted in a reduced thickness of the IPL. Despite this structural alteration, ganglion cell innervation to target brain regions and both image‐forming and non‐image‐forming visual functions were unaffected. Although the loss of retinal RBFOX3 leads to minor alterations in retinal morphology, the authors argue that it remains significant for visual function (Lin et al., 2018). In this study, immunoreactivity was observed in the nuclei of certain cells within the GCL and INL during both hibernation and pre‐hibernation periods. While the immunoreactivity in the GCL remained unchanged between these periods, notable changes were identified in the INL. In the pre‐hibernation period, immunoreactivity was primarily observed in cells near the IPL within the INL, which, based on the literature, are likely amacrine cells. Rare immunoreactivity was also detected in cells adjacent to the OPL, presumed to be horizontal cells. During hibernation, immunoreactivity in the INL increased significantly, with strong reactions observed in cells near both the IPL and OPL. Image analysis further confirmed that immunoreactivity in the INL was markedly higher during hibernation compared to the pre‐hibernation period. Additionally, noise signals were detected from the IPL but were excluded as nonspecific. During hibernation, factors such as reduced temperature, energy limitations, and physiological stress may have triggered specific posttranslational phosphorylation of NeuN in the cells of the INL in the retina, potentially resulting in enhanced immunoreactivity. This may also be related to synaptophysin expression changes. In our study, synaptophysin expression increased during hibernation, accompanied by a similar upregulation of NeuN. Previous research in mice has shown that NeuN plays a key role in regulating adult hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptogenesis (Lin et al., 2016). The parallel increase in NeuN and synaptophysin expression during hibernation suggests that NeuN may also be involved in retinal synaptogenesis during this period, potentially contributing to synaptic changes associated with metabolic and environmental adaptations.
CaBPs exhibit species‐specific variation in their expression patterns but remain consistently localized to distinct neuronal subtypes within the mammalian retina. In ground squirrels during the active period, calbindin is expressed in A‐ and B‐type horizontal cells, several amacrine cell types, and bipolar cells (Cuenca et al., 2002; Linberg et al., 2002). Calbindin‐D28k immunoreactivity was also shown to decrease in horizontal and bipolar cells following retinal detachment in the ground squirrel (Linberg et al., 2002). In the rat retina, the expression of calbindin has been identified in horizontal cells, as well as in subpopulations of ganglion and amacrine cells (Chun et al., 1999; Pasteels et al., 1990). In murine retinas, both calbindin and parvalbumin are expressed in horizontal, amacrine, and ganglion cells, while in humans, calbindin is found in subpopulations of cones, amacrine, bipolar, horizontal, and ganglion cells. Parvalbumin in humans exhibits a similar distribution among these subtypes but is notably absent in the PRL (Kovács‐Öller et al., 2019). In non‐mammalian species, the expression patterns of parvalbumin and calbindin‐D28k show similarities to those observed in mammals. In anuran amphibians such as Rana perezi, calbindin‐D28k has been identified in all cone cells, scattered amacrine and bipolar cells, as well as in cells within the GCL (Morona et al., 2008). In the chicken retina, calbindin‐D28k is localized in bipolar cells, a subset of ganglion and amacrine cells, as well as in double cones (Fosser et al., 2013). In Nothobranchius guentheri, parvalbumin expression has been reported in the RPE, PRL, INL, and GCL (Aragona et al., 2023). In this study, calbindin‐D28k expression during the pre‐hibernation period was observed in some cells within the GCL and INL, consistent with studies in mammals (Chun et al., 1999; Cuenca et al., 2002; Kovács‐Öller et al., 2019; Pasteels et al., 1990). In contrast, parvalbumin immunoreactivity was detected not only in these layers but also in the PRL, exhibiting a pattern different from that of calbindin. The presence of parvalbumin in the PRL, similar to its expression in humans, may be attributed to the dominance of cone cells within this layer (Li, 2020). The localization of calbindin‐D28k‐positive cells within the INL, particularly in regions adjacent to the plexiform layers, suggests that these cells are likely horizontal and amacrine cells. This interpretation is further supported by the findings of (Cuenca et al., 2002) and (Linberg et al., 2002) in ground squirrels, which reported calbindin expression patterns consistent with our observations. In contrast, the more uniform distribution of parvalbumin‐positive cells throughout the INL implies that bipolar cells may also exhibit parvalbumin expression. The presence of parvalbumin and calbindin‐D28k in the plexiform layers suggests their localization within the extensions of retinal neurons that project into these layers. Parvalbumin‐positive neural projections originating from GCL cells were observed extending into the NFL. To accurately identify the specific retinal cell types expressing calbindin‐D28k and parvalbumin, further studies employing advanced immunofluorescence and multiplex labeling techniques are necessary. During the hibernation period, calbindin‐D28k immunoreactivity showed a marked reduction in the INL and plexiform layers. Conversely, parvalbumin expression increased in all retinal layers that demonstrated immunoreactivity during the pre‐hibernation period. The EF‐hand protein family exhibits significant structural variability in its calcium‐binding motifs, which determines the binding affinities and kinetics of CaBPs (Gifford et al., 2007). This diversity is pivotal in regulating the spatiotemporal dynamics of cytosolic Ca2+ signals and neuronal excitability. Calbindin rapidly binds Ca2+ during the rising phase of calcium transients, truncating stimulus‐induced Ca2+ signals and reducing spiking activity throughout depolarization. Their buffering capacity also delays the Ca2+‐dependent inactivation of voltage‐gated calcium channels, extending the duration of neuronal signaling (Blatow et al., 2003). The marked reduction in calbindin‐D28k immunoreactivity during hibernation can be interpreted as an adaptive mechanism in response to the significant decrease in neural stimulation and the absence of visual input. Calbindin, as a fast‐binding calcium‐buffering protein, plays a critical role in modulating transient calcium signals during periods of heightened neuronal activity, particularly in response to visual stimuli. However, during hibernation, the retina is largely inactive due to the lack of visual environmental stimuli, resulting in diminished neural activity and a reduced demand for rapid calcium buffering. In contrast, parvalbumin binds Ca2+ with a delay, leaving the initial rise in Ca2+ levels unaffected. Upon activation, parvalbumin accelerates the decay phase, making Ca2+ signals more transient. During later stages, Ca2+ dissociates from parvalbumin, potentially enhancing neuronal excitability. These distinct kinetic profiles highlight the specialized roles of EF‐hand proteins in shaping intracellular calcium dynamics and neuronal signaling (Blatow et al., 2003; Schwaller, 2010). Parvalbumin expression has been reported to change dynamically in response to circadian rhythms, with levels in AII amacrine cells fluctuating up to 15‐fold during daily cycles. Additionally, parvalbumin‐positive neurons, such as bipolar cells and horizontal cells, emerge during specific periods (Gábriel et al., 2003; Kovács‐Öller et al., 2019). The increase in parvalbumin expression during hibernation may be related to mechanisms observed in circadian rhythms, where parvalbumin levels fluctuate to accommodate different physiological demands. During hibernation, the absence of visual stimulation and reduced retinal activity might influence parvalbumin expression to support prolonged calcium signaling and maintain neuronal stability.
GFAP, a well‐established marker of gliosis, is primarily expressed by astrocytes under physiological conditions in the retina. However, in response to injury or neurodegenerative processes, its expression is upregulated in Müller cells, reflecting reactive gliosis and retinal stress (De Hoz et al., 2016; Fernández‐Albarral et al., 2022). The reactivation of macroglia can provide early benefits by boosting their metabolic activity, enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms, and reestablishing ionic, water, and neurotransmitter homeostasis. In the early stages or short‐term gliosis, Müller cells become reactive, characterized by morphological hypertrophy, elongation of their cellular processes, and increased proliferation. These reactive responses are further accompanied by elevated production of neurotrophic factors and cytokines, as well as enhanced homeostatic functions, which support neuronal survival and communication (Peña & Vazquez, 2022; Yoo et al., 2021). In the 13‐lined ground squirrel, GFAP expression at postnatal day 15 and subsequent stages was observed in the GCL, consistent with the location of astrocytes and Müller cell endfeet. However, the labeling did not replicate the morphology of Müller cell endfeet, as revealed by GS and Rlbp1 probes, leading the authors to suggest that GFAP expression is restricted to astrocytes (Kandoi et al., 2022). In humans, retinal astrocytes exhibit two distinct morphologies: elongated forms located within the NFL and stellate forms in the GCL. However, in rats and mice, only stellate astrocytes forming a plexus parallel to the retinal surface have been reported (De Hoz et al., 2016; Pekny et al., 2016). In the present study, we observed GFAP‐positive cells in both the NFL and GCL during the pre‐hibernation and hibernation periods. As reported in studies on ground squirrels under normal physiological conditions (Linberg et al., 2002), these GFAP‐positive cells are likely astrocytes. Notably, GFAP expression was significantly higher in both the NFL and GCL during the pre‐hibernation period compared to the hibernation phase. This heightened expression in GFAP‐positive cells during pre‐hibernation is unlikely to be associated with Müller cell‐mediated pathological or reactive processes, as no immunoreactivity was observed extending into other retinal layers—a key indicator of Müller cell involvement under stress or injury. This interpretation is further supported by Linberg et al. (2002), who demonstrated that even under conditions of retinal detachment in ground squirrels, GFAP expression remained confined to astrocytes in the NFL, with no significant labeling of Müller cells. Taken together, these findings suggest that the increased GFAP expression in astrocytes during pre‐hibernation is linked to physiological adaptations to metabolic or oxidative stress. These stressors may arise as part of the preparatory changes preceding hibernation, inducing astrocytic upregulation of GFAP to fulfill a protective or homeostatic role in retinal maintenance. This observation highlights the dynamic responses of GFAP‐positive astrocytes to stressors associated with seasonal metabolic shifts.
Microglial cells exhibit remarkable flexibility in adapting their functional states based on environmental stimuli, ensuring the preservation of tissue balance within the CNS (Orihuela et al., 2016). Their activation is often described along a continuum, with two prominent states at opposite ends: M1, or classical activation, and M2, referred to as alternative activation (Sica & Mantovani, 2012; Tang & Le, 2016). The M1 state is typically triggered by pro‐inflammatory signals like interferon‐γ and lipopolysaccharide, leading to the production of various inflammatory molecules, including TNF‐α, interleukins (IL‐6, IL‐1β, IL‐12), and chemokines (Colonna & Butovsky, 2017). M1 microglia also upregulate oxidative stress mediators, such as NADPH oxidase. Furthermore, they express surface molecules like major histocompatibility complex‐II and co‐stimulatory proteins that collectively exacerbate neuroinflammation and contribute to neuronal damage (Colonna & Butovsky, 2017; Nguyen et al., 2017). Conversely, M2 microglia are activated by anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐4 and IL‐13. M2 microglia secrete a variety of molecules that support tissue repair and reduce inflammation. They also release anti‐inflammatory cytokines like IL‐10 and transforming growth factor‐beta. In addition, M2 microglia produce neurotrophic factors, such as brain‐, glial‐, and nerve‐derived growth factors, along with neurotrophins, which collectively support neuronal survival and facilitate CNS regeneration (Colonna & Butovsky, 2017). These factors promote tissue repair by facilitating extracellular matrix remodeling, clearance of cellular debris, and neuronal protection (Tang & Le, 2016). In retinal studies, microglia are activated and polarized toward the pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype during rapid rod degeneration, emphasizing their role in neuroinflammation and retinal neurodegeneration (Zhou et al., 2017). In our study, consistent with the literature (Karlstetter et al., 2015), we identified microglial cells using the pan‐microglial marker Iba‐1. During the pre‐hibernation period, Iba‐1 expression was observed primarily in the OPL, with rare expression in the IPL. Notably, during the hibernation period, we detected a significant increase in Iba‐1 expression, including additional expression in the INL. Given that microglial cells are characterized by their cellular processes, the intensified Iba‐1 staining within the plexiform layers during hibernation indicates an upregulation of Iba‐1 expression in these extensions. The increase in Iba‐1 expression during hibernation suggests a potential rise in microglial activity, which may be associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, cellular debris clearance, and neuronal protection. This upregulation likely reflects a shift toward the M2 microglial phenotype, which is known to facilitate neuroprotective functions. However, to conclusively determine the microglial subpopulations (M1/M2), further studies utilizing specific markers for these phenotypes are required.

## CONCLUSION
This study reveals significant molecular and cellular adaptations in the retina of S. xanthoprymnus during hibernation. Increased synaptophysin expression highlights synaptic plasticity, while dynamic changes in NeuN, calbindin‐D28k, and parvalbumin reflect shifts in neuronal activity and calcium homeostasis under reduced metabolic conditions. The observed downregulation of GFAP underscores astrocytic adaptations to seasonal stress, whereas the upregulation of Iba‐1 expression suggests enhanced microglial activity, likely supporting extracellular matrix remodeling, debris clearance, and neuronal protection. These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying retinal neuroplasticity and glial responses during hibernation, offering a foundation for future studies on neuroprotective strategies in low metabolic states.
