Linking Mitochondrial Gene Dysregulation to Immune Cell Shifts in Retinitis Pigmentosa.

# Linking Mitochondrial Gene Dysregulation to Immune Cell Shifts in Retinitis Pigmentosa

## Abstract
Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a hereditary retinal disease characterized by the progressive degeneration of photoreceptor and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, ultimately leading to significant vision loss. While the pathogenesis of RP is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-inflammatory responses, the specific interplay between disrupted mitochondrial gene expression and immune cell infiltration remains inadequately understood. While gene therapy has been approved for a subset of RP patients with monogenic mutations such as RPE65-associated RP, it has notable limitations beyond high costs: it only targets single-gene defects and fails to reverse advanced retinal degeneration that has already damaged photoreceptors. Thus, a robust understanding of these relationships could unveil novel therapeutic strategies and mechanistic insights into RP. In this study, we employed a rat model of RP to elucidate the interconnections between mitochondrial and immune responses. We utilized RNA sequencing to analyze retinal transcriptomes, focusing on identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) linked to mitochondrial function and immune dynamics. Additionally, we performed immune profiling to investigate the composition and alterations of infiltrating immune cell types within the RP retina. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed a plethora of DEGs significantly enriched in mitochondrial and immune pathways. Notably, we identified 280 mitochondrial-related DEGs, among which 10 hub genes (such as Uqcrh and Cox5b) demonstrated critical roles in oxidative phosphorylation and correlated with functional declines in vision. Immune profiling highlighted a pronounced inflammatory shift, characterized by elevated neutrophils and γδ T cells, alongside diminished regulatory T cells and resting natural killer (NK) cells. Further analysis established significant correlations between the ratios of these immune cell types and the expression levels of mitochondrial hub genes. Transcriptional profiling reveals co-occurring alterations in mitochondrial gene expression and immune cell infiltration in RP, with statistical correlations suggesting potential interactions between these two processes, though the direction and mechanism of this interaction require further functional validation. The identification of mitochondrial hub genes and their association with immune cell dynamics not only deepens our understanding of RP but also sets the stage for developing novel therapeutic approaches aimed at mitigating the disruptive effects of these pathways in RP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-025-05564-3.

## Introduction
Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) refers to an inherited retinal disorder characterized by the progressive degeneration of photoreceptor cells and retinal pigment epithelium cells, which leads to gradual vision loss and, in many cases, blindness. RP has been related to mutations in more than 60 different genes [1]. While studies have suggested that activated microglia, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-inflammatory responses contribute to disease progression, the detailed mechanisms underlying RP remain poorly understood [1–3]. Gaining a deeper understanding of its pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets would be of significant clinical importance for improving outcomes in RP patients.
Several studies have highlighted the pivotal role of mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-inflammatory responses in the progression of RP [1]. Mitochondrial abnormalities in retinal degenerative diseases involve dysfunction in energy production, oxidative stress, mtDNA mutations, calcium dysregulation, mitophagy, and altered mitochondrial dynamics, all contributing to retinal cell death [4, 5]. Activated microglia and the infiltration of immune cells, such as macrophages, have been observed in RP models [4]. Chronic oxidative stress and inflammation exacerbate retinal damage, leading to progressive vision loss. Moreover, a dysregulated immune response, triggered by factors like abnormal glucose levels or environmental stressors, may activate pro-inflammatory pathways [6]. Key inflammatory factors involved in this process include TNFα, TLR signaling, NLRP3 inflammasome, chemokine signaling and JAK/STAT signaling, all of which contribute to neuronal loss and structural destruction in the retina [7, 8]. These findings underscore the critical roles of mitochondrial dysfunction and immune activation in RP development, suggesting they could serve as potential targets for therapeutic intervention.
Accumulating evidence has suggested a complex interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-inflammatory responses in RP [9]. In RP, mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to metabolic changes in retinal cells that trigger immune activation and inflammation. Damaged mitochondria generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that triggers inflammatory pathways, such as NF-κB activation, resulting in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and immune cell infiltration [9]. Additionally, insulin resistance, which is linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, also disrupts the function of regulatory T cells (Tregs), leading to unchecked inflammation that accelerates retinal degeneration [10–12]. While the individual roles of mitochondrial dysfunction and immune inflammation in RP pathogenesis are increasingly recognized, the complex mechanisms underlying their interaction and the specific link between altered mitochondrial gene expression and changes in the retinal immune landscape remain poorly defined.
Therefore, addressing this critical gap, this study employed an integrated approach combining bioinformatic analysis of transcriptomic data from a rat model of RP with immune cell infiltration profiling. We aimed to identify deregulated mitochondria-related genes and, crucially, to elucidate their specific relationship with the types and proportions of immune cells infiltrating the retina during disease progression. By revealing significant correlations between the expression of key mitochondrial genes and specific immune cell populations, our findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the mitochondrial-immune interactions driving RP pathology and point towards promising therapeutic targets related to this interaction.

## Materials and Methods

## Animals
Retinal dystrophy rats (RDY) and homozygous Royal College of Surgeons rats (RCS), both 8 weeks old and weighing 180–200 g, were obtained from the Xian Provincial Experimental Animal Research Center (Xian, China). RDY rats were used as the control group, while RCS rats are widely recognized as a model for studying RP due to their inherent Mertk (-/-) mutation, in which genetic alteration impairs the RPE cells’ ability to process and clear desquamated photoreceptor outer segment debris. The animals were randomly assigned to two groups: control (RDY, n = 30) and RP (RCS, n = 30). They were housed in a controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle. All experimental procedures were conducted per the National Institutes of Health guidelines and approved by the ethics committee of Shenzhen Eye Hospital (Guangdong, China).

## Retinal Histology and Morphology
At 8 weeks of age, the RCS and RDY rats were euthanized, and their eyeballs were carefully extracted for histological analysis. The retinal tissues were rapidly dissected and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde. After embedding in paraffin, 5 µm-thick serial sections were prepared using an RM2125 microtome (Leica). Retinal morphology and histological features were assessed by hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining. The thickness of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) was measured at distances of ± 800, ± 1600, ± 2400, and ± 3200 µm from the optic nerve head (ONH) (Fig. 7A). Additionally, the ONL thickness and the number of ONL cells within the retinal region spanning from −800 to 800 µm around the ONH were evaluated using ImageJ 1.47 software.

## Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) and Fundus Photography
To evaluate retinal morphology and structure, OCT was performed using a Retinal Imaging Microscope (Micron IV; Phoenix Research Lab, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Fundus photography was also conducted for comprehensive retinal assessment.

## Electroretinography (ERG) Analysis
ERG analysis was performed using the UTAS Visual Diagnostic System with a Big Shot Ganzfeld (LKC Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Rats were dark-adapted overnight and anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.; Sanshu). Oxybuprocaine and tropicamide phenylephrine were applied for corneal anesthesia and pupil dilation. Stimuli consisted of white light flashes (intensity = 3.0 cd.s/m2). The signals were amplified with a bandpass filter (1–300 Hz) and processed with a 50-Hz notch filter. Ten scotopic and 60 photopic responses were recorded to analyze the a-b wave amplitudes using ERG View v4.380 R software [13].

## Transcriptome Analysis
Retinas from rats in the RCS and RDY groups were collected, with four individual retinas pooled into one sample and three samples analyzed per group. Before euthanasia, all rats were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to remove blood vessel-associated immune cells. RNA sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ platform (BGI, Huada Gene Technologies Ltd, Guangdong, China). Data analysis was conducted on the Dr. Tom II network platform provided by BGI (https://biosys.bgi.com).

## Differentially Expressed Gene Analysis
Sequencing data were processed using SOAPnuke (v1.5.6) for filtering and FASTQ for quality control (QC). The cleaned data were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) was then used to align the clean data to the reference gene set, and gene expression levels were determined with RSEM (v1.2.8). A heatmap was generated using Pheatmap (v1.0.8). DEGs were identified based on a q value < 0.05 and a fold change ≥ 2.0 and analyzed with DESeq2 (v1.4.5). All analyses were carried out on the Dr. Tom II network platform (https://biosys.bgi.com).

## Functional Enrichment Analysis
GSEA was performed using the R package "clusterProfiler" with the reference gene set "c2.cp.v7.2.symbols.gmt" (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). The analysis was conducted with 10,000 permutations, and the threshold of significance was set to 10. GO enrichment analysis was performed and visualized using the Dr. Tom II network platform. Additionally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the Metascape platform and visualized via the bioinformatics analysis platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn), with q value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Identification of Mitochondria-Related DEGs (MitoDEGs)
MitoCarta3.0, a mitochondrial protein database (http://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta), was visited to obtain 1,140 mitochondria-localized genes [14]. MitoDEGs were obtained via intersecting the DEGs and the 1,140 mitochondria-localized genes using a Venn Diagram, and they were visualized via the bioinformatics analysis platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

## Analysis of Protein–Protein Interactions (PPI) and Identification of Hub Genes
PPI analysis of the shared MitoDEGs was performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), and the results were visualized in Cytoscape 3.9.1 [15, 16]. The CytoHubba and MCODE plugins within Cytoscape 3.9.1 were employed for screening the hub MitoDEGs. Significant gene clusters were identified within the PPI network using the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape, applying the following parameters: degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, k-core = 3, and max depth = 100. The resulting hub MitoDEGs were further validated against retinal disease-associated genes from RetNet (https://retnet.org/summaries#a-genes) and RP genes from OMIM (https://www.omim.org), with overlapping candidates visualized via a Venn diagram on the bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn), ultimately highlighting key mitochondrial genes potentially involved in RP.

## Prediction of a Hub MitoDEGs-Transcription Factors (TF) -miRNAs Network
To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of hub MitoDEGs, we predicted their upstream transcription factors (TFs) using the iRegulon plugin within Cytoscape 3.9.1. Additionally, miRNAs that interact with these hub genes were identified through the miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de) [17]. The resulting interactions among hub MitoDEGs, their corresponding TFs, and miRNAs were then visualized as a comprehensive network in Cytoscape 3.9.1.

## Immune Infiltration Analysis
Immune cell infiltration analysis is commonly employed to investigate disease pathogenesis. The ImmuCC algorithm, paired with the mouse-specific immune cell signature matrix from the study was utilized to determine the relative proportions of various immune cell types in both healthy and RP samples [18]. This matrix was selected based on high sequence homology (≥ 90%) of core immune cell marker genes between mice and rats (per Ensembl Biomart release 110), ensuring reliable cross-species application to rat retinal tissues [19]. Box plots were generated to visually compare the immune cell distributions between the two groups. To evaluate differences in immune cell proportions, we applied the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between hub MitoDEGs and immune cell types.

## RT-qPCR Validation Analysis
Total RNA was isolated from retinal samples using the EastepTM Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. LS1040, Promega). cDNA synthesis was then carried out using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Cat. No. RR600A, Takara Bio, Inc.) along with the gDNA Remover. For real-time PCR, the S6 Universal SYBR qPCR mix (NovaBio) was used in conjunction with a Bio-Rad iCycler Real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The primers for the hub MitoDEGs are listed in supplementary Table 1. Relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

## Correlation Between Hub MitoDEGs and Retinal Function
The correlation between retinal morphology (ONL thickness) and function (a-b wave amplitudes in ERG) with hub MitoDEGs expression was assessed using the Pearson algorithm. Hub MitoDEGs expression is assessed by the number of PCR cycles in RT-qPCR.

## Statistical Analysis
The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Normality was checked with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Overview of the Analysis Strategy
This study employed a comprehensive, integrated bioinformatic and experimental approach using the RCS rat model of retinitis pigmentosa (RP) to systematically investigate the interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune-inflammatory responses at the molecular level (illustrated in the workflow diagram, Fig. 1). We initiated our analysis with RNA sequencing of retinal tissues from RCS rats and control RDY rats to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) underlying RP pathogenesis. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG pathway analysis, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), were subsequently performed to characterize the biological processes and pathways significantly impacted, with a particular focus on mitochondrial and immune-related functions. To pinpoint specific mitochondrial genes involved, we cross-referenced the DEGs with the MitoCarta database to identify mitochondrial-related DEGs (MitoDEGs). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed from these MitoDEGs, allowing for the identification of key hub genes, which represent highly connected nodes likely playing central roles. The differential expression of selected hub MitoDEGs was experimentally validated using RT-qPCR, and their association with functional visual decline was assessed by correlating their expression levels with electroretinography (ERG) parameters and retinal morphology (ONL thickness). In parallel, we performed immune infiltration analysis using the ImmuCC algorithm to quantify the proportions of various immune cell types present in the retinas of RP rats compared to controls. The critical step involved investigating the relationship between mitochondrial gene expression and the immune microenvironment: we specifically correlated the expression levels of the identified mitochondrial hub genes with the relative abundance of key immune cell populations. Finally, potential regulatory mechanisms upstream of the hub MitoDEGs were explored through prediction of transcription factors and miRNAs. This systematic workflow enabled us to dissect the molecular landscape of RP, revealing significant links between mitochondrial gene alterations and changes in the retinal immune infiltrate, thereby providing novel insights into RP pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets. ## Transcriptomic Alterations Between RP and Normal Rat Retinas
Retinal tissues were collected from RCS rats (n = 3, disease group) and age-matched RDY rats (n = 3, control group). Total RNA was extracted and subjected to paired-end RNA sequencing (150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Each biological replicate generated approximately 12 GB of raw sequencing data, achieving an average sequencing depth of 40 million reads per sample. Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct molecular signatures between RCS and RDY groups, with PCA demonstrating clear separation along PC1 (40.6% variance explained; Fig. 2A). We identified 3,073 DEGs in RP rats compared to controls, including 1,519 downregulated genes and 1,554 upregulated genes (Fig. 2B-C). Over 60% of DEGs were functionally linked to mitochondrial processes or immune regulation. GSEA robustly confirmed these findings, demonstrating significant activation of multiple immune-related pathways, including innate and adaptive immune responses, leukocyte-mediated immunity, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, and immune effector processes (Fig. 2D-K). Particularly noteworthy was the marked upregulation of key immune regulators such as complement component C1qa and toll-like receptor 4 (Tlr4), which are consistent with prior reports that C1q and Tlr4 are elevated in retinal degenerative diseases including RP[20, 21]. Concurrently, pathways associated with biotic stimuli response and visual perception were dysregulated. Complementary functional enrichment analyses through GO and KEGG pathway assessments provided deeper mechanistic insights. GO analysis categorized findings into Biological Processes (BP), highlighting visual system development and photoreceptor maintenance, Cellular Components (CC) emphasizing mitochondrial architecture, and Molecular Functions (MF) focusing on energy metabolism and ion transport (Fig. 3A-D, Supplementary Table 2). KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways related to neurodegeneration, including Oxidative phosphorylation, Chemical carcinogenesis- reactive oxygen species, and immune-related processes such as Phagosome formation and Antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 3E-F, Supplementary Table 3). Of particular interest was the significant downregulation of crucial mitochondrial genes, including Ndufa4 (NADH dehydrogenase subunit) and Cox6a1 (cytochrome c oxidase subunit)—findings that align remarkably with human RP datasets (GEO: GSE115828). These deregulated mitochondrial genes/pathways likely contribute to the observed immune activation through ROS accumulation and damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMPs) release. ## Identification of Mitochondrial Hub Genes and Regulatory Networks in RP
To dissect the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in RP pathogenesis, we integrated transcriptomic profiling with the MitoCarta3.0 database to identify hub mitochondria-associated DEGs (MitoDEGs) and their regulatory networks. Screening of MitoDEGs revealed 280 genes dysregulated in RP, including 84 downregulated and 196 upregulated candidates (Fig. 4A-C). PPI network analysis using STRING and Cytoscape revealed a highly interconnected module containing 34 nodes with 1,100 edges (Fig. 4D-E) and identified 10 hub MitoDEGs (Ndufb5, Uqcrh, Ndufv2, Cox5b, Cox4i1, Uqcrfs1, Ndufa6, Cox5a, Uqcrb, Ndufb7) using CytoHubba’s MCC algorithm (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Table 4). Notably, while all these hub MitoDEGs are annotated in the retinal disease database RetNet, none are currently listed among known RP-associated genes in the OMIM database, suggesting their potential as novel candidates for RP pathogenesis. Ventricular expression of 10 hub MitoDEGs was validated in rats using qRT-PCR. Compared to the CON group, four genes, including Uqcrh, Cox4i1, Uqcrb, and Cox5b, showed significantly reduced expression in the RP group (q < 0.05) (Fig. 4G). Further regulatory network analysis predicted 14 TFs targeting these hub genes, including Yy1, Gfi1b, Sox2, Foxo3, Cdx1, Mef2a, Esrra, Mybl2, Nr2f1, Bdp1, Jazf1, Erg, Ehf, and Nr5a2 (Fig. 4H, Supplementary Table 5), and miRWalk analysis identified 148 miRNA interactions, including four key miRNAs (rno-miR-149-3p, rno-miR-3573-5p, rno-miR-3584-5p, and rno-miR-743b-5p) showing multi-targeting capabilities (Fig. 4I, Supplementary Table 6). Among these, four key miRNAs were highlighted: rno-miR-149-3p, which interacts with Ndufb7, Uqcrb, and Cox5b; rno-miR-3573-5p, associated with Uqcrfs1, Uqcrb, and Cox5b; rno-miR-3584-5p, linked to Ndufb7, Uqcrb, and Cox5b; and rno-miR-743b-5p, targeting Ndufb7, Uqcrb, and Ndufa6. ## Immune Cell Infiltration in RP
We performed immune cell infiltration on retina tissue from RP and control groups and assessed the proportions of 25 immune cell types using bulk RNA-seq data [18]. Statistical comparisons were applied to identify differentially infiltrated immune cells between RP and control groups. Box plots were employed to illustrate the immune cell composition in the two groups, revealing significant differences in 7 immune cell types (q < 0.05). Specifically, Mast cells, M0 Macrophages, Treg cells, DC-activated cells and Resting NK cells were notably more abundant in the RP group, whereas Naive CD4 T cells, Th2 cells, and immature DCs were more prevalent in the CON group (Fig. 5A-C, Supplementary Table 7). Correlation analysis indicated varying degrees of synergistic and competitive interactions among immune cell types. The strongest synergistic relationship was observed between plasma cells and naive B cells (correlation score: 0.997), followed by Treg cells and M0 macrophages (0.993), naive CD4 T cells and Th2 cells (0.993), and Neutrophils and Eosinophils (0.987). Conversely, the strongest competitive interaction was identified between M0 macrophages and activated CD8 T cells (−0.987), followed by Treg cells and activated CD8 T cells (−0.972), and immature DCs and M0 macrophages (−0.971) (Fig. 5D). This analysis highlights the distinct immune cell infiltration patterns and their complex interactions in RP, offering insights into the immune landscape of the disease.Fig. 5Infiltration of immune cell types in RP versus CON. ## Relationship Between Hub MitoDEGs and Immune Cells
Deregulated mitochondria DEGs could be potentially related to the differential immune cell populations observed after immune infiltration. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the relationship between HUB mitochondria-DEGs and immune cell populations in the RP group. Our comprehensive immune correlation analysis revealed distinct associations between mitochondrial hub genes and immune cell populations in RP patients (Fig. 6A-J, Supplementary Table 8). Notably, Cox5a demonstrated particularly strong positive correlations with pro-inflammatory M0 macrophages while exhibiting a negative correlation with naive CD4 T cells. Cox5b demonstrated significant negative correlations with both resting NK cells and immature dendritic cells (DCs). The oxidative phosphorylation component Ndufb7 was positively associated with anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, whereas both Ndufv2 and Uqcrb showed negative correlations with naive CD4 T cells. Besides, Uqcrfs1 displayed a positive association with regulatory T cells (Tregs). However, four hub genes (Ndufb5, Uqcrh, Cox4i1 and Ndufa6) showed no significant immune correlations in our analysis. These findings reveal a complex immunometabolic network in RP, where specific mitochondrial dysfunction patterns are associated with distinct immune cell population changes, potentially contributing to disease progression through altered immune regulation and inflammatory responses. The strong association between electron transport chain components and macrophage polarization states (M0/M2) particularly suggests a crucial role of mitochondrial-immune potential interactions in RP pathogenesis.Fig. 6Correlation between hub MitoDEGs and immune cells in the RP group

## Retinal Morphological and Functional Features in RP Rats and Relationship with Hub MitoDEGs
We selected 8-week-old RCS rats for study as this intermediate disease stage after degeneration onset (18 days) but before completion (3 months) best mirrors the clinical presentation timeline of human RP patients while retaining sufficient retinal tissue for mechanistic investigations of treatable pathology. Histological analysis revealed a significant reduction in the ONL thickness at various retinal locations in RP rats (Fig. 7A-B). Additionally, the number of cells in the ONL was markedly lower compared to control rats, as observed in HE staining (Fig. 7C-E). Fundoscopic examination demonstrated characteristic features in RP rats, including retinal pigmentation and attenuated blood vessels (Fig. 7F). The four hub MitoDEGs (Uqcrh, Cox4i1, Uqcrb, and Cox5b) with distinct differential expression between the RP and CON groups were further analyzed for their associations with both functional and structural markers of disease progression in RP model rats (Fig. 7K-N). Uqcrh expression showed strong negative correlations with a-b wave amplitudes in both 3.0 dark-adapted (r = −0.870; p = 0.024) and photo-adapted (r = −0.837; p = 0.038) conditions, while Cox4i1 exhibited even stronger associations (dark-adapted: r = −0.905, p = 0.013; photo-adapted: r = −0.888, p = 0.018). The most robust correlations were observed for Uqcrb (dark-adapted: r = −0.922, p = 0.009; photo-adapted: r = −0.885, p = 0.019) and particularly Cox5b, which demonstrated strongly negative correlations (dark-adapted: r = −0.990, p < 0.001; light-adapted: r = −0.985, p < 0.001). Similarly, these hub MitoDEGs expression maintained significant negative correlations with ERG response intensities and showed positive correlations with ONL thickness (all p < 0.05), with Cox5b again displaying the strongest association (Fig. 7K-N, Supplementary Table 9). These consistent multi-parametric correlations across different stimulus intensities and structural measures demonstrate that the degree of mitochondrial gene dysregulation directly corresponds to both functional visual impairment and structural photoreceptor degeneration in RP. The particularly robust associations observed for Cox5b suggest it may play a central role in disease progression, potentially serving as a key molecular link between mitochondrial dysfunction and photoreceptor degeneration.

## Predicted Drugs Targeting the Hub MitoDEGs
To identify potential therapeutic agents for RP, we predicted six drugs targeting the four MitoDEGs, including COMPOUND 9, TERPESTACIN, AJMALICINE, TIQ-15, BERBERINE, and COMPOUND 46C. To evaluate the binding affinity of the candidate drugs to their respective protein targets, molecular docking analysis was conducted using Autodock Vina v.1.2.2. The binding energies for these interactions were calculated (Fig. 8A-F). COX4I1 exhibited a binding energy of −6.341 kcal/mol with COMPOUND 9, while COX5B displayed variable binding energies with the other compounds: −7.279 kcal/mol (AJMALICINE), −6.522 kcal/mol (TIQ-15), −6.195 kcal/mol (BERBERINE), and −7.21 kcal/mol (COMPOUND 46C). ## Discussion
Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a major inherited blindness-causing disease, with mitochondrial dysfunction and immune inflammation implicated in its pathogenesis, but the link between specific mitochondrial gene alterations and retinal immune composition remains unclear [2]. Our study addresses this gap by analyzing transcriptomic and immune infiltration data in an RP rat model, identifying key correlations between mitochondrial hub genes and immune cells that have not been previously characterized.
Mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the important causes of RP. In RP, mitochondrial dysfunction refers to the failure of mitochondria in essential cellular processes such as energy production, metabolic regulation, cell signaling, and oxidative stress response [4]. This dysfunction can result in a cascade of pathological changes that contribute to cellular and organ damage. Key mitochondrial proteins including Uqcrh, Cox4i1, Uqcrb, and Cox5b, are central to mitochondrial function, particularly in maintaining the mitochondrial respiratory chain, and their dysregulation is thought to exacerbate RP progression. Uqcrh (Ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase hinge protein) and Uqcrb (Ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase binding protein) dysregulation likely disrupts mitochondrial complex III function, reducing ATP production and increasing oxidative stress to accelerate photoreceptor degeneration [22–25]. Cox4i1 (Cytochrome C oxidase subunit 4 isoform 1) and Cox5b (Cytochrome c oxidase subunit 5B) deficiency may impair complex IV activity, leading to ROS accumulation and RPE cell damage [26]. Importantly, these four genes strongly correlated with retinal structure (ONL thickness) and function (ERG a-b wave amplitude), confirming their relevance to RP severity. Our core data further reveal a coordinated alteration: these hub genes’ downregulation coincided with pathological immune shifts in RP (2.3-fold higher M0 macrophages, 40% lower naive CD4 + T cells compared to control), with significant correlations between hub gene expression and immune cell ratios—establishing a transcriptional association between mitochondrial and immune pathways in RP.
Notably, we detected low-abundance B cells, DCs, neutrophils, and Th2 cells in wild-type retinas, consistent with the “non-absolute” retinal immune privilege. Our transcriptomic data confirmed DC markers (Cd11c, H2-Aa/H2-Eb1) in controls, aligned with prior reports of Ia⁺ DCs in rat ciliary epithelium [27] and classical DCs in normal rat posterior uvea [28]; neutrophils accounted for ≤ 5% of immune cells (matching low basal levels in normal retinas) [29, 30]; B/Th2 cells’ low frequencies reflect naive cell trafficking to maintain homeostasis which is supported by ocular immune studies [31, 32]—validating the biological rationality of our immune infiltration results. In RP, the immune microenvironment is skewed toward suppression: increased Tregs, M0 macrophages, and mast cells, a profile linked to chronic retinal inflammation and tissue damage. Mast cells may drive degeneration by releasing pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), consistent with studies showing choroidal mast cell activation exacerbates retinal oxidative stress [33–35]. For T cells, RP-specific elevation of Tregs and activated DCs, paired with abnormal T cell autoantigen responses as reported in RCS rats, disrupts immune tolerance, while excessive Tregs may impair clearance of damaged cells evidenced by our finding of negative correlations between Tregs/M0 macrophages and CD8⁺T cells. This imbalance not only fuels chronic inflammation but also limits immune surveillance, further aggravating retinal degeneration. Additionally, RP patients exhibit increased levels of activated CD8 + cytotoxic T cells and CD4 + helper T cells in peripheral blood (both contributing to tissue damage), with heightened CD26 expression on T cells suggesting enhanced Th1 responses—this may drive cytotoxic attacks on retinal cells to exacerbate degeneration [36, 37]. Notably, an imbalance between T cells and Tregs worsens retinal degeneration; while Tregs are essential for immune tolerance, their overabundance causes excessive immune suppression, impairing clearance of damaged retinal cells and tissue repair [38–40]. Our data further show negative correlations between M0 macrophages/Tregs and CD8 + T cells in RP, indicating immune suppression may limit the immune system’s capacity to clear damaged cells, further promoting disease progression. Collectively, these findings highlight that immune modulation—such as regulating Treg abundance or enhancing CD8 + T cell activity—could be a therapeutic strategy to improve retinal health by optimizing immune surveillance and repair.
Mitochondrial metabolism plays a crucial role in regulating the fate and function of immune cells, and our study highlights the significant interaction between mitochondrial pathways and the immune microenvironment in RP. Correlation analysis indicated that the mitochondrial proteins Cox5b, Uqcrh and Uqcrb were positively associated with T cells, while Cox4i1 exhibited a positive correlation with immature DCs, and all were negatively associated with mast cells. These findings align with RP’s altered immune landscape. Moreover, these hub MitoDEGs were strongly positively related to retinal ONL thickness and visual function, underscoring their potential role in maintaining retinal health. These findings identify the four hub genes as potential immunometabolism regulators in RP and highlight candidate therapeutic targets, which may offer new avenues for clinical treatment and intervention in RP. However, our study has limitations: (1) Hub gene validation was limited to RCS rats, lacking other RP models or clinical data; (2) Bioinformatic correlations do not confirm functional mechanisms; (3) Microglia—the most prevalent retinal immune cells—were excluded due to the mouse-specific matrix’s limitation. To address these, we plan follow-up experiments: validating hub genes in additional models, performing functional assays (e.g., mitochondrial gene perturbation and immune profiling), and quantifying retinal microglia via flow cytometry/immunofluorescence (IBA1/CD11b) to analyze their correlation with hub genes.

## Conclusion
In summary, our bioinformatics analysis identified key differences in mitochondrial-related genes and immune cell infiltration between RP and control groups, revealing for the first time potential interactions between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune infiltration in RP. We screened and validated four hub genes (Uqcrh, Cox4i1, Uqcrb, and Cox5b) in RP. These genes were positively correlated with T cells and negatively correlated with mast cells and closely associated with retinal ONL thickness and visual function. These findings suggest that these hub MitoDEGs are critical regulators of immunometabolism in RP pathophysiology. Our results provide new insights into RP mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets, offering a foundation for future strategies to alleviate retinitis degeneration.
